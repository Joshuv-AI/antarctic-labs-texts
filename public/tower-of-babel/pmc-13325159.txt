Controlling the Polarity of Ag:PSS Polyelectrolyte Interfacial Layers in Organic Solar Cells
Authors: Jin Hee Lee, Ju Hwan Kang, Yeasin Khan, Min-Jae Maeng, Yu Jung Park, Bright Walker, Yongsup Park, Jung Hwa Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A critical factor affecting the efficiency of organic solar cells is the interfacial material between the electrode and the active layer. Developing interlayer systems whose electronic properties can be systematically tuned through simple compositional modification is attractive because it may enable more symmetric device architectures, simplified processing, and improved control of interfacial energetics. In this study, we prepared an interfacial control material by replacing p-doped poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT) in the well-known hole-transporting material (HTM) PEDOT:poly­(styrenesulfonate) (PSS) with silver (Ag+ ions). Ag:PSS by itself was found to have insulating properties and did not yield compelling results as an HTM alone. However, we were able to control the polarity of Ag:PSS by mixing it with either PEDOT:PSS or poly­(ethylenimine) (PEI), enabling its use as both HTM and electron transport material (ETM), respectively. By employing p-type and n-type Ag:PSS, we achieved power conversion efficiencies of up to 8.482% with a PTB7:PC71BM active layer. To analyze the effects on band structure, we used ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and inverse photoemission spectroscopy (IPES) to reveal systematic changes in energy bands that followed the interlayer composition. Our study demonstrates how compositional modification of Ag:PSS influences interfacial energetics and charge extraction behavior.

Introduction

Organic solar cells (OSCs) have garnered significant attention in recent decades due to ongoing advancements in material design and device optimization aimed at achieving higher power conversion efficiency (PCE). , The performance of OSCs is primarily influenced by the open-circuit voltage (V OC), short-circuit current (J SC), and fill factor (FF). To enhance various parameters, a photoactive layer with appropriately aligned energy levels and interlayers designed to efficiently transport holes and electrons were developed. − Specifically, regulating the movement of holes and electrons at the interface between the photoactive layer and the electrode is crucial for achieving a high PCE.

The hole transport material (HTM) in the conventional structure primarily serves as the interface layer of the transparent electrode. Ensuring high transparency from visible light to the near-infrared region enhances the light collection efficiency of the bulk heterojunction. Poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly­(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS), a commonly used HTM material, offers optical transparency, high work function (WF) (>5.0 eV), and p-type conductivity. − However, it is susceptible to moisture absorption, leading to reduced electrical conductivity and stability issues due to oxidation from its high acidity. − Beyond the HTM, which directly impacts solar cell performance, the electron transport material (ETM) also plays a crucial role. ETM materials encompass a broad range, including metal oxides, − conjugated polyelectrolytes (CPE), , and nonconjugated polyelectrolytes (NPE). , Unlike metal oxides, CPE serves as an effective interfacial material processed at lower temperatures and offers compatibility with organic layers. − By introducing polarity into the polymer, self-assembling dipoles facilitate efficient carrier injection and extraction. , Furthermore, their typically thin nature ensures minimal interference with optical properties.

Beyond organic materials employed as interfacial layers, inorganic materials are also extensively utilized due to their performance and stability advantages. , Among these, silver serves as a versatile material for enhancing device efficiency across various layers. , It appears in multiple forms, including silver nanowires (AgNW) and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs). Notably, AgNW offers high conductivity and acts as an alternative electrode to indium tin oxide (ITO), a widely used transparent electrode. Similarly, AgNPs find application in diverse device structures due to their effectiveness. To capitalize on the benefits of silver, Ag:PSS was developed by incorporating polar Ag+ ions into the polymer (PSS), facilitating the formation of self-assembled dipoles for efficient carrier injection. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of developing interfacial materials with polarity control and defect passivation to improve device robustness and reproducibility, while also emphasizing the role of ionic and dipolar interlayers in tuning interfacial energetics and electronic band structure. − These studies highlight the need for versatile interlayers and deeper understanding of how interfacial modification influences electronic structure and device behavior in OSCs. Unlike previously reported metal ion-doped PSS interlayers, which primarily function as p-type hole transport layers, − the Ag:PSS-based system investigated here can be compositionally modified toward either hole- or electron-selective behavior through incorporation of poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly­(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) or poly­(ethylenimine) (PEI), respectively. While pristine Ag:PSS alone exhibited relatively poor device performance, modification with PEDOT:PSS or PEI substantially improved photovoltaic characteristics. To investigate the origin of these changes, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and inverse photoemission spectroscopy (IPES) measurements were performed to examine shifts in work function, occupied electronic states, and unoccupied electronic structure, including highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels. These analyses clarify how compositional modification of Ag:PSS influences interfacial energetics and charge extraction behavior in OSC devices.

Experimental Section

Synthesis and Materials

Ag:PSS was synthesized by mixing aqueous solutions of polystyrene sulfonic acid and silver acetate (generating acetic acid as a byproduct), and isolating Ag:PSS by precipitation in isopropanol and washing, following a previously reported procedure (Scheme ). PEDOT:PSS (Clevios PVP AI 4083) was purchased from Heraeus and used as received. Ag:PSS was dissolved in deionized water at a concentration of 0.1 mg·mL–1. PTB7 and PC71BM were purchased from 1-Materials and used without further purification. PEI (branched, 25,000 MW) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Device Fabrication

OSCs were fabricated with the structure ITO/HTM/PTB7:PC71BM/ETM/Al. ITO-coated glass substrates were first cleaned with detergent, followed by sequential ultrasonication in deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 10 min each. Three types of HTMs were employed: Ag:PSS, p-type Ag:PSS, and PEDOT:PSS. Ag:PSS was prepared at 0.1 mg·mL–1, and p-type Ag:PSS was obtained by mixing 70 vol % of the Ag:PSS solution with 30 vol % of the PEDOT:PSS solution. All HTMs were spin-cast at 2000 rpm for 60 s and annealed at 120 °C on a hot plate under ambient air. The substrates were then transferred to a nitrogen-filled glovebox for subsequent processes. The active layer solution was prepared 1 day in advance and stirred at 45 °C overnight. PTB7 and PC71BM were blended in a 1:1.5 weight ratio in chlorobenzene with 3 vol % DIO as an additive. The active layer was spin-coated at 1300 rpm for 50 s and dried at 80 °C for 10 min under an N2 atmosphere, resulting in a film thickness of ∼100 nm. The ETMs consisted of Ag:PSS, n-type Ag:PSS, and PEI. The n-type Ag:PSS was prepared by adding 3 vol % of a PEI solution (10 mg·mL–1) to 97 vol % of the Ag:PSS solution. ETM layers were spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 60 s and dried at 60 °C for 10 min under an N2 atmosphere. The film thicknesses of the Ag:PSS, p-type Ag:PSS, and n-type Ag:PSS layers were measured using XPS measurements. The approximate thicknesses of the spin-coated films are ∼6.6 nm (Ag:PSS), ∼34 nm (p-type Ag:PSS), and ∼3.2 nm (n-type Ag:PSS) under the processing conditions used in this study. Finally, the devices were transferred into a vacuum chamber, and 80 nm Al electrodes were thermally evaporated under a pressure of about 10–6 Torr.

Device Characterization

The photovoltaic performance of the OSCs was characterized using a Keithley 2400 source meter under AM 1.5 G illumination (100 mW cm–2). A solar simulator (Oriel SRC 1000 TC K KG5 N) and a Si reference cell, calibrated by VLSI Standards Inc., were used for J–V measurements. The series resistance (Rs) was extracted from the slope of the J–V curve in the high forward-bias region, while the parallel (shunt) resistance (Rsh) was determined from the slope near the zero-bias region of the dark J–V characteristics. External quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra were recorded using a MonoRa 500i monochromator, and the light intensity was calibrated with an LS150 device. Detailed device data is included in Supporting Information (Figure S1). XPS spectra were collected using a Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250XI system. In-situ UPS and IPES measurements were performed within the same ultra-high vacuum chamber using a Scienta SES-100 electron energy analyzer and a home-built IPES system, respectively. The HOMO onset was determined by linear extrapolation of the leading edge of the valence band region in the UPS spectra, while the LUMO onset was determined by linear extrapolation of the leading edge of the conduction band region in the IPES spectra. The secondary electron cutoff was obtained by linearly fitting the rising edge of the low kinetic energy cutoff region of the UPS spectra. Based on repeated fitting with slightly varied fitting windows, the uncertainty in determining the HOMO onset and secondary electron cutoff positions is estimated to be approximately ±0.05–0.1 eV and that for the LUMO onset is approximately ±0.15−0.2eV.

Results and Discussion

Scheme illustrates the preparation of Ag:PSS. The OSC devices investigated in this study employed the structure ITO/HTM/PTB7:PC71BM/ETM/Al, as illustrated in Scheme Figure 1a-b. To develop an effective p-type polyelectrolyte, we incorporated Ag+ ions into the PSS polyelectrolyte, where the immobile PSS chains provide relatively inert anions and Ag+ ions act as electron acceptors for intrinsic carriers present in the adjacent semiconductor, thereby lowering the Fermi energy and increasing the concentration of p-type carriers.

Ag+ undergoes a reversible redox reaction at a similar potential as the PEDOT oxidation/p-doping electrochemical reaction. Upon mixing Ag:PSS with PEDOT:PSS, Ag+ cations take the place of holes (radical cations) on PEDOT, while the presence of PEDOT chains in the Ag:PSS interlayer provides conductive pathways which improve the conductivity of the interlayer and lower the series resistance in devices relative to pristine Ag:PSS. , Amine ligands are known to strongly coordinate Ag+ ions, thus, incorporation of PEI may alter the effective redox behavior and electronic properties of Ag-containing species within the interlayer. − Coordination of Ag+ by amines is known to substantially shift Ag+/Ag reduction potentials to more negative values, which is consistent with the observed transition from p-type to n-type interfacial behavior. A schematic diagram showing the structure of the p-type and n-type interlayers is shown in the Supporting Information (Figure S2).

The morphology of the HTM and ETM between the electrode and the active layer significantly affects device performance and was investigated using atomic force microscopy (AFM). Optimized films of Ag:PSS, p-type Ag:PSS, and n-type Ag:PSS on ITO substrates are shown in Figure c-e. Ag:PSS (RMS roughness = 1.83 nm) exhibits higher roughness than the other films and is expected to influence the coating of the active layer. In contrast, p-type Ag:PSS allows smooth coating with minimal roughness. Although n-type Ag:PSS has very high roughness, it does not affect device performance because it is deposited on top of the active layer.

Figure a shows the J–V characteristics of an OSC device fabricated with Ag:PSS. Devices composed solely of Ag:PSS and PEI exhibited suboptimal efficiency (3.58%) due to a low V OC (0.503 V), J SC (15.62 mA·cm–2) and FF (46.66%). These results suggest that pristine Ag:PSS alone does not provide sufficiently selective hole-extracting or electron-blocking characteristics. Conversely, the p-type Ag:PSS/BHJ/PEI device, incorporating PEDOT:PSS into Ag:PSS, achieved an efficiency of 7.70%, surpassing the 6.87% efficiency observed when using only PEDOT:PSS with PEI as an ETL. This improvement may be related in part to the use of thinner interlayers, which can reduce parasitic optical losses while maintaining favorable interfacial energetics. Moreover, the efficiency of the PEDOT:PSS/BHJ/n-type Ag:PSS device (7.88%) exceeded that of the conventional PEDOT:PSS/BHJ/PEI device (6.87%, Table S1). These results suggest that incorporation of Ag:PSS measurably alters interfacial properties relative to PEDOT:PSS or PEI alone. The highest efficiency (8.48%) was obtained for the p-type Ag:PSS/BHJ/n-type Ag:PSS device, indicating that simultaneous modification of both interfaces provides the most favorable device performance among the structures investigated here. Although the efficiencies are lower than those of current state-of-the-art OPVs based on nonfullerene acceptors, the observed trends are consistent with the interfacial energetics measured by UPS, XPS, and IPES and with photovoltaic characteristics commonly reported for PTB7-based devices. In this context, PTB7 remains a useful benchmark active layer because it is one of the most thoroughly studied and well-understood OSC systems. Many interlayers first investigated in PTB7-based devices were subsequently adapted for use in modern nonfullerene acceptor systems, although the performance and energy-level alignment of each system remain dependent on the specific active-layer composition and specifically the energy levels of the active materials. ,

Compared to previously reported Ag:PSS-based interlayers, which were mainly employed as single HTMs and required additional interface engineering or bilayer configurations to achieve PCEs in the range of 12–17%, our study uniquely demonstrates that Ag:PSS can be reversibly polarity-tuned to function as both HTM and ETM. This approach enables the construction of fully symmetric p-Ag:PSS/BHJ/n-Ag:PSS devices and establishes Ag:PSS as a versatile single-material platform for interfacial layer design.

Figure b shows the EQE spectra, and the J SC values calculated from EQE measurements closely match those obtained from J–V curve. Both p-type Ag:PSS and n-type Ag:PSS devices exhibit strong and uniform optical responses. The introduction of p-type Ag:PSS increases J SC to 17.32 mA·cm–2, compared with 15.51 mA·cm–2 for pristine Ag:PSS. Because the Ag:PSS layer is about 20 nm thinner than the PEDOT:PSS, the optical field distribution within the active layer is altered, leading to enhanced absorption in the 350–400 nm range and overall higher light harvesting.

A similar enhancement is observed for p-type Ag:PSS devices. In n-type Ag:PSS devices, the stronger internal electric field compared with PEI-only devices leads to enhanced absorption across the entire spectrum, producing a further J SC increase. As a result, employing both p-type and n-type Ag:PSS maximizes light absorption and current generation, thereby improving device efficiency.

To better understand the effect of Ag:PSS on charge transport, we analyzed the series and shunt resistances extracted from the dark J–V curves (Figure c). These parameters are used as qualitative indicators for comparing charge transport among devices, rather than absolute series and shunt resistances under illumination. The shunt resistance should be as high as possible; it is strongly influenced by trap states, pinholes, and edge defects, which create leakage paths and lower the shunt resistance. In an ideal device, the series resistance approaches zero, allowing all photocurrent to flow out of the device, while a high shunt resistance minimizes backward current leakage. The p-type Ag:PSS/BHJ/n-type Ag:PSS device shows the lowest series resistance (1.922 Ω·cm2) and the highest shunt resistance (3521 kΩ·cm2), indicating efficient charge extraction. In contrast, the Ag:PSS/BHJ/PEI device, which exhibited poor performance, has relatively high series resistance and low shunt resistance. The use of both p-type and n-type Ag:PSS led to higher shunt resistance and improved fill factor, consistent with the superior J–V characteristics shown in Figure a and the higher overall PCE.

Trap-assisted recombination is an important factor influencing OSC device performance. , Its presence can be investigated through variable light intensity measurements, as shown in Figure d and e. Figure e illustrates J SC as a function of light intensity, expected to follow a power-law dependence with the formula J SC ∝ (Plight)α. The parameter α represents the slope of the fitted line; when α < 1, bimolecular recombination is present. The α values of the three devices are very close to 1, suggesting that a relatively small fraction of carriers is lost through bimolecular recombination. For pristine Ag:PSS, α was 0.939, lower than those of the doped devices, suggesting a higher degree of carrier loss. The p-type Ag:PSS/BHJ/n-type Ag:PSS structure exhibited the highest α value (0.996), indicating minimal carrier loss. Although there may still be a slightly lower recombination loss in this structure compared to the others, the difference is very small. These combined results suggest somewhat reduced recombination losses compared with the undoped Ag:PSS device, consistent with previously reported light-intensity analyses of PTB7-based devices.

The light-intensity dependence of the V OC provides qualitative insight into recombination behavior within the devices. Figure e depicts V OC as a function of illumination intensity and fits the following equation: , VOC=(kTq)ln(JSCJ0+1) where k is the Boltzmann constant, q is the electronic charge, T is the temperature in Kelvin, and J0 is the saturation current density (dark). The anomaly coefficient (n), which describes the slope of the data relative to kT/q, provides information about which type of recombination dominates the losses in the device. − If the slope of the ideality coefficient is close to 1, bimolecular recombination is dominant. Ideality factors approaching 2 are commonly associated with recombination processes involving trap states. The ideality coefficients of the four structures were 1.75 (kT/q), 1.34 (kT/q), 1.25 (kT/q), and 1.15 (kT/q) for pristine Ag:PSS, p-type Ag:PSS, n-type Ag:PSS, and p-type Ag:PSS/n-type Ag:PSS devices, respectively. Devices utilizing pristine Ag:PSS typically exhibit an ideality factor close to 2, indicating a high trap-assisted recombination rate that diminishes overall efficiency. Conversely, the ideality factor closest to 1 in the p-type Ag:PSS/n-type Ag:PSS device indicates a lower trap-assisted recombination rate, contributing to improved efficiency due to the enhanced J SC resulting from the transport of a larger number of charge carriers. This observation aligns well with the solar cell device results. Trap-assisted recombination leads to a decrease in V OC. However, the ideality coefficient values of the other two devices were similar and showed no significant difference. All together, the results suggest that trap-assisted recombination contributes to device losses, particularly in the undoped Ag:PSS device, while the lower ideality factor observed in the polarity-tuned Ag:PSS configuration is consistent with improved charge extraction and reduced recombination losses.

To characterize and understand the effects of the Ag:PSS-based interlayer on electronic band structure, we employed photoemission spectroscopy (PES) and IPES, as illustrated in Figure . PES involves exciting an electron from the ground state to a state above the vacuum level. The kinetic energy of the excited electron is then measured relative to the Fermi level using an electron analyzer. Depending on the excitation source, PES is classified into XPS and UPS, with UPS being used to determine the HOMO level and the WF. IPES, in contrast, probes the unoccupied electronic states of the sample. In this process, an electron above the vacuum level is captured into an unoccupied conduction-band state and decays to a lower state while emitting a UV photon, which is detected. Together, UPS and IPES provide the positions of the HOMO and LUMO levels, respectively. These measurements allow direct observation of the electronic structure, the charge transport capability of the interfacial layer, and the doping state.

(a) Schematic diagram of UPS and IPES, (b) transport bandgap measured by UPS and IPES and optical bandgap measured by UV–visible spectroscopy.

Transport bandgap (Eg T) can be obtained from HOMO and LUMO levels measured by UPS and IPES (Figure b). It is defined as the energy difference required for an electron to move from the valence band to the conduction band. This bandgap represents the energy at which electrons transition to a state where they can move freely. There is also an optical bandgap (Eg OPT) that can be measured optically. Eg OPT refers to the minimum energy difference that electrons can transfer when a material absorbs light. This is the energy required to excite electrons into the conduction band and create electron–hole pairs (excitons). It is typically measured by UV–visible spectroscopy. The difference between these two bandgap energies is defined as the exciton binding energy (Eexciton). (Eexciton = Eg T – Eg OPT) Eexciton is the energy required for electrons and holes to combine and form excitons. The performance of the applied device varies depending on the size of the Eexciton.

To demonstrate the capability of Ag:PSS for both p-doping and n-doping and gain a better understanding of the doping mechanism and band structure, the electronic structures of p-type Ag:PSS and n-type Ag:PSS films on ITO substrates were investigated using UPS, XPS, and IPES. Figure shows the UPS spectrum in the HOMO region and the IPES spectrum in the LUMO region, and the WF was calculated from the secondary electron cutoff in Figure S2S3. More detailed XPS spectra are provided in Figure S4. The WF of pristine Ag:PSS was 4.46 eV, while that of p-type Ag:PSS increased to 4.55 eV, indicating that the HOMO level moved closer to the Fermi level. This shift confirms p-type electronic doping and facilitates ohmic contact formation, improving hole transport by aligning the Fermi level with that of the active layer. On the other hand, the WF of n-type Ag:PSS is 3.73 eV, significantly lower than the WF of ITO. The decrease of the WF of n-type Ag:PSS from 4.46 to 3.73 eV shows that the HOMO level moved away from the Fermi level, effectively lowering the electrode WF to promote electron extraction, consistent with n-type electronic doping. HOMO and LUMO levels measured by UPS and IPES clearly show this doping effect (Figure a). For pristine Ag:PSS, the hole injection barrier (HIB) and electron injection barrier (EIB) were 2.43 and 3.12 eV, respectively. P-type Ag:PSS showed a reduced HIB of 2.14 eV and an increased EIB of 3.34 eV, which supports more efficient hole extraction. N-type Ag:PSS showed an increased HIB of 3.29 eV and a decreased EIB of 2.53 eV, favoring electron extraction. These results demonstrate that the overall energy levels shift systematically with doping, providing clear evidence of successful polarity control. Compared to previously reported metal ion doped PSS interlayers, , which act only as p-type HTMs and exhibit a narrow WF modulation (≤0.4 eV), Ag:PSS shows a much wider WF tunability (4.59 to 3.73 eV) and uniquely allows reversible switching between p-type and n-type polarity with PEDOT:PSS or PEI. This broader tunability provides greater flexibility for energy level alignment and a new pathway for unified HTM/ETM design within a single interlayer platform.

Absorption of photons in OSCs generates excitons at the donor or acceptor because of their low dielectric constant. The Coulomb attraction that stabilizes the exciton against free electron and hole carriers is defined as the Eexciton, which is a critical parameter governing the interaction of light and matter in solids. When the exciton binding energy is small, there is a high probability that the exciton will dissociate into free charge carriers (electrons and holes). Conversely, when the binding energy is large, the carriers recombine and emit light. In OLEDs, a large Eexciton is desired to enhance exciton recombination, whereas OSCs require a small Eexciton to facilitate charge generation.

To determine the optical bandgap (Eg OPT) of Ag:PSS, the absorption spectrum was measured (Figure S5). The Eg OPT of pristine Ag:PSS was 4.49 eV, and the Eexciton was calculated to be 1.07 eV by subtracting Eg OPT from the transport bandgap (Eg T) obtained by UPS and IPES. Materials commonly used as HTMs and ETMs generally have very low Eexciton (10–40 meV), which facilitates charge separation and transport. − The relatively high Eexciton of Ag:PSS can hinder charge separation and negatively affect device performance, consistent with our device results. Interestingly, Eexciton decreases to 0.99 eV for p-type Ag:PSS and increases to 1.33 eV for n-type Ag:PSS. The reduced Eexciton in p-type Ag:PSS may promote interfacial charge separation and improve carrier transport, whereas the increased Eexciton in n-type Ag:PSS may raise the probability of exciton recombination. However, because n-type Ag:PSS behaves similarly to a conjugated polyelectrolyte when used as a very thin layer, the impact of its relatively high Eexciton is likely limited. Because of its wide band gap, the interlayer absorbs little light, while excitons are primarily generated and dissociated within the active layer making Eexciton of the interlayer is secondary to that of the active layer. The interlayer mainly serves as a charge-transport and energy-level alignment layer and the doping-induced energy level shifts confirm that p- and n-type Ag:PSS can be effectively utilized for hole and electron extraction, respectively. Although device stability was not examined in this fundamental study, ion migration and interfacial degradation are known factors influencing the long-term stability of organic solar cells when mobile ionic species are present in interlayers, and the long-term stability of such ionic interlayers should be considered in order to fully assess their utility in practical OSC modules.

Conclusions

In summary, we investigated Ag:PSS-based interlayers containing a polystyrenesulfonate (PSS) backbone and Ag+ ions in organic solar cells. The polarity of the Ag:PSS polyelectrolyte was modulated by addition of PEDOT:PSS or PEI to yield interlayers capable of producing p-type or n-type behavior, for use as HTMs or ETMs, respectively. We investigated the p-doping and n-doping phenomena in Ag:PSS through UPS, XPS, and IPES measurements. The pristine Ag:PSS/BHJ/PEI structure exhibited very low efficiency (3.58%). For overall performance improvement, we modified Ag:PSS with PEDOT:PSS to create a p-type Ag:PSS interlayer, resulting in an efficiency of 7.70%, which was higher than that of the PEDOT:PSS/BHJ/PEI control device. Conversely, n-type Ag:PSS with opposite polarity increased the efficiency to 7.88% (PEDOT:PSS/BHJ/n-type Ag:PSS), surpassing the efficiency using pristine PEI (6.87%). Finally, using both p-type and n-type Ag:PSS as HTM and ETM, respectively, the efficiency increased to 8.48%. Through simultaneous measurements using UPS and IPES, we confirmed that each interlayer caused p-type or n-type doping at the anode and cathode interfaces, respectively, consistent with the improved device characteristics. This study demonstrates that Ag interlayers can be tuned toward either hole or electron selective behavior through compositional modification with PEDOT or PEI. UPS, XPS, and IPES measurements reveal systematic changes in work function and electronic structure that correlate with the observed photovoltaic characteristics. These results provide insight into how interlayer composition influences interfacial energetics and charge extraction behavior in organic solar cells.
