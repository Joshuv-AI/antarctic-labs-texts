Efficacy of everolimus in patients with hormone receptor positive, HER2 negative, metastatic breast cancer pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors
Authors: Justine Dutheil, Victor Pereira, Cyril Boisson, Laura Mansi, Marie-Justine Paillard, Fernando Bazan, Loic Chaigneau, Erion Dobi, Julien Viot, Guillaume Meynard, Morgan Goujon, Lorraine Dalens, Clément Dubourd, Sophie Marchi, Nathalie Meneveau, Dewi Vernerey, Olivier Adotévi, Aurélia Meurisse, Elsa Curtit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContext and aimsEverolimus was approved in 2012 for the treatment of advanced hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer (MBC) in combination with exemestane. Since then, the first line treatment for these cancers has evolved with the development of CDK4/6 inhibitors.MethodsPatients with histologically proved MBC and who received everolimus between 2012 and 2022 were included in a multicentric retrospective cohort. Prior exposure to CDK4/6 inhibitors was documented. The purpose of the study was to evaluate the efficacy of everolimus plus endocrine therapy in patients previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors and to identify factors associated with improved PFS on everolimus. The primary endpoint was PFS. Secondary outcomes included overall survival (OS), factors associated with improved efficacy, and grade III-IV toxicities.ResultsFrom 2012 to December 30, 2022, 325 patients were included. Of these, 75 patients (23%) had been pretreated by CDK4/6 inhibitors. In the overall population, median PFS was 6.4 months (95% CI, 5.5–7.1), with a median OS of 25 months (95% CI, 21.8–28.1). Among those previously exposed to CDK4/6 inhibitors, median PFS was 5.3 months (95% CI, 4.6–7.1), compared to 6.7 months (95% CI, 5.8–7.6) for those who had not received CDK4/6 inhibitors (p = 0.046) and the median OS was 27.3 months without CDK4/6 inhibitors before everolimus (95% CI, 23.2–30.2) versus 21.8 months (95% CI,18.5–25.5) when pretreated by CDK4/6 inhibitors (p = 0.01). Sixty-eight patients (21%) had a PFS greater than 12 months. Factors associated with an improved PFS were the lack of liver metastases (p < 0.001), no prior exposure to metastatic chemotherapy (p = 0.001), a lower tumor grade (p = 0.005), no prior treatment with CDK4/6 inhibitors (p = 0.006), and a better performance status (p = 0.008). 89 patients (28%) discontinued everolimus due to toxicity rather than disease progression or death and 23 (25%) patients treated with exemestane alone, after everolimus exposure, maintained a stable disease for at least 6 months.ConclusionPatients who had prior exposure to CDK4/6 inhibitors experienced a shorter median PFS benefit from everolimus based-treatment compared to those who were CDK4/6 inhibitor-naive. In this study, they also demonstrated shorter OS, likely due to a more pronounced endocrine-resistant profile. Although a reduction in PFS is expected with successive lines of therapy, the use of everolimus after exposure to CDK4/6 inhibitors and aromatase inhibitors remains an option and allows to propose another endocrine-based therapy and to postpone the use of chemotherapy. Managing endocrine resistance remains challenging and further research is needed to identify predictive biomarkers for improved outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02109-3.Keywords: Everolimus, Metastatic breast cancer, CDK4/6 inhibitors, Prognosis factors.

Introduction

The addition of targeted therapy to endocrine therapy has dramatically transformed the treatment landscape for metastatic hormone-receptor-positive (RH+), HER2-negative breast cancer [1–6].

Everolimus, an oral inhibitor of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway [7]was the first targeted therapy developed in combination with endocrine therapy [8]. Approved in 2012, everolimus was shown to improve outcomes in the BOLERO-2 trial, a phase III randomized study that included 724 patients with metastatic breast cancer who had previously been treated with nonsteroidal aromatase inhibitors [9]. The trial demonstrated that adding everolimus to exemestane significantly improved median PFS from 2.8 months to 6.9 months (HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.35–0.54; P < 0.001), although no significant improvement in overall survival (OS) was observed. The most common adverse events associated with everolimus included stomatitis, infections, rash, pneumonitis, and hyperglycemia [10].

No predictor of everolimus efficacy, both clinical and biological, could be identified. Notably, the efficacy of everolimus was independent of the most commonly altered genes or pathways implicated in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer oncogenesis, included mTOR pathway [11–13].

Since the approval of everolimus, CDK4/6 inhibitors have become the standard of care for first-line treatment of hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer, in combination with aromatase inhibitors or fulvestrant [14–16]. These agents have shown significant improvements in PFS, and some trials have also demonstrated statically significant OS benefits, both in the first- and second-line settings [17–23]. As a result, everolimus is now primarily considered after CDK4/6 inhibitors for patients who remain candidates for endocrine-based therapies. In the second-line setting, several therapeutic options are available, including the combination of everolimus with endocrine therapy [24–26].

The purpose of this retrospective study was to evaluate the efficacy of everolimus plus endocrine therapy in patients previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors and to identify factors associated with improved PFS on everolimus. Given the evolving treatment landscape, understanding the role of everolimus in this patient population is critical to optimizing therapeutic strategies and delaying the need for chemotherapy.

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a multicentric, retrospective cohort study conducted across seven centers, including an university hospital. We included patients with histologically confirmed estrogen receptor (ER)-positive, HER2-negative MBC who were treated with everolimus in combination with endocrine therapy. Patients must have had prior exposure to aromatase inhibitors, either in the adjuvant or metastatic setting. The study period spanned from the approval of everolimus approval in 2012, to December 30, 2022. Eligible patients were identified through an exhaustive extraction of prescription records from institutional electronic systems. In line with ethical regulations, all living patients were contacted and received an information letter and had the opportunity to participate or to opt out. One patient declined participation. For deceased patients, data collection was authorized under the institution’s general consent policy for the use of anonymized clinical data for research purposes. Ethical approval and patient consent were obtained in accordance with French legislation. The study complies with relevant guidelines, including Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and endpoints

Patient data, including clinical and pathological characteristics, were extracted from institutional databases and recorded in a computerized system. Information on treatments was retrieved from a shared chemotherapy prescribing software.

The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from treatment initiation with everolimus to disease progression (before initiation of a following treatment) or death from any cause whichever occurs first. Disease progression was defined as radiologically confirmed progression according to RECIST criteria, or, when applicable, clinical deterioration consistent with disease progression as assessed by the treating physician. Death from any cause was also considered as a progression event for the purpose of progression-free survival analysis. Secondary endpoints included the identification of clinical and pathological factors associated with improved or prolonged PFS, arbitrarily defined as PFS > 12 months and OS, defined as the time from treatment initiation with everolimus to death from any cause. Safety, tolerability, and dose reductions related to everolimus treatment were also assessed.

An exploratory endpoint was to describe the profile of patients who maintained a response with endocrine therapy alone after discontinuation of everolimus for reasons other than disease progression or death.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Qualitative and continue variables were compared with Chi-squared test (or Fisher exact test, if appropriate) and Wilcoxon test, respectively. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this was a retrospective observational study including all eligible patients treated during the predefined study period. PFS and OS were estimated using Kaplan-Meier method, and comparisons were made using the log-rank test. The reverse Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate median follow-up times.

Cox proportional hazards models were performed (Hazard Ratio (HR) and 95% CI were provided) to identify factors associated with PFS. For each type of regression, covariates with p-value < 0.2 in univariable analyses were entered into a final multivariable regression model and a backward selection were performed, after considering collinearity among variables and checking model assumptions.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Interaction terms were not included in the multivariable Cox model, as no clinically relevant interactions were prespecified or supported by existing literature. To avoid overfitting, the Peduzzi rule of at least 10 events per adjusted variable was respected. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Characteristics of the study population

From January 1, 2012 to December 30, 2022, a total of 325 patients with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative MBC were included in the study (Flow chart Fig. 1). Of these, 75 patients (23%) had been pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors. Sixty-eight patients (21%) were classified as long responders, defined as having a PFS greater than 12 months.

The median age at inclusion was 67 years old. All patients had prior exposure to aromatase inhibitors, either in the adjuvant setting (11%) or the metastatic setting (89%). Patients received everolimus in 1st to 6th line of treatment at the metastatic setting. In all cases except one (324 patients) everolimus was associated with exemestane. The median follow-up period for the cohort was 73.6 months (CI 95%, 65.1–84.8). In the overall study population, the median PFS was 6.4 months (95% CI, 5.5–7.1) and the median OS was 25 months (95% CI, 21.8–28.1) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among patients pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors, the median PFS was 5.3 months (95% CI, 4.6–7.1), compared to 6.7 months (95% CI, 5.8–7.6) for those who had not received CDK4/6 inhibitors (Fig. 2; p = 0,046) and the median OS was 27.3 months (95% CI, 23.2–30.2) versus 21.8 months (95% CI,18.5–25.5) when pretreated by CDK4/6 inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 2; p = 0.01). Factors associated with an improved PFS were the lack of liver metastases (p < 0.001), no prior exposure to metastatic chemotherapy (p = 0.001), a lower tumor grade (p = 0.005), no prior treatment with CDK4/6 inhibitors (p = 0.006), and a better performance status (p = 0.008) (Table 2).

The characteristics of patients pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors were comparable to those who had not received these agents, and the median number of prior treatment exposure in the metastatic setting was not statistically different. However, patients pre-treated by CDK4/6 inhibitors had received less chemotherapy at the metastatic setting but were more pre-treated with endocrine therapy at this setting. Among those pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors, 20% had received prior metastatic chemotherapy, compared to 41% in the non-CDK4/6 inhibitor group (p = 0.001) (Table 1); but 63% had received fulvestrant versus 44% (p = 0.004) and everolimus plus exemestane was the 1st line of metastatic treatment (after exposure to adjuvant aromatase inhibitors) in 2 CDK4/6 inhibitors-patients (3%) versus 14 (14%) CDK4/6 inhibitors-naive patients (p = 0.007) (Table 1).

Safety and tolerability of everolimus

The median duration of everolimus treatment was 4.8 months (range, 2.8–9.1 months). A total of 89 patients (28%) discontinued treatment due to toxicity, while 216 patients (67%) stopped treatment due to disease progression. Dose reductions were required in 67% of patients (Table 3).

In terms of adverse events, 63 patients (20%) experienced grade III or IV toxicities, as classified by the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 6.0 (Table 3). The most common grade III or IV toxicities were metabolic (33%) and cutaneous events (21%), primarily stomatitis and rash. There were no grade III pneumonias nor toxic deaths. Noteworthy is a grade III angioedema in the context of co-prescription with an ACE inhibitor.

Patients with prolonged efficacy of everolimus

Among the 68 long responders (defined as PFS > 12 months), 31% had bone-only metastatic disease, 24% had liver metastases, 41% had lung metastases, and 29% had synchronous metastatic disease. Additionally, 25% had been exposed to at least one line of metastatic chemotherapy prior to everolimus, and 18% had prior CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure (Supplementary Table 1).

Patients with response to endocrine therapy after everolimus discontinuation

Of the 101 patients (31%) who discontinued everolimus for reasons other than progression or death (e.g., toxicity, patient choice, or COVID-19 pandemic), 10 received another line of treatment despite not experiencing progression, likely due to either short exposure to everolimus or aggressive disease.

Ninety-one patients continued in endocrine therapy alone (exemestane), without modification of the regimen. Some patients maintained a response with endocrine therapy alone: 43 (47%) had disease progression within three months, while 25 (28%) maintained a response for 3–6 months, 14 (15%) for 6–12 months, and 9 (10%) for more than 12 months (Table 4). Discussion

Summary of results and literature review

The study included 325 patients treated with everolimus plus endocrine therapy. Of these, 75/325 patients (23%) had a prior exposure to CDK 4/6 inhibitors, and 68/325 patients (21%) had a prolonged response, defined as a PFS > 12 months. In term of survival, median PFS were 6.4 (95% CI, 5.5–7.1), 5.3 (CI 95%, 4.6–7.1) and 6.7 (CI 95%, 5.8–7.6) for the overall population, the patients with CDK 4/6 inhibitors exposure or not, respectively. While the median PFS was shorter in patients pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors (p = 0.0464), it remains superior to the PFS usually observed in endocrine therapy alone, such as the PFS in the exemestane arm in the BOLERO-2 trial (2.9 months). The PFS data remain comparable to those obtained with second-line endocrine therapy-based treatments in this population especially considering that in our study, patients were treated in an advanced metastatic setting, ranging from first-line treatment (only 11% of patients) to fifth-line treatment [23, 27–32].

Median overall survival was 25 months (95% CI, 21.8–28.1), 27.3 months (95% CI, 23.2–30.2) and 21.8 months (95% CI,18.5–25.5) for the overall population, the CDK4/6 inhibitor-naive patients’ population and the CDK4/6 inhibitors-pretreated patients, respectively. Although the median number of lines before exposure to everolimus is not statistically different between the two groups, patients pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors were more frequently exposed to hormone therapy in the metastatic phase, particularly fulvestrant. Only two patients received everolimus as first-line metastatic treatment, compared to 14 in the CDK4/6 inhibitor-naive group. This profile of greater endocrine resistance among patients exposed to CDK4/6 inhibitors may partly explain the reduced survival observed in this group.

Few studies have evaluated the efficacy of everolimus-based therapy in patients previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors, and available data remain limited. A prospective study by Vasseur et al. included 57 patients and analyzed changes in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) during treatment [33]. The study reported a PFS of 6.8 months (95% CI [5.03–11.5]) with the combination of fulvestrant and everolimus. Another prospective study involving 17 patients pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors, observed a PFS of 3.6 months compared to 4.2 months in CDK4/6 inhibitor-naïve patients [26]. A retrospective study (n = 192) found that patients who received everolimus plus exemestane following CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy had a significantly reduced PFS of 3.8 months, compared to 5.4 months in those without prior CDK4/6 inhibitor use (p = 0.013) [34]. Another retrospective study (n = 41) reported a median PFS of 4.2 months in this population [35]. The shortening of PFS post-CDK4/6 inhibitors is consistent with other studies, such as the BYLieve trial, which showed a PFS of 5.3 months after CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy when alpelisib was used. Similarly, the CAPItello-291 trial demonstrated a comparable reduction in PFS to 5.5 months in patients receiving fulvestrant plus capivasertib after CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure. Although a decline in PFS is expected with each successive line of therapy, everolimus combined with exemestane remains an option after CDK4/6 inhibitors, offering a median PFS comparable to that of other endocrine-based therapeutic strategies available in this setting.

Current evidence on second-line therapies after CDK4/6 inhibitors

As CDK4/6 inhibitors became widely adopted after 2017, the treatment landscape evolved significantly during the study period. Patients treated earlier may have received everolimus before CDK4/6 inhibitors, while more recent patients were more heavily pretreated, contributing to greater endocrine resistance and potentially influencing survival outcomes. In the post-CDK4/6 inhibitor setting, several second-line therapeutic options [22–24, 36] have emerged, including endocrine therapy alone (with selective estrogen receptor degrader SERD such as elascestrant or fulvestrant) or in combination with targeted agents. Evidence-based options for second-line treatment include combining everolimus plus exemestane (or fulvestrant), combining endocrine therapy with alpelisib or capivasertib in patients harboring PIK3CA somatic mutations, using another CDK4/6 inhibitor in conjunction with fulvestrant. Additionally, PARP inhibitors as monotherapy for patients with germline pathogenic BRCA1/2 mutations is an option. Chemotherapy remains a consideration, particularly for patients with visceral crisis or rapidly progressing disease. Trastuzumab-deruxtecan has recently demonstrated an impressive and statistically significant progression-free survival benefit in HER2-low MBC within this context [37]. However, as endocrine resistance becomes more prevalent after CDK4/6 inhibitors exposure, finding optimal second-line options and delay the time for initiation of chemotherapy remain a challenge.

Classical prognostic factors such as good performance status, prior sensitivity to endocrine therapy, and absence of visceral metastases are associated with improved PFS in hormone receptor-positive/HER2- MBC [38]. However, despite efforts to identify molecular biomarkers (e.g., PIK3CA mutations), no robust predictive factors for everolimus efficacy have emerged from the literature [13, 33]. In our study, we confirmed that clinical factors were associated with prolonged PFS (the lack of liver metastases, no prior exposure to metastatic chemotherapy, tumor grade < III (p = 0.005), and a good performance status), in line with the findings from prior research. These findings highlight the prognostic value of individual clinical factors such as liver metastases, prior chemotherapy exposure, and performance status. While no interaction terms were included in the multivariable model to avoid overfitting, it is clinically plausible that the coexistence of several negative prognostic factors—such as poor performance status combined with liver metastases—may further worsen patient outcomes. We also showed that the exposure to CDK4/6 inhibitors, often associated with more exposure to endocrine therapies at the metastatic setting, is correlated with decreased PFS and OS. However, in some patients pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors, treatment with everolimus plus exemestane may be particularly promising (16% of patients in our study had a PFS greater than 1 year). The identification of biomarkers must remain a priority.

Strengths and limitations of the study

One of the key strengths of our study is the use of real-world data, which reflects a broader and more heterogeneous patient population than those typically included in clinical trials, in a context where phase III trial data is unlikely to ever become available. The multicentric nature of the study also increases the generalizability of our findings.

Moreover, we identified an additional population of interest: patients who exhibited a prolonged response (over 3, 6, or even 12 months) after discontinuing everolimus for reasons other than disease progression or death. This group consisted of 27.5%, 15.5% and 10% of patients, respectively. Notably, these patients maintained their response with endocrine therapy – exemestane for these patients - alone after everolimus discontinuation, raising intriguing questions. The use of everolimus and exemestane was initially prompted by resistance to endocrine therapy following aromatase inhibitor exposure. We hypothesize that, in these patients, everolimus may have sustainably restored tumor hormone sensitivity. One possible explanation for this effect could be linked, at least in part, to the immunomodulatory effects induced by everolimus. Preclinical data suggest that high doses of everolimus can reverse immunosuppression and activate the host immune system’s anti-tumor response.

Our study also has limitations inherent to its retrospective design, such as potential selection biases and incomplete data collection. The relatively small number of patients pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors and the heterogeneity in the lines of therapy at which everolimus was administered may limit the generalizability of our findings. These factors, however, reflect real-world clinical practice over the past decade, during which treatment paradigms evolved significantly. Another limitation of the study is the lack of translational data and biomarker analysis, as well as the absence of information on the ESR1 and Pi3K mutation status. These limit the validity of the study. Prospective studies will be critical to validating these findings and improving our understanding of everolimus’ role in the post-CDK4/6 inhibitor setting. Biomarkers of everolimus efficacy are still wanted.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the use of everolimus combined with exemestane in patients with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative MBC, including those pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors. While patients with prior CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure showed shorter PFS compared to those who were CDK4/6 inhibitor-naive, the median PFS observed in this study remains correct when compared to other therapeutic options based on endocrine therapy. The use of everolimus after exposure to CDK4/6 inhibitors and aromatase inhibitors in patients with metastatic hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer remains a possibility and allows to propose to the patient another endocrine-based therapy and to postpone the use of chemotherapy. Toxicities associated with everolimus are common but manageable, often requiring dose reductions and careful monitoring.

A subset of patients achieved prolonged disease control, with 16% of CDK4/6 inhibitor-pretreated patients experiencing a PFS greater than 12 months. The study highlights the importance of clinical factors such as good performance status, absence of liver metastases, and no prior chemotherapy exposure in predicting improved outcomes with everolimus-based therapy. However, the reduced PFS and OS in patients pretreated with CDK4/6 inhibitors emphasizes the growing challenge of managing endocrine resistance in this setting. Despite the promising results, further research is needed to identify robust predictive biomarkers for everolimus efficacy and to optimize therapeutic sequencing for this challenging patient population.
