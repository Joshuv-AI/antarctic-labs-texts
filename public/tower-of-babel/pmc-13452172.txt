ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSuicide attempts are a critical public health concern, with a high risk of recurrence after an index attempt. We aimed to identify predictors of suicide reattempt within 12 months.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, we enrolled individuals aged 12 years or older from eight Spanish public hospitals after a suicide attempt (Nov 1, 2020–May 18, 2022). Participants were assessed within 15 days of the index suicide attempt and followed for 12 months. The primary outcome was time to suicide reattempt, documented in electronic clinical records. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify predictors, adjusting for sex, age, and recruitment site. Individuals with lived experience were involved in the study design.FindingsOf 1730 participants, the mean age was 36.5 years (SD 17.2); 1259 (72.8%) were women and 471 (27.2%) were men. Within 12 months, 340 of 1730 participants (19.7%) reattempted suicide, the median time to reattempt was 140 days (IQR 175, range 7–365). Risk was highest in the first months following the index attempt. Five independent predictors were identified: high psychotropic medication burden (four or five prescribed medications: aHR 2.55, 95% CI 1.57–4.15; p < 0.001), more frequent suicidal ideation (weekly: aHR 1.87, 95% CI 1.25–2.79; p = 0.001; several times per day: aHR 2.07, 95% CI 1.39–3.07; p < 0.001), prior suicide attempts (one to three: aHR 1.62, 95% CI 1.19–2.20; p = 0.002; four or more: aHR 1.80, 95% CI 1.25–2.61; p = 0.001), moderate to severe childhood emotional neglect (aHR 1.56, 95% CI 1.16–2.08; p = 0.002), and non-suicidal self-injury (aHR 1.45, 95% CI 1.09–1.93; p = 0.009).InterpretationNearly one in five individuals reattempted suicide within a year of an index attempt. Identified predictors underscore the need for early, targeted, and trauma-sensitive post-attempt interventions.FundingInstituto de Salud Carlos III and co-funded by the European Union (FEDER).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and PsycInfo for English-language articles from database inception to May 1, 2025, using the terms (“suicide” OR “suicidal behaviour” OR “suicide attempt” OR “self-harm” OR “parasuicide”) AND (“reattempt” OR “recurrence” OR “repeat attempt” OR “repetition”) AND (“predictors” OR “risk factors” OR “prognosis”) AND (“cohort” OR “longitudinal” OR “prospective”). The literature shows that recurrence is common, affecting approximately 15–25% of individuals within 12 months, and is associated with female sex, prior attempts, psychiatric disorders, substance use, and persistent suicidal ideation. Few studies have examined protective factors or developmental differences, and existing evidence is limited by small samples, heterogeneous follow-up periods, and inconsistent definitions of suicide reattempt.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is the largest prospective, multicentre cohort study of suicide reattempt in Spain, enrolling 1730 individuals aged 12 years or older from eight tertiary hospitals and following them for 12 months after an index attempt. Unlike many previous studies that combine suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviour, our cohort was specifically defined by the occurrence of a suicide attempt, while suicidal ideation was assessed separately as a baseline clinical dimension. This design allowed us to examine baseline factors associated with documented suicide reattempts in a real-world clinical population spanning both adolescents and adults, thereby increasing the developmental breadth and clinical relevance of the findings. Within 12 months, 340 participants (19.7%) reattempted suicide, and five independent prognostic factors for recurrence were identified: high psychotropic medication burden, higher suicidal ideation frequency, prior suicide attempts, moderate to severe childhood emotional neglect, and non-suicidal self-injury. Stratified analyses further suggested age-related differences in risk profiles, supporting the need for developmentally informed post-attempt assessment and follow-up.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings reinforce existing evidence on the importance of timely and systematic follow-up after a suicide attempt. Taken together, the available evidence suggests that assessments of patients presenting after a suicide attempt should consider psychotropic medication burden, frequency of suicidal ideation, prior suicide attempts, self-harm, and childhood emotional trauma as clinically relevant markers associated with recurrence risk. These factors may help identify patients who warrant closer monitoring and more intensive follow-up, although further intervention studies are needed to determine the extent to which modifying these factors can reduce suicide reattempts.

Introduction

Suicide is a major global public health issue, with over 700,000 deaths reported annually and high recurrence rates after an initial attempt. In addition to its fatal consequences, suicidal behaviour contributes substantially to disability, with an age-adjusted global rate of 16 deaths per 100,000 population.1 Non-fatal suicide attempts are up to 25 times more frequent than deaths, and approximately one in five individuals admitted to emergency services after an attempt will reattempt within a short period.2

According to the most recent Global Burden of Disease estimates, approximately 746 000 people died by suicide worldwide in 2021, corresponding to an age-standardised mortality rate of 9.0 deaths per 100,000 population.3 Within Europe, suicide mortality remains among the highest globally, with Western Europe showing an age-standardised rate of about 3–4 deaths per 100,000 and Eastern Europe close to 19 deaths per 100,000 in 2021. In this context, Spain has experienced substantial declines over recent decades, yet suicide continues to rank among the leading causes of death in young people, and non-fatal suicide attempts are considerably more frequent than deaths. Understanding short-term recurrence after an index attempt is therefore critical to inform secondary prevention strategies in European health systems.

While multiple risk factors for suicide attempts have been identified–including psychiatric disorders, prior attempts, substance and alcohol use, and psychosocial or socioeconomic adversity4-evidence suggests that predictors of reattempt may differ from those of a first attempt.5 Individuals who reattempt could represent a distinct clinical group requiring tailored assessment and intervention. Recent systematic reviews suggest that the likelihood of repetition is higher among individuals who are female, use less lethal methods such as cutting, have a history of previous attempts, report persistent suicidal ideation, meet criteria for psychiatric disorders—particularly substance and alcohol use disorders—and have experienced trauma.2,5

Cohort studies offer a valuable framework for understanding recurrence, enabling prospective tracking of individuals and capturing how modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors influence the recurrence of suicide attempts (SA) over time. Previous cohorts have been have varied in scope and design, limiting generalisability.6, 7, 8 Moreover, evidence on predictors of suicide reattempt remains inconsistent across settings, and large prospective multicentre cohorts are scarce.

The Suicide Prevention and Intervention Study (SURVIVE) is a multicentre prospective cohort conducted across eight public hospitals in Spain.9 It was designed to examine suicide attempt recurrence after an index attempt in a real-world clinical sample aged 12 years and older, spanning both adolescents and adults. By focusing specifically on individuals with suicidal behaviour rather than combining ideation and behaviour, and by using documented suicide reattempt as the primary outcome, the study addresses an important gap in prospective evidence on short-term recurrence and its associated baseline factors in routine clinical care. In this Article, we report 12-month findings from 1730 participants and identify prognostic factors associated with time to suicide reattempt using time-to-event analysis.

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational cohort study was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines (see the Supplementary Material for a completed STROBE checklist).

The SURVIVE cohort includes individuals aged ≥12 years who present to emergency or psychiatric services after an index suicide attempt at eight public hospitals across five Spanish regions. These are: Hospital del Mar, Hospital Clínic, and Consorci Corporaciò Sanitària Parc Taulí in Catalonia; Hospital Universitario La Paz and Hospital Clinico San Carlos in Madrid; Hospital Virgen del Rocío in Andalusia; Hospital Universitario Central de Asturias in Asturias; and Hospital Universitario Araba in the Basque Country. Each hospital serves a catchment area of approximately 300,000–560,000 inhabitants. To minimise selection and information bias, we prospectively enrolled all consecutive patients meeting eligibility criteria at each participating hospital, used standardised assessment procedures, and ascertained suicide reattempts through systematic review of electronic medical records across study sites.

Cohort entry date was defined as the date of occurrence of the registered SA episode (i.e., index suicide attempt). Consistent with our primary outcome measure, the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS),10 and the DSM-5 definition,11 a SA was conceived as any self-injurious behaviour with at least some intent to die. A suicide reattempt was defined as the occurrence of a new SA within 12 months of the index attempt, documented in the participant's medical records. Participants who presented to the study sites with suicidal thoughts but without any self-injurious act were not included in the cohort.

All sites followed a common protocol. Inclusion criteria were: 1) age ≥12 years; 2) a suicide attempt within the previous 15 days. Exclusion criteria were: 1) inability to consent; 2) cognitive impairment or language barriers; 3) unclear intentionality of the self-injurious act; or 4) medical incapacity to complete assessments. For adolescents (12–17 years), both assent and parental consent were obtained.

The study size was determined by feasibility. All consecutive patients meeting eligibility criteria and presenting to the participating hospitals during the recruitment period (2019–2022) were invited to participate, yielding a cohort of 1730 individuals with complete baseline assessment and 12-month outcome ascertainment.

Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committees of all participating sites (Hospital del Mar, ref. 2019/8629/I; Hospital Clínic, ref. HCB/2020/0174; Consorci Corporaciò Sanitària Parc Taulí, ref. 2019/312; Hospital Universitario La Paz, ref. PI-3989; Hospital Clínico San Carlos, ref. 9/394-E_BC; Hospital Virgen del Rocío ref. 02/2020; Hospital Universitario Central de Asturias, ref. 269/19; and Hospital Universitario Araba, ref., PI2019189), and the study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04343703). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. In the case of people under 18 years of age, written informed consent was additionally obtained from their legal guardian.

A clinician evaluated the participants to determine eligibility based on the study's inclusion criteria and obtained their permission to be approached by a SURVIVE research assistant. Baseline assessments were conducted face to face and repeated at 12 months. Data covered sociodemographic, psychiatric diagnoses, suicidality, psychological measures, and index attempt characteristics.

People with lived experience of suicidal behaviour contributed to the design of the study through the advice of DSAS (Después del Suicidio), a non-profit organisation of suicide survivors. In addition, people with lived experience were involved in the design and refinement of the psychological interventions tested in the nested trials within the SURVIVE programme, although these intervention data are not reported in the present article.

Choice of primary outcome measure

The primary outcome in this study was the time to suicide reattempt within a 12-month follow-up period, defined as the occurrence of a new suicide attempt documented in the electronic clinical records of the participating sites. Follow-up interviews using the C-SSRS10 were used to characterise suicidality over time. Time-to-event was calculated in days.

As our primary outcome was time to event rather than a symptom score, clinical interpretation is based on hazard ratios derived from Cox proportional hazards models, which quantify the relative risk of reattempt associated with various baseline predictors.

Other outcome measures

The Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview for adults [MINI]12 and Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents [MINI-KID]13 made diagnoses of mental disorders at the index event. Childhood trauma was assessed at baseline with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form [CTQ-SF].14 A battery of symptom-related self-reported measures was administered at baseline and follow-up. These include self-reported symptoms of depression assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire [PHQ-9],15 impulsivity assessed by the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale [BIS-11],16 and quality of life assessed using the EuroQoL–5 Dimensions–5 Levels.17

Statistical analysis

To compare participants who reattempted vs those who did not at 12 months, Student's t-test was calculated for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d for continuous variables and Relative Risk (RR) with its 95% confidence interval (CI) for categorical variables. For psychiatric diagnoses and medications, individual categories were summarized as total number of diagnoses and number of psychotropic medications. Variables analysed categorically were coded using the following boundaries: number of psychiatric diagnoses (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5–7), number of psychotropic medications (0, 1, 2, 3, and 4–5), prior suicide attempts (0, 1–3, and 4 or more), suicidal ideation frequency (less than once per week, 1–5 times per week, and daily or several times per day), and childhood emotional neglect (CTQ-SF emotional neglect subscale, score ≥15 indicating moderate-to-severe neglect). For descriptive analyses, we used available-case data and reported the specific sample size (n) for each variable in Table 1 to document missingness. Among participants who reattempted suicide, the median time to reattempt (in days) and interquartile range (IQR) were computed. Cumulative risk of suicide reattempt over the 12-month follow-up was estimated using the 1–Kaplan–Meier function, stratified by sex and age group; differences between strata were assessed using the log-rank test. Time to event was defined as the number of days from the index attempt to either reattempt or censoring at 365 days.

To examine factors associated with SA repetition, Cox proportional hazards models were fitted. Deaths during follow-up represent a potential competing risk for suicide reattempt. Because information on cause of death was incomplete, deaths were handled as right-censoring events in our Cox models.

To maintain parsimony and clinical relevance, only variables showing significant between-group differences (two-sided, p < 0.05) or moderate-to-large effect sizes (Cohen's d ≥ 0.30 or RR ≥ 1.3) in Table 1 were entered into univariable Cox models. Variables meeting these criteria in univariable analyses were then considered for inclusion in a multivariable Cox model adjusted for age and sex. To achieve a parsimonious and stable final model, we applied a backward elimination procedure based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The resulting reduced model was systematically compared with a full model containing all initial covariates (including age, sex, comorbidities, and all psychometric scales). The parsimonious model demonstrated superior fit (ΔAIC = 19.19) with no significant loss of information according to the likelihood ratio test (χ2 = 6.81, df = 13, p = 0.912), and the key significant predictors and their hazard ratios (HRs) remained consistent across both specifications, supporting the robustness of the findings. Because the proportional hazards assumption was violated for the variable ‘site’ (Schoenfeld residual test χ2 (7) = 29.05, p < 0.001), the final multivariable model was stratified by site, and model performance was assessed by overall fit (likelihood ratio test) and discrimination, quantified using Harrell's concordance index (C-index). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess whether associations differed between adolescents and adults. Descriptive analyses were done using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 24), and survival analyses using R (version 2025.05.1 + 513; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), primarily the survival package.

Discussion

In this prospective multicentre cohort of 1730 individuals presenting after a suicide attempt, nearly one in five (19.6%, n = 340) reattempted within 12 months, in line with recent meta-analytic estimates.2 Among participants who reattempted within 12 months, 25% did so in the first two months and 50% within five months of the index attempt, underscoring the early post-attempt period as a critical window of heightened risk and the need to prioritise intensive, structured follow-up to ensure continuity of care.2,18,19 Given a 12-month baseline risk of approximately 20%, the adjusted hazard ratios observed for key predictors (ranging from about 1.5 to 2.5) correspond to estimated absolute risks of roughly 25–35% over the same period.

Five independent predictors were associated with an increased risk: high psychotropic medication burden (four or five prescribed psychotropic medications), a history of NSSI, higher frequency of suicidal ideation, and a greater number of prior suicide attempts, and moderate-to-severe childhood emotional neglect. Together, these variables delineate a clinically relevant profile of individuals at comparatively higher risk of recurrence after an index attempt. The model showed moderate discriminative ability (C-index ≈0.67), indicating that baseline factors alone capture only part of the heterogeneity in reattempt risk and underscoring the need for dynamic risk assessment during follow-up. These findings should therefore be interpreted as identifying prognostic associations and higher-risk clinical profiles, rather than as establishing causal mechanisms or direct targets of intervention. Although these characteristics may help clinicians prioritise closer monitoring and more intensive follow-up, whether modifying them reduces reattempt risk must be established in specifically designed intervention studies.

Baseline NSSI increased overall risk by 50%, but predicted reattempt only in adults, despite its higher prevalence in adolescents (76% vs 41%). While previous studies have linked adolescent NSSI to suicidal ideation and attempts,20 our findings suggest that NSSI during adolescence may not necessarily translate into increased short-term risk of suicide reattempt in this context. This pattern may indicate that adolescent NSSI sometimes serves functions more closely related to emotional self-regulation than suicide attempts,21 with different psychological underpinnings and risk trajectories. These findings highlight the importance of differentiating the clinical meaning and risk implications of NSSI across developmental stages and suggest that NSSI in adulthood may require different therapeutic strategies than in adolescence, including treatments with stronger evidence for NSSI such as cognitive-behavioural therapies22 or dialectical-behavioural therapy.23

Regarding suicidal ideation, ecological momentary assessment studies have shown that ideation can fluctuate markedly over short periods (hours or days) and that such variability can be a marker of elevated suicide risk. The association between higher frequency of ideation and reattempt observed in this study reinforces the need for timely, dynamic risk assessment and for interventions that directly target suicidal thoughts and their fluctuations. Brief, structured approaches such as safety planning and cognitive-behavioural interventions focused on suicidal ideation may be particularly well suited for individuals identified as high risk on this dimension.22,24

In our study, prior suicide attempts emerged as a robust independent predictor of subsequent suicide attempts. Individuals with one to three prior attempts already showed a significantly increased risk, and this risk was further elevated among those with four or more attempts, underscoring a clear dose–response relationship. These findings are consistent with previous literature identifying a history of suicide attempts as one of the strongest clinical markers of future suicide attempts, and they highlight the need for systematic identification and intensive follow-up of patients with any history of suicide attempts in clinical settings.5

Moderate-to-severe childhood emotional neglect emerged as a robust predictor of reattempt, consistent with previous work linking early emotional deprivations to persistent vulnerabilities and suicide risk.25 These findings support routine assessment of childhood maltreatment in individuals presenting after a suicide attempt and the integration of trauma-informed care, including trauma-focused treatments for post-traumatic stress and sequenced approaches offering stabilization before trauma-treatment.

The association between high psychotropic medication burden (four or five prescribed psychotropic medications) and suicide reattempt, observed in the overall sample and retained in sensitivity analyses, aligns with prior research26 and may reflect underlying clinical complexity, treatment resistance, or fragmented care provision. Individuals prescribed psychotropic medications also frequently use these agents in suicide attempts, underlining the importance of means-safety strategies and careful prescription practices.27 At the same time, more research is needed to determine to what extent high psychotropic medication burden signals appropriate responses to severe, treatment-resistant conditions vs overprescribing or lack of treatment coordination. Clarifying this distinction could guide interventions aimed at optimising pharmacological regimens and, when indicated, supporting safe deprescribing. Existing evidence on the impact of psychotropic medications on suicide attempts and NSSI suggests modest effects, partly due to methodological limitations of previous trials28; future randomised controlled studies including individuals with SA are needed to clarify the specific role of pharmacotherapy in this population.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample included a larger number of adults than adolescents, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect age-specific predictors in the adolescent sub-group. Second, deaths during follow-up represent a potential competing risk for suicide reattempt. Although suspected suicide deaths are routinely investigated by forensic services, the final cause-of-death determination is not always reflected in hospital records, so we could not reliably classify all deaths as suicidal or non-suicidal. We therefore handled deaths as right-censoring events in our Cox models rather than using formal competing-risks methods, which may slightly affect risk estimates in subgroups with higher mortality. Third, suicide reattempts were ascertained through regional electronic clinical records, which capture non-fatal reattempts leading to hospital presentation in the participating public health systems. While prior work suggests inconsistencies between methods,29 in our 12-month follow-up, relying solely on self-report would likely have been more vulnerable to recall bias and under-reporting, so we opted for an objective, record-based definition. The use of shared regional electronic health records increases the likelihood that reattempts presenting to other public hospitals within the same autonomous communities are captured; however, we may still have missed reattempts that did not result in hospital contact, occurred outside these systems, or resulted in death by suicide. As a result, our reattempt rates should be interpreted as conservative estimates, and some residual misclassification and limited generalisability to non-hospital-presenting suicide attempts cannot be ruled out. Fourth, we did not systematically assess or record personality disorders because the cohort included individuals younger than 18 years, for whom such diagnoses are not routinely applicable, and could not be assessed consistently across all participants. Consequently, we cannot rule out residual confounding related to underlying personality pathology. Fifth, all participants received treatment as usual, which varied across sites and may have included specific follow-up programs -particularly for adolescents-that could have influenced reattempt rates. Sixth, baseline assessments were conducted up to 15 days after the index attempt, potentially limiting the capture of very acute, state-dependent risk factors that are likely to fluctuate substantially over time.30 We are therefore extending this line of research by incorporating ecological momentary assessment (EMA) in the follow-up of this cohort, which will allow for more fine-grained, moment-to-moment assessment of suicidal thoughts and related risk factors in daily life. This approach is expected to provide a more precise picture of the temporal dynamics of suicide attempts and, ultimately, to improve risk estimation and clinical decision-making. Seventh, although the models incorporated a broad set of clinical and sociodemographic variables, some established or emerging risk factors were not assessed. Eighth, although the multivariable models showed adequate overall fit, their discriminative ability was only moderate (C-index approximately 0.67), indicating that baseline variables alone cannot provide precise individual-level risk stratification. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as identifying prognostic associations and higher-risk clinical profiles, rather than as a stand-alone clinical prediction tool. Ninth, the baseline factors associated with suicide reattempt in this study should not be assumed to represent direct causal targets of intervention; whether modifying these characteristics can reduce recurrence risk must be established in specifically designed intervention studies. Finally, data collection spanned both the COVID-19 pandemic and post-pandemic periods, during which patterns of suicide attempts and service use may have been affected by contextual changes, potentially limiting generalisability to other time frames.

In summary, this large prospective multicentre cohort study of patients attending mental health services after a suicide attempt provides robust, real-world evidence on baseline factors associated with suicide attempt recurrence. Our findings have implications for clinical assessment and service planning in the WHO European Region, where suicide and repeated attempts remain a major public health challenge. By identifying clinical profiles associated with comparatively higher recurrence risk, this study may help inform risk assessment and follow-up strategies across European health systems. These findings support age-sensitive post-attempt care and may help prioritise individuals with high psychotropic medication burden, frequent suicidal ideation, prior attempts, NSSI, and histories of emotional neglect for intensified monitoring and follow-up, while the extent to which modifying these factors reduces recurrence remains to be established.
