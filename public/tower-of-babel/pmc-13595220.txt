Factors influencing antibiotic use in confirmed viral respiratory tract infections
Authors: Siti Mardhiah Muhamad Fauzii, Azonim Egwu, Maebh Kelleher, Brian D Kent, Rory Nee, Aileen Conway, Jonathan McGrath, Colm Bergin, Liam Townsend
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of antibiotic therapy in confirmed viral respiratory tract infections is controversial, with discordance amongst guidelines.ObjectivesWe evaluated antibiotic usage in patients admitted with confirmed influenza A, respiratory syncytial virus and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infections, as well as factors associated with their initiation and duration.MethodsThis was a prospective observational cohort study of patients admitted to a tertiary centre with PCR-confirmed viral respiratory tract infection. Patient characteristics, antibiotic use, choice, route and duration, as well as patient outcomes were measured. Clinical and microbiological factors related to the admission were recorded. Multivariate regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with antibiotic initiation and duration.ResultsThere were 241 admissions with confirmed viral respiratory tract infections over the 2025/26 winter period. Antibiotic therapy was commenced in 208 (86%) cases, with a median 7 days of therapy given. Additional cultures were taken from 159, with positive bacterial cultures in 35 (35/159, 22%). A requirement for supplemental oxygen (OR 4.61, P = 0.02) and increased C-reactive protein at admission (OR 1.03, P < 0.001) were associated with increased likelihood to commence antibiotics, while ongoing supplemental oxygen requirement (β coefficient 0.41, P = 0.005) was associated with increased duration of antibiotic therapy. Antibiotic duration was not influenced by bacterial culture results.ConclusionsWe demonstrate high rates of antibiotic usage in patients admitted with confirmed viral respiratory tract infection, and identify opportunities for improved antimicrobial stewardship.

Introduction

The prescription of antibiotic therapy in laboratory-confirmed viral respiratory tract infections (RTIs) is controversial. The American Thoracic Society (ATS) recommends empirical antibiotic therapy if there is clinical or radiographic evidence of community-acquired pneumonia.1 However, there are no published studies demonstrating the benefit of antibacterial therapy in viral RTI, with a recent multicentre study showing no difference in outcomes, and the IDSA have withdrawn their support for the ATS guidelines.2,3 Inappropriate antibiotic usage can have negative consequences for individual patients, as well as more broadly impacting on antimicrobial resistance.4,5

The rates of bacterial coinfection in viral RTIs at time of presentation is variable. However, overall rates of coinfection appear low.6 Where there is concern for bacterial pneumonia, there is strong evidence that short (5 days or fewer) courses of antibiotic therapy are appropriate.7,8 Furthermore, prolonged IV therapy for infections where there is a highly bioavailable oral option has been shown to increase length of stay and has increased treatment-associated complications.9

The aims of this study were to quantify antibiotic usage at time of admission in patients with laboratory-confirmed viral RTI, as well as to assess the clinical, microbiological and radiographic evidence to support antibiotic usage. We investigated the factors influencing both the initiation of antibiotic therapy and the duration of same, as well as investigating the incidence of antibiotic-associated adverse events.

Methods

Study setting and participants

This was a prospective observational cohort study spanning the 2025/2026 viral respiratory infection season at St James’s Hospital Dublin, a tertiary centre providing care to adults aged ≥18 years in Ireland. Patients admitted at our institution with a clinical syndrome consistent with severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) are recommended to undergo nasopharyngeal sampling for viral respiratory pathogens. However, this is not protocolized and requires treating physicians to order these tests. All patients with a positive nasopharyngeal swab multiplex panel PCR test for influenza A, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) or severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) within 24 h of admission to our institution were included. We excluded patients diagnosed in the outpatient setting who were not admitted, transfers from other hospitals, and patients who had a positive result more than 24 h into their admission. As the study centre provides care only for adults, all participants were ≥18 years of age. The study site has infectious diseases and antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) services on site. However, input from these services for patients with RTIs are not mandated at this institution.

Clinical covariates

Demographic data recorded included age, sex and pre-admission accommodation status (housed, long-term care or experiencing homelessness). Smoking status was also recorded, and categorized as never-smoker, ex-smoker or current smoker. Pre-admission medical data recorded included number and nature of comorbidities and co-medications, including whether the patient was in receipt of immunosuppressive treatment, as well as Rockwood Clinical Frailty Score (CFS) for those aged over 65. Factors recorded relating to the acute admission included admission C-reactive protein (CRP), admission chest X-ray (CXR) results, fever (temperature ≥38°C) reported prior to admission or documented during first 24 h, delirium at time of admission (defined as a 4AT score ≥1, calculated by assessing alertness, orientation to time and place, attention, and acute changes in cognition), need for and duration of supplemental oxygen, and severity of disease. Severe disease was a composite of needing non-invasive ventilation, ICU admission, or death. CXR results were dichotomized as either showing changes consistent with infection or not, based on the presence of pulmonary infiltrates, in line with ATS guidelines.10

Microbiological data included results of nasopharyngeal PCR and any sputum or blood cultures sent, as well as urinary antigen testing for pneumococcus and legionella. Only cultures sent within the first 48 h of admission were included, to avoid inclusion of nosocomial infection. Turnaround time for results of nasopharyngeal PCR and urinary antigen testing were less than 24 h in all cases, while all blood and sputum samples were cultured for 120 h, with real-time delivery of positive results during that time. Screening swabs for multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) were recorded, which included nasal, axilla and groin swabs for MRSA and rectal swabs for ESBL, carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (CPE), and VRE. These are performed on all patients admitted to our institution, with the exception of CPE, for which screening is reserved for patients admitted to ICU. Intercurrent stool sample analysis and associated Clostridioides difficile rates during the course of admission were recorded. The sending of a stool sample is a surrogate marker for diarrhoea, as formed stool samples do not undergo analysis at our institution. C. difficile testing is performed on all stool samples sent for PCR and culture on inpatients in our institution, even if not specifically requested by the ordering clinician. Antimicrobial data recorded included antibiotic choice, and duration and route of administration. Antibiotic use in the community prior to admission was not recorded. Our institutional guidelines for empirical community-acquired pneumonia therapy requiring admission recommend dual therapy with either a β-lactam or cephalosporin with macrolide or tetracycline. The recommended duration of therapy is 5 days. There is no institutional guideline on the use of antibiotics in the context of confirmed viral respiratory infection.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are reported as medians with IQRs and means with SDs, as appropriate. Factors associated with initiation of antibiotic therapy were analysed using Wilcoxon ranked-sum (z) for continuous non-parametric variables and Pearson’s chi-squared (χ2) test for categorical variables. Subsequently, Firth’s penalized multivariable logistic regression was used, including all variables with P ≤ 0.07 on univariate analysis. Factors associated with duration of antibiotic therapy were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation test (ρ) with a subsequent multivariable linear regression including variables with P ≤ 0.07 on univariate analysis. Variables known to be associated with antibiotic consumption a priori were also included in analysis, namely severity of disease, evidence of pneumonia on imaging, microbiological sampling for bacterial culture, and viral pathogen identified on PCR. All analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Statistical significance was indicated by P < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

There were 1036 patients admitted during the study period with a clinical syndrome consistent with a viral RTI, of whom 854 (82%) underwent nasopharyngeal PCR. There were no patient factors associated with failure to have a nasopharyngeal PCR sample taken; however, admission under a surgical service was associated with a decreased likelihood of having a diagnostic sample taken (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC Online). Nasopharyngeal PCR results were available within 24 h of admission. There were 241 positive results; 150 influenza A, 77 RSV and 15 SARS-CoV-2 (1 patient had RSV and influenza A coinfection) (Figure 1). The median age of the cohort was 72 years (IQR 57–81), and just over half were female (n = 130, 54%). This was a multimorbid group, with median comorbidity count of 4 (IQR 2–5), while 13% (n = 32) were in receipt of immunosuppressive therapy. Respiratory comorbidities were the most common, with 88 patients having a chronic lung condition (n = 60 COPD, n = 27 asthma, n = 6 bronchiectasis, n = 7 pulmonary fibrosis; 7 patients had more than one respiratory comorbidity). There was no documentation in the clinical notes of vaccine status against any of the three viruses of interest in 218 (90%). Nine (4%) had documented receipt of both influenza and SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, six (2%) patients had documented receipt of influenza vaccine alone, and one patient had documented receipt of SARS-CoV-2 vaccine alone. Seven (3%) patients had documentation that they received no seasonal vaccine.

CXR changes consistent with infection at time of admission were recorded in 108 (46%). Of these,71 had patchy infiltrates, 24 had dense consolidation, 7 had a pleural effusion and 6 had interstitial changes. Supplemental oxygen was required by 81 (34%) patients at admission. The median admission CRP was 47 mg/L (IQR 21–115, normal range 0–5), in keeping with an acute inflammatory reaction. Severe disease, as defined as a composite of death or requiring non-invasive ventilation or ICU admission, was present in 27 (11%). Complete cohort characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Antibiotic usage and microbiological sampling

Antibiotic therapy was prescribed in 208 (86%) cases. The median duration of therapy was 7 days (IQR 5–8), with median duration for IV therapy of 4 days (IQR 3–6). In those receiving antibiotic treatment, dual-agent therapy was administered in 16% (33/208). The distribution of antibiotic therapy, the initial agents used, and the cumulative days of antibiotics used are shown in Figure 2. Co-amoxiclav was the most common antibiotic prescribed. Carbapenem use was low, with no patients initiated on carbapenem therapy and two patients subsequently escalated to meropenem. Only one patient received a fluoroquinolone. Oseltamivir was prescribed for 146/150 patients with influenza, while ribavirin was prescribed in 21 patients with RSV. All patients in receipt of ribavirin had an active haematological malignancy, had received a bone marrow transplant, or had undergone chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapy. Ribavirin prescription was at the discretion of the treating physician.

At least one sample for microbiological culture was sent in 159 (66%) cases. Patients with a higher admission CRP and with an admission CXR showing parenchymal disease were more likely to have a sample sent for bacterial culture (Table S2). Blood cultures were the most common sample sent (128/241, 53%), with urinary antigens sent in 97 (40%) and sputum sample sent in 30% (72/241). At least one positive result was found in 35 (35/159, 22%) of the 159 patients who had a sample sent, with an overall confirmed coinfection rate in the cohort receiving antibiotics of 17% (35/208). Blood cultures were positive in 5 cases (5/128, 4%), urinary antigens were positive in 17 cases (17/97, 18%) and sputum culture was positive in 18 cases (18/72, 25%). The blood culture results included two CoNS. These were isolated in the context of indwelling central venous catheters and were considered true pathogens. A breakdown of microbiological sampling and outcomes is shown in Figure 1.

Carriage of MDRO was identified in 36 patients (15%). VRE was the most common pathogen isolated (14%). Stool samples were sent in 25 patients (10%). Of these 24 were in the group receiving antibiotics (24/208, 12%), while 1 was in the group that did not receive antibiotics (1/33, 3%). There were three confirmed cases of toxin- and PCR-positive C. difficile infection, all in patients who received antibiotic therapy with piperacillin/tazobactam for more than 7 days and were all older than 65. One patient with C. difficile died during their inpatient stay.

Factors influencing antibiotic use

Given the high frequency of antibiotic prescription in this cohort, we investigated variables associated with both initiation of therapy and duration of antibiotic therapy. There were no patient-related variables associated with initiation of antibiotic therapy. However, the group receiving antibiotics had a significantly higher admission CRP than those who did not receive antibiotic treatment (58 mg/L versus 19 mg/L, z = −5.28, P < 0.001), and requiring supplemental oxygen was also associated with antibiotic initiation (38% versus 9%, χ2 = 10.30, P = 0.001). The complete univariate analysis results are shown in Table S3. These associations were evaluated further using a multivariable logistic regression model. Both admission CRP (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05, P < 0.0001) and requirement for supplemental oxygen at admission (OR 3.99, 95% CI 1.22–13.08, P = 0.02) remained associated with antibiotic initiation (Table 2).

Factors associated with increased duration of antibiotic therapy were also investigated. Similar to antibiotic initiation, elevated admission CRP (Spearman’s rho = 0.18, P = 0.01) and duration of supplemental oxygen requirement (Spearman’s rho = 0.28, P = 0.01) were associated with a longer duration of therapy. Additionally, increasing age, male sex and comorbidity count were all associated with increased antibiotic therapy duration. There was no association between duration of treatment and microbiological results. Complete univariate results are shown in Table S4. A multivariable linear regression was used to further evaluate these factors. Following adjustment, only increasing days of supplemental oxygen requirement was associated with increased antibiotic therapy duration (Table 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated high rates of antibiotic use in confirmed viral RTI, with 86% of patients receiving antibiotic therapy. Clinical factors such as admission CRP level and need for supplemental oxygen therapy were associated with antibiotic initiation. Duration of therapy also exceeded guideline recommendations, with a median 7 days of therapy given, of which 4 days were IV. This was despite nasopharyngeal PCR results being available in all cases within 24 h of admission. Following antibiotic initiation, duration of therapy was not influenced by microbiological results, with confirmed bacterial coinfection identified in only 17% of patients receiving antibiotics; instead, it was associated with ongoing supplemental oxygen requirement.

Empirical antibiotic therapy when patients present with an infection syndrome is appropriate. However, choices should be based on the available data, and subsequently tailored as more information becomes available. There is an abundance of evidence that shorter courses of therapy are non-inferior and safer than longer courses, including in RTIs.11,12 Similarly, early oral switch is advised within the first 72 h.13 This was not borne out in our cohort. Despite nasopharyngeal PCR results being available within 24 h and bacterial culture results being reported in real time, patients received antibiotic therapy for a median of 7 days, with the majority given IV.

There may be several possible reasons for the discordance between evidence, guidelines and clinical practice seen. Delays in translating evidence into clinical practice are well recognized.14 Furthermore, patients with confirmed viral RTIs at our institution were not admitted under a single specialty. Instead, they were spread across various specialties with acute medicine training, and familiarity with guidelines and the relevant evidence in the field may vary. Prescriber-related factors have previously been identified as drivers of non-guideline-adherent antibiotic usage.15,16 Additionally, the patient cohort in our study was multimorbid, with most patients living with some degree of frailty. Frailty is associated with prolonged antimicrobial usage, and may drive prescribing patterns.17 Judicious antibiotic deprescribing in this cohort should be encouraged.18 Notably, neither age nor frailty were identified as being associated with duration of antibiotic therapy in our cohort.

The overall rate of confirmed bacterial coinfection at time of admission in our cohort was 17%. This is broadly in keeping with rates reported in other cohorts.19,20 However, it should be noted that there is substantial variation in coinfection estimates, in part due to differences in screening rates.21 There was variation in microbiological sampling in our study, with one-third of patients not having any microbiological sample other than a nasopharyngeal PCR sent. Rates of urinary antigen testing in our cohort were less than 50%, similar to those reported elsewhere.22 Sputum sampling was lower than that reported elsewhere for community-acquired pneumonia.23 This may be due to the relative lack of sputum production in viral RTIs.

CRP was the strongest predictor of antibiotic initiation. This is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that physicians ascribe a higher weight to CRP results over other clinical factors when choosing antibiotic therapy.24 Both initiation and ongoing administration of antibiotic therapy in our cohort was associated with supplemental oxygen requirement. Ongoing symptom burden has been recognized previously as being associated with increased antibiotic usage.25 A pre-existing diagnosis of COPD was found in 25% of patients with confirmed viral RTI. While hypoxia is a common finding in COPD exacerbations, current guidelines do not recommend its use in guiding antibiotic use outside the ICU setting.26,27 Additionally, we did not identify any other markers of severity associated with antibiotic duration, suggesting that supplemental oxygen is a true association rather than a surrogate marker.

The absence of any association between duration of antibiotic therapy and microbiological results demonstrates a significant shortcoming in the prescribing habits in this cohort. Furthermore, the use of antibiotics may be causing unnecessary toxicity in patients. While we did not directly assess adverse events from antibiotic therapy in this cohort, processing of stool samples is a proxy for diarrhoeal illness. Antibiotics are a recognized contributor to diarrhoeal illness.28 Our results are consistent with this, with 12% of the patients receiving antibiotic therapy having a stool sample processed over the course of their admission compared with only one patient in the cohort who did not receive antibiotics. There were three cases of confirmed C. difficile infection, with one death in this group. The low rate of C. difficile infection may be due to low usage of antimicrobials associated with increased risk of same, as no patients received clindamycin, and three patients received either a carbapenem or fluoroquinolone. However, those that did develop C. difficile infection were all older than 65 and received broad-spectrum penicillin therapy for more than 7 days, which are known risk factors.29

This study highlights the need for active AMS in viral RTIs. These patients represent a significant burden of disease during the winter months. An active AMS programme may improve adherence to guidelines, reduce individual adverse events for patients receiving antibiotic therapy, as well as reducing the overall risk of increasing antimicrobial resistance. This may be particularly relevant in patients with pre-existing lung disease or frailty, who have previously been highlighted as cohorts that may benefit from active stewardship.30,31 These AMS interventions have proven successful in other settings.32,33 Given the rapid diagnostic turnaround time for PCR and urinary antigen results (<24 h), review by an AMS team at 72 h (which is the recommended antibiotic duration for uncomplicated community-acquired pneumonia) may empower treating physicians to discontinue antibiotics in patients with confirmed viral RTIs and no evidence of bacterial coinfection. Pharmacist-led antibiotic deprescribing interventions have been effective in other infections.34 A less resource-intensive intervention would be a physician prompt within the electronic patient record at 72 h, mandating antibiotic review. However, alert fatigue may reduce the effectiveness of this intervention.35 This study also demonstrates the need for diagnostic stewardship. Admission under a non-medical specialty was associated with a lower rate of nasopharyngeal sampling for PCR testing despite a clinical presentation consistent with SARI. A care bundle including an order set for nasopharyngeal PCR for patients presenting with a compatible clinical syndrome may improve diagnostic sampling. Microbiological sampling was driven by laboratory CRP levels and CXR findings rather than the decision to initiate antibiotic therapy. Institutional and staff factors have previously been shown to influence sampling for microbiological culture, and this may provide an opportunity for intervention.36

There are several limitations worth noting in our study. Firstly, it was observational in nature, and a causal relationship between factors analysed and antibiotic usage cannot be inferred. We may underestimate the true prevalence of bacterial coinfection due to inconsistent sampling. Furthermore, diagnostic techniques for identifying bacterial RTI are suboptimal, and decisions regarding antibiotic therapy may be made using ancillary information such as CXR changes and serum markers of inflammation. Our study also did not evaluate the knowledge, attitudes and perceptions of prescribers towards antibiotic prescription in viral RTIs. Nosocomial bacterial pneumonia is a separate entity and has separate treatment guidelines, and was not included in this study. While stool sample processing is used as a surrogate marker for diarrhoeal illness, a causal relationship with antibiotic therapy cannot be inferred. This was a single-centre study, and the practice and recommended changes may not be applicable in other jurisdictions. However, there are opportunities to implement the principles of AMS here, which are universal. The impact of antibiotic usage on emergent antimicrobial resistance could not be assessed in this study.

This study demonstrates high rates of antibiotic consumption in patients with confirmed viral RTI, with duration of therapy exceeding those recommended in pneumonia guidelines and not tailored to microbiological results. It highlights areas for which AMS intervention may improve antibiotic usage in this cohort.
