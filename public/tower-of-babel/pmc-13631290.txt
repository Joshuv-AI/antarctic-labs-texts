Coulombic surface dipole trapping ionomer for efficient proton exchange membrane fuel cells
Authors: Siyuan Wang, Fanpeng Kong, Zhengyang Bao, Xiaozhi Zhao, Lei Zhang, Zhongxin Song, Junjie Li, Lei Du, Yongjun Xu, Bo Zhao, Geping Yin, Xueliang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUniform ionomer distribution and controlled sulfonate adsorption are critical for low-Pt-loading proton exchange membrane fuel-cell membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs), especially at high current density. In conventional Pt/C catalysts, differences between Pt and carbon surfaces promote sulfonate adsorption on Pt and ionomer aggregation on carbon, thereby limiting proton and oxygen transport. Here we show that a single Co atom dipole layer covering Pt/C (SCDC-Pt) regulates the catalyst-ionomer interface through electrostatic attraction between positively charged Co sites and sulfonate groups. The resulting MEA delivers a peak power density of 1.4 W cm−2 and only 26 mV loss at 0.8 A cm−2 after 30,000 square-wave cycles, compared with 0.86 W cm−2 and 75 mV loss for commercial Pt/C under identical conditions. Operando characterization and simulations indicate that surface dipoles promote a more uniform ionomer distribution, suppress sulfonate poisoning of Pt, and stabilize the Pt structure. Electrochemical diagnostics show lower apparent sulfonate coverage ( ~ 8%) and local O2 transport resistance (38.6 S m−1) than those of commercial Pt/C ( ~ 20% and 48.1 S m−1).Subject terms: Energy, Fuel cells, Fuel cells

Introduction

Proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) are emerging as viable power-generation devices that efficiently convert hydrogen into electricity without pollutant emissions1–3. A high Pt loading is always necessary to overcome the kinetically sluggish oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) at the cathode in industrial membrane electrode assembly (MEA)4. The cost of Pt has hindered the deployment of cost-effective and scalable low-temperature fuel-cell electric vehicles5–7. Reducing the Pt loading in MEAs (~0.1 mgPt cm−2) offers a practical route to lower cost8,9, but low-Pt-loading MEAs often suffer substantial voltage losses at high current densities10. Both the accessibility and local structure of the triple-phase boundary in MEAs, therefore, play decisive roles in device performance, distinct from their influence in conventional liquid three-electrode systems11,12. Inhomogeneous proton-conduction ionomer distribution on carbon supports causes discontinuous proton-conduction pathways and limits active-site accessibility. In addition, strong adsorption of side-chain sulfonate anions on Pt induces ionomer deformation and poor gas permeability, leading to abnormally high local O2 transport resistance13–21. A detailed understanding of chemical coupling at the ionomer-electrocatalyst interface is thus essential for tailoring interfacial structure and improving MEA performance.

Recent studies have demonstrated that rational engineering of the catalyst-ionomer interface, regulation of ionomer distribution, and optimization of the local microenvironment are essential for improving PEMFC performance and structural stability22–26. The intrinsic heterogeneity of electrocatalysts leads to strong sulfonate adsorption on the Pt surface, whereas ionomers tend to aggregate on carbon because of weak mutual interactions. Precise control of pore-size distribution and surface chemistry can regulate ionomer-dispersion kinetics and side-chain sulfonate adsorption27–37. Spatial confinement in mesoporous carbon suppresses sulfonate poisoning of the Pt surface and facilitates local oxygen transport38–40. Porous graphene encapsulation of Pt nanoparticles has also been used to physically isolate Pt nanoparticles from the ionomers and reduce ionomer coverage41–49. In addition, surface-NHx groups grafted onto carbon supports can tailor ionomer distribution through Coulombic interactions45,50–53. The sp2-hybridized N atoms interact strongly with sulfonates, and they improve ionomer-distribution uniformity. Single metal atoms coordinated with four N atoms (MN4) exhibit pronounced charge separation, with positive charge concentrated mainly at the metal-dipole sites54–56. Using single-metal-atom-dipoles with high positive charge density should therefore further promote ionomer distribution through Coulombic coupling, but this strategy remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we establish compositional homogeneity in SCDC-Pt, which delivers a peak power density of 1.4 W cm−2 and a current density of 0.14 A cm−2 at 0.85 V under an absolute pressure of 150 kPa, showing competitive performance compared to commercial Pt (0.86 W cm−2 and 0.06 A cm−2 at 0.85 V). Electrochemical diagnostics establish that SCDC-Pt has a lower apparent sulfonate coverage (~8%) and local O2 transport resistance (38.6 S m−1) than Pt (~20% and 48.1 S m−1). Operando characterization combined with theoretical analysis indicates that the single-atom dipole layer improves ionomer dispersion and modifies adsorption, thereby increasing triple-phase-boundary accessibility and oxygen permeability. The single-atom dipole layer also stabilizes the Pt geometric structure during operation, which contributes to improved MEA durability. Accordingly, after 30,000 square-wave cycles, the SCDC-Pt MEA shows only 26 mV voltage loss at 0.8 A cm−2, compared with 75 mV for the Pt MEA.

Results

Computational analysis

Uniform ionomer distribution on carbon support plays an important role in reducing local oxygen-related mass transport resistance20. Ion–dipole recognition is proposed to anchor the ionomer on the carbon substrate by locally molecular interaction. The Co single-atom dipoles are embedded in a thin poly-dopamine (PDA) shell that conformally encapsulates the entire electrocatalyst, including both Pt nanoparticles and the carbon support. This design homogenizes the ionomer distribution, in contrast to ionomer aggregation on bare carbon driven by simple physical adsorption (Fig. 1a). Meanwhile, the thin PDA shell used to stabilize the single-atom dipoles prevents the fresh Pt surface from being poisoned by ionomer sulfonate groups57. Theoretical calculations are conducted to understand the interaction between single-atom dipoles and ionomers, as well as its effect on ionomer location20. Figure 1b, c shows the differential charge densities for branched sulfonate-C and sulfonate-single-dipole composites, respectively. The diffuse charge distribution on carbon in Fig. 1b indicates weak affinity between the ionomer and bare carbon. By contrast, pronounced charge polarization reveals strong ion–dipole electrostatic interactions between sulfonate groups and single-atom dipoles (Fig. 1c). Meanwhile, a thin PDA shell alleviates sulfonate poisoning of SCDC-Pt compared with the strong adsorption on Pt (Fig. 1d, e). Notably, single-atom dipoles mediate the charge modulation of sulfonate toward SCDC-Pt relative to C-Pt, which lacks embedded single metal atoms (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are performed to understand the Pt-ionomer interface fine structure58. Figure 1f and Supplementary Fig. 2 show the equilibrium structures of SCDC-Pt-ionomer and Pt-ionomer systems, respectively. The single-atom dipoles induce a more homogeneous and thinner ionomer distribution than that in the Pt-ionomer system. Quantification analysis of ionomer density along the direction normal to the SCDC-Pt surface shows that ionomer is concentrated in a narrow range in the SCDC-Pt-ionomer system (Supplementary Fig. 3), consistent with sulfonate trapping by single-atom dipoles. The broad distribution in the Pt-ionomer system reflects the random ionomer localization, with thicker regions expected to increase local oxygen-transport resistance. The interaction between Co dipoles and sulfonate is further evidenced by the higher S-atom density shown in Fig. 1g. The radial distribution function (RDF) describes the probability of finding atom B at a distance r from atom A. The sharper C-C RDF peak between carbon atoms in the ionomer backbones indicates enhanced conformational ordering driven by the trapping effect of Co single-atom dipoles (Fig. 1h). The detailed interfacial structures of the two models are shown in Fig. 1i, j, with the substrate removed to avoid visual interference. The homogenous ionomer localization produces long-chain ion-pair clusters between sulfonate groups and hydronium ions through combined hydrogen-bonding and ion–dipole interactions (Fig. 1j). The long-range sulfonate-hydronium clusters may support proton-conduction continuity and enhance fuel-cell performance. Overall, the simulations suggest that Co single-atom dipoles interact with sulfonate groups and promote a thinner, more localized ionomer distribution. Because DFT and MD models cannot fully reproduce the complex catalyst-ionomer interface under operating fuel-cell conditions, these theoretical results should be interpreted as mechanistic support for the experimental observations rather than direct proof. The optimized atomic coordinates of the electronic-structure models and the initial and final configurations of the MD simulations are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Synthesis and structure characterizations of SCDC-Pt

Theoretical calculations suggest that single-atom dipoles promote homogeneous ionomer distribution on carbon substrate through local ion–dipole recognition between positively charged Co sites and sulfonate groups. SCDC-Pt, in which single Co-atom dipoles coat the entire Pt/C composite, including both the carbon support and the Pt nanoparticles, was synthesized in two steps. Briefly, spontaneous dopamine polymerization was used to conformally encapsulate the Pt/C catalyst composite, followed by complexation between the globally distributed polydopamine layer and Co ions to yield SCDC-Pt with a Co loading of 0.85% (Supplementary Table 1). As a control, C-Pt, in which the entire Pt/C electrocatalyst surface is coated with polydopamine but without embedded Co atoms, was prepared using a similar polymerization procedure. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) shows that SCDC-Pt contains uniformly distributed Pt nanoparticles with an average size of ~2.5 nm, similar to that of Pt nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 4). To determine whether the electrocatalysts were covered by a single-atom dipole layer, high-angle and low-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM and LAADF-STEM) were performed, respectively, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6. Owing to light-element specimens exhibiting strong elastic and inelastic scattering at low angles, LAADF-STEM provides a high signal-to-noise ratio for light elements. The contrast difference for Pt nanoparticles at the same locations in the HAADF and LAADF images indicates that the Pt nanoparticles are covered by single-atom dipole layers.

Aberration-corrected STEM and high-resolution TEM were further used to characterize the surface atomic arrangement. As shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 7, an amorphous thin PDA shell containing atomically dispersed atoms is observed; the single-atom dipoles are relatively random compared with the ordered Pt atoms. An atomically resolved HAADF-STEM image of SCDC-Pt is shown in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 8. The presence of Co single sites is supported by the difference in brightness intensity, which reflects the large difference in atomic number between Pt and Co (Fig. 2c). HAADF-STEM imaging and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 2d, e) reveal homogeneous Co distribution across the electrocatalyst, which may promote ionomer distribution through ion–dipole interactions. The EDS mapping further supports the localization of Co dipole sites in the thin coating shell. Element-selective line-scan analysis (Fig. 2f) reveals a thin shell of 2−3 coating layers, consistent with the HR-TEM observations (Supplementary Fig. 7). The co-occurrence of Co and C signals, in contrast to the Pt signal, supports the successful embedding of Co atoms in the PDA shell. As a control, C-Pt without positively charged Co atoms was also synthesized, where both Pt NPs and carbon support are covered by a thin PDA shell (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10).

X-ray absorption spectra (XAS) were further performed to probe the electronic and geometric structures of SCDC-Pt. The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) of SCDC-Pt shows a lower white-line intensity at Pt L3-edge than that of Pt, indicating a lower Pt chemical state (Supplementary Fig. 11). This lower chemical state may arise from modulation by the single-Co-atom dipoles, which affects the electronic structure. Fourier-transformed (FT) k2-weighted χ (k) functions of extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) of electrocatalysts at Pt L3-edge are performed to understand bulk geometry, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 12. SCDC-Pt exhibits a geometric structure similar to that of Pt, indicating that the surface metal dipoles do not substantially affect the Pt coordination environment. Least-squares EXAFS R-space fitting was conducted to quantify the coordinative structure as shown in Supplementary Fig. 13 and Table 2. The fitting result for SCDC-Pt reveals a dominant Pt-Pt scattering path with a coordination number (CN) of 8.8 and a bond length of 2.76 Å, nearly identical to those of Pt (CN = 8.7 and bond length = 2.75 Å). Co K-edge XANES of SCDC-Pt, shown in Fig. 2g along with Co foil and Co3O4 standards, indicates that the Co adsorption edge lies between those of Co foil and Co3O4, indicating an oxidized Co state lower than +3. The pre-edge intensity of SCDC-Pt arises from its higher structural symmetry59. The detailed Co coordination environment was further revealed by FT k2-weighted χ (k) EXAFS at the Co K-edge, as shown in Fig. 2h. The prominent peaks at ~1.6 and ~2.2 Å are assigned to the contribution of Co-O/N and Co-Co at the first shell. The dominance of a peak at ~1.5 Å for the Co dipole with the absence of the Co-Co peak indicates its intrinsic atomic dispersion, in line with the electron microscopy results. The fitting result shows the typical planar tetra-coordination CoN4 structure for SCDC-Pt (Supplementary Fig. 14). Wavelet-transform (WT) EXAFS analysis was also performed to examine the coordination environment, as shown in Fig. 2i. The maximum in the WT-EXAFS of SCDC-Pt occurs at k = 4.5 Å−1, compared with 7.0 Å−1 for Co foil and 6.1 Å−1 for Co3O4. This negative shift in maximum intensity further supports the monodispersed structure60,61. Together, the electron microscopy and XAS results confirm the successful construction of positively charged, monodispersed Co dipole sites embedded in a thin polydopamine layer.

Membrane electrode assembly performance of SCDC-Pt

To investigate the effect of single-atom dipoles on electrocatalyst performance in MEAs, single-cell measurements were conducted at 80 °C using H2 and O2 as the anode and cathode gases, respectively. Figure 3a shows the I–V polarization curves of MEA with SCDC-Pt loaded at the cathode, operated at a back pressure of 50 kPa under H2/O2 flow rates of 300/400 sccm. SCDC-Pt achieves a peak power density of 1.4 W cm−2 and a current density of 0.14 A cm−2 at 0.85 V, higher than those of C-Pt (1.0 W cm−2 and 0.08 A cm−2 at 0.85 V) as shown in Supplementary Fig. 15. The MEA with Pt at the cathode delivers a peak power density of 0.86 W cm−2 and a current density of 0.06 A cm−2 at 0.85 V (Fig. 3b), both lower than those of SCDC-Pt. Under more practical H2/air conditions, SCDC-Pt reaches a peak power density of 0.89 W cm−2, higher than those of C-Pt (0.78 W cm−2) and commercial Pt (0.70 W cm−2) (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 16). Two main findings emerge regarding the effect of the surface structure on MEA performance. First, the thin PDA layer improves reaction dynamics when the coating time is optimized at 1.5 h, balancing sulfonate adsorption and mass transport (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). Second, the single-atom dipoles are associated with the higher measured performance. Stability is another critical criterion for evaluating electrocatalysts because noble-metal dissolution and carbon-support corrosion can cause degradation. Following the stability-evaluation protocol proposed by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), 30,000 square-wave cycles between 0.6 V (3 s) and 0.95 V (3 s) in an argon atmosphere were applied to assess degradation resistance. After 30,000 square-wave cycles, the SCDC-Pt MEA shows only a 26 mV voltage loss at 0.8 A cm−2, whereas the commercial Pt/C MEA exhibits a substantially larger loss of 75 mV under identical testing conditions. The durability of SCDC-Pt is competitive with recently reported Pt-based electrocatalysts and meets the 2025 DOE stability target (Supplementary Table 3)62. Co retention and dispersion after the stability test are supported by ICP-MS and EOL EDS mapping analyses (Supplementary Fig. 19 and Table S4).

To understand why single-atom dipoles enhance fuel cell performance, a series of electrochemical tests in a single fuel cell was conducted. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of SCDC-Pt recorded at 50 mV s−1 are shown in Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 20. The H adsorption/desorption region observed for SCDC-Pt was used to calculate the electrochemical surface area (ECSA) of the electrode63. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, the average ECSA of SCDC-Pt, calculated from the H-atom desorption region, is 30 m2 mgPt−1, lower than that of Pt (53 m2 mgPt−1), owing to partial coverage of active sites by the coating shell. CO was also used as a probe molecule to evaluate the ECSA of Pt under the assumption that each Pt atom adsorbs one CO molecule. Figure 3e exhibits the CO-stripping profile of SCDC-Pt, in which the electrocatalyst surface is covered by CO in the pre-peak region and by O-containing species in the post-peak region64,65. SCDC-Pt exhibits a CO stripping ECSA of 44 m2 mgPt−1, lower than that of Pt (50 m2 mgPt−1). Given the lower ECSA of SCDC-Pt and its similar intrinsic kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 22), the performance enhancement is attributed to homogeneous ionomer distribution enabled by Coulombic trapping. Sulfonate distribution and coverage on the electrocatalysts were examined using CO-displacement measurements at 0.4 V (vs. RHE) as shown in Fig. 3f. The pronounced reduction-current peak arises when purged CO displaces pre-adsorbed sulfonate anions from the Pt surface66. The CO-displacement peak of SCDC-Pt is sharper and appears earlier than that of Pt, suggesting weaker sulfonate adsorption on the Pt surface67. The apparent sulfonate-group coverage (θdis) on the electrode was calculated as θdis = 2Qdis/Qoxi, where Qdis and Qoxi are the CO-displacement charge and CO electro-oxidation charge, respectively. Because of practical MEA complexities, including limited local accessibility and non-Faradaic contributions, these calculated values are defined as apparent coverages rather than absolute quantities. The apparent sulfonate group coverage of the SCDC-Pt electrode is ~8%, lower than that of the Pt electrode (20%) (Fig. 3g).

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to examine the charge-, mass-, and proton-transfer processes associated with the ORR68. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 23–25, both EIS spectra and distribution-of-relaxation-time analysis show decreased charge-transfer resistance (Rct), mass transport resistance (Rmt), and proton conduction resistance (Rpr) for SCDC-Pt, supporting its improved performance. Homogeneous ionomer distribution and alleviated sulfonate adsorption benefit both charge transfer and mass/proton transport. Dry proton accessibility, another key parameter for evaluating ionomer coverage (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27), was calculated by the ratio of ECSARH = 20%/ECSARH = 100%20. SCDC-Pt reveals a dry proton accessibility of ~87.5%, higher than that of Pt (41.2%), indicating a more homogeneously distributed ionomer. Lastly, local O2 transport resistance (RO2local), which originates from oxygen diffusion through the ionomer film covering Pt sites, was assessed using the limiting-current-density method under low cathode O2 concentration69. The total mass-transport resistance (Rtotal) consists of a pressure-dependent (Rp) and -independent (Rnp) mass transport resistance (Fig. 3h). Rp originates from the transport impedance in flow field channels and gas-diffusion layer. Correspondingly, Rnp is dominated by the contribution of Knudsen diffusion (RKD) in the micropore layer, catalyst layer, and local Pt-ionomer interface (RO2local). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 28, Rtotal of MEA presents a linear relationship towards applied pressure, where Rp and Rnp are calculated through analyzing the slope and intercept. It is noted that SCDC-Pt exhibits substantially reduced 0.5 S m−1 of Rp and 62.8 S m−1 of Rnp compared with Pt (0.8 S m−1 of Rp and 75.6 S m−1 of Rnp). RO2local and RKD are distinguished by measuring Rnp at different temperatures owing to their obviously different sensitivity towards temperature (Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 29, and Table 5). Recently, RO2local is demonstrated to dominate the Rtotal owing to the strong adsorption of sulfonate on Pt. SCDC-Pt exhibits a significantly lower RO2local of 38.6 S m−1 compared with 48.1 S m−1 for Pt. Thus, single-atom dipoles reduce proton-transfer resistance and RO2local by promoting homogeneous ionomer distribution and mitigating sulfonate poisoning, respectively70.

The SCDC-Pt-ionomer interfacial interactions

Both ionomer distribution and ionomer-branch sulfonate adsorption are important for controlling surface Pt-atom accessibility and diffusion dynamics71. STEM-EDS mapping was first used to visualize ionomer distribution on carbon substrates. To mitigate electron-beam damage to the ionomer phase, heavy cesium ions (Cs+) were grafted onto the ionomer backbone through an ion-exchange method72. SCDC-Pt presents a homogeneous ionomer distribution, marked by the Cs element, whereas Pt exhibits ionomer aggregation as shown in Fig. 4a, b. The uniform ionomer distribution of SCDC-Pt is also demonstrated by cross-sectional STEM-EDS mapping (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31). In addition, three-dimensional HAADF-STEM imaging confirms the homogeneous localization of small Pt nanoparticles without aggregation (Fig. 4c). These electron microscopy results support the conclusion that single-atom dipoles promote ionomer distribution on carbon substrates through ion–dipole interactions, consistent with the computational analysis.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was used to probe ionomer adsorption on carbon black and thereby examine the interaction between ionomers and single-atom dipoles52. As shown in Fig. 4d, injecting dilute ionomer dispersion into the carbon suspension produces exothermic peaks arising from interactions between carbon black and ionomers. The heat release increases with ionomer injection. For conventional XC-72, the ITC curve reaches a plateau because a limited number of energetic sites, such as defects, kinks, and surface roughness, become covered by ionomer, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 32. By contrast, for carbon black coated with single Co-atom dipoles (SCDC-C), the heat release continues to increase, indicating abundant ionomer-trapping sites (Fig. 4e). The heat release also reflects the strength of the carbon/ionomer interaction, which differs markedly between XC-72 and SCDC-C. SCDC-C shows stronger heat release for each ionomer aliquot than carbon black, demonstrating that single-atom dipoles strengthen the interaction (Fig. 4e). Notably, the heat release of Co-free N-C reference containing only the coating layer is lower than that of SCDC-Pt, highlighting the role of Co dipoles in stabilizing sulfonate groups. These observations suggest that positively charged surface sites can contribute broadly to sulfonate trapping and ionomer redistribution, while Co appears to provide a useful balance between dipole-sulfonate interaction and durability under acidic PEMFC conditions73–75. Thus, the ITC results show that single-atom dipoles strengthen ionomer interactions and promote homogeneous ionomer distribution. In summary, single-atom dipoles improve ionomer distribution through ion–dipole trapping, whereas the thin carbon matrix suppresses ionomer poisoning of Pt. Both uniform ionomer distribution and reduced sulfonate adsorption improve mass transport and active-site accessibility, thereby enhancing MEA performance.

Further, Pt L3-edge XANES was used to examine how single-atom dipoles tune the chemical interaction between ionomers and the Pt surface. Figure 4f exhibits the XANES profiles of Pt and Pt-ionomer composites. The presence of ionomer substantially decreases the white-line intensity, consistent with strong adsorption of sulfonate. By contrast, the white-line intensity is almost unchanged for the SCDC-Pt-ionomer composite, indicating suppressed ionomer adsorption (Fig. 4g). Meanwhile, SCDC-Pt-ionomer exhibits a Pt-Pt coordination number nearly identical to that of SCDC-Pt (Supplementary Fig. 33 and Table 2). In contrast, the reduced CNPt-Pt for Pt-ionomer relative to Pt indicates sulfonate adsorption on the Pt surface, consistent with WT EXAFS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 34). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was also used to examine the Pt-sulfonate chemical coupling, as shown in Fig. 4h and Supplementary Figs. 35 and 36. For Pt 4f XPS spectrum, the Pt-ionomer composite shifts by 0.48 eV to higher binding energy relative to SCDC-Pt-ionomer (Supplementary Table 6). Meanwhile, the Pt-ionomer composite shows a clear negative shift in S 2p spectrum compared with SCDC-Pt-ionomer (Fig. 4i). These shifts in Pt 4f and S 2p spectra indicate that the single-atom dipole layer effectively suppresses ionomer adsorption on the Pt surface, consistent with the XANES results.

Operando characterization

To understand the dynamic behavior of the Pt-ionomer interface, operando XANES was first used to track the evolution of Pt-ionomer coupling. Figure 5a and Supplementary Fig. 37 show operando Pt L3-edge XANES spectra of Pt in O2-saturated 0.1 M HClO4. The white-line intensity decreases as the applied potential decreases. SCDC-Pt shows a similar trend, with a lower white-line intensity at lower potential (Fig. 5b). However, the decrease in white-line intensity from 0.8 to 0.1 V is larger for SCDC-Pt (0.011 a.u.) than for Pt (0.005 a.u.). For Pt, the surface above 0.8 V may be covered by O-containing species. These O-containing species are removed at lower potentials, after which the freshly exposed Pt surface can be covered by sulfonate (Fig. 5c). For SCDC-Pt, sulfonate adsorption on the freshly exposed Pt surface is hindered, resulting in a lower chemical state at low potentials as shown in Fig. 5d. Operando XANES therefore indicates that the single-atom dipole layer suppresses sulfonate adsorption and contributes to enhanced fuel-cell performance. Operando EXAFS was further used to track the evolution of Pt coordination under dynamic conditions. For Pt, the peak at ~2.5 Å arising from the Pt-Pt path progressively increases as the potential decreases, indicating geometric distortion during electrochemical oxidation-reduction (Fig. 5e). In contrast, SCDC-Pt shows only a slight increase in Pt-Pt path intensity, suggesting limited structural distortion (Fig. 5f). Stabilization of the local Pt geometry by the single-atom dipole layer is also supported by WT-EXAFS analysis. SCDC-Pt shows nearly unchanged maximum intensity and position at different potentials, whereas Pt shows clear shifts and intensity enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 38), further indicating improved electrochemical stability. The enhanced Pt stability of SCDC-Pt is also supported by its higher Pt vacancy-formation energy (Supplementary Fig. 39) and by negligible Pt dissolution detected by in situ ICP-MS, in stark contrast to the greater dissolution of commercial Pt (Fig. 5g, h).

Operando measurements indicate reduced sulfonate adsorption and smaller changes in Pt coordination for SCDC-Pt during electrochemical operation.

Operando attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) was performed to understand the interfacial coupling between single-atom dipoles and ionomers. In ATR-FTIR spectra, upward and downward peaks represent the formation and consumption of species, respectively76. Supplementary Fig. 40 displays operando ATR-FTIR spectra collected at different potentials on Pt and SCDC-Pt, using pure Nafion solution on an Au-film electrode as the reference state. The peak at ~1100 cm−1 is assigned to the symmetric vibration of the sulfonate group (-SO3−). For Pt, the sulfonate peak intensity gradually increases as the potential decreases, indicating spontaneous sulfonate adsorption on the Pt surface at lower potentials, where electrochemical reduction removes O-containing species. This observation agrees with the operando XANES results. In contrast, SCDC-Pt shows no ionomer-adsorption signal. Although the oxidized Pt surface is thermodynamically reduced to a freshly exposed metal surface at lower potentials, the absence of a sulfonate signal indicates that the single-Co-atom dipoles mitigate poisoning of the Pt surface. Figure 5i exhibits ATR-FTIR spectra collected under different atmospheres, in which Ar-saturated electrolyte was replaced by a CO-saturated electrolyte. For Pt, the upward peak at ~1100 cm−1, assigned to sulfonate ions, rapidly evolves into a downward peak, indicating that adsorbed sulfonate is displaced by CO probe molecules, as shown in Fig. 5k. By contrast, SCDC-Pt presents a downward sulfonate peak under Ar and CO atmospheres (Fig. 5j, k). Difference-spectra analysis further excludes substantial sulfonate adsorption on the SCDC-Pt surface (Supplementary Fig. 41). Together, operando XAS, ATR-FTIR, and in situ ICP-MS demonstrate that the thin layer containing single-atom dipoles effectively suppresses sulfonate poisoning and improves active-site accessibility. Meanwhile, the surface dipole layer limits geometric distortion, thereby enhancing long-term structural stability.

Discussion

In summary, a single Co atom dipole layer covering Pt/C (SCDC-Pt) was designed to construct a surface-composition-homogenized electrocatalyst, aiming to alleviate interfacial limitations and improve MEA performance. SCDC-Pt exhibits substantially enhanced MEA performance and stability under the DOE protocol, outperforming commercial Pt and meeting the DOE 2025 target. Using operando characterization (XAS, ATR-FTIR, and ICP-MS), electrochemical diagnostics, and simulations, we show that positively charged surface metal sites facilitate homogeneous ionomer distribution through dipole-sulfonate Coulombic trapping. This surface dipole layer also effectively mitigates ionomer poisoning of the Pt surface. Weakened sulfonate adsorption and uniform ionomer distribution lead to lower apparent sulfonate coverage and faster diffusion kinetics, enabling high MEA performance. Additionally, the surface metal-dipole layer protects the Pt geometric structure from deformation through a pinning effect, accounting for the high structural stability. These results suggest that surface dipole–ion interactions offer a promising route for regulating catalyst-ionomer interfaces in low-Pt-loading MEAs.

Methods

Chemicals

Analytical graded tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane hydrochloride (ACS, ≥99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich), dopamine hydrochloride (≥98%, Aladdin), ammonium hydroxide (≥25% in H2O, Aladdin), cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (AR, 99%, Aladdin), cesium sulfate (99.99% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich), isopropanol (IPA, AR, ≥99.7%, Aladdin), perchloric acid (AR, 70.0–72.0%, Aladdin), nitric acid (AR, 65–68%, Aladdin) and hydrochloric acid (ACS, 37%, Sigma-Aldrich) were used as received. Vulcan XC72R carbon black, commercial Pt/C catalysts (JM, Pt content of 38.90–41.10 wt%), and Nafion D520 dispersion (5 wt% in a mixture of lower aliphatic alcohols and water) were purchased from Shanghai HESEN. Gore Select® Proton Exchange Membranes (Gore M765.08, 8 μm thickness) were purchased from Adamas-Beta. Gas diffusion layers (GDLs) with a micro-porous layer (AvCarb MB30) were purchased from SCI Materials Hub. High-purity argon (99.999%) and oxygen (99.999%) gases were supplied by local vendors. All chemicals were used in this work without further purification. Deionized water (resistivity ≥ 18.2 MΩ cm) was obtained from a Milli-Q ultrapure water purification system.

Synthesis of SCDC-Pt

To synthesize the SCDC-Pt catalyst, 20 mg of commercial Pt/C (40 wt%, JM) was first dispersed in 10 mL of aqueous Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane hydrochloride solution (1.2 mg mL−1) via ultrasonication for 5 min. The pH of the suspension was adjusted to approximately 8.0 by dropwise addition of aqueous ammonia (≥25% in H2O). Subsequently, 30 mg of dopamine hydrochloride was added, and the reaction was carried out under continuous magnetic stirring in an ice bath (~1 °C) for 1.5 h. Following the reaction, the intermediate dopamine-coated Pt/C product was collected by vacuum filtration, washed extensively with deionized water (3 × 50 mL) until the filtrate reached a neutral pH, and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h. Finally, the dried powder was then redispersed in 200 mL of aqueous Co(NO3)2·6H2O solution (5 mmol L−1) and stirred magnetically for 12 h at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C). The final SCDC-Pt product was collected by filtration, washed, and vacuum-dried overnight.

Synthesis of C-Pt

In a normal procedure, 20 mg of commercial Pt/C (40 wt%, JM) was first dispersed in 10 mL of aqueous tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane hydrochloride solution (1.2 mg mL−1) via ultrasonication for 5 min. The pH of the suspension was adjusted to approximately 8.0 by dropwise addition of aqueous ammonia (≥25% in H2O). Subsequently, 30 mg of dopamine hydrochloride was added to the mixture. The reaction proceeded under continuous magnetic stirring in an ice bath (maintained at ~1 °C) for 1.5 h. Following the reaction, the intermediate dopamine-coated Pt/C product was collected by vacuum filtration, washed extensively with deionized water (3 × 50 mL) until the filtrate reached a neutral pH, and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain the black C-Pt product.

Physical characterization

Atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) maps were obtained on an aberration-corrected JEM-ARM200F(C) microscope. The Pt and Co contents were quantified with a PerkinElmer Optima 5300DV inductively coupled plasma instrument after acid digestion of the samples. For ionomer-distribution analysis, Nafion-containing catalyst layers were removed from the substrates and immersed in Ar-purged Cs2SO4 solution for 72 h to exchange Cs+ with the sulfonate groups. The resulting specimens were examined by STEM-EDS, and the Cs signal was used to trace the ionomer phase.

ITC was used to evaluate the interaction between ionomer and carbon-based substrates. Measurements were conducted at 25 °C using a TAM III thermostat (TA Instruments) equipped with three nanocalorimeters with a heat-flow detection limit of 10 μJ. Dilute ionomer dispersion was injected stepwise into the carbon suspension, and the heat released after each injection was integrated to obtain the adsorption heat and binding isotherm.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a PHI 5700 ESCA system with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). For X-ray absorption fine-structure analysis, Athena was used for data processing, Feff 7.02 was used to calculate theoretical phase and amplitude functions for the relevant scattering paths, and WinXAS was used for R-space fitting.

Operando measurement of Pt dissolution rates from Pt and SCDC-Pt catalysts was performed using online ICP-MS coupled with a Teflon flow cell. During the ICP-MS test, the glassy carbon (GC) electrode with a catalyst loading of 1 mg cm−2 was immersed into the flowing electrolyte, a Pt wire was used as the counter electrode, and Ag/AgCl filled with saturated KCl was used as the reference electrode. The potentials were converted to the RHE scale using E(RHE) = E(Ag/AgCl) + 0.197 V + 0.059 × pH at 25 °C. The Ar-saturated 0.1 M HClO4 solution was the electrolyte, with a flow rate of 0.1 mL s−1. A CV protocol was used to trigger Pt dissolution at a sweep speed of 2.5 mV s−1 over the potential range 0.0–1.0 V vs. RHE.

In situ ATR-FTIR spectra were collected with a Nicolet Nexus 870 FTIR spectrometer equipped with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled MCT-A detector76. The working electrode was prepared by chemically depositing an approximately 100 nm Au film on a 20 mm × 25 mm Si plate, followed by deposition of Pt/C or SCDC-Pt from ethanol suspension to a Pt loading of 10 μgPt cm−2. The catalyst film was dried under ethanol vapor at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C), coated with an aliquot of Nafion solution, and dried again under the same conditions. A gas-diffusion electrode containing a Pt/C catalyst layer (0.4 mgPt cm−2) was used as the counter electrode, and a humidified-H2-fed GDE-type reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) was used as the reference electrode. All ATR-FTIR measurements were performed at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C).

Electrochemical characterization

Electrocatalytic performance was assessed using a rotating disk electrode (RDE) setup. To fabricate the working electrode, a GC disk electrode (5 mm diameter, Pine Research Instruments) was successively polished with 0.3 and 0.05 μm alumina slurries on a polishing cloth to achieve a mirror finish, followed by thorough ultrasonic cleaning in deionized water and ethanol. The catalyst ink was prepared by ultrasonically dispersing 2 mg of the catalyst in a mixture containing 240 μL of isopropanol (IPA), 750 μL of ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm), and 10 μL of a 5 wt% Nafion® aqueous solution for 20 min. The homogeneous ink (10 μL) was then loaded onto the polished GC electrode using a micropipette. The geometric area of the GC disk electrode is 0.196 cm2. Based on the ink concentration (2 mg mL−1) and the drop-casted volume (10 μL), the total catalyst mass loading on the electrode was calculated to be ~0.102 mg cm−2. For the catalysts containing 40 wt% Pt, this corresponds to a precise geometric Pt loading of 0.04 mgPt cm−2. The coated electrode was dried at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C) under rotation at 500 rpm prior to electrochemical characterization. All electrochemical measurements in the three-electrode system were conducted at a constant environmental temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, which was strictly maintained by an air-conditioned laboratory environment. A CHI-760E electrochemical workstation was employed for these tests. Data collection was continuously recorded and controlled by the accompanying CHI software. The processed currents were normalized to the geometric surface area of the glassy carbon disk (0.196 cm2) to yield the reported current densities. The three-electrode system consisted of a RHE as the reference and a platinum wire (99.99%, CHI Instruments) serving as the counter electrode. Prior to the electrochemical measurements, the reference electrode was carefully calibrated in the same electrolyte (0.1 mol L−1 HClO4). The calibration was performed in a high-purity H2-saturated electrolyte using a clean Pt wire as the working electrode. Linear sweep voltammetry was conducted at a slow scan rate of 1 mV s−1. The potential at which the current crossed zero was taken as the exact 0.00 V vs. RHE. All potentials reported in this work were referenced to this calibrated RHE. The measurements were performed in a standard single-compartment glass electrochemical cell. The cell was filled with 50 mL of the prepared electrolyte. The RDE system was sourced from Pine Research Instruments. Prior to the ORR activity measurements, the catalyst was electrochemically activated by cycling the potential between 0.05 and 1.2 V (vs. RHE) for 100 cycles at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 in an Ar-saturated 0.1 mol L−1 HClO4 solution (pH 1.0 ± 0.1). To strictly prevent any trace impurity contamination that could poison the electrocatalysts, the electrolyte was freshly prepared prior to the electrochemical experiments by diluting the high-purity perchloric acid (70.0%–72.0%) with Milli-Q ultrapure water (resistivity ≥ 18.2 MΩ cm). The prepared electrolyte was stored in rigorously acid-washed, clean borosilicate glass bottles at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C) and was typically utilized within a few days. To probe the ORR activity, the catalysts were investigated using RDE. The electrolyte used was a 0.1 mol L−1 HClO4 solution (pH 1.0 ± 0.1), which was saturated with high-purity O2 gas prior to all measurements. The electrode was rotated at 1600 rpm, scanning the potential from 0 to 1.2 V (vs. RHE) at a rate of 10 mV s−1.

Proton exchange membrane fuel cells characterization

Pt catalyst layer was fabricated via the catalyst-coated membrane (CCM) method, where the catalyst ink was directly sprayed onto the proton exchange membrane (Gore® 8 μm, the membrane was cut to a physical size of 3 cm × 3 cm and was used as received without any prior chemical or thermal pretreatment) placed on a 70 °C hot plate using an airbrush (PS289) to form the cathode catalyst layer (0.10 mgPt cm−2). A commercial Pt/C (JM 40 wt%) catalyst was applied similarly to the opposite side as the anode (0.10 mgPt cm−2). The active geometric area of MEA was 5 cm2. The exact catalyst loading for both the anode and cathode was strictly controlled and calculated by measuring the weight difference of the membrane before and after the ultrasonic spraying process. In detail, the catalyst ink (2 mg mL−1) was prepared by ultrasonically mixing the catalyst, IPA, deionized water, and 5 wt% Nafion solution for 20 min, with the IPA-to-water ratio controlled at 1:3. Both catalyst layers were fabricated with an I/C ratio of 0.6. Commercial carbon paper (AvCarb MB30) was used as the GDL. The MEA was assembled by sandwiching the CCM between two GDLs and hot-pressing at 140 °C and 1 MPa for 3 min. The assembled MEA was then sealed within fuel cell hardware featuring graphite bipolar plates with serpentine flow fields. Fuel cell tests were conducted using a Fuel Cell Test System (DC980, PHYCHEMI). For the H2-O2 fuel cell test, pure hydrogen (99.999%) and pure oxygen (99.999%) were fed to the anode and cathode at flow rates of 0.3 and 0.4 L min−1, respectively. For the H2-air test, the flow rates of hydrogen and synthetic air (21% O2 and 79% Ar) were controlled at 0.6 and 1.0 L min−1, respectively. Before recording the polarization curves, the single cell was pre-activated at a constant voltage of 0.5 V until the current density reached a steady state (approximately 3 h). Throughout all MEA tests (including the polarization curve evaluations and the 30,000-cycle accelerated stress tests), the cell operating temperature was strictly maintained at 80 °C. Instead of using an external climatic chamber, the cell temperature was precisely regulated using the built-in heating cartridges and internal thermocouples of the Fuel Cell Test System. The relative humidity was controlled at 100%, and the back pressure was set to 50 kPa.

For the stability test, the anode and cathode were supplied with fully humidified H2 (200 sccm) and Ar (200 sccm) with atmospheric pressure, respectively. Computational methods

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

Electronic-structure models were constructed to compare the interaction of sulfonate-containing ionomer fragments with Pt and with the surface-dipole layer. The Pt-ionomer reference model used a four-layer 4 × 4 Pt(111) slab separated from its periodic image by a 20 Å vacuum region. The ionomer was represented by a perfluorosulfonic-acid side-chain fragment containing the terminal sulfonic acid group, two backbone carbon atoms, and the associated fluorine atoms. Starting structures were generated by placing the sulfonate-bearing side chain at selected distances from the Pt surface so that both adsorbed and weakly interacting configurations could be sampled. Continuum solvation was included with VASPsol77,78. For explicitly hydrated models, water molecules were added to the vacuum region at a density of 1 g cm−3, and protons were introduced to compensate for the negative charge of deprotonated sulfonate groups.

All DFT calculations were performed with the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)79. Electron–ion interactions were described using the projector-augmented-wave approach, and exchange–correlation effects were treated with the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional within the generalized gradient approximation80–82. A plane-wave kinetic-energy cutoff of 450 eV was applied, and the Brillouin zone was sampled using a 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid. Geometry optimizations were continued until the total-energy change was below 10−5 eV and the residual force on each relaxed atom was below 0.01 eV Å−1. Charge-density-difference analysis was then performed on the optimized structures to evaluate interfacial charge redistribution.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Long-range electrostatic interactions were handled using the particle-mesh Ewald method, while van der Waals interactions were handled using a truncation scheme with a truncation distance of 1.0 nm. Temperature was controlled using a Nose-Hoover heat bath, while isotropic pressure was maintained using the Berendsen bar pressure method. The simulation time step was set to 2 fs. All simulations were performed using the Gromacs 2020.6 software83.

The Pt structural model used in the simulations consists of 7 layers of Pt with an interlayer distance of 1.96 Å. The box size is set to 7.76 nm in the X and Y directions, and the Pt layers are treated as fixed during the simulation. The Nafion polymer chain contains 13 sulfonate ions and 13 hydroxide ions, with an equivalent weight of 1100, similar to values commonly used in experiments. The CoN4 model has dimensions of 7.76 nm in both the X and Y directions and is fixed during the simulation.

First, Nafion chains, hydroxide ions, IPA molecules, and water molecules were randomly distributed on the Pt layers. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in the X-Y direction, and the Z direction was set to a height of 15 nm.
