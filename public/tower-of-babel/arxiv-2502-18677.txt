A new asymptotic regime for the KdV equation with Wigner-von Neumann type initial data Thanks: The author is supported in part by the NSF under grant DMS-2307774.
Alexei Rybkin

Abstract
. We investigate the long-time asymptotic behavior of solutions to the Cauchy problem for the KdV equation, focusing on the evolution of the radiant wave associated with a Wigner-von Neumann (WvN) resonance induced by the initial data (potential). A WvN resonance refers to an energy level where the potential exhibits zero transmission (complete reflection). The corresponding Jost solution at such energy becomes singular, and in the NLS context, this is referred to as a spectral singularity. A WvN resonance represents a long-range phenomenon, often introducing significant challenges, such as an infinite negative spectrum, when employing the inverse scattering transform (IST). To avoid some of these issues, we consider a restricted class of initial data that generates a WvN resonance but for which the IST framework can be suitably adapted. For this class of potentials, we demonstrate that each WvN resonance produces a distinct asymptotic regime—termed the resonance regime—characterized by a slower decay rate for large time compared to the radiant waves associated with short-range initial data.

O\left(\left(\log t/t\right)^{1/2}\right)
. We believe that it is a new phenomenon that holds for any initial data
producing zero transmission (full reflection) at some isolated positive
energies.

4.
Original RHP

As is always done in the RHP approach to the IST, we rewrite the (time
evolved) basic scattering identity (
1.7
) as a
vector RHP. To this end, introduce a
2\times 1
row vector

(4.1)

\mathbf{m}(k,x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c@{\quad}l}\begin{pmatrix}T(k)m^{\left(-\right)}(k,x,t)&m^{\left(+\right)}(k,x,t)\end{pmatrix},&\mathop{\mathrm{I}m}k>0,\\
\begin{pmatrix}m^{\left(+\right)}(-k,x,t)&T(-k)m^{\left(-\right)}(-k,x,t)\end{pmatrix},&\mathop{\mathrm{I}m}k<0,\end{array}\right.

where

(4.2)

m^{\left(\pm\right)}(k;x,t):=\mathrm{e}^{\mp\mathrm{i}kx}\psi^{\left(\pm\right)}(x,t;k),\text{\ \ \ (Faddeev functions).}

Note that we list
k
as the first variable as from now on it will be the
main variable, the parameters
\left(x,t\right)
being often suppressed.
Existence of
\psi^{\left(\pm\right)}(x,t;k)
for
t>0
follows from
considerations given in
[
20
]
. Also, since poles of
\psi^{\left(-\right)}
are canceled by the zeros of
T
, we conclude that
\mathbf{m}
is bounded on the whole complex plane. Details will be provided
elsewhere for a much wider class of initial data.

Introduce the short hand notation

\xi\left(k\right)=\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}t\Phi\left(k\right)\right\},\ \Phi\left(k\right)=4k^{3}+\left(x/t\right)k,\ \ x\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0.

We treat
\mathbf{m}
as a solution to

RHP#0. (Original RHP)
Find a
1\times 2
matrix (row) valued
function
\mathbf{m}\left(k\right)
which is analytic and bounded on
\mathbb{C\diagdown R}
and satisfies:

(1) The jump condition

(4.3)

\mathbf{m}_{+}\left(k\right)=\mathbf{m}_{-}\left(k\right)\mathbf{V}\left(k\right)\text{ for }k\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathbf{m}_{\pm}\left(k\right):=\mathbf{m}\left(k\pm\mathrm{i}0\right),\ k\in\mathbb{R}
,

(4.4)

\mathbf{V}\left(k\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1-\left|R\left(k\right)\right|^{2}&-\overline{R}\left(k\right)\xi\left(k\right)^{-2}\\
R\left(k\right)\xi\left(k\right)^{2}&1\end{array}\right),\text{ (jump matrix),}

and
R
is given by
\left(\ref{TR}\right)
;

(2) The symmetry condition

\mathbf{m}\left(-k\right)=\mathbf{m}\left(k\right)\sigma_{1}\text{;}

(3) The normalization condition

\mathbf{m}\left(k\right)\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\end{array}\right),\ \ \ k\rightarrow\infty.

The solution to the initial problem
\left(\ref{KdV}\right)-\left(\ref{KdVID}\right)
can then be found by

(4.5)

q\left(x,t\right)=2\lim k^{2}\left(m_{1}\left(k,x,t\right)m_{2}\left(k,x,t\right)-1\right),\ \ \ k\rightarrow\infty

where
m_{1,2}
are the entries of
\mathbf{m}
(see e.g.
[
12
]
).

The RHP approach is specifically robust (among others) in asymptotics
analysis of
\mathbf{m}\left(k;x,t\right)
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
in
different asymptotic regions
[
12
]
. But it works well if the
stationary point
k_{0}=\sqrt{-x/12t}
of
\Phi\left(k\right)
is not a
real zero of
T\left(k\right)
. Recall that in the short-range case the
only real zero of
T\left(k\right)
is
k=0
and does not create any
problems as long as
k_{0}\neq 0
. As already mentioned, the case of
k_{0}=0
is considered in
[
7
]
. Note however that
0
is the left
end point of the absolutely continuous spectrum, which is essential for the
approach to work.

Remark 4.1
.

There is no problem with the well-posedness of RHP#0 and no problem with
the deformation step as
\mathbf{V}
is a meromorphic function on the entire
plane. But there is a problem with adjusting the classical nonlinear
steepest descent
[
6
]
. Recall that in the mKdV case treated
in
[
6
]
we always have
\left|R(k)\right|<1
and
hence
R(k)/(1-|R(k)|^{2})
can be approximated by analytic functions in the

L^{\infty}
norm. As we have seen already, it is not our case and it
appears to be a good open question how to adjust the nonlinear steepest
descent to RHP#0.

Remark 4.2
.

RHP#0 does not appear singular so far. Its singularity will be clear below.

5.
Partial Transmission Coefficient

In this section we study what is commonly referred to as the
partial
transmission coefficient
, which plays the crucial role in the conjugation
step discussed below. Following
[
12
]
we introduce it by

T(k,k_{0})=\exp\int\limits_{\left(-k_{0},k_{0}\right)}\frac{\log|T(s)|^{2}}{s-k}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}.

Since we are only concerned with the asymptotics related to the resonance
region we take
k_{0}=1
and denote

(5.1)

T_{0}\left(k\right)=T(k,1)=\exp\int\limits_{\left(-1,1\right)}\frac{\log|T(s)|^{2}}{s-k}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}.

If
k_{0}=+\infty
then
T\left(k,+\infty\right)
returns the (full)
transmission coefficient
T\left(k\right)
.

T_{0}(k)
is clearly an analytic function for
k\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\left[-1,1\right]
. The following statement extends that of
[
12
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

The partial transmission coefficient
T_{0}(k)
satisfies
the jump condition

(5.2)

T_{0+}(k)=T_{0-}(k)(1-\left|R(k)\right|^{2}),\ \ k\in\left(-1,1\right)

and

(i)

T_{0}(-k)=T_{0}(k)^{-1}
,
T_{0}\left(\overline{k}\right)=\overline{T_{0}\left(k\right)}^{-1},k\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\left[-1,1\right]
;

(ii)

T_{0}(-k)=\overline{T_{0}(\overline{k})}
,
k\in\mathbb{C}
, in
particular
T_{0}(k)
is real for
k\in
i
\mathbb{R}
;

(iii)

the behavior near
k=0
is given by
T_{0}(k)\sim CT(k)
with
C\neq 0

for
\mathop{\mathrm{I}m}k\geq 0
;

(iv)

T_{0}\left(k\right)
can be factored out as

(5.3)

T_{0}\left(k\right)=T_{0}^{\left(\mathrm{reg}\right)}\left(k\right)T_{0}^{\left(\mathrm{sng}\right)}\left(k\right),

where the regular part

T_{0}^{\left(\mathrm{reg}\right)}\left(k\right)=\frac{{k}}{\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{T(s)/\left(1-s\right)}{T^{\prime}\left(1\right)}\right|^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{s^{2}-k^{2}}

is continuously differentiable at
k=\pm 1
, and the singular part

(5.4)

T_{0}^{\left(\mathrm{sng}\right)}\left(k\right)=\left(\frac{k-1}{k+1}\right)^{\mathrm{i}\nu}\exp\left\{k\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\left(1-s\right)^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\pi\mathrm{i}}\right\},

(5.5)

\nu:=-\left(1/\pi\right)\log\left|T^{\prime}\left(1\right)\right|=\left(1/\pi\right)\log a.

Moreover,

(5.6)

T_{0}\left(k\right)\sim A_{0}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\nu\log\left(k-1\right)+\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}\pi}\log^{2}\left(k-1\right)\right\},k\rightarrow 1,

where
A_{0}
is a unimodular constant given by

(5.7)

A_{0}=\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi}\left\{\int\limits_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{T(s)/T^{\prime}\left(1\right)}{1-s}\right|^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{1-s^{2}}-\pi\nu\log 2-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log s{\mathrm{d}s}}{s-2}\right\}.

Proof.

The items (1)-(3) are straightforward to check. Item (4) is a bit technical.
Since
\left|T\left(s\right)\right|
is even, we clearly have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log|T(s)|^{2}}{s-k}{\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle{=2k}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log|T(s)|^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle={2k}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log|T(s)/\left(1-s\right)|^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s+2k}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\left(1-s\right)^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s}

and therefore

T_{0}\left(k\right)=\exp\left\{k\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log|T(s)/\left(1-s\right))|^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{\pi\mathrm{i}}\right\}\exp\left\{k\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\left(1-s\right)^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{\pi\mathrm{i}}\right\}.

It remains to factor out the continuous part of the first factor on the
right hand side. To this end note that

\lim_{s\rightarrow 1}|T(s)/\left(1-s\right))|=\left|\lim_{s\rightarrow 1}T(s)/\left(1-s\right)\right|=\left|T^{\prime}\left(1\right)\right|=1/a

and therefore

\displaystyle k\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log|T(s)/\left(1-s\right))|^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{\pi\mathrm{i}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{k}{s^{2}-k^{2}}\log\left|\frac{T(s)/\left(1-s\right))}{T^{\prime}\left(1\right)}\right|^{2}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{\pi\mathrm{i}}+\log\left|T^{\prime}\left(1\right)\right|^{2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{k}{s^{2}-k^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{\pi\mathrm{i}}.

Eq
\left(\ref{sing part}\right)
follows now once we observe that

\nu\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{k}{s^{2}-k^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{\pi\mathrm{i}}=\left(\frac{k-1}{k+1}\right)^{\mathrm{i}\nu},

where
\nu
is given by
\left(\ref{nu}\right)
. It remains to
demonstrate
\left(\ref{T_0 asympt}\right)
. Consider

k\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\left(1-s\right)^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s}=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\left(1-s\right)}{s-k}{\mathrm{d}s+}\int\limits_{-1}^{0}\frac{\log\left(1+s\right)}{s-k}{\mathrm{d}s=:I}_{1}\left(k\right)+I_{2}\left(k\right).

By a direct computation, for
{I}_{1}\left(k\right)
we have

I_{1}\left(k\right)=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\left(1-s\right)}{s-k}{\mathrm{d}s\sim}\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}\left(k-1\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6},\ \ \ k\rightarrow 1.

Since
I_{2}\left(k\right)
is clearly continuously differentiable at
k=1

one has

\displaystyle I_{2}\left(k\right)

\displaystyle\sim I_{2}\left(1\right)=\int\limits_{-1}^{0}\frac{\log\left(1+s\right)}{s-1}{\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle{=}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\lambda}{\lambda-2}{\mathrm{d}\lambda},\ \ \ k\rightarrow 1

and thus

\displaystyle k\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\left(1-s\right)^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle{\sim}\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}\left(k-1\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\lambda}{\lambda-2}{\mathrm{d}\lambda},\ \ \ k\rightarrow 1.

We have

\displaystyle\exp\left\{k\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\left(1-s\right)^{2}}{s^{2}-k^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{\pi\mathrm{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle\sim\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\log^{2}\left(k-1\right)\right\}\exp\left\{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{6}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{\log\lambda{\mathrm{d}\lambda}}{\lambda-2}\right\},\ \ \ k\rightarrow 1.

Remark 5.2
.

In the classical case the singular part of
T_{0}\left(k\right)
is

T_{0}^{\left(\mathrm{sng}\right)}\left(k\right)=\left(\frac{k-1}{k+1}\right)^{\mathrm{i}\nu}

which is a bounded function. The extra factor in
\left(\ref{sing part}\right)
containing
\log^{2}
term leads to a more singular (unbounded)
behavior of
T_{0}\left(k\right)
at
k=\pm 1
. The presence of the
\log^{2}
term is responsible for the new asymptotic regime.
