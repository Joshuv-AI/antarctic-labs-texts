Connected parents, connected children: the relationship of smartphone, social media, and AI with parenting and children’s tech consumption in Türkiye
Authors: Suat Tuncay, Abdullah Sarman, Emrah Zirek, İbrahim Güngör
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe objective of this study is to investigate parental utilization of smartphones, engagement with social media, and adoption of artificial intelligence tools in child-rearing practices, alongside analyzing their children’s technology consumption habits.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in the downtown area of an eastern Turkish city. Data collection utilized various forms, including a demographic findings form, the parents’ smartphone attitudes form, parents’ use of social media and knowledge of artificial intelligence form, parents’ use of digital tools in childcare form, children’s smartphone use form, and the Smartphone Addiction Scale.ResultsThe study revealed that smartphone usage led to insomnia at night, as well as neck and hand-wrist pain. Parents utilizing artificial intelligence applications such as Google Bard/Gemini and ChatGPT demonstrated a higher potential for addiction. Those who allowed their infants to watch content on their smartphones between the first and sixth months of life showed a greater potential for addiction, with such content often utilized to soothe the baby during feeding. It was determined that parents who did not pay attention to the use of smartphones in the presence of their children and parents of children who reacted violently when the phone was taken away had higher phone addiction scores.ConclusionsThe results suggest that parents who extensively utilize social media and artificial intelligence applications tend to integrate these tools into childcare practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06970-9.

Background

Concurrent with the proliferation of digital technologies, the utilization of smartphones in various domains such as communication, collaboration, and online education has surged. These devices have become an indispensable component of everyday life [1]. Smartphones are extensively employed by individuals across all age demographics to access a myriad of services.

Younger generations utilize smartphones for activities such as video consumption, self-expression, socializing with peers, and academic pursuits, while older individuals utilize them for video communication with distant relatives and recreational gaming [2]. The portability and ubiquity of smartphones enable their usage at any time and in any place. Despite the convenience they offer, smartphones have been implicated in fostering antisocial tendencies and addictive behaviors [3]. In recent years, smartphone addiction has emerged as a significant concern, garnering the attention of numerous researchers [4, 5].

With the widespread adoption of smartphones, their accessibility and diverse features have had a significant influence on behavioral patterns. Deursen et al. identified smartphone use as a key factor contributing to the increase in social media addiction [6]. Similarly, Salehan and Negahban emphasized the role of social media applications in facilitating this addiction [7]. In addition, Park and Lee reported that addictive behaviors are more prevalent among women than among men [8]. Although some researchers propose that the instant gratification provided by social media reinforces habitual use, the underlying mechanisms remain the subject of ongoing debate [2, 9].

Globally, the integration of smartphones, social media, and emerging artificial intelligence tools has transformed the ways in which parents manage childcare. Although these technologies offer rapid access to health and developmental information, they have also raised concerns regarding excessive use and its potential effects on parent–child interaction. This digital transformation has led to the emergence of the concept of technoference, which refers to frequent interruptions in face-to-face interactions between parents and children resulting from the use of digital devices. Braune-Krickau et al. reported that such interruptions may substantially reduce parental sensitivity and responsiveness, both of which are essential for the development of healthy attachment [10].

Recent studies suggest that high levels of parental digital engagement may reduce sensitive communication and may influence children’s socio-emotional development by increasing their screen exposure [4, 7, 10–12]. However, existing research has largely focused on Western contexts, and evidence from middle-income countries such as Türkiye, where cultural and family structures emphasize close-knit interpersonal bonds, remains limited [10].

One of the most notable recent developments is artificial intelligence, which is defined as the ability of machines to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence [13]. AI applications, developed to deliver accurate information through complex algorithms, are increasingly incorporated into everyday life [14]. While previous studies have extensively examined smartphone and social media use [4, 7, 11, 12], this study uniquely positions generative AI tools as a core variable in the digital parenting landscape. To our knowledge, it is the first to empirically link specific AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini) with both parental addiction levels and functional childcare tasks. This study seeks to address this specific gap in the literature.

This study examined the extent to which parents’ levels of smartphone addiction are associated with their digital habits, health-related outcomes, and use of emerging technologies. In addition, it explored how these factors are related to children’s technology consumption patterns. To address these aims, the following sub-problems were examined:

Methods

Design

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Bingöl Province, located in the eastern region of Türkiye, among parents of children aged 3–6 years. This province was selected because it has a diverse population representing both urban and rural areas, and accessible health and educational institutions. It also facilitated participant recruitment and data collection due to the easy accessibility of individuals to the research team. A convenience sampling approach was employed due to feasibility constraints and the need to access participants within the available timeframe and resources, consistent with established methodological recommendations for situations where randomization is not possible and the target population is easily accessible [15].

Population and sample

Parents with at least one child aged 3–6 years and a minimum of six years of smartphone use were eligible. A total of 1,543 parents participated. Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. Given the target population (N = 103,441) and the achieved sample (n = 1,543), the precision for estimating proportions was acceptable: the finite-population–corrected 95% confidence margin of error was approximately ± 2.5% (± 3.3% at 99%). These calculations were conducted post-hoc (after data collection) to assess the statistical sensitivity of the achieved sample rather than to guide sample size determination.

Under two-sided α = 0.05 and 80% power, the minimum detectable difference between two equal groups in proportions was ~ 7% points (≈ 7.3–7.8% for 60/40–70/30 splits), the minimum detectable standardized mean difference (Cohen’s d) was ~ 0.14, and the minimum detectable zero-order correlation was r ≈ 0.07. The study specifically targeted parents who had at least one child aged 3–6 years and had been utilizing smartphones for a minimum of six years.

Data collection

Data collection tools

Data collection utilized various forms, including demographic findings form, parents’ smartphone attitudes form, parents’ use of social media and knowledge of artificial intelligence form, parents’ use of digital tools in childcare form, children’s smartphone use form, and the Smartphone Addiction Scale. All forms except the Smartphone Addiction Scale were prepared by the researchers. All researcher-developed forms were created following a structured, multi-step process. First, an extensive review of national and international was conducted to identify relevant constructs, existing measures, and gaps [16, 17].

Subsequently, draft items were developed with input from a multidisciplinary panel of experts to ensure content validity and cultural appropriateness. The panel included a child development specialist whose research focuses on parental behavior and a nurse with expertise in both paediatric and psychiatric–mental health nursing. The involvement of these experts was intended to support the clinical validity of items addressing adult-specific behavioral patterns (e.g., smartphone attitudes and addiction) as well as those focusing on child-related outcome.

A pilot study was carried out with parents of children aged 3–6 years, during which both the problems experienced by parents and the challenges encountered by children were noted. Based on this feedback, the researchers revised the items for clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness.

The draft forms were then individually reviewed by each author, followed by a joint review to discuss and resolve discrepancies. The final versions of the forms were agreed upon collaboratively by all authors before data collection commenced. As the forms consisted primarily of straightforward, factual, or behavioral self-report questions (e.g., demographic information, frequency of technology use), and were not designed to measure latent constructs, formal psychometric testing was deemed unnecessary.

Demographic findings form

In this form, inquiries were conducted to ascertain the personal characteristics of the parents. These encompassed inquiries regarding age, role, number of children, family structure, educational attainment, and income level.

Parents’ smartphone attitudes form

This form was prepared by the researchers to determine parents’ smartphone usage status. It aimed to assess their levels of sleep deprivation resulting from smartphone use, as well as any neck or wrist-hand pain they may experience. Additionally, the questionnaire inquired about their daily smartphone usage time and the purposes for which they use their smartphones [4, 17, 18].

Parents’ use of social media and knowledge of artificial intelligence form

This form drew inspiration from prior studies and was crafted by the authors. It delved into parents’ utilization of social media platforms and their familiarity with artificial intelligence tools, examining their daily usage patterns. The investigation encompassed various social media platforms including Instagram/Threads, YouTube, Twitter/X, WhatsApp/Telegram, and artificial intelligence tools like ChatGPT, Google Bard/Gemini, Bing, and Grok [12].

Parents’ use of digital tools in childcare form

This form was developed in accordance with relevant literature and with input from the authors. Its purpose is to ascertain the frequency and manner in which parents utilize artificial intelligence, social media, and websites for child care. Within the realm of child care and parenting, inquiries encompass aspects such as child growth and development, acquiring health-related information, supplementary feeding, diaper changing, burping, and bathing [19, 20].

Children’s smartphone use form

This form was utilized to investigate parents’ attitudes towards their children’s use of smartphones. It included inquiries regarding when parents initially provided their child with a smartphone, the circumstances under which they permitted their child to view and listen to content, the purpose behind providing the child with a smartphone, and how they monitored the smartphone’s content [4].

Smartphone addiction scale

The scale, developed by Kwon et al., assesses smartphone addiction risk through 10 items rated on a six-point Likert scale. Scores for each item range from 1 to 6 [21], yielding a total scale score range of 10 to 60. A higher score indicates a greater risk of addiction. The scale comprises a single factor without subscales. The original version demonstrates strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91 [21]. Noyan et al. conducted a validity and reliability study with Turkish university students, reporting a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.867, indicating scale reliability [22]. Additionally, in this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.940.

Application of data collection tools

The governor’s office permitted the study. Participants were recruited through two main channels: (1) hospital outpatient clinics (pediatric and family medicine units), where parents visited for routine child health check-ups or medical consultations for their children, and (2) kindergarten family groups, where teachers first discussed the study with parents and then facilitated contact between the research team and interested participants. Hospital outpatient clinics and kindergarten family groups were chosen for data collection.

Utilizing convenience sampling, parents of children aged 3–6 years, who had been using smartphones for a minimum of six years and were capable of answering the questionnaire, were invited to participate. The researchers provided an introduction and explained the study’s objectives. Parents were assured that it was a prevalence study aimed at advancing scientific knowledge, with no financial motivations. They were also informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point. To reduce the risk of selection bias, recruitment targeted both urban and rural settings, multiple institutions, and parents from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds. The same inclusion criteria and invitation procedure were applied across all recruitment channels, and no incentives were provided that could differentially influence participation.

The paper-based forms were digitized using Google Forms and distributed through various channels such as social media, email, or SMS. Subsequently, the forms were administered, a process taking approximately 20 min. In the study, attitudes about their children’s infancy were asked retrospectively.

Statistical analysis

In this study, descriptive statistics (including number, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were utilized, alongside analysis of variance between groups (such as t-tests for independent groups, One-Way ANOVA, and the Kruskal-Wallis H test) for data analysis. Additionally, Bonferroni tests were employed for parametric tests, while Games-Howell tests were utilized for nonparametric tests, aiming to identify significant differences between groups. A significance level of p < 0.05 was established, with results below this threshold indicating statistically significant differences between the groups. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Ethical approval

Permission was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Bingöl University (09.01.2024-E.139388) for the study. The necessary permissions from the relevant authorities (14.02.2024–145695) were secured to conduct the study in hospitals and kindergartens. The study’s purpose was elucidated to parents of children aged 3–6 years who had been utilizing smartphones for more than 6 years.

Informed consent was obtained from the parents for both their own participation and their children’s participation in the study. An electronic consent form was dispatched to parents who expressed willingness to participate. Upon clicking the “I agree to participate in the study voluntarily” button, parents gained access to the questionnaire. Participants retained the prerogative to discontinue their involvement at any juncture. Phone numbers, if collected, were either not retained or promptly expunged upon completion of the study. No personal data (e.g., name, email, social media accounts) were solicited or logged.

Data collected are securely stored on encrypted devices belonging to the authors. Parents were apprised of their right to withdraw from the study sans any obligation to furnish a rationale. The study was conducted in adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Most participants were mothers aged 30–39 years, and nearly half held a bachelor’s degree or higher. Full demographic distributions and their associations with smartphone addiction scores are shown in Table 1. The study found that parents aged 40 years and older had lower smartphone addiction scores compared with younger parents, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05). Furthermore, parents with only one child demonstrated higher smartphone addiction scores than those with multiple children (p < 0.05). Additionally, the study revealed a significant correlation between parents’ smartphone addiction scores and their level of education, indicating that scores increased with higher levels of education (p < 0.05; Table 1).

Many parents reported experiencing sleep disturbances, neck pain, wrist pain, prolonged daily smartphone use, and frequent engagement with social media. Details and their associations with smartphone addiction scores are presented in Table 2. Furthermore, the study found that parents who frequently used smartphones, experienced high levels of neck and hand-wrist pain, spent extended periods on their devices, and used them primarily for social media, exhibited higher scores indicating smartphone addiction. Importantly, statistical analysis demonstrated a significant relationship between these variables (p < 0.05; Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate parents’ usage patterns of smartphones, social media, and artificial intelligence tools in parenting, as well as the technological consumption habits of their children.

The findings revealed that younger parents with fewer children and higher levels of education tended to exhibit higher scores of smartphone addiction (refer to Table 1). Consistent with the findings of Hussain et al., it was observed that phone usage duration decreased with advancing age, indicating a correlation with problematic smartphone use [23]. This aligns with the expectation that younger generations are more likely to heavily utilize smartphones, potentially due to being born into or growing up during the digital age.

However, Beneito-Montagut et al. found in their research on adult smartphone use that phone usage among the elderly population increased, suggesting a diminishing significance of age groups in smartphone usage patterns [24]. Additionally, they noted a positive association between increasing levels of education and technology usage.

Conversely, Çiçek et al. did not observe a robust correlation between parental education and smartphone usage [25]. These differing findings might be attributed to the intricate interplay between age and education, where the level of education tends to decrease with age, potentially influencing smartphone usage patterns in a similar manner.

Experiencing frequent sleep deprivation was significantly associated with higher mean scores of smartphone addiction among parents (Table 2). Existing literature suggests a correlation between smartphone addiction and poor sleep quality [18]. Moreover, studies have indicated a significant association between late-night smartphone usage and smartphone addiction [26].

Researchers such as Ivarsson et al. and Pieters et al. have reported that smartphone use before bedtime activates the nervous system, leading to cognitive, emotional, and physiological arousal [27, 28]. Tang and Harvey’s [29] study suggests that increased cognitive activity before bedtime may disrupt the brain’s processing of memory and sensory information, thereby making the transition from wakefulness to sleep more difficult [29]. Additionally, Herbert et al. demonstrated that pre-sleep cognitive activation, particularly cognitive arousal, reduces total sleep time [30].

In recent years, the use of smartphones before bedtime has increasingly become a prevalent leisure activity. Research has consistently linked delaying sleep with smartphone use to smartphone addiction and prolonged periods of smartphone engagement in bed [31]. Additionally, a study by Lee et al. (highlighted that individuals who engage with smartphones before sleep tend to experience delayed onset of sleep, particularly those exhibiting a high inclination towards smartphone addiction. Notably, this study emphasized that prolonged smartphone usage directly contributes to the development of smartphone addiction [26].

Further corroborating these findings, Sarman and Çiftci underscored the association between the duration of smartphone use and addiction [4]. Moreover, Al-Mamun et al. and Kaur et al. independently found that excessive daytime smartphone usage significantly heightens the risk of addiction [1, 11].

Kaur et al. discovered a correlation between smartphone usage of three hours or more per day and an escalation in smartphone addiction severity [11]. These findings align with previous studies suggesting a direct link between smartphone addiction and the duration of daily smartphone use [32]. It was observed that parents predominantly allocate their free time to social media engagement and gaming, resulting in higher average smartphone addiction scores. Moreover, global trends indicate that individuals across all age groups employ smartphones for similar purposes. Cha and Seo noted a prolonged usage pattern among young demographics, primarily for internet browsing, gaming, and social media interaction [2]. This study also underscored a heightened duration of smartphone usage among individuals engaged in gaming and social media activities. Bian and Leung highlighted that excessive smartphone usage impedes face-to-face communication and encourages increased reliance on social media platforms [33].

The mean score of parents’ smartphone addiction has been noted to correlate with neck and hand-wrist pain. According to Wang et al., prolonged smartphone use is associated with an increased incidence of physical discomfort, particularly in the back and shoulder regions. Moreover, they found that the prevalence of musculoskeletal pain in the neck, shoulder, and wrist was notably higher among women compared to men [5].

Studies, such as the one conducted by Zirek et al., have highlighted that extended, intensive, and repetitive use of handheld electronic devices can lead to musculoskeletal issues in the hand, wrist, forearm, arm, and neck [17]. Additionally, Derakhshanrad et al. emphasized that smartphone users may encounter neck and back pain [34]. The heightened scores of neck and hand-wrist pain in smartphone addiction align with earlier research, underscoring the significant impact of smartphone addiction on the neck and shoulder region.

It was found that parents who spent 3.1 h or more per day on social media platforms such as Instagram/Threads, YouTube, and WhatsApp/Telegram had a higher average smartphone addiction score (refer to Table 3). O’Keeffe and Clarke-Pearson suggested that the use of social media, including platforms like Facebook and Instagram, increases the risk of addiction [35]. Additionally, Osorio-Molina et al. reported that while using social media platforms on smartphones enhances virtual communication, it can also lead to smartphone addiction and a decline in real-life interpersonal relationships [36].

ChatGPT, Bing, Google Bard/Gemini and Adobe Firefly were identified as the most recognized or utilized artificial intelligence applications by parents. This key finding is an observed shift from seeking traditional parenting knowledge toward what may be described as “functional outsourcing.” The data indicate that a proportion of parents (8.3%) not only seek general advice but also incorporate artificial intelligence into physical caregiving routines, such as supplementary feeding, nappy changing, and bathing. This pattern points to an emerging dimension of “technological support,” in which artificial intelligence appears to function as a form of digital co-parenting support (Table 4).

While such AI applications offer rapid access to information, they also raise significant ethical and developmental concerns [37]. For instance, reliance on AI-generated advice without professional oversight may lead to misinformation or inappropriate care strategies. From a developmental perspective, increased reliance on AI could reduce direct parent–child engagement, potentially hindering socio-emotional development. Furthermore, ethical risks regarding data privacy and the collection of sensitive information about family dynamics remain paramount. Consequently, future research should investigate not only the prevalence but also the quality and long-term developmental impact of AI-supported parenting [38].

In addition to these findings, participants who had heard of applications such as Bing, Grok, and Adobe Firefly had lower smartphone addiction scores than those who had not (p < 0.05, Table 3). This result may be considered unexpected. However, at the time the study was conducted, applications such as Bing and Grok were not yet widely used and were still in testing phases, which may explain why some active smartphone users were unfamiliar with them. Accordingly, individuals who were aware of these applications may have had higher levels of digital literacy. It is therefore plausible that higher digital literacy is associated with lower levels of smartphone addiction.

The correlation between parental phone usage and appropriate parenting behavior remains a contentious issue today [39]. In a study encompassing over 3,000 parents, Modecki et al. observed that parents who sought information about their children’s care via phone displayed more positive parenting behaviors [40]. Shorey et al. indicated that parents can utilize technologies like smartphones to access support in areas where they perceive shortcomings, potentially enhancing parent-child interaction [41].

However, some scholars have posited the contrary viewpoint, suggesting that increased smartphone usage may lead to family conflicts as parents devote a significant portion of their time to these devices [42, 43].

Allowing babies to watch content on smartphones within the first six months was significantly associated with higher mean scores of parental smartphone addiction. Additionally, it was noted that parents permitted their children to watch cartoons on smartphones as a means of reclaiming personal time. These same parents more frequently utilized their phones in the presence of their children, and this behavior was significantly associated with higher parental smartphone addiction scores. Moreover, parents whose children displayed violent reactions when their phones were confiscated also demonstrated a higher mean score of smartphone addiction (Table 5).

Radesky et al. noted that many modern parents use digital tools as functional strategies to manage their children’s behavior, most commonly as a means of entertainment or distraction [44]. This reliance on technological devices as a means to divert children’s attention is posited to potentially contribute to technology addiction among children in later years [20]. A study conducted in Türkiye revealed that children aged 0–7 years typically responded negatively, exhibiting behaviors such as crying, protesting, and even resorting to violence when their access to technological devices was restricted [19].

Limitations

The cross-sectional design of this study, coupled with its restriction to parents in a single province, represents a significant limitation. Moreover, given that more than half of the participants were women, potential gender bias should be considered when interpreting the results. A convenience sampling strategy was employed due to feasibility constraints, which may limit the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to all parents in Türkiye.

No a priori power calculation was conducted before data collection; instead, statistical sensitivity estimates were calculated post-hoc to assess the adequacy of the achieved sample size. Although these post-hoc results indicated that the relatively large sample size (n = 1,543) was sufficient to detect small-to-moderate effects, the absence of an a priori calculation means that the study may have been underpowered for detecting smaller or more subtle associations. These methodological factors should be taken into account when interpreting the findings.

Additionally, as smartphone addiction was assessed through self-report scales, further investigation is warranted to ascertain causality. Despite these limitations, the reliability of the findings is deemed relatively high, considering the achieved sample size and the internal consistency coefficient of the scale. To the best of our knowledge, this study stands as the inaugural exploration into parents’ smartphone usage, social media engagement, utilization of artificial intelligence tools in parenting, and their children’s technology consumption patterns.

Conclusion

This study suggests that smartphone addiction among parents is associated with physical, behavioral, and digital parenting practices. Higher addiction scores were observed alongside longer daily smartphone use, sleep deprivation, and musculoskeletal pain. Similarly, parents who reported both general use of AI tools and their integration into childcare processes tended to report higher smartphone addiction scores, indicating a co-occurrence of these behaviors rather than a directional relationship.

In addition, the findings indicate a significant association between parental smartphone addiction and children’s early exposure to digital devices. The use of smartphones as a means of calming or feeding children was linked to more frequent and less restricted device access for children, as well as behavioral difficulties, such as aggressive reactions when devices are taken away.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering supportive intervention approaches for parents. Such approaches may focus on encouraging healthier digital habits, offering guidance on the context-appropriate and mindful use of AI in childcare settings, and increasing awareness of the potential developmental risks associated with early and excessive screen exposure.

Rather than implying a causal role, the present findings suggest that the integration of artificial intelligence coexists with broader patterns of parental digital engagement and dependency. At the time the study was conducted, AI applications were not yet widely adopted; however, their presence in daily life has continued to expand. The findings indicate that AI tools are increasingly incorporated into the micro-routines of childcare, underscoring the potential need for tailored, AI-focused guidance for modern parents. In this way, the study contributes descriptive evidence to an emerging area of research on digital parenting practices, while emphasizing the need for future longitudinal and experimental studies to clarify directionality.

Practice implications

As smartphone use has become an integral part of daily life in modern societies, it is important for pediatric nurses to determine the effects of this technology on parents and children.

Pediatric nurses ought to arrange educational programs for parents, informing them about the detrimental effects of smartphone usage on physical health.
