Introduction

Adenosine (A) is changed into inosine (I) in dsRNA by RNA editing enzymes called adenosine deaminases acting on RNA (ADARs). Since inosine functions as a guanosine through translation and splicing [1], these editing enzymes can change mRNA codons and splice, resulting in the creation of diverse protein isoforms [2]. There are three types of ADAR enzymes present in mammals (ADAR1, ADAR2 and ADAR3) [2], with the deaminase activity having been shown ADAR1 and ADAR2 genes. The C-terminus of these three RNA-binding proteins has the deaminase catalytic domain (DM), while the N-terminus has two or three dsRNA-binding domains (RBDs). ADAR1 has two known isoforms, ADAR1p150 and ADAR1p110. ADAR1p110 is consistently expressed and localized in the nucleus, whereas ADAR1p150 translocates between the nucleus and cytoplasm [3,4]. Additionally, ADAR1p150 is an Interferon Stimulated Gene (ISG) that is induced by IFN [5].

ADARs were first discovered as an enigmatic dsRNA-unwinding activity in Xenopus laevis eggs and embryos [6,7]. Mammals, Drosophila, and squids produce ADAR-edited transcripts primarily in their central nervous systems, which enhance the variety of proteins derived from a single gene [[8], [9], [10]]. Several biological processes may be impacted by coding and noncoding dsRNA modifications caused by ADAR enzymes [[11], [12], [13]]. The early lethality of embryos with a fetal liver hematopoiesis defect and stress-induced apoptosis demonstrate the importance of ADAR1 in embryogenesis [14,15]. In mice, ADAR1 genetic disruption causes embryonic lethality and increases the expression of type I IFN and IFN-stimulated genes in hematopoietic stem cells even when there is no infection [16]. Subsequently, ADAR1 demonstrated through complementation and loss of function studies that virus-infected cells in culture required ADAR1 catalytic activity to suppress innate immune responses and produce IFN [[17], [18], [19]]. It has also been demonstrated that an ADAR1 mutation causes mice to produce more IFN and exhibit a type I IFN signature [16]. It has also been demonstrated that an ADAR1 mutation causes mice to produce more IFN and exhibit a type I IFN signature.

A large class of cytokines known as Interferons (IFNs) are produced when the host defense is challenged and are crucial for triggering the immune systems reaction to infections. Type I, type II, and type III IFNs are the three categories into which IFNs are divided based on biological activity, receptor utilization, genomic organization, and sequence similarity [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Type I and type III IFNs are specialist innate antiviral cytokines that differ primarily in their target cells and distribution. Type I IFNs react with almost all nucleated cells, whereas type III IFNs are only present in tissues having an epithelial surface that is susceptible to viral exposure and infection [24,25]. Cytokines known as type I IFNs are essential for vertebrates' innate protection against viruses. IFN-I from fish and mammals functions similarly, despite having different evolutionary histories [26,27]. The seven IFN-I subtypes identified in fish are IFNa, IFNb, IFNc, IFNd, IFNe, IFNf, and IFNh [23]. The well-established function of type I IFNs is their antiviral effect. To induce an antiviral state in cells, type I IFNs can trigger antiviral ISGs. The few ISGs that have been shown to directly inhibit fish viruses are Mx, viperin, ISG15, PKR, and PKZ [28,[28], [29], [30]]. The antiviral activity of fish type I IFNs and the antiviral activity of the ISGs they produce are obviously closely related.

The widespread and essential mechanism of alternative splicing for producing protein diversity and controlling protein expression is widely understood. It causes variable splicing and alternative promoter usage, which result in a gene producing serval protein subtypes. Alternative splicing (AS) is a key mechanism contributing to transcriptome diversity in higher eukaryotes. Recent studies have shown that AS occurs in over 90 % of human multi-exon genes [[31], [32], [33]]. The four main categories of AS events are as follows: (1) alternative 5′ splice site (ss) and (3) 3′ splice site (ss) selection, which take place when multiple splice sites are present at one end of an exon; (2) exon skipping (cassette exons), in which an exon and its surrounding introns are spliced out of the transcript; and (4) intron retention, in which an intron stays in the mature mRNA molecule [34].

ADAR1 has several isoforms in mammals due to alternative splicing and various promoters. The constitutively produced ADAR1p110 and the IFN-inducible ADAR1p150 are the two well-known ADAR1 splicing isoforms [5]. Through a shortened exon 2 and an alternate exon 1, their transcripts begin from distinct promoters. The ADARp150 transcript is specifically started by the promoter for exon 1A, whereas the ADARp110 transcripts are started by the promoters for exons 1B, exon 1C, and exon 2, respectively [5,35]. Additionally, there are several splicing isoforms of fish ADAR1. For instance, there have been reports of two grass carp ADAR1 isoforms: CiADAR1 and CiADAR1-like. The transcripts of Ctenopharyngodon Idella ADAR1 and ADAR1-like develop from exons 1A and 1B, respectively, and terminate at exon 15, the ninth intron [36,37]. This is crucial for comprehending the splicing genes and ADAR1 regulation mechanisms. AS is frequently tissue-specific and developmentally regulated because different splicing factors are expressed exclusively in particular tissues and at various developmental stages [38]. Prior research has shown that certain genes do, in fact, coordinate mRNA splicing and A-to-I RNA editing. The RNA Pol2 C-terminal domain (CTD) may be in charge of this coordination [39,40]. Since effective exonic RNA editing activity usually requires on intronic editing complementary sequences (ECS) for duplex formation, it is believed that this type of editing occurs in the pre-mRNA sequence prior to the spliceosome, excluding introns [41].

Fish are a group of vertebrates reported to have a spicing mechanism similar to that of mammals though much less studies. Grass carp have been the subject of research on an ADAR1 splicing isoform known as "ADAR1-like." It's interesting to note that grass carp have ADAR1 isoforms that are encoded by a single gene [42]. EmADAR1-like in E. malabaricus, a significant fish species that is widely cultivated worldwide, is, nevertheless, poorly understood. Enhancing knowledge about the splicing isoform of EmADAR1-like (ADAR1-like) gene in E. malabaricus was the goal of this study. mRNA tissue specific expression, developmental stage, and gene expression profiles upon Poly (I: C), LPS, and NNV challenge were all conducted in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, an online tool was used to perform comparison analysis, domain analysis, and secondary and tertiary structure predictions.

Materials and methods

Fish

M. grouper (E. malabaricus) was acquired from a nearby fish farm in Kaohsiung, Taiwan. They were kept in indoor tanks with a seawater recirculating system for a week prior to use, with the water temperature set at 18 °C. The experiments were carried out in accordance with the National Taiwan Ocean University's institutional IACUC guidelines in Keelung, Taiwan.

Cell line and viral pathogen

This grouper kidney cell line (GK) [43] and fin cell line (GF-1) [44] were employed in this investigation. Leibovitz's L-15 medium (Gibco, USA) was supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin (MD Bio Inc., Taiwan) and incubated in a plate at 28 °C. In Leibovitz's L-15 (Gibco, NY, USA), grouper kidney cells were cultured using NNV pathogen at 28 °C with 2 % FBS, 100 mg/ml streptomycin, and 10 U/ml penicillin added [44]. The viral titers, which were expressed as tissue culture infective dose (TCID50/0.1 ml), were determined using the Reed and Muench method [45].

In silico characterization

The full-length EmADAR1-like gene was found using local blast software using the query set from the E. malabaricus transcriptome database [46]. The open reading frames of the EmADAR1-like were analyzed using the Open Reading Frame Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/). The sequence was compared to the known ADAR1 isoform using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) analysis tool on the NCBI website. The various protein sequence alignments were produced by the Clustal W tool (http://www.ebi.ac.uk). The phylogenetic tree was built using the MEGA software and the neighbor-joining strategy. To assess support for internal cladding, bootstrap values were set to 1000 and bootstrap repeats were employed. The conserved domains of the EmADAR1-like protein were found by using the Conserved Domain Database (CCD) on the NCBI computer (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). ProtParam Server was used to infer the fundamental physicochemical characteristics of the inferred EmADAR1-like protein (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). NetPhos 3.1 Server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/ services /NetPhos-3.1/) was used to analyse the phosphorylated sites. The EmADAR1-like protein's subcellular localization was hypothesised using the PSORTII Prediction service (https://psort.hgc.jp/form2.html). Protscale was used to measure the hydrophobicity (http://web.expasy.org/ProtScale/). Accordingly, SOPMA was used to predict the secondary and tertiary structures of the EmADAR1-like protein (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl? page=npsa_sopma.html).

Developmental stage and tissue distribution of emadar1-like

Eggs and fry were collected at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h intervals to determine which ones were fertile [47,48]. Healthy fish tissues include the following: intestine, gas bladder, gill, muscle, skin, liver, spleen, brain, heart, head kidney, trunk kidney, and eyes. At −80 °C, three pooled samples (three E. malabaricus per pool) were taken for every tissue type and promptly stored in TRIzol® Reagent (Ambion, USA).

Expression analysis of emadar1-like after challenges

To examine the expression profile of EmADAR1-like upon different challenges, the fish maintaining according to the treatment groups and anaesthetized with 2-phenoxyethanol (Sigma Aldrich, USA) and injected by the intraperitoneal route with 0.1 ml of 5 mg/ml conc. in poly (I: C) (Sigma Aldrich) and 10 mg/ml conc. of LPS in PBS buffer, NNV 1 × 107 TCID50/ml in 100 µl and control groups was injected Phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Samples were taken from three fish per group on 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after injection. They were then promptly stored at −80 °C in TRIzol® Reagent (Ambion, USA).

NNV infection and type I IFN treatment

Following standard procedures, grouper kidney cells were cultured and passaged in L15 medium (Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 2 % FBS and antibiotics (Streptomycin and Penicillin, Gibco) in a humidified environment of 95 % air and 5 % CO2 at 28 °C. The GK cells were infected with NNV for 2 h incubation period then washed with 1X PBS for three time and added the L15 culture medium. In early studies, recombinant type I IFN was cloned and characterized, and anti-viral function was investigated using in vitro method [47,49]. Real-time PCR was used to assess the expression of the EmADAR1-like gene in the experimental samples, which were obtained at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after infection.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Following the manufacturer's instructions, TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was used to extract total RNA. Each extracted RNA sample's A260/280 ratio was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, USA). The RNA was subsequently dissolved in RNase-free water and stored at −80 °C. Using the HiScript I Reverse Transcriptase Kit (BIONOVAS, Canada), 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into single-stranded cDNA in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The resulting cDNA templates were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was used to analyze gene expression profiles on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System v2.0.6 (Applied Biosystems, USA). 2 µL of cDNA template, 10 µL of 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix, 0.5 µL of forward and reverse primers (10 pmol/µL; see Table 1), and 7 µL of distilled water were all included in each 20 µL qPCR experiment. There were 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 56 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 15 s after the initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min. The ABI 7500HD software and the 2^–ΔΔCt technique were used to normalize the relative expression levels of target genes to β-actin [47,48].

Statistical analysis

The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple comparison test were used to analyses the data from the tissue distribution and developmental stage analyses. Additionally, the student's t-test was used to analyze the qPCR data. At p < 0.05 and p < 0.001, differences were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Full-length cDNA characterization

To identify EmADAR1-like genes, BlastP was used to search the E. malabaricus transcriptome database and the identified E. malabaricus ADAR1-like gene (GenBank accession No. MF595081). An estimated 1027 amino acids are encoded by the 3815 nucleotides of the EmADAR1-like cDNA, which has an ORF of 3084 nucleotides. There are 210 nucleotides in the 5′ terminal untranslated region (UTR) and 521 nucleotides in the 3′-UTR (SFig. 1). The deducted polypeptide's molecular mass is determined to be 111.11 KDa (SFig. 2). The EmADAR1-Like DNA sequence was compared with the known EmADAR1 (SFig. 3).

Domain structure of emadar1-like protein sequence analysis

The putative protein of E. malabaricus ADAR1-like showed higher similarities (100–57 %) and identities (100–49 %) to within known fish homologs to other known ADAR1 (Table 2) (S. Data 2.2). For amino acid sequence analysis, EmADAR1-like was distinguished through its N-terminal one chitin synthesis regulation domain with a signal peptide (12–101 aa), two Z-DNA-binding domains (Z alpha) (254–313, 414–476 aa), three dsRNA binding motifs (DSRM) (623–667, 756–821, and 881–946 aa), and one tRNA-specific and dsRNA adenosine deaminase domain (ADEAMc) (970–1020 aa). Multiple alignments also demonstrated a high conservation between teleost ADAR1-like and human ADAR1, and even the sequences of teleost EmADAR1-like were shorter then E. malabaricus ADAR1 (SFig. 2). The identity, and domain structure and signal peptides of EmADAR1-Like DNA sequences shared similar homology with the known EmADAR1 (SFig. 2).

Molecular characterization

EmADAR1-like and EmADAR1 proteins were predicted to have molecular weights (MW) of 11 kDa and isoelectric points (pI) of 8.68, respectively. The EmADAR1 protein showed an alkaline isoelectric point of 8.39 and a comparable molecular weight of 15 kDa (S. Data 2.1). As a measure of protein thermos ability, the high aliphatic index values, which range from 67.06, further indicate that these EmADAR1-like proteins have thermal stability over a broad temperature range. In EmADAR1-like proteins, no signal peptides were detected (S. Data 2.3A). Serine (10.80 %) was the most abundant amino acid, followed by proline (9.44 %), alanine (9.25 %), and leucine (8.89 %), according to the amino acid composition (SFig. 3A; S. Data 2.2). The PSORT program was used to infer the subcellular distribution of EmADAR1-like protein, which included 18.5 % in nucleolar, 13 % in cytoplasmic nucleolar, 6.5 % in cytoplasm, 4 % in mitochondria, 2 %, and 1 % in peroxisomal. The findings showed that the cytoplasmic and nucleolar regions were the primary locations for EmADAR1-like (S. Data: 2.3B and SFig. 3B). The EmADAR1-like protein was found to have mixed secondary structures, with a 0.00 % beta turn, a 71.57 % random coil, a 22.78 % alpha helix, and a 5.65 % extended strand, according to the secondary structure predictions (SFig. 3C). The molecular weight, theoretical pI values of the predicted polypeptides and signal peptides was found similar comparison with the EmADAR1 proteins (SFig. 3 and S. Data 2.3B).

EmADAR1-Like structure of gene and composition of motifs

According to an analysis of the Netphos 3.1 Server, there were 99 possible phosphorylation sites, comprising 36 threonine, 22 tyrosine, and 41 serine phosphorylation sites (SFig. 4A). The MEME suite was used to examine the diversity and distribution pattern of conserved motifs in the EmADAR1-like and EmADAR1 sequences (SFig. 4A). Of the eight motifs we discovered overall (SFig. 4B), 2 motif of Z alpha and three motifs of DSRM was found in both EmADAR1-like and EmADAR1 genes. The majority of these motifs, specifically ADEAMc, not matched the conserved sequences of the tail (SFig. 3, 4).

Phylogenetic tree analysis

The neighbor-joining method was used to build a phylogenetic tree using the complete amino acid sequences of ADAR1-like from E. malabaricus and other species. SFig. 6 illustrates how all ADAR1s were divided into five groups: avian, amphibian, teleost, invertebrate, and mammalian. Evidently, the unique teleost grouper, which was different from the teleost main cluster, contained EmADAR1-like. ADAR1-like demonstrated a varied relationship to E. malabaricus ADAR1, as anticipated. According to the findings, ADAR1-like is a novel member of the ADAR1 family of E. malabaricus (SFig. 6).

Discussion

Through the proteome-diversifying process of AS, multiple mature RNA messengers are produced from a single gene; each of these transcripts may code for a distinct protein isoform with a potentially distinct function [50]. AS has been discovered to occur in many immune-related genes, demonstrating the widespread presence of AS in immune response [51,52]. The discrepancy between the estimated 22,000 protein-coding genes in the human genome and the more than 200,000 observed protein isoforms can be attributed to the fact that nearly 95 % of human genes undergo AS, producing an average of six isoforms per gene [53,54]. Numerous human disorders may be linked to these AS-induced isoforms [[55], [56], [57]]. AS has physiological significance because the majority of alternative exons show differential expression across various tissues and cell lines [33,38]. However, certain isoforms have opposing roles in controlling cellular functions [58].

Transcriptome analysis of the full-length EmADAR1 revealed the presence of an EmADAR1-like splicing isoform [47], EmADAR1-like and EmADAR1 can be expressed from the same gene through the use of alternative promotors and differential splicing. Adenosine deaminase catalytic domains, dsRNA binding domains, and a Z-alpha binding domain are among the structurally significant EmADAR1-like conservative domains that were discovered in the deducted amino acid similar to EmADAR1p150. Predicted spliced sequence regions were identified in the C-terminal region of the adenosine deaminase catalytic domain (Fig. 1). It is important to note that C. idella also possesses orthologs of these variants [59]. The results of phylogenetic analysis, which showed that EmADAR1p150 and EmADAR1-like branched into the same cluster apart from other species (Fig. 2). It suggested that EmADAR1-like was conserved with the EmADAR1p150 gene in grouper species. Several alignments revealed that EmADAR1-like shared the structure and typical features of known EmADAR1p150. EmADAR1-like conserved structures shared the highest amino acid identity (49 %) with the ADAR1 genes of C. idella and E. malabaricus. Similar to the immune response observed in C. idella, the conserved structures of EmADAR1-like throughout evolution suggested that EmADAR1 may similarly play an important role in the immune response of E. malabaricus [59].

In our earlier investigation, EmADAR1 was significantly and rapidly up-regulated in response to NNV infection and immunostimulation [47]. EmADAR1-like was also expressed in all tissues in this investigation (Fig. 5A) [59,60], and was significantly up-regulated in the brain, spleen, and head kidney tissues in response to immunostimulation and NNV infection [47]. Remarkably, EmADAR1 expression remained elevated for 24 to 72 h after infection, whereas EmADAR1-like mRNA was present earlier for 6 to 72 h. The differential expression between EmADAR1p150 [47], EmADAR1-like and CiADAR1-like [59] in vivo and in vitro experiment results suggests their similar role in the antiviral immune response.

Type I IFNs interact with their receptors to recruit Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) and Tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2), which bind to IFNAR1 and IFNAR2, respectively. After these active kinases are activated, STAT1 and STAT2 dimerize and assemble with IFN regulatory factor (IRF) 9 to form IFN stimulated gene factor 3 (ISGF3). This trimolecular complex moves into the nucleus, where it binds to IFN-stimulated response elements (ISREs) and initiates hundreds of ISGs to be transcriptionally regulated [61]. In order to activate IFN-stimulated response elements (ISRE) within the promoters of a group of ISGs, the type I IFN receptor complex docks and phosphorylates STAT1 and STAT2, which then interact with IFN regulatory factor 9 (IRF9) to translocate into the nucleus and form the heterotrimeric complex known as IFN stimulated gene (ISG) factor 3 (ISGF3) [62,63]. The ISG consists of a different panel of genes, many of which have direct antiviral or regulatory actions, that work together to produce an antiviral state that inhibits viral replication and limits the virus's ability for propagation [64].

To prove whether EmADAR1 and EmADAR1-like is factually expressed in GK cells, we prepared the r-IFN were treated (Fig. 5). EmADAR1-Like and EmADAR1 were significantly induced in GK cells by a viral infection and expression were observed in the EmADAR1-like after 6 h. The differential activation induces a gene specific immune response [65]. In our previous study, the luciferase reporter assay in GK cells revealed that STAT1/2, IRF9, ADAR1 and ADAR1-like were strongly induced by pcDNA-3.1-IFN promotor [66]. Compared to EmADAR1-like and EmADAR1 is thought to be the gene most sensitive to the effects of IFN exposure (Data not provided). IFN's antiviral activity may be mediated by activating JAK-STAT signal transduction, which is followed by an increase in EmADAR1 and splicing genes. Of course, further investigation is needed to determine the molecular of EmADAR1-like.

In conclusion, we found a splicing isoform of Epinephelus malabaricus ADAR1 (EmADAR1-like). EmADAR1-like and EmADAR1 are derived from the same gene by alternate promoter usage, with the C-terminal deaminase domain differing due to AS. The phenomenon shows that ADAR1-Like may have a wide range of functions in many teleost species. As EmADAR1-Like is selectively inducible by poly (I: C) and IFN in different tissues in grouper. Further studies are needed on the promoters' analysis and comparison of ADAR1 and ADAR1-like, as well as how they alter the results of virus-host interactions for various combinations of viruses and hosts.
