Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian finite element method for lipid membranes
Amaresh Sahu

Abstract
An arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian finite element method and numerical implementation for curved and deforming lipid membranes is presented here. The membrane surface is endowed with a mesh whose in-plane motion need not depend on the in-plane flow of lipids. Instead, in-plane mesh dynamics can be specified arbitrarily. A new class of mesh motions is introduced, where the mesh velocity satisfies the dynamical equations of a user-specified two-dimensional material. A Lagrange multiplier constrains the out-of-plane membrane and mesh velocities to be equal, such that the mesh and material always overlap. An associated numerical inf–sup instability ensues, and is removed by adapting established techniques in the finite element analysis of fluids. In our implementation, the aforementioned Lagrange multiplier is projected onto a discontinuous space of piecewise linear functions. The new mesh motion is compared to established Lagrangian and Eulerian formulations by investigating a preeminent numerical benchmark of biological significance: the pulling of a membrane tether from a flat patch, and its subsequent lateral translation.

In this paper, we present an arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) finite element

The equations governing membrane dynamics are highly nonlinear partial

One cannot in general analytically solve for the time evolution of lipid flows
and membrane shape changes, which are intricately coupled.
However, it is also difficult to solve the full membrane equations numerically,

We reiterate that many challenges arise when developing a general numerical
method that truly captures in-plane viscous lipid flows, out-of-plane membrane
bending, and their coupling—all on an arbitrarily curved and deforming

Several studies

,

,

,

took a Lagrangian approach, where the surface is discretized and
the resulting mesh is convected with the physical, material velocity.
Lagrangian implementations successfully capture membrane dynamics, but struggle
to resolve in-plane flows as they lead to highly distorted elements.
A remeshing procedure was used to maintain element aspect ratios, though it led
to unphysical oscillations in the membrane curvature

.
An alternative approach is for the mesh to only move normal to the surface,
so that it is unaffected by in-plane flows.
The mesh motion is then out-of-plane Lagrangian, as it tracks the material
surface, and in-plane Eulerian.
Such an approach, which we refer to as Eulerian, was implemented in Ref.
[
79
]
—though the membrane geometry was updated explicitly
in a piecemeal manner.
Given the importance of geometry to membrane dynamics
[
71
]
,
numerical methods relying on explicit mesh updates could suffer from issues
known to affect explicit algorithms in the study of fluids

[
80
,
81
]
.
Moreover, even if a fully implicit Eulerian mesh motion was implemented as in
our prior work
[
4
]
, scenarios arise where the method will fail

[

]

We show one such example in
§4.2

Since neither Lagrangian nor Eulerian approaches can capture commonly-observed
membrane behaviors, a more general mesh motion is required.
The ALE theory underlying a general mesh motion was independently derived by
both others
[
3
]
and ourselves
[
4
]
.
A new mesh motion was implemented in Ref.
[
3
]

mesh moved only in the direction normal to a known prior configuration of the

In practice, such a choice was again not sufficiently general and required
remeshing steps, which introduced errors in the numerical solution.
Since the aforementioned approach resembles an Eulerian mesh motion, we
hypothesize that it may not work in certain situations—such as the tether
pulling scenario discussed in
§4.2
.
There is thus still a need for numerical implementations of more general mesh
motions, which can be specified by the user when solving for membrane dynamics
in a particular scenario.

The aforementioned limitations of numerical techniques motivate our development
and implementation of a fully implicit ALE finite element method for lipid
membranes.
Rather than prescribing the mesh motion directly, we choose for the mesh
velocity to satisfy a set of partial differential equations as if the mesh were
itself another material.
We then supply appropriate boundary conditions to the mesh velocity.
In addition, the mesh and membrane are constrained to coincide via a Lagrange
multiplier—which is understood as the mesh analog of a normal force per unit
area.
The presence of a scalar Lagrange multiplier coupled to vector velocities leads
to a numerical instability reminiscent of that identified by

O.A. Ladyzhenskaya

[
82
]
,
I. Babuška

[
83
]
, and
F. Brezzi

[
84
]
,
hereafter referred to as the LBB condition.
We suppress the instability with the method of
C.R. Dohrmann
and

P.B. Bochev

[
85
]
, where the Lagrange
multiplier is projected onto a space of discontinuous, piecewise linear
functions.

\thetitle
. The governing equations: Strong and weak formulations

The membrane is treated as a single 2D differentiable manifold embedded in the
Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, for which we implicitly assume no slip between
the two bilayer leaflets.
In our ALE formulation, the membrane surface is parametrized by coordinates that
need not follow material flows, as we detailed in Ref.
[
4
]
.
Accordingly, the so-called mesh velocity
\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}
resulting from the
parametrization will in general not equal the membrane velocity
\bm{v}
of
material points.
The dynamics of both the membrane and mesh are detailed in what follows; many of
the results were derived in our prior work
[
4
,
53
]
.
The phospholipid bilayer is treated as an area-incompressible material—for
which the areal density is constant and the surface tension,
\lambda
, is a
Lagrange multiplier field to be solved for.
Our goal is to determine the fundamental unknowns
\bm{v}
,
\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}
, and

\lambda
, as well as the membrane position
\bm{x}
, over time.

\thetitle
. Surface parametrization, geometry, and kinematics

We begin by reviewing the framework with which lipid membranes are described.
Only the most relevant features are presented here, as Ref.
[
53
]

details our understanding of the membrane geometry and kinematics, while Ref.
[
4
]

:
Surface geometry.
A schematic of the mapping

\bm{x}=\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)
,

\Omega
and
the membrane patch
\mathcal{P}
.
The in-plane basis vectors
\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}

\bm{n}

\bm{\nu}

\bm{x}_{{\mathrm{b}}}
at the

.

Consider an arbitrarily curved and deforming membrane surface
\mathcal{S}
, of which
we examine a patch

\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{S}
.
At any time

, the surface position
\bm{x}
is parametrized by two
coordinates:
\zeta^{\check{1}}
and
\zeta^{\check{2}}
.
Here, as in Ref.
[

From now on, Greek indices span the set
\{1,2\}
, such that

\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

denotes the position of a point on the membrane surface.
As shown in Fig.
1
, there is a mapping from the parametric
domain
\Omega
in the
\zeta^{\check{1}}
–
\zeta^{\check{2}}

patch
\mathcal{P}
.
Area integrals over the patch are thus evaluated as

\int_{\mathcal{P}}\big(\ldots\big)~{\mathrm{d}}a\ =\,\int_{\Omega}\big(\ldots\big)\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega~,

(1)

where
J_{\Omega}
is the Jacobian of the mapping.
Here

has dimensions of area and its

At any point
\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)
, the vectors

\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}:=\partial\bm{x}/\partial\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}

form a basis of the tangent plane to the surface, which has unit normal

\bm{n}=(\bm{a}_{\check{1}}\times\bm{a}_{\check{2}})/\lvert\bm{a}_{\check{1}}\times\bm{a}_{\check{2}}\rvert

Covariant components of the metric and curvature tensors are respectively

a_{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}:=\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{\check{\beta}}

and

b_{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}:=\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\bm{x}_{,{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}=\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\bm{x}_{;{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
.
Here

(\,\bm{\cdot}\,)_{,{\check{\alpha}}}:=\partial(\,\bm{\cdot}\,)/\partial\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}

and

(\,\bm{\cdot}\,)_{;{\check{\alpha}}}
are respectively the partial and covariant derivatives with
respect to
\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}
.
The Jacobian
J_{\Omega}
appearing in Eq. (
1
) is expressed as

J_{\Omega}=\sqrt{\rule{0.0pt}{6.45831pt}}\overline{\det\,a_{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\,}\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{6.88889pt}{\vrule width=0.5pt,height=1.50696pt}
.
The mean curvature
H
is calculated as

H=\tfrac{1}{2}\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}}{\hskip-1.0pt}{}^{\text{\raisebox{-3.01389pt}{${\check{\beta}}$}}}b_{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
,
where
a^{{\check{\alpha}}}{\hskip-1.0pt}{}^{\text{\raisebox{-3.01389pt}{${\check{\beta}}$}}}
is the contravariant metric and is calculated as
the matrix inverse of
a_{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
.
In the present work, the Einstein summation convention is employed, in which
repeated raised and lowered indices are summed over.
The Gaussian curvature
K
is computed as

K=\det(b_{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}})/\det(a_{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}})
.
Finally, at points
\bm{x}_{{\mathrm{b}}}
on the patch boundary

\partial\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{P}
, additional in-plane basis vectors are defined:

\bm{\nu}=\nu^{\check{\alpha}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}=\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\,\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}

\int_{\partial\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{P}}\!\big(\ldots\big)~{\mathrm{d}}s\ =\,\int_{\Gamma}\big(\ldots\big)\,J_{\Gamma}~{\mathrm{d}}\Gamma~,

In Eq. (

When solving for the membrane state at a particular time, we seek the surface
tension, material velocity, and mesh velocity fields on the surface.
To this end, it is useful to define relevant function spaces over the parametric

\Omega
.
The space of square-integrable functions,

, is expressed as

L^{2}(\Omega)\,:=\,\Big\{\,u(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,:~\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\hskip 10.00002pt\Big(\textstyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\Big)^{\!1/2}<\,\infty\,\Big\}~.

(3)

The Sobolev space of order

H^{k}(\Omega)
, consists of functions that
are square-integrable and also have up to

square-integrable:

H^{k}(\Omega)\,:=\,\Big\{\,u(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,:~\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\,\hskip 5.0pt\underbrace{\hskip-5.0ptu_{,{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}\ldots{\mkern 3.0mu\check{\mkern-3.0mu\gamma}}}}_{m\text{ derivatives}}\hskip-6.0pt\in\,L^{2}(\Omega)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }~0\leq m\leq k\,\Big\}~.

(4)

From the definitions in Eqs. (
3
) and
(
4
), it is evident that

H^{0}(\Omega)=L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, since the material and mesh velocities are elements of

\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we also define

\bm{H}^{k}(\Omega)\,:=\,\Big\{\,\bm{u}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,:~\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\hskip 10.00002ptu_{j}\mkern 1.0mu\in H^{k}(\Omega)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }j=1,2,3\,\Big\}

(5)

as the space of functions in which each Cartesian component is an element of

H^{k}(\Omega)
.
In Eq. (
5
), we denote

u_{j}:=\bm{u}\bm{\cdot}\bm{e}_{j}
,
where

\{\bm{e}_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
is the canonical Cartesian basis in

\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We close with a discussion of membrane kinematics.
The membrane velocity

\bm{v}={\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}/{\mathrm{d}}t=\bm{\dot{x}}

is the material time derivative of the position, and is expanded in the

\{\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}},\bm{n}\}
basis as

\bm{v}=v^{\check{\alpha}}\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}+v\bm{n}
.
The mesh velocity, which is treated as a fundamental unknown independent of the
material velocity, is defined to be the rate of change of position when

\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}
is held constant—expressed as

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}=(\partial\check{\bm{x}}/\partial t)\rvert_{\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}}
.
In order for the material and mesh velocities to correspond to the same surface,
kinematics require

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}\,=\,\bm{v}\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}

(6)

such that the mesh motion is always out-of-plane Lagrangian

[
4
]
.
Importantly, the in-plane material velocity components
v^{\check{\alpha}}
and mesh
velocity components

v^{\check{\alpha}}_{{\mathrm{m}}}=\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}

need not coincide, such that within our ALE framework one can dictate how the
mesh evolves within the membrane surface.
In this work, Eq. (
6
) is referred to as
the ALE kinematic constraint.

\thetitle
. The balance of mass: Material incompressibility

As lipid membranes only stretch 2–3% before tearing

[
86
,
87
]
, we treat them as area-incompressible 2D
materials.
The local form of the balance of mass, also referred to as the continuity
equation and incompressibility constraint, is given by

\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}(\zeta^{\mkern 3.0mu\check{\mkern-3.0mu\gamma}},t)\,\bm{\cdot}\,\bm{v}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}(\zeta^{\mkern 3.0mu\check{\mkern-3.0mu\gamma}},t)\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\zeta^{\mkern 3.0mu\check{\mkern-3.0mu\gamma}}\mkern-1.0mu\in\Omega~,~~t\mkern 1.0mu\in\,[\mkern 1.0mu0,t_{\mathrm{f}}\mkern 1.0mu]~,

(7)

where
t_{\mathrm{f}}
is the end of the time interval under consideration.
For notational simplicity, the functional dependence of all quantities will be
suppressed, as will the domains of
\zeta^{\mkern 3.0mu\check{\mkern-3.0mu\gamma}}
and
t
.
The incompressibility constraint

\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{v}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}=0

is equivalently expressed as

v^{\check{\alpha}}_{;{\check{\alpha}}}-2\mkern 1.0muvH=0
,
and is enforced by the Lagrange multiplier field

\lambda=\lambda(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)
—which has dimension of energy
\mkern 1.0mu/\mkern 1.0mu
area and acts as the membrane surface tension

[
53
, Ch.V §6(a)–(c)]
.

The weak formulation of Eq. (
7
) is obtained by
multiplying it with an arbitrary surface tension variation
\delta\lambda

and integrating over the membrane area.
The tension variation is assumed to be square-integrable, for which the weak
form of the continuity equation is expressed as

\int_{\Omega}\delta\lambda\,\big(\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{v}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\big)\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)~.

(8)

Care must be taken when discretizing Eq. (
8
) in
the course of finite element analysis, as one could violate the LBB condition

[
82
,
83
,
84
]

and observe spurious surface tension oscillations in the numerical solution.
A variety of techniques were developed to prevent such oscillations and
numerically stabilize the system
[
88
,
89
,
90
,
91
]
.
We choose to employ the Dohrmann–Bochev method
[
85
]
, as
it is based on an underlying theory and is straightforward to implement
numerically
[
81
]
.

\thetitle
. The Dohrmann–Bochev method: Numerical stabilization

The Dohrmann–Bochev method prevents spurious surface tension oscillations by
projecting the surface tension onto a space of discontinuous, piecewise linear
functions.
The function space, denoted
\mkern 1.5mu\breve{\mkern-1.5muL}
, is defined in Eq. (
64
)
following a discussion of the surface discretization.
The
L^{2}
-projection of a given surface tension field

\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)

onto
\mkern 1.5mu\breve{\mkern-1.5muL}
is denoted
\breve{\lambda}
and is defined through the relation

[
85
]

\int_{\Omega}\delta\breve{\lambda}\,\big(\lambda\,-\,\breve{\lambda}\big)~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ =\ 0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\breve{\lambda}\in\mkern 1.5mu\breve{\mkern-1.5muL}~.

(9)

In practice, the Dohrmann–Bochev method
[
85
]
is
implemented by quadratically penalizing surface tension deviations from the
space
\mkern 1.5mu\breve{\mkern-1.5muL}
, for which the quantity

\dfrac{\alpha^{\text{DB}}}{\zeta}\int_{\Omega}\big(\delta\lambda\,-\,\delta\breve{\lambda}\big)\big(\lambda\,-\,\breve{\lambda}\big)~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega

(10)

is subtracted from Eq. (
8
).
Here,
\zeta
is the 2D intramembrane viscosity: a material property with
dimensions of energy
\mkern 1.0mu\cdot\mkern 1.0mu
time
\mkern 1.0mu/\mkern 1.0mu
area.
In addition,
\alpha^{\text{DB}}
is a user-chosen parameter with dimensions of area,
such that Eqs. (
8
) and (
10
)
have the same dimensions—though the value of
\alpha^{\text{DB}}
is not observed to
affect simulation results.
The numerically stabilized weak formulation of the incompressibility constraint
is written as

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)~,

(11)

where
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
is the surface tension contribution to the direct Galerkin
expression
[
92
]
given by

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\ :=\mkern 1.0mu\int_{\Omega}\delta\lambda\,\big(\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{v}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\big)\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ \,-\,\ \dfrac{\alpha^{\text{DB}}}{\zeta}\int_{\Omega}\big(\delta\lambda\,-\,\delta\breve{\lambda}\big)\big(\lambda\,-\,\breve{\lambda}\big)~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega~.

(12)

In Eq. (
12
),
\breve{\lambda}
and
\delta\breve{\lambda}
are
understood to be the
L^{2}
-projections of their respective counterparts

\lambda
and
\delta\lambda
onto
\mkern 1.5mu\breve{\mkern-1.5muL}
, according to Eq. (
9
).

\thetitle
. The balance of linear momentum: Membrane dynamics

Consider a general, arbitrarily curved and deforming 2D material for which
inertial effects are negligible.
The local form of the balance of linear momentum for such a material is given by

\bm{T}^{\check{\alpha}}_{;\mkern 1.0mu{\check{\alpha}}}\,+\,\bm{f}\,=\,\bm{0}~,

(13)

where
\bm{T}^{\check{\alpha}}
is the internal traction along a curve of constant
\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}

on the surface and
\bm{f}
is the net body force per unit area on the material
by its surroundings.
The balance of angular momentum additionally requires that the stress vectors be
expressed as
[
53
]

\bm{T}^{\check{\alpha}}\,=\,\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{a}_{\check{\beta}}\,-\,\big(M^{{\check{\beta}}{\check{\alpha}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{n}\big)_{;{\check{\beta}}}~.

(14)

Here,
\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
contains the couple-free, in-plane stress components and

M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
contains the couple-stress components.
If the constitutive form of
\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
and
M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
are known, then Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) govern the
dynamics of a general 2D material.

In lipid bilayers, the constitutive relations for
\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
and
M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
are
well-known, and depend on three material parameters.
The first is the intramembrane viscosity
\zeta
, which characterizes the
irreversibility of in-plane flows.
The other parameters are the mean and Gaussian bending moduli, denoted

and

[

We previously determined the membrane stresses and couple-stresses within the

\displaystyle M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\,=\,k_{\mathrm{b}}\mkern 1.0muH\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu+\,k_{\mathrm{g}}\mkern 1.0mu\Big(2\mkern 1.0muH\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu-\mkern 1.0mub^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\Big)

(15)

\displaystyle\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\,=\,k_{\mathrm{b}}\mkern 1.0mu\Big(H^{2}\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu-\mkern 1.0mu2\mkern 1.0muH\,b^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\Big)\,-\,k_{\mathrm{g}}\mkern 1.0muK\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu+\,\lambda\,a^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu+\,\pi^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}~,

(16)

where

\displaystyle\pi^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\,=\,\zeta\,\bm{v}_{,{\check{\mu}}}\bm{\cdot}\Big(\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\mu}}{\check{\beta}}}\,+\,\bm{a}^{\check{\beta}}\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\mu}}{\check{\alpha}}}\Big)

(17)

are the in-plane viscous stress components.
The couple-stresses (

k_{\mathrm{b}}
and
k_{\mathrm{g}}
, and
thus are purely elastic, while
\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}}{\hskip-1.0pt}{}^{\text{\raisebox{-3.01389pt}{${\check{\beta}}$}}}
contains bending, tensile, and
viscous contributions (
16

The in-plane and out-of-plane equations governing lipid membrane dynamics are
obtained by substituting Eqs. (
14
)–(

13
) and contracting the
result with
\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}
and
\bm{n}
—which yields
[

\displaystyle\zeta\,\Big(\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}v_{{\check{\alpha}}}\,+\,K\mkern 1.0muv_{\check{\alpha}}\,+\,2\mkern 1.0muv_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\mkern 1.0muH\,-\,2\mkern 1.0muv_{,{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mub^{\check{\beta}}_{\check{\alpha}}\,-\,2\mkern 1.0muv\mkern 1.0muH_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\Big)\,+\,f_{\check{\alpha}}\,+\,\lambda_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\ =\ 0

(18)

and

\displaystyle f\,+\,2\mkern 1.0mu\lambda\mkern 1.0muH\,+\,\zeta\,\Big(2\mkern 1.0mub^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0muv_{{\check{\alpha}};{\check{\beta}}}\,-\,8\mkern 1.0muv\mkern 1.0muH^{2}\,+\,4\mkern 1.0muv\mkern 1.0muK\Big)\,-\,k_{\mathrm{b}}\Big(2\mkern 1.0muH^{3}\mkern 1.0mu-\mkern 1.0mu2\mkern 1.0muH\mkern 1.0muK\,+\,\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}H\Big)\ =\ 0~.

(19)

Equations (

) and
(

) are respectively referred to as the in-plane
and shape equations, and were independently obtained via different approaches

[
2
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
.
Here, the body force per unit area
\bm{f}
is decomposed as

\bm{f}=\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}f_{\check{\alpha}}+f\bm{n}
,
and the operator
\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}
acts on an arbitrary quantity
(\,\bm{\cdot}\,)
as

\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}(\,\bm{\cdot}\,):=a^{{\check{\mu}}{\check{\nu}}}(\,\bm{\cdot}\,)_{;{\check{\mu}}{\check{\nu}}}
.
Note that while the membrane bends elastically, the in-plane viscosity
\zeta

enters the shape equation (

between in-plane stresses and curvature

]

The surface tension
\lambda

H

\thetitle
. The boundary conditions

One cannot determine a well-posed set of boundary conditions to Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
) by
inspection.
A series of systematic developments for elastic shells
[
93
,
17
,
18
]
underlie the formulation of the general
lipid membrane boundary conditions
[
94
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
95
]
.
In what follows, we highlight possible boundary conditions and provide their
physical justification.
Details of our own derivations, which reproduce earlier results, are provided in
Ch.V §5(d) of Ref.
[
53
]
.

The in-plane equations governing lipid flows (
18
)
are identical to those governing a two-dimensional Newtonian fluid

[
96
]
.
We thus expect the boundary conditions to be similar to those of a fluid, in
which one specifies either the velocity
\bm{v}
or (for a surface) the force
per length
\bm{F}
on the boundary.
For general 2D materials, the force per length on the patch boundary is given by

\bm{F}\,=\,\bm{T}^{\check{\alpha}}\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\,-\,\big(M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\mkern 1.0mu\tau_{\check{\beta}}\,\bm{n}\big)_{\!,{\check{\mu}}}\,\tau^{\check{\mu}}~,

(20)

which—for the case of lipid membranes—has bending, tensile, and viscous
contributions (recall
\bm{\nu}
and
\bm{\tau}
are boundary basis vectors;
see Fig.
1
).
In our numerical implementation, on each edge of the membrane patch we specify
a component of either the velocity
\bm{v}
or the force per length
\bm{F}
in
each of the three Cartesian directions.
We denote
\Gamma_{\!\!F}^{\mkern 1.0muj}
and
\Gamma_{\!v}^{\mkern 1.0muj}
as the respective portions of the
boundary where

F_{\!j}:=\bm{F}\bm{\cdot}\bm{e}_{j}

and

v_{\mkern-1.0muj}:=\bm{v}\bm{\cdot}\bm{e}_{j}

are specified.
For

j\in\{1,2,3\}
, the intersection

\Gamma_{\!v}^{\mkern 1.0muj}\cap\Gamma_{\!\!F}^{\mkern 1.0muj}=\varnothing

and the closure of the union

\overline{\rule{0.0pt}{7.19025pt}\hskip 32.0pt~}\hskip-35.0pt\Gamma_{\!v}^{\mkern 1.0mu\jmath}\cup\Gamma_{\!\!F}^{\mkern 1.0mu\jmath}=\Gamma
.
The boundary conditions are expressed as

v_{\mkern-1.0muj}\,=\,\bar{v}_{\mkern-1.0muj}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{\!v}^{\mkern 1.0muj}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptF_{\!j}\,=\,\mkern 4.5mu\overline{\mkern-4.5muF\mkern-0.5mu}\mkern 0.5mu_{\!j}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{\!\!F}^{\mkern 1.0muj}~,

(21)

where
\bar{v}_{\mkern-1.0muj}
and
\mkern 4.5mu\overline{\mkern-4.5muF\mkern-0.5mu}\mkern 0.5mu_{\!j}
are known quantities that we prescribe.

The boundary conditions in Eq. (
21
) are necessary but
not sufficient for a mathematically well-posed scenario.
To see why, note that the membrane bending energy gives rise to the

\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}H=a^{{\check{\mu}}{\check{\nu}}}\mkern 1.0muH_{;{\check{\mu}}{\check{\nu}}}

term in the shape equation (
19
).
Since the mean curvature contains two spatial derivatives of the surface
position through the curvature components

b_{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}=\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\bm{x}_{,{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
,
the
\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}H
term contains four derivatives of the surface position.
Following canonical developments in the theory of beam bending

[
97
,
98
]
, we expect to specify two
conditions along the entire boundary: either the out-of-plane velocity or force
per length, as well as either the slope of the surface or the boundary moment

M\,:=\,M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\mkern 1.0mu\nu_{\check{\beta}}~.

(22)

Since the velocity and force boundary conditions are already contained in Eq. (
21
), we need only specify one of the latter pair.
More precisely, we partition the boundary into the disjoint sets
\Gamma_{\!n}

and
\Gamma_{\!M}
, with

\Gamma_{\!n}\cap\Gamma_{\!M}=\varnothing

and

\overline{\mkern 1.0mu\Gamma_{\!n}\cup\Gamma_{\!M}}=\Gamma
,
and prescribe

\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\bm{v}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\,\nu^{{\check{\alpha}}}\,=\,\bar{v}_{\nu}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{\!n}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptM\,=\,\mkern 4.5mu\overline{\mkern-4.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{\!M}~,

(23)

where
\bar{v}_{\nu}
and
\mkern 4.5mu\overline{\mkern-4.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
are prescribed quantities.
With Eqs. (
20
)–(
23
), we have a
mathematically well-posed set of boundary conditions for the governing
equations.

\thetitle
. The weak formulation

The weak formulation of the balance of linear momentum is obtained by first
contracting Eq. (
13
) with an arbitrary velocity variation

\delta\bm{v}
.
At this point, we recognize the four spatial derivatives contained in the shape
equation (
19
) through the
\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}H
term will,
in the subsequent weak form, yield two spatial derivatives of both the velocity
variation and the surface position.
Since
\bm{v}
and
\bm{x}
are assumed to lie in the same space of functions,
both are elements of
\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
—for which higher-order basis
functions, with continuous first derivatives, are required in the numerical
implementation.
In accordance with the boundary conditions in Eqs. (
21
) and (
23
), the space of
admissible material velocity variations
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}
is expressed as

\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}\,:=\,\Big\{\,\bm{u}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{u}\in\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002ptu_{j}\big\rvert_{\Gamma_{\!v}^{\mkern 1.0muj}}\,=\,0,\hskip 10.00002pt\big(\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\bm{u}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\,\nu^{\check{\alpha}}\big)\big\rvert_{\Gamma_{\!n}}=\,0\,\Big\}

(24)

for

j\in\{1,2,3\}
.
We integrate the result of the contraction over the membrane area, and
substitute the general form of the stress vectors
(
14
) to obtain

\int_{\Omega}\delta\bm{v}\bm{\cdot}\Big[\,\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{a}_{\check{\beta}}\,-\,\big(M^{{\check{\beta}}{\check{\alpha}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{n}\big)_{;{\check{\beta}}}\,\Big]_{;\alpha}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ +\,\int_{\Omega}\delta\bm{v}\bm{\cdot}\bm{f}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ =\ 0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall~\delta\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}~.

(25)

Starting with Eq. (
25
), a series of algebraic
manipulations are required to determine the weak form of the linear momentum
balance.
The calculations rely on developments by Naghdi,
A.E. Green
, and

D.J. Steigmann

[
93
,
17
]
, and can
be found in Refs.
[
77
,
95
]
.
Following the notation and development of our prior efforts

[
53
, Ch.V§5(d)]
,
the weak formulation of the balance of linear momentum is expressed as

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}~,

(26)

where
‡
‡

‡

Jump forces can arise at corners of the membrane patch and enter Eq. (
27
).
They are assumed here to be zero.

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}\ &:=\,\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{2}\,\Big(\delta\bm{v}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{\check{\beta}}\,+\,\delta\bm{v}_{,{\check{\beta}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}\Big)\,\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ +\,\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{2}\,\Big(\delta\bm{v}_{;{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\,+\,\delta\bm{v}_{;{\check{\beta}}{\check{\alpha}}}\Big)\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}\,M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\\[5.0pt]
&\hskip 20.0pt-\,\int_{\Omega}\delta\bm{v}\bm{\cdot}\bm{f}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ -\ \sum_{j=1}^{3}\mkern 1.0mu\int_{\Gamma_{\!\!F}^{\mkern 1.0muj}}\delta v_{j}\,\mkern 4.5mu\overline{\mkern-4.5muF\mkern-0.5mu}\mkern 0.5mu_{\!j}\,J_{\Gamma}~{\mathrm{d}}\Gamma\ -\,\int_{\Gamma_{\!M}}\delta\bm{v}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\,\nu^{\check{\alpha}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}\,\mkern 4.5mu\overline{\mkern-4.5muM\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\,J_{\Gamma}~{\mathrm{d}}\Gamma~.\end{split}

(27)

In Eq. (
27
),

\delta v_{j}:=\delta\bm{v}\bm{\cdot}\bm{e}_{j}

is the
j^{\text{th}}
Cartesian component of the arbitrary velocity variation.
It is useful to recognize that Eq. (
27
) is general to any
material whose stress vectors can be expressed as in Eq. (
14
), with the corresponding boundary conditions in
Eqs. (
21
) and (
23
).

\thetitle
. The dynamics of the mesh

In our ALE formulation, the material velocity
\bm{v}
and the mesh velocity

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}
are independent quantities.
The evolution of the position
\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)
of the membrane surface
is dictated by the mesh velocity via the relations

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,=\,\dfrac{\partial\mkern 1.0mu{}}{\partial\mkern 1.0mu{t}}\Big(\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\Big)

(28)

and

\displaystyle\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},\mkern 1.0mut)\,=\,\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},\mkern 1.0mu0)\,+\,\int_{0}^{t}\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},\mkern 1.0mut^{\prime})~{\mathrm{d}}t^{\prime}~,

(29)

which both apply over all parametric coordinates

\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}\mkern-1.0mu\in\Omega

and for all times

t\in[\mkern 1.0mu0,t_{\mathrm{f}}\mkern 1.0mu]

under consideration (see Ref.
[
4
, §2]
for additional
details).
As we previously recognized

\bm{x}\in\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
,
Eqs. (
28
) and (
29
) reveal

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega)

as well.
In what follows, we discuss three mesh velocity schemes of increasing
complexity, all of which are consistent with the kinematic constraint
(
6
) such that the mesh and material
always overlap.
We begin with Lagrangian and Eulerian schemes, which were previously employed
by both ourselves and others, and are known to suffer from limitations

[
75
,
76
,
77
,
78
,
79
,
4
]
.
We then discuss a new class of ALE schemes in which the mesh velocity itself
satisfies dynamical equations similar to those that govern the material
velocity.

\thetitle
. The Lagrangian mesh motion

We begin with the simplest membrane motion, namely a Lagrangian scheme.
In this case, the mesh velocity is prescribed to be equal to the material
velocity:

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\mkern 1.0mu=\,\bm{v}~.

(30)

In our numerical implementation, mesh velocity degrees of freedom are mapped to
their material velocity counterparts such that Eq. (
30
)
is satisfied identically over the entire surface.
§
§

§

One could instead treat the membrane and mesh velocities separately, and
directly modify the resultant tangent diffusion matrix to satisfy Eq. (
30
).
Details of such an approach are provided in Appendix B.7 of Ref.
[
4
]
.

The direct Galerkin expression in the case of a Lagrangian mesh motion is then
given by

\mathcal{G}^{\texttt{L}}\,:=\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}\ =\ \mkern 1.0mu0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)~,~~\delta\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}~.

(31)

In Eq. (
31
), the superscript ‘
L
’ denotes a
Lagrangian scheme, where

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}=\bm{v}

throughout and mesh positions are updated according to Eq. (
29
).
In addition,
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}
are respectively provided in Eqs. (
12
) and (
27
).

\thetitle
. The Eulerian mesh motion

The second scheme we implement is in-plane Eulerian, with

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}=0
,
and out-of-plane Lagrangian, for which

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}=\bm{v}\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}
.
The in-plane and out-of-plane conditions are expressed as the single equation

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,=\,\big(\bm{n}\otimes\bm{n}\big)\mkern 1.0mu\bm{v}~,

(32)

where ‘
\otimes
’ denotes the dyadic product.
As there are no spatial derivatives in Eq. (
32
),
no mesh velocity boundary conditions are to be prescribed for an Eulerian mesh
motion.

The weak form of Eq. (
32
) is obtained by
contracting it with an arbitrary mesh velocity variation

\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
,
integrating over the membrane surface, and multiplying the result by a user-specified constant
\alpha_{{\mathrm{m}}}^{\texttt{E}}
.
The weak formulation of the Eulerian mesh velocity equation is then given by

[
4
]

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{E}}\ :=\ \alpha_{{\mathrm{m}}}^{\texttt{E}}\int_{\Omega}\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\cdot}\Big[\,\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,-\,\big(\bm{n}\otimes\bm{n}\big)\mkern 1.0mu\bm{v}\,\Big]\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ =\ 0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega)~.

(33)

In Eq. (
33
), the superscript ‘
E
’ signifies
the scheme is Eulerian, and the constant
\alpha_{{\mathrm{m}}}^{\texttt{E}}
has dimensions of
energy
\,\cdot\,
time
\mkern 1.0mu/\mkern 1.0mu
length
4
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{E}}
has the
same dimensions as
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}
.
In this study,

\alpha_{{\mathrm{m}}}^{\texttt{E}}=\zeta/\ell^{2}

for all results presented, as varying
\alpha_{{\mathrm{m}}}^{\texttt{E}}
was not observed to affect
membrane behavior (
\ell
is a chosen characteristic length).
The direct Galerkin expression for a scenario with an Eulerian mesh motion is
expressed as

\mathcal{G}^{\texttt{E}}\,:=\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{E}}\ =\ \mkern 1.0mu0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)~,~~\delta\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}~,~~\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega)~.

(34)

\thetitle
. The arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian mesh motion

The final scheme we consider is neither in-plane Lagrangian nor in-plane
Eulerian.
Instead, the mesh is modeled as a separate 2D material with its own constitutive
relations and associated dynamical equations.
The mesh analogs of the stress vectors are then expressed as [cf. Eq. (
14
)]

\bm{T}^{\check{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{m}}\,=\,\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{a}_{\check{\beta}}\,-\,\big(M^{{\check{\beta}}{\check{\alpha}}}_{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{n}\big)_{;{\check{\beta}}}~,

(35)

where
\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}
are the mesh analogs of
\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
and

M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}
.
Here
\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}
can be specified arbitrarily without altering
the dynamics of the membrane, and so the mesh dynamics can be that of an
elastic, viscous, or viscoelastic material.
When the mesh velocity
\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}
is determined by solving an arbitrary set of
governing equations, it will no longer satisfy the kinematic constraint
(
6
) across the membrane surface.
We enforce Eq. (
6
) with an additional
Lagrange multiplier field, which physically acts on the mesh as an external body
force per unit area in the normal direction—hereafter referred to as the mesh
pressure
p^{{\mathrm{m}}}
.
Following the developments of
§2.3
, the dynamical equation
governing the mesh dynamics is given by

\bm{T}^{\check{\alpha}}_{{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0mu;\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu{\check{\alpha}}}\,+\,p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{n}\,=\,\bm{0}~.

(36)

Upon specification of appropriate boundary conditions, Eq. (
6
) and the three components of Eq. (
36
) uniquely determine the mesh pressure
p^{{\mathrm{m}}}

and the three components of the mesh velocity
\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}
.
The ability to specify mesh boundary conditions can prevent undesirable mesh
distortions, and is an advantage of our ALE method.
Two choices of the mesh motion, for which
\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}
are
prescribed, are discussed subsequently.
In the first case, the mesh is area-compressible and purely viscous; in the
second case it also resists bending.
In both cases, the simplest boundary conditions for the mesh velocity are
chosen.
The investigation of more complex boundary conditions, as well as more involved
mesh behaviors, is left to a future study.

\thetitle
. The weak formulation of the kinematic constraint

The mesh pressure is an unknown Lagrange multiplier field enforcing the ALE
kinematic constraint in Eq. (
6
).
The weak form of Eq. (
6
) is obtained by
multiplying it with an arbitrary variation

\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
and integrating the result over the membrane surface to obtain

\int_{\Omega}\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\big(\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}-\,\bm{v}\big)\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)~.

(37)

Equation (
37
) bears some resemblance to Eq. (
8
), which was obtained from the incompressibility
constraint.
In both equations, the variation of a scalar Lagrange multiplier interacts with
a vector velocity.
Moreover, when Eq. (
37
) is discretized, our
numerical simulations exhibit an instability reminiscent of the checkerboarding
that arises when the LBB condition is violated.
We thus hypothesize that both numerical instabilities are similar, and attempt
to stabilize the mesh pressure with the Dohrmann–Bochev method.
Following the results of
§2.2.1
, we express the weak formulation
of the kinematic constraint (
6
) as

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{p}}\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)~,

(38)

where the numerically stabilized mesh pressure contribution to the direct
Galerkin expression is given by [cf. Eqs. (
12
),
(
37
)]

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{p}}\ :=\ -\int_{\Omega}\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\big(\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}-\,\bm{v}\big)\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ \,-\,\ \dfrac{\alpha^{\text{DB}}\ell^{2}}{\zeta}\int_{\Omega}\big(\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,-\,\delta\breve{p}^{\mkern 1.0mu{\mathrm{m}}}\big)\big(p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,-\,\breve{p}^{\mkern 1.0mu{\mathrm{m}}}\big)~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega~.

(39)

In Eq. (
39
),
\breve{p}^{\mkern 1.0mu{\mathrm{m}}}
and
\delta\breve{p}^{\mkern 1.0mu{\mathrm{m}}}
are the

L^{2}
-projections of
p^{{\mathrm{m}}}
and
\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}
onto the space
\mkern 1.5mu\breve{\mkern-1.5muL}
, as
defined through Eq. (
9
).
The factor of
\ell^{2}
, for a characteristic length
\ell
, is required for
dimensional consistency.
Our choice to employ the Dohrmann–Bochev method to stabilize

p^{{\mathrm{m}}}
does not yet sit on a firm theoretical footing, and currently can only
be justified with empirical success.
We hope to investigate the mathematical nature of the stabilization of
p^{{\mathrm{m}}}

in a future manuscript.

\thetitle
. The case where the mesh dynamics are purely viscous

For the case of purely viscous mesh dynamics, quantities are denoted with a
superscript or subscript ‘
v
.’
The scheme is referred to as ‘ALE-viscous,’ with the shorthand ‘
Av
’ or
‘
ALE-v
.’
Since the mesh velocity field is area-compressible, the mesh motion resists both
shearing and dilation.

Strong formulation.—
We prescribe for the mesh analog of the in-plane stresses and couple-stresses to
be [cf. Eqs. (
15
)–(
17
)]

\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{v}}\mkern 1.0mu=\,\pi^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0mu:=\,\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\,\,,\mkern 1.0mu{\check{\mu}}}\bm{\cdot}\Big(\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\mu}}{\check{\beta}}}\,+\,\bm{a}^{\check{\beta}}\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\mu}}{\check{\alpha}}}\Big)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptM^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{v}}\mkern 1.0mu=\,0~,

(40)

where
\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}
is a user-specified parameter that we refer to as the mesh
viscosity.
Importantly, since the mesh velocity is area-compressible, our choice in Eq. (
40
) resists both shear and dilation of the mesh.
By substituting Eqs. (
35
) and
(
40
) into Eq. (
36
), the
dynamical equations governing the mesh are found to be

[
53
, Ch.V§5(c)]

\displaystyle\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,\Big(\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}v^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{{\check{\alpha}}}\,+\,K\mkern 1.0muv^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\check{\alpha}}\,+\,2\mkern 1.0mu(v^{{\mathrm{m}}})_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\mkern 1.0muH\,-\,2\mkern 1.0mu(v^{{\mathrm{m}}})_{,{\check{\beta}}}\,b^{\check{\beta}}_{\check{\alpha}}\,-\,2\mkern 1.0muv^{{\mathrm{m}}}H_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\,+\,\big(a^{{\mkern 3.0mu\check{\mkern-3.0mu\gamma}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu(v^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\mkern 3.0mu\check{\mkern-3.0mu\gamma}})_{;{\check{\beta}}}\,-\,2\mkern 1.0muv^{{\mathrm{m}}}H\big)_{\!;{\check{\alpha}}}\Big)\ =\ 0

(41)

and

\displaystyle p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,+\,\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,\Big(2\mkern 1.0mub^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu(v^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{{\check{\alpha}}})_{;{\check{\beta}}}\,-\,8\mkern 1.0muv^{{\mathrm{m}}}H^{2}\,+\,4\mkern 1.0muv^{{\mathrm{m}}}K\Big)\ =\ 0~,

(42)

where

\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}=v^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{{\check{\alpha}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}+v^{{\mathrm{m}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{n}
.
The solution to Eq. (
41
) is
independent of
\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}
, and the mesh pressure in Eq. (
42
) will adjust so as to satisfy the
kinematic constraint (
6
).
The mesh dynamics accordingly do not depend on the choice of
\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}
.
In all numerical results presented, we choose

\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}=\zeta

for simplicity.

Boundary conditions.—
Since the mesh velocity satisfies differential equations [see Eqs. (
41
) and
(
42
)]
rather than an algebraic equation [cf. Eqs. (
30
),(
32
)], boundary conditions for the mesh velocity
are required.
To this end, we introduce the mesh analog of the force per length on the
boundary:

\bm{F}^{\mathrm{m}}_{\texttt{v}}\,=\,\bm{T}^{\check{\alpha}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{v}}\,\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\,=\,\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{v}}\,\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{a}_{\check{\beta}}~.

(43)

Recalling
M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{v}}=0
according to Eq. (
40
), the
purely viscous mesh dynamics cannot sustain moments—as is the case for a fluid
film
[
4
]
.
Consequently, neither slope nor moment boundary conditions are required on the
boundary, since the mesh analog of the moment

M^{\mathrm{m}}=0

identically throughout.
With this understanding, the parametric boundary
\Gamma
is partitioned into
the portions
\Gamma_{\!v}^{{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0muj}
and
\Gamma_{\!\!F}^{{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0muj}
, on which

v^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\mkern-1.0muj}:=\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{e}_{j}

and

F^{\mathrm{m}}_{\!j}:=\bm{F}^{\mathrm{m}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{e}_{j}

are respectively specified.
For simplicity in our numerical formulation, the mesh is assumed to be either
static or force-free on its entire boundary, for which [cf. Eq. (
21
)]

v^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\mkern-1.0muj}\,=\,0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{\!v}^{{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0muj}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptF^{\mathrm{m}}_{\!j}\,=\,0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{\!\!F}^{{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0muj}~.

(44)

Weak formulation.—
The weak formulation of the purely viscous ALE mesh behavior is obtained by
drawing analogy to the developments in
§2.3.2
.
First, the space of arbitrary mesh velocity variations is defined as

\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0,\texttt{v}}\,:=\,\Big\{\,\bm{u}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{u}\in\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002ptu_{j}\big\rvert_{\Gamma_{\!v}^{{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0muj}}\,=\,0\,\Big\}~.

(45)

By contracting Eq. (
36
) with an arbitrary mesh
velocity variation

\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0,\texttt{v}}

and repeating the steps of
§2.3.2
, the weak formulation of the
ALE-viscous mesh motion is found to be [cf. Eqs. (
26
) and
(
27
)]

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{Av}}\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0,\texttt{v}}~,

(46)

where

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{Av}}\ :=\,\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{2}\,\Big(\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{\check{\beta}}\,+\,\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{,{\check{\beta}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}\Big)\,\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{v}}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\,\ -\,\int_{\Omega}\Big(\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\cdot}p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{n}\Big)\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega~.

(47)

In Eqs. (
46
) and (
47
), the
superscript ‘
Av
’ refers to the choice of a purely viscous ALE mesh
motion.
The direct Galerkin expression for this motion is then written as

\begin{split}&\mathcal{G}^{\texttt{Av}}\,:=\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{Av}}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{p}}\ =\ \mkern 1.0mu0\\[5.0pt]
&\quad\forall\quad\delta\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)~,~~\delta\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}~,~~\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0,\texttt{v}}~,~~\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)~.\end{split}

(48)

\thetitle
. The case where the mesh dynamics are viscous and resist bending

We now consider the case where the mesh dynamics are viscous in response to
in-plane shears and area dilations, and additionally resist bending.
The mesh motion is referred to as ‘ALE-viscous-bending,’ with shorthand
‘
Avb
’ or ‘
ALE-vb
.’
Corresponding quantities are denoted with a subscript or superscript
‘
vb
.’

Strong formulation.—
We choose for the bending resistance of the mesh to arise in the same manner
as the membrane itself, with the viscous resistance identical to that of the
ALE-viscous scenario.
To this end, we choose [cf. Eqs. (
15
)–(
17
) and (
40
)]

\displaystyle M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{vb}}\,=\,k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{b}}\mkern 1.0muH\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu+\,k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{g}}\mkern 1.0mu\Big(2\mkern 1.0muH\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu-\mkern 1.0mub^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\Big)

(49)

and

\displaystyle\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{vb}}\,=\,k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{b}}\mkern 1.0mu\Big(H^{2}\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu-\mkern 1.0mu2\mkern 1.0muH\,b^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\Big)\,-\,k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{g}}\mkern 1.0muK\mkern 1.0mua^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0mu+\,\pi^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}~,

(50)

where
\pi^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{m}}
is defined in Eq. (
40
).
In Eqs. (
49
) and (
50
),
k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{b}}

and
k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{g}}
are user-specified parameters; we always choose

k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{b}}=k_{\mathrm{b}}

and

k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{g}}=k_{\mathrm{g}}

for simplicity.
It is well-known that bending terms from the Helfrich free energy do not enter
the in-plane equations
[
51
]
, and so the in-plane dynamical
mesh equations in the
ALE-vb
case are given by Eq. (
41
).
The out-of-plane dynamical equation is expressed as [cf. Eqs. (
19
) and (
42
)]

p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,+\,\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}\,\Big(2\mkern 1.0mub^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\mkern 1.0muv_{{\check{\alpha}};{\check{\beta}}}\,-\,8\mkern 1.0muv\mkern 1.0muH^{2}\,+\,4\mkern 1.0muv\mkern 1.0muK\Big)\,-\,k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{b}}\Big(2\mkern 1.0muH^{3}\mkern 1.0mu-\mkern 1.0mu2\mkern 1.0muH\mkern 1.0muK\,+\,\Delta_{\mathrm{s}}H\Big)\ =\ 0~.

(51)

Contrary to the
ALE-v
scenario (
42
),
here the relative magnitude of
\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}
and
k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{b}}
do affect the resultant
mesh pressure
p^{{\mathrm{m}}}
.
Investigating the relationship between
p^{{\mathrm{m}}}
,
\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}
, and
k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{b}}
—as well as the
ensuing mesh dynamics—is left to a later study.

Boundary conditions.—
We once again introduce the mesh analog of the force per length on the boundary,
which in this case is written as [cf. Eqs. (
20
) and
(
43
)]

\bm{F}^{\mathrm{m}}_{\texttt{vb}}\,=\,\bm{T}^{\check{\alpha}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{vb}}\,\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\,-\,\big(M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{vb}}\,\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\mkern 1.0mu\tau_{\check{\beta}}\,\bm{n}\big)_{\!,{\check{\mu}}}\,\tau^{\check{\mu}}~.

(52)

In principle, one could specify boundary forces for the mesh dynamics.
However, as in the ALE-viscous case, we choose simple boundary conditions where
the mesh is either static or force-free on the entire boundary
(
44
).
In addition, since the mesh couple-stresses are nonzero, either slope or moment
boundary conditions are required for
\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}
—where the analog of the
boundary moment is calculated as

M^{{\mathrm{m}}}=M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\mu}}}_{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0mu\nu_{\check{\alpha}}\mkern 1.0mu\nu_{\check{\mu}}

[cf. Eq. (
22
)].
At present, we take the simpler approach and specify zero-slope conditions on
the entire boundary [cf. Eq. (
23
)]:

\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\,\nu^{{\check{\alpha}}}\,=\,0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma~.

(53)

The ability to specify the slope of the mesh at a boundary will be relevant in
the tether pulling scenario of
§4.2
.

Weak formulation.—
At this point, it is straightforward to write the weak formulation of the mesh
dynamics.
The space of arbitrary mesh variations is given by

\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0,\texttt{vb}}\,:=\,\Big\{\,\bm{u}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{u}\in\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002ptu_{j}\big\rvert_{\Gamma_{\!v}^{{\mathrm{m}}\mkern 1.0muj}}\,=\,0,\hskip 10.00002pt\big(\bm{n}\bm{\cdot}\bm{u}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\,\nu^{\check{\alpha}}\big)\big\rvert_{\Gamma}=\,0\,\Big\}~.

(54)

The weak formulation is then expressed as

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{Avb}}\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\hskip 10.00002pt\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0,\texttt{vb}}~,

(55)

where we define [cf. Eq. (
27
)]

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{Avb}}\ :=&\ \int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{2}\,\Big(\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{\check{\beta}}\,+\,\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{,{\check{\beta}}}\bm{\cdot}\bm{a}_{\check{\alpha}}\Big)\,\sigma^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{vb}}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\\[5.0pt]
&\hskip 10.0pt+\,\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{2}\,\Big(\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{;{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}\,+\,\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{;{\check{\beta}}{\check{\alpha}}}\Big)\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}\ M^{{\check{\alpha}}{\check{\beta}}}_{{\mathrm{m}},\mkern 1.0mu\texttt{vb}}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ -\,\int_{\Omega}\Big(\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\cdot}p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{n}\Big)\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega~.\end{split}

(56)

The direct Galerkin expression for the scenario with the
ALE-vb
mesh motion is
written as

\begin{split}&\mathcal{G}^{\texttt{Avb}}\,:=\,\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\texttt{Avb}}\,+\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{p}}\ =\ \mkern 1.0mu0\\[5.0pt]
&\quad\forall\quad\delta\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)~,~~\delta\bm{v}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}~,~~\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0,\texttt{vb}}~,~~\delta p^{{\mathrm{m}}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)~.\end{split}

(57)

\thetitle
. The finite element formulation

With the Lagrangian, Eulerian, and ALE weak formulations, our goal is to solve
for the state of the membrane over time.
We seek to determine (
i
) the membrane velocity
\bm{v}
, (
i\mkern-1.0mui
) the mesh
velocity
\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}
, and (
i\mkern-1.0mui\mkern-1.0mui
) the membrane tension
\lambda
—for all parametric points

\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}\in\Omega

and times

t\in[\mkern 1.0mu0,t_{\mathrm{f}}\mkern 1.0mu]
.
In doing so, the surface position is obtained from the mesh velocity through
Eq. (
29
).
We cannot solve for the highly nonlinear membrane behavior exactly, and turn to
the finite element method to numerically calculate the approximate solutions

\bm{v}_{h}
,
\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}
, and
\lambda_{h}
.
An overview of our solution method is presented in what follows.
Further specifics can be found in Appendix
A
and in the

MembraneAleFem.jl
documentation
[
1
]
.

\thetitle
. The surface discretization and space of solutions

Let us begin by assuming there is a discretization
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of the parametric
domain
\Omega
into
nel
(
n
umber of
el
ements)
non-overlapping finite elements
\Omega^{e}
of characteristic length
h
:

\mathcal{T}_{h}\,:=\,\big\{\Omega^{1},\,\Omega^{2},\,\ldots,\,\Omega^{\texttt{nel}}\big\}~.

(58)

Our task is to choose the set of basis functions over each element

\Omega^{e}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
—denoted
\{N^{e}_{k}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\}
, where the index
k
ranges from
1

to
nen
(
n
umber of
e
lemental
n
odes)—such that the resultant solution spaces are consistent with their infinite-dimensional counterparts.
A complexity arises because mesh and membrane velocities belong to the space

\bm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
.
Accordingly,
\bm{v}_{h}
and
\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}
are required to be
continuous and have continuous first derivatives across elements.
We satisfy the continuity criteria with a so-called tensor product of quadratic
B-spline basis functions in each of the
\zeta^{\check{1}}
and
\zeta^{\check{2}}

directions
[
99
,
100
]
.
For our purposes, the parametric domain
\Omega
is partitioned into a
rectangular grid of finite elements, though more advanced discretizations
are now established
[
101
,
102
,
103
,
104
]
.
The resultant scalar and vector finite-dimensional spaces
\mathcal{U}_{h}
and

\bm{\mathcal{U}}_{\!h}
are respectively given by

\mathcal{U}_{h}\,:=\,\Big\{\,u(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\hskip 10.00002ptu\in C^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002ptu\big\rvert_{\Omega^{e}}\in\mathbb{Q}_{2}(\Omega^{e})~~\forall~\Omega^{e}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\,\Big\}

(59)

and

\bm{\mathcal{U}}_{\!h}\,:=\,\Big\{\,\bm{u}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\hskip 10.00002ptu_{j}\in\,\mathcal{U}_{h}\,\Big\}~,

(60)

for which

\bm{v}_{h}\in\,\bm{\mathcal{U}}_{\!h}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}\in\,\bm{\mathcal{U}}_{\!h}

(61)

by construction.
In Eq. (
59
),
C^{m}(\Omega)
denotes the space of scalar
functions on
\Omega
with
m
continuous derivatives, and

\mathbb{Q}_{n}(\Omega^{e})
denotes the tensor-product space of

n^{\text{th}}
-order polynomials in the
\zeta^{\check{1}}
and
\zeta^{\check{2}}

directions on the finite element
\Omega^{e}
.
The arbitrary variations
\delta\bm{v}_{h}
and
\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}
are also assumed
to lie in
\bm{\mathcal{U}}_{\!h}
, and are respectively elements of the spaces

\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0,h}\,:=\,\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\!0}\cap\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\bm{\mathcal{U}}_{\!h}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0,h}\,:=\,\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{\!0}\cap\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\bm{\mathcal{U}}_{\!h}~.

(62)

\thetitle
. The membrane tension, mesh pressure, and Dohrmann–Bochev
projection

In general, the Lagrange multipliers
\lambda
and
p^{{\mathrm{m}}}
are elements of

L^{2}(\Omega)
, and their finite-dimensional counterparts
\lambda_{h}
and

p^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}
need not be restricted to lie in
\mathcal{U}_{h}
.
However, for convenience in the numerical implementation, we do in fact choose

\lambda_{h}\in\,\mathcal{U}_{h}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptp^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}\in\,\mathcal{U}_{h}~.

(63)

Since the same basis functions are used for the velocity and surface tension,
the well-known inf–sup condition is violated and the LBB instability arises

[
82
,
83
,
84
]
.
We apply the Dohrmann–Bochev method
[
85
]
to stabilize
unphysical oscillations in both the surface tension and mesh pressure.
To do so,
\lambda_{h}
and
p^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}
are projected onto the space

\mkern 1.5mu\breve{\mkern-1.5muL}\,:=\,\Big\{\,u(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{such that}\hskip 10.00002ptu\big\rvert_{\Omega^{e}}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(\Omega^{e})~~\forall~\Omega^{e}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\,\Big\}~,

(64)

where
\mathbb{P}_{n}(\Omega^{e})
denotes the space of polynomials of order

n
on
\Omega^{e}
.
Since functions in
\mkern 1.5mu\breve{\mkern-1.5muL}
form a plane over each rectangular element

\Omega^{e}
, they are discontinuous across finite elements.

\thetitle
. The method of numerical solution

An overview of our numerical implementation is presented below.
Additional details are provided in Appendix
A
and the
MembraneAleFem.jl
package repository
[
1
]
.

Once the parametric domain is discretized into finite elements, all membrane
unknowns are expressed as the sum of B-spline basis functions multiplied by
membrane degrees of freedom.
The degrees of freedom are collected into a column vector and are generically
denoted
[\mathbf{u}]
; they are also referred to as nodal values and their dependence
on time is implied.
Variations of membrane unknowns are similarly decomposed, with
degree-of-freedom variations compatible with all boundary conditions collected
into the column vector
[\delta\mathbf{u}]
.
Since the direct Galerkin expression
\mathcal{G}
is linear in the arbitrary
variations, its discretized counterpart
\mathcal{G}_{h}
is expressed as

\mathcal{G}_{h}\big([\delta\mathbf{u}],[\mathbf{u}]\big)\,=\,[\delta\mathbf{u}]^{\mathrm{T}}\big[\mathbf{r}([\mathbf{u}])\big]\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall~[\delta\mathbf{u}]~.

(65)

In Eq. (
65
), the residual vector
[\mathbf{r}]
is defined to be

\big[\mathbf{r}([\mathbf{u}])\big]\,:=\,\dfrac{\partial\mkern 1.0mu{\mathcal{G}_{h}\big([\delta\mathbf{u}],[\mathbf{u}]\big)}}{\partial\mkern 1.0mu{[\delta\mathbf{u}]}}~.

(66)

Since the variations
[\delta\mathbf{u}]
in Eq. (
66
) are
arbitrary, the membrane dynamics at any time

t\in[\mkern 1.0mu0,t_{\mathrm{f}}\mkern 1.0mu]

satisfy the nonlinear equation

\big[\mathbf{r}([\mathbf{u}])\big]\,=\,[\bm{0}]~.

(67)

Equation (
67
) is solved with the Newton–Raphson method,
where a sequence of progressively better estimates of the membrane state

[\mathbf{u}]
is generated.
Given the
j^{\text{th}}
estimate
[\mathbf{u}]_{j}
, the
(j+1)^{\text{th}}

estimate is calculated according to

[\mathbf{u}]_{j+1}\,=\,[\mathbf{u}]_{j}\,-\,[\mathbf{K}]^{-1}_{j}\big[\mathbf{r}([\mathbf{u}]_{j})\big]~,

(68)

where the global tangent diffusion matrix at the
j^{\text{th}}
iteration is
defined as

[\mathbf{K}]_{j}\,:=\,\dfrac{\partial\mkern 1.0mu{\big[\mathbf{r}([\mathbf{u}])\big]}}{\partial\mkern 1.0mu{[\mathbf{u}]}}\bigg\rvert_{[\mathbf{u}]_{j}}~.

(69)

In prior studies
[
77
,
4
]
, the tangent diffusion
matrix was calculated analytically: an involved task.
In the present work, however,
[\mathbf{K}]
is calculated by numerically
differentiating
[\mathbf{r}]
with respect to each entry of
[\mathbf{u}]
.
In particular, the tangent matrix (
69
) is calculated to
machine precision by extending
[\mathbf{r}]
and
[\mathbf{u}]
into the complex plane,
following the general developments of
J.N. Lyness
and

C.B. Moler

[
105
,
106
]
(see also e.g. Ref.
[
107
]
).
We note that when calculating derivatives with respect to mesh velocity degrees
of freedom, one must also perturb the surface positions, as the two are related
through Eqs. (
28
) and (
29
).

In the scenario under consideration, there are five membrane parameters: the

Since the five quantities dimensionally involve only mass, length, and time,
two dimensionless numbers once again determine the evolution of the system.
The Föppl–von Kármán number
\mathit{\Gamma}
is given by
[
71
]

\mathit{\Gamma}\,:=\,\dfrac{\lambda_{0}\,\ell^{2}}{k_{\mathrm{b}}}~,

(81)

and can be interpreted in two ways given the morphological changes that occur
when pulling a tether.
First, when the membrane is nearly planar and height deflections are small,
gradients in membrane shape occur over the length scale
\ell
;
\mathit{\Gamma}

then quantifies the relative magnitude of tensile and bending forces.
In our simulations,
\mathit{\Gamma}^{-1}
is small, and consequently a boundary
layer of characteristic width

\mathchoice{{\hbox{$\displaystyle\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}/\lambda_{0}\,}$}\lower 0.4pt\hbox{\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=-6.00003pt}}}{{\hbox{$\textstyle\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}/\lambda_{0}\,}$}\lower 0.4pt\hbox{\vrule height=7.5pt,depth=-6.00003pt}}}{{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}/\lambda_{0}\,}$}\lower 0.4pt\hbox{\vrule height=5.25pt,depth=-4.20003pt}}}{{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}/\lambda_{0}\,}$}\lower 0.4pt\hbox{\vrule height=3.75pt,depth=-3.00002pt}}}\ll\ell

develops at the point of application of the pull force
[
28
]
.
As the membrane is pulled further, a tether grows from the boundary layer
region.
The tether is close in shape to a cylinder, and the radius of a cylindrical
membrane is well-known to be

[
16
]

r_{\!{\mathrm{c}}}\,:=\,\sqrt{\dfrac{k_{\mathrm{b}}}{4\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{0}}\,}~.

(82)

Equation (

) can be obtained from energetic
arguments alone, or equivalently from a balance of bending and tensile forces.

∥

(83)

Equations (

the Föppl–von Kármán number describes membrane energetics, as only lengths and the

k_{\mathrm{b}}
and

The second dimensionless quantity is the Scriven–Love number
S

, which
compares the magnitude of viscous and bending forces in shaping the membrane

[

]

In this scenario, we define the Scriven–Love number as

\mbox{{S\hskip-0.71562pt{L}}}\,:=\,\dfrac{r_{\!{\mathrm{c}}}\,\zeta\mkern 1.0muv_{\mathrm{p}}}{k_{\mathrm{b}}}~.

(84)

We chose to include

(85)

i.e. a ratio of the speed at which the tether is pulled to the natural velocity
scale of lipid flows in the tube
[
71
]
.
Thus, as

,

\mathit{\Gamma}
is altered by varying the patch size
\ell

while the Scriven–Love number
S
L

The majority of our findings from Lagrangian tether-pulling simulations, as well

T.R. Powers

More recently, rate effects were included in axisymmetric simulations that
focused primarily on the slip between two monolayer leaflets

[
126

General non-axisymmetric Lagrangian formulations were developed since then.
The implementation in Ref.
[

included rate effects when

transition from a tent to a tube in simulations.

[

pulled a tether from the center of

reported tether-pulling results employed a numerical stabilization scheme that
did not capture the coupling between in-plane lipid flows and out-of-plane
forces.
Thus, to the best of our knowledge, we present the first non-axisymmetric
Lagrangian simulation capturing the dynamics of a tether pulled from a membrane
patch—though we do not consider the results to be novel, as Refs.
[
75
]
and
[

]
may have been able to do
the same.

We believe an angular shape arises in the

incompatible boundary conditions for the material and mesh.
More specifically, since all nodes over a single element are translated upwards,

The observed increase in pull force with increasing Scriven–Love number can be

During tether pulling, lipids in the surrounding region flow inwards towards the

A mass balance, with the assumption of axisymmetry, indicates that the flow

Since the in-plane velocity grows as we approach the tether from its
surroundings, a surface tension gradient is required to sustain the flow of
lipids.
As the tether is pulled more quickly, larger velocities and thus larger tension
gradients ensue.
A greater surface tension in the tether results, and leads to the larger pull
force observed in simulations.

\thetitle
. The lateral translation of a membrane tether

Once a tube is pulled from a patch of membrane, a lateral force applied at the
end of the tether causes it to translate relative to the surrounding region.
Lipids quickly flow and readjust to accommodate the translating tether due to
the in-plane fluidity of the membrane.
While tether translation via a lateral force is observed in
in vitro

studies
[
127
,
128
,
129
]
, the physics of tether
translation remains poorly understood.
A theoretical description of tether translation is difficult because the system
is no longer axisymmetric, and the greatly simplified axisymmetric equations

[
28
,
125
,
31
,
33
,
78
]
no longer apply.
Tether translation also cannot be captured with general, non-axisymmetric
Lagrangian numerical methods—as laterally translating the tether induces a
rigid body translation of the entire patch (see Appendix

E
).
ALE finite element methods are thus required to simulate tether translation.

In Fig.
9
, we present snapshots from a simulation of
tether translation, which employed the
ALE-vb
mesh motion.
Starting with the

t=240\,\zeta\mkern 1.0mur_{\!{\mathrm{c}}}^{\,2}/k_{\mathrm{b}}

configuration shown in Figs.
4
and

5
, we prescribe a lateral velocity in the
x
-direction
given by

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\ =\,\begin{cases}\,+\mkern 1.0muv_{\mathrm{p}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{e}_{x}&~\text{ for }~240<t\cdot k_{\mathrm{b}}/(\zeta\mkern 1.0mur_{\!{\mathrm{c}}}^{\,2})<300\\[4.0pt]
\,-\mkern 1.0muv_{\mathrm{p}}\mkern 1.0mu\bm{e}_{x}&~\text{ for }~300<t\cdot k_{\mathrm{b}}/(\zeta\mkern 1.0mur_{\!{\mathrm{c}}}^{\,2})<420~,\end{cases}

(91)

where in both cases

v_{\mathrm{p}}=0.1\mkern 1.0muk_{\mathrm{b}}/(\zeta\mkern 1.0mur_{\!{\mathrm{c}}})

to be consistent with our choice

\mbox{{S\hskip-0.71562pt{L}}}=0.1
.
In this simulation,

\mathit{\Gamma}=1024

is unchanged.
We observe that the surface tension does not appreciably change, as the tether
is translated slowly relative to the fundamental timescale of lipid
rearrangements.
Our results demonstrate that ALE methods can be used in scenarios where
Lagrangian methods fail, and set the stage for future investigations of the
forces, geometry, and dynamics of tether translation.
A video of the laterally-pulled tether simulation is provided in the software
repository
[
1
]
.

\thetitle
. Conclusions and future work

In the present work, we (
i
) developed a robust ALE numerical method for lipid
membranes and (
i\mkern-1.0mui
) applied the method to a scenario where established
Lagrangian and Eulerian schemes fail.
In our development, the mesh is treated as a material that is independent from
the membrane—with the mesh equations of motion and corresponding boundary
conditions arbitrarily prescribed by the user.
Mesh and material surfaces are constrained to overlap with a Lagrange multiplier
field, which enters the mesh dynamics as a force per area in the normal
direction.
By choosing for the mesh to resist shear and dilation (through the mesh
viscosity
\zeta^{{\mathrm{m}}}
) and bending (through the mesh bending moduli
k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{b}}
and

k^{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{g}}
), we successfully pulled a tether from a flat patch and then translated
it laterally across the membrane surface.
In contrast, Lagrangian and Eulerian simulations are respectively unable to
translate and pull a tether.
Our results thus mark the first numerical demonstration of lateral tether
translation.
We also analyzed the dynamics of tether pulling by determining how the pull
force increases monotonically with increasing pull speed.
Our findings were presented in terms of the Föppl–von Kármán number
\mathit{\Gamma}
and Scriven–Love number
S
L
,
which define the tether-pulling scenario.

We close by highlighting that our numerical implementation
[
1
]

is structured such that one can easily choose different constitutive relations
and boundary conditions for the mesh motion.
In this manner, the mesh itself can resist shear, dilation, or their rates of
change—irrespective of the material behavior.
Only purely viscous and viscous-with-bending mesh motions were considered in the
present work;
we aim to investigate different choices of mesh motion in a future study.
As a consequence of the modular structure of the numerical implementation, it is
straightforward to adapt the code to simulate 2D materials with different
constitution.
We hope to support the open-source community in doing so.
We also intend to extend our method to describe additional phenomena governing
biological membranes—including the coupling between lipid flows and the
hydrodynamics of the surrounding fluid
[
36
,
57
,
43
]
, membrane permeability
[
123
]
, the effects
of embedded particles
[
32
,
37
,
39
]
,
and in-plane diffusion and phase transitions in multi-component membrane systems

[
130
,
44
,
46
,
48
]
.

Appendix A
The finite element implementation

The main novelty of the present work is the treatment of the mesh as an
independent material with its own constitution, as discussed in

§2.7
.
However, none of the finite element techniques used here are new.
All fundamental unknowns, their arbitrary variations, and the surface position
itself are discretized with the same basis functions—as is standard in
isoparametric finite element methods.
Over any element

\Omega^{e}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
we have

\bm{x}_{h}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,=\,\sum_{k=1}^{\texttt{nen}}N^{e}_{\mkern-1.0muk}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,\bm{x}^{e}_{\mkern-1.0muk}(t)\,=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]\,[\mathbf{x}^{e}(t)]\hskip 20.00003pt\forall~\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}\in\Omega^{e}~.

(A.1)

In Eq. (
A.1
), the matrix
[\mathbf{N}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]
contains the nonzero elemental
basis functions (see
§3.1
) and the column vector
[\mathbf{x}^{e}(t)]

collects the corresponding local degrees of freedom (or nodal positions) in the
usual manner of finite element analysis:

[\mathbf{N}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]\,:=\,\Big[\hskip 5.0ptN_{1}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,[\bm{1}]\hskip 9.0ptN_{2}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,[\bm{1}]\hskip 5.0pt\ldots\hskip 5.0ptN_{\texttt{nen}}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,[\bm{1}]\hskip 5.0pt\Big]~,

(A.2)

where
[\bm{1}]
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix in Cartesian coordinates,
and

[\mathbf{x}^{e}(t)]\,:=\,\begin{bmatrix}~\\[-13.0pt]
\,\bm{x}_{1}^{e}(t)\,\\
\,\vdots\,\\[2.0pt]
\,\bm{x}_{\texttt{nen}}^{e}(t)\,\\[2.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}~.

(A.3)

The velocity and mesh velocity are similarly decomposed as

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{h}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,

\displaystyle=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]\,[\mathbf{v}^{e}(t)]

(A.4)

and

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,

\displaystyle=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]\,[\mathbf{v}^{{\mathrm{m}},e}(t)]

(A.5)

for all

\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}\in\Omega^{e}
.
Since the membrane tension and mesh pressure are scalar quantities, they are
respectively discretized as

\displaystyle\lambda_{h}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,

\displaystyle=\,[\mkern-9.0mu\vec{\mkern 9.0mu\mathbf{N}^{e}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]\,[\bm{\lambda}^{\!e}(t)]

(A.6)

and

\displaystyle p^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{h}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,

\displaystyle=\,[\mkern-9.0mu\vec{\mkern 9.0mu\mathbf{N}^{e}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]\,[\mathbf{p}^{{\mathrm{m}},e}(t)]

(A.7)

for all

\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}\in\Omega^{e}
, where
[\mkern-9.0mu\vec{\mkern 9.0mu\mathbf{N}^{e}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]
is the row vector of nonzero
elemental basis functions given by [cf. Eq. (
A.2
)]

[\mkern-9.0mu\vec{\mkern 9.0mu\mathbf{N}^{e}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]\,:=\,\Big[\hskip 5.0ptN_{1}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\hskip 9.0ptN_{2}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\hskip 5.0pt\ldots\hskip 5.0ptN_{\texttt{nen}}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\hskip 5.0pt\Big]~.

(A.8)

Assuming a known solution at time
t
, the temporal evolution of the membrane
is obtained via the backward Euler method.
In particular, the membrane surface is updated according to [cf. Eq. (
28
),(
29
)]

\displaystyle\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t+\Delta t)\,

\displaystyle=\,\bm{x}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,+\,\Delta t\,\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t+\Delta t)~,

(A.9)

or equivalently

\displaystyle[\mathbf{x}^{e}(t+\Delta t)]\,

\displaystyle=\,[\mathbf{x}^{e}(t)]\,+\,\Delta t\,[\mathbf{v}^{{\mathrm{m}},e}(t+\Delta t)]~.

(A.10)

As discussed in
§3.2
, the mesh velocity and all other unknowns
satisfy the residual vector equation

\big[\mathbf{r}([\mathbf{u}(t+\Delta t)])\big]\,=\,[\bm{0}]~,

(A.11)

which is solved via the Newton–Raphson method—in which
[\mathbf{u}(t)]
is used as
the initial guess.
We thus close our discussion of the finite element implementation by presenting
the contributions to the residual vector; all details can be found in the
software documentation
[
1
]
.

Appendix C
The numerical calculation of the pull force

In this section, we determine how to set the membrane velocity

to a
desired value
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}
at the center of the membrane patch.
Given the use of non-interpolatory basis functions, there is no unique way to do

We thus choose to set the membrane velocity over the entire finite element
containing the point of interest.
The pull force

\thetitle

A.4
), Eq. (

) can be expressed as

(C.2)

where
\{N^{e}_{\mkern-1.0muk}(\zeta_{\mathrm{p}}\hskip-3.7pt{}^{{\check{\alpha}}})\}
and
\{\bm{v}^{e}_{\mkern-1.0muk}\}

\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}

containing

Importantly, the basis function values are set by the choice of

,
and they are non-interpolatory
[
99
]
—and thus all
nen

nodal velocities contribute to Eq. (

Since

As

[

]

).
In practice, we set

\bm{v}^{e}_{\mkern-1.0muk}(t)\,=\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }k\in\{1,2,\ldots,\texttt{nen}\}~.

(C.3)

Given the properties of B-spline functions
[
99
]
, Eq. (
C.3
) results in a uniform prescribed velocity over the
entire parametric element
\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}
—expressed as

\bm{v}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,=\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all }\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}\in\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}~.

(C.4)

\thetitle
. The pull force when a finite element is uniformly displaced

In this section, we calculate the force
\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{\!{\mathrm{p}}}
required to pull an entire
finite element at a prescribed velocity as in Eq. (
C.4
).
From the strong form of the governing membrane equations
(
18
,
19
) we
recognize the pull force is the area integral of the net body force per area

\bm{f}
on the membrane—expressed as

\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{\!{\mathrm{p}}}(t)\ =\,\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}\bm{f}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\mkern 1.0muJ_{\Omega}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega~.

(C.5)

Since the prescribed velocity in Eq. (
C.4
) can be viewed
as a constraint,
\bm{f}
is then understood as the associated Lagrange
multiplier field over the element
\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}
.

Our task now is to determine how to calculate
\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{\!{\mathrm{p}}}
numerically, which is
accomplished by considering how the weak form would be modified if Eq. (
C.4
) was not satisfied directly.
The principle of virtual power would then necessitate the quantity

\begin{split}&\delta\mkern 1.0mu\bigg\{\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}\!\bm{f}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\bm{\cdot}\Big(\bm{v}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,-\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\Big)\,J_{\Omega}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\bigg\}\\[3.0pt]
&\hskip 20.0pt=\,\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}\!\delta\bm{v}\bm{\cdot}\bm{f}\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ +\,\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}\!\delta\bm{f}\bm{\cdot}\big(\bm{v}\ -\ \bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}\big)\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\ +\,\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}\!\bm{f}\bm{\cdot}\big(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}\big)\,\delta J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\end{split}

(C.6)

be subtracted from the direct Galerkin expression, where

\delta J_{\Omega}=\Delta t\mkern 1.0muJ_{\Omega}\,\bm{a}^{{\check{\alpha}}}\bm{\cdot}\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}

(see Appendix C.2.1 of Ref.
[
4
]
).
At this point, the fundamental unknowns and arbitrary variations are discretized
as

\begin{split}\bm{v}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,&=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\mkern 1.0mu[\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]~,\hskip 20.0pt\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\mkern 1.0mu[\mathbf{v}^{{\mathrm{m}},e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]~,\hskip 20.0pt\bm{f}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)\,=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\mkern 1.0mu[\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]~,\\[6.0pt]
\delta\bm{v}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,&=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\mkern 1.0mu[\delta\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]~,\hskip 31.0pt\delta\bm{v}^{{\mathrm{m}}}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\mkern 1.0mu[\delta\mathbf{v}^{{\mathrm{m}},e}_{\mathrm{p}}]~,\hskip 30.5pt\delta\bm{f}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})\,=\,[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\mkern 1.0mu[\delta\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]~.\end{split}

(C.7)

In Eq. (
C.7
), we introduced the shorthand

[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]:=[\mathbf{N}^{e}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}})]

for all

\zeta^{\check{\alpha}}\in\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}
.
Upon substituting Eq. (
C.7
) into the second
line of Eq. (
C.6
) and defining the elemental mass
matrix
[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]
as

[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\ :=\,\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0mu[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega~,

(C.8)

we find the quantity

\begin{split}&[\delta\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0mu[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\,[\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]\,+\,[\delta\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0mu\bigg\{[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\,[\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]\,-\,\bigg(\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0muJ_{\Omega}\ {\mathrm{d}}\Omega\bigg)\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\bigg\}\\[2.0pt]
&\hskip 20.0pt\,+\,[\delta\mathbf{v}^{{\mathrm{m}},e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}_{,{\check{\alpha}}}\bm{\cdot}\big(\bm{a}^{\check{\alpha}}\otimes\bm{f}\big)\,\big(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}\big)\,\Delta t\,J_{\Omega}~{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\end{split}

(C.9)

is to be subtracted from the discretized weak Galerkin expression
\mathcal{G}_{h}
.
We now separately consider the portions of Eq. (
C.9
) arising from variations in the pull
force, material velocity, and mesh velocity.

\thetitle
. The pull force contribution

The portion of the direct Galerkin expression associated with the net force per
unit area
\bm{f}
can be expressed as

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{f}}\,=\,[\delta\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0mu\bigg(\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0muJ_{\Omega}\ {\mathrm{d}}\Omega\bigg)\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\,-\,[\delta\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0mu[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\,[\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall~[\delta\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]~.

(C.10)

Importantly, the mass matrix
[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]
is invertible and the variation

[\delta\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]
is arbitrary, so the nodal velocity degrees of freedom are found

[\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]\,=\,[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{-1}\mkern 1.0mu\bigg(\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0muJ_{\Omega}\ {\mathrm{d}}\Omega\bigg)\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)~.

(C.11)

It is a well-known property of B-splines
[
99
]
that if the
constraint

\bm{v}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)

is enforced over the entire element, then the unique solution for the nodal
degrees of freedom is given by

[\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]\ =\,\begin{bmatrix}~\\[-13.0pt]
\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\,\\
\,\vdots\,\\[2.0pt]
\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\,\\[2.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}\ =\,\begin{bmatrix}~\\[-12.0pt]
\,[\bm{1}]\,\\
\,\vdots\,\\[1.0pt]
\,[\bm{1}]\,\\[2.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)~.

(C.12)

By comparing Eqs. (
C.11
) and
(
C.12
), we recognize

[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{-1}\mkern 1.0mu\int_{\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}}[\mathbf{N}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0muJ_{\Omega}\ {\mathrm{d}}\Omega\,=\,\begin{bmatrix}~\\[-12.0pt]
\,[\bm{1}]\,\\
\,\vdots\,\\[1.0pt]
\,[\bm{1}]\,\\[2.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}~,

(C.13)

which will be useful in our subsequent developments.

\thetitle
. The material velocity contribution

The material velocity portion of the discretized direct Galerkin expression

is given by

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{v}}\,=\,[\delta\mathbf{v}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0mu[\mathbf{r}^{\mathrm{v}}]\,-\,[\delta\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mkern 1.0mu[\mathbf{M}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\,[\mathbf{f}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]\,=\,0\hskip 20.00003pt\forall~\,~[\delta\mathbf{v}]~,

(C.14)

where
[\delta\mathbf{v}]
is the global variation of the nodal velocities,

[\delta\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]
is the velocity variation of the
nen
nodes associated with

\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}
, and
[\mathbf{r}^{\mathrm{v}}]
is the velocity portion of the global residual vector
in the absence of a pull force.
Let us imagine reordering the global velocity degrees of freedom such that those
associated with
\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}
appear last, and those not associated with the pull

The global velocity unknowns, their arbitrary variation, and residual vector in

\,[\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}(t)]\,\\[2.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}~,\hskip 20.00003pt[\delta\mathbf{v}]\,=\,\begin{bmatrix}~\\[-13.0pt]
\,[\delta\mathbf{v}_{\bar{{\mathrm{p}}}}]\,\\[4.0pt]
\,[\delta\mathbf{v}^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}]\,\\[2.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}~,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt[\mathbf{r}^{\mathrm{v}}]\,=\,\begin{bmatrix}~\\[-13.0pt]

(C.15)

Here, the subscript ‘

\Omega^{e}_{\mathrm{p}}

Substituting Eq. (
C.15

C.14

(C.16)

Since the arbitrary variations

and

are independent

C.16

Additionally, the nodal body force degrees of freedom are found to be given by

By substituting Eqs. (

The mesh velocity portion of

Appendix E
The translation of a tether with a Lagrangian mesh motion

When a pulled tether is translated laterally across a membrane surface, lipids
in the surrounding patch flow in-plane to accommodate the large out-of-plane
shape deformations.
For a given prescribed lateral tether velocity as in Eq. (
91
), we do not know the corresponding velocity field

\bm{v}(\zeta^{\check{\alpha}},t)
over the membrane patch
a priori.

Thus, any boundary conditions that pin certain nodes yield unphysical results,
as shown in Fig.
10
.
If no nodes are pinned, however, a rigid body translation results—and the
dynamics of lateral tether motion are not obtained.
Lagrangian simulations are thus unable to capture the physics of tether
translation.
