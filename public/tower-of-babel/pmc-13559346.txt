Myocardial infarction and lung cancer research from 1995 to 2026: a WoSCC- and PubMed-based bibliometric analysis
Authors: Xiaojie Zheng, Yang Liu, Wenzhong Chen, Xuehua Chen, Ting Li, Yining Dai, Xuan Teng, Weiyi Kong, Zongrong Lin, Zhenyang Fu, Sicheng Chen, Jinjin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial infarction (MI) and lung cancer frequently coexist within the broader context of cardio-oncology, yet the development, contributors, and thematic evolution of this research interface have not been comprehensively mapped. This study aimed to characterize the bibliometric landscape, collaboration patterns, thematic structure, and temporal evolution of MI and lung cancer research and to identify clinically relevant evidence gaps.MethodsEnglish-language articles and reviews published from 1995 through June 2026 were retrieved independently from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and PubMed. WoSCC served as the primary dataset for citation, collaboration, co-occurrence, co-citation, and thematic analyses, whereas PubMed was used for supplementary validation using biomedical indexing and Medical Subject Headings. Bibliometric analyses were performed using R/Bibliometrix, CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and Microsoft Excel.ResultsAfter screening, 2,274 WoSCC records and 1,326 PubMed records were included. The annual growth rates were 0.91% and 3.35%, respectively. The United States contributed the largest publication output in both databases, followed by China, although the United States showed a stronger international collaboration profile. Harvard University led WoSCC institutional output, whereas Harvard Medical School ranked first in PubMed. PLoS One was the most productive WoSCC source, while the European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery ranked first in PubMed. Thematic analyses identified mortality, cardiovascular risk, smoking, thromboembolic complications, and clinical outcomes as persistent core topics. Over time, research emphasis shifted from smoking, epidemiology, and conventional cardiovascular risk toward cardio-oncology, cardiotoxicity, immunotherapy, targeted therapies, anticoagulation, thromboembolism, and treatment-related adverse events.ConclusionsResearch at the MI-lung cancer interface has expanded substantially and increasingly reflects the cardiovascular implications of contemporary lung cancer care. Future work should prioritize prospective multicenter studies, standardized cardiovascular endpoints, and integrated strategies for risk assessment, surveillance, and management in patients with lung cancer who are vulnerable to ischemic cardiovascular events.

What is known and what is new?

• Myocardial infarction (MI) and lung cancer share clinical risk factors and vascular-inflammatory mechanisms, while modern lung cancer therapies may introduce additional cardiovascular risk.

• This study quantitatively maps the contributors, collaboration patterns, conceptual structure, and temporal evolution of the MI-lung cancer literature using complementary WoSCC and PubMed datasets and identifies a recent transition toward treatment-related cardiovascular issues.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Future research should move beyond describing disease coexistence toward prospective multicenter studies using standardized cardiovascular endpoints, detailed anticancer-treatment exposure, and integrated strategies for cardiovascular risk assessment, surveillance, and management in patients with lung cancer who are vulnerable to ischemic cardiovascular events.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) and lung cancer are both major causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and represent substantial clinical burdens in cardiovascular medicine and thoracic oncology, respectively (1,2). With population aging, improved cancer survival, and increasing use of multimodal cancer therapy, patients with lung cancer are increasingly exposed to cardiovascular comorbidities and treatment-related cardiovascular complications (3-5). MI and lung cancer also share several clinical risk factors, including smoking, older age, metabolic abnormalities, chronic inflammation, and vascular dysfunction (3,4). Therefore, their intersection is not merely a coincidental overlap of two common diseases, but an increasingly relevant clinical problem within the broader field of cardio-oncology.

Previous studies have examined specific aspects of this intersection, including cardiovascular comorbidities in patients with lung cancer, acute coronary events in cancer populations, perioperative cardiovascular risk, treatment-related cardiotoxicity, and long-term survival outcomes (5-9). However, these studies have usually focused on individual clinical questions or selected patient groups. As a result, the overall development of this research area, the major contributors, the collaboration patterns, and the temporal evolution of research themes remain insufficiently characterized. Narrative and scoping reviews primarily synthesize evidence relating to defined clinical questions, whereas bibliometric analysis is specifically suited to quantitatively characterizing field development, the distribution of research contributions, collaboration networks, and thematic evolution over time (10,11).

Bibliometric analysis provides a quantitative approach to mapping publication trends, scientific contributors, collaboration networks, thematic structures, and topic evolution within a defined body of literature (10,11). This approach is particularly useful for an interdisciplinary field such as MI and lung cancer, where clinical cardiology, thoracic oncology, cancer therapy, vascular biology, and cardio-oncology overlap. By identifying dominant themes and underdeveloped areas, bibliometric mapping can help clarify how the field has been organized and where future clinical and translational research may be needed.

In the present study, we performed a bibliometric analysis of publications on MI and lung cancer using the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and PubMed databases. WoSCC was used as the primary database for publication trend, collaboration, co-occurrence, and co-citation analyses, whereas PubMed was used for supplementary validation through biomedical indexing and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)-based thematic assessment. We aimed to characterize the publication landscape, major geographic and institutional contributors, collaboration patterns, thematic organization, and temporal evolution of research hotspots in this field, and to identify potential gaps relevant to cardiovascular surveillance, treatment-related risk assessment, and multidisciplinary management of patients with lung cancer who are vulnerable to cardiovascular events. We present this article in accordance with the BIBLIO reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1009/rc).

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

A bibliometric review was conducted using the WoSCC and PubMed databases. The Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI-Expanded) within WoSCC was used as the primary data source because it provides standardized citation indexing and structured bibliographic metadata suitable for co-citation, co-occurrence, collaboration, and thematic analyses. PubMed was used as a supplementary biomedical database because of its strong coverage of clinical and biomedical literature and its standardized MeSH indexing.

The final literature search was conducted on July 8, 2026 and covered publications from 1995 through June 2026. The year 1995 was chosen as the starting point to provide a long-term observation window spanning approximately three decades, thereby allowing the assessment of publication growth, collaboration patterns, and thematic evolution over time. Only English-language articles and reviews were included to maintain consistency in bibliographic metadata and citation-based analyses. Chinese-language databases such as China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) were not incorporated because their indexing structures and citation metadata are not directly comparable with those of WoSCC and PubMed; the potential effect of this restriction is discussed in the “Discussion” section.

For WoSCC, the search was conducted using the Topic Search field, which covers titles, abstracts, keywords plus, and author keywords. The search strategy was designed to combine MI-related terms with lung cancer-related terms. The full WoSCC search settings and retrieval details are provided in Table S1. The complete search string was as follows: TS = (“myocardial infarction” OR “cardiac infarction” OR “acute myocardial infarction” OR “acute cardiac infarction” OR “ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction” OR “ST-elevation myocardial infarction” OR “non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction” OR “non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction” OR MI OR AMI OR STEMI OR NSTEMI) AND (((“lung” OR “pulmonary”) AND (“cancer” OR “carcinoma”)) OR “non-small cell lung cancer” OR “non-small cell lung carcinoma” OR “lung adenocarcinoma” OR “lung squamous cell carcinoma” OR “pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma” OR “lung squamous carcinoma” OR “lung squamous cell cancer” OR “lung squamous cancer” OR “lung adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma” OR “lung large cell carcinoma” OR “pulmonary large cell carcinoma” OR “large cell lung cancer” OR “large cell lung carcinoma” OR “lung small cell carcinoma” OR “pulmonary small cell carcinoma” OR “small cell lung cancer” OR “small cell lung carcinoma” OR NSCLC OR LUAD OR LUSC OR LCLC OR SCLC).

For PubMed, the search combined MeSH terms and title/abstract terms related to myocardial infarction and lung neoplasms. The core search logic was (“Myocardial Infarction”[Mesh] OR myocardial infarction-related title/abstract terms) AND (“Lung Neoplasms”[Mesh] OR lung cancer-related title/abstract terms). The complete PubMed search strategy is provided in Table S2.

Eligibility criteria and data refinement

Eligible records were peer-reviewed original research articles or review articles focusing on the intersection of MI and lung cancer. Records were excluded if they were editorials, letters, conference abstracts, case reports, book chapters, short communications, or retracted publications; were published in a language other than English or outside the predefined period; or did not primarily address the intersection between MI and lung cancer.

Titles, abstracts, document types, and bibliographic metadata were reviewed to identify and remove irrelevant records. Records within each database were also checked for duplicate entries and incomplete bibliographic information. Because WoSCC and PubMed were analyzed independently for different purposes, records from the two databases were not merged into a single dataset. WoSCC was used for the primary network-based bibliometric analyses, whereas PubMed served as a supplementary validation dataset. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Table S3, and the literature search and selection process is presented in Figure S1.

Data extraction

Bibliographic data were extracted independently from WoSCC and PubMed. The extracted variables included publication year, title, journal, authors, institutional affiliations, countries or regions, document type, and unique identifiers, including digital object identifier (DOI) for WoSCC records and PubMed identifier (PMID) for PubMed records. Keyword-related data included author keywords and keywords plus from WoSCC and MeSH terms from PubMed. Citation-related indicators were extracted from the corresponding database when available. All records were cross-checked for consistency before formal analysis. The complete list of extracted variables is provided in Table S4.

Bibliometric analysis and indicator definitions

WoSCC records were used for the primary bibliometric analyses, including annual publication trends, country and institutional productivity, author and journal contributions, international collaboration, keyword co-occurrence, keyword clustering, keyword burst detection, thematic mapping, multiple correspondence analysis (MCA), journal co-citation, and dual-map overlay analysis. PubMed records were analyzed separately to validate publication trends, geographic and institutional patterns, author and journal productivity, and MeSH-based thematic findings.

Total publications represented the number of eligible records attributed to a country, institution, author, or journal. Total citations represented the number of citations received by WoSCC-indexed publications. The H-index was used as a combined measure of publication productivity and citation impact; an entity has an H-index of h when h publications have each received at least h citations. Link strength represented the intensity of relationships between nodes in co-authorship, co-occurrence, or co-citation networks. In thematic mapping, centrality reflected the degree to which a theme was connected with the overall field, whereas density represented the internal development and cohesion of that theme. Single-country publications (SCPs) and multiple-country publications (MCPs) were used to distinguish domestic from international collaboration.

Software and parameter settings

Bibliometric analyses were conducted using R 4.5.1 with the Bibliometrix 5.2.1 package, CiteSpace 6.4.1, VOSviewer 1.6.20, and Microsoft Excel 2021. Bibliometrix was used for descriptive bibliometric indicators, annual publication trends, country and institutional analyses, author and source productivity, journal co-citation analysis, keyword co-occurrence analysis, thematic mapping, MCA, and trend topic analysis. VOSviewer was used to generate the PubMed MeSH-term density map for thematic validation. CiteSpace was used for keyword clustering, burst detection, and dual-map overlay analysis. Annual publication regression curves were generated using Microsoft Excel 2021.

Unless otherwise specified, the software settings defined for each analysis were applied consistently across the dataset. These included full counting, association-strength normalization, Louvain clustering, Callon centrality and density, g-index selection, Pathfinder pruning, and burst detection parameters, where applicable. Detailed software versions, analytical functions, thresholds, and parameter settings are provided in Table S5.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive bibliometric indicators were summarized using counts, percentages, averages, and annual growth rates, as appropriate. Annual publication trends were fitted using quadratic regression in Microsoft Excel 2021, and the regression equations and coefficients of determination (R2) are presented in Figure 1. No inferential hypothesis testing was performed because this study was descriptive and bibliometric in nature.

Results

The literature search and selection process is summarized in Figure S1. After screening and data refinement, 2,274 WoSCC records were retained for the primary bibliometric analyses and 1,326 PubMed records were retained for supplementary validation.

Figure 1 summarizes the overall bibliometric profile and publication trends of MI and lung cancer research. In WoSCC, the final dataset covered 1995–2026 and included 2,274 documents from 1,025 sources, written by 13,980 authors and supported by 106,093 references (Figure 1A). The annual growth rate was 0.91%, the mean document age was 12 years, the average citations per document was 71.03, and international co-authorship accounted for 21.24% of publications (Figure 1A). The annual output increased gradually over the study period, with the most active years occurring after 2016 and a peak of 133 publications in 2020; publication counts remained above 100 in 2017–2025, whereas the lower count in 2026 reflected an incomplete year at the time of retrieval (Figure 1B, Figure 1C).

In PubMed, the validation dataset comprised 1,326 documents from 740 sources and 8,601 authors, with an annual growth rate of 3.35% and a mean document age of 10.3 years (Figure 1D). The annual number of publications also rose over time and reached its highest level in 2024 (n=92), followed by 2025 (n=87), before declining in 2026 because the final year was only partially indexed at the time of search (Figure 1E, Figure 1F). Taken together, both databases showed a sustained long-term increase in publication activity, supporting the continued expansion of this research area.

Figure 2 summarizes the global collaboration patterns, geographic and institutional contributions, institutional density, and source dynamics in the WoSCC dataset. The global collaboration network showed extensive international connections, with prominent collaborative links involving North America, Europe, East Asia, and Australia (Figure 2A). The United States contributed the largest number of publications, followed by China, the United Kingdom, Japan, Italy, Germany, and Canada (Figure 2B). The United States also showed the highest numbers of both SCP and MCP, whereas China ranked second in overall output, with a greater proportion of its publications classified as SCP (Figure 2B).

At the institutional level, Harvard University ranked first in publication output, followed by Harvard University Medical Affiliates and the University of Toronto; other leading affiliations included the University of London, the University of Texas System, the University of Copenhagen, the University of California System, Harvard Medical School, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, and Karolinska Institutet (Figure 2C). The institutional density map showed the greatest concentration of research activity around Harvard University and its affiliated institutions, with additional areas of activity involving institutions from North America and Europe (Figure 2D). The cumulative source-production trajectories showed sustained contributions from several core publication sources, although the timing and rate of growth varied across journals, with some sources showing more pronounced increases in recent years (Figure 2E).

The PubMed validation showed broadly comparable geographic patterns. The United States ranked first in publication output, followed by China, while Japan, Italy, Germany, Canada, Denmark, France, and South Korea were also among the leading contributing countries (Figure S2A). Harvard Medical School was the most productive affiliation, followed by Fudan University, with Lewis Katz School of Medicine at Temple University, Stanford University, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, and the University of Washington also among the leading institutions (Figure S2B). The global collaboration network similarly demonstrated international connections across North America, Europe, East Asia, and Australia (Figure S2C). The PubMed MeSH-term density map characterized the thematic distribution of the literature, with “humans”, “female”, “aged”, “middle aged”, “lung neoplasms”, and “myocardial infarction” among the most prominent indexed terms, together with terms related to risk factors and retrospective studies (Figure S2D).

Author-level analyses further showed that publication activity was distributed among a relatively small group of recurrent contributors in both databases. The longitudinal profiles indicated that several of the most productive authors maintained sustained publication activity over multiple years, although the composition and output of leading authors differed between WoSCC and PubMed (Figure S3A,S3B). The co-authorship networks identified multiple collaborative clusters rather than a single dominant group, with both databases showing several densely connected author communities alongside smaller, more isolated clusters (Figure S3C,S3D).

Source-level analysis also showed both convergence and database-specific differences. In WoSCC, PLoS One ranked first by publication output, followed by the European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery, International Journal of Radiation Oncology Biology Physics, Lung Cancer, and Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine (Figure S4A). In PubMed, the European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery ranked first, followed by PLoS One, The Annals of Thoracic Surgery, Lung Cancer, and Scientific Reports (Figure S4B). Several leading journals, including PLoS One, the European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery, Lung Cancer, Scientific Reports, and the International Journal of Cardiology, appeared among the major publication sources in one or both databases (Figure S4A,S4B).

Figure 3 depicts the conceptual organization of MI and lung cancer research in WoSCC. The thematic map placed “cancer”, “mortality”, “cardiovascular disease”, “myocardial infarction”, “smoking”, “epidemiology”, and thromboembolic terms among the well-developed and highly central themes, indicating that the core structure of the field is strongly oriented toward clinical outcomes, risk factors, and vascular complications (Figure 3A). In contrast, more specialized or less mature themes, including acute MI, atherosclerosis, and pharmacokinetics, appeared in the emerging or declining quadrant, whereas several comorbidity-related topics occupied more peripheral positions (Figure 3A).

The MCA factorial map further separated the literature into broad conceptual groupings, including clusters related to tumor biology and progression, clinical epidemiology and prognosis, and thromboembolic or vascular complications (Figure 3B). The word cloud highlighted “myocardial infarction”, “mortality”, “risk”, “cancer”, and “lung cancer” as the most frequent terms (Figure 3C). The CiteSpace clustering map identified several major keyword clusters, such as cardiovascular disease, deep vein thrombosis, lung cancer cell, acute chest pain, MI, genetic polymorphism, and ultralight cigarette (Figure 3D). The keyword co-occurrence network likewise showed a core structure centered on MI, mortality, risk, cancer, and lung cancer, with linked subclusters involving cardiovascular disease, thromboembolism, inflammation, survival, and treatment-related topics (Figure 3E). A broadly comparable PubMed-based conceptual structure was observed in Figure S5A,S5B, with human, demographic, epidemiologic, and outcome-related themes predominating, while animal-model and experimental topics occupied more peripheral positions.

Figure 4 illustrates the temporal evolution of research themes. The Trend Topics analysis showed a gradual shift in thematic emphasis across the study period. Earlier research was characterized mainly by topics related to smoking and exposure, cardiovascular risk, oxidative stress, and chemoprevention, whereas subsequent work increasingly involved lung cancer, MI, surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy, thromboembolic complications, management, and survival. In the later period, topics related to cardio-oncology, cardiotoxicity, immunotherapy, targeted therapies such as atezolizumab and osimertinib, anticoagulation, thromboembolism, multicenter studies, and adverse events became more prominent (Figure 4A).

The burst analysis further demonstrated shifts in keyword prominence over time (Figure 4B). Early bursts beginning during 1996–2000 included morbidity, coronary heart disease, women, cell lung cancer, exposure, therapy, and metastasis. During 2001–2010, prominent burst terms included cigarette smoking, passive smoking, postoperative radiotherapy, atherosclerosis, risk factors, pulmonary embolism, and radiation therapy. Keywords with bursts beginning during 2011–2020 included acute myocardial infarction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, death, trial, noncardiac surgery, guidelines, multicenter studies, resection, care, deep vein thrombosis, growth, management, invasion, and apoptosis. In the most recent period [2021–2026], prominent burst terms included stroke, prevention, proliferation, therapy, smoking, progression, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular events, atherosclerosis, arterial thromboembolism, radiation therapy, acute coronary syndrome, multicenter studies, and non-small cell lung cancer (Figure 4B).

The PubMed validation showed a broadly comparable temporal shift in research themes. Earlier MeSH-based topics were mainly related to treatment, smoking, postoperative complications, and clinical management, whereas later topics increasingly involved multimorbidity, molecular and genetic factors, antineoplastic therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitor-related adverse effects (Figure S6A). The cumulative frequency analysis showed sustained growth of major demographic, risk-related, and treatment-outcome terms over time (Figure S6B).

Discussion

This study maps the development of research at the intersection of MI and lung cancer over approximately three decades. Publication activity increased progressively in both databases, with the United States and China contributing the largest shares of the literature but showing different patterns of international collaboration. The thematic analyses also indicate that the field has evolved rather than simply expanded in volume. Earlier-period topics were more closely associated with smoking, cardiovascular risk, epidemiology, and clinical outcomes, whereas later-period topics increasingly involved cardio-oncology, cardiotoxicity, immunotherapy, targeted therapies, thromboembolic complications, and treatment-related adverse events. These changes reflect the growing clinical overlap between cardiovascular disease and contemporary lung cancer care.

The connection between MI and lung cancer is partly explained by shared clinical determinants. Smoking is the most obvious common exposure, but older age, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, dyslipidemia, and broader metabolic abnormalities contribute to the cardiovascular and oncological risk profiles of these patients (3,4,12). Cardiovascular disease and cancer are increasingly recognized as biologically interconnected conditions that share metabolic, inflammatory, immune, and vascular mechanisms (12). A prospective cohort study also reported a bidirectional association between cardiovascular disease and lung cancer, supporting the epidemiological basis for studying this interface (4). These observations are consistent with our bibliometric findings, in which smoking, cardiovascular disease, risk factors, mortality, and related clinical terms remained prominent across different analytical approaches.

Inflammation provides an important mechanistic bridge between the two disease processes. In atherosclerosis, endothelial activation promotes leukocyte recruitment, while monocytes entering the arterial wall differentiate into macrophages and contribute to plaque formation, progression, and thrombotic complications (13). Cancer is likewise characterized by dysregulated innate immunity and inflammatory remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Shared pathways involving macrophage activation, S100A8/9, the NLRP3 inflammasome, and downstream IL-1β signaling have been implicated in both cancer and cardiovascular disease (12,14). This framework may help explain why inflammation, atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and cardiovascular events recur within the thematic structure identified in this study.

The IL-1β pathway provides a particularly informative clinical example of this biological overlap. In the CANTOS trial, 10,061 patients with previous MI and residual inflammatory risk were randomized to canakinumab or placebo, and IL-1β inhibition reduced recurrent cardiovascular events without lowering lipid levels (15). An exploratory cancer analysis from the same trial subsequently showed dose-dependent reductions in incident lung cancer and lung cancer mortality, suggesting that IL-1β-mediated inflammation may contribute to both atherothrombosis and lung carcinogenesis (16). However, this signal did not translate into established anticancer efficacy when IL-1β inhibition was evaluated specifically in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). CANOPY-A did not improve disease-free survival after complete resection and adjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy for stage II–III NSCLC (17), while CANOPY-1 did not significantly improve progression-free or overall survival when canakinumab was added to pembrolizumab and platinum-based chemotherapy in advanced NSCLC (18). These apparently contrasting findings are clinically instructive: shared inflammatory biology does not necessarily imply that the same anti-inflammatory intervention will provide equivalent therapeutic benefit in cardiovascular disease and established lung cancer.

The shift toward treatment-related cardiovascular topics is also clinically meaningful. Modern lung cancer therapy exposes patients to several distinct cardiovascular hazards. Cytotoxic regimens, including platinum-based combinations, may contribute to vascular and ischemic complications, while targeted therapies introduce additional cardiac effects (3). Osimertinib is an important example; a recent real-world study of 1,126 patients with NSCLC reported osimertinib-related cardiotoxicity in 4.7% of patients, with advanced age and pre-existing cardiovascular abnormalities among the associated risk factors (19). Immune checkpoint inhibitors have introduced another spectrum of cardiovascular toxicity, including myocarditis, heart failure, arrhythmias, and other cardiovascular adverse events (20). Thoracic radiotherapy may further contribute to coronary and myocardial injury, with risk influenced by the distribution and dose of cardiac radiation exposure (5). These treatment-related concerns provide a clinical context for the later-period concentration of terms such as cardiotoxicity, immunotherapy, osimertinib, anticoagulation, thromboembolism, and adverse events in the bibliometric analyses.

These findings also suggest several priorities for future research. Research should move beyond documenting the coexistence of MI and lung cancer toward prospective evaluation of cardiovascular risk before and during cancer treatment. Particular attention is needed for patients with established coronary disease or multiple cardiovascular risk factors who receive potentially cardiotoxic systemic therapy or thoracic radiotherapy. Multicenter studies using standardized definitions of MI and acute coronary syndromes, detailed treatment exposure, cardiovascular biomarkers, imaging information, and cancer outcomes would improve risk stratification and help distinguish pre-existing atherosclerotic disease from treatment-related cardiovascular injury. Greater international collaboration would also be valuable, particularly given the geographic differences in publication output and collaboration patterns identified in this study.

Several strengths and limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The search was updated through June 2026, and the combination of WoSCC and PubMed allowed citation-based bibliometric mapping to be complemented by biomedical and MeSH-based validation. The use of multiple analytical tools and explicit reporting of search strategies, eligibility criteria, metadata fields, and analytical settings also improves reproducibility. Nevertheless, only English-language publications were included, and Chinese-language databases such as CNKI were not incorporated. This may underestimate contributions from China and other non-English-speaking regions. WoSCC and PubMed also differ in journal coverage, indexing practices, and metadata structure and were therefore analyzed independently rather than merged. Citation-based indicators inherently favor older publications because recent studies have had less time to accumulate citations, while keyword- and MeSH-based analyses are influenced by author terminology and database indexing practices. In addition, 2026 represented an incomplete publication year at the time of retrieval. Finally, bibliometric analysis characterizes the structure and evolution of a literature base but does not assess the methodological quality of individual studies, establish causal relationships, or determine the efficacy of clinical interventions. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as a map of research development and evidence gaps rather than as direct clinical recommendations.

Conclusions

Research at the intersection of MI and lung cancer has expanded substantially over the past three decades, with research priorities shifting from smoking, epidemiology, and clinical outcomes toward treatment-related cardiovascular risk, thromboembolic complications, and cardio-oncology. Interpreted alongside contemporary clinical evidence, these bibliometric patterns highlight the relevance of shared risk factors, vascular-inflammatory mechanisms, and the cardiovascular consequences of modern lung cancer therapy. Future research should prioritize prospective multicenter studies, standardized cardiovascular endpoints, and integrated strategies for risk assessment, surveillance, and management in patients with lung cancer who are vulnerable to ischemic cardiovascular events. Given the inherent limitations of bibliometric data, these findings should be interpreted as a map of research development and evidence gaps rather than as evidence of clinical efficacy.
