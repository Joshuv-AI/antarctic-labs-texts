Effects of different warming strategies on intraoperative hypothermia: a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Dayuan Wei, Su Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this network meta-analysis was to assess the effect of different active warming strategies on intraoperative hypothermia (IH).MethodsDatabases of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library were searched for randomized controlled trials that were published from 1 January 2015 to 20 September 2025. The incidence of IH was the primary outcome.ResultsA total of 45 studies comprising 5317 patients were included in this study. Among the single warming strategies, both thermal suit [RR = 0.45, 95% CI (0.16, 1.27)] and irrigation fluid warming [RR = 0.95, 95% CI (0.27, 3.30)] demonstrated a numerically lower incidence of IH compared with forced-air warming (FAW). For FAW blanket type, the incidence of IH in FAW-Lower-body [RR = 1.54, 95% CI (1.17, 2.04)] and FAW-Surgical-access blanket [RR = 1.44, 95% CI (1.01, 2.06)] was significantly higher than that in FAW-Underbody. Compared with FAW alone, multiple combined warming strategies without prewarming exhibited numerically reduced IH incidence, such as irrigation fluid warming + intravenous fluid warming + FAW [RR = 0.44, 95% CI (0.04, 4.40)]. Compared with FAW alone, prewarming + FAW showed significantly lower IH incidence [RR = 0.66, 95% CI (0.45, 0.96)].ConclusionFAW remains the pragmatic standard for preventing IH, with the underbody type preferred whenever clinically feasible. We conditionally recommend combining FAW with prewarming and fluid warming in high-risk contexts (particularly for older patients and prolonged surgery), although wide prediction intervals suggest these added benefits should be interpreted cautiously. Introduction

Intraoperative hypothermia (IH), defined as a core temperature below 36 °C, is a prevalent condition during surgery, with reported incidences ranging from 71.8% to 81.25% [1–3]. Maintaining core normothermia is a fundamental objective of perioperative care due to the significant clinical dangers associated with IH, including an increased risk of blood loss and transfusion requirements from impaired coagulation, higher rates of surgical site infections (SSIs) due to suppressed immune function, and an elevated incidence of adverse cardiovascular events and thrombosis [2–5].

Active warming strategies are widely recognized as the cornerstone of IH prevention and are capable of significantly reducing the incidence of IH and its related complications compared with passive or routine care [6, 7]. The currently available active warming techniques are diverse, encompassing forced-air warming (FAW), conductive warming (CW), self-warming blanket (SWB), and various combined warming strategies. This technological variety is often associated with clinical uncertainties or technical drawbacks. For example, while FAW is widely used, concerns have been raised regarding its potential to disrupt laminar airflow and increase the risk of SSIs [8]. Similarly, resistive heating devices may carry risks of local overheating or electrical faults, and simpler methods, such as self-heating blankets, often prove insufficiently effective [7, 9, 10]. These conflicting factors complicate the selection of a single optimal warming strategy.

Despite the proven benefit of active warming over routine care, evidence regarding the relative efficacy of these diverse warming modalities remains inconclusive. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses typically compared the use of active warming with no active warming, or two single warming strategies [11–13]. Furthermore, a critical question remains unanswered, particularly for vulnerable, high-risk patients—such as the elderly (aged ≥ 60 years), whose thermoregulatory mechanisms are often impaired [14, 15]—of whether a single warming strategy is sufficient or if multimodal combinations are required to achieve reliable normothermia maintenance.

To address this gap, we propose conducting a systematic review and Network Meta-Analysis (NMA), a robust methodology capable of integrating both direct and indirect evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The primary objective of this NMA is to compare the relative effectiveness of different warming interventions, including single-modal and multimodal strategies, in preventing intraoperative hypothermia in surgical patients. Additionally, we aim to identify the most effective approach and explore important clinical secondary outcomes, such as blood loss, incidence of hypothermia in the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU), postoperative shivering, and surgical site infection rates. By synthesizing this evidence, we aim to provide a comprehensive, evidence-based reference for optimizing perioperative care and improving patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

We compared the effects of different warming strategies on IH using NMA. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed for reporting [16]. This study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration Number: CRD420251148645).

Search strategy

A search of the PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases was conducted for full-text papers published from 1 January 2015 to 20 September 2025. In addition, we conducted a thorough examination of the reference lists of the articles that were included to identify any publications that may have been overlooked in the initial search. The search was limited to studies conducted in the English language and involving human participants. The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Selection criteria and study design

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) RCTs; (2) research conducted on adult surgical patients (aged ≥ 18 years); (3) studies involving intervention measures for an explicit warming strategy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cranial surgery; (2) extracorporeal circulation surgery; (3) non-English literature; (4) non-human participants; (5) lack of core body temperature data.

The primary outcome was the incidence of IH. Secondary outcomes were as follows: intraoperative blood loss, the incidence of hypothermia in postoperative/post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), the incidence of shivering in postoperative/PACU, and the incidence of SSIs. To evaluate the effect of various warming strategies in older adults, we pre-specified a subgroup analysis focusing on studies where the median or mean age of the enrolled population was 60 years or older.

Data extraction and assessment for risk of bias

Data extraction and quality evaluation were performed independently by two authors, who double-checked for data consistency and completeness. Any disagreements regarding quality assessment were handled by discussion or reviewed by an independent reviewer who did not participate in this study. After duplicates were removed, the preliminary quality of studies was assessed based on their titles and abstracts. The final assessment was then done through reading the full text. The data and information were extracted including the following: first author, year of publication, sample size, age, sex, body mass index, site of surgery, anesthesia methods, temperature measurement methods, warming methods, preoperative prewarming, the incidence of IH, intraoperative blood loss, the incidence of hypothermia in postoperative/PACU, the incidence of shivering in postoperative/PACU, and the incidence of SSIs.

Two authors independently used the Cochrane risk of bias tool (RoB 2.0) to evaluate the risk of bias for the included studies. Each study was assessed from five fields: bias sourced from the randomization process, bias due to deviation from the intended intervention, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in outcome measures, and bias in the selection of reported outcomes [17]. Each section was categorized into ‘Low,’ ‘High’ risk of bias, or “Some concerns.” If necessary, the independent reviewer would join the discussions to resolve the disagreement. The Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) was applied to evaluate the quality of evidence [18].

Statistical analysis

For dichotomous outcomes, we calculated risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For continuous outcomes, we synthesized mean differences (MDs) to enhance clinical interpretability. When continuous data were reported as medians accompanied by ranges or interquartile ranges (IQRs), we precisely estimated the sample means and standard deviations using the validated methods [19]. A continuity correction was applied for zero-cell data to avoid bias from sparse event counts. We performed a frequentist network meta-analysis (NMA) using a random-effects model with the inverse variance method. Because warming methods in dataset consisted of bundled interventions, component network meta-analysis (CNMA) was conducted to disentangle the independent efficacy of single method and evaluate the additivity of their combined effects.

Heterogeneity was assessed by I2 and τ2. To reflect the expected variability of intervention effects in future clinical settings, we calculated and reported 95% prediction intervals (PIs) alongside standard 95% CIs for each network. Random-effects meta-regression analyses were performed to explore the impact of plausible continuous modifiers, specifically mean patient age and surgery duration. Furthermore, the assumption of transitivity was evaluated by comparing clinical characteristics across treatment comparisons. Network inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence was assessed globally using the Design-by-Treatment Interaction model and locally via the Node-Splitting method. To provide clinically actionable metrics, we calculated the absolute risk reduction (ARR) or absolute risk increase (ARI) per 100 patients and the Number Needed to Treat (NNT) or number needed to harm (NNH) based on the baseline risk derived from the specified control group. Treatment rankings were established using the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking curve (SUCRA), where values closer to 1 indicate a higher probability of being the superior intervention. League tables for pairwise intervention comparisons were generated for the initial and final models. Interventions were ordered with “FAW” fixed as the last column (reference group) to ensure consistency.

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were evaluated using comparison-adjusted funnel plots, complemented by the Peters test conducted on the Odds Ratio scale. Finally, the certainty of evidence for network estimates was evaluated using the CINeMA framework, considering within-study bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, incoherence, and publication bias. Data synthesis and NMA were performed using R software (Version 4.4.1) utilizing the netmeta and meta packages (seed set as 42), and StataMP (Version 17.0). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study characteristics and quality assessment

A total of 698 citations were identified from the databases. After applying the established eligibility criteria, a total of 45 RCTs comprising 5317 patients were ultimately included in the NMA [20–65]. Among the included studies, 38 were conducted under general anesthesia and 7 under spinal anesthesia. Core body temperature was measured in all included studies, including direct core sites (e.g., nasopharynx, esophagus, or bladder) and non-invasive technologies recognized as accurate proxies for core temperature (SpotOn™ system). These studies covered almost all warming strategies, including FAW, CW, SWB, thermal suit (TS), warmed and humidified insufflation (WHI), intravenous fluid warming (FW), warm water blanket (WWB), irrigation fluid warming (IFW), and various combined warming strategies. Among these studies, twenty reported intraoperative blood loss, thirteen reported the incidence of hypothermia in postoperative/PACU, nineteen reported the incidence of shivering in postoperative/PACU, seven reported the incidence of SSIs. The flow chart of study screening is presented in Fig. 1. The evaluation of methodological quality was performed based on RoB 2.0, and each domain was determined as low bias risk, high bias risk, or unclear bias risk (Fig. S1 and Fig. S2; Supplementary File 2). All quality of evidence was evaluated according to the CINeMA criterion (Table 1). All data of the included studies are outlined in 10.6084/m9.figshare.14664663.

Incidence of IH

Due to the diversity of warming strategies, the analysis of primary outcome is divided into four parts: (1) single strategy; (2) FAW blanket type; (3) No preoperative prewarming; (4) preoperative prewarming. League tables for the four strategy categories are provided in Supplementary File 3.

A total of 19 studies (2282 patients) compared single warming strategies, including FAW, FW, SWB, TS, CW, IFW, and no active warming (None) (Fig. 2A). NMA results showed that compared with FAW, None significantly increased the incidence of IH [RR = 3.27, 95% CI (1.78, 6.01)]. The incidence of IH with TS [RR = 0.45, 95% CI (0.16, 1.27)] and IFW [RR = 0.95, 95% CI (0.27, 3.30)] was numerically lower than with FAW, but the differences lacked statistical significance (Fig. 3A). Based on a baseline IH risk of 54.7% in the None group, the ARR for FAW was 35.4%, NNT was 3. SUCRA analysis (Supplementary File 2, Fig. S3) ranked TS (0.94) as the optimal strategy, followed by FAW (0.68) and IFW (0.65). The network model exhibited high heterogeneity (I2 = 74.9%, τ2 = 0.23), resulting in wide 95% PIs that crossed 1 for all comparisons (Supplementary File 2, Table S1). Regarding network inconsistency, the global design-by-treatment interaction test yielded P values of 0.005 and 0.24 under fixed-effects and random-effects models, respectively. Node-splitting analysis revealed no significant local inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence (all Pinconsistency = 0.24, Supplementary File 2, Table S2). Based on studies reporting surgery duration and age, meta-regression indicated that both factors significantly modified treatment effects (P < 0.001 and P = 0.01), partially explaining the observed high heterogeneity. No significant publication bias was detected by the Peters test (P = 0.40, Supplementary File 2, Fig. S4).

A total of 5 studies (883 patients) compared different types of FAW, including FAW-Underbody (FAW-UND), FAW-Upper-body (FAW-UB), FAW-Lower-body (FAW-LB), and FAW-Surgical-access blanket (FAW-SAB) (Fig. 2B). NMA results showed that compared with FAW-UND, the incidence of IH was significantly higher with FAW-SAB [RR = 1.44, 95% CI (1.01, 2.06)] and FAW-LB [RR = 1.54, 95% CI (1.17, 2.04)]. The incidence of IH with FAW-UB [RR = 1.07, 95% CI (0.80, 1.43)] was numerically higher than with FAW-UND, but the difference lacked statistical significance (Fig. 3B). SUCRA analysis (Supplementary File 2, Fig. S5) ranked FAW-UND (0.88) as the optimal strategy, followed by FAW-UB (0.74), FAW-SAB (0.24), and FAW-LB (0.13). Based on a baseline IH risk of 37.8% in the FAW-UND group, the ARI and NNH were 20.5% and 5 for FAW-LB, and 16.8% and 6 for FAW-SAB. Regarding network inconsistency, the global design-by-treatment interaction test yielded P values of 0.58 and 0.63 under fixed-effects and random-effects models, respectively. Node-splitting analysis revealed no significant local inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence (all Pinconsistency = 0.59, Supplementary File 2, Table S3). Due to the low heterogeneity of this network (I2 = 14.8%, τ2 = 0.006) and the limited number of included studies preventing quantitative publication bias testing (Supplementary File 2, Fig. S6), no further sensitivity analysis or meta-regression was conducted.

A total of 22 studies (2739 patients) compared the effect of single or combined strategies without preoperative warming, network diagram in Fig. 2D. Compared with FAW alone, several combined warming strategies showed numerically lower IH incidence, including FW + FAW + CW + TS [RR = 0.20, 95% CI (0.02, 2.45)], FW + FAW+IFW [RR = 0.44, 95% CI (0.04, 4.40)], FW + FAW+WWB [RR = 0.76, 95% CI (0.17, 3.43)], and FW + FAW+CW [RR = 0.85, 95% CI (0.10, 7.09)]. However, these 95% CIs included 1, indicating no significant reduction in IH incidence compared to FAW alone (Fig. 3C). SUCRA analysis (Supplementary File 2, Fig. S7) ranked TS (0.90) as the optimal strategy, followed by FW + FAW + CW + TS (0.88) and FW + FAW+IFW (0.74). The network model exhibited high heterogeneity (I2 = 73.5%, τ2 = 0.19), which was also reflected by wide 95% PI across all comparisons (Supplementary File 2, Table S1). Regarding network inconsistency, the global design-by-treatment interaction test yielded P values of 0.15 and 0.40 under fixed-effects and random-effects models, respectively. Node-splitting analysis revealed no significant local inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence (all Pinconsistency ≥ 0.12, Supplementary File 2, Table S4). Based on studies reporting surgery duration and age, meta-regression indicated that both factors significantly modified treatment effects (P < 0.001 and P = 0.01), partially explaining the observed high heterogeneity. Approaching significant publication bias was detected by the Peters test (P = 0.06, Supplementary File 2, Fig. S8).

A total of 19 studies (1777 patients) compared the effect of single or combined strategies with preoperative warming, network diagram in Fig. 2D. NMA results showed that compared with FAW alone, Pre + FAW [RR = 0.66, 95% CI (0.45, 0.96)] significantly reduced the incidence of IH. Other warming strategies showed numerically lower IH incidence but lacked statistical significance, including Pre + SWB [RR = 0.53, 95% CI (0.20, 1.38)], Pre + CW [RR = 0.64, 95% CI (0.25, 1.66)], and Pre + FW+FAW [RR = 0.76, 95% CI (0.21, 2.74)] (Fig. 3D). SUCRA analysis (Supplementary File 2, Fig. S9) ranked Pre + SWB (0.83) as the optimal strategy, followed by Pre + FAW (0.77) and Pre + CW (0.75). The network model showed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 62.2%, τ2 = 0.18), and the 95% PI for Pre + FAW accordingly crossed 1 (Supplementary File 2, Table S1). Regarding network inconsistency, the global design-by-treatment interaction test yielded P values of 0.13 and 0.18 under fixed-effects and random-effects models, respectively. Node-splitting analysis revealed no significant local inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence (all Pinconsistency ≥ 0.08, Supplementary File 2, Table S5). Based on studies reporting surgery duration and age, meta-regression indicated that both factors significantly modified treatment effects (P = 0.01 and P = 0.008), partially explaining the observed high heterogeneity. Approaching significant publication bias was detected by the Peters test (P = 0.052, Supplementary File 2, Fig. S10).

CNMA analysis revealed that prewarming and FAW were the primary active components, both demonstrating statistically significant independent protective effects against IH (P = 0.005 and P = 0.033, Supplementary File 2, Table S6). Consequently, combined strategies incorporating these core components yielded the most significant incremental benefits.

One study [27] could not be included in any network due to the complexity of warming strategies. This study enrolled 20 patients in both the control and experimental groups (all undergoing liver transplantation): the control group received FW + CW+WWB + AAW (IH incidence: 95.0%), and the experimental group received Pre + FW+FAW + CW+WWB + AAW (IH incidence: 60.0%). The experimental group showed a significantly lower IH incidence [RR = 0.11, 95% CI (0.03, 0.39), P<0.001] compared with the control group.

Intraoperative blood loss

A total of 6 studies involving 583 patients compared intraoperative blood loss between groups with and without active warming. The pooled analysis revealed no significant difference in blood loss between two groups [MD = 1.79, 95% CI (-15.82, 19.34), P = 0.84; I2 = 65.4%, τ2 = 239.58] (Fig. S11, Supplementary File 2).

The incidence of hypothermia in postoperative/PACU

Thirteen studies (1719 patients) reported the incidence of hypothermia in postoperative/PACU, 9 of which were comparable. Compared with no active warming, active warming significantly reduced the incidence of hypothermia in postoperative/PACU [RR = 0.42, 95% CI (0.25, 0.71), P = 0.001; I2 = 27.9%, τ2 = 0.06] (Fig. S12, Supplementary File 2). In addition, implementing preoperative prewarming can further reduce the incidence of hypothermia in postoperative/PACU when using active warming strategies [RR = 0.37, 95% CI (0.15, 0.89), P = 0.03; I2 = 68.7%, τ2 = 0.81] (Fig. S13, Supplementary File 2).

The incidence of shivering in postoperative/PACU

Nineteen studies (1834 patients) reported the incidence of shivering in postoperative/PACU, 17 of which were comparable. Compared with no active warming, active warming significantly reduced the incidence of shivering in postoperative/PACU [RR = 0.35, 95% CI (0.23, 0.53), P<0.001; I2 = 22.3%, τ2 = 0.08] (Fig. S14, Supplementary File 2). In addition, implementing preoperative prewarming can further reduce this incidence when using active warming strategies [RR = 0.55, 95% CI (0.33, 0.95), P = 0.03; I2 = 0, τ2 = 0] (Fig. S15, Supplementary File 2).

The incidence of SSIs

Seven studies (1424 patients) reported the incidence of SSIs, 3 of which were comparable. In three studies using single warming strategy, FAW did not significantly increase the incidence of SSIs [RR = 1.24, 95% CI (0.31, 4.91), P = 0.76; I2 = 43.2%, τ2 = 0.63] (Fig. S16, Supplementary File 2).

Subgroup analysis

A total of 14 studies (1438 patients) included patients with a mean or median age of 60 years or older. Since the 14 studies were divided into two independent networks (network diagram in Fig. 2E), a NMA was performed for the primary network (11 studies, 1258 patients) and a traditional meta-analysis for the secondary (3 studies, 180 patients). The results of the NMA (Fig. S17, Supplementary File 2) showed that combined warming or preoperative prewarming could help reduce the incidence of IH compared with FAW, including FAW + CW [RR = 0.30, 95% CI (0.11, 0.81)], Pre + SWB [RR = 0.50, 95% CI (0.30, 0.83)], and Pre + FAW [RR = 0.62, 95% CI (0.41, 0.93)]. Further continuous network meta-regression on the primary network indicated that patient age significantly modified the treatment effects (P = 0.007). Traditional meta-analysis further supported the potential value of preoperative prewarming in elderly patients: the Pre + FW + FAW [RR = 0.31, 95% CI (0.06, 1.61); I² = 51.9%, P = 0.17] demonstrated a numerical decrease in IH incidence compared with FW + FAW (Fig. S18, Supplementary File 2).

Discussion

This study is the most comprehensive to date, comparing the efficacy of diverse single or combined warming strategies in preventing IH in surgical patients [11–13, 65, 66]. Our findings strongly reaffirm that any form of active warming is significantly superior to no active warming in preventing IH. However, while active warming strategies are effective, their relative efficacies vary, and the optimal choice appears to be context-dependent, influenced by factors such as the modality of warming and the use of preoperative prewarming.

The analysis of single warming strategy revealed that TS consistently ranked highest (SUCRA > 0.9) in preventing IH. Although the point estimates and high SUCRA values suggest a numerical advantage for TS, it must be emphasized that a high ranking probability does not necessarily imply clinically meaningful superiority, particularly given the wide 95% CIs and statistically non-significant differences. This may be attributed to the TS’s ability to provide large-area contact, potentially offering more uniform warming compared to localized systems like FAW. In the comparison of FAW subtypes, the FAW-UND reduced IH incidence more effectively than FAW-LB, FAW-SAB, or FAW-UB, this finding also indicated the importance of contact area and site for maintaining body temperature [67]. The finding that CW, SWB, or FW alone were not significantly superior to FAW further underscores the concept that surface area coverage and the mechanism of heat transfer are critical determinants of effectiveness. However, the application of TS in various surgical types is limited due to the need to wear the entire set on the patient [28–30]. FAW remains the cornerstone active warming strategy; however, the choice of blanket subtype should be tailored to the specific surgical procedure to optimize body temperature maintenance.

Several combined strategies without preoperative warming and any strategies with preoperative warming exhibited numerically reduced IH incidence compared with FAW alone, but none reached statistical significance. Given the wide prediction intervals and residual heterogeneity within these network models, these potential benefits should be interpreted with caution. The combined warming strategy demonstrates potential advantages in maintaining body temperature, but this is also closely related to the increase in medical costs [29]. Although there have been studies supporting the cost-effectiveness of active warming, further RCTs are still needed to provide evidence for implementing combined warming strategy in different regions, populations, and surgical types, especially in vulnerable groups (elderly patients and long duration of surgery) [68, 69]. Preoperative warming is typically performed using FAW for 10–30 min. Current evidence suggests that this is an accessible and clinically effective intervention, as it not only reduces the incidence of IH, but is also associated with less postoperative hypothermia and shivering [70]. For patients who are already scheduled to receive FAW during surgery, preoperative prewarming is straightforward to implement and offers clinical benefits.

Our results showed no significant reduction in blood loss when comparing active warming to no warming with high clinical heterogeneity across included studies. Additionally, this non-significant result reflects the efficacy of the intervention strategy (warming) rather than the direct impact of the outcome event (IH) on blood loss at the individual patient level, thereby does not conclusively rule out the clinically important association between IH and increased blood loss [2, 71].

Regarding the SSIs, there is an ongoing controversy regarding the use of FAW, particularly in clean orthopedic implant surgeries. The underlying hypothesis suggests that FAW might disrupt laminar airflow in ultra-clean operating rooms, potentially mobilizing contaminated floor air into the surgical field [72, 73]. However, it is crucial to separate this theoretical mechanism from actual clinical evidence. Consistent with major clinical guidelines and large-scale observational studies, our network meta-analysis found no significant increase in the risk of SSIs with FAW compared to other warming modalities [74, 75]. Therefore, while the theoretical risk of air contamination exists, the FDA has been unable to identify a consistently reported association between the use of forced air thermal regulating systems and surgical site infection after a thorough review of available data [76]. Clinicians should weigh the established, highly effective thermoregulatory benefits of FAW against these unproven clinical risks, recognizing that abandoning FAW without definitive evidence of harm may expose patients to the known complications [77].

Subgroup analysis demonstrated that combination warming and preoperative prewarming significantly reduces IH incidence in elderly patients. Physiologically, aging markedly reduces the autonomic vasoconstriction threshold and metabolic heat production, making older adults exceptionally vulnerable to redistribution hypothermia. Clinically, these findings advocate implementing proactive prewarming as a routine component of care in geriatric anesthesia to mitigate these age-related physiological risks and minimize severe IH-associated outcomes [78].

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, publication bias persisted in analyses of strategies with and without preoperative warming, suggesting that negative or null results may be underreported. This could overestimate the efficacy of multimodal strategies, as smaller studies with positive findings are more likely to be published. Second, while meta-regression identified age and surgical duration as modifiers, limited reporting in primary trials (e.g., measurement time point, baseline temperature) prevented further analysis of other clinical factors. Finally, this clinical and methodological heterogeneity may challenge the NMA’s transitivity assumption, particularly when comparing complex combined warming strategies.

Conclusion

This network meta-analysis further emphasizes the importance of active warming during the perioperative period. While TS and several combined strategies show potential numerical advantages, they have limitations in application scenarios, cost, and statistical significance. Considering clinical availability, convenience, and economy, FAW remains the pragmatic standard for preventing IH, with the underbody type preferred whenever clinically feasible. We conditionally recommend combining FAW with prewarming and fluid warming for high-risk contexts, particularly for older patients and prolonged surgery. However, given the wide prediction intervals, these added benefits should be interpreted cautiously, and further high-quality trials are warranted to definitively confirm their clinical superiority.
