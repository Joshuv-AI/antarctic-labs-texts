Soil microbial diversity, succession, and greenhouse gas cycling across a Greenlandic glacial chronosequence
Authors: Grace Marsh, Massimo Bourquin, Alanna Leale, Lisa Bröder, Ianina Altshuler, Carsten Jacobsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccelerated glacial retreat in Greenland drives increased development of glacial outwash plains. These newly exposed landscapes provide an opportunity to study microbial dynamics during soil development. We explore soil microbial diversity, community assembly, and biogeochemical cycling across the Kiattuut Sermiat glacial chronosequence (southern Greenland) via 16S rRNA gene amplicon analysis paired with microbial abundance, soil physicochemical parameters, and gas flux data. Microbial diversity varied with soil depth, with more acidic topsoils abundant in Cyanobacteriota and Armatimonadota taxa. While aerobic lower soils likely contained relatively more anoxic pore spaces, hosting aerobic nitrifiers such as Nitrospirota, low-oxygen-associated Planctomycetota taxa, and less characterized Gemmatimonadota. Microbial diversity varied across the chronosequence following distance–decay principles, as communities were predominately dispersal limited and shaped by heterogeneous selection, with greater heterogeneous conditions further from the glacier terminus. Soil CO2 fluxes increased with distance from the glacier, following a shift from autotrophic sulfur- and iron-oxidizing taxa towards heterotrophic members. CH4 fluxes demonstrated greater uptake further from the glacier, and CH4-oxidation was associated with the methanotroph Methylocapsa. Considering the climatic relevancy of Greenlandic proglacial environments, we describe the largely unexplored microbial diversity and biogeochemical cycling of greenhouse gases in these developing soils.Keywords: community assembly, carbon dioxide, proglacial, soil microbiome, methane flux, glacial outwash plain

Introduction

Amplified warming across the Arctic is causing rapid environmental change in Greenland (Saros et al. 2019, Mika Rantanen 2022), with peripheral glaciers loosing ∼35 gigatons of ice per year since the beginning of this century (Hugonnet et al. 2021, Zemp et al. 2025). Glacial retreat across Greenland has driven increased development of proglacial areas, causing a shift in landcover types as vegetation, wetlands, meltwater, and fine sediment expand (Grimes et al. 2024). The total area of Greenlandic proglacial regions remains uncharacterized, although the island consists of ∼20% glacier-free exposed land (410 000 km2) (Henriksen et al. 2000), and yet few studies have focused on the microbiomes of these rapidly changing environments.

Proglacial environments, specifically, provide a useful opportunity for studying soil development, as inorganic, nutrient-poor sediments exposed by glacier retreat undergo development over time through biogeochemical processes (Bradley et al. 2014, Makoto and Wilson 2019). This forms a ‘space-for-time substitution’ known as a chronosequence (Bradley et al. 2014, Makoto and Wilson 2019), where temporal processes, including microbial primary succession during soil development, are demonstrated spatially. However, studies exploring community assembly processes during primary succession are mostly localized to European glacier forefields and focus on plant communities rather than early microbial colonization and assembly processes (Jones and Moral del 2009, Makoto and Wilson 2016, Nascimbene et al. 2017, Makoto and Wilson 2019).

Processes governing community assembly include drift, diversification, and dispersal (Vellend 2010, Hanson et al. 2012, Zhou and Ning 2017). Limited dispersal may contribute to greater β-diversity as isolated communities undergo varying changes (e.g. via stochastic drift and environmental selection), while high dispersal can instead promote homogenization between communities (homogenizing dispersal) (Zhou and Ning 2017). In proglacial environments, dispersal limitation is associated with the early stages of plant primary succession, as certain environmental or biological barriers, such as distance, nutrient limitation, or competition, prevent dispersal and establishment of plants in the community (Hanson et al. 2012, Makoto and Wilson 2016, Makoto and Wilson 2019). Whether this principle can be applied to microbial primary succession is not clear, for instance, research has evidenced that smaller microorganisms are less dispersal limited than larger microorganisms (Schmidt et al. 2014, Zhou and Ning 2017). However, active and passive dispersal mechanisms employed by microorganisms, such as spore formation, dust transport, aeolian deposition via ice nucleation, and active movement within the soil matrix (Šantl-Temkiv et al. 2018, Custer et al. 2022), highlight the complexity of microbial dispersal limitations in proglacial environments. Distance–decay principles, which describe greater community variation with larger geographic distance (Green and Bohannan 2006), are also largely unexplored for soil microbial communities in subarctic proglacial environments.

Community assembly is also influenced by deterministic processes of heterogeneous and homogeneous selection. Homogeneous selection refers to selection pressures that stabilize a community, whereas heterogeneous selection describes pressures that drive divergence in community composition (Zhou and Ning 2017). Studies of microbial selection during primary succession are limited, although greater stochasticity in fungal community assembly compared to bacterial communities has been reported across a glacial chronosequence (Brown and Jumpponen 2014, Schmidt et al. 2014), suggesting deterministic processes such as homogeneous or heterogeneous selection may play a greater role in shaping bacterial communities. Microbial assembly during primary succession requires further investigation in Arctic and subarctic regions, where microorganisms are particularly challenged by limited nutrients (Schütte et al. 2010, Brown and Jumpponen 2014).

Specifically, newly exposed glacial sediment is colonized by autotrophic microbial members, which develop soil organic carbon and nitrogen stocks (Nash et al. 2018, Wietrzyk-Pełka et al. 2020, Varliero et al. 2021), allowing heterotrophic members to establish (Bradley et al. 2016). This is observed as an environmental gradient from the glacier, with increasing organic matter and ion concentrations downstream driving greater microbial cell abundance, vegetation, and decreasing pH (Bárcena et al. 2011, Wojcik et al. 2018, Wietrzyk-Pełka et al. 2020). For simplicity, we will refer to all accumulated sediments at their varying stages of soil formation as ‘soils’. Overall, the dominant bacterial phyla identified in Arctic proglacial soil studies include Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, Acidobacteriota, and Cyanobacteriota (Górniak et al. 2017, Wojcik et al. 2018, Pessi et al. 2019, Varliero et al. 2021, Venkatachalam et al. 2021), in addition to phyla such as Chloroflexota and Planctomycetota, prevalent in Arctic glaciers and glacial streams (Girard et al. 2023, Marsh et al. 2024). However, microbial diversity in Greenlandic proglacial environments is largely unexplored with current studies limited to meltwater community analysis in West and South Greenland (Dubnick et al. 2017, Kohler et al. 2020, Vinšová et al. 2022, Vrbická et al. 2022, Ezzat et al. 2025), and even further constrained for soil proglacial microbiome studies, which are unique only to Russell Glacier and Mittivakkat Valley (Bárcena et al. 2010, Nash et al. 2018, Varliero et al. 2021).

Further, Arctic proglacial environments are sites of CH4 biogeochemical cycling and generally exhibit net CH4 uptake (Christiansen et al. 2015). Soil CH4 flux gradients have been identified in Greenlandic glacial outwash plains, with CH4 production in newly exposed soils shifting to CH4 uptake in older soils, as the microbial community changes from methanogenic to methanotrophic members (Bárcena et al. 2010). Increasing methanotrophic abundance is largely determined by oxygen availability, as the anoxic subglacial environment transitions to aerobic soils (Bárcena et al. 2010). These Greenlandic environments predominately host type II methanotrophs (Bárcena et al. 2010, Christiansen and Jørgensen 2018), such as Methylocystis sp. and Methylocapsa acidophila (Bárcena et al. 2011), which favour low oxygen and high CH4 conditions (Nguyen et al. 2021). Greater CH4-oxidation rates have been evidenced in the top 5–15 cm of vegetated and unvegetated upland soils in Greenland, highlighting the influence of oxygen conditions on methanotrophic distribution and activity with soil depth (Juncher Jørgensen et al. 2024).

Here, we explore the soil microbiome of the Kiattuut Sermiat outwash plain in southern Greenland to understand soil microbial primary succession and biogeochemical cycling in these evolving environments. We hypothesize (i) increased cell abundance and diversity across the chronosequence, with dispersal limitation shaping bacterial diversity during this primary succession, (ii) soil physicochemical parameters such as pH and soil depth to associate with bacterial diversity, and (iii) increased CO2 emissions across the chronosequence as more developed soils support greater heterotrophic respiration, and greater CH4 uptake by methanotrophic taxa in more oxygenated soils further from the glacier. Investigating the soil microbiomes of these expanding proglacial regions will provide insight into the processes underlying microbial community assembly during primary succession and highlight the climatic relevancy of greenhouse gas cycling in these shifting landscapes.

Materials and methods

Study sites and sampling

Soil samples were collected from the Kiattuut Sermiat outwash plain in Southern Greenland during June 2023. The glacial outwash plain soils are classified as fluvio-glacial deposits, consisting of glacial flour and sand (Henriksen et al. 2000). The chronosequence spans ∼12 km from glacier to fjord, with soil dating suggesting deglaciation from an approximate 1500-year-old ice margin, occurring during the late-Holocene before the Little Ice Age maximum (Winsor et al. 2014).

We sampled across seven sites consisting of replicate subsites (Fig. S1), from topsoils (0–5 cm) and lower soils (10–15 cm) (Fig. 1). The 59 total samples included soil deposits on the glacier, unvegetated soils across the outwash plain, and soil from a moss-vegetated site (Site 1 Vegetated). Soil temperature at both depths was recorded in situ (Fig. S2). Soil depths were measured with a ruler, and samples were collected with a sterile spade into sterile Whirl-Pak bags, then transported on ice before storage at –20°C.

Soil CO2 and CH4 gas fluxes

Soil gas fluxes were sampled using a static chamber approach following methods by Collier et al. (2014). Chambers were installed at Sites 1–4, and gas samples were collected from the headspace over a 4-h time series at 0, 30, 60, 105, 165, and 255 min, except for Site 4 at 0, 30, 45, and 60 min due to weather safety concerns. Samples were extracted with a syringe and stored at 4°C in evacuated 12 ml Exetainer vials (part 839) (Labco, UK) prior to analysis. Air temperature, chamber size, and surface area were recorded for gas flux calculations. CH4 and CO2 sample concentrations were analysed with the LICOR 7810 Trace Gas Analyser via triplicate 1 ml injections in an open-loop setup (LICOR, USA). The samples were run on a carrier gas of zero air (20% O2 in N2). A composite standard gas (containing 462 ppm CO2 and 2.072 ppm CH4) was also measured with 1 and 0.5 ml injections to calibrate the standard curves for each measuring period, following LICOR’s recommendations for small sample injections in open-loop setups (LICOR, USA). Changes in gas concentrations over sampling time were determined by fitting linear regressions across the first 105 min of the time series. Soil gas fluxes were calculated from this slope, using the ideal gas molar volume (0.0224 m3 mol−1), chamber volume, surface area, and air temperature (Collier et al. 2014).

Soils were prepared for pH and ion concentration analysis by drying at 30°C for 24 h and sieving at 2 mm. For pH, soil was added to 0.01 M CaCl2 (1 : 2.5 W: V ratio) and shaken for 1 h (120 r/m), following methods from Tagami and Uchida (2025). The solution was settled for 1 h and was measured with the Orion pH meter (Thermo Scientific, Germany). Soils were prepared separately for ion concentration measurements in deionized water (1 : 5 W: V ratio) and shaken for 1 h (120 r/m) (Tagami and Uchida 2025). The solution settled for 1 h, and the liquid layer was transferred to a new vial for analysis to avoid soil particles interfering with ion measurements. Ions Ca2+, Cl−, K+, Mg2+, Na+, and NH4+ were measured in addition to electrical conductivity (EC) (dS/m) using the Imacimus10 ion probe (NT Sensors, Spain) with 3 calibration standards (22HP08) (detection range between 36 and 360 mg Ca2+ l−1, 75–750 mg Cl− l−1, 39–390 mg K+ l−1, 23–230 mg Na+ l−1, 4–40 mg NH4+ l−1, 11–110 mg Mg2+ l−1). Ion concentrations in the original soil samples were calculated from the diluted measurements.

EC was calculated based on the conversion factor of 6.7 for fine grain size soils (Slavich and Petterson 1993). Grain size distribution analysis for each sample was performed after sieving (2 mm) and chemical dispersion in sodium hexametaphosphate. Size distributions reported from laser diffraction via the Mastersizer 2000 Particle Size Analyser (Malvern Panalytical, UK) were reported as mean grain size (d50).6

Cell enumeration via heterotrophic plate counts and flow cytometry

Cells were first separated from the soil matrix following a modified protocol based on Górniak et al., with 1 g soil prepared in 9 ml PBS (1x) and gently shaken for 2 h at 10°C (120 r/m) (Górniak et al. 2017). Samples were incubated vertically for 15 min at 10°C, and the liquid layer containing the suspended cells was transferred via pipette (Górniak et al. 2017). From the liquid layer, a dilution series was prepared in PBS, with 500 μl of 10−2 and 10−3 dilutions plated on R2A agar media (Neogen Culture Media, USA) (Hansen et al. 2007, Górniak et al. 2017, Dziurzynski et al. 2023). Agar plates were incubated at 12.5°C (average temperature across sites) and colony-forming units counted after 3 weeks (Dziurzynski et al. 2023).

Samples were prepared for flow cytometry following the same modified protocol based on Górniak et al. (2017), in addition to a centrifuge density gradient method based on Frossard et al. (2016). After shaking, samples were fixed with formaldehyde (3.7%) and stored in the dark at 4°C. Samples were briefly vortexed to homogenize, and 1 ml of soil slurry was placed on 500 μl Histodenz® solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) (1.3 g ml−1) (Frossard et al. 2016). The solution was centrifuged for 90 min at 4°C (17 000 × g) to separate cells from soil particles via a centrifuge density gradient (Frossard et al. 2016, Khalili et al. 2019). The upper layer containing cells was transferred via pipette and prepared in a 10−2 dilution of SYTO®13 (50 μM). Sample triplicates were aliquoted in a 96-well black plate and incubated in the dark for 15 min before analysis via the NovoCyte Flow Cytometer (ACEA Bioscience, USA) (flow rate 14 μl min−1), with subsequent gating avoiding soil autofluorescence.

DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, and analysis

DNA extractions were performed with the DNAeasy Powersoil Pro kit (Qiagen, Germany) using the following modifications to account for lower DNA concentrations. The initial soil weight was increased to 1 g, and cells were lysed in a homogenizer (2 × 30 s at 5500 r/m, 30 s pause). The final extracted DNA, in addition to a negative extraction control, was eluted in two 50 µl washes, and vacuum concentrated (30°C).

PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA full-length gene was performed with 27f (5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492r (5′-GTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) primers (Frank et al. 2008), with LongAmp Taq 2X Master Mix DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, USA), followed by PCR purification with Magnetic AMPure Beads XP (Beckman Coulter, USA). The purified PCR product, including a negative PCR control, was prepared for long-read sequencing with the Native Barcoding Kit 96 V14 and sequenced on a R10.4.1 flow cell with MinION Mk1C (MinKNOW v23.07.12) (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK).

The 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing data were base-called and demultiplexed with dorado v0.7.3 (super accuracy mode). Adaptor sequences were removed with Porechop v0.2.4, and reads were filtered by length (between 1200 and 1800 bp) and quality (Phred score > 12) using chopper v0.9.0 (Wick and Volkening 2018, Coster De and Rademakers 2023). Amplicon sequence variants were clustered at the operational taxonomic unit (OTU) level to account for the higher error rate of long-read sequencing. We utilized the reference-free tool, CONCOMPRA to cluster full-length 16S rRNA gene sequences based on sequence identity and calculate OTU abundances across samples (Stock et al. 2024). Consensus sequences generated from each OTU cluster were taxonomically assigned with the Qiime2 v2024.5.0 feature classifier using the SILVA_138_99_nb database (Quast et al. 2013, Bolyen et al. 2019).

Statistical analysis

Bacterial diversity and community assembly processes

Statistical analyses were performed in R using the package phyloseq v1.48.0 (McMurdie and Holmes 2013). Samples with less than 1000 reads were filtered out, and singletons were removed. OTUs assigned as Bacteria were used for further analysis, after filtering out Chloroplasts and Mitochondria. OTUs from the negative extraction and negative PCR controls were removed from the analysis. Bacterial names were adjusted to current nomenclature where possible (Oren and Garrity 2021), including the reclassification of Thiobacillus genus from the family Hydrogenophilaceae to Thiobacillacaeae (Boden et al. 2017).

From relative OTU abundances, within-site diversity was calculated with Shannon indices and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (picante v1.8.2) (Kembel et al. 2010). For each alpha diversity metric, significant differences between sites were determined via Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test and Dunn post hoc tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction (Butler et al. 2019).

OTU abundances were transformed with cell numbers obtained from flow cytometry using relative OTU read counts per sample multiplied by cell number to achieve absolute abundances (Props et al. 2017). The transformed counts, referred to as absolute abundances, were used in downstream analysis for β-diversity metrics.

The filtered OTU consensus sequences were input into Qiime2 phylogeny to construct a rooted tree (Bolyen et al. 2019). Variation between sites was calculated from these phylogenetic distances and absolute OTU abundances via weighted UniFrac distances (Lozupone et al. 2007). Differences between and within sites were compared with the Wilcoxon rank sum test (ggpubr v0.60) (Kassambara 2023). Distance decay relationships were explored via Mantel test (with Spearman correlations) and linear regression of the weighted UniFrac distances against geographic distances between sites. Similarly, Bray–Curtis dissimilarities between sites were also calculated and compared to the weighted UniFrac distance decay relationship via linear regression to highlight the influence of phylogenetic distance (Hanson et al. 2012, Ezzat et al. 2025).

Further, community assembly processes contributing to this variation between sites were identified with the iCAMP v1.5.12 package using the relative OTU abundances and phylogenetic distances (Ning et al. 2020). The relative importance of community assembly processes underlying the observed changes in diversity was quantified between samples, based on beta Net Relatedness Index and taxonomic β-diversities (Raup-Crick). Significant differences between sites, depths, and vegetated conditions were calculated with comparisons via the bootstrapping function. Site comparisons were false discovery rate (FDR) corrected, and significant groups were selected (P-adj < .05 and Cohen’s d ≥ 0.5) (Ning et al. 2020). The contribution of these assembly processes to sample variation was then explored through linear regression with weighted UniFrac distances, and linear regression with geographic distance.

Environmental variables

Cell abundances and soil physicochemical properties were evaluated with nonparametric Spearman correlations with FDR adjustment. Soil gas fluxes were taken at a subset of sites and were analysed separately with Spearman correlations to glacier distance. For PCA analysis, cell abundances were log normalized and all variables were z-score standardized with the vegan package v2.8-8 (Oksanen et al. 2013). Log-normalized cell abundances estimated from flow cytometry and CFU counts were compared with Wilcoxon signed rank test.

The 16S rRNA gene amplicon data were analysed through ordination with non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) of weighted UniFrac distances. Significant (FDR adjusted, P-adj < .05) abiotic and biotic variables, including centred log ratio (CLR) normalized absolute phyla abundances (microbiome v1.26.0) (Lahti et al. 2017), were fitted to the ordination.

Significant differences in variation between and within groups were calculated via PERMANOVA of the weighted UniFrac distances, testing the effect of site, depth and their interaction (full model site * depth). Pairwise multilevel comparisons were performed with the package pairwiseAdonis v0.4.1, and P-values were FDR adjusted (Martinez Arbizu 2020).

Paired sample analysis between soil depths was performed with the nonparametric Wilcoxon signed rank test for the standardized environmental variables, with FDR correction. Differential abundance between soil depths was analysed at the genera level with ANCOM-BC v2.6.0 on the absolute OTU abundances, using structural zero detection, Holm–Bonferroni P-value adjustment, and the remaining default parameters (Lin and Peddada 2024).

Taxonomic clustering and enrichment analysis

To identify taxonomic patterns along the chronosequence, OTUs were clustered according to their abundance profile across the samples via weighted correlation network analysis with the package WGCNA v1.73 (Langfelder and Horvath 2008). A core subset of CLR normalized relative OTU abundances, prevalent across at least 5% of the samples, was analysed following a previously published approach (Wilson 2018), which generated a network based on a weighted OTU co-occurrence adjacency matrix. For WGCNA, the adjacency between OTUs was calculated based on Pearson correlations transformed by a soft thresholding power of 6, selected by scale-free topology analysis (Wilson 2018). Modules were selected after clustering with a minimum module size of 20 OTUs. These modules were merged based on a co-occurrence similarity of 70% and associated with environmental variables via Spearman correlations, with FDR correction (Wilson 2018).

Significantly enriched families were identified in each taxon module. Specifically, for each module, the total number of OTUs from each family was compared to the total number of OTUs from that family across the entire dataset using a one-sided hypergeometric test to identify over-represented OTUs, with P-value FDR correction (Falcon and Gentleman 2008). Enriched families (P-adj < .01) were further selected based on total abundance (summarized from absolute OTU abundance across samples) greater than 5% of the respective module total abundance.

Finally, we summarized absolute abundances of methylotrophic genera for each sample in the 16S rRNA gene amplicon dataset. Methylotroph total abundance across samples was compared between depths and vegetated conditions using Wilcoxon rank sum tests. The potential methanotrophic relation of Methylocapsa was investigated via linear regression of absolute abundances against CH4 fluxes.

Results

Environmental gradients of the Kiattuut Sermiat outwash plain

We explored the microbial diversity of the Kiattuut Sermiat outwash plain in the context of primary succession and soil development by first characterizing the interactions between soil physicochemical properties across the chronosequence (Fig. 2A) (Figs S2 and S3). We observed a significant salinity (EC) gradient from the glacier to the fjord from 0.067 to 5.29 dS m−1 (rs = 0.40, P-adj = .019, n = 59) with Cl− increasing (rs = 0.71, P-adj < .001) and Ca2+ decreasing (rs = −0.47, P-adj = .003) (Fig. S3). This was paired with a general increase in Na+ at the fjord site (Fig. S2). pH generally increased across the outwash plain soils towards the fjord from pH 5.6 to 6.5 (Fig. S3), although this trend was not significant (Fig. S2), while pH at the glacier site varied (Fig. S3). Soils closer to the glacier and fjord exhibited greater water content, while midway sites were relatively drier (0.12%–42.74% GWC). Soil water content significantly correlated with mean grain size (rs = −0.44, P-adj = .002), as finer-grained clay soils retained more water than larger-grained sandy soils (Fig. S2). Topsoils (0–5 cm) were significantly warmer (P-adj < .001, n = 40) and demonstrated lower pH (P-adj = .012) compared with lower soils (10–15 cm) (Fig. 2B). Soil gas fluxes were analysed from a subset of the sites, excluding the glacial and fjord locations. Soil gas fluxes varied greatly between sites and biological replicates, specifically CH4 fluxes ranged from −0.84 to 0.15 nmol m−2 s−1 and revealed greater uptake further from the glacier (rs = −0.43, P = .017) (Fig. 2C), while CO2 fluxes ranged from −0.02 to 0.83 μmol m−2 s−1 and demonstrated increasing emission further from the glacier (rs = 0.34, P = .065) (Fig. 2D, Fig. S4).

Soil microbiomes of the glacial outwash plain

Microbial cell abundances, estimated via flow cytometry, ranged from 4.1 × 103 to 4.5 × 106 cells g−1, whereas viable counts ranged from below detection to 6.5 × 105 CFU g−1 (Fig. S2). Overall, flow cytometry demonstrated greater recovery than estimates from spread plate culturing (P < .001, n = 59), particularly for the glacier and fjord soils (Fig. S5). Cell abundances did not increase along the chronosequence, although more culturable cells were recovered from warmer soils (rs = 0.53, P-adj < .001) and soils with higher pH (rs = −0.59, P-adj < .001). Additionally, cell abundances (flow cytometry estimates) were greater in finer-grained soils (rs = −0.41, P-adj = .015).

Full-length 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing resulted in 4 304 038 reads with an N50 of 1494 bp and mean read quality of 14.3. Of the 1 109 441 reads assigned to OTUs, 848 846 reads were retained after filtering. Soil microbial community composition and diversity, via 16S rRNA analysis, revealed a total of 288 unique genera from 2402 filtered OTUs, predominantly from Pseudomonadota, Acidobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Planctomycetota, and Actinomycetota phyla (Fig. 3A, Figs S6 and S7). Alpha diversity varied between sites (Kruskal–Wallis χ² = 16.49, P = .011, n = 49) with diversity lowest at the glacier (Shannon mean 4.25), while the outwash plain recorded higher diversity values (Shannon mean 4.68–5.30) (Fig. 3B). Although mean microbial diversity of the vegetated soils was lower (Shannon mean 4.80) than that of the unvegetated soils (Shannon mean 4.97), this difference was not significant (Dunn post hoc P-adj = 1). Top and lower soils exhibited similar alpha diversity, with no significant difference between depths (Kruskal–Wallis χ² = 2.59, P = .11). Alpha diversity inferred from Faith’s phylogenetic diversity also revealed similar trends to Shannon diversity (Fig. S8).

Weighted UniFrac distances from absolute OTU abundances (relative abundances transformed by cell counts) increased with geographic distance between sites (Mantel r = 0.23, P = .002), suggesting β-diversity follows distance decay principles (Fig. 3C). Bray–Curtis dissimilarities increased with geographic distance between sites (Mantel r = 0.41, P = .001) to a greater extent than UniFrac distances (Δslope = 0.004, P < .001). Further, lower soils demonstrated a similar distance decay relationship (Mantel r = 0.3, P = .002) to topsoils (Mantel r = 0.23, P = .002), with no significant difference in this trend between the two depths (Δslope < 0.001, P = 0.63) (Fig. S9).

Environmental factors shaping the soil microbiome

The association between community composition and environmental parameters via NMDS analysis of weighted UniFrac distances revealed significant variation in microbial community composition between sites and soil depths (Fig. 4A, NMDS stress = 0.184, n = 49) (Fig. S10, PERMANOVA F = 2.24, r2 = 0.45, P < .001, n = 49). Distinct microbial communities were especially observed at the fjord site (Site 5; Fig. 4A). Significant environmental variables associated with this variation included glacier distance, water content, salinity, and various ions (Na+, Cl−, K+, and NH4+). In addition, the abundance of Actinomycetota, Desulfobacterota, Pseudomonadota, and Verrucomicrobiota phyla was also associated with variation across sites (NMDS envfit, P-adj < .05). Further, Armatimonadota phylum was associated with the vegetated samples (r2 = 0.49, P-adj = .006).

Taxa contributing to variation between soil depths included Acidobacteriota, Gemmatimonadota, Planctomycetota, Cyanobacteriota, and Bacteroidota (Fig. 4A). Specifically, differential abundance analysis revealed bacterial genera, which significantly varied with depth (P-adj < .05, n = 49) (Fig. 4B). The most significantly abundant genera included Crinalium_SAG_22.89, Yoonia-Loktanella, and Rhodopseudomonas in the topsoils and Subgroup_22, OM190, and AKAU4049 in lower soils (Fig. 4B). Cyanobacteriota and Deinococcota genera were associated with topsoils, while certain Acidobacteriota, Actinomycetota, Bacillota, Chloroflexota, Entotheonellaeota, Gemmatimonadota, NB1-j, Nitrospirota, and RCP2-54 genera were associated with lower soils. We also identified ice nucleating genera Pseudomonas and Pseudoxanthomonas, which did not significantly differ between soil depths (Wilcoxon rank sum test P > .05, Fig. S11).

Dispersal limited microbial community assembly

To determine the microbial community assembly processes during primary succession, we explored the contribution of dispersal limitation, drift, heterogeneous selection, homogeneous selection, and homogenizing dispersal. Dispersal limitation was the dominant assembly process between all samples across the outwash plain (mean 54.06% ± 1.30%), indicating dispersal limiting factors largely contribute to community turnover (Fig. 5A). The relatively large importance of drift (mean 27.00% ± 1.45%) is evidence of stochastic processes shaping the community across this environment. Interestingly, the relative importance of heterogeneous selection (mean 12.21% ± 0.79%) was greater than homogeneous selection (mean 1.49% ± 0.16%), suggesting that habitat heterogeneity is driving community composition changes.

Community assembly processes significantly differed between soil depths (Fig. 5B) and between unvegetated and vegetated soils (Fig. 5C). Topsoils demonstrated significantly greater homogeneous (d = 3.055, P = .012) and heterogeneous selection (d = 2.57, P = .037) than lower soils. While dispersal limitation was significantly less important in unvegetated compared to vegetated soils (d = −2.43, P = .048).

We investigated community turnover within each site and identified community assembly changes along the chronosequence. Dispersal limitation significantly varied between sites and was greatest at the fjord (mean 60.10% ± 1.68%), while drift was lowest at this site (mean 17.91% ± 1.18%) (Fig. 5D). Notably, the mean relative importance of heterogeneous selection increased across the chronosequence, from 6.73% ± 01.07% to 13.96% ± 01.61% (Fig. 5D). Homogenizing dispersal on average decreased towards the fjord; however, this trend was not significant.

We further explored the importance of each community assembly process in relation to β-diversity and geographic distance between sites (km) (Fig. S12A). Dispersal limitation (slope = 0.046, r2 = 0.0014, P = .1) and heterogeneous selection (slope = 0.14, r2 = 0.043, P < .001) increased with weighted UniFrac distance, while drift (slope = −0.11, r2 = 0.013, P < .001), homogenizing dispersal (slope = −0.058, r2 = 0.025, P < .001), and homogeneous selection (slope = −0.025, r2 = 0.013, P < .001) significantly decreased (Fig. S12A). Similar trends occurred across geographic distance between sites, including a significant increase in dispersal limitation with greater geographic distance (slope = 0.0042, r2 = 0.018, P < .001) (Fig. S12B).

Distinct taxonomic patterns across the chronosequence

Taxonomic succession across the chronosequence was characterized to identify specific taxa associated with soil development in this environment. A core subset of 2083 OTUs was prevalent across at least 5% of the samples (representing 99.77% of total relative abundance) and clustered into 11 distinct modules, based on their abundances across the samples. Various modules significantly correlated with glacier distance (P-adj < .05), as six modules were associated with soils closer to the glacier terminus and one module was associated with soils near the fjord (Fig. 6A). Two modules (Modules 4 and 10) were associated with soils near the glacier terminus and negatively correlated with CO2 emissions in the topsoils (Fig. 6A). Enrichment analysis demonstrated that Module 4 was composed of Pseudomonadota families Sphingomonadaceae, Gallionellaceae, Hydrogenophilaceae, Candidatus_Roizmanbacteria, and Ilumatobacteraceae (Fig. 6B), while Module 10 was enriched in Acidobacteriota families Vicinamibacteraceae, Subgroup_7, Nitrosomonadaceae, Gemmatimonadaceae, and Fimbriimonadaceae (Fig. 6B). In contrast, Module 9, associated with soils near the fjord, significantly correlated with more basic and saline soils and was enriched in Flavobacteriaceae and Rhodobacteraceae families (Fig. 6A, B).

Microbially mediated methane-oxidation

We explored microbial taxa in the context of methane cycling by focusing on methanotrophic and methylotrophic genera detected in the outwash plain soils. We identified one methanotrophic genus across all soils, Methylocapsa, which demonstrated a modest increase in abundance with methane uptake in both the topsoils (r2 = 0.38, P = .011) and lower soils (r2 = 0.79, P < .001) (Fig. S13). Further, we identified methylotrophic taxa from Beijerinckiaceae, Hyphomicrobiaceae, Methyloligellaceae, Methylophilaceae, and RCP2-54 families. Topsoils hosted greater methylotrophic abundance overall; specifically, topsoils were significantly more abundant in Methylotenera (P = .005) and Hyphomicrobium (P = .023), while RCP2-54 was greater in the lower soils (P = .013) (Fig. S14A). Hyphomicrobium abundance was also greater in the unvegetated soils (P = .007) compared to the vegetated soils at the same site (Fig. S14B). Varying trends in methylotrophic abundance were also observed across the chronosequence (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Glacial outwash plains are expanding across Greenland with increased glacial retreat (Hugonnet et al. 2021, Zemp et al. 2025) and provide valuable study sites for investigating successional processes, yet soil microbial diversity, function, and assembly processes are still largely unexplored (Bárcena et al. 2010, Nash et al. 2018, Varliero et al. 2021). Here, we focus on identifying environmental drivers and community assembly processes shaping the soil microbiome in these rapidly expanding biomes.

Environmental and taxonomic patterns

In our study, the soil bacterial communities were dominated by phyla consistent with other Greenlandic and Arctic proglacial soil studies (Górniak et al. 2017, Wojcik et al. 2018, Pessi et al. 2019, Varliero et al. 2021, Venkatachalam et al. 2021). Increasing CO2 flux from −0.02 to 0.83 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1 suggests greater respiration across the chronosequence further from the glacier terminus, as demonstrated in other glacial outwash plain studies (Jones MH et al. 2000, Guelland et al. 2013, Thomazini et al. 2015), which indicates a potential shift from autotrophic to heterotrophic microbial members (Bradley et al. 2016, Nash et al. 2018, Wietrzyk-Pełka et al. 2020, Varliero et al. 2021). Notably, microbial cell abundance did not increase along the chronosequence, which supports a potential change in community function and activity. In addition, flow cytometry cell enumeration demonstrated greater recovery than spread plate culturing, particularly at the glacier and fjord soils, likely as these sites hosted more extremophile bacteria which could not be cultured on the prepared media (Props et al. 2017). Soils near the glacier terminus were abundant in Pseudomonadota families Sphingomonadaceae (Han et al. 2025), chemolithotrophic iron-oxidizing Gallionellaceae (of the genera Gallionella and Sideroxydans) (Hoover et al. 2023), and sulfur-oxidizing Thiobacillacaeae (Thiobacillus genera) (Toubes-Rodrigo et al. 2021), identified previously in glacial surface (Han et al. 2025) or basal ice (Toubes-Rodrigo et al. 2021). These taxa function in carbon cycling via CO2 fixation, which is consistent with lower CO2 gas fluxes observed from these soils. Additionally, the Cyanobacteriota Pseudanabaenaceae were enriched near the glacier, while Nostocaceae and Coleofasciculaceae were abundant at the fjord; however, these taxa did not significantly associate with CO2 uptake. The ammonia-oxidizer Nitrosomonadaceae (Prosser et al. 2014) and Fimbriimonadaceae, which can perform dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (Trinh et al. 2025), were also abundant near the glacier, suggesting conserved nitrogen cycling within these soils. Other prevalent taxa align with previous findings from cryosphere environments, including Ilumatobacteraceae, Gemmatimonadaceae, Vicinamibacteraceae, Subgroup_7 (Acidobacteriota phyla), and Candidatus_Roizmanbacteria (Patescibacteria phyla) families (Zeng et al. 2021, Bourquin et al. 2022). While taxa associated with the fjord site included Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Desulfobacterota, all of which are common to marine and terrestrial environments, and the latter with anaerobic sediments (Murphy et al. 2021). More specifically, soils near the fjord were abundant in heterotrophic marine-associated Rhodobacteraceae and Flavobacteriaceae families, which metabolize complex polysaccharides and proteins (Gavriilidou et al. 2020, Liang et al. 2021).

The glacial outwash plain soils demonstrated a net CH4 sink, with fluxes from −0.84 to 0.15 nmol CH4 m−2 s−1. In the context of other proglacial Greenlandic soils, this CH4 uptake is stronger than that previously reported in Mittivakkat Valley forefield soils across a smaller 1.3 km chronosequence (−0.0024 to −0.013 nmol CH4 m−2 s−1) (Bárcena et al. 2010) but similar to glacial floodplain soils reported in Kangerlussuaq (−0.7 nmol CH4 m−2 s−1) (Juncher Jørgensen et al. 2024). CH4 uptake was associated with the one methanotrophic genus identified, Methylocapsa, which is consistent with other type II methanotrophs previously identified in Greenlandic proglacial soils (Bárcena et al. 2011). We broadened this search to methylotrophic species as they are sometimes associated with methanotrophs due to their utilization of intermediates produced during methanotrophy (Iguchi et al. 2015). Abundance of methylotrophs varied across the chronosequence. In particular, Methylophilaceae was enriched in wetter soils near the glacier, with greater abundance of Methylotenera family, a methylotroph previously reported from lake water and freshwater sediments (Li et al. 2025). Methylotroph abundance shifted across the outwash plain as soil-associated taxa such as Methylocapsa (Bárcena et al. 2011) and phyllosphere-associated Methylobacterium–Methylorubrum increased (Green and Ardley 2018), before the marine sediment-associated Methyloceanibacter increased in abundance near the fjord (Vekeman et al. 2016). Notably, Hyphomicrobium and Methylotenera were more abundant in topsoils, while greater abundance of the aerobic RCP2-54 in lower soils suggests oxygenated conditions at this depth (Rasmussen et al. 2024). The primers used in this study target bacteria, and as such we did not recover any archaeal taxa and could not investigate methanogenic members.

Overall, we recognize the challenge in disentangling the effect of the salinity gradient from successional processes relating to soil development. The influence of the fjord was indicated by an increase in salinity and water content along the chronosequence, with salinity likely varying with tidal influences. Furthermore, other chronosequence studies observe decreasing pH gradients, whereas we identified increasing pH across the outwash plain towards the more alkaline fjord water (Bárcena et al. 2011, Wietrzyk-Pełka et al. 2020). In addition, this outwash plain is annually impacted by a glacial lake outburst flood. This briefly increases the glacial river volume and transports sediment across the outwash plain, potentially depositing sediment from the glacier. During our sampling year, this flooding event occurred several months after sampling (9 September 2023); however, as this is an annual event, it may significantly disrupt microbial distributions across this environment and confound the influence of the chronosequence.

Soil depth drives variation in microbial community composition

Environmental drivers shaping community composition were largely associated with soil depth, as bacterial communities significantly varied between top and lower soils across the outwash plain. Topsoils hosted more abundant Cyanobacteriota, including Nostoc, and genera previously identified in biological soil crusts such as Crinalium and Wilmottia (Mikhailyuk et al. 2019, Zhang et al. 2021). Soil crust development was further explored in the vegetated topsoils, which were linked with greater abundance of Armatimonadota, a phylum of mostly aerobic oligotrophs previously identified in arid soil crust communities (Meier et al. 2021). The topsoils were also abundant in drought-resistant and cold-adapted genera such as Deinococcus (Cox and Battista 2005), which is highly resilient to ionizing radiation, and oligotrophic genera such as Tundrisphaera (Kulichevskaya et al. 2017). These adaptations are consistent with high summertime sun exposure of the outwash plain, in addition to drought-like conditions arising from the poor water retention of these predominately inorganic soils (Henriksen et al. 2000).

In the lower soils, nitrifying Nitrospira and ammonia-oxidizing Ellin6067 were more abundant (Shi et al. 2023), in addition to sulphur-oxidizing Sulfurifustis (Umezawa et al. 2016), suggesting aerobic conditions. Abundant genera in the lower soils were generally less characterized, including AKAU4049 (from Gemmatimonadota) (Ye et al. 2024), the CH4-oxidation-associated RCP2-54 (from RCP2-54 phylum, also known as Binatota) (Murphy et al. 2021), the low-oxygen-environment-associated Pir4 lineage (Dedysh et al. 2020), Subgroup_22 (Acidobaceriota), and NB1-j (Pushpakumara et al. 2023). These genera are largely uncharacterized due to their challenging growth conditions and the relatively limited studies focusing on deeper soils, highlighting the importance of investigating different soil depths.

Microbial diversity and distance decay along the chronosequence

While alpha diversity is generally found to increase with soil development (Schütte et al. 2010, Varliero et al. 2021, Venkatachalam et al. 2021), we observed similar alpha diversity across the chronosequence except for the glacial site, where lower Shannon diversity and Faith phylogenetic diversity suggest more closely related taxa dominate these oligotrophic soil deposits. Alpha diversity was also lower in the vegetated soils, consistent with findings from other newly vegetated proglacial soils (Mapelli et al. 2018, Feng et al. 2023). Although alpha diversity is generally reported to increase with vegetation given more available nutrients, newly vegetated proglacial soils have been found to demonstrate lower diversity (Mapelli et al. 2018), likely as specialized microorganisms associated with pioneer plants compete (Feng et al. 2023), resulting in a more homogeneous community.

Beta diversity across geographic distances revealed a distance–decay relationship. Distance–decay inferred from Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was greater than that from phylogenetically informed weighted UniFrac distances, suggesting that some abundant clades in the phylogeny were abundant in most samples (Lozupone et al. 2007, Hanson et al. 2012, McMurdie and Holmes 2013, Ezzat et al. 2025). Interestingly, the more physically isolated lower soils demonstrated a similar distance–decay relationship to the upper soils. Likely the soil depth of 10–15 cm did not reduce microbial mixing to the extent that distance–decay relationships could be distinguished between depths. However, long-read sequencing is prone to higher error rates, which can inflate alpha and β-diversity, with increased dissimilarity subsequently weakening distance–decay relationships (Hanson et al. 2012). Although we utilized a very conservative approach with CONCOMPRA clustering at the OTU level to account for long-read sequencing errors (Stock et al. 2024), this higher error rate may diminish similarity between sites and underrepresent the real distance–decay relationship.

Dispersal limitation shaping soil bacterial assembly from glacial retreat

Analysis of microbial β-diversity revealed dispersal limitation as the primary assembly process across the Kiattuut Sermiat outwash plain (Fig. 5A). Limited dispersal reduces community mixing, such that drift or selection pressures may promote dissimilarity between more isolated communities (Hanson et al. 2012). Hence, dispersal-limited microbial assembly is supported by and likely promoting the distance–decay relationship we observed. Further, we identified an increasing relative importance of dispersal limitation on community assembly with geographic distance between sites (Fig. S12B), mirroring this distance–decay relationship. This trend of increasing dispersal limitation with geographic distance (km) was also observed between glacial-fed streams with a higher slope of 0.0239 (Ezzat et al. 2025), which may be representative of the dynamic nature of these streams, which are likely more fragmented than soils from a single outwash plain. Regardless, bacterial establishment across the outwash plain may be limited by priority effects of colonizing taxa or other mechanisms reducing dispersal. Interestingly, we found the relative importance of drift decreased with increasing β-diversity, while heterogeneous selection increased (Fig. S12A), suggesting that community variation, within the context of limited dispersal, may be driven by local selection pressures more than stochastic changes. These local selection pressures appear to increase across the chronosequence (Fig. 5D), suggesting greater heterogeneity amongst soils further from the glacier terminus.

The importance of these selection pressures is further evidenced when comparing soil depths, where topsoils were more influenced by both heterogeneous and homogeneous selection pressures compared to lower soils. This greater selection in the topsoils likely arises as topsoils more readily interact with the atmosphere, where they are exposed to extreme surface conditions with environmental pressures such as higher UV, potential desiccation, and large variations in air temperature. Further, heterogeneous selection was relatively more important than homogeneous selection, suggesting that selection pressures in the topsoils are driving community change more than stabilization. Despite the lower soils being more physically isolated compared to the topsoils, the lower soils were not more dispersal limited, consistent with the similar distance–decay relationship we observed for both soil depths.

We also identified greater dispersal limitation at the vegetated site, which could be due to competition of specialized bacteria in the vegetated soils limiting establishment of other bacteria (Feng et al. 2023), consistent with the lower alpha diversity observed. Further, the role of bacteria in forming symbiotic relationships, in the context of soil crusts, with other prokaryotes or eukaryotes was not explored in this study but may be a significant factor contributing to microbial assembly processes (Schmidt et al. 2014, Pessi et al. 2019).

Conclusions

Based on our study, the bacterial communities of glacial outwash plain soils are likely dispersal limited, with increasing heterogeneous selection suggesting greater heterogeneity amongst soils further from the glacier terminus. Further, greater heterogeneous and homogenous selection pressures influenced the bacterial communities in the more exposed topsoils. Topsoils were characterized by photosynthesizing and nitrogen-fixing Cyanobacteria, and other taxa involved in soil crust formation. While lower soils were associated with nitrifying bacteria. Additionally, taxa associated with low oxygen conditions were more prevalent in deeper soils (10–15 cm), suggesting relatively more anoxic pore spaces within the soil matrix compared to the top 5 cm soils. While likely containing anoxic pore spaces, these deeper soils also demonstrated aerobic conditions, which were supported by identification of aerobic methylotrophs such as RCP2-54. Overall, CH4 uptake across the chronosequence was associated with the methanotroph, Methylocapsa, and various methylotrophic genera. Further, iron-oxidizing and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria were associated with soils close to the glacier terminus, while soils further away shifted to heterotrophic taxa, which was also reflected by greater CO2 flux emissions across the chronosequence. Future efforts focusing on quantifying soil organic carbon, soil age, and microbial functional activity via metagenomics and metatranscriptomics would further support analysis of prokaryotic and eukaryotic contributions to soil development in these environments. Additionally, the impacts of glacial lake outburst flood events on the ecology of this system require greater investigation. Regardless, Greenlandic proglacial soil microbiomes are largely understudied, and here we describe bacterial abundance and diversity, community assembly processes, and biogeochemical processing relating to CH4 and CO2 cycling, highlighting the climatic relevance of these developing soils. Large-scale trends of CO2 production in exposed and developing soils due to deglaciation may contribute to Arctic warming feedback loops, which may be partially mitigated by CH4 uptake in these sites. Further, bacterial diversity and community assembly were shaped by both successional dynamics and spatial variation between soil depths, indicating the variability in biogeochemical cycling across these soils and the complexity in predicting accurate greenhouse gas fluxes from these developing landscapes.
