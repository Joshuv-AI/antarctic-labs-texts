Semaglutide Reverses Ectopic Lipid Accumulation, Impaired Myocardial Perfusion Reserve, and Diastolic Dysfunction in a Mouse Model of Cardiometabolic Heart Disease
Authors: Thomas P Skacel, Nemati R Saleh, Caitlin M Pavelec, Kenny Quach, Leigh A Bradley, Julia E Bresticker, Amit R Patel, Matthew J Wolf, Frederick H Epstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey Words: coronary microvascular dysfunction, epicardial adipose tissue, GLP-1RA, HFpEF, obesity

Obesity and the associated expansion of visceral adipose tissue (VAT) are central to the pathogenesis of cardiometabolic heart disease, and are increasingly recognized to underlie its progression to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).1, 2, 3 Excess VAT promotes a chronic inflammatory state through the release of proinflammatory adipokines, which act via endocrine and paracrine pathways to alter cardiac structure and function.4 Notably, ectopic fat deposition around the heart such as intramyocardial lipid and epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) contributes to proinflammatory signaling, oxidative and nitrosative stress, and myocardial fibrosis.5,6 These processes promote coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD),7 as well as cardiac structural remodeling,8,9 strain abnormalities,10 diastolic dysfunction,11 and ultimately HFpEF. Despite the rising prevalence of obesity-induced cardiometabolic heart disease, effective therapies remain limited, underscoring the urgent need to identify interventions that can slow, halt, or reverse disease progression.

Recent clinical trials have shown that the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) semaglutide (SEMA) causes significant weight loss12 and improves outcomes in obesity-related cardiovascular disease.13 In patients with HFpEF, SEMA reduces physical limitations14,15 and lowers the risk of worsening heart failure events.16 It has also been associated with improved diastolic function17 and reduced EAT volumes,18,19 although the underlying cardioprotective mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Preclinical studies demonstrate that SEMA can prevent diastolic dysfunction and fibrosis in a mouse model of HFpEF independently of changes in dietary intake,20 but it is unclear whether it can reverse established features of cardiometabolic heart disease. Furthermore, the effects of SEMA on coronary microvascular function, myocardial lipid, and EAT fatty acid composition (FAC)—a cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) marker of EAT inflammation21—are unknown.

The present study sought to determine whether SEMA can reverse established features of obesity-induced cardiometabolic heart disease in mice, utilizing multiparametric CMR. Mice fed a high-fat, high-sucrose (HFHS) diet for 18 weeks develop key features of cardiometabolic heart disease, including obesity, glucose intolerance, ectopic lipid accumulation, CMD, cardiac strain impairments, diastolic dysfunction, and interstitial fibrosis.22, 23, 24 Given that SEMA’s weight loss effects are mediated in part by appetite suppression, we further investigated whether its cardioprotective benefits occur independently of its effects on reduced dietary intake.

Methods

Experimental design

All animal studies were performed in accordance with protocols that conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki as well as the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals25 and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Virginia. All mice were maintained at the University of Virginia Center for Comparative Medicine pathogen-free vivarium facility.

Experiments were designed to test the following: 1) whether SEMA can reverse established CMR features of obesity-induced metabolic heart disease in mice after 18 weeks of HFHS diet; and 2) whether changes are independent of SEMA-induced reductions in dietary intake. A schematic overview of the experimental timeline is shown in Figure 1. Four groups of male C57BL/6J mice (The Jackson Laboratory; strain #000664) were studied. One group was fed standard chow diet to serve as healthy controls, while the remaining 3 groups were fed an HFHS diet (40% kcal fat, 40% kcal sucrose; Diet 12327, Research Diets Inc) for 18 weeks to establish cardiometabolic heart disease. Diets were initiated at 10 weeks of age and were continued for 18 weeks. Following 18 weeks of HFHS diet, mice were randomized into 3 groups: SEMA-treated HFHS mice, pair-fed (PF) HFHS mice, and HFHS controls. Mice in groups fed an HFHS diet received daily subcutaneous injections of SEMA (9 nmol/kg/d, up titrated over 5 days) or vehicle (phosphate-buffered saline). Because SEMA reduces food intake,26 pair-feeding was used to distinguish effects of reduced consumption from direct drug effects.27 PF mice received food amounts matched to the average daily intake of SEMA-treated mice. Body weights and food intake were measured daily. To avoid potential confounding effects of social hierarchy under dietary restriction, mice in all groups were individually housed 2 weeks before the first CMR examination and remained single-housed until sacrifice.

Experimental Protocol

10-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were fed either standard diet (SD) or high-fat, high-sucrose (HFHS) diet for 18 weeks, after which mice underwent glucose tolerance testing (GTT) and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR). Mice were given a 1-week interval before starting treatment (SD: n = 15; HFHS: n = 15; HFHS + pair-feeding [PF]: n = 16; HFHS + semaglutide [SEMA]: n = 16), which continued for 28 days before repeat GTT and CMR at 23 weeks. Mice remained on treatment until a subgroup underwent sacrifice for histological analysis at 24 weeks (SD: n = 12, HFHS: n = 9, HFHS + PF: n = 12, HFHS + SEMA: n = 10). For epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) fatty acid composition (FAC) experiments, an independent cohort of mice (HFHS: n = 12; HFHS + PF: n = 12; HFHS + SEMA: n = 12) was used and underwent CMR after 5 days of treatment.

All mice underwent multiparametric CMR at 18 weeks postdiet, with 1 week for recovery before initiation of 4 weeks of treatment. For multiparametric CMR studies (n = 15-16/group), the protocol included the following: 1) arterial spin labeling (ASL) at rest and stress with adenosine to quantify myocardial blood flow (MBF) and myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR);28 2) displacement encoding with stimulated echoes (DENSE) to measure circumferential strain (Ecc) and peak early diastolic strain rate (PEDSR);29 3) multiecho, gradient echo imaging to measure myocardial proton density fat fraction (PDFF);21,23 and 4) black blood cine imaging covering the left ventricle (LV) to measure cardiac structure and function including LV mass, end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), ejection fraction (EF), and EAT volumes.30 These scans were repeated after 4 weeks of treatment. Imaging at each timepoint was separated over 2 sessions performed 2 to 3 days apart. Glucose tolerance tests (GTTs) were performed at 18 weeks postdiet and repeated after 4 weeks of respective treatment. After 24 weeks on diet, a subgroup of mice (n = 9-12/group) were sacrificed for histological analysis.

For EAT FAC studies, an independent cohort of mice (n = 12/group) underwent an abridged CMR protocol before and after 5 days of treatment; EAT FAC and volume were not assessed in standard diet mice because of insufficient quantity of EAT for reliable CMR measurements. This cohort did not undergo GTT and its associated overnight fasting or adenosine stress CMR, which were performed in the main cine cohort. This CMR protocol included multiecho, gradient echo imaging21,23 to measure EAT PDFF and FAC including the EAT saturated fatty acid fraction index (SFAi), the ratio of saturated fatty acid fraction in the EAT to subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), which has been shown to correlate with inflammatory markers,21 and black blood cine imaging to measure EAT volumes. This protocol was performed in a single session.

CMR protocol

All CMR studies were performed on a 9.4-T small animal MRI system (Biospec 94/20, Bruker Biospin) using a 1H transmit-receive quadrature volume radiofrequency coil with a 35-mm inner diameter (Bruker BioSpin GmbH). The radiofrequency coil had a physical length of 334 mm, with an internal coil structure extending 30 mm in the z-direction. Mice were anesthetized with 1% isoflurane and maintained at 36.0 ± 0.5 °C using circulating warm water. During CMR examinations, heart rate, core body temperature, and respiration were monitored with an MR-compatible animal monitoring system (SA Instruments).

EAT FAC and myocardial lipid imaging

FAC and PDFF mapping was performed on a short-axis midventricular slice using an electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated multiecho gradient echo sequence, as previously described.21,23 This sequence used a golden-angle radial sampling scheme across multiple echo times (TEs) to achieve incoherent undersampling. No explicit respiratory motion compensation was applied, as this radial sequence has been previously shown to provide reproducible measurements despite respiratory motion.21 Imaging parameters included: number of TEs: 20; TE1: 1.4 ms; ΔTE = 0.2 ms; flip angle: 15°; slice thickness: 1.0 mm; number of radial spokes: 21; field of view (FOV): 25 × 25 mm2; matrix size: 128 × 128; resolution = 0.2 × 0.2 mm2; total scan time approximately 20 minutes.

Myocardial perfusion imaging

Rest perfusion imaging of a midventricular short-axis slice was performed using a radial ECG-triggered and respiratory self-gated ASL method.28,31 To compensate for respiratory motion, the center of k-space was used to extract a respiratory motion signal, which was then used for retrospective respiratory binning. Subsequently, k-space lines acquired during end-inspiration were removed during reconstruction, thereby reducing respiratory motion-related artifacts while preserving acquisition efficiency. Adenosine (600 μg/kg/min) was infused for 10 minutes via an indwelling intraperitoneal catheter. Stress perfusion imaging was then performed while continuing to infuse adenosine. Imaging parameters for ASL included: TE: 2.5 ms; TR: 10.0 ms; FOV: 25.6 × 25.6 mm2; matrix size: 128 × 128; flip angle: 7°; slice thickness: 1.0 mm; saturation band thickness: 2.5 mm; number of averages: 9; resolution = 0.2 × 0.2 mm2; total scan time approximately 10 minutes per rest or stress scan.

Black blood cine imaging

A short-axis stack of 6 to 8 cine images was acquired covering the LV from base to apex using an ECG-triggered black blood gradient echo cine method.30 Images were acquired without respiratory gating, but used multiple signal averages to minimize respiratory motion artifacts. Imaging parameters included: TE: 1.35 ms; TR: 4.0 ms; FOV: 25.6 × 25.6 mm2; matrix size: 128 × 128; flip angle: 15°; slice thickness: 1.0 mm; number of averages = 4; resolution = 0.2 × 0.2 mm2; total scan time approximately 2 min/slice.

Myocardial strain imaging

DENSE strain imaging was performed on a midventricular short-axis slice as previously described using prospective ECG-triggering and respiratory gating.29 Imaging parameters included: TE: 2.45 ms; TR: 7.0 ms; FOV: 32 × 32 mm2; matrix size: 128 × 128; flip angle: 15°; slice thickness: 1.0 mm; number of averages: 4; resolution = 0.25 × 0.25 mm2; total scan time approximately 15 minutes.

CMR image analysis

Image analysis was performed using Matlab 2024b (MathWorks). Rest and stress perfusion images were analyzed for quantification of MBF using previously described methods.28 MPR was calculated as the ratio of stress to rest MBF. DENSE strain images were analyzed using the DENSE analysis tool.32,33 Global circumferential strain and PEDSR were measured as metrics of systolic and diastolic function, respectively. Black blood cine images were analyzed using Segment Version 4.1.0.1 R14284b. End-diastolic and -systolic frames were identified, and the endocardial and epicardial borders were manually traced on these frames across all slices. Using Segment, EDV, ESV, EF, and LV mass were calculated. Cine images were also analyzed to quantify the EAT volume index, defined as the EAT volume (μL) normalized to body weight (g). EAT was manually segmented at end-diastole for all slices and the total EAT volume was calculated as the product of the segmented pixel count and the voxel volume.

For EAT FAC and PDFF quantification, incoherently undersampled multiecho data were reconstructed using a higher-order singular value decomposition approach, as previously described.21,34 Parametric maps of FAC and PDFF were generated by fitting the complex signal across echo times to a multiresonance triglyceride signal model, which models the fat signal as a sum of 9 distinct resonances with known chemical shifts and relative amplitudes.35, 36, 37, 38 This allows for the quantification of the mean number of double bonds and methylene-interrupted double bonds per triglyceride, which can be used along with an assumed average chain length to yield the relative proportions of saturated, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated fatty acids in each voxel.37 PDFF was calculated as the ratio of the total fat signal to the sum of the water and fat signal within each voxel. For image analysis, EAT and SAT regions-of-interest (ROI) were manually segmented on fat-only images. To reduce partial volume effects and ensure that ROIs contained primarily adipose tissue, voxels within each ROI with a <50% PDFF were excluded. EAT quality metrics were indexed to SAT, a metabolically healthier adipose depot.39, 40, 41, 42 Indexing to SAT as an intrasubject reference accounts for individual variability in inherent adipose tissue physiology, including lipid metabolism, deposition, and synthesis.42,43 FAC CMR indexes calculated in this way have previously been shown to correlate with histological and cytokine markers of EAT inflammation.21

Glucose tolerance testing

After a 16-hour overnight fast, baseline blood samples were collected from the tail vein to measure fasting blood glucose. Mice were then administered an intraperitoneal injection of sterile glucose (2 g/kg body weight in deionized water) and additional blood samples were collected at 10, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes postinjection to measure blood glucose levels. Glucose tolerance was assessed by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) using trapezoidal approximation.44

Histology

Hearts were collected after CO2 induced death. Tissues were fixed in 10% formalin for 4 hours and transferred to 70% ethanol before paraffin embedding. Paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 10-μm sections, deparaffinized, and stained with picrosirius red (PSR) according to standard protocols. PSR imaging was performed on a Leica LAS X Multi Channel Acquisition. Images were taken with a 20× objective. PSR staining was analyzed using FIJI with the color deconvolution macro (340), set to RGB. The red channel was used for quantification of fibrotic area. A minimum of 5 PSR images were taken per mouse and fibrotic area was averaged across all images.

Statistics

All data are expressed as mean ± SD, unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0 (GraphPad Software). For repeated measures data, normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test and inspection of Q-Q plots of the residuals, and 2-way repeated measures analysis of variance was used followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference post hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons. For histological data, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test; when the assumption of normality was not met, a Kruskal-Wallis test was performed with Dunn’s post hoc test. For all analyses, a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Semaglutide promotes weight loss and improves glucose tolerance

Mice treated with SEMA exhibited a reduction in dietary intake for approximately 10 to 14 days, after which food consumption returned to levels similar to HFHS controls (Figure 2A). The dietary intake of HFHS+PF mice was matched to the daily average intake of SEMA-treated mice. Mice in all groups fed an HFHS diet for 18 weeks had significant weight gain compared with standard diet controls (Figures 2B and 2C). Following treatment, both HFHS+PF and HFHS+SEMA mice exhibited significant weight loss; however, HFHS+PF mice achieved less weight loss compared with SEMA-treated mice at the 23-week timepoint (body weight: 39.42 ± 4.40 g vs 34.55 ± 2.99 g; P = 0.001). Glucose tolerance tests performed before (Figure 2D) and after (Figure 2E) the 4-week treatment, along with corresponding AUC measurements (Figure 2F), demonstrated significant improvements in glucose tolerance in both HFHS+PF and HFHS+SEMA groups. However, HFHS+PF mice exhibited much less improvement, with significantly higher AUC values compared with HFHS+SEMA mice at the 23-week timepoint (AUC: [43.20 ± 8.15] × 103 min·mg/dL vs [28.32 ± 7.29] × 103 min·mg/dL; P < 0.001).

EAT FAC is modified in semaglutide-treated mice independently of changes in EAT volume after 5 days of treatment

To assess the effects of SEMA treatment on EAT quantity and quality, we used CMR to measure EAT volume, PDFF, and FAC following a 5-day treatment period in HFHS, HFHS+PF, and HFHS+SEMA mice. At 5 days post-treatment, both HFHS+PF and HFHS+SEMA mice lost similar amounts of body weight compared with HFHS controls (Supplemental Figures 1A and 1B). Example short-axis images showing EAT for all groups after treatment are shown in Figure 3A, with red arrows indicating EAT. EAT volume index was significantly reduced after 5 days of treatment in both HFHS+PF (1.00 ± 0.46 μL/g vs 1.88 ± 0.47 μL/g; P < 0.001) and HFHS+SEMA (0.77 ± 0.47 μL/g vs 1.91 ± 0.46 μL/g; P < 0.001) mice (Figure 3B). We observed no significant differences in EAT PDFF before or after treatment (Figure 3C). Example EAT FAC maps from the same animal before and after SEMA treatment are shown in Figure 3D. Interestingly, despite comparable reductions in EAT volume index between HFHS+PF and HFHS+SEMA mice, only HFHS+SEMA mice exhibited a significant reduction in the EAT SFA index, a marker of proinflammatory EAT, following 5-day treatment (0.85 ± 0.12 vs 0.99 ± 0.09; P = 0.006), which was significantly lower than that of HFHS and HFHS+PF mice (Figure 3E). EAT SFA was also significantly reduced following 5-day SEMA treatment (32.7% ± 4.0% vs 37.7% ± 3.6%; P = 0.002). HFHS+SEMA mice also demonstrated trends toward reduced ESV and increased EF by cine imaging (Supplemental Figures 1E and 1F).

HFHS diet-induced impairments in MPR are reversed by 4 weeks of semaglutide

We performed rest and adenosine-induced stress perfusion imaging in all groups before and after 4 weeks of treatment to assess coronary microvascular function. Example rest and stress MBF maps from a mouse before and after SEMA treatment are shown in Figure 4A. Rest MBF was similar across all groups both before and after treatment (Figure 4B). After 18 weeks of diet, groups fed an HFHS diet exhibited significant reductions in stress MBF and corresponding reductions in MPR compared with standard diet controls (Figures 4C and 4D). SEMA treatment significantly improved stress MBF at 23 weeks compared with at 18 weeks (10.18 ± 1.85 mL/g/min vs 8.48 ± 1.96 mL/g/min; P = 0.015), with a corresponding increase in MPR (1.87 ± 0.27 vs 1.55 ± 0.17; P = 0.004). In contrast, HFHS+PF mice showed no significant improvement in either stress MBF or MPR at the 23-week timepoint. Mice in all groups at both timepoints demonstrated a significant increase in heart rate in response to adenosine (Figures 4E and 4F), confirming successful infusion of the vasodilator. At the 23-week timepoint, resting heart rate was significantly elevated in SEMA-treated mice compared with mice in the other groups (Figure 4F).

Ectopic lipid accumulation is reduced with 4 weeks of semaglutide treatment

To evaluate the effects of 4-week SEMA treatment on ectopic lipid accumulation, we used CMR to measure EAT volume index and myocardial PDFF. SEMA treatment significantly reduced EAT volume index at week 23 compared with pretreatment measurements (0.36 ± 0.20 μL/g vs 0.75 ± 0.26 μL/g; P < 0.001), and this value was significantly lower than those of HFHS and HFHS+PF mice at week 23 (Figure 5A). HFHS mice had an increase in EAT volume index over the treatment period (0.93 ± 0.29 μL/g vs 0.76 ± 0.26 μL/g; P = 0.033), whereas HFHS+PF mice did not. In the myocardium, SEMA treatment significantly reduced myocardial PDFF (11.0% ± 3.3% vs 14.8% ± 7.0%; P = 0.047), and this value was significantly lower than that of HFHS mice at week 23 (Figure 5B).

Semaglutide prevents worsening LV hypertrophy and improves LV function

Cine imaging showed that LV mass increased in HFHS control mice over the 4-week treatment period (90.4 ± 11.2 mg vs 84.7 ± 10.3 mg; P = 0.019), and both HFHS and HFHS+PF mice had significantly increased LV mass at the 23-week timepoint (Figure 5C). Treatment with SEMA prevented further increases in LV mass in HFHS+SEMA mice over the 4-week period (82.6 ± 14.4 mg vs 84.9 ± 10.8 mg). There were no significant differences in EDV across groups or timepoints (Figure 5D). SEMA-treated mice also had a reduction in ESV (0.014 ± 0.004 mL vs 0.019 ± 0.006 mL; P = 0.001) (Figure 5E) with improvements in EF (74.0% ± 4.4% vs 66.2% ± 6.0%; P < 0.001) (Figure 5F), although EFs in all groups at all timepoints were preserved (>50%).

Strain impairments are reversed with 4 weeks of semaglutide treatment

DENSE CMR was used to evaluate the effects of SEMA treatment on systolic and diastolic function. SEMA reversed subclinical impairments in end-systolic circumferential strain (−0.154 ± 0.014 vs −0.136 ± 0.012; P < 0.001), whereas HFHS and HFHS+PF mice did not have significant systolic strain improvements over the treatment period (Figure 6B; representative strain maps Figure 6A). Diastolic function (PEDSR) was also improved in HFHS+SEMA mice (4.08 ± 0.65 s−1 vs 3.29 ± 0.51 s−1; P = 0.001), whereas HFHS and HFHS+PF mice showed no significant improvements in diastolic function over the treatment period (Figure 6C).

Interstitial fibrosis is ameliorated in semaglutide-treated mice

After euthanizing mice at the 24-week timepoint, we analyzed PSR stained sections of heart tissue to investigate changes in interstitial myocardial fibrosis following SEMA treatment. Representative images (Figure 6D) and quantification (Figure 6E) demonstrated elevated levels of interstitial fibrosis in HFHS and HFHS+PF mice compared with standard diet controls, with HFHS+SEMA mice having similar levels of fibrosis to standard diet controls. HFHS+SEMA mice had significantly lower levels of interstitial fibrosis compared with HFHS mice (2.31% ± 0.28% vs 5.41% ± 1.06%; P = 0.002).

Discussion

We utilized multiparametric CMR to show that SEMA treatment reversed established cardiovascular dysfunction induced by 18 weeks of HFHS diet. Specifically, SEMA reduced intramyocardial lipid accumulation as measured by CMR proton density fat fraction, reduced EAT volume index, improved CMR biomarkers of proinflammatory EAT, improved adenosine MPR, improved both systolic and diastolic function assessed by DENSE CMR, and attenuated interstitial cardiac fibrosis. The inclusion of a PF control group demonstrated that the cardioprotective effects of SEMA were largely independent of changes in dietary intake.

Comparison of SEMA-treated mice with PF controls showed that the cardiometabolic benefits of SEMA cannot be attributed solely to reduced food intake. Consistent with prior studies,45, 46, 47, 48 SEMA-treated animals transiently reduced dietary intake, with intake returning to near HFHS control levels after roughly 2 weeks; yet, they maintained sustained weight loss. PF mice lost weight after the initial reduction in food but subsequently regained it, resulting in a smaller net weight loss than SEMA-treated animals. Our results demonstrate that the EAT volume index closely parallels the observed changes in body weight. Specifically, although the PF group exhibited an initial, transient loss of EAT volume and body weight during the first 5 days, these reductions did not persist for 4 weeks. Instead, body weight rebounded in the PF group, with the final EAT volume index at week 4 returning to near pretreatment values. In contrast, the SEMA group showed initial reductions in EAT volume and body weight that were maintained throughout the entire 4-week study. Supporting a possible mechanistic explanation, recent work by Shah and Ayala48 showed that energy expenditure remains preserved during SEMA treatment despite changes in energy intake. By contrast, caloric restriction alone in rodents causes a compensatory decline in energy expenditure.45 Accordingly, compensatory reductions in energy expenditure in PF mice, but not in SEMA-treated mice, may underlie the differences in EAT volume and body weight observed after 4 weeks of treatment.

Although 5-day reductions in body weight and EAT volume index were comparable between HFHS+SEMA and HFHS+PF mice, only SEMA-treated mice exhibited changes in EAT FAC as assessed by SFAi. CMR measurements of EAT SFAi have been correlated with proinflammatory cytokine levels and macrophage infiltration,21 and our observed reduction in SFAi is consistent with a shift away from a proinflammatory EAT FAC. The reduction in SFAi in SEMA-treated mice compared with PF controls may reflect SEMA-induced changes in EAT fatty acid metabolism, altering the production, uptake, and utilization of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids. SEMA has been shown to reduce hepatic saturated fatty acid content by suppressing de novo lipogenesis,49 a pathway that preferentially generates saturated fatty acids, which could contribute to the lower SFAi observed in the EAT of SEMA-treated mice relative to PF controls. As fasting has been shown to favor the mobilization of unsaturated fatty acids,50,51 our data suggest that SEMA may shift fatty acid metabolism in EAT toward mobilization of saturated fatty acids, representing a pattern distinct from caloric restriction and potentially contributing to the differences in EAT composition observed between HFHS+SEMA and HFHS+PF mice. The observed changes toward a less proinflammatory EAT FAC align with known anti-inflammatory effects of SEMA on VAT. Withaar et al20 used RNA sequencing to show that transcriptional pathways in VAT from SEMA-treated mice were enriched for protective anti-inflammatory responses compared with PF mice despite similar reductions in VAT in a HFpEF model, and Martins et al52 demonstrated that SEMA reduced macrophage infiltration, increased lipolysis, and promoted browning in VAT. Furthermore, studies in isolated human EAT have shown that SEMA reduces proinflammatory adipogenesis.53 Although prior imaging studies of SEMA have primarily focused on EAT volume,18,19 our work provides further insights into the effects of SEMA on CMR biomarkers of EAT FAC which may further characterize EAT quality. Additionally, after 4 weeks of treatment, we found a reduction in myocardial lipid in HFHS+SEMA mice, measured by myocardial PDFF, whereas HFHS+PF mice exhibited no significant reduction in myocardial PDFF. Together, these findings suggest that SEMA may exert its cardioprotective effects in part through actions on adipose tissue, reducing ectopic lipid and shifting EAT toward a less inflammatory, more metabolically favorable phenotype. Additional studies using recently developed EAT FAC CMR methods in humans37 are warranted to establish the clinical utility of these findings.

By using serial, noninvasive CMR measurements, we demonstrated that SEMA induces reversal, rather than just prevention or attenuation, of key features of cardiometabolic heart disease. This distinction is clinically meaningful, as reversal of established symptoms more closely mirrors the therapeutic context in which patients receive treatment after disease onset. Established CMD is estimated to be present in ∼75% of HFpEF patients.54 Our results show that SEMA can reverse existing CMD in mice measured by adenosine MPR. Abdesselam et al55 showed that exendin-4 treatment increased MBF under isoflurane vasodilation in HFHS mice, although they did not measure MPR. Recent work by Chowdhary et al56 showed that liraglutide improves CMR measurements of MPR measured with dobutamine-induced hyperemia in patients with type II diabetes. In addition, we observed that resting heart rate was elevated in mice treated with SEMA. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that GLP-1RAs induce heart rate elevations in both animal models and humans.57, 58, 59, 60 Our study is the first to demonstrate that SEMA reverses impaired MPR using adenosine, a vasodilator typically used in clinical practice.61 By comparison, prior work from our group investigating the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor empagliflozin demonstrated that, despite its high effectiveness in preventing the development of cardiometabolic heart disease,62 it was unable to reverse established impairments over a treatment timeline comparable to the present study in HFHS-fed mice.63 Prior work in HFHS-fed mice showed that inhibition of inducible nitric oxide synthase (NOS2) also reverses impaired MPR, and SEMA may affect MPR through the same pathway, as proinflammatory EAT and myocardial lipid are triggers of M1-polarized macrophages which are sources of NOS2.24 These findings suggest that SEMA may provide an effective treatment for CMD, which may contribute to its cardioprotective benefits observed clinically.

We observed a reversal of diastolic and subclinical systolic strain impairments, as well as reduced interstitial fibrosis in HFHS+SEMA mice. These findings are consistent with those of Withaar et al,20 who found that SEMA prevents the development of diastolic dysfunction and fibrosis in a HFpEF mouse model. Similarly, our observed improvements in diastolic function are consistent with the clinical findings of the echocardiography substudy of the STEP-HFpEF (Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity and HFpEF) program, which showed that SEMA improves indexes of diastolic function, including E/e′ ratio and left atrial remodeling, in obese patients with HFpEF.17 Fibrosis is considered an end-stage result of adverse myocardial remodeling, and is associated with poor prognosis in HFpEF.64 The reduction of fibrosis with SEMA-treatment likely contributes to the improvements in strain and PEDSR by reducing mechanical stiffness of the myocardium. Given the central role of diastolic dysfunction and fibrosis in the pathophysiology of HFpEF, these improvements likely contribute to the clinical benefits seen in patients treated with SEMA.

Together, our findings support a pathophysiologic model in which obesity and excess proinflammatory adipose tissue drive cardiovascular dysfunction. Ectopic lipid accumulation in EAT and within the myocardium promotes secretion of proinflammatory adipokines, which stimulates macrophage recruitment into the myocardium and M1 polarization, driving local and systemic inflammation and oxidative and nitrosative stress.4 This adipokine-mediated inflammation contributes to CMD through paracrine and vasocrine signaling, leading to fibrosis, adverse remodeling, and impairments in myocardial strain. Our results highlight how SEMA, likely through its actions on adipose tissue, may represent a promising therapeutic approach for cardiometabolic heart disease.

Study limitations

Although SEMA-treated mice exhibited lower levels of interstitial fibrosis compared with HFHS and HFHS+PF controls, our study did not definitively establish that SEMA reverses already-established cardiac interstitial fibrosis. Additional CMR studies using contrast-enhanced T1 mapping to quantify extracellular volume fraction could clarify the time course and nature of these effects.

Although we did show that SEMA’s effects are independent of changes in dietary intake, our study design did not allow us to determine whether the observed benefits of SEMA are independent of weight loss, because the PF model controlled only for dietary intake rather than matching the degree of weight reduction. Recent work has shown that more aggressive calorie restriction is required to achieve weight loss comparable to that seen with SEMA,45 and additional studies would be needed to determine whether the observed benefits of SEMA are completely independent of weight loss.

These studies were only conducted in male mice, limiting generalizability across sexes. Premenopausal female mice develop milder cardiac dysfunction in response to diet-induced obesity, and their VAT exhibits a more anti-inflammatory phenotype.65,66 However, recent studies in postmenopausal female mouse models indicate greater susceptibility to metabolic and cardiac dysfunction.67,68 Future studies in these models are needed to investigate potential sex-specific effects of SEMA on cardiovascular function and adipose tissue characteristics.

Conclusions

Multiparametric CMR methods applied in an HFHS model of cardiometabolic heart disease show that SEMA treatment modifies noninvasive biomarkers of proinflammatory EAT and reverses ectopic lipid accumulation, CMD, and systolic and diastolic strain impairments. Cardiac fibrosis was also reduced in SEMA-treated mice. These findings were found to be largely independent of changes in dietary intake caused by SEMA. Collectively, these findings advance our mechanistic understanding of SEMA’s effects in obesity-related cardiometabolic heart disease.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Although SEMA has demonstrated meaningful clinical benefit in patients with obesity and HFpEF, the mechanistic basis for its cardiovascular effects remains incompletely defined. In particular, how SEMA modulates myocardial and adipose tissue metabolism, coronary microvascular function, and cardiac mechanics in the setting of cardiometabolic disease is not fully understood. Using multiparametric CMR imaging, we demonstrated that SEMA reverses key pathological features of obesity-related cardiometabolic heart disease in mice, including ectopic lipid accumulation, impaired MPR, and diastolic dysfunction. These findings provide mechanistic insight into how GLP-1RAs may restore cardiometabolic homeostasis and improve cardiac function.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: By leveraging clinically translatable multiparametric CMR techniques, we systematically interrogated ectopic lipid deposition, myocardial perfusion, and cardiac function in a preclinical model of obesity-driven cardiometabolic heart disease. Our results suggest that SEMA improves symptoms and outcomes not solely through weight reduction, but by reversing key pathophysiologic features of cardiometabolic heart disease, including excess ectopic lipid accumulation, microvascular dysfunction, and impaired diastolic function. These data provide a mechanistic framework to guide biomarker development and support further clinical investigation of GLP-1RA therapies as potential disease-modifying strategies in cardiometabolic heart disease.
