The PLAN trial: precision treatment of lumbar spine surgery with advanced nerve root blockade – protocol for a prospective, investigator-initiated, randomized non-inferiority trial
Authors: David Kocemba, Peter Andreas Andersen, Jakob Gram Carlsen, Mette Katrine Schulz, Bent Uhrbrand, Hanne Dinesen, Ronni Mikkelsen, Mathias Thygesen, Mikkel Mylius Rasmussen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLumbar radiculopathy, due to nerve root compression, leads to significant pain and functional impairment. Surgical decompression is a treatment option, but not all patients achieve good outcomes. This protocol for an ongoing randomized controlled trial aims to compare the outcomes of surgical planning with a diagnostic selective nerve root block and surgical planning without a diagnostic selective nerve root block (standard of care).MethodsThe PLAN trial is a multicenter, block-randomized, controlled, non-inferiority trial that will enroll patients diagnosed with unilateral lumbar radiculopathy. Participants who have been deemed surgical candidates will be randomly assigned to one of two groups: Group A will receive standard surgical decompression, while Group B will undergo selective nerve root block to determine what nerve root is causing the patient’s pain, followed by surgical decompression or conservative management. The primary outcome measure is the difference in the Numerical Rating Scale for Leg Pain from baseline to 3 months with a non-inferiority margin of 1.6 points. Secondary outcomes are the difference in proportions of patients reaching ≥ 1.6 points on the Numerical Rating Scale for Leg Pain, the difference in scores and time course on Numerical Rating Score for Leg Pain and Back Pain, Short-Form 36, EuroQol-5D-3 L, and Oswestry Disability Index. Analysis will also be performed after 1 year.ConclusionThis trial seeks to provide high-quality evidence on the effectiveness of integrating selective nerve root blocks to guide surgical decompression or avoid surgery in treating lumbar radiculopathy. The findings aim to inform clinical decision-making and optimize patient outcomes.​Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov registration NCT06143813. Registration date November 9, 2023. Introduction

Lumbar radiculopathy

Lumbar radiculopathy and radicular pain are common conditions. They typically result from lumbar disc herniation (LDH) or spinal stenosis, which causes mechanical nerve compression [1]. The incidence of radiculopathy is around 5 per 1,000 person-years and is the leading indication for spine surgery [2, 3]. The estimated annual surgery cost for this condition is $15 billion in the U.S. alone, and with an aging population, this is estimated to increase [4, 5].

Patient evaluation includes symptoms, a neurological evaluation, and imaging. The decision to offer surgical treatment for radiculopathy is based on clinical and objective findings while considering the surgeon’s experience and preference. While several decompressive nerve root surgical approaches have been developed, none of these techniques have shown clear superiority in leg pain improvement [6, 7].

Improving patient selection and surgical decision-making is in great demand. Diagnostic SNRB injections have been studied in complex patient groups, including atypical pain, prior back surgeries, and multi-level spinal involvement. Some studies have shown that using SNRB-guided surgery selection yields promising outcomes [8–11]. However, most studies on SNRB are retrospective, with inconsistent outcome measures, and constitute low evidence. A systematic review published in 2013 by Beynon et al. concluded that SNRB was a safe procedure, but the benefit of adding SNRB to the diagnostic workup remained uncertain. Importantly, very few studies met the quality criteria for inclusion, highlighting the need for randomized controlled trials focusing on patient-reported outcome measures [12].

This trial aims to determine if the implementation of a diagnostic SNRB in the workup prior to surgery in patients with lumbar radiculopathy due to lumbar disc herniation, foraminal stenosis, or recess stenosis results in leg pain improvement no worse than the standard of care (surgery without prior diagnostic SNRB). We hypothesize that the utilization of SNRB is non-inferior in leg pain improvement to the standard of care by a margin ≤ 1.6 [13].

Trial design

The study is designed as a prospective randomized controlled non-inferiority trial, using a two-group stratified block randomization to evaluate the effect of adding the outcome of a diagnostic SNRB to surgical decision-making. The non-inferiority design ensures diagnostic SNRB incorporated into the decision-making process, does not inadvertently compromise patient outcomes.

Intervention

SNRB is chosen as the name of the procedure in this study. Other synonyms exist with little difference in technique, i.e., peri-radicular infiltration, foraminal injection, and transforaminal injection. The SNRB procedure allows the clinician to place the injectate closer to the suspected nerve root than caudal injections or interlaminar injections. It is suggested to be more effective in alleviating pain than interlaminar or caudal injections [17–19]. The SNRB is utilized as a diagnostic adjunct to decide which spinal level is causing the patient’s symptoms. The response to SNRB is taken into account in a shared decision-making process with the patient.

Selection of level

Spine surgical specialists determine the nerve root suspected of generating pain based on clinical assessment and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The degree of nerve root compression will be determined using pathology-specific grading systems [20–22]. In patients with the affection of the traversing root, i.e., paramedian herniated disc or recess stenosis, the root is blocked at the exiting intervertebral foramen, a level below the pathology, for example, in the case of an L4/L5 paramedian herniated disc, the L5-root is blocked at the L5/S1 intervertebral foramen. In patients with foraminal stenosis or far lateral herniated disc, the exiting root is blocked at the level of the pathology. For example, in the case of an L4/L5 lateral herniated disc, the L4 root is blocked at the L4/L5 intervertebral foramen. In this study, only the blocking of one nerve root is allowed.

SNRB procedure

A trained specialist (neuroradiologist or anesthesiologist) performs the SNRB injection. The procedure is performed using a sterile technique and the posterolateral approach with the patient in the prone position [23]. No intravenous analgesics or anxiolytics are administered. The skin is anesthetized with 2 mL of lidocaine (10 mg/mL). A spinal needle is inserted para-spinal at the target level and advanced under radiographic guidance to the posterior-inferior part of the intervertebral foramen to minimize intravascular injection risk [24].

At Aarhus University Hospital, placement is guided and confirmed using computed tomography (CT) scans, and at Private Hospital Mølholm, placement is guided using fluoroscopy. Both imaging modalities are considered equally effective as guidance [25]. Aspiration is performed to check for vessel penetration. Contrast (0.2 mL of Omnipaque 350 mg/mL) is injected. Once the placement is verified, the injectate consisting of 0.5 mL Bupivacaine (5 mg/mL) and 0.5 mL Diprospan (5 mg betamethasone dipropionat and 2 mg betamethasone dinatriumphosphat/mL) is injected. Images of needle placement are stored for verification of correct placement by another research staff member. Afterwards, a sterile dressing is applied.

SNRB interpretation and follow-up

A ≥ 50% improvement in the Numerical Rating Scale for Leg Pain 0–10 (NRS-LP) is defined as a positive response. Improvement is calculated as (Pre-SNRB NRS-LP—Post-SNRB NRS-LP)/Pre-SNRB NRS-LP × 100%. An improvement ≥ 50% at 30–60 min after injection, during the first week, or during the 4-week consideration period is interpreted as a positive response. NRS-LP at 30–60 min is recorded during the observation period after the SNRB. The patient is asked to walk, sit, and stand before recording the NRS-LP score. Patients are provided a pain diary to record daily pain scores for the first week and are instructed not to change concomitant medications. Patients are telephoned one week after the procedure to record NRS-LP scores from the pain diary. If an improvement of ≥ 50% has been achieved at any point during this period, the SNRB is considered positive. In the case of a pain-free patient after one week, the patient is allowed 4 weeks of consideration. At 4 weeks post-SNRB, patients are again telephoned and asked if they have experienced a non-acceptable pain relapse. If this is the case, the patient may proceed to surgery. If not, they are followed according to the no-surgery regime. All NRS-LP scores are recorded in a designated Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database [26, 27]. The pain diary is available in the appendix.

Shared decision making

The result of the SNRB is reported to the surgeon as positive (+) or negative (-). The surgeon is asked to decide on the treatment plan going forward in collaboration with the patient. In this trial, the SNRB response is perceived as an add-on decision support tool, allowing the surgeon to choose how to proceed. Outcomes

Outcome assessment

Patients will be evaluated at one month, three months, and twelve months after surgery. Data at three months will be used for the primary analysis. Patients in the SNRB group who do not undergo surgery will be evaluated at two months, four months, and 13 months after inclusion. This accounts for the wait time for surgery that patients in the control group experience. The secondary analysis, including one-year follow-up data, will be published separately. Rationale

NRS-LP is a predominant symptom of patients suffering from degenerative nerve root radiculopathy, such as disc herniation, recess stenosis, and foraminal stenosis. NRS-LP is a widely used measure of pain, has good responsiveness [28], and is easily applied. The non-inferiority margin is chosen based on the existing literature on the minimum clinical important difference (MCID) for NRS-LP [13].

Sample size

Utilizing data from the national patient-reported outcome measures (PRO) database, DaneSpine, which includes a first follow-up at 12 months at our institution on patients undergoing surgery for lumbar radiculopathy. We found a mean improvement in NRS-LP 4.5 with a standard deviation of 3.6 at 12 months post-surgery compared to baseline. We assumed identical mean improvement values and standard deviations in the SNRB group and control group. Applying non-inferiority criteria and based on α = 0.025, β = 0.2, and a non-inferiority margin of 1.6, 81 patients in each group are needed to show non-inferiority with a one-sided 95% confidence interval. An additional 10% is added to account for dropout. A total of 180 patients is needed for patient inclusion.

Recruitment

All patients referred to our departments with suspected one-level unilateral radiculopathy and radiological evidence of root compression are eligible for inclusion. All patient referrals for patients with lower extremity affection are pre-screened by study personnel, and potential candidates are identified based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. If the surgeon examining the patient offers surgery as a treatment, then the patient is approached by study personnel for inclusion in the study. The patient is informed of the study’s aim, the role of SNRB, the shared-decision-making process, and the potential benefits and risks associated with the study.

All baseline information will be collected before randomization.

Randomization

Patients will be randomized using a two-group stratified block randomization. Stratification will be based on the study site. Block size will be 4, 6, or 8, in random order. The allocation sequence is provided by an informatics specialist not involved in the study and not available to study personnel. Randomization is performed by study personnel in REDcap after inclusion and baseline measurements. Patients and surgeons are not blinded to the group allocation. For the intention-to-treat analysis, the statistician will be blinded to group adherence.

Data collection methods

Upon enrollment, patients will complete baseline patient-reported outcome (PRO) questionnaires, including the NRS-LP, NRS-BP, SF-36, EQ-5D, and ODI in Danish. Study personnel will be available to assist patients and provide clarifications if needed.

During the hospital stay information on complications related to SNRB, perioperative, and postoperative will be collected.

Patients will receive the same set of PRO questionnaires at follow-up via a GDPR-secured platform. If they do not respond within 5 days, they will be contacted by phone and reminded to complete the PRO questionnaires. The process can be repeated if necessary.

All outcomes in this study are patient-reported. The NRS-LP, NRS-BP, SF-36, and ODI have demonstrated good responsiveness in similar patient populations [28–30]. Although the EQ-5D is less responsive to changes in pain, it remains useful for assessing overall quality of life [31].

Data management

All study data will be collected, entered, and managed in a REDCap database hosted at Aarhus University, Denmark. All patients will be assigned a unique record identification number, and all entries will be logged.

Overview of planned comparisons and statistical methods

Primary outcome

A linear mixed-effects model will be used to estimate the difference in NRS-LP improvement between the SNRB group and the control group. The analysis will follow the ITT principle, complemented by per-protocol analysis. The null hypothesis (H₀) is that the SNRB group’s NRS-LP improvement is inferior to the control group by the non-inferiority margin of 1.6 points. It will be rejected if the 95% confidence interval’s upper limit does not cross 1.6 points. The two possible scenarios for the primary outcome are shown in Fig. 2.

Secondary outcomes

A logistic regression will be used to estimate the difference in proportions of patients reaching MCID ≥ 1.6-point on NRS-LP between the SNRB group and the control group. A linear mixed-effects model will be used to estimate the differences in NRS-LP, NRS-BP, SF-36, EQ-5D, and ODI scores and time course between the SNRB group and the control group.

Sub-analysis

A logistic regression will be used to estimate the difference in proportions of patients reaching MCID ≥ 1.6 points on NRS-LP between the SNRB surgery group and the control group.

Missing data

A complete case analysis will be performed for all outcomes. We will consider multiple imputations in case of > 10% missing values for the primary outcome.

Data monitoring

This study is not a pharmaceutical trial, and under Danish law, an external Data Monitoring Committee is not required.

Harms

Any adverse events related to the SNRB procedure will be reported to the ethical committee within 7 days of occurrence. The committee will also receive annual safety reports and a final report after the study is completed. Study personnel will monitor adverse events reported in follow-up PRO questionnaires and be available by phone for all study participants during the study.

Protocol amendments

No protocol changes will occur without approval from the ethical committee. Changes to the protocol will be registered at ClinicalTrials.gov.

Ancillary care and care after the trial

Participants in this study will receive no extra follow-up or care after the end of the trial.

User involvement

A representative from the Danish arthritis association is participating in the trial’s steering committee, allowing feedback on design, study recruitment, and results.

Discussion

This trial aims to compare the outcomes of surgical planning with a diagnostic SNRB and surgical planning without a diagnostic selective nerve root block (standard of care).

The present study will contribute high-level evidence about SNRB as a diagnostic adjunct. Previous studies have been limited, primarily consisting of retrospective studies. We aim to provide unbiased evidence for the diagnostic utility of SNRB.

We have chosen a non-inferiority design to ensure that the diagnostic SNRB incorporated into the decision-making process does not inadvertently compromise patient outcomes. This study is the largest study on diagnostic SNRB to date. Our primary endpoint for this trial is 3-month outcomes, but we also aim to compare outcomes at 1 year. We did not utilize blinding of the surgeons to the group allocation because SNRB is used as a diagnostic adjunct to guide decision-making.

We anticipate enrollment to be complete by the end of 2026.
