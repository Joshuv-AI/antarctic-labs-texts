Honesty in Causal Forests: When It Helps and When It Hurts
Yanfang Hou, Carlos Fernández-Loría

Abstract
Causal forests are increasingly used to personalize decisions based on estimated treatment effects. A distinctive modeling choice in this method is honest estimation: using separate data for splitting and for estimating effects within leaves. This practice is the default in most implementations and is widely seen as desirable for causal inference. But we show that honesty can hurt the accuracy of individual-level effect estimates. The reason is a classic bias–variance trade-off: honesty reduces variance by preventing overfitting, but increases bias by limiting the model’s ability to discover and exploit meaningful heterogeneity in treatment effects. This trade-off depends on the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR): honesty helps when effect heterogeneity is hard to detect (low SNR), but hurts when the signal is strong (high SNR). In essence, honesty acts as a form of regularization—and like any regularization choice, it should be guided by out-of-sample performance, not adopted by default.



1
Introduction

Tree-based methods such as causal trees
[
1
]
and causal forests
[
6
]
have become widely used tools for estimating how causal effects vary across individuals. In fields like operations, marketing, and economics, these methods are increasingly used to inform targeting strategies, subgroup analyses, and personalized interventions. Since 2019, dozens of papers published in INFORMS journals have applied causal forests to experimental or observational data (see Appendix
C
), often to estimate individual-level effects.

At the core of these methods is a simple but powerful idea: instead of estimating a single (average) causal effect for everyone, we divide the population into subgroups based on observed features and estimate effects separately within each group. If the subgroups are well chosen, this can reveal meaningful heterogeneity and lead to better, more effective decisions.

But splitting the population also introduces risk. If we split based on noisy signals, we may overfit to random variation rather than uncover true causal differences—especially with limited data. To mitigate this risk, most implementations of causal trees and forests adopt
honest estimation
: the training data is divided into two disjoint parts—one for deciding how to split the population, and the other for estimating effects within the resulting groups. This approach aims to prevent overfitting and eliminate the selection bias that arises when the same data is used for both tasks. Today, honest estimation is the default in widely used packages, including
grf
[
2
]
and
EconML
[
3
]
.

While honesty was originally proposed to ensure that subgroup-level estimates are unbiased and come with valid confidence intervals, causal forests are now often used to estimate individual-level effects in applied work. This raises our key research question: does honesty actually improve the accuracy of individual-level estimates—or could it hurt? This paper investigates when honesty helps, when it hurts, and why.

To see how honesty prevents overfitting and selection bias, consider a simple example. Suppose we are deciding whether to split a population into two subgroups—left (L) and right(R)—based on some feature, and choose to split only if the estimated difference in effects exceeds a threshold
c
:

\text{Split if }|\hat{\tau}_{L}-\hat{\tau}_{R}|>c.

Although
\hat{\tau}_{L}
and
\hat{\tau}_{R}
are unbiased estimates within each subgroup, conditioning on this rule introduces selection bias: because we only consider differences above
c
, and those differences may be driven partly by noise, the observed gap will on average overstate the true difference. The more splits we evaluate, the more likely we are to find one that looks promising due to noise. Honest estimation avoids this bias by ensuring that the data used for splitting is independent from the data used for estimation. So even if a noisy split is selected, the effect estimates—computed on a separate sample—will not be systematically inflated.

But here’s the twist: honesty was designed for subgroup-level inference, yet individuals are not groups. So when the goal is to estimate individual-level effects, the guarantees that make honesty attractive may not translate into better decisions.

The reason lies in the cost of honesty. By dividing the training data into disjoint subsets for splitting and estimation, we effectively cut the sample size for each task in half. The key question is whether this cost is worth the protection honesty offers against overfitting. To answer this, we decompose individual-level error into bias and variance, each arising from two sources: (i) how individuals are assigned to subgroups, and (ii) how effects are estimated within those subgroups.

Honesty reduces estimation bias and variance by using independent data for effect estimation, protecting against overfitting and selection bias. But it can increase bias and variance from subgroup assignment: with less data for splitting, we may miss meaningful heterogeneity or create unstable partitions. And because only half the data is used for estimation, sampling variance within subgroups approximately doubles compared to using the full dataset.

Adaptive estimation uses all the data for both steps. This can reduce variance from subgroup assignment and sampling error, and improve the ability to detect true heterogeneity. But it also risks overfitting: subgroup-level bias rises, and selection-induced noise increases variance.

In this sense, honesty acts as a form of regularization: it curbs overfitting in effect estimation, but may hinder our ability to detect and estimate heterogeneity—especially when explainable variation in treatment effects is large relative to sampling error. Whether this trade-off is worthwhile depends on the
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
: in low-SNR settings, honesty is more likely to help; in high-SNR settings, it is more likely to hurt.

We empirically test these dynamics using 7,500 datasets from the 2016 Atlantic Causal Inference Conference (ACIC) benchmark
[
4
]
. These semi-synthetic datasets vary widely in effect heterogeneity and SNR, enabling a comprehensive comparison between honest and adaptive causal forests. The results confirm our theoretical insights: honesty tends to outperform in low-SNR regimes but increasingly underperforms as the SNR improves.

Taken together, our findings challenge the assumption that honesty is the best modeling choice for causal estimation. When the goal is to personalize decisions based on individual-level effects, and the SNR is high, honesty may in fact hurt more than help.

2
How Tree-Based Causal Estimation Works

To build intuition, we begin by analyzing how individual causal effects are estimated using a single decision tree. Although modern algorithms use ensembles (i.e., forests), this simplified setting helps isolate the key trade-offs in honest estimation. We do not present a theoretical analysis of forests, but our empirical results show that similar dynamics play out in forest-based estimation.

We consider the standard setup in which each individual is described by a feature vector
\mathbf{X}
, a binary treatment assignment
T\in\{0,1\}
, and an observed outcome
Y
. As is typical in causal inference, we assume that each individual has two potential outcomes:
Y(1)
if treated and
Y(0)
if not. However, we only observe one of these outcomes:

Y=TY(1)+(1-T)Y(0)

(1)

We assume that treatment is randomly assigned, so the standard ignorability condition holds:

\{Y(1),Y(0)\}\perp T\mid\mathbf{X}

Under this assumption, differences in outcomes between treated and control individuals can be interpreted causally, both in the population and within subgroups defined by
\mathbf{X}
.

Our quantity of interest is the
individual treatment effect (ITE)
:

\text{ITE}=Y(1)-Y(0)

(2)

Because we cannot observe both potential outcomes for any individual, we aim to estimate this quantity using the
conditional average treatment effect (CATE)
:

\beta(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}],

(3)

which captures the expected effect for individuals with covariates
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
.

Tree-based methods estimate
\beta(\mathbf{x})
by dividing the feature space into subpopulations (leaves) and estimating the causal effect separately within each one. Each individual is assigned to a leaf based on their features, and their estimated effect is the average effect for that leaf.

Let
\ell
denote a leaf. The causal effect for the subpopulation in
\ell
is:

\tau(\ell)=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\mathbf{X}\in\ell].

(4)

We call this the
subpopulation average treatment effecte (SPATE)
.

Given a sample
\mathcal{S}
, the SPATE is typically estimated by the difference in average outcomes between treated and control individuals in
\ell
:

\hat{\tau}(\ell;\mathcal{S})=\frac{1}{n_{\ell}^{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{S}\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\ell,\,t_{i}=1\end{subarray}}y_{i}-\frac{1}{n_{\ell}^{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{S}\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\ell,\,t_{i}=0\end{subarray}}y_{i}

(5)

where
n_{\ell}^{1}
and
n_{\ell}^{0}
are the number of treated and control units in the leaf.

Even though we use
\hat{\tau}(\ell)
to estimate
\beta(\mathbf{x})
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\ell
, the two are not equivalent: the former corresponds to an estimate of a group-level average, while the latter is specific to the individual.

We compare two strategies for estimating
\beta(\mathbf{x})
:

•

Honest splitting (HS).
The training data
\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}
is divided into two disjoint subsamples: a
splitting sample

\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}}
, used to determine the leaves, and an
estimation sample

\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}}
, used to estimate the average effect of each leaf.

•

Adaptive splitting (AS).
The entire training sample
\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}
is used for both splitting and estimation; that is,
\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}
.

For any individual with features
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
, the estimated effect is:

\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}},\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}})=\hat{\tau}(\ell(\mathbf{x};\pi(\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}}));\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}}),

(6)

where
\pi(\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}})
denotes the partitioning of the feature space (i.e., the definition of leaves) induced from the splitting sample, and
\ell(\mathbf{x};\pi(\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}}))
is the leaf to which the individual is assigned.

Two components determine the accuracy of this estimate:

1.

Grouping quality:
How well does the SPATE approximate the CATE?

2.

Estimation quality:
How accurately is the SPATE estimated?

This leads to a natural decomposition of the estimation error:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}},\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}})-\beta(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\tau(\ell(\mathbf{x};\pi(\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}})))-\beta(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{Grouping error}}+\underbrace{\hat{\tau}(\ell(\mathbf{x};\pi(\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}}));\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}})-\tau(\ell(\mathbf{x};\pi(\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}})))}_{\text{SPATE estimation error}}

(7)

Even if the SPATE is estimated perfectly, the individual estimate will be biased if the SPATE does not closely approximate the CATE. Conversely, even with perfect grouping, errors can arise from misestimating the SPATE. HS and AS differ in how they trade off these two sources of error.

2.1
How Groups Are Chosen

Tree-based methods grow partitions recursively by choosing splits that aim to capture heterogeneity in causal effects. Each split divides a parent node
\ell_{0}
into two child nodes,
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
. The goal is to choose splits that improve the overall quality of the CATE estimates.

A natural objective for split selection is the reduction in
mean squared error (MSE)
. We define the gain from a candidate split as the difference between the MSE before and after the split:

\text{MSE Gain}=\text{MSE}(\ell_{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\in\ell_{i}\mid\mathbf{X}\in\ell_{0})\cdot\text{MSE}(\ell_{i})

(8)

Here,
\text{MSE}(\ell)
corresponds to the average squared grouping error:

\text{MSE}(\ell)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\in\ell}\left[(\beta(\mathbf{X})-\tau(\ell))^{2}\right].

(9)

Although
\tau(\ell)
and
\beta(\mathbf{X})
are unobserved, the following empirical criterion provides an unbiased estimate of Equation (
8
):

\text{Split criterion}=\frac{n_{\ell_{1}}n_{\ell_{2}}}{(n_{\ell_{1}}+n_{\ell_{2}})^{2}}\left(\hat{\tau}(\ell_{1};\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}})-\hat{\tau}(\ell_{2};\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}})\right)^{2}.

(10)

(Proof in Appendix
A
.) This criterion encourages splits with large differences in estimated effects between subgroups, while also favoring balanced partitions that offer greater discriminatory power.

3
Honest vs. Adaptive Estimation: A Bias-Variance Perspective

This section presents the central message of the paper: honest estimation trades off different sources of error and does not dominate across settings. It guards against overfitting by using separate data for splitting and estimation, but this restraint can come at the cost of less informative partitions and noisier estimates.

To unpack the trade-off, we look at the mean squared error (MSE) of the CATE estimate
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})
:

\text{MSE}(\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x}))=\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})]-\beta(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}+\text{Var}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})].

(11)

Both components—bias and variance—are shaped by the choice between HS and AS. And because trees approximate the individual CATE with a group-level SPATE, we can further decompose each into a
grouping
and an
estimation
component.

Let’s first look at bias, which we decompose as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})]-\beta(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\ell}[\tau(\ell(\mathbf{x}))]-\beta(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{Grouping bias}}~~+~~\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\ell}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\tau}}\left[\hat{\tau}(\ell)\mid\ell(\mathbf{x})\right]-\tau(\ell(\mathbf{x}))\right]}_{\text{Estimation bias}}.

(12)

Grouping bias
arises from using the average effect of a group
\ell(\mathbf{x})
as a proxy for the individual’s CATE
\beta(\mathbf{x})
. If the partition is poorly aligned with effect heterogeneity, grouping bias can be substantial.
Estimation bias
, by contrast, refers to bias relative to the SPATE itself. It arises when the same data is used both to select subgroups and to estimate effects within them. If subgroup formation is influenced by sampling error—that is, if a split appears promising simply due to noise—then the estimated effects will be systematically exaggerated, overstating the true effect for some groups and understating it for others.

HS eliminates estimation bias by construction. One part of the data (
\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}}
) is used to define the partition
\pi
(and thus the leaf assignment
\ell
), and another (
\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}}
) to estimate the SPATE of the leaves. Because
\ell
and
\hat{\tau}
are based on disjoint samples, they are independent. So, for any
\mathbf{x}
and
\ell
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}(\ell)~|~\ell(\mathbf{x})\right]=\tau(\ell(\mathbf{x})).

(13)

AS, in contrast, uses the same data for both tasks. This introduces estimation bias, but because more data is used to learn
\pi
, it may also reduce grouping bias by making better leaf assignments.

A central claim in this paper is that the SNR is a key determinant of which strategy is preferable. Estimation bias in AS arises from selecting splits that appear informative due to noise. This bias disappears when partitions are chosen independently of the estimation data—as in HS—or when the same partitions are consistently selected because they truly capture effect heterogeneity. As the SNR increases, selection bias in AS becomes less of a concern, and its ability to reduce grouping bias becomes more valuable. In contrast, when the SNR is low and true heterogeneity is hard to detect, the regularization effect of HS tends to yield better estimates.

The same logic applies to variance: fluctuations in
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})
can be decomposed into grouping and estimation sources.

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})]=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\text{Var}_{\ell}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\tau}}[\hat{\tau}(\ell)\mid\ell(\mathbf{x})]\right)}_{\text{Grouping variance}}~~+~~\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\ell}\left[\text{Var}_{\hat{\tau}}(\hat{\tau}(\ell)\mid\ell(\mathbf{x}))\right]}_{\text{Estimation variance}}.

(14)

Grouping variance
reflects how much the assigned leaf and its corresponding average prediction vary across training samples for a given individual. It captures the instability in leaf assignment and the resulting fluctuations in average predictions across different trees. This variance tends to be low at both extremes of the SNR spectrum.

When the SNR is very low, different samples may yield very different trees, but the resulting leaves tend to have SPATEs close to the population average. When the SNR is very high, trees reliably discover the same informative splits, leading to stable partitions. In both cases, grouping variance is low: in the former because predictions do not vary much across partitions, and in the latter because similar partitions are consistently recovered.

AS uses more data than HS to detect heterogeneity, so it can help reduce grouping variance in high SNR settings by stabilizing the learned partitions. But in low SNR regimes, that same power may increase grouping variance: trees begin to diverge in their predictions as some uncover weak signal while others do not.

The SNR also shapes
estimation variance
—the variance in estimating
\tau(\ell)
for each group. Two key factors influence this variance: overfitting, which arises from noise-induced split selection, and sampling variance, which depends on the number of observations per leaf.

AS typically benefits from lower sampling variance because it uses the full dataset for estimation.
However, it also faces a multiple comparisons problem: the more splits are evaluated, the greater the chance that one appears informative purely due to noise. If such a split is selected, it inflates estimation variance, as the estimated effects reflect not only true heterogeneity but also subgroups that happen to exhibit the largest sampling error. This risk is especially pronounced in low-SNR settings, where spurious patterns are harder to distinguish from real heterogeneity.

By contrast, HS avoids this overfitting problem by using disjoint samples for splitting and estimation. Even if a split is chosen due to noise, estimation is performed on a fresh sample, so that noise does not carry over. This protection, however, comes at a cost: with fewer observations per leaf, sampling variance increases—leading to higher estimation variance.

We can summarize the trade-offs as follows:

•

In low SNR settings, HS tends to outperform
because overfitting is a greater concern. The gains from reduced estimation bias and variance are likely to outweigh any improvements in grouping bias from AS. Grouping variance may even increase under AS in these conditions.

•

In high SNR settings, AS tends to outperform
because overfitting is less of a concern. The ability to identify informative partitions (reducing grouping bias) becomes more valuable, and AS can also yield lower grouping variance (due to more stable partitions) and lower estimation variance (due to greater sample size per leaf).

The next section presents a stylized example to illustrate these trade-offs in a transparent setting.

4
A Stylized Analytical Example

To build intuition, we analyze a simple but revealing setting: estimating individual treatment effects using a tree with a single binary split.

Each instance is described by a vector of
m
binary features
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})
, where all features are independent and identically distributed:

X_{j}\sim\text{Bern}(0.5)\quad\text{for all }j.

(15)

Treatment is assigned at random:

T\sim\text{Bern}(0.5).

(16)

The CATE depends only on the first feature:

\beta(\mathbf{x})=\theta(2x_{1}-1),\quad\theta>0.

(17)

Individuals with
x_{1}=1
have a positive effect
\theta
; those with
x_{1}=0
have
-\theta
. All other features (
X_{j}
for
j>1
) are irrelevant. The population average treatment effect (ATE) is zero.

Our goal is to estimate individual effects using a one-split tree. If the wrong feature is chosen (due to noise), the subgroups are uninformative: their SPATE equals the ATE.

We define the SPATE for feature
X_{j}
and value
w
as:

\tau_{j}(w)=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X_{j}=w],

(18)

and the estimate using sample
\mathcal{S}
as
\hat{\tau}_{j}(w;\mathcal{S})
.

We compare two strategies for training this one-split tree:

•

Honest Splitting (HS):
The training data
\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}
is split in half. One subset (
\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}}
) is used to choose the split; the other (
\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}}
) to estimate effects.

•

Adaptive Splitting (AS):
All the data is used for both splitting and estimation:
\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{tr}}
.

Both strategies select the split by maximizing the observed treatment effect difference:
1
1

1

With balanced binary features, this is equivalent to the splitting rule in Equation (
10
).

k=\arg\max_{j}\left|\hat{\tau}_{j}(1;\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}})-\hat{\tau}_{j}(0;\mathcal{S}_{\text{sp}})\right|.

(19)

Then, the estimated effect for an individual
\mathbf{x}
is:

\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})=\hat{\tau}_{k}(x_{k};\mathcal{S}_{\text{es}}),

(20)

where the chosen feature
k
and the corresponding estimate depend on the strategy. To distinguish them, we use
k^{\text{H}}
,
\hat{\tau}^{\text{H}}
, and
\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}
for HS; and
k^{\text{A}}
,
\hat{\tau}^{\text{A}}
, and
\hat{\beta}^{\text{A}}
for AS.

Because treatment effects depend only on
X_{1}
, we focus on individuals with
x_{1}=1
(for whom
\beta(\mathbf{x})=\theta
). The case
x_{1}=0
is symmetric. Our goal is to compare the bias and variance of
\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x})
and
\hat{\beta}^{\text{A}}(\mathbf{x})
under this setup. The proofs for the derivations are in Appendix
B
.

4.1
Bias Comparison

We begin by analyzing the bias of HS. Because it uses separate data for splitting and estimation, the SPATE estimate is unbiased for the selected subgroup:

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x})\mid k\right]=\tau_{k}(x_{k}).

(21)

However, this does not guarantee unbiasedness for the individual-level CATE
\beta(\mathbf{x})
, which depends only on
X_{1}
. The key source of bias is the possibility of selecting a non-informative feature. Taking expectations over all possible splits:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(k=1)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\hat{\tau}_{k}^{\text{H}}(x_{k})\mid k=1]+\mathbb{P}(k\neq 1)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\hat{\tau}^{\text{H}}(x_{k})\mid k\neq 1]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(k=1)\cdot\theta+\mathbb{P}(k\neq 1)\cdot 0,

Thus, the bias is:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x})]-\beta(\mathbf{x})=\theta\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}(k=1)-1\right),

(22)

which is negative unless the correct split is always selected. The bias stems entirely from grouping error—not estimation error—and reflects underfitting due to missing the informative split.

Now consider AS, starting with the case where it selects a non-informative feature. Although the true SPATEs are zero, AS may mistake random noise for signal—assigning a spurious positive effect to one child node and a negative effect to the other. But because
\mathbf{x}
is equally likely to fall into either node (given balanced binary features), these errors cancel out in expectation:

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\beta}^{\text{A}}(\mathbf{x})\mid k\neq 1\right]=0=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x})\mid k\neq 1\right].

(23)

When AS selects the informative feature, it remains prone to overfitting. The split may have been chosen because noise exaggerated the observed difference—and because the same data is used for estimation, that noise carries over. Given that
x_{1}=1
,
\mathbf{x}
is more likely to fall on the higher-effect side of the split, so the estimated effect is systematically biased upward when
k=1
.
2
2

2

The individual may still fall on the lower-effect side if noise happens to reverse the subgroup ordering. However, unlike with non-informative splits, the errors do not cancel out in expectation: individuals with
x_{1}=1
are more likely to fall on the higher-effect side, leading to an upward bias, while those with
x_{1}=0
are more likely to fall on the lower-effect side, leading to a downward bias.

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\beta}^{\text{A}}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1\right]\geq\theta=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1\right].

(24)

Because AS uses the full sample for splitting, it is more likely to identify the informative feature:

\mathbb{P}(k^{\text{A}}=1)\geq\mathbb{P}(k^{\text{H}}=1).

(25)

Putting it all together:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{\text{A}}(\mathbf{x})]\geq\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x})].

(26)

This means that AS predicts a higher effect than HS, on average, for individuals with large CATEs. But to assess whether AS is
less
biased, we need to ask how much higher the estimate is. Even though AS overestimates when it splits correctly, it doesn’t always find the informative feature. So, uninformative splits pull the average down, and AS remains negatively biased overall:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{\text{A}}(\mathbf{x})]\leq\theta.

(27)

Because AS predicts a higher value than HS on average, but both are negatively biased, AS must have smaller bias in magnitude:

\left|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{\text{A}}(\mathbf{x})]-\beta(\mathbf{x})\right|\leq\left|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x})]-\beta(\mathbf{x})\right|.

(28)

In sum, AS reduces grouping bias by more reliably selecting the informative split, even though it introduces estimation bias. The net result in this setting is a lower overall bias compared to HS. Figure

4.2
Grouping Variance Comparison

We now turn to grouping variance, which captures the variability in
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})
that arises because different samples may assign
\mathbf{x}
to different subgroups. In our stylized setting with a single informative feature, this simplifies to:

\text{Var}_{k}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{k}(x_{k})\mid k\right]\right)=\text{Var}\left(\mathbf{1}\{k=1\}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{1}(x_{1})\mid k=1\right]^{2}.

(29)

The interpretation is straightforward. The first term reflects how often the model switches between splitting on the informative feature and any other feature. The second captures how much the estimated effect shifts when the split is informative. Grouping variance increases with both instability in split selection and the magnitude of resulting changes.

This reveals a key trade-off. The variance of
\mathbf{1}\{k=1\}
is highest when
\mathbb{P}(k=1)
is around 0.5 and falls as this probability approaches 0 or 1. Because AS increases
\mathbb{P}(k=1)
, its effect on grouping variance depends on the SNR. In low-SNR settings, where
X_{1}
is hard to identify as informative, increasing
\mathbb{P}(k=1)
initially raises variance. In high-SNR settings, where the informative split is reliably found, further increases reduce variance.
3
3

3

One subtlety is that AS tends to overestimate the effect when it selects
X_{1}
, inflating the second term in Equation (
29
). But this inflation shrinks as the SNR improves because the split selection
k=1
becomes more likely to reflect true heterogeneity rather than noise.

In short, AS increases grouping variance in low-SNR regimes but can also reduce it as the signal strengthens and split selection stabilizes.

4.3
Estimation Variance Comparison

We now turn to estimation variance, which captures the variability in estimating the SPATE for the group to which
\mathbf{x}
is assigned.

For HS, the story is relatively simple. Suppose that estimating the SPATE using the full dataset would yield variance
\sigma_{1}^{2}
when splitting on the informative feature, and variance
\sigma_{u}^{2}
when splitting on an uninformative one, with
\sigma_{1}^{2}<\sigma_{u}^{2}
. This difference arises because a split on
X_{1}
explains part of the treatment effect heterogeneity, reducing subgroup variance. In contrast, uninformative splits leave this heterogeneity unexplained, resulting in noisier estimates.

Because HS uses only half the data for estimation (reserving the other half for splitting), its sampling variance is roughly doubled:

\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\text{Var}\left(\hat{\tau}^{\text{H}}_{k}(x_{k})\mid k\right)\right]=\mathbb{P}(k=1)\cdot 2\sigma_{1}^{2}+\mathbb{P}(k\neq 1)\cdot 2\sigma_{u}^{2}.

(30)

Two forces drive this variance up. First, estimation is noisier due to the smaller sample. Second, HS is less likely to find the informative split, so the weight on
\sigma_{u}^{2}
is greater for HS than for AS, further inflating its overall estimation variance.

For AS, the sample size is doubled—but the cost is overfitting. Because it reuses the same data for both splitting and estimation, AS faces a multiple comparisons problem: among many candidate splits, some will appear to show large effect differences simply due to noise. When such a noisy split is selected, the resulting effect estimates tend to be inflated.

illustrates this. When AS splits on an uninformative feature, it does so precisely because the observed subgroup difference is large by chance. As a result, the sampling distribution becomes bimodal: extreme values are more likely to be selected. In contrast, HS uses a smaller, independent estimation sample, so its sampling distribution is wide but symmetrically centered around zero—the true SPATE.

To formalize this, let
M
be the maximum observed heterogeneity among unselected features:

M=\max_{j\neq k}\left|\hat{\tau}_{j}^{\text{A}}(1)-\hat{\tau}_{j}^{\text{A}}(0)\right|.

(31)

This quantity reflects the degree of heterogeneity—whether spurious or genuine—that the selected split had to exhibit to be chosen. The more features we consider, the larger
M
tends to be and the greater the chance that the selected split reflects noise rather than signal.

Let
\gamma=|\tau_{k}(1)-\tau_{k}(0)|
be the true effect difference for the chosen split:

\gamma=\begin{cases}2\theta&\text{if }k=1,\\
0&\text{if }k\neq 1.\end{cases}

(32)

We define an inflation factor
\nu(M,\gamma)
to quantify the additional variance introduced by overfitting—specifically, the extra variance caused by reusing the same data for both splitting and estimation. This factor reflects a fundamental balance: the likelihood that the selected split was driven by genuine signal versus a lucky chance on noise.

When
M
is large, the selected split is more likely to reflect noise, increasing variance inflation. Conversely, when
\gamma
is large, the split is more likely to be driven by a strong signal, which reduces the inflation factor. In other words,
\nu(M,\gamma)
grows with
M
and shrinks with
\gamma
, capturing how the interplay between noise and signal affects the reliability of the estimation.

This balance directly impacts the expected estimation variance under AS:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\text{Var}\left(\hat{\tau}^{\text{A}}_{k}(x_{k})\mid k\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(k=1)\cdot\sigma_{1}^{2}\cdot\left(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nu(M,2\theta)\mid k=1]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}(k\neq 1)\cdot\sigma_{u}^{2}\cdot\left(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nu(M,0)\mid k\neq 1]\right).

(33)

This expression mirrors the honest case but incorporates a penalty for data reuse through
\nu(M,\gamma)
. When the SNR is high,
\nu(M,\gamma)
remains small, allowing AS to achieve lower estimation variance than HS. Conversely, in low-SNR settings,
\nu(M,\gamma)
grows large, and the penalty for AS outweighs its benefits, making HS a better alternative. This dynamic reinforces our main claim: AS is better suited to high-SNR settings and HS to low-SNR settings.

5
Empirical Study

In practice, choosing between honest and adaptive estimation is ultimately an empirical question. Each method has potential strengths and weaknesses, and their performance depends on the structure of the data. In principle, one can (and should) compare them using held-out data to determine which generalizes better
[
5
]
.

Our goal here is different. We use an empirical study to test the theoretical insights developed earlier. Specifically, we show that the predicted advantages of honesty in low-SNR settings—and of adaptivity in high-SNR settings—hold empirically. We also provide evidence of the bias–variance trade-off: AS tends to have lower bias, while the variance advantage of HS shrinks as SNR increases. Although our theoretical analysis suggests that AS may even achieve lower variance in high-SNR settings, the empirical results indicate that this is not the case in general.

It is worth noting that our theoretical analysis focused on single trees. This was by design: trees offer a tractable setting to isolate the mechanics of honesty and adaptivity. In practice, however, ensembles—particularly causal forests—are far more common. Our empirical analysis therefore focuses on causal forests and finds that the insights developed for trees largely carry over.

5.1
Benchmark Data

We evaluate honest and adaptive estimation using the 2016 Atlantic Causal Inference Conference (ACIC) benchmarking suite
[
4
]
, a widely used collection of semi-synthetic datasets for testing causal inference methods across a broad range of conditions. Each dataset combines real covariates from the Infant Health and Development Program (IHDP) with simulated treatment assignments and potential outcomes. The full benchmark includes 77 distinct data-generating processes (DGPs), each simulated 100 times, for a total of 7,700 datasets. Each dataset contains 4,802 observations and 58 covariates, with ground-truth CATEs available.

These datasets were originally designed to evaluate performance under confounding. However, our focus is solely on modeling effect heterogeneity, not on adjusting for selection bias in treatment assignment. To eliminate confounding, we reassign treatment randomly in each dataset, preserving the original treatment proportion. This ensures that the estimated treatment effects are not biased by selection into treatment, isolating the task of modeling heterogeneous effects.

A key advantage of the ACIC suite is that it provides both potential outcomes and the true CATE for each individual. This allows us to quantify the SNR for each dataset: the ITE for a unit can be viewed as the sum of its CATE and a noise term. When most of the ITE variation comes from noise, the SNR is low and heterogeneity is hard to detect. When the CATE explains most of the variation, the SNR is high and heterogeneity is easier to learn. This makes the benchmark especially well-suited to testing our theoretical predictions.

The ACIC DGPs vary in complexity, functional form, and degree of heterogeneity, offering a diverse and rigorous testbed for comparing honest and adaptive causal forests. We exclude datasets with no treatment effect heterogeneity, yielding 7,500 datasets for analysis.

5.2
Experimental Setup

We compare honest (HS) and adaptive (AS) causal forests using a consistent experimental design across all datasets. For each dataset, the procedure is as follows:

1.

Randomized treatment assignment.
We eliminate confounding by reassigning treatment randomly, preserving the original treatment proportion.

2.

Train-test split.
Each dataset is split into 4,000 observations for training and 802 for testing.

3.

Hyperparameter tuning.
We use 5-fold cross-validation on the training set to select the best
max_depth
, exploring values from 1 to 12. Tuning is performed separately for HS and AS.

4.

Bootstrap aggregation.
We generate 100 bootstrap samples from the training set. For each sample, we train a causal forest using the selected depth, then predict individual-level treatment effects on the test set. This step allows us to decompose prediction error into bias and variance.

We assess each approach using two complementary perspectives: overall performance and the bias–variance trade-off.

Overall performance.
For each of the 100 trained forests in a given dataset (one for each bootstrap sample), we compute the mean squared error (MSE) on the test set:

\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{te}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{te}}(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\beta_{i})^{2},

where
\mathcal{S}_{te}
is the test set,
\hat{\beta}_{i}
is the predicted CATE and
\beta_{i}
is the true CATE for individual
i
.

To enable comparison across datasets with different levels of treatment effect heterogeneity, we report the proportion of CATE variance explained by the model:

S^{2}=1-\frac{\text{MSE}}{\text{Var}(\beta)},

where
\text{Var}(\beta)
is the variance of the true CATEs in the test set. This normalized score, which we denote
S^{2}
(in analogy to
R^{2}
), is averaged across the 100 bootstrap models to obtain a single performance score per dataset.

Bias-variance decomposition.
To understand how each strategy performs, we also decompose prediction error into bias and variance. For each individual in the test set, we compute:

•

Standardized squared bias:
the squared difference between the average predicted effect (across the 100 forests) and the true CATE.

•

Standardized variance:
the variance of predicted effects across the 100 forests.

Both are scaled by the variance of true CATEs,
\text{Var}(\beta)
, so that bias and variance can be directly interpreted as proportions of total signal lost. Their sum corresponds to
1-S^{2}
.

We then average these quantities across individuals in the test set to obtain dataset-level measures of bias and variance for AS and HS.

Taken together, the results affirm that the choice between honest and adaptive estimation depends on the signal structure of the data. When heterogeneity is subtle and hard to detect, honesty offers robustness against overfitting. When heterogeneity is strong and learnable, adaptivity provides sharper, more accurate estimates. These findings offer clear guidance for model selection and algorithm design in causal effect estimation.

6
Discussion

Causal forests are widely used to model treatment effect heterogeneity, with honest estimation often treated as the default modeling choice. This preference is grounded in the idea that honesty guards against overfitting and yields unbiased subgroup estimates. But our results challenge the view that honesty is necessary—or even desirable—for individual-level inference. We show that honesty can actually degrade performance when the SNR is high—precisely when treatment effect heterogeneity is strong and learnable. In these settings, adaptive estimation, though more vulnerable to overfitting, often yields more accurate estimates by leveraging the full dataset to detect and exploit meaningful patterns.

At the heart of this comparison is a classic bias–variance trade-off. Honesty protects against overfitting by ensuring that effect estimates are not conditioned on the same data used to select splits. But this protection comes at a cost: it reduces the sample size for both splitting and estimation, weakening the model’s ability to find informative partitions and to estimate subgroup effects precisely. The result is increased bias, since individuals may be grouped inappropriately, and also potentially increased variance, due to noisier SPATE estimates. In practice, however, we find that honesty does reduce overall variance—by avoiding the variance inflation that arises from selection on noise—at the expense of higher bias.

To decide whether honesty is preferable, we can draw a direct parallel to a familiar question in machine learning: should we regularize more, or allow greater complexity? From this perspective, honesty is simply a complexity control mechanism—a form of regularization that limits the ability to fit the data. There is nothing about this mechanism that makes it inherently more appropriate for causal estimation. If the goal is to estimate individual-level effects as precisely as possible, the value of honesty should be judged empirically, just like any other form of regularization.

And that is exactly what we show in our empirical study. When the SNR is low, complexity is dangerous: overfitting dominates, and honesty helps by constraining the ability to fit the data. But when the SNR is high, limiting complexity does more harm than good. In those settings, adaptivity enables the model to capture more of the true heterogeneity, yielding better estimates despite the increased risk of overfitting.

The practical implication is that modelers should not default to honesty without considering the structure of their data. Just as we tune hyperparameters in predictive modeling to control model complexity, honesty should be treated as one regularization choice among others. The decision to use honesty—or not—should be guided by its performance on holdout data, not by default assumptions about its desirability for causal estimation.

F_{M}(z;\sigma_{u})=\left[F_{\text{HN}}(z;\sigma_{u})\right]^{m-1}.

Because
F_{\text{HN}}(z;\sigma_{u})
is increasing in
\sigma_{u}
for all
z>0
, it follows that
M
is stochastically increasing in
\sigma_{u}
. Therefore,
\mathbb{P}(k=1)
is decreasing in
\sigma_{u}
.

B.5
Adaptive Estimate Is Negatively Biased

Proof for Equation (
27
).

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})]<\theta.

Proof.
By the law of total expectation:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1]\cdot\mathbb{P}(k=1)+\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k\neq 1]\cdot\mathbb{P}(k\neq 1).

From previous results (Appendices
B.2
and
B.3
):

•

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k\neq 1]=0
,

•

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}\mid|\Delta_{1}|>M]
,

so:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}(|\Delta_{1}|>M)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}\mid|\Delta_{1}|>M].

Now consider the decomposition of the unconditional expectation:

\theta=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}]=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{P}(|\Delta_{1}|>M)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}\mid|\Delta_{1}|>M]+\mathbb{P}(|\Delta_{1}|\leq M)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}\mid|\Delta_{1}|\leq M]\right).

Subtracting gives:

\theta-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}(|\Delta_{1}|\leq M)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}\mid|\Delta_{1}|\leq M].

We now show that
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}\mid|\Delta_{1}|\leq M]>0
. Because
\Delta_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(2\theta,2\sigma_{1}^{2})
, the density
f_{\Delta_{1}}(t)
is symmetric around
2\theta>0
. For any
t>0
, we have:

|t-2\theta|<|-t-2\theta|,\quad\text{so}\quad f_{\Delta_{1}}(t)>f_{\Delta_{1}}(-t)

Thus:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}\mid|\Delta_{1}|\leq z]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(|\Delta_{1}|\leq z)}\int_{-z}^{z}tf_{\Delta_{1}}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(|\Delta_{1}|\leq z)}\int_{0}^{z}t\left[f_{\Delta_{1}}(t)-f_{\Delta_{1}}(-t)\right]dt>0.

This holds pointwise for every
z\geq 0
, so:

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}\mid|\Delta_{1}|\leq M]>0,

and therefore:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})]<\theta.

B.6
Derivation of Grouping Variance

Proof for Equation (
29
).

\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k])=\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{1}\{k=1\})\cdot\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1]^{2}.

Proof.
Let
p=\mathbb{P}(k=1)
and note that
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k\neq 1]=0
(as shown in Appendix
B.2
). Let
Y=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k]
, which equals
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1]
with probability
p
and
0
otherwise. Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]

\displaystyle=p\cdot\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]

\displaystyle=p\cdot\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1]^{2},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(Y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[Y]^{2}

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1]^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{1}\{k=1\})\cdot\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k=1]^{2}.

Proof for Equation (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}\left(\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\mid k\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(k=1)\cdot\sigma_{1}^{2}\cdot\left(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nu(M,2\theta)\mid k=1]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}(k\neq 1)\cdot\sigma_{u}^{2}\cdot\left(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nu(M,0)\mid k\neq 1]\right)

) and (

), we have that:

, the conditional variance of
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{x})

) and the law of total expectation, we get:

\sigma_{j}
denote the standard error when splitting by feature
X_{j}

\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{1}

\eta_{j}
and

B.8
Lemma for Variance Inflation Factor

Proposition.
Let
\Delta\sim\mathcal{N}(\gamma,\sigma^{2})
, with
\gamma\geq 0
and consider the event
|\Delta|>M
for a threshold
M>0
. Then:

\operatorname{Var}(\Delta\mid|\Delta|>M)=\sigma^{2}\cdot\left(1+\nu(M,\gamma)\right)

where

\nu(M,\gamma)=\frac{b\,\phi(b)-a\,\phi(a)}{p}-\left(\frac{\phi(b)-\phi(a)}{p}\right)^{2},

with:

a=\frac{-M-\gamma}{\sigma},\quad b=\frac{M-\gamma}{\sigma},\quad p=\Phi(a)+1-\Phi(b),

and
\phi(\cdot)
,
\Phi(\cdot)
denote the standard normal PDF and CDF, respectively.

Remark.
The term
\nu(M,\mu)
captures how much the conditional variance increases when we restrict attention to the extreme regions where
|\Delta|>M
. Its behavior is intuitive:

•

As
\gamma\to\infty
, the distribution of
\Delta
becomes increasingly concentrated away from the truncation threshold. In this case, conditioning on
|\Delta|>M
has little effect, and
\nu(M,\gamma)\to 0
.

•

As
M\to\infty
, the truncation region moves deeper into the tails of the distribution. Since we are conditioning on increasingly rare events, the resulting variance inflates, and
\nu(M,\gamma)\to\infty
.

This justifies interpreting
\nu(M,\gamma)
as an inflation factor depending on the degree of signal (
\gamma
) and noise (
M
). A larger signal-to-noise ratio implies less inflation.

Proof.

Let
Z=\frac{\Delta-\gamma}{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, so that
|\Delta|>M
is equivalent to
Z<a
or
Z>b
, where

a=\frac{-M-\gamma}{\sigma},\quad b=\frac{M-\gamma}{\sigma}.

The conditional variance is:

\operatorname{Var}(Z\mid Z<a\text{ or }Z>b)=\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}\mid Z<a\text{ or }Z>b]-\left(\mathbb{E}[Z\mid Z<a\text{ or }Z>b]\right)^{2}.

Let the probability mass after truncation be
p=\Phi(a)+1-\Phi(b)
. We evaluate both terms of the conditional variance using standard normal identities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z\mid Z<a\text{ or }Z>b]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{a}z\phi(z)\,dz+\int_{b}^{\infty}z\phi(z)\,dz\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\phi(a)+\phi(b)}{p},

where we used the identity
\int z\phi(z)\,dz=-\phi(z)
.

Next, we compute:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}\mid Z<a\text{ or }Z>b]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{a}z^{2}\phi(z)\,dz+\int_{b}^{\infty}z^{2}\phi(z)\,dz\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\left(-a\phi(a)+\Phi(a)+b\phi(b)+(1-\Phi(b))\right)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{b\phi(b)-a\phi(a)}{p},

where we used the identity
\int z^{2}\phi(z)\,dz=-z\phi(z)+\Phi(z)
.

Putting the terms together:

\operatorname{Var}(Z\mid Z<a\text{ or }Z>b)=1+\frac{b\phi(b)-a\phi(a)}{p}-\left(\frac{\phi(b)-\phi(a)}{p}\right)^{2}.

Finally, we return to the original variable
\Delta=\gamma+\sigma Z
, so:

\operatorname{Var}(\Delta\mid|\Delta|>M)=\sigma^{2}\cdot\operatorname{Var}(Z\mid Z<a\text{ or }Z>b),

which completes the proof.
