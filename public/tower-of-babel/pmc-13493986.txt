Collagen-heparan sulfate microcarriers support mesenchymal stem cell culture in microgravity and streamline post-flight recovery
Authors: Carolina Rivera-Crespo, Leonel Vélez-Román, William Meza-Morales, Heath J. Mills, Olivia Gámez-Holzhaus, Camilo Mora, Maribella Domenech-García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) hold broad therapeutic potential, yet the absence of validated, scalable adherent culture methods remains a barrier to orbital biomanufacturing. Here, we report the first integrated workflow for MSC culture on pre-seeded microcarriers (µCs) encompassing cryopreservation, ambient thaw, and expansion aboard the International Space Station. We engineered heparan sulfate-collagen (HS/COL) µCs with defined sulfation levels to modulate integrin engagement and factor-release kinetics and benchmarked them against commercial µCs and suspension controls. Our results demonstrate that pre-seeded MSCs remain viable and adherent throughout the spaceflight lifecycle, with ambient workflows enabling parallel evaluation of multiple donors and surface chemistries while minimizing crew intervention. In microgravity, MSC proliferation peaked 3 days earlier than on Earth, with highest cell yields being obtained with low-sulfated HS/COL µCs. Secretome analyses revealed increased pro-collagen and extracellular vesicle release with preserved bioactive microRNA cargo. While HS/COL surfaces buffered microRNA homeostasis, suspension and commercial µC cultures exhibited orbit-specific miRNA signatures associated with cytoskeletal remodeling and immune regulation. Post-flight, MSCs recovered from µCs retained proliferative capacity and multipotency, though microgravity exposure favored adipogenic over chondrogenic differentiation. Collectively, these results establish a scalable culture strategy using pre-seeded µCs for stem cell research and biomanufacturing in space.

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are central to regenerative medicine given their capacity to sense and respond to mechanical cues, regulate immune function, and secrete pro-regenerative factors. To fully understand how physical forces govern MSC behavior, it is critical to study them in environments where the gravitational loading experienced on Earth is minimized. Studying MSCs in actual microgravity at low Earth orbit (LEO) aboard the International Space Station (ISS) offers a powerful setting to uncover force-dependent mechanisms that shape cell expansion, lineage potential, and therapeutic activity. Previous spaceflight studies have shown that reduced gravity alters proliferation, stemness, inflammatory signaling, and gene expression, yielding insights into tissue regeneration, aging, and disease1–4. As space biomanufacturing becomes a near-term priority for advancing cell-based therapies and tissue engineering3,4, the challenge is no longer determining whether cells can grow in orbit, but rather how to scale, standardize, and sustain their expansion under resource-limited environments.

A persistent bottleneck in orbital research is the lack of validated methods that combine scalability with operational feasibility. Standard adherent culture requires repeated media exchanges, and precise liquid handling, tasks that consume valuable crew time and are notoriously difficult in microgravity4,5. Beyond these operational constraints, the use of cryopreserved cell suspensions introduces further challenges in space. In microgravity, cells fail to settle effectively onto substrates and instead form floating aggregates until they deposit their own matrix, a process hindered by reduced focal adhesion assembly and cytoskeletal coupling6,7. Furthermore, the requirement for tightly timed cryoprotectant removal and manual fluid manipulations increases the risk of spillage and loss of sterility during open-container work8,9. While automation modules exist for specific platforms, fluid manipulations remain largely crew-facilitated, accruing significant operational overhead that restricts the number of replicates and donor samples feasible within a single mission increment.

To mitigate some of these logistical barriers for short-term studies, ambient-thaw workflows have emerged as an alternative to traditional cryopreservation. Ambient temperature has been shown to preserve viability across multiple cell types10 and, in some cases, outperform conventional dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-based cryopreservation11–13, particularly for DMSO-sensitive cells, including MSCs14,15. Reduced metabolic activity during ambient intervals can maintain viability and limit apoptosis, while simplifying logistics by eliminating the need for incubator capacity during thaw and incubation12,16. Ambient-compatible platforms (e.g., CellShip and HemSol-PTM) are already gaining traction for terrestrial cell transport, suggesting that adapting this approach to spaceflight could drastically reduce the operational footprint of early validation of culture technology.

In terms of scalability, microcarriers (µCs) offer a potential solution to the physical limitations of planar monolayers by enabling high-density adherent growth in a compact, cGMP-compatible and suspension format17,18. While collagen (COL)-coated µCs support cell attachment and expansion, co-presentation with heparan sulfate (HS) enhances COL-integrin signaling and modulates the binding and release kinetics of both endogenous and supplemented growth factors19,20. By leveraging sulfation-dependent affinity, these engineered surfaces prolong the local bioactivity of essential factors, thereby improving MSC survival and recovery in minimally supplemented environments. This biochemical synergy offers a dual advantage for orbit, as it reduces the total mass required for media replenishment and minimizes the frequency of crew intervention. However, while MSC survival on traditional planar surfaces aboard the ISS has been established21, the use of spherical, suspended substrates in microgravity may alter MSC survival and function. Consequently, the operational robustness of surface-engineered µCs and their capacity to maintain MSC phenotype from launch through orbital expansion remain to be validated in a spaceflight environments.

In this study, we integrate our DMSO-free method for the cryopreservation of cells on µCs (Cryo-MASCs)22 with an ambient-thaw and culture workflow to provide the first systematic evaluation of the performance of µCs for MSC culture aboard the ISS. By benchmarking commercial COL-coated µCs and cell suspensions against our engineered HS/COL-coated µCs, we assessed MSC performance across a comprehensive flight lifecycle, from cryopreservation to orbital ambient activation, expansion, freeze and post-flight recovery, all within a unified, closed-vial system. This approach significantly reduces reliance on cryoprotectants and manual fluid exchanges, enabling a more high-throughput analysis of donor variability and biomaterial chemistry in serum-free, minimally supplemented media for short-term (<10 days) feasibility studies. Our findings validate µCs as a scalable, operationally robust platform for adherent cell culture in orbit, establishing a critical foundation for the emerging field of space-enabled biomanufacturing.

Results

HS/COL-coated µCs mitigate serum dependence and support post-thaw MSC recovery at ambient temperature (pre-flight)

To optimize MSC recovery under ambient culture conditions, we conducted a pre-flight study that simulated ISS constraints: bench-top incubation at 20 ± 2 °C with no media exchanges. We compared SF medium (0% FBS, supplemented with albumin and bFGF) versus standard growth medium (10% FBS) using HS29/COL-coated µCs. MSCs from three independent donors (AD-323, BM-277, BM-310) were cryopreserved using the Cryo-MASCS method22 (U.S. patent application No. 63/896,337), which consists of freezing pre-seeded µCs in DMSO-free SF medium and thawing at ambient temperature. Post-thaw, cells remained in their cryogenic tubes positioned horizontally and were incubated for 0, 3, 6, or 9 days (D0, D3, D6, D9). To minimize temperature fluctuations, tubes were kept in a thermally insulated container (Crēdo Cube™).

We set a viability quality threshold of 75 ± 15% reasonable for cultures cryopreserved without DMSO, aligning with FDA guidance generally accepting ≥70% viability for somatic cell therapy products and with MSC manufacturing reports showing facility thresholds spanning 70–90%23. Overall viability remained within the prespecified quality threshold (Fig. 1A–C). Viability was lowest on day 3 across donors, likely due to ambient temperature constraints. By day 6, viability increased in all donors, and only cultures in SF (0% FBS) medium exceeded the 75% threshold, while those in 10% FBS stayed below it. By day 9, viability further increased across all donors, with SF consistently showing superior recovery compared with 10% FBS. Proliferation measured by dsDNA content (Fig. 1D–F) followed the same pattern. SF supported gradual, sustained growth under ambient incubation, whereas 10% FBS supported limited and variable proliferation over time. Furthermore, to confirm that HS29/COL-coated µCs sustained post-thaw MSC metabolism under ambient and SF conditions, an MTT assay was performed comparing the effects of cell adhesion, medium, and temperature. In SF medium, MSCs attached to HS/COL-coated µCs exhibited approximately threefold higher metabolic activity than paired suspension cultures from the same donor, and this adhesion-dependent boost persisted at both 22 °C and 37 °C, with only a modest increase at 37 °C (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Comparable µC-associated activity was observed in the other donors (Supplementary Fig. 1B). At 22 °C, MTT values were indistinguishable between SF and 10% FBS within the µC condition, whereas significant differences remained between µC and suspension (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). These results demonstrate that enhanced metabolic activity is driven primarily by cell adhesion to the HS29/COL matrix rather than by serum supplementation or modest temperature shifts. Validating HS29/COL-coated µCs as a SF, temperature-flexible platform for sustaining MSC metabolism during short-term ambient temperature operations and supporting the view that cell-matrix interactions enhance post-thaw metabolism, mitigating the serum dependence typical of suspension cultures.

MSC growth and viability supported on µCs (in-flight)

To validate the µC platform for orbital adherent culture, we conducted a culture experiment on the ISS, followed by post-flight ground studies that replicated the ISS ambient-temperature profile (Fig. 2A, B). We strategically selected two HS variants with proven compatibility with MSC culture in SF media19 to test the influence of sulfation-dependent affinity on cell recovery: HS29 (high sulfation) was chosen for its high-affinity binding to maximize the protection and local tethering of growth factors, while HS01 (low sulfation) was included to test whether lower-affinity interactions would facilitate faster growth-factor release and boost cell recovery following the physiological stress of in-flight thawing. The commercial COL µCs served as a standard benchmark. For cell survival, MSCs from identical donor lots were thawed, analyzed and compared post-flight at 3, 6, and 9 days in microgravity and ground conditions. Overall, cell viability measured via AOPI remained at the 75% (±15%) threshold across µC conditions (Fig. 2C–H). In microgravity, a distinct surface effect emerged by day 6, in which HS/COL-coated surfaces significantly outperformed (Fig. 2C–E). By day 9, all surfaces had similar viability levels. In ground controls, all surfaces performed similarly across all time points (Fig. 2F–H), with only donor BM-277 showing a significant decrease in viability on day 9 for COL and HS01/COL-coated surfaces (Fig. 2G).

Adherent cell growth on µCs was confirmed by phalloidin staining of F-actin across all donors and time points (Fig. 3), with visibly stronger cytoskeletal organization on days 6 and 9 than on day 3. Quantitative analysis of dsDNA relative to the viable cell fraction uncovered a significant shift in growth kinetics between gravitational environments. In microgravity, MSC proliferation exhibited rapid growth that peaked by day 6, followed by a plateau or decline by day 9 (Fig. 4A–C). In contrast, ground controls followed a traditional linear growth trajectory that peaked at day 9 (Fig. 4D–F), consistent with pre-flight behavior (Fig. 1). MSCs maintained in suspension (without µC support) showed initially comparable growth kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). The one exception was donor BM-277, which showed reduced viability on days 3 and 9 in microgravity, suggesting poorer adaptation to suspension culture than other cell donors. However, suspension cells remained spherical post-flight and failed to proliferate (Supplementary Fig. 2E), thereby precluding further studies. Surface chemistry effects diverged in the gravitational environment. In microgravity, HS01/COL consistently produced the highest cell yields across donors and time points, outperforming COL and HS29/COL (Fig. 4A–F). This advantage was not observed on Earth, where all surfaces performed comparably. Given that lower sulfation densities are associated with faster growth-factor turnover, these data suggest that HS01 is well balanced to support the accelerated metabolic demands of the microgravity-induced proliferation in growth-factor-limited SF media.

Secretome profiling reveals altered ECM and enhanced EV and miRNA secretion (in-flight)

Following validation of MSC viability, we characterized the secretome, a critical output for cell-free regenerative medicine and space-based biomanufacturing. Given the volume constraints of ISS-recovered conditioned media, we employed a sequential fractionation strategy to separate an ECM-enriched protein fraction from a high-density extracellular vesicle (EV)-enriched pellet (Supplementary Fig. 3).

ECM-enriched fraction

Since ECM synthesis has been shown to be altered under microgravity conditions24–26, we first assessed protein output in the ECM-enriched fraction. Total protein recovery was comparable across cell donors (Fig. 5A–C), but HS29/COL supported the highest levels, exceeding COL, HS01/COL, and suspension cultures on day 9. We then quantified pro-collagen I, a protein precursor previously reported to increase in simulated microgravity27,28. Pro-collagen I was consistently and significantly elevated in microgravity samples on HS29/COL-coated µCs (Fig. 5D). SEM imaging of ECM fractions revealed fibrous networks exceeding 50 µm, consistent with secreted matrix proteins (Fig. 5E, F). Notably, microgravity-derived ECM exhibited a loose network suggestive of disrupted COL crosslinking29, whereas ground controls displayed sheet-like, aligned fibers characteristic of mechanically reinforced matrices30. EV-enriched fraction

Analysis of the EV-enriched fractions indicated successful vesicle isolation, with total protein content markedly lower than in ECM fractions (Fig. 6A–C), consistent with vesicle enrichment. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) identified a dominant particle population between 100 and 350 nm (Fig. 6D–F), consistent with the canonical size range for MSC-derived EVs35,36. Notably, µC cultures yielded significantly higher particle concentrations than suspension controls, which exhibited the lowest secretion levels (Fig. 6G–I). To confirm the identity of these particles, we performed a targeted dot blot for HSP70, an EV-associated marker and stress-response chaperone. Dot blotting has been used to confirm EV markers in patient plasma, in which CD9 and HSP70 signals were restricted to EV isolates37. Clear HSP70 signals were detected across all EV-enriched fractions, whereas media-only controls remained negative (Supplementary Fig. 4). The presence of HSP70 is particularly relevant given its documented role in the anti-inflammatory37,38 and neuroprotective potency39,40 of MSC-EVs. SEM results confirmed the spherical particle morphology typical of MSC-derived EVs in paired samples used for NTA and HSP70 blotting (Fig. 6J).

EV-associated small RNAs

We examined the RNA cargo of the EV-enriched fractions. Small RNA sequencing yielded a median of ~23 million reads per sample, with high quality (median PHRED > 35) across read lengths (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Read length distributions peaked between 20 and 35 nt, consistent with mature miRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Mapping revealed that while the majority of reads mapped to the human genome (74–95%) and corresponded to protein-coding or long non-coding RNAs, which is consistent with the known heterogeneity of the MSC secretome41,42, a significant portion (up to 41.5%) mapped directly to miRBase (Supplementary Fig. 5C). These results establish that the µC culture and post-flight workflow successfully recovers EVs with preserved miRNA cargo and provides a solid foundation for downstream comparative analyses.

Differential miRNA regulation across gravitational and substrate environments (in-flight)

To assess global variation in human miRNA profiles, PCA was performed using the 500 miRNAs with the highest variance. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 17% and 10% of the variance, respectively. Donor identity contributed to some of the variance, but samples clustered more distinctly by suspension versus µC conditions, independent of surface chemistry (Supplementary Fig. 5D). This clustering pattern indicates that the presence or absence of a cell-matrix is the dominant driver of miRNA composition under the tested conditions. These analyses provided the basis for subsequent differential expression testing.

Comparisons were performed across gravitational environments and surface chemistries (without stratification per donor), to identify consensus miRNA regulation. In the µC groups, no significant differences in miRNA abundance (log₂ fold change ≥ |1 | , FDR < 0.05) were detected between microgravity and ground conditions for either HS29/COL or HS01/COL. Because HS29/COL and HS01/COL surfaces promote greater focal-adhesion engagement, increasing total and phospho-FAK levels in a sulfate-level-dependent fashion (Supplementary Fig. 6), this may buffer cells against microgravity-associated perturbations, an effect compounded by the reduced metabolic activity expected during ambient-temperature culture. By contrast, COL µCs exhibited significant modulation of miR-184 and miR-619-5p under microgravity (Supplementary Table 1). Suspension cultures also exhibited distinct responses, with miR-1246 and miR-1275 significantly up-regulated compared with COL-coated µCs (Supplementary Table 2).

Under microgravity conditions, differences were also observed across surface chemistry. MiR-4455 was significantly upregulated in HS01/COL and HS29/COL relative to COL-coated µCs, while miR-619-5p, miR-9901, and miR-5585-3p were downregulated (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). HS01/COL µCs further exhibited unique upregulation of miR-1-3p, miR-184, and miR-122-5p.

The majority of differentially expressed (DE) miRNAs were identified when comparing suspension cultures with µC-based cultures within each gravitational environment, as indicated by PCA separation (Supplementary Fig. 5D). To isolate the effect of cell substrate attachment, we focused on COL-coated µCs as a common baseline for comparison with suspension conditions. In total, 21 DE-miRNAs were detected in suspension versus COL-coated µCs on the ground and 21 under microgravity. Moreover, 11 miRNAs were shared across both conditions, while 10 were uniquely altered in microgravity (red box, Fig. 7A), of these, miR-335-3p, let-7a-5p, miR-744-5p, and miR-184 were the only miRNAs significantly upregulated in suspension relative to COL under microgravity. Pathway enrichment analysis of predicted gene targets of DE-miRNAs using MIENTURNET (Fig. 7B) identified modules linked to MAPK and RAS signaling, IL-3/IL-4 cytokine signaling, endochondral ossification, and hormone-regulated pathways. Chemical stability of HS/COL-coated µCs is maintained post-flight

To confirm the robustness of HS/COL-coated µCs for microgravity-compatible cell manufacturing, we evaluated the stability of their multilayer coatings and their capacity to sustain cell attachment following spaceflight handling, ambient incubation (days 3, 6, and 9), and two freeze-thaw cycles. Pre-flight, HS/COL bilayers were fabricated using an LbL deposition process as described previously19,20,43. Each µC was coated with 4.5 nano-bilayers (nine nano-layers total) of COL and HS, with either HS29 or HS01 as the terminal layer (Fig. 8A). In parallel, commercial COL-coated µCs were included and exposed to both microgravity and ground conditions to provide a benchmark against widely used substrates in MSC culture. A single production batch of HS/COL-coated µCs was evenly divided into cryovials to generate paired post-flight microgravity and ground-control groups, while fresh (<7 days old) unfrozen µCs of identical chemistry served as baseline controls. Cell seeding efficiency was assessed using our reference MSC donor (BM-277). Commercial COL-coated µCs exhibited poor performance, with a cell seeding efficiency dropping to ~40% by day 6 and showing partial recovery in microgravity but not in ground conditions (Fig. 8B). These findings suggest surface instability and limited robustness of COL coating during extended aqueous exposure, which is particularly relevant given their widespread use in ground-based MSC expansion protocols. In contrast, HS01/COL µCs displayed only minimal reductions in cell seeding efficiency after 6–9 days in ground samples, consistent with weaker electrostatic stabilization of low-sulfation coatings and possible delamination during prolonged incubation (Fig. 8C). Notably, no attachment loss was observed in HS01/COL µCs exposed to microgravity, suggesting enhanced stability in orbit. HS29/COL surfaces consistently supported high attachment across all conditions, with no significant differences between microgravity and ground (Fig. 8D). These observations suggest that microgravity may subtly influence cell-surface interactions, prompting further chemical characterization.

FTIR spectroscopy was performed to probe the chemical fingerprints of the surface coatings, focusing on the ~1200 cm⁻¹ sulfate (S = O asymmetric stretching) and ~1550 cm⁻¹ amide I (COL backbone) peaks. Both HS01/COL- and HS29/COL-coated µCs displayed the expected sulfate and amide signals across fresh, ground, and microgravity groups on day 9 (Fig. 8E, F), demonstrating preservation of bilayer composition during microgravity incubation. Zeta-potential measurements provided complementary evidence of coating integrity (Fig. 8G, H). Because charge regulation of polyelectrolyte multilayers is pH-dependent, measurements were performed at pH 5 and 7 to capture the protonation-deprotonation dynamics of sulfate and amine groups, rather than relying solely on a single neutral condition. As expected, HS-terminated µCs exhibited strongly negative surface potentials, shifting toward more positive values at pH 5 due to protonation and more negative at pH 7 following deprotonation. COL-coated µCs, by contrast, were positively charged at pH 5 and close to neutral at pH 7. Interestingly, COL-coated µCs exposed to microgravity displayed a modest increase in positive potential at pH 5, but not at pH 7, which may reflect microgravity-induced surface reorganization or altered ion adsorption at acidic pH. HS01/COL µCs remained stable across all conditions, while HS29/COL showed a slight reduction in negative charge in the ground group at pH 7, possibly due to counter-ion shielding during storage. Importantly, HS29/COL maintained highly negative surface charges at physiological pH, ensuring that these minor changes did not impair MSC attachment.

To evaluate potential bilayer shedding, protein release into 1X PBS buffered supernatants was measured by BCA assay after thawing. Protein concentrations were consistently below the detection threshold and yielded absorbance values below blank levels (not shown). These findings suggest negligible delamination of the bilayers after two freeze-thaw cycles, confirming the cohesion of the LbL multilayers under cryogenic and microgravity handling.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that HS/COL multilayers preserve their chemical signatures, charge profiles, and functional competence following two freeze-thaw cycles and ambient incubation under microgravity conditions. The coatings consistently supported MSC attachment, validating HS/COL-coated µCs as a chemically stable and operationally robust platform for microgravity cell culture and future space biomanufacturing applications.

Post-flight validation confirms preservation of MSC multipotency and lineage bias

To verify that the stresses of orbital transit and ambient incubation did not compromise the functional integrity of the MSCs, recovered cells were subjected to standardized quality assays in accordance with International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy (ISCT) criteria44, including assessments of senescence, proliferative recovery, and tri-lineage differentiation potential.

We first quantified senescence-associated β-Gal (SA-β-Gal) activity to evaluate the onset of cellular aging following microgravity exposure (Supplementary Fig. 7A–D). Regardless of culture format, microgravity exposure elicited a consistent increase in SA-β-Gal levels compared to matched ground controls. While MSCs maintained on µCs exhibited lower baseline SA-β-Gal signals than those in suspension, the relative increase associated with microgravity was preserved across both groups. Importantly, these values were normalized to viable cell number, confirming that the observed shift reflects a genuine increase in per-cell enzymatic activity rather than a byproduct of altered cell density in µCs. Notably, the HS01/COL surface yielded the lowest SA-β-Gal levels across both gravitational environments, particularly for BM-derived donors. This observation is consistent with our prior reports19 suggesting that specific GAG densities can delay the onset of senescence-associated phenotypes by stabilizing the local growth factor niche, thereby partially buffering the MSCs against the accelerated aging in orbit.

Functional multipotency was confirmed via directed differentiation into adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic lineages. While all flight-recovered MSCs successfully differentiated based on visual dye uptake (Fig. 9 and Supplementary Figs. 9–10), detailed quantification of cell differentiation was done in the BM-310 donor due to cell number limitations. Dye quantification and molecular analysis of donor BM-310 revealed a distinct, environment-driven lineage bias. Adipogenesis was significantly enhanced, with microgravity-exposed cultures exhibiting a 2-fold increase in Oil Red O absorbance compared to ground controls (Fig. 10A, B). A divergence was observed in chondrogenic differentiation. On ground controls, HS01/COL µCs significantly enhanced Alcian Blue uptake (Fig. 10E, F), which is consistent with the ability of sulfated glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) to coordinate and concentrate matrix-inductive ligands45,46, but this advantage was markedly attenuated in the microgravity group. Specifically, chondrogenic dye uptake was ~3-fold lower in flight-recovered cells than in ground controls. This was further corroborated by protein expression analysis, which showed a significant reduction in Aggrecan (ACAN) levels in the microgravity HS01/COL group, while Osteopontin (OPN) expression remained consistent (Supplementary Fig. 11A–D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that while the µCs platform preserves MSC tri-lineage potency, the space environment favors an adipogenic program at the expense of chondrogenic differentiation, likely as a response to mechanical unloading47.

Following retrieval, MSCs demonstrated successful functional recovery with a 20–30% increment in cell growth between days 6 and 9 of culture post-flight. In agreement with our in-flight observations, the HS01/COL µCs yielded the highest cell densities across all donors and gravitational conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8), further supporting the role of low-sulfation chemistries in boosting cell recovery. In contrast, while MSCs on µCs slowly re-entered the cell cycle, suspension-derived cells exhibited limited proliferative capacity, underscoring the critical role of cell-matrix interactions in supporting cell growth post-flight.

Discussion

A persistent challenge for orbital stem cell research is maintaining cell viability during the transition through cryopreservation, thawing, and extended handling under the constraints of minimal astronaut intervention48–50. This study establishes the first fully integrated workflow for MSC culture in microgravity using pre-seeded µCs, encompassing the entire lifecycle from ISS launch to post-flight recovery on Earth. While sub-physiological temperatures are not necessarily optimal for maximized biomanufacturing yields, they provide a pragmatic and logistically lean framework for early feasibility studies. By sustaining robust cell adhesion and metabolic activity under these environmental constraints, this “pre-seed-freeze-thaw-and-expand” µC-based strategy offers a scalable and operationally simplified baseline for future orbital production.

Unlike “live-load” logistics, which subject metabolically active, mechanosensitive cells to uncontrolled biomechanical stressors during launch, our cryopreserved approach ensures cells remain protected until they reach the microgravity environment. By utilizing a cryopreserved suspension as a primary control, we established a definitive baseline for cells exposed to identical flight-profile stressors while lacking the immediate structural support of the µC platform. In the context of spaceflight, such suspension-compatible systems are often prioritized to maximize culture volume and scalability within the constrained footprint of orbital bioreactors. Additionally, using the Rhodium Science Chamber™ 4MLS (NASA Part No. RhSC-4MLS) facility provided a distinct advantage over other spaceflight facility options. While the RhSC-4MLS facility provides the similar individual sample volume, cell growth environment, and cryopreservation operations compared to the Biocell hardware51, the Rhodium RhSC-4MLS is better suited for high-throughput sampling at lower cost, enabling larger sample numbers and parallel experimental conditions within a single ISS mission. Its modular architecture supports rapid reconfiguration and integration of diverse experimental components, facilitating efficient scaling and adaptation across study designs.

Furthermore, the streamlined ground testing workflows and hardware accessibility of the Rhodium system allow for iterative preflight optimization, reducing development cycles and improving experimental robustness prior to flight. When combined with the use of chemically defined SF media, which allowed for accurate monitoring of secreted factors without the confounding influence of exogenous serum components52,53, this approach provides a validated framework for high-density adherent cell growth. Because µCs are already foundational to cGMP manufacturing on Earth17,54,55, their adoption in orbit provides a scalable and translational foundation that bypasses the inherent surface-area limitations of flat substrates. To ensure our evaluation remains consistent with established terrestrial gold standards, we benchmarked post-flight cells using some of these potency markers employed in traditional planar studies. Adherence to these standardized International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy readouts44, including adherent cell growth and trilineage differentiation, confirms that our HS/COL µC platform supported by the Rhodium hardware offers the operational consistency required for future LEO biomanufacturing.

The altered proliferation kinetics of MSCs observed in microgravity underscore fundamental differences in how adherent cells interpret mechanical and biochemical cues in the absence of gravity. On Earth, gravitational settling facilitates MSC-µC interactions by driving sedimentation, thereby promoting stable adhesion, focal adhesion maturation, and the establishment of nutrient and paracrine gradients around aggregated µC clusters56,57. In microgravity, cells remain bound to µCs but lack this sedimentation-driven reinforcement, reducing the efficiency of integrin clustering and limiting the spontaneous aggregation of multicellular 3D structures58,59. Consistent with this, µCs co-presenting COL and HS increased total FAK and phospho-FAK on Earth, strongly suggesting a more robust integrin signaling platform when mechanical loading is diminished in microgravity. Despite this, in agreement with prior reports showing accelerated expansion of hMSCs in clinostat-simulated microgravity and increased proliferation of iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes during real microgravity60,61, we observed that MSC growth peaked 3 days earlier in orbit relative to ground cultures. These findings indicate that, although pre-seeded MSCs maintain attachment on COL and HS/COL-coated µCs, microgravity accelerates proliferation kinetics uniformly across conditions. This suggests that proliferative dynamics in orbit were either insufficiently buffered by enhanced FAK on HS/COL surfaces or, to some extent, uncoupled from cell-matrix engagement, reflecting a shift toward gravity-sensitive metabolic regulation.

Our secretome analyses highlight the translational potential of microgravity for producing MSC-derived bioproducts. All µC variants supported enhanced ECM and vesicle secretion in orbit, proving that engineered platforms can sustain secretory activity even in the absence of gravity. Moreover, several of the identified miRNAs have also been reported in astronaut plasma62,63 and EVs64,65, where they regulate processes such as bone homeostasis66,67 and inflammation68. The detection in these signatures highlights the translational relevance of our findings and underscores the utility of the µC platform for uncovering molecular signals of MSC adaptation in orbit. At the molecular level, miRNA profiling revealed distinct signatures linking physical culture environment to MSC adaptation. Suspension cultures upregulated miR-1246 and miR-1275, consistent with activation of paracrine69–71 and stemness-associated pathways72 typically observed in the absence of a substrate. While COL µCs appeared sensitive to microgravity, exhibiting altered levels of miR-184 and miR-619-5p associated with progenitor differentiation73,74, and senescence75, the HS/COL-coated µCs demonstrated a notable degree of molecular stability. Indeed, consensus miRNA signatures for HS/COL-coated µCs did not differ between microgravity and ground conditions, suggesting that the enhanced focal adhesion engagement may buffer MSCs against gravity-induced stress. Specifically, within the microgravity condition, HS/COL-coated µCs enriched miR-4455 (a PI3K/AKT inhibitor76 and autophagy regulator77) while downregulating pro-senescent and differentiation-related markers (miR-619-5p, miR-9901, and miR-5585-3p).This shift reflects the activation of stress-adaptive catabolic pathways rather than degenerative ones. Notably, the unique increase of miR-1-3p, miR-184, and miR-122-5p in the HS/COL flight group further implicates active cytoskeletal remodeling73,78,79, stem cell fate reinforcement, and inflammatory regulation80–82 as key mechanisms of orbit-specific adaptation.

In addition, distinct miRNA patterns also emerged when comparing suspension and COL µCs under microgravity. Only four miRNAs: miR-335-3p, let-7a-5p, miR-744-5p, and miR-184 were upregulated, implicating regulatory programs coupling stemness, lineage specification, and immune balance. MiR-335-3p is a key regulator of MSC lineage commitment and bone homeostasis66,67, while miR-184 modulates progenitor differentiation73,78 and inflammation68. Let-7a-5p, one of the most conserved miRNAs, has been reported as a biomarker of astronaut immune responses and radiation exposure62,63, underscoring translational relevance. MiR-744-5p, though less studied, has been linked to MSC-EV modulation of macrophage polarity64,65. Network analysis identified AKT1, a central pro-survival node regulated by integrin and PI3K/AKT signaling83, and GGA3, a trafficking adapter linked to EV biogenesis84, as recurrent miRNA targets, pointing to a convergence of survival and secretory pathways under microgravity. Together, these data indicate that gravitational and surface chemistry cues synergistically shape miRNA networks that can influence bone remodeling, immune regulation, and EV output. This molecular signaling architecture likely serves as the regulatory underpinning for the broader cellular transformations and stress responses observed across gravitational conditions.

The observed elevated levels in β-Gal activity following microgravity exposure align with prior reports characterizing the induction of senescence-associated phenotypes in MSCs, including the activation of stress-related molecular programs85. In particular, microgravity has been shown to trigger a transition from an early adaptive response toward a senescence-associated state, often driven by mitochondrial dysfunction and exacerbated oxidative stress86. In our study, the significantly higher levels of SA-β-Gal activity in suspension cultures suggest an accelerated cellular aging, which likely explains the observed proliferative arrest upon post-flight recovery. Conversely, the recovery and continued expansion of MSCs on uCs indicate that robust cell-matrix engagement can delay microgravity-accelerated senescence, potentially by maintaining essential integrin-mediated survival signaling. Despite this protection, our trilineage studies revealed a distinct lineage bias, where adipogenesis was favored over chondrogenic differentiation. This shift is highly consistent with the known effects of mechanical unloading, which favors adipogenesis over connective tissue87,88. This cell lineage potency shift warrants further investigation, particularly at the epigenetic level, as the persistence of such mechanical memory in MSCs recovered post-flight may translate into a compromised regenerative capacity of astronauts returning to Earth.

While these biological findings highlight the complexities of altered MSC potency in orbit, the ability to monitor these changes was enabled by the streamlined operational logic of our µC platform. By leveraging the Rhodium Science Chamber facility and ambient bench-space, this study demonstrates a high-capacity workflow that enables the parallel evaluation of multiple µC surface chemistries and donor profiles with minimal crew intervention and without the heavy logistical footprint typically required for spaceflight cell experiments. While evidence of enhanced FAK activation driven by HS sulfation on Earth strongly suggests this mechanism is retained in orbit, confirming these dynamics at physiologic temperatures will further strengthen these conclusions. Beyond environmental variables, future perspectives must also address the biological diversity of progenitor sources, as MSCs are known to retain an epigenetic memory of their tissue of origin89 that may influence their mechanosensitive response and cell fate in microgravity. Validating the µC platform across various sources, such as umbilical cord or dental pulp, will be essential to avoid overgeneralization and to ensure the translational consistency of these findings. Furthermore, while this study utilized a limited sample set for in vitro characterization of MSCs cultured post-flight, cell recovery from µCs and subsequent functional validation in vivo remain critical milestones for future investigation to ensure therapeutic efficacy post-recovery. Finally, while the nine-day experimental window was specifically selected to maintain cell health within a closed-vial system without media exchanges, it provides a strategic baseline for subsequent long-duration studies in cGMP-compatible orbital bioreactors to demonstrate full scalability and translational readiness.

In conclusion, this study establishes a robust and scalable workflow for MSC expansion in LEO, demonstrating that MSCs on surface-engineered µCs can be cryopreserved, thawed, and expanded on µCs, preserving viability, matrix adhesion, and secretory activity. Our findings reveal that the microgravity environment acts as a unique catalyst, triggering accelerated proliferation kinetics alongside enrichment of miRNAs associated with inflammatory regulation and skeletal homeostasis. These patterns are consistent with prior observations in simulated and real microgravity, validating this platform for mechanistic studies of space-responsive pathways. Furthermore, while our platform effectively preserves post-flight cell recovery and multipotency, the orbital environment appears to recalibrate MSC commitment by favoring an adipogenic program over skeletal lineages, a shift likely driven by the lack of mechanical loading. Notably, microgravity enhanced the production of vesicles with preserved bioactive cargo, highlighting LEO as a specialized environment for high-yield biomanufacturing, highlighting its promise as a modality for space-enabled biomanufacturing of cell-derived therapies. By combining pre-seeded µC technology with chemically defined media, this work provides a foundation for scalable generation of both cell-based and cell-free bioproducts in orbit.

Methods

Cell culture

MSCs, AD-323 (Lot 210323), BM-277 (Lot 310277), and BM-310 (Lot 310310), were purchased from RoosterBio and cultured according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. Cells were seeded either on TCP µCs (3779, Corning) pre-coated with HS/COL multilayers or COL-coated µCs (3786, Corning) at a seeding density of 20,000 cells/cm2, and cultured for three days in standard growth media composed of DMEM high glucose (D6429, Sigma-Aldrich), 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (25-514H, Genesse Scientific), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P4333, Sigma-Aldrich). Incubation conditions were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO₂2 for expansion. An initial 12-h static incubation followed by gentle rocking was performed to allow the cells to adhere to the µC surface. Afterward, growth media were exchanged to fresh standard growth media or serum-free (SF) media composed of DMEM high glucose supplemented with 2% bovine serum albumin (A7906, Sigma-Aldrich), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P4333, Sigma-Aldrich), and basic fibroblast growth factor at 20 ng/mL (CYT-218, ProspecBio).

Multilayer deposition on µCs

TCP µCs were coated in multilayers of recombinant HS at low (HS01, TEGA Therapeutics) and high (HS29, TEGA Therapeutics) sulfation levels, with lyophilized COL Type I (COL) from the bovine tendon (Integra Lifesciences Holdings Corporation, Añasco, PR), through a layer-by-layer (LbL) assembly. Polymeric solutions for HS and COL were individually prepared in acetate buffer at 4 ug/mL and 1 mg/mL, respectively. Acetate buffers were prepared at pH 4 and 5, by mixing sodium acetate and glacial acetic acid, and then adjusted with ultrapure deionized water (dH2O) to 18.2 MΩ-cm. Multilayer coatings of HS/COL were formed over culture surfaces after an initial 15-min incubation with polyethyleneimine (PEI) (P3143, Sigma-Aldrich) in acetate buffer (1 mg/mL). Each polymeric layer was deposited and incubated for 5 min, followed by a 3-min wash step in acetate buffer. This procedure was repeated by alternating HS with COL for nine layers. Lastly, coated µCs were rinsed once with 1X PBS (D5652, Sigma-Aldrich) and UV-sterilized for 10 min.

Multilayer characterization (FTIR and DLS)

The surface chemistry of µCs was characterized by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two spectrometer operating in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode. Measurements were conducted with a resolution of 1 cm⁻¹ and averaged over eight scans per sample. The spectral analysis was focused on the 600-2000 cm⁻¹ region to detect characteristic vibrational bands associated with the LbL coating process. For each condition, 40 µL of µC suspension was directly added to the ATR crystal, with 1X PBS serving as the background reference. FTIR analysis was performed on the day 9 samples, representing the most prolonged exposure duration and thus assumed to exhibit the most pronounced surface changes. In addition, freshly prepared HS/COL-coated µCs were analyzed as controls to establish the baseline spectral profiles.

Spaceflight Configuration

Rhodium Scientific was the ISS space flight implementation partner for the space flight mission. The pre-flight science optimization and testing, flight operations and post-flight analytics followed Rhodium’s Quality Industry Compatible (QuIC) Space Process™, to ensure proper calibrations and documentation were applied to laboratory and ISS activities to enable reproducibility. This process has been utilized in over 25 Rhodium Scientific ISS missions and was initially developed with support from the Department of Defense.

All microgravity and ground control MSC culture samples were incubated within a Rhodium Science Chamber™ 4MLS (NASA Part No. RhSC-4MLS) facility and Rhodium Cryotube™ 0005 (NASA Part No. RhCT-0005) hardware. A total of 39 RhCT-0005s were integrated into a single RhSC-4MLS. The three fully loaded RhSC-4MLS (117 RhCT-0005s) were frozen at −80 °C before handover to the NASA Cold Stowage Team at the Kennedy Space Center (KSC) Space Station Processing facility (SSPF). A duplicate set was inoculated for ground controls. These samples remained in Rhodium Scientific’s laboratory at KSC SSPF while the flight samples went to the ISS. For launch on the SpaceX Cargo Resupply Service (CRS) 31 mission (SpX-31), samples were stowed within a NASA polar freezer at −80 °C. On the ISS prior to sample incubation, the RhSC-4MLS were stowed at −20 °C in the ISS NASA MELFI freezer. The crew removed the RhSC-4MLS from MELFI and transferred it to an ambient cabin locker within the Japanese Experiment Module (JEM). The Rhodium Science Templog 4RAD™ (NASA Part No. RhST-4RAD) was co-located with the samples to measure temperature. Following ambient sample incubation for 3, 6, and 9 days, the RhSC-4MLS were transferred to a −95 °C MELFI, and then to a −95 °C NASA Polar for return on SpX-31. All on orbit ISS crew operations were directly observed and verbally verified to NASA flight controllers by Rhodium’s Space Flight Operations team at Rhodium’s Mission Control in Houston, TX. In addition, Rhodium instructed ISS crew to provide handwritten notation by a U.S. crew member notes the day and time (GMT) the transfer activity from ambient incubation to freezer stowage occurred (image depicted in Fig. 2A). Following Rhodium Scientific’s recovery of the flight samples from the NASA Cold Stowage Team, the flight and ground control samples were then returned on dry ice to the Domenech Lab at UPR-Mayaguez. RhST-4RAD temperature data was downloaded by Rhodium personnel and provided to the research team.

Cell freeze and thaw

Cells attached to HS/COL or COL-coated µCs were cultured in either standard growth media (10% FBS) or SF media (0% FBS) and cryopreserved overnight and transferred to a liquid nitrogen tank to ensure complete freezing as previously described by us22. Following storage, the samples were retrieved and subjected to thawing at ambient temperature (21–22 °C), followed by incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for up to 7 days to assess post-thaw recovery.

Cell viability and proliferation

Cell viability was assessed using ViaStain Acridine Orange/Propidium Iodide (AO/PI) staining solution (CS2-0106, Nexcelom Bioscience) at a 1:1 ratio. Cell counts were obtained using the Cellometer Vision CBA (Nexcelom Bioscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The proliferation of MSCs during initial expansion and after the post-freeze and thaw procedure was monitored using a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) quantitation fluorescent assay (DNAQF, Sigma-Aldrich). For cell lysis, samples were prepared by washing with cold 1X PBS and resuspended in molecular biology-grade water. The lysis step was performed by exposing the cells to four alternating periods of warming at 37 °C and cooling at −80 °C, each for 5 min. Quantification of dsDNA in lysed samples was performed by mixing 10 μL of sample or blank with 190 μL of a detection solution prepared with molecular biology-grade water, fluorescent assay buffer (F7171, Sigma-Aldrich), and a fluorescent bisbenzimide H33258 solution (B1302, Sigma-Aldrich). Measurements were performed in black 96-well plates and analyzed for fluorescence using a Spark multimode microplate reader (TECAN) at an excitation wavelength of 360 nm and an emission wavelength of 460 nm. All samples were run in technical quadruplicates and background fluorescence was subtracted using blank wells.

Metabolic activity

Metabolic activity of MSCs was assessed using an MTT assay, following the methodology previously described in us22, with minor modifications. Briefly, MSCs were cultured either attached to HS29/COL-coated µCs or maintained in suspension. Cells were cryopreserved under the respective conditions, thawed, and subsequently cultured for three days under their original configuration, either adherent to µCs or in suspension, to allow recovery and stabilization post-thaw. Following this recovery period, cells were transferred to 96-well plates for metabolic assessment. Culture media was removed and replaced with 50 µL of fresh media (DMEM HG only) and 50 µL of MTT reagent (Abcam Cell Proliferation Kit, ab211091). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h to allow for formazan formation. Subsequently, 150 µL of MTT solvent was added to each well and incubated for 15 min at ambient temperature to ensure complete solubilization. Absorbance was measured at 590 nm using the TECAN. Background subtraction was performed using condition-specific blanks: media with µCs (no cells) for µC groups and media-only wells for suspension controls. All samples were analyzed in triplicate.

Cell senescence

To evaluate cellular senescence following microgravity exposure, we performed a fluorescence-based senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) assay using the Senescence β-Galactosidase Activity Assay Kit (23833, Cell Signaling Technologies). Cell samples thawed from microgravity and ground conditions from day 9 were cultured on µCs in fresh culture media for nine days post-flight before analysis. After culture, the cell sample was split for cell viability and senescence analysis. For the SA-β-Gal assay, cells were washed with cold 1X PBS and lysed using 1X Senescence Cell Lysis Buffer (91029, Cell Signaling Technologies) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (5872, Cell Signaling Technologies). Lysates were incubated on ice for 5 min, homogenized by pipetting, and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis. For analysis, a 50 µL volume of each cell lysate was transferred to a 96-well plate and mixed with 50 µL of 1X assay buffer prepared fresh by combining 2X Senescence Reaction Buffer (78494, Cell Signaling Technologies) with 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol and diluting the reconstituted 20X SA-β-Gal Substrate (45954, Cell Signaling Technologies) accordingly. Samples were incubated at 37 °C, protected from light, for 1–3 h. Following incubation, 50 µL of the reaction mixture was combined with 200 µL of Senescence Stop Solution (66008, Cell Signaling Technologies) in a black wall 96-well plate. Fluorescence was measured using a plate reader with excitation at 360 nm and emission at 465 nm, and data were collected within 30 min of stopping the reaction. SA-β-Gal fluorescence intensity values were normalized to the number of viable cells.

Cell differentiation

Post-flight, MSCs on HS01/COL, HS29/COL, or COL coated µCs were transferred to 24-well plates and cultured in xeno-free media (K82016, RoosterBio) at 37 °C for 2 weeks before cell differentiation. All cell donors were then induced to differentiate into adipogenic, osteogenic, or chondrogenic lineages to assess differentiation potency after exposure to microgravity and ground conditions. Cells were stained with color dyes, and brightfield images were captured at 20X (MRH00201, Nikon) magnification using a Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope. Due to cell number limitations, the absorbance of the solubilized stain was measured only in donor BM-310 using a multimode microplate reader (Spark, TECAN) at 500–520 nm.

Adipogenesis

Adipogenic differentiation was performed by exchanging xeno-free media to MesenCultTM Basal Medium (05413, StemCell Technologies) supplemented with MesenCultTM 10X Adipogenic Differentiation Supplement (05414, StemCell Technologies), MesenCultTM 500X Adipogenic Differentiation Supplement (05415, StemCell Technologies), and L-Glutamine at 2 mM (O1391, Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were subsequently incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2, and the media were exchanged every 3.5 days for 14 days. Oil Red O (O1391, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to visualize oil droplets, after which the stain was removed with 100% isopropanol (109827, Sigma-Aldrich) and the sample was heated at 85 °C for 10 min.

Chondrogenesis

Chondrogenic differentiation was performed by exchanging SF media to ChondroMAX Differentiation Medium (SCM123, Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were subsequently incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2, and the media were exchanged every 3.5 days for 14 days. Alcian blue (ALNH9A9D6208, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to visually confirm chondrogenic commitment. Afterward, the stain was removed with 10% acetic acid (695092, Sigma-Aldrich) followed by a brief heating period at 85 °C for 10 min.

Osteogenesis

Osteogenic differentiation was performed by exchanging SF media to MesenCultTM Osteogenic Differentiation Basal Medium (05466, StemCell Technologies) supplemented with MesenCultTM Osteogenic Differentiation 5X Supplement (05467, StemCell Technologies) and L-Glutamine at 200 mM. Cells were subsequently incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2, and the media were exchanged every 3.5 days for 14 days. Alizarin Red S (TMS-008-C, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to visually confirm osteogenic commitment. Afterward, the stain was removed with 10% acetic acid (695092, Sigma-Aldrich) followed by a brief heating period at 85 °C for 10 min.

Protein quantification

Protein levels in the samples were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (71285-3, Millipore Sigma), with bovine serum albumin (BSA) serving as the calibration standard. According to the supplier’s protocol, 25 µL of either sample or BSA standard was dispensed into wells of a clear 96-well plate. A working solution was freshly prepared by combining Reagent A (BCA solution) with Reagent B (copper(II) sulfate) in a 50:1 ratio, and 200 µL of this mixture was added to each well. The plate was then incubated at 60 °C for 15 min to promote chromophore development. Standard curves were generated using BSA concentrations of 25, 150, 250, 500, and 1000 µg mL⁻¹, from which protein concentrations of unknowns were interpolated. Measurements were carried out in triplicate, with DPBS blanks included to correct background absorbance. Optical density was recorded at 562 nm using a Multiskan GO microplate reader equipped with SkanIt software v5.0 (Thermo Scientific).

For human phospho-FAK and total FAK levels, a phospho-FAK ELISA assay was performed (PEL-FAK-Y397-T-1, RayBiotech). For lineage-specific markers following differentiation in the BM-310 donor, assays included: i) ACAN for chondrogenesis (E-EL-H0294) and ii) OPN for osteogenesis (E-EL-H1347 and E-BC-K091-M, respectively) from ElabScience. In addition, a COL ELISA (ELH-COL1A1-1, Raybiotech) was performed to quantify secreted COL in non-differentiated MSC samples exposed to ground or microgravity conditions. Samples were first adjusted for total protein content using the Pierce BCA assay kit (23225, 23227 and A65453, Thermo Scientific) and then centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 2–8 °C. Supernatants were collected and diluted with the sample diluent as needed. Before assay setup, all reagents were brought to ambient temperature and prepared following the manufacturer’s directions. For each assay, 100 µL of standard or diluted sample was added to each well of a pre-coated 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Wells were aspirated, and 100 µL of biotinylated detection antibody working solution was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed three times with wash buffer before the addition of 100 µL of HRP conjugate, followed by a 30-min incubation at 37 °C. After another three washes, 90 µL of substrate solution was added and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 µL of stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a multimode microplate reader.

Fluorescent Imaging

Cells in COL-, HS01/COL, or HS29/COL-coated µCs were washed with 1X PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution (SC-281692, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 15 min. Cytoskeleton staining was performed by incubating the ActinRed™555 ReadyProbes reagent (R37112, Invitrogen) for 30 min, while cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (H1399, Invitrogen) at a 1:1000 dilution for 10 min at ambient temperature. Fluorescent images were subsequently taken at 20X (MRH00201, Nikon) magnification using a Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope.

Conditioned media processing

Conditioned media collected from days 6 and 9 vials were thawed and technically replicated, pooling each experimental group immediately after thawing. To remove cells and debris, the culture media were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, 5 mL of the supernatant was transferred to 6 mL ultracentrifuge tubes (355647, Beckman Coulter). Samples were ultracentrifuged at 120,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C using a fixed-angle rotor tabletop ultracentrifuge (Optima™ Max-XP, Beckman Coulter). Following ultracentrifugation, three distinct phases were observed: (i) an EV-enriched pellet at the bottom, (ii) a viscous intermediate layer (~1 mL), and (iii) the remaining supernatant. The EV-enriched pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of sterile 1X PBS supplemented with trehalose (T9449-25G, Sigma-Aldrich) 25 mM to enhance vesicle stability and stored at −80 °C for up to 2 weeks before proceeding with RNA sequencing. The viscous intermediate fraction was separately collected and used for COL ELISA and ECM characterization assays, while the final supernatant was discarded.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

Particle size distribution and concentration were determined by NTA using a NanoSight LM-10 instrument (Malvern Panalytical). Conditioned media collected on days 6 and 9 were processed independently from samples designated for RNA sequencing. To overcome volume constraints and ensure sufficient yield for characterization, conditioned media from both time points within each experimental group were pooled to a final volume of 5 mL for EV isolation. Following ultracentrifugation, EV pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of sterile 1X PBS supplemented with 25 mM trehalose for stabilization. Samples were then diluted 10-fold in 1X PBS to reach the optimal particle concentration for the detector’s linear range. For each measurement, a 500 µL volume was manually loaded into the NTA chamber using a 1 mL syringe. Data acquisition consisted of three 60-s videos per sample and corresponding blank (ultracentrifuged cell-free SF media), recorded at room temperature. To ensure technical comparability, the camera level and detection threshold were kept constant across all sessions. Analysis was performed using NanoSight NTA 3.1 software, applying uniform parameters to all samples. Particle size distribution and concentration were reported as mean ± standard deviation across technical replicates.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Approximately 50 μL of each sample was collected and subjected to buffer exchange (dH₂O) using 0.5 mL Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters with a 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff (UFC510096, Merck Millipore). The samples were centrifuged five times at 5000 × g for 5 min each to remove residual buffer components and concentrate the sample effectively. After exchange, 20 μL of the sample was deposited onto a glass coverslip (CS-24×50-100, AmScope) pre-mounted on an aluminum specimen stub (75220, Electron Microscopy Sciences) with carbon adhesive tabs (77825-12, Electron Microscopy Sciences). The prepared samples were then frozen at −80 °C for 12 h to solidify the material, followed by lyophilization for 6 h to achieve complete dehydration while preserving structural integrity. Once dried, samples were coated with a thin gold layer to ensure electrical conductivity and minimize charging artifacts during imaging. Finally, the samples were examined using a ZEISS Sigma 560 SEM under high-vacuum conditions.

HSP70 protein dot blot

For each sample, 2 µg of total protein was dotted onto a 0.45 µm nitrocellulose membrane (1620145, BioRad) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. For the positive control, 0.2 µg of HSP70 was dotted. Membranes were blocked with 1X TBS and 1% casein blocker (1610782, BioRad) for 16 h at 4 °C. After blocking, membranes were incubated with a primary anti-HSP70 antibody (EXOAB-KIT-1, SBI) at a 1:400 dilution in 1X TBS with 1% casein blocker for 4 h at 4 °C. After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (EXOAB-KIT-1, SBI) at a 1:800 dilution in TBST for 1 h at 4 °C. A chemiluminescent signal was generated using the luminol substrate (1705060, BioRad) and imaged on a ChemiDoc™ XRS+ System (Model Universal Hood II, BioRad).

miRNA processing and analysis

QIAGEN Genomic Services performed RNA isolation, library preparation, sequencing, and data analysis. Briefly, RNA was isolated using the exoRNeasy Midi (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with an elution volume of 14 μL. Library preparation was performed using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (331502, QIAGEN). A total of 10 ng of RNA was converted into miRNA NGS libraries. After adapter ligation, UMIs were introduced during reverse transcription. The cDNA was amplified by PCR (19 cycles), and indices were added during the reaction. After PCR, the samples were purified. Library preparation was quality-controlled using capillary electrophoresis (Tape D1000). Based on the quality of the inserts and the concentration measurements, the libraries were pooled in equimolar ratios. The library pool was quantified using qPCR. The library pool was then sequenced on an AVITI (Element Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA) sequencing instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (1 × 76, 2 ×10). Raw data were de-multiplexed, and FASTQ files for each sample were generated using Element Biosciences bases2fastq version 00.000.000.4.2.4.

All primary analysis is carried out using CLC Genomics Server 25. The workflow “QIAseq miRNA Quantification” in CLC Genomics Server, using standard parameters, is used to map the reads to miRBase version 22. In short, the reads are processed by trimming off the common sequence, UMI and adapters; and filtering of reads with <15 nt or >55 nt lengths. Reads were deduplicated using their UMI. Reads that did not map to the miRBase, neither with perfect matches or as isomiRs (maximum of two mismatches and/or alternative start-end position of two nt), were mapped to the human genome hg38 with ENSEMBL GRCh38 version 98 annotation. This was carried out using the RNA-Seq Analysis workflow of CLC Genomics Server with standard parameters. The empirical analysis of the DGE algorithm of the CLC Genomics Workbench 25.0 was used for differential expression analysis with default settings. It is an implementation of the Exact Test for two-group comparisons developed by Robinson and Smyth90 and incorporated in the EdgeR Bioconductor package91. For all unsupervised analyses, only miRNAs with at least ten counts summed across all samples were considered. Normalized miRNA expression levels were used for the principal component analysis. A variance stabilizing transformation was performed on the raw count matrix using the function vst () of the R package DESeq2 version 1.36.0. Differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-miRNAs) were defined as false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log₂FC | ≥1. DE-miRNA list was analyzed in MIENTURNET to refine the gene target set, where only genes supported as targets by at least two distinct DE-miRNAs and FDR < 0.05 were retained.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. One-way and two-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) were performed to evaluate significant differences among the tested conditions, with post-hoc multiple comparison tests applied where appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad software (GraphPad Prism 10, San Diego, CA). A p-value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.
