Genetic and phenotypic insights into shell integrity and market traits in farmed Pacific oysters
Authors: Ernest O. Chuku, Klara L. Verbyla, Gregory G. Smith, Debashish Mazumder, Steven A. Rust, Andrew J. Trotter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Genetic responses of oysters to specific environments underpin adaptation and evolutionary potential, which can be leveraged through breeding to improve outcomes. Shell traits strongly influence both survival and market value in the half-shell market. Using novel trait phenotyping methods, the heritability, genetics-by-environment interactions, and potential surrogate traits for shell integrity (hardness, chalkiness, density, compressive strength) and other market-preferred phenotypes were investigated in twenty full-sib pedigree-bred Pacific oyster family lines. Of twenty traits assessed, shell colour had the highest heritability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}^{2}$$\end{document} = 0.93, 0.78, in faster- and slower-growth estuaries respectively). Shell integrity traits exhibited moderate heritabilities (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}^{2}$$\end{document}=0.12–0.40), growth traits had low to moderate heritabilities (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}^{2}$$\end{document} = 0.08– 0.26), and meat yield/condition had values near zero. Higher trait heritabilities were identified for many morphometric and somatic metrics, as well as meat yield phenotypes, in the slower-growth estuary. The faster-growth estuary had higher heritabilities for shell colour and density. Phenotypic expression under reduced growth aligned with industry-favourable shell qualities, including denser, darker, less brittle shells with deeper cups, whereas fast growth produced diminished shell integrity but with superior meat yield. Genetic correlations indicate that selection on readily measurable whole, shell and meat weight traits, alongside colour, could improve shell integrity, reduce production losses and enable farmers to consistently deliver better-quality oysters to market.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34267-x.

Introduction

Pacific oysters (Magallana gigas) are a cornerstone of global aquaculture, with breeding efforts focused on optimising both production efficiency and marketability of the farmed animal. As Pacific oysters transition from conventional food commodities to premium niche market items, shell attributes have assumed unprecedented importance, with shell integrity, appearance and shape influencing consumer acceptance and market value1–3. Recent studies have highlighted the central role of Pacific oyster shell integrity traits like thickness, density, hardness, chalkiness, and brittleness, demonstrating their importance to survivability of the oyster and production and sales optimisation in the half-shell market1,4,5.

The growing half-shell market encompassing fresh or lightly cooked and garnished oysters served in the left shell valve in restaurants, oyster bars, and retail outlets, represents a high-value sector. Australia, China, France, Japan, the United Kingdom and United States are leading markets, while Brazil, Mexico, South Africa and United Arab Emirates are emerging markets. In this half-shell market, subpar aesthetic presentation of both the shell and meat, coupled with the risk of shell fragment contamination during shucking resulting from fragile shells, can negatively impact the quality of the final product, as well as consumer preferences and purchasing behaviours, challenging industry success5.

During farm production prior to market, farmers risk weak shells chipping away and fracturing from the rigours on-farm husbandry, impacting survival and crop turnover6,7. Shells of similar linear dimensions can differ substantially in shell thickness, density and cavity volume, leading to marked differences in overall shell mass and compactness/heft1,4,8. Such variation is evident among the Australian Seafood Industries (ASI) Pacific oyster family lines used in this study9,10, partly corroborated by observations on related indices such as shell weight in other breeding programmes11–13. This presents distinct opportunities for improved on-farm survival and market suitability for the half-shell trade. Our observations also suggest notable differences in shell integrity associated with specific growing locations – further corroborated by studies highlighting performance variations between sites in both farmed and reef oysters4,14,15. The foregoing underscores the promising industry potential of optimising the heritability of novel shell integrity traits, alongside traditional breeding metrics such as growth and disease resistance, to increase yields and meet growing consumer demand. While this position remains popular among farmers and industry collectives, particularly in Australia, there is little progress in advancing the frontiers due to historically limited research.

Aspects of the oyster shell have been substantially studied from shape, colour, microstructural development and environmental impact perspectives1,10,13,16–20. The mechanistic and environmental underpinnings of shell microstructural formation, which likely controls the integrity of shells developed under culture, have received considerable attention recently4,21–23. Yet, little is known of the genetic basis and estuarine ecology of shell integrity traits like shell hardness, density, and chalkiness, or how this could be applied to improve the sustainability of marine farming of oysters. The lagging advancement in this area in Pacific oyster breeding is also partly due to the lack of practical phenotyping solutions for shell integrity and other market quality traits.

The present study makes the first systematic assessment of the potential to breed critical market-related traits into oysters, with emphasis on shell integrity (hardness, chalkiness, density, compressive strength), contextualised among traditional oyster production traits, including colour and shape. The ‘shell integrity’ terminology used encapsulates these traits related to overall shell mechanical strength and microstructural integrity following from an industry survey revealing a nuanced industry perspective of shell quality traits5. Novel workable phenotyping approaches were developed or adapted for quantifying key shell traits – colour, chalkiness, hardness, compressive strength, and density (details in the methods). Chromatic quantisation and 3D hue modelling characterised exterior colour of the left shell valve24, capturing variations across the greyscale spectrum. Differential luminescence analysis was used to characterise chalkiness by distinguishing between chalky and foliated inner shell surfaces21,25. Ultrasonic contact impedance technology26,27 characterised shell hardness through rapid, non-destructive testing of the inner shell surface. Shell compressive strength4 was characterised using modified Hounsfield® testing, ensuring even load distribution at the shell base. Shell density was characterised through a simple and efficient hydrodensitometry method (i.e., Archimedes principle). Twenty traits in total were measured across twenty full-sib pedigree-bred Pacific oyster family lines farmed in two Tasmanian estuaries.

Results

Estuarine water quality at the oyster farming sites

Water quality differed significantly between our experimental oyster farming sites (Little Swanport and Pitt Water) for all measured parameters except for dissolved oxygen [Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test (WRST); p < 0.01]. Water quality trends are shown in Fig. 1. Respective mean conditions at the two sites were as follows: temperature (°C) = 14.74 ± 0.12, 13.13 ± 0.22; dissolved oxygen (mgL− 1) = 8.92 ± 0.04, 9.41 ± 0.05; salinity (PSU) = 27.85 ± 0.27, 25.99 ± 0.25; pH = 8.25 ± 0.00, 8.06 ± 0.01; turbidity (NTU) = 5.85 ± 0.66, 9.85 ± 2.34; chlorophyll-a (RFU) = 2.60 ± 0.45, 1.09 ± 0.13. The sampling sites correspond to sectors 30 (Little Swanport) and 8 (Pitt Water) as delineated in the hydrodynamic assessment by Crawford et al.28 with average water fluxes of 1721.00 and 691.12 tonnes m− 2, and corresponding mean current velocities of 0.057 and 0.030 m s− 1, respectively.

Phenotypic performance of farmed M. gigas in a faster- vs. slower-growth estuary

Standard shell growth

The farmed oysters exhibited significant variations in shell growth performance (SH, SL, and SW) among estuaries and genetic family lines. Figure 2 illustrates the periodic (average 93 d intervals) growth and final shell growth metrics achieved at both sites over the culture period. Little Swanport oysters reached significantly greater SH (79.01 ± 0.35 mm) than Pittwater oysters (70.99 ± 0.29 mm), with overall SGR values of 0.39% d− 1 and 0.36% d− 1, respectively. [Welch’s ANOVA (SH); df = 1, F = 343.90, p < 0.001]. Generally, the observed size structure was a unimodal normal distribution coupled with a progressive distinction in size variance among individual oysters as oysters increased in shell (growth differentiation with age) evidenced by the successive plateauing and commensurate basal expansion of the Kernel (Gaussian) density distributions (Fig. 2a). Growth rates at comparable life stages followed a similar pattern, reaching maximum growth performance (SGR = 0.46–0.55% d− 1, max ∆SH = 16.89 mm) during the second and third growth quarters (six to nine months) in Pitt Water but spread over the sixth to twelfth growth quarters in Little Swanport (SGR = 0.40–0.55% d− 1, max ∆SH = 19.70 mm) and subsequently retarding steadily with age. Beyond the estuary effect, there was ample evidence of a family-estuary interaction effect on shell growth performance phenotypes [Two-Way ANOVA; p < 0.05; (Fig. 2b-d)].

Intra-family (within family between the two estuaries) and inter-family (between families, pooled for estuaries) comparisons revealed remarkable plasticity in shell dimensions, as inferred from Tukey HSD post-hoc multiple comparisons (Supplementary Fig. S1, S2). Eleven, eighteen, and five specific families exhibited differential development in SH, SL, and SW, respectively, with Little Swanport oysters demonstrating superiority in these phenotypes (ANOVA, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. S1). A seeming correspondence between inter-family deployment size and final sizes was annulled by examining interdependencies using regression coefficients, which showed minimal to no significant family-wise initial-to-final size correlation (R2 < 0.25; Fig. 2e). This affirms genetic variation in shell growth performance.

Shell structural integrity

There were significant differences in the structural formation of shells among families and between the two estuaries (KWT; p < 0.001). Phenotypes of M. gigas shell structural integrity included shell thickness, weights (in air and in water), chalkiness, hardness (inner shell surface), compressive strength, density and breakage during shucking (first two rows; Fig. 3). The faster-growing Little Swanport oysters expressed superior performance in a significant majority (over 50%) of families than Pitt Water oysters in relation to shell thickness (3.08 ± 0.05 > 2.68 ± 0.04), weights (air = 33.33 ± 0.35 > 27.36 ± 0.27; water = 17.68 ± 0.19 > 16.40 ± 0.16), chalkiness (170.65 ± 0.50 > 159.03 ± 0.63), and hardness (2355.03 ± 25.31 > 1794.95 ± 18.09), and had greater susceptibility to breakage (0.62 ± 0.04 > 0.02 ± 0.01). Conversely, the slower-growing Pitt Water oysters expressed superior compressive strength (0.65 ± 0.00 > 0.50 ± 0.00) and density (2.55 ± 0.01 > 2.17 ± 0.01) and significantly withstood the force of shucking compared to the Little Swanport oysters. Furthermore, family-wise generalised linear modelling showed statistically incoherent effect of growth rate (shell height) on the shell structural metrics, while maintaining consistent estuary effect (GLM; p < 0.001; Fig. 4). Within Little Swanport, greater family growth performance correlated positively with weights, hardness and breakage but showed no correlation with chalkiness. However, in Pitt Water, evidence of increasing family performance in growth rates on shell structural quality was sparse.

Shell shape

The ratio of shell length to shell height (right valve perspective broadness of shell) and shell width to length (depth of shell cup) showed notable differences between estuaries and families [KWT; Pr(> F) < 0.001; Fig. 3. In several families (70%), Little Swanport oysters developed significantly broader shells compared to their Pitt Water counterparts. On the other hand, 90% of families farmed in Pitt Water developed shells with a deeper cup of the left shell valve than their Little Swanport counterparts. Within each estuary, there was a significant negative correlation between broadness and depth of shell cup (Multi. R2 = 0.58; p < 0.05). Families with broader shells tended to have shallower left shell valves and vice-versa. Specific families maintained persistent broadness and cup depth performance in both estuaries (e.g., 21005 was the broadest and shallowest cupped oyster, whereas 21059 was the narrowest and deepest cupped in both estuaries).

By substituting the ideal commercial oyster shape of 3SH:2SL:1SW1,29 into Eq. (4), an overall standard shape/roundness value of 58% is expected for a commercially acceptable adult or table-size oyster, whereas that for an Australian industry-sustainable Pacific oyster, i.e., SH: SL = 2:1-1.5 5 and assuming SW = 1, ranges from 50 to 71%. Mean roundness of the Pacific oysters farmed in this study (61.11 ± 0.50–65.15 ± 0.43) were well above the global commercial standards and within the range for Australia across families.

Overall shape estimates from the roundness/shape index between estuaries were statistically similar but had significant family effect and family-estuary interaction (KWT; significant at p < 0.001). However, shape differences among estuaries became more distinct while visualising multilinear regression plots between shell broadness and cup depth indices (Fig. 5). Here, intra- and inter-estuary family-wise shape variations were easily discernible from the slopes and positioning of regression lines along x- and y-axis scales. Despite the unified negative correlation, specific families maintained better cup depth in both estuaries (e.g., 21057, 21067) whereas Pitt Water enhanced cup depth in specific families (e.g., 21048, 21056, 21068). There was also greater evidence of size/growth relatedness (inverse) to cup depth (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Shell aesthetics (colour)

The successful implementation of chromatic quantisation of greyscale hue revealed significant effects of estuary and family on pigmentation in shell exteriors of the farmed M. gigas, considering both terms in a GLM [Pr(>|t|) < 0.05]. Family differences were as per visual expectation, with families such as 21,028 and 21,037 having the largest (lightest hue) and 21,079, 21,059, and 21,094 with the least (darkest hue) estimated effect sizes reference to family 21,005. Regarding estuary effect, Pitt Water oysters developed considerably darker hue of shell exteriors compared to Little Swanport oysters (Fig. 3). However, this colour difference appeared to be primarily an estuary effect not associated with the observed estuary-specific growth performance as GLM of estuary plus shell height predicting colour yielded statistically nonsignificant effect of shell height [Pr(>|t|) > 0.05]. Here, replacing estuary with family in the GLM resulted in a significant effect of shell height [Pr(>|t|) < 0.05] with a positive effect size, suggesting faster-growing families were more likely characterised by lighter hued shell exteriors. Furthermore, of the shell integrity phenotypes, there was a strong association of colour with hardness (R2 = 0.77) and chalkiness (R2 = 0.56) in each estuary, inferring from LMs that had estuary and colour as predictors [Pr(>|t|) < 0.05].

Meat yield and condition

The measured meat production metrics (wet and dry meat weights, meat yield, and condition index; last row Fig. 3) were significantly different between the two sites for all families (Pr(> F) < 0.001). Oysters from both estuaries showed ripe gonads with not widespread indication of gamete release prior to harvest. The faster-growing Little Swanport oysters were by far more productive. Not only were the wet and dry meat mass of these oysters larger, but they also showed superior performance in percent meat yield (relative to shell cavity volume) and condition index. There was no persistence of family performance ordering/ranking across these meat phenotypes. Nonetheless, specific families were conspicuously present in the upper halve of the dashed horizontal reference line, and there was greater similarity in family ranking between wet and dry meat weights (absolute weights), as well as between meat yield and condition index (indices of real productivity). This affirms the phenotype interchangeability of wet and dry meat weights, as well as meat yield and condition indices.

Genetic insights into M. gigas production, shell integrity and other market traits

The expression of Pacific oyster traits was found to be shaped by a combination of additive genetic effects, husbandry-related factors arising from family replication and basket deployment, and other unexplained residual sources of variation (Table 1). Estuary, fitted as a fixed effect, had a strong statistically significant influence on all traits (Wald, p < 0.001). Thus, the contribution of additive genetic variance likely differed between estuaries. However, for many traits, the genetic correlation between the two estuaries was high (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{G}$$\end{document} = 1.00; LRT, p > 0.05; Supplementary Table S1), implying consistent genetic expression regardless of location. These traits include shell length, wet and dry meat weights, shell weights in air and in water, shell hardness, shell colour, shell density, shell broadness index, condition index, and meat yield. For other traits like shell height, width, thickness, compressive strength, chalkiness, and cup depth and roundness indices, genetic correlations between estuaries were significantly reduced (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{G}<$$\end{document} 1.00; LRT, p < 0.05; Supplementary Table S1). The genetic basis underlying the performance of these traits was expressed differently across the two estuaries, confirming the presence of genotype-by-environment (G×E) interactions.

Discussion

This study highlights the complex interplay of biological (genetic), environmental (estuarine influence), and management (location of replicate culture units) factors on performance and breeding potential of key shell integrity metrics in farmed M. gigas. Culture environment not only had substantial effect on growth performance, but impacted on shell structural integrity development (hardness, chalkiness, density, thickness), shape, and colour. Even more intriguing was the genetic heritability of shell hardness, chalkiness, density, and compressive strength phenotypes. Although we present complementary data on morphological and quantitative genetics outcomes of twenty phenotypes, we centre our discussion around these emerging industry-relevant shell phenotypes5.

Distinct physicochemical parameters could explain the site-specific disparities in growth30–33 and shell performance. The high growth site (Little Swanport) exhibited warmer mean water temperatures, higher average salinity, pH, and primary productivity (chlorophyll-a), coupled with reduced turbidity. Conversely, the low-growth site presented the opposite conditions although the differences were not large. Nonetheless, Pitt Water recorded greater temperature extremes in summer (high) and winter (low). While dissolved oxygen saturation was comparable at both sites, Little Swanport was characterised by greater fluctuation, especially in summer, suggesting improved mixing and flow in this estuary. Indeed, greater hydrodynamic exchange is reported for this site28. Salinity regimes also potentially influenced the differences in oyster growth34, as Little Swanport, with slightly higher salinity and greater periodic freshwater incursions, appeared to produce greater growth. Aside from our considered parameters, other ecological factors such as air exposure could contribute to the growth differences6,35–38, whereby longer exposure would reduce growth and improve shell density, hardness, and colour in farmed Pacific oysters6. While we operationally set culture baskets for comparable tidal exchange, empirical data on air exposure period would have ascertained whether, for instance, Pitt Water oysters, which had reduced growth and slightly darker exteriors of the left shell valve, were exposed for longer periods, as we observed in a different study6 conducted at the latter stages of this study.

Oysters farmed in high productivity estuaries (with AGR = 0.15 mm/day, as observed in this study) may encounter significant challenges related to shell microstructural integrity. The observed shell brittleness in our high-productivity experimental site raises concerns regarding increased losses during shucking and transportation, potentially impacting product quality and market value1,39. Rapid growth in our farmed Pacific oysters correlated with the development of thicker, yet less dense and consequently more brittle shells, increasing their vulnerability to fracture during shucking after final harvest. This phenomenon likely stems from a metabolic trade-off21,25. The elevated metabolic rates associated with accelerated growth may divert resources away from shell mineralisation and the development of robust shell microarchitecture25,40–42. During fast growth, an increased rate of separation from the growth fronts of crystals likely suppressed the mantle’s production of calcite ions or calcium carbonate nanoaggregates, producing loosely packed laths21,22. While the influence of environmental factors on oyster growth is well-documented, the precise interplay between environmental conditions, growth rate, and shell development (structural and phenotypic) remains a subject of ongoing investigation. It is still unclear whether compromised shell microstructure (e.g., disorganised lamellar structure21,43 facilitates accelerated growth, or conversely, if rapid growth impedes the formation of a dense, mechanically sound shell matrix. Existing evidence suggests that growth rate exerts a primary influence on shell microstructural organisation, though the direct impact of environmental parameters, such as pH, on shell formation cannot be discounted44,45. Structurally compromised shells are more susceptible to damage during mechanical grading and more vulnerable to biofouling by boring and drilling predators46,47. Weak shells may have substantial economic repercussions throughout the oyster aquaculture supply chain.

Under reduced estuarine productivity (AGR = 0.13 mm/day in this study), oysters may develop robust, less chalky shells exhibiting enhanced resilience16,48. Whilst high current velocities are expected to induce mechanical stress, prompting increased energy allocation towards shell fortification at the expense of tissue growth49,50, this study presents counter evidence of the lower flow energy site in Pitt Water rather showing greater shell fortification. Slow growth in this estuary may have resulted in gradual layering of foliae with densely packed laths21, strengthening the shell and increasing resistance to shucking, compression, and inner shell surface indentation (Vickers’ hardness). Additionally, the availability of essential elements in marine sediments influences shell formation51, and the complex biochemical processes governing calcification involve specific proteins within the organic matrix that interact with calcium carbonate crystals, promoting nucleation and growth52–56. A comprehensive understanding of these mechanisms is vital for optimising oyster culture practices, particularly given the implications for adaptability to environmental changes, such as ocean acidification and temperature fluctuations47. This is critical of adaptive farming, as superior shell integrity plays a crucial role in conferring mechanical strength and resilience against predation and environmental stressors57 and is a desirable trait for withstanding the rigours of production, transportation, and processing, thereby ensuring optimal product delivery to consumers5.

Several of our newly quantified farmed oyster shell performance traits, including density, chalkiness, thickness, cup depth index, compressive strength, and colour, exhibited relatively higher heritability amongst the assessed traits. Shell exterior coloration had a strong genetic basis (family effect), but with considerable environmental influence, whereby lighter and darker hues were observed in oysters from the high- and low-productivity estuaries, respectively. While previous research has established the heritability of shell colour in Pacific oysters11,12,58, suggesting both polygenic and major gene control58, breeding for specific microstructural traits, such as hardness and chalkiness, remains relatively unexplored. The observed colour variation by estuary was not clearly linked to estuary productivity but appeared intertwined with family performance. Estuary effect on shell coloration is intriguing, but it is worthy of note that Pacific oysters farmed under prolonged air exposure are more impacted by ultraviolet irradiation, a phenomenon suspected to underly the development of darker shells compared to subtidally grown counterparts6. Further trials across more growing locations could help characterise leases for shell colour performance options for market. In the Australian industry, farmers view Pacific oysters with darkened shell exteriors to be more robust, while plump, creamy, veined meat within a pearly white inner shell with darkened margins (derived from dark shell exteriors) commands premium prices5. Considering the increasing market emphasis on shell hardness and appearance in certain regions for premium oysters sold in the shell1,39,59,60, these findings have significant implications for Pacific oyster aquaculture.

Our phenotyping approaches offer promising avenues for genetic improvement. Coupled with advancements in genomic selection61 and ongoing progress in enhancing growth and disease resistance18,62, targeted shell trait enhancement, encompassing both colour and microstructural integrity, could be a useful strategy for the future of oyster aquaculture. Shell shape, another acclaimed trait of importance to industry, was influenced by both environment and genetics, and their interactions. The experimental site supporting faster growth produced broader, shallower-cupped oysters whereas slow-growth environment produced narrower but deeper cupped oysters. Specific families also performed differently in the two estuaries, with generally improved shapes in the slower growth estuary, suggesting environments conducive to rapid growth may obscure the genetic variances that typically influence shell morphology. The heritability of shell shape indices also varied between sites; length indices (broadness/fan ratio) had comparable heritability across sites, width indices (cup depth ratio) were substantially different, favouring the slower growth estuary. Thus, whereas shell cup is a known heritable trait13,63,64, gains on selection may be greater under conditions of suppressed growth.

Morphological differences in shape/roundness or colour are not merely aesthetic but have tangible implications for the mechanical properties of the shells and vice-versa. The inferior compressive strength in faster growth oysters is not only attributable to their chalky or weak microstructural development, but can be linked to their broader, shallower shell cups. Oysters with deeper curvature, typically found in the slower growth environment, demonstrated enhanced resistance to compressive force. Lombardi et al. 4 found shells with greater curvature to withstand compressive forces better compared to their shallower counterparts in a combined analysis of Crassostrea virginica and Crassostrea ariakensis4. In the aquaculture supply chain, such strength may incorporate greater durability against cracking in bulk handling and loading for freight. Interestingly, Lombardi et al.’s findings further suggested thicker shells to have increased compressive strength4. Our extensive data from multiple estuaries in this study has shown otherwise that thicker shells arising from faster growth may well be weak shells. Therefore, previous assertions regarding the protective role of shell thickness may require environmental and shell structural integrity context. Hydrodynamic influences rather than fast growth could be responsible for the poor shell integrity in the Little Swanport estuary (lower summer and winter temperature extremes, greater DO fluctuation, higher salinity with deeper riverine incursions, and greater flow velocity). While these were associated trends, we could not establish a direct correlation with shell morphology.

Heritability estimates were generally lower within the faster-growth environment, indicating a stronger environmental contribution to phenotypic variance under favourable conditions. Of the twenty traits examined, twelve (shell chalkiness, thickness, cup depth ratio, compressive strength, shell height, roundness index, shell weights, shell length, whole weight, meat yield, and condition index) displayed significantly higher heritability in the slower-growth estuary. By contrast, only two traits (dry meat weight and breakage incidence) exhibited greater heritability under the higher-growth environment. These differences in estimated genetic parameters across environments has been observed in other Pacific oyster studies65,66. The remaining six traits (colour, density, shell width, hardness, broadness ratio, and wet meat weight) showed equivalent heritabilities across estuaries, suggesting relatively stable genetic contribution irrespective of environmental context67,68.

The analysis of standard morphometric traits in the present study yielded heritability estimates lower than those reported in earlier analyses from the same breeding population10, but remain within the range documented across other oyster studies12,61. Divergences from programme-level estimates may reflect both statistical and biological influences. While the number of full-sib families available in this study was lower than the standard program trials, the refined phenotyping and model structures employed are likely to have captured more accurate variance components for the specific traits, environments and genetic diversity present in the study. Whereas the larger number of families and multi-year datasets used to establish the reported breeding-program estimates will result in more precise and robust overall estimates for common traits69,70.

The genetic relationships between shell hardness, weight, and colour, despite the low phenotypic correlations offer alternative surrogate traits for selection. As highlighted by Vu et al. 71, selection for one trait often indirectly influences correlated traits, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of genetic potential without the restrictions of direct selection. Selecting for one of these traits is expected to provide advantageous correlated responses in others over generations, which is essential for breeding efficiency. Consequently, by prioritising traits with higher genetic correlations, breeders can maximise genetic gains across multiple traits simultaneously, thereby enhancing overall oyster production18,61. Difficult to measure traits can often be inferred from correlated easier-to-assess traits. For instance, whole weight and cup depth metrics, which are straightforward to evaluate, exhibit significant genetic and phenotypic correlations with shell thickness, a trait that presents measurement challenges. Furthermore, wet meat weight, shell weights (in air and water), and shell shape/roundness were found to be adequate surrogates for compressive strength, which requires specialised engineering expertise, while cup depth indices were significantly correlated with shell density. Such relationships highlight practical breeding approaches, where managing the cost of trait measurement is critical. Incorporating economically feasible metrics into breeding programmes aligns with the goals of modern aquaculture, where production efficiency is essential72.

The foregoing results and discussions demonstrate a promising outlook for redefining selective breeding strategies in Pacific oysters by integrating shell integrity traits to improve structural strength, aesthetics, and shape, alongside growth and meat yield. Although this study was not designed to generate operational selection indices, the trait-level patterns observed provide relevant guidance for industry-oriented breeding objectives. With the novel, precise shell phenotyping methods advanced in this study, practical metrics for assessing and selectively breeding oysters with improved shell quality for market are made available. Shell integrity traits, particularly shell thickness, compressive strength, chalkiness, cup-depth ratio, and roundness, emerge as strong candidates for improvement in programmes targeting half-shell and premium fresh markets, where shell robustness, shape, and visual quality strongly influence product value and handling losses. The moderate to high heritability of these traits indicates potential for genetic gain, provided correlated responses with growth and yield are evaluated in larger breeding populations. In contrast, traits reflecting tissue mass and short-term physiological condition (wet meat weight) and shell matrix (breakage incidence), displayed greater environmental sensitivity and lower or context-dependent heritability, suggesting that husbandry and site selection may remain more effective levers for optimisation than direct selection. Here, suppressed growth in Pitt Water estuary enhanced trait heritability. Integrative traits including condition index and meat yield showed consistent additive genetic variance and may support breeding objectives that balance shell quality with overall productivity and resilience. These two traits further showed strong genetic correlation, validating wet meat weight as a reliable proxy for dry meat weight. This streamlines productivity assessments by removing laborious demands of dry weight measurement in condition index determination.

Collectively, the findings of this study support a tiered breeding strategy prioritising shell robustness and market presentation, with growth- and yield-related traits incorporated cautiously and validated across environments, cohorts, and production cycles. Understanding the mechanistic links between environmental variability, shell microstructure and genetic control remains a key frontier for sustainable Pacific oyster aquaculture advancement. With a focus to detect and contextualise genetic signals across a diverse suite of traits, this study’s framework enabled a holistic interpretation of trait interrelationships and genetic architecture for twenty, largely novel, traits across two contrasting estuaries over a fifteen-month grow-out period to generate an unusually rich multidimensional dataset. Achieving comparable depth and resolution with a substantially higher family number would have been practically unattainable. Thus, the next step for shell-integrity traits is to measure the identified surrogate traits using the novel methods in a full performance program-level trial to confirm the genetic parameters and the potential for genetic improvement.

Methods

Oyster farming sites

Pacific oyster performance experimentation was conducted in two Tasmanian estuaries on established marine farming leases at, Little Swanport and Pitt Water (Fig. 1). Experimental sites were established at 42°18’51.51"S, 147°59’11.32"E and 42°48’3.22"S, 147°28’27.29"E, respectively. The leases were operated and owned by Little Swanport Oysters Pty. and Tasmanian Oysters Co., respectively. The Little Swanport trial site, situated on the eastern central Tasmanian coast, has direct oceanic impact (1.32 km from estuary mouth), is fed by the Little Swanport River, and exhibits pronounced seagrass meadow with organic matter-rich muddy bed. In contrast, the Pitt Water site, situated within the Pitt Water-Orielton Lagoon site in the relatively dry south of Tasmania, is further away from the estuary mouth (13.54 km) which enters an oceanic embayment (Fredrick Henry Bay), is influenced by inputs from the Coal River, and is characterised by more compact sandy-mud bed. Physicochemical parameters [temperature (°C), dissolved oxygen DO (mgL− 1), pH, salinity (PSU), turbidity (NTU), chlorophyll-a (RFU), and total algae (FRU)] were logged at 15 min intervals using EXO2 multiparameter sondes. Monthly in situ data retrieval, sensor cleaning and calibration using the KorEXO Software 1.6.0.0, and the exclusion of readings logged at less than 0.15 m depth and outside of water ensured data quality. Filtering out non-water and unstable readings at surface depths by relying on depth/pressure sensors was implemented to avoid erroneous water quality reporting – a potential pitfall in fixed meter intertidal water quality monitoring. Stock exposure to air was standardised across estuary based on farmer experience but not directly measured. Historical data was referenced for hydrodynamics at the two sites28.

Seed-stock origin

Same-age single-seed Pacific oyster spat from twenty full-sib pedigree-bred families (YC2021 cohort) were sourced from the ASI’s Pacific oyster breeding programme. Families were generated via single-pair matings using 16 sires and 17 dams, with each family formed from a unique sire \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:$$\end{document} dam combination, thus constituting distinct full-sib units connected by extended-sib links through repeated sire and dam families (not shared animal). Nine families shared lineage and 11 were pedigree-isolated. Shared lineage families comprised three paternally related (two sire families contributing to two families each, and one contributing to three families) and three maternally related families, providing informative pedigree structure for partitioning additive genetic variance. Broodstock conditioning, spawning, larval production and nursing to spat occurred at the Institute for Marine and Antarctic Studies’ biosecure hatchery facility in Taroona, Tasmania. Single seed spat (individual, unattached) were achieved by inducing metamorphosis73–75 of competent pediveligers ~ 250 μm, inhibiting natural attachment via a weeklong daily bath in 0.03 g L− 1 epinephrine bitartrate cum seawater solution (filtered, 35 ppt) for 1 h d− 19. Spat were reared in cylindrical PVC upwellers with nylon mesh bases. Seawater exchange was flowthrough. Larval nutrition comprised Chaetoceros muelleri, Isochrysis galbana, Pavlova lutheri, and Skeletonema pseudocostatum in proportions of 40%, 25%, 25%, and 10%, respectively. Algal diet concentration averaged 14,000 cells mL− 1 fed via flowthrough at 35 L min− 1. Prior to deployment to farm, spat were acclimated ex-situ (in hatchery) to experimental site conditions via gradual dry-out time (air exposure) adjustments, and a12 h photoperiod conditioning. Spat were size graded to ensure uniform initial size distribution.

Culture performance experiment design

Spat from the 20 oyster families were distributed to the two experimental sites in triplicate baskets (6 mm mesh size, 15 L GTX SEAPA®). Each basket contained 150 spat with mean shell height of 13.82 ± 2.02 mm and shell length of 10.61 ± 1.56 mm (S.D.). The baskets were secured on longlines using 20-mm standard clips. To secure spat and to ensure not losing them or prevent them from sticking through mesh holes of the growing baskets, spat were initially enclosed in 1.5-mm mesh nylon bags within the baskets and later released into the baskets after 58 d growth. Spat were cultivated further for 54 d before first sampling. Subsequent culturing over 15 mo (464 d at Pittwater and 454 d at Little Swanport) involved regular monitoring of shell development and growth/production metrics. Sampling was conducted at approximately 92 d intervals, ensuring no more than a two-day lag between sites for comparability. At each sampling event, 10 oysters were randomly selected from a basket for growth assessment and then returned to the same basket. Oysters were handled carefully to avoid excessive tumbling in baskets, avoiding removal of new shell growth. A total of 30 oysters per family, 600 per site, and 1,200 across both sites were evaluated during each sampling event (July and October in 2022, and January, April, and July in 2023). Baskets were cleaned of fouling, inspected for openings, and any dead oyster was removed with total mortality counts recorded against corresponding baskets (survival > 95% across baskets, did not warrant systematic stock density control with analogous spares). Oysters were transferred to 12-mm mesh 15 L GTX SEAPA® baskets for larger oysters (at 12 mo) to manage water circulation and supply of natural food. Baskets were perceptively placed to acquire comparable air exposure periods at both sites, mimicking regular oyster farm practice. Sampling was terminal in the final day.

Assessing culture performance and shell phenotypes

Growth and condition metrics

Standard oyster morphometric growth [whole weight (WW), shell height (SH), shell length (SL), shell width (SW)] was measured in-situ during sampling using a digital calliper (Mitutoyo, 0.01 mm precision) interfaced with a PC. Shell height represents the longest anterior-posterior distance, SL the longest dorsoventral distance, and SW the longest lateral cross-sectional distance between the left and right shell valves (cf. length, width, and depth, respectively in Australian aquaculture). Shell development phenotypes

At final sampling, oysters were terminally assessed for shell weight (ShWe) and shell thickness (ST). Shells were stored wet in a walk-in refrigerator at 4 °C until all shell data collection was completed.

Index of roundness (shape)

Oyster roundness is a perceived shape of relatively equal standard morphometrics (height, length, and width). Hypothetically, a height-length-width ratio of 1 represents a perfectly round oyster. However, oyster roundness is realistically perceived on two structural lattices; the upper lattice viewed from the right valve, and adjacent lattice from the posterior base. Length-to-height (length or width index; SR1 = L index) and width-to-length (cup depth index; SR2 = W index) ratios closer to one are realistic representations of roundness in an oyster. Therefore, shell roundness index (SRI) was calculated as the percent average of these two ratios (Eq. 3). SRI ranged from 0 to 100%, with values approaching zero indicating a slender, shallow-cupped oyster, while values closer to one suggest a rounder, deeper-cupped oyster. Inner shell chalkiness and shell exterior colour

To assess inner shell chalkiness (CHa) and shell exterior colour (Col), the left shell valves were analysed using image analysis in Fiji (ImageJ 1.54f). A rapid chromatic quantisation scheme was developed, employing mean intensity values on a single-channel grayscale to estimate shell chalkiness and colour numerically. The feasibility of this method for Pacific oysters relies on the distinct luminescence properties of their microstructural layers (chalky or foliated21,25 and shell exterior coloration. Chalky regions exhibit bright, white luminescence, while foliated regions display a dimmer luminescence (Fig. 7a). The shell exteriors of the Pacific oysters in this study exhibited a spectrum ranging from dark (black/deep purple) to light-coloured (whitish), with variations of dark dominant stripes or light-white stripes. Clean, de-fouled shells were photographed in a lightbox (Puluz® LED Portable Photo Studio PU5021) with a white backdrop. Images were captured from directly overhead at a constant height using a tripod-mounted iPhone 13 Pro Max (other high-resolution cameras are adaptable) equipped with a remote shutter release. The camera operated with fixed ISO settings under constant, diffused LED illumination in a dark environment to minimise extraneous light influence.

Shell hardness

Vickers hardness (HV) of individual oyster shell inner surfaces was measured using a SONODUR 3® Mobile Hardness Tester employing Ultrasonic Contact Impedance (UCI) technology (Fig. 7b). This method offers portability and reliability; circumventing limitations associated with traditional hardness testing. Cleaned shells were stabilised against a firm surface cushioned with folded paper towels. A diamond indenter, affixed to the tester’s probe, was applied to the inner shell surface under a controlled, spring-mediated force (50 N) for a brief period 1–2 s before release. The resulting micro-indentation induces vibrational damping, producing a measurable frequency shift that is translated into a Vickers hardness value, displayed on the Android-operated mobile device. This micro-indentation technique is non-destructive. Ten measurements were taken at pre-determined coordinates on each shell (four along the midline and three each above and below). The average of these ten readings was used as the representative hardness value for the shell.

Shell compressive strength

Compressive strength was measured using a Hounsfield Universal Testing Machine (50 kN) equipped with a 25 kN capacity flat load cell at M16 × 2.0 mm (Fig. 7b). The Hounsfield machine adhered to BSF (British Standard Fine) ¾ inch × 12 PTI (Proof Tracking Index) specifications. To reduce noise caused by point contacts between the steel bench and shell edges, two methods were trialled. The first involved capping the left shell valve with the right valve4, while the second utilized a 10 mm thick insulating rubber padding with a silver foil-laminated base (Ozatrail Model No.: EMF-EM10-B). Our preliminary testing indicated that the attachment of the right valve had a negligible effect due to the variability in shell margin contours. Therefore, the insulating rubber padding, which demonstrated optimal performance, was employed in this study (Supplementary Fig. S4). The testing parameters were set as follows: force range at 10%, extension at 50 mm, and speed at 20.0 mm min− 1. The position of the applied force was maintained constant for all shells.

For each trait, the LRT statistic was compared against a chi-squared distribution with 1 degree of freedom.
