Early Antibiotic Discontinuation for Children With a Positive Rapid Respiratory Viral Test Hospitalized for Suspected Bacterial Tracheostomy‐Associated Infections
Authors: Christopher J. Russell, Naoko Kono, Margaret Rush, Andrea Hahn, Catherine S. Forster, Jonathan D. Cogen, Sarah Hofman DeYoung, John M. Morrison, Joanna Thomson, Sukhada Vaidya Mairal, Wendy J. Mack, Michael N. Neely, Tamara D. Simon, for the Pediatric Research in Inpatient Settings Network
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTest the association between early antibiotic discontinuation and outcomes for children with a tracheostomy and positive respiratory viral PCR (RVP) hospitalized with suspected bTRAIN (bacterial TRacheostomy‐Associated INfection; pneumonia/tracheitis).MethodsWe conducted a multicenter cohort study between September 1, 2021 and August 31, 2024 of children with pre‐existing tracheostomy hospitalized for suspected bTRAIN (tracheal aspirate bacterial culture sent and bTRAIN antibiotics started) at six children's hospitals with: (1) a positive RVP test; (2) no antibiotic pre‐treatment; and (3) length of stay (LOS) < 37 days (outlier). Mixed‐effects regression models identified independent associations between early antibiotic discontinuation and our primary outcome, LOS (in days). Inverse probability treatment weighting based on propensity scoring adjusted for measured confounding. We explored the association between early antibiotic discontinuation and 3 secondary outcomes: late ICU transfer, in‐hospital mortality, and 30‐day bTRAIN rehospitalization rates.ResultsOf 542 hospitalizations in 365 children, the median admission age was 4 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 2–8 years). Common viruses included rhinovirus (n = 282; 52%), SARS‐CoV‐2 (n = 63; 12%), and respiratory syncytial virus (n = 61; 11%). The median LOS was 7 days (IQR: 5–11 days). Only 28% (n = 152) had early antibiotic discontinuation; full bTRAIN treatment was associated with a 23% (5%–45%) increased LOS, with estimated LOS means (95% CI) of 7.0 (5.8–8.4) days for early antibiotic discontinuation and 8.6 (7.8–9.4) days in the bTRAIN‐treated group. Secondary outcome rates (late ICU transfer = 14.6%; in‐hospital mortality = 0.9%; 30‐day bTRAIN rehospitalization rate = 8.3%) were similar rates between both groups.ConclusionsGiven similar rates of serious outcomes in both groups, clinicians could consider early discontinuation of antibiotic treatment in children with suspected bTRAINs who have a positive RVP test.

Introduction

Children with a tracheostomy often receive antibiotics while hospitalized for bacterial tracheostomy‐associated infections (bTRAINs), such as bacterial pneumonia and tracheitis [1, 2, 3, 4]. The absence of a gold standard for bTRAIN diagnostic testing and inconsistent reporting of test results [5, 6, 7] leads clinicians to over‐treat positive respiratory cultures with antibiotics [8, 9]. Despite a positive respiratory viral PCR (RVP) test being associated with decreased odds of bTRAIN treatment [1], many children with a tracheostomy and positive RVP continue to receive antibiotic treatment. One possible reason to continue antibiotic treatment is because of the concerns that viral infections increase pro‐inflammatory cytokines, disrupting the respiratory tract epithelium and increasing risk for bacterial superinfection [10, 11, 12]. Recent studies evaluating the microbiome in children with tracheostomy demonstrated that viral triggers were associated with increased growth of previously colonizing bacteria [13]. Taken together, these factors influence clinician decisions to treat with antibiotics for possible bacterial co‐infection.

Because most respiratory infections in children are viral [14], children with a tracheostomy may receive unnecessary antibiotics, placing them at risk for adverse events, such as development of antibiotic‐resistant organisms, Clostridium difficile infection and end‐organ damage. Additionally, bTRAIN antibiotic treatment is associated with longer length of stay (LOS) [1], putting children at increased risk for healthcare‐associated infections and other adverse events. Determining if outcomes for children with a tracheostomy and a positive RVP test differ with antibiotic treatment may improve antibiotic stewardship and decrease unnecessary antibiotic exposure. The objective of the current study was to test the association between early antibiotic discontinuation and outcomes in children with a tracheostomy and positive RVP. We hypothesized that children with a tracheostomy and a positive RVP treated with a full course of bTRAIN antibiotics would have a longer LOS, compared to those who had early bTRAIN antibiotic discontinuation. We further hypothesized that late intensive care unit (ICU) transfer (after hospital day 2) and 30‐day bTRAIN readmission rates would be low and not different between children receiving full bTRAIN treatment and those with early antibiotic discontinuation.

Methods

Study Setting and Population

We conducted a multicenter cohort study between September 1, 2021 and August 31, 2024 of children with pre‐existing tracheostomy hospitalized for suspected bTRAIN at six quaternary children's hospitals who: (1) had a positive RVP test on initial evaluation; (2) were not pre‐treated with antibiotics; and (3) without prolonged LOS (> 37 days, extreme outlier). A positive RVP test was defined as detection of a virus on a respiratory viral PCR test of any type, such as SARS‐CoV‐2 only, limited panels (e.g., respiratory syncytial virus/influenza/SARS‐CoV‐2), or expanded panels (e.g., BioFire Respiratory Pathogen Testing; in‐house developed test). All participating hospitals have in‐house on‐site RVP testing; the turnaround time was 1–2 h (for five sites) and up to 12 h (for one site) for all days of the week. Suspected bTRAIN was defined by a respiratory culture sent within 48 h of admission and receipt of empiric bTRAIN antibiotic treatment. We included multiple encounters for each patient hospitalization meeting study criteria during the study period. We excluded patients who were diagnosed and received antibiotic treatment for a non‐bTRAIN indication (e.g., urinary tract infection, skin/soft tissue infection) and those who received no dose of antibiotics. Each participating site's Institutional Review Board reviewed the parent study and deemed it exempt.

Data Collection

Trained staff at each site manually gathered information from the electronic health records in accordance with standardized procedures. Data captured related to patient demographics, comorbid conditions, home medications, and respiratory support, as well as clinical presentation and diagnostic assessments at the time of suspected bTRAIN infection. Demographic information included age, sex, race and ethnicity, insurance provider, gestational age, chronic medical conditions, and living situation (home without nursing, home with nursing, or skilled nursing facility). We classified baseline home respiratory support into three categories: mechanical ventilation (regardless of duration), oxygen support only, or no support. Symptoms reported for suspected bTRAIN infection included temperature instability (e.g., fever or hypothermia), hypoxemia, respiratory distress, elevated ventilator settings or usage (from baseline), and alterations in tracheostomy secretions. We included diagnostic tests performed upon presentation, such as: (1) systemic inflammatory markers (white blood cell [WBC] count; C‐reactive protein [CRP]; procalcitonin); (2) imaging studies [chest radiograph (CXR)]; (3) rapid respiratory viral PCR testing; (4) respiratory Gram‐stain WBC results; and (5) respiratory culture results. All data entered underwent multiple rounds of data quality control and data assurance testing.

Primary Predictor

Our primary predictor was early antibiotic discontinuation. We defined early antibiotic discontinuation as children who received empiric antibiotics for bTRAIN, but who had antibiotics discontinued and did not receive bTRAIN treatment. The comparator group, those who did not receive early antibiotic discontinuation, had: (1) a discharge diagnosis of a bTRAIN (e.g., pneumonia, tracheitis); and (2) prescription of a complete course of antibiotic treatment (inpatient and outpatient), as defined and documented by the clinical team. Our primary predictor was independently verified by two study team members, one of whom was a physician on the study team. Any discrepancies between these two reviews were adjudicated between site study team members. If still unresolved, discrepancies were reviewed with the larger multi‐institution study team members.

Primary Outcome Variable

Our primary outcome variable was LOS (in days). Our secondary outcomes included: (1) late ICU transfer, defined as admission to the pediatric, neonatal or cardiac ICU ≥ 2 days after hospital admission for those not directly admitted to the ICU; (2) in‐hospital mortality; and (3) 30‐day bTRAIN rehospitalization rates.

Statistical Analysis

Bivariate associations between demographic, clinical and diagnostic factors and early antibiotic discontinuation were tested using a logistic mixed‐effects regression model specifying a binary distribution (for early antibiotic discontinuation), logit link function, and random effects for hospital and child.

Primary Outcome

To identify the independent association between early antibiotic discontinuation and our primary outcome of LOS (in days), we performed inverse probability treatment weighted (IPTW) analyses. We computed propensity scores for early antibiotic discontinuation for each hospitalization using a multivariable logistic mixed‐effects regression model. Covariates included in the early antibiotic discontinuation propensity scores included age (in years), race and ethnicity, insurance status and clinical and diagnostic factors including: (1) outpatient medications (e.g., ipratropium, inhaled antibiotics, glycopyrrolate, nebulized saline, proton‐pump inhibitors, and histamine‐2 blockers, all entered separately); (2) historical clinical factors, such as pre‐admission ventilatory support; (3) presenting symptoms, such as hypoxia, breathing difficulties, increase ventilator needs, and changes in tracheostomy secretions; (4) testing, such as CRP, respiratory WBC count on gram stain, chest radiograph findings, respiratory culture results; and (5) late ICU admission (within 2 days of being hospitalized). We used stabilized IPTW to estimate the average treatment effect (ATE) based on the propensity scores. Covariate balance was verified between the weighted early antibiotic discontinuation groups. A generalized linear mixed‐effects regression model specifying a negative binomial distribution for LOS, log link function, random effects for hospital and patient, and robust standard errors was used on the IPTW samples to evaluate the association of early antibiotic discontinuation on LOS.

Secondary Outcomes

For those children admitted initially to the acute care floor, we examined the association between timing of ICU admission and early antibiotic discontinuation. The association between early antibiotic discontinuation and late ICU admission was evaluated using IPTW analyses. Propensity scores and stabilized ATE weights were computed on the subset of children admitted to the ICU. A mixed‐effects logistic regression model with random effects for hospital and patient, and robust standard errors was used on the IPTW sample. Associations between early antibiotic discontinuation and (1) in‐hospital mortality; and (2) 30‐day rehospitalization were assessed using Fisher's exact test for both outcomes. IPTW was not used due to the low numbers of these outcome events, which can lead to unstable weights and biased estimates.

Ethical Approval

The parent study has been granted an exemption per 45 CFR 46.104[d][#4][iii] and a waiver of HIPAA authorization per the Privacy Rule (45 CFR Part 160 and Subparts A and E of Part 164) by the following IRBs: Children's Hospital Los Angeles, Stanford University, Cincinnati Children's Hospital, Children's National Medical Center, Johns Hopkins Medicine, Seattle Children's Hospital, and University of Pittsburgh. The study was exempt and therefore there was no consent needed (All sites received a waiver of informed consent) per the same IRBs that are listed in the ethical approval statement above.

Results

Study Cohort Demographics (Table 1)

Of 542 hospitalizations in 365 children meeting inclusion criteria, 57.9% of hospitalizations were associated with male gender, and 78.4% with only public insurance. The median age for all admissions was 4 years [interquartile range (IQR): 2–8 years]. For comorbidities, 43.7% (n = 237) of children had a history of prematurity < 37 weeks gestational age and 66.4% (n = 360) a history of neuromuscular comorbidity. Over 54% of hospitalizations were associated with home mechanical ventilation prior to admission.

Viruses Detected

Common viruses detected on RVP included rhinovirus (n = 282; 52%), SARS‐CoV‐2 (n = 63; 12%), and respiratory syncytial virus (n = 61; 11%). With respect to the number of viruses detected, 92.1% (n = 499) had one virus detected, 7.6% (n = 41) two viruses, and 0.4% (n = 2) three viruses. Of the 7.6% (n = 41) with multiple viruses, all had rhinovirus plus one additional virus, with the second virus most commonly being adenovirus (n = 7), RSV (n = 4), parainfluenza 3 (n = 4), and SARS‐CoV‐2 (n = 4).

Association Between Demographic and Baseline Clinical Variables and Early Antibiotic Discontinuation (Table 1)

On bivariable testing, those in the early antibiotic discontinuation group had fewer inpatient antibiotic days (median [IQR] = 3 [2–4] days), compared to those in the bTRAIN‐treated group (median [IQR] = 6 [5–8] days; p < 0.001). There were no statistically significant associations of early antibiotic discontinuation with patient demographics or comorbidities associated with indication for tracheotomy. Children on nebulized saline were more likely to have early antibiotic discontinuation (41% of those without early antibiotic discontinuation vs. 56.6% of those with early antibiotic discontinuation; p = 0.02). Additionally, there were clinically meaningful associations between histamine‐2‐receptor antagonists and early antibiotic discontinuation, with 16.4% of those receiving early antibiotic discontinuation, compared to 24% not receiving early antibiotic discontinuation (p = 0.07).

Association Between Acute Clinical and Diagnostic Factors and Early Antibiotic Discontinuation (Table 2)

Only 28% of hospitalizations with a positive RVP test received early antibiotic discontinuation. On bivariable testing, lower inflammatory markers were associated with early antibiotic discontinuation, including WBC counts (median 9.6 [IQR: 6.6, 14.3] vs. 11.6 [IQR: 8.1, 15.5] for bTRAIN treatment; p = 0.03) and CRP (median 2.8 [IQR: 0.9, 5.0] vs. 3.8 mg/dL [IQR: 1.5, 7.7] for bTRAIN treatment; p = 0.006). Additionally, there were associations between both CXR abnormality (p < 0.001) and positive respiratory culture results (p < 0.001) and likelihood of early antibiotic discontinuation. Other clinical symptoms and tests of systemic inflammation were not associated with early antibiotic discontinuation.

Primary Outcome (Table 3)

The median LOS for the entire cohort was 7 days (IQR: 5–11 days). In unadjusted analyses, those children with a positive RVP receiving early antibiotic discontinuation had shorter lengths of stay (6, IQR: 4–9), when compared to children undergoing full bTRAIN treatment (8, IQR: 5–12; p = 0.01). On IPTW analysis to adjust for confounding by indication, when compared to those with early antibiotic discontinuation, children receiving bTRAIN treatment was associated with a 23% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 5%–45%) longer LOS, with estimated means (95% CI) from the mixed model of 7.0 (5.8, 8.4) days in the early antibiotic discontinuation group, versus 8.6 (7.8, 9.4) days in the bTRAIN‐treated group.

Secondary Outcomes (Table 3)

For those initially admitted to the acute care floor, the overall unplanned ICU transfer rate was 14.6% (n = 47). There were no differences in late ICU admission rates for children with complete bTRAIN treatment (14.8%, n = 35/322) compared to those receiving early antibiotic discontinuation group (13.9%, n = 12/322; adjusted odds ratio [aOR] (95% CI) = 1.58 (0.52, 4.78). The 30‐day bTRAIN readmission rate was 8.3% (n = 44/527), with no association between those patients in the early antibiotic discontinuation group (7.5%, n = 11), compared to those receiving complete bTRAIN treatment (8.7%, n = 33; p = 0.89). The overall in‐hospital mortality rate was 0.92%, with no association between those children with a positive RVP in the early antibiotic discontinuation group (0.66%, n = 1) versus complete bTRAIN treatment (1.03%, n = 4; p = 1.00).

Discussion

In our multicenter observational study of children with a tracheostomy hospitalized for suspected bTRAIN with a positive RVP test, bTRAIN treatment was independently associated with a 23% increase in LOS without any significant differences in outcomes, such as late ICU transfer, in‐hospital mortality, and 30‐day bTRAIN hospital readmission. The longer LOS and readmission rates are consistent with previous work testing the association between bTRAIN treatment and outcomes [1]. Our findings, which used propensity scores with inverse probability of treatment weights to adjust for confounding by indication, call into question the empiric use of antibiotic bTRAIN treatment for children with a positive RVP test.

The impact of a viral acute respiratory illness in children with tracheostomy deserves more exploration. Because viruses are common causes of acute lower respiratory illness, upon identification of a viral source, some clinicians discontinue antibiotics and monitor clinically. Others continue antibiotics because viral acute respiratory illnesses can disrupt the tracheal epithelial lining, increasing chances of bacterial invasion and subsequent secondary bacterial infections. Our findings of high antibiotic use in children with a tracheostomy and identified viral infection is consistent with other studies examining antibiotic use for viral illness in otherwise healthy children [15, 16, 17, 18] and children with a tracheostomy [19].

Our results complement a recent investigation into viral impact on the dynamics of the airway microbiome in children with a tracheostomy. In a convenience sample of 20 patients with tracheostomy and ventilator dependence, Pérez‐Losada and colleagues [13] found a significant decrease in bacterial microbiome alpha‐diversity upon development of an acute respiratory illness, with resultant predominance of Haemophilus and Moraxella species. The complex interplay between the viral and bacterial microbiomes in children with a pre‐existing tracheostomy warrants future research.

While early antibiotic treatment may prevent serious adverse events in children with a tracheostomy who have suspected bacterial infections, this must be balanced with possible adverse events due to antibiotic treatment. Antibiotic exposure is associated with diarrhea, neutropenia, development of airway multidrug‐resistant organisms, and microbiome disruption, among many other adverse effects [20, 21, 22, 23]. Children with chronic pulmonary conditions are at higher risk for more antibiotic exposures [24]. Given that children with tracheostomy experience a median of four antibiotic courses and 34 days of antibiotics per year for acute treatments [25], this is a population at particularly high‐risk for antibiotic‐associated adverse outcomes.

The current study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. While the early antibiotic discontinuation group had antibiotics discontinued after a median of 3 days, all patients received at least one dose of antibiotics. In otherwise healthy children with community‐acquired pneumonia, two meta‐analyses demonstrated similar clinical cure and treatment failure rates when comparing shorter (3–5 days) and longer (7–10 days) antibiotic courses [26, 27]. However, no evidence for the optimal antibiotic duration exists in this study population. Thus, the lack of an association between early antibiotic discontinuation and clinical outcomes in the current study may be because the pre‐discontinuation antibiotic course was sufficient for clinical cure. During the study timeframe, because there was no national clinical practice guideline for bTRAIN evaluation and treatment, this may have led to different treatment plans, including thresholds for antibiotic therapy and influence other outcomes, suggesting confounding by indication. Children with a tracheostomy admitted for suspected bTRAINs are cared for by different combinations of multiple services, including critical care, pulmonology, pediatric hospital medicine and general pediatrics, all of whom have different practice patterns and risk tolerance. We attempted to address both by using IPTW to adjust for confounding by indication. Similarly, there is no standard definition nor guidelines for diagnosis of bTRAINs in children; thus, some patients identified as receiving bTRAIN treatment may have been given antibiotics for chronic bacterial colonization in the setting of a viral respiratory tract illness. The primary exposure, a positive RVP, is dependent on the test being completed and its accuracy. Current RVP testing only detects a handful of viruses and there are no guidelines for which children and when RVP testing should be completed in children with a tracheostomy. Thus, there are likely patients who had a viral co‐infection that was not detected, either because they were not tested or because they had a virus not captured on the current RVP panels; both groups would not be included in the current study population. Due to the larger study aims, we did not include children with a positive RVP who received no antibiotics. Finally, while the absolute incidence of some secondary outcomes was low, we are likely to be underpowered to detect differences, particularly with the absolute mortality rate of 0.9%.

Despite these limitations, this study has several important implications. While viral testing is discouraged in some clinical settings, rapid identification of a viral co‐infection in children hospitalized with suspected bTRAINs may decrease antibiotic exposure without worsening clinical outcomes. Our results also highlight the need for continued research to determine the mechanisms by which viruses impact the bacterial airway microbiome in children with tracheostomy.

Conclusion

Given longer lengths of stay with similar rates of ICU transfer, in‐hospital mortality, and 30‐day bTRAIN readmission rates, regardless of bTRAIN treatment, as well as the risks of adverse events that cumulative antibiotic exposure cause in children with tracheostomy, clinicians could consider early antibiotic discontinuation in children hospitalized with suspected bTRAINs who have a positive RVP test. Prospective randomized trials using RVP testing results to guide bTRAIN antibiotic treatment are needed to confirm our study findings.
