Candidate Monotonicity and Proportionality for Lotteries and Non-Resolute Rules
Jannik Peters

Abstract
We study the problem of designing multiwinner voting rules that are candidate monotone and proportional. We show that the set of committees satisfying the proportionality axiom of proportionality for solid coalitions is candidate monotone. We further show that Phragmén’s Ordered Rule can be turned into a candidate monotone probabilistic rule which randomizes over committees satisfying proportionality for solid coalitions.

1
Proportionality and Monotonicity

Proportional multiwinner voting rules are among the most well studied in computational social choice. Among them, the
Single Transferable Vote (STV)
is used in several countries, e.g., Australia, Malta, or Scotland, to elect governing bodies. One of the main reasons STV can be considered proportional is its satisfaction of the
proportionality for solid coalitions (PSC)
axiom
(
Dummett, 1984
;
Tideman, 1995
)
. In essence, for ordinal preferences lists PSC requires that every group of voters listing the same block of candidates (in potentially different permutations) at the start of their preference list, should get proportionally many candidates from this block. For instance, if
5
candidates were to be elected and
40\%
of the voters have the same three candidates in their top three, then two out of these three candidates need to be chosen in the end.
1
1

1

This is slightly simplifying things, the commonly used variants of STV usually work with a “quota” that is slightly lower, the so-called Droop quota
\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil
with
n
being the number of voters and
k
the number of candidates to be elected. For ease of exposition, we focus on the Hare quota
\frac{n}{k}
instead. The results also hold for the Droop quota.
One of the major flaws of STV is its failure of
candidate monotonicity
: strengthening a winning candidate can make this candidate stop winning
(
Woodall, 1997
;
McCune and Graham-Squire, 2024
)
. Interestingly enough, whether this flaw is a necessity is an open question. Namely, it is unknown whether there is any voting rule satisfying PSC together with candidate monotonicity
(
Woodall, 1997
;
Aziz and Lee, 2020
)
with most known proportional voting rules failing candidate monotonicity already for very small instance sizes
(
Aziz and Lee, 2020
)
. Despite
Woodall
already writing that “a major unsolved problem is whether there exist rules that retain the important political features of STV and are also more monotonic”
(
Woodall, 1997
, page 1)
no such rule has been found or proven to be nonexistent.

Our Contribution

In this paper, we tackle this open question from two slightly weaker angles. Firstly, we study
non-resolute
voting rules: rules that do not just output one committee, but a whole set of committees. For this setting, we show that the rule outputting all committees satisfying PSC is indeed candidate monotone. Secondly, we study
probabilistic
voting rules, i.e., rules selecting a lottery over committees. For this, we show that a variant of Phragmén’s Ordered Rule
(
Janson, 2016
)
can be adapted into a candidate monotone lottery over committees satisfying PSC.

Related Work

Our work is situated in the area of proportional multiwinner voting with ranked preferences, in particular in the axiomatic study of multiwinner voting rules. This study dates back to the work of eminent philosopher
Dummett (1984)
and his development of PSC.
Aziz and Lee (2020)
were the first to give slightly improved results for monotonicity and proportionality, showing that their Expanding Approvals Rule (EAR) satisfies a weaker form of monotonicity together with a generalization of PSC in the setting of weak ranked preferences in which ties are allowed. The same generalization of PSC together with a form of monotonicity they call “indifference monotonicity” is also satisfied by the generalization of STV by
Delemazure and Peters (2024)
.
McCune and Graham-Squire (2024)
experimentally studied monotonicity violations in a real-world dataset consisting of Scottish local elections and found that out of 1079 elections 62 possessed some kind of “monotonicity anomaly”. In particular, in
23
elections STV failed the version of candidate monotonicity studied by us.

PSC was further investigated by
Brill and Peters (2023)
who criticized it for being a relatively weak axiom and introduced stronger proportionality axioms still satisfied by EAR. Further,
Aziz and Lee (2021)
introduced PSC generalizations for the context of participatory budgeting
(
Peters et al., 2021
)
and in
Aziz and Lee (2022)
provided a characterization of PSC in the standard ordinal preference setting. Finally, recently
Aziz et al. (2025)
studied committee monotonicity, i.e., the selection of a ranking of candidates, together with PSC and showed that a rule satisfying both PSC and committee monotonicity exists.

In the setting of approval-based multiwinner voting, monotonicity is significantly easier to obtain
(
Sánchez-Fernández and Fisteus, 2019
)
with most rules being monotone in the sense that voters additionally approving an already winning candidate cannot hurt that candidate.
2
2

2

In fact, all voting rules studied in the recent book of
Lackner and Skowron
on approval-based multiwinner voting satisfy candidate monotonicity
(
Lackner and Skowron, 2022
, Table 3.1)
However, it can for instance happen that adding voters only approving a single candidate can make this candidate not be chosen anymore (see
Boehmer et al. (2024)
for examples of this in the context of participatory budgeting).

Finally, our results are also situated in the area of probabilistic social choice. Here, we are closely related to recent works focusing on
best of both world
guarantees, i.e., the design of lotteries satisfying desirable properties both ex-ante and ex-post. In particular, recent work focusing on the design of randomized apportionment schemes
(
Gölz et al., 2024
;
Correa et al., 2024
)
and on achieving ex-post and ex-ante proportionality guarantees in approval-based multiwinner voting and participatory budgeting
(
Aziz et al., 2023
;
Aziz et al., 2024
;
Suzuki and Vollen, 2024
)
are close to our section on probabilistic voting rules.

Preliminaries

Notation

For
t\in\mathbb{N}
let
[t]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,t\}
. We are given a set
N=[n]
of
voters
and a set
C=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\}
of
candidates
. Each voter
i\in N

\succ_{i}\subseteq C\times C
over all candidates. Further, we are given a
committee size

k\leq m
. Together, the voters, candidates, preferences, and committee size form an instance
\mathcal{I}=(N,C,(\succ_{i})_{i\in N},k)
. A

W\subseteq C

\lvert W\rvert=k
. Let
C_{k}=\{W\subseteq C\colon\lvert W\rvert=k\}
be the set of all committees. A
rule

\mathcal{I}
to a non-empty set of committees
r(\mathcal{I})\subseteq C_{k}
. If
\lvert r(\mathcal{I})\rvert=1
for all instances

the rule is said to be resolute.

For probabilistic rules, we mostly follow the notation of
Aziz et al. (2023)
. We say that a fractional committee is an assignment of probabilities
{(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})\in[0,1]^{m}}
for each candidate with
\sum_{i\in[m]}p_{i}=k
with a fractional committee rule mapping instances to fractional committees. We let
\Delta(C_{k})
be the set of all probability distributions over
C_{k}
. A probabilistic rule
r
then maps an instance
\mathcal{I}
to a probability distribution
r(\mathcal{I})\in\Delta(C_{k})
. Each probabilistic rule also has a natural non-resolute counterpart
\supp(r(\mathcal{I}))
, i.e., the set of committees being selected with non-zero probability (or the support of
r(\mathcal{I})
). Further, each probabilistic rule

implements a fractional committee voting rule, with the probability of each candidate being the probability of this candidate being on the committee selected by
r
. On the other hand, there can be several probabilistic voting rules implementing a fractional committee voting rule.

Proportionality

Following the seminal work of
Dummett (1984)
we define the axiom of
proportionality for solid coalitions (PSC)
. For this, we first need to define the eponymous solid coalitions.

Definition 1

(Solid Coalition)
.

A group
N^{\prime}\subseteq N
of voters forms a
solid coalition
over a group
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of candidates if

c^{\prime}\succ_{i}c\text{ for all }i\in N^{\prime},c^{\prime}\in C^{\prime},\text{ and }c\in C\setminus C^{\prime}.

In essence, PSC now requires that every solid coalition is represented proportionally.

Definition 2

(Proportionality for Solid Coalitions
(
Dummett, 1984
)
)
.

A committee
W
satisfies
proportionality for solid coalitions (PSC)
if there is no group
N^{\prime}\subseteq N
of voters,
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of candidates and
\ell\in[k]
such that

i.)

N^{\prime}
forms a solid coalition over
C^{\prime}

ii.)

\lvert N^{\prime}\rvert\geq\ell\frac{n}{k}

iii.)

\lvert W\cap C^{\prime}\rvert<\min(\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert,\ell)
.

In other words, if
C^{\prime}
forms a prefix of the preferences of all voters in
N^{\prime}
then
W
needs to contain a large enough “proportional share” of them to satisfy
N^{\prime}
.

There are several rules satisfying PSC, among them the famous
Single Transferable Vote (STV)

(
Tideman, 1995
)
and the
Expanding Approvals Rule (EAR)

(
Aziz and Lee, 2020
)
.
Note that due to our definition of rules the set of all committees satisfying PSC is also a (non-resolute) rule.

Example 1
.

Consider the following instance with four voters, four candidates, and
k=2
:

•

c_{1}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}

•

c_{2}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}

•

c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{2}

•

c_{4}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{3}
.

This instance contains one non-trivial solid coalition large enough to be able to demand a candidate, namely the first and second voter over
\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
(and technically also over
\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
). Thus, any committee containing at least one of
c_{1}
or
c_{2}
satisfies PSC. Now if the third voter would swap candidate
c_{4}
up, the third and fourth voter would form a solid coalition over
\{c_{4}\}
. Therefore, in this new instance the only two PSC committees are
\{c_{1},c_{4}\}
and
\{c_{2},c_{4}\}

Monotonicity

For our definition of (candidate) monotonicity for non-resolute voting rules, we follow
Elkind et al. (2017)
.

Definition 3

(Candidate Monotonicity
(
Elkind et al., 2017
)
)
.

A rule
r
satisfies
candidate monotonicity
if for every instance
\mathcal{I}
, committee
W\in r(\mathcal{I})
and
c\in W
in any instance
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
obtained by shifting
c
one rank up in the preference list of any voter, there is still some committee
W^{\prime}\in r(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
with
c\in W^{\prime}
.

That is, if the candidate
c
is in some winning committee and is “made stronger”
c
is still in some winning committee afterward. It is known that neither STV nor EAR are candidate monotone
(
Aziz and Lee, 2020
)
. However, Phragmén’s ordered rule is indeed candidate monotone
(
Janson, 2016
)
.

To generalize candidate monotonicity to probabilistic rules, we instead focus on the probabilities of individual candidates.

Definition 4

(Candidate Monotonicity for Probabilistic Rules)
.

A probabilistic rule
r
satisfies candidate monotonicity if for every instance
\mathcal{I}
and
c\in C
in any instance
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
obtained by shifting
c
one rank up in the preference list of any voter it holds that

\Pr[c\in r(\mathcal{I})]\leq\Pr[c\in r(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})].

In other words, shifting a candidate up in the preference lists of voters does not decrease their chances to be selected. This is indeed in some sense a stronger requirement than candidate monotonicity for non-resolute rules.

Observation 1
.

If
r
is a candidate monotone probabilistic rule, then
\supp(r)
is a candidate monotone non-resolute rule.

Similarly, one can also define candidate monotonicity for fractional voting rules by looking at the individual probabilities.

3
Non-Resoluteness and Monotonicity

We start off our results by showing that the rule that selects all PSC committees is candidate monotone.
For this we crucially make use of a characterization of PSC by
Aziz and Lee (2022)
using their
minimal demand rules
. These works as follows:
We start off with an empty committee
W=\emptyset
. For each rank
r=1,\dots,m
we check if there is a group of voters forming a solid coalition over a set
C^{\prime}
of candidates of size
r
. If, additionally, this solid coalition witnesses a PSC violation, we add a candidate from
C^{\prime}\setminus W
to
W
. We repeat this until there is no further PSC violation, afterwards we increment
r
. A committee now satisfies PSC if and only if it can be obtained via this process.

Theorem 2

(Characterization of PSC
(
Aziz and Lee, 2022
)
)
.

A committee
W
satisfies PSC if and only if it can be obtained by a minimal demand rule.

Using this characterization, we can now show that the set of PSC committees is indeed candidate monotone.

Theorem 3
.

The rule selecting all PSC committees is candidate monotone.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an instance,
W
a committee satisfying PSC in
\mathcal{I}
, and
c\in W
. Assume that there is a voter swapping
c
up in their preference list and denote this instance by
\mathcal{I}_{c}
. We now construct a new committee
W_{c}
containing
c
by running a minimal demand rule on
\mathcal{I}_{c}
. We start off by initializing
W_{c}=\emptyset
and iteratively check for each rank
r=1,\dots,m
if there exists a solid coalition consisting of a set
N^{\prime}\subseteq N
of voters and
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of candidates witnessing a PSC violation in
\mathcal{I}_{c}
. If
c
is already a part of
W_{c}
we can include an arbitrary candidate from
C^{\prime}\setminus W_{c}
to
W_{c}
. Otherwise, we distinguish two cases.

Case 1:
c\notin C^{\prime}
.
If this is the case, as
c
was swapped up, the set
N^{\prime}
together with
C^{\prime}
already formed a solid coalition in the original instance
\mathcal{I}
. Thus, as
W
satisfies PSC there must be more candidates in
W\cap C^{\prime}
than in
W_{c}\cap C^{\prime}
. Hence, there exists a candidate
c^{\prime}\in(W\cap C^{\prime})\setminus W_{c}
and we can include this candidate in the committee
W_{c}
.

Case 2:
c\in C^{\prime}
.
Then we include
c
in the committee
W_{c}
.

This constitutes a valid execution of a minimal demand rule. Thus, the committee
W_{c}
satisfies PSC. If we ever entered the second case, we know that
c\in W_{c}
has to hold. Otherwise, we always entered the first case. However, then the
k
candidates selected by
W_{c}
are precisely the
k
candidates in
W
. As
c\in W
this implies
c\in W_{c}
.
∎

4
A Probabilistic Version of Phragmén’s Ordered Rule

While the previous result gives us some hope for obtaining a proportional and candidate monotone rule, the result is also quite weak. There can be a lot of committees satisfying PSC
(
Bardal et al., 2025
)
and our monotonicity notion only guarantees us that some committee includes the strengthened candidate. To remedy this, we study probabilistic voting rules. In particular, we use a variant of
Phragmén’s Ordered Rule

(
Janson, 2016
)
and turn it into a lottery over committees satisfying PSC.

Phragmén’s Ordered Rule

Phragmén’s Ordered Rule was originally introduced by a Swedish royal commission (including Phragmén himself) and was used for the intra party seat distribution in the Swedish parliament. For more historic context see the work by
Janson (2016)
.

Phragmén’s Ordered Rule works as a continuous process. Each voter starts to “eat” their top choice candidate at the same speed. Once a candidate is fully eaten, this candidate is elected onto the committee and the voters move on to their top choice “uneaten” candidate. This is done until
k
candidates are elected.
3
3

3

This is very similar to the famous probabilistic serial method of
Bogomolnaia and Moulin (2001)
and the veto by consumption rule of
Ianovski and Kondratev (2021)
. For a connection between these three methods and the problem of designing a voting rule with low metric distortion see the note by
Peters (2023)
.

Example 2
.

Consider the following instance with four voters, four candidates, and
k=2
:

•

c_{1}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{2}

•

c_{1}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{4}

•

c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{1}

•

c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{1}

In this instance Phragmén’s Ordered Rule works as follows:

1.

At time
t=0
, all voters start “eating” their top choice candidate.

2.

At time
t=0.5
:

•

c_{1}
is fully eaten and elected to the committee.

•

c_{2}
and
c_{3}
are “half eaten”.

•

The first two voters move to their next choices
c_{4}
and
c_{3}
.

•

The current committee is
\{c_{1}\}
.

3.

At time
t=0.75
:

•

c_{3}
is now fully eaten by the second and fourth voter,
c_{2}
is
75\%
eaten and
c_{4}
is
25\%
eaten.

•

c_{3}
is elected to the committee and
\{c_{1},c_{3}\}
is the outcome.

To see that Phragmén’s Ordered Rule does not satisfy PSC consider the following example.

Consider the following instance with

c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{5}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{6}\succ c_{7}

c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{5}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{6}\succ c_{7}

c_{4}\succ c_{5}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{6}\succ c_{7}

c_{5}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{6}\succ c_{7}

c_{6}\succ c_{7}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{5}

Here, Phragmén’s Ordered Rule would first elect

to

We now turn this deterministic rule first into a fractional one and then via dependent rounding into a probabilistic one. Specifically, we first define a version of Phragmén’s Ordered Rule giving us probabilities for the individual candidates to be elected and then show how to round these probabilities to guarantee that every committee in the support satisfies PSC.

We assume that each candidate has a weight of one. Now instead of stopping when
k
candidates have been selected, we stop once the total eaten weight is
k
. Candidates can now be eaten fractionally, with
\alpha
of a candidate being eaten corresponding to an
\alpha
probability of this candidate being selected. We call this rule Phragmén’s fractional rule (PFR).

Example 4
.

Consider the following instance with four voters, four candidates, and
k=2
:

•

c_{1}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}

•

c_{1}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{2}

•

c_{1}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{4}

•

c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{1}

After the total eaten weight is
\frac{4}{3}
, candidate
c_{1}
is eaten fully, while candidate
c_{3}
is eaten a third. Then the first three voters move on to their second choice. After the total eaten weight is
2

c_{2}
and
c_{4}
both have a weight of
\frac{1}{6}
, while
c_{3}
has
\frac{2}{3}
. Thus, candidate
c_{1}
is selected with probability

c_{2}
and
c_{4}
are selected with probability
\frac{1}{6}

\frac{2}{3}

In the instance in

c_{6}

While PFR gives us probabilities, it does not actually give us a distribution over committees yet. For this, we turn to the dependent rounding scheme of
Gandhi et al. (2006)
.

Theorem 4

(
(
Gandhi et al., 2006
)
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with weights
w_{e}\in[0,1]
for each
e\in E
. Then there exists a lottery over subsets of edges
(\lambda_{i},E_{i})_{E_{i}\subseteq E}
where the subset of edges
E_{i}\subseteq E
is selected with probability
\lambda_{i}
such that (i) each edge is selected with probability
w_{e}
and (ii) in each
E_{i}
a vertex
v
has at least a degree of
\lfloor\sum_{e\in N(v)}w(e)\rfloor
and at most
\lceil\sum_{e\in N(v)}w(e)\rceil
.

That is given a bipartite graph with probabilities on edges, we can round these edges such that the original probabilities are respected and the degrees in the selected graph are the rounded degrees in the original weighted graph.

Before we turn to the rounding scheme, we additionally introduce another property satisfied by PFR. Namely, it satisfies an ex-ante version of PSC, which requires that any group of at least
\frac{\ell n}{k}
voters (even for non-integer
\ell
) gets a probability mass of at least
\ell
.

Definition 5

(Ex-ante PSC)
.

A fractional committee rule
p
satisfies ex-ante PSC if there is no group
N^{\prime}\subseteq N
of voters,
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of candidates and
\ell\leq k
such that

i.)

N^{\prime}
forms a solid coalition over
C^{\prime}

ii.)

\lvert N^{\prime}\rvert\geq\ell\frac{n}{k}

iii.)

\sum_{c\in C^{\prime}}p_{c}<\min(\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert,\ell)
.

To better contexualize ex-ante PSC, we note that a (deterministic) committee satisfying ex-post PSC does not necessarily need to satisfy ex-ante PSC.
4
4

4

As a simple example consider an instance with two voters, one ranking
c_{1}\succ c_{2}
and the other ranking
c_{2}\succ c_{1}
. Here, for
k=1
, either
\{c_{1}\}
or
\{c_{2}\}
satisfies ex-post PSC. However, to satisfy ex-ante PSC, both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
need to be selected with probability
\frac{1}{2}
.

With this definition we now come to the main result of this section and show that PFR is indeed candidate monotone and satisfies this ex-ante PSC notion. Further, we use the dependent rounding scheme of
Gandhi et al. (2006)
to show that the probabilities given by PFR can be decomposed into a probabilistic rule only randomizing over committees satisfying PSC.

Theorem 5
.

PFR is candidate monotone, satisfies ex-ante PSC, and can be decomposed into a distribution over PSC committees.

Proof.

The monotonicity of PFR follows from the proof of
Janson (2016, Theorem 14.3)
, as he shows that at any time, after a candidate gets swapped up, this candidate is always “at least as much eaten” as before swapping this candidate up. In particular this holds for time step
k
and thus the monotonicity follows.

For the ex-ante PSC guarantee, consider any group
N^{\prime}\subseteq N
of voters of size at least
\ell\frac{n}{k}
forming a solid coalition over a set
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of candidates. After a total weight of
k
has been eaten, the weight of the candidates eaten by the group
N^{\prime}
is exactly
\ell
. As
C^{\prime}
forms a prefix of the preferences of these voters, it must hold that either
C^{\prime}
is eaten in total or that at least
\ell

C^{\prime}
as voters in
N^{\prime}
would first start eating candidates outside of
C^{\prime}
once everything in
C^{\prime}
is eaten. Thus, the outcome satisfies ex-ante PSC.

For the ex-post guarantee, we make use of the dependent rounding scheme of
Theorem

4
while following the general structure of the minimal demand rules of
Aziz and Lee (2022)
.

Let
p

c\in C

p_{c}(i)\in[0,1]
be the amount of
c

We start off with the bipartite graph

with an edge of weight

p^{\prime}_{c}(i)
.
If there is no group left, we increase
r
.

The dependent rounding scheme now selects committees according to the bipartite graph
G
. That is, we round the edges and select every candidate adjacent to an edge in the rounded outcome. As the weight adjacent to each group voter is integral, and as the total sum of weights is
k
this guarantees that exactly
k
candidates are selected. As the weight adjacent to each candidate is at most
1
, we also do not select any candidate twice.

Besides this, we need to show two things, (i) this rounding scheme respects the probabilities and (ii) the committees output by the rounding scheme satisfy PSC.

For the first point, consider any candidate
c\in C
and the vertices of
G

connected to
c
. These vertices correspond to solid coalitions selected throughout the construction of
G
. By construction, for each voter in these solid coalitions, the probability that
c
is selected is increased by at most
p_{c}(i)
. Hence, as the dependent rounding scheme respects the probabilities on the edges,
c
is selected with a probability of at most
\sum_{i\in N}p_{c}(i)=p_{c}
.

Further, we know that for
r=m
all voters form a solid coalition over all candidates. If at this step, there is any voter
i\in N
left with
p_{c}(i)>0
, this probability will be part of the last vertex added to
G
. Thus, we know that
c
is selected with a probability of exactly
p_{c}
.

For (ii) consider a group
N^{\prime}\subseteq N
of voters forming a solid coalition over a set of candidates
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
such that
\lvert N^{\prime}\rvert\geq\frac{\ell n}{k}
for some
\ell\in[k]
. Our goal is to show that at least
\min(\ell,\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert)
candidates are selected from
C^{\prime}
. Consider the step in PFR in which
r=\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert
. First, we notice that any candidate from
C^{\prime}
who has a probability of
1
of getting selected is already included in the committee. If this includes every candidate in
C^{\prime}
we are already done, as in this case, every candidate from
C^{\prime}
is selected independent of the rounding.

Otherwise, let
C^{\prime\prime}
be the set of candidates from
C^{\prime}
with a probability of less than
1
of being selected. As the voters in
N^{\prime}
rank all candidates in
C^{\prime}
before all candidates in
C\setminus C^{\prime}
we know that no voter in
N^{\prime}
can eat any candidate in
C\setminus C^{\prime}
before all candidates in
C^{\prime}
are assigned a probability of
1
. As we ran PFR until the total eaten weight was
k
, we get that the voters in
N^{\prime}
consumed at least
\lvert N^{\prime}\rvert\frac{k}{n}\geq\ell
many fractional candidates from
C^{\prime}
. For each rank in-between
1
and
\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert
voters in
N^{\prime}
could have only taken part in solid coalitions containing exclusively candidates from
C^{\prime}
. Now consider
p^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in N^{\prime}}\sum_{c\in C^{\prime}}p_{c}(i)
: the “budget” left at iteration
\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert
. As PFR only deducts integer values, we know that at the beginning of this iteration there must exist vertices in
G
adjacent to a weight of at least
\ell-\lfloor p^{\prime}\rfloor
in
G
exclusively connected to candidates in
C^{\prime}
. In iteration
\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert
PFR adds a vertex connected to candidates in
C^{\prime}
with a total edge weight of
\lfloor p^{\prime}\rfloor
. As we round according to
G
we know that each of these vertices will have a degree equal to the weight of their outgoing edges, with each of these vertices being included in the committee. Hence, we will select at least
\ell-\lfloor\sum_{i\in N^{\prime}}\sum_{c\in C^{\prime}}p_{c}(i)\rfloor+\lfloor\sum_{i\in N^{\prime}}\sum_{c\in C^{\prime}}p_{c}(i)\rfloor=\ell
candidates from
C^{\prime}
, and therefore, the solid coalition
N^{\prime}
is satisfies according to PSC.

∎

Adding on to this, we however also show that PFR cannot be used to obtain the recently introduced stronger guarantee of rank-PJR
(
Brill and Peters, 2023
)
. To define rank-PJR, for any
i\in N
and
c\in C
we call
\rank(i,c)\coloneqq\lvert\{c^{\prime}\in C\colon c^{\prime}\succ_{i}c\}\rvert+1
to be the rank given to
c
by voter
i
(i.e., voter
i
gives candidate
c
a rank of
r
if this candidate appears in the
r
-th place of the voters rankings). Using this, we can define the property of rank-PJR.

Definition 6

(rank-PJR)
.

A committee
W
satisfies
rank-proportional justified representation (rank-PJR)
(
Brill and Peters, 2023
)
if there is no group
N^{\prime}\subseteq N
of voters,
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of candidates,
\ell\in[k]
, and rank
r\in[m]
such that

i.)

\rank(i,c)\leq r
for all
i\in N^{\prime}
and
c\in C^{\prime}

ii.)

\lvert N^{\prime}\rvert\geq\ell\frac{n}{k}

iii.)

\lvert\{c\in W\colon\rank(i,c)\leq r\text{ for some }i\in N^{\prime}\}\rvert<\ell.

It is easy to see that any committee satisfying rank-PJR also satisfies PSC
(
Brill and Peters, 2023
)
. Further, using an example of
Brill and Peters
we can show that PFR cannot be decomposed to satisfy ex-post rank-PJR.

Example 5
.

Consider Example 5 of
Brill and Peters (2023)
with
4
voters,
6
candidates, and
k=2
:

•

c_{1}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{5}\succ c_{6}

•

c_{5}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{4}\succ c_{6}\succ c_{1}

•

c_{4}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{6}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{5}

•

c_{6}\succ c_{3}\succ c_{2}\succ c_{5}\succ c_{1}\succ c_{4}
.

Here, PFR would place a probability mass of
\frac{1}{2}
on each of
c_{1},c_{4},c_{5},c_{6}
. This, however, is not compatible with rank-PJR. Independent of which two voters get their first choice candidate in the deterministic committee, the other two voters will rank candidate
c_{2}
at a rank of at least
3
while receiving no candidate at a rank of
3
, thus constituting a rank-PJR violation. In fact, as the PFR outcome is the only ex-ante PSC outcome in this instance, this also shows that ex-ante PSC and ex-post rank-PJR are incompatible.

4.1
Consequences for Party List Elections

A special case of multiwinner voting are so-called party list elections
(
Brill et al., 2024
)
in which a candidate can not only be elected once but multiple times. Of special interest is the apportionment problem. In it, voters give their vote for a
single
party, which then is aggregated into seats for the parties. That is, there are
m
parties, with party
i
receiving a
p_{i}
share of the votes and
h
seats to be distributed among the parties. Just as for multiwinner voting, there are also “proportionality” axioms for apportionment. Most prominently, the
quota
axiom states that party
i
should receive either
\lfloor p_{i}h\rfloor
or
\lceil p_{i}h\rceil
seats, i.e., the number of seats for each party should be correctly rounded. Since allocation rules satisfying quota and other prominent desirable axioms do not exist
(
Balinski and Young, 2001
)
, recent work has studied the probabilistic allocation of seats
(
Grimmet, 2004
;
Gölz et al., 2024
;
Correa et al., 2024
)
: the vote shares of the parties get probabilistically rounded in such a way that each party receives their exact quota in expectation and always their rounded quota ex-post. Here, we shortly note that we can apply our result on PFR to show that if one additionally knew the complete rankings of each voter over all parties, one can get a randomized apportionment scheme satisfying the desirable quota properties in addition to PSC (when interpreting this apportionment instance as a multiwinner voting instance).

Theorem 6
.

There is a randomized apportionment method satisfying ex-ante exact quota, ex-post quota and PSC.

Proof.

For this, we note that PFR when run on a party-list instance, leads to probabilities exactly equal to the vote shares (as the candidates of a party never run out). Thus,
Theorem

5
immediately gives us that ex-ante exact quota and PSC are satisfied. Further, due to the dependent rounding works, we also get that no party would be either over or underrepresented thus also giving us ex-post quota.
∎

Thus, while PFR offers an appealing theoretical solution, it also inherits several key weaknesses of PSC as an axiom. Namely, due to its dependence on solid coalitions, the relative lack of meaningful solid coalitions
(
Bardal et al., 2025
)
in-turn leads to a lack of constraints for PFR, which still enables a large fraction of all PSC committees to be chosen by PFR. Hence, if one would want near-certainty on the outcome of the lottery, PFR is most likely not a good choice as an election rule. If, however, one is content with its use as a lottery, PFR might provide an interesting monotone alternative to classical deterministic voting rules.

6
Conclusion

We studied the problem of finding multiwinner voting rules that are both candidate monotone and satisfy the proportionality axiom of
proportionality for solid coalitions
. First, we show that the
non-resolute
rule selecting all committees satisfying PSC is indeed candidate monotone. Secondly, we design a
probabilistic
voting rule based on
Phragmén’s Ordered Rule
and show that this probabilistic voting rule is both candidate monotone and can be implemented to randomize only over committees satisfying PSC.

There are several open questions and directions for future work. Most importantly, whether there is a
resolute
voting rule satisfying PSC and candidate monotonicity is still open. Further, it is open whether the results presented here can be extended to stronger proportionality axioms (such as rank-PJR of
Brill and Peters (2023)
) or to the setting with weak preferences and the axiom of generalized PSC. For both, a characterization as provided by
Aziz and Lee (2022)
of the respective proportionality axioms is missing as well.
