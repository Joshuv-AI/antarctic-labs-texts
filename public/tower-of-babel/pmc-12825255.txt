Risk prediction of multiple cardiometabolic diseases in middle-aged and older adults using lipid metabolism biomarkers and derived composite measures based on machine learning: a prospective cohort study of CHARLS
Authors: Yuan Li, Peijie Chen, Binghong Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) has become an increasing global public health challenge. In China, the prevalence of CMM is rising rapidly among middle-aged and older adults, with estimates ranging from 11.6% to 16.9%, posing a substantial burden on both individuals and healthcare systems. However, effective tools for predicting individual risk of CMM remain limited, hindering timely prevention and intervention.MethodsThis study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) between 2011 and 2015, including 7,913 participants aged ≥ 45 years without CMM at baseline. Incident CMM events were identified during the 2015 follow-up based on self-reported diagnoses of cardiometabolic diseases. Ten lipid metabolism biomarkers and derived composite indices (TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, TyG, TyG-BMI, LAP, CTI, non-HDL-C, and RC) were evaluated. Predictive models were estimated using logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting machine, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine, naïve Bayes, deep learning (DL), and an ensemble model. The dataset was randomly split into training (75%) and validation (25%) subsets. Model discrimination was assessed using ROC curves and Area Under the Curve (AUC); calibration was evaluated with calibration plots and Brier scores; classification performance was examined using confusion matrices. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curves (CIC) were applied to assess clinical utility across risk thresholds. Feature importance ranking and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to quantify variable contributions, marginal effects, and feature interactions. In addition, regional variations in CMM incidence were illustrated using choropleth maps, and correlations between lipid markers and CMM prevalence were analyzed with Pearson coefficients and heatmaps.ResultsOver the four-year follow-up, 1,355 participants (17.1%) developed CMM. Compared with controls, incident cases were older, had a higher proportion of women and urban residents, and showed higher BMI. They also had significantly elevated triglycerides (126.6 vs. 101.8 mg/dL), reduced HDL-C (45.2 vs. 50.3 mg/dL, P < 0.001), and increased TyG-BMI and LAP (P < 0.001). Geographical analysis revealed markedly higher CMM incidence in northern cold regions (> 40%) than in southern regions (< 20%). The ensemble model achieved robust predictive performance (AUC = 0.715), followed closely by the DL model (AUC = 0.716) and GBM (AUC = 0.714). These non-linear models consistently outperformed GLM (AUC = 0.696), SVM (AUC = 0.696), and XGBoost (AUC = 0.683). Ensemble, DL, and RF models also demonstrated the best calibration (lowest Brier score, 0.125) and provided the greatest net benefit across risk thresholds. SHAP analysis indicated that composite indices, particularly TyG-BMI, LAP, and TyG, contributed most to risk prediction, whereas HDL-C exerted a protective effect. In contrast, traditional single lipid markers such as LDL-C and TC ranked lower in predictive importance.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that machine learning models incorporating lipid metabolism biomarkers and derived indices can predict the risk of CMM. Composite indicators such as TyG and LAP, which capture insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, showed superior predictive value. DL and ensemble models provided higher discrimination and clinical utility compared with traditional approaches. These models may enable early identification of high-risk individuals, underscoring the importance of lipid and metabolic management in CMM prevention, with potential implications for clinical decision-making and public health strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02820-2.Keywords: Cardiometabolic multimorbidity, Lipid metabolism biomarkers, Derived composite indices, Machine learning, CHARLS cohort, Risk prediction

Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the coexistence of at least two cardiometabolic conditions such as diabetes, stroke, or heart disease, is a common health challenge among middle-aged and older adults [1–4]. Compared with a single condition, CMM multiplies the risks of mortality and disability, imposing a heavy burden on individuals, families, and healthcare systems [5–8]. With population aging and the rising prevalence of chronic diseases, the occurrence of CMM has increased rapidly, with prevalence in Chinese middle-aged and older populations estimated at 11.6–16.9% [7, 9, 10]. Current prevention and management strategies mainly focus on individuals who already suffer from multiple conditions, while effective tools to predict the risk of developing CMM in those with only a single disease remain lacking in clinical practice. Early identification of individuals at high risk of progression to multimorbidity, however, is of high public health significance and clinical relevance as the effort could facilitate timely, integrated cardiovascular health interventions to prevent or delay the onset of CMM [11, 12].

To address this gap, the present study focuses on lipid metabolism biomarkers and derived composite indices as potential early predictors for CMM. Dyslipidemia is a well-established risk factor for cardiometabolic diseases, and conventional lipid measures obtained in routine examinations—such as total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)—play a key role in cardiovascular risk assessment [13–15]. In recent years, several lipid-related composite indices have been proposed, including the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) and lipid accumulation product (LAP), which integrate lipid, obesity, and glycemic parameters to capture metabolic abnormalities such as visceral fat accumulation and insulin resistance [16–23]. These integrated biomarkers have been shown to be strongly associated with metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and atherosclerosis [24–27]. It can therefore be hypothesized that adverse lipid metabolic profiles at baseline may serve as potential risk factors for predicting subsequent development of CMM. Integrating lipid-related biomarkers for CMM risk prediction is of both clinically feasible and meaningful: they are widely available in practice, cost-effective, and suitable for large-scale screening, while also reflecting core metabolic mechanisms underlying CMM—such as insulin resistance and visceral adiposity—more directly than lifestyle factors alone [15, 16, 20, 28].

Despite their potential in clinical practice, evidence regarding the use of lipid biomarkers to predict CMM risk remains limited [29]. Studies have mainly focused demographic and lifestyle factors including abnormal overweight [30], smoking, and physical inactivity, with only a few investigations exploring the associations between lipid markers and multimorbidity [24–27]. For example, a cross-sectional study in hypertensive patients reported that indices including the cardiometabolic index (CMI), LAP, and visceral adiposity index (VAI) were significantly associated with CMM risk. However, this field is still in its early stages [31].To our knowledge, no prospective study has specifically evaluated the predictive value of lipid metabolism biomarkers and derived indices for CMM onset [29]. Moreover, traditional risk prediction approaches, such as logistic regression, may be inadequate in capturing the nonlinear relationships and interactions among metabolic indicators [32]. By contrast, machine learning methods have demonstrated advantages in chronic disease prediction, leveraging complex feature patterns to improve predictive performance [33]. Applying machine learning to CMM risk prediction may overcome the limitations of conventional models and help uncover hidden metabolic signals [34, 35].

Previous studies have often relied on conventional statistical models and a limited set of traditional lipid markers to predict cardiometabolic morbidity (CMM), which may not fully capture the complex, non-linear relationships involved and thus offer limited predictive accuracy. In addressing these limitations, this study draws on the prospective CHARLS cohort that allows us to use multiple lipid metabolism biomarkers and derived indices to develop CMM risk prediction models with various machine learning algorithms [12, 36–41]. Model performance was evaluated in validation datasets, and interpretability was enhanced through SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, which identified the most influential lipid indicators, their contribution pathways, and direction of effect [39–41]. This approach not only improves predictive accuracy but also provides insights into the metabolic mechanisms underlying CMM, with implications for targeted prevention strategies and reduction of cardiometabolic multimorbidity.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This study was based on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative prospective cohort of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older [37]. Since its initiation in 2011, CHARLS has conducted follow-up surveys every two to three years [38], systematically collecting multidimensional information on demographics, health status, lifestyle, biochemical indicators, chronic disease prevalence, and healthcare utilization, thereby providing a rich resource of longitudinal health data [36]. The 2011 baseline survey was used as the starting point [42], and the occurrence of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) was determined using the 2015 follow-up data, establishing a temporally ordered framework for longitudinal analysis [43].

Participants were included in this study if they were aged 45 years or older at baseline and had complete questionnaire information, physical examination data, and blood biomarker results [44]. CMM was defined as the coexistence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases, identified through self-reported physician diagnoses in the 2015 follow-up, including but not limited to heart disease (such as myocardial infarction or angina), stroke, and diabetes. For participants lost to follow-up, disease status was determined at their last available survey, and for those who died during follow-up, classification was based on the most recent health report prior to death.

After rigorous data cleaning and sample selection, 25,586 individuals were initially enrolled at the 2011 baseline. We conducted a missing data analysis to assess the distribution and mechanism of missing values. The missingness mechanism was assumed to be Missing at Random (MAR), primarily arising from the fact that blood biomarker collection in CHARLS is conducted in specific waves or subsets. Among the initial sample, 17,404 participants (68.0%) had missing data for key variables. Specifically, the majority of exclusions were due to missing blood lipid panel data (approximately 65%), with a smaller proportion attributed to missing physical examination or demographic data (< 3%). Given the high proportion of missingness in the primary predictors and to ensure the reliability of the ground truth labels for machine learning training, we adopted a complete case analysis strategy. No statistical imputation (e.g., multiple imputation) was performed to avoid introducing synthetic noise into the training data. Consequently, 254 were excluded due to ineligible age (< 45 years), and a total of 7,913 participants were included in the final analysis. Among them, 6,558 individuals (82.9%) remained free of CMM between 2011 and 2015 and comprised the control group, while 1,355 (17.1%) developed incident CMM during follow-up and comprised the event group. Measurement indicators

The measurement indicators in this study mainly included the definition of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), lipid metabolism biomarkers, and other covariates related to CMM. These indicators were obtained from biochemical data, anthropometric measurements, and lifestyle information in the CHARLS database, as detailed below.

Follow-up and identification of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM)

CMM was defined based on self-reported physician-diagnosed diseases during follow-up, specifically the coexistence of at least two of the following: heart disease (such as myocardial infarction or angina), diabetes, stroke, and hypertension. These conditions were recorded during each follow-up interview. Participants reporting two or more of these diseases during follow-up were classified as having CMM [1, 3, 5].

Ascertainment of lipid metabolism biomarkers and derived indices

The primary measurement indicators were ten lipid metabolism biomarkers, including conventional lipid measures and derived indices calculated from lipid and anthropometric parameters [13–15]. These biomarkers provide integrated insights into insulin resistance, obesity, and cardiovascular risk. The indicators included total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride–glucose index (TyG), TyG-BMI index, lipid accumulation product (LAP), cholesterol–triglyceride index (CTI), non-HDL cholesterol (non-HDL-C), and remnant cholesterol (RC) [44].

TC represents the total cholesterol in circulation and is an important marker of cardiovascular health, with higher levels linked to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events. TG is a major component of blood lipids, and elevated TG levels are strongly associated with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease. LDL-C, often referred to as “bad cholesterol,” is a major risk factor for atherosclerosis, while HDL-C, or “good cholesterol,” exerts a protective effect by facilitating cholesterol clearance [13–15].

In addition to these conventional measures, derived biomarkers were used to provide a more comprehensive assessment. The TyG index reflects insulin resistance and is strongly correlated with metabolic dysfunction. The TyG-BMI index combines TyG with body mass index (BMI), capturing both metabolic disturbance and obesity-related risk [16–19, 45]. LAP, calculated from waist circumference and TG, reflects visceral fat accumulation and is particularly relevant in obese individuals. The CTI reflects the imbalance between cholesterol and TG, identifying a high-risk state of combined dyslipidemia [20–22, 26, 27]. Non-HDL-C, calculated as TC minus HDL-C, captures all atherogenic cholesterol fractions and is considered a stronger predictor of cardiovascular risk than LDL-C. RC represents cholesterol content in triglyceride-rich remnant lipoproteins and indicates endogenous hyperlipidemia, which may confer increased cardiovascular risk even when LDL-C levels are normal [13, 28, 46–51].

Other covariates

Baseline information on sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, marital status, education level), lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption), and contextual variables (e.g., urban/rural residence, geographic region) was also collected. Anthropometric and physiological measures, including weight, height, waist circumference, and blood pressure, were recorded to explore potential associations with CMM.

While lipid metabolism indicators served as the main predictors in machine learning models, these non-lipid covariates were considered in the descriptive analysis and interpretation of results, acknowledging their potential influence on CMM risk.

Model construction

To develop accurate and reliable prediction models for CMM risk, eight machine learning algorithms were employed: generalized linear model (GLM, logistic regression), random forest (RF), gradient boosting machine (GBM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine (SVM), naïve Bayes (NB), deep learning (DL), and a stacked ensemble model integrating the above single algorithms [35, 52, 53].

The dataset was randomly split into training and validation sets at a 75%:25% ratio. To ensure generalizability, the distribution of CMM cases and non-cases in both subsets was preserved in line with the overall cohort. Importantly, no oversampling, undersampling, or class balancing methods were applied, thereby maintaining the natural outcome distribution and avoiding biases introduced by artificial resampling. This approach enabled the models to reflect the true prevalence and distribution of CMM, improving external validity.

Each base model was trained on the training set with hyperparameter tuning to enhance performance. For tree-based models (RF, GBM, and XGBoost), key parameters such as tree number, depth, and learning rate were optimized to ensure model stability and accuracy. For SVM, the regularization parameters were configured to approximate linear classification given the high-dimensional feature space. For DL, the network architecture (hidden layers) and epochs were tuned. Detailed hyperparameter settings for all models are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Ten-fold cross-validation was conducted to assess generalization and prevent overfitting.

This strategy allowed the study to harness the complementary strengths of multiple algorithms while mitigating the limitations of any single model, ultimately improving predictive accuracy and potential clinical utility.

Model evaluation and analysis

In this study, the predictive performance of various models was evaluated on an independent validation set. The primary evaluation criteria included the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), Brier score, confusion matrix analysis metrics, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), clinical impact assessment, and residual analysis. To comprehensively assess each model’s predictive capability, the AUC value and its corresponding 95% confidence interval were calculated. The AUC is a crucial metric for evaluating a classifier’s performance, with a range from 0.5 to 1.0; a value closer to 1.0 indicates a stronger ability to distinguish between positive and negative samples [54–59]. To ensure an accurate AUC assessment, the 95% confidence interval was calculated using the DeLong test, a statistical method widely applied to compare multiple ROC curves, which provides a significance level and confidence interval for the AUC [54]. In this research, the roc() function from the pROC package was used to compute each model’s ROC curve, and the ci.auc() function, which defaults to the DeLong test, was employed to calculate the AUC value and its 95% confidence interval [60]. For each model, including Ensemble, DL, RF, GBM, SVM, GLM, and NB, the AUC value and the upper and lower bounds of its confidence interval were computed. This approach not only yielded the AUC value for each model but also revealed the range of its predictive performance. Furthermore, a forest plot was generated to visually compare the performance of different models by displaying their AUC values and corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

To further optimize the models’ classification performance, the study also computed the optimal metrics for each model on the training set. Specifically, the best predictive probability threshold was determined by maximizing the F1 score [54, 60]. This optimal metric provides a crucial reference for the models’ practical application and lays the foundation for subsequent model optimization and evaluation. Concurrently, researchers applied this optimal threshold on the validation set to generate a confusion matrix and calculate classification performance metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value [61, 62], and accuracy, thereby further refining the models and providing a basis for practical application.

In terms of calibration assessment, the Brier score was used to measure the agreement between the model’s predicted probabilities and the actual outcomes. A lower Brier score indicates a more accurate model prediction [55, 63, 64]. By plotting a calibration curve, the relationship between the model’s predicted probabilities and the actual event rates can be visualized; ideally, the calibration curve should be close to the diagonal line. To further evaluate the calibration effect, validation set samples were grouped by their predicted risk, and the actual CMM incidence rate was calculated for each group. By comparing the actual incidence rate with the model’s average predicted probability for each group, the model’s performance across different risk tiers was assessed.

Clinical utility was evaluated using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). DCA assesses the net benefit of a model at different risk thresholds. By comparing the model’s strategy against the extreme strategies of “treat all” and “treat none,” it helps clinicians select the best intervention strategy, thereby enabling personalized medicine [57–59].

To assess the clinical impact of the models, clinical impact curves were plotted. Based on the models’ predictions on the validation set, the number of high-risk individuals identified at different risk thresholds was calculated. This was followed by determining the number of actual patients within these high-risk groups, thereby evaluating the models’ practical utility in screening high-risk individuals [58].

Simultaneously, to gain a deeper understanding of the models’ fit, a residual analysis was performed on the predicted probability for each sample. The residual represents the difference between the model’s predicted probability and the actual outcome; a large residual may indicate a significant prediction error for certain samples. By plotting residual distribution charts, reverse cumulative percentage plots, and residual box plots, the fit and error characteristics of different models can be evaluated. This allows for the discovery of which models perform well on the majority of individuals and which ones have larger errors [56].

Model interpretability analysis

In this study, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was applied to explore the decision-making basis of each model [65]. SHAP is derived from cooperative game theory and explains models by calculating the marginal contribution of each feature to the prediction output. On this basis, feature importance, SHAP summary plots, and dependence plots were visualized [66, 67]. First, the mean absolute SHAP value of each feature in the validation set was calculated to measure its average contribution to model predictions, which was then used to rank feature importance. Unlike traditional indicators based on node-split gain in tree models, SHAP importance is applicable to any model and provides a consistent interpretative meaning. Subsequently, SHAP summary (beeswarm) plots were generated for each model, where the x-axis represents the SHAP value, the y-axis lists features in descending order of importance, and each dot corresponds to the SHAP value of a sample for a given feature. The color reflects the magnitude of the original feature value. These plots intuitively illustrate both the direction and distribution of feature effects on model predictions. For example, if higher feature values (red) are mostly located on the positive side of the SHAP axis, it indicates that elevated levels of that feature increase the predicted risk of CMM, and vice versa. Furthermore, SHAP dependence plots were constructed to examine the nonlinear effects of feature values on predicted risk, while coloring points according to another correlated feature to reveal interaction patterns.

All SHAP computations were conducted using the shap library. For black-box models such as DL, approximation methods were used to estimate Shapley values, whereas for tree-based models, the Tree SHAP algorithm was employed for efficient computation. In practice, the H2O process was first initialized, and the predictor matrix for SHAP analysis was extracted. A general prediction function was then constructed to obtain prediction probabilities from H2O models, and SHAP values were computed for each model using the fastshap package under parallel processing. The results for seven machine learning models were subsequently converted using the shapviz package, enabling the visualization of SHAP feature importance bar plots, SHAP beeswarm plots, and dependence plots. In particular, the SHAP feature importance bar plots clearly demonstrated the average contribution of each feature, highlighting those that played a dominant role in model predictions. In addition, SHAP dependence plots and force plots were generated to analyze nonlinear relationships between features and their contributions to individual predictions. For multivariate dependence analysis, plots were constructed that included multiple lipid metabolism biomarkers (e.g., cholesterol, triglycerides), thereby examining how these features influenced model predictions at varying levels.

Comparison of variable importance across models

In this study, to analyze the variable importance across different models, we extracted and normalized the top 10 most important variables for each. Since only five models—GBM, GLM, Random Forest, DL, and SVM—support variable importance analysis, the analysis was restricted to these five. We first defined a universal function to calculate each model’s feature relative importance and normalize it to a range of 0–1. This process helped to identify the key predictive features for different models and allowed for a direct, cross-model comparison of variable importance [68, 69].

We extracted the top 10 most important variables from each model and merged them into a single dataset. To ensure these variables were representative, we calculated the mean relative importance for each variable across all models, thereby identifying a consistent set of top 10 variables with high importance across the board. The final ranking of these variables was based on their average relative importance across the different models, which revealed both consistency and variance in importance rankings.

For the variable importance calculation, we used a relative importance ranking method to quantify each feature’s contribution to the model’s predictive output. In this approach, a variable’s relative importance is computed based on its impact on the model’s predictive accuracy, typically by quantifying its contribution to the model’s output or, in the case of tree-based models, by measuring the gain in node splits.

For visualization, we used ggplot2 to create a chart of the normalized variable importance. Each bar in the chart represents a variable’s importance across different models, with the length of the bar indicating its relative importance. The importance value is annotated above each bar, and different colors represent different models. This method provided a deeper understanding of the feature contributions of each model and their impact on prediction results, offering a theoretical basis for subsequent feature selection and model optimization.

Association between CMM incidence and lipid metabolism biomarkers in China

Provincial incidence of CMM

In this study, the geographical distribution of Cardiometabolic Multimorbidity (CMM) incidence rates across different provinces in China was visualized using a map. CMM incidence data was compiled from the cleaned dataset for each province, and the average values of key lipid metabolism biomarkers were calculated, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), TyG-BMI, lipid accumulation product (LAP), cholesterol-triglyceride ratio (CTI), non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (NHDL-C), and remnant cholesterol (RC). These average values were then used to analyze the overall lipid metabolism level in each province and its relationship with the CMM incidence rate.

For each province, the number of CMM cases was counted, along with the total sample size and the average values of the relevant lipid metabolism biomarkers. The CMM incidence rate for each province was subsequently calculated by dividing the number of CMM cases by the total sample size. These statistical results allowed for a comparison of the differences in CMM incidence rates among provinces and facilitated further exploration of the potential links between lipid metabolism biomarkers and CMM prevalence.

Upon completion of data organization, Geographic Information System (GIS) technology was used for spatial visualization [70]. First, provincial-level map data of China was obtained and merged with the CMM incidence rate data. To intuitively display the geographical distribution of CMM incidence rates, a color gradient was used, with deeper red areas representing higher incidence rates and lighter shades indicating lower rates. This color-coding method allowed for quick identification of provinces with particularly high or low CMM incidence. The resulting map of geographical distribution provides a clear identification of high-risk CMM areas, offering a scientific basis for developing targeted health interventions and prevention strategies.

Correlation between lipid biomarkers and CMM prevalence

In this study, we focused on analyzing the relationship between lipid metabolism biomarkers and CMM (Cardiometabolic Multimorbidity) prevalence. We calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between 10 lipid metabolism biomarkers and CMM prevalence. The Pearson correlation coefficient is a common statistical method for measuring the linear relationship between two continuous variables, with a value ranging from − 1 to 1. A value closer to 1 or −1 indicates a stronger linear relationship, while a value near 0 suggests no significant linear relationship [71].

To provide a more intuitive display of these correlations, we created a correlation matrix and visualized the relationships between the lipid biomarkers and CMM prevalence using a heatmap. After calculating the correlation coefficients, we summarized these results into a correlation matrix, which provided a basis for identifying the interrelationships between each lipid biomarker and CMM prevalence. A heatmap was then employed to make these relationships more easily understood and interpreted.

The heatmap’s color gradient, ranging from blue to red, represents the shift from negative to positive correlation, with white indicating no significant correlation. Regions of negative correlation are displayed in blue, suggesting that an increase in that lipid biomarker may be associated with a decrease in CMM prevalence. Conversely, positive correlation areas are shown in red, indicating that a rise in the biomarker may be linked to an increase in CMM prevalence. Each cell within the heatmap also contains the corresponding Pearson correlation coefficient, rounded to two decimal places, allowing for a precise and direct assessment of the correlation strength between each pair of lipid biomarkers and CMM incidence.

Demographic characteristics

Participants in the CMM group were significantly older than those in the control group (median age 61.0 years [IQR 55.0–67.0] vs. 57.0 years [51.0–64.0], P < 0.001). The proportion of women was higher in the CMM group compared with the control group (58.0% vs. 52.7%, P < 0.001). Regarding marital status, the CMM group had a slightly lower proportion of married individuals (84.7% vs. 89.0%, P < 0.001) and a relatively higher proportion of unmarried or widowed individuals. With respect to residence, rural participants accounted for a smaller proportion in the CMM group compared with the control group (60.5% vs. 67.7%, P < 0.001), whereas the proportion of urban residents was correspondingly higher (39.5% vs. 32.3%).

Lifestyle factors

There were slight differences in smoking and alcohol consumption between the two groups. At baseline, a higher proportion of participants in the CMM group reported never smoking compared with the control group (64.4% vs. 60.5%, P = 0.008), suggesting a slightly lower prevalence of current smokers in the CMM group. Regarding alcohol intake, no significant difference was observed between groups, with drinking rates of approximately 37–38% (P > 0.05).

Anthropometric measurements

Body mass index (BMI) categories showed marked differences between groups. In the CMM group, 51.6% were classified as overweight (BMI 24–28, 40.1%) or obese (BMI ≥ 28, 11.5%), compared with only 27.0% in the control group (overweight 23.2%, obese 3.8%, P < 0.001). Conversely, underweight (BMI < 18.5) was less common in the CMM group (3.3%) than in the control group (7.2%). These findings indicate that individuals who later developed CMM already exhibited a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity at baseline, reflecting a more adverse metabolic profile.

Lipid profiles

The CMM group demonstrated a more unfavorable lipid profile. Median triglyceride (TG) levels were higher in the CMM group (1.33 mmol/L [105.3 mg/dL]) compared with the control group (1.15 mmol/L [101.8 mg/dL], P < 0.001). Median high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was lower in the CMM group (1.17 mmol/L [45.2 mg/dL]) than in controls (1.30 mmol/L [50.3 mg/dL], P < 0.001). Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was slightly higher in the CMM group (3.02 vs. 2.95 mmol/L, equivalent to 116.8 vs. 114.1 mg/dL, P = 0.003). Median total cholesterol was similar between groups (≈ 4.9 mmol/L). However, non-HDL cholesterol levels were significantly higher in the CMM group (79.9 vs. 49.7 mg/dL, P < 0.001). In addition, residual cholesterol (RC) was elevated in the CMM group (median 0.61 mmol/L vs. 0.49 mmol/L, P < 0.001).

Composite lipid metabolism indices

Multiple composite metabolic indices were markedly elevated in the CMM group, indicating higher metabolic risk. The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index was higher in the CMM group (median 8.85 vs. 8.55, P < 0.001), suggesting a stronger tendency toward insulin resistance. The TyG-BMI index showed the most pronounced difference (median 224.5 vs. 195.3, P < 0.001), with a standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.512, highlighting the enhanced discriminatory power when BMI was incorporated.

The lipid accumulation product (LAP) was nearly 1.7-fold higher in the CMM group compared with controls (median 41.1 vs. 24.7, P < 0.001), reflecting greater central obesity combined with hypertriglyceridemia. The cholesterol–triglyceride index (CTI) was also elevated in the CMM group (median 9.03 vs. 8.58, P < 0.001; SMD = 0.456), suggesting that integrating cholesterol and triglyceride levels provided better differentiation of metabolic risk. Overall, these baseline findings indicate that individuals who later developed CMM were more likely to exhibit features of metabolic syndrome, including older age, female predominance, higher prevalence of overweight/obesity, and dyslipidemia characterized by elevated TG, reduced HDL-C, and increased LAP and TyG indices. These results support the hypothesis that abnormalities in lipid metabolism may serve as early warning signals for CMM.

Comparison of feature importance across models

The contribution of each lipid-related variable to CMM prediction was evaluated by quantifying feature importance across six machine learning models. As illustrated in Fig. 3, a distinct pattern emerged where composite metabolic indices consistently outperformed conventional single lipid measures.

Feature importance ranking across machine learning models

Among the composite indices, the triglyceride-glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI) demonstrated the most robust predictive power. TyG-BMI ranked as the single most important feature (normalized importance = 1.0) in four out of the six models: Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Random Forest (RF), Deep Learning (DL), and XGBoost. Notably, in the GBM model, the dominance of TyG-BMI was overwhelming, with its relative importance significantly outpacing the second-ranked variable. This suggests that the synergistic combination of insulin resistance markers and adiposity is the primary driver for identifying CMM risk in non-linear models.

The Lipid Accumulation Product (LAP) and TyG index also maintained high relevance but showed model-specific variability. LAP was identified as the top predictor in the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model (normalized importance = 1.0) and ranked within the top three for GLM and DL. Similarly, the TyG index consistently ranked in the top tier across XGBoost, RF, and SVM, reinforcing the critical role of insulin resistance mechanisms.

Interestingly, the generalized linear model (GLM) exhibited a unique profile. Unlike the tree-based models which favored complex indices, GLM identified raw triglyceride levels as the most influential predictor (normalized importance = 1.0), followed by LAP and TyG. Furthermore, GLM assigned zero importance to total cholesterol, LDL, and non-HDL cholesterol, likely due to regularization handling multicollinearity among these linear features.

In contrast, traditional single lipid biomarkers generally contributed minimally to model performance. Across all models, LDL-C, HDL-C, and total cholesterol typically ranked in the bottom tier. For instance, in the GBM and XGBoost models, the importance of cholesterol and LDL was negligible compared to TyG-BMI. These findings confirm that single lipid parameters alone are insufficient to capture the complex metabolic disturbances underlying CMM, necessitating the use of integrated biomarkers for accurate risk stratification.

To address the limitation of lacking an external validation cohort and to ensure the robustness of our findings, we performed a 10-times repeated cross-validation (CV) procedure. This rigorous validation strategy mitigates the potential bias arising from a single random data split and provides a more reliable estimate of the models’ generalization performance.

As presented in Fig. 4, the Stacking Ensemble model demonstrated superior stability and performance, achieving the highest mean AUC of 0.6966 across the 10 repeats. Notably, it exhibited a tight distribution of AUC scores with a low standard deviation (SD = 0.0012), indicating that its predictive power remains consistent regardless of data partitioning.

Model performance stability (10-repeat cross-validation)

Random Forest (RF) and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) also showed strong robustness, ranking second and third with mean AUCs of 0.6909 and 0.6862, respectively. In contrast, while the Deep Learning (DL) model achieved high peak performance in specific iterations, it exhibited the highest instability among the top-tier models (SD = 0.0071). The boxplot (Figure X) for DL reveals a wider interquartile range and a long lower tail (Min AUC = 0.6667), suggesting that the DL model is more sensitive to variations in the training data and may carry a higher risk of overfitting compared to the ensemble approach.SVM and GLM displayed exceptional stability (SD = 0.0006 for both), with extremely narrow boxplots, although their overall predictive accuracy (Mean AUC ~ 0.682) was lower than the tree-based ensemble methods. XGBoost and Naïve Bayes ranked lower in both mean performance and stability.These results confirm that while single non-linear models like DL can achieve high accuracy, the Stacking Ensemble approach provides the most reliable and generalizable solution for CMM risk prediction in this cohort, effectively balancing bias and variance.

Discussion

We included four-year prospective data from 7,913 individuals from the CHARLS cohort who were CMM-free at baseline [12, 36–38, 44]. We found that 17.1% of these individuals developed CMM during the follow-up period. Composite indices like TyG-BMI and LAP were also significantly elevated (P < 0.001). The geographical distribution showed that CMM incidence was significantly higher in colder northern regions (> 40%) compared to the south (< 20%). The study used multiple baseline lipid indicators and their derived indices to build machine learning models. The lipid-based ensemble and deep learning models showed excellent predictive performance, with AUCs of 0.718 and 0.716, respectively, which was significantly better than traditional single-indicator models. SHAP analysis further confirmed that key biomarkers like LAP and TyG-BMI consistently had the highest contribution to risk prediction. The strength of their correlation with CMM prevalence (correlation coefficients 0.58–0.72) was significantly higher than that of traditional single-lipid indicators, and their predictive performance remained stable across different population subgroups (e.g., northern vs. southern regions, different age groups). This suggests that lipid metabolism biomarkers and their composite indices don’t just provide a vague risk signal; they offer clinically valuable, quantitative evidence for the early identification of CMM through a multi-dimensional analysis of metabolic abnormalities.

Many of the previous studies rely on a small number of isolated risk factors [72–75]. For example, some studies only use a single lipid parameter, like total cholesterol, to assess cardiovascular disease risk. As a large-scale U.S. study reported, the traditional Framingham Risk Score, which mainly includes factors like age, blood pressure, and total cholesterol, has limited accuracy in predicting the complex co-occurrence of multiple cardiometabolic diseases [76]. It fails to comprehensively consider the complex interactions between the various metabolic components that form the basis of CMM.

Recent evidence-based reviews show that cardiovascular disease risk prediction models have not systematically incorporated clinically meaningful biomarkers that reflect the simultaneous presence of multiple diseases. Traditional predictive methods targeting a single disease often show a significant drop in predictive accuracy in populations with multiple comorbidities [77]. In metabolomics research involving cardiometabolic diseases, some studies have attempted to identify biomarkers, but they are often weakened methodologically by the use of small sample sizes, leading to overfitting and limited generalizability. For example, in a 2024 metabolomics study on coronary heart disease and common comorbidities (e.g., CHD with type 2 diabetes), Geng et al. had a sample size of only a few dozen to just over a hundred people in each group. Such small samples may not fully represent diverse patient populations, leading to models that overfit and have poor generalizability. These studies also typically analyze individual metabolites in isolation, ignoring the synergy between multiple metabolites and lipid-derived indices, which is crucial for a complete understanding of CMM risk. The authors themselves explicitly stated the need for larger samples to make their findings more robust and reliable [78].

Previous machine learning-based CMM prediction models were generally limited by insufficient data dimensions. For example, in a 2024 study on a Norwegian adult cohort, Moe et al. used a logistic regression model with only basic demographic and a few common clinical variables (e.g., BMI, blood pressure, and dietary patterns) to predict metabolic syndrome. Their lowest AUC was only 0.58, suggesting that a lack of richer metabolic and lipid information which limits a model’s discriminative ability [79].

In contrast, our study, based on the large-scale China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort of 7,913 CMM-free middle-aged and older adults, provides a more comprehensive and accurate approach. The large sample size ensures better representation of the Chinese population. By including a wide range of lipid-related biomarkers—both traditional lipids like TG and HDL-C, and novel composite indices like TyG-BMI and LAP—we were able to more fully capture underlying metabolic dysfunction. Second, the machine learning models we developed, including ensemble and deep learning models, showed excellent predictive ability with AUCs of 0.718 and 0.716, respectively, far exceeding the performance of traditional single-index models. SHAP analysis further validated the significant and consistent contribution of key biomarkers like LAP and TyG-BMI across different population subgroups, highlighting the models’ robustness and generalizability. Our study also offers clinical insights: managing blood lipids and glucose and controlling obesity may help delay or prevent multimorbidity. This provides a simple, generalizable methodological approach for the early warning of CMM. From a public health perspective, using lipid and composite indices from routine health check-ups to screen high-risk CMM individuals is a feasible and cost-effective strategy. This approach is particularly useful in primary care and resource-limited areas and has the potential to reduce the incidence and mortality of cardiometabolic multimorbidity in older adults through early warning and targeted interventions.

Therefore, our study offers a more reliable and clinically valuable method for the early risk prediction of CMM, filling a key gap in the existing literature and revealing several important findings.

Baseline metabolic abnormalities and CMM development

In the initial population without CMM, those who later developed the condition showed significantly more severe metabolic dysfunction at baseline. This included higher triglycerides (TG), lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), a larger obesity circumference, and poorer insulin sensitivity, as reflected by indices like the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG). A nationwide cohort study by Li et al. (2023) similarly found a significant association between baseline obesity and dyslipidemia with the risk of developing multiple cardiovascular and metabolic diseases [73]. Moon et al. (2023) further confirmed that an elevated TyG index was positively associated with the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [74]. These findings align with the findings from this study, suggesting that CMM likely develops on a foundation of long-term metabolic imbalance.

The close link between abnormal baseline metabolic indicators and the onset of CMM is fundamentally the result of a long-term, cumulative effect of metabolic dysfunction through a multi-pathway, multi-target pathophysiological mechanism. At its core is the synergistic damage that metabolic imbalance inflicts on the cardiovascular system and metabolic organs. The inflammatory model proposed by Hotamisligil (2017) also emphasizes that adipose tissue dysfunction and chronic low-grade inflammation are key factors in the co-occurrence of cardiometabolic diseases [80].

From the perspective of adipose tissue dysfunction, a larger waist circumference (especially abdominal obesity) signifies excessive proliferation and hypertrophy of fat cells. This transforms adipose tissue from an “energy storage organ” into an “endocrine-disrupting organ.” This dysfunctional tissue releases an excessive amount of free fatty acids, which can enter the liver via the portal vein, triggering lipotoxicity. At the same time, it secretes a large number of pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) and adipokines (e.g., resistin), which sustain a state of systemic low-grade chronic inflammation [81]. This chronic inflammation not only directly damages vascular endothelial cells but also reduces insulin sensitivity, creating a vicious cycle from “obesity to inflammation to metabolic dysfunction” that lays the foundation for the co-occurrence of diseases like hypertension, diabetes, and atherosclerosis.

Insulin resistance, a core feature of metabolic syndrome, plays a pivotal role in the development of CMM. A decline in baseline insulin sensitivity (as shown by an elevated TyG index) leads to the body’s compensatory over-secretion of insulin, causing hyperinsulinemia [82]. Hyperinsulinemia can promote elevated blood pressure by stimulating sympathetic nerve excitement, enhancing the reabsorption of sodium and water by renal tubules, and activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). Meanwhile, abnormal insulin signaling inhibits fat breakdown and lipoprotein metabolism, leading to elevated TG and reduced HDL-C, which worsens dyslipidemia. Furthermore, insulin resistance can impair vascular endothelial function, reduce nitric oxide (NO) release, and promote vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and platelet aggregation, thereby accelerating the formation and progression of atherosclerotic plaques and significantly increasing the risk of co-occurring cardiovascular and metabolic diseases.

The synergistic effect of dyslipidemia further magnifies the risk of CMM. High baseline TG levels lead to the abnormal metabolism of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (e.g., VLDL), producing numerous remnant cholesterol particles that easily deposit in vessel walls and trigger lipid peroxidation. Conversely, low HDL-C levels weaken its “reverse cholesterol transport” function and its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [83], making it less effective at clearing excess cholesterol from blood vessels and inhibiting plaque inflammation. This imbalance between “lipid deposition and impaired clearance” is a significant cause of coronary heart disease and stroke, and it can also damage pancreatic β-cell function through lipotoxicity, accelerating the development of diabetes and creating a reciprocal promotion between cardiovascular and metabolic diseases.

Notably, these metabolic abnormalities are not isolated; they are interconnected and influence one another through a complex network. For example, lipotoxicity caused by obesity can worsen insulin resistance, which in turn promotes fat synthesis and ectopic deposition. Vascular damage from dyslipidemia can reduce insulin sensitivity, while insulin resistance, in turn, can exacerbate lipid metabolism abnormalities. This synergistic effect of multiple factors causes metabolic dysfunction to progressively worsen. When the body can no longer compensate for this cumulative damage, a state of co-existing diseases like hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and coronary heart disease appears—which is the onset of CMM.

Similarly, in a analysis of hypertensive patients, Yin et al. (2023) found that CMM risk increased significantly with every 1-standard-deviation rise in key indices like the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI), Lipid Accumulation Product (LAP), and Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI) [84]. For example, a 1-SD increase in LAP led to about an 8% increase in risk, and a 1-SD increase in the Chinese population’s Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI) increased risk by 54%. The prospective analysis in our study validates the importance of these metabolic indicators at a population level and further emphasizes the value of the cumulative effect of multiple metabolic risk factors in predicting CMM.

Advantages of machine learning in CMM risk prediction

While several predictive models for cardiometabolic diseases exist, most rely heavily on single risk factors and linear statistical methods. For instance, the traditional Framingham Risk Score and recent models by Moe et al. (2024) [79] primarily utilize basic clinical variables (e.g., age, blood pressure) under strict linear assumptions. These models often fail to capture the synergistic metabolic dysregulation characteristic of multimorbidity (CMM). In contrast, our study demonstrates that machine learning methods show a significant advantage because they can overcome these limitations and accurately capture complex pathophysiological connections. Traditional Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) assume linear relationships and independence between variables. However, the development of CMM is driven by multi-factor interactions. Our results highlight a critical “non-linear threshold effect”: the relationship between lipid accumulation (LAP) and insulin resistance (TyG) is not simply additive. Once LAP exceeds a certain threshold, the predictive power of TyG for CMM increases exponentially—a complex biological interaction that traditional GLMs inherently underestimate [85]. Machine learning algorithms (such as Random Forest and Deep Learning) do not require pre-specified relationships, allowing them to autonomously learn these intrinsic links from vast datasets, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of disease risk [86].

We also found that Deep Learning (DL) models’ strength in CMM risk prediction comes from their powerful capabilities in automatic feature extraction and hierarchical modeling. Predicting the risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity involves multi-dimensional, highly complex data, including continuous lipid indicators (e.g., TG, HDL-C), composite metabolic indices (e.g., TyG-BMI, LAP), and categorical variables (e.g., sex, region). Through a multi-layered neural network structure, DL models can process raw data layer by layer: lower layers automatically extract basic features (like numerical fluctuations of a single lipid indicator), middle layers integrate simple interactions (like the link between TG and insulin levels), and upper layers capture more abstract, complex patterns (like the non-linear mapping of metabolic indicator combinations to disease risk). This “end-to-end” learning approach avoids the limitations of manual variable selection in traditional feature engineering, making it especially well-suited for a multi-factorial condition like CMM. For example, when handling the interaction between TyG-BMI and age, a DL model can independently recognize the difference between the “steep risk increase effect of high TyG-BMI in middle-aged individuals” and the “risk mitigation effect in older adults” without a researcher having to pre-define interaction terms, thus enhancing predictive accuracy [85].

The advantage of ensemble models lies in their stability and generalizability with complex data. By combining the predictions of multiple weak learners (such as a voting mechanism among many decision trees), they effectively reduce the bias and variance of a single model, improving predictive robustness. CMM risk prediction often involves issues like uneven data distribution and outlier interference (e.g., metabolic indicators of some extremely obese individuals deviating from the normal range), which can easily cause a single model to overfit. Ensemble models use strategies like “Bagging” (bootstrap aggregating) or “Boosting” to train on multiple samples and features, which reduces the model’s sensitivity to outliers while strengthening its ability to identify key features [86]. For example, in our study, the ensemble model consistently captured the core predictive value of LAP and TyG-BMI, maintaining consistent performance (AUC fluctuation less than 0.02) even when metabolic characteristics differed between northern and southern populations. Additionally, ensemble models are typically equipped with feature importance mechanisms (like SHAP values or Gini coefficients), which can visually quantify each metabolic indicator’s contribution to the prediction, providing interpretability to help us understand CMM’s risk drivers [87].

Compared to traditional logistic regression, our DL and ensemble models both showed improved AUCs (0.72 vs. 0.69) and better F1 scores, indicating that machine learning can capture non-linear relationships and enhance a model’s discriminative power. The interaction between LAP and TyG, for example, cannot be directly modeled by GLM but can be effectively handled by DL and similar models. A similar case can be seen in a UK elderly cohort study by Luo et al. (2020), which used Boruta feature selection combined with Random Forest/XGBoost to develop a stroke risk prediction model that included CMI, achieving an AUC of 0.74–0.76. This example, like our own study, demonstrates the immense potential of combining composite indices with machine learning [88].

Composite lipid indices outperform traditional lipid measures

Based on the results of both feature importance ranking and SHAP analysis, this study found that the predictive power of LAP and TyG biomarkers was significantly superior to traditional lipid markers such as LDL-C and TC. This finding corroborates recent research trends. Existing studies show that ApoB100 can predict atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk more accurately than LDL-C, as ApoB100 represents the total number of all pro-atherogenic lipoprotein particles [89]. Non-HDL-C and triglyceride-rich remnant particles also have stronger independent value in explaining the residual risk of ASCVD [90]. This suggests that for complex outcomes driven by multiple pathological mechanisms, such as CMM, comprehensive risk metrics may be more advantageous than single lipid parameters. Our study, by incorporating LAP and TyG series indices into the CMM risk prediction model, provided additional predictive information. These indices are clinically easy to obtain and simple to calculate, placing no extra burden on patients, making them valuable supplements to existing risk assessment tools.

The core value of the TyG index (triglyceride-glucose index) and its derivatives (TyG-BMI, TyG-WC) lies in their ability to capture the synergistic effects of insulin resistance (IR) and lipid metabolism disorders. IR is considered the “driving engine” of CMM’s development [91]. In a state of IR, the physiological actions of insulin—promoting glucose uptake and inhibiting free fatty acid release—are impaired. A large amount of free fatty acids enters the circulation, promoting the synthesis of triglycerides in the liver, leading to hypertriglyceridemia. At the same time, it inhibits lipoprotein lipase (LPL) activity, which reduces TG clearance efficiency and exacerbates lipid accumulation. This “IR to high TG to lipotoxicity” chain reaction can damage vascular endothelial cells through oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways and trigger β-cell functional failure through cumulative lipotoxicity, ultimately leading to the co-occurrence of diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease [92]. Multiple prospective studies have confirmed that the TyG index shows excellent efficacy in predicting both the onset of type 2 diabetes and coronary heart events [93, 94], which is highly consistent with our findings in CMM risk prediction.

LAP (Lipid Accumulation Product)’s unique value is that it directly integrates waist circumference and triglyceride levels to precisely quantify the lipid accumulation risk associated with abdominal obesity. Its gender-specific formulas (male: [WC-65] × TG; female: [WC-58] × TG) account for the differences in fat distribution and metabolic characteristics between men and women [95]. Abdominal obesity is not only a key marker of ectopic lipid deposition but also induces hypertrophy and dysfunction of fat cells, which release pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) and form “adipose inflammatory foci,” promoting the onset of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). NAFLD has a clear causal link with IR, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease [96]. Previous studies have shown that LAP is superior to single waist circumference or TG indicators in predicting metabolic syndrome (MetS) and recurrent cardiovascular events [97]. Our study’s results validate this advantage in the context of CMM, a multi-disease outcome. Furthermore, our SHAP analysis provides empirical validation for two critical pathophysiological frameworks: ‘Glucolipotoxicity’ and the ‘Lipid Overflow Hypothesis.’ The dominance of the TyG-BMI index in our model suggests that CMM risk is not driven by hyperglycemia or dyslipidemia in isolation, but by glucolipotoxicity—a synergistic process where elevated glucose and free fatty acids concurrently induce oxidative stress and β-cell failure. Similarly, the high predictive value of LAP supports the lipid overflow hypothesis, indicating that once subcutaneous adipose tissue storage is exceeded, ectopic visceral fat (captured by LAP) becomes the primary source of systemic inflammation. Unlike traditional linear models that treat these factors as independent additive risks, our study confirms that these mechanisms operate through a cumulative, non-linear threshold effect, explaining why composite indices significantly outperform single lipid markers in predicting multimorbidity.

Influence of regional and environmental factors on CMM

In terms of geographical distribution, the CMM incidence heatmap in this study shows that the prevalence of CMM is generally higher than 40% in cold northern regions (e.g., Inner Mongolia, Heilongjiang, and Xinjiang), while it is below 20% in southern coastal areas (e.g., Guangdong and Fujian). This significant north-south disparity may be closely related to factors such as reduced outdoor physical activity during winter, a dietary structure biased toward high-fat and high-salt foods, and an uneven distribution of healthcare resources. Zhang et al. (2023) pointed out that cold exposure can increase sympathetic nervous system activity and basal metabolic rate, inducing elevated blood pressure and lipid metabolism disorders, which in turn increases the risk of cardiometabolic diseases [98]. In addition, due to limited outdoor activities during the long winters, residents in northern regions have significantly less physical activity, leading to an increased metabolic load that further exacerbates the risk of CMM. This can, to some extent, be explained by the more severe metabolic risk factors observed in local residents.

This geographical trend is consistent with previous research on regional differences in cardiometabolic diseases. Liu et al. (2022) analyzed national epidemiological data and found that the cardiometabolic burden is heavier for residents in northern China, with a significant compounding effect from high-salt, high-fat diets and cold climates [99]. Wang et al. (2021) quantified the contribution of various risk factors to the north-south disparity in cardiovascular disease mortality. High-sodium diets, for example, were attributed to a 9% greater risk of heart disease mortality in the north compared to the south, while obesity contributed about 2% and low temperatures about 4.7% [100]. These figures highlight the importance of environmental and lifestyle differences. Northern residents typically have a higher salt intake, lower consumption of fruits and vegetables, and are more prone to being overweight or obese. Long-term cold exposure also has a negative impact on the cardiovascular system. At the same time, air pollution is more severe in the north, which may also contribute to the occurrence of metabolic diseases [101].

These environmental and lifestyle factors collectively contribute to the significantly higher mortality rates from CVD and metabolic diseases in the north compared to the south, a finding that is corroborated by our study. Therefore, when predicting and preventing CMM, regional differences should be fully considered, with enhanced monitoring and intervention in high-risk areas (such as the cold northern regions). Although our study did not directly incorporate regional or climate data into the model, CMM prevention and control should include tailored measures for high-risk areas. For instance, strengthening the prevention of winter cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, promoting a reduced-salt diet, and improving air quality in northern regions could all help lower the incidence of CMM and its precursor diseases.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations that exist in this study should be acknowledged. First, for outcome ascertainment, the diagnosis of CMM relied on self-reported physician diagnoses. Although this is routine practice in large-scale epidemiological investigations, it may still be susceptible to recall bias or underreporting compared with direct clinical chart documentation. In terms of model applicability, although rigorous 10-replicate cross-validation confirmed the internal stability of the model, the lack of an independent external validation cohort limits the direct generalizations of the findings. Therefore, caution should be exercised in extrapolating these models to populations with different demographic or ethnic backgrounds until further cross-cohort validation is performed. A caveat is that current machine-learning approaches focus on risk stratification rather than causal inference. We did not explicitly model causal pathways or control for all unobserved confounders. Therefore, subsequent studies using the CHARLS cohort using causal models such as propensity score matching (PSM) or inverse probability process weighting (IPTW) will help to more rigorously analyze the causal relationship between these composite lipid biomarkers and CMM.

Conclusion

In this CHARLS prospective cohort study, lipid metabolism biomarkers and their derived composite indices were found to have independent predictive value for CMM. Machine learning models, especially deep learning and stacked ensemble models incorporating these indices, appear to enhance the accuracy of prediction for future CMM risk. Our findings highlight the importance of focusing on overall lipid and metabolic disorders as captured by these composite measures, rather than on individual cholesterol levels alone. This demonstrates the feasibility of integrating lipid measures from routine physical examinations for early risk assessment of multiple diseases. This prediction strategy is simple and economical, with the potential to be generalized in the community. It is of great significance for reducing the growing burden of CMM in an aging population.
