New generation probiotics: combining probiotic and biotechnological potential of Kluyveromyces marxianus
Authors: Marta Rogalska, Katarzyna Kmita, Aleksander Gryciuk, Agata Kaczmarek, Anna Katarzyna Wrońska, Jolanta Mierzejewska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractKluyveromyces marxianus has high biotechnological potential, particularly because it can utilize a wide range of substrates and grow faster than other yeasts. It has also attracted interest as a prospective probiotic; however, this application is still not well researched. Two K. marxianus strains with promising biotechnological properties and the proven ability to efficiently produce 2-phenylethanol (2-PE) and ethanol were examined in this study to evaluate their potential as probiotics. Yeasts WUT216 and WUT240 exhibited similar survival rates in a simulated human digestive system as the reference K. marxianus strain B0399 (DiarYeast®, LongLife, Milano, Italia). Additionally, the WUT yeasts revealed a high degree of adhesion to cancer and normal cell lines in vitro. Importantly, the WUT strains did not exhibit undesirable characteristics, such as mucolytic and hemolytic activities, and toxic effects on the Caco-2 cell lines in vitro and on Galleria mellonella larvae in vivo. Furthermore, K. marxianus strains displayed attractive probiotic properties, particularly high antioxidant potential, antifungal activity, and significant β-glucan content. This study also showed that the WUT yeasts are sensitive to the commercial antifungals, fluconazole, amphotericin B, and geneticin G418. Overall, the WUT240 and WUT216 exhibit similar probiotic properties to those of B0399. Thus, the WUT yeasts are not only promising producers of 2-PE and ethanol but also attractive probiotic candidates. Additionally, discrepancies in literature reports regarding the analyses conducted were noted, particularly in adhesion and autoaggregation tests.Keypoints• K. marxianus strains combine probiotic with its biotechnological potential• WUT yeasts may positively affect the host (antioxidants, β-glucan, antimicrobials)• Methodological differences hinder the comparison of potential probioticsGraphical Abstract Keywords: Kluyveromyces marxianus, Yeast, Probiotics, Safety, Adhesion, Antimicrobial activity

Introduction

Probiotics are an integral part of human supplementation. They are defined as “live microorganisms” that must be absolutely safe for the host, survive the conditions of the digestive tract, and have a positive effect on its health in adequate amounts (Guarner et al. 2024). Most reports focus on probiotic bacteria, i.e., Lactobacillus or Bidifobacterium. By 2024, there were over 36,000 results in PubMed for the search term “bacteria + probiotic” and only 2684 for “yeast + probiotic.” However, yeasts have been receiving increasing attention. This is due to their unique properties, such as high survivability in the extreme conditions of the digestive system (low pH, resistance to bile salts), the production of antimicrobial compounds, and limited involvement in horizontal gene transfer. While most research focuses on Saccharomyces cerevisiae, other non-pathogenic yeasts, such as Kluyveromyces marxianus, can exhibit attractive probiotic properties.

K. marxianus is mainly isolated from kefir (>70% strains), as well as from other fermented dairy products and sugar industry waste (Malka et al. 2021). Compared to traditional S. cerevisiae, this yeast is characterized by better tolerance to higher temperatures, faster growth, and the assimilation of a wider range of substrates, and Crabtree-negative metabolism. These features make it attractive to the biotechnology industry (Karim et al. 2020; Bilal et al. 2022). K. marxianus strains are used in the production of enzymes, bioethanol, and fragrances, for instance. Furthermore, some of them exhibit promising properties in the context of probiotics, such as a high tolerance to stressful environments of the gastrointestinal tract and effectiveness in colonizing the intestinal mucosa. It has been reported that dietary supplementation with K. marxianus reduces blood cholesterol levels and modulates the host’s immune response, suggesting its involvement in the regulation of inflammatory reactions. Besides, K. marxianus may promote the growth of probiotic Bifidobacterium species (Maccaferri et al. 2012a, b; Xie et al. 2015). Taking all of the above into consideration, it seems that the K. marxianus strain, which is useful for biotechnological purposes, could also be applied as a probiotic. For instance, yeast biomass remaining after producing a certain compound could be reused as a probiotic dietary supplement. This approach is sometimes employed with S. cerevisiae residues (Sampaolesi et al. 2019), but not yet with K. marxianus as more detailed studies on this yeast are needed.

The strains WUT216 and WUT240 of K. marxianus, derived from spontaneously fermented cow’s and mare’s milk, have previously been shown to be promising producers of 2-phenylethanol (a compound with a rose scent and antimicrobial properties) and ethanol (Drężek et al. 2021). Sustainable batch and continuous cultivations in a cheese whey permeate-based medium have been successfully developed. This reduces production costs and also valorizes dairy industry waste (Drężek et al. 2023). However, yeast biomass remains after the bioprocesses are completed and must be disposed of. The present study therefore aimed to investigate whether the WUT216 and WUT240 strains exhibit probiotic properties and could be considered valuable dietary supplements. The research examined the following: the yeast survival during passage through a simulated human digestive system; adhesion to normal and cancerous human cell lines derived from the epithelium of the large intestine; safety in relation to a human cell line and to Galleria mellonella larvae; and the probiotic properties, including antioxidant and antimicrobial activities, and β-glucan content. The K. marxianus B0399 strain, present in the probiotic supplement DiarYeast® (LongLife, Milano, Italia), was used as a reference for the study.

Materials and methods

Yeast strain and growth conditions

Yeasts K. marxianus WUT216 and WUT240 were isolated from Polish kefir and Kyrgyz mare’s milk, respectively, as reported previously (Drężek et al. 2021). They are deposited in the Warsaw University of Technology Yeast Collection (https://www.wutyeastcollection.pw.edu.pl/) glycerol stock at −80 °C. Reference K. marxianus B0399 comes from a commercial Italian probiotic supplement named DiarYeast® (LongLife, Milano, Italia). Yeasts were seeded onto Sabouraud (SAB) agar medium (Biomaxima, Lublin, Poland) from a sample of frozen glycerol stock or directly from a supplement capsule and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Then, to set up a culture for the experiment, one colony was taken to inoculate SAB broth (Biomaxima, Lublin, Poland) or YPD broth (1% w/v yeast extract, Biomaxima, Lublin, Poland; 2% pepton w/v, Biomaxima, Lublin, Poland; 2% glucose w/v, Chempur, Piekary Śląskie, Poland).

Yeasts were characterized by their size – diameter. Aliquots of 10 µl of overnight yeast culture grown in SAB were applied to the glass slide, and microscopic observations were carried out with 600 × magnification (Nikon Eclipse Ni fluorescence microscope, Plan Fluor 60 ×/0.85 lens, CFI 10 ×/22 ocular, Nikon DS-Fi3 camera, Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). The images were captured, and the diameter of cells was measured using NIS Elements DR ver. 5.30.04 software (Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). The diameter of 300 cells was then plotted to the yeast size distribution graph.

To monitor the yeast growth over time in full medium, optical density at a 600 nm wavelength (OD600) measurements were taken, and the samples were plated on agar plates to count CFU/ml (colony forming units per ml). Firstly, cultures of WUT216, WUT240, and B0399 were prepared in SAB and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm (Benchtop shaker SI-600R Lab Companion, Jeio Tech, Daejeon, South Korea). Then, aliquots of 1.5 ml of culture were transferred to 150 ml of fresh SAB and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm. During the first 6 h, every 1 h, the absorbance was measured (spectrophotometer UV-1800 PC, AOE Instruments, Shanghai, China), and every 3 h, the yeast culture was tenfold diluted and spread on SAB agar plates, and then after 24 and 48 h. Additionally, the maximum specific growth rate µ was calculated using the following formula: µ = ln(OD600 of t2/OD600 of t1)/(t2 – t1), where t1 is the start time of exponential growth, and t2 is the end time.

Yeast viability in conditions simulating the gastrointestinal tract

The survival of WUT216, WUT240, and reference B0399 in the simulated human gastrointestinal tract was examined according to the pathway of food digestion described by Marques et al. (2011), Minekus et al. (2014), Chen et al. (2022), and Li et al. (2024). Yeasts were cultured in SAB overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm. Then, yeasts were centrifuged (2900 × g, 10 min, RT (room temperature)), washed with PBS (1 × phosphate-buffered saline w/o calcium w/o magnesium, VWR International, Leuven, Belgium), and resuspended in sterile water. The gastrointestinal fluids were prepared earlier at 2 × concentration and proceeded as described below.

Simulated saliva fluid (SSF) contained 1.1 mg/ml KCl (Chempur, Piekary Śląskie, Poland), 0.5 mg/ml KH2PO4 (Pol-Aura, Dywity, Poland), 1.1 mg/ml NaHCO3 (Chempur, Piekary Śląskie, Poland), 0.03 mg/ml MgCl2 (Chempur, Piekary Śląskie, Poland), 0.006 mg/ml (NH4)HCO3 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and 0.2 mg/ml CaCl2(H2O)2 (Chempur, Piekary Śląskie, Poland) dissolved in sterile tap water. The mixture was adjusted to a pH of 7.0. Aliquots of 5 ml yeast suspension were added to 5 ml of SSF and incubated for 2 min at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm.

Simulated gastric fluid (SGF) contained 0.5 mg/ml KCl, 0.1 mg/ml KH2PO4, 2.1 mg/ml NaHCO3, 2.5 mg/ml NaCl (Chempur, Piekary Śląskie, Poland), 0.02 mg/ml MgCl2, 0.04 mg/ml (NH4)HCO3, 0.02 mg/ml CaCl2(H2O)2 and 4000 U/ml pepsin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) dissolved in the sterile tap water. The mixture was adjusted to a pH of 3.0. After yeast incubation in SSF, aliquots of 10 ml were immediately added to 10 ml of SGF and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm.

Simulated small intestinal fluid (SIF) contained 0.5 mg/ml KCl, 0.1 mg/ml KH2PO4, 7.1 mg/ml NaHCO3, 2.2 mg/ml NaCl, 0.07 mg/ml MgCl2, 3.0 mg/ml CaCl2(H2O)2, 2.0 mg/ml pancreatin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and 5 mg/ml bile salts (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) in sterile tap water. The mixture was adjusted to a pH of 7.0. After yeast incubation in SSF, aliquots of 20 ml were immediately added to 20 ml of SIF and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm.

From each step, 20 µl of samples were taken, diluted, and seeded onto SAB agar plates to estimate CFU/ml. The viability index (VI) was calculated according to the formula: VI = (logNt/logN0) × 100%, where Nt is the yeast solution CFU/ml for the end of incubation of each mixture and N0 is the initial yeast solution CFU/ml for time 0 of incubation of each mixture (Siesto et al. 2022; Nami et al. 2024).

Factors determining survival in the gut

To estimate the content of β-glucan in yeast cell wall, the commercially available kit was used (β-GLUCAN Yeast and Mushroom, Megazyme, Bray, Ireland) and the Megazyme Mega-CalcTM software v2.4.3 (Megazyme, Bray, Ireland).

Yeasts were characterized by their antioxidant potential according to Baliyan et al. (2022). Aliquots of 1 ml of overnight yeast culture grown in SAB were centrifuged (2900 × g, RT, 10 min), washed twice, and suspended in 1 ml of 0.85% NaCl solution. Then, 500 µl of cell suspension, HCOOH (85%, POCH, Gliwice, Poland) as a positive control, or 0.85% NaCl as a control reaction was mixed with 500 µl of 0.2 mM DDPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl, 78.8 mg/l, in methanol, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and incubated for 30 min in the darkness. The samples were centrifuged (2900 × g, 5 min, RT), and aliquots of 200 µl of supernatants were transferred to a 96-well plate. The absorbance at a 517 nm wavelength was measured using a plate reader (Synergy H4 Hybrid Reader, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The antioxidant properties were determined by the following formula: Aox = [(Ac – As)/Ac] × 100%, where Ac means control reaction absorbance, As means sample absorbance.

The autoaggregation assay was performed as described by Alkalbani et al. (2022) with some modifications. The overnight yeast culture grown in SAB was centrifuged (2900 × g, 10 min, RT), washed twice, and suspended in 2 ml of PBS. The yeast suspension was transferred to a spectrophotometric cuvette and kept at RT without shaking. The optical density at 600 nm was measured at time 0 (A0). Subsequently, OD600 measurements were taken every 20 min during 6 h and with additional measurements after 22 and 24 h. The degree of autoaggregation (%) was calculated: autoaggregation = [1-(At-A0)] × 100%, where A0 is the absorbance at time 0, and At is the absorbance for subsequent measurements.

The hydrophobicity of WUT216, WUT240, and B0399 was determined according to Youn et al. (2022) with some modification. The total of 2 ml of overnight yeast culture grown in SAB was prepared as mentioned above. The OD600 measurements of the cell suspension were taken and defined as A0. The aliquots of 1 ml were transferred to centrifuge tubes, and 200 µl of chloroform (POCH, Gliwice, Poland) was added. The samples were vigorously mixed for 15 min and then incubated for 1 h at RT without shaking to allow phase separation. After that, the aqueous phase was gently mixed by pipetting to achieve a thoroughly dispersed yeast suspension, and OD600 was measured (At). The hydrophobicity was calculated with the formula: Hp = 1 – (At/A0).

Adhesion ability to the gastrointestinal surface

The yeast’s adhesion ability to mucin was determined as described by Garcia-Gonzalez et al. (2018). Yeasts were cultured in YPD broth overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm. Sterile mucin solution (10 mg/ml in PBS, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was applied to a 24-well plate and incubated overnight at 4 °C without shaking. Then, the mucin solution was gently removed and washed with PBS (VWR International, Leuven, Belgium). BSA solution (bovine serum albumin, 20 mg/ml, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was added to the mucin layer for 4 h at 4 °C and washed with PBS. The yeast cultures were centrifuged (2900 × g, 10 min, RT), washed with PBS, and then diluted 10 × in PBS and added to wells for a 1.5-h incubation at 37 °C without shaking. Initial suspensions were serially diluted tenfold in PBS, and each sample was applied onto YPD supplemented with 2% (w/v) agar (Biomaxima, Lublin, Poland) to calculate the CFU/ml. After 1.5 h of incubation, the suspension was removed, the wells were washed twice with PBS to remove non-adherent cells, and the adherent yeasts were detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, Thermofisher, Waltham, MA, USA). The cells were then collected and processed as described above.

Yeast adhesion to a confluent monolayer of human colon-derived cell lines was evaluated. The tested lines included cancer cell lines: HCT 116 ATCC CCL-247, HT-29 ATCC HTB-38, and Caco-2 ATCC HTB-37, as well as normal ones: CCD-18Co ATCC CRL-1459 and CCD 841 CoN ATCC CRL-1790. HT-29 and HCT 116 were grown in McCoy’s 5 A (VWR International, Leuven, Belgium) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Columbia origin, EURx, Gdańsk, Poland) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (pen/strep, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), Caco-2 in MEM (minimal essential medium, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 20% FBS, 1% pen/strep, and 0.1 mM NEAA (non-essential amino acids, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), normal cell lines in MEM (VWR International, Leuven, Belgium) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% pen/strep, and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The 500 µl of cancer at 5 × 105 cells/ml or normal at 2.5 × 105 cells/ml were added into a 24-well plate and incubated for 3 days at 37 °C, 5% CO2 to reach ~100% monolayer confluence. On the 2nd day, yeast cultures were set up and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm; the next day they were prepared as described in the mucin adhesion assay but in the appropriate cell medium. The yeast solutions were diluted in PBS and dropped onto agar medium, incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to count CFU/ml. On the 3rd day, Caco-2 cells were gently washed with PBS, and 500 µl of yeast solution or medium as a control was added. The incubation time, as in the mucin adhesion assay, was 1.5 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Then, the wells were washed twice with PBS to remove unattached cells, and trypsin/EDTA was added to release adherent cells. Detached yeasts were collected, diluted, and inoculated as described above. The same assay was performed on Caco-2 cells incubated for 8 days; the medium was changed every 3 days. Additionally, for experiments with Caco-2 cells incubated for 3 and 8 days before trypsinization, the images were taken using ZOE Fluorescent Cell Imager (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Adhesion was quantified using the following three formulas: I. degree of adhesion = (log CFU/ml of adhered yeasts)/(log CFU/ml of yeast solution) × 100%, where CFU/ml = number of colonies × 1/dilution × 200 (Shivani and Sathiavelu 2024); II. degree of adhesion = (CFU/ml of adhered yeasts/CFU/ml of yeast solution) × 100% (Sophatha et al. 2020); III. N = CFU of adhered yeasts/100 human cells, where N means the number of yeasts to 100 human cells, taking into account the initial cell density value (Wang et al. 2017).

Safety evaluation in in vitro and in vivo tests

Hemolytic activity was performed according to Yasmin et al. (2020). For this, one colony of each WUT216, WUT240, and B0399 from the SAB agar plate was taken and spread onto BD Columbia Blood Agar with 5% sheep blood plates (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Staphylococcus aureus ATCC6538 was used as a positive control. After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C, the presence of a clear zone around the colonies was examined, indicating hemolytic activity.

Mucolytic activity, as described by Coutinho et al. (2021) with some modification, was verified in YNB (Yeast Nitrogen Base, Biocorp, Warsaw, Poland) supplemented with 1% agar, 0.67% ammonium sulphate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 0.5% mucin in a Petri dish. Candida albicans ATCC10231 was used as a positive control. Yeasts were cultured in SAB and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm. A 10 µl aliquot of each culture was spotted onto an agar plate and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C. Then, they were stained with 0.1% amide black (Pol-Aura, Dywity, Poland) in 3.5 M acetic acid (POCH, Gliwice, Poland) for 30 min with gentle rocking and washed with 1.2 M acetic acid until the presence of a halo around the control spot; discoloration means mucin degradation.

As described by Ceugniez et al. (2017), the cytotoxicity of yeasts was investigated by propidium iodine stain. Caco-2 cell line was maintained in MEM supplemented with 20% FBS, 1% pen/strep, and 0.1 mM NEAA at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Caco-2 cells (5 × 105 cells/ml, passage 6) were plated in 24-well plates for 8 days. The medium was changed every 3 days. The day before the end of Caco-2 incubation, yeasts were cultured in SAB and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm, then washed with PBS and prepared in the MEM described above. On the following 8th day, Caco-2 cells were gently washed with PBS and treated with yeast at a ratio of 1:4 Caco-2 cells/yeast cells, for a further 24 h. Medium and 50% DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in medium served as a negative control and a positive control, respectively. Then, cells were washed with PBS and stained with 2.5% iodine propidine (Pol-Aura, Dywity, Poland). The fluorescence measurements were taken using a plate reader with excitation/emission wavelength parameters of 575/615 nm. Viability was calculated using the formula: viability = [1 – (F_sample/F_DMSO)] × 100%, where F_sample means fluorescence of the sample and F_DMSO means fluorescence of 50% DMSO. Then the relative viability (RV) was calculated as a ratio of the viability of the sample to the viability of the negative control.

The in vivo cytotoxicity of yeasts was examined using the G. mellonella wax moth (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) animal model. A colony of insects was maintained in chambers under controlled conditions (30 °C, 70% r.h. (relative humidity)), without a photoperiod (constant darkness), and fed an artificial diet consisting of wheat flour, wheat bran, corn flour, skimmed milk powder, honey, and glycerine (Sehnal 1996). Three-day-old final instar (7th instar) larvae were used in the experiment. The larvae cohort was fed 5 g of artificial diet containing 10⁶ yeast cells prepared in 100 µl of PBS. Untreated larvae and those treated with PBS were used as test and negative controls, respectively. The experiment was performed in triplicate, with 10 larvae included in each replicate. The development of treated larvae was monitored daily for 14 days, recording larval mortality, pupation, and adult emergence. Throughout the experiment, the larvae were provided with an appropriate diet and maintained under standard temperature and humidity conditions (30 °C, 70% r.h.).

Antimicrobial activity and drug susceptibility

The antagonism between K. marxianus yeasts and human pathogens such as bacteria (Escherichia coli ATCC8739, Bacillus subtilis ATCC6633, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC9027, Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium ATCC14028, and S. aureus ATCC6538), yeasts (C. albicans ATCC10231 and ATCC90028, Candida tropicalis WUT5, and Clavispora lusitaniae WUT17), and molds (Aspergillus niger ATCC16404, Fusarium sambucinum MF1, Fusarium oxysporum MF5, and Fusarium culmorum MF18; MF strains from Collection of the Institute of Plant Breeding and Acclimatisation—National Research Institute, Radzików, Błonie) was examined. For yeast culture, YPD with 1% agar was used; for bacteria, LB (Bioshop, Burlington, ON, Canada) with 1% agar; and for molds, PDA (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). After cooling, 200 µl of the overnight K. marxianus culture was added to 200 ml of sterile medium, then poured into Petri dishes. Once the agar solidified, the 5 µl aliquots of overnight pathogen culture (bacterial or yeast) were applied, and the plates were incubated for 24 h (bacteria) or 48 h (yeasts) at 37 °C. The molds were applied to the middle of the agar plates that had been seeded with K. marxianus strain using pipette tips, and the plates were then incubated for 5–6 days at 30 °C. Agar plates without K. marxianus were used as a control for the growth of pathogens. Additionally, control of Kluyveromyces strains’ growth in soil culture was provided.

The ability of yeasts to resist antifungal and antibacterial drugs was determined using the disc diffusion method. The substances tested included a solution of amphotericin B (10 mg/ml in water, BioShop, Burlington, ON, Canada), fluconazole (2 mg/ml in water, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), geneticin G418 (20 mg/ml in water, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and ready-to-use discs (OXOID, Basingstoke, UK) with gentamicin (120 µg) and kanamycin (30 µg). C. albicans ATCC10231 and E. coli ATCC8739 were used as controls. The overnight yeast and bacterial cultures were suspended in sterile 0.85% NaCl solution and adjusted to a McFarland value of 1 for yeast and 0.5 for bacteria using a DEN-1 densitometer (Biosan, Riga, Latvia). Aliquots of 100 µl of suspensions were spread onto agar plates, YPD for yeasts and LB for bacteria. Sterile filter paper discs were placed onto the agar and then loaded with 5 µl of antibiotic solution, while commercially available antibiotic discs were applied to the agar and incubated. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the diameter of the growth inhibition zone was measured.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed at least in three biological and three technical replicates, with experimental values expressed as the means ± SE (standard error) unless otherwise indicated. For data with a normal distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test) and with homoscedastic variances (Brown-Forsythe test), statistically significant differences were determined by one-way ANOVA analysis with the HSD Tukey post-hoc test. Otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test was used with Dunn’s post hoc test. For the cytotoxicity test on the Caco-2 cell line, a one-sample t-test (one-tailed) with a test value of 50% was used. Data was analyzed using Origin Pro 2021b software v9.8 with an academic license (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, Massachusetts, USA).

Results

Yeast viability under conditions mimicking the gastrointestinal tract

Two strains of K. marxianus, WUT216 and WUT240, previously reported as promising 2-phenylethanol (2-PE) and ethanol producers (Drężek et al. 2021, 2023), were compared for their probiotic properties versus K. marxianus B0399 (DiarYeast®, LongLife). First, the yeast growth at 37 °C, an important characteristic of probiotics, was evaluated (Fig. 1). All strains grow very well under these conditions, but strain WUT216 exhibits the fastest growth rate (µ = 0.697 ± 0.010 h−1) followed by WUT240 (µ = 0.681 ± 0.006 h−1) and the reference strain B0399 (µ = 0.605 ± 0.019 h−1). Additionally, WUT216 is characterized by the smallest cell size (5.6 ± 0.06 µm) compared to WUT240 (6.4 ± 0.07 µm) and B0399 (5.9 ± 0.07 µm) strains.

K. marxianus WUT216, WUT240 growth characteristics at 37 °C. a Cell size distribution (n = 300). Culture growth over time presented as b CFU/ml (t) and c OD600 (t); mean values ± SE (n = 2)

Next, the survival of yeast in environments that simulate the passage of food through the human gut—specifically the saliva (SSF), stomach (SGF), and small intestine (SIF)—was examined. Yeast viability was monitored at each stage by estimating log CFU/mL and calculating the viability index (VI).

All three strains exhibited a high VI, above 0.89, under neutral pH conditions (SSF and SIF) and acidic pH conditions (SGF) (Fig. 2). However, only WUT240’s VI remains stable during yeast passage through digestive fluids. The study revealed a slight decrease in VI after 2 h of rigorous SIF incubation for the WUT216 and B0399 strains: 0.89 ± 0.026 and 0.91 ± 0.008, respectively.

Yeast properties determining survival in the gut

Having established that the WUT216 and WUT240 yeasts can remain viable in a simulated human digestive system, the next step was to assess the features that enable them to survive. Firstly, the β-glucan content in dry biomass was evaluated. Noteworthy, this polysaccharide present in the cell wall not only enhances yeast survival in the gastrointestinal tract, but also exhibits a positive effect on the host organism and is known as a regulator of the biological response (Zhong et al. 2023). The study revealed that all three strains have a similar β-glucan content in dry biomass: WUT216 = 14.02% ± 0.45%, WUT240 = 13.10% ± 0.87%, B0399 = 12.10% ± 0.68% (no significant differences between strains; p = 0.05, Fig. 3a).

Yeast properties determining high survival in the gastrointestinal tract. a β-Glucan content in dry weight (DW). b Antioxidant potential assessed by the yeast's ability to reduce the free radical content of DPPH (HCOOH was used as a positive control). c Level of autoaggregation. d Degree of hydrophobicity (chloroform was the organic phase). Mean values ± SE (n = 3); * p < 0.05

The high antioxidant potential of yeast may facilitate its survival in the gastrointestinal tract, where large amounts of free radicals are produced during digestive processes. The study also evaluated this, and the results showed that WUT216 and WUT240 display high free radical scavenging properties (76.0% ± 1.74% and 77.1% ± 0.10%, respectively), which are similar to the reference strain B0399 (74.1% ± 0.65%; Fig. 3b).

Autoaggregation was the next physicochemical trait examined (Fig. 3c). The WUT216 strain reached an autoaggregation rate of 50% between 120 and 140 min of incubation, while WUT240 reached a rate between 140 and 160 min, similar to the reference strain. Over time, all yeasts stabilized at an autoaggregation rate of 90%, achieved between 200 and 220 min of incubation.

Another feature that may enhance yeast survival in unfavorable digestive tract conditions is hydrophobicity, which was also evaluated (Fig. 3d). The study revealed that the WUT216 strain exhibits hydrophobicity similar to that of the reference strain B0399 (87.4% ± 2.00% vs 84.7% ± 3.19%), but significantly higher than that of the WUT240 strain (66.4% ± 3.77%) (p < 0.05).

Adhesion of yeasts to surfaces imitating the digestive tract

In the next stage, the degree of adhesion of yeasts to the mucin layer (the protein lining the human digestive system) and to the human cells in vitro was studied. Both cancer cell lines (Caco-2, HT-29, and HCT 116) and normal cell lines (CCD-18Co and CCD 841 CoN) isolated from human intestinal epithelium were applied. Initially, screening tests were performed to assess yeast adhesion to 3-day cell monolayers (Fig. 4a). Subsequently, the focus shifted to studies using only Caco-2 cells. Additionally, images were taken of yeasts incubated with the Caco-2-layer to confirm their ability to adhere to the cells directly (Fig. 4b).

Three approaches for data collection were applied: I—log CFU/ml, II—CFU/ml values, and III—CFU/ml of adherent yeasts per 100 cells. Mean values ± SE

Due to the diverse methods of calculating the degree of adhesion, and the lack of standardization in the literature, the results were presented in three forms: I. log CFU/ml, II. direct CFU/ml values, and III. CFU per 100 human cells (Cao et al. 2012; Valeriano et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2015, 2017; Ceugniez et al. 2017; Menezes et al. 2020; Sophatha et al. 2020; Simões et al. 2021; Shivani and Sathiavelu 2024).

Regardless of the surface used, the K. marxianus strains demonstrated a high level of adhesion (66%—92%) when the I. method was used to calculate adhesion (Fig. 4). There were no significant differences in adhesion to the respective cell line or mucin between WUT216 and WUT240 (p > 0.05). In contrast, the B0399 reference strain adhered weaker to human normal cells CCD-18Co than to HCT 116 and HT-29 cancer cells and to mucin (p < 0.05). The B0399 strain also adhered less strongly than WUT216 or WUT240 to HCT 116, HT-29, and mucin (p < 0.05). It is noteworthy that there were no differences in the degree of adhesion of all yeasts to the Caco-2 cell layers incubated for 3 or 8 days (p > 0.05).

For calculation method II, which is based on the CFU/ml ratio, the degree of adhesion of the tested yeasts to mucin and cell lines ranged from 0.72% to 26.8% (Fig. 4). B0399 displayed the strongest adhesion to mucin (26.8% ± 6.1%), which was notably higher than its adhesion to other tested surfaces (p < 0.05). Comparing the adhesion capacity to different cellular monolayers, WUT216 exhibited higher values than WUT240 and B0399, with one exception: tests on Caco-2 layers (Fig. 4a). The WUT216 showed adhesion to cell lines ranging from 4.38% to 14.6%, the WUT240 from 1.3% to 5.0%, and B0399 from 0.7% to 6.3%. As with calculation method II, there was no statistically significant difference in adhesion to Caco-2 monolayers incubated for 3 or 8 days (p > 0.05).

The third method of analyzing the capacity of yeast to adhere to cell monolayers is based on counting CFU per 100 cells. Therefore, it was not possible to test the adhesion capacity of yeast to the mucin surface using this method. Yeast WUT216 showed the greatest adhesion to the HCT 116 cell line, significantly higher than to Caco-2, CCD-18Co, and CCD 841 CoN (p < 0.05). Furthermore, it demonstrated the highest adhesion compared to the other yeasts when adhering to HCT116, Caco-2, and CCD-18Co (Fig. 4). The reference strain achieved the lowest values for each cell line compared to the WUT yeasts, which was not evident using methods I and II. The ability of the yeast to adhere to the Caco-2 8-day monolayer was greater than to the 3-day layer (Fig. 4b).

All three comparative analyses showed that strain WUT216 exhibited the strongest, although not statistically significant, adhesion to cell lines, compared to strains WUT240 and B0399. Furthermore, analyses I and II indicated that there were no differences in yeast adhesion to 3- and 8-day Caco-2 monolayers. However, the third method of calculation revealed that all strains adhere more strongly to the 8-day Caco-2 layer. The B0399 strain exhibited the greatest ability to adhere to mucin in analyses I and II compared to other surfaces and strains. There was also a discernible trend of slightly greater adhesion to cancer lines than to normal lines. However, there are some discrepancies in the methods used.

Safety profile of probiotic candidates: in vitro and in vivo toxicity tests

Potential probiotics must be safe for consumers. Therefore, further studies were conducted to test the yeasts for hemolytic and mucolytic activity, as well as cytotoxicity, using in vitro and in vivo models.

The yeasts WUT216 and WUT240, like reference B0399, do not cause changes in the blood medium, thus showing no hemolytic activity (Fig. 5a). Also, they are not able to degrade mucin due to the lack of transparency of the zone around the colony on the mucin-supplemented dish, i.e., they show no mucolytic activity (Fig. 5b).

In vitro safety assessment of the WUT216, WUT240, and B0399 strains. a Hemolytic and b mucolytic activity tests. S. aureus (SA) and C. albicans (CA) were used as controls

Cytotoxicity tests on the eight-day-old Caco-2 cell line revealed no significant variation in survival rates among cells treated with the WUT216 (92.1% ± 1.58%), WUT240 (89.12% ± 4.46%), or B0399 (83.6% ± 9.58%) strains (Fig. 6). Furthermore, the percentage of viable cells was much higher than the IC50 threshold, below which values are considered toxic (50%) (Fig. 6a; p < 0.05). The toxicity reference in the study was cells treated with 50% DMSO. Photographs showing dead Caco-2 cells stained red with propidium iodide confirm the correctness of the analysis (Fig. 6b). The density of dead cells is statistically higher after treatment with 50% DMSO than after incubation with yeast or in untreated cells.

Due to the lack of toxicity of potential probiotics in vitro, the safety assessment was studied in vivo using a model organism—G. mellonella larvae. The supplementation of yeast did not exert an influence on larval mortality, pupation time, time to emergence, or adult attainment when compared to the control groups (Fig. 7; p > 0.05).

Antimicrobial activity and drug susceptibility of the K. marxianus strains

The final task was to determine the antagonistic potential of the K. marxianus strains against the five pathogenic bacteria, three yeasts, and four molds, as well as establishing drug susceptibility.

Moderate antibacterial activity was demonstrated by strains WUT216, WUT240, and B0399 (Fig. 8). The growth of B. subtilis and P. aeruginosa was only inhibited in the presence of all K. marxianus strains by about 50% and 30%, respectively. In terms of antifungal activity, weak antagonism toward pathogenic yeasts was observed. The strongest antimicrobial activity was revealed against the tested Aspergillus and Fusarium strains. The K. marxianus strains inhibited mold growth by over 80% relative to the control, with WUT216 demonstrating the highest activity.

Discussion

Searching for strains with broad biotechnological potential is highly desirable, as it can lead to significant advancements in various fields. There is a growing interest in the yeast K. marxianus, which can be used to produce valuable compounds and which, according to an increasing number of reports, exhibits probiotic properties. However, knowledge in this area is still limited. Thus, the probiotic potential of two K. marxianus strains (WUT216 and WUT240) was evaluated, as they had previously demonstrated promising abilities to synthesize 2-phenylethanol (Drężek et al. 2021). These yeasts were obtained from fermented foods, namely kefir of Polish origin and mare’s milk from Kyrgyzstan. The study revealed that the WUT yeasts have a good growth rate at 37 °C, which is approximately human body temperature. Notably, the WUT216 demonstrated a higher specific growth rate than the K. marxianus B0399 strain, which was sourced from the Italian supplement DiarYest®, and employed as a reference in the study. This growth rate was also higher than those values reported previously by Fonseca et al. (2013) and Dong et al. (2025).

The passage of yeast through the human digestive system was simulated in accordance with the food digestion pathway, i.e., through saliva (SSF), stomach (SGF), and small intestine (SIF). It is worth noting that most literature sources simplify the composition of digestive juices. In this study, a standardized protocol was used, taking into account the appropriate concentrations of ions, enzymes, pH, and incubation time, which made it possible to recreate conditions more similar to physiological ones, and thus more demanding than those commonly used in the literature. The presented results show that the K. marxianus WUT216 and WUT240 strains, like the B0399 reference strain, exhibit high survival rates in individual sections of the digestive tract. They are resistant to the SGF environment similarly as previously reported for K. marxianus, Kluyveromyces lactis strains, and some S. cerevisiae and S. cerevisiae var. bulardii strains (Li et al. 2021; Goktas et al. 2021; Siesto et al. 2022; Alkalbani et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2023; Buonanno et al. 2025; Meral-Aktaş et al. 2025). However, under SIF conditions, WUT216 and WUT240 achieved VI values of ≥0.89, making them more attractive than the aforementioned strains, which mostly have VI values of <0.85. As previously reported (Bezkorovainy 2001), bile salts in the small intestine are the primary obstacle to probiotic viability. Nevertheless, compared to some other strains of the S. cerevisiae (Gryciuk et al. 2025), K. marxianus yeast achieves a significantly lower survival rate in a simulated human digestive system, especially in such key sections as SGF and SIF. The greater sensitivity of Kluyveromyces to gastrointestinal conditions may explain why strains of the S. cerevisiae species are more frequently isolated from human feces than K. marxianus (Hallen-Adams and Suhr 2017; Di Paola et al. 2020; Meral-Aktaş et al. 2025).

The WUT216 and WUT240 yeasts were examined to determine the factors contributing to their remarkable survival rates in the human digestive system, which could also potentially have a beneficial impact on human health. The content of β-glucan in the cell wall was considered, as it had been proven to display anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory properties; stimulate the immune system; serve as a marker of intestinal inflammation; reduce oxidative stress; and regulate blood sugar, lipids, and intestinal microbiota (Vaithanomsat et al. 2022; Siesto et al. 2022; Chakraborty and Devi Rajeswari 2022; Zhong et al. 2023). The β-glucan content of WUT yeasts (13.10%–14.02% w/w dry matter) is comparable to that of probiotic strain B0399 and greater than that of K. marxianus TISTR 5925 (9.05% ± 0.42%; Vaithanomsat et al. 2022) and S. cerevisiae LL-1 (7.38% ± 0.68%; Siesto et al. 2022). However, it is lower than that of other S. cerevisiae strains (15%–30%), as reported by Siesto et al. (2022). It should be noted, though, that research on β-glucan production in K. marxianus is limited. Further research on the effects of different growth conditions, such as various media and temperatures, on the β-glucan content in K. marxianus cells would certainly be interesting.

Due to increased consumer awareness of healthy foods and supplements, the study of safe antioxidants, such as probiotics, is a relevant research topic (Mishra et al. 2015). The WUT216 and WUT240 strains have the potential to reduce free radicals and decrease critical levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the host environment. This is an important ability because high concentrations of ROS damage host cells, proteins, lipids, and DNA (Auten and Davis 2009). As previously reported, an antioxidant potential above 50% is an excellent feature for probiotics (Menezes et al. 2020). For instance, Bifidobacterium strains have such outstanding properties, making them popular bacterial probiotics (Yasmin et al. 2020). The WUT216 and WUT240 yeasts display 76%—77% antioxidant activity, comparable to (>70%) and distinctly higher than (<30%) that of other K. marxianus and S. cerevisiae strains isolated from food (Goktas et al. 2021; Siesto et al. 2022; Nag et al. 2023; Buonanno et al. 2025; Gryciuk et al. 2025). Thus, high free radical scavenging properties are a strain-dependent parameter.

Another important aspect is the autoaggregation of probiotics, which not only ensures their survival in the host but also facilitates their adherence to biological surfaces and thus prevents invasion by pathogenic microorganisms (Suvarna et al. 2018; Krausova et al. 2019). Here, WUT216 stood out in terms of autoaggregation rate over time compared to WUT240 or reference B0399. The rate of autoaggregation (>50%) distinguishes the WUT yeasts from many other strains of the species K. marxianus (Youn et al. 2022), S. cerevisiae var. boulardii (Tomičić et al. 2022), S. cerevisiae and Pichia kudriavzevii (Alkalbani et al. 2022). However, some recently reported yeasts have been found to aggregate faster than WUT yeasts after 1 and 2 h of incubation (Alvarez et al. 2024; Barache et al. 2024), but at 4 h of incubation, WUT yeasts aggregate faster. It follows that it is important to conduct the autoaggregation test over time, which should be standardized, in order to be able to reliably compare data throughout the entire process. Another aspect was to examine hydrophobicity, as in the case of autoaggregation, one of the physicochemical factors that is important in the process of adhesion and the formation of a cell barrier against pathogens (Aslim et al. 2007). The WUT216 strain significantly stood out due to its high degree of hydrophobicity in chloroform 87.4% ± 2.00% vs 66.4% ± 3.77% compared to the WUT240 strain. The reference yeast, B0399, also demonstrated high hydrophobicity (84.7%). As the literature shows, hydrophobicity depends on species and strain (Meral-Aktaş et al. 2025). For K. marxianus, strains can reach a level of hydrophobicity up to ~77% (Li et al. 2021), and ~69–82% (Youn et al. 2022) in chloroform. It is worth noting that these tests are performed not only with chloroform, but also with xylene, n-hexadecane, and n-hexane (Li et al. 2021; Goktas et al. 2021; Youn et al. 2022; Nag et al. 2023; Barache et al. 2024; Meral-Aktaş et al. 2025). This makes direct comparison between studies difficult.

The ability to adhere is one of the selective criteria for the probiotic properties of safe microorganisms (Youn et al. 2023b). The degree of adhesion of yeasts to the mucin layer (the protein lining the human digestive system) and to the human cells in vitro was studied. Most literature references only test adhesiveness to cancer cell lines, i.e., Caco-2, HT-29, and HCT 116, but equally important normal cell lines should not be overlooked. In this study, the scope of the research was expanded, and additional lines were included—CCD-18Co and CCD 841 CoN, normal epithelial lines from the human colon. Initially, screening tests were performed to assess yeast adhesion to 3-day cell monolayers. Subsequently, the focus shifted to studies using only Caco-2 cells, since this cell line can differentiate into a monolayer structure that exhibits characteristics typical of enterocytes in the small intestine (Lea 2015; Barache et al. 2024). As mentioned in the Results section, the lack of standardization in the method of calculating the degree of adhesiveness makes it difficult to compare the results. Therefore, adhesiveness was calculated in three ways—log CFU/ml, direct CFU/ml values, and CFU per 100 human cells. In addition, some discrepancies are noticeable. For the CFU per 100 cells method, it is not possible to calculate adhesion to non-cellular surfaces, i.e., mucin. Furthermore, B0399 achieved the lowest adhesion values for each cell line tested, using this method, which was not confirmed by the log CFU/ml and direct CFU/ml analyses. Between the 3-day and 8-day Caco-2 cell monolayers, there is a noticeable difference in the degree of yeast adhesion to cells, significantly greater for the 8-day monolayer, which was not demonstrated by the other methods. WUT yeasts effectively adhere to both the mucin layer and, to a lesser extent, to human cancer cells and normal cells derived from the colon epithelium. For each of the analyses, there is a noticeable tendency for greater adhesion to cancer lines and mucin than to normal lines. The degree of adhesion to the Caco-2 line is similar to that of K. marxianus yeast reported by Youn et al. (2023b), but weaker than in the studies conducted by Maccaferri et al. (2012b), Ceugniez et al. (2017), Hu et al. (2023), Barache et al. (2024), and Alvarez et al. (2024). In relation to research by Maccaferri et al. (2012b), adhesion for B0399 was weaker, but it is not clear whether the initial or the final Caco-2 cell concentration was used for calculations.

A mandatory feature is the safety of a given probiotic for human use. WUT yeasts have been tested for hemolytic and mucolytic activity, as well as cytotoxicity in vitro and in vivo. It has been proven that neither WUT216 nor WUT240 lyse red blood cells, just as the reference. This is likely a natural feature of microorganisms isolated from lactic acid fermentation products, including K. marxianus (Valeriano et al. 2014; Lim et al. 2019; De Jesus et al. 2022; Youn et al. 2023a; Shivani and Sathiavelu 2024; Buonanno et al. 2025; Meral-Aktaş et al. 2025). Due to the functional role of mucin, the mucolytic activity of yeast was tested. Based on amide black staining, no degradation of mucin by WUT yeast was observed. This property is also observed in S. cerevisiae (Coutinho et al. 2021). Interestingly, Se-enriched K. marxianus may have a positive effect on the host by stimulating mucin, thereby strengthening the human digestive system's barrier against pathogens with mucolytic properties (Ning et al. 2025). Furthermore, no toxic effect of live WUT yeasts on the Caco-2 cell line was observed in vitro after 24 h of incubation. The same applies to the previously described strains K. marxianus, K. lactis, and S. cerevisiae var. boulardii from the study conducted by Ceugniez et al. (2017). The cytotoxicity of live yeast cells against cell lines in vitro is not a common approach; most reports use less stringent conditions, using dead cells/cell fragments of microorganisms for this purpose. A more typical approach is to test the cytotoxicity of potential probiotics against the model organism G. mellonella larvae. According to the standards, no toxic effect of yeast was observed during the 14-day incubation of larvae after ingesting WUT216, WUT240, and B0399 in feed. This is similar to what has been observed with other strains of the species K. marxianus (Peréz-Través et al. 2021; Buonanno et al. 2025) and S. cerevisiae var. boulardii (Buonanno et al. 2025), in studies where yeast suspension was injected into the larvae’ pro-leg. It is necessary to emphasize the difference in yeast administration, as this could be a critical factor in interpreting the data. However, since probiotics are generally dosed orally, it seems more reasonable to feed the larvae the tested microorganisms than to inject them into pro-legs, though there is no regulation in this regard.

According to the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP), in order to define a strain as probiotic, it must also be characterized in terms of pathogen elimination (Meral-Aktaş et al. 2025). Thus, strains WUT216, WUT240, and B0399 were tested for their ability to inhibit the growth of human pathogens—bacterial, fungal, and mold. The WUT and B0399 yeasts did not have an adverse effect on S. aureus, which is possible for K. marxianus strains according to Barache et al. (2024). According to some reports, Kluyveromyces in co-culture with Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens is capable of inhibiting the growth of bacteria such as E. coli (González-Orozco et al. 2023), but WUT yeasts alone did not have the same effect as the reference B0399. However, these strains had an adverse effect on the growth of B. subtilis and P. aeruginosa. The tested yeasts weakly inhibited the growth of pathogenic Candida strains < 30%. This property of K. marxianus is consistent in the literature (Buonanno et al. 2025). WUT216 and WUT240 showed an attractively higher degree of inhibition of mold pathogens, including A. niger, compared to a number of newly isolated K. marxianus (Goktas et al. 2021). Literature reports suggest co-cultures of K. marxianus yeast with probiotic bacteria increase their positive effects and resistance to the external environment (González-Orozco et al. 2024). It is known that strain B0399 stimulates the growth of beneficial bacteria of the genus Bifidobacterium spp. (Maccaferri et al. 2012b), thus further research on the WUT yeast coexistence with other probiotic microorganisms could be an interesting subject. On the other hand, it has been shown that the lactic acid bacteria Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus KZM 2–11-3 and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum KC 5–12 can inhibit the growth of GRAS yeasts K. marxianus, K. lactis, and S. cerevisiae (Hoxha et al. 2023). Therefore, studying the interaction between WUT strains and probiotic bacteria would be valuable.

Probiotic yeasts should be controllable in the event of a mycosis. WUT yeasts are sensitive to three clinically important antifungal antibiotics: fluconazole, geneticin G418, and amphotericin B. Susceptibility to these drugs has recently been reported for other K. marxianus strains (Goktas et al. 2021; Buonanno et al. 2025; Meral-Aktaş et al. 2025; Lim et al. 2019). Additionally, the WUT strains and the reference strain B0399 demonstrate slight sensitivity to gentamicin, which has not been previously reported for K. marxianus. Interestingly, this broad-spectrum aminoglycoside antibiotic is produced by Micromonospora purpurea as a mixture of compounds used in antibacterial treatment. While major fractions of the gentamicin complex (C1, C1a, C2, and C2a) display weak antifungal activity, a minor component, B1, has been identified as an effective antifungal agent (Banfalvi G 2020) . The WUT and B0399 yeasts are not susceptible to kanamycin, which is another aminoglycoside drug that primarily targets bacterial ribosomes. However, some modified forms of kanamycin with antifungal properties have been developed, and certain yeast strains can become more sensitive to kanamycin due to genetic mutations (Subedi et al. 2018). Therefore, gentamicin and kanamycin should not be excluded when testing the antibiotic susceptibility of yeasts.

In summary, the WUT216 and WUT240 strains, along with the B0399 probiotic, exhibit a high level of similarity despite originating from different sources (Polish kefir, Kyrgyz mare’s milk, and Italian kefir). This is partly due to the conservative nature of genomes and the preservation of key metabolic pathways. Besides, all the strains were isolated from dairy products and experienced similar environmental conditions, including the milk composition (e.g., presence of lactose, lipids, and proteins). Nevertheless, this study revealed some differences in growth and survival rates in a simulated gastrointestinal tract (WUT240 was the most stable), antifungal drug resistance (B0399 was not susceptible to fluconazole), adhesion to various surfaces, and hydrophobicity (WUT216 exhibited a higher degree of hydrophobicity). Thus, these yeast strains are not identical. Additionally, a previous study by Drężek et al. (2021) revealed other physiological differences between the WUT216 and WUT240 strains, such as assimilation of various carbon sources. Notably, WUT216 yeast can grow on glycerol and exhibit lipolytic activity, whereas WUT240 lacks this ability.

Conclusion

WUT216 and WUT240 strains of K. marxianus are not only interesting candidates for biotechnological 2-phenylethanol and ethanol production, but also show outstanding potential in terms of probiotic applications. Strain B0399, commercially available on the market in supplement form, was used as a reference. Against its background, WUT yeasts represent a stable and promising profile of probiotic candidates. Their high survival rate in the human gastrointestinal tract, adherence to both cancerous and normal cell lines, and mucin have been demonstrated. It can be hypothesized that they may have a positive effect on the host, especially due to their high β-glucan content, their high autoaggregation, thus hindering pathogen proliferation, and their high antioxidant potential, neutralizing ROS free radicals. The WUT216 and WUT240 yeasts also exhibit attractive growth inhibitory properties against pathogenic mold species of Aspergillus and Fusarium. Most importantly, based on detailed studies, WUT yeasts can be considered safe. Of course, further advanced research in an in vivo system is necessary to bring them to the pharmaceutical market.

In addition, some inaccuracies in the literature reports on the testing of potential probiotic microorganisms are noticeable. Survival in the simulated human gastrointestinal tract mainly concerns simplified compositions, which vary greatly. The degree of adhesion is reported in the literature in at least three different variants. The degree of autoaggregation is often not carried out over time, but only at one time point. All these aspects lead to difficulties in comparative analysis.
