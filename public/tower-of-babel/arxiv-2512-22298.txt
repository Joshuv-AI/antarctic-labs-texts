Real-Time In-Cabin Driver Behavior Recognition on Low-Cost Edge Hardware
Vesal Ahsani, Babak Hossein Khalaj

Abstract
In-cabin Driver Monitoring Systems (DMS) must recognize distraction- and drowsiness-related behaviors with low latency under strict constraints on compute, power, and cost. We present a single-camera in-cabin driver behavior recognition system designed for deployment on two low-cost edge platforms: Raspberry Pi 5 (CPU-only) and Google Coral Edge-TPU. The proposed pipeline combines (i) a compact per-frame vision model, (ii) a confounder-aware label design to reduce visually similar false positives, and (iii) a temporal decision head that triggers alerts only when predictions are both confident and sustained. The system covers 17 behavior classes, including multiple phone-use modes, eating/drinking, smoking, reaching behind, gaze/attention shifts, passenger interaction, grooming, control-panel interaction, yawning, and eyes-closed sleep. Training and evaluation use licensed datasets spanning diverse drivers, vehicles, and lighting conditions ( counts in Section 6 ), and we further validate runtime behavior in real in-vehicle tests. The optimized deployments achieve ∼ \sim 16 FPS on Raspberry Pi 5 with INT8 inference (per-frame latency < 60 <60 ms) and ∼ \sim 25 FPS on Coral Edge-TPU, enabling real-time monitoring and stable alert generation on inexpensive hardware. Finally, we discuss how reliable in-cabin human-state perception can serve as an upstream input for human-centered vehicle intelligence, including emerging agentic vehicle concepts [ 1 ] .

Introduction

Driver Monitoring Systems (DMS) are increasingly required for modern safety functions, including distraction detection and drowsiness alerting, and are becoming a prerequisite for advanced driver-assistance features and higher levels of automation.
Despite rapid progress in recognition models, real-world deployment remains challenging: in-cabin illumination changes rapidly, drivers vary in appearance and habits, hands and faces are frequently occluded, and embedded platforms must satisfy strict constraints on cost, power, and thermal budget.

A recurring gap between academic prototypes and product-ready systems is that many studies emphasize offline accuracy under controlled conditions, while deployed DMS must operate as an end-to-end pipeline that:
(i) runs in real time on low-cost edge hardware,
(ii) limits false alarms caused by visually similar actions (confounders), and
(iii) produces stable alerts suitable for human feedback and downstream system integration rather than noisy frame-by-frame labels.
This paper presents a deployable MVP-to-prototype single-camera system developed for these operational requirements and validated in real in-vehicle tests.

Why in-cabin sensing can matter beyond alerts.

Recent work on
agentic vehicles
(AgVs) argues that future vehicle intelligence may extend beyond task automation toward goal-aware, human-centered interaction and adaptation
[
1
]
.
Independent of whether such agency is realized through explicit agentic architectures, a practical requirement for human-centered decision-making is reliable estimation of occupant state and behavior under real constraints.
In this sense, in-cabin behavior recognition can serve as an upstream signal for higher-level decision layers (e.g., detecting driver readiness to take over, identifying sustained secondary-task engagement, or distinguishing transient glances from prolonged distraction), while still remaining useful as a standalone safety function.

Related Work

3.1
Driver monitoring and in-cabin behavior recognition

Driver Monitoring Systems (DMS) have traditionally relied on geometric and physiological cues such as
head pose, gaze direction, eye closure metrics (e.g., PERCLOS), and facial landmarks, often combined with
rule-based logic for distraction and drowsiness detection.
Lightweight perception toolkits such as MediaPipe enable efficient face/landmark pipelines on embedded devices,
but landmark-only signals can be insufficient for fine-grained, multi-behavior recognition under occlusion,
illumination changes, and large inter-driver variation
[
3
]
.

Recent work increasingly formulates DMS as a visual recognition problem with temporal structure.
For example, long-horizon deep frameworks explicitly model variable-length temporal dependencies in drowsiness cues
(e.g., yawning, slow blinking, head nodding), and report evaluation on realistic in-vehicle datasets such as NTHU-DDD

[
6
,
7
]
.
In parallel, the community has begun to emphasize fine-grained action recognition inside the cabin.
Drive&Act provides a large-scale multi-modal benchmark with hierarchical labels and multi-view sensing,
highlighting the difficulty of recognizing subtle in-cabin activities across drivers and environments

[
4
]
.
Related distracted-driving datasets and challenges (e.g., AUC distracted driver) have also supported progress in
camera-based activity recognition under practical viewpoints
[
5
]
.

Compared to works that focus primarily on offline accuracy, our emphasis is on an end-to-end deployable pipeline
that remains stable in real driving: (i) a behavior taxonomy that includes common confounders observed in practice,
and (ii) a temporal decision head that converts noisy frame-level predictions into event-level alerts.

3.2
Efficient models and edge deployment

Edge DMS must operate under tight latency, power, and cost constraints, motivating efficient architectures and
deployment-aware optimization.
Lightweight CNN families such as MobileNetV3 are commonly used for on-device perception
[
9
]
,
and EfficientNet-style scaling has influenced accuracy–efficiency trade-offs in compact vision models

[
10
]
.
On the deployment side, integer quantization is a standard approach for reducing latency and memory footprint on
edge CPUs and accelerators
[
11
]
, and toolchains such as TensorFlow Lite support
edge-oriented inference and model conversion
[
12
]
.
Dedicated accelerators (e.g., Edge-TPU) provide stable low-latency inference for supported operator sets and
compiled models
[
13
]
.

Our implementation follows this deployment-driven perspective: we report real in-vehicle throughput on two
low-cost targets (CPU-only and TPU-accelerated), and focus on system-level robustness mechanisms (confounders +
temporal alerting) that remain effective under real illumination shifts and transient driver actions.

3.3
AgVs and the sensing-to-cognition bridge

Recent work on agentic vehicles (AgVs) argues that next-generation mobility systems will require not only autonomy
in control, but also higher-level agency such as goal adaptation, interaction, and context-sensitive decision-making

[
1
]
.
From this perspective, in-cabin sensing can be viewed as an upstream capability that grounds higher-level reasoning
in reliable estimates of human state and intent (e.g., distraction, fatigue, passenger interaction) under edge
constraints.
While this paper does not implement an agentic stack, we position robust, real-time in-cabin behavior understanding
as a practical building block that can support human-centered mobility functions envisioned in the AgV agenda.
From a systems perspective, keeping inference and alerting on-device (rather than relying on cloud round-trips) can
reduce latency and limit the exposure of raw in-cabin data and high-level context to external interfaces, which is
consistent with recent discussions of cross-layer risks in agentic vehicle stacks
[
2
]
.

The taxonomy is designed with two goals: (i) to cover high-impact distraction and drowsiness cues

6
Dataset and Diversity

Training and evaluation use a large-scale in-cabin dataset of approximately 800,000 labeled RGB images,
assembled from two licensed/commercial sources and additional in-house collection conducted by the authors.
Because parts of the dataset are proprietary, we cannot redistribute the raw images; instead, we report dataset scale,
sources of variation, and the evaluation protocol to support transparency and comparable future studies.

6.1
Scale, labeling, and split protocol

All images are annotated for the 17 behavior classes listed in Table
1
.
Labels are provided at the frame level and include both short transient behaviors (e.g., brief glances) and sustained actions
(e.g., phone interaction), which is important for validating event-level temporal alerting (Section
7.3
).

We use a fixed 80/10/10 split for train/validation/test.
To reduce identity leakage and ensure realistic generalization, the test split contains different drivers and vehicles
from those in the training and validation splits (i.e., driver-disjoint and vehicle-disjoint evaluation).

6.2
Diversity and realism factors

The combined dataset reflects practical deployment conditions and includes substantial variation across:

•

Drivers and appearance:
variation in gender/age ranges, clothing and hairstyle, and common accessories/occlusions
such as glasses/sunglasses, hats, and face masks.

•

Vehicles and cabin geometry:
multiple vehicle types and cabin geometries, with natural variation in camera mounting location.

•

Lighting and time-of-day:
day/night operation, low-light scenes, mixed illumination, reflections, and rapid lighting transitions.

•

Viewpoint and framing:
realistic viewpoints near the dashboard / rear-view mirror region with natural changes in distance and angle.

•

Behavior realism:
both brief actions and sustained events, enabling evaluation of temporal persistence gating and alert stability.

6.3
Real-world validation

In addition to offline evaluation on the held-out test set, we validated the deployed system in real in-vehicle trials
using a live camera stream on both target platforms.
This step is important because in-cabin sensing performance can shift under real driving conditions due to vibration, illumination changes,
and differences in mounting geometry.
We therefore report runtime and qualitative stability results based on on-road operation (Section
10
).

7
Method

7.1
Confounder-aware recognition design

A major failure mode in in-cabin monitoring is confusion between visually similar actions
(e.g., brief hand-to-face motion vs. phone use, or infotainment interaction vs. texting).
We reduce these errors through a
confounder-aware
design at two levels:

•

Taxonomy design:
we include explicit classes that capture common confounding activities observed in real driving
(e.g., grooming/hand-on-hair, control-panel interaction), rather than forcing these samples into nearby “unsafe” categories.

•

Decision design:
alerting is decoupled from raw per-frame classification via a temporal decision head
(Section
7.3
), reducing false alarms caused by transient gestures and short glances.

7.2
Per-frame model and training protocol

We formulate in-cabin behavior recognition as a
C
-class image classification problem
(
C=17
) on individual RGB frames.
Given an input frame
x_{t}
, the network outputs a probability vector

\mathbf{p}_{t}\in[0,1]^{C}
.

Model exploration and selection.

We evaluated multiple edge-suitable vision stacks for per-frame behavior recognition,
including (i) mobile-oriented CNN backbones (MobileNetV3-style)
[
9
]
,
(ii) EfficientNet-family models
[
10
]
, and (iii) compact hierarchical
convolutional networks with detection-inspired multi-scale feature aggregation and a
task-specific classification head.

Rather than selecting a backbone purely by offline accuracy, we performed

deployment-aware model selection
under the joint constraints of:
(a) robustness under real in-cabin variability (occlusion, low-light, viewpoint shifts),
(b) temporal stability after event gating (Section
7.3
), and
(c)
hardware executability
under INT8 quantization on both CPU-only (Pi 5) and
Edge-TPU targets (operator compatibility and sustained throughput).
Input resolution was swept from
192
to
640
;
320\times 320
emerged as the best operating point,
preserving discriminative hand/face cues while meeting real-time constraints.

The final configuration uses a compact hierarchical convolutional backbone with
multi-stage downsampling and feature mixing, followed by a lightweight
C
-way head
(
C=17
) trained for single-label behavior classification.

Architecture configuration.

The selected network comprises a multi-stage convolutional feature extractor with
progressively reduced spatial resolution and increased channel capacity, followed by a
global aggregation and a task-specific classification head.
This design provides a favorable accuracy–latency trade-off for in-cabin monitoring,
where small hand/face cues must be preserved while maintaining bounded compute.

The resulting model has approximately
15.8
million parameters and
\sim 41.9
GFLOPs at

320\times 320
input resolution, remaining feasible for real-time inference on
both CPU-only and Edge-TPU deployments after INT8 optimization.

Loss function and class imbalance handling.

In-cabin behavior data is inherently imbalanced, with safe driving dominating
rare but safety-critical behaviors (e.g., drowsiness, phone usage).
To address this, we adopt a focal loss formulation with class-dependent weighting.
Class weights are derived from inverse class frequencies and capped to a maximum
value to prevent excessive gradients from rare classes.

Specifically, the loss for a sample at time
t
is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{t}=-\alpha_{y_{t}}(1-p_{t}(y_{t}))^{\gamma}\log p_{t}(y_{t}),

where
\alpha_{y_{t}}
is the capped class weight for the ground-truth label,
and
\gamma
controls the focusing parameter (set to
\gamma=1.5
in our experiments).
This design improves minority-class recall while maintaining stable optimization.

Training protocol.

Models are trained using mini-batch optimization with the Adam optimizer,
a fixed learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
, and a batch size of
32
.
Training is performed for
50
epochs with validation-based model selection.
Horizontal and vertical flips are disabled to preserve left/right semantic consistency
(e.g., phone use on the left vs. right hand).

The selected configuration achieves strong validation accuracy while meeting
real-time constraints required for in-vehicle deployment.

7.3
Temporal decision head: confidence + persistence

Frame-level predictions are inherently noisy in real driving due to vibration, illumination flicker,
partial occlusions, and short-duration actions. Rather than triggering alerts directly from

\arg\max_{c}\mathbf{p}_{t}(c)
, we introduce a temporal gating mechanism that fires only when a behavior is both confident and sustained.

Let
p_{t}(c)
be the predicted probability for class
c
at frame
t
.
Given a confidence threshold
\tau
and persistence window length
K
(frames), an alert for class
c
is triggered at time
t
if

p_{t-i}(c)\geq\tau\quad\forall i\in\{0,1,\dots,K-1\}.

(2)

In practice,
K
is chosen to correspond to approximately one second of video (e.g.,
K=20
at
\sim
20 FPS),
and
\tau
is tunable (default
\tau=0.75
). This temporal head converts per-frame probabilities into stable event-level alerts.

Cooldown / hysteresis.

After an alert fires, we enforce a short cooldown interval (configurable) to prevent repeated triggers for a single sustained event.

8
Edge Deployment

We target two low-cost edge platforms representative of real in-vehicle pilots: a CPU-only
Raspberry Pi 5 and the Google Coral Dev Board with an Edge-TPU accelerator. For both targets,
the deployment objective is to minimize end-to-end latency while maintaining stable throughput
sufficient for the temporal decision head (Section
7.3
).

8.1
Raspberry Pi 5 (CPU-only)

On Raspberry Pi 5, per-frame inference on the CPU is the primary runtime bottleneck.
We therefore deploy an INT8-quantized version of the per-frame classifier to reduce
compute and memory bandwidth demands. Quantization is applied with a representative
calibration set and verified to preserve the input/output interface used by the online
pipeline (capture
\rightarrow
preprocess
\rightarrow
inference
\rightarrow
postprocess

\rightarrow
temporal gating). In real in-vehicle tests, the optimized INT8 deployment
achieves approximately
\sim
16 FPS with per-frame latency below 60 ms, enabling near real-time
alert generation.

8.2
Coral Edge-TPU

Deploying the classifier on Coral requires conversion to a TensorFlow Lite (TFLite) graph
compatible with the Edge-TPU compiler. We use a staged export pipeline
(PyTorch
\rightarrow
ONNX
\rightarrow
TensorFlow SavedModel
\rightarrow
TFLite
\rightarrow

Edge-TPU compiled TFLite), which provides a practical path to (i) preserve functional
equivalence across toolchains, (ii) enable post-training integer quantization, and (iii)
satisfy Edge-TPU operator constraints for accelerator-only execution.

Post-training INT8 quantization.

To minimize latency and maximize accelerator utilization, we apply full-integer (INT8)
quantization using a representative calibration set (typically 200–500 images sampled
across classes and lighting conditions). Calibration inputs follow the same preprocessing
pipeline used at runtime (resize/normalization) to reduce quantization mismatch.

Compilation and operator mapping.

The INT8 TFLite model is compiled using the Edge-TPU compiler. We validate the compilation
report to confirm full accelerator execution (i.e.,
no CPU fallback
).
In our final deployment graph, the compiler reports that
all operations are mapped to the Edge-TPU
,
which is important because partial mapping can introduce unpredictable latency and frame-to-frame timing jitter.
In real in-vehicle tests, the compiled model sustains approximately
\sim
25 FPS on the Coral Dev Board,
supporting low-latency temporal alerting.

Reproducibility.

To support reproducibility, we provide conversion and compilation scripts, environment
configuration, and calibration-data specification in an accompanying implementation repository.

Experiments and Evaluation

We evaluate the proposed system at two levels: (i)
frame-level recognition
quality of the per-frame classifier,
and (ii)
event-level alert
behavior after temporal persistence gating (Section
7.3
).
This two-level evaluation matches the deployment objective: producing stable, actionable alerts rather than jittery
per-frame labels.

9.1
Metrics

Frame-level recognition.

Let
\hat{y}_{t}=\arg\max_{c}p_{t}(c)
be the predicted class for frame
t
. We report:
(i) macro-F1 (unweighted mean of per-class F1 scores) to reflect performance under class imbalance,
(ii) balanced accuracy (mean per-class recall) to reduce dominance of the
Normal
class,
and (iii) per-class F1 to identify confusions among visually similar behaviors.

Event-level alert quality.

The temporal decision head produces alert events
\mathcal{A}=\{(c,t_{\text{start}},t_{\text{end}})\}
.
To evaluate operational stability, we report:
(i) false alert rate (alerts/min) measured on segments without the corresponding ground-truth event,
(ii) mean time-to-detect for sustained events (delay between ground-truth onset and alert onset), and
(iii) alert jitter, quantified as the number of fragmented alerts produced per ground-truth event
(lower is better).
These metrics capture the practical trade-off between fast detection and low nuisance alarms.

9.2
Baselines

We compare against three baselines that isolate the contribution of temporal reasoning and confounder handling:

•

Frame-only (no temporal head):
per-frame
\hat{y}_{t}
is used directly for alerting, i.e.,
an alert is triggered whenever
\hat{y}_{t}=c
(equivalent to
K{=}1
in Eq.
2
).
This baseline typically yields high alert jitter and nuisance alarms, and establishes the cost of not performing temporal gating.

•

Temporal smoothing (non-gated):
predictions are smoothed using a sliding-window majority vote
or exponential moving average (EMA) over probabilities, followed by per-frame thresholding.
This baseline provides temporal regularization without explicit persistence constraints.

•

Landmark-based heuristic (drowsiness proxy, optional):
a lightweight face/eye pipeline (MediaPipe)
estimates cues such as eye closure and yawning-related facial motion, combined with heuristic logic to flag drowsiness
[
3
]
.
This baseline reflects classical DMS pipelines and highlights failure modes under occlusion and illumination changes.

10.3
Qualitative robustness

Beyond throughput, we qualitatively assessed alert stability during extended driving segments.
The temporal decision head reduced nuisance triggers caused by short, transient actions
(e.g., brief glances, momentary hand movements) and produced more consistent alert segments for
sustained behaviors. In addition, the confounder-aware label design (e.g., explicit grooming/hand-on-hair
and control-panel interaction classes) reduced common visual confusions observed in early MVP iterations,
improving the practical usability of the alert stream in live operation.

AgV Implications (Discussion)

Agentic vehicle (AgV) frameworks emphasize goal adaptation and human-centered interaction beyond
classical automation pipelines
[
1
]
. In this context, reliable in-cabin perception can be
viewed as an upstream sensing layer that provides
behavioral evidence
for higher-level decision
modules. Concretely, temporally stable recognition of driver state and secondary-task engagement can
support (i) estimating attention and take-over readiness, (ii) distinguishing transient glances or gestures
from sustained distraction, and (iii) conditioning safety-aware policies on observed human context
(e.g., conservative control, escalation of warnings, or interaction timing).

However, bridging perception to higher-level reasoning introduces a dependency: cognitive layers may
treat upstream summaries as trusted state, so systematic sensing errors can propagate into downstream

. These considerations
motivate the design choices in this work—explicit confounder modeling and persistence-based alerting—as
practical safeguards that improve robustness before integrating in-cabin perception into agentic decision stacks.

12
Ethical and Privacy Considerations

In-cabin perception involves privacy-sensitive data. Practical deployments should follow a

privacy-by-design
approach: obtain explicit user consent, minimize data collection, and prefer
on-device processing to avoid transmitting raw in-cabin imagery off the vehicle. If any recordings are stored,
deployments should implement secure storage and strict retention policies. Finally, systems should be evaluated
for performance disparities across demographic groups and environmental conditions (e.g., skin tone, eyewear,
head coverings, and lighting) to reduce the risk of unequal false alarms or missed detections.

Conclusion

We presented an end-to-end, single-camera in-cabin behavior recognition system designed for real-time deployment on
low-cost edge hardware. The pipeline combines a compact per-frame classifier with a confounder-aware behavior taxonomy
and a temporal persistence decision head that converts noisy frame-level predictions into stable, event-level alerts.

The system covers 17 driver behaviors and was validated in live in-vehicle operation on two embedded targets, achieving

\sim
16 FPS on Raspberry Pi 5 (INT8,
<60
ms/frame) and
\sim
25 FPS on Coral Edge-TPU (INT8, compiled). Finally, we
note that this work provides a practical sensing foundation that can be extended toward human-centered vehicle
intelligence, including emerging agentic vehicle research directions.

14
Future Work and Outlook

A natural next step is to extend driver-centric monitoring toward broader
occupant-aware
in-cabin perception.
Beyond detecting unsafe driver behaviors, this direction supports more human-centered vehicle intelligence and can
serve as a sensing input for emerging
agentic vehicle
concepts, where vehicle behavior may adapt to occupant
context rather than relying only on fixed rule-based alerting.

From driver monitoring to multi-occupant understanding.

Future models can expand to cover passenger monitoring in addition to the driver, including basic occupancy cues
(e.g., passenger presence and posture), interaction signals (e.g., conversation or assistance requests), and
safety-relevant situations such as child presence. This broader coverage can provide context for adaptive in-cabin
interfaces and assistance functions, while still operating under real-time edge constraints.

Accessibility and inclusive evaluation.

Future work should include evaluation protocols that go beyond frame-level accuracy, incorporating user-facing
criteria such as alert acceptability (nuisance rate), perceived trust, and cognitive load. In addition, robustness
should be assessed across diverse occupants and conditions (e.g., age groups, eyewear, head coverings, and lighting)
to reduce the risk of uneven false alarms or missed detections.

Privacy-preserving deployment and learning.

Because in-cabin sensing is privacy-sensitive, future deployments should prioritize on-device processing and data
minimization. Promising directions include privacy-preserving adaptation (e.g., updating models without exporting
raw imagery) and designing outputs as temporally aggregated signals rather than continuous frame-level labels,
so downstream modules receive only the information needed for safe and useful behavior.
