Patent challenges without entry? Reassessing first generic exclusivity under the patent–linkage system in South Korea
Authors: Youran Noh, Kyung-Bok Son
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSouth Korea introduced the First Generic Exclusivity (FGE) system as part of the patent linkage system to promote patent challenges and market entry of generic drugs. Evaluating the impact of FGE on the number of generic entrants is central to assessing the system. This study elucidates factors—including patent characteristics—that influence the number of FGE applicants and holders at the ingredient level.MethodsUsing data from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, 85 ingredients with FGE applications filed between 2015 and 2025 were analyzed. For each ingredient, originator-based characteristics—including manufacturer type, patent characteristics, and drug characteristics—were collected, and the year of the first FGE application for each generic was identified. Negative binomial regression analysis was performed to determine factors affecting the number of FGE applicants, holders, and non-holders.ResultsAmong 85 ingredients, the mean number of FGE applicants was 6.59 (SD = 9.52, IQR 1–9), and the mean number of FGE holders was 4.61 (SD = 8.08, IQR 0–4). Regression results showed that the number of FGE applicants was positively associated with the number of patents and approval year, but negatively associated with the reimbursement price. The number of FGE holders showed positive associations with approval year and negative associations with reimbursement price. The application year was negatively correlated only with the number of FGE holders.ConclusionAlthough the introduction of the FGE system and the expansion of patent portfolios increased patent challenges by generic manufacturers, this did not consistently translate into actual generic market entry, underscoring the need to reexamine whether the FGE system is effectively designed to foster substantive competition.Keywords: Patent linkage, Access to medicines, First generic exclusivity, Patent portfolio, South Korea

Introduction

Generic drugs, which contain the same active ingredients and offer equivalent clinical efficacy to originator drugs, enter the market after patent expiration and promote price competition [1, 2]. Such competition plays a crucial role in improving access to medicines by lowering prices and reducing patients’ financial burden [3, 4]. As concerns over the sustainability of healthcare financing have intensified, the expanded use of generic drugs has emerged as a key cost-containment strategy [5]. Accordingly, many countries have introduced policy measures to facilitate early generic entry and encourage substitution away from originator products [6].

For generic drugs to effectively substitute for originator drugs, prices must fall to sufficiently low levels—a condition that can be achieved only when a competitively meaningful number of generic products enter the market [7, 8]. Empirical evidence from the United States shows that price reductions remain limited when only one or two generics are available, whereas prices decline sharply once five or more generics enter the market, reflecting intensified competition [9]. This suggests that the cost-saving effects of generic drugs depend not merely on market entry itself but critically on the number of generics entering the market at an early stage.

At the same time, pharmaceutical markets have become increasingly embedded in a global trade environment characterized by the expansion of free trade agreements (FTAs) and strengthened intellectual property (IP) protection [10]. While higher IP standards aim to incentivize pharmaceutical innovation, they may also delay or restrict generic entry, creating tensions between trade-related IP obligations and public health objectives [11]. In this context, the effectiveness of generic competition may be shaped by conditions formed under a globalized trade environment and strengthened IP protection [12]. As countries incorporate international norms into domestic regulation, they face a shared policy challenge of how to manage generic market entry [13].

South Korea provides an illustrative case of how these global dynamics are translated into national policy. In 2015, amid international trends toward trade liberalization and enhanced IP protection, South Korea introduced a patent linkage system [11]. By allowing the temporary suspension of generic marketing in the event of potential patent disputes, the system raised concerns about restrictions on generic market entry [14, 15]. To address these concerns and incentivize patent challenges, the Korean government simultaneously implemented the First Generic Exclusivity (FGE) system, conceptually similar to the 180-day generic exclusivity under the U.S. Hatch–Waxman Act [11]. The FGE system grants a nine-month period of market exclusivity to the first successful generic applicant [16].

However, FGE is granted only to the manufacturer that successfully challenges the patent and first launches the generic drug, which may limit the number of generic drugs able to enter the market [17–19]. This suggests that FGE may constrain early market entry and reduce the number of competitors. As a result, exclusivity-based incentives designed to promote early generic entry may paradoxically weaken the intensity of initial competition. Despite these concerns, empirical research examining how patent structures and patent challenges associated with the FGE system affect the number of generic drugs entering the market remains sparse.

This study empirically analyzes the number of FGE applicants and holders, as well as the factors associated with variation in their numbers, focusing on drug ingredients with identified FGE applications between 2015 and 2025. By accounting for patent characteristics at the ingredient level, this study systematically examines how the number of initial generic entrants has evolved over the past ten years under the patent linkage system. The findings provide evidence to inform improvements to the FGE system as a policy instrument for promoting generic competition. Beyond the Korean context, the results offer policy-relevant insights for other countries operating patent linkage and generic exclusivity mechanisms under global trade agreements and IP regimes.

Methods

Overview

This study analyzed factors influencing the number of FGE applicants, holders, and non-holders for 85 reimbursed drug ingredients with FGE applications filed between 2015 and 2025. Data from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) were used to identify these factors. To ensure accurate counts at the ingredient level, multiple applications for different dosage strengths of the same ingredient were consolidated. For example, if four manufacturers (a, b, c, d) applied for the 10 mg strength and three manufacturers (a, c, e) applied for the 20 mg strength of ingredient A, the total number of FGE applicants was counted as five (a, b, c, d, e).

Negative binomial regression analysis was conducted to examine factors associated with these counts. Independent variables were categorized into originator-level and generic-level characteristics. At the originator level, information was collected on manufacturer type (domestic vs. international), patent characteristics (number of patents and remaining patent years), and drug characteristics (initial reimbursement price, Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification, and approval year). At the generic level, the year of the first FGE application for each ingredient was recorded to capture the timing of market entry.

Data source

This study utilized two data sources. First, data from the MFDS were used to obtain information on FGE application and acquisition status, manufacturer characteristics, patent characteristics, and drug characteristics [20]. Second, initial reimbursement prices were obtained from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) drug pricing database [21].

Study design

Data refinement

Figure 1 illustrates the stepwise process of data refinement and sample selection. Initially, 182 reimbursed drug products with FGE applications submitted between 2015 and 2025 were matched to their corresponding generics to construct product-level originator–generic pairs. For ingredient-level analysis, products containing the same active ingredient but available in multiple strengths were consolidated into a single ingredient, resulting in a total of 93 active ingredients. After excluding seven non-reimbursed ingredients and one ingredient that was approved but not marketed, 85 ingredients were included in the final analysis.

Flow diagram of ingredient selection for the analysis of drug products with first-generic exclusivity (FGE) applications

Variables

This study defined count data at the ingredient level as the dependent variables. These variables include the number of FGE applicants, holders, and non-holders, corresponding respectively to the number of generic manufacturers that applied for FGE for a given ingredient, the number that obtained FGE, and the number that applied but did not obtain FGE.

Independent variables were categorized into originator-level and generic-level characteristics. Originator-level characteristics included manufacturer, patent, and drug features. Manufacturer characteristics referred to the nationality of the originator manufacturer (domestic vs. international), while patent characteristics comprised the number of registered patents per ingredient (1, 2, or ≥ 3) and the remaining years until patent expiration (continuous variable). However, patent types could not be distinguished, and thus differences between core (e.g., compound) and peripheral patents were not captured in the analysis. Drug characteristics included initial reimbursement prices, classified into four groups (< 1,000; 1,000–10,000; 10,000–100,000; and ≥ 100,000 KRW), and ATC codes, which were regrouped into four categories based on therapeutic similarity: J/L (anti-infectives for systemic use, antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents), A/B/C (alimentary tract and metabolism, blood and blood-forming organs, and cardiovascular system), M/N (musculoskeletal and nervous system), and others. Additionally, the originator’s approval year was divided into four periods (before 2007, 2007–2011, 2012–2016, and 2017–2020) to account for temporal effects associated with the introduction of the positive list system in 2007, under which only drugs demonstrating cost-effectiveness are eligible for reimbursement [22]. However, data on the market size of the originator drug were not available and thus were not included, although this factor may influence market competition and generic entry. At the generic level, the year of the first FGE application for each ingredient was included to evaluate the impact of entry timing following the system’s implementation. This variable was categorized into three periods: 2015, 2016–2020, and 2021–2024.

Statistical analysis

To examine the relationship between FGE applicants and holders at the ingredient level, we first assessed the distribution of FGE applicants and holders across 85 ingredients. Based on this relationship, FGE application efficiency was defined as the ratio of the number of FGE holders to the total number of FGE applicants for each ingredient. The mean efficiency was estimated across all ingredients and compared across subgroups defined by variables.

Descriptive statistics were used to compare ingredient-level characteristics by FGE acquisition status (non-holders vs. holders) and by the number of FGE holders (1, 2–4, ≥ 5). For comparisons between FGE holders and non-holders, Pearson’s chi-square test was applied for categorical variables, and Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were below five. For continuous variables, statistical tests were chosen based on normality assessments. Specifically, the number of FGE applicants was non-normally distributed and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas remaining patent years were normally distributed and analyzed using independent-samples t tests. Subgroup comparisons within FGE holders (1, 2–4, ≥ 5) were conducted using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test for the number of FGE applicants, and one-way ANOVA for remaining patent years.

To identify factors influencing the number of FGE applicants, holders, and non-holders, negative binomial regression analysis was performed. The negative binomial model was chosen over Poisson regression due to overdispersion in all three count-based dependent variables, as confirmed by likelihood ratio tests comparing the two models (all p < 0.001). Model I considered the number of FGE applicants as the dependent variable, Model II the number of FGE holders, and Model III the number of FGE non-holders. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and the Pearson χ²/df ratio, with lower AIC and BIC values and Pearson χ²/df values indicating better model fit. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all data management and analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.3).

The distribution of the number of applicants differed significantly between the FGE holders and non-holders groups (p = 0.007), with the FGE holders group showing approximately twice the application volume compared to the non-holders group. Ingredients in the FGE holders group averaged 7.82 applicants (SD = 10.5) and 6.53 holders (SD = 8.95), exceeding the overall means, whereas the FGE non-holders group averaged fewer applicants (3.64, SD = 5.94). In the A/B/C and J/L groups, which had higher numbers of applicants, FGE acquisition rates were relatively lower (A/B/C: 25/36, 69.4%; J/L: 11/21, 52.4%). No significant differences were observed between the FGE holders and non-holders groups in originator-level manufacturer characteristics, patent characteristics, drug characteristics (initial reimbursement price and originator’s approval year), or the year of FGE application for generic drugs.

Among the FGE holders (n = 60), a significant difference was observed according to the originator’s initial reimbursement price (p = 0.013). For high-priced drugs (≥ 100,000 KRW), 80.0% had only one manufacturer obtaining FGE, and no cases involved five or more manufacturers. In contrast, among low-priced drugs (< 1,000 KRW), only 20.0% had a single manufacturer obtaining FGE, whereas 52.0% had five or more manufacturers.

Compared with ingredients with a single patent, those with two (IRR = 2.25, p = 0.003) or three or more patents (IRR = 1.78, p = 0.050) showed a significantly higher number of applicants. Conversely, higher initial reimbursement prices were associated with fewer applicants: prices of 10,000–100,000 KRW (IRR = 0.24, p = 0.001) and ≥ 100,000 KRW (IRR = 0.27, p = 0.010) were associated with significantly fewer applicants compared with prices below 1,000 KRW.

In Model II, the number of FGE holders was significantly associated with the originator’s approval year, the initial reimbursement price, and the year of FGE application. Compared with ingredients approved before 2007, those approved in 2007–2011 (IRR = 3.98, p < 0.05), 2012–2016 (IRR = 7.37, p < 0.01), and 2017–2020 (IRR = 11.51, p < 0.01) had significantly more FGE holders. Higher initial reimbursement prices were again associated with fewer FGE holders; prices of 10,000–100,000 KRW (IRR = 0.22, p = 0.003) and ≥ 100,000 KRW (IRR = 0.16, p = 0.011) were associated with significantly fewer holders than prices below 1,000 KRW. In addition, ingredients with FGE applications in 2021–2024 had significantly fewer FGE holders than those with applications in 2015, the initial year of the system (IRR = 0.22, p = 0.028).

In Model III, the number of FGE non-holders was significantly associated with the number of patents, ATC classification, and the originator’s approval year. Ingredients with two patents had more non-holders than those with a single patent (IRR = 2.46, p = 0.039), and ingredients in the A/B/C group had more non-holders than those in the J/L group (IRR = 4.55, p = 0.012). In contrast, ingredients approved in 2007–2011 had significantly fewer non-holders than those approved before 2007 (IRR = 0.26, p = 0.020).

Discussion

Principal findings

The key findings of this study are as follows. First, substantial variation was observed in the number of FGE applicants and holders across the 85 reimbursed drug ingredients, with a positive correlation between the two measures. Second, both the price and the approval year of the originator drug were significantly associated with the number of FGE applicants and holders. High-priced originator drugs (≥ 10,000 KRW) were associated with decreases in both the number of applicants and holders, whereas more recently approved originator drugs showed increases in both measures. This pattern may be interpreted as reflecting the characteristics of high-priced drugs, which are often associated with greater manufacturing complexity and higher technical barriers, thereby limiting generic entry [23]. Third, the year of FGE application did not have a significant effect on the number of applicants but was negatively associated with the number of holders, such that more recent application years were linked to fewer FGE holders. Fourth, while the number of patents was significantly associated with the number of FGE applicants, no significant association was observed with the number of FGE holders. Ingredients with two or more patents had significantly more applicants, but this was not reflected in successful FGE acquisition.

Substantial heterogeneity in the FGE market

A notable finding of this study is the substantial heterogeneity observed in the FGE market across drug ingredients. The number of FGE applicants and holders showed considerable variation across ingredients, and this pattern was also reflected in FGE application efficiency. While some ingredients exhibited a higher likelihood that patent challenges would translate into successful FGE acquisition, others showed markedly lower efficiency despite active participation by multiple generic manufacturers.

Attention should be given to two cases in which more than ten generic manufacturers participated in FGE applications, yet none successfully obtained FGE. Both cases involved drugs in the A/B/C ATC group, which encompasses major therapeutic areas such as hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, and is typically characterized by large market size and active generic entry. In one case, generic manufacturers challenged a formulation patent but were unsuccessful in a series of legal decisions, and the case is currently under review at the Supreme Court level [24]. In the other case, an additional patent not listed in the official patent register was identified ex post, leading firms to withdraw their FGE applications [25]. The latter case illustrates how the presence of unlisted patents and informational uncertainty can substantially affect the decision-making of generic manufacturers. Furthermore, these cases suggest that patent structures and judicial decisions in patent challenges are critical determinants of FGE acquisition outcomes.

Dual effects of the FGE system on patent challenges and generic entry

This study included the originator’s approval year and the year of the first FGE application by generic manufacturers as key factors influencing the number of FGE applicants and holders. Together, these variables provide important insights into how the FGE system has reshaped the process of generic drug market entry.

Originator drugs serve as the direct targets of patent challenges by generic manufacturers. The finding that both FGE applicants and holders increased for more recently approved originator drugs suggests that patent challenges have shifted from a selective strategy employed by a limited number of firms to a more routinized procedure for generic market entry across the industry [26]. This shift can be understood as a predictable consequence of the patent linkage system, under which patent challenges were institutionalized as a formal requirement for obtaining FGE status [17]. Prior to the introduction of this system, generic drugs could enter the market despite the existence of active patents; however, the patent linkage framework transformed patent challenges into a prerequisite for generic entry. Consistent with this institutional change, Son (2022) reported a substantial increase in the number of patent challenges following the introduction of the FGE system [15].

In contrast, the year of FGE application by generic manufacturers was negatively associated only with the number of FGE holders. While more recent originator approvals were associated with increases in both applicants and holders, analyses based on the timing of generic applications showed that later application years were linked to fewer successful FGE acquisitions. Although this pattern may appear counterintuitive at first glance, it becomes coherent when the distinct nature of each variable is considered. The originator’s approval year reflects the intrinsic characteristics of the target drug, whereas the FGE application year captures the timing and competitive intensity of the actual patent challenge.

The observed decline in the number of FGE holders for more recent application years indicates that, despite the increasing prevalence of patent challenges since the introduction of the system, the probability that an individual manufacturer successfully obtains FGE has decreased [17, 26, 27]. This finding offers an alternative explanation to previous studies that attributed the limited number of FGE acquisitions to a strategic shift from joint patent challenges to individual challenges [27, 28]. Rather than reflecting changes in manufacturers’ strategic preferences, the present results suggest that restrictions on FGE acquisition are more plausibly explained by conditions in which intensified competition increases the difficulty of patent challenges and reduces the likelihood of success [17, 26]. However, alternative interpretations cannot be entirely ruled out, including competitive saturation driven by an increasing number of market participants and potential institutional fatigue in the operation of the system. Nevertheless, given that these factors were not significantly associated with the number of FGE applicants in this study, their explanatory power is likely to be limited.

Patenting and generic market entry

This study is the first to incorporate patent-related variables into an empirical analysis of generic drug market entry in South Korea. While the number of patents was significantly associated with the number of FGE applications, it showed no significant relationship with actual FGE acquisition. These divergent effects can be interpreted through the concepts of patent hierarchies and patent portfolios.

Patent hierarchies refer to the existence of a hierarchy among multiple patents surrounding the same product [29, 30]. Substance patents typically function as core patents for specific ingredients and are difficult to challenge, whereas composition, formulation, and use patents constitute peripheral patents with relatively higher challenge feasibility. Although this study did not distinguish patent types in the analysis, the presence of two or more patents implies the coexistence of at least one peripheral patent alongside the core patent. Such patent structures may incentivize generic manufacturers to initiate patent challenges, thereby increasing the number of FGE applications, a pattern consistent with previous findings from South Korea [15] and the United States [31].

At the same time, a larger number of patents indicates that originator firms have strategically constructed patent portfolios [32]. The patent portfolio effect suggests that peripheral patents can provide additional protective strength beyond the core patent. Using U.S. patent data, Hemphill and Sampat demonstrated that peripheral patents can meaningfully extend market exclusivity by requiring generic entrants to challenge not only the core patent but all associated patents [31]. When such peripheral patents are filed after the core patent, they may effectively prolong the period of market protection [33]. Under conditions of patent thickets, where multiple overlapping patents exist, barriers to generic entry become substantially higher, as potential entrants must navigate numerous patent rights [34].

These patenting dynamics are closely related to evergreening strategies in the pharmaceutical industry [35, 36]. Prior studies have shown that 78% of new patents associated with prescription drugs in the United States were granted for existing products [37], and that in Canada, half of patented drugs were protected by multiple peripheral patents, extending effective market exclusivity by an average of eight years [38]. In this context, the present findings suggest that while a greater number of patents encourages patent challenges and increases FGE applications, extensive patent portfolios simultaneously create obstacles that prevent these challenges from translating into successful FGE acquisition. This highlights a gap between patent-challenge activity and actual generic market entry.

Policy implications for individual countries under a globalized IP regime

The findings of this study provide empirical evidence on how generic drug markets are shaped under patent linkage systems and offer important insights into the policy challenges faced by countries operating within a globalized trade environment characterized by the diffusion of FTAs and IP norms. The substantial variation observed in FGE applications and acquisitions across ingredients and time periods demonstrates that generic market entry is not automatically determined by market forces alone but is strongly shaped by institutional design choices at the national level. This underscores the importance of continuously monitoring how patent linkage and exclusivity mechanisms influence entry behavior and competitive dynamics in generic markets [2, 39, 40].

Policy approaches aimed at strengthening competition should therefore move beyond assessing the mere existence of individual patents and instead consider the overall patent structure surrounding specific drugs [41, 42]. In particular, comprehensive evaluation of patent hierarchies and patent portfolios can serve as an important policy tool for countries seeking to manage generic entry under strong IP protection regimes. Multi-patent filings may hinder access to medicines by extending exclusivity, sustaining high prices, and generating legal uncertainty [41, 43]. In this regard, international efforts to restrict evergreening practices highlight the importance of granting patent protection only to genuine innovations. The Korean case illustrates why strengthening qualitative patent examination and refining criteria for evaluating peripheral patents are critical policy considerations.

Because patent challenges are driven by the balance between expected costs and expected rewards, the design of exclusivity-based incentive schemes must also be reconsidered within national contexts. Enhanced patent scrutiny can reduce uncertainty on the cost side of patent challenges, while incentives on the reward side may be strengthened by expanding early-entry advantages or adjusting effective market size [44]. Given consistent evidence that market size is a key determinant of generic entry, policy options proposed in the literature, such as flexible regulatory protection or differentiated exclusivity periods for small markets, may also be relevant to other countries operating under similar institutional constraints [45, 46]. These considerations may therefore carry broader relevance for jurisdictions that have adopted comparable patent linkage and exclusivity mechanisms.

Finally, the finding that the number of patents has divergent effects on FGE applications and acquisitions raises the need for a more fundamental reassessment of patenting and patent challenges under patent linkage systems. While the proliferation of peripheral patents may stimulate patent-challenge activity, it does not necessarily lead to meaningful generic market entry and may result in the inefficient use of social resources. Within a globalized IP regime, this suggests that policymakers should focus not only on encouraging patent challenges per se, but also on ensuring that such challenges translate into tangible generic entry and effective competition. From this perspective, reassessing whether the FGE system is adequately designed to convert increased patent-challenge activity into meaningful competitive outcomes remains a shared policy task for South Korea and other countries operating similar systems.

In particular, considering the cases identified in this study, further refinements are needed to reduce uncertainty in the patent-challenge process. First, the completeness of patent listings should be improved, particularly by ensuring that all relevant patents are properly disclosed. When additional patents not included in the official patent list are identified ex post, substantial uncertainty may arise in the decision-making of generic manufacturers, potentially leading to the withdrawal of FGE applications. Therefore, mechanisms for providing ex ante information on non-listed patents should be reinforced. Second, the timeliness and consistency of judicial decisions in patent challenges should be improved [47]. Prolonged patent disputes can delay generic market entry and complicate firms’ strategic decision-making. Moreover, inconsistent rulings across similar patent cases may reduce the predictability of patent challenges, thereby increasing uncertainty in the market. Establishing more timely adjudication processes and ensuring consistency in judicial decisions are therefore critical. Third, clearer rules for calculating damages in cases where patent infringement is recognized after generic entry are needed [48]. Uncertainty regarding the scale of damages may increase the risks of patent challenges and discourage generic market entry. Developing transparent and predictable rules for damage assessment would help mitigate these legal risks.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it did not distinguish between different types of patents and instead used the number of patents as a proxy variable. This limitation was primarily due to the lack of systematically classified and publicly available data on patent types, which limited the ability to differentiate patents by their functional roles. As a result, this approach may limit the interpretation of the findings, as it does not capture the distinction between core and peripheral patents. Second, although market size is an important factor influencing patent challenges and generic market entry, it could not be included in the analysis due to the lack of publicly available data at the individual reimbursed drug level. As a result, the findings may not fully account for market-driven incentives affecting patent challenges and generic entry. Third, the analysis was conducted at the ingredient level and did not adequately reflect characteristics of individual generic manufacturers. Finally, as this study was conducted within the specific institutional context of South Korea, caution is needed when generalizing the findings to other countries or regulatory environments.

Conclusions

This study empirically analyzed generic drug market entry patterns using ten years of data since the implementation of the FGE system in South Korea. The introduction of the FGE system, together with the expansion of patent portfolios, was associated with increased patent challenges by generic manufacturers, but did not consistently translate into a greater number of actual generic market entrants. This underscores the need to reexamine whether the FGE system is effectively designed to translate the quantitative increase in patent challenges into actual generic entry and substantive competition. From a policy perspective, it is essential to strengthen patent scrutiny through a comprehensive assessment of patent hierarchies and patent portfolios.
