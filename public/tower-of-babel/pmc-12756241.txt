Automatic crack detection in civil infrastructure based on a hybrid fine tuned MnasNet and adaptive patching
Authors: Kun Li, Feng He, Xiaojun Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetection of cracks is a critical area of automation that would help guarantee the safety and durability of the civil infrastructure at the lowest cost of upkeep. Current image processing and deep learning models are usually weak in real-life scenarios, require excessive resources, or feature manual features extraction. To overcome these shortcomings, this paper presents a new vision-based framework of crack detection that combines the lightweight MnasNet CNN with the Upgraded Lotus Effect Optimization (ULEO) algorithm that automatically adjusts the hyperparameters. The first structural health monitoring use of chaos theory and Levy flight dynamics is ULEO, which is designed to improve convergence and eliminate local optima. Another dynamic patching mechanism used in the framework is that the patch sizes utilize the complexity of the image to adjust dynamically to enhance accuracy without causing a run time increase. The proposed method was tested on the CRACK500 dataset and with various cross-validation configurations, it achieved an accuracy of 97.1, sensitivity of 96.0, specificity of 97.8, and an F1-score of 95.6, outperforming other benchmarks including VGG16, OpenMV-CNN, XGBoost and XAI/t-SNE. Particularly, the proposed model provides this performance during a 0.05 s per image computational time, which makes it more efficient and suitable to use in mobile and embedded platforms. The high accuracy and low computational intensity of the MnasNet/ULEO model make it especially appropriate with real-time and mobile-based crack inspection, which creates a new self-optimized intelligent vision model of defect detection in civil, mechanical, and industrial systems.Keywords: Crack detection, Additive manufacturing, MnasNet, Hyperparameter optimization, Upgraded lotus effect optimization algorithm, Surface defectsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Since its inception, image processing has seen increasing applications, such as in industrial inspection, medical imaging, remote sensing, and in the past few decades, the rapid advancement in digital camera technology has inspired further research in this burgeoning field of computer vision1.

Digitally based imaging to acquire images and then detect and classify cracks has progressed in iterations over the past decade2. Because of these advantages, digital image processing is widely used, but it performs a huge amount of information and automation in the process of evaluating crack characteristics3. Reliable crack detection is critical for safety and reliability of structures, equipment’s, and materials4. Crack detection has traditionally been a manual process, carried out by trained professionals5. This is effective but labor-intensive, time-consuming, and error-prone6.

Automated crack detection methods that use image processing techniques (IPTs) have been created to overcome these obstacles7. An alternative procedure are IPTs that filter images and reduce relevant features for the crack’s extraction and classification8.

Nevertheless, traditional IPTs tend to perform poorly in different practical scenes and against different types of cracks, which greatly reduces their practical effectiveness9. This scenario facilitates the advancement of more efficient and reliable crack detecting systems that can operate under various settings10.

In this decade, deep learning has emerged as a pervasive technology for enhancing image processing capabilities11. Notably, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated exceptional efficacy in tasks including identifying objects in images, image classification, and segmentation using semantics12. Of these CNNs, MnasNet is a state-of-the-art construction that has been shown to optimize the efficiency-accuracy trade-off quite well. Using MnasNet, we can build better systems capable of detecting cracks where images are simple13.

Falaschetti et al.14 established a vision system according to the two precise and lightweight Convolutional Neural Network methods used within a platform which needed low power and cost, called the OpenMV Cam H7 Plus. It was implemented for identifying and managing immediate concrete cracks, that was appropriate for detecting an early caution system’s prototype. The system had to offer a great accuracy level, and not give wrong alarms in order to be beneficial. Also, it had to be stingy in computational assets in a way that it became implemented in low power, transferable systems, which could be organized on the field. Second, the CNN framework’s accuracy and memory use enactment was compared with the smallest network, LeNet, which was trained on two dissimilar image datasets, namely the SDNET2018 dataset, and the Concrete Crack Images for categorizing dataset that were employed on the system OpenMV Cam H7 Plus. The suggested CNN frameworks performed agreeably by means of just one small fraction between 26 to 6% of the memory needed for LeNet. Actually, it offered a good accuracy within all of the assessed datasets with just one marginal growth within implication period.

The automation of crack detection has become a critically important factor for structural health monitoring considering the urgency of maintaining the safety, durability, and cost-effective maintenance of aging civil infrastructure. Traditional image processing techniques (IPTs) have long been in use for defect identification, but their performance has, from the very beginning, been limited by sensitivity to environmental noise, variation in crack morphology, and dependence on hand-crafted features that lack generalization across real-world situations19.

The aforementioned constraints hold particularly strongly in complex scenarios characterized by shadows, surface roughness, and background clutter that are ubiquitous to field deployability of inspection systems, where IPTs are likely to be high in false positive or false negative rates. Although deep learning models like VGG16 and lightweight CNNs (e.g., OpenMV-CNN) have shown promise in improving accuracy, they either suffer from excessive computational burden or lack systematic hyperparameter optimization, which limits their adaptability and deployment on resource-constrained edge devices.

To meet this demand, the new approach in this study provides a vision-based crack detection system that will optimally use MnasNet’s lightweight but effective form to be coupled with a newly improved upgraded lotus effect optimization (ULEO) algorithm, marking the first-ever site of ULEO-and its bio-inspired predecessor LEA-in the structural health inspection domain. Our method uses ULEO to fine-tune important hyperparameters under a chaotic search coupled with Lévy flight dynamics for significantly enhanced convergence speed and global optimality instead of the conventional fixed architecture or the various optimizers it uses.

An adaptive patching is also put forward, which is able to customize patch sizes to complexity levels within the image allowing for accurate detection of fine cracks and minimizing computational redundancy. Tested on the CRACK500 benchmark, our MnasNet/ULEO model outperforms all others in different terms of state-of-the-art performance, and embedded CNNs, showing significant advantages both in accuracy and robustness. This is both a breakthrough for hyperparameter-optimized mobile deployable SHM systems and a step towards using bio-inspired optimization in more civil, mechanical, and manufacturing applications that pertain to vision-based defect detection.

Class imbalance is a typical problem of datasets applied to binary classification problems, like crack detection where one of the classes (e.g., the uncracked one) may vastly outnumber the other one (e.g., the cracked one). This imbalance has the potential to affect the capacities of the model in recognition of cracks in CRACK500 dataset. To counter this, we used two main techniques which are the data augmentation and the weight of classes. The samples with the ‘cracked’ were augmented by data augmentation methods such as random rotations, flips and translations to enhance the probability of being represented in the training set. The strategy not only balanced the classes distribution but it also increased the generalization ability of the model as it was exposed to a greater range of cracks patterns. Also, the training process applied class weighting, in which the minority class (cracked) was given a greater weight to give the model a greater punishment on misclassifying these cases. Particularly, the weights of the classes were calculated as the inverse of the class frequencies such that, a more emphasis is given to underrepresented class by the model. Through combining these measures, we sought to address the issue of class imbalance and enhance the general performance of the crack detection model, especially on the accurate detection and classification of the cracked surfaces.

MnasNet: efficient neural architecture search

MnasNet has been found to be efficacious and lightweight CNN structure that has been developed via structure search29. This aims to strike a balance between quality and modeling size. This makes it suitable for application on mobile devices and contexts with constrained resources.

Neural architecture search

NAS (Neural architecture search) has been found to be an automatic procedure of finding efficacious network structures. Considering the MnasNet, NAS has been utilized for exploring network configurations’ wide solution space. This seeks to identify networks that can offer an equitable compromise between accuracy and computing efficiency.

Solution space strategy

The search space of the network is composed of diverse blocks of building, comprising squeeze-and-excitation operations, depthwise separable convolutions, and convolutional layers30. These blocks have been inserted into hierarchical design, which form the eventual model. the solution space has been developed for including several possible frameworks that allowed for various and effective networks.

Reinforcement learning-based search

MnasNet makes use of an RL (Reinforcement Learning) agent to efficiently traverse the vast solution space. The RL learns to select the most talented components to construct the model by approaching the architectural search as a sequence of decisions. The agent was developed on a proxy dataset, and its effectiveness was evaluated based on a number of measures, such as efficiency and model size.

Differentiable architecture search

MnasNet integrates DARTS (Differentiable Architecture Search)15, framing the architectural search as a continuous optimization problem. DARTS reduces the particular operational options inside the solution space, enabling the application of optimization based on gradients. This Facilitates efficient global exploration of the solution space and aids in recognizing high-performing architectures.

Multi-objective optimization

MnasNet incorporates various goals throughout the architecture search procedure. Besides striving for high accuracy, it also focuses on reducing the size of model and demands of computation. This optimization across multiple objectives is accomplished using Pareto fronts, where structures that present different balances between efficiency and accuracy have been recognized.

Resource-aware modeling

MnasNet employs resource-aware modelling to ensure that the architectures it discovers are suitable for mobile device applications. This procedure considers factors such as inference speed, energy cost, and memory utilization throughout the search phase. By implementing modeling of resource-aware, it becomes possible to recognize structures that excel in performance while also meeting the limitations of mobile applications.

Transfer learning

MnasNet employs transfer learning to enhance the efficacy of the designs it identifies. Pre-trained models such as Efficient Net or MobileNet provide the starting weights for the architectures under investigation. The transmission of knowledge accelerates training and improves overall precision.

Following, we include an in-depth breakdown of our simulation setup, results, and a comparative performance on how our approach fares.

The Upgraded Lotus Effect Optimization (ULEO) algorithm was used in the optimization process to optimize a few important hyperparameters of the MnasNet architecture. The hyperparameters that were optimized were the learning rate, batch size, momentum, dropout rate, and epochs. The learning rate was also searched over the interval [0.0001, 0.1], and a log scale was used, so that finer exploration of smaller values was performed, which tend to be the best on deep learning models. The batch size was tested to be 16, 128, with a trade-off in memory usage and the accuracy of gradient estimation.

Momentum, which was a very essential parameter in convergence of stochastic gradient descent (SGD), was also tuned in the range of 0.5 to 0.99. Between 0.2 and 0.5, the dropout rate that prevents overfitting by shutting down neurons in training was searched. Lastly, the epochs were restricted to the interval of [50, 200], which gave the model time to learn without spending too much time training. These hyperparameters were searched using values that were known to be optimal when used in similar tasks, and the surveys carried out previously. A combination of criteria was used to decide the termination of the ULEO algorithm with the aim of achieving convergence and efficiency in computing.

The optimization process was to be stopped upon any of the following conditions being met: (1) the performance index (a weighted sum of the accuracy and efficiency measures) improved by a factor less than 0.1 percent over 10 consecutive iterations, meaning that the algorithm had probably arrived at a good or near-optimal solution; (2) the maximum number of iterations, which was 100, had been reached thus ensuring that the optimization process was not made excessively time consuming. The underlying intent of selecting these termination criteria was to balance a trade-off between the desirability of the hyperparameter space being fully explored and practical considerations of computation. The use of ULEO under these particular settings for hyperparameters, search ranges, and termination criteria allowed the optimization to effectively explore the complex hyperparameter space and lead to the highly-tuned MnasNet model with the potential of dramatically increasing the accuracy and efficiency of crack detection in civil infrastructure.

In order to allow reproducibility and clarity, the adaptive patching strategy used in this work establishes particular parameters in patch generation such as patch size, stride, and overlap. The patch size was chosen automatically according to the complexity of the image, 32 × 32 pixels in strongly detailed areas, 64 × 64 pixels in medium complexity areas and 128 × 128 pixels in less complex areas to trade-off between accuracy detection and processing efficiency. A variable stride algorithm was used, where stride values were 16, 32 and 64 respectively and a full overlap of 50 percent between patches was maintained. Such overlap guaranteed the easy capture of cracks that cut across several regions without incurring huge computations. The final parameters selected after the long period of experimentation were able to provide the best trade off between accuracy and efficiency and therefore the proposed method is very suitable in terms of mobile and embedded implementation into real time crack detection system.

Why crack classification was selected over segmentation?

Considering the remark on the selection of a crack classification approach as opposed to the crack segmentation approaches, it can be explained why such decision was made, as well as the limitations perceived by them. Although the crack segmentation methods, including SDDNet and STRNet, can offer detailed pixel-level details about the morphology of cracks, our suggested classification-based method has a few unique benefits, which would be highly applicable to the unique needs of automated crack detection in civil infrastructure.

The computational efficiency of a classification-based approach is one of the main reasons why the approach was selected to be used, and in the subject it is essential to have computational efficiency as real-time execution. Although the crack segmentation techniques are more information rich, they are usually comparatively more computationally intensive since they require processing on a pixel by pixel basis, making them difficult to implement on resource limited systems (e.g., mobile platforms or embedded systems) where real-time processing is necessary.

Conversely, MnasNet/ULEO framework which emphasizes on classification is a lightweight and efficient framework that can be implemented on these devices without performing any tradeoffs on the performance of the framework where speed and reliable crack detection is a priority, which makes it very suitable in on-site inspection and real-time monitoring.

Also, in general, the methods of crack classification are less sensitive to the environmental changes, including lighting, shadows and the surface textures, than the methods of segmentation. It is due to the fact that classification is aimed at determining the existence of cracks and not specific locations, which may be more prone to noise and image quality variations. Our approach to the classification proves to be very resilient to various environmental conditions as it is based on the adaptive patching strategy and the ULEO-optimized hyperparameters, so we can be confident that our methodology detects the cracks in difficult environments.

Although it is fair that the classification techniques need an extra procedure in order to measure the cracks identified, it can be managed and simplified. The spatial distribution and density of predictions of cracks can be used to quantify cracks and this can offer important information on how severe and extensive a damage is. The adaptive patching strategy using variable patch sizes and overlapping strides makes it easy to accurately localize cracks, which in turn is required to determine the number of cracks. The use of ULEO further, provides optimality of the model parameters in both detection and quantification activities, and as a result, reduces the constraints encountered in the supplementary quantification process.

Despite being highly developed and efficient techniques of crack segmentation, which offer detailed information at the pixel level, SDDNet and STRNet possess some drawbacks, i.e. high requirements of the computational resources and vulnerability to the changes in the image quality and environment factors. On the contrary, the MnasNet/ULEO framework is a more intuitive solution since it gives a compromise between computational and robustness as well as applicability to real time which offers a dependable and efficient crack detection method that fit well to the practical requirements of infrastructure monitoring. Realizing that it is quite possible that adding segmentation functions might contribute to the system, future studies may investigate the possibility of adding segmentation methods to the MnasNet/ULEO framework to allow the system to offer detection and morphological detail information, and thereby make the system even more useful in structural health monitoring.

To sum up, the process of identifying cracks is best represented by the classification-based strategy due to its computational efficiency, robustness and ability to be applied in the real-time. Although the extra quantification step is a weakness, it can be addressed successfully by using the adaptive patching and ULEO optimization tools which we have utilized in our study and thus MnasNet/ULEO framework is a viable and workable tool in automated crack detection and as an alternate solution to more computationally intensive segmentation schemes.

Discussions

MnasNet/ULEO framework was developed keeping the major trade-offs in mind to provide a balance between efficiency and performance with automated crack detection. There is a major trade-off between convergence rate and accuracy in which ULEO is balanced in convergence rate because it employs chaos theory and Levy flight dynamics, at the expense of global search capability to avoid premature local optima. The balance enables the framework to obtain fast optimization as well as a high degree of accuracy which is critical in real time structural monitoring. There is one more important trade-off that is the issue of computational overhead and performance. Even though ULEO presents computational complexity, this is balanced with implementations, adaptive patching which focuses resources on complex parts and lightweight design of MnasNet, which makes the model mobile and embedded deployable. In general, through careful trade-off management, the MnasNet/ULEO framework provides a strong, precise and efficient solution to application in real-world crack detection.

The MnasNet/ULEO framework was designed with careful consideration of key trade-offs to balance efficiency and performance in automated crack detection. A primary trade-off exists between convergence speed and accuracy, where ULEO’s integration of chaos theory and Lévy flight dynamics accelerates convergence while maintaining global search capability to prevent premature local optima. This balance allows the framework to achieve both rapid optimization and high accuracy, essential for real-time structural monitoring. Another critical trade-off concerns computational overhead versus performance. Although ULEO introduces some computational complexity, this is mitigated through efficient implementation, adaptive patching that concentrates resources on complex regions, and the lightweight design of MnasNet, ensuring the model remains suitable for mobile and embedded deployment. Overall, by effectively managing these trade-offs, the MnasNet/ULEO framework delivers a robust, accurate, and resource-efficient solution for real-world crack detection applications.

The appearance of the algorithm of the Upgraded Lotus Effect Optimization (ULEO) in the sphere of structural health monitoring (SHM) is the important step, as the algorithm has the peculiar feature of overcoming the shortcoming of the conventional optimization methods. The dynamics of chaos theory and ability to explore hyperparameter space through ULEO is more efficient and is essential to fine-tuning complex models such as MnasNet. As opposed to traditional algorithms, ULEO exhibits a higher convergence rate and has a higher capacity to escape local minima, a trap of hyperparameter optimization. This is of special significance in SHM, wherein precision and dependability of the crack is chief.

The chaotic exploration stage of ULEO allows to search more widely among the possible solutions, the mechanism of a Levy flight can guarantee that the search does not occur locally in small areas of search, improving the global search. Moreover, adaptive patching strategy, together with ULEO, can be used to dynamically adjust the patch size depending on the complexity of the image, thus, in a manner that the maximum amount of macro and micro-cracks is detected without reducing processing speed.

Such a set of features makes ULEO a perfect candidate to SHM applications, where the tradeoff between the accuracy of detection, and the efficiency of computation is essential. Utilizing these benefits, the MnasNet/ULEO system provides a new standard of lightweight hyperparameter-optimized crack detection systems in civil infrastructure.

In another side, the concept of adaptive patching comes in with a new concept of crack detection: it adapts patch sizes to the complexity of the image content, an aspect that is absent in the classic patch-based techniques that are commonly used in SHM and computer vision. In the traditional patch-based methods, the picture is split into all-sized patches, which may be inefficient and inaccurate, particularly with cracks that are of different sizes and shapes.

As an example, small patches can not capture the width of large cracks, and large patches can reduce the complexity of small cracks, resulting in possible confusion between them. Contrastingly, our adaptive patching framework uses the complexity measure to determine the content of each region in the image, enabling them to use smaller patches in highly complex regions (e.g. complex crack patterns) and larger patches in low-complex regions (e.g. uniform backgrounds).

This dynamic variation guarantees that the network can be provided with adequate detail to properly identify cracks of any size with a minimum of computational redundancy. Moreover, the adaptive patching method is synthetically combined with the MnasNet/ULEO framework, in which the refined hyperparameters and the unstructured exploration of ULEO contribute to the model learning and generalizing to the diverse patch sizes.

This integration is essential because it enables the model to be very accurate and efficient within the range of various scales and complexities of cracks. Also, a sliding window with a varying stride is used to make sure that no cracks between multiple patches are missed, which additionally contributes to the strength of the detection procedure. Adaptive patching addresses the weaknesses of fixed-size patching and does not only enhance the spatial sensitivity of crack detection, but also balances the detection accuracy and computational cost in a favorable way, thus it is specifically useful in application in mobile and embedded environments.

Conclusions

Traditional image processing techniques (IPTs) to crack detection have been used to serve as an alternative to human-conducted onsite inspections. While helpful, these IPTs struggle with different scenarios seen in the real world and different types of cracks. In this study, we proposed a vision-based method for detecting cracks by applying MnasNet. Through an enhanced version of genome reproduction optimization, the method was capable of fully extracting hyper parameters of MnasNet, in turn avoiding the tedious feature extraction process challenged by classical methods. The proposed MnasNet/ULEO framework significantly advanced the field of automated structural inspection, offering the first implementation of ULEO in civil infrastructure monitoring and establishing a new benchmark for lightweight, hyperparameter-optimized crack detection. By chaos theory and Lévy flight dynamics, ULEO permitted a more effective exploration of the hyperparameter space with faster convergence and better model performance (97.1% accuracy, 97.8% specificity) compared to GA or PSO. Meanwhile, adaptive patching further improved spatial sensitivity, permitting good detection of micro-cracks without compromising inference speed-making the system fit for deployment on mobile or embedded platforms. This work, in addition to its immediate application, provided a basis for a new class of bio-inspired, self-optimizing vision systems which can be extended to the detection of spalling, delamination, corrosion, and other surface defects of various materials. Further studies will investigate real-time deployment on UAVs and robotic inspectors toward the vision of fully autonomous intelligent infrastructure monitoring networks.
