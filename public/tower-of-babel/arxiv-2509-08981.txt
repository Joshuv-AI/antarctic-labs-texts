Abstract
Despite growing policy interest, the determinants of supply chain resilience are still not well understood. We propose a new theory of supply chain formation with compatibility frictions: only compatible inputs can be used in final good production. Intermediate producers choose the degree of specialization of their goods, trading off higher productivity against a lower share of compatible final producers. We model supply chains as complex production processes in which multiple complementary inputs must be sourced for final production to take place. Specialization choices, production complexity, and search frictions jointly determine supply chain resilience. Relative to the efficient allocation, the equilibrium is characterized by over-specialization due to a novel network externality arising from the interplay between frictional markets, endogenous specialization, and complex production. Over-specialization makes supply chains more productive in normal times but less resilient to disruptions than socially desirable. We show how a targeted transaction subsidy can decentralize efficient resilience in supply chains, and examine the implications of setting compatibility standards.



Keywords: Supply chains, specialization, product design, resilience

Introduction

In modern economies, production is organized around long and complex supply chains. Advances in information and transportation technologies enable firms to source specialized inputs from a large number of suppliers. While the productivity gains from supply chains are undisputed, widespread concerns are mounting about the frequency and economic costs of their disruptions. Often disruptions at a single supplier create shortages of key inputs, temporarily halting production altogether. These events have sparked policy interest in the notion of supply chain
resilience
, that is, the speed at which final production recovers following a disruption.
1
1

1

In June 2021, the Biden-Harris Administration instituted the
Supply Chain Disruption Task Force
“to provide a whole-of-government response to address near-term supply chain challenges to the economic recovery” (see
White House, 2021
).

In this paper, we develop a theory of supply chain formation that captures these real-world features through three key elements: (i) strong complementarities across inputs, to allow for production halts following single-supplier disruptions, (ii) compatibility frictions, to reflect difficulties in replacing specialized inputs, and (iii) search frictions, for input procurement to be a time-consuming process. Our economy features an intuitive technological friction: inputs need to be
compatible
with the production process they are used in. Input producers choose the degree of specialization of their goods. Higher specialization increases the input value but reduces the share of compatible final producers. We model supply chains as
complex
production processes in which multiple complementary inputs must be sourced for final production to take place. In this context, supply chain
resilience
is fundamentally shaped by the endogenous specialization of goods, search frictions, and production complexity. Our framework allows us to analyze the sources of inefficiency affecting supply chain resilience and address normative questions related to the optimal organization of supply chains.
The intuitive trade-off that characterizes our economy is that higher input specialization increases the value of production in normal times, but results in a higher cost of disruptions, as replacing specialized inputs takes longer.

We start by characterizing the equilibrium in a static economy where heterogeneous intermediate producers choose the degree of specialization of their goods
(
Kiyotaki and Wright, 1993
;
Menzio, 2023
)
. Ex-ante identical final producers need to source multiple complementary inputs in the market.
The market for intermediate goods displays realistic sourcing frictions: each final producer contacts a finite number of intermediate producers, governed by the degree of search frictions. Within the set of contacted intermediate producers, it selects those compatible with its production process -- if any -- and requests a quote from each of them.
2
2

2

According to a McKinsey report, ”
At most organizations […], it takes about three months to complete a single supplier search, with a sourcing professional logging more than 40 hours of work – and yet able to consider only a few dozen suppliers from a total population of thousands.
”
(
Baptista et al., 2021
)
. We interpret this evidence as indicative of search and compatibility frictions in intermediate markets.
Intermediate producers submit a bid characterized by the surplus (profits) offered to the final producer. The final producer trades with the intermediate firm offering the highest surplus.
Whenever the procurement search is unsuccessful for any of the complementary inputs, the entire production process is halted.

In this static framework, we highlight the key inefficiency arising from endogenous specialization when production is complex. Intermediate producers choose their optimal specialization by trading off the private surplus they obtain upon trading against the probability of trading. Crucially, they fail to internalize
how their specialization choices affect the likelihood that
final production takes place, and thus the unrealized value created by complementary inputs when final production halts.

For example, consider electric vehicle (EV) production. EV manufacturers produce multiple car models, each requiring a specific type of battery – such as lithium, solid-state, or nickel. Despite their critical role, these batteries are typically sourced from external suppliers. Likewise, each car model (e.g., city car, SUV) requires other externally sourced components, such as tires, cameras, and carbon fiber.
Due to compatibility frictions, an EV producer may, in a given period, only locate battery suppliers offering an incompatible type, in which case production halts entirely. This mismatch stems from suppliers’ specialization decisions: nickel batteries, though of lower value, are compatible with many models, while lithium batteries are restricted to specialized, high-value applications. Our theory predicts that battery manufacturers, when choosing their degree of specialization, do not account for the lost value of complementary inputs when car makers cannot source compatible batteries. If EV production halts, suppliers of components such as tires and cameras also fail to generate value.
This example illustrates a
network externality
. We show that this negative externality, arising from firms’ specialization decisions, induces equilibrium
over-specialization
relative to the constrained-efficient allocation.
Intuitively, specialization choices fundamentally interact with the degree of complexity in the production process: if EVs were only made of batteries – that is, the production process were not complex – the network externality would vanish, and the equilibrium would be efficient.

The network externality arises due to the combination of three elements: i) search and compatibility frictions, which prevent final producers from finding necessary inputs with probability one, ii) endogenous specialization decisions, which imply that the equilibrium level of compatibility is the outcome of agents’ choices, and iii) complementarity among inputs, which imply that failure to source all inputs halts the production process. To the best of our knowledge, our model is the first to jointly incorporate these elements.

Next, we study a dynamic version of our model to account for the long-term nature of supplier relationships and study supply chains resilience. Once formed, supplier relationships last until the final producer – or a supplier link – faces a disruption shock, which happens stochastically.
Long-term relationships bring about a further motive for specialization, that is, tailoring the product to the needs of existing customers (
customization
).
The dynamic framework delivers a novel insight. Departing from the existing literature on supply chain fragility, the
weakest link
in our model is not the input with the highest disruption probability. Rather, it is the input for which the joint probability of supplier disruption and failed replacement is highest. Since higher disruption probability reduces the return from specialization, the weakest link may even be an input with relatively low disruption probability but that is very difficult to replace when missing, due to high equilibrium specialization.

From the normative perspective, in addition to the network externality, endogenous specialization choices in a dynamic context introduce a
search externality

(
Pissarides, 2000
)
: intermediate producers do not internalize the effect of their specialization on their aggregate meeting probability, as more specialized goods raise the share of searching final producers.
This external effect introduces a force pushing the equilibrium towards inefficiently low specialization.
However, we show that, if the production process is complex, the network externality dominates the search externality and the equilibrium always displays over-specialization relative to the planner’s allocation.

Importantly, the dynamic framework provides a model-consistent and welfare-relevant notion of supply chain resilience, defined as the probability that final production is restored following a disruption.
This allows us to characterize the determinants of supply chain resilience: the higher the extent of search frictions, average specialization, and complexity, the lower supply chain resilience. It follows that equilibrium over-specialization implies that supply chains are
less resilient than efficient
. We view this result as evidence that policy interest in supply chain resilience is warranted, since the latter is both welfare-relevant and inefficient in our economy.

In our model, both the resilience and average productivity of supply chains jointly determine output and welfare. As a result, endogenous specialization choices shape the equilibrium trade-off between productivity and resilience.
More specialized economies generate higher output during normal times, when supply chains function smoothly. However, when a disruption occurs, higher specialization makes it harder to restore production in a timely fashion. Due to the network externality, the equilibrium allocation tilts excessively toward productivity. Consequently, a social planner would optimally forgo some surplus in normal times in exchange for more resilient supply chains.

We conclude our positive analysis by studying three salient trends in modern supply chains. First, supply chains have become longer and more complex. In our model, whenever search frictions in procurement are sufficiently strong, higher complexity induces rising inefficiencies in the form of over-specialization and under-resilience. Second, the frequency of supply chain disruptions has increased due to factors such as geopolitical tensions, pandemics, or extreme weather events. In our model, the market equilibrium features rising over-specialization as supply chain disruptions become more frequent. Third, the introduction of new search technologies has lowered search frictions in procurement. In our model, the effect of declining search frictions on the efficiency of the market equilibrium is ambiguous. This is because firms may respond to declining search frictions by specializing disproportionately more, thereby reducing the resilience of supply chains. Importantly, our conclusions are robust to allowing firms to hold inventories, source the same good from multiple suppliers, produce part of the inputs in-house, and write contracts in which suppliers’ remuneration is not tied to production.

We conclude by studying the normative implications of our framework. We start by showing that, if a set of firm-specific transaction taxes/subsidies are available, a planner can decentralize the constrained efficient allocation in both our static and dynamic economies. We show that the planner would like to increase the price of all firm-to-firm transactions. This is equivalent to giving more surplus to intermediate producers for them to have
more skin in the (final production) game
, which allows decentralizing the efficient level of specialization.

While this result may sound similar to the
Hosios, 1990
condition, the underlying logic is different. In
Hosios, 1990
efficiency comes from the matching elasticity being equal to the surplus share of sellers. In our model, efficiency is obtained when the surplus share of sellers is equal to their surplus elasticity
(

.
We also show that the optimal subsidy schedule takes a simple form comprising an aggregate component and a targeting element that is decreasing in the intermediate producer’s productivity rank. Given the demanding nature of the optimal tax schedule, we consider a simpler policy instrument: the introduction of a

or minimum interoperability requirement.
4

4

This kind of policy is common in international regulation, as exemplified by the recent EU Directive on USB-C chargers.
We show that standards are welfare-improving and can be a powerful and implementable tool to address over-specialization.

Static Model

In this section, we consider the sourcing problem of a final producer that needs to source its production inputs anew from the market. Focusing on the sourcing problem of a final producer allows us to single out the specific stage at which the specialization of intermediate inputs plays a role in driving the supply chain dynamics. As highlighted in the business literature, the more specialized intermediate inputs are, the more difficult it is for final producers to source a compatible input
(
Miroudot, 2020
;
Sanz, 2023
)
. We are interested in understanding how intermediate producers choose the specialization of their inputs and whether such specialization decisions are efficient. To this end, we propose a static model of sourcing from frictional markets when intermediate producers make endogenous product design decisions.

2.1
Environment

The economy is populated by a representative household, a measure
1
of perfectly competitive final producers and a measure
m
of intermediate producers. Final producers are ex-ante identical, whereas intermediate producers are heterogeneous in their productivity
z
. The market for the consumption good is perfectly competitive, whereas the markets for
intermediate goods are frictional.
The consumption good is the numeraire. The model is static.

Representative household.

The representative household has linear preferences over an aggregate consumption good and convex disutility from labor supply.
Agents choose whether or not to purchase the indivisible consumption goods offered by each final producer
i
(
C_{i}
), with associated quality
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
at price
P_{i}
, and how many hours to work at each wage rate
w
. Formally, they solve the following utility maximization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{C_{i}\in\{0,1\},\ell}\ \mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=C+\psi\log\left(1-\ell\right)

s.t.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P_{i}C_{i}\ di=w\ell+\overline{\Pi},

(1)

\displaystyle C=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{Q}_{i}C_{i}\ di,

where
\overline{\Pi}
represents profit rebates.

Final producers.

Each final producer
i
operates a Leontief production function combining
N>1
complementary and indivisible inputs into one unit of consumption good:

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\{\min\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}=1\}.

(2)

Following
Elliott et al., 2022
, we refer to the number of inputs sourced in the market,
N

of the production process.
5
5

5

As will become apparent, the network structure of our model lends itself both to a ”snake” and a ”spider” interpretation
(

We think of the

Output quality

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}.

(3)

The input value

Notice that higher complexity of the production process increases the quality of the final good. This captures in reduced form the gains from organizing production around supply chains, resulting from complementarities across different inputs.
For example, suppose a car maker needs to source a battery and a tire to assemble an electric SUV. Yet, not all batteries and tires it can find in the market fit its production process in the same way. A lithium-ion battery would be a better fit than a lead-acid battery, while an alkaline battery would not work at all. Similarly, a large tire would fit the production process better than a small tire.
The quality function (
3
) assumes that the

of different inputs are perfect substitutes – being the inputs themselves perfect complements. Keeping with our example, a producer could make a
\$50,000

Each final producer
i
obtains profits:

\displaystyle\pi_{i}=\left(P_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}\right)\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{i}=1\}.

(4)

Profits equal the difference between the price of the consumption good and the price paid for the
N
intermediate inputs sourced. In equilibrium, the price of the consumption good reflects its quality, i.e.,
P_{i}=\mathcal{Q}_{i}

N

6

Our theory assumes that the

At the height of the (Covid) crisis, […] companies simply couldn’t get the critical parts they needed. In 2021 and 2022, semiconductor shortages prevented the production of an estimated 13 million vehicles worldwide.”

(

Intermediate market structure.

Intermediate good markets are characterized by search and compatibility frictions. Search frictions are represented by a finite number
n\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)

n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{E}[n]=\lambda

\lambda

per final producer.
Compatibility frictions are represented by an endogenous distribution of compatibility probability

\bar{\phi}
. Hence,
\bar{\phi}
represents the

Assuming that the variance of

\lambda\bar{\phi}
.
Therefore,
\lambda\bar{\phi}
represents the

per final producer.
It follows that the probability that a final producer finds a compatible input, or

\displaystyle f=1-\exp\{-\lambda\bar{\phi}\}.

(5)

Once the compatible intermediate producers – if there are any – are selected, final producers receive a quote. Intermediate producers submit it as a bid characterized by a surplus

Intermediate producers differ in productivity

Each intermediate good producer can produce all

With this market structure, we can specify the design problem for intermediate inputs. Let

\displaystyle\max_{s,x}V(s,x;z,N)=\Pi(s,x;z,N)-wq(s).

(6)

The intermediate good producer chooses specialization and surplus to maximize the value of the firm, given by the expected operating profits minus the cost of specialization. The key tradeoff they face is that more specialized goods generate more surplus conditional on trading, but a lower likelihood of trading due to the compatibility frictions. Convex labor requirements ensure an interior solution to optimal specialization.

Discussion

We conclude this section with a brief discussion of some important assumptions in our model.

We model the final production function as a Leontief aggregator of indivisible inputs. This is supported by evidence on low elasticity of substitution across production inputs at the firm level
(
Barrot and Sauvagnat, 2016
;
Boehm et al., 2019
)
.
Moreover, we show in Appendix
A.2
that our final production setting may arise from a more general class of models. In particular, our setting is observationally equivalent to models in which the final good is homogeneous and produced using a constant-elasticity-of-substitution (CES) aggregator with complementary inputs and integer constraints.
10
10

10

In that context, sourcing inputs of higher value would raise output quantity for given price – rather than output price for given quantity. We further relax the assumption of a linear quality function in Appendix
B.4
, where we allow for arbitrary substitutability or complementarity in the quality function.
The key insight is that, whenever the production function features
any
form of complementarity across inputs, the inability to source even a single input halts the entire production process.

We model search as random and simultaneous within narrowly defined markets. In other words, final producers direct their search towards firms producing the desired input, but can screen only a random subset of them. This is in line with evidence on supplier replacement
(
Miyauchi, 2024
)
. Unlike matching models of the labor market, trading probabilities are not governed by a reduced-form, market-level matching function but by micro-founded compatibility frictions. This reflects the role of technological compatibility – relative to imperfect information and geographical distance – as key friction in supply chain formation.
The search behavior of final producers mirrors common procurement practices: firms typically request quotes from multiple potential suppliers and select the best quality-adjusted bid. Another widespread arrangement is bargaining over contract terms. In Section
2.4
, we show that our price-posting protocol corresponds to the efficient benchmark of a bargaining framework in which intermediate producers hold all the bargaining power.

Lemma 1

(Surplus Offered)

The optimal surplus offered by an intermediate producer with productivity

\displaystyle x^{\star}(z)=\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}[A\left(s^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z}\right)],

The intuition behind Lemma (

z

contacted by a final producer. Hence, an intermediate producer enjoys surplus- (or price-)setting power only in the states of the world when it has the highest productivity among the set of compatible intermediate producers contacted by a final producer.
Since an intermediate producer has the power to set the surplus offered to a final producer unilaterally, the optimal choice is to make the final producer exactly indifferent between accepting and rejecting the surplus offer.
12
12

12

This is the core idea behind the well-known

(
Diamond, 1971

. Still, in our model, final producers are able to extract positive surplus in equilibrium due to their ability to locate multiple intermediate producers simultaneously

Burdett and Judd, 1983

The final producer’s outside option in case of no trade with an intermediate producer of productivity

\tilde{z}<z

Accordingly, intermediate producers with the lowest productivity

The optimal specialization function

is implicitly defined by Equation (
8

\phi^{\prime}(s)<0
). Third, higher specialization increases specialization costs, by raising the labor requirement for specialization (
q^{\prime}(s)>0
). Since higher complexity reduces the trading probability
\mathcal{P}(z;N)

. If

\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(z;N)

\lambda

In other words, if an intermediate producer were forced to offer a higher surplus to final producers (

), and vice versa.

13

13

Intuitively, offering a higher surplus

Since

The equilibrium wage rate equalizing labor supply and labor requirement for specialization is given by

\displaystyle Y=\underbrace{f^{N}}_{\text{\vphantom{\Big|} prob. active}}\ \underbrace{N\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A\left(s^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z}\right)],}_{\hskip 8.19447pt\text{expected match surplus \Big| active}}

(10)

where

The expression for aggregate output in (

On the other hand, the probability that all the complementary inputs are sourced decreases. A similar argument goes through for the endogenous specialization decisions. If firms specialize more, the expected surplus per active match increases. Yet, the probability that a final producer is active decreases, thus reducing the measure of active final producers. Previewing our findings from the dynamic model in Section

2.3
Efficiency

We study the efficiency properties of equilibrium specialization decisions by comparing the equilibrium allocation with that dictated by a benevolent social planner. The social planner is subject to the same product market frictions as market participants and solves
14
14

14

Notice that the problem setup implicitly assumes that the social planner makes final producers trade with the compatible input provider contacted with the highest productivity, which can be easily proven.

\displaystyle\max_{s_{j}(z)}\ \mathcal{W}=\ C+\psi\log(1-\ell)\qquad\text{s.t.}\

\displaystyle\ \ell=m\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int q(s_{n}(z))d\Gamma(z),

\displaystyle\ C=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{v\neq n}f(\bm{s}_{v}(z))\ \mathbb{E}_{\max\{z\}}[A(s_{n}(z);z)].

In words, the social planner chooses the specialization
s_{j}(z)
of each intermediate producer with productivity
z
producing input
j
to maximize the utility of the representative household. The maximization problem is subject to the labor resource constraint and the output resource constraint.
Unlike equilibrium specialization decisions, the social planner internalizes that higher specialization of an intermediate input reduces the expected surplus from other complementary inputs, as final producers are less likely to be active.

Since all input markets are symmetric, the first-order condition for the efficient specialization function
\mathcal{S}(z)
is given by:

\displaystyle\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(z;N)\bigg[A^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))}{\phi(\mathcal{S}(z))}\bigg(A(\mathcal{S}(z);z)-\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]

(11)

\displaystyle+(N-1)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]\bigg)\bigg]=\frac{\psi}{1-Nm\bar{q}}\ q^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z)).

The social marginal benefit of specialization on the left-hand side of (
11
) consists of four components, which can be grouped into direct and external effects.

Direct effects

(i) The first term,
\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(z;N)A^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))>0
, captures the increase in an intermediate producer’s own surplus (conditional on matching) when specialization is marginally increased, holding the trading probability fixed.
(ii) The second term,
\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(z;N)\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))/\phi(\mathcal{S}(z))A(\mathcal{S}(z);z)<0
, reflects the corresponding reduction in expected surplus from a lower trading probability, holding constant both the conditional surplus and the weighted productivity distribution,
\hat{\phi}(z,\bar{z})
.
Together, these two terms represent the direct effects of specialization on the expected surplus from trade with a final producer. Accordingly, the same forces appear in the condition for privately optimal specialization (
8
).

External effects

(iii) The third term,
-\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(z;N)\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))/\phi(\mathcal{S}(z))\mathbb{E}_{\,{\max}{\left\{\tilde{z}\right\}}|\tilde{z}\leq z}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]>0
, captures the positive spillover on the expected match surplus of other intermediate producers supplying the same input, holding constant both the conditional surplus and the compatibility probability. Intuitively, by increasing specialization, a more productive firm raises the trading probability of less productive suppliers of that input.
(iv) By contrast, the fourth term,
\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(z;N)(N-1)\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))/\phi(\mathcal{S}(z))\,{\exp}{\left(-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)\right)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]<0
, captures the negative spillover on the expected match surplus of intermediate producers of complementary inputs. Here, greater specialization reduces their trading probability, because final producers are less likely to find all the inputs they need.
Taken together, terms (iii) and (iv) represent the external effects of specialization on other intermediate producers. Unlike the direct effects, these externalities are not internalized in the privately optimal specialization condition (
8
).

The social marginal cost on the right-hand side of (
11
) is given by the marginal increase in hours worked induced by higher specialization, weighted by the marginal disutility of labor supply. The social marginal cost coincides with the private marginal cost.

We are interested in the efficiency properties of equilibrium specialization.
Comparing (
8
) with (
11
) allows us to establish our first main result.

Proposition 1

(Efficiency)
.

The equilibrium allocation is constrained efficient if and only if production is not complex, i.e.
N=1
. If the production process is complex, i.e.
N>1
, the equilibrium features over-specialization.

We discuss the intuition behind Proposition
1
by highlighting the externalities at play in the equilibrium allocation. To do so, we focus on the marginal welfare effect of specialization. By definition, efficient specialization equalizes the marginal welfare effect of specialization to zero, i.e.,
\frac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial s(z)}\big|_{s(z)=\mathcal{S}(z)}=0
.
Hence, evaluating the marginal welfare effect of specialization at the equilibrium solution allows us to single out the sources and direction of the inefficiency induced by equilibrium specialization. Formally,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial s(z)}\bigg|_{s(z)=s^{\star}(z)}\hskip-14.22636pt\propto\quad\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}\left[A(s^{\star}(\tilde{z}),\tilde{z})\right]}_{\text{business-stealing externality}}\underbrace{-\ x^{\star}(z)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{appropriability}\\
\text{externality}\end{subarray}}\underbrace{-\ (N-1)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s^{\star}(\tilde{z}),\tilde{z})]}_{\text{network externality}}.

(12)

Equilibrium specialization induces three externalities.
First, intermediate producers do not internalize that decreasing their specialization reduces the trading probability of all firms with lower productivity producing the same input (
business-stealing externality
). Second, intermediate producers do not internalize the total foregone surplus from lower trading probability, but only the part they are able to appropriate (
appropriability externality
).
Lastly, intermediate producers do not internalize that raising their own specialization reduces the trading probability of all intermediate producers of complementary inputs. More specialized inputs decrease the likelihood that the final producer finds a compatible good, which in turn reduces the probability that final production takes place and the complementary input providers are remunerated (
network externality
).

Using our running example of the EV supply chain, the decision of some battery-makers to specialize in lithium batteries of given voltage induces a business-stealing externality on the market size of battery-makers with lower productivity; an appropriability externality on the expected value of the battery sourced by EV manufacturers; and a network externality on the trading probability of tires and cameras producers.

To evaluate the efficiency properties of equilibrium specialization, we proceed in two steps. First, we consider the case where production is not complex,
i.e.
,
N=1
. Since production requires only one input, the network externality vanishes and the economy is efficient if and only if the surplus offered to final producers by an intermediate producer,
x^{\star}(z)
, is equal to the external effect it exerts on lower-productivity firms, that is if the appropriability externality exactly offsets the business-stealing externality.
From Lemma
1
, we know that the surplus offered to final producers in equilibrium equals the expected outside option of final producers when trading with the intermediate producer. The latter coincides with the expected social surplus of the best firm with lower productivity producing the same input. Hence, when production is not complex, intermediate producers offer a surplus that exactly offsets their external effect on firms with lower productivity. Therefore, under our price/surplus-posting mechanism, equilibrium specialization is efficient.
15
15

15

Readers familiar with CES monopolistic competition models will have noted the same coincidence of offsetting externalities. We discuss this parallel in detail in Section
2.4
.

When production is complex (
N>1
), the business-stealing and appropriability externalities still cancel due to the surplus-(price-)posting mechanism. However, the network externality does not vanish, and the marginal welfare effect of specialization at the equilibrium solution is negative. Hence, the planner would impose a lower level of specialization and, therefore, the equilibrium features
over-specialization
. Importantly, the network externality represents a
pure
externality, as it arises only in no-trade scenarios. In other words, higher specialization of intermediate producers of input
j
harms the intermediate producers of other inputs
i\neq j
whenever the final producers they are in contact with are unable to source input
j
.
As a consequence, there is no price mechanism that allows intermediate producers of input
j
to internalize this negative externality, simply because no trade occurs.

Network externality.

The network externality arises from the interplay of three elements. First, search and compatibility frictions imply that final producers may not find an input they are looking for (
f<1
). Second, endogenous product design decisions imply that the input finding probability is lower, the higher the specialization of an input (
f^{\prime}(s)<0
). Third, complementarity among production inputs implies that a final producer’s failure to source one input prevents the surplus from successful matches with other intermediate producers to materialize (
N>1
). We clarify the economic forces behind the network externality in the following 2-firm example.

Assume two input providers produce the only two complementary inputs needed for final production (
N=2
). Suppose further that final producers only meet one intermediate producer per input, which implies that the latter does not have to compete with anyone else and can extract the whole surplus.
16
16

16

Since in this example each firm is the only producer of its good, there is no business-stealing externality on other firms providing the same input with lower productivity. As a consequence, the fact that the intermediate producer offers
x=0
surplus is not an inefficiency per se.
Denote as
f(s_{i})
the probability that the input provider
i
is compatible with the final producer when its specialization is
s_{i}
. Therefore, the probability that a final producer source both inputs is
f(s_{1})f(s_{2})
. Higher specialization raises match surplus
y(s_{i})

(y^{\prime}>0)
, but reduces the compatibility probability (
f^{\prime}<0
).
Social surplus reads
\mathcal{W}=f(s_{1})f(s_{2})[y(s_{1})+y(s_{2})]
. Efficient specialization of firm
1
solves:

\underbrace{f(\mathcal{S}_{1})y^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}_{1})}_{\text{social MB}}=\underbrace{-f^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}_{1})[y(\mathcal{S}_{1})+y(\mathcal{S}_{2})]}_{\text{social MC}}.

The left-hand side represents the additional surplus generated by the higher specialization, while the right-hand side equals the expected foregone social surplus in case of no trade.

Expected private surplus of firm
1
reads
\pi_{1}=f(s_{1})f(s_{2})y(s_{1})
. Equilibrium specialization solves:

\underbrace{f(s^{\star}_{1})y^{\prime}(s^{\star}_{1})}_{\text{private MB}}=\underbrace{-f^{\prime}(s^{\star}_{1})y(s^{\star}_{1})}_{\text{private MC}}.

Evaluating the efficiency condition at the equilibrium specialization, we note that we are left with the term
f^{\prime}(s^{\star}_{1})y(s^{\star}_{2})<0
, which captures the negative externality of firm 1 on the trading probability of firm 2. It follows that the social marginal cost is higher than the private one, and firm 1 over-specializes. By symmetry, the same holds for firm
2
, as well.

2.4
Discussion

Before extending the model to dynamics, it is worth noticing how our static framework relates to existing models.

Monopolistic competition with CES production function

A common framework when studying firm-to-firm relations is that of
Dixit and Stiglitz, 1977
, as exemplified by
Grossman et al., 2023
;
Grossman et al., 2024
.
In this class of models, firm entry gives rise to abusiness-stealing externality and an appropriability externality. Still, the equilibrium allocation with free entry (and inelastic labor supply) is constrained efficient. The reason is that the two externalities exactly offset each other, as a consequence of the elasticity of substitution -- which governs the business-stealing externality -- and the taste for varieties -- which governs the appropriability externality -- being the same parameter. In models where the two do not coincide, the economy is generally inefficient.
17
17

17

See
Dixit and Stiglitz, 1975
;
Spence, 1976
;
Dixit and Stiglitz, 1977
;
Ethier, 1982
;
Mankiw and Whinston, 1986
;
Benassy, 1996
;
Zhelobodko et al., 2012
;
Parenti et al., 2017
;
Dhingra and Morrow, 2019
;
Bajo et al., 2025
for several treatments of this problem, and
Brakman and Heijdra, 2001
for a review.

In our model, endogenous product design decisions are akin to firm entry decisions. The average input specialization determines the mass of compatible varieties available to each final producer. Our price-posting protocol implies that equilibrium specialization endogenously generates the same offsetting externalities as in
Dixit and Stiglitz, 1977
. The key difference is that, in our model, this outcome arises from the optimal choices of input providers, who make final producers indifferent between trading with them and relying on their expected outside option – without hinging upon any parametric restriction.

Remark 1

(Monopolistic Competition & CES)
.

The combination of endogenous specialization and price posting in frictional markets with simultaneous search provides an endogenous mechanism such that the appropriability and business-stealing externalities from designing new product varieties exactly offset each other. Models of monopolistic competition with CES final production function obtain the same result by imposing the exogenous parametric restriction that the elasticity of substitution across varieties in the final production function equals the elasticity of firm-level inverse demand.

Bargaining

Our baseline model of specialization posits that intermediate producers post the price of their goods (or, interchangeably, the surplus offered) before meeting. We now show how our baseline model would change if intermediate and final producers bargained over the surplus.

Let the structure of our model be the same up to the surplus-sharing rule.
Assume that the surplus accruing to final producers is determined by generalized Nash bargaining upon meeting between a final producer and a compatible intermediate producer. Final producers can observe the total surplus
v
from matching with any intermediate producer they meet. Let
\xi\in[0,1]
denotes the exogenous bargaining power weight of final producers. The surplus accruing to final producers in equilibrium solves:

\displaystyle x^{\star}(v)=\argmax_{x}\left(x-\omega(v;\vec{v}_{k})\right)^{\xi}\left(v-x\right)^{1-\xi},

where
\omega(v;\vec{v}_{k})
denotes the outside option of a final producer which is contact with
k
compatible intermediate producers whose total surplus is lower than
v
. While negotiating with the highest-surplus compatible intermediate producer, final producers keep the option of bargaining with the other less productive compatible intermediate producers they are in contact with. Formally,
\omega(v;\vec{v}_{k})=\max\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
. In Appendix
A.5
we show that the optimal specialization function
s_{B}^{\star}(z)
with bargaining over surplus shares is implicitly defined by:

\displaystyle\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(z;N)(1-\xi)\bigg[A^{\prime}(s_{B}^{\star}(z))+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(s^{\star}_{B}(z))}{\phi(s^{\star}_{B}(z))}\left(A(s_{B}^{\star}(z);z)-\mathbb{E}[\omega(z)]\right)\bigg]=wq^{\prime}(s_{B}^{\star}(z)),

(13)

where
\mathbb{E}[\omega(z)]
is the expected outside option of a final producer trading with the intermediate producer, which equals the surplus offered to final producers in our baseline model.

The equilibrium specialization function
s^{\star}_{B}(z)
is generally inefficient due to the network externality
and
a well known hold-up externality
(
Tirole, 1999
)
.
The hold-up problem is related to the appropriability externality in our baseline model. However, there are important differences between the two. Hold-up arises when intermediate and final producers split ex-post the surplus generated by the investment in specialization. Increasing investment in specialization marginally generates
A^{\prime}
units of additional social surplus, which is split according to
\{\xi,\,1-\xi\}
shares. Hence, part of the
marginal
surplus generated by the intermediate producer’s investment goes to the final producer. Because the social and private marginal returns on investment are different, the investment level is socially inefficient.
Differently, in our model with price posting, the intermediate producer commits to a
level

x
of surplus offered to the final producer. Hence, any additional surplus generated by increasing investment in specialization marginally,
A^{\prime}
, only accrues to the intermediate producer itself. Since marginal social and private surpluses coincide, the investment level is bilaterally efficient.
As is well known, in the limit case of the bargaining model where the seller has all the bargaining power, there is no hold-up problem. This is because social and private surplus coincide – not just at the margin.
In this sense, our model allows for bilaterally efficient investment even in a setting in which both parties in the transaction obtain a positive surplus. This result is driven by the competitive pressure exerted by the simultaneous search, which implies that buyers have a non-zero outside option.

In the bargaining setting, for specialization investment to be efficient, we would need equation (
13
) to hold for all
z
. Clearly, this cannot be the case with a single bargaining weight
\xi
. If we allow bargaining weights to depend on the productivity of the intermediate producer involved in the transaction, there exists a unique schedule of bargaining weights that implements the efficient allocation,
\xi^{\star}(z)
. If intermediate producers do not hold all the bargaining power, the hold-up externality pushes equilibrium specialization to be inefficiently low. The bargaining weight schedule
\xi^{\star}(z)
makes the hold-up and network externalities exactly offset each other. Importantly, since the hold-up effect does not vanish when production is not complex, the economy is always inefficient whenever
\xi(z)\neq\xi^{\star}(z)
for some
z
.

Comparing Equations (
13
) and (
8
) allows us to make the following remark.

Remark 2

(General Bargaining Model)
.

The equilibrium allocation of our baseline model is the same as that of a general bargaining model where intermediate producers hold all
bargaining power, i.e.,
\xi=0
. The allocation of the bargaining framework is generally inefficient, and there exists a unique vector
\xi^{\star}(z)
such that the market and planner solutions coincide.

Overall, when production is not complex, our baseline model can be interpreted as the efficient benchmark of a general bargaining model.

Non-contingent contracts

Our model considers a particular set of contingent contracts: transactions between intermediate and final producers that successfully match do not happen if the latter are unable to source all the complementary inputs needed for production. This kind of contract implies that final producers bear no risk of having to pay suppliers when unable to produce. We now study what would change with an alternative, non-contingent arrangement whereby suppliers are remunerated independently of the successful sourcing of all inputs.
18
18

18

Chor and Ma, 2025
show that contracting frictions produce large welfare losses in global sourcing.
In such a setting, final producers bear all the sourcing risk. In equilibrium, final producers that cannot successfully source all inputs incur losses, which are borne by the representative household in terms of lower profit rebates.

For intermediate producers, this means that the trading probability is independent of the complexity of final production, i.e.,

In addition to the network externality, two additional forces induce over-specialization. First, contracting frictions imply that the private marginal benefit of specialization exceeds the social marginal benefit. Second, the appropriability externality exceeds the business-stealing externality.
As intermediate producers are remunerated independently of whether sourcing is successful or not, they have stronger incentives to over-specialize when contracts are not contingent. We summarize our findings in the next remark.

Remark 3

(Non-contingent contracts)
.

Economies with non-contingent contracts feature more equilibrium over-specialization than economies with contingent contracts.

Boundaries of the firm, vertical integration, and in-house production

At the heart of our model, there are frictions in sourcing inputs outside the boundaries of the firm. These frictions are exacerbated by the complexity of production
N
. On the one hand, more complex production generates more value; on the other hand, it is less likely that all inputs are successfully sourced. There are several different ways in which we could consider the problem of choosing the production recipe. We could allow firms to choose the recipe
N
or, given a production process, we could alter the boundaries of the firm. In Appendix

Suppose that final producers can vertically merge with

Remark 4

(Vertical integration)
.

Vertical integration reduces the inefficiencies associated with endogenous specialization by lowering the number of key inputs subject to search frictions. In the limit in which

, the economy is constrained efficient.

Hence, our model suggests that vertical integration is socially desirable when the cost of merger is lower than the social benefit from mitigating the network externality.

Suppose, instead, that final producers have the option to produce any intermediate good of value
\underline{A}

Importantly, since final producers do not appropriate the entire social surplus from production, they choose to use in-house production less frequently than the planner would. Hence, the network externality persists much like in the case of non-contingent contracts, where final producers bear part of the risk. If in-house production were costless on net (

Remark 5

(In-house production)
.

The option to produce any intermediate good in-house does not eliminate the network externality and brings about a further inefficiency in the frequency of in-house production.
In the limit in which
F=\underline{A}

3
Dynamic Model

In the previous section, we have studied how endogenous specialization of intermediate inputs affects the sourcing capacity of final producers. Our finding is that the equilibrium allocation features over-specialized inputs as intermediate producers do not internalize the cascading effect of halting final production on the value added produced by complementary inputs.
In this section, we show that the resilience of supply chains is closely related to the dynamic sourcing capacity of final producers after disruption shocks.
To this end, we extend the static model to a dynamic framework with long-term relationships between intermediate and final producers and stochastic disruptions of final producers or supplier links. This framework allows for a model-consistent and welfare-relevant notion of supply chain resilience,
that is, the probability that final production is restored following a disruption.

\displaystyle Y=\underbrace{\vphantom{\frac{1}{\delta}}\mu(f;N)}_{\text{market size}}\ \ \underbrace{\vphantom{\frac{1}{\delta}}\mathcal{R}(f;N)}_{\text{resilience}}\ \ \underbrace{\frac{1}{\delta}}_{\text{robustness}}\ \ \underbrace{\vphantom{\frac{1}{\delta}}N\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s^{\star}(z);z)]}_{\text{expected match surplus| active}}.

Resilience.

The dynamic model allows for a model-consistent and welfare-relevant notion of supply chain resilience, namely, the probability that a final producer is able to source all the complementary inputs it needs after a disruption. Given this definition, it is apparent how the inverse of supply chain resilience equals the average time it takes for a final producer to restore production following a disruption.
Our model allows us to single out three determinants of supply chain resilience:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f;N)\equiv f^{N}=[1-\exp\{-\lambda\bar{\phi}\}]^{N}.

(20)

First, higher search efficiency (

Second, higher resilience can be attained by relying on less specialized inputs (

Customization.

Our dynamic model allows intermediate producers to re-optimize the specialization of their products in every period. This introduces a new
customization motive
for specialization, that is, tailoring the product to the needs of the final producers
within a match

The main prediction of our dynamic model is that intermediate producers gradually increase the specialization and narrow the scope of their products during their life cycle as they progressively accumulate customers. The intuition is easily understood through the first-order condition for specialization out of steady state:

\displaystyle\overbrace{(1-\delta)\mathcal{D}_{(-1)}}^{\text{existing customers}}A^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))+\overbrace{\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(z)}^{\text{new customers}}\left[A^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))}{\phi(s^{\star}(z))}\bigg([A(s^{\star}(z);z)-x(z)]+\beta(1-\delta)\right.

\displaystyle\left.[A(s^{\star}_{(+1)}(z);z)-x(z)]\bigg)\right]=wq^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z)).

(21)

Optimal specialization features both a backward- and a forward-looking component. The marginal benefit of specialization on the left-hand side can be divided into two components. First, for the
(1-\delta)\mathcal{D}_{(-1)}

Since intermediate producers have already overcome compatibility frictions with more and more customers, they opt to increase match surplus with existing customers rather than attract new ones. Hence, for the share of attached customers, specialization corresponds to customization. It follows that both the value and the price of the inputs traded between firms increase over time.

We follow the same steps used in the static setting to derive the marginal welfare effect of equilibrium specialization. We decompose the marginal welfare effect into four terms. The first three terms represent the externalities discussed in the static model: the business-stealing, appropriability, and network externalities. Comparative statics.

Over the last decades, supply chains have become more complex

We study each of these trends through the lens of our model.

Under mild parametric restrictions, Proposition

Proposition 3

Similarly, Proposition

Proposition 4

(Efficiency and Robustness)
.

As the frequency of disruption increases, the equilibrium allocation displays more over-specialization and under-resilience.

Finally, Proposition

Proposition 5

If the elasticity of the average compatibility probability to search efficiency exceeds 1 in equilibrium, the equilibrium features more over-specialization and under-resilience as search frictions decline.

Taken together, Propositions

Through the lens of our model, we conclude that supply chains are less efficient today than they used to be, as they feature ever more over-specialization and under-resilience due to more complex production and more frequent disruptions. Proposition

If firms react to the technology improvement by increasing specialization more than proportionally, the net effect is that compatibility frictions are exacerbated, and the economy’s over-specialization and under-resilience worsen.

3.4

Efficiency.

We study the efficiency of this economy by comparing the market allocation to that of the social planner. The planner maximizes the utility subject to i) the laws of motion of demand for each input supplier and ii) of the mass of searching firms; iii) balancing of flows; and iv) the resource constraint. We relegate the formal problem to Appendix
A.8
.

Comparing efficient and equilibrium specialization, we observe that the model with link destruction features the same sources of externality as the baseline dynamic model. However, the search externality no longer has a clear sign. In this setting, there is an additional component capturing the marginal effect of specialization on welfare via the changing composition of the pool of searching final producers.
Since higher specialization increases the share of inactive firms, this additional component pushes in the opposite direction with respect to the effect going through market tightness.

We summarize the efficiency properties of this economy in the next Proposition.

Proposition 6

(Efficiency of the Dynamic Economy with Link Destruction)
.

The allocation is efficient if and only if
N=\frac{1}{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(N)}
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(N)\equiv 1-f^{N}\frac{\beta(1-\delta)}{1+\beta(1-\delta)}\frac{\chi_{2}(f;N)}{\chi_{1}(f;N)}
. If
N>\frac{1}{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(N)}
, then the economy with link destruction features over-specialization and under-resilience.

In Appendix
A.8

and

and show that
\frac{1}{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(N)}\in[1,2)
.
Hence, if the production process is complex, i.e.,

We have established that the specialization choice of input suppliers induces a net negative externality on other agents even in an economy where the key source of risk is the potential disruption of a single link.
We conclude our positive analysis by making three observations. First, we consider risk-mitigation strategies that the final producers in our model could adopt. Then, we revisit the concept of
weak link
. Finally, we highlight that the fundamental wedge between market and planner allocations is in fact trading off output in good times with the economic cost of disruptions.

3.4.1
Strategies to mitigate the impact of supplier breakdowns.

In our model, final producers occasionally halt production when they fail to source one or more key inputs. Because intermediate producers do not account for the foregone surplus from complementary inputs when their own input is missing, they tend to design overly specialized products. As a result, production halts occur more frequently than socially desirable. However, if final producers could continue operating despite disruptions in some supplier relationships, the network externality would not arise. We now examine two key strategies that final producers can adopt to mitigate the impact of supplier breakdowns: maintaining inventories and sourcing inputs from multiple intermediate producers (multi-sourcing). See Appendix
A.9
for a formal analysis.

Inventories.

Suppose that active final producers can choose to purchase more units of each input than needed for current production, setting aside inventories as a buffer.
26
26

26

The role of inventories in supply chains has been the subject of a recently active literature. See, for example,
Alessandria et al., 2023
;
Ferrari, 2022
;
Carreras-Valle, 2023
;
Carreras-Valle and Ferrari, 2025
.
If a supplier relationship breaks down, these inventories allow production to continue until a new supplier is found or until their stock is depleted. However, due to search frictions, there is always a positive probability that a final producer is unable to locate a compatible intermediate producer for any number of periods. It follows that no finite level of inventories can completely hedge final producers from disruptions.

Multi-sourcing.

Suppose final producers can establish links with multiple compatible suppliers met during a supplier search. For example, a final producer might maintain an idle backup link with the second-best compatible supplier at a fixed per-period cost. If the link with the best supplier breaks down first, the final producer can immediately switch to the backup supplier and continue production
(
Elliott et al., 2022
;
Grossman et al., 2024
, see, for example,)
. However, due to search frictions, the probability of finding multiple compatible intermediate producers is less than one. As a result, not all final producers are able to multi-source all inputs. It follows that multi-sourcing alone cannot completely hedge final producers from disruptions.

Remark 6

(Inventories & Multi-sourcing)
.

The ability of final producers to hold inventories or to source the same input from multiple suppliers can alleviate – but not eliminate – the network externality.

3.4.2
Weakest Links and Supply Chain Fragility

Much of the policy and academic discussion related to supply chain fragility has focused on the concept of the
weakest link

(
Kremer, 1993
;
Levine, 2012
, see, for example,)
. Due to the complementarities among production inputs, a supply chain is indeed only as fragile as its weakest link. Existing models of supply chains without compatibility frictions and endogenous specialization identify the weakest link,
\hat{j}
, as the supplier with the highest disruption probability:

\hat{j}^{\text{exog}}=\arg\max_{j}\delta_{j}.

For instance, if two final producers source inputs from suppliers located in an area prone to natural disasters or subject to geopolitical tensions, their supply chains would be deemed equally fragile. In contrast, our model identifies the weakest link as the input that is most likely to be missing. This is a combination of the exogenous disruption probability
\delta_{j}
and the
endogenous
compatibility probability
\bar{\phi}_{j}
:

\hat{j}^{\text{endog}}=\arg\max_{j}\delta_{j}\left[1-f(\bar{\phi}_{j})\right].

In other words, the weakest link is the input for which the probability that its current supplier is disrupted
and
no replacement is found is highest.

Accordingly, even two supply chains sourcing inputs from suppliers in the same “risky” area may differ significantly in fragility, depending on how difficult it is to replace those suppliers if needed. Supply chain fragility depends on the joint distribution of supplier disruption probabilities and input finding probabilities, reflecting endogenous specialization decisions.
Since the optimal specialization level increases as the disruption probability lowers, sectors with frequent disruptions may not be the
weakest link
as firms endogenously choose to specialize less.

Remark 7

(Weakest Link)
.

Suppose that the disruption probability
\delta_{j}
is heterogeneous across input markets
j
, while all other parameters are symmetric. The input with the highest disruption probability is not necessarily the weakest link of the supply chain: it can be that
\delta_{j}\left[1-f(\bar{\phi}_{j})\right]>\delta_{k}\left[1-f(\bar{\phi}_{k})\right]
while
\delta_{j}<\delta_{k}
.

5
Relation to Empirical Evidence

We conclude by highlighting how our model can rationalize existing empirical evidence.
First, our modeling of supply chains as complex final production processes involving multiple complementary inputs is consistent with the shock-propagation patterns observed after the 2011 Tohoku Earthquake, as documented by
Boehm et al., 2019
and
Carvalho et al., 2021
. In particular,
Boehm et al., 2019
find that “
the strong complementarity across material inputs implied that non-Japanese imported input use also fell nearly proportionately, thereby propagating the shock to other upstream firms
.” In our dynamic model with link destruction, a shock that disrupts a supplier link reduces, on average, the sales of other suppliers to the same final producer, because the latter is sometimes unable to replace the missing input. This empirical evidence is therefore consistent with our horizontal
network externality
.
Similarly, using U.S. Compustat data,
Barrot and Sauvagnat, 2016
show that shocks to suppliers propagate to firms sharing common customers with the disrupted firms only when the latter produce relationship-specific inputs that are not easily substitutable. In the same vein,
Sanz, 2023
documents that American firms with global supply chains reduce the number of shipments of “specialized" (i.e., with a few potential suppliers worldwide) inputs by
73\%
in the month following a flood hitting their input provider – the reduction being negligible for generic inputs due to supplier switching. These findings provide an important validation of our dynamic notion of resilience: supplier-link disruptions translate into supply chain disruptions only if the final producer is unable to replace the broken link. The probability of replacement is inversely related to the degree of specialization of the respective input.

Using firm-to-firm transaction data from India,
Khanna et al., 2022
document that (i) robustness of final production is positively correlated with the specialization of intermediate inputs, and (ii) final producers whose suppliers were more exposed to Covid-19 restrictions reduced output and input purchases, as some supplier links were severed and replacements could not be found. Fact (i) aligns with the endogenous choice of input specialization in our dynamic model (
19
)–(
28
): the higher the robustness of the final producer–supplier link, the higher the optimal specialization. Fact (ii) reinforces the idea that replacing missing input providers is costly (in terms of final output) and time-consuming.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we study a model of frictional supply chain formation where heterogeneous intermediate producers choose the specialization of their goods. Higher specialization increases productivity but reduces the trading probability. We model supply chains as complex production processes in which multiple complementary inputs must be sourced for final production to take place. This framework delivers a well-defined notion of supply chain resilience,
i.e.
, the speed at which final production recovers following disruptions. In the model, supply chain resilience is negatively affected by the average specialization of goods, the extent of search frictions, and the complexity of the production process.

We study the efficiency properties of equilibrium specialization. We show that the combination of endogenous specialization and price-posting in frictional markets with simultaneous search implies that appropriability and business-stealing externalities perfectly offset each other. Still, intermediate producers fail to internalize the full reduction in social surplus induced by their specialization decisions due to a novel network externality arising from the interplay between frictional markets, endogenous specialization, and complexity in production. Single intermediate producers do not internalize how their specialization choices affect the likelihood that final production takes place, and thus, the unrealized value created by complementary inputs when final production is halted. The network externality induces over-specialization in equilibrium. Hence, supply chains are too productive during normal times, when operations run smoothly. Yet, over-specialization implies that supply chains are less resilient than efficient: when a disruption occurs, supply chains take too long to recover. A social planner would re-balance this trade-off between productivity and resilience, placing a higher weight on the latter. We characterize how a social planner can decentralize efficient supply chain resilience through a targeted transaction subsidy. The optimal subsidy schedule takes a simple form comprising an aggregate component and a targeting element that is decreasing in the intermediate producer’s productivity rank. Restricting the set of policy tools to untargeted instruments, we show that setting compatibility standards is likely an effective way to address over-specialization.

This paper sheds light on the sources of inefficiencies that are likely to affect supply chain resilience.
Our analytical results call for further work to provide quantification of the qualitative mechanisms we have highlighted. An interesting avenue for future research is to revisit the social gains from vertical and horizontal integration among firms within supply chains, accounting for the network externality.

A.2
Generalized Production Function

In this section, we show how our final production structure can be derived from a more general class of models characterized by complementarity across indivisible production inputs.

Each final producer
i
operates a constant-elasticity-of-substitution (CES) production function combining
N>1
complementary inputs into a consumption good:

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\eta}+1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}y_{j}^{\eta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta}},

(A.2)

where
\eta\leq 0
. The first multiplier makes sure that final producers exhibit no (dis-)like for variety.

Final producers solve the following profit maximization problem:

\displaystyle\pi_{i}=\max_{y_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{1}_{j}}\ Y_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}y_{j}.

(A.3)

A final producer can demand any quantity from an intermediate producer of good

j
with whom she is connected, i.e., for which
\mathbbm{1}_{j}=1
.
Demand for each intermediate good is subject to an integer constraint, reflecting the indivisibility of goods typically traded in supply chains (e.g., engines, tires, chassis). The optimal demand for an intermediate good
j
reads:

\displaystyle y_{j}^{\star}:\begin{cases}Y(y_{j}^{\star};\bm{y}_{-j}^{\star})-Y(y_{j}^{\star}-1;\bm{y}_{-j}^{\star})\geq p_{j},\\
Y(y_{j}^{\star}+1;\bm{y}_{-j}^{\star})-Y(y_{j}^{\star};\bm{y}_{-j}^{\star})<p_{j}.\end{cases}

If the distribution of quality-adjusted prices,
p_{j}/A_{j}
, has sufficiently low variance – which is the case in equilibrium if the variance of the intermediate productivity distribution is small –, then the optimal demand for each intermediate good is price-insensitive – just like in our baseline Leontief specification of the production function.
Hence, output varies across final producers only because of the value of inputs sourced:

y_{j}^{\star}=y^{\star}\ \forall j\implies Y_{i}=\mathcal{Q}_{i}y^{\star},\ \mathcal{Q}_{i}\equiv\sum_{j}A_{j}.

According to this generalized CES production function, the input values
A_{j}
increase the
quantity
of final production by the respective final producers, being the quality of the final good constant. Hence, this economy differs from our baseline only in terms of quantities produced, while being observationally equivalent as far as total expenditures and welfare are concerned.

A.4
Interpretation of Optimal Surplus Offered from Auction Theory

Our optimal surplus offered function is the solution to a first-price sealed-bid auction with an unknown number of bidders. For given specialization function, let
v(z)
be the valuation of a successful match by an intermediate producer of productivity
z

z

\displaystyle\Pi(x;z)

\displaystyle=\phi(z)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}m_{k}G(x)^{k}\right)f^{N-1}(v(z)-x)

\displaystyle=\phi(z)f^{N-1}\theta\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}G(x)^{k}(v(z)-x),

denotes the probability that the number of other compatible intermediate producers contacted by a final producer is
k

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}G(x)^{k}=(v(z)-x)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}kG(x)^{k-1}G^{\prime}(x).

We follow the same steps as in the main text by guessing that the optimal offered surplus is monotonically increasing in productivity, i.e.,
x^{\prime}(z)>0
, and defining the equilibrium offered surplus distribution as
H(z)=G(x(z))
. Substituting into the optimality condition yields:

x^{\prime}(z)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}H(z)^{k}=(v(z)-x(z))\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}kH(z)^{k-1}H^{\prime}(z).

Solving the differential equation with boundary condition
x(\underline{z})=0
, we obtain the standard expression for the optimal bidding strategy in first-price sealed-bid auctions with an unknown number of bidders
(
Krishna, 2009

\displaystyle x^{\star}(z)=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(z)H^{k}(z)}\int_{\underline{z}}^{z}v(\tilde{z})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(\tilde{z})dH^{k}(\tilde{z})=\mathbb{E}_{\max\tilde{z}|\tilde{z}<z}[v(\tilde{z})].

(A.4)

Since

equals
\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}dH^{k}(\tilde{z})}{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}H^{k}(z)}

Unlike standard auction theory, the distribution of the number of bidders is not taken as given in our model but pinned down by the extent of (exogenous) search frictions and (endogenous) compatibility frictions. Therefore, it is instructive to break down (

\displaystyle x^{\star}(z)=\text{Pr}(m(z)=0)0+\text{Pr}(m(z)=1)\mathbb{E}_{\,{\max}{\left\{\tilde{z}\right\}}|\tilde{z}\leq z,\ m(z)=1}\left[A(s(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})\right],

where

From this formulation, it is apparent how the optimal surplus offered equals the expected outside option of a final producer.
With probability

, a final producer does not meet any compatible intermediate producer with productivity lower than
z
, in which case the final producer’s outside option when meeting an intermediate producer with productivity

boils down to her reservation surplus, that is, zero. With complementary probability, the final producer meets at least one compatible intermediate producer with lower productivity than
z

A.6
In-House Production Model

Suppose final producers can produce any intermediate good of value
\underline{A}
in-house at cost

\displaystyle MF\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N-M}x_{n}+M\underline{A}\iff\Delta\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N-M}x_{n},

where

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(s,x;N)\equiv\

\displaystyle\phi(s)\ \left[1-G(x)\right]\ \left[f^{N-1}+(1-f^{N-1})(1-K(\Delta))\right]

\displaystyle\phi(s)\ \left[1-G(x)\right]\ \left[1-K(\Delta)(1-f^{N-1})\right].

Since

, the network externality operates even if final producers are allowed to produce inputs in-house.

Moreover, the decision to turn on in-house production brings about a further inefficiency due to a hold-up problem. Indeed, a social planner would activate in-house production if and only if:

\displaystyle MF\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N-M}A_{n}+M\underline{A}\iff\Delta\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N-M}A_{n},

Since

, in-house production is turned on too infrequently in equilibrium -- for given specialization function.

The equilibrium with in-house production is constrained efficient if turning on in-house production is costless on net, i.e.,

detected is strictly positive, our numerical exercise allows us to conclude that, for any parametrization, the equilibrium of the dynamic model with link destruction displays over-specialization if the production process is complex.

A.9
Strategies to mitigate the impact of supplier breakdowns

In this section we examine two key strategies that final producers can adopt to mitigate the impact
of supplier breakdowns: maintaining inventories and sourcing inputs from multiple intermediate
producers (multi-sourcing). Our goal is to determine whether these strategies can prevent firm-level disruptions caused by the inability of sourcing inputs. If they do, implementing the corresponding strategy would eliminate the network externality. Indeed, from the perspective of an intermediate producer, the probability that an attached final producer is active would be one regardless of the specialization of the other inputs.

A.9.1
Inventories

Suppose that active final producers can choose to purchase more units of each input than needed for current production, setting aside inventories as a buffer. If a supplier
relationship breaks down, these inventories allow production to continue until a new supplier is
found or until the stock is depleted. We further assume that inputs sourced can be used either in the current period or in the next – otherwise they perish. This implies that final producers set aside either zero or one unit of input from an input provider. However, the analysis can be generalized to accommodate any finite number of periods after which inputs perish.

Active final producers choose optimal inventories by solving the following dynamic profits maximization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\underbrace{\mathcal{Q}_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}(1+I_{j})p_{j}}_{\pi_{i}\left(\{I_{j}^{(-1)}\}_{j=1}^{N},\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\right)}+\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\pi_{i}^{\prime}\left(\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\right)\right],

(A.10)

where
\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\max\{A_{j}^{(-1)}I_{j}^{\star(-1)},A_{j}\}
is the final good quality and
I_{j}^{\star(-1)}
denotes optimal inventories of input
j
chosen in the previous period.

To highlight the basic economic forces shaping the inventory decision, we now specialize on the case where
N=2
. The optimal inventory policy function reads:

\displaystyle I_{j}^{\star}\left(\{A_{s}\}_{s=1}^{N},p_{j},\{I^{\star}_{s}\}_{s\neq j}^{N}\right)=1\iff

(A.11)

\displaystyle p_{j}<\beta\delta_{j}\Bigg(\left[(1-\delta_{-j})+\delta_{-j}\left(f_{-j}+(1-f_{-j})I^{\star}_{-j}\right)\right]f_{j}\text{Pr}(A^{\prime}_{j}\leq A_{j})\mathbb{E}[A_{j}-A^{\prime}_{j}|A^{\prime}\leq A_{j}]\dots

\displaystyle+(1-\delta_{-j})(1-f_{j})[A_{-j}+A_{j}-(1+I^{\star\prime}_{-j})p_{-j}]\dots

\displaystyle+\delta_{-j}f_{-j}(1-f_{j})[\max\{A_{-j}I_{-j}^{\star},A^{\prime}_{-j}\}+A_{j}-(1+I^{\star\prime}_{-j})p_{-j}]\dots

\displaystyle+\delta_{-j}(1-f_{-j})(1-f_{j})I^{\star}_{-j}[A_{-j}+A_{j}]\Bigg).

The marginal cost of an additional unit of inventories is the price of the input
j
set by the current input provider. The marginal benefit comprises four terms – all discounted by the probability that the supplier relationship with the current input provider breaks down,
\delta_{j}
. First, if the final producer is able to replace the supplier relationship in the next period, she would use the unit of inventories to produce were the new input supplier of lower value than the previous one (second row). Second, if the final producer is not able to replace the supplier relationship in the next period and the other supplier relationship does not break up, she would use the unit of inventories to continue production (third row). Third, if the final producer is not able to replace the supplier relationship in the next period and the other supplier relationship gets replaced upon breaking up, she would use the unit of inventories to continue production (third row). Finally, if also the other supplier relationship breaks down and the final producer is not able to replace either of them in the next period, she would use the unit of inventories to continue production provided that she has set aside inventories of the other input, as well (fifth row).

Hence, inventories allows mitigating the impact of supplier breakdowns.
However, if inputs are perishable, inventories cannot completely hedge final producers from disruptions. The reason is that, with positive probability, the final producer will not be able to locate a compatible intermediate producer in
n<\infty
periods.

If inputs were not perishable, a final producer may set aside a large number of units (approaching infinity) of inventories to completely hedge from disruptions. However, this strategy would not be profit-maximizing. As apparent from (
A.11
), the marginal benefit of holding inventories scales with the time-discounted probability of actually using such inventories. On the other hand, the marginal cost always equals the current price. It follows that there exists an
\tilde{n}<\infty
such that, if
n>\tilde{n}
, the marginal benefit of the
n
-th unit of inventories approaches zero. Hence, holding infinite inventories cannot be a profit-maximizing strategy.

Overall, inventories do not completely hedge final producers from disruptions in equilibrium. It follows that intermediate producers will always face a probability lower than one that an attached final producer is able to source all the other complementary inputs. Since this probability is decreasing in specialization, the network externality is not eliminated by endogenous inventories.

A.9.2
Multi-sourcing

Suppose final producers can establish links with multiple compatible
suppliers met during a supplier search. Specifically, assume that a final producer may choose to maintain an idle
backup link with the second-best compatible supplier at a fixed per-period cost
c_{M}
. If the link with
the best supplier breaks down first, the final producer can immediately switch to the backup
supplier and continue production. This implies that active final producers are in contact with either one or two intermediate producers of a certain input. However, the analysis can be generalized to accommodate any finite number of suppliers per input.

Let
D_{j}(D_{j}^{M})
be an indicator of link destruction with the main (backup) provider of input
j
.
Active final producers choose the optimal number of backup links by solving the following dynamic profits maximization problem:

\displaystyle\ \max_{M_{j}\in\begin{cases}\scriptstyle{\{0,1\}}&\scriptstyle{\text{if }(D_{j}=1,N^{c}>1)\text{ or }(D_{j}^{M}=0,M_{j}^{\star(-1)}=1)}\\
\scriptstyle{\{0\}}&\scriptstyle{\text{else}}\end{cases},j=1,\dots,N}\pi_{i}-c_{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}M_{j}+\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\pi_{i}^{\prime}\left(\{M_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\right)\right].

(A.12)

In words, final producers make a supplier search when the supplier relationship with their main input provider breaks down (
D_{j}=1
). Provided that they meet more than one compatible supplier (
N^{c}>1
), they can establish a backup link with the second-best compatible supplier. If they established a backup link in a past supplier search (
M_{j}^{\star(-1)}=1
), they can roll it over unless the link breaks for exogenous reasons (
D_{j}^{M}=0
).
To highlight the basic economic forces shaping the multi-sourcing decision, we now specialize on the case where
N=2
. The optimal multi-sourcing policy function reads:

\displaystyle M_{j}^{\star}\left(\{x_{s}\}_{s=1}^{N},\{x_{s}^{M}\}_{s=1}^{N},c_{M},\{M^{\star}_{s}\}_{s\neq j}^{N}\right)=1\iff

(A.13)

\displaystyle c_{M}<\beta\delta_{j}(1-\delta_{j}^{M})\Bigg(\left[(1-\delta_{-j})+\delta_{-j}\left(f_{-j}+(1-f_{-j})M^{\star}_{-j}\right)\right]f_{j}G(x^{M}_{j})\mathbb{E}[x_{j}^{M}-x^{\prime}_{j}|x^{\prime}\leq x_{j}^{M}]\dots

\displaystyle+(1-\delta_{-j})(1-f_{j})\left[\pi_{i}(x_{j}^{M},x^{M}_{-j})-c_{M}\sum_{j}M_{j}^{\star\prime}\right]\dots

\displaystyle+\delta_{-j}f_{-j}(1-f_{j})\left[\pi_{i}(x_{j}^{M},\max\{x^{M}_{-j},x^{\prime}_{-j}\})-c_{M}\sum_{j}M_{j}^{\star\prime}\right]\dots

\displaystyle+\delta_{-j}(1-f_{-j})(1-f_{j})M^{\star}_{-j}\left[\pi_{i}(x_{j}^{M},x^{M}_{-j})-c_{M}\sum_{j}M_{j}^{\star\prime}\right]\Bigg).

The optimality condition for the number of backup links shares many similarities with that of inventories (
A.11
). The marginal cost of an additional backup link is the per-period cost of maintaining an idle link alive,
c_{M}
. The marginal benefit comprises four terms – all discounted by the probability that the supplier relationship with the main input provider breaks down and that with the backup supplier does not,
\delta_{j}(1-\delta_{j}^{M})
. First, if the final producer is able to replace the supplier relationship in the next period, she would upgrade the backup link to main supplier were the new input supplier of lower value, thus gaining the differential surplus offered by the two (second row). Second, if the final producer is not able to replace the supplier relationship in the next period and the other supplier relationship does not break up, she would resort to the backup link to continue production (third row). Third, if the final producer is not able to replace the supplier relationship in the next period and the other supplier relationship gets replaced upon breaking up, she would resort to the backup link to continue production (third row). Finally, if also the other supplier relationship breaks down and the final producer is not able to replace either of them in the next period, she would resort to the backup link to continue production provided that she has a backup link for the other input, as well (fifth row).

Hence, multi-sourcing allows mitigating the impact of supplier breakdowns.
However, if alternative input providers can be located only when performing a supplier search, multi-sourcing cannot completely hedge final producers from disruptions. The reason is that, with positive probability, the final producer will not be able to locate more than one compatible intermediate producer in a supplier search.

If final producers could search for alternative suppliers in any period (
on-the-match
search), a final producer may build up a large number of backup links (approaching infinity) to completely hedge from disruptions. However, this strategy would not be profit-maximizing. As apparent from (
A.13
), the marginal benefit of an additional backup link scales with the time-discounted probability of actually using such links. On the other hand, the marginal cost always equals the link maintenance cost. It follows that there exists an
\tilde{n}<\infty
such that, if
n>\tilde{n}
, the marginal benefit of the
n
-th backup link approaches zero. Hence, holding infinite backup links cannot be a profit-maximizing strategy.

Overall, multi-sourcing does not completely hedge final producers from disruptions in equilibrium. It follows that intermediate producers will always face a probability lower than one that an attached final producer is able to source all the other complementary inputs. Since this probability is decreasing in specialization, the network externality is not eliminated by endogenous multi-sourcing.

A.10
Normative analysis

Suppose a social planner can use standards as the only policy instrument. We now study how the social planner would choose a standard in the static economy of Section
2
.

The optimal standard for input
j
solves:

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\bar{s}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\prod_{v\neq n}f(\vec{s}_{v}(z))\int A(s_{n}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\tilde{z},\bar{z})}\lambda\phi(s_{n}(\tilde{z}))\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}+\psi\log\left(1-m\sum_{n=1}^{N}q(s_{n}(\tilde{z}))\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}\right)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\ s_{j}(z)=\min\{\bar{s}_{j},s^{\star}_{j}(z)\}\ \forall j.

Assuming that
{s^{\star}}^{\prime}(z)>0\ \forall z\in[\underline{z},\bar{z}]
, the problem underlying the choice of the standard for input
j
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\max_{\bar{s}_{j}}

\displaystyle\prod_{v\neq j}f(\vec{s}_{v}(z))\left[\int_{\underline{z}}^{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}A(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}_{j}(\tilde{z},\bar{z})}\lambda\phi(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z}))\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}+\int_{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}^{\bar{z}}A(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}_{j}(\tilde{z},\bar{z})}\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}\right]+

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq j}^{N}\prod_{v\neq j,n}f(\vec{s}_{v}(z))\int A(s^{\star}_{n}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}_{n}(\tilde{z},\bar{z})}\lambda\phi(s^{\star}_{n}(\tilde{z}))\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}+

\displaystyle\psi\log\left(1-m\left[\int_{\underline{z}}^{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}q(s^{\star}_{j}(z))\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}+(1-\Gamma(\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})))q(\bar{s}_{j})\right]-m\sum_{n\neq j}^{N}q(s^{\star}_{n}(\tilde{z}))\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}\right).

From Proposition
9
we know that the optimal standard is lower than the highest specialization observed in equilibrium, i.e.,
\bar{s}_{j}\in[s^{\star}_{j}(\underline{z}),s^{\star}_{j}(\bar{z}))
. The optimal standard solves:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq j}^{N}\frac{\partial f(\vec{s}^{\star}_{n}(z))}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\prod_{v\neq j,n}f(\vec{s}^{\star}_{v}(z))\mathbb{E}_{\max\tilde{z}}[A(s_{j}^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]+

\displaystyle\prod_{v\neq j}f(\vec{s}_{v}(z))\left[A(\bar{s}_{j};\hat{z})e^{-\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})(1-\Gamma(\hat{z}))}\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})\gamma(\hat{z})\frac{\partial\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}+\right.

\displaystyle\left.\int_{\underline{z}}^{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\left(A(s^{\star}_{j}(z);z)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}_{j}(\tilde{z},\bar{z})}\lambda\phi(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z}))\right)\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}-\right.

\displaystyle\left.A(\bar{s}_{j};\hat{z})e^{-\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})(1-\Gamma(\hat{z}))}\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})\gamma(\hat{z})\frac{\partial\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}+\right.

\displaystyle\left.\int_{\hat{z}}^{\bar{z}}\lambda\left(A^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})\phi(\bar{s}_{j})+A(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})\phi^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})\right)e^{-\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})(1-\Gamma(\tilde{z}))}\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}-\right.

\displaystyle\left.\int_{\hat{z}}^{\bar{z}}\lambda A(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})\phi(\bar{s}_{j})\lambda(1-\Gamma(\tilde{z}))\phi^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})e^{-\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})(1-\Gamma(\tilde{z}))}\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}\right]+

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq j}^{N}\sum_{m\neq n}^{N}\frac{\partial f(\vec{s}^{\star}_{m}(z))}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\prod_{v\neq j,m}f(\vec{s}^{\star}_{v}(z))\mathbb{E}_{\max\tilde{z}}[A(s_{j}^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]+

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq j}^{N}\prod_{v\neq n}\int_{\underline{z}}^{\bar{z}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\left(A(s^{\star}_{n}(z);z)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}_{n}(\tilde{z},\bar{z})}\lambda\phi(s^{\star}_{n}(\tilde{z}))\right)\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}-

\displaystyle\frac{\psi}{1-\ell}m\left[q(\bar{s}_{j})\gamma(\hat{z})\frac{\partial\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}+\int_{\underline{z}}^{\hat{s}(\bar{s}_{j})}q^{\prime}(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z}))\frac{\partial s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z})}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}-\right.

\displaystyle\left.q(\bar{s}_{j})\gamma(\hat{z})\frac{\partial\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}+(1-\Gamma(\hat{z}))q^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})+\sum_{n\neq j}^{N}\int q^{\prime}(s^{\star}_{n}(\tilde{z}))\frac{\partial s^{\star}_{n}(\tilde{z})}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}\right]:=0.

This first-order condition can be significantly simplified by using an Envelope argument. Intuitively, small perturbations around the privately optimal specialization do not have first-order effects. Hence, we make use of equation (
8
) to observe that:

\displaystyle\ \int_{\underline{z}}^{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\left(A(s^{\star}_{j}(z);z)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}_{j}(\tilde{z},\bar{z})}\lambda\phi(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z}))\right)\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}-\frac{\psi}{1-\ell}m\int_{\underline{z}}^{\hat{s}(\bar{s}_{j})}q^{\prime}(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z}))\frac{\partial s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z})}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\underline{z}}^{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial s^{\star}_{j}(z)}\int_{\underline{z}}^{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}A(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}_{j}(\tilde{z},\bar{z})}\lambda\phi(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z}))\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}-\frac{\psi}{1-\ell}mq^{\prime}(s^{\star}_{j}(\tilde{z}))\gamma(\tilde{z})\right]\frac{\partial s^{\star}_{j}(z)}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 0.

Notice that all the terms within square brackets are zero as they equal the first-order conditions for the privately optimal specialization.

Upon implementing this simplification, as well as canceling out the derivatives of the extremes of integration, the optimal standard is pinned down implicitly by the following condition:

\displaystyle\prod_{v\neq j}f(\vec{s}_{v}(z))\ \lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})\int_{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}^{\bar{z}}\left[A^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})}{\phi(\bar{s}_{j})}\left(A(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})-\tilde{x}(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})\right)\right]e^{-\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})(1-\Gamma(\tilde{z}))}\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m\neq n}^{N}\frac{\partial f(\vec{s}_{m}(z))}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}\prod_{v\neq n,m}f(\vec{s}_{v}(z))\mathbb{E}_{\max\tilde{z}}[A(s_{n}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]+\frac{\psi}{1-\ell}m(1-\Gamma(\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})))q^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j}),

(A.14)

where
\tilde{x}(\bar{s}_{j};z)\equiv\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})(1-\Gamma(\tilde{z}))A(\bar{s}_{j};z)
is the counterpart of the surplus offered in equation (
8
).

The left-hand side of (
A.14
) represents the marginal benefit of increasing the mandated standard. Intuitively, it equals an average of the marginal benefit of specializing more firms that are constrained by the standard. The right-hand side of (
A.14
) represents the marginal cost of increasing the mandated standard and comprises two terms. First, a higher (slacker) standard raises average specialization and, therefore, affects the magnitude of the network externality. Second, a higher standard raises the marginal cost of specialization. Notice that the sign of the first term is a priori ambiguous. On the one hand, raising the standard of input
j
increases average specialization of input
j
, thereby lowering the respective input finding probability (
\partial f(\vec{s}_{j}(z))/\partial\bar{s}_{j}<0
). On the other hand, higher specialization of input
j
raises the wage rate and reduces the probability that a final producer is active, thus reducing optimal specialization of complementary inputs (
\partial f(\vec{s}_{n}(z))/\partial\bar{s}_{j}>0,\ n\neq j
).

Upon imposing symmetry, equation (
A.14
) can be rearranged as:

\displaystyle f^{N-1}\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})\int_{\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})}^{\bar{z}}\left[A^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})}{\phi(\bar{s}_{j})}\left(A(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})-\tilde{x}(\bar{s}_{j};\tilde{z})\right)\right]e^{-\lambda\phi(\bar{s}_{j})(1-\Gamma(\tilde{z}))}\gamma(\tilde{z})d\tilde{z}

\displaystyle+(N-1)^{2}\frac{\partial f_{-j}}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}f^{N-2}\mathbb{E}_{\max\tilde{z}}[A(s(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]=-(N-1)\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial\bar{s}_{j}}f^{N-2}\mathbb{E}_{\max\tilde{z}}[A(s(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]

\displaystyle+\frac{\psi}{1-\ell}m(1-\Gamma(\hat{z}(\bar{s}_{j})))q^{\prime}(\bar{s}_{j})

This expression breaks the terms pertaining to the network externality into two groups: i) those depending on the effect of the standard on the specialization of the respective input (marginal cost) and ii) those depending on the effect of the standard on the specialization of the other inputs (marginal benefit).

Appendix B
Extensions

In this section, we extend our model along four dimensions.
First, we study the problem of final producers optimally choosing the level of complexity of their production process, that is, the length of their supply chain or the number of complementary inputs needed for final production. We show that, much like specialization choices, complexity is generally inefficient.

Second, we allow final producers to invest resources to reduce the likelihood of own disruptions, e.g., through prudential investment in redundancies. We show that prudential investment is generally inefficient due to a well-known hold-up problem.

Third, we consider a general quality function that allows for arbitrary substitutability or complementarity among complementary inputs.
This extension brings about a further externality induced by specialization choices associated with complementarity in quality.

Finally, we note that our baseline model features arbitrage opportunities. Specifically, we have not imposed any condition that makes the relative mass of intermediate and final producers adjust to arbitrage away any difference in the expected returns from running either class of firms. In our final extension, therefore, we allow entrepreneurs to enter the market either as intermediate or final producers. This extension brings about a further externality induced by specialization choices related to entry decisions.

For simplicity, in all extensions we assume steady-state behavior by both the private agents and the social planner – as common in the equilibrium search literature
(
Burdett and Mortensen, 1998
)
. This means that intermediate producers maximize their steady-state profits, while the social planner maximizes the steady-state utility of the representative household.
Hence, we start this section by developing the dynamic model with steady-state behavior to use as a benchmark.

B.1
Dynamic Model with Steady-State Behavior

Stationary equilibrium.

Intermediate firms solve the following profit maximization problem:

\displaystyle V(s,x;z)=\max_{s,x}

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{D}(s,x)\left(A(s;z)-x\right)-wq(s)

s.t.

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{D}(s,x)=\frac{\theta\lambda}{\delta}\mathcal{P}(s,x).

Differently from the baseline dynamic model, intermediate producers use stationary demand as demand constraint (instead of its law of motion).

The optimal surplus offered is the same as in the baseline model, as well as given by (
7
).
Optimal specialization solves:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z;N)\bigg[A^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))}{\phi(s^{\star}(z))}(A(s^{\star}(z);z)-x^{\star}(z))\bigg]=wq^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z)).

(A.15)

The only difference with respect to the optimal specialization condition of the baseline (dynamically-consistent) model (
19
) lies in the absence of any multiplier of the reduction in trading probability.

Social planner problem.

The social planner solves:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\max_{s_{j}(z)}

\displaystyle\ m\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int D_{j}(z)A(s_{j}(z);z)\ \gamma(z)dz+\psi\log(1-\ell)

s.t.

\displaystyle D_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{\delta}\frac{\mu}{m}\lambda\phi(s_{j}(z))e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(z,\bar{z})}\prod_{v\neq j}f_{v},

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\delta}{(1-\delta)\left(1-\prod_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}\right)},

\displaystyle\ell=m\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int q(s_{n}(z))\gamma(z)dz.

Hence, the social planner uses stationary firm-level demand and the stationary share of searching final producers as constraints – rather than their laws of motion.

Efficient specialization of intermediate producers with productivity
z
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z;N)\bigg[A^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))}{\phi(\mathcal{S}(z))}\bigg(A(\mathcal{S}(z);z)-\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z};\tilde{z}))]+

(A.16)

\displaystyle(N-1)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]-\frac{1-\delta}{\delta+(1-\delta)f^{N}}f^{N}Ne^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]\bigg)\bigg]=\frac{\psi}{1-Nm\bar{q}}\ q^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z)).

Comparing (
A.16
) with (
22
), we observe that the two differ due to the absence of any multiplier of the reduction in trading probability and in the multiplier of the search externality.

Rearranging (
A.16
) allows us to establish the following proposition.

Proposition A.2

(Efficiency of the Dynamic Economy with Steady-State Behavior)
.

The economy is efficient if and only if
N=\frac{1}{\mu(N)}
, where
\mu(N)\equiv\frac{\delta}{\delta+(1-\delta)f^{N}}
. If
N>\frac{1}{\mu(N)}
then the economy features over-specialization.

Hence, assuming steady-state behavior, the variable
\mathcal{M}(N)
defined in Proposition
2
boils down the share of searching final producers
\mu(N)
. Overall, the dynamic model with steady-state behavior features the same qualitative sources of externality as the baseline dynamic model.

B.2
Endogenous Complexity

In this extension, we endogenize the problem of firms choosing the complexity of their production process. In particular, firms optimize over the number of key inputs in the production function. This approach is similar to the core idea of
Oberfield, 2018
,
Boehm and Oberfield, 2020
,
Kopytov et al., 2024
and
Kim and Shin, 2023
.
For simplicity, in this extension, we treat
N
as a measure rather than an integer.

Static model.

We allow final producers to choose the complexity of the production process to maximize expected profits:

\displaystyle N^{\star}=\argmax_{N\geq 1}f^{N}N\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x(z)],

where
f
is the input finding probability, and
\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x(z)]
is the expected surplus accruing to the final producer on each input, conditional on being active. Intuitively increasing complexity induces two opposite effects. On the one hand, adding inputs increases the value of output directly. On the other hand, it reduces the probability that the final good is produced altogether.
The optimal level of complexity solves:
32
32

32

If
N
were a positive integer, the optimal level of complexity would be
N^{\star}=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{1-f}\right\rfloor
, where
\lfloor.\rfloor
denotes the floor function.

\displaystyle N^{\star}=\frac{1}{\,{\ln}{\left(1/f\right)}}.

(A.17)

Hence, complexity is increasing in the input finding probability
f
.
Efficient complexity solves:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=\argmax_{N\geq 1}\ f^{N}N\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s(z);z)]+\psi\log\left(1-Nm\bar{q}\right).

Relative to the problem of individual firms, the social planner problem features two differences. First, additional complexity is evaluated at the expected social surplus
{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s(z);z)]}
instead of the expected private surplus
{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x(z)]}
. Second, the planner accounts for the additional specialization costs associated with additional inputs, taking the form of higher disutility from labor.
Hence, efficient complexity is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\,{\ln}{\left(1/f\right)}}\left(1-\frac{w\ell}{y}\right).

(A.18)

where
y
is given by (
10
) and the labor share
w\ell/y
represents the ratio between aggregate product design costs and value added.
Comparing (
A.17
) with (
A.18
) allows us to establish the following proposition:

Proposition A.3

(Efficiency of the Endogenous Complexity Economy)
.

The equilibrium is inefficient and features excess complexity, i.e.,
N^{\star}>\mathcal{N}
, and lower sourcing capacity than efficient, i.e.,
f(s^{\star}(\bm{z}))^{N^{\star}}<f(\mathcal{S}(\bm{z}))^{\mathcal{N}}
.

Intuitively, final producers do not internalize the marginal product design cost induced by their complexity choice, thereby generating a
hold-up problem
. Choosing a higher level of complexity forces intermediate producers to invest more in product design. These costs are sunk at the time of price posting As a result, equilibrium sourcing capacity – our notion of
static resilience
– is lower than efficient.

Dynamic model.

Equilibrium complexity is the same as in the static model and given by (
A.17
).
Efficient complexity solves:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=\argmax_{N\geq 1}\

\displaystyle\mu(f;N)\bigg(\frac{f^{N-1}}{\delta}N\mathbb{E}[A(s(z);z)]\bigg)+\psi\log\left(1-mN\bar{q}\right),

where
\mu(f;N)\equiv\frac{\delta}{f^{N}+(1-f^{N})\delta}
is the stationary share of searching final producers.
Efficient complexity is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{\mu(f;N)\,{\ln}{\left(1/f\right)}}\left(1-\frac{w\ell}{y}\right).

(A.19)

Comparing (
A.19
) with (
A.18
), we observe that a further inefficiency arises in a dynamic setting.
Following the same approach adopted for specialization, the marginal welfare effects of equilibrium complexity can be decomposed into two terms:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial N}\bigg|_{N=N^{\star}}\propto\underbrace{-\ w\ell/y\ }_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hold-up}\\
\text{externality}\end{subarray}}\ \underbrace{\vphantom{-\mu}\ +(1-\mu)\ }_{\vphantom{\text{hold-up}}\text{search externality}}.

(A.20)

First, a hold-up externality induces equilibrium complexity to be higher than efficient. The reason is that final producers fail to internalize the product design costs of each additional input, which are borne by intermediate producers before meeting. The surplus share of such product design costs is represented by the labor share. Second, final producers do not internalize the effect of their complexity choice on the equilibrium share of searching final producers. This search externality pushes the private marginal cost of complexity to exceed the social one.
Rearranging (
A.19
) allows us to establish the following result:

Proposition A.4

(Efficiency of the Dynamic Endogenous Complexity Economy)
.

The equilibrium is efficient if and only if:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}N=\frac{1}{\mu}\\
LS=1-\mu\end{cases}

where
\mu=\frac{\delta}{\delta+(1-\delta)f^{N}}
and
LS=\frac{w\ell}{y}
.
The equilibrium features under-resilience if and only if
{LS}^{\star}>1-\mu^{\star}
.

In general, the equilibrium features both inefficient complexity and specialization.

B.3
Endogenous Robustness

So far, we have treated the disruption probability of final producers as an exogenous parameter. In this section, we allow final producers to make prudential investments in robustness aimed at reducing their disruption probability,
e.g.
, through redundancies. Investment in robustness comes at per-period cost
\kappa(r)
per supply relationship, with
\kappa^{\prime}>0,\ \kappa^{\prime\prime}<0
. Specifically, we posit that
\delta=\delta(r)
, with
\delta^{\prime}<0,\ \delta^{\prime\prime}>0
. For analytical tractability, we assume that final producers
choose their robustness policy before matching with their input providers.
33
33

33

More specifically, we assume that final producers commit to a robustness policy
r
at the search stage for the entire life of the firm before a disruption occurs. Implementing such a robustness policy requires paying a per-period cost
\kappa(r)
per supplier relationship. If final producers could optimize their robustness policy once matched, intermediate producers would internalize the robustness response of downstream partners when choosing the surplus offered to final producers.
We think of robustness policies as the design and implementation of prudential strategies to reduce the exposure to exogenous shocks.

Stationary equilibrium.

Final producers choose their robustness to maximize expected profits:

\displaystyle r^{\star}=\argmax_{r}f^{N}N\frac{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x(z)]-\kappa(r)}{\delta(r)},

(A.21)

where
\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x(z)]
is the expected surplus accruing to the final producer on each input, conditional on being active.
Equilibrium robustness solves:

\displaystyle-\delta^{\prime}(r^{\star})\frac{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x]-\kappa(r^{\star})}{\delta(r^{\star})}=\kappa^{\prime}(r^{\star}).

(A.22)

The privately optimal robustness trades off a lower disruption probability,
-\delta^{\prime}
, evaluated at the expected present discounted value of each supplier relationship,
\frac{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x]-\kappa}{\delta}
, against higher private marginal costs,
\kappa^{\prime}
. Importantly, note that firms discount the future with their endogenous disruption probability,
\delta
. Under the assumption that final producers decide their robustness before matching, the expected surplus offered is constant across final producers, and so is their optimal robustness. Due to the presence of robustness costs, final producers need to break even with their share of the match surplus. As a result, the
reservation surplus
of final producers,
x_{0}
, is positive and pinned down by the following expected break-even condition:

\displaystyle x_{0}+(N-1)\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x(z)]=N\kappa(r^{\star}).

(A.23)

Final producers need to source
N
key inputs. Whenever they meet with a potential supplier, they know that production will ultimately entail a
N\kappa(r)
robustness cost. They are, therefore, willing to accept any surplus such that operating profits exceed that cost, where
(N-1)\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x(z)]
represents the expected surplus they can obtain from the other
N-1
suppliers.
The reservation surplus acts as a boundary condition for the differential equation governing the equilibrium surplus offered to final producers (
7
).
Hence, the new equilibrium surplus offered to final producers reads:

\displaystyle x^{\star}(z)

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}x_{0}+\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}[A(s^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})].

(A.24)

Equations (
A.23
) and (
A.24
) jointly imply that the equilibrium reservation surplus offered to final producers is given by
x_{0}=\kappa(r^{\star})-\frac{N-1}{N}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[\tilde{x}(z)]
, where
\tilde{x}(z)=\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}[A(s^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]
. Note that
x_{0}<\kappa(r^{\star})
, suggesting that final producers are willing to take on negative net surplus from a single relation since they can cross-subsidize it with the other
N-1
relationships.
Up to the different formulation of the equilibrium surplus offered to final producers, equilibrium specialization is still determined by (
A.15
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z;N,\delta(r^{\star}))\bigg[A^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))}{\phi(s^{\star}(z))}(A(s^{\star}(z);z)-x^{\star}(z))\bigg]=wq^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z)).

Interestingly, since
\delta(r)
discounts the marginal benefit of specialization in (
A.15
),
equilibrium specialization is increasing in robustness. This result aligns well with the empirical findings of
Khanna et al., 2022
, which shows that supply chains involving more specialized inputs proved more robust to Covid-19-induced disruptions in India.

Social planner problem.

Next, we study the planner problem to evaluate the efficiency properties of equilibrium specialization and robustness decisions.
The efficient specialization condition has the same formulation as in the model with exogenous robustness, up to the definition of total match surplus as net of robustness costs. However, the efficiency properties of equilibrium specialization differ significantly from the dynamic model with exogenous robustness due to the fact that the reservation surplus of final producers is not specific to each intermediate producer. Proposition
A.5
identifies the reason why the mere presence of costs downstream makes the equilibrium surplus offered to final producers inefficient. Corollary
1
applies this general inefficiency result to the endogenous robustness economy.

Proposition A.5

(Efficiency Properties of Equilibrium Specialization with Costs Downstream)
.

If final producers sustain other costs than input purchases, then the property of the equilibrium surplus offered to final producers of perfectly balancing appropriability and business-stealing externalities breaks.

Corollary 1

(Special Case - Robustness Costs)
.

Equilibrium firm-level specialization is always inefficient in the endogenous robustness economy due to the presence of robustness costs downstream.

Intuitively, a social planner would like the reservation surplus to vary across the intermediate producers’ productivity distribution for business-stealing and appropriability externalities to offset each other in the presence of robustness costs. Since the equilibrium reservation surplus is independent of productivity, misallocation of specialization always arises in the cross-section.

Efficient robustness solves the following social planner problem:

\displaystyle\varrho=\argmax_{r}\ \mu(f;\delta(r))\frac{f^{N}}{\delta(r)}N\Big(\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s(z);z)]-\kappa(r)\Big).

Notice that robustness costs are borne by active final producers, whose equilibrium mass is
\mu\frac{f^{N}}{\delta}=\frac{f^{N}}{\delta+(1-\delta)f^{N}}
.
Efficient robustness is implicitly defined by:

\displaystyle-\delta^{\prime}(\varrho)\left(1-f^{N}\right)\mu(f,\varrho)\frac{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s(z);z)-\kappa(\varrho)]}{\delta(\varrho)}=\kappa^{\prime}(\varrho).

(A.25)

The socially optimal level of robustness trades off a lower disruption probability,
-\delta^{\prime}
, evaluated at the expected present discounted social value of each supplier relationship,
\left(1-f^{N}\right)\mu

\frac{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A-\kappa]}{\delta}
, against the marginal robustness cost,
\kappa^{\prime}
.
Comparing (
A.22
) with (
A.25
), we observe that equilibrium robustness is generally inefficient. Let the aggregate surplus share accruing to final producers be
\tilde{\xi}\equiv\frac{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x-\kappa]}{\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A-\kappa]}
.
The marginal welfare effect of equilibrium robustness can be decomposed into two terms:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=r^{\star}}\propto\underbrace{\vphantom{-\mu}\ 1-\tilde{\xi}\ }_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hold-up}\\
\text{externality}\end{subarray}}\ \underbrace{-\ [1-(1-f^{N})\mu]\ }_{\vphantom{\text{hold-up}}\text{search externality}}.

(A.26)

First, a standard hold-up externality induces equilibrium investment in robustness to be lower than efficient, as the flow private marginal benefit,
\mathbb{\hat{E}}[x-\kappa]
, falls short of the flow social marginal benefit,
\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A-\kappa]
. Second, firms do not internalize the effect on equilibrium tightness induced by their higher robustness. This search externality is evident when noting that the social planner discounts the marginal surplus at a higher rate,
\delta/[(1-f^{N})\mu]
, than private firms do,
\delta
. It follows that the search externality induces equilibrium investment in robustness to be higher than efficient.
The nature of the search externality is akin to that arising in models of endogenous match destruction, where firms ignore the effect of their reservation productivity choice on equilibrium tightness. As in those models, a specific surplus sharing rule allows decentralizing the efficient allocation
(
Pissarides, 2000
)
.
Formally, efficient robustness obtains in equilibrium (for given aggregate specialization) if the aggregate surplus share accruing to final producers equals the equilibrium share of unattached final producers,
i.e.
,
\tilde{\xi}=(1-f^{N})\mu
. Under this condition, the appropriability externality exactly offsets the search externality.
Otherwise, the net effect of the hold-up and search externality is ambiguous.
34
34

34

Standard models of endogenous match destruction imply that either the equilibrium match destruction rate is efficient or lower than efficient. The reason why, in our model, match destruction can be excessive is the presence of the hold-up externality potentially countervailing the search externality.

We now turn to determining efficient specialization. The social planner problem reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\max_{s_{j}(z)}

\displaystyle\ m\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int D_{j}(z)[A(s_{j}(z);z)-\kappa(r)]\ \gamma(z)dz+\psi\log(1-\ell)

s.t.

\displaystyle D_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{\delta(r)}\frac{\mu(\delta(r))}{m}\lambda\phi(s_{j}(z))e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(z,\bar{z})}\prod_{v\neq j}f_{v},

\displaystyle\mu(\delta(r))=\frac{\delta(r)}{(1-\delta(r))\left(1-\prod_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}\right)},

\displaystyle\ell=m\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int q(s_{n}(z))\gamma(z)dz.

Efficient specialization of intermediate producers with productivity
z
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z;N)\bigg[A^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))}{\phi(\mathcal{S}(z))}\bigg(A(\mathcal{S}(z);z)-\kappa(\varrho)-\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z};\tilde{z}))-\kappa(\varrho)]+

\displaystyle(N-1)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})-\kappa(\varrho)]-\frac{1-\delta}{\delta+(1-\delta)f^{N}}f^{N}Ne^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})-\kappa(\varrho)]\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi}{1-Nm\bar{q}}\ q^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z)).

(A.27)

The only difference with the baseline dynamic model lies in the definition of total match surplus
net
of robustness costs. Evaluating the marginal welfare effect of specialization at the equilibrium specialization yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial s(z)}\bigg|_{s(z)=s^{\star}(z)}\propto

\displaystyle\ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[A(s^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})-\kappa(r^{\star})\right]}_{\text{business stealing externality}}\underbrace{-\ x^{\star}(z)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{appropriability}\\
\text{externality}\end{subarray}}\underbrace{-\ (N-1)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})-\kappa(r^{\star})]}_{\text{network externality}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\vphantom{\int_{\underline{z}}^{z}}+\ \frac{1-\delta(r^{\star})}{\delta(r^{\star})+(1-\delta(r^{\star}))f^{N}}f^{N}e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}N\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(s(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})-\kappa(r^{\star})]}_{\text{search externality}}.

(A.28)

The only substantive difference with the dynamic model in terms of efficiency properties of equilibrium specialization is that the perfect balance between appropriability and business-stealing externalities breaks down. The reason is that the reservation surplus is not targeted to intermediate producers. Formally, the equilibrium surplus offered to final producers would keep balancing appropriability and business-stealing externalities if and only if:

x_{0}=(1-e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)})\kappa(r^{\star})\iff\kappa(r^{\star})=e^{\lambda\bar{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\frac{N-1}{N}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[\tilde{x}(z)]\quad\forall z\in[\underline{z},\bar{z}].

Since this condition never holds simultaneously for any productivity level, the net effect of appropriability and business-stealing externalities is ambiguous. This entails that firm-level equilibrium specialization is always inefficient.

We summarize our findings in the next proposition.

Proposition A.6

(Efficiency of Endogenous Robustness Equilibrium)
.

The equilibrium can feature both under- and over-robustness.
For given aggregate specialization, equilibrium robustness is efficient if and only if:

\tilde{\xi}=(1-f^{N})\mu\ ,

where
\mu\equiv\frac{\delta(r)}{\delta(r)+(1-\delta(r))f^{N}}
and
\tilde{\xi}\equiv\frac{\mathbb{E}[x-\kappa]}{\mathbb{E}[A-\kappa]}
.
Since equilibrium specialization is increasing in robustness, over-robustness makes over-specialization more likely.

The equilibrium can feature both under- and over-specialization.
For given robustness, equilibrium firm-level specialization is always inefficient.

In general, the equilibrium features inefficient investment in both robustness and specialization.

B.4
Generalized Quality Function

So far, we have looked at an economy in which final producers operate a specific technology. In particular, we assumed that intermediate goods are i) perfect substitutes in quality (intensive margin) and ii) complements in quantity (extensive margin).
While both assumptions matter for the results derived so far, only ii) is crucial for our new network externality to arise.
The intuitive reason is that, although goods are complements, an intermediate producer only cares about losing its own share of the surplus if production does not take place. As a consequence, a final producer does not have an instrument to induce bilaterally efficient specialization choices (in a way akin to the inefficiencies induced by limited liabilities). Property ii) is at the heart of this result. If goods are not complementary at the extensive margin, then no network externality arises, and the economy would be statically (constrained) efficient. Note that the same extensive-margin complementarity is underlying some of the results in
Elliott et al., 2022
and
Acemoglu and Tahbaz-Salehi, 2024
.

We now show that our assumption of perfect substitutability in quality actually induces fewer inefficiencies than a more general quality function would. In particular, suppose that the quality function of a final producer
i
takes the following generic form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\chi(A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}),

where
\chi
is a constant-return-to-scale aggregator, which is increasing and concave in each argument. Importantly, we impose
\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial A_{j}}>0,\frac{\partial^{2}\chi}{\partial A_{j}^{2}}<0\ \forall j
. Hence, we allow for arbitrary substitutability or complementarity in quality (intensive margin), while retaining complementarity in quantity (extensive margin).

Stationary equilibrium.

We analyze the equilibrium of the dynamic economy.
Intermediate producers maximize expected operating profits net of product design costs:

\displaystyle V(s,x;z)=\max_{s,x}\ \mathcal{D}(s,x)\left(\tilde{A}(s;\textbf{s}_{-i};z)-x\right)-wq(s).

where
\mathcal{D}(s,x;N)=\frac{\theta\lambda\mathcal{P}(s,x;N)}{\delta}
and
\tilde{A}(s;\textbf{s}_{-i})\equiv\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial A_{i}}A_{i}
.
Notice that the only difference with respect to the baseline dynamic model is that match surplus potentially depends on the specialization vector of all the other input providers
\textbf{s}_{-i}
. For a generic CES aggregator with substitution parameter
\sigma\in[0,1]
, it holds that
\tilde{A}_{i}=A_{i}^{\sigma}\chi^{1-\sigma}
, which nests our baseline model for
\sigma=1
.
Up to the generalized definition of
\tilde{A}
instead of
A
, equilibrium specialization of intermediate producers with productivity
z
producing input
i
is the same as in the baseline dynamic model and given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z;N)\bigg[A^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z),\bm{s}_{-i})+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))}{\phi(s^{\star}(z))}(\tilde{A}(s^{\star}(z),\bm{s}_{-i};z)-x^{\star}(z))\bigg]=wq^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z)).

Social planner problem

The social planner problem reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\max_{s_{j}(z)}

\displaystyle\ m\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int D_{j}(z)\tilde{A}(s_{j}(z),\bm{s}_{-j};z)\ \gamma(z)dz+\psi\log(1-\ell)

s.t.

\displaystyle D_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{\delta}\frac{\mu}{m}\lambda\phi(s_{j}(z))e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(z,\bar{z})}\prod_{v\neq j}f_{v},

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\delta}{(1-\delta)\left(1-\prod_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}\right)},

\displaystyle\ell=m\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int q(s_{n}(z))\gamma(z)dz.

Efficient specialization of intermediate producers with productivity
z
producing input
j
solves:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z;N)\bigg[\tilde{A}^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z),\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{-j})+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))}{\phi(\mathcal{S}(z))}\bigg(\tilde{A}(\mathcal{S}(z),\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{-j};z)-\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}[\tilde{A}(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z}),\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{-j};\tilde{z})]+

\displaystyle(N-1)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[\tilde{A}(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z}),\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{-j};\tilde{z})]-\frac{1-\delta}{\delta+(1-\delta)f^{N}}f^{N}Ne^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[A(\mathcal{S}(\tilde{z}),\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{-j};\tilde{z})]\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi}{1-Nm\bar{q}}\ q^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}(z))-(N-1)\theta\frac{f^{N-1}}{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\tilde{A}(\mathcal{S}_{-j},\bm{\mathcal{S}};z)}{\partial\mathcal{S}_{j}(z)}\right].

Efficient specialization with a generalized quality function equals (
A.16
) up to the presence of the final term, which nets out specialization costs by the marginal change in the value of input
j
by increasing the specialization of another complementary input.
Hence, we observe that a further inefficiency arises if inputs are not perfect substitutes at the intensive margin. The reason is that intermediate producers do not internalize the effect of their specialization on the match surplus generated by other input providers:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{W}}{\partial s(z)}\bigg|_{s(z)=s^{\star}(z)}\propto

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\max\{\tilde{z}\}|\tilde{z}\leq z}\left[\tilde{A}(\bm{s}^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})\right]}_{\text{business-stealing externality}}\underbrace{-\ x^{\star}(z)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{appropriability}\\
\text{externality}\end{subarray}}\underbrace{-\ (N-1)e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[\tilde{A}(\bm{s}^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]}_{\text{network externality}}

(A.29)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\vphantom{\int_{\underline{z}}^{z}}+\ N\frac{1-\delta}{\delta+(1-\delta)f^{N}}f^{N}e^{-\lambda\hat{\phi}(\underline{z},z)}\mathbb{\hat{E}}[\tilde{A}(\bm{s}^{\star}(\tilde{z});\tilde{z})]}_{\text{search externality}}\underbrace{\vphantom{\int_{\underline{z}}^{z}}-\ \frac{(N-1)e^{\lambda\bar{\phi}(z,\bar{z})}}{\lambda\phi^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\tilde{\bm{A}}(\bm{s}^{\star}_{-j};z)}{\partial s_{j}(z)}\right]}_{\text{production externality}}.

The new production externality pushes towards equilibrium under-specialization. Hence,
under the more general aggregator
\chi
, the economy features a further inefficiency: individual specialization influences not only the trading probability of other input providers but
also their match surplus. We characterize the efficiency properties of the economy with generalized quality function in the next Proposition
A.7
.

Proposition A.7

(Efficiency of the Dynamic Economy: Generalized quality function)
.

The economy with generalized quality function is efficient if and only if:

-\left(\mu(N)N-1\right)f^{\prime}(s^{\star}(z))\mathbb{\hat{E}}[\tilde{A}(s^{\star}(z);z)]-(N-1)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\tilde{\bm{A}}(s^{\star}_{-j};z)}{\partial s_{j}(z)}\right]=0,\quad\forall z\in[\underline{z},\bar{z}].

If
N<\frac{1}{\mu(N)}
, then the economy features under-specialization.

To sum up, when inputs are complements at the extensive margin, equilibrium over-specialization is more likely the more the production process is complex (high
N
) and the more substitutable input quality is (the closer
\sigma
is to
1
with CES quality function).

Free Entry

Until now, we have assumed that the mass of intermediate producers

Stationary equilibrium.

The equilibrium is the same as in the baseline dynamic model, up to the addition of the no-arbitrage condition governing the equilibrium mass of intermediate producers.
The no-arbitrage condition reads:

Social planner problem.

We consider the problem of a social planner who is constrained by the free entry condition (

Proof of Proposition
A.6
.

Comparing (
A.22
) with (
A.25
) we note that the benefits of higher robustness are evaluated differently by firms and by the planner. Firms obtain a marginal flow benefit
\mathbb{E}[x-\kappa]
smaller than the social marginal flow benefit
\mathbb{E}[A-\kappa]
. This force, in isolation, would induce under-investment in robustness. On the other hand, the planner discounts at the social discount factor
\delta(r)/[(1-f^{N})\mu]
, while private firms discount at their destruction rate

. As a consequence, the planner discounts the marginal surplus more than private firms, which pushes the equilibrium towards investing excessively in robustness. For given aggregate specialization, under-robustness obtains if and only if

. From this formulation, it is apparent that the lower the share of the match surplus accruing to final producers, the more likely under-robustness is.

. Since this condition never holds for more than one productivity level at a time, equilibrium specialization at the firm level is inefficient. Since the exit probability discounts the marginal benefit of specialization in (
19
), higher robustness induces higher equilibrium specialization, as well. Inefficiency of firm-level specialization directly follows from Corollary
1
.
∎
