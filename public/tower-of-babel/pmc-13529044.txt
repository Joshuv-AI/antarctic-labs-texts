Indian eye banking at crossroads: From volume to value
Authors: Manisha Acharya
Published: 2026

Eye banking has a long and impressive history in the field of ophthalmology, having enabled millions of sight-restoring surgeries through corneal transplantation. Globally, corneal blindness remains one of the foremost causes of avoidable vision loss, with the burden falling disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries with India bearing a significant share of this responsibility. In India, an estimated 1.3 million persons suffer from bilateral corneal blindness, with approximately 20,000 new cases added to this backlog every year.

Shortage of donor corneas is widely accepted as a major hindrance to treating cornea-blind patients through corneal transplantation. Understanding this shortage demands examining not only the number of corneas harvested but also how efficiently harvested corneas are used. As per available cumulative data with the Eye Bank Association of India (EBAI) and the Government of India, 47,676 corneas were retrieved and 29,057 were utilized in transplants in the year 2023, still far short of the estimated national requirement of 100,000 corneal transplants per year.[1] Increasing eye donations therefore remains of paramount importance in eliminating corneal blindness, but so does the rigorous stewardship of every cornea that is donated.

In eye banking, evolution is seen both in practices and in policies. Growing emphasis on in-situ corneoscleral rim excision, usage of longer-term storage media, and promulgation of law upholding cornea recovery by trained eye bank technicians are just some of the visible signs of the progress achieved. Now more than ever, rigor in the processes through which a cornea passes before being made available for surgery has become a matter of cardinal importance. Concomitantly, eye banks have raised the bar on delivering safe and reliable tissues to surgeons in order to gain their trust and to ensure better patient outcomes. In many aspects, this demands standardization and a systemic approach which ultimately ushers in enhanced efficiency, easier replicability, reduced complications, and better monitoring of tissue quality. For an eye bank to deliver volume and value to the community at large, this perspective is important.

The EBAI, in collaboration with the National Programme for Control of Blindness (NPCB) and other government and nongovernment organizations, has made various amendments to improve the corneal procurement rate in India. The 2014 amendment to the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act (THOTA) and the promulgation of the Eye Bank Standard Operating Procedures in 2013 have provided the legislative and procedural scaffolding for a more organized and accountable national system. The resumption of eye bank services following the COVID-19 pandemic, which caused a precipitous fall in collections, further demonstrated both the fragility and the resilience of this ecosystem.

In the past few years, eye banks in many parts of our country have witnessed a significant increase in the collection of corneal tissues. Cornea collection in India grew steadily from 38,646 in 2007–08 to a historic peak of 71,709 donor eyes in 2017–18, nearly doubling over a decade, reflecting the cumulative impact of HCRP expansion, legislative support, and growing public awareness.[2] Yet this trajectory was abruptly disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic, which caused a precipitous fall in collections to 21,493 in 2020–21 and a corresponding decline in corneal transplants to 13,323, wiping out years of hard-won gains in a single year.[3]

Collections have since climbed steadily, with 47,676 corneas retrieved and 29,057 utilized in transplants in the year 2023—a sign of resilience, though still well short of the estimated national requirement of 100,000 corneal transplants per year.[1] Keeping the prevailing utilization rate in mind, India needs to collect at least 200,000 corneas annually to meet this target, a target that demands both scale and quality in equal measure. Achieving this requires attention to both dimensions of quality that define an effective eye bank: Result quality, measured through outcome indicators such as adverse reaction rate and discard rate, and Process quality captured through protocol deviation rate and compliance rate.[4] Where these indicators have been systematically tracked and acted upon, the results have been compelling: adverse reaction rates reduced more than eightfold, discard rates stabilized, protocol deviation rates brought to near zero, and compliance rates rising from 59% to 96%—trends demonstrating that quality improvement in eye banking is not aspirational but eminently achievable.[45]

A striking counter-narrative has, however, emerged from the postpandemic period. Despite substantially lower collection volumes, the number of corneal transplants performed in 2022–23 reached an all-time high in India. This apparent paradox reflects genuine qualitative improvement: better storage media, wider adoption of specular microscopy for tissue evaluation, and the increasing practice of using a single donor cornea for two lamellar recipients, the anterior stroma for a patient with keratoconus or stromal scarring, and the posterior Descemet membrane and endothelium for a patient with endothelial failure. The transition from short-term McCarey–Kaufman medium to longer-term storage solutions such as Optisol-GS[6] and the indigenously developed Cornisol has been particularly significant in extending the evaluation and utilization window for donor tissues. These gains affirm that eye banking is no longer a numbers game alone; the drift is decisively from collection to utilization, from volume to value.

A structural inequity, however, continues to define the national landscape. Of the over 740 centers registered under EBAI, only approximately 100 consistently report data, and a mere 21 centers account for 70% of all transplants performed in the country.[2] This concentration is not merely a statistical observation; it is a call to action. The vast majority of registered eye banks and eye donation centers contribute marginally to the national pool, representing an enormous latent capacity that remains untapped. Bridging this gap between leading centers and the broader network is perhaps the most consequential challenge facing Indian eye banking today.

Scaling eye banking in India requires confronting geography, human behavior, and institutional capacity simultaneously. Most eye banks function on a dual strategy of voluntary donation and proactive counseling of family members, yet the gap between awareness and action remains one of the most persistent challenges in the field. Studies from North India have documented that while awareness of eye donation is high among the general public, this awareness does not reliably translate into actual donation at the critical moment of grief. Religious beliefs, myths, and the emotional state of the family at the time of bereavement are significant barriers, and awareness campaigns alone, however well intentioned, are insufficient to overcome them.[7]

What makes the difference is the trained counsellor. The impact of a skilled Eye Donation Counsellor (EDC), present at the right moment, equipped with the right communication tools, and sensitive to the family’s emotional state, has been consistently demonstrated to improve consent rates in hospital settings.[8] Equally important is the awareness and attitude of the broader healthcare workforce: Doctors, nurses, and paramedical staff who interact with families of the deceased are in a unique position to facilitate or impede the donation conversation.[9] Sensitization of healthcare workers is therefore not peripheral to the HCRP; it is foundational to its success.

The Hospital Cornea Retrieval Programme, operating through trained EDCs, has proven to be the most effective scalability strategy available to Indian eye banks. Several advantages follow from hospital-based tissue retrieval: availability of reliable medical history for donor screening, access to tissues from younger individuals with better endothelial quality, a shorter interval between death and corneal excision, and greater cost-effectiveness compared to community-based retrieval.[7] The program also allows EDCs to develop relationships within hospitals getting to know nursing staff, ward supervisors, and mortuary personnel, which facilitates early notification of potential donors.

Telephonic notification and counseling have emerged as an effective adjunct to in-person HCRP, enabling tele-counsellors to initiate donor identification and preliminary counseling remotely, ensuring that no potential donor is missed due to delays in physical deployment.

The hub-and-spoke model provides the organizational architecture within which these strategies operate most effectively. It envisages a parent eye bank managing core functions of evaluation, serology, quality assurance, and distribution, supported by satellite Eye Collection Centres handling initial retrieval. This model reduces costs, enables standardized training and quality monitoring from a single center, and ensures uniformity across the network.

The resulting four-point strategy—voluntary donations, HCRP through EDCs, telephonic screening by tele-counsellors, and a network of nodal centers within a few hours’ travel distance from the parent eye bank—is simple and replicable.[7] It has demonstrated measurable impact where implemented systematically. However, a major challenge remains in ensuring the availability of an adequate and skilled workforce to sustain it, a challenge that standardized training and competency assessment must address.

Lack of trained staff and inefficient operations are significant barriers to the functioning of a successful eye bank. While the challenge is significant, it is amendable. Periodic or annual assessment of the corneal tissue retrieval technique performed by eye bank technicians is an essential responsibility of any eye bank committed to quality. The Rubric for Assessment of Eye Bank Professionals for Eye Retrieval (RAEPER), developed as a structured competency assessment tool modeled on the ICO-OSCAR framework, addresses this need directly.[10] It assesses each step of the corneoscleral rim excision process, grading the assessee as novice, beginner, or competent, usable both on-site and in a simulated wet-lab setting. Validated in an Indo-American study conducted simultaneously at centers in India and the United States, RAEPER has demonstrated cross-cultural applicability and high inter-rater reliability.[11] It is useful not merely as an assessment instrument but as an educational intervention identifying procedural gaps and enabling targeted retraining rather than generic refresher courses.

Complementing RAEPER is the Eye Donation Counsellor Skill Evaluation Tool (EDC-SET), developed to standardize competency assessment for the counseling workforce who are the frontline personnel most directly responsible for consent rates and retrieval volumes.[12] Together, RAEPER and EDC-SET represent a coherent framework for workforce quality management across the two most critical roles in any eye bank. Building on this foundation, the Eye Bank Association of India has taken a significant step forward with the introduction of the Eye Recovery Technician Certification Program, a structured, nationally recognized certification that formally acknowledges the expertise of eye bank recovery professionals and sets a new benchmark for competency in the field. The formalization of such career pathways with defined training curricula, competency milestones, and progression opportunities, transforms eye banking from a task-based activity into a recognized profession, with meaningful implications for recruitment, retention, and the sustained quality of the workforce over time.

The future of Indian eye banking will be shaped by the convergence of technology, policy, and an unwavering commitment to reaching those who need sight most. The increasing adoption of lamellar keratoplasty techniques like DALK, DSAEK, and DMEK has already begun to transform the mathematics of tissue utility: A single donor cornea can now restore sight to two recipients, effectively doubling the therapeutic yield of every donation without requiring a single additional donor. Precut tissue preparation by specialized eye banks, already practiced by leading centers, has the potential to extend this efficiency further across the network.

Equitable distribution remains an unfinished agenda. A freely accessible, transparent national corneal transplantation registry linked with better data tracking, unique patient identification, and a central distribution system is not a distant aspiration but an operational necessity. Digital tools, including teleophthalmology platforms and real-time donor notification systems, are already beginning to reduce the interval between death and retrieval, which is the single most critical variable in corneal tissue quality. Measuring this efficiency systematically, through composite indices that capture not merely collection volumes but the full arc from retrieval to transplantation, will allow Indian eye banks to benchmark themselves rigorously and improve meaningfully.

Eye banking in India stands at an inflection point. The foundations, be it legislative, organizational, and educational, have been laid with care and with purpose. What remains is to build upon them with the same rigor, the same standardization, and the same systemic thinking that has defined the best of what Indian eye banking has achieved. The goal, ultimately, is singular: not volume alone, but value. Value to the corneal blind patient who waits, and value to the donor family whose act of generosity makes it all possible.
