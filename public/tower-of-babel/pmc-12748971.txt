Neurocognitive correlates of spatial thinking mediate the relation between socioeconomic status and STEM achievement
Authors: Jazelle Pilato, Robert Cortes, Emily Grossnickle Peterson, David Uttal, Robert Kolvoord, Adam Green
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe disparity of success in STEM between students from high and low socioeconomic backgrounds is a significant educational concern. Research in STEM education has shown that spatial skills play a role in learning, but limited research has examined whether spatial thinking could help to explain socioeconomic status (SES) disparities in STEM. The current study investigated whether spatial thinking could help to explain SES disparities, as measured by parental education and family income, across high school students’ STEM achievement at the cognitive (N = 303) and neural (N = 44) levels. The current study investigated a mostly middle- and high-income sample that was highly educated (majority of parents with a bachelor’s degree or higher). Results demonstrated a significant relation between SES and spatial ability, which partially mediated the relationship between SES and STEM achievement. Analysis of functional neuroimaging data during a spatial task revealed that SES was associated with increased activation in the premotor cortex and that this activity significantly mediated the SES-STEM achievement relation. These findings suggest that spatial skills are a key target to support students’ STEM outcomes along the socioeconomic gradient.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29345-z.Subject terms: Cognitive neuroscience, Psychology

Introduction

Socioeconomic status (SES) refers to an individual’s social and economic standing, which is utilized in research to quantify aspects of one’s quality of life and the potential opportunities available to someone, considering various societal constraints1–3. Due to the complex interplay between socioeconomic factors and education, neurocognitive processes stand as a critical component to better understand potential mechanisms in the relationship between socioeconomic factors and educational outcomes. Research has shown a relationship between SES and neurocognitive processes at the cognitive2,4 and neural levels3,5,6. Neuroscience methods can provide valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms that contribute to socioeconomic differences in academic outcomes. Specifically, neuroscience can inform how environmental factors associated with SES influence cognitive development. Moreover, by using methods from cognitive neuroscience, researchers can better understand why individuals from different socioeconomic backgrounds may exhibit distinct cognitive patterns3,4. By examining neurocognitive processes underlying learning and achievement, researchers can identify barriers to educational attainment and design interventions to reduce these disparities.

One neurocognitive process important to achievement in science, engineering, technology, and mathematics (STEM) domains is spatial thinking7–9. Spatial thinking involves the ability and capacity to mentally represent visual elements (i.e., non-linguistic, symbolic information), perform transformations and modifications on these elements, relate these elements to each other, and recreate aspects of these elements10,11. Spatial thinking has been shown to be malleable and improve with short instruction (see meta-analysis by Uttal and colleagues12) and related to STEM outcomes9,13. This malleability makes spatial thinking a critical neurocognitive skill to potentially help reduce disparities in STEM education. Spatial thinking is essential to learning and achievement in STEM domains due to the reliance on visuals to communicate scientific information14 and the abstract nature of many STEM concepts that requires mental models of the information15. Due to the importance of spatial information in STEM domains, spatial thinking has been a topic of interest to researchers attempting to increase achievement in STEM.

In addition, differences in cognitive measures of spatial thinking have been seen between students from differing socioeconomic backgrounds4,16. However, prior research has focused on SES disparities in spatial thinking during childhood, with little known about the effects of SES on spatial thinking during adolescence. Since SES disparities in spatial thinking have been previously noted, spatial thinking is a critical neurocognitive process that may help to explain disparities in STEM domains. Although students from lower SES backgrounds tend to perform worse on standardized measures of spatial thinking, there is also evidence that lower SES students may harness spatial processing to succeed in tasks such as mathematics and reasoning17,18. Additionally, spatial thinking has been shown to improve with training (see Uttal and colleagues12, for a meta-analysis). Given that spatial thinking is a key skill for achievement in STEM education, the current study investigates how SES might impact neurocognitive processes involved in spatial thinking during adolescence.

Students from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds perform worse on standardized measures of STEM achievement than their peers from advantaged backgrounds19. The socioeconomic opportunity gap is seen across formal education, with gaps becoming apparent before students enter formal school in measures of school readiness20–23 and when they graduate in measures of educational attainment24. Many factors have been suggested to play a role in the differences regarding the opportunity gap (e.g., access to resources, school quality, cognitive stimulation in the home). Yet, the cognitive and neural mechanisms through which SES disparities in achievement persist throughout students’ time in school are not well understood.

Exploring the connection between socioeconomic status (SES) and educational outcomes can be enriched by integrating neural and cognitive measures. Research aimed at understanding how environmental factors influence cognitive and neural development—and, by extension, educational performance—have led researchers to probe the neural mechanisms that may link SES with academic achievement25–27. Neuroscience has been utilized to understand cognitive processes related to learning and education (see reviews28,29) and has demonstrated that SES relates to brain function (see reviews3,30). However, it is only recently that researchers have begun to examine these factors as critical constructs that can help explain learning outcomes17,18,27,31. The majority of this recent research utilizes only one measure of SES (often parental/maternal education) to examine the effects on learning outcomes and often within a highly educated group17,18. Research investigating the relationship between childhood SES and subsequent life outcomes is complex, and researchers cannot disregard the impact of sociopolitical factors—such as healthcare access, discrimination, housing insecurity, and income inequality—on these outcomes. Nonetheless, the current line of inquiry highlights neurocognitive processes as potential mediators in this relationship. As investigations into neurocognitive tasks deepen, evidence suggests that these processes help explain variations in educational performance associated with socioeconomic factors26,32,33. Research investigating SES, spatial thinking, and educational outcomes has not yet investigated neural function as a potential mediator of this relation. Viewing neural and cognitive correlates as potential intermediaries opens new avenues for understanding and addressing educational disparities.

Despite the limited research investigating the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying SES disparities in STEM education, there is extensive research suggesting that spatial thinking and STEM learning are related7,9,13,34,35. Moreover, emerging research investigating the role of SES in spatial thinking provides preliminary evidence that spatial thinking may be one mechanism that helps explain SES disparities in STEM. Studies of SES and spatial thinking have shown that higher SES students perform better on measures of spatial thinking than lower SES students4,36–38. The majority of papers investigating SES and spatial thinking often rely on a composite of parental education and family income26,36,37 and do not look at these measures separately. These SES differences have been seen when controlling for language4 and across genders16. Not only do we see cognitive differences in spatial thinking, but these differences have also been shown to be related to educational outcomes such as mathematics in kindergarten, third, and sixth grade16. However, research on SES and spatial thinking has focused on cognitive differences16,39 and has yet to examine the neural mechanisms that support them. Research is needed to investigate the cognitive and neural components of key educational skills together, to build a more comprehensive understanding of how brain activity and cognitive processes may help to explain key relationships. Additionally, prior research has suggested that neural correlates of spatial thinking may uniquely contribute to STEM achievement above and beyond behavioral measures of task performance40. Investigation into the neurocognitive correlates of spatial thinking allows researchers to better understand how students from varying socioeconomic backgrounds might be completing tasks involving spatial thinking, which could be informative to future interventions and training to improve spatial thinking and STEM outcomes.

The neural correlates of spatial thinking have been well studied. Because spatial thinking is an assorted group of related skills (e.g., spatial visualization, mental folding) that can be applied across domains12,41,42, significant variations in neurocognitive systems are seen depending on the task43–47. One task that is especially important in STEM is mental rotation9,48–50. The mental rotation task (MRT) requires individuals to perform the action of rotating a two-dimensional or three-dimensional mental image in their mind51–54. Studies investigating the neural correlates of mental rotation demonstrate the importance of the premotor and posterior parietal cortex54–56. In a meta-analysis on neural correlates of mental rotation54, three regions were identified to be correlated with mental rotation problem-solving: the posterior parietal/occipital cortex, motor regions in the precentral cortex, and the prefrontal cortex (left inferior frontal cortex). Posterior parietal/occipital cortex activity was related to spatial image transformations, where precentral cortex motor regions suggested the use of motor simulations for solving mental rotation problems. Specifically, research suggests that activation in the premotor cortex is related to the specific activity of rotating a three-dimensional object55,57. In addition, the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) was robustly and consistently activated during the completion of the MRT throughout the studies included in the meta-analysis54. Notably, research has also suggested an important role of the IPS in the relationship between spatial thinking and mathematics58,59, demonstrating the importance of these neural correlates in STEM domains. The breadth of research documenting the neural correlates of spatial thinking measured via mental rotation makes it an ideal spatial task to better understand socioeconomic differences seen at the cognitive level.

Neuroscience research has been used to understand cognitive processes relating to learning and education (see reviews28,29) and has shown SES differences in the brain (see reviews3,30). In addition, research investigating SES and neural differences has shown differences in the frontal and parietal lobes both structurally30 and functionally17,18,60,61. Limited research has examined SES differences in spatial thinking and how this might relate to academic outcomes. However, research investigating other visual processes has shown activity in the visual ventral stream mediates SES-achievement relations62. Rosen and colleagues62 found that activation in the visual ventral stream during cued and memory-guided attention was a significant mediator in the SES-academic achievement, suggesting that differences in activation in visual processes might help to explain individual differences in academic achievement. Rosen and colleagues’62 study utilized income-to-needs ratio as a measure of SES and included a mostly middle- to high-income sample. Additionally, SES functional differences during executive functioning tasks have been demonstrated in the occipital cortex63, in the precentral cortex61,64, and the prefrontal cortex63,65,66, which the MRT research suggests these regions are also important during mental rotation.

Theories of the relationship between SES and the brain suggest that neurocognitive processes could help explain the relation between SES and life outcomes, including academic achievement3,26,27,32,33,67. Therefore, in the present study we tested Farah’s theoretical model3, which attempts to explain the mechanism through which SES differences are seen in outcomes by using neurocognitive correlates as a potential explanation (see Fig. 1). Within the current study, the term SES refers to measurements of both parental education and family income. Studies supporting this model have found that neurocognitive correlates help to partially explain some of the variance in the role of SES in outcomes such as mathematics achievement67, language/reading32, transitive inference26 and overall academic achievement27. Specifically, in the present study, we used cognitive measures of spatial thinking and neuroimaging to investigate the associations of family socioeconomic factors with spatial thinking and STEM academic achievement (Fig. 1). To investigate this topic, the following research questions were examined: RQ1. How do family socioeconomic factors predict (a) spatial thinking ability and (b) academic outcomes? RQ2. Does spatial thinking ability mediate the relation between family socioeconomic factors (family income and parental education) and academic outcomes? RQ3. Are family socioeconomic factors associated with differences in neural function during the completion of a spatial thinking task? RQ4. (a) Do the neural correlates of spatial thinking mediate the relation between socioeconomic factors and academic outcomes and (b) do the neural correlates mediate the relation above and beyond cognitive performance?

Depiction of Farah’s theoretical model3 applied to the current study. Farah’s original model places brain structure and function as a mediator between socioeconomic factors and life outcomes. Here we adapt that model for the current variables of interests, focusing on brain function using cognitive and neural correlates of spatial ability along with a focus on academic life outcomes, specifically in the case of this study STEM achievement. This model is used as a theoretical framework as well as a framework for the statistical analysis presented in the current study.

Hypotheses:

RQ1. Higher parental education and family income will be positively associated with (a) visuospatial ability (measured by a paper folding task) and (b) STEM achievement (math and science GPA).

RQ2. Visuospatial thinking ability (paper folding accuracy) will partially mediate the relation between parental education and family income and STEM achievement.

RQ3. Higher parental education and family income will be associated with differences in neural activity in the premotor, prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex during the mental rotation task.

RQ4. (a) Neural correlates of visuospatial thinking (BOLD activity during mental rotation task) will mediate the relation between parental education and family income and STEM achievement (science and math GPA) and (b) When including cognitive (paper folding task accuracy) and neural factors (BOLD activity during mental rotation task) in the mediation model, both will significantly partially mediate the relation between parental education and family income and STEM achievement (science and math GPA).

RQ1a: Do family socioeconomic factors predict spatial ability?

To investigate whether socioeconomic factors predicted spatial ability, regression analyses were run to see if parental education and family income predicted spatial ability, as measured by performance on the paper folding task. Regression analyses for parental education (N = 302) and income (N = 104) were run separately to retain maximum data for analysis. Additionally, research has shown that education and income measures of SES can have differing relations with cognitive outcomes and should be evaluated separately in models2. The model including parental education, gender, and age explained a significant proportion of variance in spatial ability, adjusted R2 = 0.04, F(3, 298) = 4.93, p = .002. As parental education increased, spatial ability scores increased, Β = 0.75, t(302) = 3.12, p = .002 (Fig. 2). The model including family income, gender, and age also explained a significant proportion of variance in spatial ability, adjusted R2 = 0.06, F(3, 93) = 5.92, p = .03. As family income increased, spatial ability scores also increased, Β = 0.99, t(104) = 2.46, p = .01 (Fig. 3). Results for regression analyses can be found in Table 2.

RQ2: Does spatial ability mediate the relation between socioeconomic factors and STEM achievement?

Mediation analyses were conducted separately for parental education and family income to investigate whether spatial ability mediated the relation between socioeconomic factors and academic achievement. Paths A and B in the mediation model (Fig. 5) were examined via regression analysis through research questions 1a and 1b. Path C of the mediation analysis, examined via regression analysis, showed that spatial ability was a significant predictor of academic outcomes when accounting for parental education (Β = 0.27, t(302) = 4.87, p < .001) and family income (Β = 0.22, t(104) = 2.10, p = .04) along with covariates of age and gender.

Regarding the mediation analysis for parental education, the effect of parental education on STEM academic achievement was found to be significantly partially mediated by spatial ability. We tested the significance of this indirect effect using bootstrapping procedures, including covariates of gender and age. Unstandardized indirect effects were computed for each of the 1000 bootstrapped samples, and the 95% confidence interval was computed by determining the indirect effects at the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The bootstrapped standardized indirect effect was 0.05, and the 95% confidence interval ranged from 0.01 to 0.09. Since the confidence interval did not cross zero, the partial indirect effect was statistically significant. Summary of indirect and direct effects can be found in Table 4.

RQ3: Do socioeconomic factors relate to neural correlates of spatial thinking?

Whole-brain activation with parental education and family income measures as parametric regressors exploring increased and decreased BOLD activity during the MRT (in separate models) was investigated. One significant cluster in the left premotor cortex (peak MNI coordinate: x = -44, y = 4, z = 50, BA = 6, k = 108 voxels) showed increased BOLD activation that was correlated to increases in parental education (Fig. 6). Family income was not significantly related to activation in the brain during the MRT task via whole-brain analysis.

RQ4a: Do the neural correlates of spatial thinking mediate the relation between socioeconomic factors and academic outcomes?

Mediation analysis included covariates of gender and age to evaluate the mediation of SES and STEM achievement by the neural correlates of spatial thinking. Mediation analysis was only run on parental education since no significant relation to neural activity was found regarding family income (RQ3). Based on the results from RQ3, a mask was made of the significant cluster in the left premotor cortex, and each participant’s activity in this region during the MRT was used for the mediation analysis. Mediation analysis, including covariates of gender and age, indicated that the activation in the PMC significantly mediated the relationship between SES and STEM achievement. Analysis revealed that the neural activity in the cluster significantly predicted STEM academic achievement, Β = 0.59, t(42) = 3.83, p < .001. The neural activity also explained a significant proportion of variance in STEM GPA, adjusted R2 = 0.29, F(4, 40) = 5.60, p = .001.

Mediation analysis demonstrated that the relation between parental education and STEM academic achievement was partially mediated by neural activity with no significant direct effect found between parental education and STEM academic achievement in this subset of the sample (but significant in the full study sample). Mediation analysis was still conducted due to past research suggesting the importance of conducting mediation analysis on these relations because of the potential role of the effect of the independent variable being transmitted only by way of the mediator and thus a direct effect would not be present68. Moreover, mediation analysis without a direct effect often has a reduced focus on the causal steps approach to mediation, which past work has proposed can be beneficial69. Additionally, past work has suggested that if concerns of power are present in mediation analyses, the direct effect can be underpowered to detect a significant effect when the mediated effect is not due to variations in standard error70. Past work suggests that the mediating relation can still be meaningful, just that the relations between the independent variable and dependent variable might only be due to the mediator since one cannot confirm a direct effect. Thus, we still tested the significance of this indirect effect using bootstrapping procedures. The bootstrapped standardized indirect effect was 0.33, and the 95% confidence interval ranged from 0.13 to 0.55. Since the confidence interval did not cross zero, the partial indirect effect was statistically significant. Summary of indirect and direct effects can be found in Table 6.

RQ4b: Do the neural correlates of spatial thinking mediate the relation between socioeconomic factors and academic outcomes above and beyond cognitive performance?

In the present study, performance on MRT was not correlated with activation in premotor cortex during task performance (r = .18, p = .25). Since there was a non-significant correlation between cognitive and neural measures, it is thus important to understand the unique contributions of these related measures to academic achievement. Therefore, to investigate if the neural activation mediated the SES-STEM relation above and beyond behavioral performance, mediation analysis for the effect of parental education on STEM academic achievement including both premotor activation and MRT performance was conducted. This analysis found the relation to be significantly partially mediated by premotor activity but not MRT performance (see Table 6). We tested the significance of this indirect effect using bootstrapping procedures, including covariates of gender and age. Unstandardized indirect effects were computed for each of the 1000 bootstrapped samples, and the 95% confidence interval was computed by determining the indirect effects at the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The bootstrapped standardized indirect effect was 0.33, and the 95% confidence interval ranged from 0.12 to 0.55. Since the confidence interval did not cross zero, the partial indirect effect was statistically significant (Fig. 7). Summary of indirect and direct effects can be found in Table 5.

Discussion

Given past research documenting differences in achievement among students of different socioeconomic backgrounds71–74, the current study investigated whether SES influences neurocognitive processes that are important to education. The present study examined the role of SES in spatial thinking, a key cognitive process important to STEM education, in high school students. Results demonstrated a relation between SES and spatial thinking at the cognitive and neural levels. Additionally, spatial thinking helped to explain the relationship between SES and STEM achievement. Our main findings were twofold. First, SES predicted adolescents’ STEM achievement and spatial thinking at the cognitive and neural levels. We found that family income and parental education were both related to students’ spatial thinking at the cognitive level, but only parental education was related to STEM achievement. At the neural level, results demonstrated that parental education significantly correlated with activity in the left premotor cortex during a spatial task. Second, spatial thinking at the cognitive and neural levels partially explained students’ from lower-SES backgrounds differences in STEM achievement. Spatial thinking at the cognitive level partially mediated the relation between SES (both parental education and family income) and STEM achievement, and activity in the left premotor cortex during a spatial task partially mediated the relation between parental education and STEM achievement. Participants in the current analyses were mostly middle/high-SES, thus generalization of results to participants in the lowest-end of the SES continuum should be taken with caution. These findings suggest the educational relevance of spatial thinking as a critical neurocognitive process in understanding the SES-achievement relationship in STEM education. Results from this study add to past research demonstrating that spatial skills are a pivotal target to help improve students’ STEM achievement.

Consistent with our predictions, parental education, and family income were significantly related to spatial thinking ability at the cognitive level. However, only parental education was correlated to neural activity during spatial thinking. Regarding the cognitive results, students whose parents had more education and came from families with higher incomes had higher spatial ability. For neural results, parental education was positively correlated with activity in the left premotor cortex. Results investigating spatial thinking at the cognitive level demonstrate a relation between SES (parental education and family income) and spatial thinking that is consistent with prior literature. Studies conducted with younger students have documented a relation between spatial thinking and both family income16,37 and parental education4,38. The results from the current study add to the limited body of research investigating spatial thinking and SES and demonstrate that these associations are also observed in older adolescents. Most research investigating the impacts of socioeconomic factors and neurocognitive outcomes has been focused on younger children due to research suggesting that socioeconomic factors have robust impacts during the earlier years of life1. Considering this, the results of the current study suggest that SES differences in spatial reasoning are still present in adolescents.

SES was correlated to activation in the left premotor cortex during a mental rotation task. Specifically, the higher the parental education, the higher the activation in the left premotor cortex. No significant correlations were seen between neural activation and family income nor in the neural activation negative direction for either family income or parental education. Past research, utilizing the family investment model, has suggested that parental education may impact parents’ investments (e.g., time, resources) in their children and that this impacts development of academic skills75,76. Research on family income often uses the family stress model, which proposes that lower income leads to economic and psychological distress. This distress can alter parenting practices—such as increasing harsh parenting or disrupting routines—which in turn affects children’s development77,78. These models suggest differing mechanisms for how parental education and family income are related to children’s outcomes, which could potentially contribute to the differences in significant neural results found in the current study. The findings of left premotor activity during the mental rotation task are consistent with prior literature showing increased activity in this region during mental rotation55,56,58,59. Research has shown that activity in the premotor cortex is related to the use of motor simulation54. This is specifically seen during the act of mentally rotating three-dimensional objects55,57. Research has suggested that increased activity in the premotor cortex could indicate preparatory motor processes or motor imagery during the MRT54,56, which is the act of manipulating information stored in working memory79. Thus, it was not surprising to see significant activity in the left premotor cortex; however, the present study also demonstrated that SES was significantly correlated with more activation in the left premotor cortex. This could suggest that higher-SES students are using more active mental manipulation or motor imagery when solving these problems than their lower-SES peers. Behavioral studies have shown that motor processes support spatial imagery tasks, and that the use of these can be beneficial to success in the task80,81. In addition, prior research has suggested that students from low-SES backgrounds might rely on spatial neurocognitive systems to perform on par with their high-SES peers during mathematics and reasoning tasks17,18. Past research in this domain only has utilized correlations between neural activity and task-based accuracy. The present study adds to past research by demonstrating differences in a larger academic domain – STEM GPA. The current study did not find that lower-SES students had higher activation in spatial regions compared to high-SES students, but this could be due to the task (a spatial task) design used in the current study. While the current study did not investigate differences in verbal and visuospatial processing due to study design limitations, results did suggest differences in reliance on the premotor cortex by SES during a spatial task. Future research should consider regions activated during STEM-based tasks to see if variations are found across broader areas, such as verbal regions, or if these differences are specific to spatial regions.

In addition to the relations between SES and spatial thinking, the current study found that spatial thinking partially mediated the relation between SES and STEM achievement. Importantly, SES was significantly related to STEM achievement when measured via parental education and family income in the larger sample, such that students whose parents had more education and who came from families with higher incomes had higher STEM GPAs. These results are consistent with prior research documenting SES differences in science and mathematics standardized test scores19,73, but also demonstrate this in science and mathematics GPA. This is important when considering the impact of GPA on college acceptance and success in STEM majors in college82,83.

Besides the relations between SES and STEM achievement, spatial thinking was found to partially explain the SES-STEM achievement relation. The mediating role of spatial thinking was specifically seen when considering parental education’s relation to STEM achievement such that students whose parents had more education had higher activation in the left premotor cortex and this helped to explain STEM GPA differences. Findings suggest that spatial thinking can help to partially explain the SES-STEM achievement relation. This study replicates prior findings in younger students suggesting a mediating role in STEM achievement in older students as well. Johnson and colleagues16, found that spatial skills partially mediated the relation between SES (as measured by income) and mathematics achievement in kindergarten, 3rd, and 6th graders. Results from the current study expand upon these findings and demonstrate a similar mediation for a different measure of SES – parental education – in high school students across mathematics and science GPAs. Interestingly, in the current study, mediation analysis investigating family income was significant only in the cognitive sample. This is consistent with past research showing that parental education typically shows larger effects on child outcomes, especially educational outcomes, than income84–86. Results from the present study add to past research demonstrating that spatial skills are a key target to help improve students’ STEM outcomes along the socioeconomic gradient. The results of this study may be informative to future interventions that are aimed at reducing the SES opportunity gap in STEM education.

The current findings suggest that differences in functional activation in the premotor cortex are correlated with parental education. Furthermore, this activity partially mediated the relation between parental education and STEM achievement. This was also found to be a significant mediator above and beyond behavioral performance during the task. This significant mediation demonstrates the educational relevance of this neuromarker such that SES-related neural differences in activation are associated with better STEM academic performance. More research is needed to understand the implications of this activity in STEM education. While spatial thinking has been shown to be important to STEM learning7, understanding the role of correlates to premotor activity, such as motor imagery, in science-specific tasks could be beneficial to understanding how to support STEM learning. Past research investigating potential mechanisms in the SES-achievement relationship has shown that neural activity can mediate these associations. Similar analysis was conducted by Rosen and colleagues62, and they found that activation in the visual ventral stream during cued and memory-guided attention was a significant mediator in the SES-academic achievement relationship. The current study adds to past literature showing that neural correlates can help to illuminate potential mechanisms in SES-achievement relations. Additionally, investigation into neurocognitive strategies could provide a useful target to understand how students are solving these tasks. For example, it has been proposed that documented sex differences in spatial tasks may be due in part to differences in approaches to the tasks themselves16. In the mental rotation task, research has shown that males use more holistic (whole shape) spatial strategies whilst females use more analytic (feature matching) strategies87. Differences in spatial thinking due to SES may also stem from varied approaches, possibly linked to the use of motor imagery or active manipulation, as suggested by differences in premotor activity. However, further research is necessary to confirm this idea. One potential support for the use of motor imagery/active manipulation is through the use of gestures. Research has shown that gesturing interventions can be beneficial in improving lower-SES childrens’ spatial thinking and subsequent mathematics performance in preschoolers88. Exploring interventions in motor imagery/active manipulation could provide valuable information regarding the connection demonstrated in the current study regarding premotor activity and STEM achievement.

Limitations and future directions

The current study has several key limitations. First, the participants included in the current analysis do not include members from the lowest end of education or income, and the majority of participants were white and non-Hispanic. Most participants in this sample had parents who had at minimum graduated college and the average income was approximately $100,000 over the federal poverty level. This leads to a significant limitation for applying the results to students from low-SES backgrounds. Although SES-related differences in brain structure and function have been noted across the entire SES continuum, greater differences are typically seen at the lower end63. Results of the current study might underestimate the effect of SES on outcomes due to this use of middle-high SES participants. Additionally, this use of middle-high SES is especially important when considering which SES factors were significant in the current study. Since participants were generally middle-high SES, the factors that were not always significant in the current study (family income), might be more influential across the SES gradient, just at lower levels of SES. Limitations regarding generalizability across participants of different racial and ethnic backgrounds and along the SES continuum must be considered when interpreting the results. Second, for the mediation analysis, directionality cannot be inferred due to the timing of data collection for the variables of interest and limitations regarding the use of cross-sectional data. Thus, the current results cannot infer causality and should be interpreted as exploratory. Additionally, caution should be taken when comparing the cognitive and neural results in the current study due to differences in the measurement of parental education and differences in tasks measuring spatial skills. In the cognitive analysis, parental education was measured via student report of mother’s education, whereas in the neural analysis, parental education was measured via parent report of their own education, which included fathers as well as mothers. Due to potential variations in reporting, any direct comparison in results should be interpreted cautiously. Studies have suggested that couples often have similarly reported education history89–91, so there is some evidence to suggest a similarity between fathers’ and mothers’ education. Regarding differences in spatial skill tasks across the two studies (paper folding and mental rotation task), research has demonstrated that paper folding and mental rotation are two key elements of spatial thinking and that they share several underlying cognitive processes92 such as both requiring dynamic spatial transformations of intrinsic shapes93. These tasks also differ in some ways, such as rigidity (mental rotation is a rigid transformation, but paper folding is not)93. Inclusion of both of these tests in the current study highlight similarities and differences in the connection between SES and spatial thinking across spatial thinking tasks, with parental education relating to both tasks and family income only relating to paper folding skills. Further, the present analysis did not include a measure of verbal ability, which has been shown to have large SES-neural differences3,4,31. Although a measure of verbal ability measured via PSAT scores was available for most students in the sample, it was not included in the analysis due to research suggesting caution in including standardized IQ/testing measures as covariates in neurocognitive studies94–96. Finally, it is important to note that the relations examined in the neural analysis may be underpowered due to the small sample size.

Results from the current study suggest that SES relates to neurocognitive processes important to education, specifically spatial thinking. More research is needed to better understand this relationship in a more representative sample with more participants from low-SES backgrounds. In addition, future investigations could address topics regarding strategy differences, which would be beneficial for intervention development. Due to the importance of differences in STEM achievement seen in students of differing socioeconomic backgrounds and the role of the premotor cortex, future work should examine if supports for motor imagery, such as gesturing, could be a beneficial intervention for the SES-achievement relationship. In addition, the current study examined the role of spatial thinking in domain-general tasks (paper folding and mental rotation tasks). Research suggests that domain-specific spatial thinking tasks differ from domain-general ones because domain-specific spatial tasks tend to be more nuanced and may involve a distinct set of spatial thinking skills97. Considering neurocognitive differences during a STEM-specific task could demonstrate more robust relations between SES, spatial thinking and STEM achievement and help to better understand exactly how spatial thinking is being applied in STEM education.

Conclusion

Results from the current study demonstrate the importance of spatial thinking in understanding the SES-achievement relationship in STEM domains and highlight premotor activity as a neural correlate that partially explains this relation. Past research has shown that premotor cortex activation during spatial cognition has been causally linked to active spatial manipulation40,57. This finding could be beneficial for the creation of training and interventions to assist low-SES students in STEM classes. These findings add to our knowledge of how SES relates to academic outcomes through exploration of spatial thinking, a key neurocognitive process in STEM education. Understanding the underlying processes through which SES impacts outcomes could have implications for reducing the SES opportunity gap and increasing diversity in STEM.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 346 adolescents enrolled in high school in the mid-Atlantic region of the United States. Participants were recruited between 2015 and 2017 through their public high schools as part of a larger study on spatial cognition40,55,98–100. Data for this study includes data from two cohorts collected a year apart. The average income for the areas in which the participants resided during this time period, as reported by the US census, was approximately $140,000 - $150,000, making this a high-income region. Informed written consent was obtained for all participants in accordance with guidelines established by the IRB and the IRB of the public schools where the students attended. Sample demographics reported for the full sample are based on the portion of participants who had at least one measure of SES (N = 303). A portion of the students completed functional neuroimaging during the Mental Rotation Task (MRT) and had parental reports of education and total household income; this subset of participants (N = 44) was included in the neural data analysis. All participants in the neural subset were right-handed, fluent English speakers, and reported no history of psychoactive drug use. The sample was majority White and non-Hispanic, with slightly more female participants than males. Parental education ranged from high school graduate to doctorate, with most participants’ parents having a bachelor’s degree or more. Procedure

Participants filled out demographic questions (including reports of mother’s education) and completed a measure of spatial ability (paper folding task101) during the spring semester of their sophomore or junior year. A large subset of students (N = 209) was invited to participate in follow-up testing during the summer/fall of the same year, where they completed computerized task-based measures of spatial thinking. Students in the neuroimaging sample completed the Mental Rotation Task (MRT)52,53 as well as other tasks not analyzed in the current study. At the end of their junior or senior year, approximately one year after the initial testing, participants involved in follow-up completed a final round of testing. During this time, parents were invited to fill out a survey that included questions regarding parental education and income. Participants’ high school science and mathematics GPAs (grade point averages) were collected from school administrators after students completed high school. As this was part of a larger study, only the procedures and measures used in the current study are presented (Fig. 8).

Timeline of procedure for variables included in analysis. Data was collected in three main timepoints across students’ junior and senior years.

Socioeconomic factors

Socioeconomic status can be measured in a multitude of ways but often includes measures of income, education, and occupation. In the current study, we investigated the role of parental income and parental education. These factors were measured one time during the study (one year) due to assumptions of the stability of parental education and income over the course of a year. Specifically, parental income, was measured via parent responses to “What is your yearly current household income in US dollars before taxes?”. This question focused on income over the last year. Parents reported total household income in 13 bins, and the mean of the income bins was used except for the lowest (under $24,999) and highest (More than $300,000) bins, which were designated $24,999 and $300,000 respectively. Income data was available for 104 participants in the full sample and 44 participants in the neuroimaging sample. Additionally, only a portion of the sample reported family income (N = 107). For analysis using family income, data in analysis only included the participants who reported income. Participants with missing family income data were not included. Parental education was measured through student-reported maternal education and parent (mother or father) reports of their own education. Students responded to the question, “What degrees did your biological mother earn?” by reporting their mother’s education in 4 bins from “less than high school” to “post-graduate” degree. A binary variable was then created where participants were separated into two groups (1 = their mother completed college; 0 = their mother did not complete college). Parents reported their education by answering, “What is the highest level of education you have completed?”. Parents reported their education in 10 bins from “No schooling completed” to “Doctorate degree.” Of the 44 parents who reported their education, 32.56% (n = 14) reported their relationship as the father of the student, and 67.44% (n = 29) reported their relationship as the mother of the student. The cognitive analysis used student-reported maternal education because 98.37% reported their mother’s education, whereas only 38.11% had parent-reported education. For the neural analysis, parental education measured via parent report was used due to greater accuracy and more variability in the parents’ responses due to options presented on the survey. Parent reported education and student-reported mothers’ education were significantly correlated (n = 116, r = .79, p < .001). All measures of SES were z-scored relative to the study for all analyses.

Spatial ability tasks

Two spatial tasks were administered. The paper folding tasks was administered to the larger cognitive sample, and the mental rotation task was administered to the neural sample. Two different spatial tasks were used in the studies to minimize practice effects in the neural sample and to accommodate time and testing constraints in the cognitive study. Both paper folding and mental rotation are small-scale dynamic spatial skills12 and are related to each other93.

Paper folding task

In the Paper Folding Task101 (PFT) participants are asked to imagine the folding and unfolding of pieces of paper when a hole is punched through the folded paper (Fig. 9). Participants must select the correct depiction of the unfolded paper with the hole punched through it out of 5 options. Participants read the instructions, which included one practice question with feedback depicting the correct answer. Participants had 6 min to complete 20 questions (3 min for the first 10, then 3 min for another 10 questions). Participants received 1 point for each correct answer and were deducted 0.2 points for each incorrect response. Participants were not penalized for leaving a question blank. The total score for the 20 items was used in the analysis.

Paper folding task. An example item from the paper folding task. It is classified as an easier item, and the correct answer is C.

Mental rotation task

To measure the neural correlates of spatial thinking, we used functional brain imaging (i.e., fMRI) while participants completed a computerized version of the Mental Rotation Task53 (MRT; Fig. 10, stimuli from52) using an event-related design. During the 84 trials, participants saw two rotated images and indicated whether the images portrayed the same 3-dimensional object or mirror opposites. Participants had up to 7 s to respond “Yes” or “No” to the trial to indicate whether the objects were a match or not a match by pressing corresponding buttons with their right hand. Objects were rotated by either 50, 100, or 150 degrees with 24 trials of each angle of rotation. Twelve trials with 0 degrees of rotation were included to use as a control for analysis. A 2:1 ratio of the same object (TRUE) to different objects (FALSE) was used across all degrees of rotation trial types102. All the FALSE trials included objects that were mirror opposites of each other. Fixations between trials were jittered, ranging from 3 to 8 s between trials. Prior to entering the MRI scanner, participants received instructions and completed ten practice problems with the correct answer and feedback provided for each problem.

Mental rotation task. An example of the mental rotation task. It includes a time course description of how students saw screens for the current task. Items were displayed until the participant answered or for a maximum of seven seconds.

STEM academic achievement

Academic achievement was measured via science GPA and mathematics GPA (e.g., A = 4.0, A- = 3.7, B + = 3.3, B = 3.0). GPAs were weighted grade point averages for all classes completed in the respective subjects during high school, and students received one extra GPA point if their science/mathematics class was classified as Advanced Placement or International Baccalaureate (e.g., A = 5.0, A- = 4.7, B + = 4.3, B = 4.0) and 0.5 points if the class was dual enrollment (e.g., A = 4.5, A- = 4.2, B + = 3.8, B = 4). Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R-studio Version 4.02 using the tidyverse package, dplyr package, ggplot2 package, process and mediation package to test the model depicted in Fig. 11.

Depiction of mediation analysis investigated in study. This is the adapted version of Farah’s original model with the variables of interest in the study specifying measurement.

fMRI data acquisition

Neural image acquisition was acquired using a 3T Siemens Trio Scanner. A high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE image (voxel size = 1mm3, TR = 1900ms, TE = 2.52ms, flip angle = 90, FOV read = 250 mm, FOV phase = 100.0%, slice thickness = 1.00 mm) was used for structural imaging and registration of functional data. fMRI data was acquired from T2*-weighted echoplanar imaging sequence (37 3.0 mm transversal slices; voxel size = 3mm3, TR = 2000ms, TE = 30ms, flip angle = 300, FOV read = 192 mm, slice thickness = 3.0 mm). The first two volumes were excluded from the analysis to account for field stabilization.

fMRI data preprocessing

As described in a previous analysis of this dataset40, fMRI data processing was performed using FEAT (fMRI Expert Analysis Tool) Version 6.0 in FSL (FMRIB Software Library). General Linear Model-based analysis in FEAT uses FSL tools, including Brain Extraction Tool (BET; Smith, 2002), an affine registration tool, FMRIB’s Linear Image Registration Tool103,104 (FLIRT), and a motion-correction tool based on FLIRT103 (MCFLIRT). FEAT carries out standard-space registration after time-series statistics. FSL time-series statistics correct for temporal smoothness by applying pre-whitening105. The following pre-statistics processing was applied: spatial smoothing using a Gaussian kernel of FWHM 5 mm; grand-mean intensity normalization of the entire 4D dataset by a single multiplicative factor; high-pass temporal filtering (Gaussian-weighted least-squares straight-line fitting, with sigma = 50.0 s). Registration to high-resolution structural and, subsequently, standard space images was performed using FLIRT. Functional images were normalized to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space.

fMRI data analysis

A design matrix was fitted to each participant’s data as part of the GLM, with trials modeled as events within the task’s duration convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function. Whole brain analysis was conducted to investigate the correlation between SES and neural activation. The use of whole brain analysis was taken due to limited research regarding SES differences in spatial tasks at the neural level, so specific regions of interest could not be reliably identified. For the current task, duration included the onset of the stimulus to the onset of the response (maximum 7000 ms). Trials were modeled using the onset and duration of the entire trials. Based on past research54,102, only the TRUE trial types (i.e., images depicting the same 3D objects at 50, 100, or 150-degree rotations) were used for the current analysis. Control trials (i.e., 0 degrees of rotation) were used for the contrast. Voxelwise contrasts were generated for each participant using the contrast of both TRUE trials > Control trials and TRUE trials < Control trials. Group-level analyses were conducted using FLAME (FMRIB’s Local Analysis of Mixed Effects) in FSL106. For whole-brain group level parametric analysis, participants’ parental education and income were z-scored and then entered separately to create a parametric regressor in the design matrix for each subject to test whether these values were predictive of variance in BOLD signal (increases and decreases in functional activation) throughout the whole brain. Family income and parental education were included separately in the model due to past research showing that these measures of SES can have differing relationships with cognitive outcomes2. Cluster-based corrections for multiple comparisons were done using Gaussian random field theory (Z > 3.1; cluster significance: p < .01, corrected). Signal change from the significant cluster (average beta-weight for each participant) where BOLD signal correlated with parental education was then extracted and used in mediation analysis.
