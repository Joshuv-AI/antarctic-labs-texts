Abstract
In this study, we introduce a method for learning group (known or unknown) equivariant functions by learning the associated quadratic form x T ​ A ​ x x^{T}Ax corresponding to the group from the data. Certain groups, known as orthogonal groups, preserve a specific quadratic form, and we leverage this property to uncover the underlying symmetry group under the assumption that it is orthogonal. By utilizing the corresponding unique symmetric matrix and its inherent diagonal form, we incorporate suitable inductive biases into the neural network architecture, leading to models that are both simplified and efficient. Our approach results in an invariant model that preserves norms, while the equivariant model is represented as a product of a norm-invariant model and a scale-invariant model, where the “product” refers to the group action. Moreover, we extend our framework to a more general setting where the function acts on tuples of input vectors via a diagonal (or product) group action. In this extension, the equivariant function is decomposed into an angular component extracted solely from the normalized first vector and a scale-invariant component that depends on the full Gram matrix of the tuple. This decomposition captures the inter-dependencies between multiple inputs while preserving the underlying group symmetry. We assess the effectiveness of our framework across multiple tasks, including polynomial regression, top quark tagging, and moment of inertia matrix prediction. Comparative analysis with baseline methods demonstrates that our model consistently excels in both discovering the underlying symmetry and efficiently learning the corresponding equivariant function.

Introduction

Symmetry serves as a fundamental principle in modern deep learning, providing powerful inductive biases that enhance model generalization and sample efficiency
Cohen 2013
;
Cohen and Welling 2014
;
Finzi et al. 2021
;
Dehmamy et al. 2021
. When the symmetry group underlying a system is known a priori, this symmetry can be explicitly encoded into the neural network architecture through invariant or equivariant operations. This architectural integration ensures the model maintains equivariance with respect to the predefined group transformations present in the data manifold. The classical example is the Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which maintain translational equivariance through weight sharing across spatial dimensions.

Recent works have explored two primary approaches to incorporating symmetries: augmenting training data using symmetry structures
(
Benton et al. 2020
)
and designing architectures with intrinsic symmetry properties
(
Zaheer et al. 2017
;
Kicki et al. 2020
)
.
(
Benton et al. 2020
)
propose a data augmentation approach by parameterizing the data distribution while
(
Zhou et al. 2020
)
introduce a meta-learning framework through reparameterization of the network layers. In our work, we present a framework for learning group-equivariant functions through quadratic forms, emphasizing applications to orthogonal groups. Our analysis proves that these functions admit a canonical decomposition into two fundamental components: scale-invariant and norm-invariant terms.

Although substantial progress has been achieved in harnessing symmetries across various domains—including images
(
Anselmi et al. 2019
;
Kondor and Trivedi 2018
)
, graphs
(
Han et al. 2022
)
, and dynamical systems such as partial differential equations (PDEs)
(
Horie and Mitsume 2023
)
—significant challenges remain. A central difficulty in integrating these group symmetries within model architectures is the often-required explicit, a priori knowledge of the underlying symmetry. Furthermore, existing models frequently suffer from high computational demands, stemming from requirements such as null-space computations and implementation of exponential maps. This complexity limits their scalability and flexibility. Additionally, in many practical applications, the group action on the data may not be immediately apparent, making it difficult to effectively leverage these symmetries in current group-equivariant models.

Consequently, existing models are constrained in their ability to effectively manage complex group symmetries, especially within real-world data contexts. This limitation is similarly observed in dynamical systems, where simple symmetries in a low-dimensional state space can appear as highly non-linear transformations when projected into high-dimensional measurements. In this work, we propose a novel framework for learning equivariant functions, which integrates two invariant functions and is applicable regardless of whether the underlying symmetry group is known or unknown, given certain constraints.

Our approach focuses on orthogonal groups and their inherent geometric properties. Specifically, we are motivated by a fundamental property of the standard orthogonal group
O(n)
: its preservation of the Euclidean norm, which leads to orbits defined by distinct norm values. In addition, we also observe scale invariance in the case
O(n)
-equivariant functions. We demonstrate that this characteristic extends to other orthogonal groups preserving an arbitrary quadratic form,
x^{\top}Ax
, and we exploit these geometric correspondences to construct a systematic framework for symmetry discovery via neural networks. Furthermore, we explore the Lorentz group, which preserves the Minkowski norm and embodies the essential symmetry underlying the spacetime structure in physics. In localized regions of spacetime, where gravitational effects are negligible, physical laws exhibit Lorentz invariance in a manner consistent with special relativity.

In Section
4
, we provide a comprehensive mathematical framework for our proposed method, particularly with respect to orthogonal groups, establishing its scalability and efficiency as a model for real-world applications.

Contributions

•

We establish that any
G
-equivariant function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
G
being the orthogonal group preserving the quadratic form

•

We extend this representation to functions defined on tuples of input vectors via a diagonal (product) group action on the product space

Related Work

2.1
Group Equivariance

Significant progress has been made both theoretically and practically in symmetry learning for deep learning architectures
(
Kondor et al. 2018
;
Bekkers 2021
;
Finzi et al. 2021
;
Bronstein et al. 2021
;
Dehmamy et al. 2021
)
. The classical example is the G-equivariant neural networks (G-CNNs)
(
Cohen and Welling 2014
)
which extends CNN’s to accommodate a broader class of symmetry groups beyond translations. Building on this,
(
Kondor and Trivedi 2018
)
developed convolution formulations for arbitrary compact group actions, while
(
Cohen et al. 2019
)
further extended G-CNNs to homogeneous spaces through equivariant linear mappings.

Equivariant multi-layer perceptrons (EMLPs) for general groups were introduced by
(
Finzi et al. 2021
)
, leveraging null space computations to achieve equivariance; however, scalability challenges persist, especially for higher-order groups and also requires explicit knowledge of the group to encode the group representations. Similarly,
(
Otto et al. 2023
)
proposed a linear-algebraic approach that enforces symmetries through linear constraints and convex penalties, though this approach also encounters scalability limitations.

In related work,
(
Park et al. 2022
)
employed symmetric embedding networks to learn equivariant features for downstream tasks, although their method requires prior knowledge of the symmetry group. Contrastive learning methods were also developed by
(
Shakerinava et al. 2022
;
Dangovski et al. 2022
)
for self-supervised equivariant representations. Symmetry learning has found practical applications in domains such as protein structure prediction, where molecular properties require invariance for 3D rotations
(
Yim et al. 2023
)
.

2.2
Automatic Symmetry Discovery

Advances have also been made in the area of automatic symmetry discovery for neural networks.
(
Dehmamy et al. 2021
)
introduced Lie algebra convolution networks (L-Conv), achieving equivariance to continuous groups by leveraging Lie algebras. Complementary to this,
(
Benton et al. 2020
)
developed parameterized distributions over training data augmentations, while
(
Zhou et al. 2020
)
proposed a meta-learning approach that reparameterizes network layers to address symmetry adaptivity. Focusing on invariant representations,
(
Anselmi et al. 2019
)
explored regularization techniques for unsupervised orbit learning.
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
introduced LieGAN, a generative adversarial framework for symmetry discovery that leverages the principle of distribution preservation under group transformations.

More recently,
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
proposed Latent LieGAN, which approximates non-linear symmetries via non-linear group actions, particularly suited for high-dimensional dynamical systems. Additionally,
(
Karjol et al. 2023
)
introduced a structural decomposition approach for identifying locally symmetric, dihedral, and cyclic subgroups through multi-armed bandit algorithms.

Definition 3.1

(
Group
)
.

A group
(G,\cdot)
is a set G equipped with a binary operation
\cdot:G\times G\to G
satisfying the following properties:

•

Closure: For all
a,b\in G
,
a\cdot b\in G
.

•

Associativity: For all
a,b,c\in G
,
(a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)
.

•

Identity element: There exists an element
e\in G
such that for every
a\in G
,
e\cdot a=a\cdot e=a
.

•

Inverse element: For each
a\in G
, there exists an element
b\in G
(often denoted
a^{-1}
) such that
a\cdot b=b\cdot a=e
.

Definition 3.2

(

)

A

is a function
G\times X\to X,\quad(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x,
such that for all
g,h\in G
and
x\in X

•

Identity:
e\cdot x=x
, where
e
is the identity element of
G
.

•

Compatibility:
(g\cdot h)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)
.

(

)
.

Given a group action of
G
on a set
X
the

of an element

is the set:

G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x\mid g\in G\}.

Definition 3.4

(
General Linear Group
)
.

The

\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})

n\times n

Definition 3.5

(
Invariant Function
)
.

An

G

f(g\cdot x)=f(x)

g\in G

. Equivariance means

4
Proposed Method

We present a general framework for learning group-equivariant functions by embedding appropriate quadratic forms within our network architecture. Specifically, we focus on groups that preserve quadratic forms of the type “
x^{T}Ax
”, known as orthogonal groups. Our framework supports two cases: when the underlying group is known and when it is unknown. In the first case, we fix the quadratic form within the architecture. In the second case, we allow the quadratic form to be learned. We begin with a brief overview of quadratic forms before presenting our main contributions.

4.1
Quadratic form

Quadratic form is a type of mathematical expression in which a function of several variables involves terms of degree two (or order two). Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be an
n\times n
matrix and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In general, a quadratic form in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be written as,

q\mathopen{}\left({x;A}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}:=x^{T}Ax

(2)

Each quadratic form defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is uniquely determined by a symmetric matrix. This is because, we can write,

q(x;A)=x^{T}Ax=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\left[{x^{T}\mathopen{}\left({A+A^{T}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}x}\,\negthickspace\right]\mathclose{},\quad\forall x\in X

(3)

Here, the
\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\left({A+A^{T}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}
is the unique symmetric matrix for the quadratic form
q\mathopen{}\left({.;A}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}
. Hence, we can simply assume that
A
is symmetric matrix in any quadratic form
q(x;A)
.

4.1.1
Diagonalizing the Quadratic Form

Given a quadratic form
q(x;A)=x^{T}Ax
, where
A
is an
n\times n
symmetric matrix, we aim to simplify this form by transforming it into a
diagonal form
.

Since
A
is symmetric, it can be diagonalized by an orthogonal matrix
U
. This means there exists an orthogonal matrix
U
such that:

A=U^{T}DU,

(4)

where
D
is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of
A
.

Now, we can express the quadratic form in terms of this diagonalization as follows:

q\mathopen{}\left({x;A}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}=x^{T}\mathopen{}\left({U^{T}DU}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}x=\mathopen{}\left({Ux}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}^{T}D\mathopen{}\left({Ux}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}=y^{T}Dy,

(5)

where
y=Ux
. Thus, learning the quadratic form
q(x;A)
reduces to learning an orthogonal matrix
U
and a diagonal matrix
D
.

4.2
Orthogonal Group:
O(n;q)

Consider the set,

G:=\mathopen{}\left\{{U\in GL\mathopen{}\left({n;\mathbb{R}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}:U^{T}AU=A}\,\negthickspace\right\}\mathclose{},

(6)

which can easily be shown to be a Lie subgroup of
GL(n;\mathbb{R})
. Such groups are called orthogonal groups, denoted as
O(n;q)
, and they preserve the quadratic form
q(x;A)=x^{T}Ax
, meaning that
q(x;A)=q(Rx;A)
for all
R\in O(n;q)
.

Examples of orthogonal groups include the standard orthogonal group that preserves the Euclidean norm
x^{T}x
, and the Lorentz group, which preserves the Minkowski norm
x^{T}\eta x
where
\eta=\operatorname{diag}\left(1,-1,-1,-1\right)
, among others.

In this work, we focus on learning a function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
f
is equivariant under an orthogonal group
G
that preserves a given or unknown quadratic form
q(x;A)
. In other words,
A
may be known or unknown.

We now state our first result in the following theorem, which characterizes the set of orbits under the action of an orthogonal group
G
. Specifically, each
G
-orbit corresponds to a unique value of the quadratic form.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
A
be a non-zero
n\times n
matrix, and define
U:=\mathopen{}\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{T}Ax=0}\,\negthickspace\right\}\mathclose{}
. Let
G
be an orthogonal group that preserves the quadratic form
x^{T}Ax
, and consider the action of
G
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus U
. For a given
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, define the set
\mathcal{O}_{c}:=\mathopen{}\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{T}Ax=c}\,\negthickspace\right\}\mathclose{}
. Then, the set of orbits under the action of
G
is given by

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left({G}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}=\mathopen{}\left\{{\mathcal{O}_{c}:c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\And\mathcal{O}_{c}\neq\varnothing}\,\negthickspace\right\}\mathclose{}.

(7)

Using the above result, we show that any
G
-equivariant function can be expressed in a canonical form that combines a norm-invariant (equivalently,
G
-invariant) function and a scale-invariant function. This formulation is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
A
be a non-zero
n\times n
matrix, and define
U:=\mathopen{}\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{T}Ax=0}\,\negthickspace\right\}\mathclose{}
. Any
G
-equivariant function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}

f(x)=\phi_{s}\mathopen{}\left({\frac{x}{\|x\|_{A}}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}\cdot\phi_{n}\mathopen{}\left({\|x\|_{A}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{},

(8)

for some functions

\phi_{n}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}

\|z\|_{A}
denotes the norm associated with the quadratic form

\|z\|_{A}=\operatorname{sign}(z^{T}Az)\sqrt{|z^{T}Az|}

4.3
Diagonal group action

We now generalize our approach to the setting where the function acts on tuples of vectors rather than on individual vectors. Specifically, let
X
be a
G
-invariant subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus U
, where

U:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{T}Ax=0\},

and define the product space

X_{p}:=\underbrace{X\times X\times\cdots\times X}_{p\text{ times}}.

On
X_{p}
, the group
G
acts diagonally, that is,

g\cdot(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{p})=(g\cdot x_{1},\,g\cdot x_{2},\,\ldots,\,g\cdot x_{p}).

We recall the standard result (see Appendix, Classification of
p
-tuples up to the
A
-orthogonal group) which states that any two
p
-tuples of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that share the same Gram matrix (with respect to a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form) are related by an isometry
(
Jacobson 1985
)
. Building upon this well-established fact, we derive a new decomposition theorem for functions equivariant under the diagonal
G
-action on tuples. This new result, formalized in the following theorem, extends our previous method to a broader and more general setting.

Theorem 4.3

(Extended Decomposition for
G
-Equivariant Functions on Tuples)
.

Let
A
be a non-singular
n\times n
real matrix and define
Let

G:=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R}):g^{T}A\,g=A\}

be the orthogonal group preserving the quadratic form
x^{T}Ax
. Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
G
-invariant set. For any positive integer
p
, consider the diagonal action of
G
on

X_{p}:=\underbrace{X\times X\times\cdots\times X}_{p\text{ times}},

defined by

g\cdot(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{p})=(g\cdot x_{1},\,g\cdot x_{2},\,\ldots,\,g\cdot x_{p}).

Then every
G
-equivariant function

f:X_{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

can be decomposed as

f(x)=\phi_{s}\mathopen{}\left({\frac{x_{1}}{\|x_{1}\|_{A}}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}\cdot\phi_{n}\mathopen{}\left({\operatorname{Gram}(x)}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{},

(9)

where
x=\mathopen{}\left({x_{1},\ldots,x_{p}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}
and Gram matrix

\operatorname{Gram}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p})=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{T}Ax_{1}&\cdots&x_{1}^{T}Ax_{p}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{p}^{T}Ax_{1}&\cdots&x_{p}^{T}Ax_{p}\end{pmatrix},

encodes all pairwise inner products with respect to the quadratic form defined by
A
.

Remark 4.3
.

When

is non-singular, Theorem

4.4
Learning Equivariant Functions

Given data
\{\left(x_{i},y_{i}=f(x_{i})\right)\}_{i\in[m]}
, where
f
is a
G
-equivariant function for some known or unknown orthogonal group
G
that preserves the quadratic form
x^{T}A_{0}x
, we implement the functions
\phi_{s}
and
\phi_{n}
through neural networks. We denote these networks by the same notation, with
\phi_{s}
and
\phi_{n}
representing the scale-invariant and norm-invariant components of the function, respectively.

4.4.1
G
is Known

When the group
G
is known, we fix the corresponding quadratic form in our network architecture, specifically fixing the matrix
A
. In this case, we aim to minimize the following loss function:

\arg\min\limits_{\theta}\sum\limits_{i}\mathcal{L}\mathopen{}\left({y_{i},\phi_{s}\mathopen{}\left({\frac{x}{\|x\|_{A}}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}\cdot\phi_{n}\mathopen{}\left({\|x\|_{A}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}

(10)

where
\theta=\left\{\theta_{s},\theta_{n}\right\}
are the weights of the networks corresponding to
\phi_{s}
and
\phi_{n}
, respectively.

4.4.2
G
is Unknown: Symmetry Discovery Framework

In this case, the goal is to discover the underlying symmetry group
G
by learning the appropriate matrix
A
. We seek to minimize the following loss function:

\arg\min\limits_{A,\theta}\sum\limits_{i}\mathcal{L}\mathopen{}\left({y_{i},\phi_{s}\mathopen{}\left({\frac{x}{\|x\|_{A}}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}\cdot\phi_{n}\mathopen{}\left({\|x\|_{A}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{},

(11)

Thus, discovering the underlying group
G
corresponds to learning the appropriate symmetric matrix
A
. However, as discussed earlier, this problem reduces to learning the appropriate orthonormal matrix
U
and diagonal matrix
D
due to the diagonalization of the symmetric matrix
A
. Therefore, we can rewrite the objective function in Eq. (
11
) as:

\arg\min\limits_{U,D,\theta}\sum\limits_{i}\mathcal{L}\mathopen{}\left({y_{i},\phi_{s}\mathopen{}\left({\frac{\hat{x}}{\|\hat{x}\|_{D}}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}\cdot\phi_{n}\mathopen{}\left({\|\hat{x}\|_{D}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{},

(12)

where
\hat{x}=Ux
,

and
D
is a diagonal matrix. In this formulation,
U
captures the orthogonal transformation and
D
scales the components of the input
x_{i}
, thereby enabling the discovery of the underlying symmetry group.

For invariance tasks, we use only the
\phi_{n}
network and omit
\phi_{s}
. When the group acts on the input domain via a product action, we adopt the function decomposition from (
9
). This decomposition is applied both when the group is explicitly known—where we incorporate it into (
10
)—and when the group is unknown, in which case it is used in (
11
).

5.5
Limitations

Our method is currently restricted to generalized orthogonal groups and supports only standard group actions and diagonal (product) group actions. In future work, we aim to overcome these limitations by extending our framework to accommodate a broader range of symmetry groups and group actions.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a framework for learning group-equivariant functions using quadratic forms, with a focus on orthogonal groups. We showed that such functions can be expressed in a canonical form, incorporating both scale-invariant and norm-invariant components. Additionally, we extended our approach to a more general setting where the function acts on tuples of input vectors under a diagonal (or product) group action. In this extension, the equivariant function is decomposed into an angular component derived from the normalized first vector and a scale-invariant component based on the full Gram matrix of the tuple. This decomposition effectively captures inter-dependencies between multiple inputs while maintaining group symmetry.

Through extensive experiments, we demonstrated the ability of our method to discover underlying symmetries and efficiently learn equivariant functions across various tasks. In future work, we aim to extend our approach to broader symmetry groups beyond the general orthogonal group and explore a wider range of group actions.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a diagonal matrix defined as:

D=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}E_{i,i},

(16)

where

E_{i,j}=e_{i}e_{j}^{T}

e_{i}
denoting the standard basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

G
be the orthogonal group preserving the quadratic form
x^{T}Dx

such that:

Ax=\alpha e_{1},

(17)

where
\alpha
is an appropriate scaling factor such that
x^{T}Dx=x^{T}A^{T}DAx

Proof.

Define
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{d_{i}}e_{i}e_{i}^{T}
. Then, we have:

S^{-1}DS^{-1}=I,

(18)

where
I
is the identity matrix.

Consider the standard orthogonal group:

O(n)=\mathopen{}\left\{{R\in GL\mathopen{}\left({n,\mathbb{R}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}:R^{T}R=I}\,\negthickspace\right\}\mathclose{}.

We know that if
y^{T}y=z^{T}z
for any

, then there exists
R\in O(n)
such that:

Ry=z.

Applying this result, we find
R\in O(n)
such that:

RSx=\beta e_{1},

(19)

where
\beta
is a scaling factor ensuring that the Euclidean norms of
Sx
and
\beta e_{1}
are equal.

Now, define
A=S^{-1}RS
. Since
S^{-1}
is also a diagonal matrix, we compute:

\displaystyle Ax

\displaystyle=S^{-1}RSx

\displaystyle=S^{-1}\beta e_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha e_{1},

(20)

where
\alpha=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{d_{1}}}
is an appropriate scaling factor.

Next, we show that
A\in G
. Consider
A^{T}DA
:

\displaystyle A^{T}DA

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left({S^{-1}RS}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}^{T}D\mathopen{}\left({S^{-1}RS}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=S^{T}R^{T}\mathopen{}\left({S^{-1}DS^{-1}}\,\negthickspace\right)\mathclose{}RS
