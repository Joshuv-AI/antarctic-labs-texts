Interpretable Network-assisted Random Forest +
Tiffany M. Tang, Elizaveta Levina, Ji Zhu

Abstract
Machine learning algorithms often assume that training samples are independent. When data points are connected by a network, the induced dependency between samples is both a challenge, reducing effective sample size, and an opportunity to improve prediction by leveraging information from network neighbors. Multiple methods taking advantage of this opportunity are now available, but many, including graph neural networks, are not easily interpretable, limiting their usefulness for understanding how a model makes its predictions. Others, such as network-assisted linear regression, are interpretable but often yield substantially worse prediction performance. We bridge this gap by proposing a family of flexible network-assisted models built upon a generalization of random forests (RF + ), which achieves highly-competitive prediction accuracy and can be interpreted through feature importance measures. In particular, we develop a suite of interpretation tools that enable practitioners to not only identify important features that drive model predictions, but also quantify the importance of the network contribution to prediction. Importantly, we provide both global and local importance measures as well as sample influence measures to assess the impact of a given observation. This suite of tools broadens the scope and applicability of network-assisted machine learning for high-impact problems where interpretability and transparency are essential.



Keywords:
interpretable machine learning, network cohesion, decision trees, ensembles

1
Introduction

Current machine learning algorithms achieve state-of-the-art prediction accuracy for many tasks, but the vast majority of these methods assume that the samples in the training data are independent of one another. In many modern applications, data points are connected by a network, which creates dependencies between samples. For example, when investigating student beliefs at a given school, there is inevitably an underlying social network (where nodes represent students and edges represent social or friendship ties between two students) which elicits peer-to-peer effects since student beliefs are highly likely to be influenced by their friends
(
Paluck et al. 2016a
)
. These network-induced dependencies present a challenge as they reduce the effective sample size and introduce unwanted biases, leading to poor generalization performance. At the same time, they offer an opportunity to improve prediction by exploiting potential similarities between network neighbors, which we informally refer to as network cohesion: for example, knowing one student’s beliefs can help predict the beliefs of that student’s close friends, and generally, network neighbors behaving more similarly than non-neighbors (cohesion) is widely observed in practice. Though some methods for improving prediction by leveraging the network information have been developed (to be discussed below), it remains challenging to balance model flexibility with interpretability, and especially to disentangle the importance of network connections for prediction from that of other node features.

In general, finding a balance between flexibility and interpretability has long been a major question in statistical learning; see for example
Rudin 2019a
. In practice, random forests (RFs)
(
Breiman 2001a
)
have often emerged as a popular solution, striking a reasonable balance between strong prediction performance and interpretability. Notably, RFs generally do not require massive amounts of training data and have been shown to perform competitively compared to deep learning across a wide array of medium-sized datasets
(
Grinsztajn et al. 2022a
)
. RFs have also been widely adopted in applications including clinical and health diagnostics
(
Loef et al. 2022a
)
, scientific experimental recommendations
(
Wang et al. 2025a
)
, and ecological modeling
(
Simon et al. 2023a
)
, primarily due to their ability to interpret how each feature contributes to the model’s predictions. Recently,
Agarwal et al. 2025a
built upon a connection between decision trees and linear regression
(
Klusowski & Tian 2024a
;
Golea et al. 1997a
)
to develop Random Forest Plus (RF
+
), an extension of RFs combining both nonlinear tree-based features and linear features.
Agarwal et al. 2025a
showed RF
+
outperforms ordinary RF on a variety of prediction tasks while maintaining interpretability. We take this method as a basis for our proposed network-assisted approach,
Ne
twork-assisted
R
andom
F
orest Plus (
NeRF
+
), because of its flexibility: NeRF
+
inherits the ability of RF
+
to include both linear and nonlinear features and to provide local and global importance measures. Moreover, it offers a framework to incorporate the network in multiple ways, as we will discuss below. We will demonstrate that, like RF
+
, NeRF
+
methods achieve highly-competitive prediction accuracy while maintaining interpretability.

Several approaches to incorporate network information into prediction are available. Starting from the flexible end of the spectrum, the class of deep learning methods called graph neural networks (GNNs)
(
Kipf & Welling 2017a
;
Hamilton et al. 2017a
;
Wu et al. 2020a
;
Zhou et al. 2020a
)
, work by incorporating the network connections into the deep learning architecture, summing up inputs from neighbors when making predictions for a node. Like all deep learning models, these are generally black-box methods. Though post-hoc explainability tools such as GNNExplainer
(
Ying et al. 2019a
)
, GraphSVX
(
Duval & Malliaros 2021a
)
, and others
(
Wu et al. 2022a
)
can aid in explaining how GNNs make their predictions, these methods rely on unstable approximations of the trained GNN, which are easily susceptible to adversarial attacks and are known to often result in misleading explanations
(
Li et al. 2024a
)
. Moreover, GNNs typically require large amounts of data to achieve their state-of-the-art prediction accuracy, limiting their utility in many high-impact biomedical and social science problems, where data availability is often limited by cost or by the nature of the problem, and where the need for transparency and ability to understand how the model is making its predictions is at least as important as the prediction itself.

In traditional statistical learning, two main approaches have been used for incorporating network information into prediction modeling – engineering network-based features and regularization.
In the former approach, researchers augment their covariate matrix with additional node features
(
Chen et al. 2018a
;
Lunde et al. 2025a
)
. These node features can be constructed from network summaries (e.g., node degrees, or neighborhood averages of other features), which are a popular approach in econometrics
(
Manski 1993a
)
, or from latent variables obtained from fitting a latent variable network model
(e.g.,
Hoff et al. 2002a
;
Belkin & Niyogi 2003a
;
Sussman et al. 2012a
)
. This approach is attractively modular: any network latent variable model or embedding method can be used to produce features that can then be fed into any learning algorithm. However, these network embeddings are typically learned in an unsupervised manner and thus not directly optimized for the prediction task at hand.

Regularization-based approaches, on the other hand, provide a supervised alternative to incorporating network information into prediction models
(
Chen et al. 2010a
;
Wang et al. 2016a
;
Li et al. 2019b
)
. An example of such regularization is regression with network cohesion (RNC), introduced by
Li et al. 2019b
, with inference tools developed under additional assumptions by
Le & Li 2022a
. Briefly, RNC adds a node-specific network effect to a standard linear regression model and uses a “cohesion penalty” to shrink network effects between neighboring nodes towards each other, hence encouraging its predictions for network neighbors to be more similar. This approach is readily interpretable but may in general lack flexibility to provide competitive prediction performance due to its underlying linear model assumption.

Finally, network-assisted variants of Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (Network BART) have been proposed by
Luo et al. 2022a
and
Deshpande 2025a
. These methods augment the splitting options during tree construction by allowing the tree to either split on a variable as usual, or by partitioning the network into two connected components, implementing an efficient search strategy over network partitions through pre-computed minimum spanning trees. While Network BART has been shown to improve prediction, there are currently no available tools for interpreting the important features in this approach.

In this paper, we address the need for an interpretable
and
flexible prediction method on networks by developing Network-assisted Random Forest Plus (NeRF
+
). We will show NeRF
+
achieves a good balance between flexibility and interpretability for network-assisted prediction, maintaining competitive prediction accuracy while providing a suite of tools to interpret findings. These interpretability tools enable researchers to quantify how a feature or the network contributes to the model’s predictions, either globally or for a particular sample, and to identify important or unusual training points through influence scores. It is our hope that this suite of interpretability tools will facilitate broader adoption of network-assisted prediction in a variety of high-stakes applications where interpretation is crucial.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
We introduce the NeRF
+
prediction models in Section
2
and the suite of interpretability tools for NeRF
+
in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we compare NeRF
+
to competitors on simulated data and examine its behavior on several illustrative cases. Section
5
presents an in-depth analysis of two case studies, one on school conflict and another on crime in Philadelphia. We conclude with a discussion in Section
6
.

2
Network-assisted Random Forest Plus (NeRF
+
)

In this section, we introduce NeRF
+
, a family of network-assisted RF-based prediction models which leverage network information to improve the overall prediction performance.
To begin, we set up notation and review relevant background in Section
2.1
before introducing NeRF
+
in Section
2.2
.

2.1
Notation and Background

Suppose we have training data for
n
samples with
p
features (covariates) and a quantitative response of interest. Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be the observed feature matrix and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the vector of responses for the training data. Without loss of generality, assume that the responses
\boldsymbol{y}
and each column of
\mathbf{X}
have been centered to have mean 0. Suppose that we also observed an undirected network
G
connecting the
n
samples, represented by an
n\times n
adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
. For simplicity, assume
\mathbf{A}
is binary, with
A_{ij}=1
if samples
i
and
j
are connected in
G
and
0
otherwise. We note, however, that all proposed methods easily extend to weighted networks, where
A_{ij}
is a non-negative weight capturing the strength of the connection between samples
i
and
j
. Define the unnormalized network Laplacian by
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{A}
, where
\mathbf{D}=\textnormal{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
is the diagonal matrix of node degrees
d_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}
.

Our proposed method, NeRF
+
, builds on ideas from three key tools — network embeddings, regression with network cohesion, and RF+ — reviewed next.

2.1.1
Network embeddings

As mentioned in Section
1
, a simple and popular network-assisted approach is to augment the original covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}
with additional network embedding features
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, where
r
is the number of latent dimensions.
Such
\mathbf{Z}
can be derived from the network using various approaches including spectral embeddings
(
Belkin & Niyogi 2003a
;
Sussman et al. 2012a
)
, GNN latent representations
(
Kipf & Welling 2017a
;
Hamilton et al. 2017a
)
, and/or other node embeddings
(
Grover & Leskovec 2016a
)
. There is also a large body of literature on how to select the number of latent dimensions
r
and how to select appropriate embedding methods for a given network structure
(e.g.,
Chen & Lei 2018a
;
Tang & Priebe 2018a
;
Li et al. 2020b
)
.

When the goal is purely prediction, these network embeddings can generally be learned using the network
\mathbf{A}
and training data
(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{y})
together. However, given our aim for interpretability, we focus on the case where the network embedding features
\mathbf{Z}
are learned using only the network
\mathbf{A}
.
In particular, for simplicity, we use the Laplacian spectral embedding
(
Belkin & Niyogi 2003a
)
to construct
\mathbf{Z}
from
\mathbf{A}
for all empirical studies in this work.

2.1.2
Regression with Network Cohesion

An alternative approach to network-assisted prediction is via regularization with network-induced penalties. One example of this approach is regression with network cohesion (RNC)
(
Li et al. 2019b
)
. RNC adds a node-specific network effect to ordinary linear regression models and models the response
\boldsymbol{y}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a vector of regression coefficients,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a noise vector, and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector of individual node effects (replacing the traditional scalar intercept). This model (which requires regularization to estimate its
n+p
parameters) is fitted by minimizing the usual least squares loss plus a network cohesion penalty, that is,

\displaystyle(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\;\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\alpha},

(1)

where
\lambda_{\alpha}>0
is a tuning parameter,
\mathbf{L}
is the Laplacian of the network
\mathbf{A}
, and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{2}
is the network cohesion penalty, shrinking node effects towards each other when nodes are connected. If desired, penalties on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be added (e.g., if
p>n
), and (
1
) can be extended to general loss functions.
Additionally, to guarantee existence of a solution for (
1
),
Li et al. 2019b
replace the Laplacian
\mathbf{L}
with a regularized Laplacian
\mathbf{L}+\lambda_{L}\mathbf{I}
for some small
\lambda_{L}>0
. For all empirical studies in this paper, we use the regularized Laplacian with
\lambda_{L}=0.05
, but still denote it by
\mathbf{L}
for notational simplicity.

2.1.3
Random Forest Plus

Random Forest Plus (RF
+
) was proposed by
Agarwal et al. 2025a
as a generalization of RF which combines the powerful predictive capabilities of RFs with the ease of interpretability of generalized linear models. The key tool of RF
+
is a particular representation of decision tree predictions through a linear model, reported in previous work
(
Klusowski & Tian 2024a
;
Agarwal et al. 2022a
)
.
Specifically, suppose we are given a fitted (regression or classification) decision tree
t
, trained on data
(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{y})
and composed of
m_{t}
total decision splits such that (i) each split in the decision tree is binary (i.e., it has two child nodes), and (ii) the tree predictions are made by averaging the training responses
\boldsymbol{y}
in each leaf node. These assumptions are satisfied by commonly used decision tree algorithms such as CART
(
Breiman et al. 1984a
)
. Then, one can extract a map from the original features
\mathbf{X}
to new features
\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})
such that the tree predictions
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t)}
can be
exactly
recovered by ordinary linear regression of the response
\boldsymbol{y}
on the new features
\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t)}=\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)},\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{t}}}\;\;\|\boldsymbol{y}-\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}.

(2)

These new features
\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})
are constructed as follows. Given the decision tree
t
, each internal node
s
in the tree
t
gets split into two children nodes defined by
s_{L}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in s\colon x_{k}\leq\tau\}
and
s_{R}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in s\colon x_{k}>\tau\}
for some feature
k
and threshold
\tau
. Let
N(s)
be the number of training points from the sample
(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{y})
that reach node
s
. Define the decision stump function for each internal node (i.e., split)
s
as

\displaystyle\psi_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{N\left(s_{R}\right)\mathbf{1}\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in s_{L}\right\}-N\left(s_{L}\right)\mathbf{1}\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in s_{R}\right\}}{\sqrt{N\left(s_{L}\right)N\left(s_{R}\right)}}.

(3)

Each split depends on the training data
(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{y})
; we suppress this dependence for simplicity of notation. The function
\psi
takes three values: positive if sample
\boldsymbol{x}
was sent to child node
s_{L}
, negative if it was sent to
s_{R}
, and 0 if it did not reach node
s
at all. Assuming the tree
t
consists of
m_{t}
splits
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m_{t}}\}
, concatenating all their decision functions yields the feature map
\Psi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=(\psi_{s_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,\psi_{s_{m_{t}}}(\boldsymbol{x}))
and the corresponding transformed training data
\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m_{t}}
to be plugged into (
2
).

Agarwal et al. 2025a
expanded upon this linear regression interpretation of decision trees, proposing RF
+
and demonstrating its improved prediction performance over RFs in a variety of real data applications.
In short, RF
+
generalizes (
2
) to allow for
different loss functions (e.g., logistic loss for classification),
adding non-stump features (e.g., original features used in at least one tree)
and regularization penalties on the coefficients, tuned via a computationally-efficient leave-one-out cross validation. Formally, given a fitted decision tree
t
, the predictions from each “tree” in RF
+
are obtained via

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t)}=\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}+\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)},

(4)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{t}}}\;\;\ell(\boldsymbol{y},\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})\boldsymbol{\gamma})+P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})+P_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}).

(5)

Here,
\ell
is a user-specified loss function, and
P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
P_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are optional penalties on the regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, respectively (e.g., the lasso
\ell_{1}
or the ridge
\ell_{2}
penalties).
Each tree in RF
+
can be viewed as a cross between a linear model
\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}
and a non-linear decision tree
\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}
, which has been adaptively learned from the data. An RF
+
is then simply an ensemble of
T
of these trees, which predicts by averaging over the trees:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}+\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}\right).

(6)

2.2
Network-assisted RF
+
(NeRF
+
) Prediction Models

Beyond its flexible form and improved prediction over RF, an attractive property of RF
+
when it comes to incorporating the network information is that it is amenable to both network-assisted approaches discussed above: it can accommodate an expanded feature matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z}]
, where
\mathbf{Z}
is learned from the network
\mathbf{A}
, and it can be regularized by adding a network cohesion-type penalty. One may then ask which of these two approaches is “better”: we argue that, as usual, the answer depends on the data. Some problems may exhibit a smoothness in responses between connected nodes that the network cohesion penalty is designed to pick up; in others, responses may exhibit more similarity for nodes close together in the learned latent embedding space
\mathbf{Z}
, which may or may not be reflected in the neighborhood smoothness encouraged by the network cohesion penalty. These two approaches are not mutually exclusive, and there is no need to choose: the most flexible approach is to allow the data to speak for itself.

With that in mind, we introduce
NeRF
+
, a family of
Ne
twork-assisted
RF
+
prediction models that incorporates network information through both network embedding features
\mathbf{Z}
and a network cohesion penalty. Given an observed feature data matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, graph adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, response
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and network embedding features
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
which are learned from
\mathbf{A}
, we fit NeRF
+
according to the following three-step procedure:

1.

Train an RF with
T
trees to predict
\boldsymbol{y}
from
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+r)}
.

2.

For each tree
t=1,\ldots,T
, extract the decision stump feature matrix
\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
from the fitted RF, and minimize

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+r}\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{t}}\end{subarray}}\;\ell(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\boldsymbol{\gamma})+\lambda_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})+P_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}),

(7)

where again
\ell
is a user-specified loss function,
\lambda_{\alpha}>0
is the cohesion penalty tuning parameter,
\mathbf{L}
is the Laplacian of the network
\mathbf{A}
, and
P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
P_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are optional penalties on the regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, respectively.

3.

Compute predictions as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)}+\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}+\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}\right).

(8)

As before, we can interpret NeRF
+
as an ensemble of
T
trees, where each tree
t
is a sum of individual node effects
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)}
, reflecting smoothness in the responses between connected nodes in the network, and linear
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}
and nonlinear effects
\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}
of the original features
\mathbf{X}
and the network embedding features
\mathbf{Z}
on the response
\boldsymbol{y}
.

In this form, NeRF
+
implicitly incurs some “double-dipping” since the responses
\boldsymbol{y}
are used to both learn the decision stumps
\Psi_{t}
and the coefficient estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}
, and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}
. Though this is a serious concern for statistical inference, we do not tackle any inferential tasks in this paper. For prediction, this is less of a concern as long as the learned prediction model generalizes well to new data (which can be assessed via a held-out test prediction metric). Nonetheless, there may be situations where one would like to be conservative with potential overfitting and avoid double-dipping altogether. In this case, double-dipping can be avoided by fitting (
7
) using only the out-of-bag (OOB) samples for each tree
t
since each tree
t
in the RF was originally trained using a bootstrap (i.e., in-bag) sample of the training data. However, as seen with RF
+
in
Agarwal et al. 2025a
, we found that NeRF
+
generally yields better predictive performance when (
7
) is fitted using the full training data
(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{y})
instead of the out-of-bag data, and we hence use this approach by default.

One may also wonder whether it is necessary to incorporate network information through both the node effect
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and the embedding features
\mathbf{Z}
. It is certainly possible to simplify NeRF
+
to include only one of these components — for example, if there is domain knowledge indicating one approach is much more appropriate than the other. We stress, however, that they can potentially capture different types of network effects: the node effects
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
capture cohesion in the observed network (nodes with a short network path between them are similar) whereas the embedding features
\mathbf{Z}
capture cohesion in the latent space (nodes close to each other in the latent space are similar). Depending on the network model and the relationship between the latent space distance and probability of edges, these two types of effects may be similar or very different (explored further in Sections
4
and
5
). Including both allows for maximum flexibility in prediction, at the expense of having to tune additional hyperparameters (discussed in Section
2.4
).

2.3
Prediction for test samples

Suppose now we want to make predictions on new test data points, which were not available at training time, and suppose we have access to the network containing both the training and test data points together. To make predictions on the test points, we require estimates of both the network embedding features and individual node effects for the new test points.

To estimate the network embedding features for these test points, we leverage existing out-of-sample extensions of the training embedding approach (e.g., using
Levin et al. 2018a
for Laplacian and adjacency spectral embedding approaches). These approaches are designed to embed new test data points into the previously-trained embedding space.

To estimate the individual node effects for the new test points, we follow the RNC approach
(
Li et al. 2019b
)
. For a given tree
t
, let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{(t)}
denote the training node effects for the
n_{1}=n
training points. To estimate the individual node effects
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}^{(t)}
for the
n_{2}
new test points, we look for the maximally cohesive effects, minimizing

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}^{(t)}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{\prime}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}),

where
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}
is the Laplacian corresponding to the network with the
n_{1}+n_{2}
nodes combining the training and test data, written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}_{11}&\mathbf{L}_{12}\\
\mathbf{L}_{21}&\mathbf{L}_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

Here,
\mathbf{L}_{11}=\mathbf{L}
corresponds to the
n_{1}
training nodes and
\mathbf{L}_{22}
corresponds to the
n_{2}
test nodes.

The estimated test network embedding features and test node effects
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}^{(t)}
can then be plugged into (
8
) to obtain the test predictions.

2.4
Choosing penalties and tuning parameters

Though the regularization penalties
P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
P_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are optional, they are generally helpful if there is a large number of parameters (e.g., if the tree
t
is deep, or if
p
is large) and/or strong correlations between the raw features
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
and the decision stump features
\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
.

Numerous penalties have been used with linear regression, with the most popular being the
\ell_{1}
(LASSO)
(
Tibshirani 1996a
)
,
\ell_{2}
(ridge)
(
Hoerl & Kennard 1970a
)
, and elastic net penalties
(
Zou & Hastie 2005a
)
. These penalties can be used in NeRF
+
to induce their usual properties (e.g.,
\ell_{1}
induces sparsity in the estimated coefficients, etc). Solving (
7
) with these penalties is straightforward using existing optimization algorithms since the additional network cohesion penalty can be written as a generalized ridge penalty
(
Li et al. 2019b
)
. In our experience, the
\ell_{2}
penalties (i.e.,
P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\lambda_{\beta}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
and
P_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\lambda_{\gamma}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}
) work very well across a wide range of problems; we recommend them by default and use them for the remainder of the paper. We also recommend allowing for separate regularization parameters
\lambda_{\beta}
and
\lambda_{\gamma}
since both the scale of
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
and
\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
and the relative strength of the linear versus nonlinear components are in general different.

To tune these regularization parameters
\lambda_{\beta}
and
\lambda_{\gamma}
, cross-validation (CV) can be used. For computational reasons, the CV data splitting is typically applied to
(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{y})
after constructing the network embedding features
\mathbf{Z}
for the full training data. Though this introduces a small amount data leakage in each held-out CV fold, we will demonstrate that the tuned regularization parameters using this approach yield good generalization performance on new test data in Sections
4
and
5
.

3
Interpreting NeRF
+

A key advantage of NeRF
+
is its dual nature, bridging linear regression and RFs. This opens the door for researchers to
interpret
the model’s predictions and gain insights into the underlying system.
While there is a substantial literature discussing different definitions and criteria for what “interpretability” means in machine learning
(e.g.,
Doshi-Velez & Kim 2017a
;
Lipton 2018a
;
Murdoch et al. 2019a
;
Molnar 2025a
;
Allen et al. 2024a
)
, we aim not to tackle these broader questions here, but instead focus on developing several interpretability tools that are commonly used and accepted by practitioners — namely, feature importance, both global and local (Figure
1
), as well as sample influences for NeRF
+
.

First, we introduce some additional notation. By construction, predictions made by a fitted NeRF
+
model
\hat{f}
take the linear form

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\Psi}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{T}\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

(9)

where
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)})^{\top}:=[1,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})^{\top},(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)})^{\top}]
,
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}):=[\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)},\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})]
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}
, and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}
are the solutions to (
7
),
T
is the number of trees in the forest,

\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}):=[\tilde{\Psi}_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\ldots,\tilde{\Psi}_{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})]
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}:=[(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)})^{\top},\ldots,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(T)})^{\top}]
.
Furthermore, let
\Psi_{t}^{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
denote the subset of columns in
\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
corresponding to splits on feature
X_{k}
, and let
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})=[\mathbf{X}_{k},\Psi_{t}^{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})]
.
Similarly, let
\Psi_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
denote the subset of columns in
\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
corresponding to splits on any network embedding feature from
\mathbf{Z}
, and let
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})=[\mathbf{Z},\Psi_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})]
.

3.1
Global Feature Importance

Global feature (or variable) importance is a classic measure of a feature’s contribution to the model’s predictions across all training observations (hence the term ”global”).
For linear models, a typical global important measure is the
t
-statistic associated with the estimated coefficient in ordinary least squares.
For RFs, mean decrease in impurity (MDI)
(
Breiman et al. 1984a
)
and permutation importance
(
Breiman 2001a
)
are the most commonly used global feature importance measures.
Briefly, MDI quantifies a feature’s importance by measuring the decrease in node impurity across splits that were made using that particular feature. Permutation importance measures the change in the model’s prediction performance when the feature’s values are randomly permuted across all training samples.

Despite their popularity in practice, both approaches have well-known shortcomings, such as MDI’s bias towards high entropy and uncorrelated features
(
Strobl et al. 2007a
;
Nicodemus 2011a
)
and permutation importance’s forced extrapolation and unpredictable performance in the presence of correlated features
(
Hooker et al. 2021a
)
. Many variants have since been proposed to help mitigate these challenges
(e.g.,
Sandri & Zuccolotto 2008a
;
Strobl et al. 2008a
;
Li et al. 2019c
;
Zhou & Hooker 2021a
;
Loecher 2022a
;
Agarwal et al. 2025a
)
. Similar ideas can be adapted to the global feature importance measures we develop below; however, we defer this to future work and focus first on developing the core permutation- and MDI-based global importance measures for NeRF
+
.

Through the development of these global feature importance methods, we highlight a unique and key task of interpreting network-assisted prediction models — that is, disentangling importance of individual node features from the importance of their network connections, which NeRF
+
can handle in a natural and intuitive way.

Permutation importance for NeRF
+
.

Similar to RF permutation importance
(
Breiman 2001a
)
, the permutation importance for NeRF
+
can be measured by randomly permuting a feature’s values across samples and measuring the resulting change in the model’s accuracy. Specifically, given a fitted NeRF
+
model
\hat{f}
, scoring metric
\mathcal{L}
, and
B
permutations, we compute the global permutation importance of feature
X_{k}
, denoted
\text{PI}_{\hat{f},k}
, as

\displaystyle\text{PI}_{\hat{f},k}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\underbrace{\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y},\frac{1}{T}\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\setminus k,b}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{prediction performance using}\\
\text{permuted data matrix}\end{subarray}}-\underbrace{\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y},\frac{1}{T}\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{prediction performance using}\\
\text{original data matrix}\end{subarray}},

(10)

where
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\setminus k,b}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
is the matrix
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
after the rows of
[\tilde{\Psi}_{1}^{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\ldots,\tilde{\Psi}_{T}^{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})]
have been randomly permuted. In other words, (
10
) is the change in prediction performance when we jointly permute all features involving
X_{k}
, both linear and those resulting from the tree.
The scoring metric
\mathcal{L}
is usually taken to be a dissimilarity measure (e.g., root mean squared error or classification error), and thus a higher
\text{PI}_{\hat{f},k}
indicates a more important feature. In practice, it is also generally recommended to evaluate this permutation importance using held-out data
(
Fisher et al. 2019a
;
Molnar 2025a
)
.

While permutation importance is a well-established idea, the opportunity to naturally extend it to network importance is unique to NeRF
+
. For general network-assisted methods, it is often unclear how to permute a network; one could, for example, rewire a random subset of all edges, rewire edges of a random subset of nodes, or use more sophisticated schemes, and it is unclear what impact the choice of permutation scheme would have on the importance measure. In contrast, to permute the network in NeRF
+
, we can directly permute the values of the network-related features
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}
and
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
. We thus compute the global permutation importance of the network, denoted
\text{PI}_{\hat{f},(\alpha,Z)}
,
as

\displaystyle\text{PI}_{\hat{f},(\alpha,Z)}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y},\frac{1}{T}\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\setminus(\alpha,Z),b}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)-\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y},\frac{1}{T}\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right),

(11)

where
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\setminus(\alpha,Z),b}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
is the matrix
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathcal{T}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
after the rows of
[\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(1)},\tilde{\Psi}_{1}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(T)},\tilde{\Psi}_{T}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})]
have been permuted. Furthermore, we can separately assess the importance stemming from network cohesion, denoted
\text{PI}_{\hat{f},\alpha}
, and from the network embedding, denoted
\text{PI}_{\hat{f},Z}
, by respectively permuting the
[\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(T)}]
or
[\tilde{\Psi}_{1}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\ldots,\tilde{\Psi}_{T}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})]
blocks separately.

MDI
+
importance for NeRF
+
.

Since RF
+
is no longer a purely tree-based model, the popular MDI measure for RF is not directly applicable to RF
+
.
Agarwal et al. 2025a
thus built upon the linear regression interpretation of decision trees to develop a generalization of MDI called MDI
+
— a feature importance framework which is equivalent to MDI when applied to an ordinary RF but is more generally applicable to RF
+
.
Specifically, the MDI
+
for a feature
X_{k}
in RF
+
is measured by evaluating the similarity between
\boldsymbol{y}
and the RF
+
predictions using the data matrix, where all features that do not involve (or split on)
X_{k}
have been imputed by their column means. This framework can be extended to define the MDI
+
importance of feature
X_{k}
in a NeRF
+
model
\hat{f}
as

\displaystyle\text{MDI}^{+}_{\hat{f},k}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}m\big(\boldsymbol{y},\;\underbrace{\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}^{\setminus k}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{th}\text{partial model}\\
\text{predictions}\end{subarray}}\big)

(12)

where
m
is some similarity metric (e.g.,
R^{2}
for regression problems and negative log-loss for classification problems),
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}
is that used in (
9
), and
\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}^{\setminus k}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
is the matrix
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
but with all columns corresponding to features that do not involve (or split on)
X_{k}
replaced by their column means, i.e.,

\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}^{\setminus k}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})=[\bar{\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{(t)},\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}_{t}^{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\ldots,\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}_{t}^{k-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}_{t}^{k+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\ldots,\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}_{t}^{p}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})].

Note that as in linear regression, categorical features in NeRF
+
are dummy-coded and thus imputed using the mean of the dummy-coded features.
Intuitively, the
k^{th}
partial model prediction is the prediction we would have gotten had we only had access to the information from feature
X_{k}
and ignored all other features (by setting them to their column means).
If the
k^{th}
partial model prediction is close to the observed response
\boldsymbol{y}
, as measured by
m
, then feature
k
is considered important and is reflected in a high MDI
+
value.

To assess the global MDI
+
importance of the network in NeRF
+
, we further define

\displaystyle\text{MDI}^{+}_{\hat{f},(\alpha,Z)}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}m\big(\boldsymbol{y},\;\underbrace{\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}^{\setminus(\alpha,Z)}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{network partial model}\\
\text{predictions}\end{subarray}}\big)

(13)

where
\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}^{\setminus(\alpha,Z)}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
is the matrix
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
but with all columns corresponding to features that do not involve (or split on) the network replaced by their column means, i.e.,

\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}^{\setminus(\alpha,Z)}_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})=[\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)},\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}_{t}^{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}),\ldots,\bar{\tilde{\Psi}}_{t}^{p}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})].

The network partial model predictions can similarly be interpreted as the best we can do using only the network information, ignoring the contributions of all other non-network features. A high value suggests that the model’s predictions are driven primarily by the network information, and the network is important. We can again separate the contributions of network cohesion
\text{MDI}^{+}_{\hat{f},\alpha}
and the network embedding
\text{MDI}^{+}_{\hat{f},Z}
by computing the partial model predictions using only the
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)}
’s or only the
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
’s.

Figure 1:
A schematic of global (left) and local (right) network importance measures for NeRF
+
. To compute the permutation network importance, the network features (blue rows) are permuted. To compute the MDI
+
network importance, the non-network features are replaced by their column means. To compute the local network importance, sample
i
’s prediction is computed using the network features only and compared to the average network contribution across the population.

3.2
Local Feature Importance

Moving beyond global feature importance, local importance measures have been proposed to quantify how much each feature contributes to each sample point’s prediction. Arguably the most popular local feature importance measures are based on Shapley values
(
Shapley 1953a
)
from cooperative game theory. Exact computation of Shapley values, however, is intractable for most machine learning models and necessitates approximations — most notably, via SHAP
(
Lundberg & Lee 2017a
)
or variants thereof, e.g., treeSHAP
(
Lundberg et al. 2020a
)
. However, linear models are an exception. For a given linear model, Shapley values can be directly read off from the summands corresponding to each feature (see
Molnar 2025a
, Chapter 17). Similar to
Liang et al. 2025a
, we exploit the convenient linear form of NeRF
+
to define the local feature importance of feature
k
for sample
i
in the NeRF
+
model
\hat{f}
as

\displaystyle\text{LFI}_{\hat{f},k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{k}(\tilde{X})\right]\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}_{k}\right),

(14)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}_{k}
is the subset of coefficients in
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}
that correspond to
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})
, and the expectation is taken over the empirical distribution of the training sample.
The first term in the summation is simply the additive portion of the total prediction
\hat{y}_{i}
coming from features involving
X_{k}
while the second term is a normalization term. There are many possible normalization choices; this choice allows us to interpret
\text{LFI}_{\hat{f},k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})
as the contribution of
X_{k}
to sample
i
’s prediction relative to the average contribution of
X_{k}
in the training sample. Importantly, the magnitude indicates the strength of the importance while the sign indicates the direction of the importance (i.e., whether the feature contributes to a higher or lower predicted value compared to the average).

Similarly, we define the local network importance for sample
i
in the NeRF
+
model
\hat{f}
as

\displaystyle\text{LFI}_{\hat{f},(\alpha,Z)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}_{i}+\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}_{Z}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)}]-\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{X})\right]\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}_{Z}\right),

(15)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}_{Z}
is the subset of coefficients in
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}
that correspond to
\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})
. As before, we interpret this quantity as the difference between the network’s contribution to sample
i
’s prediction and the average of such contributions across all samples. Further, (
15
) can be again separated into two components:
\text{LFI}_{\hat{f},\alpha}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\hat{\alpha}^{(t)}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(t)}]\right)
giving the local importance of network cohesion and
\text{LFI}_{\hat{f},Z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}_{Z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\Psi}_{t}^{Z}(\tilde{X})\right]\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)}_{Z}\right)
giving the local importance of the network embedding.

3.3
Sample Influence

To complement the feature importance measures, we lastly develop sample influence scores for NeRF
+
, which quantify the change in model fit or predictions when leaving out each sample point during training. Sample influences are typically challenging to compute for most modern machine learning settings given that a brute-force approach would require re-fitting the model for each held-out sample.
While techniques such as those developed by
Koh & Liang 2017a
have made it possible to compute sample influences for models with twice-differentiable loss functions without the need to re-fit the model, these techniques cannot be directly applied to tree-based models such as NeRF
+
, where the tree splits are inherently discrete and non-differentiable.
However, the linear formulation of NeRF
+
provides a unique opportunity to leverage the extensive classical statistical literature on sample influences
(
Hampel 1974a
;
Cook 1977a
;
Cook & Weisberg 1982a
)
.
In particular, using the linear formulation of NeRF
+
, if we keep all features (from the constructed trees
\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
and the network embeddings
\mathbf{Z}
) fixed, we can derive closed-form solutions for approximate leave-one-out (LOO) parameter estimates for NeRF
+
. Since each tree in NeRF
+
is fit independently, we provide this LOO derivation for a single tree in NeRF
+
for simplicity.

Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{\top}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}]
be the estimate from (
7
) for tree
t
, fitted on
n
training samples. For now, consider the NeRF
+
model with
\ell_{2}
penalties for
P_{\beta}
and
P_{\gamma}
. For a fixed tree
t
, if we leave out the
i^{th}
sample from the training data and re-fit the NeRF
+
coefficients, then the resulting estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}
can be efficiently computed for all samples
i=1,\dots,n
via:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}=\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}+\frac{(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}}{1-h_{i}}(\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i})-\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}\left([\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}+\frac{(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}}{1-h_{i}}\right)\right]_{-i},

(16)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=[\mathbf{I}_{n},\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})],\quad\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{\alpha}\mathbf{L}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\lambda_{\beta}\mathbf{I}_{p+r}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\lambda_{\gamma}\mathbf{I}_{m_{t}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{B}^{-1}=(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{M})^{-1},

\displaystyle\quad(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}^{-1}-\frac{1}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}^{\top},\qquad h_{i}=\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}.

This derivation (details in Appendix
A
) relies on the observation that (
7
) is a generalized ridge problem. For NeRF
+
models with non-ridge penalties, the LOO estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}
can be approximated using techniques developed in
Koh & Liang 2017a
. These LOO parameter estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}
can then be plugged into (
9
) to efficiently compute the LOO predictions and estimate the prediction error had we left out sample
i
from the training set. In particular, we define influence of a training sample
i
to be the mean squared difference between the original test predictions (computed using
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
) and the test predictions when leaving out sample
i
during training (computed using
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}
).

Note however (
16
) is not a true LOO estimate, since we do not take into account how leaving out the sample impacts the fitted tree and the resulting stump features
\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})
, the network embedding
\mathbf{Z}
, the Laplacian matrix
\mathbf{L}
used in the cohesion penalty, or the choice of regularization parameters.
Despite this limitation, we show in Appendix
B
that the estimates in (
16
) provide good approximations to the oracle, obtained by leaving out each sample and recomputing everything from scratch (the tree, network embeddings, Laplacian matrix, and regularization parameters). When computational efficiency is a priority, this closed-form approximation can be a good practical compromise.

4
Simulations

We next demonstrate the strong performance of NeRF
+
on both prediction and interpretability across a wide range of simulations. We compare NeRF
+
with two network-assisted prediction methods: its linear counterpart, RNC
(
Li et al. 2019b
)
, and an alternative tree-based method, Network BART
(
Deshpande 2025a
)
. We also included their non-network-assisted counterparts as baselines: RF
+

(
Agarwal et al. 2025a
)
, ordinary linear regression, and BART
(
Chipman et al. 2010a
)
. Tuning and implementation details are provided in Appendix
B
. Regarding interpretations, we focus specifically on the global feature importances and sample influences in these simulations and leave the investigation of local feature importances to the case studies in Section
5
.

Simulation Setup.

We generated
n=300
samples with
p=20
covariates sampled i.i.d. from
N(0,1)
connected by an unweighted, undirected network drawn from a stochastic block model (SBM) with three blocks and edge probabilities 0.2 and 0.02 within and between blocks, respectively. Responses
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
were then drawn from one of two network effect models, with the function
f
of covariates to be specified later and noise variables drawn as
\epsilon_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
:

(1)

Additive blockwise network effect
:

y_{i}=\alpha_{i}+f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},

where
\alpha_{i}=-\eta,0,\eta
for samples in SBM blocks 1, 2, and 3, respectively. This network effect model is better captured by including network embedding features that reflect the SBM block structure, as opposed to encouraging network cohesion, since the presence or absence of a connection between two nodes within the same block has no effect on their responses’ similarity.

(2)

Network autocorrelation effect
:

y_{i}=\frac{\omega}{d_{i}}\sum_{j:A_{ij}=1}y_{j}+f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},

where
d_{i}
is the degree for sample
i
. This network effect model creates a direct relationship between a node’s response and its neighbors’ responses, and thus should benefit greatly from encouraging network cohesion in the fitted model.

We examined three different forms of
f
:

Linear
:
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=x_{i1}+x_{i2}
;

Polynomial
:
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=x_{i1}+x_{i1}x_{i2}+x_{i3}+x_{i3}x_{i4}+x_{i5}+x_{i5}x_{i6}
;

Locally Spiky Sparse (LSS)
:
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
=
\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i1}>0\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i2}>0\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i3}>0\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i4}>0\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i5}>0\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i6}>0\}}
, introduced by
Behr et al. 2022a
.

These functional forms capture a wide array of structures, including linear and nonlinear regimes as well as smooth (linear and polynomial) and non-smooth (LSS) scenarios.

We also considered varying levels of signal-to-noise ratio and network effect strength. Specifically, the noise variance
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
was chosen such that the proportion of variance explained (PVE) in
\boldsymbol{y}
by
\mathbf{X}
varied across
\{0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8\}
. To vary the network effect strength, we varied
\eta\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5\}
in the additive blockwise network effect model and
\omega\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}
in the network autocorrelation effect model. In each simulation, we randomly split the data into training (80%) and test (20%) sets. The training set was used to fit the prediction models while the test set was used to evaluate the prediction performance and compute the feature importances
(
Fisher et al. 2019a
;
Molnar 2025a
)
. We replicated each simulation scenario 100 times.

In Figure
3
A, we examine permutation feature importances computed by NeRF
+
. We see that (1) the signal features (orange) in each model are correctly identified as more important than the non-signal features (gray), and (2) the overall network’s importance (blue) increases as the strength of the network effect increases as desired. The increasing network importance also agrees with the prediction accuracy results (Figure
2
), which show prediction methods that do not use the network perform worse.

We can also investigate whether the importance of the network stems from the network cohesion
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and/or the network embedding features
\mathbf{Z}
. As seen in Figure
3
B, the network embedding features have higher importance than the network cohesion in the additive blockwise network effect setting, since the only way the network is affecting the response is through the block membership, and the eigenvectors of the Laplacian capture that structure well. On the other hand, network cohesion is more important than the network embedding in the network autocorrelation effect setting, since there each node is directly affected by its neighbors, and the global importance measure picks that up correctly. By allowing both network embedding features and network cohesion, NeRF
+
can effectively capture and adapt to different types of network effects while disentangling their respective contributions to the model. Similar findings hold for MDI
+
, different PVEs, and when we have correlated features; see Appendix
B
.

4.3
Sample Influence Results

We assess the approximate LOO coefficient estimates from Section
3.3
according to both (i) how well this approximation matches the oracle LOO coefficients obtained by manually leaving out each sample and refitting NeRF
+
(including refitting the trees and network embeddings for each LOO) and (ii) its practical utility for identifying influential samples, such as outliers. On task (i), we observe strong concordance between the approximate and oracle LOO coefficients; see detailed results in Appendix
B
. Here we report on (ii), assessing the effectiveness of the approximate sample influence measures for detecting outliers.

To this end, we generated data according to the previously described simulation setup and altered the response of a randomly selected training sample to create an outlier. Specifically, for a randomly selected training sample
i^{*}
in each simulation replicate, we added a constant to its response
y_{i^{*}}
, either adding
\kappa
times the standard deviation of the response
\boldsymbol{y}
if
y_{i^{*}}>0
, or subtracting this constant if
y_{i^{*}}\leq 0
. We varied
\kappa\in\{1,2,3,4\}
with larger values corresponding to more extreme outliers. Under each scenario, we then computed the approximate leave-one-out (LOO) coefficients using (
16
) and used them to estimate each training sample’s influence, defined as the mean squared difference between the original test predictions and the test predictions after leaving out that sample from training.

Figure
4
shows that as the perturbation strength (
\kappa
) increases, the influence of the outlier also increases, as it should. Moreover, the outlier was almost always estimated to have the highest influence (i.e., rank 1) when
\kappa\geq 3
. Influence measured by using the change in the estimated model parameters behaves similarly; we omit these results for brevity. Overall, these results confirm the ability of our sample influence measure to identify influential samples, such as outliers, that have a disproportionate impact on the fitted model and its resulting predictions.

Rank 1 corresponds to the most influential sample. Results are averaged across 100 replications with shaded regions denoting
\pm 1
SE.

5
Case Studies

In this section, we illustrate NeRF
+
on two case studies on reducing school conflicts and predicting crime rates in Philadelphia. While we do not know the mechanism of network influence in case studies, our results are intuitive and illustrate well the types of insights that can be obtained from NeRF
+
and the accompanying interpretability tools.

5.1
Case study 1: School conflict intervention

Data.

This dataset comes from an experiment originally conducted to assess the impact of anticonflict intervention workshops on reducing student conflicts within middle schools
(
Paluck et al. 2016a
)
. In this study, half of the schools were randomly assigned to receive an anticonflict intervention, in which a small number of students (on average 26 per school) were selected to attend biweekly anticonflict trainings throughout the school year. Additionally, as part of the study, all students were asked to fill out surveys at the beginning and end of the school year. These surveys asked about (i) the student’s demographics and background, (ii) who the students chose to spend the most time with in the last few weeks (i.e., their friends), and (iii) their perception of their school’s “friendliness”. Setting aside the original question of the study about effectiveness of the anticonflict training intervention, we focus on a prediction question: can we predict each student’s perception of their school’s friendliness at the end of the school year from what we know about them at the start of the year, and how much of this perception stems from their social network?

Following
Le & Li 2022a
, we define the response variable as the average of 13 questions (with responses ranging from 0-5) related to the student’s perception of their school’s friendliness at the end of the school year. A higher score indicates that the student perceives the school to be more friendly. To predict this end-of-year friendliness score, we use the following covariates: the student’s grade, gender, ethnicity, whether or not they live with both parents, whether or not they are a returning student, whether or not they participated in the anticonflict training (i.e., the treatment), and the student’s beginning-of-year school friendliness score. We also constructed a friendship network from the survey responses as in previous work
(
Paluck et al. 2016a
;
Le & Li 2022a
)
. Specifically, for each of the beginning- and end-of-year surveys, we constructed an undirected, unweighted network, where
A_{ij}=1
as long as student
i
lists student
j
as a friend or vice versa. We then averaged the adjacency matrices from the two surveys to produce the final undirected, weighted network used in the prediction modeling.

Methods.

For each of the 28 schools that received intervention in the study, we trained separate prediction models (NeRF
+
, RNC, Network BART, RF
+
, RF, linear regression, and BART) using the aforementioned covariates and friendship network to predict the students’ end-of-year friendliness scores. Each model was trained on the largest connected component of students (after removing students with missing survey responses) using 75% of the data for training and evaluated on the remaining 25%. The number of training samples ranged between 62 and 467, depending on the school (full list in Appendix
C
). We repeated this random training-test split 100 times for each school and averaged the results.

Results.

This case study presents a very noisy prediction problem: no matter the school or prediction method, the test
R^{2}
never exceeds 0.4 and averages around 0.17 (see Table
1
in Appendix
C
). Given this low signal and the heterogeneity across schools, it is no surprise that different methods perform best for different schools on prediction accuracy, but on average, RNC and NeRF
+
perform the best, both yielding an average test
R^{2}
of 0.190 across all schools (Table
1
). This competitiveness of NeRF
+
is noteworthy given that RF performed worse than ordinary linear regression for the large majority (i.e., 20 out of 28) of schools. Despite the heterogeneity between schools, NeRF
+
exhibited the most consistent prediction performance, being always ranked in the top-3 performing methods regardless of the school whereas RNC appeared in the bottom-4 performing methods for 3 schools (see Table
2
). This consistency of performance is a key advantage of NeRF
+
and results from its data-driven adaptability to both nonlinear and linear structures.

For two schools in the conflict intervention study, we show (A) the global permutation feature importances from NeRF

Beyond prediction, we examined NeRF

While, unsurprisingly, the student’s perceived school friendliness score at the beginning of the school year was by far the most important predictor of their end-of-year school friendliness score, Figure

also shows that the network was moderately important in many schools and that this was driven by the network cohesion component for some schools and by the network embedding for others.

test
R^{2}

+
test
R^{2}

For example, there is a tight-knit friend group (circled) in the 8th grade whose connections are associated with a strong negative perception of the school’s atmosphere. Examining the observed school friendliness scores for this 8th grade friend group (Figure

(B)), we indeed see that their school friendliness scores are below-average compared to the other 8th graders, but without access to the local network importances, identifying this friend group solely from the observed friendliness network would have been challenging. Being able to identify subsets of the network where connections are especially important could be helpful for tailoring interventions.

5.2
Case study 2: Crime in Philadelphia

Data.

This dataset from
opendataphilly.org
, on monthly crime rates between January 2006 and December 2021 for different census tracts in Philadelphia, was studied by
Deshpande 2025a
and hence included for comparison. The response variable is the crime rate for a particular month and census tract, measured as the number of crimes per square mile per month. Following
Deshpande 2025a
, we transformed these crime rates (heavily skewed) by applying an inverse hyperbolic sine transformation. To predict the crime rates,
Deshpande 2025a
used only the time variable indicating the month (
t=1,\dots,192
). We added weather-related covariates for that time (from
https://scacis.rcc-acis.org/
) including the average temperature per month, total amount of precipitation per month, number of days with precipitation per month, and the total amount of snowfall per month in Philadelphia. These covariates are expected to be only mildly related to the crime rates (and exhibit univariate correlations no stronger than 0.1), thereby providing an opportunity to examine feature selection and focus our attention on the network effect.
The observed network is unweighted and undirected with
384
nodes representing the census tracts in Philadelphia and edges connecting spatially-adjacent tracts. In total, there are
n=73,728
observations, where each observation corresponds to a particular census tract and month.

Methods.

Using the census tract network and covariates (
p=5
), we trained NeRF
+
, RNC, Network BART, and their non-network assisted counterparts (RF
+
, linear regression, and BART) to predict the monthly crime rates. Since multiple observations belong to each node (i.e., census tract), we adapted the NeRF
+
(and RNC) optimization problem (
7
) so that all observations belonging to the same census tract shared the same
\alpha_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
.

To evaluate performance, we split the data into a training and test set as follows. First, we randomly selected
75\%
of the tracts to be “training” tracts and
25\%
of the tracts to be “test” tracts. Then from only the training tracts, we sampled
\pi\%
(
\pi=\{0.1,0.5,1,5,10\}
) of the observations to form the training dataset. The remaining observations from the training tracts, combined with the observations from the test tracts, form the test dataset. This enables us to evaluate the test prediction error both on tracts that were seen in training and those that were never seen. Results are shown over 100 random data splits.

Results.

Figure
6
A summarizes the prediction performance results for the Philadelphia crime case study. We observe that NeRF
+
alongside RNC yield the best test prediction performance and can even achieve this optimal level of accuracy using
\sim 1\%
of the data.
More generally, it is also worth highlighting that: (1) the network-assisted methods (solid lines) substantially outperform the non-network-assisted methods (dashed lines), which show little-to-no predictive power, and (2) of the network-assisted methods, the network cohesion-based methods perform the best. This is expected given the spatial smoothness (translating to network cohesion) in the observed crime rates (Figure
6
C), which makes the network information by far the most relevant.

Both the global and local feature importances from NeRF
+
yield similar insights into the underlying network effect. In particular, the global feature importances from NeRF
+
(Figure

+
and the observed crime rates (Figure
6

+
’s ability to provide valuable, granular insights into the underlying network effects.

6
Discussion

NeRF
+
provides a flexible family of models, which can exploit dependencies created by a network when they help in prediction and capture both linear and nonlinear structures. The functional flexibility stems from NeRF
+
’s roots in RF
+
while the network dependencies are captured through the network cohesion penalty, which effectively learns smoothly-varying structures across the network, and through augmenting with network embedding features, which can be chosen to encode a variety of network properties.

An important contribution of NeRF
+
lies in its accompanying interpretation tools. They can be used to identify the components that drive the model’s predictions, examine the model for potential biases, provide key insights on how much predictive power is derived from the network connections compared to the features themselves, and suggest ways to further refine and improve the model. To facilitate the use of these interpretability tools in practice, we have developed an R package available at

https://github.com/tiffanymtang/nerfplus
,
which includes a user-friendly Shiny application for interactively exploring and visualizing the interpretations. Finally, even our initial exploration of local feature and network importances shows that NeRF
+
can distill and illuminate granular insights and identify interesting or unusual nodes and features in a number of ways.

When using NeRF
+
in practice, it should be emphasized that all interpretations are with respect to the fitted NeRF
+
model. That is, the feature importances (both global and local) measure how much a feature contributes to the prediction
using the given NeRF
+
model
, and the sample influences measure how leaving out a sample impacts the fit
of the given NeRF
+
model
. Extrapolations of these interpretations from the model to the underlying scientific process require further careful and critical domain-specific examination of the model
(
Yu & Kumbier 2020a
)
.
Moreover, while the proposed feature importance measures serve as valuable starting points, correlated features and other real-world challenges
(
Strobl et al. 2007a
;
Nicodemus 2011a
;
Hooker et al. 2021a
;
Alvarez-Melis & Jaakkola 2018a
)
can complicate the interpretation of feature importances, and they are not designed to provide evidence of truly causal relationships. Causal inference on networks is an active area of research, complicated vastly by the concept of network inference, and is beyond the scope of this paper; see for example
Aronow & Samii 2017a
and
Forastiere et al. 2021a
.

There is a number of ways for building or improving upon NeRF
+
. They include learning “optimal” network embeddings and fitting the model jointly (e.g., possibly incorporating ideas from
Luo et al. 2022a
and
Deshpande 2025a
); leveraging a more sophisticated cross-validation scheme to account for network dependencies when tuning hyperparameters; and exploiting the community structure in networks to obtain community-level importances, which in a number of scientific applications, for example, in brain imaging, is the most scientifically-relevant scale. Ultimately, NeRF
+
expands the scope and applicability of network-assisted machine learning, especially for problems where interpretability and transparency are essential.

Data and Code Availability

Code to reproduce the results in this work can be found at

https://github.com/tiffanymtang/nerfplus
.
This repository also includes an R package and Shiny application to run and interpret NeRF
+
for custom datasets. Datasets used in this work are available via previous publications:
Paluck et al. 2016a
for the school conflict intervention data (available from the authors by request);
Deshpande 2025a
for the Philadelphia crime data (see supplementary materials); and
Li et al. 2020c
for the CCLE protein data (also made available at

https://github.com/tiffanymtang/nerfplus
).

Appendix A
Derivation of Sample Influences

In this section, we derive the closed-form leave-one-out (LOO) parameter estimate given in (
16
) for NeRF
+
with
\ell_{2}
penalties. This derivation only needs to be done for a single tree
t
.

Recall that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{\top}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}]
denotes the parameter estimates obtained by solving (
7
) for one tree
t
, fitted on all
n
training samples. When fitting NeRF
+
with
\ell_{2}
penalties for
P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
P_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, NeRF
+
(
7
) reduces to a generalized ridge regression problem, so that

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\left(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{M}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y},

(17)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=[\mathbf{I}_{n},\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\Psi_{t}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})]=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{w}_{1}^{\top}\\
\cdots\\
\boldsymbol{w}_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{M},\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{\alpha}\mathbf{L}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\lambda_{\beta}\mathbf{I}_{p+r}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\lambda_{\gamma}\mathbf{I}_{m_{t}}\end{bmatrix}.

Now, suppose we leave out the
i^{th}
training sample and refit (
7
) in NeRF
+
without re-estimating the tree
t
, network embeddings
\mathbf{Z}
, the node degrees in the Laplacian matrix
\mathbf{L}
, or the regularization parameters
\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta},\lambda_{\gamma}
. Then, the corresponding LOO NeRF
+
parameter estimate, denoted
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}
, is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}=\left(\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{-i,-i}\mathbf{W}_{-i,-i}+\mathbf{M}_{-i,-i}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\top}_{-i,-i}\boldsymbol{y}_{-i},

(18)

where the subscript
-i
denotes the removal of the
i^{th}
row or column of the corresponding matrix (or vector).

Letting
\mathbf{B}^{(-i)}=\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}+\mathbf{M}_{-i,-i}
, we can write

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{-i,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{-i}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}+\frac{(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}}{1-\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}}\right\}\mathbf{W}_{-i,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{-i}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}+\frac{(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}}{1-\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}}\right\}\left(\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}y_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}}{1-\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}}\left\{\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}y_{i}\right\},

where the second equality follows from the Sherman-Morrison formula.

Note that Sherman-Morrison can also be applied to show that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}

\displaystyle=[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,-i}-\frac{1}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{B}^{-1}-\frac{1}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}^{\top}\right]_{-i,-i}.

Defining
(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}-\frac{1}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}^{\top}
, it follows that
(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}=[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}]_{-i,-i}
. Using this identity, we have the following equivalences:

(i)

\;\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}=\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}=:h_{i}
,

(ii)

\;(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}=\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}
, and

(iii)

\;(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}
,

where (iii) follows from the observation that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}^{(-i)})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,-i}\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}}_{=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}y_{i}}-\frac{1}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}\underbrace{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\cdot,-i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}}_{=\hat{\nu}_{i}-[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}y_{i}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\nu}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}.

Note also that similar to the ordinary linear regression case,
h_{i}
here can be interpreted as the leverage of sample
i
in the given generalized ridge regression problem.

Plugging these into the expression for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\nu}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}\right)-\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}y_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}}{1-h_{i}}\left\{\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\nu}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}\right)-h_{i}y_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\nu}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}}{1-h_{i}}\left\{(h_{i}-1)y_{i}+\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\nu}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}-h_{i}y_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\nu}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}+\frac{\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}}{1-h_{i}}\left\{-y_{i}+\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\nu}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}+\frac{\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}}{1-h_{i}}\left\{-y_{i}+(\hat{y}_{i}-\hat{\alpha}_{i})-\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}\right\},

where the last equality follows because
\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}=\hat{y}_{i}-\hat{\alpha}_{i}
and
\hat{\nu}_{i}=\hat{\alpha}_{i}
.

Finally, since

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{i,-i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}=\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}-w_{ii}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}=\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}-[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii},

we have that

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{(-i)}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}-\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}+\frac{\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}}{1-h_{i}}\left\{-y_{i}+(\hat{y}_{i}-\hat{\alpha}_{i})-\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}-\hat{\alpha}_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{-i}+\frac{\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}}{1-h_{i}}(\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i})-\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}\left([\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{-i,i}+\frac{\left[(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right]_{-i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}}{1-h_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}+\frac{(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}}{1-h_{i}}(\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i})-\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{i}}{[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{ii}}\left([\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}+\frac{(\mathbf{B}^{(i)})^{-1}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}[\mathbf{B}^{-1}]_{\cdot i}}{1-h_{i}}\right)\right]_{-i},

completing the derivation of (
16
).

B.1
Implementation details

NeRF
+
.

We fit NeRF
+
with
\ell_{2}
penalties for
P_{\beta}
and
P_{\gamma}
. Here, the initial RF within NeRF
+
was fitted using the
ranger
R package with the following hyperparameters: number of trees
=500
, proportion of features to consider at each split
=\frac{1}{3}
, and all other default hyperparameter values. We set the Laplacian regularization parameter to
\lambda_{L}=0.05
and the network embeddings
\mathbf{Z}
to be the top two components from the Laplacian eigenmap embedding of the network. To select the optimal regularization parameters in NeRF
+
, we used 5-fold CV and performed a grid search over all possible combinations of the regularization parameters
\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta},\lambda_{\gamma}
, considering 10 values logarithmically spaced between
10^{-4}
and
10^{3}
for each regularization parameter. Because tuning these regularization parameters for each tree in NeRF
+
can be computationally expensive for our repeated simulations, we tuned these regularization parameters for 10 (out of 500) trees in each forest and randomly chose from these tuned regularization parameters for the remaining trees in the forest. Finally, when computing the permutation feature importances, we used 50 permutations for each feature and evaluated the change in the test
R^{2}
value. When computing MDI
+
, we used the
R^{2}
value to assess the similarity between the observed responses and the partial model predictions.

RNC.

Similar to NeRF
+
, we fit RNC with an
\ell_{2}
penalty applied to the regression coefficients, set the Laplacian regularization parameter to
\lambda_{L}=0.05
, and used 5-fold grid search CV to select the optimal regularization parameters
\lambda_{\alpha}
and
\lambda_{\beta}
, considering 10 values logarithmically spaced between
10^{-4}
and
1000
for each regularization parameter.

Network BART.

We implemented Network BART using the
flexBART
R package
(
Deshpande 2025a
)
. Here, we allowed for non-uniform cutpoints (and instead, searched across all possible cutpoints for each feature) and set all other hyperparameters to their defaults.

RF
+
.

RF
+
was fitted in a similar manner as NeRF
+
, but with
\lambda_{\alpha}=0
.

BART.

To fit BART, we used the
BART
R package
(
Sparapani et al. 2021a
)
with the following hyperparameters: number of trees
=500
, number of posterior draws
=2000
, and all other hyperparameters set to their default values.

Correlated features.

In the base simulations presented in Section
4
, we generated the covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}
with i.i.d. standard normal entries. To demonstrate NeRF
+
’s strong performance under more realistic correlated designs, we repeated the simulation study, keeping the same data-generating processes for the response and network but using a real dataset as the covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}
, the proteomics data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE)
(
Barretina et al. 2012a
)
, which is known to have highly correlated features. We processed the data as in previous work
(
Li et al. 2020c
)
. This dataset contains 370 cell lines (samples) and 214 proteins (features). For each simulation replicate, we randomly sampled 80% of the 370 samples for training and used the remaining 20% for testing. We also randomly sampled different features to serve as the signal features in
f
in each simulation replicate, but kept the full set of features (
p=214
) in
\mathbf{X}
when fitting the models. Note that due to the correlated nature of these real-world features, there was extremely low variability in the Locally Spiky Sparse (LSS) scenario, and thus we omitted it from the correlated features simulation study.

Figure
10
summarizes the prediction accuracies from the correlated features simulation study. As in the independent features setting, NeRF
+
consistently yields the best or is on par with the best-performing prediction model across both linear and nonlinear (i.e., polynomial) simulation settings. We also observe that under the linear simulation setting, NeRF
+
substantially outperforms RNC in terms of prediction accuracy; this is unlike the independent feature setting, where RNC and NeRF
+
yielded almost identical prediction accuracies under the linear simulation setting. We speculate this gap in performance is due to the sparse nature of the true linear model and the large number of features (
p=214
) in the data. While RNC is a dense (non-sparse) model, NeRF
+
has two mechanisms for adapting to sparsity: (i) the trees in RF inherently perform feature selection as they may not split on all features, and (ii) as recommended in
Agarwal et al. 2025a
, only those features used to split in the trees are included in the
\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
term in NeRF
+
.

In Figure
11
, we provide the corresponding NeRF
+
global feature importances from the correlated features simulation study. Similar to the independent features setting, we show that the permutation and MDI
+
importances satsify several desirable properties even in the presence of correlated features. Namely, (i) the signal features correctly have higher importance than the non-signal features, (ii) the importance of the network increases as the simulated strength of the network effect increases, and (iii) the importance of network cohesion is higher than that of the network embedding features in the network autocorrelation effect model; the opposite is generally true in the additive blockwise network effect model although it is less pronounced than in the independent features setting.
