Enhanced CD33 CAR-NK cells secreting anti-CD73scFv overcome adenosine-mediated immunosuppression and improve anti-AML efficacy
Authors: Lu Wang, Sha Gong, Jun Wang, Yingying Bao, Nan Mei, Xiaohong Lu, Weiwei Chen, Lei Xi, Huanming Zhang, Xin Chen, Pengbo Ning, Xiaohu Fan, Huaiyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive hematologic malignancy with dismal outcomes, especially in relapsed/refractory settings. Chimeric antigen receptor natural killer (CAR-NK) cell therapy holds promise but is constrained by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), where adenosine-mediated suppression is a key barrier.ObjectiveTo develop a novel CAR-NK construct cotargeting AML cells and the adenosine-rich TME to enhance antileukemia efficacy.MethodsEx vivo expanded primary NK cells were used to compare the effects of CD39 versus CD73 blockade on NK cell function via messenger RNA-electroporated antibodies. A CD33-CD73 dual-function CAR-NK construct (integrating CD33-specific lysis and anti-CD73scFv secretion for TME disruption) was designed and transduced into NK cells via retrovirus. Engineered NK cells were characterized for transduction efficiency, expansion, purity, viability, and CAR stability. In vitro cytotoxicity against AML cell lines and primary blasts was assessed, and in vivo efficacy was evaluated in a MOLM-13 xenograft mouse model.ResultsCD73 blockade more potently enhanced NK cell activity than CD39 blockade. Retroviral transduction achieved >50% efficiency, and expansion with K562-4-1BBL-mbIL-21/−15 feeder cells yielded NK cells with ≥6,000 fold expansion, >93% purity, >98% viability, and stable CAR expression. At an effector-to-target ratio of 0.5:1, CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells mediated ~80% specific lysis, with superior cytotoxicity vs conventional CD33 CAR-NK cells. In xenografts, CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells achieved robust tumor clearance, extended median survival by 24.5 days (59.5 vs 35 days) versus standard CD33 CAR-NK cells, and five out of six mice achieved long-term survival (>50 days).ConclusionThe CD33-CD73 dual-targeting CAR-NK platform synergistically targets AML cells and the adenosine-rich TME, exhibiting superior anti-leukemia efficacy. This strategy advances AML immunotherapy and provides a translational blueprint for TME-targeted therapies in other cancers.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Chimeric antigen receptor - CAR, Natural killer - NK

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Chimeric antigen receptor natural killer (CAR-NK) cells targeting CD33 show promise in treating acute myeloid leukemia (AML). However, their efficacy is limited by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. The CD73-adenosine pathway is a key mechanism suppressing immune cell function in AML. While blocking CD73 can enhance anti-tumor immunity, current strategies primarily involve co-administering checkpoint inhibitors with cell therapies.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrates a novel CD33-CD73 dual-function CAR-NK cell therapy that uniquely integrates tumor-specific cytotoxicity with local immunomodulation by secreting anti-CD73scFv antibodies. We provide direct evidence that CD73 blockade is more potent than CD39 blockade in enhancing NK cell function. Compared with conventional CD33 CAR-NK, our engineered cells show superior in vitro cytotoxicity and achieve long-term survival in five out of six treated mice, extending median survival by 24.5 days, thus offering a promising all-in-one strategy for overcoming the immunosuppressive AML microenvironment.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study introduces an integrated “all-in-one” CAR-NK cell platform, which may shift research paradigms from combining separate therapies to engineering multifunctional cells. It provides a definitive preclinical rationale for prioritizing CD73 over CD39 blockade in the AML microenvironment. For practice, if translated clinically, this strategy could simplify treatment regimens and enhance efficacy against refractory AML.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), the most common acute leukemia in adults, is a highly heterogeneous malignancy associated with poor survival outcomes—particularly in elderly patients.1 Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation remains the cornerstone of curative therapy for AML following high-dose chemotherapy. Nevertheless, its utility is constrained by limited applicability, frequent failure to fully eradicate the malignancy, and a substantial risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD).2 3 The advent of novel strategies that direct the immune system to eliminate leukemic cells by targeting unique surface markers has made immunotherapy an integral component of contemporary AML treatment paradigms.4 In other hematologic malignancies, such as B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, a novel cell therapy employing chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-modified T cells has demonstrated remarkable efficacy, ultimately culminating in its approval by the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency.5 The translation of this success to CAR T-cell therapy for AML has long been desired. Its success in AML has been limited by the ideal target antigen, myelosuppression, and immunosuppressive leukemia microenvironment.6 7 However, the long-term persistence of CAR-T cells poses a clinical challenge due to the potential for sustained hematopoietic suppression. Moreover, their autologous origin makes production costly and time-consuming, which is often unfeasible for heavily pretreated patients.

Natural killer (NK) cells are innate lymphocytes that play a vital role in antitumor immunity. A key biological advantage is their lack of T-cell receptors, which allows them to be used in allogeneic settings without inducing GVHD.8 These characteristics make NK cells a highly promising “off-the-shelf” allogeneic cell therapy candidate strategy, combining enhanced safety with the potential for large-scale production. They recognize and eliminate malignant cells through multiple mechanisms—including the release of cytotoxic granules, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and induction of apoptosis.9 A key feature of NK cell function is their ability to detect “missing-self” phenotypes: while inhibitory receptors engaging class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules suppress NK activity on healthy cells, tumor cells often downregulate MHC I to evade T cell recognition. This loss of inhibitory signaling shifts the balance toward activating signals, triggering NK-mediated lysis. Given these intrinsic attributes—potent cytotoxicity, unique recognition mechanisms, and a favorable safety profile—NK cells have emerged as a promising platform for CAR engineering. CAR-NK cells not only retain the MHC-unrestricted targeting and “off-the-shelf” potential of native NK cells but also exhibit reduced risks of on-target/off-tumor toxicity and cytokine release syndrome compared with CAR-T therapies.10 Allogeneic CAR-NK cell therapy combined with cryopreservation facilitates the production of universal off-the-shelf preparations, which greatly promotes clinical availability and lowers the cost of corresponding immunotherapies.11 12 CD33, a lineage-restricted myeloid antigen expressed in approximately 90% of AML blast cells,13 is regarded as a preferable therapeutic target with manageable off-tumor adverse effects.14–17 Accumulating evidence is establishing the potent anti-AML efficacy of CD33-targeted CAR-NK (CD33 CAR-NK) cell therapies.18–20

Although targeting CD33 allows NK cells to eliminate AML tumor cells more effectively, this potential is limited by the suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). A mainstream strategy for NK cell generation involves ex vivo expansion using genetically engineered feeder cells, such as K562 cells expressing 4–1 BBL and membrane-bound IL-21/IL-15.21 Engagement of 4–1 BBL on NK cells by tumor cells triggers the actin polymerization-dependent exocytosis of intracellular vesicles containing CD73, mobilizing it to the cell surface and extracellular space.22 Chemotherapeutic agents and various cancer therapies induce increased cell death, followed by the release of ATP into the TME.23 Within TME, ATP is rapidly hydrolyzed to adenosine (ADO) by the ectonucleotidases CD39 and CD73: CD39 first converts ATP to AMP, which is then further hydrolyzed to ADO by CD73.24 25 Extracellular ADO accumulation leads to engagement with one of the four G-protein-coupled receptors (A1R, A2AR, A2BR, A3R) expressed on tumor cells, immune cells, and endothelial cells. This signaling cascade suppresses the function of effector NK cells, thereby promoting the formation of an immunosuppressive TME. Notably, exosomes released into the TME following cell death also carry CD39 and CD73, which further amplifies this immunosuppressive pathway.24 25 Extracellular adenosine generated in response to TME hypoxia has been recognized as a critical immunosuppressive metabolite that impairs anticancer immune responses.26 Adenosine signals through A2A receptors limit NK cell maturation, inhibit proliferation, and reduce the proportion of mature NK cells.27 It has been widely recognized that the CD39/CD73/A2AR/adenosine pathway exerts a vital role in regulating immune responses.28 29 Therefore, the suppression of CD39/CD73/A2AR blocks a necessary pathway for generating adenosine within the TME and can reverse the inhibitory function of adenosine.30–33

In AML, CD39 and CD73 serve as critical purinergic immune checkpoint molecules and T cell exhaustion markers. Aberrant expression of CD39 together with TIGIT on CD8+ T cells is markedly elevated in newly diagnosed and relapsed AML, accompanied by decreased CD127/TCF-1 and increased TOX expression, driving T cell exhaustion and dysfunction. The CD39/CD73 purinergic axis further shapes an immunosuppressive bone marrow (BM) microenvironment, blunting antileukemic T and NK cell immune responses, and represents a promising therapeutic target to reverse immune evasion in AML.34 The CD39 protein is closely associated with AML progression, and its elevated expression correlates with poor prognosis and immune suppression in patients with AML. The leukemic driver mutation of the myeloid transcription factor CEBPA leads to aberrant activation of its direct transcriptional target Nt5e (encoding CD73) via the CEBPA-p30 isoform, and targeting the CD73/A2AR axis inhibits leukemic growth and improves survival in AML models.35 The CD39/CD73/adenosine-A2A receptor axis is closely associated with the formation of immunosuppressive TME and adverse clinical prognosis, and CD39/CD73 also participate in tumor cell proliferation, migration and invasion, serving as valuable prognostic biomarkers in hematologic malignancies.36 37

Here, we report the generation of CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells from peripheral blood via retroviral transduction, which exhibited stable CAR expression, robust proliferation, and the capacity to reduce adenosine levels. They demonstrated significantly enhanced cytotoxicity against CD33-positive AML cell lines and primary patient-derived AML blasts in vitro. Furthermore, in a Molm-13 xenograft mouse model, this dual-targeting approach mediated potent tumor clearance, resulting in prolonged survival with no tumor recurrence for 60 days, and was well-tolerated without observable side effects. Our research demonstrates that the combined secretion of CAR-NK targeting CD33, along with inhibitors that block the CD73-adenosine pathway, exhibits a remarkable synergistic effect in preclinical models. This finding offers a novel strategy for concurrently tackling these two significant challenges.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and primary AML cells

The OCI-AML3, U937, MOLM-13, and 293T cell lines were obtained from the Hematology Laboratory and the Gene and Cell Therapy Research Institute at The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. OCI-AML3, MOLM-13, and U937 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (HyClone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, China). The 293T-clone2 cell line, a self-established derivative of 293T cells, is a packaging cell line stably expressing essential components required for retrovirus production (including viral structural and enzymatic proteins). For retroviral packaging, this cell line was maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (HyClone, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, and enables efficient retrovirus generation on simple transfection with the target plasmid, eliminating the need for cotransfection of additional packaging plasmids. Primary AML cells, isolated from the BM of patients, were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. These BM samples of patients with AML were obtained from the Department of Hematology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University.

Expansion of NK cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donors using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (GE Healthcare, USA). The isolated PBMCs were co-cultured with thawed, irradiated K562-4-1BBL-mbIL-21/−15 feeder cells at a 1:1 ratio. The culture medium consisted of a serum-free base medium (Hangzhou Zhongying Bio-Medical Technology, China) supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated autologous plasma and recombinant human IL-2 (200 U/mL; Jiangsu Kingsley Pharmaceutical, China). 3 days post-culture, the cell suspension was centrifuged at 350 g for 5 min, and then the supernatant was discarded and an equal amount of fresh medium was added. On day 7, a second batch of thawed K562-4-1BBL-mbIL-21/−15 feeder cells were added to re-stimulate the culture. Cells were harvested between days 12 and 14 of culture. NK cell purity was determined by flow cytometry analysis.

mRNA transcription

Based on patent sequences of IPH5201 and IPH5301, single-chain variable fragments (scFv) targeting human CD39 and CD73 were constructed by linking variable heavy and light chains via a peptide linker. The corresponding CD39scfv and CD73scfv gene sequences were cloned into the pT7-EGFP plasmid vector. The linearized plasmids were used as templates for in vitro transcription to generate messenger RNA (mRNA) incorporating a poly(A) tail, which was subsequently purified (MEGAclear Transcription Clean-Up Kit). For mRNA electroporation, 1 × 10⁶ expanded NK cells (harvested between day 9 of culture) were washed with serum-free and antibiotic-free NK medium and resuspended in an electroporation mixture consisting of 10 µL Celetrix solution A and 10 µL solution B per 20 µL reaction. CD39scfv or CD73scfv mRNA was added to the electroporation buffer, with one tube reserved as EGFP-mRNA control. The cell suspension was gently transferred into electrode cuvettes to avoid air bubbles. Electroporation was performed at 520 V, after which the cells were immediately transferred to a 24-well plate for continued culture.

CAR construction and lentiviral vector production

The CD33-directed CAR (CD33 CAR) featured an N-terminal CD8 leader peptide to ensure membrane localization, and was composed of the My96 derived from gemtuzumab ozogamicin—an antibody-drug conjugate approved for the clinical treatment of AML—fused in-frame to a CD8α hinge/transmembrane domain, a 4–1BB costimulatory domain, and a CD3ζ signaling domain. To enable constitutive cytokine signaling, a membrane-bound form of interleukin-15 (mbIL-15) was co-expressed via a self-cleaving T2A peptide sequence, with IL-15 anchored to the membrane through the IL-15 receptor α chain. This base construct is referred to as CD33 CAR. A bispecific CAR construct targeting both CD33 and CD73 (designated CD33-CD73 CAR) was generated by further introducing a human anti-CD73scFv downstream of an additional T2A peptide. All CAR constructs were synthesized and subcloned into a pMSCV-puro retroviral vector using the BglII and ClaI sites for subsequent transduction. To package the retroviral particles, the resulting plasmids were transfected into 293T-clone2 cells using GMTrans Liposomal Transfection Reagent.

Transduction of primary NK cells

NK cells were expanded from healthy donor-derived PBMCs for 5 days before transduction. To improve transduction efficiency, 12-well plates were coated overnight at 4°C with RetroNectin (5 µg/mL in Dulbecco’s Phosphate‑Buffered Saline (DPBS; TaKaRa), blocked with 2% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in DPBS for 30 min at room temperature, and washed once with DPBS. On the transduction day, expanded NK cells were seeded at 1×10⁶ cells/well in 1 mL medium onto precoated plates. Viral supernatant was added at a volume optimized by predetermined viral titer and target MOI, with protamine sulfate supplemented to a final concentration of 40 µg/mL to facilitate viral entry. Spinoculation was performed at 900×g for 60 min at 32°C.

On day 7, CAR-NK cells were counted and adjusted to ~1×10⁶ cells/mL, with 1% autologous human plasma added at this time and all subsequent medium changes. Irradiated K562-FC-γ feeder cells were thawed and co-cultured with CAR-NK cells at a 1:1 ratio to support proliferation. From days 8–11, medium was refreshed as needed based on acidification and cell density, with consistent supplementation of 1% autologous plasma. Engineered CAR-NK cells were harvested on day 12 for downstream functional assays and analyses.

Flow cytometry analysis

The purity of NK cells and the expression levels of surface receptors were assessed by flow cytometry at days 14 of culture. Details are provided in the supplementary materials. Data acquisition was performed using a flow cytometer, and the resulting data were analyzed with NoveExpress software.

Cytotoxicity detection

Target cells were primary AML BM mononuclear cells as well as stable luciferase-expressing lines (MOLM-13-Luc, OCI-AML3-Luc, U937-Luc) for quantification. Briefly, effector and target cells were co-cultured at various effector-to-target (E:T) ratios (5:1, 2:1, 1:1, 0.5:1) in 384-well plates. After incubation, 30 µL of ONE-Glo Luciferase Reagent was added to each well, followed by a 3-minute incubation at room temperature. Luminescence was measured using a luminometer.

Primary AML cells were isolated from BM samples obtained from patients with relapsed and refractory AML using standard Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. These BM samples were provided by the Department of Hematology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University.

Primary AML patient-derived tumor cells were stained with Cell Trace CFSE proliferation kit (Invitrogen) and co-cultured with effector cells at various E:T ratios for the indicated time period at 37°C and 5% CO₂. Afterwards, cells were stained with propidium iodide (Miltenyi Biotec), and target cell cytotoxicity was analyzed by flow cytometry.

CD107a degranulation assay

Cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in complete NK medium. Cells were seeded into a U-bottom 96-well plate at a density of 2×10⁴ cells per well. APC-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD107a antibody (BioLegend, USA) was added directly to the wells prior to co-culture. NK cells were incubated with target cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1 for 5 hours. After the first hour of incubation, GolgiStop containing monensin (BD Biosciences) was introduced. Following a total of 5 hours of incubation, cells were stained with anti-CD56-APC and anti-CD3-FITC antibodies. Finally, cells were resuspended in 100 µL of Fluorescence‑Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) buffer and analyzed on a flow cytometer using NoveExpress software to assess CD107a surface expression.

Detection of NK cell proinflammation factors

MOLM-13 cells were co-incubated with CD33-CD73 CAR NK cells, CD33 CAR NK cells, or control NK cells at a 1:1 ratio for 6 hours. The concentration of human tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and human interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) was measured using the AuthentiKine Human IFN-gamma ELISA Kit or AuthentiKine Human TNF-alpha ELISA Kit (Proteintech, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vivo evaluation of CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells in xenografted mice

To evaluate the potential therapeutic effect of CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells for cancer therapy, an acute leukemia mouse model was employed. Female NSG mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were procured from the Saiye Model Biology Research Center. The mice were housed and maintained in individual ventilated cages under specific pathogen-free conditions, with adequate access to food and water.

On Day 0, the mice were injected via the tail vein with MOLM-13-Luc cells (5×10⁴) to establish a tumor model. On Day 4, the mice were injected with PBS, NK cells, CD33 CAR-NK cells, or CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells (1×10⁷ each) for treatment. 7 days after treatment, the mice were injected with D-fluorescein potassium salt and the bioluminescence signal was analyzed using an IVIS Lumina II Multispectral Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA) to verify the tumor cell engraftment. Tumor progression was monitored weekly through bioluminescence imaging (BLI). All mice were euthanized when either the experimental or humane endpoints were reached.

Statistics

Group differences were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance or Student’s t-test. Each experiment was conducted with a minimum of three independent replicates. The survival distributions were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the use of log-rank test between groups. The data were performed using SPSS software (V.23.0). Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism V.9 software. The following denotations for significance levels were used: *p<0.05; **p<0.01; ***p<0.001; ****p<0.0001.

Results

NK cells engineered to secrete anti-CD39 or anti-CD73scFv attenuate adenosine production and enhance tumor cytotoxicity

To mitigate TME adenosine-mediated immunosuppression, primary NK cells were engineered via mRNA electroporation to constitutively secrete CD39scFv or CD73scFv. These two ectonucleotidases drive immunosuppression by sequentially catabolizing ATP into adenosine, and our engineered NK cell strategy disrupts this immunosuppressive cascade (figure 1A). To this end, primary NK cells were expanded from PBMC of healthy donors using genetically modified K562 feeder cells, yielding a final purity of >93% and viability of >98% (figure 1B, online supplemental figure 1A). For functional validation, we first optimized an electroporation protocol tailored for primary NK cells. Using the Celetrix system at 520 V, we achieved efficient transfection with GFP-encoding mRNA, characterized by high transfection efficiency and minimal cytotoxicity. This was corroborated by well-preserved cell viability at 48 hours postelectroporation, which was comparable to that of untreated NK cell controls (figure 1C), ensuring the electroporation process did not interfere with subsequent functional assessments.

We next investigated whether scFv secretion by NK cells could attenuate extracellular adenosine accumulation. Control NK cells, or those electroporated with anti-CD39/CD73scFv mRNA, were co-cultured with OCI-AML3 at an E:T ratio of 1:1. After 9 hours, the concentration of adenosine in the supernatant was quantified. Control NK cells maintained high extracellular adenosine levels (approximately 35 µM, n=3). This adenosine production was significantly reduced in cultures with NK-39 (anti-CD39scFv) cells (to ~29 µM), and the reduction was far more robust in cultures with NK-73 cells (anti-CD73scFv) (to ~20 µM) (figure 1D). Notable differences were observed between the control NK group and both the NK-39 and NK-73 groups. These results confirm that scFv-mediated blockade of either CD39 or CD73 effectively mitigates adenosine accumulation in the co-culture system, with CD73 targeting conferring superior inhibitory activity. Concurrent cytotoxicity assays showed NK-73 exhibited the strongest killing efficiency against all AML cell lines (OCI-AML3, MOLM-13, U937) across all E:T ratios (5:1, 2:1, 1:1 and 0.5:1), followed by NK-39; control NK cells had the lowest activity (figure 1E). We further assessed NK cell activation via flow cytometric analysis of CD107a (a degranulation marker): CD107a+ cells (R3 gate) accounted for 40.18% of control NK cells, 49.84% of NK-39, and 63.45% of NK-73 (figure 1F). This graded enhancement in degranulation aligns with cytotoxicity data, confirming that CD39/CD73 blockade potentiates NK cell degranulation and contributes to improved antitumor activity. Collectively, these data demonstrate that blockade of CD39 or CD73 effectively enhances NK cell antitumor potency. On this basis, we further analyzed the functional performance of mixed CD39-scFv and CD73-scFv NK cells (online supplemental figure 3C). Finally, we measured the relative expression (fold change vs control NK) of key NK cell receptors: NK-73 showed the greatest upregulation across all receptors (eg, ~1.8 fold for NKP30, ~1.7 fold for CD69), while NK-39 exhibited moderate increases (figure 1G).

CD73 regulates the biological functions of NK cells and related mechanistic exploration

To further explore the mechanisms underlying the effects of CD73 on NK cell effector functions, we conducted a series of mechanistic studies. First, we confirmed abundant CD73 expression on OCI-AML3 cells, and characterized the endogenous surface expression of CD39 and CD73 on primary NK cells (online supplemental figure 1B and C). Moreover, we also detected the expression levels of CD39 and CD73 in primary AML cells from patients (online supplemental figure 1D). Collectively, these results indicated the existence of an adenosine-dependent immunosuppressive network within the TME. NK cells isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy donors barely expressed CD73. After 24-hour co-culture with OCI-AML-3 cells, the expression level of CD39 on NK cells increased by nearly 30%, while CD73 expression rose by more than 10% (online supplemental figure 2A). To establish a causal link between CD73 expression and NK cell activity, we knocked out CD73 in NK cells using electroporation. We assessed CD73 knockout efficiency by flow cytometry, which revealed a substantial reduction of surface CD73 in edited NK cells (online supplemental figure 2B). Consistent with the phenotypic validation, CD73-knockout NK cells exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxicity against OCI-AML-3 cells compared with control NK cells. To verify the involvement of adenosine, we added exogenous adenosine to the co-culture. The enhanced cytotoxicity of CD73-knockout NK cells was significantly reduced, confirming that CD73-mediated immunosuppression acts via the adenosine pathway (online supplemental figure 2C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CD73 negatively regulates the antitumor activity of NK cells through the adenosine signaling pathway in the AML TME.

Efficient generation of CD33 and CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells with enhanced activating phenotype

To improve the specific recognition and cytotoxicity of AML cells by NK cells, primary NK cells were subjected to genetic modification via retroviral transduction. This manipulation enabled the engineered NK cells to express a CAR targeting CD33. To concomitantly mitigate adenosine-mediated immunosuppression in the TME, we integrated a secretory anti-CD73scFv gene via a T2A peptide, creating a CD33-CD73 CAR vector (figure 2A). An mbIL-15 domain was also incorporated via a T2A peptide to enhance NK cell persistence, enabling co-translation of the CAR and mbIL-15 under the regulation of a single promoter. The functional components of two engineered NK cell constructs are schematically illustrated (figure 2B), and the preparation process of CAR-NK cells is depicted (figure 2C). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that the purity of NK cells remained above 93% for both types of retrovirally transduced CAR-NK cells, with cell viability consistently exceeding 98% (figure 2D). Following transduction, the efficiency of CAR expression on NK cells was assessed. Representative data from one donor showed that the CAR positivity rate was 70.91% for CD33 CAR-NK cells and 61.43% for CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells. Although the extended length of the CD33-CD73 CAR construct might theoretically compromise transduction efficiency, comparative analysis of CAR expression levels between CD33 CAR-NK and CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells across multiple donors revealed no statistically significant difference (figure 2E).

The fold expansion curves of unmodified NK cells, CD33 CAR-NK cells, and CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells exhibited a similar upward trend over the tested expansion period, with the fold expansion exceeding 6,000, which meets the dosage requirements for clinical manufacturing. The incorporation of the CD73scFv domain did not exert a noticeable effect on the in vitro expansion capacity of CAR-NK cells (figure 2F). This result suggests that the extended CAR construct does not impair the proliferation ability of engineered NK cells.

To further explore whether CAR modification induces phenotypic changes that may affect NK cell function, we evaluated the expression of other receptors related to target cell recognition or cytotoxicity. The activating receptors NKG2D, NKp30, and NKp44 were significantly upregulated in both CD33 CAR-NK and CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells compared with unmodified control NK cells; among these, the upregulation of NKp44 was more prominent in CD33 CAR-NK cells. The inhibitory receptor KIR2DL2/3 showed a noticeable downregulation in CD33 CAR-NK cells, while the activating receptor CD16 was moderately upregulated in CD33 CAR-NK cells relative to control NK cells. In contrast, the expression of the inhibitory receptor NKG2A did not differ substantially between the two CAR-NK constructs, though it was slightly lower than that in unmodified NK cells (figure 2G). These results suggest that CAR engineering may enhance the activating phenotype of NK cells, which could contribute to their improved target recognition and killing capacity.

CD33-CD73 CAR-NK demonstrated potent in vitro killing efficiency toward various CD33 AML cell lines and primary cells derived from patients with AML

To systematically evaluate the targeted killing specificity of CD33 CAR-NK cells against AML, we first constructed CD33-negative MOLM-13 knockout cells (MOLM-1333KO) via gene knockout combined with magnetic bead sorting, and verified their CD33 expression level by flow cytometry. Results showed that MOLM-13 cells highly expressed CD33, while MOLM-1333KO cells exhibited almost no CD33 expression (figure 3A), providing a reliable target cell model for subsequent functional studies. On this basis, we co-cultured unmodified NK cells or CD33 CAR-NK cells with the two types of target cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1, and evaluated the killing efficiency by detecting the proportion of residual cells. The results indicated that unmodified NK cells showed no significant difference in cytotoxicity against CD33-positive and CD33-knockout cells; in contrast, CD33 CAR-NK cells exhibited significantly enhanced killing activity exclusively against CD33-positive MOLM-13 cells, confirming that their cytotoxicity is strictly CD33-dependent (figure 3B).

Based on previous findings that blocking CD73 can enhance NK cell function, we further investigated whether integrating a CD73-targeting module into the CD33 CAR structure could synergistically improve the antileukemic efficacy of CD33 CAR-NK cells. To this end, we conducted cytotoxicity assays at graded E:T ratios using three AML cell lines: MOLM-13, U937, and AML-3. The results showed that the cytotoxicity of unmodified NK cells decreased markedly as the E:T ratio decreased from 5:1 to 0.5:1, indicating a clear E:T ratio dependency. In contrast, both CD33 CAR-NK and CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells maintained stable cytotoxicity exceeding 60% at all ratios tested, performing significantly better than unmodified NK cells. Notably, CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells consistently exhibited slightly stronger killing activity than CD33 CAR-NK cells across all cell lines and E:T ratios (figure 3C). Next, to validate the efficacy of the above system in a setting closer to the clinical scenario, we repeated the graded E:T ratio cytotoxicity assay using primary cells isolated from three patients with AML (figure 3C). Flow cytometry detection confirmed that CD33 was highly expressed in all three primary AML samples, with a positive rate exceeding 90% (online supplemental figure 3A). Results demonstrated that as the E:T ratio decreased from 5:1 to 0.5:1, unmodified NK cells exhibited the most pronounced reduction in cytotoxicity, while CD33 CAR-NK cells also showed a decline, although to a lesser extent. In contrast, CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells maintained the highest and most stable killing efficiency across all ratios, retaining over 60% cytotoxicity even at a 1:1 E:T ratio. These findings further support the enhanced potential of CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells for clinical translation in the treatment of primary AML.

To further clarify the regulatory mechanism of the CAR structure on the activation status and effector function of NK cells, we detected the expression level of the degranulation marker CD107a, as well as the secretion of key cytokines including IFN-γ and TNF-α. Results showed that unmodified NK cells had the lowest percentage of CD107a+ cells, while CD33-CD73 CAR-NK exhibited a significantly higher CD107a+ proportion than CD33 CAR-NK, indicating stronger degranulation capacity and cell activation level. In terms of cytokine release, CD33-CD73 CAR-NK also secreted the highest levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α, with unmodified NK cells showing the lowest secretion. These results confirm from two key dimensions—activation marker expression and cytokine secretion—that the CD33-CD73 CAR structure can synergistically enhance the comprehensive immune function of NK cells.

CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells exhibit superior anti-AML efficacy and persistence in vivo

To further evaluate the in vivo anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-NK cells, we established a humanized MOLM-13 xenograft mouse model of AML. NSG mice (female, 6–8 weeks old) received an intravenous injection of 5×10⁴ MOLM-13-Luc cells. On day 4 postinoculation, mice were randomized into four cohorts (n=6/group) and treated intravenously with 1×10⁷ cells of one of the following: unmodified NK cells, CD33 CAR-NK cells, CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells, or PBS as a vehicle control. Leukemia burden was monitored longitudinally by in vivo BLI on a weekly basis, and the expansion of adoptively transferred NK cells was tracked in parallel via flow cytometry of serial tail vein blood samples (figure 4A). Benefiting from the sustained expression of mbIL-15, infused CAR-NK cells were readily detectable in the peripheral blood of mice, whereas conventional unmodified NK cells were barely detectable in vivo (online supplemental figure 3B). The results demonstrated that CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells exerted superior antitumor efficacy over unmodified NK cells and CD33 CAR-NK cells (figure 4B). Moreover, the tumor burden in the CD33 CAR-NK group was markedly reduced relative to the unmodified NK cell group. Quantitative analysis of total flux further confirmed that the tumor burden in the CD33-CD73 CAR-NK group was significantly lower than that in other groups (figure 4C). Ultimately, the enhanced tumor suppression translated into substantial survival benefits. The median survival time of mice treated with CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells reached 59.5 days (figure 4D), whereas the survival durations of control cohorts were notably shorter (tumor-only group: 21 days; unmodified NK cell group: 26.5 days; CD33 CAR-NK cell group: 35 days). Collectively, these findings indicate that CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells can effectively eradicate AML tumor cells in vivo and prolong the survival of xenograft models, highlighting their promising therapeutic potential for clinical translation.

Discussion

In the rapidly moving field of CAR-immune cell therapies, recent studies have shown that primary CAR-NK cells are safe and can be highly effective.14 38 39 In our study, we developed and identified a novel, bifunctional CAR-NK cell therapy that can jointly target the tumor-associated antigen CD33 and secrete CD73 antibodies to target the immunosuppressive adenosine pathway in the TME. Our data indicate that this bifunctional strategy significantly enhances the anti-AML efficacy of NK cells through two synergistic mechanisms-direct killing of tumor cells and disruption of key immunosuppressive axes.

The initial part of our research is to establish the fundamental strategy for reversing adenosine-mediated immunosuppression. By modifying NK cells to secrete blocking single-chain antibodies against CD39 or CD73, we successfully reduced the accumulation of extracellular adenosine in the TME. This intervention measure alone is sufficient to significantly enhance the intrinsic cytotoxicity of NK cells against AML cell lines, among which the effect of secreting anti-CD73 single-chain antibodies is more significant than that of anti-CD39. This discovery is consistent with the key role of CD73 as a terminal and rate-limiting enzyme in the cascade reaction that catalyzes the production of adenosine. Blocking CD73 can directly prevent AMP from generating adenosine, thereby effectively disrupting the entire pathway without being affected by the activity of upstream CD39. The observed functional enhancement highlights the key limitation of the adenosine-rich TME on NK cell function and verifies that its intervention is an effective strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy.40

Based on this, we integrated the anti-CD73 single-chain antibody into the CAR structure targeting CD33, thereby creating our lead candidate CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells. A key finding is that the introduction of this relatively large gene load did not impair the in vitro proliferation ability or health status of the engineered NK cells. More importantly, we observed that the immunophenotype of these cells shifted towards a more activated state. The downregulation of inhibitory receptors (KIR2DL2/3, NKG2A) and the simultaneous upregulation of key activating receptors (NKP30, NKP44, NKG2D) suggest that the genetic engineering process or autonomous signal transduction may have promoted their preferential differentiation into highly cytotoxic NK cell subpopulations. This favorable phenotypic transformation may contribute to its enhanced target recognition and killing capabilities, providing a mechanistic explanation for its outstanding performance observed both in vitro and in vivo.

The most convincing evidence for our method comes from in vivo studies. CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells demonstrated deep and persistent control over tumor growth, resulting in nearly complete clearance of leukemia cells and significantly prolonged survival. Bifunctional cells are significantly superior to traditional CD33 CAR-NK cells, highlighting the key contribution of CD73 blocking function. Although CD33 CAR-NK cells provided temporary benefits, they failed to achieve long-term remission, revealing the limitations of relying solely on cytotoxic signaling in the immunosuppressive TME. In contrast, CD33-CD73 CAR-NK cells can simultaneously kill tumor cells and break down the surrounding immunosuppressive barrier, thereby generating strong and long-lasting anti-leukemia activity. Our research is in line with the growing consensus that overcoming TME-mediated inhibition is crucial to the success of cellular immunotherapy. Although other studies have explored systemic administration of CD73 inhibitors, our approach ensures the precise release of the blocker at the tumor site by locally delivering antibodies to NK cells, which may enhance efficacy and reduce systemic side effects. This “self-arming” strategy represents a significant advancement compared with traditional CAR design.

Several limitations of our research need to be acknowledged. First, the interaction between immunodeficient NSG mice and the adaptive immune system cannot be evaluated. Therefore, the potential impact of our therapy on triggering a broader T-cell-mediated antitumor response remains to be explored. Research conducted in future humanized mouse models may provide a more comprehensive picture. Second, although we focus on CD73, the TME contains other immunosuppressive mechanisms, which may eventually lead to drug resistance. Combining this therapy with drugs targeting complementary pathways (such as programmed cell death protein-1/programmed death-ligand 1) may be a promising future direction. Although CD33 is shared by normal myeloid progenitor cells, CAR-NK cells exhibit limited in vivo persistence, which reduces sustained myelosuppression and life-threatening profound neutropenia compared with CAR-T therapy. Comprehensive long-term hematopoietic toxicity and safety evaluation will be carried out in our subsequent preclinical studies.

In conclusion, we have successfully developed a bifunctional CAR-NK cell platform, which can effectively address two major obstacles in AML treatment: target antigen recognition and the immunosuppressive TME. Our data provide a strong preclinical basis for the continued development of this collaborative strategy and highlight the potential of engineered multifaceted immune effector cells in the treatment of refractory cancers.
