Antimicrobial activity of Vepris salicifolia leaf essential oil and δ-cadinene: in vitro evaluation and in silico mechanistic insights into δ-cadinene
Authors: Biniam Paulos, Avijit Mazumder, Peter Lindemann, Mariamawit Y Yeshak, Daniel Bisrat, Kaleab Asres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergence of antimicrobial resistance has intensified the search for new bioactive compounds from natural sources. This study investigated the chemical composition and antimicrobial activity of the essential oil (EO) extracted from the leaves of Vepris salicifolia (Engl.) Mziray. In addition, the molecular interactions and drug-like properties of δ-cadinene, the major constituent of the EO, were evaluated.MethodsThe EO was extracted by hydrodistillation and subsequently characterized chemically using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The antibacterial and antifungal activities of the EO and δ-cadinene were assessed using disc diffusion and agar dilution assays, while molecular docking and ADMET analyses were performed to elucidate the potential mechanism of antimicrobial action and evaluate the pharmacokinetic and drug-likeness properties of δ-cadinene.ResultsGC–MS analysis identified 26 compounds, representing 84.3% of the EO. The predominant constituents were δ-cadinene (23.5%), intermedeol (18.3%), germacrene D (10.2%), α-muurolol (9.0%), and germacrene D-4-ol (7.5%). The EO and δ-cadinene effectively inhibited the growth of a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and fungi. Notably, δ-cadinene showed antibacterial activity against S. aureus ML267, with an inhibition zone of 17.8 mm, comparable to that of ciprofloxacin (18.0 mm). Furthermore, the EO exhibited greater antifungal activity against Penicillium notatum, with an inhibition zone of 14.0 mm compared with 11.0 mm for griseofulvin. Docking results showed favorable binding energies for δ-cadinene with the CrtM enzyme of S. aureus (− 7.757 kcal/mol) and the CYP51 enzyme of P. notatum (− 5.756 kcal/mol). ADMET studies further revealed that δ-cadinene has favorable drug-like characteristics, including high intestinal permeability and oral bioavailability, and did not predict any cardiotoxicity.ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate that V. salicifolia leaf EO, particularly δ-cadinene, represents a promising source of antimicrobial agents for future medicinal applications. Nevertheless, the predicted molecular mechanisms of δ-cadinene should be validated through enzyme inhibition assays.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41182-026-01063-w.Keywords: Vepris salicifolia, Essential oil, δ-cadinene, Antimicrobial, Molecular docking, CYP51, CrtM, ADMET

Introduction

Essential oils (EOs) are volatile organic compounds derived from aromatic plants that have been used since time immemorial owing to their health benefits such as anti-inflammatory, anti-infective, relaxation, and antiseptic properties [1, 2]. A growing number of scientific studies prove EOs as highly active lipophilic substances that possess the ability to regulate various biological processes due to their antimicrobial, antioxidant, and immunoregulatory actions [3].

Chemically, EOs are complex mixtures of secondary metabolites, predominantly terpenes, terpenoids, and phenylpropanoids, which plants produce as defense mechanisms against environmental stresses and pathogens [4, 5]. These constituents exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral activities [6, 7]. Their lipophilic nature facilitates penetration into microbial cell membranes, disrupting membrane integrity and interfering with essential biochemical processes, thereby contributing to their antimicrobial efficacy [8–12].

Vepris salicifolia (Engl.) Mziray (syn. Teclea simplicifolia (Engl.) Engl.) (Rutaceae) is an essential oil-bearing medicinal plant widely used in East African traditional medicine for the treatment of respiratory infections, including pneumonia [13]. Despite its ethnomedicinal importance, the chemical composition and biological activities of its essential oil have not yet been investigated. Therefore, the present study aimed to characterize the chemical profile of V. salicifolia leaf essential oil, evaluate its antimicrobial activity, identify its major constituent, and investigate the biological potential of the identified major component through in silico analysis.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chemicals and solvents

The chemicals and solvents used in this study included distilled water, anhydrous sodium sulfate, n-hexane, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ciprofloxacin, griseofulvin, nutrient agar, and Sabouraud dextrose agar. All chemicals and solvents were obtained from CDH Chemicals, India. δ-Cadinene was purchased from Clearsynth Laboratories Limited, Mumbai, India.

Plant material

Fresh leaves of V. salicifolia were collected from Kubayo, Bale, southeastern Ethiopia, in January 2023. The plant was authenticated by Mr. Melaku Wondaferash at the National Herbarium, Addis Ababa University, where a voucher specimen (BP-003) was deposited for future reference. The collected leaves were gently washed with water, air-dried at room temperature, pulverized into a fine powder, and stored at 4 °C until further use.

Test microorganisms

The antibacterial activity was determined against clinically important Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria along with fungi. The Gram-positive group of microorganisms includes Bacillus pumilus, B. subtilis, and Staphylococcus aureus (MDR-1, MDR-2, and ML267). In the case of Gram-negative microorganisms, there were Escherichia coli (3:37C, 872, C600, CD/99/1, HB101, K88, LT37, NCTC 5933, NCTC 7360, and ROW 7/12), Pseudomonas aeruginosa MDR-1, Salmonella enterica TD01, S. typhi Ty2, Shigella boydii, S. dysenteriae 8, S. flexneri, S. sonnei, and Vibrio cholerae. The antifungal activity was assessed against Aspergillus niger, Candida albicans, Penicillium funiculosum, and P. notatum. All bacterial and fungal strains were obtained from the Department of Pharmaceutical Technology, Jadavpur University, India.

Methods

Hydrodistillation

Essential oil was extracted from powdered V. salicifolia leaves by hydrodistillation using a Clevenger-type apparatus, following the method described by Katekar et al. [14]. Briefly, 500 g of the leaf powder was distilled with 2 L of distilled water for 3 h. The collected essential oil was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, transferred to amber glass bottles, and stored at 4 °C until analysis.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis

The composition of the V. salicifolia EO was analyzed by GC–MS using an Agilent 7890A GC system coupled with 5975C VL MSD (Agilent Technologies, Greece). A nonpolar HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm; film thickness 0.25 mm) was used for the identification of the components in the essential oil. The essential oil of V. salicifolia was diluted with n-hexane (1:15), and then 1 μL samples were injected into the GC–MS apparatus with the split injection mode (240 °C). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 0.9 mL/min. The oven temperature was programmed from 60 °C to 240 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min, while the interface temperature was maintained at 280 °C. The electron ionization (EI) source was operated at 70 eV with a mass range of 40–400 amu.

Disc diffusion method

The antimicrobial activities of the V. salicifolia essential oil and δ-cadinene were evaluated using the disc diffusion assay. Test solutions were prepared in 1% DMSO at concentrations ranging from 5 to 800 μg/mL for antibacterial testing and 50 to 1500 μg/mL for antifungal testing. Sterile paper discs impregnated with the test solutions were placed on nutrient agar plates inoculated with bacterial strains or Sabouraud dextrose agar plates inoculated with fungal strains. The bacterial plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, whereas the fungal plates were incubated at room temperature for 72 h. Following incubation, the diameters of the zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters. Ciprofloxacin and griseofulvin were used as the positive controls for the antibacterial and antifungal assays, respectively, while 1% DMSO served as the negative control [15].

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were measured by means of the agar dilution technique. Test samples were dissolved in stock solutions containing 1% DMSO and added to liquid nutrient agar (40–45 °C) or Sabouraud dextrose agar at concentrations of 5–1000 μg/mL for the bactericidal assay and 100–1500 μg/mL for the fungicidal assay. Plates with medium were prepared and left at 4 °C for 24 h to allow an even distribution of the tested compounds. Following drying, cultures of bacteria or fungi grown overnight (105 CFU/mL) were inoculated on solidified medium by the checkerboard method and left to incubate for 24 h at 37 °C (bacteria) or 25 °C (fungi) [16, 17].

Determination of the mode of action

The bactericidal/bacteriostatic and fungicidal/fungistatic activities of the test samples were evaluated by transferring agar plugs from the inhibition zones to fresh, compound-free culture media. Following incubation at 37 °C for 96 h for bacteria and 25 °C for 96 h for fungi, the presence or absence of microbial growth was recorded. The findings were further confirmed by viability assays performed for 24 h using bacterial and fungal cultures exposed to test sample concentrations of 200 μg/mL and 1500 μg/mL, respectively [18, 19].

In silico methods

Homology generationα

Since no experimentally determined three-dimensional (3D) structure of 14α-demthylase (CYP51) from P. notatum was available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), a homology model was generated using the SWISS-MODEL Workspace [20, 21]. The amino acid sequence of the target protein, sterol 14-α-demethylase (CYP51), was retrieved from the UniProt database (accession number A0A162CR19) for P. notatum. Suitable template structures were identified by BLAST and HHblits searches against the SWISS-MODEL Template Library (SMTL) and selected based on sequence identity, coverage, and Global Model Quality Estimation (GMQE) and Qualitative Model Energy Analysis (QMEAN) scores. Homology modeling was performed using the ProMod3 modeling engine implemented in the SWISS-MODEL platform [21, 22].

The quality of the homology model was evaluated using sequence identity, Global Model Quality Estimation (GMQE), Qualitative Model Energy Analysis (QMEAN), and QMEANDisCo scores provided by the SWISS-MODEL Workspace [21]. In addition, stereochemical quality was assessed using a Ramachandran plot to verify that the majority of amino acid residues occupied the favored and allowed regions, indicating a reliable structural model. Only models that met these quality criteria were selected for subsequent molecular docking studies.

The three-dimensional model of CYP51 from P. notatum was downloaded from SWISS-MODEL in PDB format and used for docking studies.

Molecular docking evaluation

The crystal structure of dehydrosqualene synthase (CrtM) from S. aureus (PDB ID: 4F6V, 2.30 Å), complexed with bph-1034, Mg2⁺, and farnesyl monophosphate (FMP), and co-crystallized with 2,4-dioxo-4-{[3-(3-phenoxyphenyl)propyl]amino}butanoic acid (ZYM), was prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard (Schrödinger 2025–2) [23–25]. The protein was prepared with standard precision and default settings. Hydrogen addition and bond order assignment were performed, followed by removal of all but the significant crystallographic waters. Missing side chains were built with Prime [26], and their ionization states were optimized at pH 7.0 ± 2.0 using Epik [27, 28]. Ligands were prepared with LigPrep. A receptor grid for docking was generated around the co-crystallized ligand, focusing on the binding site and including NAD. The Glide SP protocol was validated by redocking the native ligand, achieving an RMSD <2.0 Å [27, 28]. Moreover, binding pockets of the target protein (CYP51) were predicted using the SiteMap module of Schrödinger Suite [23]. The site with the highest SiteScore and Dscore was selected for receptor grid generation and subsequent docking analysis. Test compounds were docked using the same parameters. All parameters were documented for reproducibility. Docking and MM-GBSA scoring results were compared based on Glide scores, binding energies, and key protein–ligand interactions such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts [26]. Docking validation was confirmed by redocking the natural ligand.

To further evaluate the drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic profile of the major compound, its ADMET properties were predicted by QikProp [23]. The ligands were submitted under default to predict key descriptors like octanol/water partition coefficient (QPlogP (o/w), aqueous solubility (QPlogS), cell permeability metrics (QPPCaco2 and QPPMDCK), brain–blood barrier partitioning (QPlogBB), human serum albumin binding (QPlogKhsa), and predicted HERG K+ (QPlogHERG) channel blockage log-IC₅₀. Compounds were screened against standard drug-likeness parameters, such as Lipinski's rule of five, to characterize their drug-like properties.

Statistical analysis

Antimicrobial data were analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 26. The zones of inhibition (ZOI) are expressed as mean ± SD, including the 6 mm disc diameter of the paper disc, based on three independent experiments (n = 3). Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values are expressed as the mean of three replicates. Differences among groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Essential oil composition

Hydrodistillation of the air-dried leaves of V. salicifolia yielded 0.6% (v/w) essential oil. GC–MS analysis identified 26 compounds, representing 84.3% of the total oil composition (Table 1). The essential oil was composed predominantly of sesquiterpenes (40.3%) and sesquiterpenoids (38.2%). The major constituent was identified as δ-cadinene, a bicyclic sesquiterpene, accounting for 23.5% of the total oil. Other major constituents included intermedeol (18.3%), germacrene D (10.2%), α-muurolol (9.0%), germacrene D-4-ol (7.5%), and geijerene (6.5%) (Supplementary Material 1).

In vitro antibacterial activity

The essential oil from V. salicifolia as well as its main constituent δ-cadinene showed a wide spectrum of antibacterial properties against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Table 2). Mostly, δ-cadinene was more effective against bacteria than the crude EO. The highest antibacterial effect was observed against S. aureus ML267, with an inhibition zone of 17.8 mm and MIC value of 50 µg/mL, which was comparable with the results obtained for ciprofloxacin (18.0 mm and 25 µg/mL, respectively). For B. pumilus and B. subtilis, inhibition zones of 17.0 mm and 16.5 mm with MICs of 50–100 µg/mL, respectively, were determined.

In contrast, the crude essential oil exhibited lower antibacterial activity, as evidenced by smaller inhibition zones and higher MIC values. Against Escherichia coli strains, δ-cadinene produced inhibition zones ranging from 12.0 to 14.7 mm, with MIC values of 100–200 μg/mL, whereas ciprofloxacin produced larger inhibition zones and lower MIC values. Both the essential oil and δ-cadinene showed relatively weak activity against multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. aureus and P. aeruginosa; nevertheless, measurable antibacterial activity was still observed.

In vitro antifungal activity

The antifungal activities of the V. salicifolia essential oil and its major constituent, δ-cadinene, were evaluated against selected fungal species and compared with those of the reference antifungal drug griseofulvin (Table 3). δ-Cadinene exhibited strong antifungal activity against Penicillium funiculosum, producing an inhibition zone of 11.17 mm, which was slightly larger than that produced by griseofulvin (11.0 mm). In contrast, the crude essential oil showed greater antifungal activity against both Penicillium notatum and P. funiculosum, producing inhibition zones of 14.0 mm, which exceeded those of both δ-cadinene and griseofulvin.

Mode of actions

δ-Cadinene exhibited bactericidal and fungicidal activities at concentrations of 200 μg/mL and 1500 μg/mL, respectively. However, against Bacillus pumilus and Bacillus subtilis, it exhibited bacteriostatic rather than bactericidal activity.

Homology-based modeled structure and validation

For CYP51, an experimentally determined three-dimensional structure from P. notatum was unavailable; consequently, a homology model was generated using SWISS-MODEL from sterol 14-α-demethylase (CYP51) from P. notatum (UniProt Accession ID: A0A162CR19). The model showed relatively high sequence identity (93.59%), a GMQE of 0.92, a QMEAN score of 0.43, a QMEANDisCo score of 0.81 ± 0.05, and 96.88% of residues in favored regions of the Ramachandran plot (Table 4; Fig. 1). These results support the suitability of the model for exploratory computational analysis, but they do not eliminate uncertainties associated with modeled side-chain conformations, active-site geometry, protein flexibility, and ligand-induced conformational changes.

Molecular docking predication

The molecular docking analysis of dehydrosqualene synthase (CrtM) from S. aureus (PDB ID: 4F6V) showed good binding affinities toward δ-cadinene (G.Score = −7.757) (Table 5). The compound showed binding affinity toward the receptor, mainly due to hydrophobic interactions with amino acids present at the active site, such as PHE22, PHE26, ALA134, VAL137, LEU141, LEU145, LEU160, LEU164, and ILE241, whereas polar interactions took place via amino acid THR142 (Fig. 2). The natural ligand, 2,4-dioxo-4-{[3-(3-phenoxyphenyl)propyl]amino} butanoic acid (ZYM), had an exceptionally high binding score (− 12.265 kcal/mol). This is likely due to several hydrogen bond interactions and π–π stacking interactions with amino acids ASP48, ASN168, ARG265, and Tyr141.

The docking results showed that δ-cadinene had a weaker predicted binding affinity for CrtM (Glide score: −7.757 kcal/mol) than the co-crystallized ligand ZYM (− 12.265 kcal/mol), which formed more extensive hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions. In contrast, δ-cadinene interacted predominantly through hydrophobic contacts, with a polar interaction involving THR142. Thus, δ-cadinene may occupy the CrtM binding pocket, but its weaker docking score suggests potentially weaker binding than ZYM. These findings should be considered a mechanistic hypothesis rather than evidence of potent CrtM inhibition, as docking scores do not directly predict experimental inhibitory potency; experimental CrtM inhibition assays are required for confirmation.

On the other hand, a docking study on sterol 14-α-demethylase (CYP51) revealed that δ-cadinene showed a good binding affinity (G.score = −5.756 kcal/mol) (Table 6). δ-Cadinene showed polar interactions with THR116, THR300, and HIE457 along with hydrophobic interactions with several amino acids, such as TYR112, PHE120, LEU373, and PHE500 (Fig. 3). The sterol 14-α-demethylase (CYP51) enzyme is an important part of ergosterol synthesis pathway in fungi and an established antifungal drug target. Considering the binding affinity and hydrophobic interactions between δ-cadinene and the CYP51 enzyme, it can be suggested that δ-cadinene may inhibit ergosterol biosynthesis by occupying the active pocket of CYP51 enzyme. However, the present study did not perform an independent cross-validation of the CYP51 docking site against experimentally determined structures of CYP51 from other fungal species.

ADMET profile

According to the QikProp prediction, δ-cadinene displays good drug-like properties (Table 7). It met all criteria of Lipinski's rule of five, had a desirable molecular weight (< 500 g/mol) and excellent predicted permeability, as shown in its higher Caco-2 and MDCK permeabilities (> 500 nm/s, 100% oral predicted absorption). Although δ-cadinene lacked good aqueous solubility, it still had desirable lipophilicity for membrane permeability. Also, δ-cadinene exhibited appropriate predicted blood–brain barrier permeability, serum albumin binding, and metabolic profile. More importantly, it had a QPlogHERG value above −5, which is not likely to induce cardiac toxicity.

Discussion

The essential oil of V. salicifolia was characterized by a predominance of sesquiterpenes and sesquiterpenoids, with δ-cadinene (23.5%), intermedeol (18.3%), and germacrene D (10.2%) as the major constituents. The abundance of these bioactive compounds is noteworthy, as sesquiterpene-rich essential oils have been widely reported to exhibit significant antimicrobial activity [29, 30]. In particular, δ-cadinene and germacrene D have been implicated in the antimicrobial activity of essential oils due to their broad-spectrum effects against various microorganisms [31]. However, the 26 compounds identified by GC–MS accounted for only 84.3% of the total oil, leaving 15.7% unidentified, whose contribution to the observed activity cannot be excluded. The greater antifungal activity of the crude EO compared with δ-cadinene alone suggests that interactions among multiple constituents may contribute to the activity; however, whether these interactions are synergistic or additive cannot be determined from the present data. Definitive evidence of synergism would require systematic combination studies, such as checkerboard assays and synergy-index determination.

The in vitro antibacterial results indicated that both the EO and δ-cadinene had a broad spectrum of activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Nevertheless, δ-cadinene was found to have more antibacterial activity than the crude EO, thus implying that δ-cadinene is one of the main components responsible for the antimicrobial activity of V. salicifolia. δ-Cadinene produced an inhibition zone of 17.8 mm against S. aureus ML267, which was comparable to that of ciprofloxacin (18.0 mm). At the same time, it produced inhibition zones of 17.0 mm and 16.5 mm for Bacillus pumilus and B. subtilis, respectively, compared to ciprofloxacin at 19.0 mm and 18.0 mm. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that sesquiterpenes exert antibacterial effects primarily through disruption of the bacterial cell membrane, leading to increased membrane permeability, leakage of intracellular constituents, and interference with cellular homeostasis [31–33]. The activity of δ-cadinene against the various E. coli isolates is particularly noteworthy because Gram-negative bacteria possess an outer membrane that restricts the penetration of many antimicrobial agents. Despite this intrinsic barrier, δ-cadinene produced inhibition zones ranging from 12.0 to 14.7 mm, comparable to those of ciprofloxacin (13.5–17.1 mm). These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that the lipophilic nature of sesquiterpenes enables them to interact with membrane phospholipids, thereby increasing membrane permeability, disrupting membrane integrity, and ultimately causing leakage of intracellular constituents [30, 32].

However, both the EO and δ-cadinene exhibited reduced antimicrobial activity against drug-resistant strains of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa compared with susceptible strains, although measurable inhibitory effects were retained. In particular, δ-cadinene showed relatively weak activity against MDR S. aureus (ZOI: 11.0–11.5 mm; MIC: 50 μg/mL) and P. aeruginosa. These findings do not support their use as stand-alone treatments for resistant infections but indicate potential for further investigation as antimicrobial leads or adjuvants in combination with conventional antibiotics. Future studies should therefore evaluate concentration-dependent effects, cytotoxicity, antibiotic combinations, and potential synergistic activity against MDR pathogens. Nevertheless, the observed antibacterial activity is noteworthy in the context of the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance. Essential oils may be particularly valuable against multidrug-resistant microorganisms because their complex mixtures of bioactive constituents can act on multiple cellular targets, including cell membranes, cell walls, energy metabolism, and quorum-sensing pathways, potentially reducing the likelihood of resistance development compared with conventional antibiotics that typically target specific molecular pathways [34–36].

In contrast, the antifungal activity of the essential oil showed a distinct pattern. Although δ-cadinene exhibited stronger activity against A. niger and C. albicans, the crude EO was more active against P. notatum and P. funiculosum, producing inhibition zones of 14.0 mm compared with 11.0 and 11.17 mm for δ-cadinene, respectively. This indicates that the antifungal activity of the EO cannot be attributed solely to δ-cadinene and may instead reflect the combined effects of multiple constituents, including intermedeol (18.3%), δ-cadinene (23.5%), germacrene D, α-muurolol, germacrene D-4-ol, and unidentified components [29, 30]. However, because the individual contributions of these constituents were not experimentally determined, the observed enhancement cannot be conclusively attributed to synergistic interactions; additive or antagonistic effects cannot be excluded without systematic combination studies.

The observation that δ-cadinene exhibits bactericidal and fungicidal, rather than merely bacteriostatic and fungistatic, activity against most tested microorganisms is pharmacologically significant. Unlike growth-inhibitory agents, bactericidal and fungicidal compounds directly eliminate microbial cells, making them particularly valuable for managing severe recurrent infections in immunocompromised patients [37, 38]. The microbicidal activity of δ-cadinene is likely associated with its highly lipophilic sesquiterpene structure, which facilitates interaction with microbial membranes, leading to membrane disruption, increased permeability, loss of membrane potential, leakage of intracellular components, and ultimately irreversible cell death [4, 30]. In fungi, this mechanism may additionally involve disruption of ergosterol-containing membranes, contributing to its fungicidal effect [4, 39]. Since membrane-targeting mechanisms involve multiple cellular processes rather than a single molecular pathway, δ-cadinene may have a reduced potential for resistance development compared with conventional antimicrobials that act on specific targets [4, 11]. Therefore, the bactericidal and fungicidal properties of δ-cadinene, combined with its favorable predicted ADMET profile, support its potential as a lead molecule for the development of broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents against both susceptible and resistant pathogens.

The molecular docking results provided further support for the experimental findings. δ-Cadinene exhibited favorable binding affinities toward dehydrosqualene synthase (CrtM) of S. aureus and sterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51) of P. notatum. CrtM plays a key role in the biosynthesis of staphyloxanthin, a virulence-associated carotenoid that protects S. aureus against oxidative stress and enhances bacterial survival. Likewise, CYP51 catalyzes a critical step in ergosterol biosynthesis and is the primary molecular target of azole antifungal agents. Therefore, inhibition of these enzymes may contribute, at least in part, to the antibacterial and antifungal activities observed for δ-cadinene [25, 40]. However, the molecular docking findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating evidence rather than definitive proof of enzyme inhibition. Although the predicted interactions of δ-cadinene with CrtM and CYP51 suggest these proteins as potential molecular targets, direct target engagement and inhibitory activity must be confirmed experimentally using purified enzymes and appropriate positive controls. Such enzyme inhibition assays would provide essential validation of the proposed mechanisms of action.

In addition, the favorable predicted ADMET profile of δ-cadinene supports its consideration as a potential lead for antimicrobial drug development. The compound complied with Lipinski’s rule of five, showed high predicted intestinal absorption and permeability, and exhibited a QPlogHERG value within the recommended range, indicating favorable drug-like and pharmacokinetic properties [41–43]. However, these in silico predictions provide only preliminary evidence and do not establish the safety or therapeutic suitability of δ-cadinene. Therefore, it should be regarded as a potential antimicrobial lead rather than a confirmed therapeutic agent, pending experimental evaluation of cytotoxicity, selectivity, pharmacokinetics, and in vivo toxicity.

Conclusion

The present study provides the first comprehensive characterization of V. salicifolia leaf essential oil, in which δ-cadinene was identified as the major constituent, and demonstrates broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal activities of both the essential oil and δ-cadinene. δ-Cadinene showed strong antibacterial activity against several bacterial strains, including activity comparable to ciprofloxacin against S. aureus ML267, whereas the crude essential oil exhibited greater antifungal activity against Penicillium species than δ-cadinene alone, suggesting that interactions among multiple constituents may contribute to the antifungal effect. However, this possible synergistic or additive contribution remains to be experimentally established. Molecular docking predicted favorable interactions of δ-cadinene with CrtM of S. aureus and CYP51 of P. notatum, identifying these proteins as potential antimicrobial targets. However, these docking results are hypothesis generating and do not constitute evidence of direct enzyme inhibition or definitive mechanisms of action. Experimental enzyme inhibition and complementary mechanistic studies are therefore essential to validate these proposed targets and mechanisms. Likewise, the predicted ADMET profile requires experimental confirmation through cytotoxicity, pharmacokinetic, and in vivo studies before therapeutic potential can be established.
