ABSTRACT
Case SummaryWe describe the case of a 45-year-old man with progressive external ophthalmoplegia and pigmentary retinopathy who was referred for evaluation of a bradyarrhythmia due to exertional dyspnea and fatigue. A baseline electrocardiogram and ambulatory monitoring revealed clinically significant conduction abnormalities.Take-Home MessagesIn Kearns-Sayre syndrome, conduction abnormalities may progress unpredictably; early permanent pacing should be strongly considered. Consider implantable cardioverter-defibrillator rather than pacemaker alone when additional high-risk features are present.

Case Presentation

A 45-year-old man developed diplopia, strabismus, ptosis, and progressive external ophthalmoplegia (Figure 1C). Ophthalmologic evaluation demonstrated pigmentary retinopathy, raising suspicion for Kearns-Sayre syndrome (KSS). He was referred for cardiac evaluation because of exertional dyspnea and fatigue in the setting of suspected conduction system involvement. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (62%) without structural abnormalities. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed normal biventricular function without late gadolinium enhancement or evidence of myocardial fibrosis. ECG Findings

The ECG showed sinus rhythm with marked bradycardia and intermittent ventricular and junctional rhythm (40 beats/min) as substitute or escape rhythm, first-degree AV block (PR: 200 ms), and QRS widening (130 ms), with left intraventricular conduction delay and left anterior fascicular block, plus voltage criteria for left ventricular hypertrophy and diffuse ST-T abnormalities (Figure 1A). Holter monitoring demonstrated sinus rhythm alternating with junctional bradycardia (Figure 1B), 110 pauses (longest: 3.6 seconds) secondary to progressive AV conduction disease, intermittent second-degree AV block (Mobitz I and Mobitz II) (Figure 2B), and 2:1 AV block (Figure 3B).

Explanation

In suspected KSS, multilevel conduction disease confers a high risk. KSS is a mitochondrial cytopathy characterized by progressive external ophthalmoplegia and pigmentary retinopathy. Cardiac involvement commonly manifests as progressive conduction system disease that may deteriorate unpredictably to advanced AV block, asystole, and sudden death.1,2

ECG plays a fundamental role in risk stratification, as a progressive conduction system involvement often precedes complete AV block and sudden cardiac death.1

In a prospective pediatric KSS cohort, intraventricular conduction defects were universal and frequently progressed to advanced AV block; pacing was proposed from the first manifestation of bifascicular block.3

In this case, suspected KSS combined with marked bradycardia and intermittent higher-grade AV block supported implantation of a dual-chamber pacemaker. Follow-up showed further conduction progression. Muscle biopsy confirmed mitochondrial myopathy; genetic testing was not performed.

Guidelines support a low threshold: The 2021 European Society of Cardiology pacing guideline states that permanent pacing should be considered (Class IIa, Level of Evidence: C) in KSS with PR prolongation, any degree of AV block, or fascicular/bundle branch block, and that prophylactic pacing may be considered (Class IIb, Level of Evidence: C) even without documented conduction disease.1 ICD therapy should be considered in selected patients with additional high-risk features such as ventricular dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, or ventricular arrhythmias.3
