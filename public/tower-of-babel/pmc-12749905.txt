Alternating current pre-treatment enhances multi-enzymatic activity of high-entropy alloy nanozymes that regulate metabolism for effective tumor immunotherapy
Authors: Danyang Li, Enna Ha, Yaoyao Zhu, Zhenli Zhou, Li Yan, Shuqing He, Shaolong Kuang, Lihai Zhang, Junqing Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nanozymes capable of inducing metabolic reprogramming and activating the immune response without external stimuli in vivo are highly pursued for malignant tumor therapy. In this paper, a PtIrFeMoZn high-entropy alloy (HEA) nanozyme is designed and synthesized via a simple one-step hydrothermal method. The HEA nanozymes not only trigger apoptosis and ferroptosis via cascade biocatalysis, thereby enhancing immunogenicity, but also enhance the immune effect by targeting the glycolytic pathway. It is worth mentioning that a simple pre-treatment of nanozymes by alternating current (AC) yielded much better therapeutic and immuno-effect. The ‘AC-treated’ nanozymes exhibit an excellent synergy of peroxidase-like (POD-like), myeloperoxidase-like (MPO-like), and glutathione peroxidase-like (GPx-like) activities, generating a sufficient reactive oxygen species (ROS) storm. Additionally, two immune pathways (ICD and the cGAS-STING) are activated simultaneously. Furthermore, the production of HClO and the depletion of NADH can regulate metabolism, further disrupting the equilibrium of the glycolysis process. This not only increases the cell death but also enhances the immune response in female tumor-bearing mice. This study proposes a multi-pronged therapeutic strategy that can significantly activate anti-tumor immunotherapeutic effects through ROS storm, GSH/NADH oxidation, and lactate/ATP depletion, triggering apoptosis/ferroptosis/immunotherapy. These findings hold significant promise for inspiring the development of HEA nanozymes for tumor immunotherapy.

Introduction

Malignant tumors are one of the major threats to human health, with more than 19 million cases reported worldwide annually, making the development of new therapeutic methods urgent1. As a new type of cancer treatment, nanozyme-mediated dynamic therapy has shown great potential in cancer treatment because of its capacity to regulate intracellular levels of oxidative stress2. Meanwhile, compared with natural counterparts, nanozymes exhibit advantages such as high stability, low cost, controllable catalytic activity, and less strict storage conditions3. However, it is always compulsory to combine these nanozymes with external stimuli or chemical drugs to achieve an efficacious therapeutic effect in vivo4. In practice, these exogenous stimuli still face a number of shortcomings in vivo, such as the limited penetration depth of ultrasound, the fixed irradiation area of near-infrared lasers, or the need for invasive subcutaneous electrodes5–7. Similarly, the phenomenon of cellular multidrug resistance and drug leakage represents a further limitation to the therapeutic paradigm of synergy with chemical drugs8. Therefore, it is urgent to develop single nanozymes with multiple enzymatic activities to achieve strong therapeutic effects without exogenous assistance in vivo.

Another scientific challenge in malignant tumor treatment is the high chance of metastasis, where immunotherapy provides an effective way of suppressing the progress. The activation of innate immunity (e.g., Toll-like receptors (TLRs) signaling and STING pathway) and adaptive immunity (e.g., the immunogenic cell death (ICD) pathway) has been widely investigated worldwide9. However, the tumor’s distinctive metabolic pattern, characterized by increased glycolysis, shows potential immunosuppressive effects10. As an important metabolite of the Warburg effect, lactic acid (LA) not only serves as an energy source for malignant tumor11, but also diminishes the cytotoxic activity of T and NK cells through its acidification effect or downstream signaling modulation12. Thus, interfering with lactate metabolism exhibits a promising approach for immune enhancement. It is well established that glucose is bio-catalyzed by a series of enzymes to produce pyruvate (e.g., hexokinase (HK) and fructose 6-phosphate kinase (G6PDH)), which subsequently produces LA in the presence of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)13. Consequently, the objective of combining activation of immune pathways with targeted metabolic reprogramming would enhance the immune response and eradicate tumors. Currently, activating immune pathways and interfering with tumor metabolic pathways simultaneously through single nanodrug has rarely been reported.

Metal alloy nanomaterials bring new horizons to the design and preparation of multifunctional nanozymes4. It is well known that various metal ions demonstrate potent biological effects, including those with the Fenton effect (Fe2+/3+, Mo4+/5+/6+ or Cu+/2+), that capable of inducing ferroptosis (Fe2+), those with immunological functions (Mn2+, Zn2+ or Mg2+) or high biocompatibility (Pt or Ir)9,14–18. Moreover, recent reports have indicated that certain alloy nanozymes are capable of producing HClO in vivo19, a substance with the potential to regulate tumor metabolism20. Specifically, high-entropy alloy (HEA) materials are capable of not only combining the advantages of individual elements, but may also burst with fascinating biological properties through the cocktail effect21. Unlike conventional alloys, HEAs have well-defined content boundaries (5-35%) for the major elements (≥ 5)22. In the alloy lattice, the internally paired d-electrons tend to be redistributed into empty or half-filled vacant d-orbitals of the other metals, resulting in strong d-orbital coupling23. The self-complementary interactions between d-electrons and the d-band center shift among constituent metals can significantly enhance the interfacial electron transfer efficiency and boost the catalytic activity. In particular, the rich variety of atomic sites in HEAs can optimize the geometrical and electronic configurations of the reaction interface, which in turn affects the adsorption energy of substrates or intermediates, modulating the catalytic activity ultimately24. Therefore, it is of great importance to rationally design HEA nanozymes for interfering with tumor cell propagation and invasion.

In this work, we designed and synthesized PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes through a simple one-pot hydrothermal method. HEA nanozymes are capable of generating a reactive oxygen species (ROS) storm within the cell via the excellent peroxidase-like (POD-like) and myeloperoxidase-like (MPO-like) activities, and attenuating the antioxidant by glutathione peroxidase-like (GPx-like) activity. Concurrently, the generation of HClO and the depletion of NADH interfere with the tumor glycolysis process, further reducing the content of lactate (LA) and ATP and ultimately boosting the apoptosis-ferroptosis synergistic tumor therapy. Additionally, the HEA nanozymes can also activate two immune pathways (ICD and the cGAS-STING) simultaneously for halting the distal metastasis of tumor cells. The reduction in LA levels resulting from the imbalance in glycolysis exerts additional immunosuppressive reversal effects and enhances the therapeutic efficacy. Notably, pre-treatment with simple alternating current (AC) yielded a more powerful nanozyme (AC-HEA), which exhibited a significant enhancement of all the enzymatic activities, achieving the best tumor killing and metastasis inhibition effect both in vitro and in vivo. This work not only developed a simple pre-treatment method (AC) capable of remarkably increasing the enzymatic activities of nanozymes, but also a versatile nanodrug to boost apoptosis/ferroptosis/immunotherapy by triggering ROS storm and interfering with the glycolytic process without any exogenous stimuli in vivo (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the antitumor mechanism of PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes.PtlrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes were synthesized via a one-step hydrothermal method, and the AC-HEA nanozymes were prepared under AC stimulation pre-treatment. Results and Discussion

Synthesis and characterization of PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes

PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanoparticles were prepared by a simple one-step solvothermal method. As illustrated in the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, PtIrFeMoZn nanozymes were successfully prepared with a uniform size and excellent water dispersibility (Fig. 2a, b). The average particle size is calculated to be 119 ± 9 nm through statistical analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). As shown in energy dispersive X-Ray spectroscopy (EDX) elemental mapping analysis (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2), Pt, Ir, Fe, Mo, and Zn within the nanoparticles were homogeneously distributed. The chemical compositions of PtIrFeMoZn nanozymes were determined to be 33 (Pt): 18 (Ir): 21 (Fe):18 (Mo): 10 (Zn) by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), respectively. The configurational entropy of PtIrFeMoZn nanoparticles was calculated as 1.54R, surpassing the boundary of 1.5R separating high-entropy and medium-entropy alloys25. The crystal structure of PtIrFeMoZn nanoparticles was characterized by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), where the typical diffraction peaks indicate the formation of intermetallic phase with no impurity peaks observed (Fig. 2d). The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) image further proved that the interplanar spacings are in consistent with those of (111), (200), and (220) planes of the cubic structure (Fig. 2e). The lattice spacings of 0.22 and 0.19 nm correspond well to the (111) and (200) facet of HEA NPs, illustrating a single FCC phase in agreement with the XRD results (Fig. 2f). In addition, the valence states of PtIrFeMoZn HEA NPs were characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 2g). The peaks located at 71.1 and 74.5 eV demonstrated metallic Pt0, and other peaks at 72.8 eV of 4f7/2 and 76.2 eV of 4f5/2 belonged to surface Pt2+ (Fig. 2h). The peaks located at 60.2 and 63.5 eV demonstrated metallic Ir0, and other peaks at 61.0 eV of 4f7/2 and 64.3 eV of 4f5/2 belonged to surface Ir3+ (Fig. 1i). Fe2+ is confirmed by the peaks at 711.6 and 724.7 eV, with a small portion of peaks at 718.3 and 731.4 eV which belong to Fe3+ (Fig. 2j). From XPS spectra of Mo 3 d, the peaks at 231.6 and 234.7 eV were assigned to Mo5+, and other peaks at 232.6 and 235.6 eV can be indexed to Mo6+ (Fig. 2k). For Zn element, the peaks located at 1021.0 and 1044.0 eV demonstrated metallic Zn0, and other peaks at 1022.1 and 1045.1 eV belonged to surface Zn2+ (Fig. 2l). The binding energy of Pt 4 f, Ir 4 f, Fe 2p, Mo 3 d and Zn 2p demonstrated that the Pt, Ir, Fe, Mo, Zn existed as alloys in the HEA NPs. These multi-valence metallic ions show great potential as the active sites for the redox reactions26. In vitro evaluation of POD-, NOX-, MPO- and GPx-like activities

Due to the overexpressed H₂O₂ in TME, massive hydroxyl radicals (•OH) can be generated through POD-like activity for tumor catalytic therapy. 3, 3, 5, 5- tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) was used to test POD-like activity of PtIrFeMoZn HEA (Supplementary Fig. 3). As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 4a, b, the optical absorbance showed a significant increase with time, and the color of the corresponding solution changed accordingly, indicating POD-like activity of HEA nanozymes. The kinetic behavior of HEA nanozymes matches well with the Michaelis–Menten saturation curve (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 5). Km and Vmax were solved to be 68.9 μM and 66.6 nM/s. Notably, the key parameters Km is not only much lower than that of other metal-based nanozymes, but also only about 1.8% of that of natural horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Km: 3700 μM for HRP), suggesting a strong affinity for H2O2 (Table S1). In addition, as shown in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 6, HEA nanozymes exhibit pH-dependent POD-like activity, thus avoiding toxic effects on normal tissues. To explore the mechanism underlying the high POD-like activity of HEA nanozymes, first-principle calculations were conducted via density functional theory (DFT) using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP). According to the calculated molecular electrostatic potential (ESP), projected density of state (PDOSs) and Gibbs free energy change as shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, the self-complementary interactions between d-electrons of constituent metals in HEA optimize the band position and enrich electron density near the Fermi level (EF). Similar to •OH, HClO is also a type of ROS that not only has a direct killing effect on tumor cells27, but also shows immunosuppression reversion effect by interfering with tumor metabolism28. It was found that HClO can be produced by myeloperoxidase (MPO) using Cl- and H2O2 as substrates. However, there is few inorganic nanozymes reported with similar activity. The MPO-like activities of HEA nanozymes were detected by monitoring the conversion of monochlorodimedon (MCD) to dichlorodimedon (DCD) (Supplementary Fig. 8)29. As shown in Fig. 3d, the characteristic absorption peak belonging to MCD at 290 nm decreases with increasing time, implying the MPO-like activity of HEA nanozymes.

The distinctive metabolic patterns and associated products of the tumor cells endow them with a considerable degree of resistance to ROS, which impedes therapeutic efficacy. A complementary strategy to disturb the tumor defense system is highly demanded. NADH and NAD+ are essential indispensable redox cofactors in metabolism, demonstrating great significance in the electron transport chain (ETC) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production30. The equilibrium states of NADH/NAD+ is therefore examined (Supplementary Fig. 9). As shown in Fig. 3e, with the extension of the reaction time between nanozymes and NADH, an obvious absorbance decrease of NADH at 340 nm was accompanied by the increase of NAD+ at 260 nm, this dramatic change was closely related to the conversion of NADH to NAD+, demonstrating NOX-like activity of HEA nanozymes. Glutathione (GSH), an antioxidant overexpressed in TME, can scavenge •OH and increase the resistance of cancer cells to oxidative stress31. Thus, depletion of GSH is an effective strategy to improve the therapeutic effect of ROS. As shown in Fig. 3f and Supplementary Figs. 10, 11, the absorbance of GSH decreased promptly with time in the presence of HEA nanozymes, demonstrating its GPx-like activity. In summary, the prepared HEA nanozymes have a variety of enzymatic activities, including POD-, NOX-, MPO-, and GPx-like enzymatic activities. These excellent enzymatic activities can originate from the asymmetric charge distribution and interaction among different metals32.

The electric field is considered to be one of the advanced means to further improve catalytic activity, widely applied in the field of chemical engineering and energy conversion33,34. In our recent work, alternating current (AC) has also been found to change the surface valence states of PtMnIr nanozymes, showing great potential for biomedical application35. Consequently, we conducted further research into this process. The AC-treated HEA nanozymes (AC-HEA) were obtained under the stimulation of square wave AC in PBS dispersion. The POD-like activity was first investigated with AC-HEA by varying either the reaction time or the concentration of the H2O2 substrate (Supplementary Fig. 12, 13). The Km and Vmax were calculated to be 65.3 μM and 117.9 nM/s for AC-HEA nanozymes (Supplementary Fig. 14), showing a much better POD-like activity than HEA nanozymes and other POD-like nanomaterials (Table S1). Similarly, MPO-, NOX-, and GPX-like activities of AC-HEA nanozymes were equally examined (Supplementary Fig. 15–17). It is encouraging to note that all four enzyme activities (POD-, MPO-, NOX-, and GPx-like) of AC-HEA nanozymes are preferable to those of HEA nanozymes by analytical comparisons (Fig. 3g–j). This evidence supports that AC pre-treatment is an effective method for enhancing enzyme activities.

Then, the zeta potentials of PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes and AC-HEA nanozymes were measured. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 18. The surface potential of the HEA nanozymes was −5.2 ± 0.5 mV, while that of the AC-HEA nanozymes was −11.8 ± 0.6 mV, indicating that the as-prepared HEA or AC-HEA nanozymes in aqueous suspensions were stable. For disclosing the catalytic enhancement mechanism, XPS was used to analyze the surface state change. The results demonstrated that the ionic valence states of the five elements on the surface of the AC-HEA nanozymes had significant variations (Supplementary Fig. 19). Compared to the HEA nanozymes, there is an increased proportion of highly reactive ions. In previous studies, the higher valence states of metal (e.g., Pt) exhibit enhanced binding efficiency to O-bearing NADH substrates and NAD* intermediates, thereby facilitating NOX-like activity36. Similarly, the high valence ions (e.g., Fe3+) impart a shallow potential energy well to the entire particle, thereby conferring considerable POD-like activity37. GPx-like activity was also likewise observed to be more potent in higher valence states38. These excellent enzyme activities (POD-, MPO-, NOX- and GPx-like) act synergistically to change the level of oxidative stress in tumor cells, which generate a ROS “storm” to kill tumor cells (Fig. 3k). In particular, we developed an innovative way that remarkably augmented the enzymatic activity through simple AC pre-treatment, which will be applicable widely in biomedical field.

Cellular uptake and biomarker probing

FITC-labeled HEA nanozymes were used to explore the cellular uptake performance by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) and as shown in Supplementary Fig. 20. The significant green fluorescence belongs to FITC-HEA nanozymes, indicating that the nanozymes were able to be uptaken efficiently. Further, the biotoxicity of HEA and AC-HEA was demonstrated by the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay for B16-F10 cells and normal Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs). As shown in Fig. 4a, HUVEC survival rate remained above 95% after incubation for 24 h at the highest concentration of 150 ppm, indicating HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes are nontoxic to normal cells. In comparison, both HEA and AC-HEA were effective in killing melanoma B16-F10 cells, and the killing effect was enhanced with increasing concentrations of nanozymes. Meanwhile, AC-HEA showed a stronger therapeutic effect at the same particle concentration. This suggests that both HEA and AC-HEA possess potential as a chemotherapeutic agent. To understand the mechanism of nanozyme-induced cell death, various fluorescent probes and kits were used to study internal physiological changes. 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was selected as a probe for ROS detection. As shown in Fig. 3b, HEA and AC-HEA groups exhibited higher green fluorescence compared with control groups, indicating the massive generation of intracellular ROS. The O76 probe was also used for HClO detection. With the addition of HEA or AC-HEA, significant intracellular HClO was produced, while the AC-HEA group exhibited a stronger HClO signal. We further monitored the changes in mitochondria and found them fragmented with a punctiform morphology when treated with HEA or AC-HEA nanozymes (Fig. 4b). The mitochondrial membrane potential was also measured using fluorescent probe JC-1 (Supplementary Fig. 21). In the HEA and AC-HEA groups, mitochondrial membrane potential transformed from J-aggregates (red fluorescence) to monomers (green fluorescence). Then, MFI was conducted to analyze the ratio of J-aggregates and monomers, where the proportion of monomer increased to ~57.0% with HEA, and boosted to ~86.2% for AC-HEA. Such significant damage to the mitochondria is a symbol of early apoptosis (Fig. 4c). HClO is not only capable of acting as a highly cytotoxic ROS, but also functions as an endogenous substance that regulates the metabolism of tumor cells. Previous research has indicated that HClO can cause disturbance of various plasma membrane functions, which in turn affects glucose uptake39. Therefore, we examined the key enzymes in the glycolytic pathway and showed that both HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes were able to inhibit the activity of HK, G6PDH, and LDH (Fig. 4d), ultimately affecting mitochondrial function. Additionally, a metabolic shift in B16-F10 cells was performed to evaluate the profiles of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and glycolysis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22, the control group exhibited typical curves of oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), whereas HEA nanozymes and AC-HEA nanozymes showed significant inhibition of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and downregulation of cellular glycolytic function. The metabolic dysfunction of cancer cells induced by HEA nanozymes and AC-HEA nanozymes can promote tumor cell death. Based on these findings, it is reasonable to infer that mitochondrial function is significantly inhibited by HEA or AC-HEA nanozymes. Therefore, LA content and ATP production were further examined. The results demonstrated that LA was significantly inhibited after treatment with HEA or AC-HEA (Fig. 4e). The reduction in LA content in tumor cells not only diminishes the energy source required for tumor survival but also alleviates the tumor from immunosuppression12,40. In addition, the ATP content of cells treated with HEA or AC-HEA nanozymes produced a significant decrease of 34% and 63%, respectively (Fig. 4e). In summary, the prepared HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes were not only able to trigger cell death by generating ROS storm, but also further enhancing the therapeutic effect through the metabolism regulation caused by the generated HClO and glycolysis interference.

Ferroptosis, an emerging type of nonapoptotic cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides, usually arises from the accumulation of ROS and the failure of GSH antioxidant defenses41. Thiol-tracker violet was used to detect the depletion process of GSH in vitro. As shown in Fig. 3f, green fluorescence of the experimental groups (HEA and AC-HEA) was much weaker compared with the control group, indicating GSH is depleted. MFI also shows the significant differences of GSH content between HEA and AC-HEA groups (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Next, we monitor lipid peroxidation (LPO), a typical symbol for ferroptosis, using the Liperfluo staining method (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 23b. Significant green fluorescence was found in the HEA and AC-HEA groups, indicating that LPO occurred and was much higher in the AC-HEA group. In addition, the bio-TEM images of HEA or AC-HEA nanozyme-treated B16-F10 cells showed mitochondrial fragmentation and cristae alterations, which are the characteristic features of ferroptosis (Fig. 4g).

Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a signature protein in the ferroptosis process, is capable of preventing the reduction of intratumorally lipid peroxide (LOOH) to hypotoxic hydroxyl fatty acids (LOH)42. The down-regulation of GPX4 levels in HEA and AC-HEA groups leads to the accumulation of intracellular peroxides, thus promoting ferroptosis (Fig. 4h, i). These evidences suggest that HEA or AC-HEA nanozymes are able to induce ferroptosis, synergizing with apoptotic pathways to exert better therapeutic efficacy.

In TME, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) can degrade various proteins such as collagen and elastin in the extracellular matrix (ECM), which in turn promotes tumor cell infiltration and metastasis43. Therefore, activating the body’s immune response while decreasing the expression of MMP proteins in tumor cells is a potential cooperative strategy. A recent study shows that the expression level of MMP proteins, especially MMP2 and MMP9 proteins, can be affected by endogenous HClO and Zn2+ 44,45. Therefore, the relative protein was analyzed by WB experiments as shown in Fig. 4j, k. The results showed that tumor cells stimulated with HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes had reduced levels of MMP2 and MMP9 expression, demonstrating the potential of tumor invasion inhibition. Furthermore, wound healing experiments and invasion assay were conducted to investigate the effect of nanozymes on cell migration and invasion (Supplementary Fig. 24, 25), which demonstrated that the motility and invasion of tumor cells were severely impaired by HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes. In detail, the mobility results obtained by mathematical analysis demonstrated a high mobility of 95.2% in the control group at 48 h, while this value was reduced to 54.8% and 33.7% in the HEA and AC-HEA groups, respectively (Fig. 4l). In the cell invasion assay, the relative invasion rates of the HEA group and the AC-HEA group were 63.2% and 36.5% respectively (Fig. 4m). These results demonstrate that HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes show great potential to inhibit tumor metastasis.

In vitro and in vivo ICD effect, immune cell infiltration, and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion

It has been reported that Zn2+ can promote DNA-induced phase separation and activation of cGAS, further enhancing STING activity and inducing the STING-dependent interferon responses46. The ROS cascade amplification in tumor cells could increase DNA damage, which is able to activate the cGAS-STING pathway47–49. Then, the related proteins were analyzed by western blot assay (Fig. 5a, 5b). The expression level of STING and IFN-β in HEA and AC-HEA groups was up-regulated, confirming the strong activation of STING. Although it appeared to be little different in the expression content of IRF3 protein, its phosphorylation state (pIRF3) was significantly higher in the HEA and AC-HEA groups. Then, a nuclear translocation assay kit was employed to monitor the translocation of p65 (the main subunit of NF-κB) into the nucleus. The results showed that the p65 factor in B16-F10 cells treated with HEA or AC-HEA nanozymes was more likely to translocate into the nucleus, indicating the enhanced NF-κB pathway (Fig. 5c). These data indicated that HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes can enhance the cGAS-STING pathway. The apoptotic cancer cells caused by HEA or AC-HEA nanozymes also elicited the immune response by the generation of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and calreticulin (CRT)50. As shown in Fig. 5d, CRT expression on the surface of the B16-F10 cell membrane was observed by CLSM after being incubated with different groups. No obvious CRT expression was observed in the control group, and the tumor cells showed positive expression of CRT in the HEA or AC-HEA group (Fig. 5e). We also excised the tumors from the mice at the end of the treatment cycle and performed immunofluorescence analysis. The results showed a similar trend to the in vitro experiments (Fig. 5f). The HMGB1 release was also greatly enhanced by HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes in a similar trend (Fig. 5g, h). Similarly, the immunofluorescence intensity of HMGB1 in the control group was the highest in the excised tumor, while that in the HEA or the AC-HEA group decreased significantly (Fig. 5i). In summary, multifunctional PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes were prepared by a simple one-step hydrothermal method. It is exciting to obtain a more powerful AC-HEA nanozyme platform with superior catalytic activity by simple AC pre-treatment. In TME, AC-HEA nanozymes work in concert to generate substantial quantities of ROS through POD-, MPO- and GPx-like activities, which result in apoptosis and ferroptosis. Additionally, these nanozymes are capable of inhibiting the glycolytic pathway of tumor cells through NOX-like activity in conjunction with the produced HClO, thereby reducing LA production. The reduction of LA not only cuts off the energy source of tumor cell metabolism, but also hinders the immunosuppression of tumor cells, greatly enhancing the therapeutic effect. Moreover, AC-HEA nanozymes can also inhibit the expression of MMP protein in tumor cells and synergize with two immune pathways (ICD and cGAS-STING) to jointly induce the immune response and inhibit the distant metastasis of tumors. This work provides a highly attractive therapeutic platform with cascade enzymatic/immunotherapy in just one material without any exogenous assistance in vivo. We believe that this work will shed more light on the preparation of highly active alloy nanozymes and the related clinical translation.

Materials

All the chemicals were used as purchased without further purification, including potassium tetrachloroplatinate (K2PtCl4, 98%), iridium chloride (IrCl3, Ir≥62.0%), zinc nitrate hexahydrate (ZnNO3·6H2O, 99%), sodium molybdate (Na2MoO4, 98%), ferric chloride (FeCl3, 98%), glutathione (C10H17N3O6S, GSH, 98%), 5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (C14H8N2O8S2, DTNB, 98%), ethylene glycol (EG, 98%), sodium iodide (NaI, 99.5%), L-proline (C5H9NO2, 99%) hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), was purchased from Aladdin Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China), and 3,3’,5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine (C16H20N2, TMB, 98%), monochlorodimedon (MCD, 98%) was purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China), and SH-PEG-FITC (MW = 1000) was purchased from Ruixi Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Xi’an, China), and Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, MW = 360k) was purchased from Yuan Ye Biology (Shanghai, China). Liperfluo was purchased from Dojindo China Co., Ltd. Seahorse Glycolysis Stress Test Assay Kit and Seahorse Cell Mito Stress Test Assay Kit were purchased from Agilent Technologies Co., Ltd.

Cell lines and animals

B16-F10 and HUVEC cells were obtained from Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Hunan Province). All cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37 °C. Female C57 mice (6−8 weeks old, 18−20 g) were obtained from Zhuhai Bestone Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangdong, China). The animals were kept in ventilated cages and exposed to light for around 10 h. The ambient temperature was controlled at 22 °C, and the humidity was controlled between 50% and 60%.

Synthesis of PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes

PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes were synthesized by one-pot hydrothermal method. Specifically, PVP (300 mg), IrCl3 (0.02 mol), K2PtCl4 (0.02 mol), FeCl3 (0.02 mol), Na2MoO4 (0.02 mol), ZnNO3·6H2O (0.02 mol) were dissolved in a mixture of water, ethanol and ethylene glycol (30 mL) in a volume ratio of 20:7:3. Subsequently, HCl solution (1 M, 1 mL), NaI (20 mg) and L-proline (3g) were added with stirring until a homogeneous solution formed. The mixture was transferred into a tetrafluoroethylene vessel, which was sealed in a stainless-steel autoclave. The autoclave was heated at a rate of 3 °C/min and maintained at 210 °C for 16 h. Then, the suspension was centrifuged twice at 9000 g for 8 min and washed with water. Finally, PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes were dispersed in water and stored for further use.

AC-treated PtIrFeMoZn HEA (AC-HEA) nanozymes: HEA were dispersed in PBS, then stimulated for 10 min with alternating current. The square wave electric field of 3.0 V with a frequency of 10 mHz was chosen.

Characterization

The morphology and elemental compositions of the samples were analyzed by transmission electron microscope (Talos F200X, Thermo Scientific) and scanning electron microscope (SEM) (GeminiSEM 300, Zeiss). The crystal structure of materials was obtained from an X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab XRD, Rigaku Corporation). UV-Vis-NIR spectra were collected by a UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Lambda 1050+, Perkin Elmer). The concentrations of nanoparticles were measured by ICP-MS (NexION 2000G, Perkin Elmer). Fluorescence staining of cells is observed by a Confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM980, Zeiss). The XPS patterns were obtained on an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Escalab Xi+, Thermo Scientific). Zeta potential was measured by ZSU3100 (Malvern). Density functional theory calculations

Spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) computations were conducted using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP.6.3.2)56,57. The projector augmented-wave (PAW) method with an energy cutoff of 450 eV was utilized to describe the ion-electron interactions58,59. The exchange-correlation interactions were modeled using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional60, supplemented by Grimme’s DFT-D3 dispersion correction for van der Waals interactions. The high-entropy alloy system was constructed using a primitive cell of pure Pt metal with the Fm-3m crystal structure. A 7 × 7 × 7 supercell expansion was performed prior to metal doping via the Alloy Theoretic Automated Toolkit (ATAT). Special quasirandom structures (SQS) with a cubic lattice were optimized according to the experimental composition results of Pt0.33: Ir0.18: Fe0.21: Mo0.18: Zn0.10. For cluster extraction, a spherical region with radius of 8.5 Å was defined around an arbitrarily selected atom in the bulk SQS structure as the centroid. The final cluster model exhibited a centroid-to-farthest-boundary distance of 8.32 Å and contained 147 atoms, serving as the structural unit for subsequent analyses. To accommodate the vacuum truncation for the isolated cluster, the simulation box dimension is defined as a = b = c = 25.0 Å. Brillouin zone integration utilized Gamma-centered 1×1×1 k-point sampling61. Structural relaxations were performed until atomic forces fell below 0.02 eV/Å, and the energy convergence standard was 10−5 eV. POD-like activity and kinetic assay

POD-like activity of PtIrFeMoZn HEA was evaluated using TMB as substrates in the presence of H2O2 in PBS solution (0.2 M, pH 4.0). A UV-Vis spectrophotometer was used to record the absorbance of the color reactions after a certain reaction time, and the chromogenic reactions represent POD-like activity.

The determination of catalytic kinetics: the steady-state kinetic assays were performed in NaAc solution (2 mL, 0.2 M, pH 4.0) with PtIrFeMoZn HEA in various tubes. Then, TMB solution (100 μL, 10 mg/mL in DMSO) and various H2O2 concentrations (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 mM) were added to the above mixture, followed by measuring the absorbance at 652 nm.

Absorbance data were back-calculated to concentration by the Beer–Lambert Law using a molar absorption coefficient of 39000 M−1 cm−1 for TMB-derived oxidation products [c = A/εb, where ε = 39000 M−1 cm−1, b = 1 cm].

GPx-like activity

DTNB solution (3.0 mg/mL, 240 μL), glutathione (GSH) solution (10 mM, 30 μL), and 1.63 mL DI water were mixed with PtIrFeMoZn HEA solution. After that, the mixtures were maintained at 25 °C under continuous magnetic stirring. The optical absorbance at 412 nm was recorded.

NADH oxidase-like activity

NADH (20 µg/mL) was mixed with PtIrFeMoZn HEA nanozymes solution (final concentration of 10 µg/mL) for various times. The UV-Vis absorbance of the supernatant was tested every 10 min. NADH oxidase-like activity was confirmed by the increase in the absorption value at 260 nm and the decrease in the absorption value at 340 nm.

MPO-like activity

The MPO-like activity of HEA nanozymes was assayed in acetate buffer (0.2 M, pH 4.0). A mixture of nanozymes (10 μL, 1 mg/mL), KCl (1.6 M, 50 μL), H2O2 (40 mM, 10 μL), and MCD (1 mM, 5 μL) was combined with acetate buffer to a total volume of 2 mL. The solution was incubated at 37 °C for various times, and the absorbance change of MCD at 290 nm was measured.

Lyse the cells with the lysis buffer on ice for 5 min. Finally, the lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for later use.

The corresponding supernatant was mixed with the ATP detection working solution (Beyotime, S0026). Subsequently, a microplate reader was used to detect chemiluminescence, which served as an indicator of the ATP content.

Mitochondria morphology study

B16-F10 cells (5 × 104 cells per well) were seeded into 24-well plates in the standard cell media. B16-F10 cells were treated with (1) blank, (2) HEA, and (3) AC-HEA. After 12 h co-incubation. The cells were slightly washed with PBS and stained with Mito-tracker to label mitochondria according to the product manual after 4 h. The images were visualized with a CLSM.

Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential in vitro

B16-F10 cells (5 × 104 cells per well) were seeded into 24-well plates in the standard cell media. B16-F10 cells were treated with (1) blank, (2) HEA, and (3) AC-HEA. After 12 h co-incubation. The cells were slightly washed with PBS and stained with the JC-1 working solution after 4 h. The images were visualized with a CLSM.

Calreticulin (CRT) and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) expression

Briefly, B16-F10 cells were plated in 24-well plates (5 × 104 cells per well) and incubated with different formulations, including (1) blank, (2) HEA, and (3) AC-HEA. After 12 h of incubation in the dark, the cells were washed repeatedly with PBS and then incubated with the corresponding primary antibody for 12 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cells were further incubated with FITC-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min at 37 °C. Finally, cells were stained with DAPI for 10 min and then observed under CLSM.

In vitro hemolysis assay

The hemolysis assay was performed to assess the hematotoxicity of HEA nanozymes in vitro. Initially, a 2% red blood cell (RBC) suspension was prepared and washed three times with PBS to remove impurities. Subsequently, the washed RBCs were co-incubated with HEA nanozymes of varying concentrations in PBS to simulate interactions under physiological buffer conditions. Deionized water was used as a positive control group. After incubating the mixtures at 37 °C for 4 h, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min. Finally, the optical absorbance of the collected supernatant was detected at 570 nm to quantify the hemolysis rate.

Here, hemolysis percent (%) = (Asample−Anegative)/(Apositive−Anegative) × 100%, where Asample, Anegative, and Apositive are the absorbance of the samples, the negative control, and the positive control, respectively.

In vivo biodistribution

All C57 mice (female, 4–5 weeks old) were obtained from Zhuhai Bestone Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangdong, China). To obtain the tumor-bearing mice, C57 mice were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 105 cells in 100 μL PBS into the flank region of the right back. Mice were randomly divided into three groups and treated with: (1) control, (2) HEA, and (3) AC-HEA, respectively. When the tumors grew to 60–100 mm3, all mice were randomly divided into three groups. HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes were intravenously injected (100 µL, 20 mg/kg), while the control group was injected with saline solution. After the injections, the mice were dissected at different time points. The mice’s blood, organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney), and tumors were transformed into aqua regia for digestion. After complete digestion, the mixed system was filtered through a 0.22-μm filter membrane to obtain the test solution. Then, the Pt content was measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).

In vivo antitumor efficacy

All C57 mice (female, 4 or 5 weeks old) were obtained from Zhuhai Bestone Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangdong, China). To obtain the tumor-bearing mice, 4–5 weeks female C57 mice were subcutaneously injected of 1 × 105 cells in 100 μL PBS into the flank region of the right back of the mice. Mice were randomly divided into three groups and treated with: (1) blank, (2) HEA, and (3) AC-HEA, respectively. When their tumors grew to 60–100 mm3, all mice were randomly divided into three groups. HEA and AC-HEA nanozymes were intratumorally injected (100 µL, 20 mg/kg), while the blank group was intratumorally injected with saline solution. The size of the tumor was detected using a digital caliper. The tumor volume was calculated, and the body weight of mice was also recorded at specific time periods (1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, 10th, 14th day). At the 14th day, tumors of different treatments were dissected and weighed. The main organ and tumor issues were sectioned into slices and stained with H&E and TUNEL for histological analysis.

Wound healing assay

Cell motility was evaluated with a wound-healing assay. In brief, B16-F10 cells were uniformly seeded into a 6-well plate (2 × 105 cells per well) and incubated at 37 °C until they reached 80% confluence. A sterile 200-µL pipette tip was used to scratch a line at the center of the cell monolayer. B16-F10 cells were cultured in a low serum medium treated with HEA or AC-HEA. Plate images were taken at 0, 12, and 24 h under a microscope, and the cell migration rate was calculated with Image J software.

Invasion assay

Transwell upper chamber membranes were precoated with 100 μL cold Matrigel at 37 °C for 4 h for gelatinization. Then, the chambers were soaked in a 24-well plate containing DMEM complete medium (600 μL/well). Cells were inoculated with 105 cells/well at 200 µL into the upper chamber. After 48 h of culture, invasive cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 10 min.

To quantify the invasion of B16-F10 cells, 30% acetic acid solution was used to dissolve the crystal violet. The absorbance at 570 nm was detected via a Microplate Reader.

Immune response analysis

To evaluate the effect on DC and T cells, after completing the 14-day treatment cycle, we dissected the tumors from the mice and then carried out the following treatments. Tumors from each group were homogenized with the digestion buffer of collagenase type IV (0.2 mg/mL) (Yeasen, 40510ES60), hyaluronidase (0.2 mg/mL) (Yeasen, 20426ES60), and deoxyribonuclease l (0.1 mg/mL) (Yeasen, 10608ES25) to isolate lymphocytes. Subsequently, the mixtures were ground and filtered to collect single cells. Antibodies were used to stain immune cells, shown as follows: APC anti-CD80, PE anti-CD86, Cy5.5 anti-CD11c, APC anti-CD3, PE anti-CD8, BV421 anti-CD45. Finally, samples were tested by flow cytometry.

ELISA

To examine the cytokine expression, the collected tumors were homogenized, and the supernatant was sampled by centrifugation. The levels of IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α were determined using corresponding mouse ELISA kits.

In vivo anti-metastasis study

Firstly, 2 × 105 B16-F10 cells were subcutaneously injected (s.c.) to C57 mice to induce a primary tumor. 7 days later, when the orthotopic tumors grew to 60–100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into three groups: (1) control, (2) HEA, and (3) AC-HEA groups. Subsequently, saline, HEA, or AC-HEA nanozymes were injected via the tail vein, respectively. 24 h after the injection of these agents, B16-F10 cells (100 μL, 1 × 107/mL) were injected into the tail vein to establish a tumor lung metastasis model. Finally, lung tissues were isolated from mice after 14 days. Lung tissues were observed using an optical camera and stained immunohistochemically.
