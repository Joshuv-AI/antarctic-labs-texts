Entangled quantum trajectories in relativistic systems
Yannick Noel Freitag, Julien Pinske, Jan Sperling

Abstract
Quantum entanglement is a key resource for quantum technologies, including emerging ground-to-satellite quantum communication. In such a scenario, an important challenge to be overcome is to consider entanglement between two or more quantum particles in different inertial frames, potentially experiencing relativistic effects affecting quantum correlations. In this paper, we present a consistent framework that overcomes this challenge. To this end, we establish the notion of factorizable and entangled multi-time trajectories and derive a class of Euler–Lagrange equations under the constraint of a non-entangling behavior. Comparing this restricted evolution to the solutions of the unrestricted equations of motion allows one to investigate the trajectory-based entanglement of general systems. We solve our equations for interacting particles in a Klein–Gordon-type setting, thereby quantifying the dynamic and relativistic impact of entanglement in a self-consistent manner.

Introduction

Today’s strong interest in the fundamental notion of quantum entanglement can be attributed to its essential role in quantum information processing and quantum communication

The need for a consistent framework to investigate entanglement of spacetime trajectories also becomes apparent when considering certain, seemingly contradicting findings in the literature.

In this paper, we overcome aforementioned limitations by constructing a consistent framework to describe relativistic entanglement.
To this end, a frame-independent notion of factorizable quantum trajectories is introduced.
For this concept, we then derive equations of motion, dubbed relativistic factorizability Euler–Lagrange (ReFEL) equations, that forces the particles’ trajectories to remain factorizable under arbitrary spacetime transformations.
To demonstrate the method’s capabilities, we study the buildup of entanglement due to interaction between relativistic particles.
The comparison of both the restricted and unrestricted evolution yields insight into the entangling dynamics.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we review concepts about entanglement pertinent to this work.
This includes the concept of multi-time wave functions.
An insightful example is provided that showcases the need for the consistent description of relativistic entanglement.
Finally, the Euler–Lagrange equations are recapitulated as a means to later derive equations of motions under non-entangling constraints.

II.1
Entanglement

A core difference between classical and quantum mechanics is the concept of entanglement
NC10
;
BL06
;
B09
.
A consequence of this phenomena is that a quantum state of entangled particles cannot be described through individual states of each particle.
For one of
N
particles,
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the state of a quantum system is expressed through the wave function in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{j}
,

|\psi_{j}\rangle=\int dx_{j}\,\psi(x_{j})|x_{j}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{j},

(1)

where
\psi_{j}(x_{j})
is the square-integrable wave function of the particle’s one-dimensional position
x_{j}
.
When each particle can be described individually their combined state corresponds to a tensor product,

|\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}\rangle=|\psi_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|\psi_{N}\rangle.

(2)

In the context of entanglement, the factorizable state in Eq. (
2
) clearly differs from the most general form of an
N
-partite state,

|\psi\rangle=\int dx_{1}\cdots dx_{N}\,\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\,|x_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|x_{N}\rangle.

(3)

Thus, if the wave function
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
does not admit a product form,
\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\psi_{1}(x_{1})\cdots\psi_{N}(x_{N})
, the state
\psi\neq\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}
is said to be entangled.

We remark that, in the context of mixed states, entanglement is superseded by the notion of inseparable states
W89
.
Throughout this work, however, we focus on pure states.
Still, a generalization can be done noting that mixed states are convex combinations of pure states, such as argued in Ref.
SW20
.

II.2
Multi-time wave function

Thus far, we focused on a time-independent description although we aim at exploring dynamical entanglement.
One commonly has only one fixed reference frame;
thus, a single time coordinate
\bar{t}
for studying entanglement dynamics suffices
SW17
.
However, when, for example, different Lorentz boosts are applied to individual particles, a so-called multi-time wave function approach can be introduced
LPT17
;
LPT20
, originally established by Dirac
D32
.

Rather than employing wave functions of the form
\psi(\bar{t},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
, one takes
\psi\left(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{N},x_{N}\right)
in the multi-time approach, which now contains
N
pairs of spacetime coordinates—again restricting ourselves to one spatial dimension for the sake of simplicity.
Both single-time and multi-time concepts are related by the identity

\psi(\bar{t},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\psi\left(\bar{t},x_{1},\ldots,\bar{t},x_{N}\right).

(4)

Non-relativistic investigations of dynamical entanglement, like in Ref.
SW17
, were based on one time coordinate, factorizing states at each time as

|\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}(\bar{t})\rangle=|\psi_{1}(\bar{t})\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|\psi_{N}(\bar{t})\rangle.

(5)

This formulation, however, leads to inconsistencies that are demonstrated next, and the multiple times coordinates
t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}
can be exploited to overcome such limitations as we show later.

II.3
Ill-defined notions of relativistic entanglement

Time-dependent entanglement with only one time coordinate may lead to fundamental issues.
For showing this, suppose the two-particle wave function

\psi(t_{1},x_{1},t_{2},x_{2})\propto f_{1}(x_{1}-t_{2})f_{2}(x_{2}-t_{1}).

(6)

Mind the swapped indices of times in arguments of the two functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.
Also note that we are working in natural units,
c=1=\hbar
.
When setting both times equal,
t_{1}=\bar{t}=t_{2}
, the joint wave function

\psi(\bar{t},x_{1},x_{2})=f_{1}(x_{1}-\bar{t})f_{2}(x_{2}-\bar{t})

(7)

is factorizable for all times
\bar{t}\in\mathbb{R}
.

On the other hand, we can also apply identical Lorentz transformations to both coordinate systems in Eq. (
6
),

x_{j}\mapsto\frac{x_{j}+vt_{j}}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad t_{j}\mapsto\frac{t_{j}+vx_{j}}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}},

(8)

for the velocity
v
.
Already for
t_{1}=0=t_{2}
after the Lorentz transformation, the formerly factorizable state takes a generally non-factorizable form,

\psi(0,x_{1},x_{2})\propto f_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1}-vx_{2}}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\right)f_{2}\left(\frac{x_{2}-vx_{1}}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\right).

(9)

For example, the choice
f_{1}(z)=f_{2}(z)\propto\exp(-z^{2})
shows that the boosted state is no longer factorizable.
Thus, a change of reference frames—and even a common change—alters whether or not the two particles are considered entangled, which is certainly problematic for a consistent description of relativistic entanglement.

II.4
Lagrangian mechanics

Until this point, we were agnostic towards which precise physical model underlies the evolution of the multi-time wave function.
In order to obtain equations of motion, we consider the physical model under study to be given by an action
S
defined as the integral over a Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}
, which is a function of the wave function that includes
N
particles’ spacetime coordinates, viz.

S=\int dt_{1}\cdots dt_{N}\int dx_{1}\cdots dx_{N}\,\mathcal{L}.

(10)

Let
\psi^{\ast}
be the complex conjugate of the wave function
\psi
and
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
.
The principle of stationary action and variational calculus result in the well-known Euler–Lagrange equation

\displaystyle 0={}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\psi^{\ast}}-\partial_{t_{1}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{t_{1}}\psi^{\ast})}-\cdots-\partial_{t_{N}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{t_{N}}\psi^{\ast})}

(11)

\displaystyle-\partial_{x_{1}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{x_{1}}\psi^{\ast})}-\cdots-\partial_{x_{N}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{x_{N}}\psi^{\ast})}.

In the next section, we derive equations of motion under the additional constraint of its solution being factorizable at all times, see also Ref.
SW20
in this context.

III
Factorizable trajectories

After preliminary considerations, including the example of ill-defined entanglement, a consistent notion thereof is introduced in this section.
After that, we derive the ReFEL equations, whose solutions are strictly factorizable trajectories independent of an observer’s reference frame.

III.1
Product quantum trajectories

Here, we refer to a quantum state, including its propagation in time, as a quantum trajectory,
\left\{|\psi(\bar{t})\rangle\right\}_{\bar{t}\in\mathbb{R}}
.
In other words, a trajectory resembles the particle’s complete path in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
In a single-time description, an
N
-factorizable trajectory in
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{N}
is defined as

\Big\{|\psi_{1}(\bar{t})\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|\psi_{N}(\bar{t})\rangle\Big\}_{\bar{t}\in\mathbb{R}},

(12)

and
\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}(\bar{t},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\psi_{1}(\bar{t},x_{1})\cdots\psi_{N}(\bar{t},x_{N})
in the wave function description.
Such a description accounts for spatial factorizability but fails to incorporate factorizability, when particles’ reference frames are subject to transformations, as was illustrated by the counterexample in Sec.
II.3
.

To overcome this limitation, we can consider each particle with its own time coordinate, resulting in a product of trajectories,

\left\{|\psi_{1}(t_{1})\rangle\right\}_{t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\left\{|\psi_{N}(t_{N})\rangle\right\}_{t_{N}\in\mathbb{R}}.

(13)

Likewise, in terms of multi-time wave functions, we have

\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{N},x_{N})=\psi_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})\cdots\psi_{N}(t_{N},x_{N}),

(14)

straightforwardly generalizing a purely spatial separation to the concept of spacetime factorizability.

Note an important distinction between a trajectory of tensor products [Eq. (
12
)] and a tensor product of trajectories [Eq. (
13
)] used here.
Unlike the preceding definition, the latter multi-time approach even allows for exploring entanglement between only temporal degrees of freedom, as we see with later examples.
Specifically, if an
N
-particle wave function cannot be factorized according to its spatiotemporal coordinates, as in Eq. (
14
), it is said to be entangled.
Thus, an important feature of the updated, multi-time factorizability approach is that factorizability is invariant under any coordinate transformation.
These operations of each particle’s coordinates include all boost-like transformation that mix temporal and spatial degrees of freedom.
Factorizable multi-time trajectories thus resolve the previously discussed issue of entanglement in relativistic systems, which had a clear spatial separation but showed interference in time coordinates.
As a final remark, the multi-time approach is the same as the single-time approach for equal times,
t_{1}=\cdots=t_{N}=\bar{t}
.

III.2
Factorizability Euler–Lagrange equations

For the now established notion of factorizability of trajectories, Eq. (
13
), we can derive equations of motion.
Comparing the solutions of these equations with the ones obtained from the conventional Euler–Lagrange equation—i.e., the one not being restricted to factorizable trajectories—enables us to study entanglement in arbitrary reference frames.

Derivation.

Suppose
\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}
to be a function of the product form in Eq. (
14
).
A gradient of the function can be expressed as

\nabla_{q}\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\partial_{q_{1}}\psi_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\cdots\psi_{N}\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{1}\cdots\psi_{N-1}\left(\partial_{q_{N}}\psi_{N}\right)\end{bmatrix},

(15)

for temporal
q=t
and spatial
q=x
degrees of freedom.
Next, the action can be recast into the form

S=\int dt_{j}\int dx_{j}\,\mathcal{L}_{j},

(16)

where we defined the effective Lagrangian density for the
j
th subsystem as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{j}={}

\displaystyle\int dt_{1}\cdots dt_{j-1}dt_{j+1}\cdots dt_{N}

(17)

\displaystyle\times\int dx_{1}\cdots dx_{j-1}dx_{j+1}\cdots dx_{N}\,\mathcal{L},

which explicitly depends on the wave function
\psi_{j}
while the other components have been integrated over—i.e., traced out.
Applying the variation of the action under the constraint of factorizable trajectories directly yields the equation of motion for the
j
th particle only, analogously to Eq. (
11
),

0=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{j}}{\partial\psi_{j}^{\ast}}-\partial_{t_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{j}}{\partial(\partial_{t_{j}}\psi_{j}^{\ast})}-\partial_{x_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{j}}{\partial(\partial_{x_{j}}\psi_{j}^{\ast})}.

(18)

The ReFEL equations for
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
in Eq. (
18
) are a central finding that allows us to compare the unconstrained dynamics, Eq. (
11
) for
\psi(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{N},x_{N})
, with the one confined to factorizable trajectories,
\psi_{1}(t_{1},x_{1})\cdots\psi_{N}(t_{N},x_{N})
.
Since the variation is done for each
j
separately, the solutions of ReFEL equations result in an evolution that preserves factorizability in relativistic scenarios and rectifies the previously shown inconsistency.
Note that for a single, joint time coordinate in a non-relativistic scenario, Eq. (
18
) is identical with the separability Schrödinger equation from Ref.
SW17
, being limited to spatial factorizability.
Furthermore, in a closed system, one approach from Ref.
SW20
allows one to extend our equations of motion to an ensemble of trajectories, thus generalizing the method to mixed states.

It is also noteworthy that our method has loose resemblance with the Hartree–Fock method for many-body systems.
Therein, one describes the dynamics of a single particle in terms of an effective potential that incorporates the (average) interactions with other particles.
Likewise, Eq. (
18
) describes the individual trajectory (i.e., factors) of a particle subject to an effective Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{j}
, containing the interactions with all other particles in possibly different inertial frames.

Integral representation.

Equation (
18
) can be rewritten in an equivalent form using the effective Lagrangian as given in Eq. (
17
).
To this end, notice the following relations for the initial Lagrangian and a product trajectory that follow from the chain rule:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{q_{j}}\psi_{j}^{\ast})}=\psi_{1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{j-1}^{\ast}\psi_{j+1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{N}^{\ast}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{q_{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast})},

(19)

for
q\in\{x,t\}
, and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\psi_{j}^{\ast}}=\psi_{1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{j-1}^{\ast}\psi_{j+1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{N}^{\ast}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast}}

(20)

\displaystyle{+}\sum_{q\in\{t,x\}}\left[(\partial_{q_{1}}\psi_{1}^{\ast})\psi_{2}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{j-1}^{\ast}\psi_{j+1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{N}^{\ast}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{q_{1}}\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast})}\right.

\displaystyle+\cdots

\displaystyle\left.+\psi_{1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{j-1}^{\ast}\psi_{j+1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{N-1}^{\ast}(\partial_{q_{N}}\psi_{N}^{\ast})\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{q_{N}}\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast})}\right].

Integrating over all spacetime coordinates but the
j
th one, we obtain Eq. (
18
) with
\mathcal{L}_{j}
as in Eq. (
17
).
Additionally applying integration by parts,
\int dq_{k}\,(\partial_{q_{k}}\psi_{k}^{\ast})f=\psi_{k}^{\ast}f-\int dq_{k}\,\psi_{k}^{\ast}(\partial_{q_{k}}f)
, and assuming vanishing boundary terms, we find

\displaystyle 0={}

\displaystyle\int dt_{1}\cdots dt_{j-1}dt_{j+1}\cdots dt_{N}

(21)

\displaystyle\times\int dx_{1}\cdots dx_{j-1}dx_{j+1}\cdots dx_{N}

\displaystyle\times\psi_{1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{j-1}^{\ast}\psi_{j+1}^{\ast}\cdots\psi_{N}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast}}-\partial_{t_{1}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{t_{1}}\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast})}\cdots-\partial_{t_{N}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{t_{N}}\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast})}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\partial_{x_{1}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{x_{1}}\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast})}\cdots-\partial_{x_{N}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{x_{N}}\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}^{\ast})}\right).

This reformulation of the ReFEL equations contains an integrand akin to the one in the original Euler–Lagrange equation for
\psi=\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}
[Eq. (
11
)].
Beyond that, however, the ReFEL equations additionally include integrals of the form
\int dt_{k}\int dx_{k}\,\psi_{k}^{\ast}f
for
k\neq j
that correspond to projections onto trajectory factors,
\{\langle\psi_{k}(t_{k})|\}_{t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}}
.

III.3
Preliminary discussion

Equation (
18
) for
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
enables us to consistently derive the propagation of factorizable solutions, including in relativistic systems.
The derivation of the equation was based on non-entangled, multi-time product trajectories, Eq. (
13
).
Using product trajectories rather than spatial separation only, it becomes impossible for entanglement to occur, even when general coordinate transforms are applied to a particle’s spacetime coordinates, like a Lorentz transform.
This resolves the inconsistencies from Sec.
II.3
.
The integral form in Eq. (
21
) directly relates ReFEL equations to the equations of motion without factorizability constraints.

IV
Application: Interacting Klein–Gordon particles

For benchmarking our methodology, we here apply the ReFEL equations to a model of interacting particles obeying a Klein–Gordon-like equation.
As interactions commonly produce entanglement, the solutions of the conventional Euler–Lagrange equations generally differ from the constrained ReFEL equations.
By comparison of both cases, the entangling features can then be studied.

IV.1
Model under study

The Klein–Gordon equation is a relativistic wave equation, which can be enhanced by including a multi-time description and interactions, e.g., via the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(|\partial_{t_{k}}\psi|^{2}-|\partial_{x_{k}}\psi|^{2}-m_{k}^{2}|\psi|^{2}\right)-V|\psi|^{2}.

(22)

Therein, the potential energy
V=V(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{N},x_{N})
describes external forces and interactions between the
N
particles, described through their spacetime coordinates
(t_{k},x_{k})
;
see, e.g., Refs.
SE04
;
OIERSA19
.

Equations of motion.

For the given Lagrangian density in Eq. (
22
), the unconstrained Euler–Lagrange equation (
11
) reads

0=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\partial_{t_{k}}^{2}-\partial_{x_{k}}^{2}+m_{k}^{2}\right)\psi+V\psi.

(23)

Evaluating Eq. (
21
) for a factorizable wave function,
\psi=\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}=\psi_{1}\cdots\psi_{N}
, we obtain the wave equation

\displaystyle 0={}

\displaystyle\prod_{l:l\neq j}\left(\int dt_{l}\int dx_{l}\,|\psi_{l}|^{2}\right)\left(\partial_{t_{j}}^{2}-\partial_{x_{j}}^{2}+m_{j}^{2}\right)\psi_{j}

(24)

\displaystyle+\left[\sum_{k:k\neq l}\prod_{l:l\neq j,k}\left(\int dt_{l}\int dx_{l}\,|\psi_{l}|^{2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\int dt_{k}\int dx_{k}\,\psi_{k}^{\ast}\left(\partial_{t_{k}}^{2}-\partial_{x_{k}}^{2}+m_{k}^{2}\right)\psi_{k}\right]\psi_{j}

\displaystyle+\left[]\prod_{l:l\neq j}\left(\int dt_{l}\int dx_{l}\,|\psi_{l}|^{2}\right)V\right]\psi_{j}

for the
j
th particle with
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Using spacetime-like expectation values,

\langle\langle\hat{f}\rangle\rangle_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def.}}}{{=}}\frac{\int dt_{k}\int dx_{k}\,\psi_{k}^{\ast}\hat{f}\psi_{k}}{\int dt_{k}\int dx_{k}\,\psi_{k}^{\ast}\psi_{k}},

(25)

the above equation readily simplifies to

\displaystyle 0={}

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t_{j}}^{2}-\partial_{x_{j}}^{2}+m_{j}^{2}\right)\psi_{j}+\langle\langle V\rangle\rangle_{1,\ldots,j-1,j+1,\ldots N}\,\psi_{j}

(26)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k:k\neq j}\left(-\langle\langle\hat{E}_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}+\langle\langle\hat{p}_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}+m_{k}^{2}\right)\psi_{j},

where
\hat{E}_{k}=i\partial_{t_{k}}
and
\hat{p}_{k}=-i\partial_{x_{k}}
denote the energy and momentum operators, respectively, of the
k
th particle.
Equations (
23
) and (
26
) for the entangling and factorizable case, respectively, yield the distinct spacetime behavior of the two cases.

Chosen potential.

Furthermore, for the sake of concreteness, we can specify the potential
V
.
In particular, we consider a second-order Taylor expansion that is akin to a spacetime-symmetric version of Hooke’s law,

V=-t^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega t+x^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega x,

(27)

where
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})
,
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
, and
\Omega=\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
Note that first-order terms can be ignored by introducing local displacements, i.e., centralized coordinates
\Delta q_{k}=q_{k}-\langle\langle q_{k}\rangle\rangle_{k}
for
q\in\{t,x\}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
For example, spacetime-distance-based interactions, mediated by a coupling constant
\kappa
, and an external potential given by
\eta
can be introduced via the potential

\displaystyle V={}

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\left(t_{j}-t_{k}\right)^{2}-\left(x_{j}-x_{k}\right)^{2}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle-\eta\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(t_{j}^{2}-x_{j}^{2}\right).

This corresponds to

\Omega=\left(\eta+N\kappa\right)\mathbbm{1}-\kappa\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&\ldots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\ldots&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]

(29)

in Eq. (
27
), with
\mathbbm{1}
denoting the
N\times N
identity matrix.

IV.2
Gaussian solutions

As a proof of concept, we here focus on Gaussian solutions to explore continuous-variable entanglement.
General, non-Gaussian solutions in terms of parabolic cylinder functions
MathFunctLibrary
are derived in Appendix
A
.
Moreover, we consider massless particles,
m_{1}=\cdots=m_{N}=0
, unlike in the appendix.

Unconstrained solution.

Under the above premises, including centralized coordinates, Eq. (
23
) for the entangling case can be approached by using the spacetime-symmetric ansatz

\psi\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}t\\
x\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\begin{bmatrix}M&C\\
C&M\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t\\
x\end{bmatrix}\right),

(30)

where
M=M^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and
C=C^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
.
Note that the normalization of
\psi
does not affect the property of being a product or not and is thus ignored here and in the following.
Substituting the wave function into Eq. (
23
) for the potential in Eq. (
27
) yields

\displaystyle 0={}

\displaystyle\left[t^{\mathrm{T}}(M^{2}-C^{2}-\Omega)t+x^{\mathrm{T}}(C^{2}-M^{2}+\Omega)x\right.

(31)

\displaystyle\left.+2t^{\mathrm{T}}(MC-CM)x\right]\psi.

Each distinct quadratic term in this equation ought to be zero, i.e.,
M^{2}=\Omega+C^{2}
and
MC=CM
.
Since
C
commutes with
M
and
M
is a function of
\Omega
, we take

M=(\Omega+C^{2})^{1/2},\quad\text{for }C\text{ such that }C\Omega=\Omega C

(32)

and with the matrix square root that takes the branch with a positive real part for all eigenvalues of
M
to ensure the boundary constraint of integrability, including a vanishing wave function for the boundary at infinity.

Constrained solutions.

Similarly, we solve the factorizable case, employing the potential in Eq. (
27
) with the matrix
\Omega
and its entries
\Omega_{k,l}
.
That is, we take

\psi_{j}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}t_{j}\\
x_{j}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{j}&\gamma_{j}\\
\gamma_{j}&\mu_{j}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t_{j}\\
x_{j}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(33)

for
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
\mu_{j},\gamma_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
.
This ansatz results in the following form of Eq. (
26
):

\displaystyle 0={}

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t_{j}}^{2}-\partial_{x_{j}}^{2}-\Omega_{j,j}t_{j}^{2}+\Omega_{j,j}x_{j}^{2}\right)\psi_{j}

(34)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k:k\neq j}\Big(-\langle\langle\hat{E}_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}+\langle\langle\hat{p}_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle-\Omega_{k,k}\langle\langle t_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}+\Omega_{k,k}\langle\langle x_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}\Big)\psi_{j},

keeping in mind that
m_{k}=0
,
\langle\langle t_{k}\rangle\rangle_{k}=0=\langle\langle x_{k}\rangle\rangle_{k}
for all
k
.
Similarly to the previous case, the second-order terms from the potential and the derivatives that are proportional to
t_{j}^{2}
,
x_{j}^{2}
, and
x_{j}t_{j}
give

\mu_{j}=\sqrt{\Omega_{j,j}-\gamma_{j}^{2}}

(35)

for
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and the root’s branch with nonnegative real part for integrability.
Common Gaussian integrals also yield
\langle\langle x_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}=\langle\langle t_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}
and
\langle\langle\hat{E}_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}=\langle\langle\hat{p}_{k}^{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}
, implying that constant terms in Eq. (
34
) sum to zero.
Combining the solutions for all
j
, we can write

\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}t\\
x\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\begin{bmatrix}M_{\mathrm{fac}}&C_{\mathrm{fac}}\\
C_{\mathrm{fac}}&M_{\mathrm{fac}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t\\
x\end{bmatrix}\right),

(36)

where

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{fac}}={}

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}\left(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N}\right)\quad\text{and}

(37)

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{fac}}={}

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\Omega_{1,1}+\gamma_{1}^{2}},\ldots,\sqrt{\Omega_{N,N}+\gamma_{N}^{2}}\right).

The factorizable multi-time wave function only includes matrices with vanishing off-diagonal elements, i.e., spatial (
x_{j}x_{k}
), temporal (
t_{j}t_{k}
), and spatio-temporal (
x_{j}t_{k}
and
t_{j}x_{k}
) degrees of freedom.
Thus, the state does not include quantum correlations between the
j
th and
k
th particle for arbitrary
k\neq j
in this relativistic scenario of
N
interacting particles.

Again, more general, non-Gaussian solutions for both the entangling and factorizable case are provided in Appendix
A
.
Furthermore, note that we can also write our solutions as

\displaystyle\psi\propto{}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}t\\
x\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\begin{bmatrix}M&C\\
C&M\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t\\
x\end{bmatrix}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle\propto{}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(x+M^{-1}Ct\right)^{\mathrm{T}}M\left(x+M^{-1}Ct\right)\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}t^{\mathrm{T}}\left[M-CM^{-1}C\right]t\right),

including the factorizable case by setting
M=M_{\mathrm{fac}}
and
C=C_{\mathrm{fac}}
for
\psi=\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}
.
This allows one to consider the spatial components as a function of times
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})
and a time-dependent factor.

IV.3
Continuous-variable entanglement tests

To probe the entanglement for the states under study, we employ the criteria put forward in Ref.
SV13
which has also been applied in experiments
GSVCRTF15
;
GSVCRTF16
.
Specifically, for a Gaussian state
\psi\propto\exp(-q^{\mathrm{T}}Qq/2)
, where
Q=Q^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{(N_{1}+N_{2})\times(N_{1}+N_{2})}
, we can define operators

\displaystyle\hat{f}=\nabla_{q}+Qq,\quad\hat{f}^{\dagger}={}

\displaystyle-\nabla_{q}^{\mathrm{T}}+q^{\mathrm{T}}Q^{\dagger},

(39)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\hat{L}={}

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\dagger}\hat{f}.

It is not hard to see that
\psi
is the ground state of
\hat{L}
because of
\hat{f}\psi=0
.

Furthermore, we apply the optimization carried out in Ref.
SV13
, in which one minimizes the expectation value of
\hat{L}
over all non-entangled states.
This yields the state
\psi_{\mathrm{fac,min}}\propto\exp(-q_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}Q_{1}q_{1}/2)\exp(-q_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}Q_{2}q_{2}/2)
with respect to a bipartition of the degrees of freedom as
q=(q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}}
.
More specifically, we have the block-matrix representations

Q=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1,1}&Q_{1,2}\\
Q_{1,2}^{\mathrm{T}}&Q_{2,2}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{\mathrm{fac,min}}=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1}&0\\
0&Q_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(40)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{1}={}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{1,1},\quad\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{2}=\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{2,2},

(41)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{1}={}

\displaystyle\Big([\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{1,1}]^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{1,2}\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{1,2}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{1,2}\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{1,2}^{\mathrm{T}}\Big)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{2}={}

\displaystyle\Big([\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{2,2}]^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{1,2}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{1,2}+\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{1,2}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{Im}\,Q_{1,2}\Big)^{1/2}.

With this factorizable minimum state, we can determine the expectation value of
\hat{L}
, establishing the lower bound for factorizable states as

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{fac,min}}

(42)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(Q_{\mathrm{fac,min}}{-}Q)(\mathrm{Re}\,Q_{\mathrm{fac,min}})^{-1}(Q_{\mathrm{fac,min}}{-}Q)^{\dagger}\right].

Technical details of formalism are provided in Refs.
SV13
;
GSVCRTF15
;
GSVCRTF16
and their supplemental materials.
Here, we apply this kind of entanglement criterion in a sense where
q
can represent arbitrary spacetime coordinates, and entanglement is certified when

\langle\langle\hat{L}\rangle\rangle-g_{\mathrm{fac,min}}<0

(43)

holds true.
Beyond bipartitions, note that the method is also rather effective for probing multi-partite entanglement but is not used in the following two-particle example.

IV.4
Comparison and discussion: trajectories of two interacting particles

We now explicitly study the two-particle case (
N=2
) for a potential that depends on the particles’ spacetime distance,
V=-\kappa[(t_{1}-t_{2})^{2}-(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}]
.
This implies a coupling matrix

\Omega=\begin{bmatrix}\kappa&-\kappa\\
-\kappa&\kappa\end{bmatrix}=2\kappa\,e_{-}e_{-}^{\mathrm{T}}+0\,e_{+}e_{+}^{\mathrm{T}},

(44)

where the two orthonormal eigenvectors
e_{\pm}=(1,\pm 1)/\sqrt{2}
of
\Omega
describe center-of-mass coordinates (
+
) and difference coordinates (
-
).

Explicit solutions and initial values.

In the entangling case, the Gaussian function
\psi
is determined through matrix
C
that correlates temporal and spatial degrees of freedom and commutes with
\Omega
.
In particular, Eq. (
32
) yields

C=\gamma_{-}e_{-}e_{-}^{\mathrm{T}}+\gamma_{+}e_{+}e_{+}^{\mathrm{T}}

(45)

and

M=\sqrt{2\kappa+\gamma_{-}^{2}}e_{-}e_{-}^{\mathrm{T}}+\sqrt{0+\gamma_{+}^{2}}e_{+}e_{+}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(46)

Analogously, Eq. (
37

\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}
for the example studied here results in the matrices

C_{\mathrm{fac}}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\right)

(47)

and

M_{\mathrm{fac}}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\kappa+\gamma_{1}^{2}},\sqrt{\kappa+\gamma_{2}^{2}}\right).

(48)

For a viable comparison, we want that both scenarios start with the same initial state,
\psi(0,x_{1},0,x_{2})=\psi_{\mathrm{fac}}(0,x_{1},0,x_{2})=\psi_{1}(0,x_{1})\psi_{2}(0,x_{2})

Using the representation in Eq. (

\sqrt{\gamma_{+}^{2}}=\sqrt{2\kappa+\gamma_{-}^{2}}=\sqrt{\kappa+\gamma_{1}^{2}}=\sqrt{\kappa+\gamma_{2}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def.}}}{{=}}\mu,

(49)

with
\mathrm{Re}(\mu)>0

In other words, the initial values yield

\displaystyle M=M_{\mathrm{fac}}={}

\displaystyle\mu\mathbbm{1},

(50)

\displaystyle C={}

\displaystyle\sigma_{-}\sqrt{\mu^{2}-2\kappa}\,e_{-}e_{-}^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma_{+}\mu\,e_{+}e_{+}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad C_{\mathrm{fac}}={}

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma_{1}\sqrt{\mu^{2}-\kappa},\sigma_{2}\sqrt{\mu^{2}-\kappa}\right),

with signs

V
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a methodology for studying dynamical entanglement in relativistic systems.
Rather than imposing spatial separations only, we here used a multi-time approach to consider products of quantum trajectories that serve as uncorrelated states, and entangled trajectories that do not obey such a factorization.
Moreover, we derived the equations of motion that govern the dynamics for the factorizable case, applying variational methods that also yield the entangling dynamics.

Firstly, we demonstrated the need for a coherent description of relativistic entanglement by presenting an instance of entanglement inconsistencies when changing the reference frame.
A resolution of this issue was formulated in terms of products of trajectories, rather than products of states.
We also showed that relaxing the underlying multi-time approach to a single time led to the known notion of single-time factorizability.
With our approach, transforming each particle’s spacetime coordinates separately does not affect factorizability, superseding the need for a common reference frame.

Secondly, we put forward our ReFEL equations, being Euler–Lagrange equations that govern the propagation for a strictly factorizable spacetime trajectory.
Importantly, those equations are based on the same Lagrangian that is used to determine the evolution of non-factorizable trajectories, allowing for interactions without resulting in entanglement.
Comparing the multi-time wave functions from the restricted and unrestricted treatment then yields insight into the spacetime properties of entanglement between any number of quantum particles.
The removal of entanglement in the ReFEL equations can also be seen as an (artificial) censorship of entanglement
PS24
;
PM24
allowing for a comparison with the actual (possibly) entangling dynamics.

Finally, we applied our findings by solving the ReFEL equations for two particles obeying a Klein–Gordon-type equation with interactions proportional to their space-time separation.
Thereby, continuous-variable entanglement was characterized.
We explored the entanglement between the trajectories via a witness that jointly addresses two-particle spacetime correlations.
Furthermore, we analyzed the impact of changing reference frames on the purely spatial entanglement via distinct Lorentz boosts for two particles.
In the original frame, the initial values were chosen such that no purely spatial entanglement was observable, which changed after applying boost operations.
Importantly, the factorizable trajectory did not show spatial entanglement for any relative velocities.

Our broadly applicable framework paves the way to identify dynamical entanglement in relativistic settings.
Moreover, the approach generalizes to other kinds of particles, e.g., particles described by the Dirac equation, and may be extended from first quantization used here to second quantization, allowing for entanglement between fields
SA23
.
Moreover, a deeper analysis of factorizable quantum trajectories is fruitful for determining quantum speed limits
YS24
or area laws
WVHC08
which can be overcome by entangled quantum trajectories.

Appendix A
Non-Gaussian solution for various forms of multipartite entanglement

In this appendix, we formulate rather general solutions of the problem at hand.
This includes solutions for partial entanglement, e.g., arbitrary subsystem partitions, and non-Gaussian wave functions.
Special cases of the general solutions are the Gaussian, fully factorizable cases discussed in the main part.

A.1
Weber differential equation and parabolic cylinder functions

As a primer, we briefly discuss a family of differential equations and their solutions that pertain to the problems in the main text.
Parabolic cylinder functions
MathFunctLibrary
, depending on a parameter
\nu
, are the solutions of the Weber differential equation

0=\partial^{2}_{z}D_{\nu}(z)+\left(\nu+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z^{2}}{4}\right)D_{\nu}(z).

(54)

In other words, solutions of the more general differential equation

0=-\partial_{q}^{2}w(q)+\left(a+bq+cq^{2}\right)w(q)

(55)

with constants
a
,
b
, and
c
, can be expressed in terms of parabolic cylinder functions, viz.

w(q)\propto D_{\left(\frac{b^{2}}{8\sqrt{c^{3}}}-\frac{a}{2\sqrt{c}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\left(\sqrt{2}\sqrt[4]{c}\,q+\frac{b}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt[4]{c}}\right).

(56)

Parabolic cylinder functions can be related to Hermite polynomials
H_{n}
,

D_{n}(z)=2^{-n/2}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{4}}H_{n}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\quad\text{for $n\in\mathbb{N}$.}

(57)

Recall that orthonormal Hermite functions read

h_{n}(z)=(2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi})^{-1/2}e^{-z^{2}/2}H_{n}(z)
, where
H_{n}(z)=(-1)^{n}e^{z^{2}}\partial_{z}^{n}e^{-z^{2}}
.

A.2
Multipartite entanglement and reduced partial differential equation

We begin with an
N
-partite system, which we decompose into
K
subsystems containing
N_{1},\ldots,N_{K}
individual parties, where
N_{1}+\cdots+N_{K}=N
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that, for
q\in\{t,x\}
, we have a
K
partition of coordinates as follows:

\vec{q}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\\
\vdots\\
q_{N_{1}}\end{bmatrix},\ldots,\vec{q}_{K}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{K-1}+1}\\
\vdots\\
q_{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{K}}\end{bmatrix}.

(58)

We now seek solutions of the form

\psi(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{N},x_{N})=\psi_{1}(\vec{t}_{1},\vec{x}_{1})\cdots\psi_{K}(\vec{t}_{K},\vec{x}_{K})

(59)

for studying
K
-partite entanglement, i.e., non-factorizability according to the above form.
We considered the special cases
K=N
of full factorizability and
K=1
for unrestricted wave functions (likewise,
N_{1}=\cdots=N_{K}=1
and
N_{1}=N
, respectively) in the main part of the paper.

Using the
K
partition under study, our equations of motions [mostly from Eqs. (
21
) and (
23
)] can be recast into the form

\displaystyle 0={}

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{\vec{t}_{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{\vec{t}_{j}}-\nabla_{\vec{x}_{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{\vec{x}_{j}}\right)\psi_{j}

(60)

\displaystyle+\left(-\vec{t}_{j}^{\mathrm{\,T}}\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{j,j}\vec{t}_{j}+\vec{x}_{j}^{\mathrm{\,T}}\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{j,j}\vec{x}_{j}\right)\psi_{j}

\displaystyle+2\left(\vec{\tau}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\vec{t}_{j}-\vec{\xi}_{j}^{\mathrm{\,T}}\vec{x}_{j}\right)\psi_{j}+m^{2}\psi_{j}

\displaystyle+\left(\sum_{k:k\neq j}\langle\langle\nabla_{\vec{t}_{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{\vec{t}_{k}}-\nabla_{\vec{x}_{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{\vec{x}_{k}}\rangle\rangle_{k}\right)\psi_{j}

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{k,l:k,l\neq j}\langle\langle\vec{t}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{k,l}\vec{t}_{l}-\vec{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{k,l}\vec{x}_{l}\rangle\rangle_{k,l}\right)\psi_{j},

for
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.
Therein, we have a mass term
m^{2}=m_{1}^{2}+\cdots+m_{N}^{2}
,

N_{k}\times N_{l}
submatrices
\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{k,l}
of the full matrix
\Omega=\Omega^{\mathrm{T}}
, where
\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{k,l}^{\mathrm{\,T}}=\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{l,k}
, and the vectors

\vec{\tau}_{j}=\sum_{k:k\neq j}\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{j,k}\langle\langle\vec{t}_{k}\rangle\rangle_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\vec{\xi}_{j}=\sum_{k:k\neq j}\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{j,k}\langle\langle\vec{x}_{k}\rangle\rangle_{k}.

(61)

In the following, we apply a diagonalization of the submatrices, separation of variables, and parabolic cylinder functions to solve these equations.

A.3
General solutions

Suppose the submatrix
\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{j,j}
is diagonalized through orthonormal eigenvectors
\vec{e}_{s}
and eigenvalues
\omega_{s}^{2}
, where
s\in\{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{j-1}+1,\ldots,N_{1}+\cdots+N_{j}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{P}_{j}
.
Then, we can apply a separation of variables according to this diagonalization.

Spatial contributions.

We take the spatial parts for
x_{s}=\vec{e}_{s}^{\mathrm{T}}\vec{x}_{j}
in the direction of the
s
th eigenvector.
And we suppose

\left[-\partial_{x_{s}}^{2}+\omega_{s}^{2}\left(x_{s}+\frac{\vec{\xi}_{j}^{\mathrm{\,T}}\vec{e}_{s}}{\omega_{s}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]\phi_{s}=\omega_{s}\left(n_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\phi_{s}.

(62)

This means, we have Hermite functions

\phi_{s}(x_{s})=h_{n_{s}}\left(\sqrt{\omega_{s}}x_{s}+\frac{\vec{\xi}_{j}^{\mathrm{\,T}}\vec{e}_{s}}{\sqrt{\omega_{s}^{3}}}\right).

(63)

We can combine those solutions for all
s\in\mathcal{P}_{j}
and find
\phi_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}=\phi_{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{j-1}+1}\cdots\phi_{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{j}}
as the product of Hermite functions.
Also, from the differential equation, we obtain the following sum over
s\in\mathcal{P}_{j}
as

\displaystyle\left(-\nabla_{\vec{x}_{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{\vec{x}_{j}}+\vec{x}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\overleftrightarrow{\Omega_{j,j}}\vec{x}_{j}+2\vec{\xi}_{j}^{\mathrm{\,T}}\vec{x}_{j}\right)\phi_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\vec{x}_{j})

(64)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{P}_{j}}\omega_{s}\left[n_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\right]-\vec{\xi}_{j}^{\mathrm{\,T}}\left[\overleftrightarrow{\Omega_{j,j}}\right]^{-1}\vec{\xi}_{j}\right)\phi_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\vec{x}_{j}).

Temporal contributions.

The previous spatial solution can be substituted into Eq. (
60
), also using the separation
\psi_{j}(\vec{t}_{j},\vec{x}_{j})=\phi_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\vec{x}_{j})T_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\vec{t}_{j})
.
This results in

0=\left(\nabla_{\vec{t}_{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{\vec{t}_{j}}-\vec{t}_{j}^{\mathrm{\,T}}\overleftrightarrow{\Omega}_{j,j}\vec{t}_{j}-2\vec{\tau}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\vec{t}_{j}\right)T_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}+K_{j}T_{\mathcal{P}_{j}},

(65)

where
K_{j}
collects all terms which are constant with respect to temporal degrees of freedom in the subsystem consisting of elements of
\mathcal{P}_{j}
.

We can now proceed similarly to what we did for spatial degrees of freedom.
However, we do not necessarily select Hermite functions but general parabolic cylinder functions.
In particular, the differential equation

0=\partial_{t_{s}}^{2}T_{s}(t_{s})-\left(a_{s}+\left[2\vec{\tau}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\vec{e}_{s}\right]t_{s}+\left[\omega_{s}^{2}\right]t_{s}^{2}\right)T_{s}(t_{s})

(66)

is solved via

T_{s}(t_{s})=D_{\nu_{j}}\left(\sqrt{2}\left[\sqrt{\omega_{s}}t_{s}+\frac{\vec{\tau}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\vec{e}_{s}}{\sqrt{\omega_{s}}}\right]\right),

(67)

where

\nu_{j}=\frac{\left(\vec{\tau}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\vec{e}_{s}\right)^{2}}{2\omega_{s}^{3}}-\frac{a_{j}}{2\omega_{s}}-\frac{1}{2}.

(68)

From that, we obtain
T_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}
as the product of all
T_{s}
for
s\in\mathcal{P}_{j}
.
Furthermore, the analogous sum as carried out in the spatial case yields

K_{j}=a_{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{j-1}+1}+\cdots+a_{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{j}},
