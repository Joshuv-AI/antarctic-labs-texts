Sanguinarine identified as a natural dual inhibitor of AURKA and CDK2 through network pharmacology and bioinformatics approaches
Authors: Xiang Li, Qi You
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer (CA) continues to be a female malignant tumor with limited therapeutic options, resulting in a high mortality rate. Sanguinarine (SANG), a naturally occurring alkaloid, has demonstrated notable efficacy in preclinical treatment of CA. However, the mechanism through which SANG acts against CA is not fully understood. To address this, utilizing nine drug target prediction databases, we have successfully identified 379 potential targets for SANG. Venn diagram analysis compared 2367 CA-related targets from the GeneCards disease database, 2618 CA-closely related targets derived from multiple datasets in GEO through WGCNA analysis, and the 379 potential targets of SANG, resulting in 35 shared targets. Subsequently, by employing PPI network analysis, the Cytohubba plugin, the Human Protein Atlas, TCGA database data, and ROC curve analysis, we have identified AURKA and CDK2 as key targets of SANG in combating CA. Single-gene GSEA results suggest that the overexpression of AURKA and CDK2 is closely correlated with DNA replication, cell cycle progression, and various DNA repair pathways in CA. Molecular docking and molecular simulation dynamics analyses have confirmed the stable binding of both AURKA and CDK2 to SANG. In summary, by integrating diverse methodological approaches, this study discovered that SANG potentially inhibits the malignant features of CA by targeting AURKA and CDK2, thereby regulating DNA replication, cell cycle progression, and multiple DNA repair pathways. This lays a solid foundation for further exploring the pharmacological role of SANG in CA therapy. However, further in-depth in vitro and in vivo experiments are required to corroborate our findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81063-0.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CA) is among the top four most incident and fatal gynecological malignancies globally. In 2020, 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths were recorded, particularly among women from low- and middle-income countries1,2. Despite advancements in CA treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy, which have extended the survival of patients in the early stages, the long-term survival rate for advanced CA patients remains under 30%. Hence, it is crucial to actively explore innovative and effective treatment strategies to prolong the survival of CA patients.

Natural compounds, noted for their antitumor traits such as high efficiency, ability to target multiple points, and minimal side effects, have gained increasing attention from researchers3–5. Preclinical studies abundantly confirm the significant medicinal potential of these compounds in halting the progression of malignant tumors, including migration and invasion suppression6, proliferation inhibition7, radio- and chemotherapy resistance prevention8,9, tumor stem cell formation inhibition10, and immune evasion prevention11. Sanguinarine (SANG), a quaternary ammonium salt benzophenanthridine alkaloid derived from traditional herbs such as Chelidonium, Corydalis, Sanguinarum, and Borovula, possesses diverse pharmacological activities, such as anti-inflammatory, anti-osteoporosis, neuroprotective, and antipathogenic microbial effects12. Furthermore, SANG exhibits notable antitumor efficacy across multiple malignancies, such as melanoma13, breast cancer14, colorectal cancer15, hepatocellular carcinoma16, and pancreatic cancer17. The primary mechanisms of SANG involve regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition, inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and promoting immune response. Recent studies reveal that SANG promotes ferroptosis, cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and the generation of reactive oxygen species, ultimately inhibiting tumor growth. Hence, we deduce that SANG possesses immense potential in CA treatment through its ability to modulate multiple targets, pathways, and biological processes. Nonetheless, research on the precise targets and pathways of SANG in CA treatment remains nascent, underscoring the critical need for a systematic pharmacological mechanism analysis.

Network pharmacology utilizes the theories and methodologies of systems biology and bioinformatics, enabling an in-depth exploration of the intricate interactions between drugs and biological systems. In contrast to traditional drug development approaches, network pharmacology facilitates swifter and more accurate identification of potential drug targets, subsequently optimizing the drug design process and providing comprehensive insights into drug mechanisms. This innovation offers a fresh perspective and serves as a potent tool for modern drug development18. The seamless integration of network pharmacology and bioinformatics offers robust data support and effective analytical tools crucial for drug development. As an example, during the study of Saussurea involucrata’s antiviral effects on COVID-19, researchers employed bioinformatics tools to extract pertinent drug and gene data from publicly accessible databases. Subsequently, they used network pharmacology techniques to predict drug targets and elucidate their mechanisms of action19. This integrated approach aided researchers in precisely filtering potential drug candidates from an abundance of biological data, thereby efficiently propelling the drug development process forward. Additionally, simulation techniques for drug mechanisms play a critical role in the drug development process. Utilizing molecular simulation techniques, including molecular dynamics and docking simulations, allows us to predict the binding modes and affinities between drugs and biomolecules, thereby increasing the efficiency and accuracy of novel drug design. These simulation techniques offer robust support for conducting in-depth investigations into the interaction mechanisms between drugs and biomolecules.

This study employed a comprehensive approach, utilizing network pharmacology, bioinformatics, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation techniques, to thoroughly investigate the anti-CA mechanism of SANG. The objective is to establish a firm theoretical basis for the clinical application of SANG. Figure 1 illustrates the methodological workflow of our study.

Materials and methods

Comprehensive and systematic identification of putative targets for SANG

We initially conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) parameters of SANG by utilizing the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCSMP, located at https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php/). This evaluation encompassed various indicators, including oral bioavailability (OB) and drug-likeness (DL)20. From the PubChem database (located at https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), we acquired the corresponding SDF files and SMILES codes21. Following this, we screened for potential targets of SANG through searching nine databases, namely: Binding DB (http://www.bindingdb.org/bind/index.jsp)22, Comparative Toxicogenomics database (CTD; http://ctdbase.org/)23, GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/)24, PharmMapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/index.html)25, SEA (http://sea.bkslab.org/)26, STITCH (http://stitch.embl.de/)27, SwissTargetPrediction web server (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/)28, TCSMP, and BATMAN-TCM database (http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/)29. To ensure consistency in our data, we standardized the naming conventions of the acquired targets by referencing the UniProt database30. Shortly afterward, we visually represented and summarized the quantity of potential targets for SANG, sourced from the aforementioned databases, through the use of a petal diagram. Lastly, we employed Cytoscape software for the visualization of all potential targets of SANG.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

In summary, we obtained the datasets GSE63514 and GSE6791 from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database located at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/. The datasets, published by den Boon JA et al. and Pyeon D et al., describe the collection and processing of cervical tissue samples31,32. From these datasets, we extracted mRNA expression profiles and grouping data pertaining to CA tissues and normal cervical tissues. Following the removal of batch effects from both datasets, we proceeded with their merger. We then conducted WGCNA on the merged dataset. The WGCNA analysis was executed using R 4.3.1 software, accessible at https://www.r-project.org/. Utilizing the goodSamplesGenes function, we eliminated outliers and chose the top 25% of genes based on Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) as input for our analysis. We incorporated clinical features, encompassing both normal and tumor samples, and proceeded with sample clustering. We employed the pickSoftThreshold function to determine the soft threshold power. Subsequently, the computed adjacency was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). We applied hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting techniques, setting the minimum gene count per module to 30. Further, we merged similar modules. In conclusion, we constructed gene modules linked to CA clinical features and identified module genes significantly correlated with the clinical traits of CA.

Screening relevant targets for SANG treatment of CA

Using “cervical cancer” as the keyword, we conducted a search in the GeneCards database to identify targets linked to CA. Following this, we computed the median correlation score of these targets and chose those with scores exceeding the median for further examination. Subsequently, we employed a Venn diagram to compare several sets of data: clinically significant module genes associated with CA, derived from WGCNA analysis; targets from the GEO database with above-median correlation scores; and highly correlated targets sifted from the GeneCard database, alongside the pertinent targets of SANG. The overlap of these three target groups was deemed as the potential therapeutic targets of SANG for treating CA.

Functional enrichment analysis of SANG’s anti-CA related targets

Utilizing the R programming environment (specifically, version 4.3.1) coupled with the org.Hs.eg.db backend database, we efficiently extracted relevant target gene IDs (known as EntrezIDs) for more detailed examination. Our methodology incorporated the Disease Ontology Semantic and Enrichment (DOSE) analysis, leveraging Bioconductor’s clusterProfiler and Pathview packages. These resources facilitated comprehensive Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses on the inferred targets of SANG during CA therapy. To ensure statistical rigor, we established a threshold of 0.05 for both p-values and q-values in our GO and KEGG analyses, thereby pinpointing significantly enriched terms. The GO analysis encompassed three key areas: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). To offer an intuitive visualization of the enrichment findings, we devised bubble charts highlighting the top 10 most significantly enriched terms within each domain. Likewise, for the KEGG analysis, we crafted a distinct bubble chart, emphasizing the top 30 most pertinent pathways based on their rankings. In essence, this investigation employed proven bioinformatics tools to delve into the functional roles and signaling pathway involvement of the postulated SANG targets.

Construction of PPI network for SANG’s anti-CA related targets

In this study, data pertaining to 35 shared targets were imported into the STRING 12.0 database to establish a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network tailored exclusively for human proteins33. The parameters were adjusted to concentrate on “Homo sapiens” as the primary focus of investigation, and a minimal interaction score threshold of 0.7 (denoting high confidence) was implemented. Following this, the PPI network was exported and visualized utilizing Cytoscape 3.10.0 software.

Screening of core targets for SANG’s anti-CA activity using six algorithms in CytoHubba

Utilizing the cytoHubba plugin within Cytoscape 3.10.0 software, we pinpointed crucial nodes in the network, thus facilitating the identification of core targets34. Based on this, we employed six commonly used algorithms for in-depth network analysis, including Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), Degree, Edge Percolated Component (EPC), Eccentricity, Closeness, and Betweenness. From each algorithm, the top 6 targets were selected. Ultimately, an Upset diagram was utilized to compare the top 6 targets identified across all six algorithms. Targets appearing in the top 6 lists of all six algorithms were designated as key candidate targets, enhancing the precision of selection.

Analysis of the expression of key targets in CA tissues and normal tissues and their clinical significance

We downloaded STAR-processed RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database for the Cervical Squamous Cell Carcinoma and Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-CESC) project.

We organized the data and extracted it in Transcripts Per Million (TPM) format. Additionally, we extracted corresponding data for adjacent normal tissue samples. Considering the data’s format and characteristics, appropriate statistical methods were chosen, and analysis was conducted using the ‘stats’ and ‘car’ packages in R. Data visualization was achieved using the ‘ggplot2’ package in R. Following this, the Human Protein Atlas (THPA) database was utilized to assess the influence of core targets on patients’ overall survival (OS). Ultimately, the original TPM values were transformed using the formula log2(value + 1). ROC curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) for target genes was calculated using the ‘timeROC’ and ‘ggplot2’ packages in R. These assessments were aimed at exploring the diagnostic value of key targets for CA patients.

Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Initially, we divided the integrated GSE63514 and GSE6791 datasets into high and low expression cohorts based on the median expression level of the core targets. Subsequently, utilizing the KEGG pathway database as a reference, we performed single-gene Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using various R packages, including GseaVis, clusterProfiler, ggplot2, and ggpubr. Finally, after normalization correction, we visualized the results with the highest and second highest Normalized Enrichment Scores (NES).

Molecular docking

Using ChemBio3D Ultra 14.0 software, energy minimization was performed on the SDF format of SANG molecules, which were then converted to the mol2 file format. The structure of the key receptor protein was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/). Subsequently, Pymol was utilized to refine the structure by eliminating water molecules and other non-critical non-protein elements. Following this, Autodock Tools v.1.5.7 was employed to prepare the receptor proteins and ligands, and convert them to the required pdbqt format for molecular docking simulation. With the aid of Autodock Vina software, the optimal binding conformation between the receptor protein and ligand was explored, culminating in the completion of the molecular docking process. Lastly, PyMol software facilitated the visualization of the docking results pertaining to the optimal conformation.

Molecular dynamics simulation (MDS)

The “Desmond” module in Schrödinger 2019 software was employed for this study. The protein-small molecule complex was immersed in a solvated SPC water model, with a default 0.15 M NaCl concentration. The OPLS2005 force field was utilized to model the behavior of water molecules. To neutralize the system’s charge, appropriate amounts of chloride and sodium ions were randomly introduced into the solvated system. Following the construction of the solvated system, energy minimization was performed using the default protocol integrated within the Desmond module, which employs OPLS 2005 force field parameters (Desmond Molecular Dynamics System, version X.Y, D. E. Shaw Research, New York, NY). The Nose-Hoover temperature coupling method and isotropic scaling were implemented to maintain the temperature at 300 K and the pressure at 1 atmosphere35. Upon completion of the aforementioned operations, a 30 ns NPT simulation was conducted. Trajectory data was subsequently recorded at 30 ps intervals.

Results

Related targets of SANG

We acquired the ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion) parameters for SANG from the TCMSP database, specifically noting an OB of 37.81% and a DL of 0.86. OB signifies the efficacy and degree of a drug’s absorption into the circulatory system following oral administration, serving as a pivotal indicator for assessing drug potency. DL illustrates the traits of drug molecules bearing analogous functional groups or physical properties. Based on the recommended standards from the TCMSP database, an OB of ≥ 30% and a DL of ≥ 0.18 are typically regarded as benchmarks for identifying potent drug active ingredients. Leveraging these two robust evaluative metrics, we infer that SANG, as a potential drug candidate, possesses significant developmental prospects. The two-dimensional ball-and-stick structure of SANG is depicted in Fig. 2A. Subsequently, we methodically identified and screened for potential targets of SANG across nine databases. As illustrated in Fig. 2B, we have identified and enumerated the potential targets of SANG, sourcing from nine databases: BindingDB (10 targets), CTD (77 targets), GeneCards (47 targets), PharmMapper (216 targets), SEA (10 targets), STITCH (10 targets), SwissTargetPrediction server (21 targets), TCSMP (4 targets), and BATMAN-TCM (37 targets). Upon successfully acquiring this target data, we utilized the UniProt database to standardize the terminology of these targets. Following rigorous target consolidation and deduplication procedures, we ultimately derived 379 putative SANG targets, as illustrated in Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 1.

Targets closely related to CA in the integrated dataset of GSE63514 and GSE6197

In this study, we selected two datasets, GSE63514 and GSE6791, from the GEO database. From the 63,514 dataset, we collected 24 normal and 28 CA tissues; from the GSE6791 dataset, we obtained 8 normal and 20 CA tissues (see Supplementary Figs. 1–2 ). After eliminating batch effects and amalgamating, we obtained a total of 80 samples, including 32 normal cervical tissue samples and 48 cervical cancer tissue samples (see Supplementary Figs. 3–4 and Supplementary Tables 2–3 for details). Genes with expression levels below 1 in at least 10% of the samples were excluded, resulting in a final selection of 19,861 genes for subsequent analysis. Next, we performed differential expression analysis based on the MAD and selected the top 25% most differentially expressed genes, totaling 4,965 genes, for further investigation. Following this, we used a clustering tree method to cluster the samples and removed outlier samples. We set the soft threshold at β = 16 to ensure a minimum R² value greater than 0.85. Additionally, we visualized the relationship between power and R², as well as power and Connectivity (see Fig. 3A for details). Through analysis, we obtained 14 modules, as shown in Fig. 3B. Figure 3C displays a co-expression heatmap of 800 randomly selected genes. We integrated clinical characteristic data, particularly focusing on normal and tumor samples, and calculated the correlation between these modules and the characteristics. The results are presented in Fig. 3D. Notably, the turquoise and salmon modules exhibited a strong positive correlation with GC tissue. Specifically, the turquoise module contained 1,486 genes, while the salmon module comprised 44 genes. On the other hand, the magenta, brown, and yellow modules demonstrated a strong negative correlation. These three modules contained 153, 481, and 454 genes, respectively. We included the genes from these five modules in our study and ultimately selected 2,618 module genes significantly associated with CA clinically for subsequent analysis. Scatter plots were created to illustrate the correlation between genes in these five modules and tumor tissue (see Fig. 3E), and detailed gene count statistics are provided in Fig. 3F and Supplementary Table 4.

Identification of CA-related targets for SANG therapy

To improve the precision of identifying targets linked to CA, we searched the GeneCards database using “cervical cancer” as a keyword, retrieving 5636 possible targets (detailed in Supplementary Table 5). We narrowed down this list to 2367 targets with a relevance score exceeding the median of 1.0086025, designating them as highly pertinent to CA. Utilizing a Venn diagram, we compared 2618 targets significantly associated with CA via WGCNA analysis, 2367 highly relevant CA targets from GeneCards, and 379 potential SANG targets, identifying their intersections. This comparison resulted in the identification of 35 common targets, deemed as SANG’s anti-CA-related targets. These targets were further visualized using Cytoscape software, as depicted in Fig. 4.

Analysis results of the expression of key targets and their clinical value

By analyzing the CESC dataset from the TCGA database, we found that the expression levels of AURKA and CDK2 in CA tissues were significantly higher than those in normal cervical tissues (Fig. 7A, C). To assess the clinical value of AURKA and CDK2, we employed the THPA online database to investigate the association between the expression levels of these genes and the OS of CA patients. The results showed that CA patients with high expression of AURKA had significantly shorter survival periods (Fig. 7B). However, the expression level of CDK2 did not seem to significantly affect the overall survival of CA patients (Fig. 7D). Using the TCGA-CESC dataset, we constructed the ROC curve for CDK2 and observed an AUC of 0.964 (refer to Supplementary Fig. 5 for detailed information). An AUC value falling within the range of (0.5, 1) signifies that the gene possesses a strong predictive capability. Consequently, CDK2 exhibits a considerable predictive value for CA patients. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry findings from the THPA database indicated the absence of CDK2 protein expression in 3 normal cervical tissue samples, whereas it was present in all 24 tumor samples obtained from 12 CA patients. Specifically, 4 samples exhibited high expression (strong staining), 16 samples demonstrated medium expression (moderate staining intensity), and 4 samples displayed low expression (weak staining) (refer to Supplementary Fig. 6 for detailed information). In conclusion, AURKA and CDK2 potentially play crucial roles in the mechanism of SANG’s anti-CA activity.

The regulation of DNA replication and cell cycle may be the core mechanism of SANG’s anti-CA effect

To further investigate the anti-CA effects of SANG, we utilized the integrated GSE6791 and GSE63514 datasets to perform a single-gene GESA enrichment analysis, concentrating on SANG’s primary anticancer targets: AURKA and CDK2. The results of our analysis, presented in Figs. 8− 1 and Supplementary Table 9, demonstrate a strong correlation between elevated AURKA expression and the upregulation of gene sets pertaining to the cell cycle, DNA replication, mismatch repair, Fanconi anemia pathway, nucleotide excision repair, and homologous recombination in the context of CA. Likewise, CDK2 overexpression exhibits a significant correlation with the upregulation of gene sets involved in DNA replication, cell cycle, mismatch repair, base excision repair, Fanconi anemia pathway, and nucleotide excision repair in CA. (Details are provided in Figs. 9− 2 and Supplementary Table 10.) Based on these findings, we hypothesize that SANG may hinder the malignant progression of cancer by modulating DNA replication, cell cycle, and multiple DNA repair pathways.

Molecular docking analysis results of the interaction between SANG and key receptor proteins

We used Autodock Tools 1.5.7 and Autodock Vina software to conduct a molecular docking analysis of SANG with its key receptor proteins, AURKA and CDK2. The aim was to assess the interaction between SANG and biologically pertinent targets, corroborating our prior research outcomes. Typically, a binding energy below − 5.0 kcal/mol is regarded as indicative of favorable binding affinity36. Detailed parameters of the docking between SANG and the AURKA and CDK2 receptor proteins can be found in Supplementary Table 11. With the help of pymol software, we visualized the optimal binding conformations (refer to Fig. 11A, B) and depicted the binding energies of the top 20 conformations via a heatmap (Fig. 10C). Notably, the binding energies for both AURKA and CDK2 were below − 11.0 kcal/mol, implying a potentially strong interaction between SANG and these receptor proteins. In conclusion, our analysis suggests that AURKA and CDK2 likely play pivotal roles in SANG’s suppression of the malignant progression of CA.

MDS results

To further corroborate the reliability of the molecular docking results, We initially conducted a 30-nanosecond (ns) molecular dynamics simulation on the AURKA-SANG complex exhibiting the best binding energy. RMSD analysis demonstrated that during the binding process of SANG with AURKA, spanning from 5 to 30 ns, the fluctuation of the protein backbone stabilized to less than 1 angstrom (Å), whereas the fluctuation of SANG remained within the 1–3 Å range. During the 20–30 ns phase of the simulation, both SANG and AURKA exhibited fluctuations below 1 Å during binding (refer to Figs. 10-1A). This degree of fluctuation is considered acceptable for small molecule proteins, indicating a stable interaction between SANG and AURKA. Simultaneously, during the 30 ns simulation process, the RMSF curves of both AURKA protein and AURKA protein bound to SANG demonstrated relatively stable fluctuations. (refer to Figs. 10-1B, C). Throughout the entire 30 ns simulation of the interaction between SANG and AURKA, we observed the formation of salt bridges, hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds, and water bridges between specific residues of SANG and AURKA, including GLU-260, LEU-139, ALA-213, and LEU-263 (see Figs. 10-1D, E). Additionally, Figs. 12-1F illustrates multiple interactions between AURKA and SANG, collectively enhancing the stability of their binding.

After analysis, the SANG-CDK2 complex with the lowest binding energy exhibits the following characteristics: During the 0–30 ns simulation of SANG binding to CDK2, RMSD data shows that the fluctuation of the protein backbone stabilizes within the range of 1–3 Å. Especially during the 1–30 ns period, its fluctuation further stabilizes at about 1 Å and shows a stable trend. Meanwhile, the fluctuation of SANG also remains within the range of 1–3 Å throughout the simulation, especially during the 25–30 ns period, where the fluctuation of SANG’s protein backbone stabilizes within 1 Å, indicating a more stable state (see Figs. 10-2A for details). These data suggest a stable interaction between SANG and CDK2. Additionally, both the CDK2 protein and SANG exhibit consistent stability in their RMSF curves throughout the entire simulation process (see Figs. 10-2B, C). Similar to the MDS results of the AURKA-SANG complex, during the entire 30ns simulation of CDK2-SANG interaction, we observed various interactions such as hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonding, and water bridges formed between specific residues of SANG and CDK2 (e.g., LYS-89, ASP-86, ILE-10, PHE-82, HIS-84, and LEU-134) (see Figs. 10-2D-F for details). Likewise, these multiple interactions between CDK2 and SANG further enhance the stability of the CDK2-SANG binding. In summary, SANG may exert anti-CA effects by targeting AURKA and CDK2.

Discussion

This study comprehensively analyzed multiple cancer-related datasets from databases such as Swisstarget, GeneCards, and GEO to systematically explore the potential targets of SANG against CA. By utilizing various analytical tools including WGCNA, Venn diagrams, protein-protein interaction network analysis, the cytoHubba plugin algorithm, and upSet plots, AURKA and CDK2 were successfully identified as key targets for the anti-CA growth effect of SANG.

AURKA (Aurora Kinase A) belongs to the Aurora kinase family. Aurora kinase was first discovered by Glover et al. in 1995, who demonstrated its crucial role in cellular mitosis37. The expression of Aurora kinase is strictly regulated during the cell cycle, reaching its peak during mitosis38. AURKA interacts with various mitotic regulators, including Ajuba39, TPX240, and D-TACC41, which are essential for spindle assembly and chromosome separation. Furthermore, AURKA interacts with γ-tubulin through centrosomin, participating in the regulation of centrosome assembly in cells42.

Analysis of multiple datasets in the TCGA database has revealed that AURKA expression is significantly upregulated in various tumor types, including CA (see Supplementary Fig. 7), as compared to normal tissues. Activation of AURKA plays a pivotal role in numerous cancer types, including neuroendocrine prostate cancer43, gastric cancer44, triple-negative breast cancer45, and non-small cell lung cancer46. This is consistent with the findings of Shao et al., who reported that hsa_circ_0075341 promotes the proliferation and invasion of CA cancer cells by regulating the miR-149-5p/AURKA axis47. Furthermore, according to mRNA expression profiling studies, AURKA is regarded as a significant target in CA treatment48. Gabrielli et al. further substantiated the crucial role of AURKA in the survival of HPV-driven CA cancer cells49. These findings are in accordance with our KEGG enrichment analysis results, suggesting that SANG may inhibit the growth of CA cancer by modulating HPV infection, viral oncogenesis pathways, and apoptotic processes. Recent data has demonstrated that AURKA is localized to the centrosome during the S phase of the cell cycle, potentially identifying it as an initiating or critical component in the centrosome and cellular reorganization processes during the transition from interphase to mitosis50. Collectively, these findings suggest that AURKA may be a pivotal molecule in the carcinogenic process of CA.

CDK2 plays a crucial role in cell cycle regulation and participates in various biological processes. By interacting with specific proteins like Cyclin E and p53, and facilitating their phosphorylation, CDK2 is implicated in diverse biological pathways, encompassing DNA damage response, intracellular transport, protein degradation, signal transduction, as well as DNA and RNA metabolism and translation51. Analysis of CDK2 expression in tumors compared to normal tissues in the TCGA database has shown that CDK2 is overexpressed in multiple tumors, including CA (see Supplementary Fig. 8for details). Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting CDK2 have proven to be an effective anti-tumor approach52. Based on experimental data from Tang et al., CDK2 activation promotes the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of TRIM32, thereby enhancing resistance to radiotherapy in triple-negative breast cancer53. Liu et al. have demonstrated that the N6-methyladenosine reader protein YTHDF2 facilitates cell cycle progression and stimulates the proliferation of multiple myeloma cells via the EGR1/p21cip1/waf1/CDK2-Cyclin E1 pathway. Earlier preclinical studies have indicated that targeting CDK2 can effectively suppress the proliferation of CA cell lines54. The CDK2/cyclin E complex also plays a key role in regulating centriole duplication, and Aurora A kinase plays a role in the separation of two newly formed centrosomes during the formation of the two poles of the mitotic spindle55. Collectively, these findings imply that SANG may suppress the proliferation of CA by binding to CDK2 and AURKA proteins, thus impeding the formation of centrosomes in CA cells.

To delve deeper into the mechanism underlying SANG’s anti-CA effects, we performed GSEA on an integrated dataset encompassing AURKA and CDK2 genes. Our results revealed a significant correlation between high expression of AURKA and CDK2 and the upregulation of gene sets associated with DNA replication, cell cycle progression, and various DNA repair pathways. This finding is consistent with prior research, which suggests that aberrant expression of CDK2 and AURKA stimulates DNA replication, accelerates cell cycle progression, and abnormally activates numerous DNA repair pathways, ultimately driving malignant tumor progression56–62. Radiotherapy constitutes a pivotal element in the current clinical management of CA. Nonetheless, resistance to radiotherapy markedly diminishes its therapeutic effectiveness, with abnormal activation of DNA repair pathways emerging as a key mechanism underlying this resistance. Consequently, targeting abnormally activated DNA repair pathways in radiotherapy-resistant patients is deemed a crucial strategy for restoring radiotherapy sensitivity63,64. Additionally, research has demonstrated that the inhibition of DNA repair pathways can result in the accumulation of DNA damage, subsequently promoting tumor cell apoptosis65–67. Numerous studies have established that SANG can suppress CA cell growth through the induction of apoptosis68–70. These findings imply that the regulation of DNA repair pathways could represent a novel mechanism through which SANG induces CA cell apoptosis. Collectively, our results suggest that SANG possesses the potential to suppress CA growth by modulating DNA replication, cell cycle progression, and various DNA repair pathways.

We conducted a molecular docking study to briefly investigate the regulatory effects of SANG on the key targets AURKA and CDK2. The results indicated that the top 20 conformations of both AURKA-SANG and CDK2-SANG complexes exhibited binding energies below − 11.0 kcal/mol. The best conformation binding energies were − 11.2 kcal/mol for AURKA-SANG and − 11.8 kcal/mol for CDK2-SANG, respectively. Previous studies have indicated that a binding energy lower than − 5.0 kcal/mol typically suggests significant interactions between a small molecule ligand and a protein receptor. Based on these results, we hypothesize that there are strong interactions between SANG and both AURKA and CDK2. For further verification, we conducted a 30 ns molecular dynamics simulation study on the AURKA-SANG and CDK2-SANG complexes. Our study revealed that both complexes maintained stable binding across various interactions. Furthermore, our results demonstrated that the CDK2-SANG complex exhibited a wider range of interaction types, greater stability, and reduced fluctuation during binding, which is consistent with the molecular docking results.

Overall, our study suggests that SANG targets AURKA and CDK2 proteins, leading to the disruption of spindle assembly in CA cells, inhibition of centrosome replication, separation, and assembly, as well as prevention of chromosome separation. Consequently, this results in cell cycle arrest, impaired DNA replication, and disruption of multiple DNA repair pathways, ultimately inhibiting CA growth. Our findings elucidate the mechanism of action of SANG and establish a theoretical foundation for its potential clinical application in CA treatment.

Finally, it is important to highlight that our study presents robust evidence supporting the potential of SANG in CA treatment, thereby establishing a strong theoretical basis for its future clinical application. However, it is crucial to acknowledge the inherent limitations of this study. Firstly, the accuracy of target identification is highly contingent upon the precision of the utilized databases. Furthermore, to further augment the efficacy of SANG in clinical development and application, future studies must rigorously validate our findings through in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Conclusion

In summary, this study has discovered SANG, a potential natural inhibitor targeting CDK2 and AURKA. Utilizing network pharmacology, bioinformatics analysis, and computational simulation, we revealed that SANG primarily functions by modulating the key targets, AURKA and CDK2. Consequently, SANG inhibits DNA replication, blocks multiple DNA repair pathways, promotes apoptosis, and induces cell cycle arrest, ultimately suppressing CA growth. Based on this discovery, we infer that the combination of SANG and radiotherapy will exhibit significant clinical value in the treatment of patients with radio- and chemo-resistant as well as advanced-stage cancers. This further underscores the enormous potential of SANG in the field of CA prevention and treatment. This study establishes a solid theoretical foundation for future in-depth exploration of SANG’s anti-cancer mechanisms. Nevertheless, further in vitro and in vivo experiments are required to validate our findings.
