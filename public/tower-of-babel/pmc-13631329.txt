Identifying a broad-spectrum influenza inhibitor that blocks multiple functions of viral NS1 protein
Authors: Yang Zhang, Yanan Luo, Wenrui Gai, Jinyu Wang, Lianghao Huang, Zihan Wang, Lishan Sun, Wanting Jiao, Wei Wang, Dehai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonstructural protein 1 (NS1) is the key virulence factor of influenza A virus (IAV) that affects the replication and pathogenicity of IAV, which can be used as a promising anti-IAV target. Herein, we identify a marine derived NS1 inhibitor HDN2398 with broad-spectrum anti-IAV effects in different cell lines and female mice, superior to oseltamivir and baloxavir. HDN2398 attenuates the interferon antagonist function of NS1 by blocking NS1 dimerization and its binding to dsRNA. HDN2398 can block NS1 nucleolar localization, viral mRNA splicing and nuclear export through destroying the interaction between NS1 and nucleolin or NXF1. Phe9 and Trp187 may be required for the interaction between HDN2398 and NS1. In summary, our findings support the development of HDN2398 as a promising anti-IAV agent targeting NS1-host interaction.Subject terms: Drug development, Influenza virus, Pharmacodynamics, Antivirals

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) is the most formidable pathogen and has been the cause of at least three pandemics in the last century. The most severe IAV pandemic caused more than 40 million deaths worldwide from 1918 to 19191,2. Seasonal influenza caused by H1N1 and H3N2 viruses spreads worldwide every year, leading to ~3–5 million serious diseases and 250–500 thousand deaths3,4. To date, there are only three classes of anti-IAV drugs approved for the treatment of influenza, which are directed against the viral M2 protein (amantadine and rimantadine), NA protein (zanamivir, oseltamivir, and peramivir) and PA protein (baloxavir)4–6. Despite these successes, drug resistance, toxicity, and cost remain unresolved issues in the fight against IAV infection6–8. Hence, the development of anti-IAV agents with high efficiency and low toxicity is highly important.

Nonstructural protein 1 (NS1) is the main component of IAV that antagonizes the natural immune response of the host and is also a key virulence factor affecting the pathogenicity and host adaptability of IAV9. NS1 can exert various functions, such as antagonizing interferon production, promoting viral mRNA splicing and export, and regulating cell apoptosis10–14. Thus, the multiple functions of NS1 make it a hot target for anti-IAV drug development15–17. The pyrazolopyridine derivative ML303 was reported to attenuate the interferon antagonistic ability of NS1, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of IAV in vitro18. The flavonoid baicalin can not only inhibit the interferon antagonistic function of NS1 but also interfere with the activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway to inhibit IAV infection19. Thus, NS1 inhibitors are expected to be developed into novel anti-IAV agents in the future.

To further search for the IAV inhibitors with significant activity, we screened the marine fungus-derived compound library via MDCK cell-based IAV infection assays and identified one indole alkaloid derivative (HDN2398) with broad-spectrum antiviral effects and low toxicity. HDN2398 can block the nuclear export of both the NS1 protein and viral mRNA by blocking the interaction between NS1 and NXF1. HDN2398 also inhibited the interferon antagonist function of NS1 by reducing the NS1-dsRNA interaction and the ubiquitination of RIG-I. Importantly, HDN2398 can protect mice against IAV-induced death and weight loss. Thus, our results support the development of HDN2398 as a promising anti-IAV agent that targets the NS1-host interaction.

Results

Identification of a broad-spectrum inhibitor against IAV both in vitro and in vivo

In this study, we first screened the marine fugus-derived compound library (over 200 compounds) using MDCK cell-based IAV infection assays and identified several indole alkaloid derivatives with strong anti-IAV effect. We expanded our in-house compounds library by synthesizing ten additional pseudo-natural products characterized by the three-clavatol scaffold (Supplementary Fig. S1). Through further comprehensive screening, we identified two hit compounds (HDN2398 and HDN3635) from fifty-six candidates with higher antiviral activity (>60% CPE inhibition) and lower toxicity (>90% viability) at the concentration of 50 μM (Fig. 1A). The structure-relationship study revealed that the substitution at 5-position was crucial for the anti-IAV effect of indole alkaloid derivatives, and compound HDN2398 with the fluorine substitution presented the strongest H1N1 virus A/Virginia/ATCC1/2009 inhibition (IC50 = 3.3 ± 0.3 μM) in MDCK cells, which was better than the effects of HDN3635 (IC50 = 28.1 ± 1.4 μM) and oseltamivir carboxylate (IC50 = 28.8 ± 0.9 μM) (Fig. 1A–C, Supplementary Figs. S2, S3 and Supplementary Table S1). The selectivity index (SI = CC50/IC50) for HDN2398 in MDCK cells was ~159.8, which was superior to that of oseltamivir (SI = 38.8) and ribavirin (SI = 14.0) (Supplementary Table S1). In addition, HDN2398 treatment significantly reduced the virus titer and plaque formation in A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (PR/8)-, A/Aichi/2/1968 (Aichi)-, and A/Virginia/ATCC1/2009 (Vir09)-infected cells in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values ranging from 2.6 to 7.7 μM, which was superior to the effects of oseltamivir (IC50 = 22.4 or 31.1 μM) (Fig. 1D, E, Supplementary Fig. S4A, B and Supplementary Table S2). HDN2398 exhibited no significant cytotoxicity at concentrations below 100 μM in different cell lines, with 50% cytotoxicity concentration (CC50) values of about 463.5 ± 13.8 and 151.7 ± 6.9 μM in MDCK and A549 cells, respectively (Fig. 1F). In addition, HDN2398 treatment also significantly reduced the expression of virus NS1 and NA proteins in IAV-infected cells (Supplementary Fig. S4C, D). Thus, these results suggest that HDN2398 possesses broad-spectrum anti-IAV activities in vitro with low toxicity.

The anti-IAV (PR8) effects of HDN2398 in vivo were further evaluated by using a mouse pneumonia model (Fig. 1G)20. In line with the in vitro anti-IAV activity, oral administration of HDN2398 (2.5 or 5 mg/kg/day) significantly increased the survival rates compared with those of the placebo-treated control group (Fig. 1H). Remarkably, 90% of the mice treated with 5 mg/kg/day HDN2398 survived IAV infection on day 14, which was greater than the effect of oseltamivir (80%) (Fig. 1H). In addition, the weights of the mice in the placebo group decreased sharply from the first-day post-infection (p.i.), and all the mice died at 8 days p.i. (Fig. 1I). However, the body weights of the HDN2398 (5 mg/kg/day)-treated mice gradually increased without obvious weight loss (Fig. 1I). Besides, oral administration of HDN2398 (2.5 or 5 mg/kg/day) significantly decreased pulmonary viral titers compared with those in the virus control group (Fig. 1J, K). The histopathological analysis indicated that after oral treatment of HDN2398 (5 or 10 mg/kg/day) for 4 days, the IAV-infected mice had intact columnar epithelia in the bronchiole without inflammatory cell infiltration, comparable to the effect of oseltamivir (10 mg/kg/day) (Supplementary Fig. S5). Consequently, HDN2398 significantly improved the lung indices of the IAV-infected mice, with 30–45% inhibition compared with that of the PBS-treated mice, comparable to the effect of oseltamivir (10 mg/kg/day) (Supplementary Table S3), suggesting that HDN2398 also attenuated the viral pneumonia caused by IAV infection. In summary, these results strongly demonstrate that HDN2398 strongly inhibits lethal IAV infection in mice.

Compound HDN2398 mainly inhibits virus replication through targeting NS1

To determine the stage(s) at which HDN2398 exerts its inhibitory effects, a time-of-addition assay was performed in PR8-infected MDCK cells as previously described21. The results showed that HDN2398 treatment (6.25–50 μM) after virus adsorption significantly inhibited virus multiplication with CPE inhibition rates over 70% (Fig. 2A). However, pretreatment of PR8 virus or MDCK cells with HDN2398 before infection or treatment during adsorption had very weak anti-IAV effects with CPE inhibition less than 35% (Fig. 2A). Moreover, another time course study indicated that HDN2398 treatment for the first 6 h (0–6 h p.i.) after adsorption resulted in a significant reduction of virus protein levels (Fig. 2B), suggesting that HDN2398 may inhibit some steps of the IAV life cycle that occur 0–6 h post-adsorption.

To further examine the antiviral mechanism of HDN2398, the influence of HDN2398 on virus surface proteins was evaluated via HA and NA inhibition assays22,23. The data showed that HDN2398 did not inhibit IAV-induced aggregation of red blood cells (RBCs) and did not significantly inhibit the NA activity of IAV (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). Moreover, the mini-genome assay revealed that the NP inhibitor nucleozin can significantly reduce the luciferase activity by over 70% at 10 μM, while HDN2398 only decreased the luciferase activity less than 10% and without significance (Fig. 2C), suggesting that HDN2398 may have no direct interaction with the virus NP or RdRp proteins. However, the results of the IFN-β reporter assay indicated that HDN2398 treatment significantly restored the levels of the IFN-β reporter, which was reduced by NS1, similar to the effect of the NS1 inhibitor ML303 (Fig. 2D). Thus, HDN2398 may block some IAV steps after adsorption through targeting NS1.

Since HDN2398 may inhibit IAV infection by targeting NS1, we further explored the direct interaction between HDN2398 and NS1 by conducting a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay23. The results indicated marked binding of HDN2398 to NS1 in a dose-dependent manner, with a KD equivalent to ~5.91 µM, indicating a high affinity of HDN2398 for NS1 (Fig. 2E). The binding of NS1 to ML303 (an NS1 inhibitor) was also confirmed by an SPR assay, with a KD value of 8.39 μM (Fig. 2F). However, there was nearly no binding of NA protein to compound HDN2398 (Supplementary Fig. S6C). In addition, a drug affinity responsive target stability assay (DARTS) was performed further to verify the interaction between HDN2398 and NS124. The results indicated that HDN2398 (25 µM) can markedly protect virus NS1 rather than NA protein from protease digestion especially under pronase treatment at 6 μg/ml (Fig. 2G), suggesting that NS1 may be a specific target of HDN2398. Thus, HDN2398 may inhibit IAV replication through direct interaction with NS1.

HDN2398 blocks the nuclear export of NS1 by interfering with its binding to nucleolin

To further explore the influence of HDN2398 on the functions of NS1, we first detected the localization of NS1 with or without drug treatment via an immunofluorescence assay. As shown in Fig. 3A, B, at 6 and 8 h p.i., the green fluorescence of NS1 was detected in both the cytoplasm and the nucleolus of non-treated PR8/H1N1 and Aichi/H3N2-infected cells (Fig. 3A, B). However, after HDN2398 (25 μM) treatment, the fluorescence of NS1 was largely observed in the cell nucleus but not in the nucleolus, indicating that HDN2398 may block the nuclear export and nucleolar localization of NS1 (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast, treatment with ML303 did not obviously change the cytoplasmic or nucleolar localization of NS1, which is quite different from the effect of HDN2398 (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Interestingly, HDN2398 treatment also significantly inhibited the nucleolar localization rather than the nuclear export of NS1 in NWS/H1N1-infected MDCK cells or Aichi/H3N2-infected A549 cells at 6 h and 8 h p.i. (Supplementary Fig. S7B, C). Thus, HDN2398 may directly interact with NS1 to change its intracellular localization in IAV-infected cells.

Furthermore, in the non-treated PR8-infected cells, the marked co-localization of NS1 with nucleolin protein was detected in the cell nucleus at 8 h p.i. (Fig. 3C). However, after HDN2398 treatment, the cytoplasmic localization of NS1 was inhibited, and there was nearly no co-localization signal in the nucleolus (Fig. 3D), verifying that HDN2398 can truly block the nucleolar localization and nuclear export of NS1. Besides that, HDN2398 did not affect the nucleolin staining and its nucleolar localization under uninfected conditions (Supplementary Fig. S7D). In addition, the NLS2 of NS1 was reported to be largely related to the nucleolar localization of NS1, so the influence of HDN2398 on the localization of NS1-NLS2 was determined by using NLS2-GFP chimeric proteins (Fig. 3E). The results indicated that NLS2-GFP of PR8/H1N1, Aichi/H3N2 and Vietnam/H5N1 exhibited significant nucleolar localization in untreated MDCK cells, whereas in HDN2398 (25 μM)-treated cells, the nucleolar localization of NLS2-GFP disappeared, and the green fluorescence was evenly distributed in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm (Fig. 3F). However, HDN2398 had nearly no influence on the localization of NLS1-GFP or NES-GFP (Fig. 3F). Moreover, the Co-IP assay revealed that HDN2398 (12.5, 25 μM) treatment significantly reduced the binding of nucleolin to NS1-Flag beads in a dose-dependent manner as compared to that in the non-treated control group (Fig. 3G, H). Besides that, the SPR assay indicated that HDN2398 (6.25–50 μM) treatment markedly decreased the binding of purified NS1 protein to nucleolin on CM5 chip in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3I). Thus, HDN2398 may block the nucleolar localization of NS1 by disrupting the interaction between NS1 and nucleolin.

HDN2398 affects the mRNA splicing and nuclear export by blocking the binding of NS1 to NS1-BP and NXF1

NS1 protein was reported to be involved in the nuclear export of IAV mRNA25, so the influence of HDN2398 on viral mRNA or vRNA export was then determined by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay26. The results showed that the red fluorescence of M1 vRNA located mainly in the cytoplasm at 6 h p.i. and LMB rather than HDN2398 can markedly reduce the nuclear export of M1 vRNA (Fig. 4A). In contrast, HDN2398 rather than LMB markedly reduced the green fluorescence of M1 mRNA in the cytoplasm at both 4 h and 6 h p.i., suggesting that HDN2398 may inhibit the nuclear export of viral mRNA rather than vRNA (Fig. 4A). Besides that, the quantitative RT-PCR assay indicated that treatment of HDN2398 rather than LMB significantly reduced the cytoplasmic distribution of viral NA and M1 mRNAs rather than their vRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S8), verifying that HDN2398 can truly hinder the nuclear export of viral mRNAs. Moreover, the results of Co-IP assay showed that HDN2398 treatment dose-dependently inhibits the interaction between NS1 and NXF1 rather than UAP56 or Aly (Fig. 4B–E), implicating that HDN2398 may block nuclear export of viral mRNA and NS1 protein through directly interfering with the interaction between NS1 and NXF1 rather than other transport factors.

NS1 is also reported to be associated with virus mRNA expression and splicing, so the influence of HDN2398 on mRNA splicing was explored via quantitative RT-PCR assay. In contrast, HDN2398 (12.5–50 µM) significantly reduced the expression level of M2 mRNA in PR8 virus-infected cells in a dose-dependent manner, and the relative percentage of M2/M mRNA also decreased significantly (Fig. 4F, G), suggesting that HDN2398 may interfere with the function of NS1 related to virus mRNA splicing. NS1-BP is a binding protein of NS1 which can improve the splicing of virus M1 mRNA3,11, so the influence of HDN2398 on the interaction between NS1 and NS1-BP was evaluated by using Co-IP assay. The results indicated that HDN2398 (12.5, 25 µM) dose-dependently reduced the binding of NS1-BP to NS1 in PR8 virus-infected cells (Fig. 4H, I), suggesting that HDN2398 can also block virus mRNA splicing via targeting NS1. Taken together, these findings suggest that HDN2398 may directly bind to NS1 to inhibit virus mRNA splicing and nuclear transport.

HDN2398 attenuates the interferon antagonistic function of NS1 by reducing the NS1-dsRNA interaction and the ubiquitination of RIG-I

NS1 has been reported to possess interferon antagonistic function, so we further explored whether HDN2398 can attenuate this function by using RT-PCR assay. As shown in Fig. 5A, poly[I:C] treatment significantly induced the expression of host IFN-β mRNA in non-infected cells, but PR8 infection did not significantly induce the production of IFN-β, verifying the antagonistic effect of NS1 on interferon. However, after treatment with HDN2398 (25 µM) or NS1 inhibitor ML303 (10 µM) for 4 h, the levels of IFN-β mRNA significantly increased compared with those in the non-treated control group, suggesting that HDN2398 may attenuate the antagonist effect of NS1 on interferon (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S9A). HDN2398 or ML303 treatment also reduced the expression levels of M2 mRNA (Fig. 5B). However, HDN2398 treatment alone did not induce the production of IFN-β (Supplementary Fig. S9B) and can’t affect the IFN-β production stimulated by poly(I:C) (Supplementary Fig. S9C) in non-infected MDCK cells, verifying that the enhancement effect of HDN2398 on interferon production is largely related to its inhibition of NS1 function. Moreover, an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was used to explore whether HDN2398 can block NS1-dsRNA interactions (Fig. 5C). The results showed that the NS1-dsRNA complex was significantly reduced by HDN2398 treatment in a dose-dependent manner, and the free RNA dose-dependently increased accordingly (Fig. 5D), suggesting that HDN2398 can block the NS1-dsRNA interaction to attenuate the antagonistic effects of NS1 on interferon production.

NS1 can bind directly to TRIM25 and prevents it from activating the cellular RNA sensor RIG-I, an important step in the activation of the IFN response27. Thus, we further evaluated the effects of HDN2398 on the interaction between NS1 and TRIM25 via Co-IP assay28. The results showed that HDN2398 (12.5–50 µM) treatment significantly reduced the amount of TRIM25 bound to the NS1 beads in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5E, F), suggesting that HDN2398 can block the binding of NS1 to TRIM25. Moreover, the HA-Ub-pCMV plasmid expression combined with pull-down and western blot assay were used to detect the ubiquitination levels of RIG-I. The results showed that, in PR8-infected cells, RIG-I reached its highest level of ubiquitination at 4 h p.i. and then gradually decreased from 4 h to 8 h p.i., indicating that NS1 may inhibit the ubiquitination of RIG-I beginning at 4 h p.i. (Fig. 5G). However, HDN2398 (25 μM) treatment significantly promoted the level of ubiquitinated RIG-I at 4 h and 8 h p.i. (Fig. 5H), and dose-dependently increased the levels of MAVS and phosphorylated IRF-3 at 4 h p.i. (Fig. 5I–K). Taken together, all these findings suggest that HDN2398 may also block the TRIM25-NS1 interaction to restore the activation of the RIG-I/MAVS signaling pathway.

HDN2398 inhibits NS1 oligomerization mainly through binding to the effector domain

To further examine the binding modes of HDN2398 at NS1, the molecular docking analysis was performed with full-length H6N6 NS1 (PDB ID: 4OPH) which has 92% sequence homology with H1N1 NS1 ED and 87% sequence homology with H5N1 NS1 ED. As shown in Fig. 6A, HDN2398 was predicted to occupy the pocket consisting of residues K108-K110, I117, M119, Q121, G183, G184, and W187, which would otherwise accommodate W187 from the other dimer upon oligomerization. Among them, W187 is located in the ED domain of NS1 and is required for NS1 dimerization and interaction with CPSF3012,29, suggesting that HDN2398 may largely bind to the ED domain to disrupt the ED-ED interface in the oligomer state. Notably, HDN2398 binding to the mutant W187G was predicted to maintain most of the important interactions but lost two hydrogen bonds, resulting in a slight twist of the clavatol fragments within the pocket (Fig. 6B).

We then estimated the binding affinity of HDN2398 using molecular mechanics generalized born surface area (MM-GBSA), a computationally efficient method more accurate than most docking scoring functions30. HDN2398 was predicted with a binding affinity of −66.7 kcal/mol, which is more favorable than the predicted binding affinity of the ED-ED interface (−55.3 kcal/mol), suggesting that in the presence of HDN2398, oligomerization via ED-ED interactions is likely disrupted (Fig. 6C–E). Besides that, we also modeled HDN2398 to the RBD using induced fit docking. As shown in Fig. 6F, the hydrophobic fluorine substitution on the indole ring is near the key residue F9, which may interact favorably with the hydrophobic F9 side chain (predicted binding affinity −51.5 kcal/mol). Nevertheless, the binding is weak compared to the RBD-RBD dimer interface (−122.2 kcal/mol calculated from MM-GBSA), which has a notably extensive interaction surface (Fig. 6G). In addition, the Co-IP assay showed that HDN2398 (25, 50 μM) can significantly reduce the binding of wide type NS1-myc to NS1-flag beads in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6H, I), verifying that HDN2398 can truly block the dimerization of NS1 which is required for binding to dsRNA and host factors. Thus, HDN2398 may mainly block NS1 oligomerization via disrupting the ED-ED interface of NS1.

Considering the highly conserved sequence in regions surrounding the key W187, and conformational similarities (average RMSD of 1.6 Å between the ED structures of H1N1 and H5N1) from structural alignments shown in Supplementary Fig. S10A, we extended our study to assess the potential of HDN2398 to inhibit the oligomerization of H5N1 NS1 ED, which has recently raised significant global health concerns. The binding pose of HDN2398 with H5N1 differed slightly from that with H1N1, with the indole ring forming pi-cation interactions with K105, the oxygen atoms of the clavatol fragment forming hydrogen bonds with K104, W182, and the backbone of Q109, as well as a pi-pi interaction with W182 (Supplementary Fig. S10B). Although docking score was not optimal, the binding affinity evaluated by MM-GBSA (−60.1 kcal/mol) suggested that HDN2398 may favorably occupy the pocket of the key W182 (five residues in the linker region of H5N1 NS1 were not defined31) to disrupt ED dimer interface upon oligomerization, possibly sharing the same inhibition mechanism as H1N1. In addition, the results of DARTS assay showed that HDN2398 markedly protected the H5N1 NS1 from protease digestion (Supplementary Fig. S10C), suggesting that HDN2398 may also have interaction with H5N1 NS1. Thus, HDN2398 may have broad-spectrum inhibition effects on NS1 protein.

Identification of the key binding sites of HDN2398 to the virus NS1 protein

Since the molecular docking indicated that HDN2398 might be able to bind to the W187 pocket in the ED and F9 in the RBD, we further identify the key binding sites of HDN2398 on NS1 by using the escape mutant screening method as described previously23. The results revealed that an obvious cytopathic effect (CPE) was not detected until the ninth passages (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. S11A), and the viral HA titer increased to ~100% of that in the virus control group after the eighth passage (Supplementary Fig. S11B, C), suggesting that the escape of mutant viruses may have occurred at the eighth and ninth passages. The wild-type virus (H1N1-WT) was still sensitive to HDN2398, with an IC50 value of ~3.3 µM, whereas the selected virus at the 9th passage (H1N1-ET) exhibited resistance to HDN2398, with an IC50 value of 22.1 µM (Fig. 7B). Sequence analysis revealed that the H1N1-ET virus has three nucleotide mutations, which produce three amino acid mutations (F9C, W187G, R211G) (Fig. 7B). Considering that Phe9 is located in the RBD of NS1, we speculate that F9 may be related to the inhibitory effect of HDN2398 on the NS1-dsRNA interaction. In addition, Trp187 and Arg211 are located in the ED domain and NLS sequence, which are necessary for NS1 dimerization and nucleolar localization. Therefore, we propose that F9, W187 and R211 may be potential binding sites for HDN2398 on the NS1 and are involved in the inhibition of HDN2398 on the NS1-host interaction and nucleolar localization.

To verify the key binding sites of HDN2398 to NS1, the recombinant wide-type (WT) and mutated NS1 (F9C or W187G) proteins were firstly expressed and purified, and then subjected to SPR assay, respectively. The results indicated that the binding affinity of HDN2398 to the F9C or W187G mutated NS1 proteins was markedly decreased as compared to the WT NS1 (Fig. 7C–E), demonstrating that the F9 and W187 of NS1 are truly required for HDN2398 binding. Consistently, the Co-IP assay showed that the HDN2398’s inhibition of NS1-myc binding to NS1-flag beads was diminished in the F9C or W187G mutants as compared to the WT NS1 (Supplementary Fig. S12), verifying that HDN2398 may inhibit NS1 dimerization via binding to F9 in the RBD and W187 in the ED. We next used a reverse genetics approach to generate recombinant PR8/H1N1 viruses with the WT or mutated NS1 proteins (F9C or W187G) (Fig. 7F). The inhibitory effects of HDN2398 on these recombinant viruses were subsequently evaluated via plaque assay (Fig. 7G), HA assay (Fig. 7H) and western blot (Supplementary Fig. S13A) at 24 h p.i. The results indicated that HDN2398 (6.25 or 12.5 µM) treatment significantly reduced the plaque titer in WT virus-infected cells but nearly had no inhibition effect on the multiplication of the mutant virus (W187G or F9C) (Fig. 7G). Similarly, the results of HA assay revealed that HDN2398 also significantly reduced the HA titer in WT virus-infected cells but only had a very few inhibition on the virus with mutated NS1 (W187G or F9C) (Fig. 7H). In addition, HDN2398 (12.5 μM) treatment significantly reduced the expression of NS1 proteins in WT virus-infected cells as compared to that in the non-treated virus control group (Supplementary Fig. S13A). However, HDN2398 hardly decreased the expression of NS1 in mutated (W187G or F9C) IAV-infected cells (Supplementary Fig. S13B). Besides that, HDN2398 can significantly improve the IFN-β production in the WT virus- rather than NS1 mutated virus-infected cells at 25 μM (Supplementary Fig. S13C), suggesting that the Phe9 and Trp187 of NS1 are required for HDN2398 binding so as to inhibit the interferon antagonist function of NS1.

Furthermore, we evaluated the inhibition effects of HDN2398 against these mutant viruses in mouse pneumonia model. As shown in Fig. 7I–K, after treatment of 5 mg/kg/day HDN2398, about 90% of the mice survived in wide-type PR8 virus-infected group on day 14, while only 10% of mice survival was observed in F9C or W187G mutant viruses infected groups. Additionally, HDN2398 can reduce the weight loss of mice infected with wide-type (WT) virus rather than the NS1 mutated viruses (F9C or W187G) before day 9 post infection (Fig. 7L–N). Besides that, HDN2398 significantly reduced the pulmonary virus titers and attenuated the pneumonia symptoms in the WT virus-infected group but failed to achieve similar effects in the F9C and W187G mutant virus-infected groups (Fig. 7O, P). Thus, both the W187G and F9C mutations attenuated the inhibition of NS1 by HDN2398 in vivo. Taken together, Trp187 or Phe9 may be required for the interaction between HDN2398 and NS1, and the binding pocket around these amino acids may be a promising target for anti-IAV drugs.

Discussion

Currently, natural products derived from microbial metabolites have attracted increasing interest in the development of potential antiviral drugs32,33. Herein, we demonstrate that the marine alkaloid HDN2398 possesses broad-spectrum anti-IAV activity with low toxicity. HDN2398 can directly bind to the viral NS1 protein to attenuate the interferon antagonist function of NS1 by blocking the NS1-dsRNA interaction and activating the RIG-I/MAVS pathway. HDN2398 can also inhibit the nucleolar localization and nuclear export of NS1 by destroying the interaction between NS1 and nucleolin or NXF1. Interestingly, HDN2398 possesses superior in vitro anti-IAV activity than oseltamivir under higher MOI (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Tables S1, S2). Importantly, oral therapy of HDN2398 significantly improved the survival of IAV-infected mice, suggesting that HDN2398 merits further investigation as a potential anti-IAV agent targeting NS1.

Our studies indicated that HDN2398 may block several steps (0–6 h p.i.) of the IAV life cycle after adsorption. However, HDN2398 did not obviously inhibit NA activity and block HA-mediated aggregation of red blood cells, suggesting that HDN2398 does not directly inhibit virus entry. Interestingly, the mini-genome and IFN-β reporter assays indicated that HDN2398 may directly target NS1 rather than NP or RNA polymerase (PA, PB1 or PB2) to block virus replication. Further studies revealed that HDN2398 may block the nuclear export of NS1 through direct interaction with NS1. HDN2398 also inhibited the nuclear export of virus mRNA rather than vRNA and reduced the binding of NS1 to NXF1, suggesting that the inhibition of HDN2398 on the export of both NS1 and virus mRNA is mostly due to its interaction with NXF1. Additional studies confirmed that HDN2398 can also block the direct binding of NS1 to nucleolin and reduce the co-localization of NS1 with nucleolin in IAV-infected cell nucleoli, suggesting that HDN2398 may inhibit the nucleolar localization of NS1 by blocking the NS1-nucleolin interaction. Moreover, the NLS2 sequence of NS1 may be targeted by HDN2398 to destroy the nucleolar localization of NS1. Besides that, HDN2398 can also inhibit virus mRNA splicing through interfering with the interaction between NS1 and NS1-BP. Therefore, all these findings suggest that HDN2398 may directly bind to NS1 to inhibit virus mRNA splicing and nuclear transport.

The NS1 protein of IAV is the key virulence factor affecting the pathogenicity and host adaptability of IAV9. NS1 possesses various functions, such as antagonizing interferon production, promoting viral mRNA splicing and export, and regulating cell apoptosis10–12. NS1 also binds directly to TRIM25 to prevent it from activating the cellular RNA sensor RIG-I, an important step in the activation of the IFN response27. Current reported NS1 antagonists mainly target the dsRNA- or CPSF30-binding sites of NS1 to inhibit IAV infection18,19. Kleinpeter et al. reported that compound A9 and A22 may attenuate IAV replication by inhibiting the interaction between CPSF30 and the ED domain of NS134. The flavonoid baicalin can inhibit the interferon antagonistic function of NS1 by binding to the RBD domain of NS119. Herein, we found that HDN2398 can dose-dependently reduce the formation of the NS1-dsRNA complex, suggesting that HDN2398 may block the NS1-dsRNA interaction to attenuate the interferon antagonistic effect of NS1. Interestingly, HDN2398 can also markedly block the binding of NS1 to TRIM25 and enhance the ubiquitination of RIG-I and the production of MAVS in IAV-infected cells (Fig. 5), suggesting that HDN2398 may block the TRIM25-NS1 interaction to restore the activation of the RIG-I/MAVS signaling pathway. Thus, HDN2398 may attenuate the inhibition of NS1 on interferon system via blocking the binding of NS1 to dsRNA or TRIM25.

NS1 contains two functional domains, namely, the N-terminal RNA-binding domain (RBD), which functions against the host immune response, and the C-terminal effector domain (ED), which interacts with host proteins to regulate virus replication and cell apoptosis11. The molecular docking analysis indicated that HDN2398 may occupy the pocket consisting of residues K108-K110, I117, M119, Q121, G183, G184, and W187, which would otherwise accommodate W187 from the other dimer upon oligomerization. Considered that W187 is mainly required for NS1 dimerization and interaction with CPSF30, we suppose that HDN2398 may largely bind to the ED domain via interaction with W187 of NS1. The subsequent MM-GBSA analysis indicated that addition of HDN2398 may competitively inhibit the ED-ED interaction to block NS1 dimerization. The Co-IP assay further verified that HDN2398 can truly block the dimerization of NS1 which is required for binding to dsRNA and host factors (Fig. 6). Besides that, the results of induced fit docking showed that HDN2398 may interact favorably with the hydrophobic F9 side chain but the binding is weaker than the RBD-RBD interaction. Therefore, HDN2398 may block NS1 oligomerization through binding to F9 in the RBD and W187 in the ED.

The escape mutant virus screening was often used to identify the potential binding sites of anti-viral drugs. In this study, the escape mutant viruses were selected and possessed three amino acid mutations (F9C, W187G, and R211G) (Fig. 7B). F9 is located in the RNA-binding domain (RBD) of NS1 and may be related to the inhibition of HDN2398 on the NS1-dsRNA interaction. In contrast, Trp187 and Arg211 are located in the ED domain and NLS sequence, which are necessary for NS1 dimerization and nucleolar localization35,36. The SPR assay indicated that HDN2398 had lower binding affinity to the F9C or W187G mutated NS1 as compared to the wide-type (WT) NS1. Consistently, the Co-IP assay showed that HDN2398’s inhibition of NS1 dimerization was diminished in the F9C or W187G mutants (Supplementary Fig. S12). Subsequent experiments using the recombinant virus with WT or mutated NS1 proteins (W187G or F9C) revealed that both the two point mutations (W187G or F9C) can largely attenuate the inhibition of NS1 by HDN2398 both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 7), suggesting that W187 or F9 may be required for the interaction between HDN2398 and NS1. Besides that, the binding affinity evaluated by MM-GBSA suggested that HDN2398 may favorably occupy the pocket of the key W182 of H5N1 NS1 (similar to W187 in H1N1 NS1) to disrupt ED dimer interface upon oligomerization, possibly sharing the same inhibition mechanism as H1N1. Taken together, HDN2398 may have broad-spectrum inhibition on the functions of NS1 via binding to the Trp187 or Phe9 of NS1.

In conclusion, the indole alkaloid HDN2398 derived from marine fungi significantly inhibits IAV infection both in vitro and in vivo and may block IAV infection by targeting virus NS1 protein. HDN2398 can inhibit NS1-mediated viral mRNA splicing and export, as well as restore the interferon system by destroying the NS1-host interaction. Thus, HDN2398 can block the multiple functions of NS1 to limit the infection of IAV efficiently (Fig. 8). Further studies on the antiviral effects of HDN2398 against highly pathogenic IAV strains (H5N1 or H7N9 strains) are needed to advance the use of HDN2398 for drug development. Nevertheless, our findings open the possibility of using marine indole alkaloids for the discovery of anti-IAV agents targeting NS1 in the future.

Methods

Reagents, cells, and viruses

Marine-derived indole alkaloid derivative HDN2398 and HDN3635 were obtained from the School of Medicine and Pharmacy, Ocean University of China. The structures of HDN2398 and HDN3635 were characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR) analysis (Supplementary Figs. S14, S15). MDCK cells were grown in RPM1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. 293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS, 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. A549 cells were grown in Ham’s F-12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. Influenza virus (A/Puerto Rico/8/34 [H1N1]; PR/8) was propagated in 9-day-old embryonated eggs for 3 days at 36.5 °C. Influenza H1N1 virus A/Virginia/ATCC1/2009 (Vir09) and H3N2 strain A/Aichi/2/1968 (Aichi) were propagated in MDCK cells at 37 °C for 3 days.

Cytopathic effect (CPE) inhibition assay

MDCK cells in 96-well plates were firstly infected with IAV (MOI = 0.1), and then treated with different compounds in triplicate after removal of the virus inoculum. After 48 h incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature (RT). After removal of the formaldehyde, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. The plates were then washed and dried followed by solubilization of the dye with methanol, and the intensity of crystal violet staining for each well was measured at 570 nm.

Hemagglutination (HA) assay

Standardized chicken red blood cell (cRBC) solutions were prepared according to the WHO manual. Virus propagation solutions were serially diluted 2-fold in round bottomed 96-well plate and 1% cRBCs were then added at an equal volume. After 60 min incubation at 4 °C, RBCs in negative wells sedimented and formed red buttons, whereas positive wells had an opaque appearance with no sedimentation. HA titers are given as hemagglutination units/mL (HAU/mL).

Time of addition study

MDCK cells were infected with PR8 virus (MOI = 1.0) under four different treatment conditions: (i) Pretreatment of virus: IAV was pretreated with 25 μM of HDN2398 at 37 °C for 1 h before infection. (ii) Pretreatment of cells: MDCK cells were pretreated with 25 μM of HDN2398 before infection. (iii) Adsorption: cells were infected in media containing 25 μM of HDN2398 and, after 1 h adsorption at 37 °C, were overlaid with compound-free media. (iv) After adsorption: After 1 h adsorption at 37 °C, the inoculum was removed and the infecting media containing 25 μM of HDN2398 were added to cells. At 24 h p.i., the antiviral activity was determined by HA assay. Mean percentage HA titers were calculated as a percentage of HA titers from untreated control group.

Plaque assay

Confluent cell monolayers in 6-well or 12-well plates were incubated with 10-fold serial dilutions of IAV at 37 °C for 1 h. After removal of the inoculum, cells were washed with PBS and overlaid with maintenance DMEM medium containing 1.5% agarose, 0.02% DEAE-dextran, 1 mM L-glutamine, 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin and 1 µg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin. After incubation for 3 days at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, cells were fixed with 0.05% glutaraldehyde, followed by staining with 1% crystal violet in 20% ethanol for plaque counting.

Mini-genome assay

HEK293T cells were cultured in 24 well plates at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, cells were transfected with plasmids encoding the proteins of influenza A/PR8/34 (H1N1): PB1/pcDNA3.1, PB2/pcDNA3.1, PA/pcDNA3.1 and NP/pcDNA3.1, which control viral genome replication and transcription, and the luciferase RNA expression vector pHH21-vNS-Luc. After transfection for 16 h, the cells were treated (or not) with the test compounds, and the luciferase activity was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions after incubation at 37 °C for 48 h.

Interferon promoter-based luciferase reporter assay

293T cells were transfected with 0.2 μg of p125Luc and 0.8 μg of pcDNA3.1-NS1 (PR8) using Polyfect transfection reagent (Invitrogen Lipofectamine 2000). At 16 h post-transfection, the cells were stimulated with 50 μg of poly[I:C] (Sigma-Aldrich)/ml and treated with 50 μM drugs for 24 h. The cells were lysed with 1 × passive lysis buffer (Promega), and 20 μl of the lysate was used to measure luciferase activity using the luciferase assay system (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All transfection experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

SPR assays were conducted on a SPR biosensor instrument GE BiacoreT200 (GE, USA). H1N1/PR8 NS1 proteins (Sino Biological Inc., Beijing, China) were firstly immobilized onto the surface of a carboxymethylated dextran sensor chip (CM5) via amino group coupling. To assess real-time binding of compounds to NS1 on CM5 chips, HDN2398 or ML303 with different concentrations dissolved in DMSO, was injected over the sensor chip surface with NS1 immobilized within 2 min, followed by a 10-min wash with 1 × PBST buffer. The sensor chip surface was then regenerated by washing with NaOH (2 mM) for 30 s. All binding experiments were carried out at 25 °C with a constant flow rate of 2 μl/s PBS buffer. To correct for non-specific binding and bulk refractive index change, a blank channel without NS1 was used and run simultaneously for each experiment. Then, the SPR evaluation software was used to calculate the kinetic parameters, and the changes in mass due to the binding response were recorded as resonance units (RUs).

Immunofluorescence assay

After drug treatment, IAV-infected MDCK cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min, and permeabilized using 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min before incubated with 2% BSA/PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were then incubated with anti-NS1 or anti-nucleolin antibody (Sant Cruz, USA) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the cells were incubated with FITC or Cy5 conjugated secondary antibodies (Boster, Wuhan, China) for 50 min at 37 °C. Nuclear DNA was labeled with 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma-Aldrich, Poland, USA). Finally, cells were washed and observed using confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 510, Jena, Germany).

RT-PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from IAV infected MDCK cells using an RNAiso™ Plus Kit (Takara, Japan), and analyzed by using the One Step RT-PCR Kit (Takara, Japan). The RT-PCR assay was performed using the following primers: NS1 mRNA, 5’-CTTCGCCGAGATCAGAAATC-3’ and 5’-TGGACCATTCCCTTGACATT-3’; M2 mRNA, 5’-ATGATCTTCTTGAAAATTTGC-3’ and 5’-CTCCAGCTCTATGCTGAC-3’; M1 mRNA, 5’-TGACAACAACCAACCCACTA-3’ and 5’-CTCTGCTGCTTGCTCACTCG-3’; M1 vRNA, 5’-AGATGAGTCTTCTAACCGAGGTC-3’ and 5’-GACCTCGGTTAGAAGACTCATCT-3’; NA mRNA, 5’-ATCACACTCAACAGACCAATGG-3’ and 5’-GAATCTTGACC AACAGTCGCAA-3’; NA vRNA, 5’-TTGCGACTGTTGGTCAAGATTC-3’ and 5’-CCATTGGTCTGTTGAGTGTGAT-3’; IFN-β mRNA, 5’-CCAGTTCCAGAAGGAGGACA-3’ and 5’-CCTGTTGTCCCAGGTGAAGT-3’; β-actin mRNA, 5’-GGCATCCTGACCCTGAAGTA-3’ and 5’-GGGGTGTTGAAAGTCTCGAA-3’. The RT-PCR assay was performed at 42 °C 5 min, 95 °C 60 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C 5 s, 60 °C 34 s, 72 °C 60 s, 4 °C 10 min, followed by agarose gel electrophoresis analysis. All reactions were performed in triplicate and the results were normalized to β-actin. In addition, the real-time quantitative RT-PCR was performed at 42 °C 5 min, 95 °C 10 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C 5 s, 60 °C 34 s, followed by melting curve analysis, according to the instrument documentation (ABI PRISM 7500, Applied Biosystems, USA). All reactions were performed in triplicate and the results were normalized to β-actin. The relative amounts of these mRNAs or vRNAs were determined using the comparative (2−ΔΔCT) method.

Western blot assay

After drug treatment, the cell lysate was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane. After being blocked in TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (v/v) and 5% BSA (w/v) at RT for 2 h, the membranes were rinsed and incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies against virus NS1, NA proteins (Santa Cruz, USA), cellular ubiquitinated RIG-I, RIG-I, MAVS, β-actin or GAPDH proteins (CST, Danvers, USA) as control. The membranes were washed and incubated with AP-labeled secondary antibody (1:2000 dilutions) at RT for 2 h. The protein bands were then visualized by incubating with the developing solution (p-nitro blue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) and 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate toluidine (BCIP)) at RT for 30 min. The relative densities of proteins were all determined by using ImageJ (NIH) v.1.33 u (USA).

Drug affinity responsive target stability assay (DARTS)

For DARTS assay, IAV (MOI = 3.0) infected MDCK cells were lysed with NP-40 cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, Nantong, China) and treated with or without HDN2398 (25 μM) for 30 min at room temperature (RT) followed by digested with Pronase (1.5, 3.0, 6.0 μg/ml) in reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM CaCl2) for 10 min at RT. The digestion was stopped by addition with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer and inactivation by boiling. Protein samples were separated with 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and analyzed by western blot with antibodies against IAV NS1 or NA proteins or cellular actin protein as control.

FISH analysis

RNA FISH was performed in IAV infected MDCK cells by using probes targeting mRNA or vRNA of virus M1 protein. The probes targeting the M1 mRNA (5’-FAM-GCGACATGTCTGATTAGTGGGTTGGTTGTTGTCGC-Dabcyl-3’) or vRNA segment (5’-Cy5-GCGACAACAACCAACCCACTAATCAGACATGTCGC-BHQ-3’) were synthesized from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Briefly, MDCK cells were infected with PR8 virus (MOI = 3.0) in the absence or presence of HDN2398 (25 μM) or LMB (10 ng/mL), and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 h or 6 h post infection (p.i.). After washing twice with 1× PBS, cells were permeabilized using 0.5% (v/v) Triton-x-100 for 5 min followed by one PBS wash. Then cells were dehydrated in 75% ethanol overnight at 4 °C. Next, the samples were rehydrated using 2× SSC, 50% formamide for 5 min and then incubated with the hybridization mixture containing 2× SSC, 1 mg/ml of tRNA, 10% dextran sulfate, 50% formamide, 0.01% BSA, 20 U/ml ribonuclease inhibitor, 2 mM vanadyl ribonucleoside complexes, and 500 nM of labeled probes. Hybridization was performed in a humidified chamber maintained at 37 °C for 16 h. Then cells were washed two times for 20 min with 2× SSC, 50% formamide and one time with 0.5× SSC for 10 min at RT. Then the cells were washed with PBS before observed under a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

To evaluate the effect of HDN2398 on the dsRNA binding function of NS1, the purified recombinant PR8 NS1 proteins were incubated with HDN2398 at 25 °C for 30 min, then a 35-nucleotide dsRNA oligomer (Sense: CGAGCUCGCCCGGGGAUCCUCUAGAGUCGACCUGC; Anti-sense: CUGCAGGUCGACUCUAGAGGAUCCCCGGGCGAGCU) with 5’-end biotin modification and 2’-OH methylation was added and incubated for an additional 30 min. The final concentration of small RNA oligomer was 4 μM and the molar ratio of NS1/RNA was maintained at 2:1. After incubation, the samples were mixed with EMSA/gel-shift loading buffer (Beyotime, China) and separated by nondenaturing 6.6% Bis-Tris NuPAGE gel (Invitrogen, USA). Then the protein samples were transferred to the nitrocellulose membrane in pre-cooled 0.5× TBE before incubated with the streptavidin linked Alkaline Phosphatase at room temperature for 45 min. Finally, the dsRNA oligomer or dsRNA-NS1 complex were visualized by using the BCIP/NBT Color Development Kit (Beyotime, China).

Pull-down assay

For analysis of the interaction between NS1 and cellular transport factors, the Flag magnetic beads coupled to NS1-Flag protein were incubated with MDCK cell lysate in the presence or absence of HDN2398 (25 μM). After incubation at RT for 2 h, the NS1-Flag beads were washed five times with wash buffer and the bound proteins were separated in 10% SDS-PAGE gels. Binding of host factors to NS1-Flag beads was detected by western blotting with anti-nucleolin, NXF1 or CRM1 antibodies (Cell signaling, USA).

For analysis of the interaction between NS1 and TRIM25 or splicing factors, the His beads were first incubated with NS1-His protein at RT for 2 h before washing with PBST for three times. The purified NS1-His proteins were then incubated with the MDCK cell lysate in the presence or absence of HDN2398 (12.5, 25, 50 µM). After incubation at RT for another 2 h, the incubated beads were washed three times and then subjected to 10% SDS-PAGE. Binding of host factors to the NS1-His beads was detected by western blotting using the anti-TRIM25, NXF1, UAP56, or Aly antibodies (Cell signaling, USA), respectively. The relative densities of proteins were all determined by using ImageJ (NIH) v.1.33 u (USA).

Serial passage experiments and resistant mutation identification

PR8 virus (MOI = 0.1) infected MDCK cells were treated with HDN2398 (10 μM) during 0–2 h p.i. at 37 °C before replacement with compound-free media. Then at the end point of each passage, the viruses that developed a significant 50% CPE were harvested and subjected to the following passages. The virus titers of IAV at each passage were determined by HA assay. The relative yield of progeny virus by HA assay at each passage was calculated as the percentage of HA titers in HDN2398-treated cultures compared to the untreated virus control group. The viral RNA was isolated from the plaque-purified viruses at 9th passage using an RNAiso™ Plus Kit (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RT-PCR was carried out with the High Fidelity RT-PCR Kit using primer pairs: NS1-Forward: 5’-ATGGATCCAAACACTGTGTCAAGC-3’; NS1-Reverse: 5’-TCAAACTTCTGACCTAATTGTTCC-3’. PCR products were identified by gel electrophoresis and the purified DNA was sequenced by Sangon (Shanghai, China).

Mice experiments

Four-week-old female BALB/c mice (average weight, 14.0 ± 2.0 g) were housed and studied under protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Ocean University of China. Mice were inoculated intranasal with PR8 (500 PFU/mouse) diluted in 40 μL of 1× PBS, and randomly divided into experimental groups. Four hours after inoculation, mice received oral therapy of HDN2398 (2.5 or 5 mg/kg/day), Oseltamivir (10 mg/kg/day), Baloxavir (2.5 mg/kg/day), or placebo, and the treatments were repeated once daily for five days. Mice were weighed and euthanized on Day 3 by spinal dislocation, and the lungs were removed and weighed. The lungs were homogenized in 1× PBS for determination of viral titers by plaque assay. Histopathological analysis was also performed on hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) stained lung tissues.

In the survival experiments, 10 mice per group were intranasal infected with PR/8 virus (1000 PFU/mouse) at Day 0. Oseltamivir (10 mg/kg/day) was used as positive control drug. The drug administration was repeated once daily for five days. Mice were monitored daily for weight loss and clinical signs. If a mouse lost body weight over 25% of its pre-infection weight, it was defined as dead and humanely euthanized immediately; the rest of the mice were sacrificed at the end of experiment on 14 dpi.

Ligand docking and MM-GBSA calculation

Structural models of H1N1 NS1 ED, H5N1 NS1 ED and H1N1 NS1 RBD were generated using homology modeling of Schrödinger Release 2024-3 (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024). For ED homology models, the crystal structure of full-length H6N6 NS1 (PDB ID: 4OPH) which has 92% sequence homology with H1N1 NS1 ED and 87% sequence homology with H5N1 NS1 ED, was used as the template. For the RBD homology model, the crystal structure of NS1 RBD from H1N1 influenza A/California/07/2009 (PDB ID: 3M8A), which has 85% sequence homology with H1N1 NS1 RBD, was used as the template. The generated homology models were then prepared for molecular modeling using the Protein Preparation Workflow, during which H-bond assignments were optimized using PROPKA for pH 7.4. The structures were then minimized, converging heavy atoms to RMSD value of 0.3 Å. The mutant H1N1 W187G structure was constructed by in silico mutation using the Build tool and then prepared following same preparation process.

HDN2398 was prepared using LigPrep of Schrödinger Release 2024-3 (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024) and tautomers were generated using Schrödinger’s Epik with a target pH of 7.4 ± 2.0. Receptor grids for H1N1 NS1 ED and H5N1 NS1 ED were generated using the homology models generated above, with the center of the receptor grid defined as the centroid of residues K110, V180 and W187 (H1N1 numbering). HDN2398 was docked to the ED using Glide XP precision with default docking parameters. To model HDN2398 to H1N1 NS1 RBD, induced fit docking was employed (Schrödinger Release 2024-3, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024). The homology model for H1N1 NS1 RBD generated above was used as the receptor, with the center of the receptor grid defined as the centroid of residues T5, C13, R46, L50. During initial Glide docking stage, the side chain of R46 was trimmed to give access to the binding site near F9. The side chain conformation of R46 was optimized during Prime refinement stage of the induced fit docking procedure. In addition, all residue side chains within 5 Å if the docked ligand were optimized. The ligand is then re-docked into the refined receptor conformation using Glide XP precision. MM-GBSA calculations were conducted with flexibility allowed for protein side chains within 5 Å of HDN2398. Images were generated using the PyMOL molecular graphics system (Version 2.5, Schrödinger, LLC).

The crystal structure of the H1N1 ED-ED dimer was downloaded (PDB ID: 3O9S), the H1N1 RBD-RBD dimer was generated by homology model. Then they were prepared following the same procedure described above. The dimer was then separated, and the monomers were used as receptor and ligand to calculate binding affinity using MM-GBSA, with no flexible protein allowed.

Statistical analysis

Randomization was used to assign samples to the experimental groups and treatment conditions for all in vivo studies. Data collection and acquisition of all in vitro and in vivo experimental paradigms were performed in a blinded manner. All data are representative of at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance was calculated by GraphPad Prism 7.0 (San Diego, CA, USA) using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or unpaired, two-tailed t-test. The differences in mouse survival rates were compared using a log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Statistical significance was considered to be P < 0.05.
