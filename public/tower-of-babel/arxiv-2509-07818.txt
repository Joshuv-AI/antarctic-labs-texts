On fixed points of pseudo-Anosov maps
Tarik Aougab, David Futer, Samuel J. Taylor

Abstract
. We give a formula to estimate the number of fixed points of a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism of a surface. When the homeomorphism satisfies a mild property called strong irreducibility , the log of the number of fixed points is coarsely equal to the Teichmüller translation length. We also discuss several applications, including an inequality relating the hyperbolic volume of a mapping torus to the rank of its Heegaard Floer homology.



1.
Introduction

Let
S
be an orientable finite-type surface with
\chi(S)<0
, and let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism of
S
. In this paper, we address the question of how combinatorial and geometric data associated to
f
determines its number of fixed points.

We begin by stating a simplified version of our main theorem in the presence of a mild condition on
f
, before giving some additional context to the problem. This is followed by our general result and applications to a question of Wright and to invariants of knots and
3
–manifolds.

Main results

Say a homeomorphism
f\colon S\to S
is
strongly irreducible
if for every essential simple curve
\alpha
on
S
, the curves
\alpha
and
f(\alpha)
intersect essentially. (The terminology originated in
[
44
]
.) For a surface of sufficient complexity, this means the displacement of
f
on the curve graph of
S
is at least
2
. Note that this condition depends only on the isotopy class of
f
.
We denote the set of fixed points of
f
by
\operatorname{Fix}(f)
and the Teichmüller translation length of
f
by
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)
. To simplify notation, we also declare
\log(0)=0
.

For strongly irreducible maps, our main theorem is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
f\colon S\to S
is a strongly irreducible pseudo-Anosov, then

\log\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)\asymp\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f).

The notation
a\asymp b
means that the two quantities are equal up to additive and multiplicative error depending only on
S
. See
Section

2.1
for more details.

Theorem

1.1
fits into the following context. First, if we denote the stretch factor of
f
by
\lambda
and note that
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)=\log\lambda
, then a theorem of Thurston
[
46
]
says that
\#{\rm Fix}(f^{n})\sim\lambda^{n}
. So the novelty of the theorem is that it counts fixed points of
f
rather than fixed points of sufficiently large powers. Second,
Theorem

1.1
asserts an upper and a lower bound on
\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)
. The upper bound is not new; indeed, the stronger bound
\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)\prec\lambda=\exp(\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f))
follows by considering a bounded-size Markov partition of
f
. See
Remark

7.7
for details. So the real content of
Theorem

1.1
is the lower bound on
\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)
, which is entirely new.

It is also classically known that a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism
f
minimizes the number of fixed points among all maps homotopic to
f
.
(See Thurston
[
46
]
, as well as Birman and Kidwell
[
6
]
.) Thus, if
f
is a strongly irreducible pseudo-Anosov,
Theorem

1.1

produces a lower bound on
\#\operatorname{Fix}(f^{\prime})
for any
f^{\prime}\sim f
. We may thus write

\#\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi)=\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)
for the mapping class
\varphi=[f]
of a pseudo-Anosov
f
.

Finally, we note that the conclusion of
Theorem

1.1
is false without the hypothesis that
f
is strongly irreducible. Indeed, Los produces infinitely many pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms on the same surface without nonsingular fixed points
[
25
]
. The following proposition is a mild improvement using a top-level construction.

Proposition 1.2
.

For
S
a closed surface of sufficiently large genus
g
, there are fixed-point-free pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms
f_{n}\colon S\to S
such that
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

One could also produce examples where the
f_{n}
have orientable foliations (as in Los) or have the property that
\ell_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{n})\to\infty
, where
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(S)
is the pants graph.
The point is that given any pseudo-Anosov
f
that sends a curve to a disjoint curve, one can compose
f
with Dehn twists about that curve to increase Teichmüller translation length without increasing the number of fixed points. Also, one can produce examples without fixed points using an easy construction within a fibered cone.
See
Section

9
for details.

Recall that every mapping class
\varphi
acts on the complex of curves
\mathcal{C}(S)
, with stable translation length
\ell_{\mathcal{C}(S)}(\varphi)
.
See (
2.2
) for the precise definition.

Theorem

1.1
has the following corollary:

Corollary 1.3
.

For any mapping class
\varphi
,
we have

\ell_{\mathcal{C}(S)}(\varphi)\prec\log\#\operatorname{Fix}(\varphi).

Proof.

To prove the claimed coarse inequality, it suffices to assume
\ell_{\mathcal{C}(S)}(\varphi)\geq 2
. This implies
\varphi=[f]
is pseudo-Anosov, as reducible and periodic mapping classes have stable translation length
\ell_{\mathcal{C}(S)}(\varphi)=0
. It also implies
f
is strongly irreducible.
Since the systole map
\mathcal{T}(S)\to\mathcal{C}(S)
is coarsely Lipschitz
[
26
]
, the result follows from
Theorem

1.1
.
∎

When
f
is not strongly irreducible, we still obtain a coarse formula for its number of fixed points, but this is more involved. The formula is in terms of
certain subsurface distances between the invariant laminations
\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-}
of
f
(see
Section

2.3
for definitions).

We say that a subsurface
Y\subset S
is
overlapped by
f
if
Y
and
f(Y)
intersect essentially. In particular, the full surface
S
is overlapped by every
f
.
We define
\mathcal{D}_{f}
to be the set of (isotopy classes of) overlapped essential subsurfaces of
S
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism.
Then

\log\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)\asymp\ell_{\mathcal{AC}(S)}(f)+\sum_{\langle f\rangle Y\subset\mathcal{D}_{f}\smallsetminus\{S\}}[d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})]+\sum_{\langle f\rangle A\subset\mathcal{D}_{f}}[\log d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})],

where the first sum is over
f
-orbits of proper non-annular subsurfaces and the second is over
f
-orbits of annuli.

See
Definition

2.1
for background on sums with cutoffs, such as the two sums appearing in the above statement.

Of course, when
f
is strongly irreducible, every subsurface is overlapped. The way in which
Theorem

1.4
reduces to
Theorem

1.1
uses a new combinatorial formula for Teichmüller space translation length
that we prove in
Section

6
.

Fixed points and surface subgroups of mapping class groups

Besides being of intrinsic interest, the search for estimates on fixed points of pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms is motivated by the following question
of Alex Wright:

Question 1.5

(Wright; cf Question 5 of
[
19
]
)
.

Does there exists a fixed-point-free homeomorphism
f\colon S\to S
of a closed, hyperbolic surface, with the property that every essential simple closed curve
\alpha
on
S
fills with its image
f(\alpha)
?

Futer
[
14
]
, and independently Wright, observed that any map
f
as in the question must belong to a pseudo-Anosov mapping class. By Thurston’s theorem
[
6
]
, the pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism isotopic to
f
must itself have no fixed points.
Hence, we can assume that
f
is itself pseudo-Anosov.

Wright showed that if such a pseudo-Anosov
f\colon S\to S
exists, then the map
S\hookrightarrow S\times S\smallsetminus\Delta
given by
x\mapsto(x,f(x))
induces an embedding from
\pi_{1}(S)
into
\pi_{1}(S\times S\smallsetminus\Delta)
, a surface braid group, with purely pseudo–Anosov image.
See
[
19
, Lemma 9]
for a proof. Subsequently, Kent and Leininger showed that the image of
\pi_{1}(S)
must be distorted in the surface braid group
[
19
, Lemma 13]
.

Theorem

1.1
and
Corollary

1.3
illustrate the tension between the hypotheses of
1.5
.
Furthermore, one consequence of
Theorem

1.1
is that on a fixed surface
S
there are at most finitely many conjugacy classes of pseudo-Anosov maps satisfying
1.5
. Indeed, any
f
satisfying Wright’s question would have to be strongly irreducible, hence
Theorem

1.1
provides an upper bound on the translation length
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)
. (The reduction to finitely many conjugacy classes also follows from a theorem of Bowditch
[
7
]
.) We remark that an
effective
version of

Theorem

1.1
would imply an explicit upper bound on
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)
,
hence an explicit bound on the number of tetrahedra in the veering triangulation of the mapping torus
M_{f}

[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
.
This would make it
theoretically feasible to find all candidate mapping classes for any given surface
S
.

In very recent work, Kent and Leininger constructed the first examples of purely pseudo-Anosov surface subgroups of mapping class groups
[
19
]
using a related approach. However, Wright’s
1.5
is still open.

Connections between
3
–manifold invariants

Every homeomorphism
f\colon S\to S
defines a
mapping torus
, namely the
3
–manifold

M_{f}=\,{\raisebox{1.99997pt}{$S\times[0,1]$}\!\!\left/\raisebox{-1.99997pt}{\!$(x,1)\sim(f(x),0)$}\right.}.

It is well-known that
M_{f}
depends only on the mapping class
\varphi=[f]\in\mathrm{Mod}(S)
, hence we may write
M_{\varphi}
. By a theorem of Thurston (see Otal
[
33
]
),
M_{\varphi}
admits a hyperbolic metric if and only if
\varphi
is a pseudo-Anosov class.

The most natural geometric invariant of
M_{f}
is its hyperbolic volume, denoted
\vol(M_{f})
. There is now an extensive literature relating
\vol(M_{f})
to the dynamical properties of the map
f
. Notably, Brock
[
9
]
gave a two-sided estimate on
\vol(M_{f})
in terms of the
pants graph translation length
\ell_{\mathcal{P}}(f)
and the Weil–Petersson translation length
\ell_{WP}(f)
. Kojima and McShane
[
20
]
gave an explicit upper bound on
\vol(M_{f})
in terms of the Teichmüller translation length
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)
:

(1.1)

\vol(M_{f})\leq 3\pi|\chi(S)|\cdot\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f).

In very recent work, Liu
[
24
]
proved an upper bound on
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)
in terms of
\vol(M_{f})
and the length of the shortest closed geodesic in
M_{f}
, which is independent of the topology of
S
.

From a very different perspective, Heegaard Floer theory is a powerful package of homological invariants associated to closed oriented
3
–manifolds and knots in
3
–manifolds. To a closed oriented
3
–manifold
Y
, the theory associates a finitely generated abelian group
\widehat{HF}(Y)
that splits as a direct sum over classes in
H^{2}(Y)
. (There are also more refined invariants
HF^{+}(Y)
,
HF^{-}(Y)
, and
HF^{\infty}(Y)
, which are beyond the scope of our discussion.) To a null-homologous knot
K\subset Y
, it associates a finitely generated abelian group
\widehat{HFK}(Y,K)
that splits as a direct sum over gradings valued in
\mathbb{Z}
. See Ozsváth and Szabó
[
36
,
35
,
34
]
and Rasmussen
[
43
]
for the original papers defining these invariants, and
[
37
]
for an excellent survey.

While hyperbolic geometry and Heegaard Floer homology have both had immense applications to the study of
3
–manifolds (and especially to Dehn surgery problems), there are very few known connections between the two sets of invariants. Given that the two theories have reached maturity, Lin and Lipnowski asked

Question 1.6

(Lin–Lipnowski
[
23
]
)
.

For a hyperbolic three-manifold
Y
, is there any relationship between the topological
invariants arising from the hyperbolic geometry of
Y
(e.g. the volume, injectivity radius,
lengths of geodesics, etc.) and the invariants arising from Floer homology?

One promising avenue for building a bridge between hyperbolic and Floer-theoretic invariants comes from recently discovered connections between the Heegaard Floer invariants of a mapping torus and the fixed points of its monodromy. The connection originated in the work of Cotton-Clay on symplectic Floer homology
[
12
]
.
Very recently, Liu
[
24
]
used the work of Cotton-Clay and the relationship between several different Floer theories to show that if
S
is a closed surface surface of genus
g(S)\geq 3
and
f\colon S\to S
is a pseudo-Anosov map,

(1.2)

\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)\leq 2\cdot\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{HF}(M_{f}).

See
[
24
, Proposition 4.3]
and
[
24
, computation on page 29]
.

For knots in
3
–manifolds, the connection between Heegaard Floer invariants and fixed points is even more precise.
Indeed, if
K\subset Y
is a hyperbolic knot with Seifert surface
S
of genus
g
, and
M=Y\smallsetminus K
fibers over
S^{1}
with fiber
S
and monodromy
f
, Ni
[
32
,
31
]
and Ghiggini and Spano
[
15
]
showed that

(1.3)

\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)+1=\text{rank}\widehat{HFK}(Y,K;g(S)-1).

This result also uses Cotton-Clay
[
12
]
in an essential way.

Combining the above results with
Theorem

1.1
gives a partial answer to
1.6
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
S
be a closed surface of genus
g(S)\geq 3
. Let
f\colon S\to S
be a strongly irreducible pseudo-Anosov. Then the mapping torus
M_{f}
satisfies

\vol(M_{f})\prec\log\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{HF}(M_{f}),

where the coarse constants depend only on the genus
g(S)
.

Proof.

We compute as follows:

\displaystyle\vol(M_{f})

\displaystyle\leq 3\pi|\chi(S)|\cdot\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)

\displaystyle\prec\log\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\log\text{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{HF}(M_{f})+\log 2.

The fist inequality is (
1.1
), the second is
Theorem

1.1
, and the third is (
1.2
).
∎

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
Y
be a closed orientable
3
–manifold. Let
K\subset Y
be a knot whose complement
M=Y\smallsetminus K
is hyperbolic and fibered over
S^{1}
, with fiber a Seifert surface
S
and strongly irreducible monodromy
f
. Then

\vol(Y\smallsetminus K)\prec\log\operatorname{rank}\widehat{HFK}(Y,K),

where the coarse constants depend only on the genus
g(S)
.

Proof.

This is identical to the proof of
Corollary

1.7
, using (
1.3
) in place of (
1.2
).
∎

Remark 1.9

(Weakening strong irreducibility)
.

By using
Theorem

1.4
in place of
Theorem

1.1
, one can obtain many variations of
Corollaries

1.7
and
1.8
. For example, for both corollaries it suffices to assume that
f
satisfies the weaker property that it overlaps all subsurfaces that are
not
annuli or pants. In this case,
Theorem

1.4
implies that

\log\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)\>\succ\>\ell_{\mathcal{AC}(S)}(f)+\sum_{\langle f\rangle Y}[d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})]\>\asymp\>\ell_{\mathcal{P}}(f).

Here, the sum ranges over all
f
–orbits of non-annular subsurfaces, and
\ell_{\mathcal{P}}(f)
is the translation length of
f
in the pants graph. The coarse equality between the sum and
\ell_{\mathcal{P}}(f)
is proved by
essentially the same argument as
Proposition

6.1
.
Since
\ell_{\mathcal{P}}(f)\asymp\vol(M_{f})
by a result of Brock
[
9
]
, the conclusion of the corollaries follows.

Proof strategy and organization

The central objects in the proof of
Theorems

1.1
and
1.4
are veering triangulations, singular flat metrics on surfaces, and distance formulas involving subsurface projections. All of these topics are reviewed in
Section

2
. At a very top level, estimates on fixed points come from intersections between a veering edge and its image; intersection numbers can be estimated in terms of subsurface distances; and translation lengths in Teichmüller space can likewise be estimated in terms of subsurface distances.

We work in a singular flat metric on
S
whose horizontal and vertical foliations are the stable/unstable foliations of the map
f
. In such a metric (given by a quadratic differential
q
), a special role is played by the saddle connections that span singularity-free rectangles with sides along the horizontal and vertical leaves. A key observation (recorded as the Fundamental Lemma in
Section

3
) is that for every singularity-free saddle connection
\sigma
, every intersection point in
\sigma\cap f(\sigma)
gives rise to a fixed point of
f
. The correspondence between intersection points and fixed points is not quite one-to-one, but the degree of overcounting can be controlled.

Singularity-free saddle connections also arise as edges in the
veering triangulation
; see
Figure

1
. Letting
\mathring{S}
denote the complement of the singularities of
S
, this is an ideal triangulation of
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
that enjoys very strong geometric and combinatorial properties. By a theorem of Minsky and Taylor
[
29
, Theorem 1.4]
, veering edges form a totally geodesic subset of
\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{S})
. In
Section

4
, we review and build on their work
[
29
,
30
]
to show that veering triangulations are also nearly geodesic in every essential subsurface of
\mathring{S}
. This allows us to use sections of the veering triangulation to estimate the distances between the stable and unstable laminations
\lambda^{\pm}
in every essential surface (
Section

5
).

As mentioned above, there are combinatorial estimates for distance in
\mathcal{T}(S)
and for intersection numbers between triangulations that package together a sum of distances in subsurfaces. We review these distance formulas (due to Rafi
[
41
]
and Choi–Rafi
[
10
]
) in
Section

6
, while adapting Rafi’s formula to give an estimate for the translation length
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(S)
.

In
Section

7
, we assemble the above ingredients to prove
Theorem

1.1
. In
Section

8
, we prove
Theorem

1.4
, which requires all of the above ingredients in addition to some technical results from Vokes
[
47
]
and Kopreski
[
21
]
. In
Section

9
, we discuss examples and prove
Proposition

1.2
.

2.
Background

Here we collect some basic background, mostly related to tools from surface topology and the mapping class group. The standard reference for most of this material is Masur and Minsky
[
27
]
.

2.1.
Coarse equalities, distance formulas, and cutoffs

Fix a surface
S
.
For quantities
a
and
b
and a constant
C\geq 1
, we write
a\prec_{C}b
to mean “
a\leq C\cdot b+C
”. The equivalence relation
a\asymp_{C}b
means “
a\prec_{C}b
and
b\prec_{C}a
”.
As a common shorthand, we will write
a\asymp b
to mean that the implicit constant
C
depends only on the surface
S
, whereas
a,b
depend on other parameters.

For a constant
K\geq 0
, the cutoff function
[\cdot]_{K}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined so that

[a]_{K}=\begin{cases}a&a\geq K,\\
0&a<K.\end{cases}

Cutoffs often appear in distance formulas, which are of the form

A\asymp_{C}\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}[a_{Y}]_{K}

where
a_{Y}
is a nonnegative quantity associated to some (equivalence class of) essential subsurfaces
Y\subset S
drawn from a collection
\mathcal{Y}
. A paradigmatic example is Masur and Minsky’s distance formula in
\mathrm{Mod}(S)

[
27
, Theorem 6.12]
.

We wish to remove the explicit mention of the constants
C
and
K
from distance formulas. This can be done as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a collection of (equivalence classes of) essential subsurfaces of
S
.
The expression

A\asymp\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}[a_{Y}]

means that there is a constant
K_{0}\geq 0
such that for any
K\geq K_{0}
there exists a constant
C>1
(depending only
K
and the surface
S
), so that

A\asymp_{C}\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}[a_{Y}]_{K}.

If this holds, then the expression
A\asymp\sum[a_{Y}]
is called a
hierarchy-like sum
.

For example, the Masur–Minsky distance formula in
\mathrm{Mod}(S)

[
27
, Theorem 6.12]
is a hierarchy-like sum. This is proved in Minsky
[
28
, Section 9.4]
; see
[
28
, Lemma 9.6]
in particular. Similarly, Rafi’s combinatorial distance formula in
\mathcal{T}(S)

[
41
, Theorem 6.1]
is a hierarchy-like sum. See
[
42
, Remark 2.5]
, and compare
Proposition

6.1
.

The following elementary lemma underlies many later computations involving hierarchy-like sums.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a collection of subsurfaces of
S
. Suppose
a_{Y}
and
b_{Y}
are quantities associated to the subsurfaces
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and
c\geq 1
is a constant depending only on
S
such that
a_{Y}\asymp_{c}b_{Y}
for each
Y
. If
\sum_{\mathcal{Y}}[a_{Y}]
is a hierarchy-like sum, then
\sum_{\mathcal{Y}}[b_{Y}]
is also a hierarchy-like sum, and
\sum_{\mathcal{Y}}[a_{Y}]\asymp\sum_{\mathcal{Y}}[b_{Y}]
.

Proof.

For each
Y
, we have that
a_{Y}\leq c\cdot b_{Y}+c
. For any
K\geq c
, we will show that

(2.1)

\sum_{Y}[a_{Y}]_{2cK}\leq(c+1)\sum_{Y}[b_{Y}]_{K}.

Now, suppose that
[a_{Y}]_{2cK}
is a nonzero summand on the left side of (
2.1
), meaning
a_{Y}\geq 2cK
.
Then

b_{Y}\geq\frac{a_{Y}-c}{c}\geq\frac{2cK-c}{c}=2K-1\geq K,

where the last inequality holds because
K\geq c\geq 1
. Therefore
b_{Y}=[b_{Y}]_{K}
appears as a nonzero summand on the right side of (
2.1
).

To complete the proof, it remains to show that whenever
[b_{Y}]_{K}>0
, we have
a_{Y}\leq(c+1)b_{Y}
. Since
b_{Y}\geq K\geq c
, we have

a_{Y}\leq c\cdot b_{Y}+c\leq c\cdot b_{Y}+b_{Y}=(c+1)b_{Y},

as desired. This proves (
2.1
).
The reverse inequality is proved in a totally analogous way by swapping the roles of
a_{Y}
and
b_{Y}
.
∎

2.2.
Arc and curve graphs

Let
S
be an orientable surface of finite type, potentially with non-empty boundary or punctures. The
arc and curve graph

\mathcal{AC}(S)
is the graph whose vertices are isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves and simple proper arcs in
S
. Here,
essential
means that the curve or arc is not isotopic into a small neighborhood of a point, a boundary component, or a puncture.
Two vertices are joined by an edge in
\mathcal{AC}(S)
if they have disjoint representatives.
If we follow the same construction with vertices restricted to be closed curves on
S
, we obtain the
curve graph

\mathcal{C}(S)\subset\mathcal{AC}(S)
, and similarly restricting to arcs yields the
arc graph

\mathcal{A}(S)\subset\mathcal{AC}(S)
.

When
S=A
is an annulus, the definition of
\mathcal{AC}(A)
is modified so that isotopies of arcs are required to fix endpoints pointwise, so that
\mathcal{AC}(A)
becomes quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{Z}
; see Masur and Minsky
[
27
]
for details.
When
S
is a pair of pants,
\mathcal{C}(S)=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{AC}(S)
is finite, hence pants are generally ignored in hierarchy-like sums.
In all other cases, the inclusion
\mathcal{C}(S)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{AC}(S)
is a
2
–quasi-isometry
[
27
, Lemma 2.2]
. We will primarily work in
\mathcal{AC}(S)
.

When equipped with the standard path metric, the graphs
\mathcal{C}(S)
and
\mathcal{AC}(S)
are
\delta
–hyperbolic. See Masur and Minsky
[
26
]
for the original proof. Aougab
[
3
]
, Bowditch
[
8
]
, Clay–Rafi–Schleimer
[
11
]
, and Hensel–Przytycki–Webb
[
18
]
proved that the hyperbolicity constant
\delta
is independent of
S
.

The
mapping class group
of
S
, denoted
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
, is the group of isotopy classes of orientation preserving homeomorphisms of
S
. Note that
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
acts on
\mathcal{AC}(S)
by graph automorphisms. For a mapping class
\varphi\in\mathrm{Mod}(S)
, we define the
stable translation length

(2.2)

\ell_{S}(\varphi)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d_{\mathcal{AC}(S)}(\alpha,\varphi^{n}(\alpha))}{n},

where
\alpha\in\mathcal{AC}(S)
is an arbitrary reference vertex. It follows from triangle inequalities that the limit exists and does not depend on
\alpha
.

2.3.
Subsurface projection

A subsurface
Y\subset S
is called
essential
if the inclusion map
Y\hookrightarrow S
is
\pi_{1}
-injective, and
Y
is neither a pair of pants nor a peripheral annulus.
We will often refer to
Y
as simply a subsurface of
S
.

There is a partially defined projection operator
\pi_{Y}
from
\mathcal{AC}(S)
to
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
, defined as follows (again, we refer to
[
27
]
for additional details).
Let
S_{Y}
the the cover of
S
corresponding to the subgroup
\pi_{1}(Y)<\pi_{1}(S)
. Fix an arbitrary complete hyperbolic metric on
S
, and consider the Gromov compactification
\overline{S}_{Y}
of
S_{Y}
.
Then, given any essential simple closed curve or arc
\alpha
on
S
, its unique geodesic representative admits a lift
\tilde{\alpha}
to
\overline{S}_{Y}
. We define
\pi_{Y}(\alpha)
to be the subset of (isotopy classes of) curves and arcs in this lift that are essential in
\overline{S}_{Y}
; this can be either a curve, an arc, a collection of pairwise disjoint curves or arcs, or empty in the event that
\alpha\cap Y=\emptyset
. As such,
\pi_{Y}
is technically a map from
\mathcal{AC}(S)
to the power set of
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
, but when nonempty the diameter of the image of
\alpha
is always at most one.

Given two curves or arcs
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{AC}(S)
, we define their
Y
-
subsurface distance
to be the diameter of the projection
\pi_{Y}(\alpha)\cup\pi_{Y}(\beta)
in
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
:

d_{Y}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{AC}(Y)}(\pi_{Y}(\alpha)\cup\pi_{Y}(\beta)).

The definition extends immediately to other
1
–dimensional objects, such as multi-arcs and laminations whose leaves essentially intersect
\partial Y
.
We adopt the convention that
\mathrm{diam}(\emptyset)=0
. In particular, if
\pi_{Y}(\alpha)=\pi_{Y}(\beta)=\emptyset
, then
d_{Y}(\alpha,\beta)=0
.

The
bounded geodesic image theorem
, proved by Masur and Minsky
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
, will serve as a key tool in controlling projections of geodesics to subsurfaces:

Theorem 2.3
.

There is a constant
M
depending only on
S
such that if
Y\subset S
is a proper essential subsurface and
g\subset\mathcal{AC}(S)

is a geodesic that does not enter the
1
–neighborhood of
\partial Y
in
\mathcal{AC}(S)
, then

\mathrm{diam}(\pi_{Y}(g))<M.

Webb
[
48
]
gave a more combinatorial proof of Theorem
2.3
, showing that
M
can be taken to depend only on the hyperbolicity constant of
\mathcal{AC}(S)
. Since there is a uniform hyperbolicity constant
\delta
that does not depend on
S

[
3
,
8
,
11
,
18
]
,
it follows that
M
can be taken to be a single universal constant.

2.4.
Pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms

A homeomorphism
f\colon S\to S
is called
pseudo-Anosov
if there is a pair of transverse singular measured foliations
\mathcal{F}^{s},\mathcal{F}^{u}
on
S
and
\lambda>1
, called its
stretch factor
, so that
f(\mathcal{F}^{u})=\lambda\cdot\mathcal{F}^{u}
and
f(\mathcal{F}^{s})=\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\mathcal{F}^{s}
. The measured foliations
\mathcal{F}^{s/u}
are called the
stable/unstable
foliations of
f
.

Given these foliations (which are unique up to scaling their respective measures), there is a unique singular flat structure
X
on
S
whose singularities are precisely those of
\mathcal{F}^{s/u}
, and whose vertical and horizontal foliations are
\mathcal{F}^{s}
and
\mathcal{F}^{u}
, respectively. Hence, our convention is that
f
stretches along the horizontal leaves and contracts along the vertical leaves of
X
. More formally,
X
can be described as a marked complex structure on
S
together with a quadratic differential
q
so that the image of
X
under
f
is equal to the image of
q
under the time
\log(\lambda)
Teichmüller flow.

Throughout the paper, it will often be convenient to puncture
S
at the singularities of
f
. The resulting surface is denoted
\mathring{S}
and there is an induced homeomorphism
\mathring{f}\colon\mathring{S}\to\mathring{S}
.

2.5.
Veering triangulations

Veering triangulations were first defined and studied by Agol
[
1
]
, with a later description by Guéritaud
[
16
]
in terms of the singular flat structure on a surface. Our description follows Guéritaud’s definition, with notation and conventions following Minsky and Taylor
[
29
,
30
]
. We refer to those sources for additional details.

An (immersed)
rectangle
in
X
is the image of an embedded rectangle in the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
, with its induced singular flat structure. By a rectangle in
\widetilde{X}
we mean the embedded image of a (necessarily singularity-free) Euclidean rectangle whose vertical sides map to segments in
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{s}
and whose horizontal sides map to segments of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{u}
. A
saddle connection
in
X
or
\widetilde{X}
is a straight segment between two singularities, i.e. a geodesic in the flat metric that meets the singularities exactly at its endpoints. Particularly important to this paper are the saddle connections that span rectangles; these will be called either
singularity-free saddle connections
or
veering edges
, depending on the perspective. In slightly more detail, these are the saddle connections that arise by taking a rectangle
\widetilde{R}
in
\widetilde{X}
that contains two singularities in its boundary and connecting them with the straight line segment to produce a saddle connection
\sigma
. The induced (sub)-rectangle of
\widetilde{R}
whose opposite (singular) corners lie at the endpoints
\sigma
is called the
spanning rectangle

R_{\sigma}
of
\sigma
. The same terminology applies in
X
after applying the covering projection (although spanning rectangles in
X
are only immersed; see
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
).

Besides spanning rectangles,
we also define
maximal rectangles
to be rectangles that are not contained in a larger rectangle. In
\widetilde{X}
, these are the embedded rectangles that have exactly one singularity in the interior of each of its four sides.

Every maximal (singularity-free) rectangle
R
defines an oriented ideal tetrahedron
\mathfrak{t}\subset\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
with a map
\mathfrak{t}\to R
, as follows. The vertices of
\mathfrak{t}
map to the four singularities in the boundary of
R
. The edges of
\mathfrak{t}
map to the six saddle connections spanned by these four vertices. The orientation of
\mathfrak{t}
is determined by the convention that the more-vertical edge (whose endpoints are on the horizontal edges of
R
) lies above the more-horizontal edge. See
Figure

1
.

Figure 1.

Performing this construction for all maximal rectangles
gives a countable collection of tetrahedra whose ideal vertices map to singularities of
X
. If tetrahedra
\mathfrak{t}
and
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
contain the same triple of saddle connections (equivalently, if maximal rectangles
{R}
and
{R}^{\prime}
intersect along a sub-rectangle that meets three punctures), we glue
\mathfrak{t}
to
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
along their shared face. By a theorem of Guéritaud

]
(see also
[
29

), the resulting
3

of

. Moreover, the maps of tetrahedra to their defining rectangles piece together to form a fibration
\Pi\colon{\mathring{S}}\times\mathbb{R}\to{\mathring{S}}
. We call
\tau_{X}
the
veering triangulation associated to

.

The map
f\colon X\to X
preserves the foliations
\mathcal{F}^{s},\mathcal{F}^{u}
and
permutes the maximal rectangles, inducing a simplicial map
F\colon\tau_{X}\to\tau_{X}
. Accordingly,
\tau_{X}
projects to an ideal triangulation of the punctured mapping torus
\mathring{M}_{f}=(\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R})/F
.

Observe that a saddle connection of
X
corresponds to an edge of
\tau_{X}
if and only if it spans a singularity-free rectangle. In other words, edges of
\tau_{X}
are in natural bijection with veering edges; hence the name.

A
section
of
\tau_{X}
is simply a triangulation of
X
by singularity-free saddle connections. We also think of a section as an ideal triangulation of
\widetilde{X}
.
The name comes from the fact that sections are in natural bijective correspondence with simplicial sections of the fibration
\Pi\colon{\mathring{S}}\times\mathbb{R}\to{\mathring{S}}
.

Given a section
T
, an upward diagonal exchange is defined as follows: locate an edge

that is wider (i.e. has greater mass with respect to
\mathcal{F}^{s}
) than the other edges in either of the two triangles
t_{1},t_{2}
containing
e
. Then there is a maximal rectangle
R
containing
t_{1},t_{2}
such that
e
joins the singularities in the vertical edges of
R
. The upward diagonal exchange replaces
e
with the saddle connection that joins the horizontal edges of
R
to give a new section
T^{\prime}
. See

Figure

1
, where the reader can also deduce the
3
-dimensional interpretation of an upward diagonal exchange in
\tau_{X}
(or they can consult
[
29
, Section 3]
). The reverse of an upward diagonal exchange is called a downward diagonal exchange.

According to Minsky and Taylor, any collection of noncrossing singularity-free saddle connections can be completed to a section
[
29
, Lemma 3.2]
and any two such sections differ by a sequence of diagonal exchanges leaving the initial set of saddle connections untouched
[
29
, Proposition 3.3]
.

Remark 2.4
.

Thinking of a section
T
as an ideal triangulation of
\mathring{S}
that simplicially embeds in the ideal triangulation
\tau_{X}
of
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
, there is a natural sense in which each veering edge
\sigma
either lies in
T
, below
T
, or above
T
. In terms of the triangulation
T
, this directly translates as follows:
\sigma
lies below

if whenever an edge

of
T
crosses
\sigma
it crosses its spanning rectangle

from bottom to top (i.e.
e
crosses the horizontal sides of
R_{\sigma}
). Equivalently, whenever
\sigma
crosses an edge

of
T
, it crosses
R_{e}
from side to side (i.e.
\sigma
crosses the vertical sides of
R_{e}
). The analogous statement can be made when
\sigma
lies above

. Several variants of this ordering will be useful below.

Conflating the distinction between foliations and laminations, we will refer to the vertical foliation
\mathcal{F}^{s}
as
\lambda^{+}
and the horizontal foliation
\mathcal{F}^{u}
as
\lambda^{-}
in the remainder of the paper. This matches the notation in the Introduction.

3.
The fundamental lemma

This section describes a straightforward but crucial result that enables us to estimate the number of fixed points of
f
by counting the intersections between a veering edge
\sigma
and its image. This result is so fundamental to our estimates that we call it the
fundamental lemma
.

Here and throughout, we fix a singular flat structure
X
on
S
associated to
f
, whose vertical and horizontal foliations are denoted
\lambda^{+}
and
\lambda^{-}
, respectively.

Let
R=R_{\sigma}\subset S
be an (immersed) spanning rectangle, as in
Section

2.5
, with
an embedded lift
\widetilde{R}\subset\widetilde{S}
and a projection map
\pi\colon\widetilde{R}\to R
. The
degree
of
R
is the maximal cardinality of a preimage
\pi^{-1}(z)
for an interior point
z\in\mathrm{int}(R)
.

The following straightforward lemma relating fixed points to intersections is the primary way in which we find and count fixed points of a pseudo-Anosov map
f
.

Lemma 3.1

(Fundamental lemma)
.

Let
\sigma\subset S
be a
singularity-free saddle connection,
with immersed spanning rectangle
R=R_{\sigma}
of degree
k
. Then the number of nonsingular fixed points of
f
contained in
R
is between

\frac{i(\sigma,f(\sigma))}{k}
and
i(\sigma,f(\sigma))
.

Here and below, we emphasize that quantities such as
i(\sigma,f(\sigma))
and
i(T,f(T))
count interior, transverse intersection points contained in
\mathring{S}
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\sigma}
be a lift of
\sigma
to
\widetilde{S}
, and let
\widetilde{R}\subset\widetilde{S}
be the singularity-free rectangle spanned by
\widetilde{\sigma}
.
For every (nonsingular, interior) point of intersection
x_{i}\in\sigma\cap f(\sigma)
, we choose a lift
\widetilde{f}_{i}
such that
\widetilde{x}_{i}\in\widetilde{\sigma}\cap\widetilde{f}_{i}(\widetilde{\sigma})
is a lift of
x_{i}
. Then
the interiors of
\widetilde{R}
and
\widetilde{f}_{i}(\widetilde{R})
have nonempty intersection.
We emphasize that distinct intersection points in
\sigma\cap f(\sigma)
lift to distinct points in
\widetilde{\sigma}
and lead to distinct lifts of
f
, and drop the subscripts going forward.

Since
f(\sigma)
is more horizontal than
\sigma
(see
Remark

2.4
), it follows that
\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{\sigma})
crosses the singularity-free rectangle
\widetilde{R}
from left to right. The endpoints of
\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{\sigma})
lie outside the closure of
\widetilde{R}
, because the intersection point
\widetilde{\sigma}\cap f(\widetilde{\sigma})
is in the interior of
\widetilde{\sigma}
. Similarly, the endpoints of
\widetilde{\sigma}
lie strictly above and below the closure of
\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{R})
.

The map
\widetilde{f}
acts affinely on
\widetilde{R}
, contracting the vertical direction and stretching the horizontal direction. By the contraction mapping theorem, there is a unique vertical coordinate in
\widetilde{R}
that is fixed by
\widetilde{f}
. By the same theorem applied to
\widetilde{f}^{-1}
, there is a unique horizontal coordinate in
\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{R})
that is fixed by
\widetilde{f}^{-1}
. At the intersection of these coordinates lies the unique fixed point of
\widetilde{f}
in
\widetilde{R}
. This projects to a fixed point of
f
in the interior of
R
.

Since
R
has degree
k
, and since distinct points of
\sigma\cap f(\sigma)
define distinct lifts of
f
, the above construction produces at least
\frac{i(\sigma,f(\sigma))}{k}
distinct fixed points in
\mathrm{int}(R)
.

In the other direction, observe that every nonsingular fixed point of
f
in
R
must belong to
\mathrm{int}(R)
: for, if a point
x\in\partial R
is fixed, then the vertical or horizontal segment from
x
to a singular corner of
R
would also be fixed, contradicting the stretching behavior of
f
.
Thus every nonsingular fixed point
x\in R
lifts to a fixed point of some lift
\widetilde{f}
contained in
\mathrm{int}(\widetilde{R})
. This lift
\widetilde{x}
must correspond to an intersection point
\widetilde{\sigma}\cap\widetilde{f}(\sigma)
because
\widetilde{R}
is a singularity-free rectangle. Hence the fixed point
x\in R
must be the projection of one of the
i(\sigma,f(\sigma))
fixed points in
\widetilde{R}
constructed above.
∎

Tools from veering triangulations

This section contains a number of useful technical results about subsurface projections and veering triangulations. Taken together, these results will enable us to use veering triangulations to estimate the sort of subsurface distances that appear in the statement of

obtained by removing these (open) outward sides.

When

is the maximal flat cylinder in

By

of

. In particular, if

.

Let

Lemma 4.2
.

A subsurface

fails to be

4.2.
Filling subsurfaces

For any essential subsurface
\mathring{W}
of
\mathring{S}
, let

be the associated subsurface of
S
obtained by capping off any components of
\partial\mathring{W}
that are inessential in
S
, as well as any punctures of
\mathring{W}
that are filled in
S
.

In general,
\operatorname{Fill}_{S}(\mathring{W})
might be inessential in
S
, meaning that it is a disk or boundary parallel annulus. However, the situation is better when
\mathring{W}
is
q
–compatible.

Lemma 4.3

(Essential filling)
.

Let
\mathring{W}
be a proper essential subsurface of
\mathring{S}
such that
d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})\geq 4
. Let
Y=\operatorname{Fill}_{S}(\mathring{W})
.
Then

is an essential and
q
–compatible (not necessarily proper) subsurface of
S
, satisfying

\mathrm{int}_{q}\mathring{W}\subset\mathrm{int}_{q}Y
.
Furthermore, if
Y
is an annulus, then
\mathrm{int}_{q}\mathring{W}=\mathrm{int}_{q}Y
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.1
,
\mathring{W}
is
q
–compatible. That is,
\mathrm{int}_{q}\mathring{W}
is
an embedded (open) representative of
\mathring{W}
in
\mathring{S}
. Since
\partial_{q}\mathring{W}
does not pass through any punctures interior to
\mathring{W}
, every interior angle along
\partial_{q}\mathring{W}
(as measured in
S
) is at least
\pi
. This is the key fact we will use.

Now lift
\mathrm{int}_{q}\mathring{W}
to the cover
\widetilde{S}_{Y}
of
S
associated to
Y=\operatorname{Fill}_{S}(\mathring{W})
and let
\overline{W}
denote its closure. As above, each interior angle of
\overline{W}
in
\widetilde{S}_{Y}
is at least
\pi
and so
\widetilde{S}_{Y}
cannot be a disk. This is a consequence of the combinatorial Gauss–Bonnet formula (see e.g.
[
40
, Theorem 3.3]
).
Moreover, for essentially the same reason, each boundary component of
\overline{W}
either bounds a disk to the outside of
\overline{W}
or is homotopic to a flat geodesic not meeting the interior of
\overline{W}
.
In particular,
\overline{W}\subset Y_{q}
, proving that
Y
is essential and
q
-compatible. By taking interiors and projecting to

, we also see that
\mathrm{int}_{q}\mathring{W}\subset\mathrm{int}_{q}Y
.

Moreover, if
Y
is an annulus, then
Y_{q}
is a flat annulus. Since
\overline{W}
has singularities (or punctures) in its boundary, we must have that both
\overline{W}
and
Y_{q}
are equal to the maximal flat annulus of
\widetilde{S}_{Y}
.
∎

We remark that when
Y=\operatorname{Fill}_{S}(\mathring{W})
, pulling curves tight to saddle connections induces a
1
–Lipschitz map
\mathcal{AC}(Y)\to\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{W})
.
Compare
Definition

8.10
.

Lemma 4.4

(Cutting subsurfaces)
.

Let
\mathring{W}
be a proper essential subsurface of
\mathring{S}
with
d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\geq 4
.
Let
Y=\operatorname{Fill}_{S}(\mathring{W})
, and suppose that
f
overlaps
Y
. Then, for any section
T
, there is an edge
\sigma
such that both
\sigma
and

cut
\mathring{W}
.

In

4.4
, the hypothesis that
f(Y)\cap Y\neq\emptyset
is absolutely crucial. By contrast, the hypothesis on distance in
\mathring{W}
is mainly included for convenience. Observe also that the conclusion of the lemma holds trivially if
\mathring{W}=\mathring{S}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.3
,
Y
is an essential (not necessarily proper) subsurface of
S
.
By construction,
\mathring{W}
can be recovered from

by removing some inessential disks and peripheral annuli. Thus the hypothesis that

f(Y)
overlaps

implies that
f(\mathring{W})
overlaps
\mathring{W}
.

Since
\mathring{W}
is
q
–compatible by

, the interior
\mathrm{int}_{q}\mathring{W}
is a copy of the interior of
\mathring{W}
, with boundary consisting of the saddle connections of
\partial_{q}\mathring{W}
.

Now, let
T
be a section, let
t
be a triangle of
T
, and consider the intersection

. Since
\partial t
consists of veering edges, and
\partial_{q}\mathring{W}
consists of saddle connections, every component of

is a

–convex polygon. Hence, the intersection of

with
\mathrm{int}_{q}\mathring{W}
is a cellulation of
\mathrm{int}_{q}\mathring{W}
.

Since

, there must be at least one triangle

such that

and

are both non-empty polygons, hence there is a veering edge

such that

and

are both non-empty. This edge

∎

4.3.
Constructing geodesic paths in a subsurface

The point of this section is to make rigorous the following heuristic: subsurfaces with large projection distance are represented by embedded simplicial ‘pockets’ of the veering triangulation; furthermore, sections sweeping through these pockets closely track geodesics in the curve and arc graph of the subsurface. Since the details will be crucial here, we explain how these properties can be obtained from those in
[
29
]
.

For the next statement, if
a
and
b
are veering edges, then we say that
a

lies below

b
and write
a<b
if
a
crosses the spanning rectangle
R_{b}
from left to right (or equivalently,
b
crosses the spanning rectangle
R_{a}
from top to bottom). We will also sometimes say that
b
is
more vertical
than
a
or that
a
is
more horizontal
than
b
. Compare
Remark

2.4
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathring{W}
be a proper essential subsurface of
\mathring{S}
, let
h
be a veering edge, and let
R_{h}
be the singularity-free rectangle spanned by
h
.

(1)

If there is a saddle connection
\sigma\subset\partial_{q}\mathring{W}
such that
\sigma<h
,
then
d_{\mathring{W}}(h,\lambda^{+})\leq 3
. If
h<\sigma
, then
d_{\mathring{W}}(h,\lambda^{-})\leq 3
.

(2)

If there is a veering edge
e\subset\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
such that
e<h
, then
d_{\mathring{W}}(h,\lambda^{+})\leq 3
. Similarly, if
h<e

In all cases, there exists a lift of

to
\widetilde{S}_{\mathring{W}}

See

for a very similar statement, with the extra hypothesis that
\mathring{W}
is
\tau
–compatible.

Proof.

To prove (
1
), let
\widetilde{S}_{\mathring{W}}
be the cover of
\mathring{S}
corresponding to
\mathring{W}
, and let
\mathring{W}_{q}\subset\widetilde{S}_{\mathring{W}}
be the

–hull representative of
\mathring{W}
constructed above. Recall that every component of
\widetilde{S}_{\mathring{W}}\smallsetminus\mathring{W}_{q}
is an annulus or disk, called an outward side.
By
q
–convexity, any flat geodesic in
\widetilde{S}_{\mathring{W}}
meets
\mathring{W}_{q}
in a connected subset.

By hypothesis, there is a saddle connection
\sigma\subset\partial_{q}\mathring{W}
that crosses
R_{h}
from left to right. In the cover
\widetilde{S}_{\mathring{W}}
, there is a lift
\widetilde{\sigma}
that is a subset of
\partial\mathring{W}_{q}
, and a lift
\widetilde{h}
that intersects

\widetilde{\sigma}
.
Then
\widetilde{h}
has at least one endpoint

whose neighborhood lies in an outward side of
\widetilde{S}_{\mathring{W}}\smallsetminus\mathring{W}_{q}

Let

be the endpoint of
\widetilde{h}
opposite

.
Let

be the leaf of
\lambda^{+}
starting at

and containing the associated side of

. (Compare
[
29
, Figure 22]

We claim that

is embedded in the cover
\widetilde{S}_{\mathring{W}}
(rather than merely immersed).
For this, orient
\ell^{+}
from

and note that by the convexity of
\mathring{W}_{q}

can intersect

at most once with each sign (otherwise, there would be a subarc of

Since

is a component of

, and

is a component of

,
according to the definition of
Section

2.3
.
This proves (
1

To prove (
2
), let

be a veering edge such that

crosses the spanning rectangle

horizontally, from left to right. Since

, there is a saddle connection

, the edge

whose diagonal is a segment of

.

For any set

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathring{W}\subset\mathring{S}

\mathrm{int}_{\tau}\mathring{W}\bigcup\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
.
For essentially the same reasons,
{\bf p}(a_{i}^{\prime})
is essential in
\mathring{W}
(this is only to say that
{\bf p}(a_{i}^{\prime})
cannot be entirely contained in
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
). We may thus select a veering edge
e_{i}\subset{\bf p}(a_{i}^{\prime})\smallsetminus\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
. By construction,
e_{i}
is disjoint from
e_{i+1}
, and
\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\}
is a path in
\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{W})
from
e_{-}=a_{0}=e_{0}
to
e_{+}=a_{n}=e_{n}
. This path of veering edges forms a geodesic in
\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{W})
, because
d_{\mathring{W}}(e_{-},e_{+})=n
. This is our geodesic
g
.

Now let
T_{i}\in T(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
be a section through
e_{i},e_{i+1}
(that is,
T_{i}\in T(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}\cup e_{i}\cup e_{i+1})
).
Hence
T^{-},T_{0},\ldots,T_{n-1},T^{+}
project under
\pi_{\mathring{W}}
to the
1
–neighborhood of the geodesic path
g=\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\}
. Moreover, thinking of these sections as lying in
(\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R},\tau)
, adjacent sections have an edge in common and therefore determine a connected chain of sections from
T^{-}
to
T^{+}
. Hence any other section
T\in T(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
must intersect this chain (in a veering edge) and hence projects within a
2
–neighborhood of our geodesic
g
. Meanwhile, any edge
h
that lies above or below
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
must project within distance
5
of one of
e_{+}
or
e_{-}
, completing the proof of (
1
).

For conclusion (
2
), consider a section
T
. If
T\notin T(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
, then
Lemma

4.6
implies
\min\{d_{\mathring{W}}(T,\lambda^{+}),d_{\mathring{W}}(T,\lambda^{-})\}\leq 3
. Otherwise, we have just shown that
d_{\mathring{W}}(T,e_{j})\leq 2
for some veering edge
e_{j}
in the geodesic
g
. Thus some endpoint of
g
(say,
e_{-}
for concreteness) satisfies
d_{\mathring{W}}(T,e_{-})\leq\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathring{W}}(e_{-},e_{+})+2
, and we conclude

d_{\mathring{W}}(T,\lambda_{-})\leq d_{\mathring{W}}(T,e_{-})+3\leq\tfrac{1}{2}d_{\mathring{W}}(e_{-},e_{+})+5\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\left(d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})+6\right)+5,

where the third inequality holds because
d_{\mathring{W}}(e_{\pm},\lambda^{\pm})\leq 3
.

For conclusion (
3
), let
T_{a}
and
T_{b}
be a pair of sections. We consider two cases. If neither
T_{a}
nor
T_{b}
belongs to
T(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
, then each one projects within distance
3
of some
\lambda^{\pm}
, hence
d_{\mathring{W}}(T_{a},T_{b})\leq d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})+6
. Alternately, if
T_{b}\in T(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
, then
\pi_{\mathring{W}}(T_{b})
lies within distance
2
of the geodesic
g=\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, while
T_{a}
lies within distance
5
of
g
. Thus

d_{\mathring{W}}(T_{a},T_{b})\leq d_{\mathring{W}}(e_{-},e_{+})+7\leq\left(d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})+6\right)+7.

This proves conclusion (
3
) and the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

The proof of
Theorem

4.7
can be adapted to prove the following “non-backtracking statement”. Suppose that
T_{a},T_{b}\in T(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
are a pair of sections such that
T_{a}<T_{b}
. Let
g=\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be the geodesic of
Theorem

4.7
, so that
e_{a}
is the closest vertex to
T_{a}
and
e_{b}
is the closes to
T_{b}
. Then the entire upward flip sequence from
T_{a}
to
T_{b}
projects within distance
2
of the sub-geodesic
\{e_{a},\ldots,e_{b}\}
.

If
\mathring{W}
is not
\tau
–compatible, then the entire upward flip sequence from
T_{a}
to
T_{b}
must contain the saddle connection

\sigma\subset\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
such that
\pi_{\mathring{W}}(\sigma)\neq\emptyset
. Thus the flip sequence projects within distance

of

.

Now, we may assume
\mathring{W}
is
\tau

T_{a}
must intersect some

.

4.4.
Sections and intersections

Recall that a section
T
can simultaneously be thought of as an ideal triangulation of
\mathring{S}
by veering edges, and as a copy of
\mathring{S}
simplicially embedded in
the veering triangulation
\tau_{X}
on
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
. In what follows, we make use of both perspectives.

Now, suppose that
T_{a}
and
T_{b}
are a pair of sections. Then
T_{a}
and
T_{b}
partition
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
into two unbounded components and some number of bounded components. Each bounded component of
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus(T_{a}\cup T_{b})
is called a
pocket
. Each pocket
P
projects to a region
B=B(P)\subset\mathring{S}
called the
base
of the pocket, whose boundary is a union of veering edges. The top of the pocket
P
, denoted
P^{+}
, is the sub-section of one section (
T_{a}
or
T_{b}
) lying over
B
. The bottom of the pocket, denoted
P^{-}
, is a subsection of the other section lying over
B
.

For a singularity-free saddle connection
\sigma
, set
T^{+}_{\sigma}=T(\sigma)^{+}
to be the highest section in
T(\sigma)
, meaning the highest section containing
\sigma
as an edge.
This can be characterized by the property that if
T^{\prime}\in T(\sigma)
is any other veering triangulation of
\mathring{S}
containing
\sigma
, then for any crossings between edges of
T^{+}_{\sigma}
and
T^{\prime}
, the edges of
T^{+}_{\sigma}
are more vertical. See
[
29
, Section 3]
for more details.

Similarly, let
T^{-}_{\sigma}=T(\sigma)^{-}
be the lowest section containing
\sigma
.

Definition 4.9
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are crossing singularity-free saddle connections, such that
\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2}
.
In this case, the sections
T^{-}_{\sigma_{1}}
and
T^{+}_{\sigma_{2}}
together cut
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
into two unbounded regions and a collection of pockets.
Let
P=P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
be the unique pocket containing
\sigma_{1}
on its top side and
\sigma_{2}
on its bottom side. The top and bottom of the pocket are denoted
P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})^{+}
and
P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})^{-}
, respectively.
Observe that both
P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})^{\pm}
are triangulations of
B
, the base of the pocket, and are called the
partial sections
defined by
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
.
As triangulations of
B
, we have

(4.1)

P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})^{+}=T^{-}_{\sigma_{1}}\cap B\qquad\text{ and }\qquad P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})^{-}=T^{+}_{\sigma_{2}}\cap B.

(Note the somewhat counterintuitive interchange of pluses and minuses.)
In the important special case where
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
fill
\mathring{S}
, we have
T^{-}_{\sigma_{1}}>T^{+}_{\sigma_{2}}
, and the two disjoint sections co-bound a single pocket over
B=\mathring{S}
.

Lemma 4.10

(Closest sections)
.

Let
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
be crossing singularity-free saddle connections such that
\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{2}
.
Let
P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
be the pocket defined by
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, with top and bottom sides

P_{1}=P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})^{+}
and
P_{2}=P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})^{-}
.
Then

i(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\>\leq\>i(P_{1},P_{2})\>\leq\>(9\chi(S))^{2}\cdot i(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}).

Proof.

First, we check that the number of edges in any section
T
is at most
|9\chi(S)|
. Since
T
is an ideal triangulation of the punctured surface
\mathring{S}
, it has exactly
|3\chi(\mathring{S})|
edges. Furthermore, since
\mathring{S}
is created by puncturing the singularities of
q
, and the number of singularities is largest when all interior singularities are trivalent, the Euler–Poincaré formula implies

\#\mathrm{sing}(q)\leq 2|\chi(S)|
, hence

|\chi(\mathring{S})|\leq 3|\chi(S)|
. Putting it all together shows the number of edges is at most
|9\chi(S)|
.

Now, we prove the lemma. The first inequality in the statement is immediate by set inclusion, since
\sigma_{j}\subset P_{j}
.

For the second inequality, the key idea is contained in the proof of
[
29
, Lemma 3.4]
.
Recall from
(
4.1
) that
P_{1}=T^{-}_{\sigma_{1}}\cap B
and
P_{2}=T^{+}_{\sigma_{2}}\cap B
.
By construction, since
T^{-}_{\sigma_{1}}
is the lowest section containing
\sigma_{1}
, the only downward flippable edge of
T^{-}_{\sigma_{1}}
is
\sigma_{1}
. But
[
29
, Lemma 3.4]
shows that for any edge
e\subset P_{2}
, the tallest edge of
T^{-}_{\sigma_{1}}
crossing
e
is always downward flippable. Thus, for every
e\subset P_{2}
, the tallest edge of
P_{1}
crossing
e
must be
\sigma_{1}
.

From this, it follows that every edge
h\subset P_{1}
satisfies
i(h,e)\leq i(\sigma_{1},e)
. Indeed, starting at
h
there is a sequence
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
of edges of
P_{1}
with
h_{0}=h
,
h_{k}=\sigma_{1}
, and, for each
i=0,\ldots,k-1
,
h_{i}
and
h_{i+1}
cobound a triangle for which
h_{i+1}
is the tallest edge.
Fixing a lift
\widetilde{e}
of
e
to the universal cover, each intersection of
h
with
e
determines a unique lift
\widetilde{h}
of
h
that intersects
\widetilde{e}
. The above sequence has a unique lift staring at
\widetilde{h}
, and the discussion above implies that each edge along the lifted sequence must intersect
\widetilde{e}
. Moreover, using uniqueness of the lifted path, it is easy to see that the associated map from
h\cap e
to
\sigma_{1}\cap e
is injecitve.

As the number of edges
h\subset P_{1}
is at most
|9\chi(S)|
,
we obtain

i(P_{1},e)\leq|9\chi(S)|\cdot i(\sigma_{1},e)\quad\Rightarrow\quad i(P_{1},P_{2})\leq|9\chi(S)|\cdot i(\sigma_{1},P_{2}).

Now, performing the same analysis for
\sigma_{2}
in place of
\sigma_{1}
gives

i(\sigma_{1},P_{2})\leq|9\chi(S)|\cdot i(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}),

completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
T
be a section of the veering triangulation and
\sigma
an edge of
T
.
Assume that
i(\sigma,f(\sigma))>0
. Let
P=P(\sigma,f(\sigma))
be the associated pocket, with top and bottom sides
P^{+}
and
P^{-}
.
Then
for any essential subsurface
\mathring{W}\subset\mathring{S}
,

d_{\mathring{W}}(\sigma,f(\sigma))\>\leq\>d_{\mathring{W}}(P^{+},P^{-})\>\leq\>d_{\mathring{W}}(\sigma,f(\sigma))+9.

Proof.

Let
B
be the base of the pocket
P=P(\sigma,f(\sigma))
. If
B\cap\mathring{W}=\emptyset
, then
\pi_{\mathring{W}}(P^{+})=\pi_{\mathring{W}}(P^{-})=\emptyset
. In this case,
the statement is immediate because all distances in the lemma are
\mathrm{diam}(\emptyset)=0
. Regardless of intersection
B\cap\mathring{W}
, the first inequality is also immediate because
\sigma\subset P(\sigma,f(\sigma))^{+}
and
f(\sigma)\subset P(\sigma,f(\sigma))^{-}
.

For the second inequality, we may assume that
\mathring{W}
is
\tau
–compatible: otherwise,

Theorem

4.7
implies
d_{\mathring{W}}(P^{+},P^{-})\leq 9
because
P^{\pm}
are partial sections.

If
\sigma
and
f(\sigma)
both cut
\mathring{W}
, observe that
\pi_{\mathring{W}}(P^{+})
lies in a
1
–neighborhood of
\pi_{\mathring{W}}(\sigma)
, and
\pi_{\mathring{W}}(P^{-})
similarly lies in a
1
–neighborhood of
f(\sigma)
. Thus we have
d_{\mathring{W}}(P^{+},P^{-})\leq d_{\mathring{W}}(\sigma,f(\sigma))+2
.

It remains to prove the second inequality in the special case where
\mathring{W}
is
\tau
–compatible,
\mathring{W}\cap B\neq\emptyset
, and one of
\sigma
and
f(\sigma)
misses
\mathring{W}
. We assume for concreteness that
\sigma\cap\mathring{W}=\emptyset
. In particular,
\mathring{W}
is a proper subsurface.

Since the subsurface
\mathring{W}
is
\tau
–compatible, it is isotopic to the base
\mathring{W}_{\tau}
of a pocket
P(\mathring{W}_{\tau})
. Since
\sigma
misses
\mathring{W}
, the section
T^{-}_{\sigma}
lies no higher than the bottom side of
P(\mathring{W}_{\tau})
.
This is to say that either some edge of
T^{-}_{\sigma}
lies below an edge of
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
, or all of
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
belongs to
T^{-}_{\sigma}
. In the first case, we have
d_{\mathring{W}}(T^{-}_{\sigma},\lambda^{-})\leq 3
by
Lemma

4.6
, and in the second case we have
d_{\mathring{W}}(T^{-}_{\sigma},\lambda^{-})\leq 4
by applying
Lemma

4.6
to a section that lies one flip below
T^{-}_{\sigma}
.
Since
P^{+}\subset T^{-}_{\sigma}
by (
4.1
),
we have
d_{\mathring{W}}(P^{+},\lambda^{-})\leq 4
.

Finally, recall that
f(\sigma)\subset P^{-}
, which lies below
P^{+}
.
Thus some section containing
P^{-}
must also be within distance
4
of
\lambda^{-}
. We conclude that
d_{\mathring{W}}(P^{-},\lambda^{-})\leq 4
, hence
d_{\mathring{W}}(P^{+},P^{-})\leq 8.

∎

5.
Isolated pockets and annular-avoiding sections

In distance formulas in Teichmüller space (such as
Proposition

6.1
and
Proposition

6.5
), annular projections play a special role.
The special role of annuli is also visible in
Theorem

1.4
.
In this section of the paper, we will carefully build sections of the veering triangulation that avoid passing through deep annular pockets. This will enable our formulas to correctly count annuli.

We will also prove that for each (proper) subsurface
\mathring{W}
, a well-chosen section
T
satisfies a coarse equality of the form
d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\asymp d_{\mathring{W}}(T,f(T))
. These coarse equalities are recorded in
Lemmas

5.4
,
5.5
and
5.6
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathring{W}\subset\mathring{S}
be a
\tau
–compatible subsurface. A pocket
V=V_{\mathring{W}}
over
\mathrm{int}_{\tau}\mathring{W}
is called
isolated
if the following properties hold:

•

Every edge
e\subset V_{\mathring{W}}\smallsetminus\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
satisfies
d_{\mathring{W}}(e,\lambda^{-})\geq 6
and
d_{\mathring{W}}(e,\lambda^{+})\geq 6
.

•

The top and bottom boundaries of
V_{\mathring{W}}
, denoted
V^{+}
and
V^{-}
, intersect only in
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
.

We remark that
Definition

5.1
differs slightly from the notion of isolated pockets in
[
29
, Section 6.3]
. This is because Minsky and Taylor
[
29
]
use a different convention for subsurface distances: to compute
d_{\mathring{W}}(\alpha,\beta)
, they consider the minimal distance between vertices in
\pi_{\mathring{W}}(\alpha)
and
\pi_{\mathring{W}}(\beta)
rather than the maximal distance. After adjusting for the slightly different distance convention, it follows that
Definition

5.1
is slightly stronger.

Lemma 5.2

(Isolated pockets)
.

For every proper essential subsurface
\mathring{W}\subset\mathring{S}
such that
d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})>12
, there is an isolated pocket
V_{\mathring{W}}
over
\mathring{W}_{\tau}
. This pocket has the following properties:

(1)

Every section
T
that lies below the interior of
V_{\mathring{W}}
satisfies
d_{\mathring{W}}(T,\lambda^{-})\leq 6
.
Every section
T
that lies above the interior of
V_{\mathring{W}}
satisfies
d_{\mathring{W}}(T,\lambda^{+})\leq 6
.

(2)

If
V_{\mathring{W}}
and
V_{\mathring{Y}}
are isolated pockets whose bases
\mathring{W}
and
{\mathring{Y}}
are overlapping proper subsurfaces, then a section
T
cannot intersect the interiors of both
V_{\mathring{W}}
and
V_{\mathring{Y}}
. In particular, these interiors are disjoint.

Proof.

Conclusion (
1
) follows from the argument used to prove
[
29
, Lemma 6.4]
. Since the definition has changed slightly, we give a brief summary.

By
Lemma

4.1
,
\mathring{W}
is
\tau
–compatible.
Let
T_{0}=T^{-}(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}),T_{1},\ldots,T^{n}=T^{-}(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
be an upward flip sequence from
T_{0}=T^{-}(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
to
T_{n}=T^{-}(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
.
Let
a
be the largest integer in
\{0,\ldots,n\}
such that
d_{\mathring{W}}(e,\lambda^{-})<6
for some edge
e\subset T_{a-1}
that projects to
\mathring{W}
; such an
a
exists by
Lemma

4.6
. Similarly, let
b
be the smallest integer in the sequence such that
d_{\mathring{W}}(e,\lambda^{+})<6
for some edge
e\subset T_{b+1}
projecting to
\mathring{W}
. The hypothesis
d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})>12
implies that
a<b
, and that
T_{a},T_{b}
cannot share any edges over
\mathring{W}
.
Now, let
V_{\mathring{W}}
be the pocket over
\mathring{W}
whose bottom side is contained in
T_{a}
and whose top side is contained in
T_{b}
.
By construction, every edge in
T_{a},\ldots,T_{b}
that lies over
\mathring{W}
satisfies
d_{\mathring{W}}(e,\lambda^{-})\geq 6
and
d_{\mathring{W}}(e,\lambda^{+})\geq 6
. Thus
V_{\mathring{W}}
is an isolated pocket, and property (
1
) also follows.

For conclusion (
2
), suppose for a contradiction that
T
intersects the interiors of both
V_{\mathring{W}}
and
V_{\mathring{Y}}
.
Then
T
contains an edge
h\subset V_{\mathring{Y}}
that is disjoint from
\partial_{\tau}{\mathring{Y}}
. Since the subsurfaces
\mathring{W}
and
{\mathring{Y}}
are
\tau
–compatible and have overlapping boundaries, we may assume for concreteness that
some edge
h^{\prime}\subset\partial_{\tau}{\mathring{Y}}
lies below an edge of
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
. By
Lemma

4.5
, we have
d_{\mathring{W}}(h^{\prime},\lambda^{-})\leq 3
, which implies
d_{\mathring{W}}(h,\lambda^{-})\leq 4
and
d_{\mathring{W}}(T,\lambda^{-})\leq 5
. On the other hand, since
T
intersects the interior of
V_{\mathring{W}}
,
Definition

5.1
implies
d_{\mathring{W}}(T,\lambda^{-})\geq 6
, which is a contradiction. It follows that the interiors of
V_{\mathring{W}}
and
V_{\mathring{Y}}
cannot intersect the same section, and are therefore disjoint.
∎

We remark that the disjointness of isolated pockets (as defined in Minsky–Taylor
[
29
, Section 6.3]
) is proved in
[
29
, Proposition 6.5]
. It is also worth observing that
the interiors of isolated pockets
V_{\mathring{W}}
and
V_{\mathring{Y}}
must automatically be disjoint whenever
\mathring{W}
and
{\mathring{Y}}
have disjoint interiors.

Definition 5.3
.

A section
T
is called an
f
–section
if
f(T)\leq T
. Here the ordering is with respect to
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
. Equivalently, if
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are edges of
T
such that
f(\sigma_{1})
and
\sigma_{2}
cross, then
f(\sigma_{1})
crosses the spanning rectangle of
\sigma_{2}
from left to right.

The following lemma essentially states that there exists an
f
–section
T
that does not cut deeply through any annular pocket.
By
Lemma

3.2
, this will imply that any veering edge
\sigma\subset T
has a spanning rectangle of bounded degree, enabling us to apply the fundamental lemma to count fixed points.

Lemma 5.4

(Annular avoiding section)
.

There is an
f
–section
T
of
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
such that for any essential annulus
A
of
\mathring{S}
, we have

(5.1)

\min\big\{d_{A}(T,\lambda^{-}),d_{A}(T,\lambda^{+})\big\}\leq 14.

Furthermore, if
d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\geq 25
, there is a unique
f
–translate
A^{\prime}=f^{i}(A)
such that
d_{A^{\prime}}(T,f(T))>12
. This translate
A^{\prime}
satisfies

(5.2)

d_{A^{\prime}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})-12\>\leq\>d_{A^{\prime}}(T,f(T))\>\leq\>d_{A^{\prime}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})+13.

An
f
–section
T
satisfying the conclusion of this lemma is called
annular-avoiding
.

Proof.

We begin by proving that there exists a section
T
satisfying (
5.1
) for every annulus
A
.
If
d_{A}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})\leq 12
, then
Theorem

4.7
implies that
every
section
T
satisfies

\min\{d_{A}(T,\lambda^{-}),d_{A}(T,\lambda^{+})\}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}d_{A}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})+8\leq 14.

Thus it suffices to consider annuli with
d_{A}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})>12
.

For any annulus
A
such that
d_{A}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})>12
,
Lemma

5.2
says that there is an isolated pocket
V_{A}
. Since any pair of annuli
A
and
A^{\prime}
either overlap or are disjoint,
Lemma

5.2
says that the interiors of all isolated annular pockets are pairwise disjoint. Thus we may perform the following procedure. We begin with an arbitrary section
T_{0}
, and flip
T_{0}
down to the bottom of every isolated pocket that it crosses. The resulting section
T
is disjoint from the interior of every isolated pocket. Thus, by
Lemma

5.2
, we have

(5.3)

\min\big\{d_{A}(T,\lambda^{-}),d_{A}(T,\lambda^{+})\big\}\leq 6

for every annulus
A
with
d_{A}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})>12
. We have thus found a section
T
(not necessarily an
f
–section) satisfying (
5.1
) in every annulus.

To obtain an
f
–section, consider the family of sections
\{f^{i}(T):i\geq 0\}
. For sufficiently large
i
— where
i>(9\chi(S))^{2}
suffices — these sections lie strictly below
T
. Now, we define

to be the maximum of this family of sections. (The maximum operation is carefully defined just above
[

, and coincides with the pointwise max along the

–fibers.)
It is clear that

, since

is the maximum of a smaller family of sections. Moreover, since

is disjoint from the interior of every isolated annular pocket, the same is true for its translates, hence
T^{+}
is disjoint from the interior of every isolated pocket as well. Thus
T^{+}
satisfies (
5.3
) in every annulus

with

, and we conclude that
T^{+}
is an
f
–section satisfying (
5.1
).

To prove that there is an
f
–translate of
A
satisfying (
5.2
), let

be an annulus such that
d_{A}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})\geq 25
. Consider the family of sections
\{f^{i}(T^{+}):i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Since
T^{+}
is disjoint from (the interior of) every isolated annular pocket, the same is true for its translates. In particular, every
f^{i}(T^{+})
is either above or below the isolated pocket
V_{A}
. Since
f^{i+1}(T^{+})\leq f^{i}(T^{+})
for every

, there is a single integer

such that

is above

for

and below

for

. Thus, for every

,
Lemma

5.2
implies
that

and
f^{i+1}(T^{+})
are

–close to the same lamination, hence

d_{A}(f^{i}(T^{+}),f^{i+1}(T^{+}))\leq 12<d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})-12\quad\text{for}\quad i\neq n.

On the other hand, we have
d_{A}(f^{n}(T^{+}),\lambda^{+})\leq 6
and
d_{A}(f^{n+1}(T^{+}),\lambda^{-})\leq 6
, hence

d_{A}(f^{n}(T^{+}),f^{n+1}(T^{+}))\geq d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})-12.

Translating everything over by

is the only

–translate of

and

, holds for every section by

.
∎

Annular-avoiding
f
–sections are needed for two reasons. First, as mentioned above, the edges of an annular-avoiding section

have bounded degree, enabling us to apply the fundamental lemma. Second, annular projections will be counted with a

, so we do not want to break up a big annular projection into two projections. (For non-annular subsurfaces
\mathring{W}

gets broken up into a large (but bounded) number of large projections; see

.)

In the remainder of this section, we prove coarse comparisons similar to (

) that hold for non-annular subsurfaces of
\mathring{S}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any section
T
of
\mathring{S}\times\mathbb{R}
, we have

d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T))-4\>\leq\>\ell_{\mathring{S}}(\mathring{f})\>\leq\>d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T)).

Recall from (
2.2
) that
\ell_{\mathring{S}}(\mathring{f})
denotes the stable translation length of
\mathring{f}
in
\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{S})
.

Proof.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, triangle inequalities imply that
d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f^{n}(T))\leq n\cdot d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T))
.
Thus

\ell_{\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{S})}(\mathring{f})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(T,f^{n}(T))}{n}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\cdot d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T))}{n}=d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T)).

For the other inequality, we may assume that
d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T))>4
(otherwise, there is nothing to prove).
By
[
29
, Theorem 1.4]
, there is a bi-infinite geodesic axis
g=\{e_{i}:i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
that runs through
\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{S})
from
\lambda^{+}
to
\lambda^{-}
, such that every
e_{i}
is an edge of the veering triangulation.
As in the proof of
Theorem

4.7
, let
T_{i}
be a section through
e_{i}\cup e_{i+1}
. Then every section
T^{\prime}
intersects some

in a veering edge. In particular, every
T^{\prime}
lies within distance
2
of some
e_{i}
.

For every

, let

be an integer such that
d_{\mathring{S}}(f^{k}(T),e_{i_{k}})\leq 2
. Then triangle inequalities imply

i_{k+1}-i_{k}=d_{\mathring{S}}(e_{i_{k}},e_{i_{k+1}})\geq d_{\mathring{S}}(f^{k+1}(T),f^{k}(T))-4=d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T))-4>0.

In particular, the veering edges

are monotonically ordered along

.

Now, fix an

. Then, for all

, we have

\ell_{\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{S})}(\mathring{f})\geq\frac{d(T,f^{n}(T))}{n}-\varepsilon
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle d(T,f^{n}(T))

\displaystyle\geq d_{\mathring{S}}(e_{i_{0}},e_{i_{n}})-4

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}d_{\mathring{S}}(e_{i_{j}},e_{i_{j+1}})\quad-4

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T))-4)\quad-4

\displaystyle=n(d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T))-4)\quad-4

which implies
\ell_{\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{S})}(\mathring{f})\geq(d_{\mathring{S}}(T,f(T))-4)-\frac{4}{n}-\varepsilon
.
Letting

and then

∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let

be an

–section of
\mathring{S}
. Let

\mathring{W}\subset\mathring{S}
be a proper non-annular subsurface such that

, and such that

\big[d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big]_{K}\asymp\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\big[d_{f^{i}(\mathring{W})}(T,f(T))\big]_{K}.

Proof.

Since

,

5.2
says there is an isolated pocket

over
\mathring{W}
. Thus, for every

, the translate

is an isolated pocket over

First, suppose that

intersects the interior of

for some translate

.
Since

implies that

cannot intersect the interior of

and

both lie above

for every

cannot intersect the interior of

, hence

Now, suppose that

Then there is exactly one translate

such that the interior of

and above

. Since

translates downward, it once again follows that

Combining the cases, we learn that

For the distance estimate of the lemma, observe that

Remark 5.7
.

There is a more subtle version of

Lemma

5.6
that holds without the hypothesis that
\mathring{W}
is overlapped. In that setting, there might be multiple sections

that all intersect the same isolated pocket

. The number
k
of these sections is bounded by the number of translates of
\mathring{W}
whose interiors are pairwise disjoint, which is itself bounded by

. The nearest-point projections of
T,f^{-1}(T),\ldots,f^{-(k-1)}(T)
to the geodesic
g=\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{AC}(\mathring{W}^{\prime})
guaranteed by

4.7
are coarsely non-backtracking by
Remark

4.8
.
By computing as in

, one can prove that

d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})=\sum_{i=n_{0}}^{n_{0}+k}d_{f^{i}(\mathring{W})}(T,f(T))+O(k).

Hence there is still a hierarchy-like sum of the form

d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\asymp\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[d_{f^{i}(\mathring{W})}(T,f(T))\right].

6.
Distance formulas

As part of his
work on the coarse geometry of the Teichmüller metric
[
41
]
, Rafi has proved a hierarchy-like distance formula for Teichmüller distance. (See also Durham
[
13
, Theorem 7.4.7]
for an alternate proof.) In this section, we show how Rafi’s formula can be used to give a uniform coarse estimate for Teichmüller translation length. This result,
Proposition

6.1
, appears to be new.

For the statement, recall our conventions for hierarchy-like sums (
Definition

2.1
).

Proposition 6.1
.

For any pseudo-Anosov
f
with invariant laminations
\lambda^{\pm}
, there is a hierarchy-like sum

\ell_{\mathcal{T}(S)}(f)\asymp\ell_{S}(f)+\sum_{\langle f\rangle Y}\big[d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big]+\sum_{\langle f\rangle A}\big[\log d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big].

where the first sum is over
f
–orbits of proper non-annular subsurfaces and the second is over
f
–orbits of annuli.

Before proving
Proposition

6.1
, we introduce a definition and two lemmas.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{AC}(S)
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a proper essential subsurface
Y\subset S
is called
n
–separated
(with respect to
x
) if no geodesic ray in
\mathcal{AC}(S)
from
f^{-n}(x)
to
\lambda^{-}
or from
f^{n}(x)
to
\lambda^{+}
enters the
1
–neighborhood of
\partial Y
. Observe that every
Y
is
n
–separated for sufficiently large
n
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{AC}(S)
, and let
Y
be an
n
–separated subsurface with respect to
x
. Then

d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})-2M\leq d_{Y}(f^{-n}(x),f^{n}(x))\leq d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})+2M,

where
M
is the constant of
Theorem

2.3
.

Proof.

Set
x_{n}=f^{-n}(x)
and
y_{n}=f^{n}(x)
.
By the bounded geodesic image theorem (
Theorem

2.3
), any ray from
x_{n}
to
\lambda^{-}
(respectively
y_{n}
to
\lambda^{+}
) projects to a path of diameter at most
M
in
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
. It follows by the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle d_{Y}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y}(\lambda^{-},x_{n})+d_{Y}(x_{n},y_{n})+d_{Y}(y_{n},\lambda^{+})

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y}(x_{n},y_{n})+2M.

The other inequality in the lemma is proved in exactly the same way.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
Y
be a proper essential subsurface with
d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})>M
. Then, for all
x\in\mathcal{AC}(S)
and all sufficiently large
n
(depending on
x
), there exist
n
distinct
n
–separated translates
Y_{1},...,Y_{n}\in\langle f\rangle(Y)
.

Proof.

Since
d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})>M
,
Theorem

2.3
implies that
\partial Y
lies in the
2
–neighborhood of every geodesic in
\mathcal{AC}(S)
connecting
\lambda^{-}
to
\lambda^{+}
. Let
g
be one such bi-infinite geodesic. Since
d_{f^{i}Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})>M
for every
i
,
it follows that
every
\langle f\rangle
–translate of
\partial Y

lies in a
2
–neighborhood of
g
. In particular, for every
i,j
, the distance
d_{S}(f^{i}(\partial Y),f^{i+j}(\partial Y))
is within uniformly bounded additive error, say
\kappa_{1}
, from
|j|\cdot\ell_{S}(f)
.

As above, set
x_{n}=f^{-n}(x)
and
y_{n}=f^{n}(x)
. For every
n
, let
p_{n}
be a vertex of
g
closest to
x_{n}
, and let
q_{n}
be a vertex of
g
closest to
y_{n}
. Since all
x_{n},y_{n}
lie in a uniformly bounded neighborhood of
g
, it follows that there is a uniform
\kappa_{2}
such that

(6.1)

\displaystyle 2n\ell_{S}(f)-\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{S}(p_{n},q_{n})\leq 2n\ell_{S}(f)+\kappa_{2},

(6.2)

\displaystyle 2n\ell_{S}(f)-\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{S}(x_{n},y_{n})\leq 2n\ell_{S}(f)+\kappa_{2}.

Combining (
6.1
) with the above estimate on
d_{S}(f^{i}(\partial Y),f^{i+2n}(\partial Y))
, we learn that the number of
f
–translates of
\partial Y
whose closest points on
g
lie between
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
is within uniform additive error of
2n
. If
f^{j}(\partial Y)
is such a translate whose closest point on
g
is more than
\delta+1
past
p_{n}
, then no geodesic ray from
x_{n}
to
\lambda^{-}
can enter the
1
–neighborhood of
f^{j}(\partial Y)
. An analogous statement holds near
q_{n}
. We conclude that for large

, there are at least
n
distinct
n
–separated subsurfaces in the
f
–orbit of
Y
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

6.1
.

We will make use of Rafi’s combinatorial model for the Teichmüller metric
[
41
, Theorem 6.1]
and refer the reader to his paper for additional details. A convenient restatement of Rafi’s distance estimate

[
42
, Theorem 2.4 and Remark 2.5]
says that for every
x,y\in\mathcal{T}(S)
,

(6.3)

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{T}}(x,y)

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{Y}\big[d_{Y}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})\big]+\sum_{\gamma\notin\mu_{x}^{\circ}\cup\mu_{y}^{\circ}}\big[\log d_{A_{\gamma}}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})\big]

(6.4)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha\in\mu_{x}^{\circ}\smallsetminus\mu_{y}^{\circ}}\log\frac{1}{\ell_{x}(\alpha)}+\sum_{\beta\in\mu_{y}^{\circ}\smallsetminus\mu_{x}^{\circ}}\log\frac{1}{\ell_{y}(\beta)}

(6.5)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\gamma\in\mu_{x}^{\circ}\cap\mu_{y}^{\circ}}d_{\mathbb{H}}\left(\Big(\mbox{twist}_{\gamma}(x,y),\frac{1}{\ell_{x}(\gamma)}\Big),\>\Big(0,\frac{1}{\ell_{y}(\gamma)}\Big)\right).

In the above expression,
\mu_{z}
is a carefully chosen “short marking” on the Riemann surface
z\in\mathcal{T}(S)
, and
\mathring{\mu}_{z}
is the set of base curves (not transversals) in the marking
\mu_{z}
. This marking is chosen in a
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
–equivariant manner.
The first sum in (
6.3
) runs over all non-annular sub-surfaces
Y\subset S
, including
S
itself.
In the second sum of (
6.3
),
A_{\gamma}
is the annulus with core curve
\gamma
. Finally, in (
6.4
) and (
6.5
),
\ell_{z}(\gamma)
denotes the length of
\gamma
in the hyperbolic structure
z\in\mathcal{T}(S)
.
While the expressions in the last two lines are quite complicated, we will show that they do not contribute to the estimate of translation length
\ell_{\mathcal{T}(S)}(f)
.

Now, let
x
be an arbitrary point on the Teichmüller axis for
f
, and set
x_{n}=f^{-n}(x)
and
y_{n}=f^{n}(x)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then we have

(6.6)

\ell_{\mathcal{T}(S)}(f)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}(x_{n},y_{n}).

Moreover, Rafi’s combinatorial formula gives an expression for the right hand side of (
6.6
) in terms of subsurface projections, lengths, and twist parameters. Indeed, denoting the sums in (
6.3
), (
6.4
), and (
6.5
) by
A(x,y),B(x,y)
, and
C(x,y)
respectively, one has

(6.7)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}(x_{n},y_{n})\asymp\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(x_{n},y_{n})+B(x_{n},y_{n})+C(x_{n},y_{n})}{n}

We next claim that
B(x_{n},y_{n})
and
C(x_{n},y_{n})
are both bounded above uniformly in
n
. Indeed,
the uniform upper bound on
B(x_{n},y_{n})
holds because
all points
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
lie in the same
\mathrm{Mod}(S)
orbit, hence there is a uniform lower bound on injectivity radius over all such points.
As for
C(x_{n},y_{n})
, we simply note that for
n
sufficiently large, the sum is empty because
f
is pseudo-Anosov and so for instance, it follows that the distance in the curve complex between
\mu^{\circ}_{x_{n}}
and
\mu^{\circ}_{y_{n}}
goes to infinity. In particular, these pants decompositions share no curves in common.
Hence, (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) simplify to

(6.8)

\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\cdot d_{\mathcal{T}}(x_{n},y_{n})\asymp\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(x_{n},y_{n})}{n}.

In what follows, we fix a short marking
\mu=\mu_{x}
, and let
\mu_{x_{n}}=f^{-n}(\mu)
and
\mu_{y_{n}}=f^{n}(\mu)
be short markings at
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
, respectively.
For any subsurface
Y
, we use the shorthand notation
\pi_{Y}(x_{n})
to mean
\pi_{Y}(\mu_{x_{n}})=\pi_{Y}(f^{-n}(\mu))
, and similarly for
y_{n}
. In particular,
d_{Y}(x_{n},y_{n})
denotes the diameter in
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
of the projections of
f^{-n}(\mu)\cup f^{n}(\mu)
. Similarly,
d_{S}(x_{n},y_{n})
denotes the distance in
\mathcal{AC}(S)
between the short markings at
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
.

Recall from
Definition

6.2
that every proper essential subsurface
Y
is
n
–separated (with respect to the marking
\mu=\mu_{x}
) for sufficiently large
n
.
Thus, by
Lemma

6.3
, if
d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})
is sufficiently large, then
Y
eventually corresponds to a summand of
A(x_{n},y_{n})
. By
Lemma

6.4
, there are
n
distinct translates of
Y
by powers of
f
that correspond to summands of
A(x_{n},y_{n})
.

Combining the two lemmas, we conclude that for each proper non-annular subsurface
Y
such that
d_{Y}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})
is sufficiently large, there are at least
n
distinct summands of
A(x_{n},y_{n})
that are all within
2M
of
d_{Y}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})
. For each annular subsurface
A
such that
d_{A}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})
is sufficiently large, there are at least
n
distinct summands of
A(x_{n},y_{n})
that are all within a bounded distance of
\log d_{A}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})
. Finally, by (

), we also have
d_{S}(x_{n},y_{n})\geq 2n\cdot\ell_{S}(f)-\kappa_{2}
in the full surface
S
.
Putting it all together gives

A(x_{n},y_{n})\geq 2n\cdot\ell_{S}(f)-\kappa_{2}+\sum_{\langle f\rangle Y}\Big(n\,\big[d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big]\Big)+\sum_{\langle f\rangle A}\Big(n\,\big[\log d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big]\Big).

Note that the sums on the right hand side range over
f
-orbits of subsurfaces.
Now, dividing by

, letting

, and applying (

) establishes the lower bound on
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)
in Proposition
6.1
.

For the upper bound on
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)
, suppose
Y\subset S
is a proper subsurface such that
d_{Y}(x_{n},y_{n})
is large enough to survive the cutoff in

. In particular, we may assume that
d_{Y}(x_{n},y_{n})>M
.
By the bounded geodesic image theorem,

2.3
,
\partial Y
must intersect the

-neighborhood of a given geodesic in
\mathcal{AC}(S)
from
x_{n}
to
y_{n}
. By an identical argument, any translate
f^{i}(Y)
satisfying
d_{f^{i}Y}(x_{n},y_{n})>M
must also have its boundary intersecting the
1
-neighborhood of the same geodesic from
x_{n}
to
y_{n}
. Thus, by the argument in the paragraph surrounding (
6.2
), the number of such translates is coarsely bounded by
n
.

In particular, the number of
\langle f\rangle
–translates of
Y
that make a nonzero contribution to
A(x,y)
is coarsely bounded by
n
.

To estimate the summand
[d_{Y}(x_{n},y_{n})]
, we use
a theorem of Rafi
[
42
, Theorem 6.1]

which states that the projection of a Teichmüller geodesic to
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
does not backtrack, and in particular, the diameter of a projection of a sub-geodesic can be at most the diameter of the projection of an entire geodesic, up to some uniform additive error,
J\geq 0
. Since
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
lie along the Teichmuller geodesic from
\lambda^{-}
to
\lambda^{+}
, Rafi’s result gives

d_{Y}(x_{n},y_{n})\leq d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})+J.

Combining this fact with (
6.2
) gives

A(x_{n},y_{n})\prec 2n\cdot\ell_{S}(f)+\sum_{\langle f\rangle Y}\Big(n\,\big[d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big]\Big)+\sum_{\langle f\rangle A}\Big(n\,\big[\log d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big]\Big).

The upper bound on
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f)
follows from dividing both sides by
n
and taking a limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

We will also need the following result of Choi and Rafi
[

, Corollary D]
, which is also an application of Rafi’s distance formula
[

, Theorem 2.4]
.

Proposition 6.5

(Choi–Rafi)
.

For any pair of markings

on

\log i(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\asymp d_{S}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+\sum_{Y}\big[d_{Y}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\big]+\sum_{A}\big[\log\left(d_{A}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\right)\big],

where the first sum is over proper non-annular subsurfaces of
S
, and the second sum is over annuli.

We remark that the statement of
[
10
, Corollary D]
includes specified cutoffs, and does not assert that the sum is hierarchy-like. However, the essential ingredient in the proof of
[
10
, Corollary D]
is Rafi’s distance formula
[
42
, Theorem 2.4]
, and that distance formula is hierarchy-like by
[
42
, Remark 2.5]
.

For the next proposition, which is the most important and novel portion of the proof of

Definition 7.2
.

Let

\mathring{S}

\mathring{S}
and the second is over annuli.
Recall from
Section

2.3
that projection distances are defined as a diameter, and the diameter of the empty set is
0

We observe that the proof of

Proposition

7.1
already involves the sum
\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
f
be a pseudo-Anosov, and let
\sigma
be any edge of the veering triangulation. Then

\log i(\sigma,f(\sigma))\asymp\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma)),

with the convention that
\log(0)=0

Proof.

Observe that if

\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma))\asymp 0

Now, suppose that

\sigma>f(\sigma)
since our convention is that

stretches horizontally. Let
P=P(\sigma,f(\sigma))
be the unique pocket defined by
\sigma
and

, as in

4.9
. We extend the top and bottom partial sections, namely
P(\sigma,f(\sigma))^{+}
and
P(\sigma,f(\sigma))^{-}

and

Now,

\displaystyle\log i(\sigma,f(\sigma))

\displaystyle\asymp\log i(P(\sigma,f(\sigma)^{+}\!,\,P(\sigma,f(\sigma))^{-})

\displaystyle\asymp\mathcal{S}(P(\sigma,f(\sigma)^{+}\!,\,P(\sigma,f(\sigma))^{-})

\displaystyle\asymp\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma)).

The first coarse equality is

4.10
. The second coarse is

6.5
, where we are treating the ideal triangulations
T(\sigma,f(\sigma))^{+}
and
T(\sigma,f(\sigma))^{-}
as markings; recall that all intersections (hence, all subsurface distances) come from the top and bottom of the pocket
P
.
The third coarse equality follows from
Lemma

4.11
, together with
Lemma

2.2
.
∎

We can now prove the following proposition, which is the one crucial step of the proof that requires

to be strongly irreducible.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
f
be strongly irreducible, and let
T
be a section.
Let
\sigma
be an edge of
T
for which the intersection number
i(\sigma,f(\sigma))
is largest. Then

\log i(\sigma,f(\sigma))\asymp\log i(T,f(T)),

with the convention that
\log(0)=0
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma^{\prime}
be an arbitrary edge of
T
. By
Lemma

7.3
, combined with the hypothesis that
\sigma
is the edge of
T
that maximizes the intersection number
i(\sigma,f(\sigma))
, we have

\mathcal{S}(\sigma^{\prime},f(\sigma^{\prime}))\asymp\log i(\sigma^{\prime},f(\sigma^{\prime}))\leq\log i(\sigma,f(\sigma))\asymp\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma)).

Comparing the first and last terms, we obtain

(7.5)

\mathcal{S}(\sigma^{\prime},f(\sigma^{\prime}))\prec\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma))

for every
\sigma^{\prime}\subset T
.

Next, we relate
\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma))
to
\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))
by a partitioning argument, as follows.
Since
f
is strongly irreducible, every subsurface
\mathring{Y}
is overlapped by
f
.
Thus, by
Lemma

4.4
,
every subsurface
\mathring{Y}
that contributes to the sum
\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))
must be cut by both
\sigma^{\prime}
and
f(\sigma^{\prime})
for
some

\sigma^{\prime}
of
T
, depending on
\mathring{Y}
. Hence, we have
d_{\mathring{Y}}(T,f(T))=d_{\mathring{Y}}(\sigma^{\prime},f(\sigma^{\prime}))+O(1)
for
this choice of
\sigma^{\prime}
.
Then we obtain

\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma))\leq\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))\prec\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\subset T}\mathcal{S}(\sigma^{\prime},f(\sigma^{\prime}))\prec\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma)).

Here, the first inequality is just the fact that
\sigma\subset T
. The next (coarse) inequality comes from decomposing
\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))
into a sum over edges: for each edge
\sigma^{\prime}
, let
\mathcal{S}(\sigma^{\prime},f(\sigma^{\prime}))
be the sub-sum of
\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))
such that
\sigma^{\prime}
and
f(\sigma^{\prime})
cut the subsurface in question. Since each subsurface with an associated summand in
\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))
contributes to a summand in at least one such

6|\chi(S)|
edges of

,
combined with (
7.5
).

Since the first and last terms of the above equation are equal, we obtain

\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma))\asymp\mathcal{S}(T,f(T)).

Putting it all together gives

\log i(\sigma,f(\sigma))\asymp\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma))\asymp\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))\asymp\log i(T,f(T)),

where the last coarse equality is again
Proposition

6.5
.
∎

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
T
be an annular avoiding
f
–section. Let
\sigma
be an edge of
T
for which the intersection number

is largest. Then

For one direction of the estimate, observe that every nonsingular point
of

. Furthermore, the number of singular points is at most

Thus

\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)\leq\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\subset T}i(\sigma^{\prime},f(\sigma^{\prime}))+2|\chi(S)|\leq|9\chi(S)|i(\sigma,f(\sigma))+2|\chi(S)|.

The second inequality holds because there are at most
|9\chi(S)|

, and

Now, consider the other direction.
Since

imply that the immersed spanning rectangle for

Remarks on the argument

Next, we make two remarks on the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Remark 7.6
.

The above proof of
Theorem

1.1
is slightly inefficient. Our line of argument applies
Proposition

6.5
to go from
\log i(T,f(T))
to
\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))
in the proof of
Proposition

7.1
, and then applies
Proposition

6.5
again to go from
\mathcal{S}(T,f(T))
back to

in the proof of

. It would be more efficient to carry the quantity

all through the proof. However, this would come at the cost of making the statements of

and

potentially harder to read and absorb.

Remark 7.7
.

There is a straightforward upper bound on
\log\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)
in terms of
\ell_{\mathcal{T}(S)}(f)
that does not use the methods developed in this paper. To see this, take a Markov partition for
f\colon S\to S
, with
n
rectangles. Using the method in the Bestvina–Handel algorithm
[
5
]
, we may take
n
bounded by a linear function of
|\chi(S)|
. Let
A
be the
n\times n
transition matrix for the partition. Then the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue of
A
is the stretch factor
\lambda
, where
\ell_{\mathcal{T}(S)}(f)=\log\lambda
. Since
\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)
is within an additive error of
\operatorname{trace}(A)
, and
\operatorname{trace}(A)\leq n\lambda
, we obtain

where

is linear in

. It follows that

\log\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)\leq\log\lambda(f)+O(1)=\ell_{\mathcal{T}(S)}(f)+O(1).

This estimate is stronger than the upper bound of

, because it does not involve any multiplicative error.

8.
Without strong irreducibility

When
f
is not strongly irreducible, it is no longer true that the log of the number of fixed points is comparable to Teichmüller translation length. Indeed, explicit counterexamples are produced
in
9.1
.

Recall from the introduction that
f

overlaps
an essential subsurface
Y\subset S
if
f(Y)
intersects
Y
essentially.
Thus, strong irreducibility is equivalent to every subsurface (including
S
itself) being overlapped. In the absence of strong irreducibility, one might still hope to relate the log of the number of fixed points to the action of the map on the subsurfaces which
are
overlapped. For this, let
\mathcal{D}_{f}
be the set of (isotopy classes of) subsurfaces that
f
overlaps:

\mathcal{D}_{f}=\{Y:f\text{ overlaps the subsurface }Y\}.

Theorem 8.1

(Fixed points; general case)
.

Let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov. Then

\log\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)\asymp\ell_{S}(f)+\sum_{\langle f\rangle Y\subset\mathcal{D}_{f}\smallsetminus\{S\}}\big[d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big]+\sum_{\langle f\rangle A\subset\mathcal{D}_{f}}\big[\log d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big],

where the first sum is over
f
–orbits of proper non-annular subsurfaces and the second is over
f
–orbits of annuli. In each case, we take exactly one subsurface per orbit.

Remark 8.2
.

We emphasize that this theorem does not follow from the arguments in previous sections. Indeed,

Proposition

7.4
uses strong irreducibility in a crucial way to
deduce a comparison between
i(T,f(T))
for a section
T
to
i(\sigma,f(\sigma))
for a single edge
\sigma\subset T
. The proof of this proposition requires a careful consideration of all the subsurfaces at once. In what follows, we will argue more locally, relating the action of the map
f
on a particular overlapped subsurface
Y\subset S
to the action of
f
on all subsurfaces of the
punctured
surface
\mathring{S}
that fill to
Y
.

Going in the other direction, the combination of
Theorem

8.1
and
Proposition

6.1
immediately implies
Theorem

1.1
, because every subsurface of
S
is overlapped when
f
is strongly irreducible.
However, the arguments that prove
Theorem

8.1
are far more technical than the proofs in previous sections.
In addition, the arguments that will follow also rely on many of the lemmas in the preceding sections.

The proof uses the following setup. For any essential subsurface
\mathring{W}\subset\mathring{S}
, recall from
Section

4.2
that we defined
\operatorname{Fill}_{S}(\mathring{W})
be the subsurface
Y\subset S
obtained by capping off any components of
\partial\mathring{W}
that are inessential in
S
, as well as any punctures of
\mathring{W}
that are filled in
S
. Now, given an essential subsurface
Y\subset S
, we define
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
to be the collection of (isotopy classes of) subsurfaces of
\mathring{S}
that fill to
Y
:

\mathcal{F}_{Y}=\{\mathring{W}\subset\mathring{S}:\operatorname{Fill}_{S}(\mathring{W})=Y\}/\text{(isotopy)}.

A priori, the set
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
may be difficult to control: for example, it contains many subsurfaces created by complicated point-pushes around the singularities of
S
contained in and outside of
Y
.
However, we primarily care about subsurfaces
\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}
such that
d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})
is large. By
Lemma

4.1
, any such subsurface
\mathring{W}
is
q
-compatible and has a canonical
q
–geodesic representative.
In fact, when
Y
itself is
q
–compatible (the only case of interest here), we can define a canonical element of
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
using the
q
–geometry:

\mathring{Y}=\mathrm{int}_{q}Y\smallsetminus\mathrm{sing}(q)\subset\mathring{S}.

The following proposition says that distances in

can be estimated in terms of precisely the elements of
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
for which
d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})
is sufficiently large.

Proposition 8.3

(Distance shuffling)
.

Puncturing
S
to produce
\mathring{S}
affects subsurface distances as follows:

(1)

For the full surface
S
, we have

\ell_{S}(f)\asymp\ell_{\mathring{S}}(\mathring{f})+\sum_{\langle f\rangle\mathring{W}\subset\mathcal{F}_{S}\smallsetminus\{\mathring{S}\}}\big[d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big],

where the sum is over
f
–orbits of proper subsurfaces that fill to
S
.

(2)

For any proper non-annular subsurface
Y\subsetneq S
, we have

d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\asymp\sum_{\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}}\big[d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\big].

(3)

For any essential annulus
A\subset S
, any subsurface
\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{A}
with

must be a single annulus, satisfying

d_{A}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})=d_{\mathring{W}}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-}).

8.1.
Applying distance shuffling

We will next apply

.
To begin the argument, recall that
\mathcal{D}_{f}
is the set of
subsurfaces that are overlapped by

.
In parallel with this, define
\mathring{\mathcal{D}}_{f}
to be the set of essential subsurfaces

The following lemma shows that (under a mild distance hypothesis) the subsurfaces in

are exactly the ones that fill to elements of

.

The sets

and

For the first bullet, if

For the second bullet, suppose that

,

\mathrm{int}_{\tau}\mathring{W}
. Since
d_{\mathring{W}}(\sigma,f(\sigma))\geq 10
, both
\sigma
and
f(\sigma)
intersect
\mathrm{int}_{\tau}\mathring{W}
, hence both edges must lie above or below
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
. If both
\sigma
and
f(\sigma)
lie above
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
, then

4.5
implies both edges are
3

, hence

, a contradiction. The same contradiction arises if both
\sigma
and
f(\sigma)
lie below
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
. Since

translates downward, we conclude that
\sigma
lies above
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
and
f(\sigma)
lies below
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
.

Applying

, we learn that
f(\sigma)
lies below
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
and above
f(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
. Thus some saddle connection in
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
must cross the spanning rectangle of
f(\sigma)
from top to bottom, while some saddle connection in
f(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
must cross the spanning rectangle of
f(\sigma)
from left to right. It follows that
\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W}
intersects
f(\partial_{\tau}\mathring{W})
essentially, hence

overlaps
\mathring{W}
.
∎

Corollary 8.7
.

For any veering edge
\sigma
, we have
\mathcal{S}(\sigma,f(\sigma))\asymp\mathcal{S}_{\mathring{\mathcal{D}_{f}}}(\sigma,f(\sigma))
.

Proof.

By

∎

Definition 8.13

Let

Let

be a

\mathring{W}

For any curve/arc

Lemma 8.15
.

Let
Y\subset S
be a non-annular,
q
–compatible subsurface. Given essential arcs or curves
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{AC}(Y)
, let
\mathring{\alpha},\mathring{\beta}
be arbitrary lifts to
\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})
obtained by isotoping
\alpha,\beta
off the singularities in the interior of
\mathrm{int}_{q}Y
.
Then

d_{Y}(\alpha,\beta)\prec\sum_{\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}}\left[d_{\mathring{W}}(\mathring{\alpha},\mathring{\beta})\right].

Proof.

First, observe that
d_{Y}(\alpha,\beta)\leq d_{\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})}(\mathring{\alpha},\mathring{\beta})
, because the inclusion-induced map
\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})\to\mathcal{AC}(Y)
is
1
–Lipschitz. Thus the proof will follow from the following distance formula in
\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})
:

(8.4)

d_{\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})}(\mathring{\alpha},\mathring{\beta})\asymp\sum_{\mathring{W}\in\Omega(\mathring{Y})}\big[d_{\mathring{W}}(\mathring{\alpha},\mathring{\beta})\big]=\sum_{\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}}\big[d_{\mathring{W}}(\mathring{\alpha},\mathring{\beta})\big].

The second equality of (
8.4
) is just
Lemma

8.14
. However, the first (coarse) equality is nontrivial. We will derive this estimate from a theorem of Vokes
[
47
, Corollary 1.2]
which, for a family of graphs that will cover our situation, simplifies the axioms for hierarchical hyperbolicity and establishes the desired distance formula.

Vokes works in the general setting of
twist-free multicurve graphs
; see
[
47
, Definition 2.1]
.
Here are the five defining properties of a twist-free multicurve graph
\mathcal{G}(\mathring{Y})
, combined with the reasons why
\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})
or a sufficiently close variant qualifies:

(1)

The graph
\mathcal{G}(\mathring{Y})
is connected.
In our setting, the connectedness of
\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})
follows
from a lemma of Putman
[
39
, Lemma 2.1]
, by a standard argument
using the change-of-coordinates principle.

(2)

Each vertex of
\mathcal{G}(\mathring{Y})
represents a multicurve in
\mathring{Y}
.
In our setting, this hypothesis is satisfied by the induced subgraph
\mathcal{C}^{s}(Y)
whose vertices are surviving curves. Since every arc in
\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})
is distance
1
from a curve, this is sufficient for the desired distance formula.

(3)

The action of
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathring{Y})
on the surface
\mathring{Y}
induces an isometric action on
\mathcal{G}(\mathring{Y})
.
Kopreski
[
21
]
has loosened this hypothesis to only require an action of the pure mapping class group
\textup{P}\mathrm{Mod}(\mathring{Y})
. In our setting, the pure mapping class group
\textup{P}\mathrm{Mod}(\mathring{Y})
fixes every (singular) point of
Y\smallsetminus\mathring{Y}
, and therefore acts isometrically on
\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})
.

(4)

There exists a universal bound on the intersection number
i(a,b)
for any pair of adjacent vertices

of
\mathcal{G}(\mathring{Y})
.
In our setting, adjacent vertices of
\mathcal{AC}^{s}(\mathring{Y})
represent disjoint curves/arcs.

(5)

The set of witnesses for
\mathcal{G}(\mathring{Y})
does not contain annuli.
In our setting, this holds by
Lemma

8.14
, because an annulus cannot fill to the non-annular surface
Y
.

Therefore, Vokes’s
[
47
, Corollary 1.2]
implies the distance formula (
8.4
), concluding the proof.
∎

Then

Now, (

\ell_{S}(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d_{S}(\pi_{S}(T),\pi_{S}(f^{n}(T))}{n}.

Lemma 8.17
.

Let
T
be any section. Then, for any

,

d_{S}(\pi_{S}(T),\pi_{S}(f^{n}(T))\asymp\sum_{\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{S}}\big[d_{\mathring{W}}(T,f^{n}(T))\big],

where the implicit constants are independent of

.

Proof.

By construction, every curve or arc
\gamma\in\pi_{S}(T)
has its
q
–geodesic representative supported in
T
, so
\mathbf{s}\circ c(\gamma)\subset T
. Thus, for every
\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{S}
,

implies that
\pi_{\mathring{W}}^{q}\circ\pi_{S}(T)
lies within distance

\pi_{\mathring{W}}(T)
. Accordingly, applying
Lemma

8.12
to
\alpha=\pi_{S}(T)
and

\beta=\pi_{S}(f^{n}(T))

gives

\displaystyle d_{S}(\pi_{S}(T),\pi_{S}(f^{n}(T))

\displaystyle\succ\sum_{\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{S}}\left[d_{\mathring{W}}\Big(\pi_{\mathring{W}}^{q}\circ\pi_{S}(T),\pi_{\mathring{W}}^{q}\circ\pi_{S}(f^{n}(T))\Big)\right]

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{S}}\big[d_{\mathring{W}}(T,f^{n}(T))\big].

For the other direction, observe again that any curve or arc
\gamma\in\pi_{S}(T)
is a
q
–geodesic consisting of saddle connections in

Thus

: one that circumnavigates every singular point on the left, and the other on the right. We define
\mathring{\gamma}
to be the multi-curve or multi-arc consisting of these two lifts, and observe that
\mathring{\gamma}
has bounded intersection number with

\mathring{\pi}_{S}(T)

. We follow the same process to get a lift

It follows that for every

\asymp\sum_{\mathring{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{S}}\big[d_{\mathring{W}}(T,f^{n}(T))\big],

9.
Examples and constructions

In this section, we construct various examples that, in spirit, demonstrate the sharpness of our results and the necessity of our hypotheses.

In the following construction, we describe a family of pseudo-Anosovs whose fixed points do not grow, but whose Teichmüller translation lengths become arbitrarily large. Of course, these maps are not strongly irreducible.

Construction 9.1

(Twisting along nonoverlapped annuli)
.

Let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism and let
\alpha
be a curve in
S
such that
f(\alpha)
is disjoint from
\alpha
. Then there is an annulus
A
about
\alpha

that is disjoint from
f(A)
; in particular,
A
contains no fixed points of
f
. Therefore, if we denote by
t_{\alpha}
a Dehn twist homeomorphism supported on
A
, the map
f\circ t_{\alpha}^{n}
has no more fixed points than
f
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, for all but finitely many
n
, these maps are isotopic to pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms
f_{n}
with the property that
\#\operatorname{Fix}(f_{n})\leq\#\operatorname{Fix}(f\circ t_{\alpha}^{n})\leq\#\operatorname{Fix}(f)
. We note that the subsurface distances in the annular complex
\mathcal{AC}(\alpha)
between the invariant laminations of
f_{n}
go to infinity as
n\to\infty
, hence
\ell_{\mathcal{T}}(f_{n})\to\infty
.

One can make the discrepancy between fixed points and translation length even more dramatic, as in the following example of pseudo-Anosovs with
no
fixed points:

Construction 9.2

(Fibered cones)
.

Let
M
be a fibered hyperbolic
3
–manifold such that
b_{1}(M)\geq 2
. Let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be distinct fibers in the same fibered cone of
H^{1}(M)
, with associated pseudo-Anosov monodromies
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. Let
\Phi
be the pseudo-Anosov flow on
M
defined by this fibered cone. Then, after an isotopy,
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are cross-sections of
\Phi
(i.e. they transversely meet every orbit) and the first return maps to these surfaces are
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, respectively.
Observe that any closed orbit of
\Phi
intersects a fiber
S_{i}
at least once, and that fixed points of
f_{i}
correspond to closed orbits that intersect
S_{i}
exactly once.

Now, let
S_{3}
be the cross-section obtained from
S_{1},S_{2}
by smoothing their intersections while maintaining transversality to
\Phi
. Thus
S_{3}
represents the homology class
[S_{1}]+[S_{2}]
.
Then any closed orbit of
\Phi
intersects
S_{3}
at least twice, hence the first return map
f_{3}\colon S_{3}\to S_{3}
is a pseudo-Anosov without fixed points. Note that to make
S_{3}
connected, we must choose
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
so that
[S_{1}]+[S_{2}]
is not a multiple.

Moreover, if
\alpha
is a component of the intersection
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
that is essential in each
S_{i}
, then the smoothing
S_{3}
contains two disjoint preimages of
\alpha
, which are permuted under the first return map
f_{3}
. In other words, there is a curve on
S_{3}
which is disjoint from its image under
f_{3}
, hence
f_{3}
is fixed-point-free and not strongly irreducible.

Proof of
Proposition

1.2
.

By plugging the fixed-point-free map
f_{3}\colon S_{3}\to S_{3}
of
9.2
into the twisting procedure of
9.1
, we obtain infinitely many conjugacy classes of fixed-point-free pseudo-Anosovs on the same surface
S_{3}
, with dilatations going to
\infty
.
∎

In
9.1
, the growing Teichmüller translation length comes from iterating a Dehn twist, which does not arbitrarily increase the volume of the mapping torus. However, it is easy to generalize the construction to produce pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms whose number of fixed points stays bounded but whose mapping tori volume is unbounded:

Example 9.3

(Unbounded volume)
.

Let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov map such that
Y\subset S
is a non-annular, non-overlapped essential subsurface. That is,
f(Y)\cap Y=\emptyset
. In particular, since
Y
is non-annular,
Y
itself supports a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism
g\colon Y\to Y
. Then just as above, for all but finitely many
n
, the map
f\circ g^{n}
is isotopic to a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism whose number of fixed points is no greater than that of
f
. Moreover, it is easy to see (e.g. using the work of Brock
[
9
]
) that the volume of the mapping tori of
f\circ g^{n}
go to
\infty
.
