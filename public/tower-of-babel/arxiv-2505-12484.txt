Fourier Multipliers on Quasi-Banach Orlicz Spaces and Orlicz Modulation Spaces
Albin Petersson

Abstract
. We find that if a Fourier multiplier is continuous from L Φ 1 L^{\Phi_{1}} to L Φ 2 L^{\Phi_{2}} , then it is also continuous from M Φ 1 , Ψ M^{\Phi_{1},\Psi} to M Φ 2 , Ψ M^{\Phi_{2},\Psi} , where Φ 1 , Φ 2 , Ψ \Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\Psi are quasi-Young functions and Φ 1 \Phi_{1} fulfills the Δ 2 \Delta_{2} -condition. This result is applied to show that Mihlin’s Fourier multiplier theorem and Hörmander’s improvement hold in certain Orlicz modulation spaces. Lastly, we show that the Fourier multiplier with symbol m ⁡ ( ξ ) = e i ​ μ ​ ( ξ ) m(\xi)=e^{i\mu(\xi)} , where μ \mu is homogeneous of order α \alpha , is bounded on quasi-Banach Orlicz modulation spaces of order r r , assuming r ∈ ( d / ( d + 2 ) , 1 ] r\in\big(d/(d+2),1\big] and α ∈ ( d ⁡ ( 1 − r ) / r , 2 ] \alpha\in\big(d(1-r)/r,2\big] .

1.
Introduction

Fourier multipliers are obtained by performing a Fourier transform, multiplying by
a suitable function (which we call the symbol), and lastly performing an
inverse Fourier transform.
With
T
as the operator and
m
as the symbol, we formally write this as

T(f)=(\mathscr{F}^{-1}\circ m\cdot\circ\mathscr{F})(f).

Since
\partial_{j}f(x)=\mathscr{F}^{-1}[i\xi\cdot\hat{f}](x)

(where
\hat{f}=\mathscr{F}[f]
), partial differential operators with constant coefficients
are Fourier multipliers, and because of this relationship between
T
and its symbol

m
, we write
T=m(D)
.
Evidently, Fourier multipliers naturally appear when solving partial differential equations.
A fundamental question is whether a symbol
m
will give rise to a bounded operator
m(D)

on a certain function space.
In this paper, we investigate such boundedness conditions on (quasi-Banach) Orlicz spaces
and Orlicz modulation spaces.

Orlicz spaces, initially introduced by W. Orlicz
[
19
]
, are a
generalization of Lebesgue spaces which are, roughly speaking, obtained by replacing
the integrand
|f(x)|^{p}
in the expression
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}}^{p}
with
\Phi(|f(x)|)
,
where
\Phi
is a certain type of convex function.
In general,
\rho_{\Phi}(f)=\int\Phi(|f(x)|)dx
is not a norm (for instance,
it may fail to be homogeneous), hence one has to define the norm in a different way.
There are several (equivalent) ways to do this. Here, we use the Luxemburg norm

[
16
, 43]
given by

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\Phi}}=\inf\left\{\,\lambda>0\;\middle|\;\rho_{\Phi}(f/\lambda)\leq 1\,\right\}.

Thus, the Orlicz space
L^{\Phi}
is the space of measurable functions
f
such that

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\Phi}}
is finite.
If
\Phi(t)=t^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, then

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{\Phi}}=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{p}}
and
L^{\Phi}=L^{p}
.
Other examples of Orlicz spaces include
L^{\Phi_{1}}
and
L^{\Phi_{2}}
, where

\Phi_{1}(t)=t\log(1+t)
and
\Phi_{2}(t)=e^{t^{2}}-1
.

By replacing
L^{p}
norms with
L^{\Phi}
norms in the definitions of other spaces,
we obtain different Orlicz type spaces.
Among these are the so-called Orlicz modulation spaces, where the usual
L^{p,q}
norm
is replaced by the mixed Orlicz norm
L^{\Phi,\Psi}
. The usefulness of such Orlicz modulation spaces is illustrated by the following example.
Let
E_{\varphi}
be the entropy functional given by

E_{\varphi}(f)=-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V_{\varphi}f(x,\xi)|^{2}\log|V_{\varphi}f(x,\xi)|^{2}\,dxd\xi+\lVert V_{\varphi}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\log\lVert V_{\varphi}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Here,
V_{\varphi}
is the short-time Fourier transform and
\varphi
is a window function.
This functional appears when dealing with kinetic energy estimates in quantum systems
(cf.
[
12
]
).
While
E_{\varphi}
is not continuous on
M^{2}=L^{2}
, it is continuous on
M^{\Phi}
,
where
\Phi
is a Young function satisfying

\Phi(t)=-t^{2}\log t
for
0\leq t\leq e^{-3/2}
.
Moreover,
M^{\Phi}
is a dense subset of
L^{2}
, and for any
p<2
,

M^{p}\subseteq M^{\Phi}
, so that
M^{\Phi}
is, in some sense, a better setting for
the study of this functional than
M^{p}
is for any
p<2
.
See
[
8
, Section 3]
for more details.

Here, we consider Fourier multipliers on (quasi-Banach) Orlicz spaces and
Orlicz modulation spaces, fulfilling various conditions.
In particular, we obtain boundedness properties for Fourier multipliers with symbol

m(\xi)=e^{i\mu(\xi)}
, where
\mu
is homogeneous of order
\alpha\leq 2
.
Such operators appear when solving certain evolution equations.
Consider the initial value problem

\partial_{t}f(t,x)=i\mu(D)f(t,x)

where
f(0,x)=f_{0}(x)
.
For example, the free Dirac and Schrödinger equations can be described in this way, where

\mu
is homogeneous of order
1
and
2
, respectively.
Formally, the solution to the equation is given by
f(t,x)=e^{it\mu}f_{0}(x)
, hence
concerns of existence of solutions to the equation are directly linked to
the boundedness of the aforementioned operator
m(D)
.

2.
Preliminaries

We write
d
for dimension, and we let

\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}

be the set of natural numbers.
For multi-indices
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, meaning
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
,

\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})
where
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
,
j=1,\dots,d
,
we write
\alpha\leq\beta
to mean
\alpha_{j}\leq\beta_{j}
for every
j=1,\dots,d
.
Further, we let
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}
, and for
r\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let

\alpha^{r}=\alpha_{1}^{r_{1}}\dots\alpha_{d}^{r_{d}}
.
Moreover, for
j,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we let

(2.1)

\displaystyle Q_{r}(j)

\displaystyle=j+[0,r]^{d},

\displaystyle Q(j)

\displaystyle=Q_{1}(j),\text{ and }

\displaystyle Q(j,k)

\displaystyle=Q(j)\times Q(k).

The Fourier transform of
f
is denoted
\mathscr{F}[f]
or
\hat{f}
, and is given by

\mathscr{F}[f](\xi)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-i\langle x,\xi\rangle}dx.

Recall that a
quasi-norm
of order
r\in(0,1]
, or an
r
-norm
, to
the vector space
\mathcal{B}
is a functional
\Lambda
on
\mathcal{B}
for
which the following holds:

(1)

\Lambda(f)\geq 0
for all
f\in\mathcal{B}
and

\Lambda(f)=0
if and only if
f=0
;

(2)

\Lambda(\alpha f)=|\alpha|\rho(f)

for all
f\in\mathcal{B}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
;

(3)

\Lambda(f+g)^{r}\leq\Lambda(f)^{r}+\Lambda(g)^{r}
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{B}
.

Although quasi-norms are typically defined in a different way, we use the terms
“quasi-norm of order
r
” and “
r
-norm” interchangeably, and justify this choice
by the Aoki-Rolewicz theorem (cf.
[
1
,
21
]
).

A
quasi-Banach space of order
r
or
r
-Banach space
is a
complete quasi-normed space, meaning, it is complete with respect to
the topology induced by a quasi-norm of order
r
.
For more information about quasi-Banach spaces, see
[
15
]
.

2.1.
Lebesgue type spaces

For
p\in(0,\infty]
, let
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=L^{p}
denote the usual Lebesgue space with norm

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\equiv\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}}\equiv\begin{cases}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p<\infty,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle{\esssup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}}|f(x)|,&p=\infty,\end{cases}

where
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is a Lebesgue measurable function.
The norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{p}}
simultaneously imposes decay and growth conditions on
the functions in
L^{p}
which depend on the variable
p
.
Using different such conditions with respect to different variables, we arrive at
the definition of mixed norm Lebesgue spaces, which we recall below.

{defn}

The
mixed norm Lebesgue space

L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
consists of all
Lebesgue measurable functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}\equiv\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p,q}}\equiv\lVert f_{2}^{p}\rVert_{L^{q}}

is finite, where

f_{2}^{p}(y)=\lVert f(\cdot,y)\rVert_{L^{p}}.

For a window function
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\{0\}
, we let

V_{\varphi}\colon\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
denote the
short-time Fourier transform

given by

V_{\varphi}f(x,\xi)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int f(y)\overline{\varphi(y-x)}e^{-i\langle y,\xi\rangle}\,dy.

We recall further the following definition.

{defn}

The
modulation space

M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consists of all
tempered distributions
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\lVert f\rVert_{M^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\equiv\lVert f\rVert_{M^{p,q}}\equiv\lVert V_{\varphi}f\rVert_{L^{p,q}}

is finite, where
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\{0\}
is a window function.

{rem}

Observe that the norm

\lVert f\rVert_{M^{p,q}}^{*}\equiv\lVert\tilde{V}_{\varphi}f\rVert_{L^{p,q}},

with

\tilde{V}_{\varphi}f(x,\xi)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int f(y+x)\overline{\varphi(y)}e^{-i\langle y,\xi\rangle}dy,

is equivalent to
\lVert f\rVert_{M^{p,q}}
, where
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}\setminus\{0\}
.

By letting
L_{*}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
consist of all
Lebesgue measurable functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\lVert f\rVert_{L_{*}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}\equiv\lVert f\rVert_{L_{*}^{p,q}}\equiv\lVert f_{1}^{q}\rVert_{L^{p}}

is finite, where

f_{1}^{q}(x)=\lVert f(x,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{q}},

we obtain the following definition.

{defn}

The space
W^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consists of all
tempered distributions
f\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\lVert f\rVert_{W^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\equiv\lVert f\rVert_{W^{p,q}}\equiv\lVert V_{\varphi}f\rVert_{L_{*}^{p,q}}

is finite, where
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\{0\}
is a window function.

Analogous to the
L^{p}
spaces, we recall that
the
discrete Lebesgue spaces

\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
consist of
sequences
a=\{a(j)\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
for which

\lVert a\rVert_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\equiv\lVert a\rVert_{\ell^{p}}\equiv\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|a(j)|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&0<p<\infty\\
\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|a(j)|,&p=\infty\end{cases}

is finite. On this topic, we recall further.

{defn}

The
discrete mixed Lebesgue space

\ell^{p,q}(\mathbb{Z}^{2d})
consists of
sequences
a=\left\{a(j,k)\right\}_{j,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
such that

\lVert a\rVert_{\ell^{p,q}(\mathbb{Z}^{2d})}\equiv\lVert a\rVert_{\ell^{p,q}}\equiv\lVert a_{2}^{p}\rVert_{\ell^{q}}

is finite, where
a_{2}^{p}(k)=\lVert a(\cdot,k)\rVert_{\ell^{p}}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Parallel to
L_{*}^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, we let
\ell_{*}^{p,q}(\mathbb{Z}^{2d})
consist of
all sequences
a=\left\{a(j,k)\right\}_{j,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
such that

\lVert a\rVert_{\ell_{*}^{p,q}(\mathbb{Z}^{2d})}\equiv\lVert a\rVert_{\ell_{*}^{p,q}}\equiv\lVert a_{1}^{q}\rVert_{\ell^{p}}

is finite, where
a_{1}^{q}(j)=\lVert a(j,\cdot)\rVert_{\ell^{q}}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

To finish our excursion into definitions of Lebesgue spaces, modulation spaces, and
Wiener amalgam spaces, we have the following.

{defn}

Let
\mathcal{B}=\ell^{p,q}(\mathbb{Z}^{2d})
or
\mathcal{B}=\ell_{*}^{p,q}(\mathbb{Z}^{2d})
,
0<p,q\leq\infty
.
The
Wiener space

W^{r}(\mathcal{B})
consists of
all functions
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\lVert F\rVert_{W^{r}(\mathcal{B})}\equiv\lVert a\rVert_{\mathcal{B}}<\infty,

where

a(j,k)=\lVert F\rVert_{L^{r}(Q(j,k))},\quad j,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

with
Q(j,k)
as in (
2.1
).

{rem}

Note that, for any window function
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}\setminus\{0\}
, there exists
a constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}\lVert V_{\varphi}\cdot\rVert_{L^{p,q}}\leq\lVert V_{\varphi}\cdot\rVert_{W^{\infty}(\ell^{p,q})}\leq C\lVert V_{\varphi}\cdot\rVert_{L^{p,q}}

so that the norms are equivalent when restricted to short-time Fourier transforms.
Note also that this is independent of the choice of window function
\varphi
,
in the sense that different choices for
\varphi
lead to equivalent norms
(cf.
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
).
In a similar manner,
\lVert V_{\varphi}\cdot\rVert_{L_{*}^{p,q}}
and

\lVert V_{\varphi}\cdot\rVert_{W^{\infty}(\ell_{*}^{p,q})}
are also equivalent.
See
[
7
]
(in particular Theorem 3.3) for more details.

Lastly, we recall the definition of a Fourier multiplier, which plays a pivotal role
in this paper.

{defn}

Let
m\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then the
Fourier multiplier

m(D)\colon\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
symbol

m
is given by

m(D)=\mathscr{F}^{-1}\circ(m\cdot)\circ\mathscr{F}.

Observe that if, for example,
m\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
1\leq p<\infty
, then

m(D)f(x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}m(\xi)\hat{f}(\xi)e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}d\xi,\quad f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

2.2.
Quasi-Young functions and quasi-Orlicz spaces

We recall the following definitions of Young functions and quasi-Young functions.

{defn}

Let
\Phi
be a function from
[0,\infty)
to
[0,\infty]
.
Then
\Phi
is called a
Young function
if

(1)

\Phi
is convex,

(2)

\Phi(0)=0
,

(3)

\Phi(t)<\infty
for some
t>0
,

(4)

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(t)=+\infty
.

{defn}

A function
\Phi
from
[0,\infty)
to
[0,\infty]

is called a
quasi-Young function
if
there exists
r\in(0,1]
such that
t\mapsto\Phi(t^{1/r})
is a Young function.
The largest such
r
is called the
order
of
\Phi
.

Note that a quasi-Young function must be increasing.
The concept of a quasi-Young function is explored in
[
24
]
, but
can also be found under the name
s
-convex
N
-function
[
20
, 43]

(with the additional assumptions that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}=0
and

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}=\infty
).

We will briefly consider Lebesgue exponents.
These originally appeared in
[
22
]
, whence they are also known as
“Simonenko indices” (cf.
[
17
, 20]
).
Using the notations of
[
4
]
, we recall their definition
in the following form.

{defn}

Let
\Phi
be a quasi-Young function and let
\Omega=\{t>0\mid 0<\Phi(t)<\infty\}
.
Then the
Lebesgue exponents

p_{\Phi}
and
q_{\Phi}
are given by

\displaystyle p_{\Phi}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{cases}{\displaystyle{\sup_{t\in\Omega}\left(\frac{t\Phi_{+}^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\right)}},&\Omega=(0,\infty),\\[4.30554pt]
\infty,&\Omega\neq(0,\infty),\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle q_{\Phi}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{cases}{\displaystyle{\inf_{t\in\Omega}\left(\frac{t\Phi_{+}^{\prime}(t)}{\Phi(t)}\right)}},&\Omega\neq\emptyset,\\
\infty,&\Omega=\emptyset.\end{cases}

{rem}

Young functions are not necessarily differentiable, but being convex,
they are still semi-differentiable.
Since the definition above is the same whether one uses the left derivative or
the right derivative, we will simply choose to use the right derivative, arbitrarily.

{rem}

A Young function is said to fulfill the so-called
\Delta_{2}
-condition

(cf.
[
3
, 6]
) if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\Phi(2t)\leq C\Phi(t),\quad t\geq 0.

It can be shown that
\Phi
fulfills the
\Delta_{2}
-condition if and only if

p_{\Phi}<\infty
.
(This is a well-known result, but for an explicit proof,
see
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
, for instance.)

{rem}

Let
\Phi
be a quasi-Young function, let
r
be its order, and
let
\Psi=\Phi(t^{1/r})
, so that
\Psi
is a Young function.
Then
q_{\Phi}=rq_{\Psi}
and
p_{\Phi}=rp_{\Psi}
.
Evidently, this means that
p_{\Phi}<\infty
if and only if
p_{\Psi}<\infty
,
meaning that
\Phi
fulfills the
\Delta_{2}
-condition if and only if

\Psi
fulfills the
\Delta_{2}
-condition.

We are now equipped to recall the definitions of the various Orlicz type spaces which
we will explore in this paper.
To simplify notations, we let

\rho_{\Phi}(f)=\lVert\Phi(|f|)\rVert_{L^{1}}.

{defn}

Let
\Phi
be a quasi-Young function.
The
Orlicz space

L^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consists of all
Lebesgue measurable functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\equiv\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\Phi}}\equiv\inf\left\{\,\lambda>0\;\middle|\;\rho_{\Phi}(f/\lambda)\leqslant 1\,\right\}

is finite.

{rem}

If
\Phi
is a quasi-Young function of order
r
and
\Psi(t)=\Phi(t^{1/r})

(meaning
\Psi
is a Young function), then

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{\Phi}}\equiv\lVert|\cdot|^{r}\rVert_{L^{\Psi}}^{1/r}
becomes a quasi-norm of
order
r
and
L^{\Phi}
a quasi-Banach space of order
r
.

{rem}

We note that the analysis of
[
9
, Chapter 3]
can be applied in
the case of quasi-Young functions fulfilling the
\Delta_{2}
