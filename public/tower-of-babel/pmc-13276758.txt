Downregulation of lactosylceramide synthases B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 alters glycosphingolipid profiles and enhances oxaliplatin sensitivity in colon cancer cells
Authors: Barbora Lujka, Tereza Šošolíková, Gerardo Vázquez-Gómez, Ondrej Kováč, Miroslav Machala, Alena Hyršlová Vaculová, Jan Vondráček
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlycosphingolipids (GSLs) represent an important group of bioactive lipids that serve both as structural components of membranes, and as signaling molecules involved in the regulation of numerous biological processes, such as control of cell growth and cell death. Their significant deregulation in colon cancer cells suggests their potential role in the disease progression. Lactosylceramide (LacCer) is a key intermediate in GSL metabolism and an important precursor of more complex GSLs. Here, we studied the impact of inhibition of LacCer synthesis (using selective targeting of specific LacCer synthases (LCS), B4GALT5 or B4GALT6, via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockdown) on control of the human DLD-1 colon adenocarcinoma cell proliferation, death and chemosensitivity. The downregulation of the selected enzymes, as verified by a decrease in respective mRNA and protein levels, significantly reduced levels of LacCers and several more complex GSLs (in particular, GM3 and GM1a gangliosides) in LCS-knockdown cells. Importantly, it enhanced the sensitivity of DLD-1 cells to the cytotoxic effects of oxaliplatin, a chemotherapy drug commonly used in colorectal cancer treatment. This was demonstrated by a general decrease in cell viability, an enhanced apoptotic cell death, caspase-8, −9, −3 cleavage/activation and cleavage of caspase substrates. We also observed a modulation of endoplasmic reticulum stress response, in particular decreased levels of ATF6, in LCS-knockdown cells treated with oxaliplatin. The present findings support the functional role of LCS in regulating the chemosensitivity of colon cancer cells towards the action of chemotherapy drugs, such as oxaliplatin, with possible further implications for the mechanisms underlying toxic action of platinum-based drugs in cancer cells.Keywords: Glycosphingolipids, Lactosylceramide synthesis, B4GALT5, B4GALT6, Oxaliplatin, Cell death, Colon cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health concern that ranks as the third most frequently diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related death globally, due to a poor prognosis in advanced stages and treatment resistance (Bray et al., 2024). Most of the sporadic CRC cases are associated with non-hereditary spontaneous mutations and epigenetic abnormalities, often without a known genetic predisposition or family history, which arise due to a wide range of risk factors (Giovannucci, 2002). Characterization of genetic and epigenetic aberrations, as well as the abnormal activation of associated signaling pathways, improves prognosis prediction and helps to optimize individual therapy selection; however, commonly used therapy protocols still involve mainly surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy using 5-fluorouracil, irinotecan or oxaliplatin (Shin et al., 2023). Chemosensitivity to these drugs thus plays a major role in a patient outcome, and the mechanisms underlying their toxic action have received a considerable attention.

Glycosphingolipids (GSLs), which form an important part of membrane structures, play crucial roles in cell signaling, membrane dynamics, and cell-to-cell communication. Their biosynthesis begins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) with the formation of ceramide (Cer) followed by sequential glycosylation by membrane-bound glycosyltransferases in the Golgi apparatus (GA). This process creates diverse GSL structures, with galactosylceramide (GalCer) and glucosylceramide (GlcCer) serving as primary precursors (Merrill Jr., 2011). Lactosylceramide synthases (LCS) then facilitate the addition of galactose to GlcCer, thus generating lactosylceramides (LacCer). The LCS family consists of seven β-1,4-galactosyltransferases (B4GALT1–7) localized in the lumen of the trans-GA, where they can access substrates required for glycosylation reactions. Among them, two isoforms, B4GALT5 and B4GALT6, which exhibit over 60% sequence similarity, were described as the principal enzymes responsible for LacCer synthesis in mammalian cells (Chatterjee et al., 1992; Qasba et al., 2008; Kumagai et al., 2010; Yoshihara et al., 2018). LacCer serve as the backbone for the biosynthesis of more complex GSLs, which are classified into different series, such as globo-, ganglio-, neolacto-, and lacto-series based on the common core oligosaccharide structures (D'Angelo et al., 2013).

Over the recent years, several studies have indicated that significant changes occurring in lipid metabolism and composition during the development of CRC may actively contribute to the disease progression (Machala et al., 2019). In particular, GSLs have been shown to modulate several hallmarks of cancer, as they may influence some key signaling pathways involved in cancer cell growth and survival (Jennemann et al., 2017) and contribute to immune evasion, since changes in their pattern on the cell surface may help tumor cells to avoid immune detection (Zhang et al., 2019). GSLs, including gangliosides, can also promote metastasis by regulating cell adhesion and migration, making them key players in both primary tumor growth and in further disease progression (Furukawa et al., 2019; Guan et al., 2009). Importantly, GSL synthesis has been documented to play a role in apoptosis resistance, as demonstrated in various cancer types (Roh et al., 2015; Stefanovic et al., 2016), such as e.g. increased B4GALT5 levels linked to drug-resistant phenotype in leukemia cells (Zhou et al., 2013). These observations might be particularly relevant for CRC, where chemoresistance remains a significant issue. Previously, it has been reported that formation of GlcCer, a precursor to LacCer and thus to more complex GSLs, plays a significant role in resistance of colon cancer cells to oxaliplatin (Madigan et al., 2020).

Oxaliplatin is a platinum-based chemotherapeutic agent widely used in the treatment of CRC, particularly at its advanced stages. It is commonly administered in combination with 5-fluorouracil and leucovorin, in treatment regimens such as FOLFOX (Shaham et al., 2025). Oxaliplatin exerts its effects by interfering with DNA replication and transcription, ultimately leading to cancer cell death. Although it remains a key component of standard treatment protocols, resistance to oxaliplatin poses a significant challenge in CRC therapy. Colon cancer cells may develop resistance through various mechanisms, including reduced drug uptake, increased drug efflux, enhanced DNA repair, or the inactivation of cell death pathways (Hsu et al., 2018). Despite the progress in our understanding of the CRC lipidome over the recent decades, many aspects of SL and GSL metabolism, particularly those concerning upregulated levels of specific groups of GSLs and their role in the action of commonly used cytostatic drugs including oxaliplatin, remain insufficiently explored. Our previous study has revealed a significant elevation of LacCer species in colon cancer cells isolated from CRC patients, as well as an upregulation of B4GALT5 and B4GALT6 enzymes responsible for LacCer synthesis, in patient tumor samples, as compared with non-tumor tissue (Procházková et al., 2020). This observation, together with another report indicating that increased expression of B4GALT5 may serve as a diagnostic biomarker in CRC (Chatterjee et al., 2019), suggests that inhibition of LacCer synthesis represents a potential target for overcoming drug resistance. This study shows that a suppression of LacCer synthesis, together with alterations of GSL composition, may significantly increase apoptotic response towards oxaliplatin. A deeper understanding of the role of LacCer in CRC may then offer new valuable strategies for the development of novel therapeutic approaches and improving patient outcomes.

Material and methods

Cell culture and treatments

The DLD-1 human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line was established from moderately to poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma of the sigmoid colon (Dexter et al., 1979), and it is widely used as an in vitro model of CRC. The cells, obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Biowest, Nuaillé, France). The cells were cultivated in TPP plastic (TPP, Trasadigen, Switzerland) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, passaged twice a week after exposure to EDTA/PBS (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and trypsin (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) solutions. Cell numbers and viability were assessed using a CASY Model TT Cell Counter and Analyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination. Twenty-four hours after seeding, cells were treated with oxaliplatin (5 mM stock solution in Mili-Q ultrapure water, #O9512, Sigma-Aldrich); the corresponding treatment concentrations are specified in the respective Figure legends. Tunicamycin (5 mM stock solution in DMSO, Tocris Bioscience, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used as a positive control for ER stress induction at final concentrations of 2.5 or 5 μg/ml.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing

A CRISPR/Cas9 expression vector with GFP (pSpCas9(BB)-2 A-GFP(PX458), Addgene, Cambridge, MA, USA) was used for the integration of guide RNA (gRNA) targeting exon 5 of B4GALT5 (target sequence TGCTCGTGGCGGTTCCGGAAGGG) and B4GALT6 (target sequence CAGGTAGATGATCCACATCGTGG) genes, respectively, into DLD-1 cells. For transfections, 4 × 105 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate and transfected in 60–70% confluency with a mixture of vector DNA and Lipofectamine3000 + P3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The selection of transfected cells with positive GFP signal was done by sorting the cells in a single cell suspension in a 96-well plate (BD Aria II Sorp, Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Then, single cell-derived clones were screened and identified based on the expression of B4GALT5 and B4GALT6. Genomic DNA from selected clones was isolated with QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and amplified using the following specific primers: for B4GALT5, left: CCCCTTCAAACCTCAGGCAA, right: AGATAGCCAGCCTCCCAGAA; for B4GALT6, left: AAAGCCACTCTGTGTCCTGG, right: TCTCTCGGTCTGCTCCATGA. Finally, the PCR amplification products were purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagene) and verified by sequencing (Eurofins Genomics, Ebersberg, Germany). The final sequencing analysis was done using the CRISP-ID software (Dehairs et al., 2016). The resulting DLD-1 cell clones with B4GALT5 knockdown (B4GALT5 KD) or B4GALT6 knockdown (B4GALT6 KD) were used in this study, together with appropriate controls: cells transfected with the CRISPR/Cas9 construct lacking the target-specific gRNA (ctrl), and control non-transfected wild-type cells.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using the High Pure RNA Isolation Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Penzberg, Germany) and quantified with a BioSpectrometer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The cDNA synthesis was carried out using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche Diagnostics) in a Biometra TOne 96G thermocycler (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the TaqMan assays provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific for the following genes: B4GALT5 (Assay ID: Hs00941041_m1), B4GALT6 (Assay ID: Hs00999574_m1), HSPA5 – binding immunoglobulin protein (BiP) (Assay ID: Hs00607129_gH), DDIT3 - C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) (Assay ID: Hs00358796_g1), ATF4 - activating transcription factor 4 (Assay ID: Hs00909569_g1) and TBP - TATA-box binding protein (Assay ID: Hs00427620_m1). TBP was used as a reference gene for relative quantification. Reactions were set up in 384-well plates (Roche Diagnostics) and run on a LightCycler® 480 thermocycler (Roche Diagnostics) under the following conditions: pre-incubation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 amplification cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 s, with a final cooling step at 40 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was normalized to TBP, and fold changes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

SDS-PAGE and Western blotting

The whole cell lysates were prepared, separated by SDS-PAGE, and Western blotting analysis was performed as described previously (Lauková et al., 2015). Immunodetection was carried out using the following primary antibodies: polyclonal rabbit anti-cleaved (Asp214) poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) (1:1000, #9541), polyclonal rabbit anti-cleaved (Asp175) caspase-3 (1:500, #9661), polyclonal rabbit anti-cleaved (Asp315) caspase-9 (1:250, #9505), monoclonal mouse anti-caspase-8 (1C12) (1:500, #9746), monoclonal mouse anti-CHOP (L63F7) (1:500, #2895), monoclonal rabbit anti-BiP (C50B12) (1:1000, #3177) (all from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), monoclonal mouse anti-ATF-6 (1: 500, #ab122897, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), polyclonal rabbit anti-B4GALT5 (1:1000, #BS61542, bioWORLD, Dublin, OH, USA), polyclonal rabbit anti-B4GALT6 (1:1000, custom-made antibody against synthetic peptide, Moravian Biotechnology, Brno, Czech Republic) antibodies. The proteins were recognized using horseradish peroxidase-labelled secondary antibodies: anti-mouse IgG (1:3000, #7076) or anti-rabbit IgG (1:3000, #7074) (both from Cell Signaling Technology), Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and chemiluminescence was detected using ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (BioRad, Hercules, CA, United States). A monoclonal mouse anti-β-actin (1:5000, #A5441, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to verify an equal protein amount in the samples. All results from Western blotting are representative of at least three independent experiments.

HPLC-MS/MS analysis

For lipid extraction, cells were harvested into glass tubes (13x100mm) with 1.5 ml of methanol (Honeywell, Seelze, Germany) and homogenized with sonic probe (about 20 strong strikes). Next, MS standards (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL, USA and Cayman chemical company, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) were added to the samples before total lipid extraction. A list of standards used in this study is provided in Suppl. Table 1. After sonication, 0.75 ml of chloroform (Honeywell) was added, solution was shortly homogenized with a sonic probe and incubated overnight at room temperature for extraction. After extraction, samples were shortly sonicated with a sonic probe, and they were placed in a heating box, evaporated under gentle flow of N2 and then reconstituted in 400 μl of methanol/chloroform 1:1 mixture. Samples were then transferred into vials, centrifuged, and analyzed on HPLC-MS/MS.

HPLC-MS/MS analyses of SL and GSLs were performed as follows: SL species (with exception of gangliosides) were separated by reversed phase HPLC (Dionex Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) on Gemini column C18, 5 μm, 250 × 4,6 mm (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) with flow rate 0.7 mL/min and gradient mobile phases RA/RB (RA = methanol/water 60/40, RB = methanol), starting ratio RA/RB 60/40 and total time 69 min, formic acid and ammonium formate as eluent additives. GlcCer and GalCer were separated by normal phase HPLC (Dionex Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) on Spherisorb column 5 μm Silica, 2.1 × 250 mm (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) with flow rate 0.3 mL/min and gradient mobile phases NA/NB (NA = acetonitrile/methanol 99/1, NB = acetonitrile/methanol/water 40/47/13) and total time 48 min, formic acid and ammonium formate as eluent additive. Gangliosides were separated by reverse phase HPLC (Dionex Ultimate 3000) on ARION ® C8 4.6 × 300 mm column (Chromservis, Prague, Czech Republic), flow rate 0.8 ml/min gradient mobile phase RA/RB (RA = water/methanol 90/10, RB = methanol/isopropanol 1/1), starting ratio RA/RB 61/39, total analysis time 60 min., ammonium formate as eluent additive. Tandem mass spectrometer (Hybrid triplequad QTRAP 4500, AB Sciex, Marlborough, MA, USA) was operated under the following conditions: ESI in positive mode, drying air, collision energy and fragmentor voltage were optimized for each species, spectrometer running in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) scan mode. GSL levels were quantified using an internal standard normalization approach. For each analyte, the chromatographic peak area was divided by the corresponding internal standard peak area, and the concentration was calculated based on the known amount of the internal standard. The obtained values were then normalized to the control group, which was set to 100%, and presented as a percentage of SL/GSL levels in wild-type and ctrl cells.

Cell counting and cell cycle analysis

Cells were harvested at 24, 48, 72 or 96 h post-seeding by detachment with EDTA/PBS followed by trypsinization. Cell numbers were determined using the CASY Model TT Cell Counter and Analyzer (Roche Diagnostics), and growth curves were generated based on the calculated cell density (cells/cm2). Cells were washed in PBS, centrifuged (200 g, 5 min), and fixed in 70% ethanol at 4 °C for at least 30 min. Subsequently, they were stained with Vindelov solution (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8; 0.7 mg/mL RNase; 50 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI); 0.1% Triton X-100; 10 mM NaCl in MQ H₂O) and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Cell cycle analysis was performed using a BD FACSVerse™ flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA) and BD FACSuite™ Software. Acquired data were analyzed with ModFit LT™ 5.0 software (v3.3.11; Verity Software House, Topsham, ME, USA), and results were expressed as the percentage of cells in individual cell cycle phases.

Resazurin assay

Cells seeded in 96-well plates (Corning Inc. Corning, NY, USA) were treated with oxaliplatin (25–200 μM). After 72 h, resazurin (25 μg/mL in PBS; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to assess cell viability based on its reduction to fluorescent resorufin by metabolically active cells. Fluorescence intensity (excitation/emission: 544/590 nm), which correlates with the number of viable cells, was measured using a Fluostar Galaxy reader (BMG Labtechnologies, Ortenberg, Germany). Data were corrected for background fluorescence and normalized to untreated controls.

Annexin V/PI assay

Cells were detached from the culture dish using EDTA/PBS followed by trypsinization, washed with PBS, centrifuged (200 g, 5 min), and subjected to Annexin V/PI staining according to manufacture's instructions (Apronex, Prague, Czech Republic). The cells were incubated with Annexin V-FITC for 20 min at 37 °C, and PI was added immediately prior to analysis. Flow cytometric measurements were performed using a BD FACSVerse™ cytometer and BD FACSuite™ software. The percentage of apoptotic cells with translocated phosphatidyl serine (PS) and intact plasma membrane (Annexin V+/PI−) as well as total dead cells (Annexin V−/PI+ and Annexin V+/PI+) was determined.

Detection of caspase-3 cleavage

Harvested cells were washed with PBS, centrifuged (200 g, 5 min), incubated for 15 min with Live/Dead Far Red stain (1:1000 dilution; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and washed again with PBS. Caspase-3 cleavage was assessed following cell fixation, permeabilization, and staining using the FITC Active Caspase-3 Apoptosis Kit, according to the manufacturer's instructions (BD Biosciences). Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a BD FACSVerse™ cytometer using BD FACSuite™ software. The percentage of cells positive for cleaved caspase-3 with either intact (Live/Dead-negative) or disrupted (Live/Dead-positive) plasma membrane integrity was determined.

Statistical analysis and graphical presentation

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.2; R Core Team, 2023). To evaluate differences across experimental conditions, linear models were applied with relevant factors (e.g., treatment and cell model, or condition and time point) including their interaction term. The dependent variable (cell count) was log-transformed to meet model assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. Proportion data (percentage of apoptotic, dead, or caspase-3–positive cells) were analyzed using beta regression models with a logit link function, including treatment, cell model, and their interaction (betareg package, v3.2–4). For the analysis of cell proliferation across different conditions and time points, the interaction term was not statistically significant and was therefore removed from the final model. For analyses involving oxaliplatin response, models included treatment (oxaliplatin: yes/no), cell model (e.g., wild-type, ctrl, knockdown), and their interaction, allowing explicit testing of whether the treatment effect differed between cell models. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means (emmeans package, version 1.10.0) with Tukey's adjustment for multiple comparisons. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was assessed at the 0.05 level.

Other statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.1.0 for Windows; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). GraphPad Prism was also used for graphical data presentation. Results are expressed as mean ± SD or mean + SD from at least three independent experiments. For additional details regarding statistical tests and p values, please refer to the respective Figure legends. The exact number of independent biological replicates is specified in each figure legend, corresponding to the individual experimental setup, to ensure clarity and transparency.

Results

Characterization of DLD-1 colon cancer cell models of inhibition of B4GALT5 or B4GALT6

Our previous work demonstrated a significant upregulation of SLs and GSLs in DLD-1 cell line, particularly reflected by increased relative levels of HexCer and LacCer, compared to normal colon mucosa or colon adenoma cell lines (Machala et al., 2019). Notably, the observed increase in LacCer in DLD-1 cells was consistent with our findings in primary colon cancer cells isolated from colorectal cancer patients, further supporting the translational relevance of these lipid alterations. (Procházková et al., 2020).This makes DLD-1 cell line a relevant model for studying lipid metabolic dysregulation in colorectal cancer. Its well-characterized genetic background and established use in oxaliplatin sensitivity assays further support its suitability for investigating lipid metabolism in the context of cancer cell survival, death responses, and drug response mechanisms. Therefore, we selected DLD-1 cell model to evaluate the impact of downregulation of LacCer synthesis on its phenotype and sensitivity to oxaliplatin. As LacCer are synthesized principally by B4GALT5 and B4GALT6 isoenzymes, we prepared DLD-1 human colon cancer cell variants with downregulation of either of these two enzymes. Following CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing, a significant decrease in B4GALT5 mRNA and protein levels was observed in B4GALT5 KD but not B4GALT6 KD cells, as compared with control (ctrl) clone. Similarly, a significant downregulation of B4GALT6 levels was apparent in B4GALT6 KD but not in B4GALT5 KD cells (Fig. 1), supporting a selective manipulation of the respective isoenzymes in the relevant cell clones. The models of B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD were also validated through sequencing (Suppl. Fig. 1), which confirmed the presence of a mutation in one of the two alleles of the respective genes.

Importantly, the impact of LCS downregulation in DLD-1 cells was also confirmed at the functional level. A significant decrease of total LacCer levels (Fig. 2A) and all individual LacCer species (C16:0, C18:0, C20:0, C22:0, C24:0, C24:1) (Fig. 2B) relative to ctrl clone or to wild-type DLD-1 cells was detected in both B4GALT6 or B4GALT5 KD cells, with a more pronounced response in the latter cell line. Since LacCer serve as direct precursors for synthesis of further GSLs, including GA2 and GM3 gangliosides, we also evaluated levels of these direct LacCer products. GM3 also serves as a precursor for further more complex gangliosides, GM1a and GD3. We also analyzed levels of sulfatides that are produced from galactosylceramide, in parallel to LacCer synthesis and levels of ceramides, in order to observe whether suppression of one pathway of ceramide metabolism would alter their levels in knockdown cells.

In B4GALT5 KD cells, the total levels of GM3 gangliosides (in this case C16:0, C24:0 and C24:1 species) were markedly decreased, whereas no such reduction was observed in B4GALT6 KD cells (Fig. 2A, B). The reduction of total GM1a levels and all individual GM1a species that were detected (C16:0, C24:0, C24:1) was similar in both B4GALT5 and B4GALT6 KD cells. For GA2, the reduction of their total cellular content was observed in both B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells; this effect was linked especially with reduction of C16:0 and C22:0 species (Fig. 2A, B). The downregulation of LCS levels was not associated with altered levels of GD3 ganglioside or sulfatide species (Fig. 2A). The knockdown of LCS did not induce major alterations of total Cer levels when compared with wild-type cells, and only a minor decrease in comparison with ctrl cells (Fig. 2A). To allow a broader perspective, a full profile of SLs and GSLs in the cell model used for clones preparation, DLD-1 wild-type, is presented in Fig. 2C. For further reference, we also include Suppl. Table 2, which summarizes original data from lipid analyses for all evaluated SL and GSL species, in all four groups that were analyzed. Taken together, the results indicated a successful development of KD models for the inhibition of the corresponding LCS enzymes, leading to a major downregulation of LacCer (and some of their products) in DLD-1 colon cancer cells.

DLD-1 cell proliferation and cell cycle progression remain largely unaffected by B4GALT5 and B4GALT6 KD

We then evaluated the potential impact of B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 downregulation on the growth characteristics of DLD-1 cells. A minor but not significant decrease in cell numbers was observed in B4GALT5 KD or B4GALT6 KD cells over 96 h cultivation period compared to ctrl clone or wild-type cells (Fig. 3A). Minor changes were also observed in the percentage of cells in the G1 and G2/M phases in case of B4GALT6 KD clone (during 96 h cultivation), but not in B4GALT5 KD cells, as compared with ctrl cells. No statistically significant changes were detected in the S phase percentage of either B4GALT5 KD or B4GALT6 KD cells (Fig. 3B). Overall, these findings indicate that within the studied time interval, B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD, accompanied with a corresponding decrease of LacCer (and several more complex GSLs), did not substantially alter the growth characteristics of DLD-1 cells.

DLD-1 cells with reduced expression of B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 are more sensitive to the cytotoxic effects of oxaliplatin

We further focused on the possible impact of B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 downregulation on the DLD-1 cell sensitivity to cell death induced by oxaliplatin. Based on preliminary experiments, we used oxaliplatin at a concentration range of 25–200 μM. Compared to wild-type or ctrl cells undergoing the same treatment, a significant reduction in B4GALT5 KD cell viability was detected after 72 h of exposure to 25 μM oxaliplatin (Fig. 4A). Following the treatment with two-fold higher dose of oxaliplatin (50 μM), a further significant decrease in viability of both B4GALT5 and B4GALT6 KD cells was apparent, as compared with similarly treated wild-type and ctrl cells. This indicated that downregulation of both B4GALT5 and B4GALT6 significantly modulated the sensitivity of DLD-1 colon cancer cells to the drug-induced cell death. Based on these results, 50 μM concentration of oxaliplatin was then selected for the following experiments.

Oxaliplatin-induced apoptosis is enhanced in DLD-1 cells with reduced B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 expression

A significant increase in B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cell death/apoptosis following oxaliplatin treatment compared to their respective controls was confirmed by annexin V/PI assay (Fig. 4B and C). A majority of dying cells exhibited an intact plasma membrane with translocated phosphatidylserine, suggesting that apoptosis was the preferential mode of oxaliplatin-induced cell death in this model. Given the central role of caspases in the regulation of apoptosis, we then examined potential differences in their cleavage/activation in cells with reduced B4GALT5/6 expression and their respective controls in response to oxaliplatin treatment. Following oxaliplatin treatment, flow cytometric analysis revealed an increase in the proportion of B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells positive for cleaved caspase-3 while maintaining plasma membrane integrity, when compared with wild-type cells and the ctrl clone (Fig. 4 D, E). This trend was already observed after 48 h treatment and it became statistically significant after 72 h. At this later time point, B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells also exhibited an increased cleavage of caspase-3 accompanied with a loss of plasma membrane integrity relative to the respective controls (Suppl. Fig. 2), indicating progression towards secondary necrosis. Enhanced oxaliplatin-induced cleavage of effector caspase-3 in both B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells was further confirmed by Western blotting analysis (Fig. 5 A, B). In addition, oxaliplatin treatment promoted increased cleavage of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), a major caspase-3 substrate, in B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells compared with either the ctrl clone or wild-type cells (Fig. 5 A, B). Moreover, oxaliplatin-treated cells with reduced B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 expression displayed more pronounced cleavage of initiator caspase-9 and caspase-8 relative to wild-type or ctrl clone (Fig. 5 A, B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate a stronger activation of apoptotic pathways in response to oxaliplatin treatment in cells with reduced B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 expression, when compared with their respective controls.

A, B) The levels of cleaved PARP, caspase-3, caspase-9 or pro-caspase-8/cleaved caspase-8 (Western blotting) in B4GALT5 KD, B4GALT6 KD, wild-type or ctrl cells, following 48 h(A) and 72 h (B) treatment with oxaliplatin (50 μM). An equal protein loading was verified using anti-β-actin antibody. Results are representative of at least three independent experiments.

Changes in levels of ER stress markers in oxaliplatin-treated DLD-1 cells with B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD

Next, we investigated the pattern of activation of ER stress pathways in DLD-1 cells with downregulated B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 undergoing cell death following the oxaliplatin treatment. For the initial experiments, levels of CHOP, BiP and ATF4 proteins as biomarkers of ER stress signaling (Sicari et al., 2020) were examined in oxaliplatin-treated (6–72 h) B4GALT5 KD cells in order to identify suitable markers and time points for subsequent comparative analyses. Increased CHOP mRNA levels were detected in B4GALT5 KD cells after 24–72 h treatment with oxaliplatin (Suppl. Fig. 3), similar to an increase in its protein level (data not shown). A gradual time-dependent decrease of BiP mRNA in oxaliplatin-treated (48–72 h) cells was also observed (Suppl. Fig. 3), similar to BiP protein (data not shown). A minor decrease of ATF4 mRNA in oxaliplatin-treated B4GALT5 KD cells was also detected but only after 48 h (Suppl. Fig. 3).

Based on these results, we selected 48 h after oxaliplatin treatment as the time point for further experiments assessing changes in mRNA levels of selected ER stress markers, while two time points, 48 and 72 h, were selected for analyzing their protein level alterations. We observed a similar increase in CHOP mRNA (Fig. 6A) at 48 h, and protein levels at both 48 (Fig. 6B) and 72 h (Suppl. Fig. 4) in both KDs and in control cells (ctrl clone and wild-type cells) treated with oxaliplatin. The levels of BiP protein also decreased similarly in both LCS KD and control cells (Fig. 6B; Suppl. Fig. 4). In contrast, the oxaliplatin-treated B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells exhibited a more prominent decrease in levels of ATF6 protein, a major ER stress transducer (Hillary and FitzGerald, 2018), as compared with similarly treated controls (ctrl or wild-type) (Fig. 6B; Suppl. Fig. 4).

Discussion

In recent years, SLs and GSLs have attracted an increased attention due to their diverse regulatory functions in fundamental cellular processes, positioning them as key modulators in various physiological and pathological contexts, including CRC development (Machala et al., 2019; Hannun and Obeid, 2018). Based on findings from other authors as well as our own data (Procházková et al., 2020; Chatterjee et al., 2019), which demonstrate increased levels of LacCer and modulations of B4GALT5 and B4GALT6 in CRC cell lines as well as in clinical samples from CRC patients, we sought to test the hypothesis that these alterations may influence colon cancer cell proliferation, death, and their response to chemotherapy. To address this, we employed the DLD-1 cell line model, which has been validated in our previous studies as a suitable system for investigating lipid metabolism–dependent regulation of colon cancer cell behavior (Machala et al., 2019), and oxaliplatin as a model drug frequently used to treat CRC patients.

Previously, conducting a comprehensive LC-MS/MS analysis of SL profiles in six colon cancer cell lines as compared with cells derived from normal colon mucosa, we have observed that the DLD-1 cancer cell line exhibited distinct alterations in specific SL/GSL subclasses (including LacCer) that closely resembled sphingolipidome changes observed in clinical CRC samples (Machala et al., 2019; Procházková et al., 2020). We therefore prepared two DLD-1 clones, B4GALT5 KD and B4GALT6 KD, with reduced expression of B4GALT5 and B4GALT6, respectively, for experiments addressing the functional roles of these LCSs. Their KD was associated with significantly reduced levels of all LacCer species, as well as downregulation of GM3, GM1a and GA2.

Despite the reduction in GM3 levels in B4GALT5 KD cells, downregulation of its product, GD3, was not observed. One possible explanation is that, as GD3 levels in DLD-1 cells were substantially lower than GM3 levels (Suppl. Table 2), even the reduced GM3 pool may still have been sufficient to sustain GD3 synthesis. Alternatively, the remaining GM3 may have been preferentially channeled towards GD3 synthesis to maintain adequate GD3 levels. We also did not observe major alterations of total Cer levels, suggesting that inhibition of GSL formation did not lead to Cer accumulation. Cer levels are tightly regulated in cells through their rapid conversion into less harmful SLs; however, we did not observe major changes in other SLs. Previously, inhibition of GSL synthesis, particularly GlcCer synthesis, has been proposed as a potential strategy to induce Cer accumulation in cancer cells, thereby promoting cancer cell death and increasing chemosensitivity (Huang et al., 2011; Wegner et al., 2018). Here, suppression of LCS was not associated with increased Cer concentrations in DLD-1 cells. However, because GlcCer and especially sphingomyelins were present at much higher concentrations than Cer (Suppl. Table 2), a potential increase in Cer levels may have been compensated for by enhanced formation of these SLs without producing discernible changes in their total levels. This may have prevented identification of the specific SL metabolic pathways responsible for ceramide compensation. Consistently with previous findings of Chatterjee et al. (Chatterjee et al., 2019), our data support a more prominent role for B4GALT5 in LacCer biosynthesis, as downregulation of this enzyme resulted in a more pronounced reduction of all LacCer species, as compared with B4GALT6 KD, with the latter one leading to an approximately 50% decrease of LacCer levels relative to control cells (both ctrl clone and wild-type cells).

Previous studies have indicated that the upregulation of GSLs and the enzymes responsible for their synthesis may promote tumor progression via enhanced cancer cell proliferation. B4GALT5 has been identified as a key enzyme in maintaining the stemness of breast cancer cells, with its overexpression significantly accelerating tumor growth (Tang et al., 2020). Similarly, reduced tumor growth was observed in B16-F10 melanoma cells with suppressed B4GALT5 expression, accompanied by decreased levels of LacCer and GM3 (Shirane et al., 2014). Importantly, in a renal cancer xenograft model, inhibition of GSL synthesis using low-molecular-weight chemical inhibitor D-PDMP suppressed LacCer production and led to reduced tumor growth (Chatterjee et al., 2013). Notably, D-PDMP–mediated inhibition of GSL synthesis and suppression of B4GALT5 activity also reduced the proliferation of colon cancer HCT-116 cells (Chatterjee et al., 2019). In contrast, our experiments in DLD-1 cells with B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD did not result in a statistically significant reduction of cell proliferation during 96 h cultivation. Furthermore, only minor alterations in cell cycle progression were observed in B4GALT6 KD cells. These subtle changes likely reflected shifts in cell cycle dynamics rather than true cell cycle arrest or proliferative suppression. It is also possible that these effects would be more pronounced in complete knockout models, as opposed to the partial KD systems used in this study, or after extended cultivation period. DLD-1 cells may also rely on LacCer for proliferation less than other CRC cell lines. Nevertheless, it cannot be excluded that the previously reported effects of D-PDMP may, at least in part, arise from off-target interactions with other enzymes involved in GSLs synthesis.

A growing body of evidence indicates that deregulated SLs and GSLs, along with their biosynthetic enzymes, could contribute to mediating chemoresistance in multiple cancers. Pharmacological inhibition of GlcCer synthesis, the precursor of LacCer, has been shown to increase sensitivity to cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity in non-small cell lung cancer cells (Tyler et al., 2015) or in head and neck cancer cells (Roh et al., 2015). Similarly, inhibition of GlcCer synthesis sensitized p53-R273H mutant colon cancer cells and tumor xenografts to doxorubicin (Hosain et al., 2016). In multidrug-resistant K562 leukemia cells, inhibition of GlcCer synthase UGCG, either pharmacologically or via its knockdown, restored their sensitivity to cisplatin- or adriamycin-induced apoptosis, mediated by enhanced mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and downregulation of Bcl-2, respectively (Salustiano et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2014). In the case of LCS, the role of B4GALT5 in the development of multidrug resistance in leukemia cells has been reported, possibly through modulation of Hedgehog signaling and altered expression of key mediators of multidrug resistance, including P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and MRP1 (Zhou et al., 2013). However, despite their potential biological relevance, the roles of B4GALT5 and B4GALT6 in cell chemosensitivity are still largely unexplored, including their impact on the regulation of oxaliplatin sensitivity.

Oxaliplatin predominantly induces cell death − apoptosis via the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, involving modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins, disruption of mitochondrial membrane integrity, release of cytochrome c, and activation of caspase-9 (Arango et al., 2004). Here, we documented that the downregulation of either B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 sensitized DLD-1 cells to oxaliplatin-induced apoptosis, as reflected by increased phosphatidylserine translocation, cleavage of caspases-3 and effector caspase substrate PARP. The oxaliplatin-treated B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells also exhibited an apparent increase in caspase-9 cleavage when compared to the appropriate control, supporting the importance of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. In addition, increased caspase-8 cleavage observed in these cells may result from feedback activation through caspase-9-dependent mitochondrial signaling, as previously reported for colon cancer cells treated with oxaliplatin and TRAIL (El Fajoui et al., 2011). Alternatively, we cannot rule out that non-canonical caspase-8 activation triggered by ER stress (Hattori et al., 2024) also participated in the observed effects.

Alterations in GSL metabolism may influence cellular sensitivity to oxaliplatin by modulating ER stress pathways (Park and Park, 2020). A sustained or excessive ER stress may shift the balance towards pro-apoptotic signaling, often involving CHOP upregulation and caspase activation (Zhang et al., 2024). Conflicting data exist on the role of ER stress in oxaliplatin cytotoxicity. While some studies suggest that ER stress enhances oxaliplatin sensitivity in colon cancer cells, others indicate that it may instead promote cytoprotective autophagy through ROS-associated pathways (Liu et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2012). In our experiments, CHOP levels appeared to increase to a similar extent in both oxaliplatin-treated B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells versus controls, suggesting that CHOP is unlikely to play a major role in mediating their differential sensitivity to the chemotherapy. Similarly, following oxaliplatin treatment, BiP protein levels were reduced to a comparable extent across all clones. In contrast, ATF6 levels decreased more markedly in oxaliplatin-treated B4GALT5 or B4GALT6 KD cells compared with similarly treated controls, which was consistent with their higher responsiveness to the drug's cytotoxic effects. In line with these findings, 5-fluorouracil-resistant SNUC5 colon cancer cells transfected with ATF6 siRNA were more sensitive to 5-fluorouracil than control siRNA-transfected cells (Kim et al., 2016). ATF6 silencing may also render RKO and HCT-116 colon cancer cells more sensitive to combined treatments with adriamycin and possibly to other DNA-damaging agents commonly used to treat CRC (Benedetti et al., 2022). Taken together, these data raise the possibility that ATF6, known to be associated with reduced disease-free survival or genomic instability (Coleman et al., 2018), could also represent a potential target for improving the cytotoxic response to chemotherapeutic drugs such as oxaliplatin in colon cancer cells.

Altered profiles of GSLs after the knockdown of LCS suggest that reduced concentrations of some GSLs, e.g. GM gangliosides, may perhaps also contribute to an increased chemosensitivity of colon cancer cells. Nevertheless, ambiguous results were also reported in several in vitro studies showing possible roles of GM3 and GM1a in deregulation of cancer cell proliferation and cancer progression (Cumin et al., 2021). Therefore, specific roles of GM gangliosides and other GSLs in pro- and anti-tumorigenic processes, including acquired chemoresistance or chemosensitivity, need further attention.

Here we found that the downregulation of two important LacCer-synthesizing enzymes, B4GALT5 or B4GALT6, significantly enhanced colon cancer sensitivity to the cytotoxic effects of oxaliplatin, a drug frequently used in CRC treatment, primarily via enhanced caspase-dependent apoptosis, which was also accompanied with modulation of ER stress response. Collectively, these data highlight the role of GSL metabolism in the modulation of chemosensitivity in colon cancer cells, supporting its potential as a promising target for further research.
