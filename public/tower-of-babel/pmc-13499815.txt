Fatal Injuries Caused by Falling Trees: Correlation Between Postmortem Computed Tomography and Forensic Autopsy Findings in Eight Cases
Authors: Tomoya Kobayashi, Akihito Usui, Yoshikazu Okamoto, Sohtaro Mimasaka, Masato Funayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Falling trees and large branches can result in severe blunt trauma; however, detailed correlations between postmortem computed tomography (PMCT) and forensic autopsy findings in such deaths remain limited. We retrospectively analysed eight consecutive deaths occurring between 2012 and 2025 in which impact or compression by a tree trunk or large branch constituted the primary traumatic event and in which both PMCT and complete forensic autopsy were performed. Thin-slice PMCT of the head and trunk was obtained using standard brain, soft-tissue, lung, and bone windows, supplemented by a gas-optimised thoracic setting. Scene information, PMCT, and autopsy findings were integrated to determine the primary mechanism of death and to compare intracranial, thoracoabdominal, skeletal, and asphyxial injuries. Four lethal mechanisms were identified: instantly fatal head or brainstem injury (n = 2), haemorrhagic shock without cardiac rupture (n = 2), traumatic shock with cardiac rupture and/or aortic injury (n = 2), and asphyxia due to smothering or thoracic compression (n = 2). PMCT demonstrated complex fractures of the ribs, scapulae, spine, and pelvis, as well as haemothorax, haemoperitoneum, pneumomediastinum, and pre-existing coronary artery calcification, and suggested aortic injury in one case. However, brainstem lesions, precise sites of cardiac and aortic rupture, and low-contrast parenchymal injuries were identified only at autopsy. These results suggest that PMCT is valuable for delineating global injury patterns and high-energy marker lesions in falling-tree deaths, whereas full forensic autopsy remains essential for establishing the exact mechanism of death.

Introduction

Falling trees and tree-related activities represent an important but relatively infrequent cause of severe and fatal trauma. Epidemiological studies in forestry and arboricultural work report high fatality rates, with logging and tree felling remaining among the most hazardous occupations in high-income countries [1–3]. Recent analyses have identified distinct risk patterns across professional forestry, landscaping, and non-professional do-it-yourself felling, including differences in age distribution, injury mechanism, and work organisation [1–3]. Outside occupational settings, non-professional chainsaw use and private tree felling contribute substantially to tree-related deaths and often involve older males working alone [1, 3].

Natural tree failure, including storm-related falls and spontaneous branch drop, accounts for only a small proportion of trauma admissions, approximately 0.15% in one Australian series, yet still results in several deaths annually, most commonly among vehicle occupants struck by trunks or large branches [1, 4, 5]. From a forensic standpoint, these incidents involve high-energy blunt trauma affecting the head, thorax, abdomen, and spine. In some circumstances, death may also result from positional asphyxia or smothering when the body is trapped beneath the tree [1, 5]. Autopsy findings reported in fatal cases include complex skull fractures with intracranial haemorrhage, multiple rib and pelvic fractures, solid organ lacerations, injury to the great vessels or heart, and external or internal signs consistent with asphyxia [1, 5].

In many medico-legal systems, postmortem computed tomography (PMCT) has become a routine investigation before forensic autopsy in traumatic deaths. PMCT allows rapid, non-invasive assessment of skeletal injuries, gas collections, and major haemorrhage, even in cases with extensive body damage or early decomposition. Comparative studies of PMCT and conventional autopsy in blunt trauma demonstrate high sensitivity of PMCT for fractures and gas collections, whereas its ability to detect certain soft-tissue and solid-organ injuries remains limited when non-contrast CT is used. Autopsy therefore continues to provide essential information on parenchymal damage and subtle vascular lesions [6]. Accordingly, PMCT and autopsy should be viewed as complementary methods rather than alternatives.

Despite the expanding use of PMCT, detailed imaging descriptions of fatalities caused by falling trees are scarce. Existing publications primarily address epidemiology, occupational safety, and gross autopsy findings, with limited emphasis on systematic PMCT–autopsy correlation for this specific injury mechanism [1–5]. In particular, data are lacking on the ability of PMCT to characterise lethal pathways such as immediately fatal central nervous system injury, haemorrhagic shock from thoracoabdominal trauma, cardiac rupture, or asphyxia, and on how imaging findings correspond to autopsy observations, including intracardiac blood volume and asphyxial features.

The aim of this study was to describe PMCT and autopsy findings in eight consecutive fatalities caused by falling trees, all of which were examined using PMCT immediately before forensic autopsy at a single institution. The analysis focused on four categories of lethal mechanisms: instantly fatal head/brainstem injury, haemorrhagic shock due to thoracic and pelvic trauma, polytrauma with cardiac rupture, and asphyxial death related to thoracic compression or smothering. For each category, we assessed the extent to which PMCT identified the critical injuries. The present study should therefore be considered a descriptive case series intended to characterise the PMCT–autopsy correlation in the setting of this relatively underreported cause of fatal blunt trauma, rather than establish broadly generalisable diagnostic rules.

Materials and methods

Case selection

From April 2012 to March 2025, 2,587 decedents underwent PMCT before forensic autopsy at our institution. Eight consecutive deaths for which a falling tree constituted the primary traumatic event were identified. PMCT was performed within 1–2 days after death, before forensic autopsy, in all cases.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fatal blunt trauma due to impact or compression involving a tree trunk or large branch, (2) PMCT performed before autopsy, and (3) a complete forensic autopsy with macroscopic documentation of injuries. Cases were excluded when tree-related trauma was not considered the principal cause of death or when PMCT or autopsy data were incomplete.

Postmortem CT acquisition

PMCT was performed with a 64-detector row CT scanner (Aquilion 64; Canon Medical Systems Corp., Otawara, Japan) in seven of the eight cases. Head CT parameters were 120 kV, 1.0 s/rot, and tube current adjusted to a standard deviation (SD) of 2.0 HU using automatic exposure control (AEC) to limit image noise. The field of view (FOV) was 240 mm, and images were reconstructed with head and bone algorithms.

For the trunk, including the thorax and abdomen, tube voltage was set at 120 kV or 135 kV based on body habitus, with a rotation time of 1.0 s/rot. The tube current was adjusted to SD = 5.0 HU using AEC, and the FOV was 320 mm. Reconstructions used soft tissue, lung, and bone algorithms. Acquisition for both head and trunk used 64-row mode with a nominal slice thickness of 0.5 mm and filtered back projection.

In one earlier case, PMCT was performed using an 8-detector row scanner (Aquilion; Toshiba Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan). Head CT parameters were 120 kV, 300 mA, 1.0 s/rot, 4-row acquisition, 1.0-mm slice thickness, and a 240-mm FOV. Trunk CT parameters were 120 kV, 300 mA, 0.75 s/rot, 8-row acquisition, 2.0-mm slice thickness, and a 500-mm FOV. Reconstruction algorithms were comparable to those used in the 64-row protocol.

Image reconstruction and analysis

Image reconstruction and review were performed on a medical imaging workstation (Ziostation2, version 2.4.3.3; Ziosoft Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Axial, coronal, and sagittal images were assessed using standard brain, soft-tissue, lung, and bone windows. A gas-optimised window (width 450 HU, level − 70 HU) was additionally applied in the thorax to evaluate intrathoracic gas and air–fluid interfaces. This setting facilitated differentiation of gas from adjacent low-attenuation fat, which may overlap on standard soft-tissue windows. Lung windows were less suitable for assessing subtle gas–fat interfaces in the mediastinum and chest wall.

Maximum-intensity-projection (MIP) and volume-rendered (VR) images of selected regions were generated in some cases. Additional VR and MIP images used for figures were created using a dedicated 3D workstation (SYNAPSE VINCENT, version 7.0.0003; FUJIFILM Medical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Slice thicknesses for all reconstructions were identical to the acquisition thickness for each scanner. All PMCT images were retrospectively reviewed by a single radiological technologist with 29 years of experience in diagnostic radiography and 16 years of experience in postmortem CT imaging. Because PMCT was performed before forensic autopsy, detailed autopsy findings were not available at the time of image acquisition; only limited case information was available in the pre-autopsy setting. The gas-optimised setting refers to a specific window width and level combination that enhances visualisation of very low-attenuation structures, allowing small gas collections to be distinguished more readily from adjacent fat.

Autopsy procedure

All cases underwent full forensic autopsy according to standard protocols, including external examination, dissection of the thoracic, abdominal, and pelvic cavities, and removal of the brain and cervical organs. The heart was opened to record intracardiac blood volume in millilitres and the presence or absence of intracardiac clots. Pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal fluid volumes were measured using graduated containers when feasible. Of the eight cases, seven autopsies and medicolegal evaluations were performed by a board-certified forensic pathologist with > 35 years of experience, and one case was examined by a board-certified forensic pathologist with > 25 years of experience.

The lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, brain, and heart were examined for lacerations, contusions, congestion, and other traumatic changes. Cardiac rupture and injury to the great vessels were specifically documented. Routine forensic assessment also included evaluation for petechial haemorrhages of the palpebral conjunctivae and oral mucosa, haemorrhage within the tongue on sectioning, and cervical soft-tissue haemorrhage. Skeletal injuries involving the skull, ribs, spine, pelvis, scapulae, clavicles, and long bones were described and correlated with PMCT findings when appropriate. Although histological sampling was performed as part of routine forensic practice when appropriate, histopathological findings were not systematically analysed as study variables. Toxicology screening with routine drug screening kits was performed when sufficient blood was available. In the present series, screening toxicology, including alcohol testing, yielded negative results in all cases, and no further confirmatory toxicological analyses were performed.

Data items and definitions

Scene information was obtained from police and medicolegal records and included tree characteristics when available, occupational versus non-occupational context, weather conditions, body position relative to the tree, and visible external bleeding. Autopsy data included the certified cause of death, presence of petechiae of the palpebral conjunctivae and oral mucosa, intracardiac blood volume, intracardiac clotting (present/absent), major organ injuries, and whether cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) had been attempted.

From PMCT, the presence or absence of skull base and facial fractures; intracranial haemorrhage (subdural, subarachnoid, or intraventricular); fluid and/or foam within the upper and lower airways; thoracic skeletal injuries (rib, sternum, scapula, thoracic spine) and thoracic organ injuries, including haemothorax and haemopneumothorax, with pleural volumes referenced to autopsy measurements; abdominal and pelvic injuries (solid-organ lacerations, pelvic fractures, and lumbar spine fractures); additional skeletal fractures; and cardiac, pericardial, and mediastinal findings (including pericardial effusion, pneumomediastinum, and gas adjacent to the cardiac silhouette or great vessels) were recorded.

Intracranial haemorrhage was defined by visible hyperattenuating extra-axial or intraventricular blood on brain windows. Pleural fluid was considered present when dependent collections were visible on CT, with volume derived from autopsy records. Pericardial effusion was defined as a circumferential fluid-attenuation collection surrounding the heart; in cases with marked pneumomediastinum and cardiac collapse, assessment was considered unreliable. Airway contents were recorded when fluid, foam, or air–fluid levels were present in the trachea or major bronchi.

Ethical approval.

This retrospective study of PMCT examinations performed before forensic autopsy was conducted as part of a research project approved by the institutional ethics committee of our university (Approval No. 2022–1–100).

Results

Eight fatalities caused by falling trees were examined. The circumstances included professional forestry work and non-professional activities. Deaths due to head injury (Cases 1 and 3)

Case 1

Case 1 involved immediate death due to brainstem injury following posterior cranial impact. PMCT demonstrated a displaced right posterior skull fracture (Fig. 1a, 1b) and minimal haemorrhage with cerebrospinal fluid egress in the posterior fossa; the brainstem lesion itself could not be reliably evaluated on PMCT (Fig. 1c). Additional injuries included multiple rib fractures, a right scapular acromion fracture, and lumbar transverse process fractures, with a left haemothorax of approximately 140 mL and no definite major organ laceration. Case 3

Case 3 sustained a fatal traumatic brain injury after impact to the anterior cranial vault and face. PMCT showed multiple left-sided calvarial and facial fractures, dense subarachnoid blood within the basal cisterns, and a left scalp haematoma (Fig. 1e, 1f). Autopsy of the extracted brain confirmed diffuse subarachnoid haemorrhage and identified multiple cortical contusions involving the vertex and skull base, including a contusion on the undersurface of the right temporal lobe (Fig. 1g, 1 h), some of which were not clearly detected on PMCT. Thus, although PMCT identified the major subarachnoid haemorrhage, it did not depict the complete extent of the fatal traumatic brain injury; moreover, it underestimated the cortical contusions.

Deaths due to haemorrhagic shock without cardiac rupture (Cases 5 and 7)

Case 5

Case 5 died from haemorrhagic shock caused by multiple left-sided rib fractures with extensive soft-tissue bleeding. PMCT demonstrated contiguous fractures of the left ribs with a left haemothorax and a large subcutaneous haematoma of the left lateral thigh (Fig. 2a–2c). On axial CT at the level of maximal thigh girth, the left thigh circumference measured approximately 460 mm compared with 420 mm on the right, consistent with a substantial haematoma. Autopsy confirmed extensive subcutaneous and intramuscular haemorrhage of the left chest wall (Fig. 2d) and thigh, with approximately 50 mL of blood in the left pleural cavity and no major cardiac or great-vessel injury. Case 7

Case 7 died from haemorrhagic shock due to pelvic-ring fractures and associated retroperitoneal and intra-abdominal bleeding. PMCT showed haemoperitoneum adjacent to the caudal kidney, a disrupted pelvic ring with fractures of the iliac wing, sacral ala, pubic rami, and ischium, and fractures involving the L2 vertebral body and lumbar posterior elements (Fig. 2e, 2f). Additional lower-extremity fractures were identified on PMCT and confirmed at autopsy (Fig. 2h). Autopsy revealed a large retroperitoneal haematoma and marked soft-tissue haemorrhage in the posterior lumbar region (Fig. 2g). The liver and kidneys showed only mild congestion on cut surfaces, and the cardiac chambers contained approximately 80 mL of blood, findings consistent with systemic hypoperfusion from severe blood loss. These findings supported a diagnosis of fatal haemorrhagic shock primarily due to pelvic and retroperitoneal bleeding.

Deaths due to traumatic shock with cardiac rupture (Cases 2 and 8)

Case 2

Case 2 died from traumatic shock in the context of severe polytrauma involving the heart, aorta, lungs, liver, spine, and pelvis. PMCT demonstrated extensive pneumomediastinum with an apparently collapsed cardiac silhouette and gas tracking along the thoracic aorta, findings suggestive of aortic injury (Fig. 3a, 3b). Case 8

Case 8 sustained polytrauma with cardiac rupture, pulmonary injury, extensive thoracic skeletal trauma, and spinal fractures. PMCT showed bilateral pleural fluid collections with pneumothorax and multiple rib and clavicular fractures but no diagnostic pericardial effusion, and the cardiac rupture was not visualised (Fig. 3e–3g). Autopsy revealed approximately 750 mL and 450 mL of blood in the right and left pleural cavities, respectively, consistent with massive bilateral haemothoraces, and identified a transmural tear extending from the left ventricle to the apex on the posterior cardiac surface (Fig. 3h). Pericardial fluid volume was minimal, and pericardial tearing precluded reliable estimation of haemopericardium. Death was attributed to traumatic shock due to polytrauma, with undiagnosed cardiac rupture as a major contributing factor.

Case 4

Case 6

Case 6 died from asphyxia caused by thoracic compression. PMCT showed pleural fluid collections and haemopneumothorax with intrathoracic gas displacing the right atrium and ventricle, extensive bilateral rib fractures, and thoracic spinal injuries, including a T6–T7 distraction injury and a T9 vertebral body fracture (Fig. 4e–4j). Because the right heart was collapsed and surrounded by gas, evaluation of pericardial effusion on CT was unreliable. Autopsy revealed bloody contents within the airways, haemorrhage on the surface and cut surface of the tongue base, numerous petechiae on the palpebral conjunctivae and oral mucosa, focal haemorrhages on the posterior aspect of the right thyroid lobe, and pleural blood volumes of 50 mL on the right and 75 mL on the left, with 5 mL of pericardial fluid. Collectively, the severe thoracic cage disruption, haemopneumothorax, airway haemorrhage, and asphyxial findings supported thoracic compression as the primary mechanism of death.

Discussion

This case series suggests that fatal trauma caused by falling trees is associated with a limited set of lethal mechanisms, namely immediate head or brainstem injury, haemorrhagic shock without cardiac rupture, traumatic shock with cardiac rupture, and asphyxia, while still causing extensive multisystem trauma in many victims. Across all eight deaths in this study, PMCT provided a rapid overview of skeletal injuries, thoracoabdominal trauma, and the distribution of pleural and peritoneal fluid. Rather than proposing a new general principle regarding PMCT and autopsy, this case series illustrates their established complementary roles in the specific and relatively underreported setting of fatal trauma caused by falling trees. These observations are consistent with prior epidemiological and clinical reports on the severity of tree-related impacts and suggest that PMCT has practical value as a triage tool in medicolegal investigations.

In this series, the mechanisms of injury in several cases appeared biphasic, with an initial high-energy impact followed by sustained compression. The initial impact from a falling trunk or large branch produced focal catastrophic injuries such as skull fractures, brainstem damage, pelvic-ring disruption, or cardiac rupture. In some victims, this was followed by prolonged compression, with the body pinned between the tree and the ground. The coexistence of catastrophic focal lesions with more diffuse findings, including multiple rib fractures, thoracic cage instability, haemothorax, and signs of asphyxia, is consistent with this combined mechanism of impact and compression. In the thorax, PMCT permitted limited inferences regarding this process and was useful for depicting both skeletal marker injuries and the in situ structural effects of compression, such as haemopneumothorax with marked right heart displacement. In Case 6, the combination of multilevel rib fractures, haemopneumothorax with marked displacement of the right heart, and bloody airway contents documented at autopsy was consistent with prolonged thoracic compression resulting in ventilatory failure, superimposed on the initial impact.

Previous PMCT–autopsy comparison studies have shown that unenhanced PMCT is highly sensitive for traumatic findings with clear attenuation differences, particularly fractures, ectopic gas, and large blood collections, while remaining less sensitive than autopsy for certain soft-tissue and organ injuries [6, 7]. This pattern of strengths and limitations is directly relevant to fatal falling-tree incidents, which often involve both severe skeletal disruption and potentially lethal low-contrast lesions. These observations are also consistent with the results of early forensic imaging research by Thali et al. [8], which highlighted the particular value of postmortem imaging for skeletal trauma, gas-related findings, and injury reconstruction. They are also consistent with the findings of a large validation study by Roberts et al. [9], which showed that postmortem imaging can identify many major injuries but remains less reliable than autopsy for certain soft-tissue, cardiovascular, and intra-abdominal lesions. In the four cases with severe thoracoabdominal trauma and haemorrhagic or traumatic shock, PMCT delineated the overall distribution of skeletal injury and major haemorrhage, whereas autopsy was necessary to identify specific organ injuries, assess blood loss, and document subtle parenchymal changes.

In the two deaths attributed to haemorrhagic shock without cardiac rupture, PMCT demonstrated multilevel rib fractures, scapular and pelvic-ring fractures, lumbar spine injuries, and high-attenuation intra-abdominal fluid, allowing reconstruction of chest-wall, thigh, and retroperitoneal bleeding that was subsequently confirmed at autopsy. In the two deaths involving cardiac rupture and great-vessel injury, PMCT showed extensive pneumomediastinum, bilateral pleural fluid collections, and multiple thoracic skeletal fractures, with gas tracking along the thoracic aortic wall in one case, suggesting aortic injury. However, precise localisation of aortic and cardiac tears and reliable estimation of pericardial blood volume were only possible at autopsy. These findings parallel those of clinical CT studies of blunt thoracic trauma, in which traumatic aortic injury and cardiac disruption are often inferred from indirect signs and may remain uncertain in the setting of complex associated injuries [10–13].

The frequent presence of scapular and thoracic spine fractures in this small series is noteworthy. Because the scapula is anatomically protected by the thoracic cage and surrounding musculature, scapular fractures are widely recognised as indicators of high-energy blunt chest trauma and are commonly associated with other severe thoracic injuries, including rib fractures and pulmonary lesions [14]. In our cases, scapular fractures occurred alongside extensive rib and spinal fractures and large pleural blood volumes, supporting the interpretation that all eight deaths resulted from very high-energy impacts. In one case, the felled trunk reportedly weighed approximately 300 kg; measured trunk lengths and diameters in the remaining cases were comparable, suggesting that the impacts generally involved tree masses of several hundred kilograms.

In the two asphyxial deaths, PMCT did not independently demonstrate asphyxia but provided important contextual information when interpreted together with scene findings and autopsy results. In Case 4, prone positioning with the nose and mouth firmly compressed against the ground, combined with multiple rib and spinous process fractures and an old myocardial scar, supported death due to smothering with contributory chronic ischaemic heart disease. PMCT documented thoracic injuries, subcutaneous emphysema, and coronary calcifications but could not directly visualise upper airway obstruction. In addition, as illustrated in this case, subcutaneous emphysema may be appreciated by palpation in postmortem examination, although PMCT allows more rapid detection and more objective documentation of its extent and distribution. In Case 6, PMCT revealed extensive bilateral rib fractures and haemopneumothorax with right heart collapse and displacement. Importantly, PMCT is particularly advantageous for demonstrating tension physiology associated with pneumothorax. Once the thoracic cavity is opened during autopsy, intrathoracic pressure is lost and the original spatial relationships may be altered. In contrast, PMCT preserves the in situ configuration, allowing clear visualisation of tension-related displacement and compression of the right heart chambers. When correlated with autopsy findings of bloody airway contents and petechiae, these observations were consistent with fatal thoracic compression. These cases illustrate that, in suspected asphyxial mechanisms, PMCT primarily contributes by depicting chest wall instability, haemothorax or haemopneumothorax, and associated skeletal injuries, whereas the diagnosis of asphyxia continues to rely on scene information and classical autopsy findings. Although cardiopulmonary resuscitation-related fractures are a recognised confounding factor, the extensive dorsal rib fractures and vertebral injuries in this case were more consistent with primary impact from the falling trunk than with resuscitation alone. Furthermore, the distribution of intrathoracic gas on early postmortem scans, limited to the pleural spaces and mediastinum without diffuse intra-organ gas, supported a traumatic rather than putrefactive origin, in line with previous evaluations of postmortem gas patterns [15].

From a technical perspective, these observations reaffirm that PMCT is most sensitive to traumatic findings with clear attenuation differences, including large haematomas, gas collections, and fractures. In Case 1, the brainstem lesion was missed, and this was the only case examined with an 8-detector row CT scanner rather than a 64-detector row system. This older scanner used a 1.0-mm head slice thickness, whereas the later 64-detector protocol uses a 0.5-mm head slice thickness; such technical differences may have reduced the detectability of subtle lesions. However, a 1.0-mm slice thickness would generally still be expected to depict major skull fractures and extra-axial haemorrhage. Thus, the failed detection of the focal brainstem lesion is more likely attributable to the limited soft-tissue contrast in non-contrast PMCT for subtle parenchymal haemorrhages than to the scanner generation alone. Thin, film-like blood layers distributed over broad surfaces and early parenchymal injuries may be subtle or unapparent on CT. One plausible explanation is that, in rapidly fatal trauma, insufficient time exists for oedema or mass effect to develop to a degree detectable on CT; however, this remains a pathophysiological hypothesis rather than an imaging-confirmed finding. Use of a dedicated gas-optimised window aided differentiation of intrathoracic gas from adjacent fat and improved visualisation of abnormal gas around the heart and great vessels. Even with this optimisation, CT frequently demonstrated cardiac and aortic ruptures only indirectly, through patterns of gas and mediastinal or pleural fluid. These limitations are consistent with prior PMCT–autopsy comparison studies of blunt trauma, which emphasise the strength of PMCT for skeletal injury and gas detection while noting its reduced sensitivity for certain soft-tissue and organ lesions in the absence of contrast enhancement [6, 7].

In one case involving brainstem death, the combination of nearly bloodless cardiac chambers with minimal clot formation and the absence of major extracranial haemorrhage resembled the so-called ‘empty heart sign’ described in sudden deaths from catastrophic central nervous system injury [16] and in exsanguination through skull-base fractures with occult extracranial blood loss [17]. Severe occipital skull-base injuries, including ring fractures around the foramen magnum, have also been reported to cause fatal brainstem and vascular damage [18], supporting the plausibility that high-energy occipital impact can result in catastrophic brainstem injury even when the lesion itself is poorly visualised on PMCT. However, because this sign was observed in only a single case, it should be considered a non-specific, adjunctive finding rather than an independent marker of the mechanism. In summary, the posterior skull fracture and brainstem haemorrhages identified at autopsy in this case were considered the primary indicators of the lethal mechanism, whereas the empty-heart sign was interpreted as a supportive finding only.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First and most importantly, the sample size was very small (n = 8), and the categorisation of distinct lethal mechanisms resulted in only two cases per category. This inherently limited the possibility of formal pattern analysis and precluded broad generalisation of the findings. Second, this was a retrospective, single-centre study with a long inclusion period (13 years), which may have introduced selection and temporal biases. Third, although all PMCT examinations were obtained within 1–2 days after death, the intervals between death, PMCT, and autopsy were not systematically analysed in this retrospective series. In addition, although advanced decomposition was excluded, interpretation of gas-related findings may still be limited when traumatic/postmortem changes overlap with early putrefactive change. Fourth, the study lacked a control group of cases involving blunt trauma not related to falling trees for direct comparisons. Finally, the PMCT images were retrospectively evaluated by a single reader, and no formal inter-observer validation was performed. Although PMCT was performed before forensic autopsy, the retrospective design and integration of case information may still have introduced interpretive bias. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as those derived from a descriptive, exploratory case series. Larger multi-centre series with systematically collected cases are warranted to validate these observations.

Taken together, this small series supports the complementary use of PMCT and forensic autopsy in fatalities caused by falling trees. In this setting, PMCT was useful for delineating overall injury patterns, documenting skeletal trauma, gas, and larger blood collections, and identifying pre-existing cardiovascular disease, whereas autopsy remained essential for confirming low-contrast soft-tissue lesions and establishing the precise mechanism of death. From a practical standpoint, PMCT may also help estimate external force severity through markers such as scapular and upper thoracic spine fractures and guide the focus of autopsy dissection in complex high-energy trauma. These observations should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating findings, and larger series of systematically collected cases should determine their applicability to other forms of blunt trauma and their usefulness for refining injury pattern classifications for falling tree-related fatalities. An integrated approach combining PMCT, autopsy, and meticulous scene investigation is therefore critical for accurate reconstruction of these events.
