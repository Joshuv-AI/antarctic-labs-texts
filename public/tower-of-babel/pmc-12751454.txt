Construction and validation of a lung adenocarcinoma prognostic model based on neutrophil extracellular traps and oxidative stress-related genes
Authors: Long Xu, Yuanyuan Zhao, Shuxi Song, Junling Liu, Jingyu Li, Zhendong Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality globally, with challenges in prognosis and treatment due to its complex pathogenesis and heterogeneous tumor microenvironment (TME). Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and oxidative stress play critical roles in tumor progression: NETs promote tumor cell adhesion, migration, and immune suppression, while oxidative stress induces DNA damage and activates pro-tumor signaling pathways. Moreover, oxidative stress is an important inducer of NETs, and their crosstalk shapes the LUAD immune microenvironment. However, systematic exploration of LUAD immunotherapeutic response prediction based on NETs and oxidative stress-related genes remains lacking.MethodsThe gene set related to oxidative stress was obtained from MSigDB. The gene set related to NETs was sourced from relevant literature. Transcriptomic and clinical data were integrated from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD (training set) and GSE31210 (validation set). Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was employed to screen gene modules and characteristic scores related to NETs and oxidative stress signatures. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened, and prognostic model was established using univariate and LASSO Cox regression. Immune infiltration was analyzed using ESTIMATE algorithm, MCP-counter and ssGSEA methods. And we developed a nomogram incorporating clinicopathological features and RiskScore model, and performed drug sensitivity analysis. Finally, the biological role of CPS1 in lung cancer cells was investigated through CCK-8, wound-healing, and Transwell experiments.Results22 co-expression modules were screened, among which the brown module showed significant correlations with NETs and oxidative stress signature scores. This module was intersected with DEGs, yielding 624 overlapping genes implicated in immune-relevant pathways (like leukocyte differentiation, neutrophil activation involved in immune response). A prognostic model was established utilizing 8 key genes (ADGRE3, ARHGEF3, CD79A, CLEC7A, CPS1, EPHB2, LARGE2, and OAS3). In the TCGA database, the model demonstrated robust prognostic discrimination (area under the curve (AUC) > 0.6), with high-risk patients exhibiting shorter overall survival (OS) (p < 0.05). Its stability was validated in GSE31210 (AUC > 0.6). The RiskScore showed negative correlations with immune infiltration (like T cells, CD8 T cells, and natural killer cells) as well as immune/stromal scores. A nomogram model combining RiskScore with N staging was developed and validated, demonstrating strong predictive accuracy through calibration and decision curve analyses. High-risk patients were more sensitive to drugs like BI-2536, BMS-509744, and Pyrimethamine. Finally, in vitro tests showed that CPS1 knockdown markedly decreased the viability, migration, and invasion of lung cancer cells.ConclusionThe constructed prognostic model by NETs and oxidative stress-relevant genes effectively predicts LUAD prognosis, correlates with immune microenvironment characteristics, and guides drug sensitivity, providing novel insights for LUAD prognostic assessment and personalized therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03553-9.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Prognostic model, Neutrophil extracellular traps, Oxidative stress, Immune infiltration, Drug sensitivity

Introduction

Lung cancer is globally acknowledged as the most prevalent malignancy and the primary cause of cancer-associated deaths [1, 2]. Among the five major malignancies in China, it ranks first in both incidence and mortality rates [3–5]. Based on global cancer statistics from 2022, lung cancer accounts for around 12.4% (close to 2.5 million) of newly identified cancer cases and 18.7% (1.8 million) of deaths linked to cancer [6, 7]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is recognized as the most prevalent histological subtype, making up roughly 40% of all lung cancer cases [8]. The therapeutic strategy for LUAD is determined by multiple factors, including tumor stage, disease progression, and the patient’s overall health status. Surgical intervention is generally recommended for early stage patients, whereas systemic therapy is required for advanced and metastatic cases [9], highlighting the necessity of personalized treatment. In recent years, although advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches have led to modest improvements in the survival of LUAD patients, the prognosis remains unfavorable due to tumor recurrence and chemoresistance [10, 11]. Therefore, to substantially enhance the overall survival (OS) of these patients, novel therapeutic targets must be identified, and specific prognostic prediction methods urgently need to be developed.

Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) refer to reticular formations secreted by activated neutrophils via degranulation, with their main components being DNA, histones, and a range of granular proteins [12–14]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that NETs promote lung cancer progression through multiple mechanisms. Specifically, the activation of NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway has been evidenced to be mediated by NETs, which subsequently induces epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and consequently accelerates the migratory capacity of lung cancer cells [15]. Given these findings, investigations into NETs and their underlying mechanisms are considered pivotal for the excavation of novel antitumor therapeutic strategies. NETs are capable of suppressing T-cell responses through metabolic and functional exhaustion, thereby promoting tumor growth. Additionally, targeting PD-L1-containing NETs with anti-PD-L1 agents has been shown to reduce tumor growth [16]. Oxidative stress, characterized as a cellular response process resulting from the imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and elimination, leads to biomolecular damage [17]. This process has been recognized to be a critical factor in lung cancer development. An imbalance between the production of ROS and protection against ROS can significantly impair T-cell activation, and oxidative stress can induce T-cell suppression [18]. Excessive ROS generation has been demonstrated to induce intracellular oxidative stress responses, thereby activating multiple signaling pathways that promote neutrophil activation and NET formation [12]. Chronic inflammation typically involves continuous NET activation, tissue damage, and tissue repair. ROS, together with NETs, are involved in the progression and development of cancer [19]. Additionally, NETs drive mitochondrial homeostasis in tumors to promote their growth. These findings confirm that NETs and oxidative stress may exert correlative effects in cancer, yet relevant bioinformatics studies remain scarce [20]. A key focus in LUAD research now lies in whether the expression profiles of NETs and genes associated with oxidative stress can mirror the biological behaviors of lung cancer and the patient prognosis.

The objective of the current study is to integrate expression profiling data of NETs and oxidative stress-related genes, utilize Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) [21] to recognize relevant gene modules, screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and establish a prognostic RiskScore model for LUAD, followed by validation of its efficacy. Finally, the biological significance and clinical application potential of the model will be analyzed from the perspectives of immune microenvironment infiltration and drug sensitivity. Meanwhile, the biological roles of genes in lung cancer cells were investigated through CCK-8 assays, wound-healing experiments, and Transwell assays. This study suggests that the target genes may jointly act through NETs and oxidative stress-related pathways to influence the development of LUAD. This investigation is expected to provide novel prognostic assessment tools and predictive biomarkers for immunotherapy in LUAD patients, thus boosting the advancement of individualized therapeutic approaches.

Materials and methods

Data collection

RNA-Seq data of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD were derived from TCGA database through the GDC API [22]. We performed sample selection according to the following criteria: complete survival status information; and OS time > 0 days. After rigorous screening, 500 tumor and 59 normal samples were incorporated in the analysis. All expression data were converted to FPKM values and log2-transformed. Finally, TCGA-LUAD cohort was established as the training dataset for this study.

Gene expression data from GSE31210 dataset were retrieved from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [23]. Samples lacking survival status details were first excluded, and then, probe IDs were converted to gene names based on the annotation file of the relevant chip platform. When multiple probes corresponded to the same gene, the average of their expression values was adopted as the gene’s final expression level. This dataset served as an independent validation set.

The gene set related to NETs is from the reference [24].

The sources of oxidative stress response-associated genes

The oxidative stress-associated gene set (GOBP_RESPONSE_TO_OXIDATIVE_STRESS.v2024.1.Hs.gmt) was obtained from MSigDB [25].

Identification of DEGs

DEGs between tumor and normal samples was screened utilizing the limma package [26]. Significantly DEGs were filtered by setting thresholds of adj. p value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)|> log2(1.5) [27].

WGCNA

To begin with, we performed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) in GSVA package to compute the signature scores for NETs and oxidative stress. This method evaluates the overall enrichment level of genes within predefined gene sets by ranking the sample gene expression matrix, thereby reflecting the activation level of the corresponding biological processes. A higher score indicates a higher activation level of the corresponding biological pathway in the sample.

Next, WGCNA [21] was carried out to pinpoint modules of highly correlated genes, outline the relationships between these modules and external sample characteristics, and detect candidate biomarkers. In the present study, WGCNA was built using the R package “WGCNA” [28], with pickSoftThreshold function utilized to ascertain the soft-thresholding power (β). Subsequently, we performed hierarchical clustering to recognize gene modules, following the standard that each module should contain at least 60 genes (minModuleSize = 60). The goal here was to select modules with the most significant associations with NETs and oxidative stress signature scores in lung cancer patients for use in subsequent analyses. Finally, DEGs were overlapped with genes in the key modules, and the genes present in both groups were designated as differentially expressed NETs-related oxidative stress genes, which would be further explored [29].

Functional enrichment analysis

Further, we performed enrichment analysis on the obtained overlapping genes (including both upregulated and downregulated genes). Enrichment analyses encompassed both Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses. GO comprised three categories: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). In the current research, the R package “clusterProfiler” [30] was employed to carry out functional enrichment analyses [31].

Construction of prognostic RiskScore model

To identify differentially expressed NETs-related oxidative stress genes relevant to LUAD patient prognosis, univariate Cox regression analysis was carried out using TCGA database. Only NETs-related oxidative stress genes with p values < 0.01 were employed for subsequent risk signature construction. The patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups using the optimal cut-off value determined by survminer package [33]. Survival differences between the two risk groups were compared through Kaplan–Meier (K–M) analysis. The prognostic performance of the risk signature was evaluated via time-dependent receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Furthermore, the predictive robustness of the model was externally verified in GSE31210 dataset using identical analytical procedures.

Analysis of immune infiltration

To start with, ESTIMATE algorithm [34] was utilized to compute stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores for every sample. Correlations between these microenvironmental scores and RiskScore were subsequently analyzed. Second, immune and stromal cell infiltration abundances were quantified using the MCP-counter method [35]. The associations between these cellular infiltration patterns and risk stratification were then evaluated. Furthermore, ssGSEA was conducted via GSVA R package [30] to compute activity scores of immune-related pathways.

In addition, the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) method was used to evaluate the response to immunotherapy. Normalized transcriptomic data were input into the TIDE web server (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) to calculate the TIDE score. A higher TIDE prediction score indicates a greater likelihood of immune escape, suggesting that patients are less likely to benefit from immunotherapy.

Development of the nomogram

Uni/multivariate Cox regression analyses were carried out to assess the prognostic significance of clinicopathological characteristics (pathological T stage, N stage, M stage, overall stage, age, and gender) and the RiskScore model, aiming to recognize independent prognostic factors for LUAD. The rms package [36] was employed to develop a prognostic nomogram capable of predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities. Following this, calibration curve and decision curve analysis (DCA) were performed to verify the clinical dependability of nomogram.

Immunological therapy

Chemosensitivity assessment was conducted utilizing the pRRophetic R package [37] to evaluate therapeutic responses to conventional chemotherapeutic agents in the TCGA-LUAD cohort.

Cell culture and transfection

Human pulmonary epithelial cells (BEAS-2B, C5382) were purchased from BDBio Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Human lung cancer cells, A549 (BNCC337696) and NCI-H838 (BNCC100696), were purchased from Bei Na Biotech (Xinyang, China). NCI-H838 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (BNCC338360, Bei Na Biotech, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). A549 cells were cultured in F-12 K complete medium (BNCC338550, Bei Na Biotech, China) containing 10% FBS. BEAS-2B cells were incubated in DMEM medium (C5382-500, BDBio, China) containing 10% FBS. These cells were cultured under conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO₂.

In addition, small interfering (si) RNAs targeting CPS1 (si-CPS1#1: 5’-AGGATTTTGACAGCTTTCAAAGT-3’; si-CPS1#2: 5’-TGGAACTAAGATGAAAGGTTACT-3’) and a negative control (si-NC) were provided by Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. for the knockdown of CPS1 expression in lung cancer cells. Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent kit (11668027, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to transfect si-CPS1 into A549 and NCI-H838 cells.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Initially, total RNA was acquired from BEAS-2B, A549, and NCI-H838 cells using the TriZol RNA Extraction Kit (15596026CN, Invitrogen, USA). Subsequently, the concentration and purity of the RNA were measured. The total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA by the SuperRT cDNA Synthesis Kit (S665657-100 T, Aladdin, Shanghai, China). Thereafter, qRT-PCR was conducted utilizing the SYBR Green qPCR Mix (D7260, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Finally, data analysis was conducted through 2−ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The primer sequences for each gene are presented in Table 1.

Cell viability

To evaluate the effects of si-NC and si-CPS1 transfection on the proliferation of A549 and NCI-H838 cells, we performed a colorimetric Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) test. Specifically, cells (5 × 103 cells per well) were cultured in 96-well microplates for 24, 48, and 72 h. Afterward, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent were injected to each well and incubated at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm in each well, which reflects cell proliferation, was measured utilizing a microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) to construct the CCK-8 growth curve [38].

Cell migration assay

For the migration assay, the transfected cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in a 6-well plate. Two milliliters of cell suspension were added to each well and then incubated in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After the cells adhered to the plate, a uniform wound was formed using 10-µL plastic pipette tip. To remove any detached cell debris, the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (E607016, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Images of the wound edges were taken at 0 and 48 h post-wounding to evaluate cell migration. All experiments were repeated three times [39].

Cell invasion test

For the invasion test, A549 and NCI-H838 cells (3 × 104cells/well) were prepared and cultured in serum-free medium. Subsequently, 100 µL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA), which had been pre-coated with Matrigel (30 µg per well; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). The lower chamber of the Transwell insert was filled with complete medium-containing serum. After incubation, the culture plate was removed, and cells were gently washed with PBS (E607016, Sangon Biotech, China) to remove dead cells and residual medium. The cells were then fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with crystal violet solution. The number of invasive or migratory cells was counted using five randomly selected fields under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 3.6.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2). To compare differences in continuous variables between the two groups, Wilcoxon rank-sum test was conducted; Spearman method was applied to compute correlations; and log-rank test was applied to evaluate variations in survival duration across patient groups. All experiments were performed with three independent replicates. Differences among three or more groups were compared utilizing two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P value < 0.05 was deemed as statistical significance.

Development and validation of prognostic model

Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted on the overlapping genes to filter out those significantly correlated with LUAD patient outcomes. Ultimately, 99 genes with a close association to LUAD prognosis were identified (p < 0.01). Subsequently, LASSO Cox regression analysis combined with tenfold cross-verification was conducted on these 99 candidate genes to further compress the number of variables and construct a more concise and robust risk prediction model (Fig. 3A, B). On this basis, stepwise multivariate Cox regression analysis was further conducted, and 8 independent prognosis-relevant genes were finally determined for constructing the RiskScore model (Fig. 3C). The prognosis-related RiskScore for each sample was computed using this formula, and patients were split into high-risk and low-risk groups by the osptimal cut-off value. To evaluate the prognostic predictive ability of the model, time-dependent ROC curves for 1-year to 5-year survival were plotted, and area under the curve (AUC) values were computed. For 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS, the corresponding AUC values were 0.69, 0.72, and 0.68, respectively (Fig. 3D). The results showed that the model exhibited good predictive accuracy at each time point, with relatively high AUC values, indicating its strong capability to distinguish the outcome of LUAD patients. Additional K–M survival analysis indicated that the OS rate of patients in high-risk group was notably lower than low-risk group, with the OS difference between two groups being statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3E). When survival status in the two groups was further analyzed, it was discovered that the percentage of deceased patients in high-risk group was markedly higher than low-risk group (Fig. 3F), which further verified the application potential of the model in risk stratification and prognostic assessment.

To further confirm the robustness and generalization ability of RiskScore model, the same method was applied to the independent validation dataset GSE31210 for analysis. The findings demonstrated that the percentage of deaths in high-risk group was notably higher than low-risk group (Fig. 3G). In this cohort, the model maintained relatively high AUC values over 1 to 5-year period, with AUC values of 0.80, 0.65, and 0.66 for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year ROC curves, respectively (Fig. 3H), implying that the model has good discriminative ability. A subsequent K–M survival analysis (Fig. 3I) indicated that the survival probability of patients in high-risk group was notably lower than that in low-risk group, with the difference between two groups being statistical significance (p < 0.05), which further supports the validity and stability of RiskScore model in external data.

The relationship between Riskscore and immunity

ESTIMATE algorithm was employed to evaluate immune infiltration. Results indicated a significant negative relationship between RiskScore and stromal score, immune score, as well as ESTIMATE composite score, implying that high-risk patients might exhibit lower infiltration levels of immune and stromal cells (Fig. 4A). To delve deeper into the relationship between RiskScore and immune function, the MCP-counter algorithm was utilized to compute immune cell scores in TCGA dataset. It was observed that cells like T cells, CD8 T cells, and cytotoxic lymphocytes had a marked negative correlation with RiskScore (Fig. 4B). Finally, an analysis of the correlations among RiskScore, prognostic genes, and ssGSEA immune cell scores revealed that several cell types—including activated B cells, mast cells, macrophages, natural killer cells, immature B cells, and activated dendritic cells—were significantly negatively related to RiskScore (Fig. 4C).

The results of immune-related characteristics showed that RiskScore was significantly negatively correlated with multiple immune checkpoint molecules (Figure S1A, B, p < 0.05), indicating that patients in the high-risk group had lower expression levels of immune checkpoint genes, which may lack sufficient immune activation signals and present an “immune-cold” or “immune-excluded” tumor microenvironment. Further TIDE analysis revealed that the TIDE, Exclusion, and MDSC scores in the high-risk group were significantly increased, while the Dysfunction score was decreased (Figure S1C, p < 0.05), suggesting that the high-risk group exhibited stronger immune exclusion and immunosuppressive characteristics, and was predicted to have a poor response to immune checkpoint inhibitors. In addition, tumor mutational burden (TMB) analysis results showed that the TMB level of patients in the high-risk group was significantly higher, and a large proportion of TMB-High samples were distributed in the high-risk group (Figure S1D, E, p < 0.05). Collectively, the high-risk group reflects an immunosuppressive microenvironment dominated by immune exclusion or an immune-cold state, which may lead to its poor response to immunotherapy.

Establishment of nomogram

To provide better guidance for clinical practice, univariate/multivariate Cox regression analyses were carried out to assess their independent prognostic value, aiming to clarify whether RiskScore could be utilized as an independent prognostic factor for predicting survival of LUAD patients. These regression analyses identified N stage and RiskScore as marked prognostic factors (Fig. 5A, B). To further enhance the prognostic accuracy and predictive power of our model, nomogram was developed integrating N stage and Riskscore (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the predictive accuracy of the model was assessed utilizing calibration curves (Fig. 5D), where the predicted calibration curves for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year calibration points were observed to nearly overlap with standard curves, implying that the nomogram exhibits favorable predictive performance. Furthermore, Fig. 5E presents the results of decision curve analysis, demonstrating that the nomogram model achieved the highest net clinical benefit across most risk thresholds, followed by the RiskScore model, both outperforming the traditional N staging system. Figure 5F displays time-dependent AUC curves, revealing that the nomogram maintained the optimal predictive performance over the 1–5-year follow-up period, with the RiskScore model ranking second, while the predictive ability of N staging was relatively weaker. These results collectively validate the advantages and practical utility of the integrated model in survival prediction and clinical decision-making.

Drug sensitivity

To evaluate the predictive potential of the RiskScores for immunotherapy efficacy, the melanoma immunotherapy cohort GSE91061 was incorporated for validation. Survival analysis showed that, compared with low-risk group, patients in high-risk group had significantly shorter OS (Fig. 6A). In terms of treatment response, outcomes of patients in GSE91061 cohort receiving anti-PD-L1/anti-CTLA4 treatment were divided into complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). It was observed that all high-risk patients were identified as SD/PD, with the proportion of SD/PD being notably higher than low-risk cohort (Fig. 6B). Further analysis uncovered that SD/PD patients had significantly elevated RiskScores compared to those with other response types (Fig. 6C), suggesting the RiskScore may serve as a potential predictor for immunotherapy response. Additionally, in TCGA-LUAD cohort, the relationship between RiskScore and sensitivity to conventional chemotherapeutic agents was assessed. A notable negative correlation was identified between RiskScore and responsiveness to multiple drugs (including BI-2536, BMS-509744, Bleomycin, Pyrimethamine, and GW843682X) (Fig. 6D). This finding indicates that high-risk patients may demonstrate enhanced sensitivity to these agents, which could hold clinical relevance for therapeutic decision-making.

The migration and invasion of lung cancer cells were affected by the downregulation of CPS1

Firstly, the mRNA expression levels of ADGRE3, ARHGEF3, CD79A, CLEC7A, CPS1, EPHB2, LARGE2, and OAS3 in BEAS-2B, A549, and NCI-H838 cells were assessed using qPCR technology. The qPCR data indicated that, compared with BEAS-2B cells, the expression levels of ADGRE3, ARHGEF3, CD79A, CLEC7A, and LARGE2 genes were markedly reduced in A549 and NCI-H838 cells; compared with BEAS-2B cells, the expression levels of CPS1, EPHB2, and OAS3 genes were notably elevated in A549 and NCI-H838 cells (Fig. 7A). These observations suggested that the abnormal expression of these genes exerts a significant role in the lung cancer progression, highlighting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers for lung cancer. In this work, the effect of CPS1 gene silencing on the viability of A549 and NCI-H838 cells was investigated utilizing the CCK-8 assay. Two knockdown plasmids targeting CPS1 were constructed and introduced into A549 and NCI-H838 cells, and the cell lines that achieved the most effective silencing (A549, si-CPS1#1 and NCI-H838, si-CPS1#1) were selected for subsequent experimental steps (Fig. 7B). The results showed that CPS1 gene silencing markedly reduced the viability of A549 and NCI-H838 cells (Fig. 7C, D). Subsequently, wound-healing and Transwell invasion tests were conducted to evaluate the role of CPS1 in regulating the invasive and metastatic capabilities of A549 and NCI-H838 cells. The results demonstrated that CPS1 gene silencing significantly inhibited the migration and invasion of A549 and NCI-H838 cells (Fig. 7E, F). These findings confirmed that CPS1 may be a key factor in promoting the migration and invasion of tumor cells during LUAD development.

Discussion

LUAD, as the most prevalent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), is recognized as a major challenge in the global public health field due to its significant morphological heterogeneity and prognostic variability. Statistics show that this disease accounts for a significant proportion of cancer-relevant morbidity and mortality [40, 41]. Recent studies have indicated that NETs might facilitate the adhesion, migration, and metastasis of tumor cells in various solid tumors, including lung cancer, and accelerate cancer progression via inducing immunosuppression and angiogenesis [42, 43]. In the process of tumor occurrence and progression, oxidative stress not only triggers DNA damage and gene mutations but also activates a variety of signaling pathways, thereby facilitating the proliferation, invasion, and therapy resistance of cancer cells [44]. In addition, oxidative stress has been confirmed as one of the important biological processes inducing the formation of NETs. This study established a lung cancer prognostic model on the basis of NETs and oxidative stress-associated genes to explore its value in lung cancer prognosis evaluation and treatment. Using the TCGA cohort, 8 signature genes associated with LUAD prognosis were identified: ADGRE3, ARHGEF3, CD79A, CLEC7A, CPS1, EPHB2, LARGE2, and OAS3, and a prognostic model was developed using these genes. Afterward, TCGA dataset and external validation sets were used to assess how well this model predicts the survival of LUAD patients, while differences in functional enrichment and immune infiltration between high-risk and low-risk score groups were also analyzed. Through drug sensitivity analysis, it was demonstrated that patients in high-risk group were more sensitive to drugs like BI-2536, BMS-509744, Pyrimethamine, and GW843682X. Finally, in vitro tests demonstrated that the knockdown of CPS1 notably decreased the viability, migration, and invasion of lung cancer cells. The present study offers novel perspectives on prognostic prediction and immunotherapeutic approaches for LUAD.

In this study, eight key oxidative stress-related genes were screened in LUAD. Among these, CPS1, EPHB2, and OAS3 are risk genes, while ADGRE3, ARHGEF3, CD79A, CLEC7A, and LARGE2 are protective genes. ADGRE3, a member of the adhesion G protein-coupled receptor (aGPCR) family, is involved in the regulation of multiple cellular signaling pathways. Research has demonstrated that ADGRE3 exhibits lower expression levels in LUAD tumor samples. Through WGCNA and machine learning approaches, ADGRE3 has been recognized as a protective gene for LUAD [9]. Notably, ADGRE3 expression has been found to positively correlate with sensitivity to cisplatin, ribociclib, and pemivanserin-chemotherapeutic agents that are either commonly used or under investigation in lung cancer clinical trials [45–47]. ARHGEF3 is a gene encoding a guanine nucleotide exchange factor and belongs to the Rho GEF family. Existing bioinformatics studies have confirmed that ARHGEF3 is downregulated in LUAD tissues [48]. CD79A is a key component of the B-cell receptor (BCR) complex and belongs to the immunoglobulin superfamily. It typically forms a heterodimer with CD79B, jointly participating in the regulation of B-cell activation and immune responses [49]. In LUAD, CD79A is closely relevant to the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Studies have evidenced that the CD79A expression in LUAD correlates with B-cell infiltration, and its high expression is linked to favorable prognosis [50]. CLEC7A plays a crucial immunomodulatory role in tumor microenvironment (TME). It has been demonstrated to enhance the tumor-killing efficacy of natural killer cells [51]. Conversely, activation of CLEC7A can promote the differentiation of CD4 T cells into T helper 9 cells by upregulating interleukin-33 and interleukin-9 expression in dendritic cells, thereby exerting potent antitumor immune effects [52]. The expression of CPS1 has been shown to closely correlate with the TME in LUAD. Increased expression of this gene might accelerate tumor progression via activating the JAK/STAT pathway [53]. EPHB2, a key member of the Eph receptor family, is classified as a receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK). Together with their ephrin ligands, Eph receptors form a crucial cell communication system that plays significant roles in cell adhesion, migration, axon guidance, and tissue morphogenesis [54]. In LUAD, EPHB2 has been identified to be highly expressed and relevant to tumor aggressiveness and poor outcome. Research has demonstrated that elevated EPHB2 expression correlates with enhanced tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasive capabilities [55]. In summary, these eight genes may influence the initiation and progression of LUAD through multiple dimensions, demonstrating significant potential as biomarkers for both therapeutic intervention and prognosis evaluation in LUAD.

The RiskScore showed significant negative correlations with multiple drugs (including BI-2536, BMS-509744, Bleomycin, Pyrimethamine, and GW843682X), suggesting that high-risk patients may demonstrate greater sensitivity to these agents, which carries potential clinical significance. BI-2536 is a small-molecule inhibitor specifically targeting Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1). PLK1 is a crucial enzyme involved in mitotic processes that is frequently overexpressed in various cancers, including NSCLC [56]. Pyrimethamine is an antimalarial drug that exerts antitumor effects by inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) [57]. Studies have shown that in various types of cancer models, pyrimethamine inhibits oncoproteins, such as Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3), NF-κB, DX2, Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK), DHFR, thymidine phosphorylase, and telomerase. In addition, it has been reported that pyrimethamine could synergize with other antitumor drugs (like temozolomide) to induce cancer cell apoptosis [58]. As a STAT3 inhibitor, pyrimethamine can reduce tumor burden in mouse cancer models, inhibit cancer cell proliferation and invasion, suppress the activation of STAT3 in tumor cells, attenuate tumor growth, alleviate tumor-related inflammation, and, at the same time, increase the LAMP1 expression in tumor-infiltrating CD8 T cells, enhancing the release of cytotoxic granules [57]. With its known pharmacokinetic properties and low toxicity, pyrimethamine is expected to be applied in lung cancer treatment [59]. Similar to BI2536, GW843682X can significantly induce the death of various cells, especially those that preferentially express PLK1, and the degree of cell death it induces is related to the expression level of PLK1. However, in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and human colorectal cancer cells with high expression of both PLK1 and PLK2, the level of induced cell death is extremely low [60]. In the nude mouse model of nasopharyngeal carcinoma with lung metastasis, GW843682X can slow down the progression of nasopharyngeal carcinoma [60]. Its role in LUAD remains to be investigated.

This study found that overlapping genes are mainly enriched in leishmaniasis and immune-related pathways, such as leukocyte differentiation and neutrophil activation. Leishmaniasis is a vector-borne disease, and its immunotherapy has been found to be closely related to controlling patients’ condition [61]. The enrichment of immune-related pathways further indicates that overlapping genes have a significant correlation with the TME, which may affect the occurrence of LUAD through the TME. Therefore, further exploration of the TME in patients revealed that the infiltration of certain immune cell subsets—like activated B cells, mast cells, macrophages, and activated dendritic cells—were notably negatively correlated with Riskscore, indicating that these cells may exert a role in suppressing LUAD progression. Activated B cells exhibit an immunomodulatory phenotype, which can inhibit T cells through metabolic competition, reduce the production of cytokines by T cells, and suppress their proliferation. These cells hold significant importance in immune regulation and tumor immunotherapies [62]. Studies have discovered that the increased infiltration and co-localization of Mast cells and CCR2⁺ CTLs are closely associated with favorable postoperative prognosis. CCR2⁺ CTLs represent a subset of pre-exhausted T cells characterized by a tissue-resident memory phenotype and heightened cytotoxicity. Notably, the co-localization of mast cells with these cells might participate in anti-lung cancer tumor immunity [63]. Another study found that the infiltration of Mast cells can serve as a potential predictive indicator for lung metastasis of breast cancer, and a decrease in their levels is related to a higher risk of lung metastasis [64]. In lung cancer, the antitumor immune functions that natural killer cells should exert (such as clonal proliferation and cytotoxic killing) are impaired due to exhaustion. Changes in their functional status and receptor expression are closely associated with the occurrence of lung cancer, making them important functionally impaired immune cells in the lung cancer immune microenvironment [65]. Given these analyses and the aforementioned relationships between some of the genes, neutrophils, and adaptive immunity, it is implied that these cells may influence LUAD prognosis through the TME, though the specific mechanisms require further experimental validation.

This study has the following limitations. For one thing, the data are derived solely from retrospective datasets in the TCGA and GEO databases. Only simple cellular inflammation analyses were performed for a gene, lacking validation from prospective clinical cohorts. Second, although NETs and oxidative stress-related genes were screened through WGCNA, the specific molecular mechanisms of these genes and their interactions in the TME were not verified through experimental methods. Third, the immune infiltration analysis was based on bioinformatics algorithms without validation from histopathology or flow cytometry to confirm the composition of immune cells. In addition, the drug sensitivity analysis was only conducted through computational prediction and lacked support from in vitro or in vivo assays. Therefore, all associations revealed by bioinformatics analyses in this study are merely correlational findings, and a definitive causal relationship cannot be established. To further validate the biological authenticity of these associations and clarify their underlying regulatory mechanisms, future studies should supplement in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, such as conducting molecular mechanism verification of relevant signaling pathways and constructing LUAD cell and animal models for targeted validation.

Conclusion

This research built and verified an 8-gene prognostic model for LUAD using NETs and oxidative stress-associated genes through univariate and LASSO Cox regression. And further analyzed the differences in the immune microenvironment and characteristics of drug sensitivity between high-risk and low-risk populations. This study not only provides novel potential molecular targets for the prognostic evaluation of LUAD patients but also offers reliable references for clinicians to assess patients’ survival time and formulate individualized diagnosis and treatment strategies. On one hand, this research expands the understanding of the association between the TME and disease progression. On the other hand, future studies can leverage this model to explore the specific regulatory mechanisms of NETs-related genes in the occurrence and development of LUAD, promote its clinical translation, lay a foundation for the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting NETs or oxidative stress pathways, and ultimately contribute to improving the survival outcomes of LUAD patients.
