Effects of a Pre-Dialysis Breathing and Exercise Program on Quality of Life, Hemodynamic Parameters, and Dialysis Adequacy in Hemodialysis Patients
Authors: Gizem Zorlu Görgülügil, Aysun Ünal, Gamze Nazik, Esra Kocabıyık, Alperen Burak Kocabıyık, Gökhan Köker, Üstün Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePhysical inactivity and reduced quality of life are common in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. Although intradialytic exercise has been extensively investigated, the effects of structured pre-dialysis exercise combined with breathing techniques on mental well-being, hemodynamic parameters, and dialysis adequacy remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a pre-dialysis structured exercise program on quality of life, hemodynamic status, and dialysis adequacy in maintenance hemodialysis patients.MethodologyThis prospective, controlled, non-randomized interventional study was conducted in a tertiary-care hemodialysis unit over 8 weeks. A total of 53 maintenance hemodialysis patients were included. The intervention group received a 20 minute structured exercise and breathing program before each dialysis session, three times weekly, while the control group received standard care. Outcomes included Physical and Mental Component Summary scores derived from SF-36, PSQI, FAS, hemodynamic parameters, Kt/V, and URR. Time × group interactions were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models.ResultsA greater increase in MCS was observed in the intervention group; however, the time × group interaction did not reach statistical significance (p=0.081), indicating a trend toward improved mental well-being. Significant reductions were found in systolic blood pressure (p=0.049) and pulse rate (p=0.033). No significant interaction was observed for PCS, PSQI, FAS, diastolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, or oxygen saturation. Significant time - group interactions were detected for Kt/V (p=0.018) and URR (p=0.013); however, the observed changes were small and values remained within clinically acceptable ranges.ConclusionPre-dialysis structured exercise was associated with favorable hemodynamic effects and a trend toward improved mental well-being without compromising dialysis adequacy. These preliminary findings suggest that supervised pre-dialysis exercise may be a feasible supportive strategy in hemodialysis care.Keywords: dialysis adequacy, exercise therapy, hemodialysis, quality of life

Introduction

Physical inactivity, depressive symptoms, autonomic dysfunction, and reduced quality of life are common problems in patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis and are associated with an adverse prognosis. Although there is evidence suggesting that exercise programs may improve functional capacity and quality of life in this population, most interventions have focused on intradialytic exercise. The effects of structured interventions including breathing exercises implemented prior to the hemodialysis session on mental components and hemodynamic parameters have not been adequately investigated. Furthermore, findings regarding the potential impact of exercise on dialysis adequacy remain inconsistent.

The present study aims to evaluate the effects of a structured exercise and breathing program performed before the hemodialysis session on quality of life, hemodynamic parameters, and dialysis adequacy.

Recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that exercise interventions in dialysis patients may lead to improvements in quality-of-life outcomes.1 Meta-analyses evaluating the effects on depressive symptoms similarly suggest potential psychological benefits of exercise.2 However, a substantial proportion of the existing literature has focused on intradialytic exercise programs, primarily examining outcomes related to physical performance or functional capacity.3 Although studies assessing the relationship between intradialytic aerobic exercise and quality of life and dialysis adequacy are available, there is considerable heterogeneity in study protocols and outcome measures.4 Moreover, observational studies demonstrating an association between physical activity levels and quality of life in hemodialysis patients further support the clinical relevance of exercise-based interventions in this population.5 However, most available evidence originates from intradialytic exercise programs performed during dialysis sessions. Patients may experience fatigue, intradialytic hypotension, vascular access-related concerns, or limited mobility during treatment, which can affect participation and adherence. Therefore, pre-dialysis exercise may represent a practical alternative that allows greater patient engagement before treatment initiation.

On the other hand, breathing exercises and yoga-based approaches appear to have the potential to reduce fatigue and symptom burden in patients undergoing hemodialysis.6 Randomized studies have also examined the effects of respiratory exercises on sleep quality and symptom management in this population.7 Nevertheless, studies evaluating the feasibility of yoga based interventions and their effects on patient centered outcomes in hemodialysis patients remain relatively limited in number.8 Breathing-based interventions may complement exercise by targeting autonomic regulation, stress reduction, and symptom management. Therefore, combining structured movement with controlled breathing techniques may provide synergistic benefits that extend beyond physical conditioning alone.

From a hemodynamic perspective, exercise and controlled breathing techniques are proposed to influence autonomic balance and may exert a regulatory effect on heart rate and blood pressure parameters.9 However, the effects of short-term, structured exercise and breathing protocols performed prior to the hemodialysis session on hemodynamic parameters have not been clearly elucidated.10 With respect to dialysis adequacy, while reviews have reported that intradialytic exercise may increase Kt/V and urea reduction ratio (URR), results appear to vary depending on the timing and type of exercise intervention.3 Because autonomic dysfunction is highly prevalent among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, evaluating both patient-reported outcomes and hemodynamic responses may provide a more comprehensive assessment of intervention effects.

Despite growing evidence supporting exercise interventions in hemodialysis populations, limited data are available regarding combined breathing and exercise programs performed before dialysis sessions. Furthermore, mental well-being, hemodynamic regulation, and dialysis adequacy are rarely evaluated simultaneously within the same intervention framework. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of a structured pre-dialysis breathing and exercise program on quality of life, hemodynamic parameters, and dialysis adequacy in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Hypotheses

This study was designed as a prospective, controlled, non-randomized interventional trial conducted to evaluate the effects of a structured exercise and breathing program administered prior to the hemodialysis session on quality of life, hemodynamic parameters, and dialysis adequacy in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis.

The primary hypothesis was that the program would result in a significant improvement in mental quality of life measured by the Mental Component Summary (MCS) compared with the control group. Secondary hypotheses were that the intervention would lead to reductions in systolic blood pressure and heart rate, demonstrate a trend toward improvement in the Physical Component Summary (PCS), and not adversely affect dialysis adequacy (Kt/V and urea reduction ratio [URR]). Improvements in sleep quality and fatigue scores were also anticipated.

Study Setting and Study Population

The study was conducted over an 8-week period in a tertiary level hemodialysis unit. The study population consisted of patients with chronic kidney failure receiving regular maintenance hemodialysis at the center.

Inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years, receiving hemodialysis three times per week for at least three months, hemodynamic stability, and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included acute infection, major surgery within the previous three months, active cardiac instability, severe orthopedic or neurological conditions contraindicating exercise, and significant cognitive impairment.

Eligible patients were enrolled using a consecutive sampling method. Randomization was not performed; instead, patients were allocated according to their weekly dialysis schedule. Patients receiving dialysis on Monday-Wednesday-Friday constituted the intervention group, whereas those dialyzed on Tuesday-Thursday-Saturday comprised the control group. Allocation according to dialysis schedule was selected to facilitate implementation of the intervention and to minimize disruption of routine dialysis unit operations. Because the intervention was delivered immediately before dialysis sessions, this approach also reduced the risk of contamination between groups. Nevertheless, the non-randomized allocation strategy may have introduced residual confounding and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

During the study period, one patient in the intervention group and one patient in the control group died due to progression of underlying disease, and five patients were transferred to other centers because of relocation. None of the losses were related to the intervention. Final analyses were conducted on 53 patients with complete pre–post measurements (29 controls, 24 intervention) (Figure 1).

Intervention Protocol

The exercise program was structured by an internal medicine specialist and a certified yoga instructor. The intervention was implemented prior to the hemodialysis session, three times per week for 8 weeks, and lasted approximately 20 minutes. During the sessions, patients were positioned in a 30–45° elevated Fowler’s position.

The intervention was integrated into routine dialysis care and conducted in close collaboration with the multidisciplinary dialysis team. Dialysis nurses and technicians actively participated in patient monitoring, safety assessment, and implementation of sessions under physician supervision. This team-based approach facilitated feasibility and continuity within standard clinical practice.

The program initially incorporated diaphragmatic breathing and pursed-lip breathing techniques to support autonomic balance. This was followed by upper and lower extremity mobilization exercises to enhance circulation and concluded with controlled rhythmic breathing.

The control group received standard hemodialysis care without additional intervention. All exercise sessions followed a predefined standardized protocol and were supervised by the same physician throughout the study period. Exercise intensity was maintained at a low-to-moderate level and individualized according to patient tolerance. Attendance and patient participation were monitored throughout the intervention period to ensure intervention fidelity, patient safety, and consistency of protocol implementation across the 8-week study period.

Measurements and Outcome Variables

All assessments were performed at baseline and at the end of the 8-week intervention period.

Quality of life was evaluated using the Short Form-36 (SF-36). Subscale scores were calculated on a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better health status. The Physical Component Summary (PCS) was derived as the mean of the Physical Functioning (PF), Role Physical (RP), Bodily Pain (BP), and General Health (GH) subscales. The Mental Component Summary (MCS) was calculated as the mean of the Vitality (VT), Social Functioning (SF), Role Emotional (RE), and Mental Health (MH) subscales. Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and fatigue was evaluated using the Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS). The Turkish versions of the SF-36 and PSQI have previously demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability.11,12 The FAS has been widely used for fatigue assessment in chronic disease populations. Hemodynamic parameters included systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation (SpO2). Dialysis adequacy was determined by Kt/V and urea reduction ratio (URR). The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was recorded as an inflammatory marker.

The primary outcome variable was defined as MCS. Secondary outcomes included PCS, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, Kt/V, and URR. PSQI, FAS, diastolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, SpO2, and NLR were evaluated as exploratory outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

A formal sample size calculation was not performed because the study was designed as a preliminary exploratory intervention study. All eligible patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis during the study period who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate were invited to enroll.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline comparisons between groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test and chi-square test. Group balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD).

To evaluate the effect of the intervention, linear mixed-effects models were constructed for each outcome variable. Time (pre/post), group (control/intervention), and the time × group interaction were included as fixed effects, and a random intercept term was added to account for within-subject correlation. Linear mixed-effects models were selected because they account for within-subject correlations and allow inclusion of repeated measurements in the presence of missing observations under the missing-at-random assumption. The model structure was defined as:

Outcome ~ time * group + (1 | id)

The intervention effect was assessed based on the time × group interaction coefficient, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Primary and secondary outcomes were predefined to reduce the risk of bias related to multiple comparisons.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval date: 19 June 2025; decision number: 10728). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrollment.

Quality of Life

According to the linear mixed-effects model analysis, a significant main effect of time was observed for PCS (β = 23.69, p < 0.001); however, the time × group interaction was not statistically significant (β = −9.15, p = 0.326). In the control group, PCS increased from 51.3 to 75.0, whereas in the intervention group it increased from 51.6 to 66.1 (Figure 2A).

For MCS, a more pronounced increase was observed in the intervention group. MCS increased from 53.9 to 73.0 in the intervention group, compared with an increase from 56.8 to 65.8 in the control group (Figure 2B). The time × group interaction was at the threshold of statistical significance (β = 10.02, p = 0.081; Table 2). The effect size in the intervention group was large (Cohen’s d ≈ 1.32).

Although improvements in PSQI and FAS scores were observed in the intervention group, the time × group interaction was not statistically significant for either outcome (p = 0.514 and p = 0.114, respectively).

Hemodynamic Parameters

In the linear mixed-effects model analysis of hemodynamic findings, the time × group interaction for systolic blood pressure was statistically significant (β = −13.32 mmHg, p = 0.049; Table 2). Systolic blood pressure values decreased markedly in the intervention group, whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (Figure 3A).

Estimated marginal mean changes in systolic blood pressure (A), pulse rate (B), and diastolic blood pressure (C) in control and intervention groups over time. Significant time × group interactions were observed for systolic blood pressure and pulse rate, but not for diastolic blood pressure. Error bars indicate 95% confidence intervals.

Similarly, heart rate demonstrated a significant reduction in the intervention group compared with the control group (β = −6.79 beats/min, p = 0.033; Table 2 and Figure 3B).

For diastolic blood pressure, the time × group interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.389; Figure 3C). No significant time × group interaction was observed for respiratory rate or oxygen saturation parameters.

Dialysis Adequacy

When dialysis adequacy parameters were evaluated, the time × group interaction for Kt/V was found to be statistically significant (β = −0.13, p = 0.018). In the control group, Kt/V showed a slight increase from 1.49 to 1.53, whereas in the intervention group a small decrease was observed, from 1.58 to 1.50 (Figure 4A). Although this bidirectional change reached statistical significance, Kt/V values in both groups remained above the recommended adequacy threshold (≥1.2). The approximately 0.08-unit decrease observed in the intervention group was clinically limited and did not indicate impaired dialysis efficiency.

Changes in dialysis adequacy parameters over time in control and intervention groups. Kt/V (A). Urea reduction ratio (URR) (B).

Similarly, a significant time × group interaction was detected for URR (β = −9.82, p = 0.013; Table 2). While URR values tended to increase in the control group, a relative decrease was observed in the intervention group (Figure 4B). Nevertheless, URR values in both groups remained within the clinically acceptable range.

Overall, these findings suggest that the structured exercise program did not enhance dialysis clearance; however, the small observed changes did not result in clinically meaningful inadequacy.

Inflammatory Markers

At baseline, the mean ± SD neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) for the entire sample was 3.67 ± 2.51 (minimum–maximum: 1.26–13.2). Following the intervention, the mean NLR was 3.37 ± 1.73 (minimum–maximum: 0.8–9.84). No statistically significant difference was observed between groups with respect to baseline NLR values.

In the linear mixed-effects model analysis, neither the main effect of time (β = −0.26, p = 0.379) nor the time × group interaction (β = −0.07, p = 0.870) reached statistical significance. These findings indicate that the 8-week structured exercise and breathing program did not produce a significant change in systemic inflammatory burden.

Overall, the linear mixed-effects model analyses (Table 2) suggest that the structured exercise program was associated with a trend toward improvement in the mental component score and with significant improvements in selected hemodynamic parameters. Although statistically significant interactions were observed for dialysis adequacy measures, these findings did not indicate a clinically meaningful enhancement in dialysis efficiency. Instead, the results suggest that the intervention did not adversely affect dialysis adequacy and may be associated with favorable cardiovascular effects and a trend toward improved mental well-being in maintenance hemodialysis patients.

Although statistically significant time × group interactions were identified for dialysis adequacy parameters, Kt/V and URR values remained within clinically adequate ranges in both groups. No significant changes were detected in inflammatory markers or respiratory parameters.

Discussion

In this prospective controlled study, a structured pre-dialysis breathing and exercise program was associated with a trend toward improvement in mental quality of life and significant improvements in selected hemodynamic parameters. In contrast, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for physical quality-of-life scores, sleep quality, fatigue, or inflammatory status. Although significant time × group interactions were identified for Kt/V and URR, dialysis adequacy remained within clinically acceptable ranges throughout the study period.

The effects of exercise on quality of life in hemodialysis patients have previously been demonstrated in systematic reviews and meta-analyses.1,2,13 Positive effects have been particularly emphasized for depressive symptoms and psychological well-being.2,14 However, a substantial proportion of the existing literature has focused on intradialytic exercise interventions and has primarily evaluated physical performance outcomes.3,4,15 Anding-Rost et al reported that exercise may exert meaningful effects on clinical outcomes in dialysis patients.16 Nevertheless, relatively few studies have examined the impact of combined exercise and breathing protocols performed prior to the hemodialysis session on the mental component of quality of life.

Yoga- and breathing-based interventions have been reported to improve fatigue and psychological symptoms in hemodialysis patients.6–8,17,18 Almutary et al demonstrated that breathing training may improve depression and quality of life outcomes.14 Randomized controlled trials indicating that deep breathing exercises reduce symptom burden in hemodialysis patients further support the clinical relevance of this approach.19 Additionally, evidence suggests that breathing-based approaches and mindfulness components are feasible and acceptable in the dialysis population.20 The increase observed in MCS scores in our study is consistent with this literature and suggests that the intervention may exert benefits not only at the physical but also at the psychological level. However, the observed difference in MCS did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as a trend rather than definitive evidence of improved mental well-being. Nevertheless, the direction of change was consistent with previous studies evaluating breathing-based and mindfulness-oriented interventions in patients receiving hemodialysis.

From a hemodynamic perspective, low-to-moderate intensity exercise and controlled breathing techniques are known to have regulatory effects on the autonomic nervous system.9 Increased parasympathetic activation and reduced sympathetic tone may result in reductions in heart rate and systolic blood pressure. Hsiao et al reported that acute pre-dialysis exercise may modify cardiopulmonary responses.10 Nakoui et al compared aerobic and resistance exercise and demonstrated cardiovascular benefits alongside improvements in quality of life and biochemical parameters.21 The significant reductions in systolic blood pressure and heart rate observed in our study are consistent with these physiological mechanisms.

Several studies have reported that intradialytic exercise may increase Kt/V and URR.3,4 In most of these studies, exercise was performed during the dialysis session, potentially enhancing blood flow and acutely increasing urea clearance. In addition to intradialytic approaches, studies have shown that exercise performed before or after dialysis sessions may improve functional capacity.22 In our study, exercise was administered prior to the hemodialysis session, and no increase in Kt/V or URR was observed. Although small changes were detected in the intervention group, these did not reach a clinically meaningful level of deterioration, and dialysis adequacy remained within acceptable ranges for all patients. These findings indicate that while the structured exercise program did not enhance dialysis clearance, it also did not compromise dialysis adequacy. Network meta-analyses comparing different exercise modalities suggest that outcomes may vary depending on intervention timing and intensity.3 Systematic reviews focusing on frailty further indicate that functional gains associated with exercise may occur independently of changes in solute clearance.13 While several studies have reported improvements in dialysis adequacy following intradialytic exercise interventions, our findings do not support a clinically meaningful enhancement in dialysis efficiency. Instead, the results suggest that the intervention did not adversely affect dialysis adequacy, as both Kt/V and URR remained within accepted clinical targets.

Studies of respiratory muscle training in hemodialysis patients have reported increases in diaphragmatic thickness and respiratory muscle strength.9,23,24 However, short-term interventions have generally not demonstrated significant effects on inflammatory markers or oxygen saturation.18 The absence of significant changes in NLR and SpO2 in our study is consistent with these findings.

The intervention duration was limited to 8 weeks. Current consensus statements and exercise recommendations in chronic kidney disease suggest that longer exercise programs, typically lasting at least 12 weeks, may be necessary to achieve more substantial improvements in physical performance, cardiorespiratory fitness, and quality of life.25 Therefore, extending the duration of exercise interventions may yield more pronounced psychological and physiological benefits. Consequently, the absence of statistically significant changes in PCS, PSQI, FAS, and several physiological parameters may partly reflect the relatively short duration of the intervention rather than the absence of a true intervention effect.

One of the strengths of this study is the comprehensive evaluation of a combined pre-dialysis exercise and breathing protocol across mental, hemodynamic, and dialysis adequacy parameters. Whereas many previous studies have focused on a single outcome variable,13 this study offers a multidimensional assessment. Nonetheless, the non-randomized design and relatively small sample size constitute important limitations. In addition, regular social interaction provided in both groups may represent a potential confounding factor in the interpretation of mental health outcomes. Furthermore, the intervention was integrated into routine dialysis care through collaboration with the multidisciplinary dialysis team, including dialysis nurses and technicians, which enhances the feasibility and potential real-world applicability of the program.

In conclusion, short-term structured exercise and breathing programs administered prior to the hemodialysis session may be associated with favorable cardiovascular effects and a trend toward improved mental well-being without compromising dialysis adequacy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged.

First, the study did not employ a randomized design, and group allocation was based on hemodialysis days. This approach does not entirely eliminate the risk of selection bias. Although baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally balanced between groups, moderate standardized mean differences were observed for certain parameters. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Second, the sample size was relatively limited. The study was conducted among patients receiving regular maintenance hemodialysis at our center who were clinically eligible for exercise and agreed to participate. Consequently, the sample represents a specific subgroup of patients both numerically and demographically. The generalizability of the findings to other centers and more heterogeneous populations may therefore be limited. In particular, outcomes that approached statistical significance (eg, MCS and FAS) may have been influenced by the limited sample size.

Third, although the structured exercise program was implemented in the intervention group, regular communication, one-on-one interactions, and daily social engagement were maintained with patients in both groups. This makes it difficult to fully exclude the potential contribution of social support and therapeutic interaction to the observed improvements in mental component scores. Accordingly, improvements in mental outcomes may not be attributable solely to the physical exercise intervention but may also reflect increased social interaction and a potential Hawthorne effect.

In addition, blinding of participants, care providers, and outcome assessors was not feasible because of the nature of the intervention. Therefore, the possibility of performance and detection bias, particularly for patient-reported outcomes, cannot be completely excluded.

Fourth, the exercise program was administered prior to the hemodialysis session, and no direct comparison with intradialytic exercise was performed. Therefore, the findings cannot be directly compared with the intradialytic exercise literature. In addition, exercise intensity was maintained at a low-to-moderate level, and cardiorespiratory capacity was not assessed using objective measurements.

Finally, only the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was evaluated as an inflammatory marker. More comprehensive biochemical inflammatory markers (eg, C-reactive protein, interleukin-6) were not analyzed. This limitation restricted the assessment of the potential effects of exercise on systemic inflammation.

Implications for Clinical Practice

The findings of this study suggest that incorporating a brief, structured breathing and movement-based exercise program before hemodialysis sessions may offer practical benefits in routine clinical care. The observed improvements in mental well-being and reductions in systolic blood pressure and pulse rate indicate that pre-dialysis exercise may contribute to better psychological status and cardiovascular stability in maintenance hemodialysis patients.

Importantly, the intervention did not compromise dialysis adequacy, as Kt/V and URR values remained within clinically acceptable ranges. This supports the feasibility and safety of implementing supervised low-to-moderate intensity exercise protocols in dialysis units.

The involvement of dialysis nurses and technicians in delivering the intervention highlights the potential for nurse-supported exercise programs to be incorporated into routine renal care settings.

Given the short duration and simple structure of the program, such interventions may be integrated into standard hemodialysis workflows without requiring extensive resources. However, structured supervision and individualized adjustment remain essential to ensure patient safety and adherence.

Conclusion

In this prospective controlled study, a structured pre-dialysis breathing and exercise program was associated with favorable hemodynamic effects and a trend toward improved mental well-being in maintenance hemodialysis patients. Although statistically significant differences were observed in dialysis adequacy parameters, values remained within clinically acceptable ranges, suggesting no detrimental effect on dialysis effectiveness. Larger randomized studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm these findings.
