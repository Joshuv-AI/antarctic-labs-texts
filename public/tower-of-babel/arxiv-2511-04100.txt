Unifying contextual advantages in state discrimination
Kieran Flatt, Joonwoo Bae

Abstract
Quantum state discrimination, alongside its other applications, has recently found use as a tool for witnessing generalised contextuality. In this article, we derive noncontextuality inequalities for both conclusive and inconclusive outcomes across various guessing strategies. For minimum-error discrimination, the advantage is in terms of the confidences of individual outcomes, while for unambiguous state discrimination, it is in terms of the average guessing probability. For maximum-confidence discrimination, we show that contextual advantages occur not only for the confidence but also their average, the guessing probability, as well as the inconclusive outcome rate. Our results unify the contextual advantages across all state discrimination schemes and figures of merit. We envisage that various quantum information applications based on state discrimination may offer advantages over non-contextual theories.



I
Introduction

If gaps exist between the behaviour of classical and quantum systems, it is possible to take advantage of the differences for enhanced information processing. Contextuality, which tells us that measurements do not merely reveal pre-existing features of quantum states, has long been considered one such distinguishing feature. Despite this, it proved difficult for a long to know how to use this property for practical applications. This was in part due to the fact that non-projective measurements, which are required in realistic, noisy scenarios, were difficult to describe in the original framework of Kochen-Spekker contextuality. For this reason, amongst others, a generalised form of contextuality was later put forward
23
.

Taking contextuality as a defining feature of quantum theory implies a natural definition of classicality as the set of statistics which can be generated by theories lacking this property. With this in mind, recent work in the field of generalised contextuality has provided a series of noncontextual inequalities
21
;
15
. These consist of proofs that the upper bound of a given operationally meaningful quantity is lower in theories lacking contextuality compared with the equivalent quantum figure of merit. Of particular importance here, as a fundamental communication primitive, is state discrimination
1
;
3
;
2
. It was first shown that quantum advantages may be witnessed in the setting of minimum-error state discrimination (MESD)
22
and subsequent works generalised this to aspects of unambiguous state discrimination (USD) and maximum confidence measurements (MCMs).
8
;
20
. Our aim in this paper is to complete this picture. We show that the ability to witness quantum advantages is universal across all forms of state discrimination, and in all measures of success.

Demonstrations of quantum advantage take one of two approaches. On the one hand, one can propose a measure of the average performance of a given task and then show that quantum implementations can attain a higher value than classical ones. The relevant notion of classicality in this case will be statistics that can be realised in non-contextual theories. In contrast, one can consider deterministic phenomena that can be witnessed in a single-shot scenario. The proof of the PBR theorem
19
, for example, follows an analogous procedure by showing the existence of a task which can be performed perfectly in
\psi
-epistemic models but not in quantum theory
14
.

State discrimination, the problem of determining by measurement which state, taken from some ensemble, was prepared, allows one to witness both forms of advantage. MESD is concerned with the success probability observed on average over a run of experiments. USD and MCM, on the other hand, are concerned with inferring preparations from a single detection event, as what one learns does not depend on averaged data. More generally, we may also ask about the single-shot properties of the former scheme as well as the averaged properties of the latter two.

In order to do this, we note that the three different schemes may each be analysed according to three distinct figures of merit: the average probability of a correct detection event, the probability of conclusive measurement events, and the retrodictive confidence that a state was prepared given a measurement outcome. Optimisation of these three quantities gives arise to MESD, USD and MCM respectively. Less often noted is that the remaining figures of merit also play a role in each scenario. In this work, we show that they are also witnesses of contextuality. We show that, for MESD, the confidences of each individual outcome give rise to an associated contextual advantage. The same is true for the average guessing probability of USD. We also investigate the average guessing probability and inconclusive outcome rate of an MCM and derive further noncontextual inequalities.

We begin by summarising both what is known so far and what the precise contributions are of this article. After reminding the reader of relevant background information, we then proceed with our main results: demonstrations that the confidences in MESD as well as the average success rates in USD and MCMs give rise to contextual inequalities. We close with remarks on the relevancy of these results to realistic experiments and applications.

III
Background

III.1
State discrimination

Any state discrimination task
1
;
2
;
3
is characterised by a finite, discrete ensemble
\{q_{i},\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of
N
quantum states
\rho_{i}
each prepared with a probability
q_{i}
. The average state produced will be
\rho=\sum_{i}^{N}q_{i}\rho_{i}
. One party, Alice, prepares a system in one of the states and then passes it on to a receiver, Bob. Bob’s task is to determine the state using measurements, represented by a positive-operator-valued-measure (POVM)
\{\pi_{j}\}
, such that
0\leq\pi_{i}\leq 1
and
\sum_{j}\pi_{j}=\mathbf{1}
, in which the outcomes are are associated with either a given state or an inconclusive detection. This set-up is displayed in Figure
1
. Bob will design his measurement to be optimal according to a chosen figure of merit, giving rise to MESD, USD and MCM depending on the choice. We summarise the key points of each scheme in Table
1
.

Three basic quantities can be used to measure the success of a protocol. One is the
average guessing probability
, given by

{\rm P}_{g}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}{\rm P}_{M|P}(i|i)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}{\rm Tr}[\rho_{i}\pi_{i}],

(1)

which is the weighted average that measurement outcome
i
occurs given that state
\rho_{i}
was prepared. We employ (as throughout this article) the notation
{\rm P}_{M|P}(i|k)
for the probability of measurement (
M
) given preparation (
P
).

The second figure of merit which is involved is the
inconclusive outcome rate
. In certain protocols, it is necessary to include an
0
th outcome signifying that the measurement did not reveal anything about the prepare state. This may be by design or due to experimental flaws. The rate at which this happens is given by

{\rm P}_{0}={\rm Tr}[\rho\pi_{0}].

(2)

Finally, the set of
confidences
,

{\rm C}(i)={\rm P}_{P|M}(i|i)=\frac{q_{i}{\rm P}_{P|M}(i|i)}{{\rm P}_{M}(i)}.

(3)

are the retrodictive probabilities that a particular state was prepared given that the relevant measurement outcome was observed.

Figure 1:
A general state discrimination scheme requires a part to send one state from an ensemble
\{q_{i},\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
to another. The latter measures with a POVM which contains both conclusive (
\pi_{1},...,\pi_{N}
) and inconclusive (
\pi_{0}
) outcomes.

Optimisation over each of these three quantities, with the potential of additional constraints, can be associated with a different form of state discrimination. If the average guessing probability, Eq.
1
is maximised over an
N
outcome measurement, one is said to perform minimum error state discrimination. In the simplest case of two states the upper bound is known to be the Helstrom bound
9
.

Unambiguous state discrimination
10
;
7
;
17
refers to scenarios in which all conclusive outcomes, those labelled
1
to
N
, determine the prepared state with certainty. This result is achieved by the
i
th POVM element projecting onto a subspace outside of the support of the span of the other states. A necessary condition, referred to as Chefles’ criterion
5
, for such a measurement to be implementable is that the states in the ensemble are all linearly independent. POVMs implementing this will typically include an inconclusive outcome, and minimising its occurence rate, Eq.
2
, will be optimal.

A maximum confidence measurement
6
;
16
;
13
is defined as one in which every confidence, Eq.
3
, reaches its maximum value for all
i
. This is known to happen if each POVM element
\pi_{i}
is a rank-one measurement proportional to the projector onto the eigenvector of
\rho^{-1}\rho_{i}\rho^{-1}
with the highest associated eigenvalue. For cases in which this set of quantities is difficult to calculate analytically, an SDP approach has been developed
13
Note that, under this approach, we can redefine USD as the subset of MCMs in which it is possible to attain
{\rm C}(i)=1
for all
i
in the ensemble.

III.2
Contextual advantages

Different definitions of contextuality have been proposed and here we focus on the ‘generalised contextuality’ approach developed by Spekkens
23
. This framework shows that operational theories subject to a constraint of non-contextuality cannot reproduce all of the same statistics as quantum theory.

An operational theory represents experimental procedures as mathematical functions on an ontic state space
\Lambda
, in which each state is associated with a point
\lambda
encoding all physical properties of the system. An experiment is then decomposed into preparations, transformations and measurements. Preparations
P
are defined as epistemic states,
\mu_{x}(\lambda)
, probability distributions over
\Lambda
. Measurements
M
are treated as sets of positive response functions
\{\xi_{i|M}(\lambda)\}
normalised so that
\sum_{i}\xi_{i|M}(\lambda)=1\,\,\forall\lambda
. The probability of a measurement outcome
i
given measurement
M
and preparation
j
in such models is given by

{\rm P}(j|i,M)=\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\mu_{j}(\lambda)\xi_{i|M}(\lambda).

(4)

Two epistemic states are said to be
preparation equivalent
if every choice of measurement gives the same statistics for both states:

\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\mu_{P_{1}}(\lambda)\xi_{i|M}(\lambda)=\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\mu_{P_{2}}(\lambda)\xi_{i|M}(\lambda)\,\,\,\forall\{i|M\}.

(5)

We can now define a noncontextual theory: a theory is said to be preparation noncontextual if it assigns the same epistemic state to all equivalent preparations in a model. A similar definition holds for measurement noncontextuality.

The final piece of terminology required is a measure for the distance between two pure states. In an operational model this is called the confusability,
c_{1,2}
,

\begin{split}c_{1,2}&=\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\mu_{2}(\lambda)\xi_{1}(\lambda)\\
&=\int_{{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]}d\lambda\mu_{2}(\lambda)\end{split}

(6)

where the second line follows from imposing noncontextuality and
\xi_{1}(\lambda)
is a response function giving
{\rm P}_{M|P}(1|1)=1
. This function is symmetric such that
c_{1,2}=c_{2,1}
. When comparing noncontextual theories with quantum theory, this value is to be interpreted as the squared overlap of the state. That is, if
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
are associated with the epistemic states
\mu_{1}(\lambda)
and
\mu_{2}(\lambda)
respectively, then the two theories are compared by fixing
c_{1,2}=|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}
.

Two additional assumptions are typically made of the epistemic model. One is that, for each pure state there exists a deterministic response function. The second is that probabilistic mixing over quantum states maps onto probabilistic mixing over the associated epistemic states:

p\rho_{1}+(1-p)\rho_{2}\rightarrow p\mu_{1}(\lambda)+(1-p)\mu_{2}(\lambda).

(7)

Both of these assumptions will be used in the calculations that follow.

IV
Contextual advantages in state discrimination

We begin in earnest by reviewing the previously known contextual advantages in state discrimination.

IV.1
MESD: Success Probability

The first contextual advantage to be shown in a state discrimination task was the guessing probability of a minimum error measurement
22
. The specific scenario investigated was a binary, equiprobable state discrimination task, represented in quantum theory by two pure states
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
. The quantum upper bound of this problem is given by the well-known Helstrom bound

{\rm P}^{(Q)}_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}}\right).

(8)

This is compared with the maximum probability of distinguishing two preparations
\mu_{1}(\lambda)
and
\mu_{2}(\lambda)
with a confusability
c_{1,2}
in a noncontextual theory.

In order to impose the constraint of noncontextuality, preparation equivalence is required. There, however, do not exist any equivalences if we invoke only two pure states. In order to get around this, the existence of a ‘mirror ensemble’
\{|\overline{\psi}_{1}\rangle,|\overline{\psi}_{2}\rangle\}
consisting of the pair of states orthogonal to the initial ensemble
\langle\psi_{i}|\overline{\psi}_{i}\rangle=0
is assumed. With this addition we now have an equivalence between alternative preparations of the maximally mixed state:

\frac{1}{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|+\frac{1}{2}|\overline{\psi}_{1}\rangle\langle\overline{\psi}_{1}|=\frac{1}{2}|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|+\frac{1}{2}|\overline{\psi}_{2}\rangle\langle\overline{\psi}_{2}|

(9)

which then implies that in a noncontextual model the following constraint will apply:

\frac{1}{2}\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mu}_{1}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mu}_{2}(\lambda)

(10)

in which
\overline{\mu}_{i}(\lambda)\cong|\overline{\psi}_{i}\rangle\langle\overline{\psi}_{i}|
. This relation constrains the relationship between the epistemic states in the considered model. In particular, one finds that

\mu_{1}(\lambda)=\mu_{2}(\lambda)\,\,\,\forall\,\,\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}].

(11)

Application of this constraint along with the assumption that all epistemic states are associated with a deterministic response function then allows one to bound the guessing probability. The result is that

{\rm P}_{g}^{(NC)}=1-\frac{c_{1,2}}{2}

(12)

which is strictly smaller than the Helstrom bound.

IV.2
USD: Success Probability and Inconconclusive Outcome Rate

We now turn our attention to the quantum advantages associated with unambiguous state discrimination, which were first demonstrated in Ref.
8
. Full technical details can be found in that article. These quantum advantages are associated with the inconclusive outcome rate, which is strictly higher in a non-contextual theory, and the guessing probability. We begin by noting that, as the probability of incorrectly guessing will be equal to zero, these two quantities are directly related to one another in USD,
P_{g}=1-P_{0}
, and it suffices to consider just one property.

It is trivial to show that in the binary discrimination scenario, USD is possible in a noncontextual theory. The response functions which satisfy these will be of the form

\xi_{i}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\gamma,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{i+1}]\\
0,&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}

(13)

where addition in the subscripts is modular and
0<\gamma\leq 1
. Such response functions are equivalent to POVM elements of the form
\pi_{i}=\gamma|\psi_{i+1}\rangle\langle\psi_{i+1}|
. This observation is furthermore supported by a recent theorem
25
stating that a sufficient condition for a set of states to be preparable in a preparation-noncontextual model is that they are linearly independent. This is in fact the same criterion, as for states to be unambiguously discriminable.

One can then optimise the choice of
\gamma
in order to minimise the inconclusive outcome rate, given that the response functions are constrained by preparation noncontextuality. The ‘mirror ensemble’ approach, as taken in the minimum error calculation, is again used and the result is found to be

{\rm P}_{0}^{(NC)}=\frac{1}{2}(1+c_{1,2}).

(14)

This value is strictly larger than or equal to the inconclusive outcome rate for quantum USD. In this manner, the contextuality of state discrimination was extended to USD.

IV.3
MCM: Confidences

The next result to review is the advantage associated with the confidence in MCMs. As noted in the background, MCMs and USD coincide if the latter form is possible. The simplest case in which it is not is a dephasing of the binary pure state ensemble. That is, two states with the form

\rho_{i}=(1-p)|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|+p\frac{\mathds{1}}{2}\,\,\,i=1,2

(15)

where
0\leq p\leq 1
characterises the purity of the pair of states and
\{|\psi_{1}\rangle,|\psi_{2}\rangle\}
are two arbitrarily chosen pure states. The quantum problem can be solved using typical methods and the maximum confidence for each state is found to be

{\rm C}^{(Q)}(i)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{(1-p)\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-(1-p)^{2}|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}}}\right).

(16)

Using the same methods as discussed above, the noncontextual bound to the confidence can also be calculated. This is found to be

{\rm C}^{(NC)}(i)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{(1-p)(1-c_{1,2})}{1-(1-p)c_{1,2}}\right)

(17)

which again is smaller than the equivalent quantum confidence seen above.

It was shown in these three results that quantum advantages are common to different forms of state discrimination. What we will see in the remaining sections is that this advantage can also be observed for other figures of merit.

V
Characterising contextual advantages

V.1
MESD: Confidences

Our task in this section is to complete Table
1
, and we will start with confidence associated with minimum error state discrimination. We assume that the measurement performed optimises the guessing probability
P_{g}
and then calculate the confidence provided by this measurement. Again we will focus on the binary, equiprobable state discrimination task, where both states are pure and the optimal POVM is given by the Helstrom measurement.

Let us first calculate this quantity for the quantum guessing task. One finds that this is equal to the optimal guessing probability. The confidence will be given by

\begin{split}{\rm C}(i)&=\frac{{\rm P}_{M|P}(i|i){\rm P}_{P}(i)}{{\rm P}_{M}(i)}\\
&=\frac{{\rm P}_{M|P}(i|i)}{{\rm P}_{M|P}(i|i)+{\rm P}_{M|P}(i|j\neq i)}\end{split}

(18)

where the second line follows from equiprobability. Now we note that, for a Helstrom measurement, the probability of success is balanced, in the sense that it is equal for both outcomes:
{\rm P}_{M|P}(0|0)={\rm P}_{M|P}(1|1)
. Given also that there is no inconclusive outcomes in a minimum error measurement, we can see that

{\rm P}_{M|P}(i|i)+{\rm P}_{M|P}(i|j\neq i)=1

(19)

and therefore the quantum confidence given by this measurement is simply

{\rm C}^{(Q)}(i)={\rm P}_{M|P}(i|i)={\rm P}_{g}.

(20)

The next stage is to calculate this quantity for the noncontextual ontological model. In order to do this, we require the response functions which implement noncontextually optimal MESD. These can be found via inspection. One choice is the following structure, which we label
M_{1}
:

\begin{split}\xi_{1}(\lambda)&=\begin{cases}1,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\\
0,&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}\\
\xi_{2}(\lambda)&=\begin{cases}0,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\\
1,&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}\end{split}

(21)

corresponding, in the language of quantum theory, to the measurement outcome identifying
1
being a projection onto the state
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and the outcome identifying
2
being a projection onto the orthogonal state
|\overline{\psi}_{1}\rangle
. Alternatively the inverse of this structure, which we label
M_{2}
, also allows for noncontextually optimal guessing probability:

\begin{split}\xi_{1}(\lambda)&=\begin{cases}0,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]\\
1,&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}\\
\xi_{2}(\lambda)&=\begin{cases}1,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]\\
0,&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}\end{split}

(22)

Importantly, any classical probabilistic mixing of strategies of the form
\omega M_{1}+(1-\omega)M_{2}
, where
0\leq\omega\leq 1
will also result in the same guessing probability. It is, however, important to note that the confidences associated with these measurements will not be the same. To see this, we can compare the confidences calculated from
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. One finds:

{\rm C}^{(NC)}_{M_{1}}(1)=\frac{1}{1+c_{1,2}},\,\,\,\,\,{\rm C}^{(NC)}_{M_{1}}(2)=1

(23)

and

{\rm C}^{(NC)}_{M_{2}}(1)=1,\,\,\,\,\,{\rm C}^{(NC)}_{M_{2}}(2)=\frac{1}{1+c_{1,2}}.

(24)

Thus, in order to analyse the contextual advantages associated with the MESD confidence fully, we must optimise over the whole set of mixed strategies. The response functions given by
\omega M_{1}+(1-\omega)M_{2}
have the following structure:

\begin{split}\xi_{1}(\lambda)&=\begin{cases}\omega,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]\\
1,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]\\
0,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]\\
1-\omega,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]\end{cases}\\
\xi_{2}(\lambda)&=\begin{cases}1-\omega,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]\\
0,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]\\
1,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]\\
\omega,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]\end{cases}\end{split}

(25)

It can be calculated than this class of response functions will give
P^{(NC)}_{g}
for any value of
\omega
. Note that this situation differs from quantum MESD, which is an extremal measurement and therefore implemented by a single POVM only. We must now calculate the confidences
{\rm C}(1)
and
{\rm C}(2)
for this strategy. Let us begin with
{\rm C}^{(NC)}(1)
. The full expression for this will be given by

{\rm C}^{(NC)}(1)=\frac{\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{1}(\lambda)\mu_{1}(\lambda)}{\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{1}(\lambda)\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{1}(\lambda)\mu_{0}(\lambda)}.

(26)

We can evaluate this expression using Eq.
25
and begin from the numerator:

\begin{split}\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{1}(\lambda)\mu_{1}(\lambda)=\omega&\int_{{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]}d\lambda\mu_{1}(\lambda)\\
&+\int_{{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]}d\lambda\mu_{1}(\lambda)\end{split}

(27)

We can simplify this expression by noticing the following relation:

\begin{split}\int_{{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]}d\lambda\mu_{1}(\lambda)=&\int_{{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]}d\lambda\mu_{1}(\lambda)\\
&+\int_{{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]}d\lambda\mu_{1}(\lambda)\\
&=\int_{{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]}d\lambda\mu_{1}(\lambda)\\
&=c_{1,2}.\end{split}

(28)

An analogous process can be used to simplify the remaining integrals in Eq.
26
and one finds that the confidence will be given by

{\rm C}^{(NC)}(1)=\frac{1-(1-\omega)c_{1,2}}{1-(1-2\omega)c_{1,2}}

(29)

and likewise

{\rm C}^{(NC)}(2)=\frac{1-\omega c_{1,2}}{1+(1-2\omega)c_{1,2}}

(30)

Let’s examine these two formulae, which display a tunable trade-off in the confidence on each arm of the detector in a noncontextual MESD measurement. When
\omega=0
or
\omega=1
we find that one state is discriminated unambiguously
\mathrm{C}^{(NC)}(i)=1
while the other is determined with a sub-quantum confidence of
1/(1+c_{1,2})
. When
\omega=1/2
, both outcomes occur with equal probability and their confidences coincide as
\mathrm{C}^{(NC)}(1)=\mathrm{C}^{(NC)}(2)=1-\frac{1}{2}c_{1,2}
, the noncontextual upper bound on the guessing probability. Both confidences are monotonic and symmetric under
\omega\rightarrow 1-\omega
. Thus we are led to see that, in a noncontextual theory, for some class of the possible measurement strategies a quantum advantage is observed. We can calculate this for
\mathrm{C}(1)
as:

\begin{split}\mathrm{C}^{(Q)}(1)&\geq\mathrm{C}^{(NC)}(1)\\
&\rightarrow\omega\geq\omega^{*}\frac{(1-c_{1,2})(1-\sqrt{1-c_{1,2}})}{2c_{1,2}\sqrt{1-c_{1,2}}}\end{split}

(31)

where the identification
|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}=c_{1,2}
has been made. We will have a similar bound for
\mathrm{C}(2)
to express a quantum advantage when
1-\omega*
is above the same bound. Thus, when
\omega
is in the range
\omega*\leq\omega\leq 1-\omega*
, the quantum value of both
\mathrm{C}(1)
and
{\rm C}(2)
is strictly greater than the noncontextual one. Outside of this range, either one arm or the other is in fact greater for noncontextual measurements. The behaviour of these two confidences is displayed in Fig.
2
alongside the quantum guessing probability.

The confidences found when a minimum error measurement is performed are displayed for both quantum and noncontextual theories. In a quantum measurement both arms have equal confidence
\mathrm{C}^{(Q)}(1)=\mathrm{C}^{(Q)}(2)=\mathrm{C}^{(Q)}(i)
, displayed as a solid black line. In noncontextual theories the two arms have different confidences (blue dashed and red dotted lines) depending on the measurement, parameterised by

. We use fixed overlap

.

V.2
MCM: Inconclusive Outcome Rate

We now turn our attention to the inconclusive rate in maximum confidence measurements, and will study the noisy discrimination scenario, when the aim in the quantum case is to discriminate the states in Eq.
15
and the analogy of this in the ontological model is to discriminate the epistemic states

\tilde{\mu}_{i}(\lambda)=(1-p)\mu_{i}(\lambda)+p\mu_{\mathds{1}/2}(\lambda),\,\,\,\,\,i=1,2.

(32)

Here,
\mu_{i}(\lambda)
are two extremal epistemic states associated with the two pure states in the quantum framework and
\mu_{\mathds{1}/2}(\lambda)
is the epistemic-state representation of the maximally mixed quantum state
\mathds{1}/2
. Note that in noncontextual theories the latter takes the same representation for all methods of preparation (i.e., all decompositions in the quantum framework).

The quantum task has been studied in detail
13
. If the states are parameterised as

\begin{split}|\psi_{1}\rangle&=\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})|0\rangle-\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})|1\rangle\\
|\psi_{2}\rangle&=\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})|0\rangle+\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})|1\rangle\end{split}

(33)

then optimal POVM elements will be rank-one and follow the structure
\pi_{i}=\alpha|\phi_{i}\rangle\langle\phi_{i}|\,\,i=1,2
, where
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
is a free parameter and

\begin{split}|\phi_{i}\rangle&=\cos(\frac{\phi}{2})|0\rangle+(-1)^{i}\sin(\frac{\phi}{2})|1\rangle\\
\tan(\phi)&=p\cos(\theta)\sqrt{\frac{1-p^{2}\cos^{2}(\theta)}{1-\cos^{2}(\theta)}}\end{split}

(34)

The important point here is to note that the optimal quantum measurement is a function of the amount of dephasing noise. From this construction we can write the inconclusive POVM element as
\mathds{1}-\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}
and minimise over the parameters
\alpha
the inconclusive outcome rate, which is found to be

{\rm P}^{(Q)}_{0}=(1-p)|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|.

(35)

This is to be compared with the equivalent quantity in a noncontextual model of MCMs. The feature which we must use is that the noncontextually optimal measurement is represented by the same measurement as is used for unambiguous state discrimination:

\begin{split}\xi_{1}(\lambda)&=\begin{cases}\gamma_{1},&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]\\
0,&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}\\
\xi_{2}(\lambda)&=\begin{cases}\gamma_{2},&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\\
0,&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}\end{split}

(36)

where the response function representing an inconclusive outcome will be given by
\xi_{0}(\lambda)=1-\xi_{1}(\lambda)-\xi_{2}(\lambda)
and
0<\gamma_{i}\leq 1
for
i=1,2
. This response function in full will have the structure

\xi_{0}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}1,&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]\\
1-\gamma_{1},&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]\\
1-\gamma_{2},&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]\\
1-\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2},&\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]\end{cases}\\

(37)

From this it is possible to calculate the inconclusive outcome rate. First note that the average density matrix for the noisy epistemic states will be

\tilde{\mu}(\lambda)=\frac{1-p}{2}\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\frac{1-p}{2}\mu_{2}(\lambda)+p\mu_{\mathds{1}/2}(\lambda)

(38)

Then the quantity we wish to evaluate is

\begin{split}{\rm P}^{(NC)}_{0}&=\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\tilde{\mu}(\lambda)\xi_{0}(\lambda)\\
&=\frac{1-p}{2}\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{0}(\lambda)\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\frac{1-p}{2}\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{0}(\lambda)\mu_{2}(\lambda)\\
&+p\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{0}(\lambda)\mu_{\mathds{1}/2}(\lambda)\end{split}

(39)

We can evaluate each of these integrals in turn using Eq.
37
. First, we find

\begin{split}\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{0}(\lambda)\mu_{1}(\lambda)&=\int_{{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\mu_{2}]}d\lambda\mu_{1}(\lambda)\\
&+(1-\gamma_{1})\int_{{\rm supp}[\mu_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]}d\lambda\mu_{2}(\lambda)\\
&=c_{1,2}+(1-\gamma_{1})c_{1,\overline{2}}\\
&=1-\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{1}c_{1,2}\end{split}

(40)

A similar process gives

\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{0}(\lambda)\mu_{2}(\lambda)=1-\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{2}c_{1,2}

(41)

The final piece to evaluate is the integral over the maximally mixed epistemic state. In order to do this, we can decompose the epistemic state into either preparation, noting that the two will be the same due to the model’s noncontextuality. We can thus evaluate:

\begin{split}\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{0}(\lambda)\mu_{\mathds{1}/2}(\lambda)&=\int_{\Lambda}d\lambda\xi_{0}(\lambda)\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu_{1}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mu}_{1}(\lambda)\right)\\
&=1-\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\end{split}

(42)

where the second line follows using similar techniques as above.

Bringing the previous few results together we now have

{\rm P}^{(NC)}_{0}=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-(1-p)c_{1,2}\right)\left(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\right)

(43)

We need to find the minimum value of this subject to the constraints imposed on the non-negativity of the response function. Clearly, this function reaches a minimum when
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
as is allowed. Examining the inconclusive response function, Eq.
37
, we can see that the piece
\xi_{0}(\lambda)
on the domain
\lambda\in{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{1}]\cap{\rm supp}[\overline{\mu}_{2}]
requires that
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\leq 1
to be non-negative. Therefore we can take the upper bound
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}=1
and the minimum inconclusive rate in a non-contextual model will be

{\rm P}^{(NC)}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+(1-p)c_{1,2}\right)

(44)

Which is to be compared with Eq.
35
using
c_{1,2}=|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}
. We display the two functions in Fig.

c_{1,2}
and fixed noise
p
where it can be seen that the inconclusive rate for a noncontextual model is greater than or equal to that of quantum theory.

V.3
MCM: Guessing probability

The final piece which we fill in is the guessing probability, Eq.
1
, of a maximum confidence measurement. We can recast the guessing probability into a form more amenable to the current task by writing it in terms of the confidences and outcome rates of the ensemble:

{\rm P}_{g}=\sum_{i}{\rm P}_{M}(i){\rm C}(i),

(45)

which follows simply from the definition and Bayes rule. The index
i
here runs over the states in the ensemble.

We can again note that for pure quantum states and extremal epistemic states, the MCM coincides with unambiguous state discrimination. For these cases, we need simply to repeat that the guessing probability in USD is
P_{g}=1-P_{0}
and we have the same advantage shown above. For this reason, we need only to focus on the noisy ensemble discussed in the previous subsection.

The simplest way to demonstrate the contextual advantage here is to note that both confidences in a binary discrimination task are equal, as can be seen in Eqs.
16
and
17
. We can therefore write
{\rm C}(1)={\rm C}(2)={\rm C}
and Eq.
45
becomes

\begin{split}{\rm P}_{g}&=\sum_{i}{\rm P}_{M}(i){\rm C}\\
&=(1-{\rm P}_{0}){\rm C}\end{split}

(46)

Both
P_{0}
and
{\rm C}
have been previously calculated for both quantum and contextual theories, and both were seen to display a quantum advantage. As we have
{\rm P}_{0}^{Q}\leq{\rm P}^{(NC)}_{0}
and
{\rm C}^{(Q)}(i)\geq{\rm C}^{(NC)}(i)\,\,\forall i
, it trivially follows that
{\rm P}^{(Q)}_{g}\geq{\rm P}^{(NC)}_{g}
and a quantum advantage therefore exists also for this parameter. For completeness, the full expressions for these two quantities will be:

\begin{split}{\rm P}_{g}^{(NC)}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{p}{2}-(1-p)c_{1,2}\right)\\
{\rm P}_{g}^{(Q)}&=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-(1-p)|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|\\
&+(1-p)\sqrt{\frac{1-(1-p)|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|}{1+(1-p)|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|}}\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}}\Bigr)\end{split}

(47)

VI
Discussion

Demonstrating advantages of quantum systems over classical ones plays a central role in the developing of useful applications for quantum technologies. As state discrimination is the communication primitive underlying many applications, it is important to firmly state which property of quantum theory allow for it to perform above the classical limit. Here, we show that it is contextuality. While the contextual advantages associated with state discrimination were previously known, it was not clear which aspects were able to show a contextual advantage. The main lesson of this paper is that all figures of merit are reliant on this property. This was shown by demonstrating advantages for the guessing probability and inconclusive outcome rate in a maximum confidence measurement, as well as showing that under certain circumstances the confidence associated with a minimum error measurement also witness contextuality.

Maximum confidence measurements, and the measurement confidences more generally, play an important role in this work. One reason is that, in experimental settings, one is forced to deal with preparation and measurement noise, including non-detection events. These can be naturally taken into account using the confidence as a figure of merit, unlike the average success probability. Another reason is that they allow us to witness two notions of quantum advantage. In a single-shot setting, we can make no claims about the average statistics. The confidence, however, is a well-defined quantity in this setting, and is able to see beyond-classical statistics.

Our results should find use in experimental tests of contextuality, in which data are unavoidably noisy. It would be interesting to connect the inequalities found in state discrimination, a form of prepare-and-measure scenario, to nonlocal inequalities through the recent works bridging the two topics
18
;
24
, as well as understand the noise conditions under which contextuality can be witnessed
11
in state discrimination settings. As state discrimination plays an important role in many uses of quantum theory, for example randomness generation s
20
;
4
and sequential communications
12
, we, furthermore, believe that this work will find applications in practical use cases.
