A novel immersion chemical mechanical polishing device and tailored slurry for processing heavy duty gas turbine compressor blades
Authors: Qiyuan Li, Yaowen Wu, Liguang Dong, Yihai Liu, Qiang Dong, Lin Yang, Dingjun Li, Xiaocong Xiong, Ke Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a key component of heavy-duty gas turbines, the compressor blades typically have complex free-form surfaces. These blades are widely made of martensitic stainless steel, which has excellent high strength, high corrosion resistance, and wear resistance, making it a typical difficult-to-machine material. To address these challenges, a novel green chemical mechanical polishing (CMP) slurry has been developed. Its components include silica, zirconia, malic acid, hydrogen peroxide, and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na). By applying this environmentally friendly CMP process, the surface roughness (Sa) of the stainless steel is reduced to 0.271 ± 0.04 nm, and the material removal rate reaches 127.11 nm/min. To verify its practical application effect, this formulation was applied to the polishing of compressor blades using an immersion CMP equipment. Finally, a surface roughness of 103.0 ± 0.5 nm was achieved, and the material removal rate reached 650.67 nm/min. The thickness of the damaged layer after polishing decreased to 28.78 nm. Results from both scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy indicated no chemical residues and no abnormal elemental enrichment after polishing.

Introduction

Martensitic stainless steels1 are commonly employed in the manufacturing of compressor blades for heavy-duty gas turbines2, owing to their superior wear and corrosion resistance3. The surface roughness of these materials has a direct impact on their performance. Before practical applications, most engineering components must meet the minimum linear surface roughness requirements4. Under static and dynamic loads, high surface roughness usually leads to severe crack formation5,6. A study by Bae et al.7 examined how surface roughness influences the fatigue limit. It was observed that a reduction in surface roughness from 2.053 to 0.226 μm led to an increase in the fatigue limit from 470 to 509 MPa, corresponding to a 7.66% improvement. Consequently, polishing processes aimed at minimizing surface roughness are essential for enhancing the mechanical properties of martensitic stainless steels.

Shao et al.8 applied the shear thickening mechanism of non-Newtonian fluids to polish the concave surfaces of high-temperature nickel-based alloy turbine blades, achieving both efficiency and high quality. For the front and rear edges of aircraft engine blades, Zhu et al.9 developed a flexible polishing wheel technology to overcome issues of low efficiency and poor consistency. With the increasing complexity of blade geometries and operational conditions, novel processing approaches are necessitated10. Yun et al.11 proposed a synchronous polishing method utilizing multi-axis machine tools to enhance blade profile consistency. Xiao et al.12 presented an integrated polishing technique combining rotary polishing (RBP) and seven-axis linked sanding belt polishing (SLBP). The notable enhancement in surface uniformity post-integrated polishing demonstrates the feasibility of integrating belt and rotary methods for automated precision machining of compressor blades. Therefore, precise polishing is crucial during the manufacturing of heavy-duty gas turbine compressor blades to improve surface quality and performance. Traditional techniques, including mechanical13,14 and chemical15, have inherent limitations: mechanical polishing may introduce surface stress and burns (During processing or treatment, localized overheating causes irreversible changes in the microstructure of the material, potentially resulting in microcracks, changes in hardness, or surface oxidation), whereas chemical polishing can lead to environmental contamination and surface defects16.

Traditional polishing fluids frequently contain strong acids, strong bases, or toxic compounds, exemplified by sulfuric acid (H2SO4)17, nitric acid (HNO3)18, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), benzotriazole (BTA), and ethylenediamine (EDA)18. In a study by Liu et al.19, chemical mechanical polishing (CMP)20–22 of GCr15 steel was performed using H2SO4 and KOH for pH adjustment, along with 5-methyl-1H-benzotriazole (5-methyl-BTA) and thiazole (TA) as corrosion inhibitors, resulting in a surface roughness of Ra (average surface roughness) 0.8 nm. Similarly, Peng et al.23 carried out CMP on 9Cr18Mo martensitic stainless steel with a solution containing H2SO4, H2O2, and glycine, achieving an ultra-smooth surface of Sa (arithmetical mean height) 0.63 nm without detectable defects. Nevertheless, the employment of strong acids, strong bases, and hazardous substances poses significant health risks to operators, such as skin burns and respiratory damage from acids and bases, as well as potential chronic poisoning or allergic reactions from toxic materials. Moreover, environmental pollution from industrial discharges or accidental spills can severely degrade soil, water, and air quality, leading to long-term ecological consequences.

In this work, a custom CMP slurry (the slurry adopts low-corrosive and low-toxic organic acids and conventional chelating agents) was developed for martensitic stainless steel compressor blades used in heavy-duty gas turbines. The slurry consisted of l-malic acid, hydrogen peroxide, and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na). l-malic acid, a naturally occurring dicarboxylic acid, served as a pH regulator, while hydrogen peroxide acted as an oxidizing agent. EDTA-2Na functioned as a chelating agent, binding to metal ions in the solution. Through an optimized polishing process, surface roughness of Sa 0.271 ± 0.04 nm (50 × 50 μm2) and material removal rate (MRR) of 127.11 nm/min were achieved. When applied to the overall immersion polishing of heavy-duty gas turbine compressor blades, this formulation resulted in a surface roughness (Sa) of 103.0 ± 0.5 nm and an MRR of 650.67 nm/min, thereby satisfying the surface quality requirements for compressor blades.

Experimental details

Experimental design

The compressor blades (Martensitic stainless steel) were cut into samples with dimensions of 10 × 10 × 3 mm3. Three samples were evenly attached to each polishing disk, as shown in Fig. 1a. The metallographic images of the steel are shown in Fig. 1b–d, where acicular and lath structures are interwoven, representing the tempered martensite phase. The samples were first rough-polished using a polyurethane polishing pad to achieve an average surface roughness of Sa 300 ± 10 nm. The rotational speed of the polishing disc is 120 rpm, and the applied pressure is 40 kPa. These ensure consistent surface roughness of the samples before fine polishing. During rough polishing, silica abrasives with an average particle size of 1 μm were employed, followed by fine polishing using silica abrasives with an average particle size of 10 nm. Black abrasive leather polishing pads were used for fine polishing. Both the rough and fine polishing processes were carried out using a planar polisher (UNIPOL-1200S, manufactured by Shenyang Kejing Automation Equipment Co., Ltd.). The silicon carbide and silica abrasives were purchased from Zhongye New Materials Co., Ltd. As can be seen from Fig. 2, before polishing, the blade sample mainly consisted of an iron matrix, containing 12.41% Cr, 0.63% Mn, 0.61% Ni, 0.36% Mo, 0.25% Si, and other trace elements. The elemental composition was consistent with the nominal composition of this martensitic stainless steel. No abnormal enrichment of characteristic elements related to the polishing liquid or other foreign impurity elements was detected. The surface morphology and elemental distribution of the blade samples were detected using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) and an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) (JSM-7800F, JEOL, Japan). EDS surface scanning (Voltage: 5 kV; Emission Current: 8; Working distance: 11 mm) results indicated that the matrix elements were uniformly distributed, without local element enrichment or abnormal distribution, demonstrating that there were no pretreatment residues on the original blade surface, which could serve as a reference sample for detecting residues after polishing.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of CMP process and microstructure of the steel: (a) Schematic diagram of CMP process for blade samples; (b–d) Microstructural characterization of the steel.

Experimental characterization

During slurry preparation, abrasive particles were ultrasonically dispersed in DI water for 30 min, and continuous stirring was maintained throughout the CMP process. Each polishing trial lasted 40 min under consistent polishing pressure and linear velocity, with a slurry flow rate of 9 mL/min. Post-polishing, specimens were sequentially rinsed with DI water and anhydrous ethanol, followed by drying with lint-free cloths to ensure contaminant-free surfaces. The pH value (adjusted by malic acid) was measured using a Leici pH meter (PHS-2F, Shanghai Instrument and Electronics Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.), with a minimum resolution of 0.01 pH. The corrosion potential and corrosion current of the polishing fluid on the stainless steels were analyzed using an electrochemical workstation (CHI760E, Shanghai Chenhua Instrument Co., Ltd.). During the electrochemical test, a saturated mercury chloride electrode was used as the reference electrode, a platinum wire as the auxiliary electrode, and the blade samples as the working electrode. The samples underwent surface pretreatment using progressive wet sanding with 800-grit, 1200-grit, and 2000-grit water-resistant silicon carbide sandpaper. The grinding process followed a sequence from coarse to fine. Each time the sandpaper grit was changed, the sample was rotated 90° to perform cross-grinding, thoroughly removing residual scratches from the previous grit and the original surface oxide layer until a uniform, smooth, fresh metal substrate surface was obtained. Prior to the test, the sample was immersed in the electrolyte for 400 s to reach a steady-state open-circuit potential. The voltage range for the Tafel polarization curve test was open-circuit potential (OCP) ± 0.5 V, with a scanning rate of 5 mV/s.

Analysis of functional group changes using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR, Thermo Scientific™ Nicolet™ iS50). The chemical action mechanism was characterized via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA). The damage layer thickness of the material was examined using a high-resolution field-emission transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Talos F200 × 200 kV, USA). A focused ion beam microscope (FIB, Helios G4 UX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed to prepare TEM cross-sectional samples of the blade. The specific steps for FIB sample preparation are as follows: (1) Adhere the sample to the sample stage using conductive adhesive and place it into the focused ion beam chamber. (2) In the focused ion beam dual-beam system, tilt the sample stage to an angle of 52°. Deposit a C protective layer with a length of 20 μm and a thickness of 2 μm on the sample surface. (3) On both sides of the C protective layer, dig two deep pits using a large ion beam current of 30 kV and 20 nA to remove excess material. Then, perform pre-thinning with a medium beam current of 30 kV and 9 nA. Tilt the sample stage to 54° for frontal finishing and to 50° for back-side finishing. After completion, tilt the sample stage back to 0°. (4) Cut off the bottom of the sample and the wafer, and extract the target sample using a nanomanipulator. Weld it onto a copper mesh. (5) Use a low beam current of 2 kV and 40 pA to finish-machine, thin the sample, and remove the amorphous substances caused by the ion beam. (6) Extract a thin slice of 8 × 5 × 1 μm3 from the cross-section of the damaged sample. Finally, thin this slice to 50–70 nm. Characterize, test, and analyze the surface damage and sub-damage using a transmission electron microscope.

Mass variations before and after polishing were quantified with an analytical balance (Readability: 0.01 mg; Shanghai Huachao Electric Appliance Co., Ltd.) to calculate MRR. The surface roughness measurement adopts the five-point sampling method, measuring the roughness at five positions in the central area and four corner areas of each sample, and taking the average value. Surface roughness and three-dimensional morphology were characterized using a white light interferometer (SuperView W1, CHOTEST TECHNOLOGY INC., China). All experimental protocols were rigorously standardized to ensure measurement reproducibility. The MRR (nm/min) was calculated using the formula in Eq. Experimental results and analysis

Electrochemical analysis

To analyze the effects of malic acid and EDTA-2Na in the formulation on the Tafel curves, corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (Icorr) of the blades, five groups of solutions were prepared respectively, and the test results are shown in Fig. 3. Figure 3a and b present the Tafel curves, Ecorr and Icorr with malic acid adjusting different pH values. Figure 3c and d show the Tafel curves, Ecorr and Icorr of EDTA-2Na at different concentrations. In Fig. 3a, the fluctuation range of the Tafel curve is within 0.5 V, while in Fig. 3c, the fluctuation range of the Tafel curve is within 0.3 V. From the fluctuation range of the Tafel curves, it can be concluded that pH has a more significant impact on the blade CMP than EDTA-2Na. Figure 3b shows that the corrosion potential Ecorr first increases and then decreases, with an inflection point at pH 4. The corrosion current density Icorr first increases and then decreases, reaching its maximum at pH 4.5, indicating that pH 4.5 is the optimal parameter. Figure 3d shows that the corrosion potential Icorr gradually increases with the increase of the EDTA-2Na concentration, and an inflection point of Ecorr appears at a concentration of 0.9 wt%, indicating that CMP corrosion is inhibited at this concentration25. Evidently, the addition of EDTA-2Na plays a corrosion-inhibiting role in CMP, preventing the blades from being over-corroded.Fig. 3Tafel curves, Ecorr and Icorr of samples after immersion: (a,b) pH solution; (c,d) EDTA-2Na solution.

Effect of concentration of slurry components on roughness and MRR

To analyze the effects of the three main components in the polishing solution, namely the pH value, H2O2 concentration, and EDTA-2Na concentration, on the blade surface roughness and MRR, a single-factor experiment was designed. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 4. Figure 4a and d–h illustrate the influence routes of the pH value on the surface roughness and material removal rate of the blade samples. As the pH value increases, the surface roughness first decreases and then increases, while the material removal rate first increases and then decreases. The surface roughness reaches its minimum value at pH 4.5, and the material removal rate reaches its maximum value at pH 5. Considering both the surface roughness and the material removal rate, pH 4.5 is selected as the optimal parameter for the pH value.Fig. 4The influence of different reagent concentrations on blade surface roughness and MRR: (a,d–h) The influence of pH; (b,i–m) The influence of H2O2; (c,n–r) The influence of EDTA-2Na.

The influence of different reagent concentrations on blade surface roughness and MRR: (a,d–h) The influence of pH; (b,i–m) The influence of H2O2; (c,n–r) The influence of EDTA-2Na.

Under the condition of pH 4.5, the effects of the hydrogen peroxide concentration on the surface roughness and material removal rate are shown in Fig. 4b and i–m. When the hydrogen peroxide concentration is low, although the material removal rate increases to some extent, the surface roughness value also exceeds 3 nm. When the hydrogen peroxide concentration is 1 wt%, the surface roughness is the lowest, reaching 1.378 nm, and the material removal rate is at the second peak value. Further increasing the hydrogen peroxide concentration will lead to the formation of a dense oxide film on the surface26, affecting the improvement of the surface roughness and material removal rate. Based on the above analysis, the optimal mass fraction of hydrogen peroxide is determined to be 1 wt%.

Figure 4c and n–r show the influence routes of EDTA-2Na on the surface roughness and material removal rate of the blade samples. As the EDTA-2Na concentration increases, the surface roughness first decreases and then increases, while the material removal rate first increases and then decreases. The surface roughness reaches its minimum value and the material removal rate reaches its maximum value at 0.9 wt%. Considering both the surface roughness and the material removal rate, 0.9 wt% is selected as the optimal concentration of EDTA-2Na27. Under this parameter, the surface roughness value (Sa) is 0.271 ± 0.04 nm, and the material removal rate MRR is 127.11 nm/min.

The test results of the polished sample are shown in Fig. 5. SEM observation revealed that there were no obvious scratches, attachments or corrosion spots on the surface, presenting a uniform polished morphology28. The EDS surface scanning results further confirmed that the matrix elements on the surface of the polished blade were uniformly distributed, without local element aggregation or abnormal signal peaks, and the elemental distribution patterns were highly consistent with those of the original sample. This indicates that during the CMP, the chemical medium only participated in the gentle removal and planarization of the surface material29, without the adsorption, deposition, or reaction residues of chemical substances. CMP of compressor blades

To verify the polishing effect of the polishing slurry on curved parts, a self-developed immersion CMP equipment was used to polish the compressor blades of a heavy-duty gas turbine, as shown in Fig. 6. The abrasive grains used for blade polishing are spherical zirconia abrasive grains with an average particle size of 2 mm. Through the two-way rotation design of the spindle and the polishing bucket, the blades form a stable contact interface with the polishing slurry during high-speed rotation, enabling the abrasive to be uniformly embedded in the curved gullies. At the same time, by means of the alternating forward and reverse rotation mode of the spindle, the “edge effect” that easily occurs during unidirectional polishing of curved parts is counteracted, ensuring a uniform distribution of the abrasive’s grinding energy across all areas of the curved surface and avoiding local over-removal or insufficient polishing. In addition, the oxidant in the polishing slurry undergoes an oxidation reaction with the metal elements on the blade surface, forming an oxide layer with a hardness lower than that of the substrate, providing a chemical basis for efficient mechanical removal.Fig. 6Overall immersion CMP of compressor blades: (a,b) Immersion CMP Equipment; (c,d) Three installed compressor blades; (e) The blade is immersed in the CMP slurry.

Overall immersion CMP of compressor blades: (a,b) Immersion CMP Equipment; (c,d) Three installed compressor blades; (e) The blade is immersed in the CMP slurry.

FTIR analysis

To investigate the surface chemical reactions involving malic acid and EDTA-2Na on turbine blades, two comparative experiments were designed. The first group, used as a control, consisted of pure malic acid crystals and pure EDTA-2Na crystals. In the second group, malic acid and EDTA-2Na were crystallized after blade samples had been immersed for 12 h. The obtained results are presented in Fig. 8a and b.Fig. 8FTIR spectrum: (a) Malic acid crystals and recrystallization of Malic acid solution from soaked blade samples; (b) EDTA-2Na crystals and recrystallization of EDTA-2Na solution from soaked blade samples.

FTIR spectrum: (a) Malic acid crystals and recrystallization of Malic acid solution from soaked blade samples; (b) EDTA-2Na crystals and recrystallization of EDTA-2Na solution from soaked blade samples.

Malic acid is a dicarboxylic acid characterized by two carboxyl groups (–COOH). In aqueous solution, these carboxyl groups undergo ionization, releasing hydrogen ions (H+) and thereby imparting acidity. These ions, in combination with hydrogen peroxide, participate in the redox reactions of metal atoms. In the FTIR spectrum of malic acid crystals, absorption peaks were observed at 3456 cm−1 and 1730 cm−1, which are attributed to the stretching vibrations of O–H and C=O bonds, respectively30. Following blade sample immersion, the O–H peak shifted to 3441 cm−1, and the C=O peak shifted to 1724 cm−1. These shifts suggest that the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups in malic acid are involved in chelation with metal ions. Consistent with the results of in “XPS analysis” section “XPS Analysis”, it is inferred that H+ ions released from malic acid facilitate the dissolution of the surface oxide layer, thereby releasing metal ions into the slurry. Coordination between the –COOH group and metal ions in solution results in a shift of the C=O stretching vibration band. As illustrated in Fig. 8a, the C=O stretching peak of free malic acid, originally located at 1730 cm−1, was displaced to 1724 cm−1. This shift confirms the coordination interaction between the carboxyl oxygen atom and the metal ion31.

Additionally, EDTA-2Na is capable of chelating with metal ions present in the polishing solution. The formation of such chelated complexes improves the stability of dissolved metal ions, which further facilitates surface material dissolution and accelerates the corrosion behavior of the blades. EDTA-2Na is an organic compound with four carboxylic acid groups and two nitrogen atoms32, having six coordination atoms (four oxygen atoms and two nitrogen atoms), which can form stable chelates with various metal ions. EDTA-2Na crystals exhibit absorption peaks at 3391 cm−1 and 1628 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of the N–H bond and COO−, respectively33. After immersing the blade samples, the -NH- peak shifts to 3462 cm−1, and the COO⁻ peak shifts to 1637 cm−134,35. This indicates that the functional groups in serine that chelate with metal ions are the secondary amino group and the carboxyl group. Combining with the XPS results, it can be seen that EDTA-2Na promotes the dissolution of the blade samples. Original Control Group (“Origin”): The surface samples of the blades without any chemical treatment were used as the benchmark reference for all chemical action analyses. Hydrogen Peroxide Single-factor Group (“H2O2”): The samples were immersed in a 1 wt% H2O2 solution, aiming to explore the oxidation effect of hydrogen peroxide on the blade material alone. Malic Acid Single-factor Group (“pH”): The samples were immersed in a malic acid solution with a pH of 4.5 to evaluate the corrosion behavior of malic acid on the blade material at this pH value. EDTA-2Na Single-factor Group (“EDTA-2Na”): The samples were immersed in a 0.9 wt% EDTA-2Na solution, focusing on analyzing the complexation effect of EDTA-2Na with the metal ions of the blade substrate. Composite Slurry Group (“Slurry”): The optimized CMP slurry formulation (containing 1.0 wt% H2O2, a malic acid system with a pH of 4.5, and 0.9 wt% EDTA-2Na) was adopted. By comparing with the previous four single-factor experimental groups, the synergistic chemical action mechanism among multiple components was revealed.

Given that the blade material used in the experiment mainly consists of iron (Fe) and chromium (Cr) (with the total proportion of trace elements < 1%), this study focused on the changes in the surface chemical states of the two major elements, Fe and Cr, and characterized them using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Figures 9 and 10 show the high-resolution XPS narrow-scan spectra of Fe and Cr elements, respectively43, which can directly reflect the distribution characteristics of the chemical states of the two elements in different chemical environments. Based on the peak-fitting results of the XPS spectra44, Figs. 9 and 10 quantitatively analyzed the relative contents of key surface phases such as metallic Fe, Fe(II), Fe(III), metallic Cr, Cr2O3, and Cr(OH)345, providing a quantitative basis for further analyzing the chemical action paths of each slurry component.

On the untreated blade surface, metallic Fe, Fe(II), and Fe(III) exist, with proportions of 35%, 1%, and 64% respectively. Meanwhile, metallic Cr, Cr2O3, and Cr(OH)3 are also present, with proportions of 17%, 63%, and 20% respectively. As a dibasic weak acid, malic acid gradually dissociates into H+ and hydroxyl groups (-OH) in an aqueous solution. H+ slightly corrodes the surface metals Fe and Cr, promoting the oxidation of some metallic Fe and Cr to Fe2+ and Cr3+. Under the oxidation of hydrogen peroxide, metallic iron is oxidized to ferric iron, while metallic chromium is converted into chromium oxide. Iron is more easily oxidized to ferric iron than chromium. As a typical strong amino carboxylic acid complexing agent, EDTA-2Na can combine with (Fe2+, Fe3+, Cr3+) in the polishing liquid to form stable chelates.

The core of the chemical action (malic acid + H2O2 + EDTA-2Na) lies in “controlled surface softening”: through mild etching and directional oxidation, metallic Fe/Cr is converted into highly soluble/chelated high-valent ions45, preventing excessive surface damage. The core of the mechanical action lies in “precise removal of the softened layer”: the abrasive grinding rate is matched to the chemical softening rate, ensuring thorough elimination of surface defects (such as micro-protrusions and oxide inclusions) without introducing new mechanical scratches46. Through the synergistic interaction of the three chemical components and the abrasive, a balance between chemical and mechanical actions is achieved, resulting in a mirror-like surface finish.

Both the experimental group (CMP-treated) and the control group (original machined surface) were taken from the same part of the blades in the same batch, eliminating interference factors such as material composition inhomogeneity and initial stress differences. As can be seen from Fig. 11a–c, during the milling process of the blade, the mechanical friction between the tool and the blade surface generates local high temperatures and shear stresses, which cause severe plastic deformation of the surface-layer metal47, forming a “polycrystalline disorder layer”. At the interface between the crushed-grain layer and the dislocation-accumulation layer, the stress-concentration effect is significant, introducing defects such as micro-cracks and dislocations. These defects may become the initiation points of fatigue cracks under cyclic loading, thus reducing the fatigue strength and service life of the blade48. For the blade after CMP treatment, its cross-section (Fig. 11d–f) shows that the thickness of the damaged layer is significantly reduced to 28.78 nm. The thickness of the damaged layer after CMP is reduced by more than 95% compared to that of the machined sample (a total of 550–950 nm). These results indicate that CMP can effectively reduce machining—induced material damage.

In addition, surface-scanning elemental analysis was performed on the polished sample to further detect whether there were chemical residues before and after CMP polishing. The test results are shown in Fig. 11g and h. The results of the surface-scanning elemental analysis show that there are no chemical residues related to the polishing liquid on the surface layer of the blade sample after CMP polishing, and the matrix elements are evenly distributed. From the perspective of compositional cleanliness, this further demonstrates the applicability of the CMP process in high-end blade manufacturing.

CMP mechanism for compressor blades

Based on the above comprehensive analysis, the chemical–mechanical synergistic mechanism of compressor blade CMP can be summarized as follows, and its schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 12. Under polishing pressure and relative motion, H2O2 in the CMP slurry penetrates the blade surface, oxidizing Fe, Cr in the substrate and surface low-valence metal oxides via electron transfer in an acidic environment to form a dense high-valence oxide layer (mainly Fe2O3, Cr2O3 and a small amount of hydroxides). Malic acid, a dibasic hydroxycarboxylic acid, dissociates to release H+, maintaining a weakly acidic environment to corrode and soften the oxide layer, forming micro-pores, reducing its stability and facilitating subsequent abrasive micro-cutting while avoiding low polishing efficiency from excessive oxide layer thickness. Dissolved Fe2+, Fe3+, Cr3+ chelate with EDTA-2Na via its carboxyl and amino groups, continuously consuming metal ions to promote oxide dissolution, prevent redeposition and secondary pollution. The water-soluble chelates are carried away by slurry circulation, ensuring a clean polishing environment. Finally, abrasive micro-cutting synergizes with the above chemical modification. Adjusting slurry component concentrations and polishing parameters balances “chemical modification-mechanical removal”, achieving uniform material removal and a smooth surface with Sa 103.0 ± 0.5 nm, meeting compressor blade surface quality requirements.Fig. 12Schematic diagram of CMP mechanism for compressor blades.

Conclusions

In summary, aiming at the machining difficulty of martensitic stainless steel for heavy-duty gas turbine compressor blades and the defects of traditional polishing, a custom CMP slurry and self-developed immersion CMP equipment were proposed. The process was optimized and the polishing mechanism was revealed.
