Universal priors: solving empirical Bayes via Bayesian inference and pretraining
Nick Cannella, Anzo Teh, Yanjun Han, Yury Polyanskiy

Abstract
We theoretically justify the recent empirical finding of ( TJP 25 ) that a transformer pretrained on synthetically generated data achieves strong performance on empirical Bayes (EB) problems. We take an indirect approach to this question: rather than analyzing the model architecture or training dynamics, we ask why a pretrained Bayes estimator, trained under a prespecified training distribution , can adapt to arbitrary test distributions . Focusing on Poisson EB problems, we identify the existence of universal priors such that training under these priors yields a near-optimal regret bound of O ~ ​ ( 1 n ) \widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{n}) uniformly over all test distributions. Our analysis leverages the classical phenomenon of posterior contraction in Bayesian statistics, showing that the pretrained transformer adapts to unknown test distributions precisely through posterior contraction. This perspective also explains the phenomenon of length generalization , in which the test sequence length exceeds the training length, as the model performs Bayesian inference using a generalized posterior.

There exist several ways to solve empirical Bayes problems in the literature. Specializing to the Poisson empirical Bayes model, the earliest example is the Robbins’s estimator based on
f
-modeling (i.e., mimicking the form of the Bayes estimator
\theta_{G_{0}}(X)
). A numerically more stable approach is the
g
-modeling, which learns a prior from data and uses the Bayes estimator under the learned prior. A notable example for learning the prior is the nonparametric MLE (NPMLE), as well as a broader class of minimum distance estimators
(
JPW 25
;
VKWL 21
)
. Finally, a more modern approach is to use empirical risk minimization (ERM), which minimizes a properly constructed loss function evaluated on
X^{n}
over a suitably chosen function class
(
BZ 22
;
JPTW 23
)
.

The recent work
(
TJP 25
)
proposes to solve empirical Bayes problems via a different strategy of using a
pretrained estimator
. Unlike the previous ERM approach which trains a separate model for each sample
X^{n}
, a pretrained model learns a function
\widehat{\theta}^{n}(\cdot)
from a large pool of properly generated training data and enables extremely fast computation at test time (namely, applying
\widehat{\theta}^{n}(\cdot)
directly to
X^{n}
). This strategy is inspired by recent successes of TabPFN
(
HMEH 23
;
HMP+ 25
)
, which similarly applies a single pretrained model across diverse categorical datasets.
This therefore achieves the
cost amortization
objective in a similar spirit with amortized inference (see, for example,
(
ZMSDH 25
)
), where after pretraining with synthetic data, a transformer can perform quick inference on millions of batches of new observations. As shown via experimental results in
(
TJP 25
)
for Poisson-EB, a well-trained transformer indeed achieves a better performance than the state-of-the-art NPMLE-based estimator at only a tiny fraction of inference time.

In this paper, we further investigate the approach of
(
TJP 25
)
and address the question of why such a pretrained estimator can solve empirical Bayes problems. Specifically, we explain why a pretrained Bayes estimator trained under a
prespecified training distribution
can adapt to
all possible
test distributions; we refer to such training distributions as

. One might expect that universal priors must be carefully engineered to achieve this ambitious goal; interestingly, this turns out not to be the case. As a concrete illustration,

1.2
Main result II: Length generalization

As shown empirically in
(
TJP 25
)
, the pretrained transformer exhibits
length generalization
: transformers trained on training data with sequence length
n
achieve lower regret when applied to longer test sequences
{n_{\mathsf{test}}}>n
. Our perspective of posterior contraction framework provides a theoretical explanation for this phenomenon.

We first need to generalize our definition of the Bayes estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{n}:\mathbb{N}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
into a map that can incorporate any input sequence length. We use the following length generalization model for transformers
(
FdHP 25
, Section 2.2)
: a transformer
\mathsf{T}
with input sequence
X^{n}
outputs a sequence
(\mathsf{T}_{1}(X^{n}),\dots,\mathsf{T}_{n}(X^{n}))
of the same length, where the
i
-th output
\mathsf{T}_{i}(X^{n})=f(X_{i},\mu_{n})
is a fixed map
f:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})\to{\mathcal{Y}}
applied to the
i
-th input
X_{i}
and the
empirical distribution

\mu_{n}
of all inputs
X^{n}
. This form is motivated by the structure of the self-attention mechanism; for instance, if
\mathsf{T}(X^{n})=Y^{n}
, then
\mathsf{T}(X^{n},X^{n},\dots,X^{n})=(Y^{n},Y^{n},\dots,Y^{n})
.
This formulation enables the transformer to incorporate input sequences of arbitrary length: for input
X^{{n_{\mathsf{test}}}}
, the
i
-th output is now
\mathsf{T}_{i}(X^{n_{\mathsf{test}}})=f(X_{i},\mu_{n_{\mathsf{test}}})
.

Under the above length generalization model, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5

\Pi

Related work

Empirical Bayes.

Empirical Bayes was introduced alongside compound decision theory
(
Rob 51
;
Rob 56
)
, motivated by the idea that estimating a sequence of parameters can achieve lower risk when each component is allowed to depend on the entire sequence. This phenomenon is classically illustrated by the James–Stein estimator
(
JS+ 61
;
Ste 56
)
.
For the Poisson model, known estimators are based either on the Tweedie’s formula
(
Rob 56
)
,

(
KW 56
;
Lin 83
;

;
JPW 25
;
HNWSW 25
)
, or ERM
(
JPTW 23
)
.
The former two approaches are also dubbed
f
-modeling and
g
-modeling, respectively
(
Efr 19
)
.
Neural approaches have also been explored to approximate the maximal likelihood via generative modeling
(

;
WMB 19
)
; see also
(
GIKL 25
;

)
for the normal means model.
For the regret in the Poisson-EB problem, tight bounds
\Theta(\frac{1}{n}(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})^{2})
and

are established for compactly supported and subexponential priors
(

, with highly non-trivial extensions to unbounded support in
(

Statistical estimators via minimax games.

For statistical estimation problems, data-driven approaches have also been proposed to learn estimators with worst-case frequentist guarantees. By the minimax theorem, the minimax risk equals the Bayes risk under a least favorable prior, leading naturally to formulations as minimax games in which the estimator and the prior act as the min and max players, respectively. Both early work
(
Ber 85
, Chapter 5.3)
and more recent efforts
(
DGPS 22
;
FBO+ 25
)
study this statistical minimax game, but largely restrict attention to discrete priors with small support. For more complex priors,
(
GSP+ 20
)
proposed an oracle-based online learning approach to learn minimax estimators for several statistical problems. In contrast, although the family of PoPs in our Poisson-EB setting is substantially richer (priors over an infinite-dimensional space), we identify explicit universal PoPs that serve as approximate least favorable priors, thereby eliminating the max player and reducing the overall procedure to ERM.

Analysis via posterior contraction

In this section, we present the formal analysis of posterior contraction in the proofs of
Theorem

and

.

2.1
Preliminaries

We first provide some preliminaries on the Poisson mixture model and the hierarchical Bayes model induced by a general training PoP
\Pi
. For
\theta\sim G
and
X|\theta\sim\text{\rm Poi}(\theta)
, the Bayes estimator of
\theta
under the squared loss is the posterior mean, defined as

\displaystyle\theta_{G}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{G}[\theta|X=x]=(x+1)\frac{f_{G}(x+1)}{f_{G}(x)},

(5)

where
f_{G}(x)=\int\text{\rm Poi}(x;\theta)G(\mathrm{d}\theta)
is the marginal pmf of
X
defined in
Equation

2
. Here and throughout, we will use
\mathbb{E}_{G}
to denote the expectation with respect to the prior
G
. The following result, taken from
(
JPW 25
, Lemma 4)
, is a functional inequality that relates the posterior mean difference
\theta_{G}-\theta_{G_{0}}
to the Hellinger distance
H(f_{G},f_{G_{0}})
.
2
2

2

The squared Hellinger distance between
P
and
Q
is defined as
H^{2}(P,Q):=\int(\sqrt{\mathrm{d}P}-\sqrt{\mathrm{d}Q})^{2}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G_{0},G
be two priors supported on
[0,A]
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be any real number. Then for an absolute constant constant
C=C(A)>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim f_{G_{0}}}\left[\left(\theta_{G}(X)-\theta_{G_{0}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]\leq C\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log\log(1/\varepsilon)}H^{2}(f_{G},f_{G_{0}})+\varepsilon\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}.

For the class of Poisson mixtures
{\mathcal{P}}=\{f_{G}:\mathrm{supp}(G)\subseteq[0,A]\}
, we will use a metric entropy upper bound under the Hellinger metric. We invoke the following result, which is an easy consequence of Poisson moment matching in
(
WY 16
, Lemma 3)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
{\mathcal{P}}=\{f_{G}:\mathrm{supp}(G)\subseteq[0,A]\}
, there is an absolute constant
C=C(A)>0
with

\displaystyle\log N(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{P}},H)\leq C\frac{\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{\log\log(1/\varepsilon)}.

Here
N(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{P}},H)
denotes the
\varepsilon
-covering number of
{\mathcal{P}}
under the Hellinger metric.

Finally, we turn to the hierarchical Bayes structure induced by the PoP
\Pi
in the training process. In
Algorithm

1
, each training batch is an i.i.d. copy of
(G,\theta^{n},X^{n})
, whose joint distribution is characterized by the hierarchical structure
G\sim\Pi
,
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}|G\overset{\textrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}G
, and
X_{i}|(\theta^{n},G)\overset{\textrm{ind.}}{\sim}\text{\rm Poi}(\theta_{i})
. Throughout, we will slightly abuse notation and use the same letter
\Pi
to denote this joint distribution, and use
\Pi_{X^{n}},\Pi_{G|X^{n}}
, etc. to denote the marginal or conditional distributions induced by the joint distribution
\Pi
. In particular, the readers should be warned about the difference between the training distribution
X^{n}\sim\Pi_{X^{n}}
and the test distribution
X^{n}\sim f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes n}
.

When there are infinite number of batches (i.e.,
M\to\infty
), the global minimizer of
Equation

ERM
is the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[\theta^{n}|X^{n}]
under the joint distribution
\Pi
above. By simple Bayes computation in this hierarchical Bayes structure, we have the following characterization.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
i\in[n]
, there are two equivalent characterizations of the posterior mean:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[\theta_{i}|X^{n}]=\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\Pi_{G|X^{n}}}[\theta_{G}(X_{i})]=\theta_{G_{i}}(X_{i}).

(6)

Here
G_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\Pi_{G|X_{\backslash i}}}[G]
, and
\theta_{G}
is the posterior mean defined in
Equation

5
.

Both characterizations in
Equation

6
will be used later in different scenarios. The first characterization
\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\Pi_{G|X^{n}}}[\theta_{G}(X_{i})]
is more symmetric, and useful in the context of length generalization. The second characterization
\theta_{G_{i}}(X_{i})
enjoys a technical advantage that
G_{i}
depends only on
X_{\backslash i}
, and is thus independent of
X_{i}
under the test distribution
X^{n}\sim f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes n}
. This independence structure will be technically convenient in the next section.

2.2
Posterior contraction

The key technical step in proving
Theorem

1.3
is the following posterior contraction lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Pi
be
B_{n}
-universal in the sense of
1
. Then for every prior
G_{0}
supported on
[0,A]
, there is an absolute constant
C=C(A)>0
such that

\displaystyle f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes(n-1)}\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\hbox{$\left\{\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}H^{2}(f_{G_{0}},\Pi_{X_{n}|X^{n-1}})\leq\frac{C}{n}\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\frac{\log^{2}n}{\log\log n}+B_{n}\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\hbox{$\left\}\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{9}}.

Here the probability is with respect to
X^{n-1}\sim f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes(n-1)}
, and
\Pi_{X_{n}|X^{n-1}}
is the conditional distribution of
X_{n}
given
X^{n-1}
under the training distribution
\Pi
.

The connection between Lemma
2.4
and the usual notion of posterior contraction can be seen by marginalizing out
\theta^{n}
from the joint distribution
\Pi_{G,\theta^{n},X^{n}}
. Conditioned on a prior
G\sim\Pi
drawn from the PoP, the observations
X^{n}
are i.i.d. drawn from
f_{G}
. Posterior contraction asserts that, given test data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}\sim f_{G_{0}}
from prior
G_{0}
, the posterior distribution
\Pi_{G|X^{n-1}}
“concentrates” around the true prior
G_{0}
with high probability. Usually, this concentration is characterized through the induced pushforward measure from
G
to
X
: namely, the conditional distribution
\Pi_{X_{n}|X^{n-1}}
is statistically close to
f_{G_{0}}
with high probability. This is precisely the statement of Lemma
2.4
.

The proof of Lemma
2.4
mirrors the classical posterior contraction arguments in
(
GGVDV 00
)
, with a few adaptations to obtain a high-probability statement; the proof details are postponed to the appendix. For general models
{\mathcal{P}}
other than Poisson, our proof also gives a high-probability
H^{2}
bound

O(\frac{1}{n}(\log N(\frac{1}{n},\mathcal{P},H)+B_{n}))
.
This means our technique is versatile enough for models other than Poisson (see
Section

3.3
for a Gaussian example).

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
, assuming Lemma
2.4
.

By the regret definition in (
1
) and symmetry, we focus on the last coordinate and write

\displaystyle\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{X^{n}\sim f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes n}}\left[\left(\widehat{\theta}_{n}(X^{n})-\theta_{G_{0}}(X_{n})\right)^{2}\right].

By assumption that the global minimizer of
Equation

ERM
is found by the training procedure with
M\to\infty
, we have
\widehat{\theta}_{n}(X^{n})=\theta_{G_{n}}(X_{n})
,
with
G_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\Pi_{G|X^{n-1}}}[G]
. Next, invoking Lemma
2.1
with
G=G_{n}
and
\varepsilon=n^{-10}
yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G_{0})\leq C\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{X^{n-1}\sim f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes(n-1)}}\left[H^{2}(f_{G_{n}},f_{G_{0}})\right]+\frac{1}{n^{10}}\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$},

where by the definition of
G_{n}
,
f_{G_{n}}(x_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\Pi_{G|X^{n-1}}}[f_{G}(x_{n})]=\Pi_{X_{n}=x_{n}|X^{n-1}}
. Finally, we conclude from the Hellinger upper bound in Lemma
2.4
that (recall that
H^{2}\leq 2
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X^{n-1}\sim f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes(n-1)}}\left[H^{2}(f_{G_{n}},f_{G_{0}})\right]\leq\frac{C}{n}\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\frac{\log^{2}n}{\log\log n}+B_{n}\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}+\frac{2}{n^{9}}.

A combination of the above displays establishes
Theorem

1.3
.
∎

2.3
Posterior contraction for the
\alpha
-posterior

Similar to Lemma
2.4
, we have the following posterior contraction result for
\alpha
-posteriors.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Pi
be universal in the sense of
1
with rate
B_{n}
, and
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then for every prior
G_{0}
supported on
[0,A]
and an absolute constant
C=C(A)>0
,

\displaystyle f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes(n-1)}\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\hbox{$\left\{\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\Pi^{\alpha}_{G|X^{n-1}}}[H^{2}(f_{G_{0}},f_{G})]\leq C\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\frac{\log^{2}n}{n\log\log n}+\frac{B_{n}}{\alpha n}\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\hbox{$\left\}\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{9}},

where
\Pi^{\alpha}
is the
\alpha
-posterior defined in (
4
) with
{n_{\mathsf{test}}}
replaced by
n
.

Compared with Lemma
2.4
, the squared Hellinger rate in posterior contraction increases from
\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{n})
to
\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\alpha n})
. For

Finally, using generalizations of Lemma

and Lemma
2.1
(cf. Lemma

\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{n})
even if

3.1
Finite number of batches

Our analysis in
Theorem

1.3
assumes that
M\to\infty
to make sure the global minimizer of
Equation

ERM
is the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[\theta^{n}|X^{n}]
. In practice, the number of batches
M
used in pretraining is finite and large. To this end,
we extend to the scenario where
M<\infty
and

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}^{n}=\argmin_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{PI}}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|\theta^{n,(m)}-f(X^{n,(m)})\|_{2}^{2},

(7)

where
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{PI}}
denotes the class of all permutation-invariant functions
f(X^{n})
, i.e.
\pi\circ f(X^{n})=f(\pi\circ X^{n})
for all permutations
\pi\in S_{n}
.
Note that all EB estimators (Robbins, NPMLE, ERM-monotone, etc.),
as well as a transformer without positional encoding or masking,
belong to
{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{PI}}
; any estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{n}
can also be symmetrized into a PI estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{n,\mathrm{PI}}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\pi^{-1}\circ\widehat{\theta}^{n}(\pi\circ X^{n})
without increasing the pointwise regret. The performance of this permutation-invariant ERM in the Poisson model is summarized in the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

For universal PoPs with rate
B_{n}
, the estimator in (
7
) with
M\geq\exp(C(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\log\log n}+B_{n}))
and an absolute constant
C=C(A)
satisfies the same regret guarantee in
Theorem

1.3
.

Lemma
3.1
shows that for the simple PoP in
Algorithm

1
to attain the same regret of
Theorem

1.1
with
M=\exp(\Omega(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\log\log n}))
batches.
This is superpolynomial in
n
, but it agrees with the practical intuition that pretraining typically requires a huge amount of training data. We leave it to future study if a better condition on
M
can be obtained by assuming different structures of
\widehat{\theta}^{n}
.

3.2
Subexponential priors

Our analysis can also be generalized to the case where
G
is not compactly supported, but follows a lighter-tailed structure.
Formally, we define the class of subexponential priors as

\mathsf{SubE}(s):=\{G:\forall t\geq 0:\mathbb{P}_{G}[\theta>t]<2e^{-t/s}\}
.
Since subexponential priors are effectively supported on
[0,O(\log n)]
, we modify
Algorithm

1
to pick
k=\lceil c_{0}(s)\log n\rceil
atoms for
G\in\mathsf{SubE}(s)
, chosen uniformly at random on
[0,c_{1}(s)\log n]
.
We have the following results for the subexponential case.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
M\to\infty
, and assume that the training procedure finds the global minimizer of (
ERM
). Then for large enough hyperparameters
c_{0}(s),c_{1}(s)>0
, the pretrained estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{G_{0}\in\mathsf{SubE}(s)}\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G_{0})\leq C(s)\frac{\log^{4}n}{n}.

Compared with the previous regret bound in
Theorem

1.3
, the new rate here follows from a new metric entropy bound of
O(\log^{2}n)
and an additional overhead of
O(\log^{2}n)
in the Hellinger-regret inequality (cf.
(
JPW 25
, Lemma 4)
) for priors effectively supported on
[0,O(\log n)]
.

One may also consider the regime where
G_{0}
is supported on
[0,A]
with
A=A_{n}\gg\log n
. It was shown in
(
SW 22
)
that the optimal regret scales as
\widetilde{\Theta}(\frac{A^{1.5}}{n})
for
A=O(n^{2})
. This regret bound can be recovered by a pretrained transformer with
k=\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{A})
atoms drawn uniformly at random on
[0,A]
, provided that one can establish
-\log\Pi_{X_{n}|X^{n-1}}=O(\mathsf{polylog}(n))
with probability at least
1-(n\sqrt{A})^{-1}
over the randomness of the test data
X^{n}\sim f_{G_{0}}^{\otimes n}
. Indeed, a metric entropy bound of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{A})
was established in
(
SW 22
, Lemma 8)
, and the Hellinger-regret inequality in
(
SW 22
, Theorem 4)
with
\rho=\exp(-O(\mathsf{polylog}(n)))
incurs an additional overhead of
\widetilde{O}(A)
. The desired high-probability upper bound on
-\log\Pi_{X_{n}|X^{n-1}}
precisely corresponds to choosing
\rho=\exp(-O(\mathsf{polylog}(n)))
. However, we find it difficult to rigorously establish such a bound, and therefore leave it as a conjecture.

3.3
Gaussian empirical Bayes problem

In a similar spirit to the Poisson model, we may adapt our algorithm and results to the normal means model, where
\theta^{n}\sim G_{0}^{\otimes n}
with some prior
G_{0}
supported on
[-A,A]
, and
X^{n}|\theta^{n}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{N}}(\theta_{i},1)
.
The regret is defined in the same manner as (

k=\lceil c_{0}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\rceil
for a large enough constant
c_{0}>0
, but now draw
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\sim\text{\rm Unif}([-A,A])
and replace
\text{\rm Poi}(\lambda_{j})
by
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda_{j},1)
. The following theorem summarizes the regret bound of the pretrained estimator in the new algorithm.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
M\to\infty
, and assume that the algorithm finds the global minimizer of (
ERM
) under the normal means model.
Then the pretrained estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{n}
of the above algorithm satisfies

\sup_{G_{0}\in\mathcal{P}([-A,A])}\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G_{0})\leq C(A)\frac{\log^{5}n}{n\log\log n}.

This upper bound matches the NPMLE regret bound in
(
JZ 09
)
, and is near-optimal compared with the lower bound
\Omega(\frac{1}{n}(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})^{2})
in
(
PW 21
)
. The proof is entirely similar to our Poisson-EB case, where we establish posterior contraction based on the covering number of the normal mean model, and obtain the final regret bound via the Hellinger-regret inequality in
(
JZ 09

. We defer the details to the appendix.

Motivated by the recent work

A.1
Proof of Lemma
1.2

We first utilize the continuity of regret as follows.

Lemma A.1
.

For all estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{n}:\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
G\in\mathcal{P}([0,A])
,

\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G)
is continuous in
G
under the metric of weak convergence.

The proof can be found in Appendix
A.1.1
. Next, note that since
[0,A]
with the Euclidean metric is a compact Hausdorff space,
by Prokhorov’s Theorem,
\mathcal{P}([0,A])
is compact under the metric of weak convergence (see, for example,
(
Dud 18
, Chapter 11.5)
).
This means the supremum can always be attained, i.e. for fixed estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{n}
,

\sup_{G\in\mathcal{P}([0,A])}\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G)=\max_{G\in\mathcal{P}([0,A])}\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G).

Next, we note that the LHS has the following equivalent formulation:

\inf_{\widehat{\theta}^{n}}\max_{G\in\mathcal{P}([0,A])}\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G)=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}^{n}}\max_{\Pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}([0,A]))}\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\Pi}[\mathsf{Regret}(\widehat{\theta}^{n};G)]

x\in[-(A+\sqrt{2\log n},A+\sqrt{2\log n}]
, we have

f_{G^{\prime}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim G^{\prime}}[\varphi(x-\theta)]\geq\varphi(2A+\sqrt{2\log n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{(2A+\sqrt{2\log n})^{2}}{2})\geq\frac{1}{n^{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-4A^{2}}\geq\rho

where we used the fact that
G^{\prime}
is supported on
[-A,A]
and
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
.
This means that for
x
in the said range we have

\theta_{G^{\prime}}(x)=\theta_{G^{\prime}}(x;\rho)
.
In addition, the Gaussian tail bound in
(
Was 04
, Theorem 4.7)
implies that

\mathbb{P}_{x\sim f_{G^{\prime}}}[x>A+\sqrt{2\log n}]\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{n\sqrt{2\log n}}
.
Therefore, the extra regret contributed by the tail at
|x|>A+\sqrt{2\log n}
is at most

A^{2}\cdot 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{n\sqrt{2\log n}}\leq\frac{2A^{2}}{n}
, as desired.
