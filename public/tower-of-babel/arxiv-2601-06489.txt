Plastic limit of a viscoplastic Burgers equation – A toy model for sea-ice dynamics
Xin Liu, Marita Thomas, Edriss S. Titi

Abstract
We study the plastic Burgers equation in one space dimension, i.e., the Burgers equation featuring an additional term formally given by the p p -Laplacian with p = 1 p=1 , or rather, by the multivalued subdifferential of the total variation functional. Our study highlights that the interplay of the advection term with the stresses given by the multivalued 1 1 -Laplacian is a crucial feature of this model. Eventhough it is an interesting model in itsef, it can also be regarded as a one-dimensional version of the momentum balance of Hibler’s model for sea-ice dynamics. Therein, the stress tensor is given by a term with similar properties as the 1 1 -Laplacian in order to account for plastic effects of the ice. For our analysis we start out from a viscoplastic Burgers equation, i.e., a suitably regularized version of the plastic Burgers equation with a small regularization parameter ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . For the viscoplastic Burgers equation, we construct a global BV \mathrm{BV} -solution. In the singular limit ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 we deduce the existence of a BV \mathrm{BV} -solution for the plastic Burgers equation. In addition we show that the term arising as the limit of the regularized stresses is indeed related to an element of the subdifferential of the total variation functional. Keywords: Burgers equation; (visco)plastic fluid; singular limit; total variation flow with advection; subdifferential of non-smooth, convex potential; Hibler’s sea-ice model. Introduction

As a feature of climate change the rapidly progressing melting of sea-ice is observed.
This has severe impact on the global (especially northern hemispherical) atmospheric and oceanic currents as well as the local and global ecosystem (Thomas and Dieckmann
[
45
]
), but it does open the opportunity for marine routes connecting the Atlantic and Pacific oceans, which will drastically reduce the marine shipping time. A widely used model, describing the dynamics and thermodynamics of sea-ice, was introduced by Hibler
[
26
]
. This model was further discussed and developed by a large number of scientists in the field. To name a few, we refer to Hunke
[
30
]
, Dansereau et al.
[
16
]
, Schreyer et al.
[
43
]
, and Wilchinsky and Feltham
[

]
; see also Pritchard
[
40

for a summary of models.

As mentioned in
[
34
]
, Hibler’s original sea-ice model poses the challenging and fundamental problem of well-posedness of solutions to the nonlinearly coupled parabolic system, featuring the momentum balance as a subdifferential inclusion due to the non-smoothness of the plastic potential, with a hyperbolic system of conservation laws for the height and the compactness of the sea-ice. In particular, the loss of hyperbolicity and linear ill-posedness was discussed in Gray and Killworth
[
24
]
, and Gray
[
25
]
. A regularization of Hibler’s model that retains its original coupled parabolic-hyperbolic character
was introduced, and its local well-posedness was investigated, in
[
34
]
. Therein the regularization used for the analysis was also motivated by similar choices made for numerical simulations in e.g.
[
36
,
37
,
35
]
. Independently, the model was also studied analytically in
[
10
]
with an additional parabolic regularization of the hyperbolic conservation laws.
Hereafter, in relatively short time a comparably large body of work has been developed by different authors addressing different aspects on regularized versions of Hibler’s model, also relying on the parabolic regularization of the model and the powerful tool of maximal parabolic regularity, cf. e.g.
[
8
,
6
,
9
,
18
]
. As has been observed in e.g.
[
29
]
the advection term is at least one order of magnitude smaller than the acceleration term and therefore it has been omitted in numerical simulations based on the elastic-viscous-plastic (EVP) sea-ice model, which emerged from Hibler’s model in order to overcome numerical difficulties. Global well-posedness of the EVP model has been recently established in
[
7
]
with an inviscid Voigt regularization.
Most recently,
[
17
]
studies the singular limit from viscoplastic to plastic rheology of a gradient flow, without advection as it has been suggested in
[
7
]
, involving the stress tensor proposed by Hibler in
[
26
]
. In two space dimensions the appearance of the symmetrized velocity gradient requires sophisticated convergence analysis in the space of bounded deformations. Yet, neglecting the advection term in the momentum balance of Hibler’s model allows the authors in
[
17
]
to study this problem as an
L^{2}
-gradient flow of a convex, positively
1
-homogeneous functional. Due to its non-smoothness the gradient flow evolution can be formulated in terms of a variational inequality in
L^{2}
. This notion of solution was already introduced and studied for the total variation functional in e.g.
[
22
,
21
]
and e.g.
[
2
,
1
,
4
,
3
]
.

In contrast, in this paper, our goal is to make progress in the investigation of the sea-ice momentum balance with
the plastic rheology of Hibler’s model in the unregularized, plastic version
in presence of the advection term
. This significantly changes the structure of the equation from a gradient flow to a damped Hamiltonian system.
This is mainly due to the fact that the dynamics of sea-ice is described in the Eulerian frame involving the convective time derivative, which in general does not satisfy a gradient flow structure.
To better understand the mathematical nature of this problem, in particular the interplay of the advection term with the non-smooth stress term, we will also make a number of simplifications to Hibler’s original momentum balance, which we explain in the following.
The momentum balance in Hibler’s model in the time interval
[0,T]
and the sea-ice domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
takes the form

m(\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u})-\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{H}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})\,,

(1.1a)

where
\boldsymbol{u}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the unknown ice velocity,
m
is the ice mass, and
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})
comprises external forces, such as forces due to wind and ocean stresses as well as the Coriolis force. The stress tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{H}}
in Hibler’s model from
[
26
,
27
]
reads

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{H}}&:=\frac{2}{\mathrm{e}^{2}}\zeta(\boldsymbol{e},P)\boldsymbol{e}+\big(\zeta(\boldsymbol{e},P)-\eta(\boldsymbol{e},P)\big)\mathrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{e}\,\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}-\frac{P}{2}\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}\,,\;\;\text{with }\\
\zeta(\boldsymbol{e},P)&:=\frac{P}{2\triangle(\boldsymbol{e})}\,,\;\;\;\eta(\boldsymbol{e},P):=\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{2}}\zeta(\boldsymbol{e},P)\,,\;\;\text{ and}\\
\triangle(\boldsymbol{e})&:=\Big((e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2})\big(1+\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{2}}\big)+\frac{4}{\mathrm{e}^{2}}e_{12}^{2}+2e_{11}e_{22}\big(1-\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{2}}\big)\Big)^{1/2}\,.\end{split}

(1.1b)

Herein,
\boldsymbol{e}:=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{\top})
is the symmetrized gradient of the velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
with components
e_{ij},

i,j=1,2
,
P
is the prescribed ice pressure, and the constant
\mathrm{e}>0
denotes the ratio of the principal axes of the ellipse that describes the plastic yield surface in terms of
\triangle(\boldsymbol{e})
. Moreover,
\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is the identity matrix.
It can be readily checked that the stress tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{H}}
is obtained as the derivative
\partial_{\boldsymbol{e}}\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{e})
of potential

\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{e}):=\psi_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{e})-\frac{P}{2}\,\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}:\boldsymbol{e}\quad\text{ with }\;\psi_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{e}):=P\cdot\triangle(\boldsymbol{e})\,.

(1.2)

Note that
\psi_{\mathrm{H}}:\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to[0,\infty)
is convex and positively
1
-homogeneous with
\psi_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{0})=0
. In particular,
\psi_{\mathrm{H}}
is not classically differentiable at
\boldsymbol{e}=0,
so that the relation for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{H}}
rather has to be understood as a subdifferential inclusion for the non-smooth potential
\psi_{\mathrm{H}},
i.e.,

\big(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{H}}+\frac{P}{2}\,\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}\big)\in\partial\psi_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{e})\,.

Furthermore note that choosing the ratio
\mathrm{e}
of the principal axes as
\mathrm{e}=1,
turns the ellipse into a ball as it results with this choice in

\triangle(\boldsymbol{e})=\sqrt{2}\,\big|\boldsymbol{e}\big|\,.

Also note that the characteristic properties of
\psi_{\mathrm{H}}
are retained for the choice
\mathrm{e}=1
, i.e., also for the plastic yield surface being a ball,
the potential
\psi_{\mathrm{H}}
is convex, positively
1
-homogeneous, and non-smooth for
\boldsymbol{e}=0
.

Exactly this very non-smoothness of
\psi_{\mathrm{H}}
in combination with the advection term makes the mathematical analysis of the momentum balance (
1.1a
) challenging. The inherent features are already visible in one space dimension. Hereby, the external loadings and the effect of the pressure do not cause additional obstructions. For our mathematical study of the problem we shall therefore make the following simplifications:
We restrict the analysis to one space dimension,
set the parameters
m=P=1
in (
1.1a
),
\mathrm{e}=1
in (
1.1b
) and also neglect the external loadings
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})
as well as the the linear term
-\frac{P}{2}\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}:\boldsymbol{e}
in
\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{H}}
from (
1.2
). With these simplifications we arrive at the following toy model for Hibler’s sea-ice momentum balance:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}({u^{2}}/{2})-\partial_{x}\sigma

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{ in }D\quad\;\text{with }

(1.3a)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\in\partial\psi(\partial_{x}u)\,,\quad\text{where}

(1.3b)

\displaystyle\psi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty),\quad\psi(e)

\displaystyle:=|e|\,.

(1.3c)

This is the Burgers equation featuring an additional stress term that arises as in (
1.3b
) from the subdifferential of the convex, positively
1
-homogeneous potential
\psi
from (
1.3c
). As explained above, this corresponds to the special case of the yield surface being a ball in Hibler’s model in more than one space dimension.

Classical results in literature, cf. e.g.
[
12
,
15
,
20
]
, are established for the Burgers equation, i.e. with
\psi\equiv 0
in (
1.3c
), on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. In this line we also choose

D:=[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}

(1.3d)

in (
1.3a
). Accordingly, equation (
1.3a
) is complemented by the initial condition

u(0,x)=u_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(1.3e)

Since
\psi
from (
1.3c
) is convex, relation (
1.3b
) has to be understood in the sense of subdifferentials of convex potentials, i.e., for all
e\in\mathbb{R}
there holds

\sigma\in\partial\psi(e)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\forall\,\tilde{e}\in\mathbb{R}:\;\sigma\cdot(\tilde{e}-e)\leq\psi(\tilde{e})-\psi(e)\,,

(1.4)

and
\partial\psi
is set-valued. For the potential
\psi
from (
1.3c
) we have in particular

\begin{split}\partial\psi(e)=\mathrm{Sign}(e)&:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\big\{\frac{e}{|e|}\big\}&\text{if }e\not=0\,,\\[4.30554pt]
{[-1,1]}&\text{if }e=0\,,\end{array}\right.\\[6.45831pt]
&\,=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\{-1\}&\text{if }e<0\,,\\[2.15277pt]
{[-1,1]}&\text{if }e=0\,,\\[2.15277pt]
\{1\}&\text{if }e>0\,.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(1.5)

Although the plastic Burgers equation (
1.3a
) has the potential to apply also for other physical scenarios, we here give an interpretation in the context
of sea-ice. The advection term in (
1.3a
) is responsible for the flow of sea-ice.
The stress tensor obtained from the non-smooth potential describes the non-Newtonian, plastic rheology of sea-ice. Possible shock formation from the nonlinear advection can be seen as the ridging of sea-ice, where the ice flow compresses locally to form a jump. The (formation of) plateaus, where
\partial_{x}u=0,
can be seen as (the formation of) a large block of ice mass, moving at the same speed. The interaction of shocks and plateaus then corresponds to the collision of ice ridges and large ice blocks.

1.1
Burgers equation, total variation flow, and very singular diffusion

Our approach to the study of Cauchy problem (
1.3
) is heavily inspired by classical studies of the Burgers equation, which was proposed in
[
12
]
as a model for turbulence and is itself an important equation in the study of hyperbolic conservation laws. We refer e.g. to Bressan
[
11
]
, Dafermos
[
14
]
, and Lax
[
33
]
for monographs on hyperbolic PDEs. In particular, the notion of
\mathrm{BV}
-solutions, which serves as a weak solution concept for first order hyperbolic conservation laws, will also be applied to (
1.3
). Regularizing the Burgers equation by the
2
-Laplacian stemming from the quadratic viscous potential
\psi_{2}(e):=\frac{1}{2}|e|^{2}
leads to the viscous Burgers equation. Due to its parabolicity it has smooth solutions and its vanishing-viscosity limit leads to Oleinik’s entropy condition
[
28
,
39
]
, which together with the Rankine-Hugoniot condition ensures the uniqueness of
\mathrm{BV}
-solutions for the inviscid Burgers equation.
Kurganov and Rosenau investigated in
[
32
]

the equation

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}(u^{2}/2)-\partial_{x}\biggl(\dfrac{\partial_{x}u}{\sqrt{|\partial_{x}u|^{2}+1}}\biggr)=0\,.

(1.6)

This is a regularized version of (
1.3a
), where
\psi
from (
1.3c
) is replaced by

\tilde{\psi}(e):=\sqrt{|\partial_{x}u|^{2}+1}\,.

It is observed in
[
32
]
that large gradients will lead to jumps. That is, the parabolicity of (
1.6
) is too weak to prevent shock formation for given near-shock data. Unique smooth solutions with small data are also constructed in the same paper. See also
[
31
,
23
,
5
,
42
]
, for related studies.

However, these studies do not apply to (
1.3
), where the stress term degenerates to an element of the multi-valued subdifferential if
\partial_{x}u=0
.
Such a degeneracy also appears in the study of the total variation flow; note that
\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
arising from (
1.3c
) results by relaxation in the total variation of a function
u\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R})
. The total variation flow is formally given by the evolution equation

\partial_{t}u=\mathrm{div}\,\biggl(\dfrac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\biggr)\,,

(1.7)

and also called the very singular diffusion equation in
[
21
,
22
]
. It can be formulated as an
L^{2}
-gradient flow for the total variation functional. The non-smoothness of the total variation functional at
\mathrm{D}_{x}u=0
thus leads to a formulation in terms of a variational inequality, in view of the definition (
1.4
), naturally replacing the multivalued subdifferential of the total variation functional, cf.
[
21
,
22
]
as well as e.g.
[
2
,
1
,
4
,
3
]
.
Yet, again, for the plastic Burgers equation (
1.3
), this gradient flow structure is not available due to the presence of the advection term, which rather results in a damped Hamiltonian system.

2.1.2
Further, improved estimates

Next, we deduce further uniform estimates by testing (
2.1a
) by suitably tailored test functions. For this, for instance, one would like to test (
2.1a
) for
\varepsilon>0
fixed with the characteristic function of
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-measurable sets
A^{\varepsilon}\subset(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, such as
A^{\varepsilon}:=\{\alpha^{\varepsilon}>\beta\}
with some
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-measurable functions
\alpha^{\varepsilon}:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and a constant
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
\alpha^{\varepsilon}
here serves as a placeholder for the smooth, approximate solutions
u^{\varepsilon}
or its derivatives
\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon},

\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}
.
However, the characteristic function of such a set
A^{\varepsilon}
is not an admissible test function for (
2.1a
). Instead, we will use a suitable regularization of it, which can be constructed in terms of a regularization of the Heaviside function by means of Lemma
2.3
. We point out that this result can be found in
[
15
, Thm. 6.3.2]
.
However, for the readers’ convience we give the statement and proof of the result here below in the way we use it to conclude the uniform estimates in Proposition
2.4
lateron.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
be a nonnegative, even function such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(y)=0\;\text{ for }|y|\geq 1\,.

(2.11)

For any
\sigma\in(0,1)
define the following function

H^{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1],\;H^{\sigma}(y):=\int_{-\infty}^{y}\frac{1}{\sigma}\rho\big(\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sigma}\big)\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\,.

(2.12)

Then,
(H^{\sigma})_{\sigma>0}
approximates the Heaviside function pointwise for all
y\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
; more precisely, as
\sigma\to 0,
there holds

H^{\sigma}(y)\to\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0&\text{ if }y<0,\\
1/2&\text{ if }y=0,\\
1&\text{ if }y>0.\end{array}\right.

(2.13)

Let
\alpha\in L^{p}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})

such that also for all
t\in[0,\infty)
it is
\alpha(t,\cdot)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for some
p\in[1,\infty)
. Further, let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
be a constant and set

\chi_{\{\alpha>\beta\}}^{\sigma}:=H^{\sigma}(\alpha-\beta)\,.

(2.14)

Then, as
\sigma\to 0
, there holds for all
t\in[0,\infty)

(\alpha(t)-\beta)\chi_{\{\alpha>\beta\}}^{\sigma}(t)\to(\alpha(t)-\beta)^{+}\;\text{ pointwise a.e.\ in }\mathbb{R}\,,

(2.15)

where
(\cdot)^{+}:=\max\{\cdot,0\},

as well as

\displaystyle(\alpha(t)-\beta)\chi_{\{\alpha>\beta\}}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle(\alpha(t)-\beta)^{+}\;\text{ in }L^{p}(\mathbb{R})\,,

(2.16a)

\displaystyle(\alpha-\beta)\chi_{\{\alpha>\beta\}}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle(\alpha-\beta)^{+}\;\quad\text{ in }L^{p}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})\,.

(2.16b)

Moreover, there holds

\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sigma}\rho\big(\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sigma}\big)\to 0\quad\text{ pointwise a.e.\ in }(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\quad\text{ as }\sigma\to 0\,.

(2.17)

Furthermore, let
f\in L^{1}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
. Then, there is a constant
c>0
such that

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\rho\big(\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\big)f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq c\int_{\{|\alpha-\beta|<\sigma\}\backslash\{|\alpha-\beta|=0\}}\hskip-21.52771pt|f|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0\quad\text{ as }\sigma\to 0\,.

(2.18)

Proof.

Proof of (
2.13
):
With
z:=y^{\prime}/\sigma,
the integral is transformed as follows

H^{\sigma}(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{y}\frac{1}{\sigma}\rho\big(\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sigma}\big)\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{y/\sigma}\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

(2.19)

Accordingly, for
y<0,
we find that
H^{\sigma}(y)\to\int_{-\infty}^{-\infty}\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=0
and for
y>0
we see that
H^{\sigma}(y)\to\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=1
by (
2.11
).
Instead, since
\rho
is nonnegative, even, and satisfies (
2.11
), we conclude that
H^{\sigma}(0)=1/2
for all
\sigma>0,
which yields (
2.13
).

Proof of (
2.15
):
In view of (
2.13
) we now deduce that

\begin{split}&(\alpha(t)-\beta)\chi_{\{\alpha>\beta\}}^{\sigma}(t)\\
&=(\alpha(t)-\beta)H^{\sigma}(\alpha(t)-\beta)\to\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0&\text{ if }(\alpha(t)-\beta)<0,\\
0&\text{ if }(\alpha(t)-\beta)=0,\\
(\alpha(t)-\beta)&\text{ if }(\alpha-\beta)>0\end{array}\right\}=(\alpha(t)-\beta)^{+}\,,\end{split}

which proves (
2.15
).

Proof of (
2.16
):
For brevity we set
y:=(\alpha-\beta)
and conclude by the dominated convergence theorem that

\begin{split}&\|y\chi_{\{y>0\}}^{\sigma}-(y)^{+}\|_{L^{p}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})}^{p}\\
&=\int_{\{y<0\}}\Big|y\int_{-\infty}^{y/\sigma}\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\Big|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\{y>0\}}\Big|y\Big(1-\int_{-\infty}^{y/\sigma}\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\Big)\Big|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad\longrightarrow 0\,,\end{split}

where we used that each of the integrands tends to
0
pointwise a.e. on its domain of integration by (
2.15
) and that the function
y
serves as a
p
-integrable majorant for both of them.

Proof of (
2.17
):
Again with
y:=\alpha-\beta
we note that

\frac{y}{\sigma}\rho\big(\frac{y}{\sigma}\big)\in\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\{0\}&\text{ if }|y|=0,\\
(0,c)&\text{ if }|y|<\sigma,\\
\{0\},&\text{ if }|y|\geq\sigma\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.20)

with some constant
c>0
. In fact, the existence of the bound
c>0
for
|y|<\sigma

is due to
|y|/\sigma<1
and
\rho\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.

Since, for any
t\in[0,\infty)
the function
y(t,\cdot)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
by assumption, we have that for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
y(t,x)\not=0
there also holds
|y(t,x)|<\infty
. Hence, there is some
\sigma_{x}^{t}>0
such that
\frac{|y(t,x)|}{\sigma_{x}^{t}}\geq 1
and, accordingly

\rho\big(\frac{|y(t,x)|}{\sigma_{x}^{t}}\big)=0
. Thus,

\frac{|y(t,x)|}{\sigma}\rho\big(\frac{y(t,x)}{\sigma}\big)=0\quad\text{ also for any }\sigma\leq\sigma_{x}^{t}\,,

(2.21)

which proves pointwise convergence for a.e.
x
with
|y(t,x)|>0
. Using that

\frac{y(t,x)}{\sigma}\rho\big(\frac{y(t,x)}{\sigma}\big)=0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
y(t,x)=0

completes the proof of convergence property (
2.17
).

Proof of (
2.18
):
The existence of the constant
c>0
has been
justified in (
2.20
). Also by (
2.20
) we further deduce the estimate

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\rho(y)f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq c\int_{\{|y|<\sigma\}\backslash\{|y|=0\}}\hskip-21.52771pt|f|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

(2.22)

Moreover, for any
\sigma_{2}<\sigma_{1}
it is
\{|y|<\sigma_{2}\}\subset\{|y|<\sigma_{1}\}
and hence

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\{|y|<\sigma\}=\bigcap_{\sigma>0}\{|y|<\sigma\}=\{|y|=0\}\,.

(2.23)

Using (
2.23
) in (
2.22
) concludes the proof of (
2.18
) for
f\in L^{1}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
.
∎

&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2\phi}{t}\,\mathrm{d}x+4\|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\|\phi\|_{\infty}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2\phi}{t}\,\mathrm{d}x+4C\,.\end{split}

(2.82)

Here we used the mean value theorem of differentiability with suitable intermediate values
\tilde{h}_{\pm}\in(0,h)
in the second estimate, and the uniform bound (
2.27
) to arrive at the last estimate. Making use of the strong
L^{2}
-convergence
(
2.63d
) for a.a.
t>0,

we can pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
2.82
) and thus obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(t,x+h)-u(t,x-h)}{h}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x<\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{2\phi}{t}\,\mathrm{d}x+4C\quad\text{ for all }\phi\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\text{ with }\eqref{limit-Oleinik21}\,,\end{split}

(2.83)

at first, for a.e.
t\in(0,\infty)
. Arguing again that
u\in\mathrm{C}([0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
like above for (
2.80a
), we find that (
2.83
) holds true for all
t>0
.
By the regularity property (
2.62b
) for
u
we ultimately conclude (
2.80b
) from (
2.83
).
∎

In the following we want to characterize the limit

To obtain this relation we first extend in Section

3.1
Weak formulation in
V^{*}:=\big(L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}))\big)^{*}

Recall that estimates (

2.26

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}+\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{1})}+\|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C\,,

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{1})}+\|\partial_{x}(u^{\varepsilon})^{2}/2\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{1})}+\|\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{1})}

By (

For this, let

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(u_{\varepsilon})^{2}/2

\displaystyle\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\beta\quad\text{ in }V^{*}\,,

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}R_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\gamma\quad\text{ in }V^{*}\,

(3.4c)

along a (not-relabeled) subsequence.

Theorem 3.1
.

(3.6)

so that (

2.65

for all

. In addition to regularity properties (

(3.7)

Moreover, for

in (

\displaystyle\langle\alpha,u\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|u(T)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|u_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\,,

(3.8a)

\displaystyle\langle\beta,u\rangle_{V}

(3.8b)

Proof.

Proof of (
3.5

The uniform bound (
3.3
) and convergence statements (

equation (

) by elements
v\in V
.
Passing to the limit

Proof of the identification (
3.6

The first of (

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon},\phi\rangle_{V}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\to\langle\alpha,\phi\rangle_{V}\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

for all

) and (

For the second result in (

\langle\beta,\phi\rangle_{V}=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

Now, for the third statement in (

Proof of regularity property (

It follows from the bounds (

Proof of the identification (
3.8
):

To find (
3.8a
) we note that
W=(L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}))\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\subset(L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}))^{*}=W^{*}
forms a Gelfand triple
with

continuously and densely.
Then
W^{1,p,p^{\prime}}(0,T;W,W^{*}):=\{v\in L^{p}(0,T;W),\,\partial_{t}v\in L^{p^{\prime}}(0,T;W^{*})\}\subset\mathrm{C}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
and for all

t_{1}\leq t_{2}\in[0,T]
the following integration-by-parts formula holds true, cf.
[

\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(t_{2})w(t_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(t_{1})w(t_{1})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\big(\langle\mathrm{D}_{t}v,w\rangle_{W}+\langle\mathrm{D}_{t}w,v\rangle_{W}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

For

In order to verify (

\hat{u}_{r}:=u\chi_{[0,T]\times B_{r}}
with

\chi_{[0,T]\times B_{r}}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V)

the characteristic function of the set
[0,T]\times B_{r}
. For every

\begin{split}\langle\beta,\hat{u}_{r}\rangle_{V}=\langle\mathrm{D}_{x}(u^{2}/2),\hat{u}_{r}\rangle_{V}&=\langle\mathrm{D}_{x}(\hat{u}_{r}^{3}/3),\chi_{[0,T]\times B_{r}}\rangle_{V}\\
&=\langle-\hat{u}_{r}^{3}/3,0\rangle_{V}+\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,r)-\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,-r)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t\,.\end{split}

(3.10)

Accordingly, (

) holds true, if

\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,r)-\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,-r)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0\quad\text{as }r\to\infty

(3.11)

in (

Since

\langle\beta,u\rangle_{V}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\langle\beta,\hat{u}_{r}\rangle_{V}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,r)-\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,-r)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\exists\,c>0\;\;\forall r>0:\;\;\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,r)\Big|+\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,-r)\Big|\,\mathrm{d}t>c

leads to a contradiction. Indeed, by regularity result (

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,r)\Big|+\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,-r)\Big|\,\mathrm{d}t&\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,r)\Big|+\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,-r)\Big|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}r\\

&\leq\frac{T}{3}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}))}^{2}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))}\end{split}

(3.13)

and therefore, putting (

\displaystyle\infty>\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,x)\Big|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\geq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,r)\Big|+\Big|\frac{\hat{u}_{r}^{3}}{3}(t,-r)\Big|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}r

in

In the following we want to characterize the limit

), we introduce

\begin{split}\Psi&:X\to[0,\infty]\,,\\
\Psi(u)&:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\int_{0}^{T}|\mathrm{D}_{x}u|(\mathbb{R})\,\mathrm{d}t&\text{ if }u\in Y,\\

(3.14)

(3.15)

The identification of

Proposition 3.2

Let
\Phi:X\to[0,\infty]

\eta\in\partial\Phi(v)\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{lrl}\text{i)}&\eta&\!\!\in\partial\Phi(0),\\[2.15277pt]
\text{ii)}&\langle\eta,v\rangle_{X}&\!\!=\Phi(v).\end{array}\right.

(3.16)

In Theorem
3.3
we will show that the limit pair
(u,R)
satisfies property
(
3.16
), i) for the functional
\Psi

from (
3.14
) and in Theorem
3.4
that also property
(
3.16
), ii) holds true. More precisely, with these two theorems we obtain the existence of an element

\zeta\in X^{*}
with the properties

\zeta\in\partial\Psi(u)\quad\text{ and }\quad\zeta=-\mathrm{D}_{x}R\;\text{ in the sense of distributions.}

Recall that
|R|\leq 1
a.e. in

by (

). Therefore the obtained characterization of

is in line with results in literature that give a characterization of the subdifferential of the total variation functional,

[

[

Theorem 3.3

(Characterization of the limit

Set

(u,R)
be the limit pair obtained from the sequence of approximate solutions

2.6

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon}\in\partial\Psi(0)\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0\,,

(3.17a)

and there is a subsequence and a limit

\zeta\in X^{*}

(3.17b)

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}

(3.17e)

Proof of (

\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{R})

\kappa>0

\varepsilon>0

\big|\langle-\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon},v\rangle_{X}-\langle-\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon},v_{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}\big|\leq\|-\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon}\|_{X}\|v-v_{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\|_{X}\leq C(\varepsilon,T)\|v-v_{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\|_{X}\leq\kappa\,,

by estimate (
4.4i

\big|\langle-\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon},v\rangle_{X}-\langle-\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon},v_{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}\big|\leq\varepsilon\|-\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{X}\|v-v_{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\|_{X}\leq C(\varepsilon,T)\|v-v_{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\|_{X}\leq\kappa\,,

by estimate (
4.4e

Thanks to (

\kappa>0
and all
\varepsilon>0

\begin{split}\langle-\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon},v\rangle_{X}&\leq\langle-\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon},v_{\kappa}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{X}+\kappa=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}R_{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}v^{\varepsilon}_{\kappa}\,\mathrm{d}t+\kappa\\

2.7b

\varepsilon>0
and
\kappa>0,
and for every

\kappa>0

\langle-\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon},v\rangle_{X}\leq\Psi(v)\quad\text{ for all }v\in X\,.

(3.21)

\Psi(v)=\infty
for any

3.17a

However, note that estimates (
3.18b
) and (

) do not allow us to conclude a

is obtained from

will be very large, so that its

(3.23)

This proves (

Proof of (
3.17d

For every
\varepsilon>0
and for all

4.4e
) we therefore find

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}R^{\varepsilon,r}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{4}}\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon,r}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int\int_{B_{r}}\frac{|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon,r}|^{2}}{(|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon,r}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3}}\,|\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon,r}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{4}}\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon,r}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\varepsilon,T)\,,\end{split}

which proves (
4.4i
) and concludes the proof of the uniform a priori bounds (
4.4
).
∎

To Step 3: Limit passage
r\to\infty
:

Proposition 4.3

(Well-posedness of a global
H^{2}
solution)
.

Let the assumptions of Prop.
4.2
be satisfied. Keep
\varepsilon>0
and
T>0
fixed. Assume that the sequence of initial data

(u^{\varepsilon,r}_{\mathrm{in}})_{r}
is well-prepared, such that
u^{\varepsilon,r}_{\mathrm{in}}\to u^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}
in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with

u^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{|x|\to\infty}u^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=0\,.

(4.17)

Then there exists a (not relabelled) subsequence
r\to\infty
and a limit function
u^{\varepsilon}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
r^{\prime}>0

fixed, the subsequence
(u^{\varepsilon,r})_{r}
converges to
u^{\varepsilon}
in the weak topology imposed by the uniform bounds
(
4.4
)
locally on
B_{r^{\prime}}
as
r\to\infty
. The limit function
u^{\varepsilon}
has the following regularity

\begin{split}u^{\varepsilon}&\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\quad\text{ with }\\
\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}&\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\,,\end{split}

(4.18)

and it is a weak solution of Cauchy problem (
2.1
). In particular,
u^{\varepsilon}
satisfies the pointwise far-field boundary condition
(
2.1b
), i.e.,

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}u^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=0\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T]\,.

(4.19)

Proof.

The statements of the proposition directly follow from the a priori bounds (
4.4
), which are uniform in
r
. In particular, for
r_{1}>0
fixed one finds a subsequence of solutions which converges in the topology imposed by (
4.4
) on

B_{r_{1}}
to a limit
u^{\varepsilon}_{r_{1}}
. For any
r_{2}>r_{1}
one finds a further subsequence of solutions which converge in the topology imposed by (
4.4
) on

B_{r_{2}}
to a limit
u^{\varepsilon}_{r_{2}}
and by the uniqueness of the limit one concludes that
u^{\varepsilon}_{r_{2}}\big|_{B_{r_{1}}}=u^{\varepsilon}_{r_{1}}
.
In this way, when selecting a sequence
r_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty,
one finds a subsequence that converges in the topology imposed by (
4.4
) on each

B_{r_{n}}
to a limit
u^{\varepsilon}_{r_{n}}
such that
u^{\varepsilon}_{r_{n}}\big|_{B_{r_{m}}}=u^{\varepsilon}_{r_{m}}
for every
r_{m}<r_{n}
. This results in the limit function
u^{\varepsilon}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},
that inherits the regularity properties (
4.18
) from the uniform bounds (
4.4
).

It remains to verify that also the pointwise far-field boundary condition (
4.19
) holds true.
For this we observe that the regularity (
4.18
) implies that
u^{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{C}(0,T;\mathrm{C}(\overline{\mathbb{R}})),
i.e., in particular,
for all
t>0
the function
u^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
is uniformly continuous in
\mathbb{R}
. Let now
t>0
be fixed and assume that there is a subsequence
(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with
x_{j}\to\infty
and such that
u^{\varepsilon}(t,x_{j})\to c\not=0
as
j\to\infty
.
Thus, for any
\kappa>0
sufficiently small such that
|c-\kappa/2|>\kappa/2,
there is an index
j_{\kappa}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
j\geq j_{\kappa}
there holds
|u^{\varepsilon}(t,x_{j})-c|<\kappa/4
.
By the uniform continuity of
u^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
there is
\delta_{\kappa}>0
such that for all
j\geq j_{\kappa}
and for all

x\in B_{\delta_{\kappa}}(x_{j})
there holds
|u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-u^{\varepsilon}(t,x_{j})|<\kappa/4
. Therefore we have

|u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\geq|c-\kappa/2|>\kappa/2\quad\text{ for all $x\in B_{\delta_{\kappa}}(x_{j})$ and for all $j\geq j_{\kappa}$}\,.

In view of regularity (
4.18
) this results the following contradiction:

\infty>\|u^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}>\sum_{j=j_{\kappa}}^{\infty}\int_{B_{\delta_{\kappa}}(x_{j})}|\kappa/2|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{j=j_{\kappa}}^{\infty}\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathcal{L}^{1}(B_{\delta_{\kappa}}(x_{j}))=\infty\,.

This shows that (
4.19
) must be satisfied.
∎

To Step 4: Verification of the integral far-field relations for the limit solutions:

Proposition 4.4

(Integral far-field relations)
.

Let the assumptions of Prop.
4.3
be satisfied. Keep
\varepsilon>0
and
T>0
fixed. Then the weak solution
u^{\varepsilon}
of Cauchy problem (
2.1
) also satisfies the integral far-field relations (
2.4
), i.e., for all
t\in[0,T]
there holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{3}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)^{3}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{as }r\to\infty\,,

(4.20a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)\varepsilon\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{as }r\to\infty\,,

(4.20b)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)\frac{\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)}{\sqrt{|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{as }r\to\infty\,.

(4.20c)

Proof.

We first show that

\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0\quad\text{as }r\to\infty\,.

(4.21)

For this, we argue by contradiction and assume that there is a subsequence
r_{j}\to\infty
such that

\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(r_{j},t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\to c>0\quad\text{as }j\to\infty\,.

(4.22)

Hence, there is an index
j_{c}>0
such that for all
j\geq j_{c}
we have
\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(r_{j},t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\geq c/4
.
The regularity properties (
4.18
) imply that the map
x\mapsto\|u^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,x)\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}
is uniformly continuous.
Thus, there is
\delta_{c}>0
such that for all
j\geq j_{c}
and for all
x\in B_{\delta_{c}}(r_{j})
we have
\|u^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,x)\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}>c/2
.
Again by the regularity properties (
4.18
) we therefore arrive at the following contradiction

\begin{split}\infty>\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))}^{2}&\geq\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|u^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\geq\sum_{j=j_{c}}^{\infty}\int_{B_{\delta_{c}}(r_{j})}\int_{0}^{\infty}|u^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\sum_{j=j_{c}}^{\infty}\frac{c}{2}\mathcal{L}^{1}(B_{\delta_{c}}(r_{j}))=\infty\,.\end{split}

(4.23)

Hence, (
4.21
) holds true.

In order to deduce (
4.20a
) we use that (
4.18
) implies that
\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C
and therefore we find with the aid of Hölder’s inequality

\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{3}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)^{3}\,\mathrm{d}t\Big|\leq\int_{0}^{T}\tfrac{1}{3}|u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{3}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\tfrac{1}{3}\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0

(4.24)

as
r\to\infty
thanks to (
4.21
).
Similarly we also conclude (
4.20b
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)\varepsilon\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)\varepsilon\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\varepsilon\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{1/2}\to 0\,,

as well as (
4.20c
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)\frac{\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)}{\sqrt{|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}t\Big|\leq\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|\frac{|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|}{\sqrt{|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|\frac{|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\varepsilon\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|u^{\varepsilon}(\pm r,t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{1/2}\to 0\,,

again by regularity properties (
4.18
) and (
4.21
).
∎
