Upregulation of S100A8/A9 Potentiates Radiation-Induced Intestinal Injury via MAPK and NF-κB Signaling Pathways
Authors: Yu Sun, Cong-Cong Han, Shui Quan, Chuan-Ying Hong, Fei Ge, Jia-Liang Liu, Sheng-Long Li, Xin Guan, Jia-Guo Zhou, Bao-Dong Tang, Rui-Ping Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRadiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) is a serious complication of radiation therapy without effective therapies. The alarmin, S100A8/A9, a biomarker of gastrointestinal inflammation, is highly elevated following abdominal irradiation (ABI). However, it remains unclear whether it functions as an active driver of RIII pathogenesis. The study aims to assess the effects of S100A8/A9 on the pathogenesis of RIII. Our goal is to identify its dynamic alteration during RIII and determine whether it can be used as a therapeutic target in the injury and repair phases of acute RIII.Methods and MaterialsC57BL/6J mice were subjected to ABI, and S100A8/A9 expression was analyzed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and immunofluorescence at multiple time points (days 1, 4, and 8) during RIII. The small-molecule inhibitor, paquinimod, was used to block S100A9 in vivo, and recombinant proteins S100A8/A9 were used in rat intestinal epithelial cell line 6 cells and organoids for in vitro validation. Epithelial regeneration was tracked through lineage tracing assays using Lgr5-eGFP-IRES-CreERT2; R26-CAG-LSL-tdTomato mice.ResultsS100A8/A9 expression in the mouse intestine was obviously upregulated post-ABI in a time-dependent manner, which started to increase from day 1 and reached the peak on day 4. The increased S100A8/A9 was primarily released from neutrophils infiltrated into the intestinal crypts. Inhibition of S100A8/A9 with paquinimod substantially alleviated irradiation-induced epithelial apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, although enhancing crypt regeneration and restoration of intestinal barrier integrity. Mechanistically, S100A8/A9 exacerbated irradiation-induced intestinal injury via binding to toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) receptors and subsequently activating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways.ConclusionsThis study identifies S100A8/A9 as a critical pathogenic contributor to acute RIII and provides strong evidence supporting S100A8/A9 as a potential therapeutic target for RIII.

Introduction

Radiation-induced intestinal injury (RIII) is a serious adverse effect when patients receive abdominal or pelvic radiation therapy for cervical, bladder, or colorectal cancers.1 Up to 90% of patients receiving high doses of abdominal irradiation (ABI) develop acute symptoms such as diarrhea and mucosal bleeding despite advances in radiation therapy. Chronic RIII, which normally appears months to years after irradiation, can result in fibrosis, stenosis, or fistula in the intestine.2 No effective therapies have been approved for gastrointestinal (GI) acute radiation syndrome, and the treatments currently used in clinics mainly provide symptomatic relief but fail to prevent progression to chronic complications, highlighting the necessity of mechanism-driven interventions.

The pathogenesis of acute RIII is a dynamic process involving acute injury, repair, and chronic remodeling. Intestinal epithelium, particularly crypt-based intestinal stem cells (ISCs), is highly radiosensitive because of its rapid proliferative turnover.3 Ionizing radiation induces DNA damage and impairs epithelial repair and regeneration through apoptosis by activating the p53/p53 up-regulated modulator of apoptosis (PUMA) pathway; this occurs simultaneously with oxidative stress, leading to greater death of crypt cells and atrophy of villi.4 RIII also features immune cell infiltration involving neutrophils and macrophages into the lamina propria within hours postirradiation.5 The interplay between the immune system and epithelial cells is essential for determining the outcome of RIII.

S100A8/A9, historically referred to as calprotectin in clinical fecal assays, is a heterodimer of S100A8 and S100A9 that is mainly produced by myeloid cells and can serve as a widely used biomarker of GI inflammation.6 Elevated levels of S100A8/A9 correlate with disease severity in inflammatory bowel disease and acute GI graft-versus-host disease,7,8 and its inhibition shows therapeutic potential in colitis models.9,10 This evidence indicates that S100A8/A9 is not merely an alarming factor but also an essential driver of inflammatory-associated diseases. Whether this inflammatory role of S100A8/A9 also extends to RIII remains an important question. Although fecal S100A8/A9 has not yet been established as a specific diagnostic biomarker for RIII, clinical observations in patients receiving pelvic radiation therapy have linked fecal calprotectin with radiation therapy–associated intestinal damage. Fecal calprotectin, either alone or together with other inflammatory markers such as lactoferrin, has been reported to increase during pelvic radiation therapy and to be associated with acute radiation–induced proctitis, intestinal toxicity, and GI mucosal injury.11, 12, 13 These findings suggest that S100A8/A9-associated inflammatory responses are clinically relevant to radiation therapy–induced intestinal damage. However, existing clinical evidence mainly supports an association between S100A8/A9 expression and mucosal injury, rather than establishing its causal role in RIII. Whether S100A8/A9 is merely a bystander released during radiation-induced inflammation or an active driver of epithelial damage and impaired regeneration remains a critical unanswered question.

This study aims to investigate the dynamic changes of S100A8/A9 expression during RIII, particularly focusing on its functional role in the acute injury and repair phases. The goal is to clarify whether S100A8/A9 is a potential therapeutic target that would enable the design of more effective, mechanism-based treatments for RIII.

Methods and Materials

An expanded Methods section is provided in Appendix E1, including the sequences of qRT-PCR primers and siRNAs (Table E1) and details of the antibodies used in this study (Table E2).

Animals and treatment

Male wild-type C57BL/6J mice were purchased from BesTest Company; Lgr5-eGFP-IRES-CreERT2 mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Company; R26-CAG-LSL-tdTomato mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. All the animal experiments were performed according to the policies of the Sun Yat-Sen University Committee for Animal Research and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institute of Health in China.

To inhibit S100A8 and S100A9 in vivo, mice were intraperitoneally injected with paquinimod (Paq) (HY-100442; MCE) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide and diluted in sterile corn oil at a dose of 10 mg/kg/d at a specific time point before and after radiation continuously. This dose was selected based on previous studies using Paq in murine inflammatory disease models14, 15, 16, 17 and our preliminary tolerability observation in nonirradiated mice, in which repeated administration of Paq at 10 mg/kg/d did not cause obvious changes in the general condition, body weight, or intestinal morphology. The vehicle group received an equivalent volume of sterile corn oil containing 1% dimethyl sulfoxide.

For ABI, mice were anesthetized and exposed to a 3 cm area between the xiphoid process and the pubic symphysis while the remaining parts were covered with a lead plate. X-rays (Rad Source RS2000pro) were used for irradiation at a dose rate of 1.08 Gy/min. Different irradiation doses were selected according to the specific experimental endpoints. An 8 Gy ABI dose was used for time-course and proteomic analyses because it induced reproducible but sublethal intestinal injury, allowing evaluation of S100A8/A9 dynamics on days 1, 4, and 8 postirradiation, which correspond to the acute injury, regenerative, and recovery phases of RIII, respectively. A 10 Gy ABI dose was used for histologic, mechanistic, and regenerative analyses because it produced more sustained epithelial damage than 8 Gy while still permitting evaluation of intestinal repair and providing an appropriate window to evaluate the protective and proregenerative effects of Paq. A 12 Gy ABI dose was used for survival analysis to establish a severe lethal ABI model.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the means ± standard error of the mean. GraphPad Prism (10.6.1) was used for all statistical analyses and graph plotting. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was applied for two-group comparisons, and one-way ANOVA test was applied for multigroup comparisons. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method and the log-rank test. P values < 0.05 were considered to be a significant difference.

Results

Neutrophils release S100A8/A9 into intestinal crypts after ABI

To identify the potential contributors to RIII, we first performed proteomic analysis of jejunal tissue on day 4 post-ABI. Proteomic profiling data showed that 84 proteins were upregulated and 111 proteins were downregulated (2-fold change and P value < 0.05). Among the upregulated proteins, inflammation- and stress-associated molecules, including S100A8, S100A9, ORM2, MT2, and SPRR1A, were prominently increased, suggesting activation of innate immune and epithelial stress-response programs during the regenerative phase of RIII. In contrast, several antimicrobial and epithelial functional proteins, such as Defa39 and Defa42, were decreased, indicating impaired crypt barrier function. Notably, S100A8 and S100A9 were among the most significantly upregulated proteins, prompting us to further investigate their role in RIII (Fig. 1A). Given that RIII undergoes dynamic phases—acute injury, repair, and homeostasis—we then examined the expression profile of S100A8/A9 during the dynamic phases. After exposure to 8 Gy ABI (Fig. E1A), weight loss continued for the first 4 days, followed by a gradual increase (Fig. E1B). Morphologic analysis showed that from day 1 to day 4 after irradiation, villus length and crypt depth did not change significantly initially, but villus length exhibited a pronounced decrease while crypt depth increased by day 4, then recovered to near-normal levels by day 8 (Fig. E1C-E). In line with the time-course of pathologic alterations, the mRNA and protein levels of S100A8/A9 also displayed time-dependent increases after irradiation, which reached the peak on day 4 and then decreased to near the normal level on day 8 post-ABI (Fig. 1B-D). In addition, irradiation-induced S100A8/A9 expression also displayed a dose-dependent fashion (Fig. 1E). Then, we examined whether Paq treatment could ameliorate radiation-induced intestinal damage. Histopathology showed that there was no significant difference in intestinal structure between the control group and the Paq groups under normal conditions (Fig. 2D). On day 1 after irradiation, both groups showed the same level of nuclear condensation and cell swelling at jejunal crypt bases. The body weight, small intestine length, villus height, and crypt depth also showed no significant differences on day 1 between the 2 groups (Fig. 2C-G). By day 4 postirradiation, the control group exhibited severe crypt-villus structure destruction, with shortened villus height and increased crypt depth, and a notable reduction of body weight. Paq treatment obviously ameliorated all these pathologic alterations (Fig. 2C-G).

All these data suggest that upregulation of S100A8/A9 is not only a biomarker of radiation-induced intestinal damage, but also a critical contributor to RIII.

Inhibition of S100A8/A9 attenuates radiation-induced intestinal apoptosis and oxidative stress

RIII is characterized by a cascade of pathologic events, including epithelial apoptosis, crypt stem cell depletion, oxidative stress, and dysregulated inflammation, all of which contribute to the breakdown of mucosal barrier function and subsequent systemic complications. Hence, we next explored the pathologic processes in which S100A8/A9 is involved in the intestine following irradiation. On day 1 postirradiation, during the intestinal acute injury phase, ionizing radiation directly damages the DNA of radiation-sensitive cells such as Lgr5+ stem cells and induces oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species generation, promoting apoptosis.1 We observed that Paq administration significantly reduced terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) positive cells in intestinal crypts (Fig. 3A, B). Western blotting analysis showed that Paq treatment remarkably decreased the levels of DNA damage marker, phosphorylated histone H2AX (γ-H2AX), and proteins associated with acute apoptotic stress signaling, including p53, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3, although increasing the expression of the antiapoptotic protein, Bcl-2 (Fig. 3C, D). Inhibition of S100A8/A9 promotes the regeneration of intestinal crypts after irradiation

By day 4 after irradiation, cells within the intestinal crypt proliferate and differentiate to produce new crypts. To further elucidate whether S100A8/A9 is involved in the regenerative process after RIII, lineage tracing assays were first conducted using Lgr5-eGFP-IRES-CreERT2; Rosa26-CAG-tdTomato heterozygotes. The results showed that the Paq group exhibited a greater amount of regenerated intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), as evidenced by the increase in the red fluorescence area, indicating that blocking S100A8/A9 induces a stronger regenerative response of Lgr5+ ISCs and produces more tdTomato⁺ offspring (Fig. 4A-C). Consistent with lineage tracing data, the immunofluorescent analysis revealed that Ki67⁺/PCNA⁺ cells within the crypts were significantly increased in the Paq group compared to the controls (Fig. 4D-F). Western blotting further demonstrated that cyclin E1, as well as the markers of stem cells and progenitor cells, including Lgr5, Olfm4, and Sox9, were all increased after Paq treatment (Fig. 4G, H). To further verify the effect of S100A8/A9 on intestinal crypt regeneration following irradiation, we isolated mouse intestinal crypts for organoid culture. Following 6 days of in vitro culture, mature organoids were switched to medium containing recombinant S100A8/A9 proteins and subjected to 4 Gy irradiation. The recombinant S100A8/A9 exposure was limited to 48 hours to mimic a transient acute inflammatory burst after irradiation; thereafter, the medium was replaced with normal organoid culture medium to evaluate the delayed consequences of early S100A8/A9 exposure on regenerative growth. The results indicated that from days 2 to 4 postirradiation, the effect of S100A8/A9 on the growth of the organoids was minimal. However, by day 8, the size and budding rate of organoids in the S100A8/A9 treatment group were significantly decreased (Fig. 4 I, J), confirming that early S100A8/A9 exposure suppresses the regenerative capacity of intestinal crypt–derived organoids after irradiation.

S100A8/A9 inhibition restores epithelial barrier integrity and attenuates inflammation postirradiation

Radiation-induced alterations in the epithelial damage and repair processes would result in a defective intestinal barrier.2 The results here revealed elevated expression of tight junction proteins, ZO-1 and occludin, in jejunal tissues of Paq-treated mice compared to controls on day 4 postirradiation (Fig. 5A, B). In addition to the epithelial barrier, the intestine also has a mucus barrier secreted by Paneth cells and goblet cells.18 Alcian blue staining showed an increase in Paneth/goblet cells in the Paq group (Fig. 5C-E). The expression of Paneth cell marker protein lysozyme also showed a consistent increase (Fig. 5F, G). Under normal physiological conditions, the intestinal epithelial barrier prevents the translocation of luminal materials. However, radiation disrupts intestinal tight junctions and increases permeability, facilitating the translocation of these substances. This microbiota-driven inflammatory response differs from the sterile inflammation observed in radiation-induced damage in other organs, as it further activates immune cells and triggers the release of large amounts of proinflammatory factors.19 Additionally, radiation can directly damage intestinal vascular endothelial cells, promoting microthrombosis and local ischemia-hypoxia, thereby indirectly exacerbating inflammation and tissue injury.20 We observed that Paq treatment decreased irradiation-induced F4/80⁺ macrophage infiltration within the lamina propria (Fig. 5H) and the expression of proinflammatory genes such as Tnf-α, Il-1β, and Il-6 (Fig. 5I-K). Western blotting further showed that Paq could decrease intestinal tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) protein levels and increase anti-inflammatory interleukin-10 expression (Fig. 5L, M). S100A8/A9 intensifies apoptosis and inflammation via NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways

To understand the molecular mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects of Paq on irradiation-induced intestinal damage, we performed proteomic analysis on intestinal tissues from irradiated mice treated with Paq or solvent. The results identified 232 differentially expressed proteins (P < 0.05; |FC| ≥ 1.1) between the 2 groups. Among these, 121 proteins were upregulated, including key molecules such as the anti-inflammatory protein intestinal alkaline phosphatase, the proapoptotic protein Bcl-2-like protein 2, the mitochondrial calcium uniporter dominant–negative subunit beta, the antioxidant metallothioneins MT1/MT2, and the NF-κB inhibitor IκBα. Conversely, 111 proteins were downregulated (Fig. E2A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of several signaling pathways, including MAPK and NF-κB, alongside processes relevant to radiation injury such as the cell cycle, p53 signaling, oxidative phosphorylation, and reactive oxygen species pathways (Fig. E2B).

To answer whether MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways are involved in the deleterious effects of S100A8/A9, in vitro experiments in IEC-6 cells were performed. Using a 10 Gy radiation dose could cause substantial cell injury (Fig. E3A, B). Pretreatment with S100A8/A9 recombinant protein did not affect cell viability in nonirradiated cells but significantly decreased cell viability and amplified the apoptotic rate after radiation (Fig. 6A-C). S100A8/A9 amplified irradiation-induced increases of acute apoptotic stress–associated proteins, such as p53 and cleaved caspase-3, as well as the proinflammatory protein, TNF-α, while further suppressing irradiation-induced reduction of antiapoptotic protein, Bcl-2 (Fig. 6D-G). Radiation exposure resulted in increased levels of phospho-p65 (NF-κB), which further elevated following pretreatment with S100A8/A9. Similarly, S100A8/A9 enhanced the radiation-induced phosphorylation of key MAPK kinases, including p38, extracellular regulated protein kinases (ERK), and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), when compared to the irradiation-only controls (Fig. 6H, I). S100A8/A9 exacerbates radiation-induced apoptosis and inflammation in IECs by activating TLR4/RAGE-dependent NF-κB and MAPK signaling

The S100A8/A9 heterodimer, a well-characterized damage-associated molecular pattern, plays a critical role in various diseases by engaging cell surface receptors such as TLR4 and the RAGE.21,22 To determine whether S100A8/A9 exacerbates RIII via these two canonical receptors, we examined its effects on NF-κB and MAPK signaling in irradiated IEC-6 cells. Using siRNA-mediated knockdown of TLR4 (Fig. E4A, B) and RAGE (Fig. E4C, D), we found that silencing either receptor partially suppressed S100A8/A9-induced activation of the NF-κB (p65) and MAPK (p38, ERK, and JNK) pathways (Fig. 6J, K). Moreover, the silencing of either receptor effectively inhibited the levels of the proinflammatory cytokine, TNF-α, and acute apoptotic stress–associated proteins, p53 and cleaved caspase-3, while increasing the expression of antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 (Fig. 6L, M). Together, these results indicate that S100A8/A9 aggravates inflammatory and apoptotic responses in irradiated IECs by upregulating NF-κB and MAPK signaling through TLR4 and RAGE.

Discussion

This study identified the S100A8/A9 complex, released predominantly by infiltrating neutrophils, as a critical pathogenic contributor in the acute phase of RIII. Inhibition of S100A8/A9 with Paq reduced epithelial cell apoptosis and oxidative stress in the acute injury phase (day 1 postirradiation). In addition, Paq treatment also exerted beneficial effects on the regenerating repair process (day 4 postirradiation) as evidenced by the improvements in the epithelial and mucus barriers, functional regeneration, and suppression of the inflammatory response in a murine model. Moreover, our results delineated the mechanistic pathways by which S100A8/A9 engages both TLR4 and RAGE on IECs to activate NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades, thereby amplifying epithelial apoptosis and inflammatory response (Fig. E5).

Currently, the clinical assessment of RIII primarily relies on symptom scoring combined with endoscopic/imaging examinations because of the lack of firmly established serum or fecal diagnostic biomarkers.1 Recent accumulating data suggest that S100A8/A9 may be a valuable biomarker for RIII, whose serum levels are increased in patients receiving radiation therapy and which positively correlates with the severity of mucosal damage after exposure to irradiation.11, 12, 13 In accordance with these reports, our data here also showed that S100A8/A9 expression in the murine intestine is significantly elevated after irradiation, further confirming its potential role in the diagnosis of RIII. Of note, S100A8/A9 is not specific for RIII because its elevation was also observed in several autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease,23 as well as in cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and aortic aneurysm.24

Besides serving as a potential biomarker of inflammatory diseases, S100A8/A9 was also proposed to be a critical causative factor for various types of diseases. S100A8/A9 is primarily emitted from activated neutrophils and monocytes. Under various pathologic conditions, a substantial increase in S100A8/A9 would drive the recruitment of more leukocytes into the inflammatory site, which would amplify the production of proinflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress, thus promoting the development of diseases.25, 26, 27 In contrast, under certain experimental conditions, such as endotoxemia, S100A8/A9 exhibits anti-inflammatory and protective effects. Infusion of exogenous S100A8 or S100A9 enhances animal survival by limiting inflammation and oxidative damage. These inconsistent findings raise the question of whether S100A8/A9 is a suitable therapeutic target for inflammation-associated diseases. Notably, a recent study on lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–treated mice showed that LPS-induced S100A8/A9 elevation occurred in a time-dependent fashion, which started to increase from 6 hours and reached a peak at 48 hours after LPS administration. Blockade of S100A8/A9 at the early stage (0 and 6 hours) mitigated systemic inflammation and sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction.28 Moreover, the work on the myocardial infarction animal model also showed that S100A8/A9 had an opposing effect at the early and later stages during the disease. Seven days after the acute infarct, inhibition of S100A9 reduced inflammation. However, continuous 21-day blockade of S100A9 is deleterious to cardiac repair.29 These results indicated that S100A8/A9 may exert different functions during different stages of the disease.

RIII is a highly dynamic process that includes the apoptotic phase (1-2 days postirradiation), regenerating phase (3-4 days postirradiation), and resolution phase (after 5 days postirradiation).30 Here, we observed that irradiation induced an increase of S100A8/A9 in a time-dependent manner, which started to increase from 1 day, reached a peak on day 4, and declined to near the normal level on day 8 after irradiation. Blocking S100A8/A9 with Paq before irradiation markedly reduced epithelial apoptosis and oxidative stress on day 1, indicating that elevation of S100A8/A9 aggravates irradiation-induced intestinal damage. Day 4 is the crucial switch point of a successful recovery, where the proliferative activation of surviving crypt cells, including Lgr5⁺ stem cells and progenitor cells, occurs. Blockage of S100A8/A9 from 0 to 3 days before and postirradiation dramatically ameliorated crypt regeneration and intestinal integrity, demonstrating that excessive S100A8/A9 impairs the epithelial regenerative program. This conclusion was further confirmed in the organoid culture, which showed that S100A8/A9 aggravated irradiation-induced inhibition of organoid growth. These observations suggested that elevation of S100A8/A9 has detrimental effects on RIII and that inhibition of S100A8/A9 at the early stage could ameliorate irradiation-induced intestinal damage.

These findings do not exclude the possibility that S100A8/A9 may exert protective or proreparative functions under other biological contexts or at later stages of tissue repair. Previous studies have shown that S100A8/A9 or its individual subunits can facilitate cutaneous wound healing,31,32 promote macrophage-dependent renal repair after ischemia/reperfusion injury,33 and enhance reparative responses in certain cardiovascular injury models.29,34 Notably, in myocardial infarction, S100A9 appears to play stage-dependent roles: short-term inhibition during the inflammatory phase is beneficial, whereas prolonged blockade may impair reparative macrophage responses and adversely affect cardiac recovery.29 These observations suggest that S100A8/A9 is not a uniformly detrimental inflammatory mediator, but rather a context- and stage-dependent regulator of inflammation and tissue repair.

In this regard, S100A8/A9 may act as a double-edged sword during inflammation-associated tissue injury. A transient and well-controlled increase of S100A8/A9 may help initiate danger sensing, host defense, inflammatory cell recruitment, clearance of damaged tissue, and subsequent repair. However, excessive or sustained accumulation of extracellular S100A8/A9 may amplify inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress, thereby aggravating tissue injury and disrupting regeneration. In RIII, epithelial barrier disruption, crypt stem/progenitor cell vulnerability, oxidative stress, and neutrophil infiltration during the early phase may shift S100A8/A9 from a physiological danger signal to a maladaptive inflammatory amplifier. Therefore, our findings support the concept that the pathogenic effect of S100A8/A9 predominates during the early phase of RIII.

This stage-dependent interpretation is also relevant to the role of p53 in intestinal radiation injury. In the present study, Paq reduced p53 expression together with Bax, cleaved caspase-3, γ-H2AX, and oxidative stress markers on day 1 after irradiation, when acute epithelial apoptosis and DNA damage-associated responses were prominent. This finding should be interpreted as attenuation of acute p53-associated apoptotic stress signaling after S100A8/A9 blockade, rather than evidence that p53 activation is uniformly detrimental in RIII. Indeed, p53 may exert distinct functions depending on the timing, injury severity, cellular context, and downstream effector pathways. On one hand, p53-dependent activation of the PUMA/Bax/caspase axis can contribute to acute epithelial apoptosis after irradiation35,36; on the other hand, p53 may also support epithelial protection and regeneration through apoptosis-independent mechanisms,4 including modulation of immune responses37 and activation of fetal-like/revival stem cell–associated regenerative programs.38 Given that Paq also promoted Lgr5⁺ stem cell expansion and organoid regeneration, future studies using p53 knockdown, pharmacologic inhibition, or epithelial-specific p53 deletion models will be needed to determine whether the proregenerative effects of S100A8/A9 blockade are partially mediated through p53-dependent regenerative pathways.

Importantly, Paq was administered within this early injury window rather than throughout the entire repair course, which may limit pathologic S100A8/A9 overactivation while preserving its potential physiological or reparative functions during the later resolution phase. Further studies are warranted to determine whether S100A8/A9 also participates in late epithelial remodeling, immune resolution, or p53-dependent regenerative regulation after irradiation.

It is well-established that S100A8/A9 functions through activation of cell membrane receptors including TLR4 and RAGE.21,22 On binding to TLR4 and RAGE, S100A8/A9 would activate the MAPK pathway and increase the phosphorylation of p38, ERK1/2, and JNK as well as NF-κB and NOD-like receptor protein 3 signaling pathways. Our findings here showed that S100A8/A9 protein exacerbates irradiation-induced inflammatory response and apoptosis in epithelial cells via interactions with both TLR4 and RAGE. Although NF-κB is generally thought to promote prosurvival and inflammatory gene expression, such as TNF-α and IL-6, it may also be involved in proapoptotic signaling under sustained or specific stress conditions, such as irradiation.39 Concurrently, the balance between the MAPK subfamilies is disrupted, and proinflammatory and proapoptotic signals mediated by p38 and JNK are potentiated, potentially overriding the compensatory survival signals typically transduced by ERK.40 The linking of both pathways is very important, as crosstalk between both NF-κB and MAPK pathways determines the final cellular fate, which will shift from adaptation and survival to inflammatory cell death in response to severe stress. Consequently, our findings indicate that S100A8/A9 may function as an indispensable amplifier of RIII through the TLR4/RAGE axis, disrupting homeostatic balance and promoting NF-kB and MAPK-mediated inflammatory and apoptotic programs. However, our receptor-specific siRNA experiments would be further strengthened by pharmacologic or antibody-based validation. Future studies using TAK-242 to inhibit TLR4 signaling, FPS-ZM1 or neutralizing antibodies to block RAGE, and combined TLR4/RAGE inhibition in epithelial cells, organoids, and animal models will be important to confirm whether simultaneous blockade of these receptors provides additive protection against S100A8/A9-induced epithelial apoptosis, oxidative stress, and impaired regeneration. These approaches will further define the therapeutic relevance of the S100A8/A9–TLR4/RAGE axis in RIII.

Consequently, our findings indicate that S100A8/A9 may function as an important amplifier of RIII through the TLR4/RAGE axis, disrupting epithelial homeostasis and promoting NF-κB–associated and MAPK-associated inflammatory and apoptotic programs. Given the vulnerability of oncology patients, future translational studies should carefully evaluate whether short-term S100A8/A9 blockade can reduce pathologic intestinal inflammation without impairing host defense, infection control, epithelial repair, or antitumor efficacy.
