Subspaces with or without a common complement
Esteban Andruchow, Eduardo Chiumiento

Abstract
Let ℋ {\cal H} be a separable complex Hilbert space. Denote by G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) Gr({\cal H}) the Grassmann manifold of ℋ {\cal H} . We study the following sets of pairs of elements in G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) Gr({\cal H}) : 𝚫 = { ( 𝒮 , 𝒯 ) ∈ G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) × G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) : ∃ 𝒵 ∈ G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) ​ such that ​ 𝒮 ​ + ˙ ​ 𝒵 = 𝒯 ​ + ˙ ​ 𝒵 = ℋ } , {\bf\Delta}=\{({\cal S},{\cal T})\in Gr({\cal H})\times Gr({\cal H}):\exists{\cal Z}\in Gr({\cal H})\hbox{ such that }{\cal S}\dot{+}{\cal Z}={\cal T}\dot{+}{\cal Z}={\cal H}\}, which are pairs of subspaces that have a common complement, and 𝚪 = G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) × G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) ∖ 𝚫 , {\bf\Gamma}=Gr({\cal H})\times Gr({\cal H})\setminus{\bf\Delta}, which are pairs of subspaces that do not admit a common complement. We identify 𝒮 ∼ P 𝒮 {\cal S}\sim P_{\cal S} , the subspace 𝒮 {\cal S} with the orthogonal projection P 𝒮 P_{\cal S} onto 𝒮 {\cal S} . Thus we may regard 𝚫 {\bf\Delta} and 𝚪 {\bf\Gamma} as subsets of ℬ ⁡ ( ℋ ) × ℬ ⁡ ( ℋ ) {\cal B}({\cal H})\times{\cal B}({\cal H}) (here ℬ ⁡ ( ℋ ) {\cal B}({\cal H}) denotes the algebra of bounded linear operators in ℋ {\cal H} ). We show that 𝚫 {\bf\Delta} is open, and its connected components are parametrized by the dimension and codimension of the subspaces. The connected component of 𝚫 {\bf\Delta} having both infinite dimensional and co-dimensional subspaces is dense in the corresponding component of G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) × G ​ r ​ ( ℋ ) Gr({\cal H})\times Gr({\cal H}) . On the other hand, 𝚪 {\bf\Gamma} is a (closed) C ∞ C^{\infty} submanifold of ℬ ⁡ ( ℋ ) × ℬ ⁡ ( ℋ ) {\cal B}({\cal H})\times{\cal B}({\cal H}) , and we characterize the connected components of 𝚪 {\bf\Gamma} in terms of dimensions and semi-Fredholm indices. We study the role played by the geodesic structure of the Grassmann geometry of ℋ {\cal H} in the geometry of both 𝚫 {\bf\Delta} and 𝚪 {\bf\Gamma} . Several examples of pairs in 𝚫 {\bf\Delta} and the connected components of 𝚪 {\bf\Gamma} are given in Hilbert spaces of functions.

These notes reflect on the paper

Denote by

Basic facts

Let
{\cal H}
be a separable complex Hilbert space. We denote by
{\cal B}({\cal H})
the algebra of bounded operators acting on
{\cal H}
. For
T\in{\cal B}({\cal H})
, let
R(T)
and
N(T)
be the range and nullspace of
T
, respectively.
The symbol
\dot{+}
stands for direct sum of subspaces of
{\cal H}
; while we reserve the symbol
\oplus
for orthogonal sums of subspaces. We write
P_{\cal S}\in{\cal B}({\cal H})
for the orthogonal projection with range
{\cal S}
. Given a decomposition
{\cal H}={\cal S}\dot{+}{\cal Z}
, we will denote by
P_{{\cal S}\parallel{\cal Z}}\in{\cal B}({\cal H})
the idempotent with range
{\cal T}
and nullspace
{\cal Z}
.
Let us state below several preliminary facts.

Subspaces with a common complement.
Two closed subspaces
{\cal S},{\cal T}\subset{\cal H}
have a
common complement
if there exists a closed subspace
{\cal Z}\subset{\cal H}
such that
{\cal S}\dot{+}{\cal Z}={\cal T}\dot{+}{\cal Z}={\cal H}.

Remark 2.1
.

Let us state the following results from
[
22
]
by M. Lauzon and S. Treil.

1.
The subspaces
{\cal S},{\cal T}
have a common complement if and only if there exist a bounded (not necessarily orthogonal) projection
E
onto one of the subspaces (say
{\cal T}
), such that

E\big|_{\cal S}:{\cal S}\to{\cal T}

is an isomorphism (
[
22
, Prop. 1.3]
). This result is valid even in the context of Banach spaces.

2.
Denote by
G=P_{\cal T}\big|_{\cal S}
, regarded as an operator
G:{\cal S}\to{\cal T}
(so that
G^{*}:{\cal T}\to{\cal S}
is
P_{\cal S}\big|_{\cal T}
). Denote by
{\cal E}
the projection valued spectral measure of
G^{*}G
.
Note that

N(G)={\cal S}\cap{\cal T}^{\perp}\ \hbox{ and }\ N(G^{*})={\cal S}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}.

Then
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
have a common complement if and only if

\dim N(G)+\dim{\cal E}(0,1-\epsilon){\cal S}=\dim N(G^{*})+\dim{\cal E}(0,1-\epsilon){\cal S}

(1)

for some
\epsilon>0
(equivalently, for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
). This characterization also holds for non separable Hilbert spaces (
[
22
, Thm. 0.1]
).

3.
As a straightforward consequence of the previous statement, Lauzon and Treil oberved that the subspaces
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
do not have a common complement in a separable Hilbert space
{\cal H}
if and only if
\dim{\cal S}\cap{\cal T}^{\perp}\neq\dim{\cal S}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}
and the operator
(1-G^{*}G)\big|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
is compact
(
[
22
, Rem. 0.5]
).

Remark 2.2
.

Later on J. Giol proved the following equivalences (see
[
20
, Prop. 6.2]
):

i)

{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
are subspaces with a common complement.

ii)

There exists
P\in{\cal P}({\cal H})
such that
\|P_{\cal S}-P\|<1
and
\|P-P_{\cal T}\|<1
.

iii)

There exist four idempotents
P_{1},P_{1}^{\prime},P_{2},P_{2}^{\prime}
such that
{\cal S}=R(P_{1})
,
R(P_{1}^{\prime})=R(P_{2}^{\prime})
,
R(P_{2})={\cal T}
and
{\cal S}^{\perp}=N(P_{1}^{\prime})
,
N(P_{1})=N(P_{2})
,
N(P_{2}^{\prime})={\cal T}^{\perp}
.

The Grassman manifold.
The Grassmann manifold
Gr({\cal H})
of
{\cal H}
is defined as the set of all the closed subspaces of
{\cal H}
. We identify the Grassmann manifold with the manifold of all orthogonal projections in
{\cal H}
given by

{\cal P}({\cal H})=\{P\in{\cal B}({\cal H}):P=P^{2}=P^{*}\}.

We observe that this identification can be made more precise: there is a diffeomorpshim between the presentation as subspaces and orthogonal projections (see
[
1
]
).
{\cal P}({\cal H})
is a complemented submanifold of
{\cal B}({\cal H})
with tangent space
(T{\cal P}({\cal H}))_{P}
at
P\in{\cal P}({\cal H})
given by

(T{\cal P}({\cal H}))_{P}=\{XP-PX:X=-X^{*}\in{\cal B}({\cal H})\}.

Notice that tangent vectors are co-digonal selfadjoint operators. For each projection
P
, one can define the idempotent
{\cal E}_{P}:{\cal B}({\cal H})\to(T{\cal P})_{P}
,
{\cal E}_{P}(X)=PX(1-P)+(1-P)XP
. The family
\{{\cal E}_{P}\}_{P\in{\cal P}({\cal H})}
induces a linear connection in
{\cal P}({\cal H})
: for
X(t)
a tangent vector field along a curve
\gamma(t)\in{\cal P}({\cal H})
, set

\frac{DX}{dt}={\cal E}_{\gamma}(\dot{X}).

Let
Gl({\cal H})
be the group of all the invertible operators, and
{\cal U}({\cal H})
the subgroup consisting of unitary operators. Recall that
{\cal U}({\cal H})
acts on
{\cal P}({\cal H})
by
U\cdot P_{{\cal S}}=P_{U({\cal S})}=UP_{\cal S}U^{*}
,
U\in{\cal U}({\cal H})
.

The geodesics of this connection can be computed (see
[
28
]
,
[
13
]
): they are induced by one parameter unitary groups, namely, the geodesics starting a
{\cal S}
are of the form:

\delta(t)=e^{itZ}P_{\cal S}e^{-itZ},

where
Z^{*}=Z
is co-diagonal with respect to
{\cal S}
, meaning that
Z({\cal S})\subset{\cal S}^{\perp}
and
Z({\cal S}^{\perp})\subset{\cal S}
(i.e.,
Z
has co-diagonal matrix in terms of the decomposition
{\cal H}={\cal S}\oplus{\cal S}^{\perp}
). It is useful to pick also
\|Z\|\leq\pi/2
, we shall call these normalized geodesics. It is known (see
[
13
,
28
]
or also
[
2
]
) that there exists a geodesic between
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
if and only if

\dim{\cal S}\cap{\cal T}^{\perp}=\dim{\cal S}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}.

(2)

Moreover, there exists a unique normalized geodesic joining
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
if and only if
{\cal S}\cap{\cal T}^{\perp}=\{0\}={\cal S}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}
.

We shall measure the distance between closed subspaces
{\cal S},{\cal T}\subset{\cal H}
by means of the corresponding orthogonal projections and the usual operator norm:

d({\cal S},{\cal T}):=\|P_{\cal S}-P_{\cal T}\|.

If
\|P_{\cal S}-P_{\cal T}\|<1
, then one can find a unitary operator
U=U_{{\cal S},{\cal T}}
which is a
C^{\infty}
expression in terms of
P_{\cal S},P_{\cal T}
such that
U{\cal S}={\cal T}
. There is more than one way to do this, but if one chooses the geometric argument developed in
[
28
]
and
[
13
]
, based on the existence of a unique minimal geodesic of the Grassmann manifold, one has

\|U_{{\cal S},{\cal T}}-1\|=2\sin\left(\frac{\arcsin(\|P_{\cal S}-P_{\cal T}\|)}{2}\right).

We remark that the connected components of
{\cal P}({\cal H})
are parametrized by the rank and the co-rank.
We denote by
{\cal P}_{i,j}
the connected component of
{\cal P}({\cal H})
consisting of projections with rank
i
and corank
j
, where the indices satisfy
0\leq i,j\leq\infty
and
i+j=\infty
(usual convention if both are infinite).

Halmos’ model for a pair of projections / subspaces.

Several papers consider the following decomposition of a Hilbert space in the presence of two projections (or subspaces): mainly Dixmier
[
15
]
, Halmos
[
18
]
. We transcribe Halmos’ model. Let

P
,
Q
be ortohogonal projections in
{\cal H}
, then the following five space decomposition reduces
P
and
Q
:

{\cal H}=R(P)\cap R(Q)\oplus N(P)\cap N(Q)\oplus R(P)\cap N(Q)\oplus N(P)\cap R(Q)\oplus{\cal H}_{0},

(3)

where
{\cal H}_{0}
is the orthogonal complement of the sum of the former four, and is called the generic part of
P
and
Q
.
The subspace
{\cal H}_{0}
reduces both
P
and
Q
, we shall denote the reductions by
P_{0}:=P\big|_{{\cal H}_{0}}
,
Q_{0}:=Q\big|_{{\cal H}_{0}}
.

There exists a unitary isomorphism between
{\cal H}_{0}
and a product space
{\cal L}\times{\cal L}
, and a positive operator
X
in
{\cal L}
with
N(X)=\{0\}
and
\|X\|\leq\pi/2
, such that the projections
P
and
Q
correspond, under this isomorphism, with the matrices (in
{\cal L}\times{\cal L}
)

P_{0}\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\ \hbox{ and }\ Q_{0}\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C^{2}&CS\\
CS&S^{2}\end{array}\right).

Here
C=\cos(X)
and
S=\sin(X)
.

Remark 2.3
.

The subspace
{\cal L}
is determined by the following decomposition

R(P)=R(P)\cap R(Q)\oplus R(P)\cap N(Q)\oplus{\cal L}.

Take
{\cal S}=R(P)
,
{\cal T}=R(Q)
in Remark
2.1
, then
N(G)^{\perp}={\cal S}\cap{\cal T}\oplus{\cal L}
and the operator
(1-G^{*}G)|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
is compact if and only if
S^{2}=\sin(X)
is compact in
{\cal L}
. Equivalently,
X
is compact in
{\cal L}
. Thus,
({\cal S},{\cal T})\in{\bf\Delta}
if and only if
\dim{\cal S}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}=\dim{\cal S}\cap{\cal T}^{\perp}
or
X
is not compact.
This characterization was observed in
[
20
, Rem. 4.2]
.

Index of a pair of projections.

The following facts were taken from
[
9
]
.
A pair
(P,Q)
of orthogonal projections is called a
Fredholm pair
if

Q\Big|_{R(P)}:R(P)\to R(Q)

is a Fredholm operator. Its index is

{\rm index}(P,Q)={\rm index}\left(Q\Big|_{R(P)}:R(P)\to R(Q)\right)=\dim R(P)\cap N(Q)-\dim N(P)\cap R(Q).

Among the various characterizations of Fredholm pairs we transcribe the following.

Remark 2.4
.

(P,Q)
is a Fredholm pair if and only if

1.

+1
and
-1
are isolated in the spectrum
\sigma(P-Q)
of
P-Q
.

2.

R(P)\cap N(Q)
and
N(P)\cap R(Q)
are finite dimensional.

Notice that a condition for a pair of projections
(P,Q)
that is stronger than being a Fredholm pair, which shall appear later on, is that
P-Q
is compact. It is an easy exercise that this is in fact so.

Structure of the set of pairs of subspaces with a common complement

In this section we discuss elementary properties of
{\bf\Delta}
.
We begin by determining its connected components.

Remark 3.1
.

Recall that
{\cal P}_{i,j}
denotes de connected components of
{\cal P}({\cal H})
. If
i=k<\infty
or
j=l<\infty
, then
{\cal P}_{i,j}\times{\cal P}_{k,l}\subseteq{\bf\Delta}
. Indeed, take
(P,Q)\in{\cal P}_{i,j}\times{\cal P}_{k,l}
with
i=k
. Since
T:=Q|_{R(P)}:R(P)\to R(Q)
is an operator defined in finite-dimensional spaces, we have
k=\dim N(T)+\dim R(T)=\dim N(T^{*})+\dim R(T^{*})
. From

\dim R(T)=\dim N(T)^{\perp}=\dim R(T^{*})
, it follows that
\dim R(P)\cap N(Q)=\dim N(T)=\dim N(T^{*})=\dim R(Q)\cap N(P)
. Hence
(P,Q)\in{\bf\Delta}
. Next we observe that the case where
j=l<\infty
follows by using the previous case and the map
\perp:{\cal P}({\cal H})\times{\cal P}({\cal H})\to{\cal P}({\cal H})\times{\cal P}({\cal H})
,
\perp(P,Q)=(P^{\perp},Q^{\perp})
. This is an isomorphism that preserves connected components, and from the characterization in Remark
2.2
, one easily sees that
\perp({\bf\Delta})={\bf\Delta}
.

On the other hand, assume now that
i\neq k
or
j\neq l
, and take
(P,Q)\in{\cal P}_{i,j}\times{\cal P}_{k,l}
with
i\neq k
. Thus,
R(P)
nad
R(Q)
cannot be isomorphic, and according to Remark
2.1

i)
, we get
(P,Q)\notin{\bf\Delta}
. One can consider also the case where
(P,Q)\in{\cal P}_{i,j}\times{\cal P}_{k,l}
with
j\neq l
, which follows again that
(P,Q)\notin{\bf\Delta}
by using the map
\perp
as above.

From these facts, we obtain that

{\bf\Delta}_{ij}:={\bf\Delta}\cap({\cal P}_{i,j}\times{\cal P}_{i,j})={\cal P}_{i,j}\times{\cal P}_{i,j},

whenever
i<\infty
or
j<\infty
, are the only connected components of
{\bf\Delta}
with finite dimensional rank or corank.

To analize the case of
{\cal P}_{\infty,\infty}
we need the following (known) property:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
P,Q
be orthogonal projections such that
\|P-Q\|<1
, and let
P(t)
be the unique minimal geodesic of
{\cal P}({\cal H})
such that
P(0)=P
and
P(1)=Q
. Then for all
t\in[0,1]
we have that
\|P-P(t)\|<1
.

Proof.

P(t)
is of the form
P(t)=e^{itX}Pe^{-itX}
, for
X^{*}=X
,
P
-co-diagonal with
\|X\|<\pi/2
(see Section
2
). That
X
is
P
-co-diagonal means that
X
anti-commutes with
2P-1
. Then

2P(t)-1=e^{itX}(2P-1)e^{-itX}=e^{i2tX}(2P-1),

and thus

\|P-P(t)\|=\frac{1}{2}\|(2P-1)-(2P(t)-1)\|=\|(e^{i2tX}-1)(2P-1)\|=\|e^{i2tX}-1\|<1,

because
\|2tX\|\leq 2\|X\|<\pi
, by a standard functional calculus argument.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

The subset
{\bf\Delta}_{\infty}
of
{\bf\Delta}
, consisting of pairs of pojections in
{\cal P}_{\infty,\infty}
with a common complement, is arcwise connected. Therefore, in view of the above remark,
{\bf\Delta}_{\infty}
is the connected component of such pairs.

Proof.

Let
(P_{\cal S},P_{\cal T})\in{\bf\Delta}_{\infty}
. We shall prove that there is a continuous path inside
{\bf\Delta}
connecting
(P_{\cal S},P_{\cal T})
with
(P_{\cal S},P_{\cal S})
. We proceed in two steps. First we show that there is a continuous path inside
{\bf\Delta}
connecting
(P_{\cal S},P_{\cal T})
with a pair
(P_{\cal S},E)
such that
\|P_{\cal S}-E\|<1
. Indeed, by Remark
2.2
there exists
E\in{\cal P}({\cal H})
such that
\|P_{\cal S}-E\|<1
and
\|P_{\cal T}-E\|<1
. Let
E(t)
be the minimal geodesic of
{\cal P}({\cal H})
with
E(0)=E
and
E(1)=P_{\cal T}
. Then the curve
(P_{\cal S},E(t))
remains inside
{\bf\Delta}

Remark 4.12

Let

•

Chandler Davis in fact showed much more: that the decompositions of

We shall say that

T=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}\langle\ \ \ ,\xi_{n}\rangle\psi_{n}=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}\psi_{n}\otimes\xi_{n},

Remark 4.13

(see

Except for

Remark 4.14

An elementary observation concerning this property, is that

Putting these fact together, we have the following:

Theorem 4.15
.

Let

2.

The proof is just the translation of the two conditions proven by Lauzon and Treil

\displaystyle N(P_{t})\cap R(Q_{t})

\displaystyle\supseteq\{(f,-T_{t}f):f\in R(c)\},

which gives
\dim(N(P_{t})\cap R(Q_{t}))=\infty
.

3. Set
G_{t}=Q_{t}|_{R(P_{t})}:R(P_{t})\to R(Q_{t})
. Notice that
N(G_{t})^{\perp}=(R(P_{t})\cap N(Q_{t}))^{\perp}=R(P_{t})
by the first item. We claim that
1-G_{t}^{*}G_{t}|_{R(P_{t})}:R(P_{t})\to R(P_{t})
is compact if and only if
c^{\perp}\sin^{2}(t(\frac{\pi}{2}c^{\perp}-Z))
is compact. For we observe that
XY=YX
, so that
e^{-tX}e^{tY}=e^{t(Y-X)}
. Therefore, we have that
P_{t}-G_{t}^{*}G_{t}=e^{tX}[P_{0}-P_{0}e^{t(Y-X)}Q_{0}e^{-t(Y-X)}P_{0}]e^{-tX}
is compact if and only if
P_{0}-P_{0}e^{t(Y-X)}Q_{0}e^{-t(Y-X)}P_{0}
is compact. Set
P_{t}=t(\frac{\pi}{2}c^{\perp}-Z)
, we compute

\displaystyle P_{0}e^{t(Y-X)}Q_{0}e^{-t(Y-X)}P_{0}

\displaystyle=P_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(P_{t})&-\sin(P_{t})\\
\sin(P_{t})&\cos(P_{t})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(P_{t})&\sin(P_{t})\\
-\sin(P_{t})&\cos(P_{t})\end{pmatrix}P_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}(P_{t})+c\sin^{2}(P_{t})&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,
P_{0}-P_{0}e^{t(Y-X)}Q_{0}e^{-t(Y-X)}P_{0}
is compact if and only if
c^{\perp}\sin^{2}(P_{t})
is compact, which proves our claim. Also observe that one can construct examples, where
Z
satisfies the previous conditions, such that
X_{t}:=c^{\perp}\sin^{2}(P_{t})
is compact or not. For instance, if
Z
satisfies
N(Z)^{\perp}=R(Z)\subseteq N(c)
, then this depends on when
\frac{\pi}{2}c^{\perp}-Z
is compact or not.

From these observations, we obtain that
(P_{t},Q_{t})\in\Delta
for all
t\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
,
t\neq 0
, if and only if we take
Z
such that
X_{t}
is not compact. Thus, we can choose
Z
satisfying this condition to get a family of curves
\delta^{t}(s)=(\delta^{t}_{1}(s),\delta^{t}_{2}(s))
,
s\in[0,1]
, defined by

\displaystyle\delta^{t}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle:=e^{2stX}e^{-tX}P_{0}e^{tX}e^{-2stX},

\displaystyle\delta^{t}_{2}(s)

\displaystyle:=e^{2stY}e^{-tY}Q_{0}e^{tY}e^{-2stY},

which are geodesics in
{\cal P}({\cal H})\times{\cal P}({\cal H})
joining
(P_{-t},Q_{-t})\in{\bf\Delta}
and
(P_{t},Q_{t})\in{\bf\Delta}
such that
\delta^{t}(s)\in{\bf\Delta}
,
s\neq\frac{1}{2}
and
\delta^{t}(\frac{1}{2})=(P_{0},Q_{0})\in{\bf\Gamma}
. Furthermore, we know that
\delta^{t}
has minimal length since
t\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
,
\|Z\|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
,
X
is co-diagonal with respect to
P_{0}
and
Y
is co-diagonal with respect to
Q_{0}
. Minimizing geodesics of
{\cal P}({\cal H})
are uniquely determined for endpoints lying at distance less than
1
. Hence, the points
(P_{-t,}Q_{-t}),(P_{t},Q_{t})\in{\bf\Delta}
can be made arbitrary close to each other if one takes
t
small enough, and they cannot be joined by a minimizing geodesic contained in
{\bf\Delta}
.

Properties of the Fredholm index can be used to derive sufficient conditions to obtain minimizing geodesics contained in
{\bf\Delta}
or
{\bf\Gamma}
that join pairs in
{\cal P}_{\infty,\infty}\times{\cal P}_{\infty,\infty}
.
We now recall the definition and some properties of the restricted Grassmannian; see for instance
[
12
,
29
,
30
]
. The restricted Grassmannian is given by

Gr_{res}=Gr_{res}({\cal H}_{+},{\cal H}_{-}):=\{P\in{\cal P}({\cal H}):P-P_{+}\text{ is compact}\},

(5)

for some fixed projections
P_{\pm}
associated to a decomposition
{\cal H}_{+}\oplus{\cal H}_{-}={\cal H}
, where
{\cal H}_{\pm}

are both infinite dimensional subspaces.
Recall from Remark
2.4
that given two projections
P,Q
such that
P-Q
is compact, then
Q|_{R(P)}:R(P)\to R(Q)
is a Fredholm operator, with index

\displaystyle\hbox{index}(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=\text{index}(QP|_{R(P)}:R(P)\to R(Q))

\displaystyle=\dim(R(P)\cap N(Q))-\dim(R(Q)\cap N(P)).

We observe that the sets

Gr_{res}^{(k)}:=\{P\in Gr_{res}:\hbox{index}(P_{+},P)=k\},\,\,\,\,k\in\mathbb{Z},

describe all the connected components of
Gr_{res}
.

Proposition 5.4
.

The following assertions hold:

i)

For every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
Gr_{res}^{(k)}\times Gr_{res}^{(k)}\subseteq{\bf\Delta}
, and there is a minimizing geodesic contained in
{\bf\Delta}
joining any pair of points in
Gr_{res}^{(k)}\times Gr_{res}^{(k)}
.

ii)

For
k\neq j
,
Gr_{res}^{(k)}\times Gr_{res}^{(j)}\subseteq{\bf\Gamma}
, and there is a minimizing geodesic contained in
{\bf\Gamma}
joining any pair of points in
Gr_{res}^{(k)}\times Gr_{res}^{(j)}
.

Proof.

i)
Given three projections
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
such that
(P_{1},P_{2})
and
(P_{2},P_{3})
are Fredholm pairs, and either
P_{1}-P_{2}
is compact or
P_{2}-P_{3}
is compact, then
(P_{1},P_{3})
is a Fredholm pair and
\hbox{index}(P_{1},P_{3})=\hbox{index}(P_{1},P_{2})+\hbox{index}(P_{2},P_{3})
(see
[
9
, Thm. 3.4]
). Thus, if one has two projections
P,Q\in Gr_{res}
satisfying
\hbox{index}(P_{+},P)=\hbox{index}(P_{+},Q)=k
, then
\hbox{index}(P,Q)=\hbox{index}(P,P_{+})+\hbox{index}(P_{+},Q)=-\hbox{index}(P_{+},P)+\hbox{index}(P_{+},Q)=0
. That is, we obtain that
(P,Q)\in{\bf\Delta}
. Take now two pairs
(P_{0},Q_{0}),(P_{1},Q_{1})\in Gr_{res}^{(k)}\times Gr_{res}^{(k)}
. By the same properties of the index, we find that
\hbox{index}(P_{0},P_{1})=\hbox{index}(Q_{0},Q_{1})=0
. It was shown in
[
8
]
that there exists a minimizing geodesic joining every pair of points in the same connected of the restricted Grassmannian.
Actually this results was proved for the restricted Grassmannian relative to the ideal of Hilbert-Schmidt operators; but it is not difficult to check that the same holds for the above defined restricted Grassmannian relative to the ideal of compact operators. Thus, there is a minimizing geodesic
\delta(t)=(P_{t},Q_{t})\in{\bf\Delta}
,
t\in[0,1]
, joining the points
(P_{0},Q_{0})
and
(P_{1},Q_{1})
.

ii)
Similarly as in the previous item, we see that
\hbox{index}(P,Q)\neq 0
, whenever
\hbox{index}(P_{+},P)=k
and
\hbox{index}(P_{+},Q)=j
(
k\neq j
). Also note that
P-PQP=P(P-Q)P=P(P-P_{+})P+P(P_{+}-Q)P
is compact since we assume that
P,Q\in Gr_{res}
. Hence
(P,Q)\in{\bf\Gamma}
. Again the same argument as in the previous item shows that there is a minimizing geodesic
\delta(t)=(P_{t},Q_{t})\in{\bf\Gamma}
,
t\in[0,1]
, joining any pairs of points
(P_{0},Q_{0})
and
(P_{1},Q_{1})
with
(P_{0},Q_{0}),(P_{1},Q_{1})\in Gr_{res}^{(k)}\times Gr_{res}^{(j)}
.
∎

6
Examples in function spaces

In previous works
[
4
,
6
,
7
]
we studied the condition for the existence of minimal geodesics joining two subspaces in certain functional spaces. In this section we discuss if those subspaces and other related subspaces satisfy the weaker condition of having a common complement.

Let
L^{2}
and
L^{\infty}
be the spaces of square-integrable and essentially bounded complex valued functions with respect to the Lebesgue measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|=1\}
.
For
p=2,\infty
, we consider the corresponding Hardy spaces

H^{p}=\{f\in L^{p}:\hat{f}(n)=0\hbox{ for }n<0\}.

Here
\{\hat{f}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are the Fourier coefficients of
f\in L^{2}
. These are closed subspaces of
L^{p}
. As usual we identify
H^{2}

with the space
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
consisting of all the functions
f
analytic in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
satisfying

\|f\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}:=\left(\sup_{0\leq r<1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{it})|^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Given
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, one has that
\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}f(re^{it})=f^{*}(e^{it})
exists for the
L^{2}
norm, and
\|f^{*}\|_{L^{2}}=\|f\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
. Analogously the subspace
H^{\infty}
is identified with the space
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
consisting of bounded analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
endowed with the norm
\|f\|_{H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|f(z)|
; though in this case the limit defining
f^{*}
has to be taken in the weak* - topology. These classical results and others mentioned without references in this section can be found for instance in the books
[
10
,
16
,
26
]
.

Our first set of examples concerns subspaces of the form
fH^{2}
,
f\in L^{\infty}
. These are closed subspaces of
L^{2}
if and only if
f\in(L^{\infty})^{\times}
, where
(L^{\infty})^{\times}
are the invertible functions in the algebra
L^{\infty}
(see
[
6
, Lemma 3.1]
).
In such case,
fH^{2}
is a (single) shift-invariant subspace. Conversely, the Beurling-Helson theorem gives that
a closed (single) shift-invariant subspace must be of the form
fH^{2}
, for some
f\in L^{\infty}
,
|f|=1
a.e.
We consider the Riesz projection

P_{+}:L^{2}\to H^{2},\,\,\,\,P_{+}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(n)e^{int}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\hat{f}(n)e^{int}.

We recall that the Toeplitz operator
T_{f}:H^{2}\to H^{2}
with symbol
f\in L^{\infty}
is defined by
T_{f}h=P_{+}(fh)
,
for
h\in H^{2}
.

Example 6.1

(
Sarason algebra and restricted Grassmannian of the Hardy space
)
.

Let
C
denote the space of continuous complex valued functions on
\mathbb{T}
. The
Sarason algebra
is defined as

H^{\infty}+C=\{f+g:f\in H^{\infty},\,g\in C\}.

It is the smallest closed subalgebra of
L^{\infty}
that properly contains
H^{\infty}
. We write
(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}
for the invertible functions of the algebra
H^{\infty}+C
, which has a characterization in terms of the harmonic extension. Recall that the harmonic extension to the unit disk of a function
f\in L^{\infty}
is given by

(hf)(re^{it})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}k_{r}(t-s)f(e^{is})ds=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(n)r^{|n|}e^{int},\,\,\,\,0\leq r<1,\,\,\,0\leq t<2\pi,

where
k_{r}(t)=(1-r^{2})/(1-2r\cos(t)+r^{2})
is the Poisson kernel.
Then, one has that
f\in(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}
if and only if there exist
\delta,\epsilon>0
such that
|(hf)(re^{it})|\geq\epsilon
for

1-\delta<r<1
. For
f\in(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}
, one can define an index
ind(f)
as minus the winding number with respect to the origin of the curve
(hf)(re^{it})
for
1-\delta<r<1
. This index is stable under small perturbations and it is an homomorphism of
(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}
onto the group of integers.

In this example, we take
L^{2}
as the underlying Hilbert space, and subspaces of the form

{\cal S}=fH^{2},\,\,\,\,\,{\cal T}=gH^{2},\,\,\,\,\,f,g\in(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}.

Let
Gr_{res}
the corresponding restricted Grassmannian associated with the Riesz projection
P_{+}
(see (
5
)). According to
[
6
]
, for
f
an invertible function in
L^{\infty}
, we have
f\in(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}
if and only if
P_{fH^{2}}\in Gr_{res}^{(k)}
, where
k=-ind(f)
. Applying Proposition
5.4
we obtain that the subspaces
fH^{2}
,
gH^{2}
admit a common complement if and only if
ind(f)=ind(g)
.
Notice that
1-G^{*}G|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
is always compact since
P_{\cal S}-P_{+}
and
P_{\cal T}-P_{+}
are compact operators for
P_{\cal S},P_{\cal T}\in Gr_{res}
. In the case in which
ind(f)\neq ind(g)
, we observe that
(P_{fH^{2}},P_{gH^{2}})\in{\bf\Gamma}_{1}^{n}
, where
n=ind(g)-ind(g)
.

Remark 6.2
.

We observe some relevant cases contained in the previous example, and we recall several ways to compute the index, which can be useful to construct concrete examples.

i)
Let
(H^{\infty})^{\times}
and
C^{\times}
be the invertible functions in
H^{\infty}
and
C
, respectively.
Of course,
C^{\times}
are the non-vanishing continuous functions on
\mathbb{T}
; meanwhile, for
f\in H^{\infty}
, one has
f\in(H^{\infty})^{\times}
if and only if
|(hf)(z)|\geq\epsilon>0
, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
.
Since
(H^{\infty})^{\times}\subset(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}
and
C^{\times}\subset(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}
, we can give as particular examples subspaces of the form
fH^{2}
, where
f\in(H^{\infty})^{\times}
or
f\in C^{\times}
.

At this point, it can be of interest to recall another characterization of the invertible functions in the Sarason algebra. For
f\in H^{\infty}+C
, one has that
f\in(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}

if and only if
f=f_{1}f_{2}
, for some functions
f_{1}\in(H^{\infty})^{\times}
and
f_{2}\in C^{\times}
(see
[
26
, Section 2.5.3]
).

ii)
The index of a non-vanishing differentiable function
f
on
\mathbb{T}
is given by

ind(f)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{f^{\prime}(e^{it})}{f(e^{it})}e^{it}dt.

When
f
is
C^{1}
and
|f(e^{it})|=1
,
0\leq t<2\pi
, it can be rewritten as

ind(f)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n|\hat{f}(n)|^{2}.

In the case of a rational function
f
which does not vanish on
\mathbb{T}
,
ind(f)=z-p
, where
z
and
p
are the number of zeros and poles of
f
in
\mathbb{D}
counted with multiplicities.

We now recall the inner-outer factorization. A function
f\in H^{2}
is called
inner
if
|f(e^{it})|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. A function
f\in H^{2}
is
outer
if
\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{f\chi_{n}:n\geq 0\}=H^{2}
, where
\chi_{n}
are the functions on
{\mathbb{T}}
defined by
\chi_{n}(e^{it})=e^{int}
(
n\in\mathbb{Z}
). Every
f\in H^{2}
can be expressed as
f=f_{inn}f_{out}
, where
f_{inn}
is an inner function and
f_{out}
is an outer function. This factorization is unique up to a multiplicative constant.

An inner function
f
can also be factorized uniquely as
f=\lambda bs
, where
\lambda\in{\mathbb{T}}
,
b
is a Blaschke product and
s
is a singular inner function. This is known as the
canonical factorization
. We recall that a
Blaschke product
is an inner function
b
with zeros
\{a_{j}\}_{1}^{n}
in
\mathbb{D}
(
1\leq n\leq\infty
) defined by

b(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\bar{a}_{j}}{|a_{j}|}\frac{a_{j}-z}{1-\bar{a}_{j}z},\,\,\,\,z\in\mathbb{D}.

For
a_{j}=0
we interpret
\bar{a}_{j}/|a_{j}|=-1
.
In the case where
n=\infty
, the sequence of zeros
\{a_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
satisfies the Blaschke condition
\sum_{j\geq 1}1-|a_{j}|<\infty
, and the product converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
.
On the other hand, a
singular inner function

s=s_{\mu}
is defined by

s(z)=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\psi+z}{\psi-z}d\mu(\psi)\right),\,\,\,z\in\mathbb{D},

for
\mu
a positive finite measure on
{\mathbb{T}}
that is singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Thus, for
f
an inner function,
f=\lambda bs
,
b
is the Blaschke product is associated with the zero set of
f
and
s>0
on
\mathbb{D}
.

Example 6.3

(
Inner functions with distinct supports
)
.

The
support
of an inner function
f
denoted by
supp(f)
is the set of all points in
{\mathbb{T}}
that are limits of zeros of
f
or belong to the support of
\mu
(here
s=s_{\mu}
). In this example, we consider
{\cal H}=L^{2}
,
{\cal S}=fH^{2}
and
{\cal T}=gH^{2}
, for
f
,
g
inner functions with distinct support. Thus, there is point
z_{0}\in supp(f)\setminus supp(g)
or
z_{0}\in supp(g)\setminus supp(f)
. We may assume that the first of these conditions holds true for our purpose of determining if
fH^{2}
and
gH^{2}
have a common complement. Then from the proof of
[
23
, Thm. 1]
we know that
T_{f\bar{g}}
is not left invertible in
H^{2}
, which is equivalent to say that
T_{f\bar{g}}
is not injective or
R(T_{f\bar{g}})
is not closed. Below we consider these two cases.

In the first case,
T_{f\bar{g}}
is not injective, so by Coburn’s lemma we know that
T_{f\bar{g}}^{*}=T_{\bar{f}g}
is injective. Hence
\dim fH^{2}\cap(gH^{2})^{\perp}=\dim N(T_{f\bar{g}})>0
and
\dim gH^{2}\cap(fH^{2})^{\perp}=\dim N(T_{g\bar{f}})=0
.
Then, we have to discuss whether
1-G^{*}G|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
is compact or not, where we take the operator
G
as
G:=P_{\cal S}|_{{\cal T}}:{\cal T}\to{\cal S}
.
Note that
P_{\cal S}=M_{f}P_{+}M_{\bar{f}}
and
P_{\cal T}=M_{g}P_{+}M_{\bar{g}}
. Here
M_{h}
is the multiplication operator by a function
h\in L^{\infty}
acting on
L^{2}
.
Then

G^{*}G=P_{\cal T}P_{\cal S}P_{\cal T}|_{\cal T}=M_{g}P_{+}M_{f\bar{g}}P_{+}M_{g\bar{f}}P_{+}M_{\bar{g}}|_{gH^{2}},

and

N(G)=N(G^{*}G)=M_{g}N(P_{+}M_{f\bar{g}}P_{+}M_{g\bar{f}}P_{+}).

The unitary operator
M_{\bar{g}}=M_{g}^{*}
maps
{\cal T}
onto
H^{2}
. Note also that
M_{\bar{g}}
maps
N(G)
onto

N(P_{+}M_{f\bar{g}}P_{+}M_{g\bar{f}}P_{+})=N(P_{+}M_{g\bar{f}}P_{+}).

Then instead of showing that
1-G^{*}G\big|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
is compact, we can show, equivalently, that

1-P_{+}M_{f\bar{g}}P_{+}M_{g\bar{f}}P_{+}\big|_{N(P_{+}M_{g\bar{f}}P_{+})^{\perp}}

is compact. Note that
P_{+}M_{g\bar{f}}P_{+}
on
H^{2}
coincides with the Toeplitz operator
T_{g\bar{f}}
.
Since we have assumed that
N(T_{\bar{f}g})=\{0\}
, our task now reduces to analyze if the operator

1-T_{\bar{f}g}^{*}T_{\bar{f}g}

(6)

is compact in
H^{2}
. To this end, we need to recall here some facts. The operator
T_{v}T_{u}-T_{vu}
is called the semicommutator of the Toeplitz operators
T_{u}
and
T_{v}
for
u,v\in L^{\infty}
. The Hankel operator with symbol
u\in L^{\infty}
is the bounded linear operator
H_{u}:H^{2}\to H^{2}_{-}
defined by
H_{u}(f)=P_{-}(uf)
, where
f\in H^{2}
and
P_{-}
is the orthogonal projection onto
H^{2}_{-}:=(H^{2})^{\perp}
. The following well-known formula relates semicommutators and Hankel operators:

T_{uv}-T_{u}T_{v}=H_{\bar{u}}^{*}H_{v}

Recall also Hartman’s theorem, which states that
H_{u}
is compact if and only
u\in H^{\infty}+C
. Applying these results with
u=f\bar{g}
,
v=\bar{f}g
, we find that the operator in (
6
) is compact if and only if
H_{\bar{f}g}^{*}H_{\bar{f}g}
is compact. This is equivalent to say that
H_{\bar{f}g}
is compact, or by Hartman’s theorem, that
\bar{f}g\in H^{\infty}+C
.

We now show that this is a contradiction with our assumption on the supports of
f
and
g
. Indeed, for
z_{0}\in supp(f)
there is a sequence
\{z_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
in
\mathbb{D}
such that

z_{n}\to z_{0}
and
(hf)(z_{n})\to 0
(
[
23
, Lemma 2]
). On the other hand, if
z_{0}\notin supp(g)
, then
|(hg)(z_{n})|\to 1
. Notice that the harmonic extensions
hf
and
hg
are the analytic extensions because
f
,
g
are inner. Take
a\in H^{\infty}
,
b\in C
and
\epsilon>0
. Recall that the harmonic extension is asymptotically multiplicative on
H^{\infty}+C
, so there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{D}
such that
\|(hf(a+b))(z)-(hf)(z)\,(ha)(z)-(hf)(z)\,(hb)(z)\|<\epsilon
, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}\setminus K
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\|\bar{f}g-(a+b)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle=\|g-f(a+b)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\geq\|hg-hf(a+b)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\geq\|hg-hf(a+b)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}\setminus K)}

\displaystyle\geq\|hg-(hf)(ha)-(hf)(hb)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}\setminus K)}-

\displaystyle-\|hf(a+b)-(hf)(ha)-(hf)(hb)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}\setminus K)}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\epsilon.

We have used that
|(hg)(z_{n})|\to 1
,
(hf)(z_{n})(ha)(z_{n})\to 0
since
ha
is bounded for
a\in H^{\infty}
and
(hf)(z_{n})(hb)(z_{n})\to 0
since
hb
is continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
for
b\in C
. The above arguments imply

d(\bar{f}g,H^{\infty}+C)=1
, and in particular, we obtain
\bar{f}g\notin H^{\infty}+C
. Hence the operator
1-G^{*}G\big|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
is not compact.

In the second case, we suppose that
T_{f\bar{g}}
is injective and
R(T_{f\bar{g}})
is not closed. We change the definition of
G
here by setting
G:=P_{\cal T}|_{{\cal S}}:{\cal S}\to{\cal T}
. Similar arguments as in the previous case show that, under the assumption
N(T_{f\bar{g}})=\{0\}
, we have the operator
1-G^{*}G|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
is compact if and only if
1-T_{\bar{g}f}^{*}T_{\bar{g}f}
is compact in
H^{2}
. But
R(T_{f\bar{g}})
is not closed, and consequently, neither
R(|T_{f\bar{g}}|^{2})
is closed, so that
0
is not an isolated point of its spectrum. Hence
1
is not an isolated point of the spectrum of
1-T_{\bar{g}f}^{*}T_{\bar{g}f}
, which clearly implies that this operator cannot be compact.

From the above two cases, we conclude that
fH^{2}
and
gH^{2}
have a common complement, whenever
f
and
g
are inner functions with distinct support.

Remark 6.4
.

i)
The pair of subspaces considered in Example
6.1
differ from that of Example
6.3
. Indeed, if
fH^{2}
and
gH^{2}
are subspaces such that
f
,
g
inner functions with
supp(f)\neq supp(g)
,
then we must have that
supp(f)\neq\emptyset
or
supp(g)\neq\emptyset
. But the unique inner functions invertible in
H^{\infty}+C
are the finite Blaschke products (see
[
26
, Sec. 2.5.3]
).

ii)
We observe that under the stronger assumption
supp(f)\setminus supp(g)\neq\emptyset
and
supp(g)\setminus supp(f)\neq\emptyset
, it can be shown that
N(T_{\bar{f}g})\simeq gH^{2}\cap(fH^{2})^{\perp}=\{0\}
and
N(T_{f\bar{g}})\simeq fH^{2}\cap(gH^{2})^{\perp}=\{0\}
(see
[
23
, Thm. 1-2]
and
[
6
, Ex. 5.6]
). However, one can give examples of inner functions
f
,
g
with
supp(g)\subset supp(f)
and
N(T_{\bar{f}g})\neq\{0\}
. As a simple example, take
h
a Blaschke product with infinite zeros converging to
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{T}}
,
g
another Blaschke product with infinite zeros converging to
z_{1}\in{\mathbb{T}}
,
z_{0}\neq z_{1}
and take
f=gh
. Clearly, we have
N(T_{\bar{f}g})=N(T_{\bar{h}})\neq\{0\}
.

Example 6.5

(
Inner functions with same supports
)
.

We take
{\cal H}=L^{2}
,
{\cal S}=fH^{2}
and
gH^{2}
for
f
,
g
inner functions with the same support. Furthermore, we consider only the case
supp(f)=supp(g)=\{1\}
.
This situation is illustrated by the following examples.

For the first type of examples we consider
f
a singular inner function and
g
a Blaschke product. More precisely, let
f_{a}(z)=\exp(a(z+1)/(z-1))
,
a>0
. A Koosis function is a Blaschke product
g
such that
f_{a}\bar{g}\in H^{\infty}+C
for all
a>0
.
A Koosis function is characterized in terms of the sequence of zeros
\{a_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of the Blaschke product
g
. Indeed, let
\lambda_{k}=i(1+a_{k})/(1-a_{k})
,
k\geq 1
, which defines a sequence in the upper-half plane. Then,
g
is a Koosis function if and only if
{\rm Im}(\lambda_{k})\to\infty
and

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\sum_{k}\frac{{\rm Im}(\lambda_{k})}{|x-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}=0,\,\,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

In particular,
g
is a Koosis function if it has real zeros converging to
1
.
It was proved that
N(T_{\bar{f}_{a}g})\simeq gH^{2}\cap(f_{a}H^{2})^{\perp}
is infinite dimensional and
R(T_{\bar{f}_{a}g})=L^{2}
for every Koosis function
g
and
a>0
(see
[
23
, Thm. 4]
). Thus, we have
\dim gH^{2}\cap(f_{a}H^{2})^{\perp}=\infty
and
\dim f_{a}H^{2}\cap(gH^{2})^{\perp}=0
. We can repeat the argument of Example
6.3
, where now
N(T_{\bar{f}_{a}g})=\{0\}
and
G=P_{gH^{2}}|_{f_{a}H^{2}}:f_{a}H^{2}\to gH^{2}
, to find that the operator
1-G^{*}G|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
must be compact because
f_{a}\bar{g}\in H^{\infty}+C
by definition of a Koosis function. Hence
f_{a}H^{2}
and
gH^{2}
do not admit a common complement, and
(P_{f_{a}H^{2}},P_{gH^{2}})\in{\bf\Gamma}^{r}_{\infty}
.

The second type of examples concerns two infinite Blaschke products converging to
1
. It was mentioned in
[
21
]
that there exist infinite Blaschke products
f
,
g
such that
f/g\in(H^{\infty}+C)^{\times}
(i.e.
f
,
g
are codivisible in
H^{\infty}+C
). This can be constructed by taking two infinite Blaschke products with zeros converging to one point in
{\mathbb{T}}
, where one of the zeros is a suitable perturbation of the other. Using this idea for studying examples of geodesics in the Grassmann manifold, we proved in
[
7
, Thm. 5.5]
that for every integer
n\geq 0
there exist two disjoint sequences
\{a_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{b_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
in
\mathbb{D}
satisfying Blaschke condition,
a_{k}\to 1
,
b_{k}\to 1
, and their corresponding Blaschke products
f
and
g
are such that
\dim(fH^{2})^{\perp}\cap gH^{2}=0
and
\dim fH^{2}\cap(gH^{2})^{\perp}=n
. Furthermore,
f
and
g
are codivisible in
H^{\infty}+C
, so again by the previous arguments we have that
1-G^{*}G|_{N(G)^{\perp}}
is compact. Hence there is a common complement for
fH^{2}
and
gH^{2}
if and only
n=0
. In the case
n\neq 0
, we observe that
(P_{fH^{2}},P_{gH^{2}})\in{\bf\Gamma}_{1}^{n}
.

Example 6.6

(
Functions supported on arcs and odd functions
)
.

Let again
{\cal H}=L^{2}=L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, let
{\cal J}\subset\mathbb{T}
be the arc
{\cal J}=\{e^{it}:t\in[0,T]\}
, and consider the subspace

{\cal S}={\cal S}_{\cal J}=\{f\in L^{2}:\sup(f)\subset{\cal J}\}.

Here
\sup(f)
denotes the (essential) support of
f\in L^{2}
.
We also consider the subspace

{\cal T}={\cal T}_{odd}=\{g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}):\hat{g}(m)=0\hbox{ for }m\hbox{ even}\}.

Notice that
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
are both infinite dimensional subspaces. The following facts follow from straightforward computations.

1.

If
T=\pi
, then
{\cal S}_{\cal J}
and
{\cal T}_{odd}
are in generic position, in particular,
{\cal S}_{\cal J}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}^{\perp}=\{0\}={\cal S}_{\cal J}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}
, and therefore
{\cal S}_{\cal J}
and
{\cal T}_{odd}
have a common complement.

2.

If
T<\pi
, then
{\cal S}_{\cal J}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}^{\perp}=\{0\}
. Set
{\cal J}_{T}=\{e^{it}:-\pi\leq t\leq-T\hbox{ or }T\leq t\leq\pi\}
, then

\dim{\cal S}_{\cal J}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}=\dim\{f\in L^{2}:\hat{f}(m)=0\hbox{ for }m\hbox{ even},\,\sup(f)\subset{\cal J}_{T}\}=\infty.

Nevertheless in the second case above
{\cal S}_{\cal J}
and
{\cal T}_{odd}
have a common complement. Indeed, note that

1-P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}\big|_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}=P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}-P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}\big|_{L^{2}({\cal J})}=P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}(1-P_{{\cal T}_{odd}})\big|_{L^{2}({\cal J})}.

Next observe that
(1-P_{{\cal T}_{odd}})f(e^{it})=\frac{1}{2}(f(e^{it})+f(-e^{it}))
. If
h\in L^{2}
is supported in
{\cal J}
, then
h(-e^{it})=0
a.e. for
t\in[0,T]
, and one has that

(1-P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}})h=\frac{1}{2}h,

which clearly implies that
1-P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}\big|_{{\cal S}_{\cal J}}
is non compact.

The Hardy space
H^{2}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, where the reproducing kernels are given by the Szego kernels

k_{b}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{b}z},\,\,\,z,b\in\mathbb{D}.

For our next example we recall that that a sequence
\{b_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
in
\mathbb{D}
is said to be an
interpolating sequence
if there exists
\delta>0
such that

\delta<\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\Big|\frac{b_{i}-b_{j}}{1-\bar{b}_{j}b_{i}}\Big|\ ,\ \ i=1,2,\dots.

(7)

Let
\Pi_{\bf B}
denote the Blaschke product associated to a sequence
{\bf B}=\{b_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
satisfying Blaschke condition. Carleson proved the following result: the normalized Szego kernels form a Riesz basis
\{\frac{k_{b_{i}}}{\|k_{b_{i}}\|}\}_{i\geq 1}
of the model space
{\cal K}_{\Pi_{\bf B}}=H^{2}\ominus\Pi_{\bf B}H^{2}
if and only
{\bf B}
is an interpolating sequence.

Example 6.7

(
Blaschke products and odd functions
)
.

Let now
{\cal H}=H^{2}
, and
{\bf B}=\{b_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
satisfying the Blaschke condition.
Consider the subspaces
{\cal T}={\cal T}_{odd}
of odd functions in
H^{2}
, and

{\cal S}={\cal S}_{\bf B}=\{f\in H^{2}:f(b_{j})=0\hbox{ for }j\geq 1\}.

We give two type of examples in this setting.

In the first case, suppose additionally that
{\bf B}\cap(-{\bf B})=\emptyset
and that the (disjoint) union
{\bf B}\cup(-{\bf B})
is an interpolating sequence. In what follows we use that
\|k_{b}\|=(1-|b|^{2})^{-1/2}
.
Suppose that
h\in{\cal S}_{\bf B}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}={\cal K}_{\Pi_{\bf B}}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}
, then

h=\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}(1-|b_{j}|^{2})^{1/2}k_{b_{j}}
is odd. Since
k_{b}(-z)=k_{-b}(z)
, we get

\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}(1-|b_{j}|^{2})^{1/2}k_{b_{j}}(z)=h(z)=-h(-z)=-\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}(1-|b_{j}|^{2})^{1/2}k_{-b_{j}}(z),

Using that
{\bf B}\cap(-{\bf B})=\emptyset
, note

0=\sum_{k\geq 1}\delta_{k}(1-|c_{k}|^{2})^{1/2}k_{c_{k}},\hbox{ where }\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\delta_{k}=\gamma_{j}\hbox{ for }c_{k}=b_{j}\,,\\
\delta_{k}=\gamma_{i}\hbox{ for }c_{k}=-b_{i}.\end{array}\right.

Since
\{(1-|c_{k}|^{2})^{1/2}k_{c_{k}}\}_{k\geq 1}
form a Riesz basis, it follows that
c_{k}=0
for all
k
. Hence
{\cal S}_{\bf B}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}=\{0\}
.

Next observe that
{\cal T}_{odd}^{\perp}={\cal T}_{even}
consists of even function in
H^{2}
. Apparently, if
f\in{\cal T}_{even}
and
f(b)=0
, then also
f(-b)=0
. Therefore if
f\in{\cal S}_{\bf B}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}^{\perp}
, the products

\frac{b_{j}-z}{1-\bar{b}_{j}z}\frac{b_{j}+z}{1+\bar{b}_{j}z}=\frac{b^{2}_{j}-z^{2}}{1-\bar{b}^{2}_{j}z^{2}}

divide
f
. Note that these products are even functions. It follows that

{\cal S}_{\bf B}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}^{\perp}=\{g\ \Pi_{{\bf B}\cup-{\bf B}}:g\in H^{2}\hbox{ is even}\},

which is an infinite-dimensional subspace. Now take
G:=P_{{\cal S}_{\bf B}}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}:{\cal T}_{odd}\to{\cal S}_{\bf B}
, which has trivial nullspace. Note also that
1-G^{*}G=1-P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\bf B}}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}\big|_{{\cal T}_{odd}}=P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\bf B}}^{\perp}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}\big|_{{\cal T}_{odd}}
.
Recall that if
u
is an inner function, then the projection
P_{u}:L^{2}\to{\cal K}_{u}\subset L^{2}
is given by

P_{u}f=f-uP_{+}(\bar{u}f),

where
P_{+}
is the Riesz projection (see
[
19
, Prop. 5.14]
).

We claim that
P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\bf B}}^{\perp}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}\big|_{{\cal T}_{odd}}
is non compact, which would imply that
{\cal S}_{\bf B}
and
{\cal T}_{odd}
have a common complement.
Clearly
P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\bf B}}^{\perp}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}\big|_{{\cal T}_{odd}}
is compact if and only if
P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\bf B}}^{\perp}
is compact in
H^{2}
. This operator is compact
if and only if its natural extension to
L^{2}
, namely

P_{odd}P_{\Pi_{\bf B}}
is compact. Here

P_{odd}f(z)=\frac{1}{2}(f(z)-f(-z))
is the orthogonal projection onto odd elements in
L^{2}
. Indeed,

P_{odd}P_{\Pi_{\bf B}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P_{{\cal T}_{odd}}P_{{\cal S}_{\bf B}}^{\perp}&\hbox{ in }H^{2},\\
0&\hbox{ in }(H^{2})^{\perp}.\end{array}\right.

Clearly
P_{odd}P_{\Pi_{\bf B}}
is compact if and only if
P_{odd}P_{\Pi_{\bf B}}M_{\Pi_{\bf B}}
is compact, where
M_{\Pi_{\bf B}}
denotes the (unitary) multiplication operator by the unimodular element
\Pi_{\bf B}
. Note that

P_{odd}P_{\Pi_{\bf B}}M_{\Pi_{\bf B}}f=P_{odd}(\Pi_{\bf B}f-\Pi_{\bf B}P^{+}f)=P_{odd}M_{\Pi_{\bf B}}P_{-}f,

where
P_{-}
is the orthogonal projection onto
(H^{2})^{\perp}
. This operator is clearly non compact: consider for instance
g_{n}=\bar{\Pi}_{\bf B}z^{2n-1}
for integers
n\leq 0
. These elements are orthonormal and belong to
(H^{2})^{\perp}
, and clearly

P_{odd}M_{\Pi_{\bf B}}P_{-}g_{n}=z^{2n-1}.

In the second case, we consider a variation of the above example. Put
{\bf B}_{0}
an interpolating sequence which is symmetric with respect to the origin, i.e.
{\bf B}_{0}={\bf B}\cup-{\bf B}
for some
{\bf B}=\{b_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}\subseteq\mathbb{D}
. Then, we have again

{\cal S}_{{\bf B}_{0}}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}^{\perp}=\{g\ \Pi_{{\bf B}_{0}}:g\in H^{2}\hbox{ is even}\}.

But now
{\cal S}_{{\bf B}_{0}}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}
consists of odd functions of the form
h=\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{k}(1-|b_{k}|^{2})^{1/2}k_{b_{k}}+\sum_{j\geq 1}\delta_{j}(1-|b_{j}|^{2})^{1/2}k_{-b_{j}}
. Since

\{\frac{k_{b_{j}}}{\|b_{j}\|}\}_{j\geq 1}\cup\{\frac{k_{-b_{j}}}{\|k_{-b_{j}}\|}\}_{j\geq 1}

forms a Riesz basis of
{\cal K}_{{\bf B}_{0}}
, this means that

\gamma_{k}=-\delta_{k},\ k\geq 1.

This clearly defines an infinite dimensional subspace of
H^{2}
.
Therefore, here
{\cal S}_{{\bf B}_{0}}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}^{\perp}
and
{\cal S}_{{\bf B}_{0}}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}_{odd}
are infinite dimensional, and hence
{\cal S}_{{\bf B}_{0}}
and
{\cal T}_{odd}
have a common complement.

The following example concerns well-known facts on the Uncertainty Principle in Harmonic Analysis (see for instance
[
24
]
or the survey article
[
17
]
). We also refer to
[
4
]
for the relation with the geometry of the Grassmann manifold.

Example 6.8

(
Measurable sets and Fourier-Plancharel transform
)
.

Let
I,J\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be measurable sets with finite and positive Lebesgue measure. Consider
{\cal H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with Lebesgue measure and the projections
P_{I}
onto the elements of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
supported in
I
and
Q_{J}
onto the elements whose Fourier-Plancherel transform is supported in
J
. The following facts are known:

•

R(P_{I})\cap R(Q_{J})=R(P_{I})\cap N(Q_{J})=N(P_{I})\cap R(Q_{J})=\{0\}
and
N(P_{I})\cap N(Q_{J})
is infinite dimensional (
[
24
]
).

•

P_{I}Q_{J}P_{I}
is compact, in fact, nuclear (
[
17
]
).

Therefore we have the following:

1.

{\cal S}_{I}=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\sup(f)\subset I\}
and
{\cal T}_{J}=\{g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\sup(\hat{g})\subset J\}
have a common complement since it is straightforward to check that
{\cal S}_{I}\cap{\cal T}_{J}^{\perp}=\{0\}={\cal S}_{I}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}_{J}
.

2.

{\cal S}_{I}
and
{\cal T}_{J}^{\perp}
do not have a common complement.
The role of
{\cal T}
is reversed: now
{\cal S}_{I}\cap({\cal T}_{J}^{\perp})^{\perp}=R(P_{I})\cap R(Q_{J})=\{0\}
, but
{\cal S}_{I}^{\perp}\cap{\cal T}_{J}^{\perp}
is infinite dimensional. Moreover

1-P_{{\cal S}_{I}}\big|_{{\cal T}_{J}^{\perp}}=P_{I}-P_{I}Q_{J}^{\perp}P_{I}=P_{I}Q_{J}P_{I}

is compact. Hence
(P_{{\cal S}_{I}},P_{{\cal T}_{J}^{\perp}})\in{\bf\Gamma}_{\infty}^{l}
.
