Effects of potassium polyaspartate on phenolic compounds, volatile compounds, color, and sensory properties of cabernet sauvignon wine
Authors: Zeyu Zhou, Yibin Lan, Meng Zhou, Wenzhe Tong, Yixuan Ge, Cong Yu, Xuerong Duan, Changqing Duan, Xuemei Fan, Nan Meng, Guangfeng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch on potassium polyaspartate (KPA) in wines has focused primarily on tartrate stabilization. This first systematic study on the impact of KPA on flavor offers novel production insights. KPA was applied at commercially relevant dosage levels, either alone or following 48 h of pre-freezing, together with an untreated control and an extended cold-treatment reference (−4 °C for 30 d). Phenolic and volatile compounds, along with sensory properties, were analyzed before and after bottle aging for 6 months. Although the untreated wine already exhibited high potassium bitartrate (KHT) stability (Δχ < 30 μS/cm), KPA-containing treatments further decreased Δχ, indicating a lower residual KHT crystallization tendency under the test conditions. Individual application of 45 mL/hL KPA maintained a deeper, redder hue at 6 M, associated with a higher vitisin concentration. Concurrently, it contained more norisoprenoids, carbonyl compounds, and fatty acids. The combined treatment of 48 h pre-freezing with 35–45 mL/hL KPA preserved phenolic substances, including monomeric anthocyanins, anthocyanin derivatives, and flavonols, and showed no significant effect on aroma compounds or sensory properties at 6 M. Overall, under the conditions of this study, KPA treatments produced more pronounced changes in the color-related characteristics of the Cabernet Sauvignon wine than in its sensory aroma profile.Keywords: Potassium polyaspartate, Potassium bitartrate, Phenolic compounds, Volatile compounds, Sensory properties

Introduction

During wine storage, tartrate precipitates composed of potassium bitartrate (KHT) and calcium tartrate dihydrate (CaT) gradually precipitate out. This natural physicochemical phenomenon is frequently misinterpreted by consumers as a quality defect, resulting in significant economic losses for wineries (Cui et al., 2024).

There are currently two distinct technological approaches for tartaric stabilization. The first approach is the “subtraction strategy”, which involves removing either existing bitartrate or components that may potentially form it in wine. Applicable methods include cold stabilization, ion exchangers (IE), and electrodialysis (ED). The cold stabilization method is based on the principle that the solubility of tartrates decreases as the temperature decreases. By cooling the wine from ambient conditions (20 °C) to near its freezing point, tartrates are precipitated, followed by filtration to remove crystallized precipitates. This method is currently widely adopted and proven effective in winery applications. However, it is time-consuming, economically costly, and generates environmental pollution (Dabare et al., 2023; Reilly et al., 2023). This process may lead to the loss of wine color and specific aromatic compounds, with partial oxidation due to the continuous stirring required for heat exchange and the high solubility of oxygen at low temperatures (Cosme et al., 2024; Martínez-Pérez et al., 2020). In the IE method, the cations (H+) displace potassium ions (K+) and calcium ions (Ca2+) in wine. This process reduces the content of both polyphenolic and aromatic substances (Benı´tez et al., 2002; Lasanta et al., 2013), with the most pronounced effect on monomeric anthocyanins (Sarapulova et al., 2018). ED utilizes an external electric field to induce unidirectional migration of K+, Ca2+, and hydrogen tartrate (TH−) from wine into a nitric acid buffer solution. This method demonstrates eight times lower energy consumption than cold stabilization (Bdiri et al., 2020). However, it must be noted that the International Organization of Vine and Wine (OIV) limits acidification to a maximum pH reduction of 0.3 units, which implies that certain wines cannot achieve complete stabilization (Canals & Zamora, 2017; Pasechnaya et al., 2023).

The second approach adopts an “additive strategy” by employing appropriate stabilizers to prevent the aggregation or formation of bitartrate. Available additive options include metatartaric acid (MTA), carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), and yeast mannoproteins (YMP). MTA undergoes hydrolysis after 12–18 months of storage at 10–18 °C, making it suitable only for immediate consumption or short-term marketing wine (Sprenger et al., 2015). CMC can adversely affect the chromatic characteristics and turbidity of red wines. Consequently, the OIV has restricted its oenological application exclusively to white and sparkling wine productions (Sommer et al., 2016). The interaction between YMP and tannins decreases tannin concentration and astringency intensity in wine (Mekoue Nguela et al., 2016).

Overall, traditional tartrate stabilization techniques each have their own limitations. Balancing effective tartrate stabilization with maintaining wine sensory and qualities remains a challenge, highlighting the need for more sustainable and versatile stabilization strategies. Furthermore, while stabilizers effectively inhibit tartrate crystallization, they perform ineffectively against cold-labile compounds in wine, such as pigments, proteins, polysaccharides, and tannins. These cold-labile compounds may aggregate to form cold colloidal haze, potentially interfering with stabilizer efficacy. Thus, hybrid techniques combining two or more methods are gradually emerging as a developmental trend. For instance, integrating cold treatment with stabilizer addition to mitigate the limitations of the “additive strategy”. Nevertheless, these novel technologies still require progressive validation and optimization.

In recent years, the novel tartrate-stabilizing additive potassium polyaspartate (KPA) has gained attention. The European Commission evaluated the safety of potassium polyaspartate as a food additive. It concluded that its application in wine at maximum levels of 300 mg/L, typical usage range 100–200 mg/L, posed no safety concerns (Galbusera et al., 2017). The National Health Commission of China approved potassium polyaspartate as a novel food additive in 2023. As KPA has been widely approved for use, researchers have begun to focus on its tartrate stabilization effects. Studies have found that compared to traditional additives such as MTA and CMC, KPA offers advantages in stabilization efficacy at higher temperatures and during long-term storage (Bosso, Motta, Panero, Lucini, et al., 2020; Canuti et al., 2019; Eder et al., 2019). In fact, KPA is a highly effective tool for KHT stabilization, preventing precipitation in Austrian white and red wines over a 12-month period (Philipp, Strauss, Willach, et al., 2022). However, for wines with elevated calcium ion concentrations, KPA does not eliminate the risk of CaT precipitation (Philipp, Strauss, Schlögl, et al., 2022). Overall, existing research has primarily focused on the tartrate stabilization effects and applicable scope of KPA. The effects of varying KPA concentrations and combining with pre-freezing treatment remain to be explored.

Considering all the above context, experiments were conducted involving the addition of KPA at varying concentrations and with or without pre-freezing treatment. The primary objective of this study was to systematically evaluate the effects of these treatments on wine phenolic compounds, volatile compounds, color, and sensory quality under commercially relevant application conditions. Conductivity was additionally measured to characterize treatment-related differences in residual KHT crystallization tendency. The findings were expected to provide reference information regarding the compositional and sensory changes associated with KPA application in Cabernet Sauvignon wine.

Materials and methods

Materials

The potassium polyaspartate sample was the commercial Enartis stabilizer ZENITH UNO (marketed as “Zhenli No.1” in China, potassium polyaspartate A-5D K/SD*, containing 0.3 ± 0.1% SO2) chosen from ESSECO Co., Ltd. (Italy). Its formulation is a high-purity KPA solution, free of other tartrate stabilizers, which allows for a scientific evaluation of the singular impact of KPA on the flavor characteristics of the wine.

Standards of phenolic and aroma compounds were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St Louis, MO, USA). Chromatographic grade solvents, including methanol (≥99.9%) and acetonitrile (≥99.9%), were purchased from Honeywell Burdick & Jackson (Morris Plains, NJ, USA), while formic acid (≥99.9%) was purchased from Roe Scientific Inc. (Newark, NJ, USA). Analytical grade chemicals, including gallic acid, hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, and absolute ethanol, were purchased from Beijing Chemical Works (Beijing, China), with tartaric acid, sodium hydroxide, and sodium chloride purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Deionized water was prepared using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA).

Sample treatments

After the completion of malolactic fermentation in Cabernet Sauvignon (blended dry red wine, provided by a commercial winery), the sample treatments were divided as follows. (1) Control group (CK): No treatment; (2) extended cold-treatment reference group (Cold-T): the wine was maintained at −4 °C for 30 d; (3) Combined application of 48 h pre-freezing and KPA addition groups (PF-A1, PF-A2, PF-A3): Cooled the wine to −4 °C for 48 h, filtered (0.45 μm pore size) and added KPA at different concentrations (35, 45, 55 mL/hL); (4) Individual KPA addition groups (NF-A1, NF-A2, NF-A3): skipped the pre-freezing step, directly added KPA at different concentrations (35, 45, 55 mL/hL). The KPA dosages were selected to represent commercially relevant application levels for practical winemaking. Each treatment was repeated three times. Samples were taken for analysis at the initial stage (0 M) and after bottle storage for 6 months.

Conductivity test

The potassium bitartrate (KHT) crystallization tendency of the wines was assessed using a TartarCheck conductometer (Exacta Labcenter SpA, Italy) (Bosso et al., 2016). Aseptically filtered and degassed wine sample (25 mL) was placed in a cold bath maintained at 0 °C (±1.0 °C) with continuous agitation. After calibrating the conductivity meter, the probe was inserted into the sample, and the initial conductivity reading (C1) was recorded when the sample reached the test temperature. Subsequently, the precipitating agent KHT (16 g/L) was added, and measured again (C2) after 30 min of reaction.

Phenolic compounds analysis

Analysis of monomeric anthocyanins, non-anthocyanin phenolics, and anthocyanin derivatives was performed using an Agilent 1200-6410B high-performance liquid chromatography triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-QqQ-MS/MS, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The chromatographic column was an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm). Mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; Mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of methanol and acetonitrile. Wine samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone membrane filter (Jinteng Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., China) prior to analysis.

Monomeric anthocyanins

Analysis of monomeric anthocyanins was conducted according to our previously reported method (Li et al., 2016). The chromatographic conditions were gradient elution with an injection volume of 1 μL. Mobile phase B was from 10% to 46% for 28 min, from 46% to 10% for 1 min, and re-equilibration at 10% for 5 min. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the column temperature was set at 55 °C. Mass spectrometry was operated in positive electrospray ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Capillary voltage was 4 kV. Drying gas temperature was 350 °C with a flow rate of 12 L/min. Nebulizer pressure was 35 psi.

Identification was achieved by comparing retention times and qualitative transition ions with reference standards and compounds in our in-home library. Quantification was performed using calibration curves established for the corresponding primary anthocyanins in the simulated wine solution. The simulated solution was prepared as an aqueous solution containing 14% (v/v) ethanol, 7 g/L tartaric acid, and 4 g/L glucose, with the pH adjusted to 3.3. The phenolic standard was dissolved and subjected to a two-fold serial dilution, resulting in a total of 10 concentration gradients. This dilution series could cover the concentration range of phenolic compounds in the wine samples.

Non-anthocyanin phenolics

MS was operated in negative electrospray ionization mode. Other conditions were identical to 2.4.1.

Anthocyanin derivatives

Analysis of anthocyanin derivatives was conducted according to our previously reported method (X.-K. Zhang et al., 2021). The chromatographic conditions were gradient elution with an injection volume of 10 μL. Mobile phase B was 0% for 1 min, from 0% to 25% for 2 min, from 25% to 30% for 12 min, and 100% for 5 min. The concentration of anthocyanin derivatives was calculated and expressed as malvidin-3-O-glucoside equivalents. Other conditions were identical to 2.4.1.

Volatile compounds analysis

According to our previously reported method (Tong et al., 2023), headspace solid-phase microextraction combined with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) was used to determine volatile compounds in wine. The HS-SPME was performed using a CTC CombiPAL autosampler (CTC Analytics, Zwingen, Switzerland) equipped with a 2 cm DVB/CAR/PDMS (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA., USA; 50/30 μm). A 5 mL wine sample was placed in a 20 mL vial with 1.5 g NaCl and 10 μL 4-methyl-2-pentanol (internal standard, 1.0086 g/L). The vial was capped with a PTFE‑silicone septum. The sample was equilibrated at 40 °C with 500 rpm agitation for 30 min, followed by extraction for 30 min. Subsequently, the fiber was desorbed in the GC injector for 8 min.

GC–MS (7890 A-5975C, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was equipped with an HP-INNOWAX column (60 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm, J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). A 5:1 split injection was used with the injector temperature at 250 °C. The carrier gas was helium at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The temperature program: 50 °C held for 1 min, increased to 220 °C at 3 °C/min, and held at 220 °C for 5 min. Mass spectrometry used an electron impact ionization mode. The ion source temperature was 230 °C, the quadrupole temperature was 150 °C, and the acquisition range was m/z 29–350.

Identification was verified by matching mass spectra with standards and the NIST 2011 library, supplemented by calculating retention indices. Quantification was performed using calibration curves of volatile compound standards in simulated wine, as described in 2.4.1.

Color measurements

CIELab color parameters (lightness L*, red/green a*, yellow/blue b*) were analyzed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) (Ayala et al., 1997). Wine samples were filtered through 0.22 μm polyethersulfone membranes (Jinteng Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., Tian, China). Using distilled water as the blank reference, absorbance values at 440 nm, 530 nm, and 600 nm were measured in a 2 mm optical path length glass cuvette.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted using the quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) method (Stone et al., 1974). For this study, 20 individuals with wine tasting experience (15 females and 5 males, aged 22–32 years) were recruited from the Center for Viticulture and Enology (CFVE) at China Agricultural University to participate in sensory training. All assessors, including the experts, provided written informed consent prior to participation in accordance with the principles of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for this study involving human subjects was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University (Approval No. CAUHR-20231103).

Prior to the formal tasting, the recruited individuals underwent a total of 32 h (16 sessions) of systematic sensory training based on the GB/T 46555–2025 standard. The training content covered learning descriptor development, familiarization with reference standards, intensity calibration, and pre-experimental evaluations. Six experts conducted a preliminary evaluation to determine descriptor for 6 aroma attributes (red berry, black berry, flowery, herbaceous, cooked fruit, and overall aroma) and 4 palate attributes (astringency, acidity, bitterness, and smoothness), and selected representative samples as training references. The members underwent descriptor discrimination and screening training using reference standard solutions (Supplementary Table 1), followed by standard intensity training using reference standard solutions with dilution gradients and untreated Cabernet Sauvignon wine samples from the control group. An 11-point scale was utilized (0–10, where 0 = “absent”, 1 = “barely perceptible”, 3 = “perceptible”, 5 = “directly identifiable”, 7 = “relatively prominent”, and 9 = “extremely intense”), and consensus was reached through open discussion. Finally, the members performed pre-evaluations on representative training samples over three consecutive days. A three-way ANOVA was conducted using PanelCheck v1.4.2 software (https://www.panelcheck.com) to determine if significant differences existed among the members and across rounds (p > 0.05). The sensitivity, stability, and scoring consistency of the members were also evaluated. Ultimately, 16 panelists (12 females and 4 males, aged 22–32 years) were selected to participate in the formal experiments.

Before each formal tasting session, two significantly different wine samples from the training set were selected as references for scoring and discussion to ensure a unified evaluation scale. The formal tasting procedure was identical to the training. Specifically, 30 mL of each wine sample was poured into transparent ISO tasting glasses in a standardized tasting booth and presented to the assessors in a randomized order to eliminate systematic errors caused by the sample sequence. Assessors used water and unflavored soda crackers as palate cleansers to maintain their oral condition. Finally, PanelCheck software was used again to evaluate and calibrate the data of each assessor, excluding data from panelists demonstrating poor reproducibility for the same sample.

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA and Duncan's multiple comparison test (p < 0.05) were performed using SPSS 24.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). The graphs were drawn using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc).

Results and discussion

Conductivity response

The conductivity change (Δχ) of all wine samples, including the untreated control (CK), was below 30 μS/cm. The lowest Δχ values were observed for PF-A2, PF-A3, NF-A2, and NF-A3, followed by PF-A1 and NF-A1 (Fig. 1). Previous research indicated that a conductivity change of less than 50 μS/cm before and after stabilizer addition indicates stability, while a change below 30 μS/cm signifies a very stable wine (Bosso et al., 2016). As dedicated CaT stability testing was not included in this study, the conductivity results should be interpreted strictly in relation to KHT stability. Accordingly,the untreated control wine already exhibited a low residual potassium bitartrate (KHT) crystallization tendency. Within this already stable wine matrix, the lower Δχ values observed at the higher KPA addition rates (45 and 55 mL/hL) indicate relatively lower residual KHT crystallization tendencies. This finding aligns with previous research, which observed a progressive decrease in Δχ with KPA additions of 10, 20, 50, and 100 mL/hL. Only at the excessive dosage of 100 mL/L, a negative effect on the colloidal balance of the wine was observed, resulting in a significant increase in turbidity (Bosso, Motta, Panero, Petrozziello, et al., 2020).

Different letters represent significant differences in data between different treatments (Duncan, p < 0.05).

The NF-A and PF-A treatments exhibited lower Δχ values than the extended cold-treatment reference. However, because the untreated control wine already showed a low Δχ value, these differences should be interpreted only as indicating that KPA treatment resulted in a relatively lower residual KHT crystallization tendency than the extended cold treatment within an already stable wine matrix. Previous studies have also shown that KPA has certain advantages (Bosso, Motta, Panero, Lucini, et al., 2020; Canuti et al., 2019; Eder et al., 2019). Studies have shown that MTA, CMC, and KPA can all effectively inhibit KHT crystallization, although they differ in their duration of action, sensitivity to storage temperature, and applicability to different wine matrices. In particular, KPA has demonstrated persistent efficacy under elevated temperatures and prolonged storage conditions (Bosso, Motta, Panero, Lucini, et al., 2020). Only KPA consistently prevented tartrate precipitation across all tested wine varieties, even at half the recommended dosage (Eder et al., 2019), or under demanding storage conditions such as elevated storage temperatures and prolonged aging (40 °C for 45 days) (Canuti et al., 2019). The precise mechanism by which KPA inhibits KHT crystallization in wine has not yet been fully elucidated. It may be associated with the previously reported metal-chelating capacity of polyaspartate, whereby adjacent carboxyl groups and the lone pair electrons from the nitrogen (CONH) in the polymer can bind metal ions (Adelnia et al., 2023). One possible explanation is that such metal chelation may interfere with KHT nucleation or crystal growth. The strong KHT-stabilizing effect of KPA should not be extrapolated as being equally effective for CaT stability. Although the specific calcium concentration of the studied Cabernet Sauvignon wine was not directly measured in this work, Cabernet Sauvignon red wines generally exhibit relatively low calcium levels (75–80 mg/L) compared with certain white wines (Laurie et al., 2026). From a practical standpoint, CaT instability is particularly difficult to control due to its prolonged and unpredictable induction period, where precipitation may only emerge months after bottling or even after reaching the market (Mckinnon et al., 1995). Furthermore, CaT instability is governed not only by calcium concentration but also by factors such as pH, tartaric acid and its ionic species, ethanol content, organic acid composition, and the wine colloidal matrix (Cataldo et al., 2026). Previous studies have demonstrated that KPA cannot reliably eliminate the risk of CaT precipitation in wines with elevated calcium levels. Moreover, the precipitation of CaT can act as a physical nucleus, disrupting the thermodynamic equilibrium of the colloidal system and triggering the co-precipitation of previously stabilized KHT (Philipp, Strauss, Schlögl, et al., 2022). Therefore, for wines with elevated calcium concentrations or a potential risk of exogenous calcium contamination, CaT risk should be independently evaluated, and necessary enological measures should be implemented. Such measures include delayed bottling to allow natural CaT to partially precipitate before adding KPA, the staggered addition of KPA (Philipp, Strauss, Schlögl, et al., 2022), or alternative strategies like cation exchange resins, electrodialysis, or sodium alginate (Laurie et al., 2026).

Although the combination of cold stabilization and stabilizer addition represents a novel direction in tartrate stabilization techniques (Cui et al., 2024), the group PF-A and the group NF-A with equivalent KPA concentration showed no significant difference in Δχ values (Fig. 1), indicating that 48 h of pre-freezing did not produce additional improvement in KHT stability based on conductivity evaluation. Furthermore, because CaT exhibits slow crystallization kinetics, typically requiring weeks or even months to begin precipitating (Philipp, Strauss, Schlögl, et al., 2022), the 48 h pre-freezing treatment was fundamentally insufficient to induce CaT stabilization. This further confirms that the performance of KPA in the combined pre-freezing process was primarily attributed to its inhibitory effect on KHT. In fact, the pre-freezing and filtration processes might alter certain wine matrix components that contribute less to total conductivity, such as macromolecular cold colloids (formed through the aggregation of pigments, proteins, polysaccharides, tannins, and so on) (Prusisz et al., 2008). Therefore, in the NF-A treatment, KPA acts directly on the intact original colloidal matrix; whereas in the PF-A treatment, KPA is added to a partially altered wine matrix. This matrix difference caused by the processing methods may provide a physicochemical basis for the subsequent differences observed between the two treatments regarding phenolic and volatile compounds.

Phenolic composition

To comprehensively investigate the impact of KPA on phenolic compounds in wine, monomeric anthocyanins and their derivatives, as well as non-anthocyanic phenolics, were detected. Before and after bottle aging, a total of 22 anthocyanic phenolics were quantified (Table 1), including 5 primary anthocyanins, 5 corresponding acetylated anthocyanins, 5 corresponding coumaroylated anthocyanins, and 7 anthocyanin derivatives. Additionally, 34 non-anthocyanic phenolics were quantified (Table 2), including 9 flavan-3-ols, 16 flavonols, 6 hydroxybenzoic acids, and 3 hydroxycinnamic acids.

Principal component analysis

At 0 M of bottle storage, the first two principal components explained 81.4% of the variance among the sample set, with specific clustering observed according to the different experimental treatments. The KPA supplementation groups (group NF-A and PF-A) were clustered on the right side of the plot. Group PF-A was concentrated in the upper-right quadrant, while group NF-A was distributed in the lower-right quadrant. In contrast, the control and extended cold-treatment reference groups (CK, Cold-T) occupied the left side (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, direct flavanol-anthocyanin condensation product (F-A) was grouped on the right side of the plot, while nonacylated anthocyanins, acetylated anthocyanins, coumaroylated anthocyanins, flavanyl-pyranoanthocyanins (A-v-F), and pinotin clustered in the upper-right quadrant. In contrast, anthocyanin ethyl-linked flavanols (A-e-F) were located on the left side of the plot (Fig. 2C). This indicates that KPA addition was associated with higher direct flavanol-anthocyanin condensation product (F-A) levels and lower anthocyanin ethyl-linked flavanols (A-e-F) levels. Furthermore, combined pre-freezing and KPA showed strong correlations with higher flavanyl-pyranoanthocyanins (A-v-F), pinotin, and various monomeric anthocyanins. A possible mechanism underlying these treatment-related associations might result from interactions between the carboxylate-rich KPA chain and multivalent metal ions, which may modify the oxidative environment of wine pigments (Vernhet et al., 1999). This hypothesis still needs to be verified in the future by measuring metal ion concentrations and their binding behavior.

During 6 months of bottle storage, 71.5% of the variance among the sample set could be explained by the first two PCs. Clustering according to the different experimental treatments was also observed. These loading patterns indicate treatment-related differences in phenolic composition. The extended cold-treatment reference (Cold-T) was more closely associated with flavanyl-pyranoanthocyanins (A-v-F), anthocyanin ethyl-linked flavanols (A-e-F), and hydroxycinnamic acids, whereas the combined pre-freezing and KPA treatments were more closely associated with monomeric anthocyanins, direct flavanol-anthocyanin condensation products (F-A), pinotins, and flavanols.

Anthocyanin-related phenolic compounds

Table 1 shows the composition and content of anthocyanin phenolic compounds in wines subjected to various treatments before and after bottle storage for 6 months. The monomeric anthocyanins and anthocyanin derivatives provide insights into the color characteristics of wines. In Cold-T, all types of monomeric anthocyanins were consistently significantly lower than the control group (CK), both before and after bottle storage. The lower monomeric anthocyanin concentrations in Cold-T may be related to anthocyanin adsorption onto and coprecipitation with tartrate crystals during the extended cold treatment. As previous research detected in tartrate crystals after cold stabilization, anthocyanins accounted for 0.2%–0.3% of their composition (Vernhet et al., 1999). The total content of anthocyanin derivatives in Cold-T remained slightly higher than that of the control group both at 0 M and 6 M. Previous studies have reported that low-temperature stabilization may increase dissolved oxygen levels in wine (Cant, 1960). Accordingly, the slightly higher anthocyanin derivative content observed in Cold-T may be associated with differences in oxidative exposure during the extended cold treatment (X.-K. Zhang et al., 2021).

Within the NF-A group, only the NF-A3 exhibited a significantly higher total monomeric anthocyanin content than the control group (CK) at 0 M, and the contents of nonacylated anthocyanins, acetylated anthocyanins, and coumaroylated anthocyanins were all relatively high. After 6 months, the total monomeric anthocyanin content in all NF-A groups was lower than that of the control group. Previous studies similarly reported unchanged (Bosso, Motta, Panero, Lucini, et al., 2020) or reduced (Martínez-Pérez et al., 2020) total anthocyanins after KPA addition for 6 months. Regarding anthocyanin derivatives, NF-A3 was significantly higher than the control at 0 M, while both NF-A2 and NF-A3 were significantly higher than the control after bottle storage for 6 months. This is primarily attributed to vitisin. In summary, within the individual KPA addition treatment, the treatment concentration of 45–55 mL/hL (NF-A2 and NF-A3) showed the optimal performance for wine anthocyanin phenolics. Although it had a slightly negative impact on the retention of monomeric anthocyanins post-bottling, it enhanced the content of anthocyanin derivatives. A previous study also observed that a KPA-type tartrate stabilizer (containing potassium polyaspartate, gum arabic, and approximately 0.4% sulfur dioxide) had a significantly negative effect on anthocyanins but a less pronounced effect on their derivatives (Wang et al., 2025). Notably, there is some similarity between our results using high-purity KPA and the findings of Wang et al. (2025), as both observed lower monomeric anthocyanin contents in the treatment groups compared to the control group. This indicates that this phenomenon is primarily driven by KPA itself or the stabilization process, rather than being a side effect induced by the gum arabic additive.

In fact, during wine aging and storage, monomeric anthocyanins can react covalently with other substances, particularly phenolics, to form more stable anthocyanin derivatives with unique colors (X.-K. Zhang et al., 2021). One possible mechanism is that the polyaspartic acid chains, rich in carboxylate groups, can partially chelate multivalent metal ions involved in wine oxidation reactions (Adelnia et al., 2023). Consequently, the oxidative degradation of monomeric anthocyanins might be somewhat impeded, allowing them to continue reacting with substances like phenolics to form anthocyanin derivatives. Therefore, the decrease in monomeric anthocyanin content alongside the increase in derivatives such as vitisin likely reflects a redistribution of pigments from monomeric anthocyanin forms to anthocyanin derivative forms. Nevertheless, this hypothesis requires further verification through future measurements of metal binding, oxidative status, and pigment-conversion kinetics.

In the PF-A group, at 0 M, the content of all types of monomeric anthocyanins was significantly higher than or equal to that in the control group (CK), with PF-A1 and PF-A2 exhibiting the highest total content. After 6 months, only some monomeric anthocyanins in PF-A3 were lower than those of the control group. Regarding anthocyanin derivatives, at 0 M, all the PF-A groups were not lower than the control group. 6 months later, the content of anthocyanin derivatives in PF-A1 and PF-A2, particularly vitisin, was significantly higher than that of the control group. Vitisin in wine primarily originates from monomeric anthocyanins reacting with pyruvic acid to form vitisin A or with acetaldehyde to form vitisin B (De Freitas & Mateus, 2011). This may be attributed to either low-temperature processing conditions or the metal chelation between potassium ions (K+) in KPA and anthocyanin derivatives, effectively stabilizing the compound (Enaru et al., 2021). In summary, within the process, combined pre-freezing and KPA addition, the optimal treatment concentrations were 35 mL/hL (PF-A1) and 45 mL/hL (PF-A2). They promoted the retention of monomeric anthocyanins and increased the content of anthocyanin derivatives after bottle storage.

Non-anthocyanin phenolic compounds

This study further analyzed non-anthocyanin phenolic compounds (Table 2), including flavanols, flavonols, and phenolic acids, which can also act as co-pigments contributing to wine color formation. The Cold-T group showed no significant change at 0 M. However, after bottle storage, the content of non-anthocyanin phenolics, particularly flavanols and flavonols, was significantly lower than that of the control group (CK). Previous research similarly found that after cold stabilization treatment exceeding 4 days, the concentration of flavonols was only about 75% of the initial level (Wang et al., 2025).

In the NF-A group, the contents of flavanols, flavonols, hydroxybenzoic acids, and hydroxycinnamic acids were significantly lower than those in the control group (CK) both before and after bottle storage, with NF-A3 showing the smallest loss. At 0 M, the non-anthocyanin phenolic content in the NF-A group was approximately 80% of the control group, while at 6 M it was about 95%. Previous research similarly found that after short-term treatment (6 d) with a KPA mixed stabilizer, hydroxybenzoic acids, hydroxycinnamic acids, and flavanols in wine suffered substantial losses, whereas flavonols were relatively unaffected (Wang et al., 2025). Our treatment with pure KPA also resulted in a reduction of non-anthocyanin phenolic compounds, indicating that the interaction with the specific non-anthocyanin phenolics is an inherent characteristic of KPA itself.

In the PF-A group, only PF-A3 showed no significant difference in total non-anthocyanin phenolic content compared to the control group (CK) at 0 M. In PF-A1, the contents of almost all types of non-anthocyanin phenolics were significantly lower than those in the control group. PF-A2 exhibited lower levels of hydroxybenzoic acids and hydroxycinnamic acids. During aging, hydroxycinnamic acids can serve as substrates for derivative formation reactions. For example, pinotins are generated from anthocyanins and hydroxycinnamic acids, while Portisins (B type) are formed from anthocyanins, pyruvic acid, and hydroxycinnamic acids (Quaglieri et al., 2017). This mechanism might concurrently explain the decrease in phenolic acid content and the increase in certain anthocyanin derivatives. After bottle storage for 6 months, the flavonol content in PF-A2 and PF-A3 was significantly higher than that of the control group. This increase was the contribution of myricetin-3-O-glucoside, quercetin-3-O-galactoside, quercetin-3-O-glucoside, isorhamnetin-3-O-glucoside, and syringetin. It demonstrated that the combined application of pre-freezing and KPA facilitated the retention of flavonoids, aligning with findings from Bosso, Motta, Panero, Lucini, and Guaita (2020).

Volatile compounds

To comprehensively analyze the impact of KPA on wine aroma, a total of 68 volatile compounds were detected before and after bottle storage (Supplementary Table 2). These included 28 esters (10 ethyl esters of straight-chain fatty acids, 4 higher alcohol acetates, 4 ethyl esters of branched acids, and 10 other esters), 19 higher alcohols, 4 terpenes and norisoprenoids, 7 medium- and short-chain fatty acids, 6 aldehydes and ketones, and 4 other volatile compounds. Among them, some esters (including straight-chain fatty acid ethyl esters, higher alcohol acetates, and ethyl esters of branched acids), terpenes and norisoprenoids, medium- and short-chain fatty acids, as well as aldehydes and ketones among the different experimental treatments showed significant differences compared to the control group.

Principal component analysis

Prior to bottle aging, 68% of the variance among the sample sets could be explained by the first two PCs, with observable clustering according to the different experimental treatments. CK was located on the left side of the plot, Cold-T was positioned in the upper middle region, while the PF-A and NF-A groups were predominantly clustered on the right side (Fig. 3A). Terpenes and lactones were grouped on the right side of the plot (Fig. 3C). This indicates that, compared to the control group, the addition of KPA, particularly when combined with the pre-freezing process, resulted in wine samples exhibiting stronger correlations with volatile compounds such as terpenes and lactones. In red wine, there is limited direct research on the influence of KPA addition on the content of volatile compounds. In white wine, KPA could significantly reduce unstable proteins in wine, thereby indirectly supporting aroma stability by preventing protein-related haze and instability that could otherwise affect the aromatic profile (Quaglieri et al., 2017).

Esters

Esters are key contributors to the freshly fermented fruit aroma of wine, typically categorized into ethyl esters of straight-chain fatty acids, higher alcohol acetates, ethyl esters of branched acids, and other esters.

At 0 M, the total content of ethyl esters of straight-chain fatty acids in the extended cold-treatment reference (Cold-T) and KPA addition treatment (PF-A and NF-A groups) showed no significant difference compared to the control group (CK) (Fig. 4A). This is because ethyl acetate, which accounts for over 99% of the total ethyl esters of straight-chain fatty acids, did not show a significant difference in its content across the various experimental groups. Although the contents of other ethyl esters of straight-chain fatty acids were very low, they still contribute to the aroma. Because even sub- or perithreshold esters can significantly affect the overall odour intensity of red wine (de-la-Fuente-Blanco et al., 2020). At 0 M, the contents of ethyl butanoate, ethyl hexanoate, and ethyl octanoate in the KPA-added groups (PF-A and NF-A groups) were significantly lower than those in the control group (CK) (Supplementary Table 2). After 6 M of bottle storage, the contents of ethyl esters of straight-chain fatty acids in PF-A2, PF-A3, NF-A1, and NF-A2 were lower than those in the control group (Fig. 4A). Similarly, Natolino et al. (2023) also observed a reduction in esters due to KPA treatment.

Different uppercase or lowercase letters in the figure indicate significant differences among treatment groups (Duncan, p < 0.05).

Before and after bottle storage, the extended cold-treatment reference (Cold-T) and KPA treatment alone (NF-A group) did not significantly affect the content of higher alcohol acetates. In contrast, the combined treatment of pre-freezing and KPA (PF-A group) resulted in a significantly lower content of higher alcohol acetates compared to the control group (CK) (Fig. 4B), which was primarily attributed to the lower contents of isoamyl acetate and isobutyl acetate (Supplementary Table 2). The differences among the various treatment groups may be attributed to whether cold treatment was applied and the duration of treatment. For example, Xia et al. (2022) observed that short-term cold stabilization (10 d) led to a significantly lower isoamyl acetate content in wine before bottle storage compared to the control, with no significant difference after bottle storage for 6 months. In contrast, long-term cold stabilization (15 d) had no effect on isoamyl acetate either before or after bottle storage for 6 months.

For ethyl esters of branched acids, the KPA-added groups (PF-A and NF-A groups) were significantly lower than the control group (CK) both before and after bottle storage, and the negative impact was more pronounced in the KPA treatment combined with pre-freezing (PF-A group) (Fig. 4C). This was primarily due to the lower contents of ethyl 3-methylpentanoate, ethyl 3-methylbutanoate, and ethyl 2-methylbutanoate (Supplementary Table 2). Only the extended cold-treatment reference (Cold-T) showed no significant difference in the ethyl esters of branched acids content compared to the control group.

Terpenes and norisoprenoids

Before and after bottle storage, 2 norisoprenoids were detected, including β-damascenone and β-ionone. These compounds are characterized by extremely low thresholds and intense floral and fruity aromas, representing one of the core varietal aromas of Cabernet Sauvignon (Falcão et al., 2008), the wine sample used in this study. Additionally, 2 terpenes were detected, including linalool and α-terpineol, which also contribute potentially to the aroma profile (Mele et al., 2021).

At 0 M, the contents of terpenes and norisoprenoids in nearly all treatment groups were significantly higher than those in the control group (CK). After 6 M, only the individual KPA treatment (NF-A group) maintained this trend, with the highest levels observed at a concentration of 35 mL/hL (NF-A1) (Fig. 4D). This was mainly attributed to the higher content of β-damascenone (Supplementary Table 2).

Proteins and polysaccharides can retain volatile compounds such as norisoprenoids and fatty acids within the wine matrix through non-covalent interactions like hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces, and hydrogen bonding (Jones-Moore et al., 2022). Furthermore, studies on protein-containing food and beverage systems indicate that protein addition generally reduces aroma release intensity and prolongs the retention of volatile compounds in the matrix (Barallat-Pérez et al., 2024). Conversely, under wine pH conditions, KPA contains multiple negatively charged carboxylate groups that can interact with unstable proteins and other colloidal components, promoting their aggregation or precipitation (Natolino et al., 2023). One possible explanation is that this could reduce the macromolecular binding sites for aroma substances, thereby increasing the gas-liquid partition coefficients of volatile compounds (Terta et al., 2006). Therefore, the elevated measured concentrations of certain norisoprenoids and fatty acids likely stem from their release from the wine matrix and improved analytical availability, rather than KPA inducing the formation of new aroma compounds.

Medium- and short-chain fatty acids

Medium- and short-chain fatty acids also contribute significantly to the aroma profile of wine. Among them, branched-chain fatty acids (e.g., isobutanoic acid) imparted odors described as rancid or fatty, while medium-chain fatty acids (e.g., hexanoic, octanoic, and decanoic acids) exhibited cheesy or sweaty notes (Francis & Newton, 2005).

At 0 M, 3 medium- and short-chain fatty acids were detected in the wine samples, including isobutanoic acid, hexanoic acid, and 1-decanoic acid. The content of medium- and short-chain fatty acids in the Cold-T group was significantly lower than that in the control group (CK), whereas both the PF-A and NF-A groups showed higher levels than the control. After bottle storage for 6 months, the variety of medium- and short-chain fatty acids increased, with a total of 6 types detected, including acetic acid, butyric acid, 2-methylbutyric acid, hexanoic acid, octanoic acid, and 1-decanoic acid were detected. Only the NF-A group maintained a higher content of medium- and short-chain fatty acids compared to the control, with NF-A1 showing the highest level (Fig. 4E). This indicates that the addition of KPA alone, particularly at 35 mL/hL, can result in a higher content of medium- and short-chain fatty acids. However, this finding is not entirely consistent with previous research. Natolino et al. (2023) observed no significant fatty acid changes in Cortese and Lugana wines after 100 mg/L KPA treatment at 90 d. This discrepancy may be due to differences in the initial chemical composition of the wine samples and the storage duration.

Aldehydes and ketones

Aldehyde and ketone compounds, including furfural and furfuryl alcohol, are considered important contributors to the roasty aroma notes in wine, imparting characteristics such as toasted, baked, caramel, and nutty scents (Dumitriu (Gabur et al., 2019).

At 0 M, 3 aldehyde and ketone compounds were detected in the wine, namely difurfuryl ether, furfural, and furfuryl alcohol. The furfural content in all treatment groups was significantly higher than that in the control group (Supplementary Table 2). Because furfural has been described as an oxidation marker in wines not aged in oak barrels (Escudero et al., 2002), its higher concentration could indicate differences in oxidative exposure among the treatments. One possible mechanism is that wine handling during the extended cold treatment and KPA dispersion might introduce dissolved oxygen and might result in oxidative reactions. Fenton-type reactions represent one possible pathway involved in wine oxidation (Han, 2015), and it will be possible to determine whether the mechanism in this study involves this pathway by measuring relevant parameters in the future. From a practical winemaking perspective, inert-gas protection, such as CO2 or a CO2/Ar gas mixture, may be considered to limit oxygen exposure during stabilizer addition (Del Barrio-Galán et al., 2023). After 6 M, 6 aldehyde and ketone compounds were detected, including 3 newly emerged compounds (3-furaldehyde, octanal, and 2-methylcyclopentanone). Only the treatment with 45 mL/hL KPA (NF-A2) showed a higher content of aldehyde and ketone compounds compared to the control group (CK) (Fig. 4F), primarily due to its higher level of furfuryl alcohol.

Color parameters

Compared to the control group (CK), the Cold-T group exhibited a significantly lower L* value and a significantly higher a* value at 0 M. This trend persisted after bottle storage for 6 months, with the b* value of the Cold-T group also being significantly lower than that of the control group (Fig. 5). This indicates that the extended cold-treatment reference was associated with lower brightness and higher red hue, and slowed down the yellowing of wine during bottle storage. This variation in apparent color can be reasonably explained by the content of phenolic compounds, which is associated with the higher levels of anthocyanin derivatives in the Cold-T group compared to the control group. Because anthocyanin derivatives can contribute up to 50% of wine color after prolonged aging (X.-K. Zhang et al., 2021).

Compared with the control group (CK), the NF-A group showed significantly lower L* and b* values and a significantly higher a* value at 0 M. After 6 M of bottle storage, the same trends for L* and a* values were maintained, while the b* value in the NF-A group were higher than that in the control group (Fig. 5). A lower L* value indicates higher color intensity (CI). This suggests that KPA treatment had a positive effect on wine color, resulting in stronger color intensity and higher redness. Martínez-Pérez et al. (2020) similarly observed that the combination of potassium polyaspartate and gum arabic increased the CI of red wine after both 6 and 12 months of storage. Notably, our study demonstrated that the use of KPA alone can also significantly enhance the CI and red hue of red wine during aging. This comparison confirms that KPA itself plays a key role in color preservation and stabilization, independent of the presence of gum arabic. Bosso et al. (2015) also reported a decrease in the color hue (A420/A520) in some red wine samples after KPA addition, which indicated a shift toward redder tone. In the NF-A group, the addition of KPA at 45 mL/hL (NF-A2) yielded the lowest brightness (L*) and yellow hue (b*) and the highest red hue (a*) after bottle storage. The superior color performance of NF-A2 may be related to the higher content of anthocyanin derivatives, especially vitisin, as mentioned earlier. Compared with its precursor malvidin-3-O-glucoside, vitisin exhibited a shift of the maximum absorption peak in the visible spectrum toward shorter wavelengths, presenting an orange-red hue in the acidic medium of wine (Sun et al., 2020). Moreover, vitisin A has a higher molar extinction coefficient than malvidin-3-O-glucoside, thus contributing to a deeper color (He et al., 2010).

Both before and after bottle storage, the PF-A group exhibited significantly higher brightness (L* value) and yellow hue (b* value) than the control group (CK), while its red hue (a* value) was lower (Fig. 5). This indicates that the combined use of pre-freezing and KPA performed poorly in terms of color. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon remains unclear. There is scarcely any literature investigating the effect of combining KPA with the pre-freezing process on wine color.

Overall, the addition of KPA alone resulted in a wine with outstanding color intensity and red hue, with an optimal dosage of 45 mL/hL. This may be associated with the higher content of anthocyanin derivatives, particularly vitisin, in the wine samples after the addition of KPA alone treatment. Although the combination of pre-freezing and KPA performed well in terms of phenolic composition, specific process parameters still require further investigation to improve its color performance.

Sensory evaluation

The sensory characteristics of each treatment group were obtained by a trained evaluation panel, which evaluated the aroma (red berry, black berry, flowery, herbaceous, cooked fruit, and overall aroma) and mouthfeel parameters (astringency, acidity, bitterness, and smoothness) (Fig. 6).

CK: Control, no pre-freezing treatment or addition of KPA; Cold-T: Extended cold-treatment reference at −4 °C for 30 d; PF-A1, PF-A2, PF-A3: Pre-freezing for 48 h with KPA addition at concentrations of 35, 45, 55 mL/hL; NF-A1, NF-A2, NF-A3: KPA addition without pre-freezing at concentrations of 35, 45, 55 mL/hL. Different letters represent significant differences in data between different treatments, and ns indicates that there were no significant differences among all groups. (Duncan, p < 0.05).

At 0 M, Cold-T and PF-A1 achieved the highest overall aroma scores. Cold-T exhibited the most prominent cooked fruit aroma, followed by KPA-added groups (PF-A and NF-A), while the control group (CK) displayed the weakest cooked fruit aroma (Fig. 6A). Lactones, furanones, furaneol, and certain ketones are the main chemical groups correlated with the intensity of cooked fruit aromas in Cabernet Sauvignon wines (X. Zhang et al., 2023). Therefore, the enhanced cooked fruit aroma in treatment groups (Cold-T, PF-A, and NF-A) might derive from higher contents of whisky lactones, β-damascenone, and furfuryl alcohol. Compared to CK, nearly all treatments (group Cold-T, PF-A, and NF-A) enhanced astringency (Fig. 6A). Astringency primarily results from interactions between wine polyphenols (notably tannins) and salivary proteins, leading to protein precipitation and reduced oral lubrication (X. Zhang et al., 2023). Therefore, the more pronounced astringency in the treated groups (group Cold-T, PF-A, and NF-A) may be attributed to the preservation of substances such as catechin and epicatechin-3-gallate.

At 6 M, there were no significant differences among all the treatment groups and the control group (Fig. 6B). Martínez-Pérez et al. (2020) similarly found that KPA addition had no negative impact on sensory characteristics. It is not contradictory that the analytical differences in volatile compounds observed in this study ultimately manifested as only minor sensory differences. The sensory contribution of an individual volatile compounds depends partly on its concentration relative to its odour threshold and can be preliminarily assessed using its odour activity value (OAV). Compounds present below or close to their odour thresholds may have limited sensory effects, even when their concentrations differ significantly among treatments. However, OAVs alone cannot fully predict wine aroma because sensory perception is also influenced by the wine matrix and interactions among volatile compounds (Cosme et al., 2025). Non-volatile wine components, particularly high-molecular-weight polymers, can interact with odorants and alter their headspace release and sensory expression (Gabler et al., 2023). Moreover, synergistic, masking, or suppressive interactions among aroma compounds may further weaken the direct correspondence between analytical concentrations and sensory responses (Li et al., 2025).

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the effects of KPA, applied at commercially relevant dosage levels either alone or in combination with 48 h of pre-freezing, on phenolic compounds, volatile compounds, color, and sensory quality in Cabernet Sauvignon wine. In conclusion, under the conditions of this study, KPA treatments produced significant changes in the color and phenolic composition of the Cabernet Sauvignon wine examined. Treatment-dependent differences were also observed in some volatile compounds, whereas the sensory aroma was only slightly affected. Specifically, the alone addition of KPA at a concentration of 45 mL/hL produced the optimal color effect, yielding a pronounced red hue after bottle storage for 6 months, attributable to its higher vitisin content. The alone addition of KPA (45–55 mL/hL) also exhibited higher contents of norisoprenoids, carbonyl compounds, and medium- and short-chain fatty acids, while showing no significant effect on the sensory aroma profile. The combined use of 35–45 mL/hL KPA and pre-freezing processing preserved phenolic compounds such as monomeric anthocyanins, anthocyanin derivatives, flavanols, and flavanones, without making a significant contribution to volatile compounds or aroma characteristics.

Current research on KPA in enology primarily focuses on tartaric stabilization efficacy. This study provides the first comprehensive investigation into the effects of KPA on the flavor compounds and sensory quality of a Cabernet Sauvignon wine. However, the effects of KPA on residual KHT crystallization tendency and its impact on wine quality may vary depending on the specific wine composition, including colloidal characteristics, pH, tartaric acid and potassium levels, as well as concentrations of calcium and other metal ions, among other factors. Future research should include wines from diverse grape varieties, regions, vintages, and matrix compositions with different initial degrees of KHT instability, together with cold-stabilization procedures that more closely reflect industrial practice and extended storage durations, to further validate these findings.
