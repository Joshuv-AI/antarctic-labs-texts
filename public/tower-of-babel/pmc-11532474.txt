Strong association between high burden of soil-transmitted helminthiasis and household transmission among Malaysian Negritos, urging anthelmintic treatment beyond children
Authors: Shezryna Shahrizal, Mohd Shafiq Aazmi, Yvonne Ai Lian Lim, Norfatini Sajidah Rozani, Yi Xian Er, Mohd Yusri Idorus, Azdayanti Muslim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Orang Asli (OA), Malaysia’s indigenous minority, continue to experience ongoing soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections despite efforts including the Resettlement Programme (RPS). This study explores STH epidemiology and associated risks of moderate-to-heavy infections among 259 indigenous Negritos across four states in Peninsular Malaysia. Stool samples, anthropometrics, haemoglobin levels (finger-prick) and sociodemographic and environmental-sanitation factors via pretested questionnaires were collected. Parasitological examinations (direct faecal smear, formol-ether concentration, and Kato-Katz techniques) were thoroughly performed. The study revealed a 63.3% overall STH prevalence, with Trichuris trichiura (57.9%) being the most common, followed by Ascaris lumbricoides (35.5%) and hookworm (8.9%). Moderate-to-heavy infections were observed in 39.3% for T. trichiura and 48.9% for A. lumbricoides, with mixed infections of these two species being the most prevalent (50.6%). The risk for moderate-to-heavy infection increased significantly if other household members were infected (P < 0.001). Adults and adolescents also showed high infection rates, with no significant differences with children’s groups. These findings emphasize the need for anthelmintic treatment across all age groups and strategize local policies targeting older groups of the OA community to control ongoing transmission and reduce the STH burden and protect the future well-being of OA children, who deserve a healthier future.Keywords: Soil-transmitted helminths, Orang Asli, Intensity, Moderate- to- heavy infection, Risk factorsSubject terms: Microbiology, Diseases, Risk factors

Introduction

Roundworms (Ascaris lumbricoides), whipworms (Trichuris trichiura), and hookworms (Ancylostoma duodenale and Necator americanus) collectively constitute the soil-transmitted helminths (STH), a group of parasitic worms causing infections that persist as a formidable public health challenge. Globally, an estimated 1.5 billion vulnerable individuals or 24% of the world’s population, particularly children in rural areas of the developing world, are heavily affected by these conditions1. Classified among neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), these infections are prevalent among pre-school aged children, school-aged children, and women of reproductive age, all of whom require essential micronutrients. The long-term effects extend beyond impaired nutritional status and cognitive development in young children, including significant social and economic consequences such as reduced productivity and overall well-being2. Infections with A. lumbricoides and T. trichiura typically occur through the ingestion of contaminated food or water containing viable eggs of these helminths, while hookworm infections are mainly acquired through direct skin contact with contaminated soil, often from walking barefooted, as the infective filariform larvae can penetrate the skin.

In Malaysia, widespread STH infections persist, predominantly affecting the indigenous Orang Asli (OA) communities, with varying prevalence rates across different area populations. Previous reports include children and teenagers aged fifteen and below having a prevalence rate between 15–65% for A. lumbricoides, 29–97% for T. trichiura and 2.6–28% for hookworms3–5. In general, the OA constitute indigenous minority communities representing 0.8% of the population in Peninsular Malaysia. There are three major OA tribes in Peninsular Malaysia: Negrito, Proto-Malay and Senoi. As of 2023, the total OA population is 215 215, with the Negrito tribe accounting for 3.04% of the population, the lowest compared to Proto-Malays (Aboriginal Malay) (42.07%) and Senoi (54.89%)6.

Despite governmental initiatives aimed at enhancing socio-economic conditions through programmes like the Resettlement Programme (RPS) since 1978, the transition to modernization has not significantly reduced STH prevalence among the OA7,8. The contributing factors could be multifaceted, due to the lack of access to clean water and sanitation, poor hygiene behaviours (e.g. playing with soil, indiscriminate defaecation), young children, low socio-economic factors, belonging to large household members (≥ 8), and no previous history of deworming resulting in risk of acquiring STH infections3,8–10. Though studies have focused on such factors, the focus on studying the risk of getting moderate- to- heavy infections are still lacking especially in the OA population where STH infections are generally common. It is important to consider such risks due to a few reasons including the impact they bring to the individual’s health, since the severity of STH infection is related to the worm burden11. It has also been reported that those with moderate- to- heavy A. lumbricoides infection were 1.85 times more likely to have malnutrition, in addition to anaemia and other nutrition disturbances2,12.

Here, we present findings on the current epidemiological patterns of STH infection, intensity, and associated risk factors, examining both overall infection and moderate and heavy STH intensities among Negritos in Peninsular Malaysia.

Results

Sociodemographic and characteristics of the Negrito participants

A total of 259 Negritos participated in this study, comprising 49.4% male (n = 128) and 50.6% female (n = 131) individuals, with ages ranging from 2 to 70 years old. Notably, the majority of respondents (32.0%, n = 83) were young children aged 2 to 6 years old. Lubuk Legong stands out with the highest number of adult participants (50.5%, n = 50) aged ≥ 19 years old. In terms of socio-economic status, the majority of participants (69.9%, n = 181), particularly those living in inland villages, had a monthly household income of ≤ RM600 (~ USD 126), which is considered hard-core poverty13. For environmental-sanitation, 59.1% (n = 153) of Negritos rely on untreated water for daily activities, with inland villages solely dependent on such sources, emphasizing the difficulties in accessing clean water in such areas. In the forest periphery, although treated piped water is available, 31.3% (n = 81) of people use both untreated and treated water, while only 9.7% (n = 25) use only treated water. Prevalence, intensity and polyparasitism status of STH infections and their association with selected variables

Out of the 259 participants, the overall prevalence of STH infections was 63.3%, with 164 Negritos found to be positive with at least one type of STH infection. The most common STH species was T. trichiura, affecting 57.9% (n = 150) of participants, followed by A. lumbricoides (35.5%; n = 92) and hookworm (8.9%; n = 23). T. trichiura infection was significantly higher (P = 0.03) in villages located on the forest periphery (62.4%) versus inland (48.2%).

In terms of severity, 39.3% and 48.9% of the participants were found with moderate- to- heavy intensity of T. trichiura and A. lumbricoides infections, respectively. Further analysis indicated that this higher intensity problem was more prevalent in inland villages, with significant findings for T. trichiura infection (P = 0.01). Notably, the intensity for all hookworm infections were classified as light. Regarding polyparasitism, more than half of the participants had mixed infections accounting for 56.1% of cases, with double infections of A. lumbricoides and T. trichiura being the most predominant (50.6%). While mixed infection was relatively common in inland areas (69.6%), we also found that half of the participants living in the forest periphery had mixed infections (50.8%). We then explored the infections and their associations with various selected variables, including socio-demographic factors, environmental sanitation, hygiene practices, nutritional status, and history of receiving AHT (Table 3). Our findings indicated that having other family members infected with STH (76.0%) was highly associated with the infection (P < 0.001). We also found that males, those with untreated or mixed water supplies, and individuals with close contact with soil were significantly associated with STH infections. There was no significant association between age groups and STH infections. Another interesting result showed that, despite the OA claiming they practiced good hand hygiene (washing hands after defecation and before eating), there was a higher percentage of STH infections among them compared to those who only sometimes washed their hands, although this difference was not statistically significant.

Factors associated with moderate- to- heavy intensity of T. trichiura and A. lumbricoides infections

Table 4 presents the potential risk factors associated with moderate- to- heavy T. trichiura infections. In univariate analysis, being male and having other family members infected with STH were found to have 2.2 times (P = 0.009) and 4.9 times (P = 0.006) greater odds of getting moderate- to- heavy infections, respectively. Additionally, being anaemic was associated with 2.2 times greater odds (P = 0.03) of moderate- to- heavy T. trichiura infection. However, further multivariate analysis identified being male, having other family members infected with STH, and low monthly household income as significant predictors for higher burden of T. trichiura infection.

Discussion

This study provides data on the epidemiology of soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections and examines the potential risk factors associated with moderate- to- heavy worm burdens among the Negrito tribe of Orang Asli (OA) in Peninsular Malaysia. Despite numerous public health efforts, STH infections remain endemic in the OA population. Therefore, it is crucial to monitor these infections to prevent them from reaching high severity levels, which are known to be associated with significant morbidity, including cognitive impairments among OA children.

In this study, the overall prevalence rate of STH was 63.3%, slightly lower than the 86.1% prevalence previously reported among the Negritos in Malaysia8. In comparison with other OA tribes, a prevalence of 96% was reported among the Temuan subtribe of the Proto-Malays14, and 62.1% prevalence among the Senoi population, including the Semai and Mah Meri subtribes15. Our findings, along with these previous studies, highlight that soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections remain prevalent and continue to pose a significant public health challenge within the OA community in Malaysia.

Trichuris trichiura was found to be the most dominant species in Malaysia, followed by A. lumbricoides, and hookworm. These findings are consistent with previous local studies, suggesting a persistent pattern of infection over the years where trichuriasis was found to be the most prevalent among the soil-transmitted helminths (range from 56.1% to 93.3%), followed by ascariasis (range from 11.9% to 48.8%) and hookworm infection (range from 7.4% to 20.2%)3,8,15–17. Similar dominant species were found in Ecuador12, but differ with the findings observed in Kenya, Indonesia, and India where ascariasis was found to be the most prevalent, followed by trichuriasis and hookworm infection9,10,18. These differences in prevalence patterns could be attributed to the lower efficacy of single dosage of albendazole treatment against T. trichiura and higher re-infection rate especially in forest periphery community19, apart from geographical landscape, socio-economic factors, health education and other practices that are unique to each studied population, contributing the outcome of STH infection.

We highlighted that a significant portion of the Negrito population experienced moderate- to- heavy worm burdens for T. trichiura (39.3%) and A. lumbricoides (48.9%) infections, while hookworm infections were primarily light. These findings are in line with previous reports that have documented similar intensity levels among Orang Asli, with moderate- to- heavy STH infections for T. trichiura ranging from 6.5%–88.0%, A. lumbricoides from 10%–50%, and hookworm infections from 1%–35.9%8,15,16. Interestingly, the moderate- to- heavy intensity of infection was higher in inland Negritos compared to forest-periphery villages, contradicting the findings from 20198. We postulate that this change is related to their transition to a more sedentary lifestyle, even while living in inland forested areas. We also found that having other family members infected with STH was not only highly associated with the infection (P < 0.001), but also increased the risk of moderate- to- heavy burden of both T. trichiura and A. lumbricoides infections. The result was further supported by the fact that no significant difference was discovered in infection intensity between age groups for T. trichiura infection. The focus of local efforts on controlling STH infection primarily by targeting preschool and school-aged children (particularly those below 12 years old) while neglecting adults and adolescents in the community may have contributed to this problem. This is concerning given its association with more severe health outcomes, such as malnutrition, anaemia, and impaired cognitive development12 as shown as well in our univariate analysis which indicated the association of anaemia with T. trichiura infection. It is well established that the morbidity caused by STH infections is proportional to the number of worms infecting the host20, making moderate and heavy worm burdens responsible for significantly greater morbidity as evaluated using disability-adjusted life years (DALY)21.

Additionally, having low income can have a significant impact, as it leads to families not having proper housing and basic amenities such as treated water, which could result in STH exposure from the environment. A similar finding was reported, where having low household income (< RM500, ~ USD106) was a significant predictor for T. trichiura infection in both the Negrito and Proto-Malay tribes3. We also found that males, those with untreated or mixed water supplies, and individuals with close contact with soil were significantly associated with STH infections. Interestingly, although the difference was not statistically significant, a higher percentage of STH infections was observed among OA individuals who claimed to practice good hand hygiene (washing hands after defecation and before eating) compared to those who indicated they only sometimes washed their hands. Although personal hygiene practices, such as regular hand washing and proper latrine usage have been shown to be inversely related to STH prevalence9,22, addressing these practices alone is insufficient. However, considering that 59.0% of the studied population heavily relies on untreated water sources, such as rivers, for daily use, it is challenging to reduce infection rates without addressing this root cause. The absence of latrines with proper sewage disposal forces these individuals to defecate in open fields, which triples the risk of soil contamination and subsequent contact with contaminated soil23.

In line with these concerns, the World Health Organization’s (WHO) 2021–2030 Neglected Tropical Diseases Roadmap aims to eradicate STH as a public health problem by specifically targeting to reduce moderate and heavy intensity infections to below 2% in 70% and 96% of endemic countries by 2025 and 2030, respectively24. To achieve this, the WHO suggests employing a combination of strategies including mass drug administration (MDA) to populations at risk, improving water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, and continuous monitoring and evaluation using sensitive diagnostic tools. These strategies are crucial, not only for controlling and reducing the prevalence of STH infections but also for preventing their resurgence25. The effort to reach these goals involves the strategy of regularly treating preschool and school-aged children regularly with anthelmintic medications. However, expanding the treatments to include adults is crucial especially in high prevalence areas, as demonstrated in this study. In conclusion, we emphasize the importance of administering anthelmintic treatment across all age groups and developing targeted strategies for older members of the OA community. This approach is essential for controlling ongoing transmission, reducing the overall STH burden, and protecting the future well-being of OA children, who deserve a healthier future.

There are several limitations we would like to address in this study. Firstly, the data was collected at a single time point, which limits the ability to identify the relationships of current infection with the previous and future STH infections. The number of adult participants was also low in the inland areas because they were usually out in the jungle, especially during the daytime. Additionally, the studied Negrito population was relatively small and may not represent the overall OA population. Fourth, we also relied completely on microscopic examinations, mainly the Kato-Katz method due to its cost-effectiveness and as the standard method for assessing STH burden in large-scale epidemiological studies, but acknowledge the higher sensitivity of molecular techniques, which unfortunately were not incorporated in this study.

Methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Universiti Teknologi MARA [(Approval number: REC/03/2022 (ST/FB/3)]. All method and procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Additional approval was also granted by the Department of Orang Asli Development (JAKOA), twice during the course of the entire study [Reference no: JAKOA.OO.30.032 JLD 52 (49) and JAKOA.PP.30.052 JLD 54 (18) for period extension]. Prior to the commencement of the study, participants were informed about the research objectives. Written or thumb-printed informed consent forms were requested from both adults (above 18 years old) and legal representatives of the children (under 18 years old) participating in the study. Only participants who provided consent were included, and they retained the right to withdraw from the study on any occasion or time without prejudice.

Study design and sample size calculation

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among the Orang Asli of the Negrito tribe from July 2022 to July 2023. The Negritos were selected as the study population due to their relatively pristine and traditional lifestyle compared to other tribes, and some are still hunter-gatherers26. Besides, their population size is small and there are limited reports available. Participants were recruited using a convenient, snowball sampling method, which was considered the most suitable and practical option due to challenges in reaching the communities and obtaining faecal samples from Negrito participants.

The sample size required for screening baseline study was calculated based on both the anticipated (expected future rates) and latest prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections among the Orang Asli. Study population

The Negrito population is dispersed along the mountain range that forms the backbone of Peninsular Malaysia. Figure 1 shows the sampling sites of the study. This study took place in the northern and central regions of Peninsular Malaysia, covering six areas that include four Negrito sub-tribes: Bateq, Jahai, Kintaq, and Kensiu. The villages were chosen at random from a list of Negrito villages permitted by JAKOA, with additional authorization from the Tok Batin, the tribe’s chieftain.

We categorised the villages into two groups: Resettlement at the forest periphery which are considered more advance (ex-situ development) and resettlement/village within the inland tropical rainforest (in-situ development), where hunting and gathering practices still dominate8. Each village was then given a development score ranging from 1 to 4, based on accessibility, infrastructure, and access to treated water and healthcare systems. The resettlement at the periphery exhibited high village accessibility, with moderate to high proximity to the nearest town. For example, Lubuk Legong received the highest development score of 4 due to its better infrastructure, high accessibility, access to treated water, and a well-structured community organization. The economic activities in these villages included modern jobs such as manual labour and factory work. In contrast, the inland villages demonstrated low to moderate levels of accessibility and were farther from town. Notably, reaching Taman Negara required additional transportation by boat. These inland villages mainly depended on rivers as their water source. In terms of dietary habits, both types of villages have a similar main staple diet, primarily consisting of rice. However, inland communities also rely on tapioca, bamboo shoots, and forest plants and wildlife. In contrast, forest-periphery communities have greater exposure to modern foods, such as canned and processed items.

Sample (stool and blood) and data collection

To gather anthropometric data, the height and body weight of each participant were measured and recorded. For stool sampling, a screw-capped container inside a sterile plastic bag were distributed to each household throughout the village upon arrival. This ensured that participants had sufficient time to provide the required sample. Participants were informed on how to collect the faecal sample using the container, and they were directed to a designated location in their village, typically a community hall, where they could submit their samples. Upon collection, the fresh faecal samples were immediately labelled with a unique ID. The samples were then divided into two additional aliquots: one in 10% formalin solution and the other in 2.5% potassium dichromate solution. All samples were stored in dry ice and transported to the Institute for Medical Molecular Biotechnology (IMMB), Universiti Teknologi MARA (UiTM) where they were refrigerated at − 20 °C prior to processing. Haemoglobin (Hb) concentration measurements were also obtained to evaluate the anaemia status of the participants. A finger prick was performed using a lancet, and the blood collected was directly applied onto the Hb strip connected to the portable Hb measurement device (Mission® Ultra Hb Haemoglobin Testing System, ACON Biotech).

All participants underwent a series of interviews using a modified pre-tested questionnaire8. For young children, the information was gathered through interviews with their parents or guardians at the community hall or in their homes. Interviews were conducted in Bahasa Malaysia (the national language for Malaysia). The questionnaire primarily focused on the participants’ demographic information (e.g., household info, age, gender, subtribe), socio-economic factors (e.g., occupation, monthly household income), educational status (e.g., level of education) as well as general sanitation and environmental conditions (e.g., source of water supply and latrine availability). Additional inquiries covered behavioural risks related to personal hygiene, such as participants’ defaecation practices preferences for using latrines or engaging in open defaecation in various environments (such as rivers and bushes) and whether they had history of anthelmintic treatment (AHT) for the past 6 months.

Parasitological examination, nutritional and anaemia status

All stool samples collected were processed and examined for the presence of intestinal parasitic helminths using formol-ether sedimentation and direct faecal smear technique. To improve detection accuracy, all samples were prepared in triplicate and thoroughly examined blindly by three experienced individuals. Based on this screening, the positive samples were later subjected to Kato-Katz technique to quantify the worm burden (light, moderate, heavy) while the negative samples underwent another qualitative examination which is formol-ether concentration technique to confirm the absence of STH eggs. In Kato-Katz technique, WHO recommends the use of a template holding 41.7 mg of faeces, which corresponds to a multiplication factor of 24 (24 × 41.7 mg ~ 1 g)28. The calculation for worm burden is the number of eggs observed multiplied by the conversion factor (number of eggs × 24). The lower limits of moderate and heavy infections were 5000 and 50,000 epg for Ascaris lumbricoides; 1000 and 10,000 epg for Trichuris trichiura; and 2000 and 4000 epg for hookworms, respectively. Stool samples were considered positive if the intestinal parasites were detected by any of these methods.

Anthropometric profiles employing body mass index (BMI) were utilized to evaluate the nutritional status of adult participants (aged > 19 years). BMI was calculated as body weight (in kilograms, kg) divided by the square of height (in meters, m2), regardless of age or sex. Participants aged 2 to 19 years had their BMI assessed using percentile calculations provided by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)29. The cutoff points were defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) and CDC as follows: < 18.5 for underweight, 18.5–24.9 for normal weight, 25.0–29.9 for overweight, and ≥ 30.0 for obese.

The WHO threshold was used as the standard reference to define anaemia30. Anaemia was determined by Hb levels: < 11 g/dL for children under 5, < 11.5 g/dL for those 5–12 years old, < 12 g/dL for individuals over 12 and non-pregnant women (aged 15 and above), and < 13 g/dL for men. The severity of anaemia was classified as mild, moderate, or severe based on Hb levels.

Data management and statistical analysis

The data entry of the collected questionnaire was completed using Microsoft Office Excel 2016, followed by statistical analysis conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics 20. A blank map of Malaysia was edited using Microsoft Office PowerPoint 2016. Demographic data, socio-economic factors, environmental conditions, and hygiene practices were treated as categorical variables. Monthly household income was grouped into two categories: ≤ RM600 and > RM600 (approximately ≤ USD126 and > USD126), due to more than half of the population reported a gross monthly income below RM600 (USD126).

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted for each Orang Asli village. Frequency, percentages (rate), measures of central tendency (mean and median) and dispersion (standard deviation) were computed to depict the characteristics of the studied Negrito population. Pearson’s Chi-squared test (χ2) was used to determine the independence between categorical independent variables and the outcome, as well as to measure the association of soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections with the test variables. To assess the STH intensity (moderate-to-heavy) and associated risk factors, we categorized the infection status into two groups: one for negative and light infections, and another group for moderate and heavy infections. This approach was chosen because STH is widely common among indigenous populations. The crude odds ratio (COR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were analysed using binomial logistic regression analysis (univariate). Variables with a P-value lower than 0.25 in univariate analysis were considered for further multivariate (backward stepwise, likelihood ratio) logistic regression (presented as adjusted odds ratio, AOR and 95% confidence interval). Model fitness was determined by using Hosmer–Lemeshow statistic. A significance level of less than 0.05 (P < 0.05) was deemed statistically significant and labelled with an asterisk (*) in the tables.
