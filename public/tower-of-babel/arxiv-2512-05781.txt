Simulating non-trivial incompressible flows with a quantum lattice Boltzmann algorithm
David Jennings, Kamil Korzekwa*, Matteo Lostaglio*, Paul Mannix* †, Richard Ashworth, Emanuele Marsili, Stephen Rolston

Abstract
Quantum algorithms have been identified as a potential means to accelerate computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations, with the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) being a promising candidate for realizing quantum speedups. Here, we extend the recent quantum algorithm for the incompressible LBM to account for realistic fluid dynamics setups by incorporating walls, inlets, outlets, and external forcing. We analyze the associated complexity cost and show that these modifications preserve the asymptotic scaling, and potential quantum advantage, of the original algorithm. Moreover, to support our theoretical analysis, we provide a classical numerical study illustrating the accuracy, complexity, and convergence of the algorithm for representative incompressible-flow cases, including the driven Taylor–Green vortex, the lid-driven cavity flow, and the flow past a cylinder. Our results provide a pathway to accurate quantum simulation of nonlinear fluid dynamics, and a framework for extending quantum LBM to more challenging flow configurations.

Introduction

In settings where the understanding and prediction of the behavior of fluid flow is the ultimate aim and, in particular, when experiments are either impossible (e.g., in oceanography and metrology) or prohibitively expensive/time-consuming (e.g., building and testing multiple prototypes in wind-tunnels), computational fluid dynamics (CFD) models have become indispensable
[

Due to the challenges associated with numerically modeling the non-trivial flow around a complex geometry with multiple different boundary conditions, a number of specialized high performance CFD codes have been developed. Albeit based on fundamentally different numerical methods including the finite-volume

, spectral-element
[

and lattice Boltzmann method
[

As a result, it has been possible to simulate applications as diverse as landing-gear, wind farms and turbo-machinery, respectively. However, as these simulations seek to grow larger and better resolve the flow physics, computational constraints emerge. Notwithstanding the computational complexity increasing at least cubically with the Reynolds number
\mathrm{Re}

, super-resolution machine learning methods
[

Instead of bits that are 0 or 1, a quantum computer uses quantum states that can explore many possibilities at once, and carefully designed algorithms ‘interfere’ these possibilities to amplify the desired solution. Since the quantum-encoded data is a potentially exponentially large vector, the aim is not to print out the entire flow field, but to extract specific features or observables faster than classical methods might allow, especially for very large problem sizes. That said, potential speedups come with caveats: data must be encoded efficiently, the lifted system must be well-conditioned enough for the solver to work, and today’s hardware remains noisy, so most proposals target future, fault-tolerant machines

Existing framework

2.1
Lattice Boltzmann method

The
lattice Boltzmann equation
(LBE) is a kinetic model of fluid dynamics that
describes the fluid mesoscopically
[
41
]
. It conserves mass and momentum by construction and its low-order moments recover the Navier-Stokes equations in the regime where the characteristic length scales of the problem are much larger than the molecular mean path. Within LBE, the fluid is modeled at time
t
and position
\bm{r}
in
D
-dimensional space by a vector-valued function
\bm{g}(\bm{r},t)
, whose components
g_{m}(\bm{r},t)
, with
m\in\{1,\dots,Q\}
, are proportional to the probability density of finding a fluid particle with a discrete velocity
\bm{e}_{m}
. LBE is derived from the
discrete Boltzmann equation
(DBE) describing continuous dynamics via:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{m}(\bm{r},t)+\bm{e}_{m}\cdot\nabla g_{m}(\bm{r},t)=\Omega_{m}(\bm{r},t),

(1)

where
\bm{e}_{m}\cdot\nabla g_{m}
is an advection term that describes
streaming
, i.e., the flow of the fluid in space, while the collision operator
\Omega_{m}
describes
collisions
that are local in space. We adopt the Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) operator
[
42
]
:

\Omega_{m}(\bm{r},t)=-\frac{1}{\tau}\left[g_{m}(\bm{r},t)-g_{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\bm{r},t)\right],

(2)

where
\tau
is the relaxation time determining the speed of equilibration, and
\bm{g}^{\mathrm{eq}}
is the Taylor expansion of the local Maxwell equilibrium up to second order terms, which for an incompressible flow is given by
[
39
]
:

\displaystyle g_{m}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\bm{r},t)=\frac{p}{c_{s}^{2}}\;w_{m}+\rho_{0}\left(\frac{\bm{e}_{m}\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)}{c_{s}^{2}}+\frac{(\bm{e}_{m}\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{r},t))^{2}}{2c_{s}^{4}}-\frac{|\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)|^{2}}{2c_{s}^{2}}\right)w_{m}+O(\mathrm{Ma}^{3}).

(3)

In the above,
\rho_{0}
denotes the constant fluid density,
c_{s}
the speed of sound,
\mathrm{Ma}:=|\bm{u}|/c_{s}
the Mach number, and
\bm{w}
is a vector of Maxwell weightings for the different discrete velocities satisfying
w_{m}\geq 0
and
\sum_{m}w_{m}=1
. The pressure
p
and velocity vector
\bm{u}
of the fluid are recovered as moments via:

p(\bm{r},t):=c_{s}^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}g_{m}(\bm{r},t),\qquad\bm{u}(\bm{r},t):=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}g_{m}(\bm{r},t)\bm{e}_{m}.

(4)

As shown in Ref.
[
39
]
, this formulation recovers the Navier-Stokes equations, albeit with the divergence-free condition approximated as
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=\frac{1}{c_{s}^{2}}\partial_{t}p\sim O(\mathrm{Ma}^{2})
.
1
1

1

Although this is suitable for steady or slowly varying flows, the collision operator proposed in Ref.
[
40
]
(also quadratic in
g_{m}
) is by contrast exact and therefore preferable for time-dependent flows.
Therefore, at the expense of discarding weak compressibility effects, which for
\mathrm{Ma}\ll 1
are already negligible, an
O(\mathrm{Ma}^{2})
incompressible LBE formulation whose collision integral is quadratic in
g_{m}
is obtained.

To obtain the numerical solution of Eqs. (
1

r_{i}\in[\Delta x,2\Delta x,\dots,N_{i}\Delta x]
, and in time,
t\in[0,\Delta t,2\Delta t,\dots,N_{t}\Delta t]
. Integrating the left hand side of Eq. (

g_{m}(\bm{r}+\bm{e}_{m}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=g_{m}(\bm{r},t)-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau+\Delta t/2}[g_{m}(\bm{r},t)-g^{\mathrm{eq}}_{m}(\bm{r},t)],

(5)

which is second order accurate in both space and time
[
41
]
. To determine how Eq. (
5
) relates to the macroscopic Navier-Stokes equations, Chapman-Enskog analysis must be applied, which shows that the kinematic shear viscosity,

\nu={c_{s}}^{2}\left(\tau-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right),

(6)

is a function of the relaxation time, time-step and speed of sound. As discussed in Ref.
[
41
]
, the factor of
(\tau-\Delta t/2)
appearing in Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) is not general, but rather a consequence of the time-discretization employed. Because we have used a second-order time discretization, the relaxation time
\tau
is shifted by
\Delta t/2
. Although physically significant length- and time-scales are typically chosen, the system from Eqs. (
3
) - (
6
) is instead made non-dimensional by choosing the lattice spacing
\Delta x
and time step
\Delta t
(also known as lattice units) along with the reference density
\rho_{0}
, such that:

\displaystyle t=t^{\star}\Delta t,\quad T=N_{t}\Delta t,\quad\tau=\tau^{\star}\Delta t,\quad\bm{r}=\bm{r}^{\star}\Delta x,\quad L_{i}=N_{i}\Delta x,\quad\bm{e}_{m}=\bm{e}_{m}^{\star}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t},\quad\bm{u}=\bm{u}^{\star}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t},\quad p=p^{\star}c_{s}^{2}\rho_{0},

(7)

where superscript star denotes a non-dimensional variable,
L_{i}
is the length of the
i^{th}
side of the domain and
T
is the size of time-interval considered. This choice restricts
t^{\star}\in\{0,\dots N_{t}\}
and
r_{i}^{\star}\in\{1,\dots,N_{i}\}
to integer values, such that for an incompressible isothermal flow
c_{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}
and

are given by Table
2

Anticipating the quantum formulation of Eq. (
5
), the system state at each time step
t^{\star}
can be represented as:

\bm{g}(t^{\star})=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}g_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star},t^{\star})\ket{\bm{r}^{\star}}\otimes\ket{m},

(8)

where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product and
\ket{\bm{r}^{\star}},\ket{m}
denote position and velocity registers, respectively, using the Dirac ket notation. The dimension of
\bm{g}
is
d=NQ
, where
N
is the total number of spatial lattice points. Equation (
5
) can be then be divided into collision (C) and streaming (S) steps, and rewritten in a matrix form as:

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{C}(t^{\star})

\displaystyle=\left(I+F_{1}\right)\bm{g}(t^{\star})+F_{2}\bm{g}(t^{\star})^{\otimes 2},

(9a)

\displaystyle\bm{g}(t^{\star}+1)

\displaystyle=S\bm{g}^{C}(t^{\star}),

(9b)

where
F_{k}
are
d\times d^{k}
matrices describing linear and quadratic contributions from collisions and
S
is a
d\times d
permutation matrix encoding streaming, see the Appendix for details.

2.2
Carleman embedding of the nonlinear dynamics

The lattice Boltzmann equation is nonlinear, whereas quantum algorithms running on quantum computers are built from linear (unitary) operations, so there is a structural mismatch. The way in which this mismatch is resolved is by embedding the nonlinear dynamics into a much higher-dimensional linear system, whose variables are functions of the original ones. While this lifted system can be overwhelmingly large for classical solvers, quantum circuits act on superpositions and apply linear operators implicitly, so the cost need not scale with the full dimension in the same way as explicit classical representations. In practice, this makes it viable to work with high-dimensional embeddings on a quantum computer, provided the embedding can be implemented efficiently and remains well-behaved.

In order for the incompressible LBE problem, captured by Eqs. (
9a
)-(
9b
), to be amenable for the quantum computer, we use the Carleman embedding technique
[
27
]
. Instead of representing the state of the fluid at each moment in time by a
d
-dimensional vector
\bm{g}
, it is now represented by the
Carleman vector
:

\bm{y}(t^{\star}):=[\bm{y}_{1}(t^{\star}),\bm{y}_{2}(t^{\star}),\dots,\bm{y}_{N_{C}}(t^{\star})],\qquad\bm{y}_{k}(0)=\bm{g}^{\otimes k}(0)

(10)

of dimension

d_{C}:=d+d^{2}+\dots+d^{N_{C}}=\frac{d(d^{N_{C}}-1)}{d-1},

(11)

which carries information about all powers of the incompressible LBE evolution up to the
N_{C}
-th power. Here,
N_{C}
is the finite Carleman truncation order leading to Carleman truncation error
\epsilon_{C}
, i.e., the evolution obtained from a truncated linear system will generally differ from the original LBE dynamics.

The update rules for a
d
-dimensional vector
\bm{g}(t^{\star})
from Eqs. (
9a
)-(
9b
),
can now be approximated by a linear update rule for a
d_{C}
-dimensional vector
\bm{y}(t^{\star})

[
38
]
:

\bm{y}(t^{\star}+1)={\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}}\bm{y}(t^{\star}),

(12)

where
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
are
d_{C}\times d_{C}
Carleman streaming and collision matrices, see the Appendix for details. The above recurrence relation has a clear solution given by:

\bm{y}(t^{\star})=({\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}})^{t^{\star}}\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}},\qquad\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}}=[\bm{g}(0),\bm{g}(0)^{\otimes 2},\dots,\bm{g}(0)^{\otimes N_{C}}].

(13)

Therefore, for the initial state
\bm{g}(0)
, the state of the system after
N_{t}
streaming and collision steps is approximated by:

\bm{g}(N_{t})\approx_{\epsilon_{C}}\left[({\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}})^{N_{t}}\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}}\right]_{1},

(14)

where subscript 1 indicates the first block of the Carleman vector.

Next, the LBE problem with
N_{t}
streaming and collision steps can be written in the form of the following linear system of dimension
d_{C}(N_{t}+1)
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
-{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}}&I&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&-{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}}&I&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&I&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&-{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{y}(0)\\
\bm{y}(1)\\
\bm{y}(2)\\
\vdots\\
\bm{y}(N_{t}-1)\\
\bm{y}(N_{t})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}}\\
0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(15)

or in a more compact form as

A\bm{Y}=\bm{b}.

(16)

The vector
\bm{Y}
is the
Carleman history state
, carrying information about Carleman vector at all times during the simulated evolution:

\bm{Y}=A^{-1}\bm{b}=\left(\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}},{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}}\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}},({\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}})^{2}\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}},\dots,({\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}})^{N_{t}-1}\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}},({\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{C}})^{N_{t}}\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}}\right)^{\top}.

(17)

The problem therefore reduces to performing a matrix inversion, and outputting the solution to the above set of linear equations. Although the solution vector
\bm{Y}
can be exponentially large, as a function of the Carleman truncation scale, one can now exploit the particular features of a quantum computer that allow one to process such vectors efficiently. More precisely, it is known that
quantum linear solvers
(QLS) have exponential speedups over classical methods for certain structured problems
[
14
]
and, more recently, it has been shown that they can also provide exponential speedups in the context of simulating nonlinear dynamical systems via the above Carleman method
[
33
]
.

2.3
Solving exponentially large linear systems on a quantum computer

Quantum algorithms for solving systems of linear equations as in Eq. (
16
) typically return the solution in an encoded (quantum) form rather than a full list of numbers
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
43
]
, so they are most useful when one only needs certain features, like averages or correlations, rather than every entry. Their performance also depends on how well-behaved the equations are (in terms of the condition number
\kappa_{A}=\|A\|\cdot\|A^{-1}\|
) and on whether the problem has structure that allows one to load data and apply matrices efficiently. It is rigorously established that promising quantum speedups exist, but only for the right kinds of large, structured problems
[
14
]
.

The ability of quantum linear solvers to provide large speedups relies crucially on the output of a quantum computer, which differs radically from what one has from a classical computer. This is because the solution
\bm{Y}=A^{-1}\bm{b}
is encoded in a
quantum state
. Physically, this corresponds to a quantum mechanical system (such as a system of photons, superconductors, atoms, etc.) being in a superposition of two or more classical states, e.g., a particle being in a superposition of two locations
A
and
B
. Mathematically, we write this superposition state in the Dirac notation as a vector
\ket{\psi}=a\ket{A}+b\ket{B}
, where
a
and
b
are complex numbers that obey
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1
. Within quantum theory, the classical states are represented as orthonormal basis vectors, and so in this basis we have that
\ket{\psi}
is the vector with components
(a,b)
. More generally, we label classical states with integers, and a general
d
-dimensional quantum state takes the form:

\ket{\psi}=a_{0}\ket{0}+a_{1}\ket{1}+\cdots+a_{d-1}\ket{d-1},

(18)

with the condition
\sum_{j}|a_{j}|^{2}=1
. If we measure the system, we collapse the quantum state probabilistically onto one of the states
\ket{k}
, and the probability that this occurs is
p_{k}=|a_{k}|^{2}
. Therefore, the normalization condition is simply the condition that measurement probabilities in quantum theory sum to
1
. Upon getting outcome
k
, the quantum state collapses
\ket{\psi}\rightarrow\ket{k}
. At the level of linear algebra, this can be described (up to normalizing the vector) by the application of a projection matrix:

(a_{0},\dots,a_{d-1})\rightarrow P_{k}(a_{0},\dots,a_{d-1})=(0,0,\dots,0,a_{k},0,\dots,0).

(19)

Here,
P_{k}
is the projection matrix onto the
k
-th basis vector that can be written in the Dirac notation as
P_{k}=\left|k\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k\right|
.

More generally, we have the compact Dirac notation convention defining rank-1 matrices
\left|m\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k\right|
given by:

\left|m\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k\right|\sum_{j}a_{j}\ket{j}=\sum_{j}a_{j}\left|m\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k\right|j\rangle=a_{k}\ket{m},

(20)

in other words
\bra{k}j\rangle=\delta_{j,k}
for any
k,j
. Given two quantum states
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{j}a_{j}\ket{j}
and
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{j}b_{j}\ket{j}
, we then define the conjugate transposed vector
\bra{\phi}:=\sum_{j}b^{*}_{j}\bra{j}
, the matrix

\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\phi\right|=\sum_{j,k}a_{j}b^{*}_{k}\left|j\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k\right|,

(21)

and the complex number obtained from taking the inner product of these two vectors:

\bra{\phi}\psi\rangle=\sum_{j,k}a_{j}b^{*}_{k}\delta_{j,k}=\sum_{k}a_{k}b^{*}_{k}.

(22)

We also can have multiple copies of quantum states and denote, e.g., two systems with the first in the state
\ket{\psi}
and the second in the state
\ket{\phi}
as
\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\phi}
. In other words: multiple copies are denoted with terms separated by tensor products, and
A^{\otimes n}=A\otimes A\otimes\cdots\otimes A
denotes
n
copies of
A
. In this setting, the rules of quantum theory correspond to linear transformations (matrices on vectors) and linear algebra manipulations (compositions of matrices, inner products, tensor products, etc.).

To implement quantum algorithms on a quantum computer, every component must be encoded in a
quantum circuit
(a sequence of instructions as to how to manipulate the quantum system). Mathematically, a quantum circuit is described by an exponentially large unitary matrix that acts on quantum states. This exponential matrix could describe various data, such as vectors and matrices. For example, the primitive of
state preparation
involves a quantum circuit
U_{\bm{b}}
that acts on a fiducial vector
\ket{0}=(1,0,0\dots,0)
and prepares the new vector
\ket{\bm{b}}=U_{\bm{b}}\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\|\bm{b}\|}(b_{0},b_{1},\dots b_{d-1})
. In other words, the first column of
U_{\bm{b}}
is simply the normalized state vector
\ket{\bm{b}}
. Another key ingredient is the encoding of an arbitrary matrix
A
inside a unitary circuit. In order to respect unitarity, the matrix
A
is typically rescaled
A\rightarrow A/\alpha_{A}
by some positive constant
\alpha_{A}\geq\|A\|
, and then a quantum circuit
U_{A}
is constructed such that the top-left block of the matrix is identical to
A/\alpha_{A}
; this is called a
unitary block-encoding
of the matrix
A
.

A standard approach to solving a linear system
A\bm{Y}=\bm{b}
on a quantum computer
[
14
,
15
,
43
]
assumes access to (i) an efficient
unitary state preparation

U_{\bm{b}}
such that
U_{\bm{b}}\ket{0}=\ket{\bm{b}}
, and (ii) a
block-encoding

U_{A}
of
A
, i.e., a unitary
U_{A}
acting on
\log d+n_{A}
qubits whose top-left block equals
A/\alpha_{A}

for some
block-encoding prefactor

\alpha_{A}\geq\|A\|
.
Given
(U_{\bm{b}},U_{A})
, modern
quantum linear solvers
output a state proportional to
A^{-1}\ket{\bm{b}}
as a normalized quantum state. Optimal quantum linear solvers prepare the solution
\ket{\bm{Y}}
within error
\epsilon
using
O\!\left(\alpha_{A}\,\kappa_{A}\,\log\!\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
applications of
U_{A},U^{\dagger}_{A}
and of the preparation unitaries
U_{\bm{b}},U_{\bm{b}}^{\dagger}
, where
\kappa_{A}
is the condition number of
A
on the relevant subspace.
The dependence on both the condition number and the block-encoding prefactor is linear; thus large
\alpha_{A}
directly inflates runtime. In practice, (quantum or classical) preconditioning can reduce
\kappa_{A}
, but any gains must be balanced against changes in
\alpha_{A}
and additional oracle costs.

One final subtlety exists for QLS: the output. The output of the quantum linear solver applied to the Carleman system will be a
quantum state

\ket{\bm{Y}}
of the quantum computer. This is quite distinct from the output of a classical computer, which can, e.g., print the data on a screen to be perused. Instead, the quantum output
\ket{\bm{Y}}
is an exponentially large vector that is ‘stored’ in the quantum computer (e.g., for a quantum computer with
1000
qubits, the vector has dimension
2^{1000}\approx 10^{300}
). In order to extract meaningful information, one must perform a carefully chosen measurement that extracts a much smaller amount of information from
\ket{\bm{Y}}
. The full end-to-end quantum simulation must therefore build in the exact information that is required into the full pipeline, and the information extraction becomes itself a potential source of inefficiency. While we do not discuss that in this work, we refer the interested reader to Ref.
[
38
]
, where it is shown how, and at what complexity cost, one can extract the drag force on an obstacle from a quantum state
\ket{\bm{Y}}
.

3
Extensions to nontrivial flows and geometries

Having introduced the incompressible Carleman LBM algorithm from Ref.
[
38
]
and discussed how to recover its solution using QLS, we now extend it to accommodate non-trivial geometries and driving via a body forcing or moving boundaries. These extensions will allow us to investigate the performance of our quantum algorithm on benchmark CFD problems, such as the forced Taylor-Green vortex, the lid-driven cavity flow and the flow past a cylinder, considered in Sec.
4
.

3.1
Geometry

3.1.1
Extending the model

For periodic boundary conditions, there are only fluid nodes and the system can be encoded in the streaming matrix:

S=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}\left|\bm{r}^{\star}+\bm{e}_{m}^{\star}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\bm{r}^{\star}\right|\otimes\left|m\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle m\right|,

(23)

which translates populations
g_{m}
from position
\bm{r}^{\star}
and with a discrete velocity
m
to the next spatial position
\bm{r}^{\star}+\bm{e}_{m}^{\star}
, where the addition should be interpreted as modulo the number of lattice points per direction, thus encoding the periodic domain. We want to modify this streaming matrix in order to account for the no-slip (Dirichlet) and free-slip (Neumann) boundary conditions required to consider domains with walls, inlets and outlets.

The effect of a Dirichlet boundary condition can be modeled via the bounce-back rule following Ref.
[
41
]
, whereby one prescribes these macroscopic boundary conditions via the populations as:

g_{\bar{m}}(\bm{r}^{\star}_{b},t^{\star})=g^{C}_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star}_{b},t)-2w_{m}\frac{\bm{e}^{\star}_{m}\cdot\bm{u}^{\star}_{w}}{{c^{*}_{s}}^{2}}.

(24)

Here,
\bm{r}^{\star}_{b}
is the boundary node,
g^{C}_{m}
the post-collision population,
\bar{m}
the reflection of/population opposite
m
, and
\bm{u}^{\star}_{w}
the velocity vector of the wall located at
\bm{r}^{\star}_{w}=\bm{r}^{\star}_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{e}^{\star}_{m}
. As the second term in Eq. (
24
) accounts for the motion imparted by a moving wall, we will include it in the driving matrix described in section
3.2
. The effect of a Neumann boundary condition can be modeled via the non-equilibrium extrapolation scheme following Refs.
[
44
,
45
]
. This scheme provides the populations in terms of their equilibrium and non-equilibrium components at the boundary via:

g_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star}_{b},t^{\star})=g^{\mathrm{eq}}_{m}(p^{\star}_{b},\bm{u}^{\star}_{b})+\left(g_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star}_{f},t)-g^{\mathrm{eq}}_{m}(p^{\star}_{f},\bm{u}^{\star}_{f})\right),

(25)

where
\bm{r}^{\star}_{f}
is one node to the interior of the boundary and
(p^{\star}_{f},\bm{u}^{\star}_{f})
its corresponding pressure and velocity. For a Neumann-boundary condition at which we want to enforce
\partial_{x}\bm{u}^{\star}=\partial_{x}p^{\star}=0
, for example, we can set
(p^{\star}_{b},\bm{u}^{\star}_{b})=(p^{\star}_{f},\bm{u}^{\star}_{f})
, such that the scheme reduces to prescribing populations for the unknown
g_{m}
at
\bm{r}^{\star}_{b}
by copying them from
\bm{r}^{\star}_{f}
.

To illustrate how these boundary conditions can be incorporated into the streaming matrix, we consider a domain bounded by a wall region, an inlet region and outlet region, all with particular geometries.
Every node is either an inlet node, an outlet node, a wall node, or otherwise a fluid node interior to the simulation region. We introduce the following indicator functions on the discrete phase space
\{(\bm{r}^{\star},m)\}
that encode the boundary regions within the model:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{\bm{r}^{\star}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&:~\bm{r}^{\star}\mathrm{~is~a~wall~node,}\\
0&:\mathrm{~otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{\bm{r}^{\star},m}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&:~\bm{r}^{\star}\mathrm{~is~an~inlet~node~and~}\bm{r}^{\star}+\bm{e}^{\star}_{m}\mathrm{~is~outside~the~simulation~region,}\\
0&:~\mathrm{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{r}^{\star},m}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&:~\bm{r}^{\star}\mathrm{~is~an~outlet~node~and~}\bm{r}^{\star}+\bm{e}^{\star}_{m}\mathrm{~is~outside~the~simulation~region,}\\
0&:~\mathrm{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

At the walls we impose a no-slip Dirichlet condition such that the effect of the first term in Eq. (
24
) can be achieved via the following modification of matrix elements of the streaming matrix:

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{wall}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}(1-{\mathcal{W}}_{\bm{r}^{\star}+\bm{e}_{m}^{\star}})(1-{\mathcal{W}}_{\bm{r}^{\star}})\left|\bm{r}^{\star}+\bm{e}_{m}^{\star}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\bm{r}^{\star}\right|\otimes\left|m\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle m\right|

(27)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}{\mathcal{W}}_{\bm{r}^{\star}+\bm{e}_{m}^{\star}}(1-{\mathcal{W}}_{\bm{r}^{\star}})\left|\bm{r}^{\star}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\bm{r}^{\star}\right|\otimes\left|\bar{m}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle m\right|

(28)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}{\mathcal{W}}_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\left|\bm{r}^{\star}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\bm{r}^{\star}\right|\otimes\left|m\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle m\right|.

(29)

In the above, the first term corresponds to unmodified streaming when both nodes considered are fluid; the second term encodes bouncing, when a particle in a fluid node hits a wall node; and the third one simply means no evolution (identity matrix) for the wall nodes. We note that the streaming matrix modified according to the above recipe is still a permutation matrix, so
S_{\mathrm{wall}}
stays unitary and therefore can be realized directly by a quantum circuit.

At an outlet, we impose a Neumann-boundary condition. To account for this condition in the streaming matrix, we introduce one more correction of the following form:

S_{\mathrm{Neumann}}=I+\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{r}^{\star},m}\left|\bm{r}^{\star}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{r}^{\star})\right|\otimes\left|\bar{m}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\bar{m}\right|,

(30)

and consider
S_{\mathrm{Neumann}}S_{\mathrm{wall}}
instead of
S_{\mathrm{wall}}
. This prescribes to simply replace the missing outlet occupations after streaming at position
\bm{r}^{\star}
with a value copied from the neighboring interior node at position
{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{r}^{\star})
, one lattice node away along the normal to the outlet towards the simulation region
2
2

2

For example, in a two-dimensional setting with a vertical outlet being given by the points
(N_{x},y)
with
y\in\{1,\dots,N_{y}\}
and
N_{x}
the horizontal location of the outlet, we simply have
{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{r}^{\star})=\bm{r}^{\star}-(1,0)
.
.

To model the edges of the simulation regions at an inlet and outlet, we also introduce the following correction term:

S_{\mathrm{edge}}=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}(1-{\mathcal{I}}_{\bm{r}^{\star},m})(1-{\mathcal{O}}_{\bm{r}^{\star},m})\left|\bm{r}^{\star}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\bm{r}^{\star}\right|\otimes\left|m\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle m\right|,

(31)

and consider
S_{\mathrm{wall}}S_{\mathrm{edge}}
instead of
S_{\mathrm{wall}}
. This term ensures that the fluid can go outside the simulation region via an inlet and an outlet, instead of moving periodically to the other side of the region. The final streaming term that accounts for walls, inlets and outlets is thus given by:

S^{\prime}=S_{\mathrm{Neumann}}S_{\mathrm{wall}}S_{\mathrm{edge}}.

(32)

This linear map is fully defined once we specify the indicator functions for the wall nodes, the inlet nodes, and the outlet nodes. If one can efficiently encode these three regions in a sufficiently compact functional description, then one can construct a block-encoding of
S^{\prime}
, which governs the linearized dynamics of the fluid. For simplicity, we shall assume straight boundaries aligned with the lattice nodes, with inlet and outlet nodes forming orthogonal lines to the walls at either end of the desired simulation region.

3.1.2
Algorithmic extensions

As described in detail in Ref.
[
38
]
, the workhorse of our quantum algorithm for LBE is the quantum linear solver of Ref.
[
46
]
applied to the linear system from Eq. (
15
). This solver takes as its inputs the state preparation unitary
U_{\bm{b}}
and the unitary block-encoding
U_{A}
of the linear system
A
. Since the modifications to the streaming matrix described in the previous section changes
A
, we will now explain how to block-encode the required linear system in a way that incorporates the wall boundary and inlet/outlet physics.

Let us denote by
A^{\prime}
the linear system using a modified streaming matrix
S^{\prime}
, and by
A
the original system with periodic
S
. Let us also recall that with an appropriately chosen tensor encoding
[
38
]
, the Carleman streaming matrices can be represented by
{\mathcal{S}}=S^{\otimes N_{C}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}=S^{\prime\otimes N_{C}}
. In Ref.
[
38
]
, it was shown that the block-encoding prefactor
\alpha_{A}
and the number of ancillary qubits needed
n_{A}
are given by:

\alpha_{A}=\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}+1,\qquad n_{A}=n_{\mathcal{C}}+2,

(33)

where
\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}
and
n_{\mathcal{C}}
are the block-encoding parameters for the Carleman collision matrix. This result was obtained under the assumption that
{\mathcal{S}}
is unitary (so that
\alpha_{{\mathcal{S}}}=1
and
n_{\mathcal{S}}=0
). However, it is straightforward to show that for a general matrix, such as
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
, the expressions for the block-encoding parameters become:

\alpha_{A^{\prime}}=\alpha_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}+1,\qquad n_{A}=n_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}}+n_{\mathcal{C}}+2.

(34)

We can relate these parameters to the block-encoding parameters for the modified streaming term for a single-copy of the system. In particular, if we can construct a block-encoding of
S^{\prime}
with block-encoding prefactor
\alpha_{S^{\prime}}
using
n_{S^{\prime}}
ancillary qubits, the linear system
A^{\prime}
could be block-encoded with:

\alpha_{A^{\prime}}=(\alpha_{S^{\prime}})^{N_{C}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}+1,\qquad n_{A^{\prime}}=N_{C}n_{S^{\prime}}+n_{\mathcal{C}}+2.

(35)

In order to achieve this, we will employ the following construction. First, recall that a partial permutation matrix is a matrix in which each row and each column contains at most one 1, and all other entries are 0. Given a partial permutation matrix
\tilde{\Pi}
, one can always complete it to a permutation matrix
\Pi
. These two matrices then satisfy:

\tilde{\Pi}\tilde{\Pi}^{\dagger}=\tilde{\Pi}\Pi^{\dagger}=\Pi\tilde{\Pi}^{\dagger}=\tilde{I},

(36)

where
\tilde{I}
is the identity matrix on the image of
\tilde{\Pi}
. One can then block-encode
\tilde{\Pi}
with a unit block-encoding prefactor,
\alpha_{\tilde{\Pi}}=1
, using a single ancillary qubit via:

U_{\tilde{\Pi}}=\tilde{\Pi}\otimes I+(\tilde{\Pi}-\Pi)\otimes X,

(37)

where we used a Pauli
X
unitary on the ancillary qubit. Indeed, using Eq. (
36
), one can verify that
U_{\tilde{\Pi}}
is unitary and that

(I\otimes\bra{0})U_{\tilde{\Pi}}(I\otimes\ket{0})=\tilde{\Pi}.

(38)

Now, we observe that
S_{\mathrm{edge}}
is a partial permutation matrix, whereas
S_{\mathrm{Neumann}}
is a sum of two partial permutation matrices. Hence, using the block-encoding described above together with a linear combination of unitaries (LCU)
[
43
]
applied to two components of
S_{\mathrm{Neumann}}
, we can straightforwardly construct the block encoding of
S^{\prime}
with a block-encoding prefactor
\alpha_{S^{\prime}}=2
using
n_{S^{\prime}}=3
ancillary qubits. We thus conclude that:

\alpha_{A^{\prime}}=2^{N_{C}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}+1\approx 2^{N_{C}}\alpha_{A},\qquad n_{A^{\prime}}=n_{A}+3N_{C},

(39)

so that incorporating non-trivial geometries into our quantum algorithm for LBE increases the query complexity by a factor of
2^{N_{C}}
and requires additional
3N_{C}
ancillary qubits. This additional cost should not have a significant effect on the efficiency of the algorithm, as it is expected that relatively low
N_{C}
should suffice for simulations. More precisely, using the Carleman truncation error model
[
38
]
, which predicts exponential damping of the Carleman error as
\epsilon_{C}\propto 2^{-|\Gamma|N_{C}}
(as long as one is within the convergence radius), the increase in the query complexity is scaling as
O(1/\epsilon_{C}^{1/|\Gamma|})
. For example, for the lid-driven cavity flow discussed in Sec.
4.2
, one can estimate
|\Gamma|
to be approximately
4.19
for
\mathrm{Re}=20
, which translates into an increase in the query complexity scaling as
O(1/\epsilon_{C}^{0.24})
.

Also, note that the complexity increase discussed above is due to a modified block-encoding prefactor,
\alpha_{A^{\prime}}
instead of
\alpha_{A}
, but the query complexity can also increase due to the dependence on the condition number
\kappa_{A^{\prime}}
, and not on
\kappa_{A}
. However, due to the fact that the difference between
S^{\prime}
and
S
is only on the boundary nodes, this is expected to not be significant. The reasoning here is that this will depend on the norm
\|S^{\prime}-S\|
, however this is a matrix of zeroes except for nodes on the boundary of the simulation region, and so will not scale with the volume of the region and should be sub-leading relative to other terms. As we shall see in Sec.
4.4
, this is indeed confirmed by our numerical studies.

3.2
Driving

We now proceed to extending the model and quantum algorithm from Ref.
[
38
]
to accommodate flows driven by a divergence-free body force or a moving boundary.

3.2.1
Extending the model

In LBM, one can include the effect of a divergence-free body force
\bm{F}(\bm{r})
by discretizing this force in velocity space, such that its discrete representation
\phi_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star})
, ensures that the zeroth moment respects mass conservation,
\sum_{m}\phi_{m}=0
, and that the first moment recovers the forcing applied in the momentum equation,
\sum_{m}\bm{e}_{m}\phi_{m}=\bm{F}
. While a second-order accurate discretization (in space and time) can be obtained following Ref.
[
47
]
, by adding
\phi_{m}
to the right hand side of Eq. (
9a
) and including a velocity shift, we will instead pursue a first-order accurate approach as this does not require modifications of collision matrices
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. As the implementation of a no-slip velocity boundary condition also includes a component which forces the flow (see Eq. (
24
)), we incorporate this driving which acts locally at
\bm{r}^{\star}_{b}
as:

\displaystyle g_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star}_{b},t)\rightarrow g_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star}_{b},t)+\phi_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star}_{b}),

(40a)

such that
\phi_{m}(\bm{r}^{*}_{b})
in this case corresponds to
-2w_{m}\frac{\bm{e}^{\star}_{m}\cdot\bm{u}^{*}_{w}}{{c^{*}_{s}}^{2}}
, the correction of the bounce back rule for a moving boundary. Our matrix formulation of the problem from Eqs. (
9a
)-(
9b
) then gets extended by a driving step as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{C}(t^{\star})

\displaystyle=\left(I+F_{1}\right)\bm{g}(t^{\star})+F_{2}\bm{g}(t^{\star})^{\otimes 2},

(41a)

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{S}(t^{\star})

\displaystyle=S\bm{g}^{C}(t^{\star}),

(41b)

\displaystyle\bm{g}(t^{\star}+1)

\displaystyle=\bm{g}^{S}(t^{\star})+F_{0},

(41c)

where
F_{0}
is the driving vector given by:

F_{0}=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}}\sum_{m=1}^{Q}\phi_{m}(\bm{r}^{\star})\ket{\bm{r}^{\star}}\otimes\ket{m}.

(42)

The effect that this third driving update rule from Eq. (
41c
) has on the Carleman vector can be inferred from its effect on
k
copies of the state vector:

\displaystyle[\bm{g}(t^{\star}+1)]^{\otimes k}

\displaystyle=\left[\bm{g}^{S}(t^{\star})+F_{0}\right]^{\otimes k}=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\left[I^{\otimes l}\otimes F_{0}^{\otimes k-l}+\mathrm{perms.}\right]\bm{g}^{S}(t^{\star})^{\otimes l}=\sum_{l=0}^{k}D_{l}^{k}\bm{g}^{S}(t^{\star})^{\otimes l},

(43)

where we have introduced
d^{k}\times d^{l}
matrices:

D_{l}^{k}:=I^{l}\otimes F_{0}^{\otimes k-l}+\mathrm{perms.},

(44)

and where
+\ \mathrm{perms.}
denotes a sum over distinct permutations over
k
subsystems, with
l
subsystems of type 1, and
k-l
subsystems of type 2. This means that a single time step of the evolution can be compactly written as:

\displaystyle\bm{y}(t^{\star}+1)={\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}{\mathcal{C}}\bm{y}(t^{\star})+{\mathcal{F}}_{0},

(45)

with the
Carleman driving matrix
:

{\mathcal{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0&\dots&0\\
D^{2}_{1}&I^{\otimes 2}&0&\dots&0\\
D^{3}_{1}&D^{3}_{2}&I^{\otimes 3}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
D^{N_{C}}_{1}&D^{N_{C}}_{2}&D^{N_{C}}_{3}&\dots&I^{\otimes N_{C}}\end{pmatrix},

(46)

and the
Carleman driving vector
:

{\mathcal{F}}_{0}=[F_{0},F_{0}^{\otimes 2},\dots,F_{0}^{\otimes N_{C}}]^{\top}.

(47)

The linear system that captures the full evolution over
N_{t}
time steps, including non-trivial geometry encoded by
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
and driving encoded by
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
, is now given by:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
-{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}{\mathcal{C}}&I&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&-{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}{\mathcal{C}}&I&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&I&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&-{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}{\mathcal{C}}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{y}(0)\\
\bm{y}(1)\\
\bm{y}(2)\\
\vdots\\
\bm{y}(N_{t}-1)\\
\bm{y}(N_{t})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}}\\
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\\
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\\
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\end{pmatrix},

(48)

and we will again write it compactly as
A^{\prime\prime}\bm{Y}=\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}
.

3.2.2
Algorithmic modifications

The algorithmic modifications needed to include driving are as follows. First, instead of block-encoding
A^{\prime}
, we now need to block-encode
A^{\prime\prime}
. And second, instead of constructing a state preparation unitary
U_{\bm{b}}
for the undriven system, we need to construct
U_{\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}}
. In both cases, the central new object is the driving vector
F_{0}
. Using the quantum data encoding proposed in Ref.
[
38
]
, preparing
F_{0}
is equivalent to block-encoding a square matrix
\bar{F}_{0}
such that its first column is
F_{0}
. Note that as long as
\|F_{0}\|\leq 1
, one can block-encode
\bar{F}_{0}
with a unit prefactor using a single ancillary qubit. Moreover, the choice of LBE parameters proposed by Ref.
[
38
]
, which guarantees that the norm of the LBE state vector is always bounded by 1, also ensures that the condition
\|F_{0}\|\leq 1
is satisfied.

We start with constructing a block-encoding of
A^{\prime\prime}
. As in the case of
A^{\prime}
analyzed in the previous section, it is straightforward to show that the block-encoding parameters for
A^{\prime\prime}
can be expressed via:

\alpha_{A^{\prime\prime}}=\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}\alpha_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}+1,\qquad n_{A}=n_{\mathcal{D}}+n_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}}+n_{\mathcal{C}}+2.

(49)

Hence, the actual task is to block-encode the Carleman driving matrix
{\mathcal{D}}
. For that, we will use some well-known block-encoding results
[
43
]
. First, employing linear combination of unitaries (LCU), if a matrix
M
can be written as:

M=\sum_{k=1}^{K}M_{k},

(50)

where each
M_{k}
can be block-encoded with a prefactor
\alpha_{M_{k}}
using
n_{M_{k}}
ancillary qubits, then one can construct a block-encoding of
M
with parameters:

\alpha_{M}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{M_{k}},\qquad n_{M}=\log K+\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}n_{M_{k}}.

(51)

A variation of LCU applicable to block-diagonal matrices of the form:

M=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|k\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k\right|\otimes M_{k}

(52)

allows one to block-encode
M
with parameters:

\alpha_{M}=\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\alpha_{M_{k}},\qquad n_{M}=1+\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}n_{M_{k}}.

(53)

Finally, for any
m\in\{1,\dots,N_{C}-1\}
, the following
N_{C}
-dimensional shift matrix,

\Delta_{m}=\sum_{k=m+1}^{N_{C}}\left|k\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k-m\right|,

(54)

can be block-encoded with a unit prefactor using a single ancillary qubit, as it is a partial permutation matrix.

Now, let us write
{\mathcal{D}}
explicitly, using the encoding with the first register keeping track of the Carleman order:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{C}}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\left|k\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle l\right|\otimes D^{k}_{l}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{C}}\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\left|k\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k-m\right|\otimes D^{k}_{k-m}=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{C}-1}\sum_{k=m+1}^{N_{C}}\left(\left|k\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle k\right|\otimes D^{k}_{k-m}\right)\left(\Delta_{m}\otimes I\right)=:\sum_{m=0}^{N_{C}-1}B_{m}.

(55)

Using Eq. (
51
), we then have:

\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{C}-1}\alpha_{B_{m}},\qquad n_{\mathcal{D}}=\log N_{C}+\max_{m\in\{0,\dots,N_{C}-1\}}n_{B_{m}},

(56)

and the problem reduces to constructing block-encodings of
B_{m}
matrices. To achieve this, we can block-encode the two factors of
B_{m}
separately. As already mentioned, the second factor,
\Delta^{m}\otimes I
, can be block encoded with a unit prefactor and one ancillary qubit, whereas for the first factor, we will employ Eq. (
53
). This results in the following block-encoding parameters for
B_{m}
:

\alpha_{B_{m}}=\max_{k\in\{m+1,\dots,N_{C}\}}\alpha_{D^{k}_{k-m}},\qquad n_{B_{m}}=2+\max_{k\in\{m+1,\dots,N_{C}\}}n_{D^{k}_{k-m}}.

(57)

Finally, recalling Eq. (
44
), we note that each
D^{k}_{k-m}
consists of a sum over
\binom{k}{m}
terms, each containing
m
copies of
\bar{F}_{0}
. Hence, using Eq. (
51
), we have:

\alpha_{D^{k}_{k-m}}=\binom{k}{m},\qquad n_{D^{k}_{k-m}}=\log\binom{k}{m}+m.

(58)

Putting it all together, we arrive at:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathcal{D}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{N_{C}-1}\binom{N_{C}}{m}=2^{N_{C}}-1,

(59a)

\displaystyle n_{\mathcal{D}}=

\displaystyle\log N_{C}+2+\max_{m\in\{0,\dots,N_{C}-1\}}\left(\log\binom{N_{C}}{m}+m\right)\leq\log N_{C}+2N_{C}+1.

(59b)

Substituting the expressions derived above, together with the previous results from Eq. (
39
), into Eq. (
49
), we thus conclude that:

\alpha_{A^{\prime\prime}}=(2^{N_{C}}-1)2^{N_{C}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}+1\approx 4^{N_{C}}\alpha_{A},\qquad n_{A^{\prime\prime}}=n_{A}+5N_{C}+\log N_{C}+1.

(60)

Thus, the complexity cost related to extending the algorithm from Ref.
[
38
]
to include both non-trivial geometries and forcing increases the query complexity by a factor of
4^{N_{C}}
, and requires additional
5N_{C}
ancillary qubits (up to leading order in
N_{C}
). As already discussed in the previous section, this cost should not strongly affect the algorithm’s efficiency, as
N_{C}
can be chosen to be small in the sub-threshold regime, due to exponential convergence of the Carleman expansion. However, there are two other sources of potential complexity increase: the difference between the condition numbers of
A
and
A^{\prime\prime}
, and the cost related to state preparation unitary
U_{\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}}
instead of
U_{\bm{b}}
. We postpone the discussion of the first effect to Sec.
4.4
, and now focus on constructing
U_{\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}}
.

Let us first assume that one can efficiently construct a unitary
U_{F_{0}}
that prepares a quantum state
\ket{F_{0}}
that is a normalized version of the driving vector
F_{0}
from Eq. (
42
). Then, as was shown in Ref.
[
28
]
, one can prepare
\ket{{\mathcal{F}}_{0}}
using
O(N_{C})
queries to
U_{F_{0}}
and
O(N_{C})
single qubit gates. Hence, each query to a state preparation unitary
U_{{\mathcal{F}}_{0}}
translates into
O(N_{C})
queries to
U_{F_{0}}
. Next, for simplicity, let us assume that one initializes each node of the fluid system in an equilibrium state corresponding to zero velocity and zero pressure (as is typical in CFD simulations, and as we do in all examples presented in Sec.
4
). This then means that
\bm{y}_{\mathrm{ini}}
is a zero vector. Additionally, adding a single driving step before the start of the evolution, we can rewrite
\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}
as

\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{t^{\star}=0}^{N_{t}}\ket{t^{\star}}\otimes{\mathcal{F}}_{0}.

(61)

Hence, preparing
\ket{\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}}
just requires a uniform state preparation on the time step register and a single preparation of
\ket{{\mathcal{F}}_{0}}
, which in turn requires
O(N_{C})
uses of
U_{F_{0}}
. Therefore, one can conclude that, as long as one can efficiently prepare
\ket{F_{0}}
, one can also efficiently prepare
\ket{\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}}
.

Although the complexity cost of preparing
\ket{F_{0}}
may be significant for random or unstructured driving, in practice one is usually interested in driving with explicit functional dependence on position. For example, in the lid-driven cavity flow and flow past a cylinder, analyzed in the next section, driving has a particularly simple form:

F_{0}=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\star}\in\mathcal{R}}\ket{\bm{r}^{\star}}\otimes\sum_{m=1}^{Q}\phi_{m}\ket{m}.

(62)

In the above,
\mathcal{R}
is a subset of nodes (neighboring the lid and inlet ones, respectively) and
\phi_{m}
are just a constant driving coefficients encoded in a small velocity register (consisting of
2D
qubits for fluid in
D
dimensions). Hence, we just need a uniform state preparation on the position register and a general state preparation on a small velocity register. The complexity cost of preparing
\ket{F_{0}}
is thus constant. Therefore, we will assume that the complexity cost of
U_{\bm{b}^{\prime\prime}}
is negligible compared to the cost of unitary block-encoding
U_{A^{\prime\prime}}
of the linear system.

4
Numerical studies

In Sec.
2
, we introduced a quantum algorithm based on a Carleman embedding of the incompressible lattice Boltzmann method as described in Ref.
[
38
]
. In the previous Section
3
, we demonstrated how this approach can be extended to model non-trivial flows in domains which do not have periodic boundary conditions. In particular, we showed how: no-slip boundary conditions, free-slip boundary conditions, body forcing and non-trivial geometries can be accommodated. In this section, we implement and numerically test these theoretical developments in order to assess the ability of the Carleman embedding to approximate three different flows demanding these extensions.

We focus on the driven Taylor-Green vortex, lid-driven cavity flow, and the flow past a cylinder, enclosed within a simulation region of size
L_{x}\times L_{y}
. They are described by the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\left(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\right)\bm{u}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla p+\nu\nabla^{2}\bm{u},

(63a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(63b)

together with an appropriate set of boundary conditions. In each case, the forcing applied (via the boundary conditions or via a body forcing
\bm{F}
), has an associated characteristic velocity-scale
U
and length-scale
L
, which allows a physical Reynolds number
\mathrm{Re}=UL/\nu
to be constructed. For the lid-driven cavity flow, for example, these scales correspond to the speed of the wall and the cavity size, while for the flow past a cylinder they correspond to the inlet velocity and the cylinder diameter. However, given that we numerically solve the lattice Boltzmann equation, Eq. (
5
), which is non-dimensionalized in terms of lattice units (i.e., using a velocity scale
\Delta x/\Delta t
and length-scale
\Delta x
), it is useful to first establish the relationship between
\mathrm{Re}
and these lattice units. As described in Ref.
[
38
]
, guided by turbulence theory and the scaling required to maintain
\|\bm{g}\|\sim O(1)
, we set:

\mathrm{Ma}\approxeq\frac{u^{\star}_{0}}{c_{s}^{\star}\sqrt{N_{x}N_{y}}},

(64)

to obtain this relation. The dimensionless parameter
u^{\star}_{0}
introduced as a control of the temporal resolution is set to
u^{\star}_{0}=1
unless otherwise stated. For a periodic box, the number of grid-points per direction required to resolve homogeneous isotropic turbulence scales as
N_{x}\gtrsim\eta_{x}\mathrm{Re}^{3/4}
and
N_{y}\gtrsim\eta_{y}\mathrm{Re}^{3/4}
, where
\eta_{i}=L_{i}/L
(see Ref.
[
48
]
). On this basis, we fix
N_{i}=\eta_{i}Re^{\beta}
with
\beta\geq 3/4
, while for the temporal resolution we make use of the advection time-scale
T=L/U
, which non-dimensionally translates into the number of time-steps
N_{t}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\frac{L}{U}
. Using the definition of the Mach number
\mathrm{Ma}=U/(c_{s}^{\star}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t})
, together with Eq. (
64
), we can eliminate
\Delta t
giving
N_{t}=N_{x}N_{y}/(u^{\star}_{0}\sqrt{\eta_{x}\eta_{y}})
. For a given simulation with fixed
\eta_{x}
and
\eta_{y}
, it therefore suffices to specify
\mathrm{Re}
and
\beta
, as this fixes the remaining parameters according to:

N_{x}=\left\lceil\eta_{x}\mathrm{Re}^{\beta}\right\rceil,\qquad N_{y}=\left\lceil\eta_{y}\mathrm{Re}^{\beta}\right\rceil,\qquad N_{t}=\left\lceil N_{x}N_{y}/(u^{\star}_{0}\sqrt{\eta_{x}\eta_{y}})\right\rceil,\quad\tau^{\star}=\frac{3u_{0}^{\star}}{\sqrt{\eta_{x}\eta_{y}}}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}.

(65)

Varying
\beta
and
u^{\star}_{0}
then allows one to control the spatial and temporal resolution if needed.

Our numerical implementation of the classical LBM and Carleman LBM is programmed in such a way that linear operations including streaming (which incorporates the boundary conditions) and forcing are represented as matrices. These matrices are then used by both our classical and Carleman LBM routines when performing these operations. This construction was intentionally chosen, as by validating our classical LBM code, we in-turn validate all linear components of the Carleman LBM. We performed validation tests by correctly reproducing the time evolution of the decaying and forced Taylor-Green vortex with second-order error in both space
O(\Delta x^{2})
and time
O(\Delta t^{2})
(see Fig.
8
in the Appendix). Given that the Taylor-Green vortex does not test the validity of our implementation of the no-slip and slip-free boundary conditions, we also performed our simulations with a second independent code, which we used to verify our implementation.

To assess the ability of the Carleman LBM to correctly reproduce the classical LBM, we use the relative
L_{2}
error:

\epsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}(t^{\star}):=\frac{\|\bm{u}^{N_{C}}(\bm{r}^{\star},t^{\star})-\bm{u}(\bm{r}^{\star},t^{\star})\|}{\|\bm{u}(\bm{r}^{\star},t^{\star})\|},\qquad\epsilon_{C}:=\underset{t^{\star}\in\{1,\cdots,N_{t}\}}{\text{max}}\epsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}(t^{\star}),

(66)

where

and superscript

indicates the Carleman LBM solution with truncation order
N_{C}
. Note that only

is independent of the embedding method. Although it would, in principle, be desirable to fix

4.1
Driven Taylor-Green vortex

In a 2D domain
\Omega:=[0,L]\times[0,L]

\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)=[\sin(kx)\cos(ky),-\cos(kx)\sin(ky)]f(t),\quad p(\bm{r},t)=-\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}(\sin^{2}(kx)+\sin^{2}(ky))f^{2}(t),

(67)

\bm{r}=(x,y)

63a

67

\partial_{t}\bm{u}=0

67

\bm{F}=2\nu k^{2}\bm{u}(\bm{r},t=0).

(68)

Choosing the velocity scale
U_{TG}
and length-scale
L

\mathrm{Re}=U_{TG}L/\nu
characterizes the relative strength of this forcing. In order to test the extension of the Carleman LBM to include driving as described in Sec.
3.2
, we follow the approach of Ref.
[
47

that considered the addition of a divergence-free body force for the incompressible LBM
[
39
]
. For a steady or slowly varying flow such that
\partial_{t}\bm{u}\approxeq 0
, it is consistent to add

\phi_{m}=w_{m}\frac{\bm{e}_{m}\cdot\bm{F}}{c_{s}^{2}}

(69)

to the right hand side of Eq. (
5

67
) is a steady solution of the forced problem, we validated our implementation of the driving is
nearly

4.4
Condition number

As already mentioned in Sec.
3
, the extensions to non-trivial geometries and driven flows introduced in this paper may bring additional complexity cost as compared to the original algorithm from Ref.
[
38
]
, as these modifications may affect the condition number of the linear system from Eq. (
48
). This, in principle, might negate the potential for a quantum advantage reported in Ref.
[
38
]
. However, the numerical results that we will now present paint an optimistic picture, in which this extra cost is small and does not affect the asymptotic scaling of the algorithm’s query complexity.

Due to the immense size of the linear system whose condition number we want to investigate (recall Table
1
), we limit our numerical studies to
\beta=0.75
,
\mathrm{Re}\in[5,20]
,
N_{C}=1,2
, and do not consider the flow past a cylinder case. Using a Lanczos-based algorithm, we first numerically compute the condition number
\kappa_{A_{\mathrm{per}}}
of the linear system describing undriven and periodic LBE, i.e.,
{A_{\mathrm{per}}}
is given by Eq. (
48
) with Carleman driving
{\mathcal{D}}
replaced by an identity matrix and Carleman streaming
{\mathcal{S}}
constructed from the periodic streaming matrix given by Eq. (
23
). Next, we compute
\kappa_{A_{\mathrm{vor}}}
for
{A_{\mathrm{vor}}}
describing the forced Taylor-Green vortex, i.e., again with periodic streaming, but this time with driving captured by Eq. (
69
). Given that the Carleman driving matrix
{\mathcal{D}}
is non-trivial only for
N_{C}\geq 2
(as for
N_{C}=1
driving is accounted for only by the Carleman driving vector
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
), we only perform computations for
N_{C}=2
(as for
N_{C}=1
the results are identical to the undriven periodic case). Finally, we compute
\kappa_{A_{\mathrm{lid}}}
for
{A_{\mathrm{lid}}}
describing the lid-driven cavity flow, where now both the streaming and driving terms differ from the trivial case. As discussed above, for
N_{C}=1
, the driving has no effect and the difference between
\kappa_{A_{\mathrm{lid}}}
and
\kappa_{A_{\mathrm{per}}}

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have extended the recent quantum algorithm for the incompressible lattice Boltzmann method to include a class of flows and geometries that are much closer to those encountered in practical CFD, including walls, inlets, outlets, and body forcing. At the algorithmic level, we have shown how these ingredients can be incorporated into the Carleman streaming and driving operators, how they modify the linear system that underpins the quantum linear-solver–based algorithm, and what is the impact on the complexity of solving it. The resulting block-encodings for non-trivial geometries and driving lead to a controlled overhead, scaling as a multiplicative factor of order
4^{N_{C}}
in the query complexity and a linear in the Carleman truncation order
N_{C}
increase of ancillary qubits, which preserves the asymptotic scaling, and hence the window for a quantum advantage, identified in earlier work
[
38
]
.

To assess the practical consequences of these extensions, we implemented a fully matrix-based classical Carleman LBM and benchmarked it against a standard incompressible LBM for three canonical two-dimensional flows: the driven Taylor–Green vortex, the lid-driven cavity flow, and flow past a cylinder. For the forced Taylor–Green vortex, we observed clear evidence of convergence of the Carleman approximation as the truncation order
N_{C}
is increased, with
N_{C}=3
yielding a substantial reduction in error, and a relative error that saturates rather than grows in time. For the lid-driven cavity, the Carleman scheme with no-slip walls reproduces the main features of the recirculating flow and the associated shear layers. Moving from
N_{C}=1
to
N_{C}=2
significantly reduces the truncation error, even though the error grows with Reynolds number as the flow structure sharpens near the lid and corners. Finally, for flow past a cylinder at low Reynolds numbers, we demonstrated that moving from
N_{C}=1
to
N_{C}=2
again significantly reduces the truncation error for short times and that the same formalism can handle mixed inlet/outlet conditions and embedded obstacles. Note that a recent work
[
37
]
has performed a related Carleman convergence study in a time-continuous lattice Boltzmann setting, i.e., simulating the discrete Boltzmann equation. This was done without the incompressibility assumption, with the Carleman truncation to second order, and for the Kolmogorov and lid-driven cavity flows. Our results are consistent with Ref.
[
37
]
(in particular, in both works the relative error as a function of time plateaus), however they also extend and complement this work by including inlet/outlet boundaries and driving.

From the perspective of quantum resources, our numerical study of the condition number of the Carleman linear system indicates that the inclusion of realistic boundaries and driving does not introduce a qualitatively new bottleneck, at least in the parameter ranges investigated. The dominant algorithmic costs remain those already identified for periodic, undriven flows, and the additional overhead from geometry and forcing enters mainly through the block-encoding prefactor and a modest increase in the number of ancillae. This supports the conclusion that, in regimes where the Carleman expansion converges, the end-to-end quantum LBM pipeline can be extended to more realistic CFD setups without closing the potential window for a quantum speedup. However, this window is currently far narrower than what was initially suggested in Ref.
[
34
]
, which claimed a potential for exponential quantum advantage. Therefore, it strongly motivates more work on pre-conditioning methods, problem-optimized block-encodings, and other algorithmic optimizations. Such advances could have a meaningful impact on the complexity scaling of these Carleman-based algorithms.

Our results also highlight important limitations and open questions. The present study is restricted to two-dimensional, low-Reynolds-number flows and relatively small Carleman orders
N_{C}\leq 3
, dictated by the classical cost of simulating the embedded linear systems. The observed trends in truncation error with Reynolds number and geometry suggest that more complex flows, particularly three-dimensional turbulence, high-Reynolds-number flows, and strongly time-dependent configurations, will require both improved control of the Carleman convergence radius and more efficient representations of the embedded dynamics. Likewise, our analysis did not include observable extraction beyond the drag force already investigated in Ref.
[
38
]
, which is an important line to push forward in future work.

Looking forward, several directions appear particularly promising. On the algorithmic side, it will be important to develop alternative linear embedding schemes adapted for particular CFD problems, preconditioning tailored to the LBM structure, and more economical block-encodings for boundary and forcing operators. On the application side, extending the classical numerical analysis to three dimensions, to higher-Reynolds-number benchmark flows, and eventually to industrially relevant geometries would provide sharper tests of whether practically meaningful quantum advantages can be realized in CFD. In particular, it would be of interest to extend classical simulations of the Carleman LBM implementation to Reynolds numbers at which the transition to time-dependent solutions and vortex shedding emerges, e.g.,
\mathrm{Re}\approxeq 83
for the flow around a cylinder. This would test the ability of the Carleman method to accurately resolve such key physics. More broadly, our results illustrate that end-to-end quantum algorithm designs can be systematically pushed from idealized toy systems towards realistic engineering flows, and that they provide a concrete foundation for future studies at the interface of quantum computing and computational fluid dynamics.
