Spatial dynamics of flexible nano-swimmers under a rotating magnetic field Preprint: APS/123-QED
Chapnik Zvi, Or Yizhar

Abstract
Micro-nano-robotic swimmers have promising potential for future biomedical tasks such as targeted drug delivery and minimally-invasive diagnosis. An efficient method for controlled actuation of such nano-swimmers is applying a rotating external magnetic field, resulting in helical corkscrew-like locomotion. In previous joint work, we presented fabrication and actuation of a simple magnetic nano-swimmer composed of two nano-rods connected by a short elastic hinge. Experiments under different actuation frequencies result in different motion regimes. At low frequencies, in-plane tumbling; at higher frequencies, moving forward in a spatial helical path in synchrony with the rotating magnetic field; in further frequency increase, asynchronous swimming is obtained. In this work, we present mathematical analysis of this nano-swimmer motion. We consider a simple two-link model and explicitly formulate and analyze its nonlinear dynamic equations, and reduce them to a simpler time-invariant system. For the first time, we obtain explicit analytic solutions of synchronous motion under simplifying assumptions, for both solutions of in-plane tumbling and spatial helical swimming. We conduct stability analysis of the solutions, presenting stability transitions and bifurcations for the different solution branches. Furthermore, we present analysis of the influence of additional effects, as well as parametric optimization of the swimmer’s speed. The results of our theoretical study are essential for understanding the nonlinear dynamics of experimental magnetic nano-swimmers for biomedical applications, and conducting practical optimization of their performance.



I
Introduction

Nano-swimmers are of great interest due to their promising potential for biomedical purposes, with applications ranging from internal sensing to targeted drug delivery and even carrying out medical procedures
Li et al. 2017
. In order to understand how to create propulsion in the nanoscale dimensions, where motion is dominated by viscosity, one first needs to examine microorganisms’ motion. Microorganisms such as Salmonella, Helicobacter pylory, or Euglena gracilis are propelled by corkscrew motion of their tail
Crenshaw 1996
, and theoretical models have been developed
Purcell 1977
to investigate their motion in the dominant viscosity regime. The model of a sphere with a tail shows two types of locomotion mechanisms. First, rotation of a helical propeller rigid tail creating corkscrew forward motion; second, planar undulation of a flexible tail creating a forward motion.

In order to fabricate an artificial nano-machine capable of achieving those two types of locomotion, there is a need for actuation. Internal actuation in this scale is not yet applicable mechanisms and would increase the manufacturing cost and complexity of the nanomachine. Thus, external actuation is an easier, practical solution. Using ultrasound, optic, electrical or magnetic forcing to create actuation torque were shown to be applicable
Balk et al. 2014
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2017
.

The most common way is applying a time-varying external magnetic field while the nano-swimmer’s structure contains a magnetic part, resulting in application of a magentic torque actuating the swimmer. Experiments showed that applying a rotating magnetic field on rigid nano-helix with a magnetic heads creates forward locomotion
Dreyfus et al. 2005
;
Zhang et al. 2009a
. However, fabricating such chiral/helical three-dimensional structure in nanoscale is a long and delicate procedure
Wang and Pumera 2015
. In recent years, new kind of flexible nano-swimmers has been fabricated
Gao et al. 2010
;
Jang et al. 2018
, using rigid links connected by a short flexible hinge. Those nano-swimmers are much easier to manufacture. Actuated by a rotating magnetic field, it has been shown
Gao et al. 2010
that such nano-swimmers can also create spatial helical forward locomotion. Such magnetic nano-swimmers display different types of motion under different ranges of rotational frequencies of the magnetic field. At low-frequency, wobble in a plane without net swimming, and in higher frequencies swimming forward with spatial helical corkscrew motion
Gao et al. 2010
. At even higher frequencies, the nano-swimmer’s motion loses its synchronization with the magnetic field’s rotation, an effect named step-out
Ishiyama et al. 2001
.

In order to investigate these phenomena, there is a need to devise a simple mathematical model, which is amenable to explicit analysis. Some models have been developed for a single rigid body, starting from simple magnetic rigid ellipsoid under constant external torque
Ghosh et al. 2013
, showing frequency-dependent stability transitions. Later, a model of a magnetic rigid chiral helix swimmer
Morozov and Leshansky 2014
shows frequency-dependent motion regimes, their stability, transitions, and step-out frequency for the loss of the synchronous motion.

For the flexible nano-swimmer under a rotating magnetic field, experimental results
Gao et al. 2012
followed by elastohydrodynamic model taking into account the elasticity of the nanowire and its hydrodynamic interaction with the fluid medium
Pak et al. 2011
, considered a model with a rigid head and a flexible tail nano-swimmer, showing spatial motion with agreement to the theoretical model. Recent work
Mirzae et al. 2020
presents a multiple bead-spring model, allowing for arbitrary filament geometry and flexibility, and taking into account hydrodynamic interactions. This numerical work of multiple degrees-of-freedom (DOF) model also showed the motion dependence on frequency. In the case of a planar oscillating magnetic field, the work
Gutman and Or 2014
proposed a simple two-link model with a passive revolute joint acted by torsion spring under a planar oscillating magnetic field. Using this simple model allows finding analytically the motion’s dependence on the oscillation frequency, including optimal swimming speed, as well as stability transitions
Harduf et al. 2018
and bifurcations
Paul et al. 2023
.
For the case of a rotating magnetic field, the recent work
Wu et al. 2021
considered the same two-link model in order to study the behavior of the experimental nano-swimmer, and showed, by numerical simulations, the frequency-dependent motion regimes. However, an analytical investigation of the solution regimes, stability analysis, and performance optimization has not been done in
Wu et al. 2021
.

The goal of this work is to complement the numerical simulations in
Wu et al. 2021
by presenting full analytical treatment of the two-link model dynamics. We obtain explicit expressions for the frequency-dependent solutions and their transitions, and conduct numerical optimization of the nano-swimmer’s performance with respect to various parameters.

II
Problem formulation

We now present the two-link model and formulation of its dynamic equations of motion, as derived previously in
Wu et al. 2021
.

Figure 1:
Schematic sketch of the two-link nano-swimmer model. p is the center of the head link, J is the pointed revolute joint point that connects the head and tail link. The joint’s axis of rotation is
\mathbf{{z^{\prime}}}
, whereas the swimmers’ links lie within
\mathbf{{x^{\prime}}}-\mathbf{{y^{\prime}}}
plane.

The nano-swimmer’s simplified model from
Wu et al. 2021
, as shown in Fig.
1
, consists of two rigid links: the magnetic link, named “head” and the nonmagnetic link, named “tail”. A short thin nano-wire connects them, acting as a flexible hinge. The nano-swimmer moves in a 3D environment, where the position of the head link’s center is denoted by coordinates
x,y,z
. In order to describe its orientation, we assign a moving reference frame
\{\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{{z}}}^{\prime}\}
fixed to the center of the head link, whose orientation with respect to the world frame is parametrized by three Euler angles
\phi,\theta,\psi
. The short flexible hinge is represented by a pointed uniaxial revolute joint along a common normal to the longitudinal axes of the head and tail links, which are assumed to lie on a common plane. The relative angle of this joint is denoted by
\delta
, as shown in Figure
1
. The nano-swimmer’s motion is described by a vector of 7 coordinates,
\mathbf{{q}}=(x,y,z,\phi,\theta,\psi,\delta)^{T}
.
The matrix describing the orientation of the rotating reference frame fixed to the head
\{\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{{z}}}^{\prime}\}
with respect to the world reference frame
\{\hat{\mathbf{{x}}},\hat{\mathbf{{y}}},\hat{\mathbf{{z}}}\}
, is obtained as the product of three extrinsic rotation matrices according to the Euler ZXZ convention as

\mathbf{{R}}_{1}=\mathbf{{R}}_{\phi}\mathbf{{R}}_{\theta}\mathbf{{R}}_{\psi}
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{{R}}_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}C_{\phi}\,C_{\psi}\text{-}C_{\theta}\,S_{\phi}\,S_{\psi}&\text{-}C_{\phi}\,S_{\psi}\text{-}C_{\psi}\,C_{\theta}\,S_{\phi}&S_{\phi}\,S_{\theta}\\
C_{\psi}\,S_{\phi}\text{+}C_{\phi}\,C_{\theta}\,S_{\psi}&C_{\phi}\,C_{\psi}\,C_{\theta}\text{-}S_{\phi}\,S_{\psi}&\text{-}C_{\phi}\,S_{\theta}\\
S_{\psi}\,S_{\theta}&C_{\psi}\,S_{\theta}&C_{\theta}\end{array}\right),

where
C_{\phi}\!=\!\cos\phi,C_{\psi}\!=\!\cos\psi,C_{\theta}\!=\!\cos\theta,S_{\phi}\!=\!\sin\phi,S_{\psi}\!=\!\sin\psi,S_{\theta}\!=\!\sin\theta
.
In order to have a unique description for each orientation, we define the ranges of Euler angles as:
-\pi\leq\phi<\pi
,
0<\theta\leq\pi
, and
0\leq\psi<2\pi
.

In order to formulate the dynamics of the system, we now describe the forces and torques acting on the nano-swimmer. First, we consider the hydrodynamic forces and torques. Assuming Stokes flow and neglecting hydrodynamic interaction between the links, the drag force and torque acting upon each link can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\textbf{{f}}}}^{h}_{i}=-c_{t}^{i}(\mathbf{{v}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i})\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i}-c_{n}^{i}(\mathbf{{v}}_{i}-(\mathbf{{v}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{1})\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i})=

(4)

\displaystyle\left((c_{n}^{i}-c_{t}^{i})l_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i}^{T}-c_{n}^{i}\mathbf{{I}}_{3x3}\right)\mathbf{{v}}_{i}

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\tau}}^{h}_{i}=-d_{t}^{i}(\mathbf{{\omega}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i})\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i}-d_{n}^{i}(\mathbf{{\omega}}_{i}-(\mathbf{{v}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{1})\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i})=

(5)

\displaystyle\left((d_{n}^{i}-d_{t}^{i})l_{i}\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{{t}}}_{i}^{T}-c_{n}^{i}\mathbf{{I}}_{3x3}\right)\mathbf{{\omega}}_{i}

where
c_{n}^{i},c_{t}^{i},d_{n}^{i},d_{t}^{i}
for
i=1,2

are the linear and rotational drag coefficients along the normal and tangent to the longitudinal axis of the head and tail links.
The swimmer’s links can be modeled as prolate spheroids or finite slender rods, where drag coefficients for prolate spheroid are given in
Happel and Brenner 2012
;
Jeffery 1922
, and for finite slender rods in
Ortega and Garcıa de la Torre 2003
.

Next, we describe the magnetic effects on the nano-swimmer.
It is assumed that the magnetization vector of the head link is directed along its longitudinal axis
\mathbf{{\hat{t}}}_{1}
and has magnitude
m
.
The external magnetic field is assumed to be spatially uniform and time varying, with rotating component in
x-y
plane, and possibly an additional constant component in
\hat{\mathbf{{z}}}
direction. (Note the differences from
Wu et al. 2021
in the choice of the plane of rotation, as well as the addition of constant component). That is, the magnetic field
\mathbf{{B}}(t)
rotates along the surface of a cone, and is formulated as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{{B}}(t)=b(\sin(\Omega t)\hat{\mathbf{{x}}}+\cos(\Omega t)\hat{\mathbf{{y}}}+h\hat{\mathbf{{z}}})

(6)

The torque acting on the head link due to the rotating magnetic field is:

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}_{m}=m(\mathbf{{\hat{t}}}_{1}\times\mathbf{{B}}(t))

(7)

Lastly, we consider the nano-swimmer’s elasticity.
The short flexible wire connecting the head and tail link is modeled as a pointed uni-axial joint with torsion spring, applying torque:

\displaystyle\tau_{k}=-k\delta

(8)

Due to the negligibility of inertial effects in Stokes flow, the swimmer is always in force and torque balance. Using kinematic relations (see detailed derivation in
Wu et al. 2021
;
Chapnik 2022
), one can write the balance equations and derive the nano-swimmer’s dynamic equations of motion in the form of:

\displaystyle\mathbf{{A}}(\mathbf{{q}})\mathbf{{\dot{q}}}=\mathbf{{b}}(\mathbf{{q}},t)

(9)

This gives a time-dependent system of 7 coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations in
\mathbf{{q}}(t)
, which can be integrated numerically.

:

\begin{split}&k=3.25\times 10^{-17}[N\cdot m],b=15[mT],m=9000[A/m]\\
&l_{1}=l_{2}=7.2[\mu m],\mu=0.0124[N\cdot s/m^{2}]\end{split}

(10)

We integrate the equations of motion (
9
) using Matlab’s

.
In all simulations, we consider initial conditions of orientation angles

In higher frequencies as shown in Figure

IV
Analysis

We now present analytical investigation of the two-link nano-swimmer dynamics. First, we reduce the equations of motion and make some simplifying assumptions that further reduce the number of parameters and simplify the equations. Then we study synchronous solutions, their bifurcations and stability transitions.

IV.1
Reducing the Equations of Motion

In order to investigate the system’s dynamic equations analytically, we now reduce the equations to a more compact form.
First, as shown in Figures
2
,
3
, three of the coordinates
\theta,\psi,\delta
are constant in steady state synchronous motion, but
\phi
is not. Since
\phi
maintains constant phase shift from the magnetic field orientation
\Omega t
, following
Morozov and Leshansky 2014
, we define a new coordinate of phase shift
\beta=\phi-\Omega t
which in steady state is also constant, as shown in Figures
2
and
3
. Using the new coordinates vector:
\tilde{\mathbf{{q}}}=(x,y,z,\beta,\theta,\psi,\delta)^{T}
, the system’s equations of motion (
9
) are obtained as:

\tilde{\mathbf{{A}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{{q}}})\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{{q}}}}=\tilde{\mathbf{{b}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{{q}}})

(11)

Importantly, this new system is now time-invariant, and in the synchronous steady state, four coordinates converge to constant values, implying that it is an equilibrium state for those coordinates:

\dot{\theta}=\dot{\beta}=\dot{\psi}=\dot{\delta}=0
. In Figures
2
,
3
, the numerical simulations results for the coordinate
\phi
and the coordinate
\beta=\phi-\Omega t
are presented. One can see
that
\beta(t)
in both cases converges to constant values in steady state, after a transient response.

In order to further reduce the system’s dynamic equations, we decompose the coordinates
\tilde{\mathbf{{q}}}
into displacement part
\mathbf{{q}}_{d}
and angular part
,\mathbf{{q}}_{a}
as:

\tilde{\mathbf{{q}}}=\left(\mathbf{{q}}_{d},\mathbf{{q}}_{a}\right)^{T},\mathbf{{q}}_{d}=\left(x,y,z\right)^{T},\mathbf{{q}}_{a}=\left(\beta,\theta,\psi,\delta\right)^{T}

(12)

Then, we decompose the matrices
\mathbf{{\tilde{A},\tilde{b}}}
from (
11
) into blocks as:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{{A}}_{dd}&\mathbf{{A}}_{da}\\
\mathbf{{A}}_{ad}&\mathbf{{A}}_{aa}\end{matrix}\right)}_{\mathbf{{\tilde{A}}}(\mathbf{{q}}_{a})}\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{{\dot{q}}}_{d}\\
\mathbf{{\dot{q}}}_{a}\end{matrix}\right)=\underbrace{\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{{0}}\\
\mathbf{{g}}\end{matrix}\right)}_{\mathbf{{\tilde{b}}}(\mathbf{{q}}_{a})}

(13)

Note that equations (
13
) depend only on the angles
\mathbf{{q}}_{a}
due to translational invariance. The translational velocity vector
\mathbf{{\dot{q}}}_{d}
can thus be extracted from (
13
) as:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{{q}}}_{d}=-(\mathbf{{A}}_{dd})^{-1}\mathbf{{A}}_{da}\dot{\mathbf{{q}}}_{a}

(14)

By substituting (
14
) to (
13
), one obtains:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{{A}}_{ad}\left(-(\mathbf{{A}}_{dd})^{-1}\mathbf{{A}}_{da}\right)+\mathbf{{A}}_{aa}\right)}_{\mathbf{{A^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{{q}}_{a})}\dot{\mathbf{{q}}}_{a}=\mathbf{{g}}(\mathbf{{q}}_{a})

(15)

Thus, the system is reduced to a 4-DOF time-invariant system in
\mathbf{{q}}_{a}
only. Assuming
\mathbf{{A^{\prime}}}
is an invertible matrix, one can solve the equilibrium equation
\mathbf{{\dot{q}}}_{a}=0
from the right side of equation (
15
) as:

\mathbf{{g}}(\mathbf{{q}}_{a})=\mathbf{{0}}

(16)

Solutions
\mathbf{{q}}_{a}=\mathbf{{q}}_{e}
of equation (
16
) correspond to steady-state synchronous motion.

IV.2
Simplifying Assumptions and Scaling

The dynamic equations (
15
) depend on 15 physical constants, which calls for simplifications and reduction.
We now apply the following simplifying assumptions in order to reduce the number of parameters. First, we assume a planar magnetic field with no
\hat{\mathbf{{z}}}
component,
h=0
in (
6
). Second, we consider equal links’ lengths
l_{1}=l_{2}=l
. (These two assumptions are relaxed later in Section
V
). Third, drag coefficients are simplified, assuming slender prolate spheroids according to
Happel and Brenner 2012
;
Jeffery 1922
. We use the following assumptions to reduce the number of drag coefficients. The translational coefficients are approximated as
c_{t}\approx\frac{1}{2}c_{n}
. For the rotational drag coefficients, since slender bodies satisfy
d_{t}\ll d_{n}
, we neglect it and assume
d_{t}=0
. We also take
d_{n}=c_{n}l^{2}/12
. These simplifications are fairly accurate approximation for slender spheroids
Happel and Brenner 2012
;
Jeffery 1922
.

Non-dimensional equations:

First, we define
l
as the characteristic length. Next, we define two characteristic time scales,
t_{m}
the magnetic characteristic time and
t_{k}
the stiffness characteristic time:

t_{m}=\frac{c_{t}l^{2}}{bm},t_{k}=\frac{c_{t}l^{2}}{k},

(17)

Follwoing
Gutman and Or 2014
, the ratio between these two time scales is defined as the nondimensional parameter
\alpha=t_{m}/t_{k}=k/bm
.

We scale the time in (
15
) by the magnetic characteristic time
t_{m}
, and define the nondimensional frequency
\omega=\Omega t_{m}
. The scaled and reduced dynamic equations now have the form:

\mathbf{{A^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{{q}}_{a})\mathbf{{\dot{q}}}_{a}=\mathbf{{g}}(\mathbf{{q}}_{a})

(18)

Where “dot” now represents derivative with respect to the scaled time

. The matrix

in (

) is given in Table
1
, where

.

IV.3
Planar Tumbling Solution

We now solve the equilibrium equations (
16
) for planar tumbling motion. The equilibrium values of
\mathbf{{q}}_{a}
will be denoted by
\mathbf{{q}}_{e}=(\beta_{e},\theta_{e},\psi_{e},\delta_{e})
. Looking at the first two terms in (
19
), one can easily observe that they both vanish for
\sin(\theta_{e})=0
, meaning a planar solution.

\sin\theta_{e}=0\rightarrow\theta_{e}=\pi

(21)

By substituting this solution into the other terms in (
19

\displaystyle\cos\beta_{e}\sin\psi_{e}-\left(\sin\beta_{e}\cos\psi_{e}+\omega a_{3}(\delta_{e})\right)=0

\displaystyle\alpha\delta_{e}=-\omega a_{4}(\delta_{e})=\omega\frac{6+\cos(\delta_{e})\left(4+\frac{1}{3}\cos(\delta_{e})\right)}{2\left(9-\cos^{2}(\delta_{e}\right))}

Where

are given in (
20

Equation (

\omega

VI
Concluding discussion

In this work, we revisited the simplified two-link model of a nano-swimmer with flexible hinge under a rotating magnetic field, which was studied numerically and demonstrated experimentally in
Wu et al. 2021
. We explicitly formulated and analyzed the nano-swimmer’s nonlinear dynamic equations of spatial motion. We reduced the dynamic equations to a 4-DOF time-invariant system using transformation of variables. We obtained explicit analytic solutions for synchronous motion under simplifying assumptions, for both regimes of in-plane tumbling and spatial helical corkscrew swimming. We conducted stability analysis of the solution branches, and found stability transitions and bifurcations of multiple solution branches. We obtained an explicit expression for the forward speed and pitch, and found optimal frequencies maximizing speed and pitch. Finally, we presented analysis of the influence of additional effects such as unequal links length and conically-rotating magnetic field, as well as numerical parametric optimization.

We now briefly discuss the limitations of our current analysis. First, the suggested swimmer model is simple and does not represent the fabricated swimmer accurately. It assumed a pointed uni-axial hinge, while the actual hinge is a flexible beam that undergoes both bending and torsion. In addition, our model did not account for hydrodynamic interaction between the links, and did not consider the influence of boundaries of the fluid domains. All those effects can be implemented into a new swimmer model in possible future extension of the research. Other possible generalizations can be interesting for future research, such as the effect of varying the directionality of link’s magnetization, nano-swimmer composed of multiple magnetic links, flexible links, and additional choices in the time-varying profile of the external magnetic field.

Despite all limitations, this work showed a simple model that captures most of dominating phenomena observed in experiments on the fabricated nano-swimmer, allowing us to better understand the dynamics of magnetic nano-swimmers. With this improved understanding, new and optimized magnetic nano-swimmers may be fabricated, which eventually will allow the designing of artificial magnetic nano-bots for bio-medical purposes advancing towards the grand challenge of micro-nano-biomedical robotics.
