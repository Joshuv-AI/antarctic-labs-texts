CMV PCR Monitoring for Hematological Malignancy
Authors: Hinpetch Daungsupawong, Viroj Wiwanitkit
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We are writing in response to the article titled “Is CMV PCR Monitoring Necessary for Hematological Malignancy During Chemotherapy? A Prospective Single-Center Study,” published in this issue of the journal [1]. Although this research has strengths in its prospective design and consistent monitoring of cytomegalovirus (CMV)-DNA levels using twice-weekly polymerase chain reaction, it also has several methodological limitations. Being a single-center study limits its applicability to populations in other contexts. The inclusion of patients with both leukemia and lymphoma receiving different chemotherapy regimens may lead to inconsistencies in the risk of CMV reactivation. Furthermore, using the number of hospitalization episodes as the unit of analysis instead of individual patients may have resulted in repeated measures, affecting data independence. Without statistical models that support such data, the results may be biased.

Thus, the results should be interpreted with caution. While post-neutropenia lymphopenia and comorbidities are independent risk factors for CMV reactivation, these associations may reflect the severity of immunodeficiency rather than being direct causes. Additionally, there is a lack of data on CMV serostatus before treatment initiation, chemotherapy intensity, corticosteroid or other immunosuppressant use, and comorbidity burden, all of which are significant confounders, and this may have led to an incomplete assessment of the influence of risk factors.

Another important point is that CMV-DNA monitoring alone may not fully reflect clinical significance, as some patients may have viremia without developing disease, while others may develop symptoms due to other comorbidities. Further analysis of viral load threshold, CMV reactivation time, survival outcomes, length of hospital stay, treatment costs, and the effectiveness of preemptive therapy compared to post-disease treatment will help to determine more appropriate and cost-effective surveillance guidelines.

In the future, larger multicenter studies classifying patients by cancer type, chemotherapy regimen, immunological level, and CMV serology status before treatment initiation should be conducted. The application of time-to-event models, longitudinal analysis, and the development of predictive models based on clinical data, along with biomarkers and artificial intelligence techniques, may help to identify patient groups that should receive intensive CMV screening or preemptive therapy more accurately. This will lead to precision supportive care, reducing complications, improving treatment efficacy, and optimizing the use of medical resources.

We thank Daungsupawong and Wiwanitkit for their commentary on our article, “Is CMV PCR Monitoring Necessary for Hematological Malignancy During Chemotherapy? A Prospective Single-Center Study” [1], and for raising several methodological points. We welcome the opportunity to clarify these issues, several of which were in fact already addressed in the original manuscript, and to acknowledge the points that genuinely warrant discussion.

First, we agree that a single-center design limits generalizability, and this was explicitly stated as the first item in our section on “Study Limitations.” We would also note, however, that the cohort was drawn from 316 consecutively enrolled, prospectively followed patients across 504 chemotherapy-related hospitalization episodes over an 18-month period, which is a comparatively large prospective sample for this specific non-transplant population, where, as we noted in the introduction of our study, existing data remain limited. We fully agree that multicenter validation is the logical next step, and we stated this explicitly in our conclusion.

Regarding the heterogeneity of diagnoses and chemotherapy regimens, the letter suggests that including both leukemia and lymphoma patients receiving different regimens may have introduced inconsistency in CMV reactivation risk. This heterogeneity was not overlooked: diagnosis category (leukemia vs. lymphoma) was entered as a covariate in both the univariate and multivariate logistic regression models (Table 4 of the original study), and regimen type (rituximab-based, platinum-based, gemcitabine-based) and number of chemotherapy lines were likewise included as independent variables. Lymphoma diagnosis and rituximab-based therapy were both significantly associated with CMV viremia in univariate analysis, and this association is addressed at length in the discussion of the study with reference to B-cell depletion as a plausible mechanism. Far from being a confound left unaddressed, this heterogeneity was analyzed directly and is one of the more informative findings of the study.

Furthermore, the letter lists pretreatment CMV serostatus, corticosteroid use, and chemotherapy intensity among confounders that were not accounted for. We would like to clarify that all three were in fact assessed. Serological screening at baseline showed that all 316 patients were CMV-IgG positive (CMV-IgM negative), consistent with the near-universal CMV seroprevalence reported in the adult Turkish population. Because serostatus did not vary across the cohort, it could not function as a differentiating covariate in this dataset, though we agree it is an essential parameter to record and report, and we did so. High-dose corticosteroid use (>1 mg/kg prednisolone-equivalent for ≥5 consecutive days) was included in the univariate model (Table 4) and was not significantly associated with CMV viremia (p=0.37) in our cohort. Treatment intensity was represented through regimen type and number of prior chemotherapy lines, both included as covariates. However, we agree that comorbidity burden was not systematically captured (e.g., via the Charlson Comorbidity Index or a similar instrument), and we acknowledge this as a genuine limitation that should be addressed in future multicenter studies.

The use of hospitalization episodes as the unit of analysis is, in our view, the most substantive point raised in the letter and merits a direct response. We chose hospitalization episodes rather than individual patients as the analytical unit because the key exposures of interest, including post-neutropenia lymphopenia, presence of co-infection, and disease status (remission vs. relapsed/refractory), are time-varying: a given patient’s exposure status can differ meaningfully from one chemotherapy course to the next. An episode-level analysis was therefore necessary to capture exposure-outcome relationships at the time they actually occurred, rather than collapsing a patient’s varying risk profile into a single time-invariant summary. We acknowledge, however, that with 504 episodes arising from 316 patients (a mean of approximately 1.6 episodes per patient), some patients contributed more than one episode to the dataset, and our logistic regression model did not apply a clustering correction (e.g., generalized estimating equations or a mixed-effects model with a patient-level random intercept) to account for the resulting within-patient correlation. This is a legitimate methodological limitation that was not explicitly listed among our stated “Study Limitations,” and we thank Daungsupawong and Wiwanitkit for highlighting it. We agree that future prospective multicenter analyses of recurrent CMV reactivation events should apply clustering-adjusted models to confirm whether the magnitude and significance of the identified risk factors (post-neutropenic lymphopenia, co-infection) are preserved once within-patient correlation is formally modeled.

Regarding the clinical significance of CMV-DNA monitoring, we agree that CMV-DNA positivity alone does not equate to clinical disease, which is precisely why our study distinguished asymptomatic CMV DNAemia from CMV disease using the standardized definitions of the CMV Drug Development Forum [2], and why we report outcomes separately for viremia (Tables 3 and 4) and for organ-involving disease (CMV pneumonia, CMV retinitis). Of the 48 episodes with CMV-DNA positivity, only 13 (27%) met the criteria for CMV disease, and this distinction is discussed explicitly in the results and discussion sections. We agree, however, that further work on viral load thresholds predictive of progression to disease, time-to-reactivation modeling, and cost-effectiveness of preemptive versus disease-triggered therapy would meaningfully extend these findings, and we view these as natural next steps rather than shortcomings of the present descriptive analysis.

In conclusion, we are grateful to Daungsupawong and Wiwanitkit for their letter, which allows us to reiterate several points that were already addressed in our analysis (diagnosis heterogeneity, corticosteroid use, treatment intensity, CMV serostatus, and the DNAemia/disease distinction), while also acknowledging two genuine limitations—the absence of a clustering correction for repeated episodes per patient and the lack of a formal comorbidity index—that we had not explicitly stated and that should be addressed in future multicenter, prospective work. None of these points, in our assessment, alter the central finding of the study: routine polymerase chain reaction-based CMV surveillance in non-transplanted patients with hematological malignancies identifies a clinically meaningful rate of reactivation, concentrated among patients with post-neutropenic lymphopenia and concurrent infection, and this supports continued, structured CMV monitoring in this population pending larger confirmatory studies.

Sincerely,
