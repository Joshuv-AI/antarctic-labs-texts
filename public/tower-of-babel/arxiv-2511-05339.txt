Convexity and strict convexity for compositional neural networks in high-dimensional optimal control
Lars Grüne 1 , Konrad Kleinberg 2 , Thomas Kruse 3 , Mario Sperl 4 1 Mathematical Institute, University of Bayreuth, Germany; e-mail: lars.gruene \texttt{a}⃝ uni-bayreuth.de 2 Department of Mathematics & Informatics, University of Wuppertal, Germany; e-mail: kleinberg \texttt{a}⃝ uni-wuppertal.de 3 Department of Mathematics & Informatics, University of Wuppertal, Germany; e-mail: tkruse \texttt{a}⃝ uni-wuppertal.de 4 Mathematical Institute, University of Bayreuth, Germany; e-mail: mario.sperl \texttt{a}⃝ uni-bayreuth.de

Abstract
Neural networks (NNs) have emerged as powerful tools for solving high-dimensional optimal control problems. In particular, their compositional structure has been shown to enable efficient approximation of high-dimensional functions, helping to mitigate the curse of dimensionality in optimal control problems. In this work, we build upon the theoretical framework developed by Kang & Gong (SIAM J. Control Optim. 60(2):786-813, 2022), particularly their results on NN approximations for compositional functions in optimal control. Theorem 6.2 in Kang & Gong (SIAM J. Control Optim. 60(2):786-813, 2022) establishes that, under suitable assumptions on the compositional structure and its associated features, optimal control problems with strictly convex cost functionals admit a curse-of-dimensionality-free approximation of the optimal control by NNs. We extend this result in two directions. First, we analyze the strict convexity requirement on the cost functional and demonstrate that reformulating a discrete-time optimal control problem with linear transitions and stage costs as a terminal cost problem ensures the necessary strict convexity. Second, we establish a generalization of Theorem 6.2 in Kang & Gong (SIAM J. Control Optim. 60(2):786-813, 2022) which provides weak error bounds for optimal control approximations by NNs when the cost functional is only convex rather than strictly convex.

Introduction

High-dimensional problems arise naturally in a multitude of applications, e.g., partial differential equations, (stochastic) optimal control, and stability of dynamical systems.
For such tasks, many conventional numerical algorithms are subject to the curse of dimensionality (COD) (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
29
,
30
]
). This means that their computational complexity to obtain a solution grows exponentially with the problem dimension and thus renders such algorithms impractical in high dimensions.
The utilization of machine learning, in particular through (deep) neural networks (NNs), has demonstrated empirical success in various high-dimensional tasks which were previously intractable due to the COD (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
4
,
8
,
2
,
1
,
24
,
35
,
22
]
).

The COD is particularly pronounced in optimal control when grid or mesh-based methods are employed to solve the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equations.
Early contributions already investigated the use of neural networks to approximate optimal value functions or feedback laws in nonlinear control problems (see, e.g.,
[
36
,
27
,
5
]
). More recently, this line of research has been extended to high-dimensional settings, where neural networks have been employed to approximate optimal value functions and compute feedback laws in problems with state dimensions beyond the reach of traditional grid-based approaches (see, e.g.,
[
20
,
21
,
26
,
25
,
38
]
).

It is important to emphasize that, although neural networks (NNs) are universal approximators for continuous functions (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
15
]
), in general, NN approximations of functions with high-dimensional input are subject to the COD (see, e.g.,
[
23
,
31
,
13
]
).
Thus, the objective of attaining theoretical guarantees for when and why NNs overcome the COD has inspired a broad and active field of research. A variety of such results have been obtained in different contexts: for instance, for (control) Lyapunov functions
[
12
,
37
]
, for partial (integro-)differential equations
[
16
,
10
]
, and for problems in (stochastic) optimal stopping and control
[
33
,
18
,
11
,
17
]
.

One particular mechanism that allows NNs to mitigate the COD is when the target function exhibits favorable structural properties, such as a compositional structure in terms of lower-dimensional components. The role of compositionality in enabling efficient approximation has been emphasized in
[
32
]
and investigated further in various directions (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
19
,
9
,
34
,
7
,
12
,
37
]
).

In this paper, we explore the role of compositionality in optimal control problems to obtain curse-of-dimensionality-free representations using neural networks. To this end, we build on the theory of neural network approximation for compositional functions developed in
[
18
]
, with a particular focus on the results in Section 6 in this reference. It has been established in
[
18
, Theorem 6.2]
that, under suitable assumptions on the compositional structure and its so-called compositional features, optimal control problems with terminal costs (also known as Mayer term) that are strictly convex in the control admit a curse-of-dimensionality-free representation of the optimal control.
We extend this result in two main directions. First, we examine the strict convexity assumption on the terminal cost. We demonstrate that the reformulation of a linear optimal control problem with stage costs as a terminal cost problem ensures the necessary strict convexity. Second, we prove a generalization of
[
18
, Theorem 6.2]
that requires only convexity in the control input, rather than strict convexity. This relaxation comes at the expense that the network yields a control that only approximates the optimal value (a so-called “weak approximation”) rather than a control that approximates the optimal trajectory (a so-called “strong approximation”).

2
The compositional framework and applications in optimal control

2.1
Preliminaries on compositional functions

Throughout this paper, we use the concept of compositional functions as introduced in
[
18
, Definition 3.1]
and discussed thereafter. To ensure that our exposition is self-contained, we provide a brief summary of the main aspects in the following.

Definition 2.1

(Definition 3.1 in
[
18
]
)
.

A compositional function is a triplet
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
, where

(i)

\mathcal{G}^{f}=(\mathcal{V}^{f},\mathcal{E}^{f})
is a directed acyclic graph with node set
\mathcal{V}^{f}
and edge set
\mathcal{E}^{f}\subset\mathcal{V}^{f}\times\mathcal{V}^{f}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{L}^{f}\colon\mathcal{V}^{f}\to\mathbb{N}
assigns a layer number for each node in
\mathcal{V}^{f}
such that

(g,h)\in\mathcal{E}^{f}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{L}^{f}(g)<\mathcal{L}^{f}(h).

Nodes with no inward edge are called input nodes and have layer number
0
, while nodes with no outward edge are called output nodes and have layer number

l^{f}_{\max}=\max_{g\in\mathcal{V}^{f}}\mathcal{L}^{f}(g).

All remaining nodes are called hidden nodes.

(iii)

For
0\leq i\leq l^{f}_{\max}
we denote the set of all nodes with layer number
i
as
\{f_{i,1},\dots,f_{i,N_{i}}\}
.
Each node
f_{i,j}
with
i>0
,
1\leq j\leq N_{i}
, is a function

f_{i,j}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{i,j}}\to\mathbb{R},

where
d_{i,j}
is the number of incoming edges to
f_{i,j}
in
\mathcal{G}^{f}
.

(iv)

With
d:=N_{0}
, we assign an input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the input nodes
f_{0,1},\dots f_{0,d}
. Then, recursively we define for all remaining nodes
f_{i,j}

z_{i,j}(x)=f_{i,j}\big((z_{i-1,k}(x))_{(f_{i-1,k},f_{i,j})\in\mathcal{E}^{f}}\big),

where the vector
(z_{i-1,k}(x))_{(f_{i-1,k},f_{i,j})\in\mathcal{E}^{f}}
collects the outputs of all predecessors
f_{i-1,k}
of
f_{i,j}
.

(v)

Finally, with
q:=N_{l^{f}_{\max}}
, the function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
is given by

f(x)=\big(z_{f_{l^{f}_{\max},1}}(x),\dots,z_{f_{l^{f}_{\max},q}}(x)\big).

Figure 1:
Illustration of a compositional function.

Figure
1
illustrates the structure of a compositional function as introduced in Definition
2.1
. For simplicity, we occasionally refer to a compositional function
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
simply as
f
in the following. For a compositional function, we denote the set of nodes that are either an input node or that represent a linear affine function by
\mathcal{V}_{L}^{f}
. The set of general nodes is defined as
\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}:=\mathcal{V}^{f}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{L}^{f}
. Altogether, this notion of compositional functions allows to express how a mapping
f
is built from the lower dimensional functions
f_{i,j}
of its nodes in
\mathcal{G}^{f}
. Throughout this paper, we adopt the following assumption on compositional functions, which is taken from
[
18
, Assumption 1]
.

Assumption 2.2
.

We assume that
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
is a compositional function, where
f
maps from
[-R,R]^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{q}
for some
R>0
and
d,q\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. Further, we assume that the nodes
f_{i,j}\in\mathcal{V}^{f}_{G}
are functions in
W^{\infty}_{m_{i,j}}([-R_{i,j},R_{i,j}]^{d_{i,j}})
for some
m_{i,j},d_{i,j}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and
R_{i,j}>0
. The ranges and domains of all nodes are compatible for composition, i.e., if
(f_{i,j},f_{l,k})
is an edge in
\mathcal{G}^{f}
, then the range of
f_{i,j}
is contained in the interior of the domain of
f_{l,k}
.

In the spirit of Definition 4.3 in
[
18
]
, we view a neural network (
\mathrm{NN}
) as a compositional function of a particular type.

Definition 2.3

(Definition 4.3 in
[
18
]
)
.

A neural network with activation function
\sigma
is a compositional function
(f^{\mathrm{NN}},\mathcal{G}^{f^{\mathrm{NN}}},\mathcal{L}^{f^{\mathrm{NN}}})
, where each function
f_{i,j}^{\mathrm{NN}}\in\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f^{\mathrm{NN}}}
is of the form

f_{i,j}^{\mathrm{NN}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d_{i,j}}\to\mathbb{R},\quad x\mapsto\sigma(w^{T}x+b),

where
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i,j}}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
are parameters assigned to
f_{i,j}^{\mathrm{NN}}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i},j}
is the vector of input values to the node
f_{i,j}^{\mathrm{NN}}
. Further, each function in the output layer is given as a weighted sum of the nodes in the previous layer. The complexity of a
\mathrm{NN}
is defined as total number of hidden nodes in
\mathcal{G}^{f^{\mathrm{NN}}}
denoted by
\operatorname{size}(f^{\mathrm{NN}})\in\mathbb{N}
.

A neural network with one hidden layer is called a shallow neural network. For compositional functions satisfying Assumption
2.2
a quantitative approximation result with neural networks has been shown in
[
18
, Theorem 4.10]
. There, the main idea is to replace each general node of the given compositional function with a shallow neural network. This results in a neural network that approximates the given compositional function. The resulting approximation error depends on the so-called features of a compositional function. For the convenience of the reader, we re-state the definition of the compositional features and the approximation result here:

Definition 2.4

(Definition 4.1 in
[
18
]
)
.

Let
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
satisfy Assumption
2.2
. Then the numbers
\big(r_{\max}^{f},\Lambda^{f},L_{\max}^{f},\lvert\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}\rvert\big)\in(0,\infty)^{4}
are called
features
of
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
if
r_{\max}^{f},\Lambda^{f},L_{\max}^{f}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
satisfy for all
f_{i,j}\in\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}
that

\displaystyle d_{i,j}m_{i,j}^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq r_{\max}^{f},

\displaystyle\max\left\{R_{i,j}^{m_{i,j}},1\right\}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m_{i,j}}\|D_{\alpha}f_{i,j}\|_{\infty,\Omega_{i,j}}

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda^{f},

\displaystyle\lvert L_{i,j}^{f}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq L_{\max}^{f},

where
\Omega_{i,j}=[-R_{i,j},R_{i,j}]^{d_{i,j}}
and
L_{i,j}^{f}
is a Lipschitz constant associated to
f_{i,j}
, see
[
18
, Definition 3.6]
.

In the remainder of this paper, the notation
\big(r_{\max}^{f},\Lambda^{f},L_{\max}^{f},\lvert\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{G}}^{f}\rvert\big)
will always indicate compositional features of a compositional function
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
, even if this is not explicitly mentioned.

Theorem 2.5

(Theorem 4.10 in
[
18
]
)
.

Let
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
satisfy Assumption
2.2
and let
\big(r_{\max}^{f},\Lambda^{f},L_{\max}^{f},\lvert\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}\rvert\big)
be features of
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
. Then for any
n_{w}\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
\mathrm{NN}

f^{\mathrm{NN}}
with
\operatorname{size}(f^{\mathrm{NN}})=n_{w}\lvert\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}\rvert
such that for all
x\in[-R,R]^{d}
it holds that

\lVert f(x)-f^{\mathrm{NN}}(x)\rVert\leq C\Lambda^{f}L_{\max}^{f}\lvert\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}\rvert n_{w}^{-\frac{1}{r_{\max}^{f}}},

where
C>0
is a constant determined by
\{(d_{i,j},m_{i,j})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:f_{i,j}\in\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}\}
.

2.2
Optimal control with compositionality

We aim to leverage the fact that compositional functions can be approximated well with neural networks for high-dimensional optimal control problems. Our starting point is the result stated in Theorem 6.2 in
[
18
]
. There, the authors consider an optimal control problem with dynamics
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
over a fixed time horizon
t\in[0,T]
together with a terminal cost functional of the form
J(x,U)=\Psi(\Phi(x,U))
, where
\Psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\Phi(x,U)
denotes the state of the system starting at
x
and and controlled using a piecewise constant control over
N\in\mathbb{N}
time steps specified by a vector
U\in\mathbb{R}^{qN}
. The statement of
[
18
, Theorem 6.2]
is that, under suitable assumptions, the optimal control can be approximated with a
\mathrm{NN}
whose complexity grows sub-exponentially with the dimension. The main ingredients required for the result are:

1.

The compositional structure of both
f
and
\Psi
,

2.

Suitable assumptions on the growth of the involved compositional features,

3.

Positive-definiteness of the Hessian of
J(x,U)
with respect to
U
at all considered initial values
x
.

We refer to Section 6 in
[
18
]
for the detailed description of the theorem and its assumptions.
The following example shows that Item
3
above, i.e., strict convexity of
J(x,U)
with respect to
U
does not hold in general, even if the dynamics are linear and
\Psi
is strictly convex.

Example 2.6
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
let
\Psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
that
\Psi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)>0
, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
We consider a discrete-time optimal control problem with
N
time steps, dynamics governed by

x^{+}=ax+bu,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad u\in\mathbb{R},

(2.1)

and terminal costs
\Psi
, allowing for discrete-time control inputs
U=[u_{0},\dots,u_{N-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where the control
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is applied at stage
k
. By induction one shows that the terminal stage of the system given an initial state
x\in\mathbb{R}
and a sequence of control inputs
U=[u_{0},\dots,u_{N-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
can be described by
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Phi(x,U)=a^{N}x+b\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}a^{N-1-j}u_{j}
. The associated cost functional to be mimimized is thus given by
J(x,U)=\Psi(\Phi(x,U))
. The Hessian of
J
w.r.t.
U\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is of the form

D^{2}_{U}J(x,U)=vv^{\top}b^{2}\Psi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\Phi(x,U)),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad U\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

(2.2)

where
v=(a^{N-1},a^{N-2},\dots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Note that for all
N\geq 2
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
it holds that
\det(D^{2}_{U}J(x,U))=0
, since the Hessian is a rank-1 matrix. Hence it holds for all
N\geq 2
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
that
D_{U}^{2}J(x,U)\not\succ 0
. Moreover, it holds for all
N\geq 2
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
that
w^{\top}D_{U}^{2}J(x,U)w\geq 0
. Thus,
J
is convex but not strictly convex w.r.t.
U
.

2.6
motivates the investigation of the two following directions: First, in
Section

3
, we introduce stage costs to gain strict convexity in the cost functional for linear dynamics. Second, we study how the approximation result can be extended to settings where only convexity, but not strict convexity, is present. In
Section

4
, we weaken the strict convexity requirement of Theorem 6.2 in
[
18
]
and establish an approximation result for convex setups. Since optimal controls are not necessarily unique without strict convexity, the result is stated in a weak form: instead of comparing the neural network output with
the
optimal control, we compare its performance with the performance of
an
optimal control, where performance is measured via the cost functional.

Remark 2.7
.

While our focus in this work is on discrete-time optimal control problems, we note that the extension to continuous-time dynamics is also possible. In particular, Theorem 5.1 in
[
18
]
shows that continuous-time systems can be approximated via Euler discretization in a manner that preserves the compositional structure relevant for neural network approximation.

3.1
Strict convexity for problems with linear dynamics and separated stage costs

The following remark provides a representation of the state in case of linear dynamics. This is utilized in proving strict convexity of the cost functional in the case of separated stage costs (see

Remark 3.2

4
Weak approximations for non-strictly convex problems

In this section, we address the second direction that emerged from our discussion following Example
2.6
. Specifically, we study settings in which only convexity rather than strict convexity of the cost functional with respect to the control vector
U
is present and prove a generalization of
[
18
, Theorem 6.2]
to this case. As a result of this relaxed convexity assumption, the uniqueness of minimizers is no longer guaranteed. Consequently, the approximation of the optimal control by neural networks can only be established in a weak sense, that is, we measure the performance of the NN approximation through closeness of the cost functional values instead of comparing the resulting control and the optimal control as in the strictly convex setting.

4.1

\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\left\lVert f(x)-\tilde{f}(x)\right\rVert\leq\varepsilon
for all
x\in\Omega
. Then for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\Omega
it holds that

\left\lVert f^{k}(x)-\tilde{f}^{k}(x)\right\rVert\leq k\varepsilon.

Proof of
Lemma

4.2
.

The assertion holds for
k=1
by assumption. Suppose that it is true for
k-1
. Then we obtain for
x\in\Omega

\displaystyle\left\lVert f^{k}(x)-\tilde{f}^{k}(x)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f^{k}(x)-f(\tilde{f}^{k-1}(x))\right\rVert+\left\lVert f(\tilde{f}^{k-1}(x))-\tilde{f}^{k}(x)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f^{k-1}(x)-\tilde{f}^{k-1}(x)\right\rVert+\varepsilon\leq(k-1)\varepsilon+\varepsilon=k\varepsilon.

∎

4.2
Neural networks approximations for optimizers in non-strictly convex minimization problems

In the following theorem, we address an optimization problem whose cost function is convex. Assuming that this function can be approximated arbitrarily well by a neural network, we construct a neural network that approximates an optimizer and establish an associated error bound together with the required network complexity. This result extends Theorem 6.1 in
[
18
]
to the case of convex functions and serves as the main building block for the subsequent result on neural network approximations of optimal controls in convex problems.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be compact,
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be convex, and
J\colon\Omega\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(x,U)\mapsto J(x,U)
be a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function that is convex in
U
for all
x\in\Omega
. Assume that there exists some
U_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\gamma>0
with
\mathcal{B}_{3\gamma}(U_{0})\subset\mathcal{U}
such that for all
x\in\Omega
there exists some
U^{*}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}(U_{0})
satisfying

\inf_{U\in\mathcal{U}}J(x,U)=J(x,U^{*}(x)).

Suppose that for each
\delta>0
there exists a NN
J_{\delta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\operatorname{size}(J^{\mathrm{NN}}_{\delta})
neurons that approximates
J
with accuracy
\delta>0
in the sense that for all
(x,U)\in\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})

\lvert J(x,U)-J_{\delta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(x,U)\rvert\leq\delta.

(4.3)

Define the constants

L_{1}\coloneq\max_{x\in\Omega,\\
U\in\mathcal{B}_{3\gamma}(U_{0})}\left\lVert\frac{\partial J}{\partial U}(x,U)\right\rVert,\quad L_{2}\coloneq\max_{x\in\Omega,U\in\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})}\left\lVert\frac{\partial^{2}J}{\partial U^{2}}(x,U)\right\rVert.

(4.4)

Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and
h,\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
small enough such that

k\left(h\sqrt{m}+\frac{2}{hL_{2}}\sqrt{m}\delta\right)\leq\gamma

(4.5)

there exists a NN
U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{B}_{3\gamma}(U_{0})
with
\operatorname{size}(U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta})=2km\operatorname{size}(J^{\mathrm{NN}}_{\delta})
neurons such that for all
x\in\Omega
we have

J(x,U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta}(x))-J(x,U^{*}(x))\leq L_{1}k\left(h\sqrt{m}+\frac{2}{hL_{2}}\sqrt{m}\delta\right)+\frac{2L_{2}}{k+4}\gamma^{2}.

(4.6)

Remark 4.4
.

Note that the NN
U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta}
in Theorem
4.3
depends on the constants
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
h\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. These parameters are crucial for two main reasons: On the one hand, the appropriate choice of these parameters allows to achieve an arbitrarily low approximation error in the right-hand side of equation (
4.6
). On the other hand, in the case that
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathbb{R}^{m}
the choice of
k,h
, and
\delta
is also essential to guarantee
U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{U}
., i.e., that the range of the NN contains only admissible controls.

Proof of
4.3
.

We want to utilize the fact that the gradient descent method is globally convergent for convex functions with a Lipschitz gradient, see
Lemma

4.1
. First note that the gradient
\frac{\partial J}{\partial U}(x,U)
is Lipschitz in
U
on
\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})
with Lipschitz constant
L_{2}
. Now set
\alpha\coloneq\frac{1}{L_{2}}
, and define

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi\colon\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})&\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\\
(x,U)&\mapsto U-\alpha\frac{\partial J}{\partial U}(x,U).\end{split}

(4.7)

Applying inequality (
4.1
) from Lemma
4.1
yields that for all
x\in\Omega
and
k\in\mathbb{N}

\lVert\Psi^{k}(x,U_{0})-U_{0}\rVert\leq\lVert\Psi^{k}(x,U_{0})-U^{*}(x)\rVert+\lVert U^{*}(x)-U_{0}\rVert\leq 2\lVert U^{*}(x)-U_{0}\rVert\leq 2\gamma.

(4.8)

Thus, we have
\Psi^{k}(x,U_{0})\in\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and the gradient step method given by
\Psi
with starting point
U_{0}
is well-defined on
\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})
. Further, by equation (
4.2
) from
Lemma

4.1
for all
x\in\Omega
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

J(x,\Psi^{k}(x,U_{0}))-J(x,U^{*}(x))\leq\frac{2L_{2}}{k+4}\lVert U_{0}-U^{*}(x)\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{2L_{2}}{k+4}\gamma^{2}.

(4.9)

Next, as in the proof of Theorem 6.1 in
[
18
]
, we use finite differences to approximate
\frac{\partial J}{\partial U}(x,U)
. For any
h>0
define for
x\in\Omega
,
U\in\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})
, and
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}

\tilde{\psi}_{j,h}(x,U)\coloneq\frac{1}{h}\left(J(x,U_{1},\dots,U_{j}+h,\dots,U_{m})-J(x,U)\right).

(4.10)

We have

\left\lvert\frac{\partial J}{\partial U_{j}}(x,U)-\tilde{\psi}_{j,h}(x,U)\right\rvert\leq C_{j}h,

where
C_{j}\coloneq\max_{x\in\Omega,U\in\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})}\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}J}{\partial U_{j}^{2}}(x,U)\right\}
. Hence, by defining

\tilde{\Psi}_{h}(x,U)\coloneq U-\alpha\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\psi}_{1,h}(x,U),\dots,\tilde{\psi}_{m,h}(x,U)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

we obtain an approximation of
\Psi
satisfying

\lVert\Psi(x,U)-\tilde{\Psi}_{h}(x,U)\rVert_{2}\leq\alpha h\sqrt{m}\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,m\}}C_{j}\leq\alpha h\sqrt{m}L_{2}=h\sqrt{m}

(4.11)

for all
(x,U)\in\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})
, where we utilized the fact that for any matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
it holds
2
2

2

For any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
it is
\left\lVert A\right\rVert=\sup_{\left\lVert y\right\rVert=1}\left\lVert Ay\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert Ae_{j}\right\rVert=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{ij}^{2}}\geq\sqrt{A_{jj}^{2}}=\lvert A_{jj}\rvert
.
that
\lvert X_{jj}\rvert\leq\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let
\delta>0
and use the assumption on the existence of some NN
J_{\mathrm{NN},\delta}
approximating
J
to construct an approximation
\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{h,\delta}
of
\tilde{\Psi}_{h}
by substituting
J^{\mathrm{NN}}_{\delta}
for
J
in the definition of
\tilde{\psi}_{j,h}
in (
4.10
) and then defining
\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{h,\delta}
analogously to
\tilde{\Psi}_{h}
. Using (
4.11
) and (
4.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\lVert\Psi(x,U)-\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{h,\delta}(x,U)\right\rVert&\leq\left\lVert\Psi(x,U)-\tilde{\Psi}_{h}(x,U)\right\rVert+\left\lVert\tilde{\Psi}_{h}(x,U)-\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{h,\delta}(x,U)\right\rVert\\
&\leq\sqrt{m}h+2\frac{\alpha}{h}\sqrt{m}\delta.\end{split}

(4.12)

Moreover, for all
(x,U)\in\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})
it holds that

\left\lVert I-\alpha\frac{\partial^{2}J}{\partial U^{2}}(x,U)\right\rVert\leq 1

(4.13)

since
\alpha=\frac{1}{L_{2}}
and
\frac{\partial^{2}J}{\partial U^{2}}(x,U)\succeq 0
, and thus each eigenvalue of
I-\alpha\frac{\partial^{2}J}{\partial U^{2}}(x,U)
is of the form
1-\lambda
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Hence, we obtain that the mapping
\Psi
defined in (
4.7
) is non-expansive on
\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})
.
Consequently, from
Lemma

4.2
we get for all
(x,U)\in\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})

\left\lVert\Psi^{k}(x,U)-(\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{h,\delta})^{k}(x,U)\right\rVert\leq k\left(h\sqrt{m}+2\frac{\alpha}{h}\sqrt{m}\delta\right).

(4.14)

Now let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
h\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
small enough such that (
4.5
) is satisfied, i.e.,
k\left(h\sqrt{m}+2\frac{\alpha}{h}\sqrt{m}\delta\right)\leq\gamma
. By (

) this yields together with (
4.8
) that for any

\left\lVert U_{0}-(\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{h,\delta})^{k}(x,U_{0})\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert U_{0}-\Psi^{k}(x,U_{0})\right\rVert+\left\lVert\Psi^{k}(x,U_{0})-(\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{h,\delta})^{k}(x,U)\right\rVert\leq 3\gamma.

(4.15)

Now define the NN
U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta}(x)\coloneqq(\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{h,\delta})^{k}(x,U_{0})
and observe that by (
4.15
) it holds that
U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{B}_{3\gamma}(U_{0})\subset\mathcal{U}
. Applying (
4.9

yields for all

\displaystyle J(x,U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta}(x))-J(x,U^{*}(x))

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert J(x,U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{k,h,\delta}(x)-J(x,\Psi^{k}(x))\right\rvert+J(x,\Psi^{k}(x))-J(x,U^{*}(x))

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}k\left(h\sqrt{m}+\frac{2}{hL_{2}}\sqrt{m}\delta\right)+\frac{2L_{2}}{k+4}\gamma^{2}.

The number of neurons of the network

is given as

, which equals

. This concludes the proof.
∎

4.3
Neural network approximations of optimal controls in non-strictly convex problems

We now use Theorem
4.3
to state the main result of this section. The key step is to replace the assumption of the existence of a given neural network approximating
J
, as used in Theorem
4.3
, by explicitly constructing such an approximating network from the compositional structure of the problem data. Parts of the proof of the following theorem follow the proof of
[
18

.

Theorem 4.5
.

Consider a discrete-time optimal control problem as described in Setting
3.1
with
\ell\equiv 0
. Assume that
(f,\mathcal{G}^{f},\mathcal{L}^{f})
and
(g,\mathcal{G}^{g},\mathcal{L}^{g})

[
18

f
and
g
are twice continuously differentiable, respectively. Consider a compact set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of initial values and a convex set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{qN}
of admissible control values. Assume that the cost functional
J\colon\Omega\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R},(x,U)\mapsto J(x,U)
is convex in
U
for all
x\in\Omega
. Further, suppose that there exists some
U_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\gamma>0
with
\mathcal{B}_{3\gamma}(U_{0})\subset\mathcal{U}
such that for all
x\in\Omega
there exists some
U^{*}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}(U_{0})
satisfying

\inf_{U\in\mathcal{U}}J(x,U)=J(x,U^{*}(x)).

Then, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a NN
U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{\varepsilon}\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{U}
that approximates
U^{*}
in the weak sense, i.e., for all
x\in\Omega

J(x,U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{\varepsilon}(x))-J(x,U^{*}(x))\leq\varepsilon,

holds and the complexity of

\operatorname{size}(U^{*\mathrm{NN}}_{\varepsilon})\leq C_{1}^{r}C_{2}\,\left(N\lvert\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}\rvert+\lvert\mathcal{V}_{G}^{g}\rvert\right)\left(1+\mathfrak{C}^{r}\right)\frac{(qN)^{r+1}}{\varepsilon^{4r+1}}

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are constants that depend at most polynomially on
\gamma,L_{1},
and
L_{2}
, and
\mathfrak{C}
is a constant depending linearly on

N
on
L^{f}
.
Here

,
r_{\max}^{f}

are part of the compositional features of the functions
f\colon[-R,R]^{n+q}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g\colon[-R,R]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
L^{f}
and
L^{g}
are Lipschitz constants of

and
g

is a constant large enough so that
\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma(U_{0})}\subset[-R,R]^{qN}
and

f^{k}(x,\cdot)(U)\in\left[-\frac{R}{2},\frac{R}{2}\right]^{n}

(4.16)

for all
x\in\Omega
,
U\in\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})
, and
0\leq k\leq N-1
. Further, the Lipschitz constants
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are defined as in Theorem
4.3
and
C_{f}
and
C_{g}
are determined by
\{(d_{i,j},m_{i,j})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:f_{i,j}\in\mathcal{V}_{G}^{f}\}
and
\{(d_{i,j},m_{i,j})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:g_{i,j}\in\mathcal{V}_{G}^{g}\}
, respectively.

Proof of
4.5
.

Suppose that for each
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
we can prove the existence of a NN
J_{\delta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{qN}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\operatorname{size}(J_{\delta}^{\mathrm{NN}})\in\mathbb{N}
neurons such that for all
(x,U)\in\Omega\times\mathcal{B}_{2\gamma}(U_{0})

(4.17)

Now for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we set

\bar{k}=\frac{6L_{2}\gamma^{2}}{\varepsilon},\quad\bar{h}=\frac{1}{\bar{k}\sqrt{qN}}\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{3L_{1}},\gamma\right\},\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\delta}=\frac{\bar{h}L_{2}}{2\sqrt{qN}\bar{k}}\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{3L_{1}},\gamma\right\}.

(

) and to estimate the total amount of needed neurons. Since

5
Conclusion

In this paper we have highlighted how compositional structures can be exploited in high-dimensional optimal control when using neural network approximations. By connecting neural network approximation theory for compositional functions with the analysis of control problems, we have identified conditions under which high-dimensional optimal controls can be represented without suffering from the curse of dimensionality.
The developments presented here broaden the scope of existing results by relaxing convexity requirements and by showing how classical formulations of control problems can be adapted to fit those requirements. In this way, the theory is made applicable to a wider class of problems, including linear-quadratic settings and beyond.
For future research, a natural direction is to investigate how the theoretical representations derived here can inform practical algorithms for large-scale control. In particular, methods that automatically detect or exploit compositional features in nonlinear systems may provide a path toward efficient feedback synthesis with neural networks in high-dimensions.
