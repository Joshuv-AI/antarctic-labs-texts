Sharp Bounds on Lengths of Linear Recolouring Sequences
Stijn Cambie, Wouter Cames van Batenburg, Daniel W. Cranston

Abstract
A recolouring sequence, between k k -colourings α \alpha and β \beta of a graph G G , transforms α \alpha into β \beta by recolouring one vertex at a time, such that after each recolouring step we again have a proper k k -colouring of G G . The diameter of the k k -recolouring graph, diam ⁡ 𝒞 k ​ ( G ) \diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(G) , is the maximum over all pairs α \alpha and β \beta of the minimum length of a recolouring sequence from α \alpha to β \beta . Much previous work has focused on determining the asymptotics of diam ⁡ 𝒞 k ​ ( G ) \diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(G) : Is it Θ ⁡ ( | G | ) \Theta(|G|) ? Is it Θ ⁡ ( | G | 2 ) \Theta(|G|^{2}) ? Or even larger? Here we focus on graphs for which diam ⁡ 𝒞 k ​ ( G ) = Θ ⁡ ( | G | ) \diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(G)=\Theta(|G|) , and seek to determine more precisely the multiplicative constant implicit in the Θ ⁡ ( ) \Theta() . In particular, for each k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , for all positive integers p p and q q we exactly determine diam ⁡ 𝒞 k ​ ( K p , q ) \diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(K_{p,q}) , up to a small additive constant. We also sharpen a recolouring lemma that has been used in multiple papers, proving an optimal version. This improves the multiplicative constant in various prior results. Finally, we investigate plausible relationships between similar reconfiguration graphs.

2
Optimal Renaming Lemma

Bonamy and Bousquet
[
5
, Lemma 5]
proved a variant of the Optimal Renaming Lemma
below; for clarity, we call their version the Rough Renaming Lemma. It has been reused in several papers as a subroutine.
In a typical application, we are given two colourings
\alpha
and
\beta
that can be efficiently recoloured to some carefully chosen colourings
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
that induce the same partition into colour classes. The Rough Renaming Lemma is then used as a ‘finishing blow’ to recolour
\alpha^{\prime}
to
\beta^{\prime}
, thus establishing a short recolouring sequence between
\alpha
and
\beta
.
But the upper bound in the Rough Renaming Lemma, of
2n
, is suboptimal.
In fact, this recolouring can be done with only
\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor
steps, which is sharp.
So reusing the Rough Renaming Lemma is wasteful, especially in papers that prove a linear bound on the
diameter of the reconfiguration graph.
In a sense, the ideas needed to prove the Optimal Renaming Lemma were already present in the argument of Bonamy and Bousquet,
but authors of subsequent papers seem to be unaware. By making this improvement explicit, we indirectly strengthen the upper bounds in
the papers that have used this Rough Renaming Lemma.

Optimal Renaming Lemma
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices, fix
k\geq\chi(G)
, and let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two proper
k
-colourings of
G
that induce the same partition of vertices into colour classes
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
. If
\ell\geq k+1
, then
\alpha
can be recoloured to
\beta
in
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}(G)
in at most
\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor
steps, with each vertex recoloured at most
2
times.

Proof.

We define an auxiliary loopless digraph
D
with vertex set
V(D):=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}\}
, and with
(C_{i},C_{j})
a directed edge of
D
if and only if
\alpha(C_{i})=\beta(C_{j})
and
i\neq j
.
By construction, the vertices of
D
have pairwise distinct colours, so each vertex has
in-degree and out-degree both at most
1
. So the vertices of
D
can be partitioned into directed paths and directed cycles. Now we sequentially recolour the vertices of each directed path and cycle.
For a directed path
C_{i_{1}},\ldots,C_{i_{t}}
(possibly with
t=1
), we simply recolour
C_{i_{1}}
to
\beta(C_{i_{1}})
, then recolour
C_{i_{2}}
to
\beta(C_{i_{2}})
, etcetera, finally recolouring
C_{i_{t}}
to
\beta(C_{i_{t}})
. This takes
\sum_{j=1}^{t}|C_{i_{j}}|
recolouring steps.
Note that every recolouring step induces an updated directed graph
D
. But this is of no consequence for us since every colour class
C_{i}
that is currently coloured
\beta(C_{i})
has in-degree and out-degree in
D
both
0
.

For a directed cycle
C_{i_{1}},\ldots,C_{i_{t}}
with
t\geq 2
, we assume without loss of generality that
C_{i_{1}}
has minimum size among
C_{i_{1}},\ldots,C_{i_{t}}
. We first recolour
C_{i_{1}}
to some free colour in
[\ell]\setminus\gamma(V(G))
, where
\gamma
denotes the current colouring. Note that this cannot create a new arc incident to the current directed cycle.
Next, we proceed along the directed cycle, starting by recolouring
C_{i_{2}}
to
\beta(C_{i_{2}})
and ending by recolouring
C_{i_{t}}
to
\beta(C_{i_{t}})
. To finish, we recolour
C_{i_{1}}
again, this time to
\beta(C_{i_{1}})
. In total, the number of recolouring steps we use is
|C_{i_{1}}|+\sum_{j=1}^{t}|C_{i_{j}}|
. This is at most
\lfloor 3/2\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{t}|C_{i_{j}}|\rfloor
because
t\geq 2
and we chose
C_{i_{1}}
to be of minimum size.
In this way we can recolour every directed path and cycle, one after the other.
So the total number of recolouring steps we use is at most
\lfloor 3/2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}|C_{i}|\rfloor=\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor
. Moreover, every vertex is recoloured only once or, if it belonged to the smallest colour class of its directed cycle, twice.
∎

Distances in reconfiguration graphs of complete bipartite graphs

In this section, we consider
k
-colourings of
K_{p,q}
and prove
Theorem

1
.
To begin, we prove the lower bound.

3.1
Lower Bounds for
K_{p,q}

It is trivial to get a lower bound of the order of the graph,
p+q
, by constructing two colourings that differ on each vertex.
To improve this bound, our basic idea is to construct two
k
-colourings that require the colours on many pairs of vertices (in opposite parts) to “swap”; that is, the old colour of vertex 1 is the new colour of vertex 2 and vice versa. For every such pair, a recolouring step must be “wasted”, since the first vertex in the pair to be recoloured cannot immediately receive its desired new colour.

Proof of the lower bound in
Theorem

1
.

Fix integers
a,b,k
with
1\leq a\leq b
and
k=a+b
. For convenience, we assume through most of the proof that
ab
divides both
p
and
q
; however, near the end we comment about how to remove this assumption.
In fact we will mainly care about the cases that
(a,b)\in\{(1,k-1),(\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor\mathclose{},\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{})\}
, but for the time being we consider the more general case above.
We define the colourings
\alpha
and
\beta
using
ab
pairs of sets
U_{i,j},V_{i,j}
of sizes (respectively)
\frac{p}{ab}
and
\frac{q}{ab}
,
with
i\in[a]
and
j\in[a+1,k]
,
where
U
and
V
are the unions of these sets.
The colourings
\alpha
and
\beta
are defined by
\alpha(U_{i,j})=i=\beta(V_{i,j})
and
\beta(U_{i,j})=j=\alpha(V_{i,j})
.
For convenience, we also let
U_{i}:=\cup_{j\in[a+1,k]}U_{i,j}
and
V_{j}:=\cup_{i\in[a]}V_{i,j}
.

Since the colours on
U
and
V
swap from
\alpha
to
\beta
, there is always a first moment that
U
resp.
V
, and in particular
U_{i}

(or
V_{j}
), is completely coloured with colours different from
i
(resp.
j
). We call this the time when colour
i
or colour
j
is
erased
. (We do not care about a colour disappearing and reappearing on a part, since we are only proving a lower bound.)
We order the sets
U_{i}
(renaming colours if needed) so that colour 1 is erased from
U_{1}
before colour 2 is erased from
U_{2}
, which happens before colour 3 is erased from
U_{3}
, etc. Similarly, if
j<j^{\prime}
, then we assume that colour
j
is erased from
V_{j}
before colour
j^{\prime}
is erased from
V_{j^{\prime}}
.

When colour
i
gets erased from
U_{i}
, let

denote the number of the
b
colours on
V
that are not yet erased.
So there are at least
f(i)
sets
U_{i,j}
which have been recoloured entirely with colours different from their corresponding target
colour
j
. Analogously, we define
f(j)
, for each
j\in[a+1,k]
, as the number of colours that are not yet erased on
U

when
j
is erased from
V
(for the first time).

For each pair
(i,j)\in[a]\times[a+1,k]
, one of the two colours
i
and
j
must be erased first, when the other is not yet erased.
Since we do not consider reoccurence of a colour after being erased,
this implies
\sum_{h=1}^{k}f(h)=ab
. More specifically, for all distinct
i\in[a]
and
j\in[a+1,k]
, we define
X_{i<j}
to be 1 if colour
i
is erased from
U
before colour
j
is erased from
V
and define it to be 0 otherwise; and we define
X_{j<i}
as

.
Now we get

\sum_{h=1}^{k}f(h)=\sum_{h=1}^{a}f(h)+\sum_{h=a+1}^{b}f(h)=\sum_{h=1}^{a}\sum_{\ell=a+1}^{k}X_{h<\ell}+\sum_{\ell=a+1}^{k}\sum_{h=1}^{a}X_{\ell<h}=\sum_{h=1}^{a}\sum_{\ell=a+1}^{k}(X_{h<\ell}+X_{\ell<h})=\sum_{h=1}^{a}\sum_{\ell=a+1}^{k}1=a(k-(a+1)+1)=ab
.

When colour
i
is erased from
U
, for each of the
f(i)
colours
j
that are not yet erased from

, the vertex subset
U_{i,j}

has been completely recoloured with colours other than

, so all its vertices will need to be recoloured again to reach
\beta
.
An analogous argument works when each colour

is erased from
V
.
Thus, a lower bound for the number of recolourings needed to transform
\alpha
into
\beta
is

p+q+\sum_{i=1}^{a}f(i)\frac{p}{ab}+\sum_{j=a+1}^{k}f(j)\frac{q}{ab}=2p+q+\sum_{j=a+1}^{k}f(j)\frac{q-p}{ab}.

(1)

Before continuing, we mention that if
q\geq kp
, then we let
(a,b):=(1,k-1)
; and if
q<kp
, then we let
(a,b):=(\lfloor k/2\rfloor,\lceil k/2\rceil)
.
Depending on the order in which the colours were erased, we get different lower bounds.
Recall from above that the first colour erased is either
1
or
a+1
, and the last colour erased is either
a
or
k
.
Thus, we are in one of the following two cases.

Case 1:
\bm{a+1}
is the first colour erased or
\bm{a}
is the last colour erased.

In the first case
f(a+1)=a
, and in the second case
f(j)\geq 1
for every

.
In both cases
\sum_{j=a+1}^{k}f(j)\geq a
.
Hence the number of recolourings needed is at least
2p+q+a\frac{q-p}{ab}=2p+q+\frac{q-p}{b}
.
If
q\geq kp
, then

, so we have the desired bound. And if
q\leq\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p
, then

so we again have the desired bound. Finally, suppose that
\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p<q<kp
. In this case, substituting

shows that the above bound

exceeds the desired bound:

\frac{q-p}{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}}-\left(p+\frac{q-kp}{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}\right)\geq\frac{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p-p}{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}}-\left(p+\frac{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p-kp}{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}\right)=\left(p-\frac{p}{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}}\right)-\left(p-\frac{p\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}\right)=0

In this case, we are done. So we now assume that we are in Case 2, below.

Case 2:
\bm{1}

\displaystyle 2p+q+(k-x-y-1)\frac{q-p}{ab}+xy\frac{p}{ab}.

(2)

Because we are in Case 2, we know that

, since we first erase colour 1, and all vertices that were coloured 1
must be recoloured with other colours in

. This implies that

and thus that

and

and

. But in fact we may assume

.

, before erasing

. So we must show that the minimum value of (
2

the value claimed in the theorem. We have 2 subcases, depending on whether or not

First suppose that

Since
\max\{x,y\}\leq b-1\leq\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}-1\leq\frac{q-p}{p}

In that case

Now suppose instead that

.
Consider an arbitrary pair
(x,y)
. If

, then we can either increase or decrease

without increasing the value of (
2

Due to the symmetry of (

.
Thus, we assume that

When

, as in the previous paragraph, we get the lower bound

. Since

When

When
(x,y)=(1,1)
, the conclusion is immediate if

, since then

\frac{(a+b-3)(q-p)}{ab}\geq\frac{q-p}{b}
.
Instead suppose

. Now (
2
) gives the lower bound
2p+q+\frac{q-p}{4}+\frac{p}{4}=2p+q+\frac{q}{4}
.
By assumption

. Thus,
\frac{q}{4}>\frac{q}{4}+\frac{q-2p}{4}=\frac{q-p}{2}=\frac{q-p}{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}}
.

The paragraph above finishes the proof when
ab
divides both
p
and
q
.
Now we handle the general case.

•

We begin with the first regime; that is, when
q\geq kp
. We show that in the lower bound we do not lose anything due to rounding except for what comes from the floor (in the statement of the theorem). In the construction of
\alpha
and
\beta
, we make each set
U_{1,j}
of size
\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{p}{ab}\right\rceil\mathclose{}
or size
\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{p}{ab}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}
, and each set

of size
\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{q}{ab}\right\rceil\mathclose{}
or size
\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{q}{ab}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}
. Furthermore, we take the larger sets (those of size
\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{p}{ab}\right\rceil\mathclose{}
or size
\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{q}{ab}\right\rceil\mathclose{}
) to be the ones with smaller indices. Now it is straightforward to check that
|V_{1,j}|-|U_{1,j}|-\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{q-p}{ab}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}\geq 0
for all
j\in[2,k]
. (For example, it is enough to consider 2 cases based on whether or not
q\pmod{b}\geq p\pmod{b}
.)

Recall that when
q\geq kp
, we let
a:=1
and
b:=k-1
. Thus, we must be in Case 1; that is, colour 1 is not the first colour erased. In other words,
X_{j<1}=1
for some
j\in[2,k]
. Now the argument proving (
1
) can be refined to give the following lower bound:

\displaystyle d(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{k}\left(|U_{1,j}|+|V_{1,j}|+(|U_{1,j}|X_{1<j}+|V_{1,j}|X_{j<1})\right)

\displaystyle=|U|+|V|+\sum_{j=2}^{k}\left((|U_{1,j}|X_{1<j}+|U_{1,j}|X_{j<1})+(|V_{1,j}|-|U_{1,j}|)X_{j<1}\right)

\displaystyle=2|U|+|V|+\sum_{j=2}^{k}(|V_{1,j}|-|U_{1,j}|)X_{j<1}

\displaystyle\geq 2p+q+\min_{j\in[2,k]}(|V_{1,j}|-|U_{1,j}|)

\displaystyle\geq 2p+q+\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{q-p}{b}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}.

•

Now we consider the second regime; in particular,
q\geq\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p
. If
q\equiv p\pmod{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}
, then instead of a term

(in the calculation above), we have
xy
terms that are each equal to
\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{p}{ab}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}
or
\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{p}{ab}\right\rceil\mathclose{}
.
So the number of terms that are rounded is

xy\leq(a-1)(b-1)=ab-k+1
; thus,
g_{1}(k,p,q)<ab-k+1.

Next, we consider the case when
q\not\equiv p\pmod{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}
.
Let
q^{\prime}
be the largest integer which is at most

q
and satisfies
q^{\prime}\equiv p\pmod{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}
. In that case, we obtain a lower bound on the diameter observing that
\diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(K_{p,q})\geq\diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(K_{p,q^{\prime}})+(q-q^{\prime})
; we let the
q-q^{\prime}
additional vertices satisfy
\alpha(v)\not=\beta(v)
.
By comparing the main terms in the estimate of the diameter, we can conclude an upper bound on

For this, we check two cases.

If
\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p\leq q^{\prime}
, then
we get
g_{1}(k,p,q)\leq g_{1}(k,p,q^{\prime})+1
, since
\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{q-kp}{ab}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}-\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{q^{\prime}-kp}{ab}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}\leq 1
.
So assume instead that
q^{\prime}<\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p\leq q
.
We have analogously
g_{1}(k,p,q)\leq g_{1}(k,p,\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p)+1=g_{2}(k,p,\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p)+1\leq\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}+1.

For this equality, note that in
Theorem

1
the bounds for the second and third regimes are equal when
q=\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p
. For this second inequality, we applied the upper bound for
g_{2}
, which we prove in the following paragraph.

•

Finally, we consider the third regime. If
q\equiv p\pmod{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}},

then we can ensure that

\left|V_{i,j}\right|-\left|U_{i,j}\right|=\frac{q-p}{ab}
for every
(i,j)\in[a]\times[a+1,k]
.
Now the analysis is nearly identical to that above when

and

are both divisible by
\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}
; consequently,

.
In the more general case, let

, where
q^{\prime}\equiv p\pmod{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}
and

.
Now
\diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(K_{p,q})\geq\diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(K_{p,q^{\prime}})+r
.
This holds because any pair of colourings
(\alpha,\beta)
of

can be extended to colourings of

such that each of the additional

vertices has different colours in
\alpha
and in

.
In the third regime,
\diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(K_{p,q})\geq\diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(K_{p,q^{\prime}})+r
implies
g_{2}(k,p,q)\leq\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{q-p}{b}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}-\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{q^{\prime}-p}{b}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}\leq\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{q-q^{\prime}}{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}}\right\rceil\mathclose{}\leq\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}.
∎

Now we can sketch the proof of Proposition

. For convenience, we restate it.

Proposition 2
.

For every complete bipartite graph
K_{p,q}
, we have
\diam\mathcal{C}_{3}(K_{p,q})=\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{3(p+q)}{2}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}
.

Proof.

Let

. Note that each of the three upper bounds in Theorem
1
(for easier reference, see Theorem
11
below) is equal to

; these bounds are
2p+q+(q-p)/2
and
3p+q+(q-3p)/2
and
2p+q+(q-p)/2
.
Since the diameter is always an integer, in all 3 regimes Theorem

gives the desired upper bound. So we just need a matching lower bound. For this, we note that the lower bound in the previous proof, stated for the case

, actually works for all values of

and

. (Although when

and

and

.
∎

In the remainder of this section, we prove the upper bounds in Theorem

First, we prove a proposition and a lemma that will be used in the proofs of the three statements of the theorem.

Definition 7

Fix a complete bipartite graph

and

By symmetry, we assume

Fix positive integers

and

Recolour each vertex of

with

with
\beta(v)
, and recolour each

with

∎

Remark 9
.

Throughout this section, we assume Definition
7

1
, we assume that
\alpha(V)\cup\beta(V)=[k]
, since otherwise we are done by Lemma

,
C_{2}
,

partition

.
Note also that

and

is coloured under both
\beta
and

Proposition 10
.

Fix integers
p,q,k
, the graph
K_{p,q}
, and
[k]
-colourings
\alpha
and
\beta
, as in
Definition

7
.
Assume that
\alpha(U)=\beta(V)=C_{3}
and
\alpha(V)=\beta(U)=C_{2},
where

. If
\lvert C_{3}\rvert\geq\lvert C_{2}\rvert
,
then

d(\alpha,\beta)\leq q+2p+\min\left\{\frac{q-p}{|C_{3}|},\frac{q-kp}{|C_{2}||C_{3}|}+p\right\}.

Proof.

Fix a non-empty subset
C^{\prime}_{3}\subset C_{3}
(not equal to the whole set
C_{3}
; this is possible, since
|C_{3}|\geq\lceil k/2\rceil\geq 2
)
and a non-empty subset
C^{\prime}_{2}\subseteq C_{2}
(possibly equal to
C_{2}
).

We now sketch a possible series of recolourings from
\alpha
to
\beta.

First recolour
\alpha^{-1}(C^{\prime}_{3})\subset U
(with colours from
C_{3}\backslash C^{\prime}_{3}
).
Then recolour each vertex

, with its colour
\beta(v)
whenever
\beta(v)\in C^{\prime}_{3}
, and otherwise recolour each such

with an arbitrary colour in
C^{\prime}_{3}\cup(C_{2}\setminus C_{2}^{\prime})
.
Now recolour all of

with
C^{\prime}_{2}
, such that

does not use any colour of
C_{3}
, and such that each

is coloured with
\beta(u)
if possible.
Next, we recolour each remaining vertex

with
\beta(v)
; finally, we recolour each vertex

with
\beta(u)
.

In total, the number of steps we used is
\lvert\alpha^{-1}(C_{3}^{\prime})\rvert+\lvert\alpha^{-1}(C_{2}^{\prime})\rvert+\lvert\alpha^{-1}(C_{2}\setminus C_{2}^{\prime})\rvert+\lvert\alpha^{-1}(C_{2}^{\prime})\cap\beta^{-1}(C_{3}\setminus C_{3}^{\prime})\rvert+\lvert\beta^{-1}(C_{2}\setminus C_{2}^{\prime})\rvert=p+q+\lvert\alpha^{-1}(C^{\prime}_{3})\rvert+\lvert\alpha^{-1}(C^{\prime}_{2})\cap\beta^{-1}(C_{3}\backslash C^{\prime}_{3})\rvert+\lvert\beta^{-1}(C_{2}\backslash C^{\prime}_{2})\rvert

Rather than fix sets
C_{2}^{\prime}
and
C_{3}^{\prime}

and
|C_{3}^{\prime}|
.
Let

Averaging over all possible sets

Doing this for

Noting that
\frac{1}{|C_{2}|}+\frac{1}{|C_{3}|}=\frac{|C_{2}|+|C_{3}|}{|C_{2}||C_{3}|}

We are now ready to prove the upper bounds of
Theorem

1
. For ease of presentation, we restate the upper bounds. Since the diameter is always an integer, the bounds also hold when taking the floor function.

Theorem 11
.

Fix positive integers
p,q,k
. If
p\leq q
and
k\geq 3
, then

\diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(K_{p,q})\leq\left.\begin{cases}2p+q+\frac{q-p}{k-1}&\text{if }q\geq kp\\
\\
3p+q+\frac{q-kp}{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}&\text{if }\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p\leq q\leq kp,\\
\\
2p+q+\frac{q-p}{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}}&\text{if }q\leq\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}p.\end{cases}\right.

Proof.

Fix
K_{p,q}
,
[k]
,
\alpha
,
\beta
,
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,
C_{3}
as in Definition
7
.

If
\left|C_{1}\right|=0
, then we are done by
Proposition

10
; below, we check this for the 3 regimes:

•

In the first regime, it is sufficient to note that
q-kp\geq 0
and
\left|C_{3}\right|\left|C_{2}\right|\geq k-1
. Together, these imply that

\frac{q-kp}{|C_{2}||C_{3}|}+p\leq\frac{q-kp}{k-1}+p=\frac{q-p}{k-1}
.

•

In the second regime, since
q-kp\leq 0
and
|C_{2}||C_{3}|\leq\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}
, we have

\frac{q-kp}{|C_{2}||C_{3}|}+p\leq p+\frac{q-kp}{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}.

•

In the third regime, it is sufficient to note that
\left|C_{3}\right|\geq\lceil k/2\rceil
and thus

\frac{q-p}{|C_{3}|}\leq\frac{q-p}{\mathopen{}\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil\mathclose{}}
.

Thus, we assume below that
\left|C_{1}\right|\geq 1
.

We proceed by induction on
k
.
The base case is
k=3
, and thus
\left|C_{1}\right|=\left|C_{2}\right|=\left|C_{3}\right|=1.

When
k=3
, we also denote by
C_{i}
the single colour of each set
C_{i}
.
We describe
2
recolouring sequences; by an averaging argument we will show that at least one of them has length at most
p+q+\frac{p+q}{2}
(when
k=3
, all
3
upper bounds above are equal).

As a first method to recolour
\alpha
into
\beta
, we: (i) recolour
\alpha^{-1}(C_{2})
with
C_{1}
, (ii) recolour
U
with
C_{2}
, and (iii) recolour
\beta^{-1}(C_{3})
with colour
C_{3}.

This implies

d(\alpha,\beta)\leq p+\left|\alpha^{-1}(C_{2})\right|+\left|\beta^{-1}(C_{3})\right|.

(3)

As a second method, we: (i) recolour
\alpha^{-1}(C_{1})
with
C_{2}
, (ii) recolour each
u\in U
with
C_{1}
,
(iii) recolour each
v\in V
with
C_{3}
, (iv) recolour each
u\in U

with
\beta(u)=C_{2}
, and (v) recolour each
v\in\beta^{-1}(C_{1})
with
\beta(v)=C_{1}
. The number of steps this takes is

\lvert\alpha^{-1}(C_{1})\rvert+p+q+p+\lvert\beta^{-1}(C_{1})\rvert
. That is,

d(\alpha,\beta)\leq 2p+q+\lvert\alpha^{-1}(C_{1})\rvert+\lvert\beta^{-1}(C_{1})\rvert.

(4)

Since
|\alpha^{-1}(C_{1})|+|\alpha^{-1}(C_{2})|=|V|=|\beta^{-1}(C_{1})|+|\beta^{-1}(C_{3})|
,
adding (
3
) and (
4
) gives
2d(\alpha,\beta)\leq 3(p+q)

4
Non-relationships between
\mathcal{C}_{k}(G)
,
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}(G)
, and
\mathcal{C}_{L}(G)

In
[
7
]
, the authors sketched some typical behaviour of configuration graphs.
In particular, they mentioned that by increasing the number of colours, the recolouring sequences typically become shorter and shorter.
Note that two factors are at play.
If the number of colours,
k
, increases, then this also increases both the total number of colourings and the number of possible local changes, the degree of the reconfiguration graph.
The first factor often leads to a higher diameter, but the second to a lower diameter.
Here, we give some exceptions to the typical behaviour.
A classical example from
[
10
, Prop. 2]
is the following.

Example 14
.

If
G=K_{m,m}\backslash M
(
K_{m,m}
minus a perfect matching), then
\mathcal{C}_{k}(G)
is connected if and only if
k\geq 3
and
k\not=m
.

The key idea in
Example

14
is that if
k=m
, then
G
has a frozen colouring, namely, the one that uses each colour once on each part; see
Fig.

4
. (Otherwise, by pigeonhole principle each part has a colour that is used either 0 times or at least 2 times.)

Example

14
is the inspiration for more counterexamples.
Bard
[
1
,
16
]
asked whether
\mathcal{C}_{k}(G)
being Hamiltonian implies that
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}(G)
is Hamiltonian.
In
https://github.com/StijnCambie/reconfiguration
(document
Check_Ham
), it is e.g. verified that if
G=K_{5,5}\backslash M
then the reconfiguration graph
\mathcal{C}_{3}(G)
is Hamiltonian.
Since
\mathcal{C}_{5}(G)
is disconnected and thus not Hamiltonian,
this answers the question of Bard negatively (either for
k=3
or for
k=4
, but it is not clear which one). This counterexample was mentioned in
[
16
, Sec. 9.6]
, attributed to the first author of the present paper, but no proof was provided; so the code referenced above provides this proof.

Our next example modifies

K_{4,4}\backslash M
,
by replacing one vertex with
3
(each of which
inherits exactly
2
of its
3

4
-colourings shown (see the right of

Not surprisingly, the two 4-colourings are far apart.

Example 15

The graph
G
presented in

10
, matching number
4
, and degeneracy
\delta^{\star}(G)=2.

For

As we know from

The graph in

We do not know if this ratio can become arbitrarily large, while staying finite.

(d), exhibiting an even larger (unbounded) gap between
\diam\mathcal{C}_{L}(G)
and
\diam\mathcal{C}_{k}(G)
.

Lemma 19
.

For all
n\geq 1
there exists an
n
-vertex graph
G_{n}
with a
4
-fold list-assignment
L
, such that
c_{1}\cdot n\leq\frac{\diam\mathcal{C}_{L}(G_{n})}{\diam\mathcal{C}_{4}(G_{n})}\leq c_{2}\cdot n
, where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are some uniform positive constants.

Proof.

For each positive integer
k
, we construct a graph
G_{k}

with
|V(G_{k})|=260k
and a
4
-assignment
L
such that

(k^{2}-1)/4+255k\leq\diam\mathcal{C}_{L}(G_{k})\leq 2k^{2}+255k
and
260k\leq\diam\mathcal{C}_{4}(G_{k})\leq 691k
. We use these graphs
G_{k}
to prove the result when
n=260k
for some positive integer
k
. It is straightforward (but a bit tedious) to extend the result to all positive integers
n
; so we just sketch the ideas.
In general when
n\geq 260
, we pick an integer
k
such that
260k\leq n<260(k+1)
, and we take our graph
G
to be an
n
-vertex subgraph of
G_{k+1}
that contains as an induced subgraph
G_{k}
. Now
n/2\leq 260k\leq n<260(k+1)\leq 2n
, so by applying the bounds for
G_{k}
and
G_{k+1}
, we lose at most a constant factor in both the upper and lower bounds.

The most interesting part of our construction is the lower bound on
\diam\mathcal{C}_{L}(G_{k})
, so we begin with that. We start with a path of order
k
and “hang off” of each path vertex a copy of
K_{4,255}
that uses its lists to enforce that the
recolouring of the path simulates the recolouring of
a path with only 3 colours (and no lists).
Since
[
6
]
showed that
(k^{2}-1)/4\leq\diam\mathcal{C}_{3}(P_{k})\leq 2k^{2}
, we are done. Thus, we need only to describe these
lists and show how they enforce the desired simulation
of
\mathcal{C}_{3}(P_{k})
.

Let
H
be the complete bipartite graph
K_{4,255}
. To the four vertices
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}
in the part of size
4
, assign four disjoint lists:
L(v_{1})=\{1,2,3,4\},L(v_{2})=\{5,6,7,8\},L(v_{3})=\{9,10,11,12\},L(v_{4})=\{13,14,15,16\}
. In the part of size
255=4^{4}-1
, assign each vertex a unique list that contains precisely one element from each of
L(v_{1}),L(v_{2}),L(v_{3}),L(v_{4})
, except that no vertex is assigned
\{1,5,9,13\}
. Note that in every proper
L
-colouring
c
of
H
, we must have
c(v_{1})=1,c(v_{2})=5,c(v_{3})=9
and
c(v_{4})=13
. From this it follows that
\mathcal{C}_{L}(H)
is connected, as any two
L
-colourings only differ on the part of size
255
(since that is an independent set, in fact the diameter of
\mathcal{C}_{L}(H)
is
255
). In what follows we call
v_{1}
the
special vertex
of
H
and we only need that it is coloured
1
in every
L
-colouring.

Now consider a path
P_{k}=p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
on
k
vertices. Let
G_{k}
denote the graph formed by taking the disjoint union of
P_{k}
and
k
copies
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
of
H
, and then for every
i\in[k]
adding an edge between
p_{i}
and the special vertex of
H_{i}
.

Here we tacitly assumed that each
H_{i}
is equipped with the list-assignment
L
described above. Moreover, we assign each vertex of
P
the list
\{1,a,b,c\}
, where
\{a,b,c\}
is disjoint from
\{1,2,\ldots,16\}
. Since in a proper
L
-colouring each special vertex must have colour
1
, it follows that each vertex of
P_{k}
must use a colour in
\{a,b,c\}
. In other words, restricted to
P
the
L
-colourings correspond precisely to the
3
-colourings of
P
.
As noted above, by
[
6
, Thm. 3]
, we know that
(k^{2}-1)/4\leq\diam\mathcal{C}_{3}(P_{k})\leq 2k^{2}
.

It follows that
\diam\mathcal{C}_{L}(G_{k})=\diam\mathcal{C}_{L}(P_{k})+k\cdot\diam\mathcal{C}_{L}(H)=\diam\mathcal{C}_{3}(P_{k})+255k=\Theta(k^{2})
, because any two
L
-colourings of
G_{k}
only differ on
P_{k}
and the size-
255
parts of the copies of
H
, taking note that these parts are separated from each other by the special vertices that have the same colour in every
L
-colouring.

Now we must prove that
\diam\mathcal{C}_{4}(G_{k})=\Theta(k)
.
The lower bound is trivial, since
|V(G_{k})|=(255+4+1)k
and permuting the colors (with no fixed points) in an arbitrary colouring
\alpha
gives a colouring
\beta
with
d(\alpha,\beta)\geq|V(G_{k})|=260k
.
For the upper bound, let
\alpha,\beta
be two
4
-colourings of
G_{k}
, and let
\alpha(P_{k}),\beta(P_{k})
be their restriction to
P_{k}
. As
4=\Delta(P_{k})+2
, it follows (e.g. see
[
9
, Thm. 1]
) that
\diam\mathcal{C}_{4}(P_{k})\leq 2k
. Thus there is a sequence
\mathcal{S}
of at most
2k
vertex recolourings that transforms
\alpha(P_{k})
into
\beta(P_{k})
while maintaining a proper colouring of
P_{k}
, but not necessarily of
G_{k}
.
Consider an arbitrary step of
\mathcal{S}
where we need to recolour some vertex
p_{i}
of
P_{k}
, without loss of generality from colour
1
to colour
2
. This recolouring step is also allowed in
G_{k}
unless the special vertex
v(H_{i})
of
H_{i}
has colour
2
as well. In this case, we first recolour
v(H_{i})
to either
3
or
4
.
This can be done in one step if either
3
or
4
does not appear in the size-
255
part
B_{i}
of
H_{i}
. Otherwise, we recolour
B_{i}
to remove either
3
or
4
from
B_{i}
, using at most
\lfloor 255/2\rfloor=127
steps, and then recolour
v(H_{i})
to either
3
or
4
. In total we need at most
128
extra recolourings to accomodate the recolouring of
p_{i}
.
Doing this for every step of
\mathcal{S}
, we need at most
129\cdot 2k
steps to recolour
\alpha
in such a way that it agrees with
\beta
on the vertices of
P_{k}
.

After recolouring
P_{k}
, we must finally recolour each copy of
H
, which can be done in at most
433

steps per copy. (This needs a small argument; it does not follow directly from
Theorem

1
because throughout the colour
\beta(p_{i})
is forbidden at the special vertex of
H_{i}
.) Here is one way to prove this upper bound.
Let
\widehat{\alpha}
denote the current colouring. For some colour
c\in L(v_{1})
, we first remove
c
from part
B_{i}
(using
\beta(w)
for each
w\in B_{i}
when possible, and using some other colour in
\beta(B_{i})
when needed); then we recolour
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\}
with
c
; then we recolour each
w\in B_{i}\setminus\beta^{-1}(c)
with
\beta(w)
; then we recolour each
v_{i}
with
\beta(v_{i})
; and finally we recolour each
w\in\beta^{-1}(c)
with
c
. We choose colour
c
to minimise
|\widehat{\alpha}^{-1}(c)\cap V|+|\beta^{-1}(c)\cap V|
. Thus, the number of recolouring steps is at most
|\widehat{\alpha}^{-1}(c)\cap V|+4+|B_{i}|+4+|\beta^{-1}(c)\cap V|=255+8+|\widehat{\alpha}^{-1}(c)\cap V|+|\beta^{-1}(c)\cap V|\leq 263+\lfloor 255\cdot 2/3\rfloor=263+170=433
.
Thus, in total the distance between
\alpha
and
\beta
is at most
129\cdot 2k+433k=691k
.
∎

Remark 20
.

The construction above can be generalized to
k
-colourings and
k
-fold list-assignments for any
k\geq 4
, by replacing the graph
H
with a larger complete bipartite graph, and replacing the path with a
(k-2)
-colourable
n
-vertex graph with
(k-1)
-colour
diameter
\Theta(n^{2})
and
k
-colour diameter
\Theta(n)
, the existence of which is guaranteed by constructions in
[
6
]
.
