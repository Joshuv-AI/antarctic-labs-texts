Chitosan-based nano-encapsulation enhances the anti-diabetic efficacy of Eremina desertorum extract by ameliorating hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation in diabetic rats
Authors: Sahar Fakhry, Salwa Fouad, Alyaa Farid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study developed a novel Eremina desertorum snail extract (Ere extract) loaded-chitosan nanoparticles (Ere-CS NPs) to enhance the anti-diabetic efficacy of Eremina desertorum snail extract. The nanoparticles, synthesized via ionic gelation, were spherical (~80 nm) and exhibited storage stability when lyophilized, with consistent physicochemical properties upon reconstitution over a 90-day period, high encapsulation efficiency (73.56%) and a sustained, pH-dependent release profile. Biochemical analysis confirmed the successful loading of the extract’s rich profile of amino acids, fatty acids, minerals, and vitamins. In vitro, Ere-CS NPs demonstrated significantly superior antioxidant (scavenging DPPH, H₂O₂, OH-, O₂⁻), anti-inflammatory (inhibiting erythrocyte hemolysis), and antimicrobial activities compared to the free extract and blank nanoparticles, alongside high biocompatibility. In a streptozotocin-induced diabetic rat model, an 8-week oral treatment with Ere-CS NPs (100 and 200 mg/kg) produced a potent, dose-dependent anti-diabetic effect, outperforming the unencapsulated extract. The nano-formulation effectively normalized hyperglycemia, hypoinsulinemia, and body weight, and reduced serum fructosamine. It also provided significant hepatorenal protection, corrected diabetic dyslipidemia by normalizing hepatic and serum triglycerides, cholesterol, LDL-C, and HDL-C, and markedly alleviated pancreatic oxidative stress by reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) and restoring superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activities. Concurrently, Ere-CS NPs potently suppressed pancreatic levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6). These findings demonstrate that chitosan nano-encapsulation robustly enhances the bioavailability and multi-targeted therapeutic potential of Eremina desertorum extract, positioning Ere-CS NPs as a promising comprehensive nanomedicine for managing diabetes and its complications.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) has become an important issue in global healthcare because of its dramatic rise in prevalence; it impacts individuals across all demographics, ages, and socioeconomic classes in almost every nation [1]. Despite being a worldwide problem, low- and middle-income nations are seeing the fastest increases in both incidence and mortality rates [2]. The history of diabetes is long; early peoples documented disorders that revealed its characteristics, but modern understanding was propelled by the recognition that diabetes was not a one disease entity [3]. The treatment and management of DM represent a significant global health challenge, as a definitive cure remains difficult to find. The disease affects multiple organ systems, necessitating a multifaceted strategy that integrates both non-pharmacological and pharmacological interventions. A multifactorial approach to treatment is crucial for minimizing the risk of both macrovascular and microvascular complications associated with diabetes [4].

Diet and physical activity are fundamental to diabetes management, serving as primary determinants of energy balance and metabolic control. While both type 1 (T1DM) and type 2 (T2DM) DM require lifestyle considerations, their etiologies and therapeutic approaches differ substantially [5–7]. T1DM results from autoimmune-mediated destruction of pancreatic β-cells, leading to absolute insulin deficiency and lifelong dependence on exogenous insulin. Despite advances in insulin delivery and monitoring, individuals with T1DM remain at high risk for acute and chronic complications, including hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, and progressive micro- and macrovascular damage [8, 9].

Beyond glycemic instability, T1DM is associated with systemic complications that profoundly affect multiple organs. Chronic hyperglycemia promotes the formation of advanced glycation end products, increases oxidative stress, and triggers inflammatory pathways, contributing to endothelial dysfunction and tissue injury [10, 11]. Consequently, individuals with T1DM frequently develop hepatorenal impairment, dyslipidemia, and accelerated atherosclerosis, elevating cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [12, 13]. Notably, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and diabetic nephropathy are increasingly recognized as significant comorbidities in T1DM, further underscoring the need for therapies that offer both metabolic and organ-protective effects [14, 15].

Although intensive insulin therapy can reduce the risk of complications, it does not fully normalize metabolic disturbances or halt disease progression [16]. Moreover, the rising global incidence of T1DM, particularly among younger populations, highlights an urgent need for safe and effective adjunctive treatments that address not only hyperglycemia but also the underlying oxidative stress, inflammation, and organ damage characteristic of the disease [17, 18].

In response to the limitations and side effects of conventional pharmacotherapy, there is a paradigm shift towards exploring complementary and alternative strategies rooted in natural products. This approach focuses on utilizing a spectrum of bioactive natural compounds, including vitamins, essential amino acids, beneficial fatty acids, and critical minerals, to target the multifactorial pathology of diabetes. For instance, Vitamin E and C act as potent antioxidants, mitigating the oxidative stress that impairs β-cell function and promotes insulin resistance [19, 20]. Specific amino acids are precursors to glutathione, the body’s master antioxidant, and can influence insulin signaling pathways [21]. Furthermore, omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are renowned for their potent anti-inflammatory effects and their ability to improve insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues [22].

Zinc (Zn) is essential for insulin crystallization and storage within pancreatic β-cell secretory granules, while also modulating insulin secretion and signaling [23, 24]. Magnesium (Mg) acts as a cofactor for numerous enzymes involved in glucose metabolism and insulin action, and its deficiency is linked to insulin resistance [25]. Although less well-defined in type 1 diabetes, chromium (Cr) has been reported to enhance insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues in some models of insulin resistance [26]. Together, these minerals contribute to glucose homeostasis through complementary mechanisms relevant to diabetes pathophysiology. The therapeutic rationale is that targeted, multi-component delivery of these essential nutrients can systemically address fundamental metabolic imbalances, concurrently attenuate chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, and thereby restore glucose homeostasis with a favorable safety profile. Among the diverse sources of natural therapeutics, land snails represent a particularly promising yet underexplored reservoir. Terrestrial gastropods have been integral to traditional medicine across various cultures for centuries, revered for their therapeutic potential in wound healing and treating inflammation. Modern scientific inquiry has begun to validate this traditional use, revealing that snails are not merely a source of single compounds but a complex matrix of synergistic bioactive nutrients. Their physiological makeup, including their mucus and body tissues, is rich in a wide spectrum of therapeutic molecules, positioning them as a unique source of multi-component nutraceuticals.

Focusing on a specific candidate, the desert snail Eremina desertorum (Forsskal, 1775) has shown significant promise. This species has adapted to survive harsh arid conditions; a trait often associated with the production of unique and potent secondary metabolites. Medicinally, extracts from E. desertorum have demonstrated remarkable biological activities. For instance, its mucin has exhibited potent anticancer and antioxidant properties, showing efficacy against colon and liver cancer cells in vitro, while its crude tissue extract has displayed significant anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects [27, 28]. This suggests its potential use in therapies targeting chronic diseases driven by oxidative stress and inflammation, such as diabetes and its complications.

Beyond its direct medicinal applications, the body muscle of Eremina desertorum is also a valuable food source, consumed in several regions and appreciated for its high nutritional density. The tissue is characterized by a rich composition of fundamental nutrients. It is an exceptional resource of high-quality protein, containing all essential amino acids necessary for physiological functions and repair processes. Its lipid profile is composed of beneficial saturated, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated fatty acids, including omega-3 and omega-6 families, which are crucial for anti-inflammatory responses and metabolic health. Furthermore, the snail is a rich source of essential minerals, such as zinc, which is vital for insulin synthesis and secretion; magnesium, a cofactor for numerous enzymatic reactions in glucose metabolism; and calcium, alongside fat-soluble vitamins like vitamin E, a powerful antioxidant, and water-soluble B-complex vitamins that act as essential coenzymes in energy production [29, 30]. This comprehensive nutritional profile, combined with its documented bioactivities, positions Eremina desertorum not just as a food, but as a therapeutic agent whose full potential can be unlocked through advanced delivery systems like nano-encapsulation.

While the Eremina desertorum extract (Ere extract) possesses a compelling therapeutic profile, its translation into an effective oral drug faces significant pharmacokinetic challenges. The bioactive components within the extract, including its proteins, peptides, and sensitive fatty acids, are susceptible to degradation in the harsh gastrointestinal environment, leading to reduced bioavailability and inconsistent therapeutic effects. To overcome these barriers and fully control the extract’s potential, advanced drug delivery systems are essential. Chitosan nanoparticles (CS NPs) represent an ideal vehicle for this purpose. As a biocompatible, biodegradable, and mucoadhesive polymer, chitosan can be engineered into nanoscale carriers that encapsulate the complex mixture of the snail extract. This nano-encapsulation provides a multifaceted solution as the chitosan matrix acts as a protective shield, safeguarding the labile bioactive compounds from premature degradation by stomach acid and digestive enzymes [31]. Furthermore, the positive surface charge of CS NPs promotes a strong interaction with the negatively charged intestinal mucosa, prolonging residence time at the site of absorption and enhancing permeation through the gut wall [32]. Crucially, CS NPs can provide a sustained and controlled release of the encapsulated extract, ensuring a prolonged therapeutic stimulus rather than a rapid release. This approach not only maximizes the bioavailability of the individual nutrients and active compounds but also leverages the potential for synergistic action between the chitosan polymer, known for its own intrinsic anti-diabetic and antioxidant properties, and the multi-component Ere extract, thereby creating a superior therapeutic system for the complete management of diabetes [33].

Therefore, the present study focuses on evaluating a novel nano-encapsulated natural extract for its potential to ameliorate T1DM and its complications in a streptozotocin-induced diabetic rat model, a well-established model of insulin-deficient diabetes that replicates key features of human T1DM, including β-cell loss, hyperglycemia, and secondary organ pathology. The primary aim was to load the Ere extract into chitosan nanoparticles (Ere-CS NPs) to protect its bioactive components and improve their delivery. Also, the study aims to evaluate the anti-diabetic efficacy of this formulation by thoroughly investigating its effects on blood glucose, insulin levels, oxidative stress, inflammation, and organ protection in a diabetic rat model, comparing its performance to the free extract.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Chitosan (low molecular weight, degree of deacylation >85%), sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), MTT reagent, DPPH, indomethacin, heparin sodium, human plasma and streptozotocin (STZ) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Glacial acetic acid, absolute ethanol, sodium hydroxide, and sodium citrate buffer components were obtained from El-Gomhouria Company (Egypt). Commercial ELISA kits for insulin, fructosamine, urea, creatinine, triglycerides, malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), interleukin (IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and IL-6 were sourced from MyBioSource (San Diego, CA, USA), while alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) kits were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and Biovision (Milpitas, CA, USA), respectively.

Biological materials

Live specimens of Eremina desertorum were collected from the Egyptian desert. Male Sprague Dawley rats (200–220 g) were obtained from the National Organization for Drug Control and Research (NODCAR, Cairo, Egypt). Microbial strains, including Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 6633), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), and Candida albicans (ATCC 10231), were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Additionally, a clinical isolate of Mucor sp. was generously provided by the Microbiology Department, Faculty of Science, Cairo University, Egypt.

Instruments and equipment

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis was conducted on an Agilent 7890B GC coupled to an Agilent 5977B MSD. Mineral content was determined via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; PerkinElmer Avio 500). Nanoparticles characterization included dynamic light scattering (DLS; Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL JEM-1400Plus). Spectrophotometric measurements were made using a Shimadzu UV-1900i, and microplate readings were performed on a BioTek Synergy H1. Additional equipment included a Heidolph rotary evaporator, a Christ Alpha 1–4 LDplus freeze dryer, a Potter-Elvehjem homogenizer, Eppendorf and Hermle centrifuges, a Mettler Toledo pH meter, and an Accu-Chek Active glucometer.

Preparation of Eremina desertorum body muscle extract (Ere extract)

Live specimens of the desert snail, Eremina desertorum, were collected from their natural habitat and immediately transported to the laboratory. The shells were carefully broken to remove the soft body tissues. The whole-body muscle (approximately 50 g wet weight) was then separated, rinsed with distilled H2O to eliminate any remains, and cut into small pieces using a sterile scalpel to increase the surface area for extraction. The extraction protocol was adapted from the method described by Periyasamy et al. [34]. The minced tissue sample was subjected to ethanolic extraction using absolute ethanol as the solvent in a 1:5 (w/v) ratio. The mixture was cold-steeped for 24 h at −18 °C to facilitate the extraction of bioactive compounds. Following maceration, the crude extract was filtered under vacuum (Whatman filter paper No. 1) to remove particulate matter. The resulting filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure at 40 °C to completely remove the ethanol solvent. The concentrated extract was then transferred into a pre-weighed petri dish and dried to constant weight in a desiccator containing anhydrous silica gel at 25 °C for 48 h to obtain the final dried extract. The dry weight of the extract was recorded, and the yield was calculated in triplicate from three independent extractions. According to the formula: Extraction yield (%) = [(dry weight of extract) / (weight of fresh starting material)] × 100, the mean yield was 3.6 ± 0.4% (mean ± SD, n = 3).The obtained Ere extract was stored at −20 °C in an airtight, light-proof container until further use for loading into CS NPs.

Preparation of Eremina desertorum extract-loaded chitosan nanoparticles (Ere-CS NPs)

Ere-CS NPs were produced using the ionic gelation method, based on the electrostatic interaction between the amino groups of chitosan (+ve charge) and the cross-linker TPP (-ve charge) [35]. A chitosan (CS) solution (2 mg/mL) was prepared by dissolving low molecular weight chitosan (degree of deacetylation >85%) in 1% (v/v) aqueous acetic acid solution. The mixture was stirred continuously at 600 rpm at room temperature (~25 °C) until completely dissolved. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 5.0 using 1 M NaOH and subsequently filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane filter. A cross-linking solution of TPP was prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in deionized water. For the loaded nanoparticles, 100 mg of the dried Ere extract was dissolved in 10 mL of the TPP solution (TPP dissolved in deionized water at 1 mg/mL) under mild stirring to ensure complete dissolution prior to nanoparticle formation. The nanoparticle formation was achieved by adding the TPP solution containing the dissolved Ere extract drop-wise (at a rate of 0.5 mL/min) into 50 mL of the chitosan solution (2 mg/mL) under constant magnetic stirring at 800 rpm. The mixing continued for an additional 60 min at room temperature to ensure complete ionic cross-linking and the formation of Ere-CS NPs. This protocol resulted in a CS:TPP mass ratio of 5:1. For comparison, blank CS NPs were prepared following the same protocol but using a TPP solution without the Ere extract. The resulting Ere-CS NP suspension was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C to separate the nanoparticles from the free, unentrapped extract. The pellet was collected, washed twice with deionized water, and re-dispersed in 10 mL of PBS (pH 7.4). The purified suspension was then lyophilized to obtain a dry powder for further characterization and biological studies. The final yield of the lyophilized Ere-CS NPs from three independent preparations was 90 ± 1.5 mg.

The long-term colloidal stability of CS NPs and Ere-CS NPs was evaluated over a 90-day period. To prevent Ere extract release and nanoparticles aggregation during storage, nanoparticles were lyophilized with 5% (w/v) trehalose as a cryoprotectant using a Christ Alpha 1–4 LDplus lyophilizer. The lyophilized powders were stored in sealed, light-protected vials with desiccant at 4 °C. At each time point (0, 15, 30, 60, and 90 days), nanoparticles were reconstituted in deionized water at a concentration of 1 mg/mL by gentle vortexing (30 s) and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min to ensure complete dispersion.

Particle size and zeta potential

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were conducted using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Nanoparticle suspensions were diluted 1:100 (v/v) in deionized water to avoid multiple scattering effects and equilibrated at 25 °C for 60 s before measurement. The hydrodynamic diameter was derived from intensity-weighted distributions using the manufacturer’s software (Zetasizer Software v7.13). Zeta potential was determined via electrophoretic light scattering in folded capillary cells (DTS1070) using the Smoluchowski approximation. Measurements were performed in triplicate at 25 °C.

Morphological analysis

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) was performed using a JEOL JEM-1400Plus microscope (JEOL Ltd., Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Nanoparticle suspensions were diluted 1:50 (v/v) in deionized water, and a 10 µL aliquot was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid (300 mesh) and allowed to air-dry for 5 min. Excess liquid was gently blotted with filter paper. Samples were imaged without staining. Micrographs were analyzed using ImageJ software (v1.53) to determine mean particle size from at least 100 indal nanoparticles per sample.

Biochemical characterization of Ere extract and Ere-CS NPs

The dried Ere extract and Ere-CS NPs were subjected to detailed biochemical profiling to determine its composition of amino acids, fatty acids, minerals, and vitamins. The biochemical composition of the encapsulated material within the purified Ere-CS NPs was determined to confirm successful loading and to calculate encapsulation efficiency. The methods for amino acid, fatty acid, mineral, and vitamin analysis were adapted to first liberate the encapsulated components from the CS NPs matrix.

Prior to specific analysis, the purified Ere-CS NPs suspension was subjected to a disruption process to break down the chitosan matrix and release the encapsulated extract. Briefly, a weighed quantity of lyophilized Ere-CS NPs powder (10 mg) was resuspended in 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 5.5) and incubated with chitosanase (from Streptomyces sp., Sigma-Aldrich; activity: 1 unit per mg of chitosan) [36] at 37 °C for 4 h under gentle shaking (100 rpm) to ensure complete enzymatic hydrolysis of the chitosan matrix and release of the encapsulated extract components. After digestion, the enzyme was inactivated by heating the mixture at 90 °C for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min to pellet any insoluble debris and denatured enzyme. The resulting supernatant containing the released extract components was carefully collected and used for subsequent biochemical analyses.

Amino acid analysis

HPLC was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a quaternary pump, autosampler, thermostated column compartment, and diode array detector (DAD). Amino acid analysis was conducted after acid hydrolysis [37]. Approximately 50 mg of sample was hydrolyzed with 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 24 h under a nitrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidation. After cooling, the hydrolysate was filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and dried under vacuum. The residue was reconstituted in 0.1 M borate buffer (pH 9.5) and derivatized with o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) as a pre-column derivatization agent. Derivatized amino acids were separated on a reversed-phase C18 column (Zorbax Eclipse Plus, 4.6 × 100 mm, 3.5 μm; Agilent Technologies, cat. no. 959757-902) maintained at 40 °C.

A binary gradient elution was employed at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min using the following mobile phases:

Solvent A: 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.8) containing 0.02% (v/v) triethylamine

Solvent A: 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.8) containing 0.02% (v/v) triethylamine

Solvent B: acetonitrile:methanol:water (45:45:10, v/v/v)

Solvent B: acetonitrile:methanol:water (45:45:10, v/v/v)

The gradient program was as follows: 0–2 min, 5% B; 2–15 min, 5–50% B; 15–20 min, 50–95% B; 20–25 min, 95% B; 25–26 min, 95–5% B; 26–30 min, 5% B for re-equilibration. Detection was performed at 338 nm using the DAD. Individual amino acids were identified and quantified by comparison of retention times and peak areas with those of authenticated standards (Amino Acid Standard H, Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA).

Fatty acid analysis

GC-MS was used to examine the composition of fatty acids [38]. Using a chloroform-methanol combination (2:1 v/v), lipids were initially extracted from the sample (100 mg) using the Folch technique. After that, the extracted lipids were refluxed with methanolic sulfuric acid (2% H₂SO₄ in methanol) at 70 °C for two hours in order to transesterify them into fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). A GC system with a fused silica capillary column and a mass spectrometric detector was used to evaluate the resultant FAMEs after they had been extracted using hexane. The carrier gas used was helium. Mass spectra and relative retention durations were compared with commercial standards in order to identify individual FAMEs. The peak area of each FAME in relation to the total area of all identified fatty acids served as the basis for quantification.

Mineral analysis

The minerals content was revealed using ICP-OES [39]. Approximately 200 mg of the dried sample was digested with 5 mL of concentrated HNO₃ in a closed-vessel microwave digestion system. The digested sample was then cooled, diluted to a known volume with deionized water, and filtered. The solution was directly aspirated into the ICP-OES. The concentrations of individual minerals were calculated by comparing the emission intensities of the sample against a series of multi-element standard calibration curves.

Vitamin analysis

The analysis of vitamins was targeted based on expected water-soluble (B-complex) and fat-soluble (A and E) vitamins.

Fat-soluble vitamins: these were analyzed by HPLC [40]. The sample was saponified with ethanolic KOH to release vitamins from esters. The unsaponifiable matter, containing the vitamins, was extracted with hexane. The hexane layer was evaporated, and the residue was reconstituted in methanol. Separation was achieved on a reversed-phase C18 column using a gradient mobile phase (e.g., methanol:acetonitrile) and detected using a UV/Vis detector.

Fat-soluble vitamins: these were analyzed by HPLC [40]. The sample was saponified with ethanolic KOH to release vitamins from esters. The unsaponifiable matter, containing the vitamins, was extracted with hexane. The hexane layer was evaporated, and the residue was reconstituted in methanol. Separation was achieved on a reversed-phase C18 column using a gradient mobile phase (e.g., methanol:acetonitrile) and detected using a UV/Vis detector.

Water-soluble vitamins: these were analyzed individually using HPLC with UV detection [41]. The sample was dissolved in a weak acid and filtered. The filtrate was injected into an HPLC system equipped with a C18 column. A buffer system, such as potassium phosphate (pH- adjusted) with acetonitrile gradient was used for elution. Vitamins were identified and quantified by comparison with authentic standards.

Water-soluble vitamins: these were analyzed individually using HPLC with UV detection [41]. The sample was dissolved in a weak acid and filtered. The filtrate was injected into an HPLC system equipped with a C18 column. A buffer system, such as potassium phosphate (pH- adjusted) with acetonitrile gradient was used for elution. Vitamins were identified and quantified by comparison with authentic standards.

In vitro release study

The in vitro release profile of the Ere extract from CS NPs was evaluated using the dialysis bag method under simulated physiological and gastrointestinal conditions. Lyophilized Ere-CS NPs were re-dispersed in PBS (pH 7.4) and sealed in a dialysis membrane (MWCO 12 kDa). The bag was immersed in release medium maintained at 37 °C ± 0.5 °C under continuous stirring (100 rpm). To mimic relevant biological environments, the study was conducted in four PBS media adjusted to different pH values:

pH 7.4 (simulating systemic/blood conditions)

pH 7.4 (simulating systemic/blood conditions)

pH 5.5 (simulating mildly acidic inflammatory or endosomal environments)

pH 5.5 (simulating mildly acidic inflammatory or endosomal environments)

pH 3.9 (simulating the fasted-state rat stomach)

pH 3.9 (simulating the fasted-state rat stomach)

pH 3.2 (simulating the fed-state rat stomach)

pH 3.2 (simulating the fed-state rat stomach)

Aliquots (1 mL) of the release medium were withdrawn at predetermined time intervals (0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h) and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium to maintain sink conditions. The concentration of released extract in each aliquot was quantified by measuring total amino acid content via HPLC as described previously. The cumulative percentage of drug release was calculated and plotted against time to establish the pH-dependent release profile. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Antimicrobial activity assay

The antimicrobial activities of the Ere extract, blank CS NPs, and Ere-CS NPs were evaluated against a panel of Gram-positive bacteria [Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 6633), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923)], Gram-negative bacteria [Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853)], and fungi [Candida albicans (ATCC 10231), Mucor sp. (clinical isolate, Microbiology Department, Cairo University) using the agar well diffusion method [42]. A standardized inoculum was prepared by adjusting the turbidity of fresh microbial suspensions to 0.5 McFarland standard, corresponding to approximately 1.5 × 108CFU/mL for bacteria and 1 × 107 CFU/mL for fungi. Mueller-Hinton agar plates (for bacteria) or Sabouraud Dextrose agar plates (for fungi) were surface-inoculated uniformly using a sterile swab. Wells (6 mm diameter) were punched into the agar, and 50 μL of each test sample (at fixed concentrations of 10 mg/mL for nanoparticles and extract) was added to the wells. Gentamicin (Sigma-Aldrich) at a concentration of 10 μg/mL was used as the standard antibacterial control, while fluconazole (10 μg/mL) served as the antifungal control for C. albicans. For bacteria, plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C; for fungi, incubation was carried out for 48–72 h at 25 °C. The diameter of the inhibition zone (including the well diameter) was measured in millimeters. All tests were performed in triplicate.

Anti-inflammatory activity (erythrocyte hemolysis assay)

The in vitro anti-inflammatory activity was evaluated using a rat erythrocyte membrane stabilization assay, which measures the ability of test samples to inhibit hypotonicity-induced hemolysis; an indicator of membrane-stabilizing potential linked to anti-inflammatory effects [43]. Fresh whole blood was collected from Sprague Dawley rats via cardiac puncture into heparinized tubes (10 IU/mL blood) and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. After removal of the plasma, the packed erythrocytes were washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4) and resuspended in the same buffer to obtain a 10% (v/v) erythrocyte suspension. Test samples (Ere extract, CS NPs, and Ere-CS NPs) were prepared in PBS at final concentrations of 100, 200, 400, 600, 800 and 1000 µg/mL. Indomethacin (200 µg/mL) served as the standard anti-inflammatory control. For the assay, 1 mL of erythrocyte suspension was incubated with 1 mL of each sample or control at 37 °C for 30 min, after which 2 mL of a hypotonic saline solution (0.36% NaCl) was added to induce hemolysis, followed by an additional 30 min incubation at 37 °C. The reaction was halted by cooling on ice, and the mixtures were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 540 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1900i). Appropriate controls included a complete hemolysis control (erythrocytes in distilled water), a negative control (erythrocytes in isotonic PBS), and sample blanks (test samples without erythrocytes). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cytotoxicity assay (cell viability)

Cytotoxicity was evaluated using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay according to a standardized protocol [44]. The normal fibroblast cell line (BJ, ATCC® CRL-2522™) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For the assay, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing various concentrations of CS NPs, Ere extract, and Ere-CS NPs (ranging from 31.25 to 1000 µg/mL) and incubated for an additional 24 h. Following treatment, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved by adding 100 µL of DMSO to each well, followed by gentle shaking for 10 min. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Coagulation assay (prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time)

The effect of the samples on blood coagulation was assessed by measuring the prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (PTT) [45]. Human plasma (product #P9523, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was obtained from a commercial supplier and originated from pooled, anonymized healthy donors collected under ethical oversight. Plasma was incubated with different concentrations of Ere extract, CS NPs and Ere-CS NPs. Heparin was used as a standard anticoagulant control. For PT measurement, pre-warmed thromboplastin reagent was added to the plasma-sample mixture, and the time to clot formation was recorded. For PTT measurement, the plasma-sample mixture was incubated with PTT reagent, followed by the addition of calcium chloride, and the clotting time was recorded. The results were expressed in seconds (sec.).

Antioxidant activity assays

The antioxidant potential of Ere extract, CS NPs and Ere-CS NPs was evaluated using four different assays:

DPPH radical scavenging activity: the capacity of the samples to scavenge the stable 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical was assessed [44]. In short, a methanolic solution of DPPH was combined with various concentrations of the substances. The absorbance at 517 nm was measured after the mixture was incubated in the dark. A common antioxidant control was ascorbic acid. The DPPH scavenging activity percentage was determined.

DPPH radical scavenging activity: the capacity of the samples to scavenge the stable 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical was assessed [44]. In short, a methanolic solution of DPPH was combined with various concentrations of the substances. The absorbance at 517 nm was measured after the mixture was incubated in the dark. A common antioxidant control was ascorbic acid. The DPPH scavenging activity percentage was determined.

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) scavenging assay [46]: a solution of H₂O₂ was added to the samples at various concentrations. After incubation, the absorbance was measured at 230 nm, and the H₂O₂ scavenging % was calculated.

In vivo anti-diabetic study design

Induction of experimental diabetes

Experimental diabetes was induced in rats via a single intravenous injection of STZ, following an established protocol [49]. Briefly, after a 6-hour fasting period, STZ (Cat# S0130, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was freshly dissolved in ice-cold sodium citrate buffer (50 mM, pH 4.5) at a concentration of 32.5 mg/mL. The STZ solution was administered to the rats via intravenous injection at a dose of 65 mg/kg body weight (injection volume: 2 mL/kg). To prevent fatal hypoglycemia following the beta-cell cytotoxic shock, the rats were provided with a 10% sucrose solution as their drinking water for the first 24 hours post-injection. Diabetes was allowed to develop over a 7-day period. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels were monitored on days 3, 5, and 7 post-STZ injection from tail vein blood using a calibrated glucometer (Accu-Chek® Active, Roche Diagnostics, Germany). On day 7 post-injection, after a 6-hour fast, FBG was measured again. Rats with confirmed stable hyperglycemia, defined as a FBG level greater than 200 mg/dL on two consecutive measurements (days 5 and 7), were included in the study as the diabetic group. To further validate the diabetic state, serum levels of fructosamine (FTA), a marker of medium-term glycemic control, and insulin were quantified using specific commercial ELISA kits (FTA: MBS2601586; Insulin: MBS724709, MyBioSource, USA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Animals and ethical statement

Ten-week-old male Sprague Dawley rats weighing 200–220 g were purchased from the National Organization for Drug Control and Research in Cairo, Egypt. Rats were kept in regular experimental environments (12-hour light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 50–60% humidity), with full access to water and a standard rat food. Cairo University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) thoroughly examined and approved every experimental procedure (CUIF6822). The National Institutes of Health’s (NIH) guidelines for the use and care of laboratory animals were strictly followed during the investigation.

The administration volume was standardized to 5 mL/kg body weight for all groups. The doses were expressed as the equivalent weight of the administered formulation (extract or nanoparticles) per kg body weight of the rat.

Sample collection and processing

Rats were given an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (80 mg/kg) to induce anesthesia at the end of the 8-week treatment period. Cardiac puncture was used to obtain blood samples. After letting the blood clot for half an hour at room temperature, the serum was separated by centrifuging it for ten minutes at 1500 × g. For the following biochemical investigations, the collected serum aliquots were kept in storage at −80 °C. Vital organs, such as the liver and pancreas, were quickly removed after blood was drawn. A Potter-Elvehjem homogenizer was used to homogenize the tissue (1:2 w/v) in an ice-cold Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4). After centrifuging the homogenate for 15 min at 4 °C at 2000 × g, the supernatant was collected and kept at −80 °C to measure inflammatory and oxidative stress markers.

Assessment of liver function

Hepatotoxicity and liver integrity were evaluated by measuring the serum levels of key liver enzymes. ALT and AST are cytoplasmic enzymes released into the bloodstream upon hepatocyte damage. Serum ALT and AST activities were quantified using specific rat ELISA kits (ab234579, Abcam, UK, and E4321, Biovision, USA, respectively) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Assessment of renal function

Renal function was assessed by measuring the serum concentrations of urea and creatinine, which are the primary markers of kidney health. Elevated levels indicate impaired renal filtration. Serum urea and creatinine levels were determined using commercial rat ELISA kits (MBS2600001 and MBS007289, respectively; MyBioSource, USA) following the provided protocols.

Assessment of lipid profile

To evaluate the effect of treatments on metabolism and cardiovascular risk factors, the lipid profile was analyzed. Levels of triglycerides and total cholesterol were measured in both serum and liver tissue homogenates using a Triglyceride Assay Kit (MBS726298, MyBioSource, USA) and a Cholesterol Assay Kit (ab65390, Abcam, UK), as per the manufacturers’ guidelines.

Assessment of oxidative stress markers

The in vivo antioxidant capacity of the treatments was evaluated by measuring key oxidative stress parameters in pancreatic tissue homogenates. The extent of lipid peroxidation was assessed by quantifying MDA level. Furthermore, the activity of the endogenous antioxidant defense system was determined by measuring the enzymatic activity of SOD and GPx. These were analyzed using specific rat ELISA kits (MBS268427 for MDA, MBS266897 for SOD, and MBS727547 for GPx; MyBioSource, USA).

Assessment of pro-inflammatory cytokines

The anti-inflammatory effect of the treatments was investigated by measuring the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines in pancreatic tissue homogenates. The concentrations of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were determined using specific rat ELISA kits (MBS843359, MBS2507393, and MBS355410, respectively; MyBioSource, USA) in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols.

Morphometric and immunohistochemical analysis of pancreatic tissue

Following the 8-week treatment period, pancreatic tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, processed through graded ethanol and xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 µm thick) were cut and mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides. For immunohistochemistry, deparaffinized sections underwent antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and blocking with 5% normal goat serum. Primary antibodies against insulin (ab6995, Abcam, USA) and NF-κB p65 (ab16502, Abcam, USA) were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and detection with DAB chromogen. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and cover slipped.

Morphometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (v1.53, NIH, USA) for the following parameters (1) insulin-positive area expressed as a percentage of total pancreatic area (%), (2) islet density (number of islets per mm²), (3) mean islet area (µm²), and (4) NF-κB immunostaining intensity scored semi-quantitatively (0 = no staining, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong). All morphometric measurements were performed by two independent observers blinded to the treatment groups, and the mean values were used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Inter-group comparisons were conducted using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When the ANOVA indicated a significant difference, the Tukey post-hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons between group means. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant for all tests.

In vitro release study of Ere extract from Ere-CS NPs

The in vitro release profile of the Eremina desertorum extract from CS NPs was evaluated over 48 hours in PBS at different pH levels to simulate physiological (pH 7.4) and acidic (pH 5.5, 3.9 and 3.2) conditions (Fig. 4C). The results demonstrated a biphasic release pattern, characterized by an initial burst followed by sustained release, and clear pH-dependent behavior. At pH 7.4, the release was gradual and moderate, reaching a cumulative release of 16.0 ± 0.35% within the first hour and plateauing at 52.8 ± 0.60% by 48 h. In contrast, the release at pH 5.5 was significantly faster and more extensive, exhibiting a substantial initial burst of 30.0 ± 0.20% in the first hour and achieving a final cumulative release of 80.4 ± 0.55% at the 48-hour endpoint. Under more acidic conditions (pH 3.9 and 3.2), an extensive burst release was observed within the first hour (60.6 ± 0.5% at pH 3.9 and 63.5 ± 0.5% at pH 3.2), followed by near-complete release by 2 h (93.2 ± 0.3% at pH 3.9 and 94.0 ± 0.6% at pH 3.2). Release plateaued thereafter, exceeding 98% by 48 h at both acidic pH values. These results demonstrate a pronounced pH-dependent release behavior, where lower pH markedly enhances both the rate and extent of extract liberation, consistent with the protonation-driven swelling of chitosan under acidic conditions.

DL% and EE% of Ere-CS NPs

The efficiency of the encapsulation process was evaluated by determining the DL% and EE% based on the total amino acid content. The pure Ere extract used for nanoparticles preparation had a total amino acid content of 13312.33 mg/100 g. A total of 100 mg of this extract, containing 13.31 mg of amino acids, was used for the synthesis of Ere-CS NPs. The resulting batch of lyophilized Ere-CS NPs yielded 90 mg of powder. Analysis of this powder revealed a total amino acid content of 10880.33 mg/100 g, equating to 9.79 mg of amino acids in the entire batch. Based on these values, the EE% was calculated to be 73.56%, indicating a high efficiency of incorporation of the extract into the CS NPs.

In vitro safety evaluation of Ere extract, unloaded CS NPs and Ere-CS NPs

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activities of Ere extract, unloaded CS NPs, and Ere-CS NPs were evaluated against a panel of Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, and fungi using the agar well diffusion assay (Fig. 5A). All test samples were used at a fixed concentration of 10 mg/mL (50 μL per well), while gentamicin (for bacteria) and fluconazole (for fungi) were used as reference controls at 10 μg/mL. All assays were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and results were expressed as the mean inhibition zone diameter (mm) ± SD. Against the Gram-positive bacterium Bacillus subtilis, Ere-CS NPs produced an inhibition zone of 33.0 ± 2.6 mm, which was larger than those of Ere extract (26.0 ± 2.0 mm), unloaded CS NPs (27.7 ± 1.5 mm), and the gentamicin control (28.7 ± 1.8 mm). Similarly, for Staphylococcus aureus, Ere-CS NPs exhibited the strongest activity (36.3 ± 1.5 mm), followed by the gentamicin control (31.2 ± 0.8 mm), CS NPs (25.0 ± 2.0 mm), and Ere extract (19.7 ± 1.5 mm).

Among the Gram-negative bacteria, Ere-CS NPs showed pronounced activity against Escherichia coli with an inhibition zone of 48.7 ± 2.5 mm, substantially exceeding the effects of Ere extract (28.5 ± 1.3 mm), CS NPs (29.0 ± 2.6 mm), and the gentamicin control (20.2 ± 0.7 mm). Against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Ere-CS NPs also demonstrated superior inhibition (38.0 ± 2.0 mm) compared to Ere extract (27.7 ± 2.3 mm), CS NPs (21.3 ± 1.5 mm), and the gentamicin control (23.4 ± 1.5 mm).

Collectively, these data demonstrated that nano-encapsulation of Ere extract within CS NPs significantly enhanced its antimicrobial potency against both bacterial and fungal pathogens.

Anti-inflammatory activity

The in vitro anti-inflammatory activity was assessed using a rat erythrocyte hemolysis inhibition assay, performed in triplicate (n = 3). Results were expressed as mean percent inhibition of hemolysis ± SD across a range of concentrations (100–1000 µg/mL) (Fig. 5B). At the highest concentration tested (1000 µg/mL), Ere-CS NPs exhibited 96.0 ± 2.0% inhibition of hemolysis, which was comparable to the standard anti-inflammatory drug indomethacin (200 µg/mL; 96.7 ± 1.5%). In contrast, the free Ere extract showed 89.7 ± 1.5% inhibition and unloaded CS NPs showed 90.3 ± 1.5% inhibition at the same concentration. A clear dose-dependent response was observed for all formulations. At 800 µg/mL, Ere-CS NPs inhibited hemolysis by 92.7 ± 1.5%, while Ere extract and CS NPs showed 83.7 ± 1.5% and 82.7 ± 2.5% inhibition, respectively. At 600 µg/mL, inhibition values were 84.3 ± 3.1% for Ere-CS NPs, 80.0 ± 1.0% for Ere extract, and 77.3 ± 2.1% for CS NPs.

Lower concentrations further highlighted the superior membrane-stabilizing effect of the nano-formulation. At 400 µg/mL, Ere-CS NPs achieved 77.3 ± 2.1% inhibition, compared to 73.7 ± 2.1% (Ere extract) and 70.3 ± 1.5% (CS NPs). At 200 µg/mL, the values were 67.3 ± 2.1% (Ere-CS NPs), 56.7 ± 4.5% (Ere extract), and 65.0 ± 3.0% (CS NPs). Even at the lowest concentration tested (100 µg/mL), Ere-CS NPs maintained notable activity (60.7 ± 2.5%), whereas Ere extract and CS NPs showed 52.0 ± 2.6% and 55.7 ± 3.5% inhibition, respectively. These results demonstrated that chitosan-based nano-encapsulation significantly enhanced the membrane-stabilizing (anti-inflammatory) activity of Ere extract, with Ere-CS NPs exhibiting potency like that of the reference drug indomethacin at higher concentrations.

Cytotoxicity activity

Cell viability was evaluated after exposure to Ere extract, unloaded CS NPs, and Ere-CS NPs across a concentration range of 31.25–1000 µg/mL (performed in triplicate) (Fig. 5C). All formulations exhibited high biocompatibility, with viability consistently above 85% even at the highest concentration tested. At 1000 µg/mL, viability % was 89.8 ± 1.3% for Ere extract, 87.0 ± 2.4% for CS NPs, and 94.7 ± 1.0% for Ere-CS NPs. Ere-CS NPs showed significantly the highest viability across all concentrations, reaching 98.2 ± 0.6% at 31.25 µg/mL (Vs 94.5 ± 3.4% and 93.7 ± 1.5% for Ere extract and CSNPs, respectively). While both Ere extract and CS NPs maintained excellent biocompatibility ( > 87% viability at all doses), Ere-CS NPs consistently supported significantly higher cell viability, indicating that the nano-formulation not only preserved but slightly enhanced cellular tolerability compared to the free extract or blank nanoparticles.

Anticoagulant activity

The effects of Ere extract, unloaded CS NPs, and Ere-CS NPs on blood coagulation were evaluated by measuring PT and PTT. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and results were expressed as mean clotting time (seconds) ± SD across concentrations of 0, 25, 50, and 75 µg/mL (Fig. 5D). At baseline (0 µg/mL), PT values were similar for all samples (14.7 ± 0.6 s). Heparin, used as a positive anticoagulant control, markedly prolonged PT in a concentration-dependent manner, reaching 121.0 ± 2.0 s at 75 µg/mL. In contrast, the test samples showed only modest increases in PT even at the highest concentration. At 75 µg/mL, Ere extract prolonged PT to 24.3 ± 1.5 s, CS NPs to 26.3 ± 1.5 s, and Ere-CS NPs to 28.3 ± 2.5 s. These values remained significantly shorter than those of heparin at all corresponding concentrations. Baseline PTT was 26.7 ± 0.6 s for all samples. Heparin strongly prolonged PTT, with values reaching 157.7 ± 3.1 s at 75 µg/mL. The test samples produced moderate, concentration-dependent increases. At 75 µg/mL, PTT values were 51.3 ± 3.5 s for Ere extract, 53.7 ± 1.5 s for CS NPs, and 62.3 ± 1.5 s for Ere-CS NPs. As with PT, all test formulations resulted in significantly shorter clotting times compared to heparin at equivalent concentrations. The results indicated that while Ere-CS NPs, CS NPs, and Ere extract exhibited mild concentration-dependent anticoagulant effects in vitro, their impact on both the intrinsic and extrinsic coagulation pathways remained substantially lower than that of the standard anticoagulant heparin. Notably, Ere-CS NPs did not induce clinically relevant prolongation of clotting times at the tested concentrations, supporting their favorable hemocompatibility profile.

The nano-formulation retained appreciable activity at 1.95 µg/mL (33.7 ± 2.5%), while ascorbic acid, Ere extract, and CS NPs scavenged 6.3 ± 1.5%, 8.0 ± 2.0%, and 24.3 ± 3.2%, respectively.

At 125 µg/mL, inhibition values were 55.3 ± 4.7% (Ere-CS NPs), 12.3 ± 2.5% (ascorbic acid), 35.3 ± 2.5% (Ere extract), and 26.7 ± 0.6% (CS NPs). Even at the lowest concentration (1.95 µg/mL), Ere-CS NPs scavenged 12.0 ± 2.0% of OH- radicals, whereas ascorbic acid, Ere extract, and CS NPs scavenged 2.7 ± 1.2%, 9.7 ± 2.1%, and 4.0 ± 1.0%, respectively.

O₂⁻ scavenging activity (Fig. 6D): Against the superoxide anion, Ere-CS NPs again showed the highest activity, scavenging 85.0 ± 1.0% at 1000 µg/mL, while ascorbic acid, Ere extract, and CS NPs scavenged 51.7 ± 1.5%, 68.7 ± 0.6%, and 54.3 ± 1.5%, respectively. At 250 µg/mL, Ere-CS NPs inhibited 67.0 ± 2.6%, compared to 35.3 ± 3.5% (ascorbic acid), 48.7 ± 3.1% (Ere extract), and 40.3 ± 1.5% (CS NPs). At 1.95 µg/mL, scavenging activity was 14.0 ± 2.6% for Ere-CS NPs, 6.3 ± 1.5% for ascorbic acid, 9.7 ± 1.5% for Ere extract, and 10.0 ± 1.0% for CS NPs.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that nano-encapsulation significantly enhanced the in vitro antioxidant capacity of Ere extract, with Ere-CS NPs exhibiting superior scavenging activity against DPPH, H₂O₂, OH-, and O₂⁻ across a wide concentration range.

In vivo anti-diabetic effect of Ere extract, unloaded CS NPs, and Ere-CS NPs

Metabolic parameters and glycemic control

The therapeutic efficacy of the Ere-CS NPs on the core metabolic characteristics of diabetes was evaluated over the eight-week treatment period. As expected, induction of diabetes with STZ resulted in severe hyperglycemia and its characteristic features. The diabetic control group exhibited a significant reduction in final body weight (171.8 ± 5.1 g) (Fig. 7A) and insulin levels (2.1 ± 0.2 ng/mL) (Fig. 7B), accompanied by elevated fasting blood glucose (FBG: 315.6 ± 3.0 mg/dL) (Fig. 7C) and serum fructosamine (FTA: 310 ± 4.3 µmol/L) (Fig. 7D) compared to the healthy control group (Body weight: 217.4 ± 1.6 g; Insulin: 4.04 ± 0.1 ng/mL; FBG: 120.4 ± 2.1 mg/dL; FTA: 141.2 ± 2.6 µmol/L).

Therapeutic effect of Ere extract, unloaded CS NPs and Ere-CS NPs on animal’s body weight (A), insulin level (B), FBG level (C) and FTA level (D). Results were expressed as mean ± SD (n = 5), a and b represented significance (P < 0.05) when compared to diabetic control and healthy control groups, respectively

Treatment with Ere-CS NPs produced a marked, dose-dependent reversal of these diabetic alterations. In diabetic rats, the high dose of Ere-CS NPs (200 mg/kg) effectively restored metabolic parameters to near-normal levels, demonstrating a final body weight of 220.6 ± 1.8 g, an insulin level of 4.1 ± 0.2 ng/mL, a FBG of 123.4 ± 2.9 mg/dL, and a FTA level of 142.8 ± 2.8 µmol/L. The lower dose of Ere-CS NPs (100 mg/kg) also showed significant anti-diabetic activity, with values of 216.0 ± 2.9 g, 3.8 ± 0.2 ng/mL, 157.8 ± 3.3 mg/dL, and 168.6 ± 5.2 µmol/L for body weight, insulin, FBG, and FTA, respectively. In contrast, the unloaded CS NPs and the pure Ere extract, particularly at the lower dose, elicited only modest or non-significant improvements in these parameters compared to the diabetic control. All treatments administered to the healthy control branch had no significant effect on body weight, insulin, FBG, or FTA levels, indicating a lack of toxicity and a specific therapeutic action in the diabetic state.

Hepatorenal protective effects

The impact of experimental diabetes and subsequent treatments, with Ere extract, unloaded CS NPs and Ere-CS NPs, on liver and kidney function was assessed by measuring serum biomarkers. Induction of diabetes resulted in significant hepatorenal impairment, as evidenced by elevated serum levels of AST (55.4 ± 1.1 U/L) (Fig. 8A), ALT (43.4 ± 2.4 U/L) (Fig. 8B), urea (90.6 ± 1.5 mg/dL) (Fig. 8C), and creatinine (2.66 ± 0.3 mg/dL) (Fig. 8D) in the diabetic control group compared to the healthy control (AST: 29.6 ± 2.1 U/L; ALT: 23.8 ± 2.2 U/L; Urea: 39.8 ± 1.3 mg/dL; Creatinine: 0.9 ± 0.2 mg/dL). Treatment with Ere-CS NPs demonstrated a potent, dose-dependent protective effect against diabetes-induced organ damage. Administration of the high dose of Ere-CS NPs (200 mg/kg) to diabetic rats effectively normalized all measured parameters (AST: 29.6 ± 2.1 U/L; ALT: 23.6 ± 1.5 U/L; Urea: 39.4 ± 3.4 mg/dL; Creatinine: 0.98 ± 0.3 mg/dL), restoring them to levels like those of healthy control group. The lower dose of Ere-CS NPs (100 mg/kg) also offered significant protection, bringing the values close to the normal range (AST: 31.0 ± 1.6 U/L; ALT: 27.6 ± 2.5 U/L; Urea: 44.6 ± 3.0 mg/dL; Creatinine: 0.94 ± 0.1 mg/dL). In contrast, the pure Ere extract and unloaded CS NPs provided only partial amelioration. The high dose of Ere extract (200 mg/kg) showed a moderate beneficial effect (AST: 41.8 ± 1.4 U/L; ALT: 33.4 ± 1.1 U/L; Urea: 65.8 ± 2.9 mg/dL; Creatinine: 1.42 ± 0.3 mg/dL), while the lower doses of both Ere extract and CS NPs resulted in minimal improvement. Importantly, none of the treatments administered to the non-diabetic healthy rats induced any significant alterations in liver or kidney function markers, confirming the safety of the formulations at the tested doses.

Amelioration of diabetic dyslipidemia

The study evaluated the effect of treatments on lipid metabolism, a key aspect of diabetic complications, by measuring triglyceride and cholesterol levels in both liver tissue and serum. Induction of diabetes resulted in pronounced dyslipidemia, characterized by significant elevated serum lipid levels. The diabetic control group exhibited marked accumulations of hepatic triglycerides (76.4 ± 1.5 mg/g) (Fig. 9A) and cholesterol (72.4 ± 2.1 mg/g) (Fig. 9B), which were reflected systemically by elevated serum triglycerides (142.0 ± 1.9 mg/dl) (Fig. 9C), total cholesterol (152.0 ± 2.7 mg/dl) (Fig. 9D), and LDL-cholesterol (99.6 ± 2.7 mg/dl) (Fig. 9F), alongside a reduction in protective HDL-cholesterol (42.6 ± 2.4 mg/dl) (Fig. 9E) compared to the healthy control.

Treatment with Ere-CS NPs demonstrated a superior, dose-dependent normalization of the diabetic lipid profile. The high dose of Ere-CS NPs (200 mg/kg) was particularly effective, reducing hepatic lipid accumulations to near-normal levels (triglycerides: 63.8 ± 3.0 mg/g; cholesterol: 61.4 ± 1.1 mg/g) and completely restoring the serum lipid profile to that of the healthy control (triglycerides: 83.0 ± 2.2 mg/dl; cholesterol: 106.6 ± 3.0 mg/dl; LDL-C: 52.2 ± 3.8 mg/dl; HDL-C: 50.6 ± 2.3 mg/dl). The lower dose of Ere-CS NPs (100 mg/kg) also produced a significant corrective effect on both hepatic and serum lipids.

In contrast, the pure Ere extract and unloaded CS NPs provided only a partial mitigation of dyslipidemia. The high dose of Ere extract showed a moderate reduction in serum triglycerides (119.8 ± 2.4 mg/dl) and cholesterol (123.0 ± 1.6 mg/dl), but was less effective at normalizing hepatic cholesterol (64.6 ± 2.1 mg/g) and LDL-C levels (77.4 ± 4.2 mg/dl). Unloaded CS NPs had a minimal impact on the lipid profile. All treatments in the healthy control groups had no adverse effects on lipid parameters, confirming the safety and targeted action of the formulations.

Reduction of pancreatic oxidative stress and inflammation

The therapeutic mechanism of Ere-CS NPs was further elucidated by assessing their impact on pancreatic oxidative stress and inflammation, key drivers of beta-cell dysfunction in diabetes. Induction of diabetes resulted in a profound pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory state within the pancreatic tissue. The diabetic control group exhibited a significant increase in the lipid peroxidation marker malondialdehyde (MDA: 22.6 ± 3.4 nmol/g) (Fig. 10A) and a severe depletion of the antioxidant enzymes GPx (1216.8 ± 9.9 U/g) (Fig. 10B) and SOD (21.6 ± 2.4 U/g) (Fig. 10C). This was accompanied by a marked elevation in the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β (121.6 ± 2.1 pg/g) (Fig. 10D), TNF-α (978.0 ± 3.7 pg/g) (Fig. 10E), and IL-6 (102.4 ± 3.6 pg/g) (Fig. 10F), compared to the healthy control.

Results were expressed as mean ± SD (n = 5), a and b represented significance (P < 0.05) when compared to diabetic control and healthy control groups, respectively

Treatment with Ere-CS NPs demonstrated a potent, dose-dependent ability to counteract these pathological processes. The high dose of Ere-CS NPs (200 mg/kg) effectively normalized the pancreatic markers, reducing oxidative stress by lowering MDA levels (11.8 ± 2.4 nmol/g) and fully restoring the activities of GPx (1887.0 ± 2.9 U/g) and SOD (58.6 ± 2.7 U/g) to those of the healthy control. Concurrently, this treatment completely ameliorated the inflammatory response, bringing cytokine levels (IL-1β: 61.8 ± 1.9 pg/g; TNF-α: 324.4 ± 5.1 pg/g; IL-6: 67.0 ± 2.0 pg/g) back to baseline. The lower dose of Ere-CS NPs (100 mg/kg) also produced a substantial and significant improvement across all measured markers.

In contrast, the pure Ere extract and unloaded CS NPs provided only partial protection. The high dose of Ere extract showed a moderate benefit, particularly in boosting SOD activity (38.4 ± 2.4 U/g) and reducing TNF-α (722.6 ± 3.9 pg/g) but was consistently less effective than the nano-formulated extract. Unloaded CS NPs exhibited a mild antioxidant effect, primarily on SOD, but had a minimal impact on the inflammatory cytokines. None of the treatments induced significant changes in these parameters in healthy rats, confirming the therapeutic action of the formulations against diabetes-induced pancreatic damage.

Morphometric and immunohistochemical analysis of pancreatic islets

Morphometric analysis of pancreatic sections immunostained for insulin and NF-κB was performed to quantitatively assess β-cell preservation and inflammatory status following 8-weeks of treatment (Figs. 11 and 12). The total insulin-positive area in the diabetic control group was markedly reduced (2.4 ± 1.1%) compared to healthy controls (7.8 ± 0.8%). Treatment with Ere-CS NPs (200 mg/kg) in diabetic animals restored the insulin-positive area to near-normal levels (8.0 ± 0.7%), significantly exceeding the effects of the free Ere extract (5.4 ± 0.9%) or unloaded CS NPs (4.4 ± 0.5%) at equivalent doses. In non-diabetic animals, all treatments had no significant effect on insulin-positive area. This finding aligns with the broader safety profile of the formulations demonstrated throughout the study, including high cell viability in vitro (Fig. 5C), minimal anticoagulant activity (Fig. 5D), and the absence of adverse effects on body weight, glycemic parameters, hepatorenal function, lipid profile, or pancreatic oxidative stress and inflammatory markers in healthy rats (Figs. 7–10). Islet density in diabetic controls was severely diminished (4.6 ± 0.9 islets/mm²) relative to healthy controls (11.6 ± 0.9 islets/mm²). Ere-CS NPs (200 mg/kg) significantly increased islet density in diabetic rats (10.8 ± 0.8 islets/mm²), approaching healthy values, while lower doses of Ere-CS NPs (100 mg/kg) also produced a substantial improvement (8.4 ± 0.9 islets/mm²). The free extract and CS NPs alone provided only moderate recovery.

The near-complete normalization of insulin-positive area, islet density, and NF-κB expression in the high-dose Ere-CS NPs group correlates with the observed restoration of circulating insulin levels and supports the conclusion that the anti-hyperglycemic effect of the nano-formulation is mediated through protection of residual β-cells and attenuation of pancreatic inflammation.

Discussion

Land snails have been a component of traditional medicine across various cultures for centuries, revered for their therapeutic potential in treating ailments ranging from inflammation to skin disorders. Modern science has begun to validate this traditional use, identifying land snails as a rich source of diverse bioactive compounds, including peptides, mucopolysaccharides, fatty acids, and minerals, which exhibit antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities. The unique ecological niche and defense mechanisms of snails, particularly the desert species Eremina desertorum, suggest a specialized metabolic profile that may hold significant promise for developing novel therapeutics, especially for complex multifactorial diseases like DM.

However, the translation of these promising biological activities into effective and reliable medicines faces significant hurdles. Crude biological extracts are often complex mixtures containing labile compounds that are susceptible to degradation by environmental factors, such as light, oxygen, and temperature. Furthermore, issues with poor solubility, low bioavailability, and non-specific targeting can severely limit their in vivo efficacy and therapeutic potential, rendering a potent in vitro extract ineffective in a living system. To overcome these limitations and harness the full therapeutic potential of the Ere extract, we employed nanotechnology-based drug delivery. CS NPs were selected as the encapsulating vehicle due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and mucoadhesive properties. This strategy was designed to protect the sensitive bioactive components from degradation, enhance their solubility and stability, control their release kinetics, and improve their bioavailability at the target site.

The comprehensive biochemical profiling confirmed the successful encapsulation of the complex mixture of bioactive compounds, including amino acids, fatty acids, minerals, and vitamins, present in the Ere extract within the CS NPs. The presence of all essential and non-essential amino acids in the Ere-CS NPs, although at slightly lower concentrations than the pure extract, demonstrated that the ionic gelation process effectively entrapped a wide spectrum of hydrophilic and proteinaceous components. This is a significant finding, as amino acids are not only fundamental building blocks but also possess their own bioactive properties, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, which can contribute to the overall therapeutic efficacy of the formulation [21].

The lipid profile of the extract revealed a rich and diverse fatty acids composition, which was also successfully preserved in the nanoparticles. The high concentrations of saturated (SFA), monounsaturated (MUFA), and particularly polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) like linoleic acid (an omega-6) are noteworthy. PUFAs are known for their potent anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing effects, which are highly relevant for diabetes management [22]. The successful loading of these lipophilic compounds is crucial, as CS NPs are particularly effective at encapsulating a diverse range of molecules with varying solubilities, thereby creating a multi-component therapeutic system [33]. The presence of key minerals, such as Ca, K, Mg, and Zn in both the Ere extract and the Ere-CS NPs was another critical aspect. Zn plays a well-documented role in insulin crystallization, secretion, and signaling, and its deficiency is linked to insulin resistance [23]. The co-encapsulation of these essential minerals with the organic bioactive compounds (amino acids and fatty acids) suggested a potential synergistic effect, where the Ere-CS NPs deliver a comprehensive micronutrient and bioactive components package targeted at the metabolic dysregulations of diabetes.

Furthermore, the significant levels of vitamins, especially the fat-soluble vitamins A and E and the water-soluble B3 (niacin), add another layer of therapeutic potential. Vitamin E is a potent lipid-soluble antioxidant that protects cell membranes from lipid peroxidation, a key process in diabetic complications [50]. The presence of B-complex vitamins, which are essential cofactors in energy metabolism, alongside the other bioactive components, positions the Ere-CS NPs as a multifaceted therapeutic agent capable of addressing oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction concurrently. The successful co-encapsulation of this broad spectrum of nutrients (amino acids, beneficial fatty acids, essential minerals, and vitamins) within a single CS NPs system presented a promising and holistic nanomedicine approach for the management of diabetes and its associated complications.

The successful development of a drug delivery system hinges on its encapsulation efficiency and release profile. Our results demonstrated that the ionic gelation technique was effective in producing Ere-CS NPs with favorable characteristics. The EE% of 73.56% indicated a high incorporation of the bioactive Ere extract into the chitosan matrix. This efficient loading is a hallmark of the ionic gelation process, where the electrostatic interaction between positively charged chitosan and negatively charged TPP creates a dense network that traps therapeutic agents effectively [51]. The subsequent DL% of 10.88% is a typical and respectable value for natural extract-loaded polymeric nanoparticles, balancing the payload with the structural integrity of the nanocarrier [52].

The in vitro release study revealed a critical and desirable property of the formulated nanoparticles: a biphasic and strongly pH-dependent release profile. The initial burst release, observed within the first few hours, can be attributed to the rapid diffusion of the extract molecules adsorbed on or near the surface of the nanoparticles [53]. This phase is often beneficial for achieving a rapid initial therapeutic concentration. More importantly, the release kinetics were profoundly accelerated under acidic conditions. Compared to physiological pH (7.4), release was significantly enhanced at pH 5.5 and further intensified under rat stomach-relevant pH values (pH 3.9 in fasted and pH 3.2 in fed animals). At pH 3.9 and 3.2, over 93% of the encapsulated extract was released within 2 h, demonstrating near-complete and rapid liberation in the gastric environment. This behavior is characteristic of chitosan-based systems, where protonation of amine groups in acidic media increases electrostatic repulsion within the polymer matrix, leading to greater swelling, matrix loosening, and consequently faster drug diffusion [33].

This pH-responsive release profile is therapeutically advantageous for oral delivery. The nanoparticles remain relatively stable in the neutral pH of the intestine but undergo rapid, extensive release in the stomach, ensuring efficient liberation of bioactive compounds prior to intestinal absorption. Furthermore, this property can be exploited for targeting diabetic conditions, where chronic inflammation may create localized acidic microenvironments, and upon cellular uptake, the nanoparticles can release their payload efficiently in acidic endosomes and lysosomes [54]. The sustained release observed at pH 7.4 over 48 h following the initial burst suggested that a fraction of the payload remains protected for prolonged action, which is crucial for managing a chronic condition like diabetes. The Ere-CS NPs exhibited a smart, pH-triggered release mechanism that aligned with both the physiological conditions of oral delivery and the pathophysiological needs of diabetes treatment.

In this study, TEM and DLS were used to determine the size of prepared CS NPs and Ere-CS NPs on different intervals (0, 15, 30, 60 and 90 days after preparation) to evaluate the stability of nanoparticles. According to the results, the size and zeta potential of CS NPs and Ere-CS NPs non-significantly increased after their preparation (15 days) then remained constant up to the 90 days after their preparation. These results indicated the stability of prepared nanoparticles. The observed stability in particle size and zeta potential over a 90-day period is a critical indicator of the strength of the formulated Ere-CS NPs. The minimal fluctuations in size confirmed that the ionic gelation process produced a stable colloidal system resistant to aggregation and Ostwald ripening [35]. The high and consistently positive zeta potential values, particularly for the loaded Ere-CS NPs (> +49 mV), were a primary factor in their stability. According to the DLVO theory, a high surface charge (typically > |±30| mV) generates strong electrostatic repulsion between individual nanoparticles, effectively preventing aggregation and ensuring long-term colloidal stability in suspension [55].

The consistent difference between the TEM and DLS measurements, where the hydrodynamic diameter was approximately 10 nm (CS NPs) and 3 nm (Ere-CS NPs) larger than the TEM size, is a well-documented phenomenon. TEM provides a direct image of the nanoparticle’s solid core in a dry state, whereas DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter of the particle in suspension, which includes the core, the polymer shell, and the associated solvent layer (hydration shell) that moves with the particle as it diffuses [56]. Furthermore, DLS is inherently biased towards larger particles due to its intensity-weighted distribution and higher sensitivity to aggregation, which further explains the size difference [57]. The excellent stability of the Ere-CS NPs, combined with their favorable nanoscale size, makes them a highly promising candidate for pharmaceutical applications, ensuring consistent performance from manufacture until administration.

The in vitro pharmacological profiling revealed that the Ere-CS NPs significantly enhanced its bioactivity across multiple therapeutic domains. The antimicrobial activity of unloaded CS NPs is attributed to the cationic nature of chitosan, which interacts electrostatically with negatively charged microbial cell walls, leading to membrane disruption, leakage of intracellular contents, and inhibition of growth [33]. The enhanced activity of Ere‑CS NPs likely resulted from a synergistic combination of this membrane‑destabilizing effect and the bioactive constituents of the Ere extract (e.g., antimicrobial peptides, and fatty acids), which were delivered more effectively to the microbial surface via the nanocarrier [28]. The similarity in inhibition zones between CS NPs and gentamicin against certain strains (e.g., B. subtilis) underscored the intrinsic antibacterial potential of CS NPs, while the superior performance of Ere‑CS NPs highlighted the added benefit of extract loading. Collectively, these data demonstrated that nano-encapsulation of Ere extract within CS NPs significantly enhanced its antimicrobial potency against both bacterial and fungal pathogens.

The significantly improved antioxidant capacity of Ere-CS NPs, as demonstrated across four distinct assays (DPPH, H₂O₂, OH-, and O₂⁻), indicated a robust ability to neutralize a wide range of ROS. This enhanced activity is likely due to the protective effect of the chitosan matrix, which can shield the antioxidant compounds from premature degradation and improve their bioavailability at the reaction site [27, 33]. The nano-formulation’s ability to effectively scavenge both free radicals and non-radical oxidants like hydrogen peroxide underscored its potential to mitigate oxidative stress, a central pathophysiological feature in diabetes and its complications.

Furthermore, the potent anti-inflammatory effect of Ere-CS NPs, evidenced by their dose-dependent inhibition of erythrocyte hemolysis (a model for membrane stabilization), and their efficacy comparable to indomethacin, points to a strong protective action against inflammatory mediators. This activity is crucial, as chronic inflammation is intimately linked with insulin resistance [58]. The excellent cell viability and favorable coagulation profile of Ere-CS NPs are equally important findings. The high biocompatibility of all formulations, with cell viabilities consistently above 85%, confirmed the safety profile of the Ere extract, CS NPs, and Ere-CS NPs. Notably, Ere-CS NPs supported higher viability compared to the free Ere extract or blank CS NPs, suggesting that nano-encapsulation offer a protective/compatibility benefit. Moreover, while the formulations did not inherently prolong coagulation times like heparin, their significantly lower PT and PTT compared to the anticoagulant control indicated a low risk of bleeding complications, a critical safety consideration for any therapeutic agent.

The in vivo findings conclusively demonstrated that Ere-CS NPs constituted a highly effective therapeutic strategy for managing STZ-induced diabetes and its multifaceted complications. The robust, dose-dependent restoration of core metabolic parameters, including hyperglycemia, hypoinsulinemia, and body weight loss, confirmed the superior efficacy of the nano-formulation over the pure Ere extract. The normalization of serum FTA, a marker of medium-term glycemic control, provided strong evidence of sustained glycemic management by Ere-CS NPs [59].

The profound anti-hyperglycemic effect is likely attributable to the nano-encapsulation enhancing the systemic bioavailability of the Ere extract’s bioactive compounds [31, 33]. While direct pancreatic targeting was not explicitly demonstrated in this study, the small nanoparticle size (~80 nm), positive surface charge, and pH-responsive release profile could promote passive accumulation in inflamed pancreatic tissue via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which is often heightened in diabetic microenvironments due to vascular leakage and inflammation [60]. Furthermore, the chitosan matrix facilitates mucoadhesion and prolonged residence in the gastrointestinal tract, thereby improving intestinal absorption and subsequent systemic exposure of the extract, ultimately leading to greater delivery to insulin-sensitive tissues, including the pancreas [32, 54].

Another key finding in this study is the significant hepatorenal protection afforded by Ere-CS NPs, as evidenced by the normalization of serum ALT, AST, urea, and creatinine levels. Diabetes-induced hyperglycemia promotes the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and oxidative stress, leading to cellular dysfunction and apoptosis in the liver and kidneys [10, 61]. The ability of Ere-CS NPs to prevent this damage underscores their systemic protective effect, which is crucial since hepatorenal toxicity is a common limitation of many anti-diabetic therapies.

Furthermore, the remarkable reversal of diabetic dyslipidemia by Ere-CS NPs highlights their capacity to correct metabolic disturbances beyond glycemia. The reduction in hepatic and serum triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL-C, coupled with the elevation of HDL-C, indicated an improvement in lipid metabolism and a reduced risk of atherogenesis. This lipid-normalizing effect is a critical attribute, as diabetic dyslipidemia is a major driver of cardiovascular disease, the leading cause of mortality in diabetic patients [62].

The mechanistic insights from the pancreatic tissue analysis revealed that the therapeutic benefits of Ere-CS NPs resulted from their potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The efficient reduction in MDA level, a toxic byproduct of lipid peroxidation, and the restoration of the key antioxidant enzymes (SOD and GPx), demonstrated a direct mitigation of oxidative stress. This is particularly important in the pancreas, where beta-cells are exceptionally vulnerable to oxidative damage due to their low expression of antioxidant enzymes [63]. Concurrently, the suppression of the pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) in pancreatic tissue indicated a reduction in the chronic inflammatory state that keeps beta-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance [64]. The superior performance of Ere-CS NPs over the Ere extract in all these parameters can be attributed to the CS NPs acting as a protective delivery vehicle, shielding the bioactive compounds from degradation, enhancing their tissue penetration, and providing a sustained release, thereby ensuring a more potent and prolonged therapeutic presence at the target site [44].

In conclusion, this study successfully developed, characterized, and evaluated Ere-CS NPs as a novel nanotherapeutic strategy. The ionic gelation technique proved its effectiveness, yielding nanoparticles with efficient drug loading and a smart pH-responsive release profile. This formulation created a synergistic system where the chitosan matrix enhanced the bioavailability and delivery of the snail extract’s rich bioactive components (amino acids, fatty acids, minerals and vitamins). This resulted in a superior multi-targeted therapeutic profile, demonstrating potent anti-hyperglycemic, hypolipidemic, antioxidant, and antimicrobial activities in vitro and in vivo, alongside significant hepatorenal protection. This direct cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory action on the pancreas underscored its renewing potential. Consequently, Ere-CS NPs emerge as a highly promising and comprehensive nanomedicine for managing diabetes and its associated complications.

While the results of this study are highly promising, some limitations should be acknowledged to provide context for the findings and guide future research. Although the present study confirms the storage stability of the lyophilized Ere-CS NPs, further investigations into their long-term colloidal stability in relevant biological media would be valuable for advancing their translational potential. The cohort size was deliberately minimized in adherence to the ethical principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), yet the statistically significant results obtained validate the findings within this framework. Furthermore, the study was conducted exclusively in a rat model of STZ-induced diabetes. Future research should focus on four key areas to advance Ere-CS NPs: 1) expanding validation in larger animal cohorts and alternative diabetic models to confirm efficacy; 2) assessing long-term effects and chronic toxicity over several months; 3) elucidating molecular mechanisms; and 4) enhancing translational potential by scaling up good manufacturing practice-compliant synthesis and exploring targeted delivery systems. By addressing these points, the translational pathway of Ere-CS NPs can be further clarified, paving the way for its development into a potential therapeutic agent.
