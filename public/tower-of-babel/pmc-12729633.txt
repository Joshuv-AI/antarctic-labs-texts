Synergistic enhancement of Sorghum bicolor nutrient uptake and growth by microbiomes in enhanced biological phosphorus removal system and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi
Authors: Yejin Son, Peisheng He, Eric J Craft, Miguel A Piñeros, Mathew Baldwin, Zijian Wang, April Z Gu, Jenny Kao-Kniffin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding soil microbial interactions is essential for developing biofertilizers in regenerative agriculture. Polyphosphate-accumulating organisms (PAOs) play a pivotal role in enhanced biological phosphorus removal (EBPR) systems by sequestering phosphorus from wastewater and storing it as intracellular polyphosphate. However, their role in terrestrial phosphorus cycling remains poorly characterized, despite their potential to serve as a reservoir of plant-available phosphorus. This study investigates PAO-enriched microbiomes in the sorghum rhizosphere, focusing on their novel interactions with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). By integrating PAOs derived from EBPR biosolids and compost with AMF, we assessed their synergistic effects on plant growth and nutrient uptake in Sorghum bicolor (sorghum), as well as their broader influence on rhizosphere microbial traits and functional dynamics.ResultsWe employed plant biometry analysis, nutrient assays, 31P NMR spectroscopy, single-cell Raman microspectroscopy (SCRS), and microbiome profiling to comprehensively evaluate rhizosphere microbial interactions and their effects on plant physiology and nutrient dynamics. 31P NMR confirmed polyphosphate accumulation by PAOs derived from both compost and EBPR biosolids, demonstrating the soil adaptability of EBPR-derived PAOs. AMF showed enhanced synergy with EBPR-derived microbiomes, significantly enhancing sorghum growth, nutrient acquisition, and microbial diversity. Key PAOs, Thauera, Rhodanobacter, and Paracoccus, were successfully incorporated into the rhizosphere and positively correlated with improved phosphorus uptake. PICRUSt2 analysis indicated enrichment of microbial functions linked to motility and xenobiotic metabolism in EBPR-treated rhizospheres. SCRS revealed AMF-induced phenotypic shifts in EBPR-derived microbiomes, while network analysis showed that AMF reorganized community connectivity, fostering novel microbial interactions in EBPR-amended environments.ConclusionsThis study explored the interactions between AMF and microbiomes derived from EBPR biosolids, in comparison with those from compost, uncovering novel microbial synergies that enhance phosphorus uptake in Sorghum bicolor and promote plant productivity. The findings underscore the potential of targeted microbial co-inoculation such as integrating EBPR microbiomes with AMF as an innovative strategy for improving soil fertility and advancing biofertilizer development through microbial-driven nutrient recycling. By harnessing wastewater-derived phosphorus via PAOs, this approach offers a sustainable alternative to conventional fertilization, supporting regenerative agriculture, nutrient circularity, and the broader application of microbial biofertilizers in crop production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00809-z.Keywords: Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), Biofertilizer, Enhanced biological phosphorus removal (EBPR), Phosphorus, Polyphosphate-accumulating organisms (PAOs), Soil microbiome, Sorghum, Single-cell Raman spectroscopy (SCRS)

Introduction

Soil nutrient dynamics are shaped not only by elemental availability but also by the structure and function of microbial communities and their interactions with plant. Microbial networks in the soil orchestrate nutrient cycling, bolster plant defenses against environmental stress, and underpin the stability of agroecosystems [1]. Despite its abundance in Earth’s crust, plant-available phosphorus (P) is scarce in soils, limiting crop uptake and productivity [2, 3]. A promising strategy for sustainable P management involves leveraging biotic agents in the soil, from both natural and commercial sources. These biotic players are soil microorganisms (e.g. fungi and bacteria) with distinct functions that can transform plant-unavailable forms of P into accessible forms for plant uptake [4]. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and phosphorus-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) are renowned for their capacity to increase P availability in the soil and help plants overcome P deficiency [5]. They can release organic anions and siderophores to solubilize inorganic P (precipitated or mineral P) and secrete enzymes such as phosphatase, phytase, and cellulolytic enzymes to mineralize humic-associated P and organic P in soil organic matter, plant residues, and microbial necromass composed mainly of cellulose, starch, xylan, and lipids [6–8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that inoculation with AMF, PSB, or both enhances P uptake and plant growth in poaceae crops such as Zea mays (maize) [9], Triticum aestivum (wheat) [10], Hordeum vulgare (barley) [11], and Sorghum bicolor (sorghum) [12, 13]. Notably, co-inoculation of AMF and PSB yielded even greater improvements in both parameters [9, 10, 14]. Given the limited bioavailability of P in soils, harnessing microbial strategies offers a promising path toward sustainable nutrient management.

Other microorganisms associated with P cycling include the polyphosphate accumulating organisms (PAOs), which have received greater attention in recent years for their growing significance in soil P cycling [15, 16]. The PAOs are well known for their role in enhanced biological phosphorus removal (EBPR) systems, which create bioreactor microbiomes highly enriched in PAOs. In this process, the PAOs absorb significant amounts of P from contaminated sewage or wastewater, storing it as long chains of phosphate, known as polyphosphate (PolyP), within their cells [17]. By sequestering P in this manner, PAOs contribute to both P contaminant removal and wastewater recycling, making them essential for maintaining environmental P balance. However, the current terrestrial P cycling model is largely limited to AMF and PSB, overlooking the crucial role of PAOs and PolyP in global P dynamics [16]. Notably, PolyP can be hydrolyzed into bioavailable forms of P (orthophosphate) via microbial enzymatic activities and under edaphic factors, such as acidic conditions [18]. This process allows PolyP to act as a reservoir or sink for P in the soil, making it available for plant uptake when needed. He et al. [18] and Saia et al. [19] highlighted that the future study of PAOs should be guided toward agriculture to investigate their roles in PAO-mediated soil P cycling and contribution to plant production. In particular, PAO-mediated soil P cycling can support a circular economy by enabling P recovery from wastewater, mitigating losses to waterways, and accumulation in waste bioresources [20]. In addition, in our previous study, using EBPR biosolids to grow maize revealed the abundant proliferation of the PAO species Candidatus Accumulibacter phosphatis in the rhizosphere of Zea mays (maize). The bacterial PAO is common in wastewater treatment plants, and its ability to thrive in the maize rhizosphere suggests its importance to agriculture [15].

In this study, we examined the potential of using EBPR biosolid-derived microbiomes containing enriched PAOs as biofertilizers to support plant growth. We selected Sorghum bicolor (sorghum) because it is a promising crop for bioenergy applications and is well known to form beneficial relationships with other P-cycling associated microorganisms, such as the AMF [21]. We hypothesized that PAOs derived from EBPR biosolids can effectively regulate soil P cycling and enhance nutrient uptake in sorghum plants, particularly when paired with the AMF. This hypothesis is based on the observed synergistic effects of AMF and PSB on plant nutrition and growth in previous studies [22–24] and the beneficial impact of PAOs on maize [15]. PAOs, which store P as intracellular polyphosphate granules, may function as a microbial P reservoir, making them potentially advantageous for AMF in supporting nutrient uptake and soil P cycling in agricultural systems. Importantly, leveraging PAOs from EBPR biosolids aligns with key principles of regenerative agriculture by transforming P-rich microbial communities from wastewater treatment into biologically active soil amendments. Although many studies have examined PAOs in the EBPR system and identified PAOs as endemic to soil [25, 26], no study has explored the co-existence of AMF and PAOs within a plant production setting. Building on the enhanced outcomes observed with AMF–PSB co-inoculation compared to single inoculation of either AMF or PSB [9, 10, 14], AMF and PAOs may similarly interact synergistically to boost plant growth and P uptake. Therefore, this study aimed to examine how interactions between PAO-enriched microbiomes and AMF impact sorghum growth, nutrient uptake, and soil microbial communities.

Methods

Experiment design and growing sorghums

A growth chamber experiment was conducted in a controlled plant growth chamber facility at Cornell University, Ithaca, New York, USA (42° 26′ 49.2″ N, 76° 28′ 37.2″ W). Sorghum seeds were purchased from Roundstone Native Seed Company (Upton, US) and sterilized with 5% sodium hypochlorite ethanol and 2% fungicide, Captan 50W (Hi-Yield, Texas, USA). Potting soil (LM 111, Lambert) was autoclaved twice to remove native microbiota. Sorghum seedlings with epicotyls were selected for uniform growth, acclimated for 1 week in small pots, then assigned to treatment groups (12 replicates) and grown in 5-inch pots for seven more weeks, reaching 9 weeks at harvest. Six additional seedlings per treatment were used to assess root morphology at weeks 3 and 5 post-transplant.

We used a commercial AMF product (DYNOMYCO, Mazor, Israel) containing Glomus intraradices and Glomus mosseae. PAO sources included EBPR biosolids and Vermont manure compost (Vermont Compost Company, US), both rich in diverse PAO taxa, identified in our previous study involving maize [15]. Vermont manure compost is a nutrient-rich, high-performance potting medium composed of a diverse mixture of organic materials including multiple types of manure (cow, horse, donkey, mule, chicken, and worm), food scraps, bedding hay, spoiled silage, and hardwood bark [27]. The sources and abundance of PAOs in both EBPR biosolids and manure compost (Vermont Compost Company, US) were confirmed using SCRS-based phenotypic identification as reported in both EBPR engineering and soil natural systems [15, 28–30]. Identification of known PAOs, based on established phylogenetic knowledge from EBPR systems, revealed the presence and dominance of canonical taxa such as Candidatus Accumulibacter and Pseudomonas through 16S rRNA gene sequencing, with these PAOs comprising a relatively abundant portion (10–30%) of the total microbial community [15]. This biologically active compost emulates natural terrestrial ecosystems, with its microorganisms reflecting terrestrial origins, while EBPR biosolids harbor aquatic lineages adapted to wastewater. The treatments included: (1) Control that did not receive either PAO-enriched microbiomes or AMF (referred to as Control sorghums); (2) sorghum treated only with activated sludge retrieved from the EBPR system (EBPR sorghums); (3) sorghum treated only with composted manure (Compost sorghum); (4) sorghum treated only with AMF spores (AMF sorghums); (5) sorghum treated with EBPR sludge and AMF spores (AMF + EBPR sorghums); and (6) sorghums treated with compost and AMF spores (AMF + Compost sorghums). AMF spores were applied at 10.5 g L−1 initially and 1.3 g L−1 post-transplant into 7-inch pots. To avoid cross-contamination, groups were spaced 1 m apart and rotated weekly. Additional plants were grown for root imaging at weeks 3 and 5.

We measured the total P in the EBPR sludge and compost in advance and added a corresponding amount of sludge and compost at a rate of 44 mg total P per liter of soil (44 ppm). Although compost and EBPR sludge differ in P speciation, we assumed microbial communities would gradually mobilize and transform their respective P sources, making total P availability a more ecologically relevant metric over time. EBPR sludge was added biweekly to EBPR and AMF + EBPR treatments, while Control and AMF groups received KH₂PO₄ at the same rate. A half-strength Hoagland solution (without KH₂PO₄) was applied twice weekly to support growth and promote microbiome-driven P uptake.

Sample harvest and preparation

Six weeks after transplantation, at 9 weeks of age, sorghum plants were harvested and assessed using non-destructive biometric traits: shoot/root length, stalk diameter, adventitious root count, and leaf number. Shoots were dried at 50 °C for biomass calculation. Roots were either stained for AMF colonization or ground for nutrient analysis via inductively-coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). Rhizosphere soils were sieved (2 mm) and stored at − 80 °C for 16S rRNA gene sequencing, 31P NMR, and GRSP analysis. Five soil samples per treatment were also dried and ground for carbon, nitrogen, and organic matter analysis at the Cornell Soil Health Laboratory (Ithaca, NY, USA).

AMF colonization analysis

Root staining was performed using the ink-vinegar method [31] to detect AMF in 72 samples (6 treatments × 12 replicates). Roots were cleared with KOH, acidified with HCl, and stained in 5% ink-vinegar at 80 °C. After destaining, samples were stored in 50% glycerol at 4 °C. After destaining, samples were stored in 50% glycerol at 4 °C. For microscopy, 25 root fragments per sample were mounted and scored for hyphae, arbuscules, and vesicles using McGonigle et al.’s method [32], with the average colonization rate calculated as the percentage of root segments containing AMF structures out of 100 total observations. Supplementary Fig. 1 confirms AMF presence in sorghum roots.

GRSP extraction and quantification

GRSP was first proposed by Wright et al. [33], as a key soil component produced by AMF, contributing to soil aggregation, carbon storage, and overall soil health [34]. We followed Wu et al. [35] to isolate total GRSP (T-GRSP) and easily extractable GRSP (EE-GRSP), representing long-term and recent microbial activity, respectively. T-GRSP and EE-GRSP were extracted from 1 g soil using citric acid (50 mM, pH 8; 20 mM, pH 7) at 121 °C, with repeated autoclaving and centrifugation until extracts turned yellow. Combined supernatants were cleaned and quantified via Bradford assay using a 96-well microplate reader at 595 nm (Synergy HT Multi-Mode Microplate Reader, BioTek).

Soil 31P NMR spectroscopy

Soil 31P NMR spectroscopy was performed following the procedure introduced by Turner [36]. The protocol employed the use of NaOH-Na2EDTA for the extraction of P from soils instead of NaOH-EDTA, since Na2EDTA is more effective to improve the detection of pyrophosphate (organic phosphate) and PolyP [36, 37]. We focused on qualitative NMR to identify the presence of PolyP synthesis from imported PAOs from EBPR sludge and compost in soils, rather than quantitative NMR for determining the relative abundance of P chemical species. Samples were neutralized to pH 7.1 with 1 M HCl prior to lyophilization to prevent PolyP degradation [38, 39]. Lyophilized extracts (~ 300 mg) were re-dissolved in D2O and NaOH–Na2EDTA solution, incubated on ice for 30 min, and vortexed periodically before transfer to 5-mm NMR tubes. 31P NMR spectra were acquired using a Bruker AV-501 spectrometer (500 MHz) with standard parameters until 10,000 scans were completed. Peak identification followed established literature [38, 40].

DNA extraction, amplification, and next-generation sequencing

Soil DNA was extracted from 0.25 g of frozen samples using DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kits (Qiagen, Carlsbad, CA). The 16S rRNA V4 region (~ 253 bp) was amplified with universal primers 515F/806R. The PCR reactions included 10 μL of 2× AccuStart II PCR ToughMix (QuantaBio, Beverly, MA, USA), 1 μL of 10 μM forward and reverse primers, 6 μL of DNase-free PCR water (MoBio, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and 2 μL of template DNA. The thermocycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 25 cycles of 94 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. Libraries were dual-indexed (Nextera, Illumina), normalized, and paired-end sequenced (2 × 250 bp) using the MiSeq v2 500 bp kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the Cornell Genomics Facility (Ithaca, NY, USA).

Microbiome profiling

Raw sequence processing and microbial analysis were performed using the QIIME2 platform (version 2023.2; https://qiime2.org) [41]. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated via DADA2, with chimeras removed using Uchime and a Bayesian method [42]. The 16S rRNA gene taxonomy assignment was performed by a Naive-Bayes classifier trained on the Greengenes2 2022.10 database using a q2-feature-classifier plugin [43]. The resultant ASV dataset was imported into R v4.4.2 [44], and rarefied using phyloseq’s “rarefy_even_depth” function [45]. Rarefaction curves confirmed sufficient sequencing depth and minimal diversity loss (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Data analyses

For plant and soil data, downstream analyses were conducted using various packages in R v4.4.2 [44], with plots generated using ggplot [46]. All data were z-score standardized for comparability. Post-hoc test used was pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum test at Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) adjusted p < 0.05. For ecological analyses, ASVs found in ≥ 70% of replicates were retained and normalized to relative genus-level abundance. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was visualized via non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) with the metaMDS function in the vegan package [46], and permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, 999 permutations) was conducted to calculate significant differences in microbial community composition. Environmental variables (nutrient concentration of roots determined by ICP-OES) were introduced into the NMDS ordination using the “envfit” function in the vegan package (permutation = 999) [46]. Taxonomic composition was visualized at phylum and genus levels, focusing on dominant taxa (> 100 reads, > 0.5% mean abundance). Phylum-level differences were tested using pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with BH correction (adjusted p < 0.05). Alpha diversity metrics (richness, Simpson evenness, Shannon diversity) were calculated with the vegan package [46] and compared using Wilcoxon tests at BH adjusted p < 0.05.

Differential abundance analysis

We used non-rarified ASV data for differential abundance (DA) analysis to avoid bias from rarefaction [47, 48], applying the ALDEx2 package in R [49]. ALDEx2 is designed to work with raw count data and includes built-in normalization procedures that account for differences in library size and the compositional nature of microbiome datasets. ALDEx2 normalizes raw counts and accounts for library size and compositional effects, reducing false positives. Monte Carlo sampling and CLR transformation prepared data for Wilcoxon rank-sum testing. Significant results were identified using a BH adjusted P value threshold of < 0.05. To ensure biological relevance, taxa with a median effect size greater than 2 were retained for visualization. Full results are in Supplementary Table 1.

Correlation between bacterial communities and plant nutrient uptake

We used rarified ASV data and the microeco package [50] to perform Spearman correlations between environmental factors and microbial communities. Pairwise correlations (cal_cor) were considered significant at BH adjusted p < 0.05. This analysis revealed both taxon-specific and community-level links to root nutrient uptake. Statistical outcomes for taxonomic associations with sorghum root nutrient uptake are presented in Supplementary Table 2, while results of Spearman correlations between alpha diversity indices and nutrient uptake are detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

PICRUST2 functional annotation

Functional pathway abundance from 16S rRND sequence data was predicted using PICRUSt2 and analyzed in R (v4.4.2) with ggpicrust2 [51] and edgeR [52]. Pathways with BH-adjusted p < 0.05 and ≥ 0.5% relative abundance were z-score normalized and visualized using ComplexHeatmap [53], to identify microbial functional profiles across treatments. A summary of the PICRUSt2 results is provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Network analysis

Network analysis was used to identify treatment-specific patterns of microbial co-abundance and co-exclusion in the rhizosphere. Microbial networks were inferred using SPIEC-EASI (v1.1.3) with neighborhood selection [54], and edge weights were derived from beta matrices [55]. Networks were converted to igraph objects [56], and hub species were identified based on top 90% quantiles of betweenness and degree [57]. Hub networks were visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.3) and phylum-level interactions with chorddiag [58]. Structural differences between networks were quantified using Portrait Divergence (https://github.com/bagrow/network-portrait-divergence) and the NetLSD distance (https://github.com/xgfs/NetLSD) [57], revealing microbial responses to soil amendments.

Single-cell Raman spectroscopy analysis

We utilized single-cell Raman spectroscopy (SCRS) to analyze the phenotypic profiles of the sorghum rhizomicrobiome to examine the impacts of different treatments. Details on soil sample pretreatment and preparation for Raman acquisition, Raman spectroscopy instrument setup, Raman spectra acquisition conditions, and downstream data processing have been described in our previous publications [15, 59–62]. Due to the time-sensitive and labor-intensive nature of Raman pretreatment and preparation to reserve the metabolic integrity of microbial cells, for each specific microbial treatment, we collected 5 biological replicates and pooled the soil samples into one homogenized sample to represent the overall microbial community to reduce the bias of batch effects from the pre-treatment process, similar to the approach as described in our previous publication [29]. In addition, previous study of plant-soil feedback involving microbiome evaluated and compared the approach using original biological replicates versus using pooled samples followed by randomly re-sampling the replicates, and it was concluded that there is no evidence that soil sample pooling systematically biases estimate of variance across many studies [63]. At least five replicates were obtained from each treatment and were analyzed using SCRS, where the number of single cells measured met the required sampling depth to capture the functional diversity of the community, as determined by our previously established algorithm [64]. Briefly, fresh soil samples were processed through density-gradient centrifugation to extract and separate the rhizo-microorganisms, which were then harvested and diluted properly before being loaded onto the CaF2 Raman slides [15]. Raman acquisition of different separated single cells was performed until it reached a sufficient sampling size, determined through SCRS sample size sufficiency statistical analysis [61]. Raman profiles of each sample were constructed based on the statistical distribution of the Raman intensities (CCD counts, a.u.) at each Raman shift (wavenumber, cm−1). Multivariate clustering on the Raman spectra of all recognized rhizo-microorganisms was conducted to categorize the subgroups, namely ASVs, based on the similarities of their phenotypic profiles reflected from the Raman spectra [60]. Shannon diversity and Simpson’s evenness were calculated from SCRS profiles following established methods.

Correlation between SCRS-derived OPUs and 16S rRNA-derived ASVs

We used Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) inference to model SCRS data distribution and generate 10 technical replicates per treatment for consistent representation across biological replicates [64]. MCMC sampling was performed in PyMC (v5.22.0) using a multivariate Gaussian model, with spectral features treated as Gaussian variables. Parameters were initialized from empirical data, and a half-normal prior constrained variance estimates. Posterior inference used the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) with 1000 samples per chain and a target_accept rate of 0.9 for stability. Sampling was run on a single CPU core to ensure macOS compatibility. We assessed relationships between taxonomic and phenotypic alpha diversity using linear and log-transformed OLS regression (statsmodels v0.14.1). Log1p transformation (NumPy v2.2.4) [65] handled zero values. R2 and p values were extracted to evaluate model fit, and seaborn (v0.13.2) heatmaps visualized results, with significance marked by asterisks (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Effects of PAO-enriched microbiome and AMF additions on sorghum growth and nutrient uptake

The impact of PAO and AMF inoculation became more pronounced during the early stages of sorghum root development (Fig. 1a). Notably, root growth was enhanced in sorghum plants treated with AMF such as the AMF, AMF + EBPR, and AMF + Compost groups. Particularly, the AMF + EBPR sorghums showed the most significant root expansion by week 3. This trend continued into week 5, with the AMF + EBPR sorghums producing more adventitious roots, indicating sustained and enhanced root development. The sorghum plants showed markedly enhanced growth under the EBPR and AMF + EBPR treatments relative to the control and other treatments (Fig. 1b). In the AMF + EBPR sorghum plants, shoot height, root length, shoot drymass, stalk diameters, and the number of adventitious roots demonstrated substantial increases, with the latter nearly doubling relative to the control groups. AMF colonization rates ranged from 6 to 48% in sorghum roots inoculated with AMF spores, indicating successful establishment of the symbiosis in treated plants. The AMF inoculation (AMF, AMF + EBPR, and AMF + Compost groups) did not result in significant increases in the concentrations of T-GRSP, EE-GRSP, total carbon, total nitrogen, or soil organic matter (SOM) compared with the control sorghums (Fig. 1c). However, the total carbon was notably lower in the AMF + EBPR treated plants compared to the controls. No significant treatment effects were observed in rhizosphere SOM concentrations, potentially due to the limited duration of plant growth (9 weeks). The AMF + EBPR sorghums showed elevated root concentrations of Al, Ca, Fe, K, Mg, P, S, and Zn compared to untreated controls (Fig. 1d). Notably, Ca, Mg, P, and S levels were the highest among all treatment groups, indicating enhanced nutrient uptake under AMF + EBPR conditions. Interestingly, P uptake was significantly higher in sorghum plants treated with EBPR and AMF + EBPR compared to controls and other treatments, indicating that P-rich EBPR biosolids effectively enhanced P acquisition. However, we also acknowledge that the nutrient-rich nature of these amendments may also contribute to the observed plant growth responses, making it difficult to fully disentangle the effects of PAO activity from those of direct nutrient inputs. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as the collective effects of the media and the microorganisms introduced with them.

Identifying PAO biosynthetic activity in soils

The chemical shifts of the signals were determined in parts per million (ppm) (Fig. 2). Signals were assigned to individual P compounds or functional groups based on previous soil 31P NMR reports [36, 66, 67]. They were as follows: orthophosphate (approximately δ 5.75 ppm), phosphate monoesters (between δ 5.3 and δ 3 ppm), phospholipids (between δ − 0.3 and δ 1.2 ppm), DNA between δ − 0.8 and δ − 1 ppm, pyrophosphate (at approximately δ − 4 ppm), and polyphosphates (between δ − 19 and δ − 21 ppm). Spectra were processed using MestreNova software for Windows (Mestrelab Research, Santiago de Compostela, Spain). The accumulation of PolyP was identified only in the soils of EBPR sorghums, Compost sorghums, and AMF + Compost sorghums. Given that the current 31P NMR data are qualitative and lack internal standards or normalization, it remains difficult to distinguish between PolyP originally introduced via EBPR sludge or compost and PolyP potentially synthesized de novo by PAOs after soil inoculation. However, the absence of PolyP in the Control sorghums indicates that native soil PAOs were either absent or removed prior to the experiment by autoclaving, inhibiting their natural PolyP synthesis. This suggests that the detected PolyP presence in EBPR sorghums, Compost sorghums, and AMF + Compost sorghums likely reflects input from the EBPR biosolids and compost amendments, rather than from pre-existing soil microbiota.

Phylogenetic characterization of soil bacterial communities

A total of 968,828 ASVs were obtained with a median of 14,479 ASVs per sample. A total of 3651 bacterial species belonging to 32 phyla, 323 families, and 522 genera were identified. Figure 3a displays the relative abundances of bacterial phyla and genera, highlighting treatment-specific patterns in taxonomic dominance. These species belonged to the phyla Acidobacteriota, Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Myxococcota, Planctomycetota, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobiota, and other miscellaneous phyla that accounted for less than 0.5% of the total phyla abundance. The phyla that increased in both the EBPR and AMF + EBPR treatments in comparison to the controls were Myxococcota and Planctomycetota, with Bacteroidota being significantly enriched only in the AMF + EBPR group (Fig. 3b). Myxococcota exhibited increased abundance across all sorghum groups inoculated with AMF, including AMF, AMF + EBPR, and AMF + Compost treatments than the controls. In contrast, Actinobacteriota showed a significant increase in abundance exclusively within the Compost sorghums. Site variations explained 56% of the observed variance between microbial communities (PERMANOVA, F = 13.51, df = 5, p < 0.001, NMDS stress = 0.12) (Fig. 3c). The Spearman correlation test showed that the microbial variations were significantly correlated with the uptake of nutrients such as K (R2 = 0.21), Mg (R2 = 0.11), P (R2 = 0.14), and S (R2 = 0.13) in sorghum roots. Pairwise PERMANOVA analyses revealed significant differences in sorghum microbiome composition across all treatment comparisons, indicating distinct microbial community shifts induced by each treatment. The EBPR and AMF + EBPR treatments demonstrated greater richness and Shannon–Wiener diversity compared to controls and other groups, suggesting a more balanced and diverse microbial community (Fig. 3d). Conversely, the Compost and AMF + Compost groups exhibited minimal changes or even reduced diversity relative to the controls, especially regarding species evenness and Shannon–Wiener diversity.

We explored the effects of soil bacteria on the nutrient uptake of sorghum roots and found several significant relationships (Fig. 4b). A total of 139 predicted KEGG orthologs (KOs) from soil microbiomes were classified at KEGG level 3 and visualized in Fig. 4d, highlighting pathways with a total relative abundance exceeding 0.5% across treatments. Compared to other treatments, EBPR and AMF + EBPR sorghums showed increased abundance of pathways related to the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, including isoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis. Enhanced activity was also observed in cell motility pathways, such as bacterial chemotaxis and flagellar assembly, as well as in lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis (within glycan metabolism) and arachidonic acid metabolism (within lipid metabolism). Moreover, several xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism pathways were potentially more prevalent, including bisphenol degradation, fluorobenzoate degradation, chlorocyclohexane and chlorobenzene degradation, metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, and drug metabolism–cytochrome P450. Circos plots visualize interconnectivity within soil microbial communities at the phylum level across sorghum treatments (Fig. 5b). The EBPR treatment contained 125 microbial interactions at the phylum level, representing the most highly interconnected microbial network, followed by Compost (104) and AMF + EBPR (100) treatments. In contrast, the Control sorghums were much less interconnected with only 32 microbial interactions. In both EBPR and AMF + EBPR sorghum treatments, Proteobacteria–Proteobacteria exhibited the most frequent interactions (23 in EBPR and 14 in AMF + EBPR). This was followed by Proteobacteria–Actinobacteriota associations (10) in the EBPR treatment, while Proteobacteria–Bacteroidota interactions (11) were more prominent in the AMF + EBPR treatment.

When analyzing microbial networks using Portrait Divergence and NetLSD Distance, we observed varying levels of structural differences and overall dissimilarity in microbial interaction patterns across treatments (Fig. 5c). Portrait Divergence quantifies network similarity by analyzing network portraits, which represent the distribution of shortest path lengths and serve as a graph invariant that captures topological features across all structural levels [73, 74]. This metric ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates identical networks, and 1 reflects maximally different networks, making it an effective tool for comparing overall structural variations in networks. In contrast, NetLSD is another tool to investigate fine-scale structural differences (e.g. connectivity patterns and structural details such as node attributes, edge weights, network dynamics, and functional properties) using a compact vector signature derived from the Laplacian spectrum and heat kernel to capture network topology [57, 74]. The most distinctive shifts in networks occurred between Control and EBPR treatments, with the highest Portrait Divergence, indicating substantial structural differences, and the highest NetLSD distance, suggesting the greatest dissimilarity in differences in spectral properties [74]. We analyzed the effects of AMF across control, EBPR biosolids, and compost treatments to assess microbiome responses to AMF. Microbial interaction networks exhibited more pronounced structural shifts between Control and AMF treatments than between EBPR and AMF + EBPR or Compost and AMF + Compost treatments, suggesting that AMF inoculation had a stronger impact when introduced into untreated soils. The comparison between EBPR and AMF + EBPR networks revealed that their overall microbial network structure remained largely unchanged, as indicated by low Portrait Divergence, suggesting minimal shifts in global topology. However, high NetLSD distance highlighted notable differences in connectivity patterns, indicating that AMF uniquely shaped microbial interactions when paired with EBPR-derived microbiomes, fostering distinct interaction patterns that differed from those observed in compost-derived microbiomes.

SCRS-based phenotypic characterization of soil bacterial communities

To assess the phenotypic traits and diversity of the sorghum rhizomicrobiome, we employed SCRS-based microbial community phenotyping to obtain and analyze a total of 3857 single-cell Raman spectra out of the six treatments [15, 59]. To further resolve microbial phenotypic diversity, single-cell Raman spectral data were clustered to form the operational phenotypic units (OPUs)—similar to how operational taxonomic units categorize organisms based on the sequence similarities [60]. This classification approach helps capture microbial functional diversity at the single-cell level, providing insights into metabolic states, physiological traits, and phenotypic heterogeneity within microbial communities. Aside from 269 unclassified spectra, a total of 145 OPUs were found, but only ten of them (OPU-0 to OPU-9) existed in substantial relative abundances greater than 0.5% across different samples (Fig. 6a). All other 135 minor OPUs, although low in relative abundances individually, together constituted a substantial fraction of the community, particularly in the AMF-associated treatments (Fig. 6a), suggesting the presence of a highly diverse and potentially rare microbial fraction. Notably, all three groups treated with AMF observed a lower fraction of OPU-1 (Fig. 6a), indicating treatment-driven selection for distinct microbial phenotypes.

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity revealed that the OPUs across treatments were clearly separated in the PCoA ordinations (Fig. 6b). The first principal coordinate, PCo1, captured 55.3% of observed metabolic diversity, from which AMF-treated sorghums (i.e., AMF, AMF + EBPR, and AMF + Compost) significantly separated from their corresponding non-AMF groups in comparison (i.e., Control, EBPR, and Compost), suggesting that treatment-specific metabolic fingerprints were likely spurred by interactions with AMF. On the second principal coordinate, PCo2, which explained 15.1% of the variance, Compost treatment, especially combined with AMF, exhibited further separation from other groups. Notably, the standalone application of EBPR biosolids did not significantly differ from the control along PCo1 or PCo2 axes respectively, suggesting their limited influence on metabolic structure of the microbial community (Fig. 6b).

Microbial treatments also significantly influenced the richness, diversity, and evenness of community phenotypes, as elucidated by the SCRS-metabolites profiles of sorghum rhizomicrobiome via the OPUs showing that AMF treatments significantly increased OPU richness, Shannon diversity, and evenness, with the strongest effects observed when AMF was co-applied with EBPR biosolids (Fig. 6c–e). Interestingly, the phenotypic diversity indices revealed from SCRS-enabled metabolic profiling showed consistent patterns in terms of the treatment impacts on the rhizo-microbiome composition with those observed with the phylogenetic diversity (Fig. 3d). To further assess the relationship between taxonomic and phenotypic diversity, we performed linear and log–log regression analyses between ASV- and OPU-derived alpha diversity metrics (Fig. 6f). Notably, AMF + Compost sorghums showed strong and significant coupling, with log-transformed models generally yielding higher R2 values (0.40–0.44, p < 0.05), especially for Shannon and evenness. These results suggest that in these treatments, phenotypic diversity increases at scales nonlinearly with taxonomic diversity, indicating a high functional redundancy as recently elucidated in large-scale studies across soil and marine ecosystems [75, 76].

Additionally, insights from the distance-based redundancy analysis (dbRDA) also highlighted AMF as a key driver of phenotypic divergence, with AMF-containing treatments shifting community structure away from the controls along distinct axes (Fig. 6g). Specifically, predominant microbial taxonomy loadings angled away from AMF treatments and towards Compost and control groups, while the majority of key OPUs angled towards AMF-containing treatments. While EBPR biosolids and compost alone showed moderate changes, their combination with AMF induced stronger, directionally differentiated shifts (Fig. 6g). AMF- and compost-associated treatments aligned with distinct OPUs and ASVs, including Streptomyces, Nocardioides, and Solimonas. These patterns suggest a potential synergy between fungal AMF and bacterial communities from EBPR systems, jointly expanding the functional repertoire of the rhizomicrobiome (Fig. 6c, d and g).

Discussion

Our findings offer promising insights into using AMF and microbiomes imported from EBPR systems or compost as biofertilizers for sorghum plants. We examined whether two distinct microbial communities, one originating from aquatic EBPR systems and the other from compost-derived terrestrial sources, maintain their metabolic polyphosphate-accumulating activity when introduced into soil environments. Qualitative 31P NMR analysis detected polyphosphate signals in the rhizosphere of sorghum plants amended with EBPR sludge and compost, but no such signals were observed in the AMF + EBPR treatment. This absence may reflect active microbial PolyP recycling, potentially driven by synergistic interactions between AMF and EBPR-derived microbiomes that accelerate P turnover and reduce residual PolyP. Supporting this interpretation, a recent study reported that under phosphorus-limited conditions, AMF-inoculated sorghum showed elevated acid phosphatase activity, which is associated with enhanced P solubilization, as well as increased concentrations of organic acids in roots and leaves that contribute to P scavenging [12]. AMF and EBPR-derived microbiomes may therefore act synergistically to enhance P bioavailability through complementary mechanisms, ultimately supporting more efficient P uptake by sorghum plants.

Our study identified that the synergy between AMF and EBPR-derived microbiomes for plant growth was greater than that between AMF and compost-derived microbiomes, as evidenced by enhanced root and shoot growth, increased stalk diameter, and a higher number of adventitious roots. Adventitious roots play an important role in a plant’s acquisition of water and nutrients from the soil under abiotic stress, such as drought or poor soil conditions [77–79]. Although the present study does not elucidate the mechanisms by which the EBPR-derived microbiomes induce the development of adventitious roots in sorghums, the observed increase in adventitious root growth in both EBPR and AMF + EBPR treatments suggests a positive interaction between these factors, potentially leading to enhanced nutrient uptake by the plants.

Paired with AMF, EBPR-derived microbiomes not only increased the uptake of P by sorghums but also enhanced uptake of other essential nutrients such as Ca, K, Mg, S, and Zn. Our data align with previous findings that reported AMF-improved nutrient acquisition for multiple plant species, including tomato seedlings for nitrogen (N), P, and Ca [80]; tomatoes for K, Ca, Mg, and Fe under drought conditions [81]; sorghum for P, N, and S [82]; sorghum seedlings for N, P, and K [83]; and wheat for P [84]. The increased P uptake likely resulted from the substantial accumulation of PolyP within PAOs, which act as major intracellular reservoirs of PolyP. Notably, EBPR-derived PAOs have been shown to store up to 35% of their cellular P as PolyP, underscoring their capacity to buffer and mobilize P in soil environments [85]. This PolyP can be made available to plants and other soil organisms through microbial processes such as cell death and turnover, which increases P availability for plants and plays an essential role in P cycling within soil microbial communities. Additionally, PAOs in EBPR systems not only require the acquisition and accumulation of K and Mg ions for their PolyP accumulation in aerobic conditions, but also release K and Mg ions along with their phosphate release to the environment under anaerobic conditions [86, 87]. When PAOs release PolyP, they also liberate the stored K and Mg ions back into the soil environment, which can be taken up by AMF and delivered to sorghums via the mycorrhizal pathways [88, 89]. This process indicates that PAOs can contribute, not only to P cycling, but also to the availability of K and Mg in the soil, as identified by higher K and Mg uptake in the AMF + EBPR sorghums in our study. Therefore, the combination of PAOs and AMF creates a beneficial cycle where P and other essential nutrients are efficiently absorbed, stored, and released, making them readily available for plant uptake and contributing to improved soil and plant health.

Several microbial members were successfully transferred from the EBPR system to the rhizosphere of sorghum. The genera Thauera, Paracoccus, and Rhodanobacter are examples of denitrifiers and PAO members originating from the EBPR systems into the rhizosphere of sorghum [68, 71]. These PAOs are capable of utilizing a wide range of substrates and can adapt to different environmental conditions, making them well-suited for survival and integration into the sorghum rhizosphere. For example, Thauera, a member of the Rhodocyclaceae family, is a denitrifying PAO that demonstrates metabolic flexibility by processing a wide range of organic compounds including aromatic compounds, amino acids, and other substrates under both aerobic and denitrifying conditions [90]. Similarly, Paracoccus bacteria are adaptable in their ability to utilize substrates, as they are capable of using a wide range of organic compounds, including C1 and C2 compounds, short-chain alkanes, and various organic substrates, under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions [91–93]. Rhodanobacter bacteria demonstrate ecological adaptability by thriving in diverse environments, where they contribute to nitrogen cycling through complete denitrification or nitrate respiration, enabling energy production under oxygen-deprived conditions [94]. Additionally, certain Rhodanobacter strains possess genes linked to heavy metal resistance, DNA repair, and defense mechanisms, which collectively enhance their resilience to environmental stressors and support long-term persistence in extreme conditions such as acidic and metal-contaminated subsurface environments [95]. These versatility and adaptability allow the three EBPR-derived PAOs to prosper in the rhizosphere of sorghum, after transferring from an EBPR sludge ecosystem. The imported PAOs continued contributing to PolyP accumulation in the soils of EBPR and AMF + EBPR sorghums, as confirmed by our NMR results, highlighting the successful integration of PAOs from EBPR sludge into the sorghum rhizosphere. Additionally, the imported PAO abundances of Thauera, Paracoccus, and Rhodanobacter were highly correlated with P uptake in sorghums, thus showing the roles of EBPR-derived PAOs in soil P cycling and sorghum uptake.

Our SCRS analysis demonstrated that integrating microbiomes from EBPR biosolids and compost with AMF enhanced both functional and phenotypic diversity within the rhizosphere. While EBPR biosolids and compost independently caused moderate shifts in microbial metabolic profiles, their combined application with AMF led to more distinct and pronounced directional changes. These findings suggest that AMF–microbe interactions, rather than individual amendments with EBPR sludge or compost, were the primary forces behind microbiome metabolic reorganization. The synergy between AMF and EBPR-derived microbiota was particularly impactful, driving greater phenotypic variation and reinforcing the functional resilience of the rhizomicrobial community. The underlying reasons for this reorganization likely involve enhanced direct exchanges between AMF and EBPR-derived microbiomes, with AMF-mediated carbon transfer playing a central role in fostering diverse microbial interactions. Through their extraradical mycelial network, AMF transport photosynthates from host plants to the rhizosphere, supplying plant-derived carbon that fuels microbial activity and supports cooperative associations with functionally beneficial bacteria [96–98]. The more pronounced synergistic interactions between AMF and EBPR-derived microorganisms, compared to those with compost-derived communities, may stem from a higher abundance of metabolically compatible or functionally beneficial taxa within EBPR-derived microbiomes. These communities may possess traits that AMF preferentially engage with, such as metabolic capabilities that support cooperative nutrient exchange or stress resilience, thereby fostering tighter metabolic coupling and more effective cooperation in the rhizosphere [96–98]. The close relationship between AMF and EBPR-derived microbiota appears to promote a metabolically diverse and resilient rhizomicrobial community, potentially improving microbial functions related to organic matter decomposition and nutrient cycling. These effects may ultimately enhance plant fitness and P nutrition in sorghum treated with both AMF and EBPR biosolids.

While PICRUSt2 predictions offer useful directional insights, they rely on amplicon-based inference and may not fully capture functional gene content or expression. As such, interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, pending validation through metagenomic or transcriptomic approaches. In this study, PICRUSt2 analysis suggested enrichment of microbial metabolic pathways, most notably those associated with motility and xenobiotic degradation, in EBPR and AMF + EBPR sorghum rhizospheres. Motility and chemotaxis confer critical ecological advantages by enhancing nutrient foraging, colonization efficiency, host-microbe interactions, and adaptive responses to dynamic soil environments [99]. Similarly, the predicted enrichment of xenobiotic biodegradation pathways may indicate either the successful establishment of xenobiotic-degrading bacteria from EBPR biosolids or the selective activation of indigenous soil microbial functions. Wastewater commonly contains trace levels of persistent xenobiotics, including pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and plastics, that conventional treatment systems fail to fully remove [100, 101]. Moreover, the enrichment of xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism pathways in the rhizospheres of sorghum treated with EBPR biosolids may indicate either the successful settlement of xenobiotic-degrading bacteria from the biosolids into rhizosphere microbial communities or the selective activation of indigenous soil microbial pathways. Notably, PAO genera associated with EBPR biosolid treatments such as Thauera, which harbors genes for benzoate, chlorinated hydrocarbon, and nitrotoluene degradation [102], and Paracoccus, known to degrade pesticides, PAHs, plastics, antibiotics, and dyes [103] could exemplify taxa with broad catabolic versatility that may contribute to functional integration within EBPR-amended rhizospheres. Taken together, microbial traits such as enhanced motility and enzymatic versatility likely support the ecological persistence of EBPR-derived microorganisms in the sorghum rhizosphere. However, given the predictive nature of PICRUSt2, these findings should be interpreted with caution and serve as a foundation for future functional validation, using metagenomic or functional assays.

Based on microbial network structure, we provided key insights into how PAOs originated from EBPR systems and compost respond differently to AMF. The introduction of AMF with EBPR biosolids significantly altered the dynamic behavior in microbial networks between EBPR and AMF + EBPR treatments, whereas the comparison between Compost and AMF + Compost treatments revealed moderate structural differences and similar network dynamics. Consistent with our SCRS findings, AMF-microbe interactions, particularly involving EBPR-derived microbiota, greatly enhanced functional and phenotypic diversity in the sorghum rhizosphere. These variations likely originated from the presence of certain PAOs in EBPR biosolids, which can coexist with AMF through complementary P-related processes and form new interactions, resembling the cooperative dynamics between PSB and AMF in natural soils [104]. By incorporating specialized PAOs, EBPR biosolids foster novel AMF interactions with other microorganisms, creating complex and dynamic microbial ecosystems that support sorghum growth and nutrient acquisition. On the contrary, the PAOs derived from compost may not show the same level of specialization or efficiency in P cycling as those derived from EBPR biosolids. Unlike EBPR-derived PAOs, which originate from aquatic ecosystems, compost-derived PAOs are naturally adapted to terrestrial environments and have co-evolved alongside AMF through long-standing nutrient and microbial interactions. However, their extended evolutionary presence in soils may lead to more direct competition with AMF and plants for nutrients [98]. Studies have shown that intense competition among different microbial species, including AMF and ammonia-oxidizing microorganisms, can decrease overall fungal abundance and limit the diversity of interactions within the microbial community [6, 8, 105, 106]. This competitive effect was evident in our study, as AMF + Compost sorghums resulted in lower plant growth and nutrient uptake such as Mg, P, and S, compared to AMF + EBPR, possibly due to increased microbial competition between AMF and compost-derived microbiota, ultimately reducing AMF efficiency in supporting plant nutrition and growth.
