1.
Introduction

In this article, we study the quasilinear problem

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u+g(u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
N\geq 1
) is a smooth bounded domain,
p\in(1,\infty)
,
\lambda>0
is a parameter and
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is just a continuous function. Moreover, we suppose that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous, has two consecutive positive zeros, and is positive between them. We denote these two zeros by
\alpha
and
\beta
with
0<\alpha<\beta
. Then, we are assuming that
f(\alpha)=f(\beta)=0
and
f(s)>0
for all
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
. We underline that
f
is allowed to change sign outside the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
.

Under these hypotheses, problem (
1.1
) has been widely studied when the natural growth term is not present, i.e., when
g\equiv 0
. The study of problems like

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

comes back to
[
20
]
. For the usual Laplacian operator (
p=2
) and assuming
f(0)\geq 0
, the author proved that an area condition on
f
is sufficient to guarantee the existence of two nonnegative solutions to (
1.2
) with maximum in
(\alpha,\beta]
for large
\lambda
. Specifically, this area condition is

(1.3)

\int_{s}^{\beta}f(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta>0,\ \forall s\in[0,\beta).

Later, in
[
8
]
and
[
11
]
, the authors showed using different techniques that (
1.3
) is also necessary in order to have, for some
\lambda>0
, a nonnegative solution
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
to (
1.2
) (with
p=2
) such that
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
.

For the
p
-Laplacian, these results have been suitably extended. Two different proofs of the sufficiency and necessity of (
1.4
) for the existence of solutions to (
1.2
) with maximum in
[\alpha,\beta]
can be found in
[
17
]
and
[
24
]
. Beyond existence, some qualitative properties of these solutions have also been studied, mainly in the case
p=2
. Regarding the behaviour of the solutions as
\lambda
goes to infinity, we refer the reader to
[
3
,
16
,
12
,
22
]
when
f
is regular (say
C^{1}
), and to
[
18
,
19
]
when
f
is not differentiable at
\alpha
or
\beta
. The specific structure of the solution set of (
1.1
) is investigated in
[
7
,
28
]
.

In recent years, these kind of Dirichlet problems in bounded domains with sign-changing nonlinearities have been widely studied for other operators. Among the operators considered are the
p(x)
-Laplacian (
[
21
]
), the
\phi
-Laplacian (
[
10
]
), the 1-Laplacian (
[
14
]
), the fractional Laplacian (
[
5
]
), a Kirchhoff operator (
[
1
]
) and a Schrödinger-type operator (
[
9
]
). In all these works, condition (
1.3
) is assumed to establish the existence of nonnegative solutions with maximum in
[\alpha,\beta]
; however, the necessity of (
1.3
) has been less explored. We point out that the situation changes under Robin boundary conditions; in that case, no area condition is required, as shown in
[
6
]
.

An original contribution of the present work is the derivation of an area condition for problems of the form (
1.1
), extending the classical condition (
1.3
). The condition for problem (
1.1
) reflects an interaction between the nonlinearities
f
and
g
that has not been previously characterized which, when
g\not\equiv 0
, completely differs from (
1.3
). This highlights new structural properties in Dirichlet problems involving nonlinearities with multiple zeros. Concretely, we prove that the area condition for (
1.1
) is

(1.4)

\int_{s}^{\beta}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}G(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta>0,\ \forall s\in[0,\beta),

where
G(s)\coloneq\int_{0}^{s}g(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. A major difficulty in dealing with (
1.1
), unlike the operators mentioned above, is the absence of a variational structure. We overcome this issue by transforming (
1.1
) into an equivalent variational problem and show that (
1.4
) is sufficient and necessary for the existence of a pair
(\lambda,u)
, with
u\geq 0
and
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
, solving (
1.1
).

Our first result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
. Then the following holds:

i)

If
f
verifies (
1.4
) and
f(0)\geq 0
, then there is some
\overline{\lambda}>0
such that, for every
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
, problem (
1.1
) has a nonnegative solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in(\alpha,\beta]
.

ii)

If
f
does not satisfy (
1.4
), then problem (
1.1
) admits no nonnegative solution with maximum in
[\alpha,\beta]
for any
\lambda>0
.

A significant consequence of this work is that, in certain cases, condition (
1.4
) imposes a less restrictive constraint on
f
than the classical condition (
1.3
). Therefore, even when problem (
1.2
) admits no nonnegative solutions with maxima in
[\alpha,\beta]
because (
1.3
) is not satisfied, the gradient term in (
1.1
) may induce a regularizing effect, allowing the existence of such solutions for (
1.1
) nonetheless. This establishes a novel mechanism by which the gradient term expands the solvability framework of problem (
1.2
). To better illustrate this phenomenon, we include a parameter multiplying the natural growth term and we analyse the existence and nonexistence of solution when the parameter varies. The family of problems considered is

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u+Lg(u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
L\in\mathbb{R}
. In addition,
g
is assumed to have constant sign. Since
L\in\mathbb{R}
, without loss of generality we may assume that
g
is positive.

We begin by studying the range of
L
’s for which a solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
1.5
) with
u\geq 0
and
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
exists for some
\lambda>0
. To simplify the notation, we define the set

(1.6)

\mathfrak{L}\coloneqq\left\{L\in\mathbb{R}:\exists\lambda>0\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:PbL}\text{ has a solution }0\leq u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}\text{ with }\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]\right\}.

As we prove, this set, which is closely related to the area condition, is nonempty for any given
f
, regardless of its shape. Furthermore, for
L\in\mathfrak{L}
, we also analyse the behaviour as
L
varies of the quantities

(1.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm min}(L)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf\left\{\lambda\geq 0:\eqref{eq:PbL}\text{ has a solution }0\leq u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}\text{ with }\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]\right\},

(1.8)

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf\left\{\overline{\lambda}\geq 0:\eqref{eq:PbL}\text{ has a solution }0\leq u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}\text{ with }\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]\text{ for any }\lambda>\overline{\lambda}\right\}.

Observe that, by definition,
\lambda_{\rm min}(L)\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)
.

The next result asserts that, regardless of the behaviour of
f
outside the interval
[\alpha,\beta]
, there always exists a parameter
L\in\mathbb{R}
such that problem (
1.5
) admits a solution with its maximum attained in
[\alpha,\beta]
for sufficiently large values of
\lambda
. Hence, the presence of the natural growth term induces a regularizing effect on problem (
1.1
). In heuristic terms, the magnitude and sign of the function
g
modulate this effect. Roughly speaking, the more negative
g
is, the stronger the regularizing effect becomes. Conversely, if
g
is sufficiently positive, a nonexistence phenomenon arises and no solution with maximum in
[\alpha,\beta]
exists for any
\lambda>0
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
be positive. Then there exists
\tilde{L}\in(-\infty,\infty]
such that
\mathfrak{L}
, defined in (
1.6
), verifies

\mathfrak{L}=(-\infty,\tilde{L}),

where
\tilde{L}=\infty
if
f\geq 0
and
\tilde{L}<\infty
if
f
changes sign in
[0,\beta]
.

Furthermore, for
L<\tilde{L}
, the quantities
\lambda_{\rm min}(L)
and
\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)
defined in (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) are positive and satisfy the following:

i)

\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)\to 0
as
L\to-\infty
,

ii)

\lambda_{\rm min}(L)\to\infty
as
L\to\tilde{L}
.

An important corollary of Theorem
1.2
is that, given any fixed
\lambda
, there are two numbers
L_{1}<L_{2}
such that, if
L<L_{1}
, problem (
1.5
) admits a solution
0\leq u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
1.5
) with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
, whereas if
L>L_{2}
, no such solution exists for (
1.5
). This allows us to prove, for any fixed
\lambda
, the existence of a maximal solution to (
1.5
) in the interval
[0,\beta]
when
L
is negative enough. In the following, we investigate the behaviour of these maximal solutions as
L\to-\infty
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
be positive. Given
\lambda>0
, there is some
L_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
such that the maximal solution
\overline{u}_{L}
in the interval
[0,\beta]
to problem (
1.5
) exists for every
L\leq L_{\lambda}
, and

\|\overline{u}_{L}\|_{{C(\overline{\Omega})}}\to\beta\text{ as }L\to-\infty.

We point out that all our results can be suitably extended to the more general problem

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\operatorname{div}(a(u)|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)+g(u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
a\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is positive, under the area condition

(1.10)

\int_{s}^{\beta}f(\eta)a(\eta)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}\int_{0}^{\eta}\frac{g(\sigma)}{a(\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma}\ \mathrm{d}\eta>0,\ \forall s\in[0,\beta).

When
g=\frac{1}{p}a^{\prime}
, the problem has a variational structure and (
1.10
) reduces to the usual area condition (
1.3
). This is the case, for instance, of the Schrödinger operator (cf.
[
9
]
). We further discuss this topic in Section
4.1
.

The plan of the paper is the following. In Section
2
, we present the approach adopted throughout the article and prove Theorem
1.1
. By means of a detailed analysis of how the area condition varies with
L
in (
1.5
), in Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
. Finally, in Section
4
, we extend our results to certain generalizations of (
1.1
) and provide further insights. In particular, we study problem (
1.9
), which includes a more general divergence term, and we analyse (
1.1
) in the case where
g(s)
depends also on
x
. Moreover, we discuss some regularity conditions on
f
which ensure that the solutions have norm different from
\beta
. We also show that a regularizing effect similar to the one observed in (
1.5
) as
L\to-\infty
can also be obtained in (
1.1
) if
p\to 1^{+}
.

2.
An initial approach

Throughout this work, we always deal with bounded solutions. In this setting, solutions to (
1.1
) enjoy good regularity properties. The notion of solution we use is the following.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
is a
subsolution
(resp. a
supersolution
) to (
1.1
) if it verifies

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega}g(u)|\nabla u|^{p}\varphi\overset{(\geq)}{\leq}\lambda\int_{\Omega}f(u)\varphi,\ \forall\varphi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\text{ with }\varphi\geq 0,

and
u\leq 0
(resp.
u\geq 0
) on
\partial\Omega
. In the same way, we say that
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
is a
solution
to (
1.1
) if

(2.1)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega}g(u)|\nabla u|^{p}\varphi=\lambda\int_{\Omega}f(u)\varphi,\ \forall\varphi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Remark 2.2
.

Thanks to the structure of problem (
1.1
) and to the boundedness of the solutions, the usual weak solution concept, where solutions belong to the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, is equivalent to our definition. Indeed, any
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
-solution to (
1.1
) belongs to
C^{1,\mu}(\overline{\Omega})
, where
\mu\in(0,1)
depends on the specific shape of (
1.1
) (see
[
23
]
).

We stress that, in general, due to the degeneracy of the
p
-Laplacian operator, solutions are not expected to belong to
C^{2}(\Omega)
even if
g\equiv 0
(see
[
25
]
). This is a major difference with respect to the usual Laplacian operator.

In this section, our main aim is to study existence and nonexistence of solutions to problem (
1.1
). Our strategy is to transform (
1.1
) into a semilinear problem via a suitable change of variables. To this end, let
\Psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function given by

(2.2)

\Psi(s)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\frac{1}{p-1}G(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta,\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R},

where
G(s)\coloneq\int_{0}^{s}g(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We stress that
\Psi
is an increasing
C^{2}
function whose derivative is

\Psi^{\prime}(s)=e^{-\frac{1}{p-1}G(s)},\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

Its inverse, whose domain is
\operatorname{Dom}\big(\Psi^{-1}\big)=\big(\lim_{s\to-\infty}\Psi(s),\lim_{s\to\infty}\Psi(s)\big)
, is increasing and belongs to
C^{2}
. Indeed, its derivative is

\left(\Psi^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{\Psi^{\prime}(\Psi^{-1}(s))}=e^{\frac{1}{p-1}G(\Psi^{-1}(s))},\ \forall s\in\operatorname{Dom}\big(\Psi^{-1}\big).

Observe that if
u
is a solution to (
1.1
), then
v=\Psi(u)
is formally a solution to the problem

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}v&=\lambda\tilde{f}(v)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
v&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\tilde{f}\colon\operatorname{Dom}\big(\Psi^{-1}\big)\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

(2.4)

\tilde{f}(s)\coloneqq f\left(\Psi^{-1}(s)\right)e^{-G(\Psi^{-1}(s))},\ \forall s\in\operatorname{Dom}\big(\Psi^{-1}\big).

In the next result, we formalise this equivalence.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
f,g\in C(\mathbb{R})
. Then
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
is a solution to (
1.1
) if and only if
v=\Psi(u)\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
is a solution to (
2.3
).

Remark 2.4
.

Since
\Psi
is increasing and
\Psi(0)=0
, let us note that
u\geq 0
if and only if
v\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
be a solution to (
1.1
). Given
\phi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, we take
e^{-G(u)}\phi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
as test function in (
2.1
). After cancelling terms, we obtain that

\int_{\Omega}e^{-G(u)}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\nabla\phi=\lambda\int_{\Omega}f(u)e^{-G(u)}\phi,\ \forall\phi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

If we define
v\coloneq\Psi(u)
, this can be rewritten as

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{p-2}\nabla v\nabla\phi=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}(v)\phi,\ \forall\phi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Then,
v=\Psi(u)
is a solution to (
2.3
).

Reciprocally, let
v\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
be a solution to (
2.3
). To show that
u\coloneqq\Psi^{-1}(v)
is a solution to (
1.1
), given
\varphi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, we take
e^{G(u)}\varphi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
as test function in (
2.3
). Rewriting the integrals in terms of
u
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}e^{G(u)}|\nabla\Psi(u)|^{p-2}\nabla\Psi(u)\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega}g(u)e^{G(u)}\nabla u|\nabla\Psi(u)|^{p-2}\nabla\Psi(u)\,\varphi

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}(\Psi(u))e^{G(u)}\varphi,\ \forall\varphi\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Taking into account the definition of
\tilde{f}
(see (
2.4
)) and using that
|\nabla\Psi(u)|^{p-2}\nabla\Psi(u)=e^{-G(u)}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u
, we obtain that (
2.1
) holds. In this way, we conclude that
u=\Psi^{-1}(v)
is a solution to (
1.1
).
∎

Remark 2.5
.

For simplicity, we have assumed that
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
in order to obtain
e^{G(u)}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
from
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
. The same argument remains valid if
g
is merely a bounded integrable function on the range of
u
. A similar remark applies to
f
: it is sufficient for
f
to be bounded.

In the following, we prove Theorem
1.1
. The strategy is to obtain, using Proposition
2.3
, the existence and nonexistence results for problem (
1.1
) from those already known for problem (
2.3
).

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Under our assumptions on
f
and
g
, the function
\tilde{f}
, defined in (
2.4
), has two consecutive zeros,
\Psi(\alpha)
and
\Psi(\beta)
, and is positive between them. Moreover,
f(0)\geq 0
if and only if
\tilde{f}(0)\geq 0
.

Thanks to
[
24
]
(see also
[
17
, Section 4]
), we know that the existence of nonnegative solutions to (
2.3
) with
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
-norm between
\Psi(\alpha)
and
\Psi(\beta)
is extremely related to the area condition

(2.5)

\int_{s}^{\Psi(\beta)}\tilde{f}(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta>0,\ \forall s\in[0,\Psi(\beta)).

Indeed, when (
2.5
) holds and
\tilde{f}(0)\geq 0
,
[
24
, Theorem 1.1]
ensures the existence of some
\overline{\lambda}
such that, for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
, problem (
2.3
) has a nonnegative solution
v\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
with
\|v\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\Psi(\alpha),\Psi(\beta)]
. In contrast, when (
2.5
) is not verified, problem (
2.3
) has no nonnegative solutions with maximum between
\Psi(\alpha)
and
\Psi(\beta)
for any
\lambda>0
(see
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Now, observe that the area condition (
1.4
) imposed on
f
is equivalent to (
2.5
). In fact, performing the change of variables
\rho=\Psi^{-1}(\eta)
, one obtains

(2.6)

\begin{split}\int_{s}^{\Psi(\beta)}\tilde{f}(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta&=\int_{s}^{\Psi(\beta)}f\left(\Psi^{-1}(\eta)\right)e^{-G(\Psi^{-1}(\eta))}\ \mathrm{d}\eta=\int_{\Psi^{-1}(s)}^{\beta}f\left(\rho\right)\Psi^{\prime}(\rho)e^{-G(\rho)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho\\
&=\int_{\Psi^{-1}(s)}^{\beta}f\left(\rho\right)e^{-{\frac{p}{p-1}}G(\rho)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho,\ \forall s\in\left[0,\Psi(\beta)\right).\end{split}

The proof ends just by applying Proposition
2.3
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Under certain regularity conditions on
f
, we can ensure that the maximum of any nonnegative solution to (
1.1
) is different from
\beta
. To facilitate the reading, we leave this topic for Section

.

Remark 2.7
.

Imposing an additional assumption on the behaviour of

at 0, it can be shown that every nonnegative (and nontrivial) solution to (

) is positive. Concretely, this occurs whenever

\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(s)}{s^{p-1}}>-\infty.

This can be proved using the strong maximum principle (
[

]
), following the same reasoning as in Section
4.3
.

Remark 2.8
.

When the existence conditions of Theorem

hold, the existence of a maximal solution
\overline{u}_{\lambda}
to (

) in
[0,\beta]
for each

is guaranteed because

is a supersolution (see

, Theorem 4.2]
). The variational arguments of
[

show that
\overline{v}_{\lambda}
, the maximal solution to (

) in
[0,\Psi(\beta)]
, verifies
\|\overline{v}_{\lambda}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\to\Psi(\beta)
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Using Proposition
2.3
, one deduces that
\|\overline{u}_{\lambda}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\to\beta
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

If
f
is more regular and behaves well near the zeros, it is shown in
[
8
, Theorem 2]
and
[
16
, Theorem A]
that, for
\overline{v}_{\lambda}
, the convergence to
\Psi(\beta)
is uniform in compact subsets of
\Omega
. Thanks to Proposition
2.3
, it also holds that
\overline{u}_{\lambda}\to\beta
uniformly in
K
as
\lambda\to\infty
for every compact set
K\subset\Omega
.

Remark 2.9
.

Under the existence conditions of Theorem
1.1
, we can ensure the existence of two nonnegative solutions to (
1.1
) with maximum in
(\alpha,\beta]
for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
. Again, this is a consequence of the multiplicity results known for (

) (see
[

,
10
]
), that also hold for (

) thanks to Proposition

.

3.
Regularizing effect of the natural growth term

Here, we study the effect of the interaction between the gradient term and
f
on the existence and nonexistence of solutions. To this end, we introduce a parameter
L\in\mathbb{R}
multiplying the gradient term, and we analyse how the solution set of problem (
1.5
), i.e., of

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u+Lg(u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

varies as
L
varies.

We divide this section into three parts. The first is more technical and concerns the behaviour of the solutions as
L\to-\infty
. In the second part, we present a useful stability result. Finally, we prove Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
.

3.1.
Asymptotic behaviour as
L\to-\infty

First of all, we prove an elemental lemma that will be useful for studying certain properties of the functions defined later in (
3.7
) and (
3.9
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be an increasing function and let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
. Then, one has

\lim_{L\to-\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-LG^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\right)^{-1}\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta=1.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we assume
L<0
. First, we use Taylor’s Theorem to write

(3.2)

G(s)=G(\gamma_{2})-G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})(\gamma_{2}-s)+o(\gamma_{2}-s),\ \forall s\in[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}],

where
o(\gamma_{2}-s)
is such that
\frac{o(\gamma_{2}-s)}{\gamma_{2}-s}\to 0
as
s\to\gamma_{2}
. Therefore, given
\varepsilon\in(0,G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2}))
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
small such that

(3.3)

\left|\frac{o(\gamma_{2}-s)}{\gamma_{2}-s}\right|<\varepsilon,\ \forall s\in(\gamma_{2}-\delta,\gamma_{2}).

On the one hand, using the monotonicity of
G
we deduce that

0\leq\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}-\delta}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\leq(\gamma_{2}-\delta-\gamma_{1})e^{-LG(\gamma_{2}-\delta)},\ \forall L<0.

Since
G(\gamma_{2}-\delta)<G(\gamma_{2})
, then
\lim_{L\to-\infty}Le^{L(G(\gamma_{2})-G(\gamma_{2}-\delta))}=0
. Hence, we obtain that

(3.4)

\lim_{L\to-\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-LG^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\right)^{-1}\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}-\delta}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta=0.

On the other hand, taking (
3.3
) into account, we have that
e^{-L(-\varepsilon)(\gamma_{2}-s)}\leq e^{-Lo(\gamma_{2}-s)}\leq e^{-L\varepsilon(\gamma_{2}-s)}
when
s\in(\gamma_{2}-\delta,\gamma_{2})
and, using (
3.2
), we deduce

(3.5)

e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}\int_{\gamma_{2}-\delta}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{L(G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})+\varepsilon)(\gamma_{2}-\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\leq\int_{\gamma_{2}-\delta}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\leq e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}\int_{\gamma_{2}-\delta}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{L(G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})-\varepsilon)(\gamma_{2}-\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta.

Calculating explicitly the first and the last integral in (
3.5
), we obtain

e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}\frac{1-e^{L(G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})+\varepsilon)\delta}}{-L(G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})+\varepsilon)}\leq\int_{\gamma_{2}-\delta}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\leq e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}\frac{1-e^{L(G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})-\varepsilon)\delta}}{-L(G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})-\varepsilon)}.

In this way, since
G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})-\varepsilon>0
, we deduce that

(3.6)

\frac{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})+\varepsilon}\leq\liminf_{L\to-\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-LG^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\right)^{-1}\int_{\gamma_{2}-\delta}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\leq\limsup_{L\to-\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-LG^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\right)^{-1}\int_{\gamma_{2}-\delta}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\leq\frac{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})-\varepsilon}.

Joining (
3.4
) and (
3.6
), we obtain that

\frac{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})+\varepsilon}\leq\liminf_{L\to-\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-LG^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\right)^{-1}\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\leq\limsup_{L\to-\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-LG^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\right)^{-1}\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\leq\frac{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})-\varepsilon}.

Our claim follows immediately by tending
\varepsilon
to 0.
∎

Now, we introduce some notation. To make explicit the dependence on
L
, for any
L\in\mathbb{R}
we define, as in (
2.2
), the function

(3.7)

\Psi_{L}(s)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\frac{1}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta,\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R},

where
G(s)=\int_{0}^{s}g(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We point out that
G
is increasing when
g
is positive. In relation to the area condition (
1.4
), for any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in[\alpha,\beta]
with
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
we define the continuous function

(3.8)

H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s,L)\coloneqq\int_{s}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta,\ \forall(s,L)\in[0,\gamma_{1}]\times\mathbb{R},

and we set, for any
L\in\mathbb{R}
,

(3.9)

{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}\coloneqq\min_{s\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s,L)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}\coloneqq\max_{s\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s,L).

The next result collects some asymptotic properties of these functions when
L\to-\infty
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
be positive. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in[\alpha,\beta]
be such that
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
, and let
\Psi_{L}(s)
,
{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}
and
{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}
be the functions defined in (
3.7
) and (
3.9
). Then it holds:

i)

If
\gamma_{2}<\beta
, there is some
L_{0}<0
and some
C>0
such that

{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}\geq C\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-L},\ \forall L<L_{0}.

If
\gamma_{2}=\beta
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
small there is some
L_{0}=L_{0}(\varepsilon)<0
and some
C=C(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\beta}(L)\geq C\frac{e^{{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\beta-\varepsilon)}}}{-L},\ \forall L<L_{0}.

ii)

Both
{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}
and
{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}
grow at the same rate as
L\to-\infty
, i.e.,

\lim_{L\to-\infty}\frac{{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}{{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}=1.

iii)

If
\gamma_{2}<\beta
, then

\lim_{L\to-\infty}\frac{\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{2})^{p}}{{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}=0.

Proof.

During the whole proof, we assume
L<0
. Each item is proved as follows:

i)

Assume first that
\gamma_{2}<\beta
. Let
\varepsilon_{0}\geq 0
be such that
\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}\in(\alpha,\gamma_{2})
. Taking into account that
\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)\leq 0
and that
\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}]}f(t)>0
, we deduce for any
s\in[0,\gamma_{1}]
and any
L<0
that

(3.10)

\begin{split}H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s,L)=&\int_{s}^{\gamma_{1}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta+\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\\
\geq&(\gamma_{1}-s)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)+\int_{\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\\
\geq&\gamma_{1}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)+\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}]}f(t)\int_{\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\\
=&\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-L}\left(-\gamma_{1}Le^{\frac{p}{p-1}L(G(\gamma_{2})-G(\gamma_{1}))}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)\vphantom{\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma)}}{-L}\right)^{-1}}\right.\\
&+\left.\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-L}\right)^{-1}\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}]}f(t)\int_{\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\right).\end{split}

Therefore, for any
L<0
we obtain that

\begin{split}{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}\geq&\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-L}\left(-\gamma_{1}Le^{\frac{p}{p-1}L(G(\gamma_{2})-G(\gamma_{1}))}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)\vphantom{\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma)}}{-L}\right)^{-1}}\right.\\
&+\left.\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-L}\right)^{-1}\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}]}f(t)\int_{\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\right).\end{split}

Using Lemma
3.1
and that
G(\gamma_{1})<G(\gamma_{2})
, we deduce our claim because

\begin{split}\lim_{L\to-\infty}&\left(-\gamma_{1}Le^{\frac{p}{p-1}L(G(\gamma_{2})-G(\alpha_{1}))}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)+\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-L}\right)^{-1}\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}]}f(t)\int_{\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}}^{\gamma_{2}}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta\right)\\
&=\frac{p-1}{pG^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}]}f(t)>0.\end{split}

When
\gamma_{2}=\beta
, it suffices to observe that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\beta-\gamma_{1})
we have

\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\beta}(L)\geq\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\beta-\varepsilon}(L),\ \forall L\in\mathbb{R},

and then the result for the case
\gamma_{2}<\beta
can be applied with
\gamma_{2}=\beta-\varepsilon
to conclude our claim.

ii)

To unify the proof for the cases
\gamma_{2}<\beta
and
\gamma_{2}=\beta
observe that, thanks to item i) and to the fact that
G
is increasing, given
\varepsilon>0
with
\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon\in(\gamma_{1},\beta)
, there is
L_{0}<0
and
C>0
such that

(3.11)

{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}\geq C\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon)}}{-L},\ \forall L<L_{0}.

We point out that, for this range of
L
,
{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}
is positive. For any
L\in\mathbb{R}
, we define
s_{L}\in[0,\gamma_{1}]
as the point where
H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s,L)
achieves its maximum in
s
. Then, it holds

(3.12)

{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}=\max_{s\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s,L)=H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s_{L},L)=\int_{s_{L}}^{\gamma_{1}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta+\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta.

On the one hand, using (
3.11
) and that
\max_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)\geq 0
, we have that

\begin{split}0&\leq\frac{\int_{s_{L}}^{\gamma_{1}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}{{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}\leq\frac{\gamma_{1}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\max_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)}{{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}\\
&\leq\frac{\gamma_{1}\max_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)}{C}\frac{-L}{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}L(G(\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon)-G(\gamma_{1}))}},\ \forall L<L_{0}.\end{split}

As
G(\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon)>G(\gamma_{1})
, we immediately obtain that

(3.13)

\lim_{L\to-\infty}\frac{\int_{s_{L}}^{\gamma_{1}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}{{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}=0.

On the other hand, reasoning as in (
3.10
), we deduce that

{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}\geq\gamma_{1}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)+\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta>0,\ \forall L<L_{0},

and then

(3.14)

\begin{split}1&\leq\frac{\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}{{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}\\
&\leq\frac{\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}{\gamma_{1}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)+\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}\\[5.69054pt]
&=\frac{1}{\frac{\gamma_{!}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)}{\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}+1},\ \forall L<L_{0}.\end{split}

Now, given
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}\in(\alpha,\beta)
and
\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}<\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon
, observe that, since
\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)\leq 0
and
\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon]}f(t)>0
, one has

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\gamma_{1}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)}{\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\gamma_{1}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)}{(\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon-\gamma_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0})}\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon]}f(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{1}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)}{(\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon-\gamma_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})\min_{t\in[\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0},\gamma_{2}-\varepsilon]}f(t)}\,e^{\frac{p}{p-1}L(G(\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0})-G(\gamma_{1}))},\ \forall L<L_{0}.

Since
G(\gamma_{1}+\varepsilon_{0})>G(\gamma_{1})
, we deduce that

\lim_{L\to-\infty}\frac{\gamma_{1}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{1})}\min_{t\in[0,\gamma_{1}]}f(t)}{\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}=0.

Therefore, we can take limits in (
3.14
) to obtain that

(3.15)

\lim_{L\to-\infty}\frac{\int_{\gamma_{1}}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta}{{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}=1.

Joining (
3.12
), (
3.13
) and (
3.15
) we deduce our claim.

iii)

By Lemma
3.1
, there is some
C_{1}>0
and some
L_{1}<0
such that

\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{2})\leq\frac{C_{1}(p-1)}{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}{-L},\ \forall L<L_{1}.

In this way, using item i), we deduce the existence of some
L_{2}<0
and some
C_{2}>0
such that

0\leq\frac{\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{2})^{p}}{{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}}\leq C_{2}\left(\frac{C_{1}(p-1)}{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\right)^{p}\frac{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}{e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\gamma_{2})}}\frac{-L}{(-L)^{p}}=C_{2}\left(\frac{C_{1}(p-1)}{G^{\prime}(\gamma_{2})}\right)^{p}\frac{1}{(-L)^{p-1}},\ \forall L<L_{2}.

Taking the limit as
L\to-\infty
, we obtain the desired result. ∎

The next result establishes that, given any
\lambda>0
and any
\gamma_{1}\in[\alpha,\beta)
, one can always find an unbounded range of
L
’s such that problem (
1.5
) has a nonnegative solution whose maximum lies between
\gamma_{1}
and
\beta
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
be positive. Then, for each
\overline{\lambda}>0
and each
\gamma_{1}\in[\alpha,\beta)
, there is some
\overline{L}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
and any
L<\overline{L}
problem (
1.5
) has a nonnegative solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in(\gamma_{1},\beta]
.

Proof.

Since we are interested only in nonnegative solutions less than
\beta
, we redefine
f
and
g
, without changing the notation, as

f(s)\coloneq\left\{\begin{aligned} (1+s)f(0)\quad&\mbox{if}\;\;-1\leq s<0,\\
f(s)\quad&\mbox{if}\;\;0\leq s<\beta,\\
0\quad&\mbox{in other case},\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\quad\text{and}\quad\qquad g(s)\coloneq\left\{\begin{aligned} (1+s)g(0)\quad&\mbox{if}\;\;-1\leq s<0,\\
g(s)\quad&\mbox{if}\;\;0\leq s<\beta,\\
(\beta+1-s)g(\beta)\quad&\mbox{if}\;\;\beta\leq s<\beta+1,\\
0\quad&\mbox{in other case}.\end{aligned}\right.

We observe that the boundedness of
G
implies that the range of
\Psi_{L}
, defined in (
3.7
), is
\mathbb{R}
, and consequently
\operatorname{Dom}\left(\Psi_{L}^{-1}\right)=\mathbb{R}
. We then define, as in (
2.4
), the function

\tilde{f}_{L}(s)\coloneqq f\left(\Psi_{L}^{-1}(s)\right)e^{-LG\left(\Psi_{L}^{-1}(s)\right)},\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R},

and we denote
\tilde{F}_{L}(s)\coloneq\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{f}_{L}(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\overline{\lambda}>0
and let
\gamma_{1}\in[\alpha,\beta)
. Thanks to Proposition
2.3
, it suffices to show the existence of some
\overline{L}\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
and any
L<\overline{L}
, problem

(3.16)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}v&=\lambda\tilde{f}_{L}(v)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
v&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

admits a nonnegative solution
v\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
with
\|v\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in(\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{1}),\Psi_{L}(\beta)]
.

Consider the functional

I_{\lambda,L}(v)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{p}-\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{F}_{L}(v),\ \forall v\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega).

Since
\tilde{F}_{L}
is continuous and bounded, the functional
I_{\lambda,L}
is coercive and weakly lower semicontinuous for every
\lambda>0
and every
L\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore,
I_{\lambda,L}
attains a global minimum at some
v_{\lambda,L}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
. As
v_{\lambda,L}
is a solution to (
3.16
), since
\tilde{f}_{L}(s)\geq 0
when
s\leq 0
and
\tilde{f}_{L}(s)=0
when
s\geq\Psi_{L}(\beta)
, it can be easily deduced that
0\leq v_{\lambda,L}\leq\Psi_{L}(\beta)

\mathrm{a.e.}
in
\Omega
. Moreover, the regularity theory (
[
23
]
) implies that
v_{\lambda,L}\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
.

In the following, we show that there is some
\overline{L}\in\mathbb{R}
such that this minimizer has
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
-norm in
(\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{1}),\Psi_{L}(\beta)]
for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
and any
L<\overline{L}
.

Assume, by contradiction, that there is some sequence
L_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
L_{n}\to-\infty
and some
\lambda_{n}>\overline{\lambda}
such that
v_{n}\coloneq v_{\lambda_{n},L_{n}}
verifies
\|v_{n}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{1})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\gamma_{2}\in(\gamma_{1},\beta)
and consider an open set
\Omega_{0}\subset\subset\Omega
. We take a cutoff function
w_{n}\in C_{0}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
w_{n}\equiv\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})
in
\Omega_{0}
,
0\leq w_{n}\leq\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})
in
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}
, and
\|\nabla w_{n}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq c_{0}\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})
, where
c_{0}>0
is a constant not depending on
n
. We have that

(3.17)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\left(\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(w_{n})-\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(v_{n})\right)&=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(w_{n})+\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(w_{n})-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(v_{n})\\
&=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2}))+\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(w_{n})-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(v_{n})\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2}))-\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}\left(\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2}))-\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(w_{n})\right)-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(v_{n})\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\int_{v_{n}}^{\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})}\tilde{f}_{L_{n}}(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta-\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}}\int_{w_{n}}^{\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})}\tilde{f}_{L_{n}}(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta\\[2.84526pt]
&\geq\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})|\Omega|-\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|,\end{split}

where
{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}
and
{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L)}
are defined in (
3.9
). Here, we have taken into account (reasoning as in (
2.6
)) that

\displaystyle\int_{w_{n}}^{\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})}\tilde{f}_{L_{n}}(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle\leq\max_{0\leq s\leq\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})}\left\{\int_{s}^{\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})}\tilde{f}_{L_{n}}(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta\right\}=\max_{0\leq s\leq\gamma_{2}}\left\{\int_{s}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\rho)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}L_{n}G(\rho)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq s\leq\gamma_{1}}\left\{\int_{s}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\rho)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}L_{n}G(\rho)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho\right\}=\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})

and that, since
v_{n}\leq\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{1})
, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{v_{n}}^{\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})}\tilde{f}_{L_{n}}(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta=\int_{\Psi_{L_{n}}^{-1}(v_{n})}^{\gamma_{2}}f\left(\rho\right)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}L_{n}G(\rho)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho\geq\min_{0\leq s\leq\gamma_{1}}\left\{\int_{s}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\rho)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}L_{n}G(\rho)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho\right\}=\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n}).

Taking (
3.17
) into account and using that
\|\nabla w_{n}\|_{{C(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N})}}\leq c_{0}\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})
, we deduce

(3.18)

\begin{split}I_{\lambda_{n},L_{n}}(w_{n})-I_{\lambda_{n},L_{n}}(v_{n})&=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla w_{n}|^{p}-|\nabla v_{n}|^{p})-\lambda_{n}\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(w_{n})-\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(v_{n}))\\
&\leq\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{n}|^{p}-\lambda_{n}\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(w_{n})-\tilde{F}_{L_{n}}(v_{n}))\\
&\leq\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w_{n}|^{p}-\lambda_{n}\left(\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})|\Omega|-\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{p}c_{0}^{p}\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})^{p}|\Omega|-\lambda_{n}\left(\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})|\Omega|-\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|\right)\\
&=\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})\left[\frac{c_{0}^{p}|\Omega|}{p}\frac{\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})^{p}}{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}-\lambda_{n}\left(|\Omega|-|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|\frac{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}\right)\right].\end{split}

Since
L_{n}\to-\infty
, as a consequence of Lemma
3.2
, we can take
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

|\Omega|-|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|\frac{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}>0,\ \forall n\geq n_{0}.

As
\lambda_{n}>\overline{\lambda}
, from (
3.18
) we deduce for any
n\geq n_{0}
that

I_{\lambda_{n},L_{n}}(w_{n})-I_{\lambda_{n},L_{n}}(v_{n})\leq\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})\left[\frac{c_{0}^{p}|\Omega|}{p}\frac{\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})^{p}}{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}-\overline{\lambda}\left(|\Omega|-|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|\frac{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}\right)\right].

Finally, by Lemma
3.2
, we have that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})=\infty
and that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left[\frac{c_{0}^{p}|\Omega|}{p}\frac{\Psi_{L_{n}}(\gamma_{2})^{p}}{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}-\overline{\lambda}\left(|\Omega|-|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|\frac{\overline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}{\underline{h}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(L_{n})}\right)\right]=\overline{\lambda}(|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}|-|\Omega|)<0.

Therefore, we can find some
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

I_{\lambda_{n},L_{n}}(w_{n})<I_{\lambda_{n},L_{n}}(v_{n}),\ \forall n\geq n_{1},

but this contradicts the fact that
v_{n}
is a minimizer of
I_{\lambda_{n},L_{n}}
.

Then, we conclude that there is some
\overline{L}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\Psi_{L}(\gamma_{1})<\|v_{\lambda,L}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\Psi_{L}(\beta)
for any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
and any
L<\overline{L}
, as desired.
∎

3.2.
Stability

In some situations, it is necessary to pass to the limit in problems such as (
1.5
). Although it is standard, we include the following result for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f,g\in C(\mathbb{R})
. Let
u_{n}\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
be a solution to

(3.19)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u_{n}+L_{n}g(u_{n})|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}&=\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u_{n}&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\lambda_{n}
and
L_{n}
are sequences of real numbers. If
L_{n}\to L\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and for some
M>0
it holds
\|u_{n}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leq M
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}\to u
in
C(\overline{\Omega})
, where
u
is a solution to (
1.5
).

Proof.

This result is a straightforward consequence of the seminal paper
[
23
]
. Indeed, problem (
3.19
) satisfies, uniformly in
n
, all the structure conditions required in
[
23
, Theorem 1]
. Therefore, there exists some
C>0
and some
\mu\in(0,1)
such that

\|u_{n}\|_{C^{1,\mu}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C,\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

As a consequence,
u_{n}
has a subsequence, still denoted by
u_{n}
, such that
u_{n}\to u
in
C(\overline{\Omega})
and
\nabla u_{n}\to\nabla u
in
C(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N})
for some
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
. Then one can pass to the limit in the weak formulation of (
3.19
) to conclude that
u
is a solution to (
1.2
).
∎

3.3.
Structure of the solution set as
L
varies

Here, we aim to prove Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
. For clarity, we will subdivide Theorem
1.2
into smaller results, from which it will be easily deduced. In contrast, Theorem
1.3
will follow directly from Proposition
3.3
.

We start by studying for which values of
L
problem (
1.5
) has, for some
\lambda>0
, a nonnegative solution with maximum in
[\alpha,\beta]
. In other words, we study the specific shape of the set
\mathfrak{L}
defined in (
1.6
).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
be positive. If
\mathfrak{L}
denotes the set defined in (
1.6
), then there exists
\tilde{L}\in(-\infty,\infty]
such that

\mathfrak{L}=(-\infty,\tilde{L}).

Moreover,
\tilde{L}=\infty
if
f\geq 0
, whereas
\tilde{L}<\infty
if
f
changes sign in
[0,\beta]
.

Proof.

For problem (
1.5
), the area condition (
1.4
) becomes

\int_{s}^{\beta}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta>0,\ \forall s\in[0,\beta).

Observe that this area condition is verified for some
L\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)>0
, where
\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)
is defined in (
3.9
). Due to Theorem
1.1
, we have the equivalence

\mathfrak{L}=\left\{L\in\mathbb{R}:\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)>0\right\}.

The easiest case arises when
f\geq 0
. Here, it is straightforward to see that
\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)>0
for any
L\in\mathbb{R}
. We deduce that
\mathfrak{L}=\mathbb{R}
and hence
\tilde{L}=\infty
.

When
f
changes sign in
[0,\beta]
, we begin by noting that
\mathfrak{L}
is not empty. Indeed, thanks to item i) of Lemma
3.2
, we have
\lim_{L\to-\infty}\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)=\infty
, which implies
\mathfrak{L}\neq\emptyset
. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{L}
is bounded above. To prove this, let
s_{0}\in(0,\alpha)
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be such that
f(s)<0
for all
s\in[s_{0},s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0}]
, and choose
c_{0}>0
such that
f(s)\leq-c_{0}<0
for any
s\in[s_{0},s_{0}+\varepsilon_{0}]
. Then, using that
G
is increasing, we have for
L>0
that

\displaystyle\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)

\displaystyle\leq H_{\alpha,\beta}(s_{0},L)=\int_{s_{0}}^{s_{0}+\varepsilon_{0}}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta+\int_{s_{0}+\varepsilon_{0}}^{\beta}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle\leq-c_{0}\varepsilon_{0}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(s_{0}+\varepsilon_{0})}+\int_{s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0}}^{\beta}f(\eta)e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle\leq-c_{0}\varepsilon_{0}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(s_{0}+\varepsilon_{0})}+(\beta-s_{0}-2\varepsilon_{0})e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0})}\max_{t\in[s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0},\beta]}f(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}LG(s_{0}+\varepsilon_{0})}\left(-c_{0}\varepsilon_{0}+(\beta-s_{0}-2\varepsilon_{0})e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}L(G(s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0})-G(s_{0}+\varepsilon_{0}))}\max_{t\in[s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0},\beta]}f(t)\right).

Since
G(s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0})>G(s_{1}+\varepsilon_{0})
, then

\lim_{L\to\infty}\left(-c_{0}\varepsilon_{0}+(\beta-s_{0}-2\varepsilon_{0})e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}L(G(s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0})-G(s_{0}+\varepsilon_{0}))}\max_{t\in[s_{0}+2\varepsilon_{0},\beta]}f(t)\right)=-c_{0}\varepsilon_{0}.

Hence, we deduce the existence of some
L_{0}>0
such that
\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)<0
for any
L>L_{0}
.

As a consequence, if we define

\tilde{L}\coloneqq\sup\mathfrak{L}=\sup\left\{L\in\mathbb{R}:\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)>0\right\},

then we have that
\tilde{L}\in\mathbb{R}
.

It remains for us to show that
\mathfrak{L}=(-\infty,\tilde{L})
. First, observe that
\mathfrak{L}
is an open set because
\underline{h}_{\alpha,\beta}(L)
is continuous. In particular, this implies that
\mathfrak{L}\subseteq(-\infty,\tilde{L})
. Now, we prove that the reverse inclusion also holds. Let
L\in(-\infty,\tilde{L})
. By definition of supremum, we can find some
L_{1}\in\mathfrak{L}
such that
L<L_{1}
. As
L_{1}\in\mathfrak{L}
, this means that for some large
\lambda
there is
0\leq u_{1}\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
with
\|u_{1}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
weak solution of

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u+L_{1}g(u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Since
L<L_{1}
, we have that
u_{1}
is a strict subsolution of (
1.5
). As
\overline{u}\equiv\beta
is a supersolution of (
1.5
), by
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
there is a solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
of (
1.5
) such that
u_{1}\leq u\leq\beta
in
\Omega
. Then,
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
. Due to the definition of
\mathfrak{L}
, we obtain that
L\in\mathfrak{L}
and thus
(-\infty,\tilde{L})\subseteq\mathfrak{L}
. Therefore, we conclude that
\mathfrak{L}=(-\infty,\tilde{L})
.
∎

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
, the sets

\displaystyle\Lambda_{L}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\lambda\geq 0:\eqref{eq:PbL}\text{ has a solution }0\leq u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}\text{ with }\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]\right\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\overline{\Lambda}_{L}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\overline{\lambda}\geq 0:\eqref{eq:PbL}\text{ has a solution }0\leq u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}\text{ with }\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]\text{ for any }\lambda>\overline{\lambda}\right\}

are nonempty and closed for any
L\in(-\infty,\tilde{L})
as a consequence of Lemma
3.4
and Proposition
3.5
. Then, for any
L\in(-\infty,\tilde{L})
one can define

\lambda_{\rm min}(L)\coloneqq\min\Lambda_{L}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)\coloneqq\min\overline{\Lambda}_{L}.

By definition, it holds
\lambda_{\rm min}(L)\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)
for any
L\in(-\infty,\tilde{L})
. Moreover, both quantities are always positive because for
\lambda=0
the only solution to (
3.16
) is
v\equiv 0
.

In the following, our aim is to study the behaviour of
\lambda_{\rm min}(L)
as
L\to\tilde{L}
. We distinguish two cases: when
f
changes sign and
\tilde{L}<\infty
, and when
f\geq 0
and
\tilde{L}=\infty
. For the latter, we employ a strategy introduced in
[
2
]
, which is based on the Stampacchia regularity method (
[
26
]
). The next lemma, whose proof can be found in
[
26
, Lemma 4.1]
, will be needed.

Lemma 3.6

(
[
26
]
)
.

Let
k_{0}\geq 0
and let
\Theta:[k_{0},\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a nonincreasing function. If there exist
c,a>0
and
b>1
such that

\Theta(h)\leq\frac{c}{(h-k)^{a}}[\Theta(k)]^{b},\quad\forall h>k>k_{0},

then

\Theta(k_{0}+d)=0,\quad\text{for }d^{a}:=c2^{\frac{ab}{b-1}}[\Theta(k_{0})]^{b-1}.

Now, we are ready to study the behaviour of
\lambda_{\rm min}(L)
as
L\to\tilde{L}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
be positive. Then,
\lambda_{\rm min}(L)\to\infty
as
L\to\tilde{L}
.

Proof.

Arguing by contradiction, suppose that
\lambda_{\rm min}(L)\not\to\infty
as
L\to\tilde{L}
. Then, there is some
L_{n}\to\tilde{L}
such that
\lambda_{n}\coloneq\lambda_{\rm min}(L_{n})
is bounded, say by
\overline{\lambda}
. Since
\lambda_{n}\in\Lambda_{L_{n}}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a nonnegative solution
u_{n}\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
3.19
) such that
\|u_{n}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
.

When
f
changes sign, one has that
\tilde{L}<\infty
(see Proposition
3.5
). Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
\lambda_{n}\to\tilde{\lambda}
for some
\tilde{\lambda}\geq 0
. By Lemma
3.4
, one can pass to the limit in (
3.19
) to deduce that
\tilde{L}\in\mathfrak{L}
, but this is a contradiction with Proposition
3.5
.

Now, we study the case where
f\geq 0
. In this setting, one has
\tilde{L}=\infty
. First, we prove that
u_{n}
converges to 0 in
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, we define the truncation function
T_{\varepsilon}(s)=\min\{s,\varepsilon\}
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Taking
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}T_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})
as test function in (
3.19
), we deduce that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\nabla T_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})|^{p}+L_{n}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}T_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})g(u_{n})|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}=\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})\frac{1}{\varepsilon}T_{\varepsilon}(u_{n}).

After dropping the first integral, we obtain

L_{n}\min_{t\in[0,\beta]}g(t)\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}T_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}\leq L_{n}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}T_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})g(u_{n})|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}\leq\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})\frac{1}{\varepsilon}T_{\varepsilon}(u_{n})\leq\overline{\lambda}|\Omega|\max_{t\in[0,\beta]}f(t).

Note that
\min_{t\in[0,\beta]}g(t)>0
. If
C_{1}\coloneq\overline{\lambda}|\Omega|\max_{t\in[0,\beta]}f(t)\left(\min_{t\in[0,\beta]}g(t)\right)^{-1}>0
, one deduces tending
\varepsilon
to 0 that

L_{n}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}=L_{n}\int_{\{u_{n}>0\}}|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}\leq C_{1}.

Since
L_{n}\to\infty
, we can ensure that
u_{n}\to 0
strongly in
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
.

In the following, we show that
u_{n}
converges to 0 in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
. This will lead to a contradiction because
\|u_{n}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. To this end, we use a strategy of
[
2
]
based on the Stampacchia regularity method. Since we make use of the Sobolev embeddings, we present here the proof only for the case
N>p
; the case
N=p
can be treated similarly, while for
N<p
the result follows directly from Morrey’s Theorem.

Given
k>0
, we define the function
G_{k}(s)=\max\{0,s-k\}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Taking
G_{k}(u_{n})
as test function in (
3.19
), we obtain that

(3.20)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})|^{p}+L_{n}\int_{\Omega}G_{k}(u_{n})g(u_{n})|\nabla u_{n}|^{p}=\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})G_{k}(u_{n}).

We set
\Omega_{n}(k)=\{x\in\Omega:u_{n}>k\}
. We point out that
\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})
is bounded in
{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
by
C_{2}\coloneq\overline{\lambda}\max_{t\in[0,\beta]}f(t)
. Dropping a nonnegative term in (
3.20
), and using the Hölder inequality with exponents
p^{*}=\frac{pN}{N-p}
and
(p^{*})^{\prime}=\frac{pN}{N(p-1)+p}
, we deduce that

\begin{split}\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)}^{p}&=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla G_{k}(u_{n})|^{p}\leq\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{n}f(u_{n})G_{k}(u_{n})\leq C_{2}\int_{\Omega_{n}(k)}G_{k}(u_{n})\leq C_{2}|\Omega_{n}(k)|^{\frac{1}{(p^{*})^{\prime}}}\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

Using the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p^{*}}(\Omega)
, we find
C_{3}>0
such that

(3.21)

\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega)}^{p}\leq C_{3}|\Omega_{n}(k)|^{\frac{1}{(p^{*})^{\prime}}}\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega)}.

Observe that, if we take
h>k>0
, then
\Omega_{n}(h)\subseteq\Omega_{n}(k)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Using that
G_{k}(s)\geq h-k
for all
s\geq h
, we deduce that

(3.22)

\|G_{k}(u_{n})\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\Omega_{n}(k)}G_{k}(u_{n})^{p^{*}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{*}}}\geq(h-k)|\Omega_{n}(h)|^{\frac{1}{p^{*}}}.

Combining (
3.21
) and (
3.22
), we obtain, for some
C_{4}>0
, that

(3.23)

|\Omega_{n}(h)|\leq\frac{C_{4}}{(h-k)^{p^{*}}}|\Omega_{n}(k)|^{\frac{p^{*}}{(p-1)(p^{*})^{\prime}}}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
fixed. We consider the distribution function of
u_{n}
defined as
\Theta_{n}(k)=|\Omega_{n}(k)|
for every
k\geq 0
. As
\Theta_{n}
is nonincreasing, thanks to relation (
3.23
) we can apply Stampacchia’s Lemma
3.6
with
k_{0}=\varepsilon
,
a=p^{*}
,
c=C_{4}
and
b=\frac{p^{*}}{(p-1)(p^{*})^{\prime}}=\frac{N(p-1)+p}{(p-1)(N-p)}>1
to obtain, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that

(3.24)

\Theta_{n}(\varepsilon+d_{n})=|\Omega_{n}(\varepsilon+d_{n})|=0,\text{ with }d_{n}^{a}=c2^{\frac{ab}{b-1}}|\Omega_{n}(\varepsilon)|^{b-1}.

Notice that, by the definition of
\Omega_{n}(k)
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\varepsilon|\Omega_{n}(\varepsilon)|\leq\int_{\Omega_{n}(\varepsilon)}u_{n}\leq\|u_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

Since
u_{n}
converges to 0 strongly in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, we can find
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
(depending on
\varepsilon
) such that
d_{n}<\varepsilon
for any
n\geq n_{0}
. As
\Theta_{n}
is nonincreasing, from (
3.24
) we deduce that
|\Omega_{n}(2\varepsilon)|=0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
or, equivalently, that

\|u_{n}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leq 2\varepsilon,\ \forall n\geq n_{0}.

Hence, we conclude that
u_{n}\to 0
strongly in
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
, which contradicts the fact that
\|u_{n}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

All the results established in this section reduce Theorem
1.2
to a mere corollary. Its proof is given below.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Thanks to Proposition
3.5
and Proposition
3.7
, it only remains for us to show that
\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)\to 0
as
L\to-\infty
. Given any
\overline{\lambda}>0
, by Proposition
3.3
we can always find some
\overline{L}\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for any
L<\overline{L}
and any
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
, a nonnegative solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
1.5
) with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in(\alpha,\beta]
exists. Then, by the definition of
\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)
, we have that

\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)\leq\overline{\lambda},\ \forall L<\overline{L}.

Thus, we obtain that
\overline{\lambda}_{\rm min}(L)\to 0
as
L\to-\infty
.
∎

To conclude, we fix
\lambda>0
and we show that maximal solutions to (
1.5
) in
[0,\beta]
exists when
L
is less than a constant. Furthermore, we analyse its behaviour as
L\to-\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\lambda>0
fixed. As a consequence of Proposition
3.3
, we can find some
L_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every
L<L_{\lambda}
problem (
1.5
) has a nonnegative solution
u_{L}\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
with
{C(\overline{\Omega})}
-norm in
(\alpha,\beta]
. Since
\beta
is a supersolution, by
[
4
, Theorem 4.2]
there is a maximal solution
\overline{u}_{L}\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
1.5
) in the interval
[0,\beta]
such that
u_{L}\leq\overline{u}_{L}\leq\beta
.

Finally, thanks to Proposition
3.3
, for any
\varepsilon>0
we can find some
\overline{L}<L_{\lambda}
such that
\|\overline{u}_{L}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in(\beta-\varepsilon,\beta]
for any
L<\overline{L}
. Then, we conclude that
\|\overline{u}_{L}\|_{{C(\overline{\Omega})}}\to\beta\text{ as }L\to-\infty
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

All the results in this section also hold when
Lg(u)
in problem (
3.1
) is replaced by
Lg_{1}(u)+g_{2}(u)
, where
g_{1}\in C(\mathbb{R})
is positive and
g_{2}\in C(\mathbb{R})
may change sign.

4.
Generalizations and further results

4.1.
A general divergence problem

All the results of this work also hold for the more general problem

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\operatorname{div}(a(u)|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)+g(u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfies
f(s)>0
for all
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
;
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
; and
a\in C(\mathbb{R})
is such that
a(s)>0
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that for
a\equiv 1
one recovers the usual
p
-Laplacian operator. Although the proofs are similar, problem (
4.1
) presents some differences with respect to (
1.1
). For (
4.1
), the function
\Psi
used for the change of variables (cf. (
2.2
)) is

\Psi(s)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}a(\eta)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}e^{-\frac{1}{p-1}\int_{0}^{\eta}\frac{g(\sigma)}{a(\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma}\ \mathrm{d}\eta,\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

The function
\tilde{f}
defined in (
2.4
) also changes. Indeed, Proposition
2.3
still holds for (
4.1
), but now, if
u
is a solution to (
4.1
), then
v=\Psi(u)
solves (
2.3
) with
\tilde{f}
given by

\tilde{f}(s)\coloneqq f\left(\Psi^{-1}(s)\right)e^{-\int_{0}^{\Psi^{-1}(s)}\frac{g(\sigma)}{a(\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma},\ \forall s\in\operatorname{Dom}\big(\Psi^{-1}\big).

As these two functions change, so does the area condition. Arguing as in Theorem
1.1
, one can show that the area condition for (
4.1
) is

(4.2)

\int_{s}^{\beta}f(\eta)a(\eta)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}e^{-\frac{p}{p-1}\int_{0}^{\eta}\frac{g(\sigma)}{a(\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma}\ \mathrm{d}\eta>0,\ \forall s\in[0,\beta).

Note that when
a\equiv 1
the condition obtained coincides with (
1.4
). The following result generalizes Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
a,g\in C(\mathbb{R})
with
a
positive. Then the following holds:

i)

If
f
verifies (
4.2
) and
f(0)\geq 0
, then there is some
\overline{\lambda}>0
such that, for every
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
, problem (
4.1
) has a nonnegative solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in(\alpha,\beta]
.

ii)

If
f
does not satisfy (
4.2
), then problem (
4.1
) admits no nonnegative solution with maximum in
[\alpha,\beta]
for any
\lambda>0
.

In the same way, Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
also hold for (
4.1
) if one includes a parameter
L
multiplying the gradient term. One of the keys is that, since
g/a
is positive (because
g
is assumed to be positive), the function
\int_{0}^{s}\frac{g(\eta)}{a(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta
is increasing for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
. This allows us to replicate all the arguments of Section
3
.

An important particular case of problem (
4.1
) is when
g=\frac{1}{p}a^{\prime}
. In this case, problem (
4.1
) has a variational structure and its associated Euler-Lagrange functional is

J(u)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}a(u)|\nabla u|^{p}-\int_{\Omega}F(u),\ \forall u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega),

where
F(s)=\int_{0}^{s}f(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
. This functional is well defined because, since we are only interested in solutions contained in
[0,\beta]
, one can assume without loss of generality that
a
is bounded. Here, the area condition obtained by substituting in (
4.2
) is

(4.3)

\int_{s}^{\beta}f(\eta)\ \mathrm{d}\eta>0,\ \forall s\in[0,\beta).

Therefore, in this case the usual area condition is recovered. In fact, assuming (
4.3
), the variational arguments of
[
8
, Lemma 2.1]
can be replicated to show the first part of Theorem
1.1
without making a change of variables.

In this family of variational problems, we also find the celebrated Schrödinger operator (
[
9
]
). In its most basic form, it takes the shape
Lu=-\Delta u-u\Delta(u^{2})
. For the
p
-Laplacian, one can consider its extension

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u-\frac{\kappa}{2}|u|^{\kappa-2}u\Delta_{p}(|u|^{\kappa})&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\kappa\geq 1+\frac{1}{p}
. This problem can be rewritten as (
4.1
) with
a(s)=1+\frac{\kappa^{p}}{2}|s|^{p(\kappa-1)}
and
g=\frac{1}{p}a^{\prime}
. Its associated functional is

J(u)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}\left(1+\frac{\kappa^{p}}{2}|u|^{p(\kappa-1)}\right)|\nabla u|^{p}-\int_{\Omega}F(u),\ \forall u\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega),

and the area condition is (
4.3
). Finally, we point out that our work generalizes some of the results of
[
13
]
, where the authors establish Theorem
1.1
for the classical Schrödinger operator
Lu=-\Delta u-u\Delta(u^{2})
.

4.2.
More general first order terms

Our results can be applied to establish the existence of solutions for non-autonomous problems. Consider the problem

(4.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u+\tilde{g}(x,u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

We stress that, for this problem, no change of variables is available. Assume that
\tilde{g}\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Carathéodory function (that is, measurable with respect to
x
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and continuous with respect to
s
for almost every
x\in\Omega
) such that

(4.5)

-M\leq\tilde{g}(x,s)\leq g(s),\ \forall(x,s)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R},

where
M>0
and
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
.

If
u
is a nonnegative solution to (
1.1
) with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in[\alpha,\beta]
, then
u
is a subsolution to (
4.4
). A sub-supersolution argument (
[
4
]
) with
\beta
as a supersolution yields to the existence of a solution
\tilde{u}
to (
4.4
) such that
u\leq\tilde{u}\leq\beta
. Hence, as a consequence of Theorem
1.1
, we can state the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
\tilde{g}(x,s)
be a Carathéodory function satisfying (
4.5
). If
f(s)
verifies (
1.4
) and
f(0)\geq 0
, then there is some
\overline{\lambda}>0
such that, for every
\lambda>\overline{\lambda}
, problem (
4.4
) has a nonnegative solution
\tilde{u}\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
with
\|\tilde{u}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\in(\alpha,\beta]
.

The regularizing effect is also present in this case. Let us introduce, as in (
1.5
), a parameter
L\in\mathbb{R}
multiplying the gradient term, so that we have

(4.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u+L\tilde{g}(x,u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

In this case, we assume the existence of
M_{1},M_{2}>0
such that

(4.7)

M_{1}\leq\tilde{g}(x,s)\leq M_{2},\ \forall(x,s)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}.

Observe that the maximal solutions to (
1.1
) with
g\equiv M_{1}
given by Theorem
1.3
are subsolutions to (
4.6
) provided
L<0
. Using again the sub-supersolution method (
[
4
]
) with
\beta
as supersolution, one obtains the following.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
\tilde{g}(x,s)
be a Carathéodory function satisfying (
4.7
). Given
\lambda>0
, there is some
L_{\lambda}<0
such that the maximal solution
\overline{u}_{L}
in the interval
[0,\beta]
to problem (
4.6
) exists for every
L\leq L_{\lambda}
, and

\|\overline{u}_{L}\|_{{C(\overline{\Omega})}}\to\beta\text{ as }L\to-\infty.

4.3.
Nonexistence of solutions with maximum equals to
\beta

In Theorem
1.1
, we establish the existence of nonnegative solutions
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
1.1
) whose maximum belongs to
(\alpha,\beta]
. Our aim here is to discuss conditions under which
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\neq\beta
.

As shown in Proposition
2.3
, problem (
1.1
) is closely related to the problem without the gradient term (
1.2
). If
f
satisfies

(4.8)

f(s)\leq M(\beta-s)^{p-1},\ \forall s\in[0,\beta],

for some
M>0
, or equivalently

(4.9)

\limsup_{s\to\beta^{-}}\frac{f(s)}{(\beta-s)^{p-1}}<\infty,

then the strong maximum principle precludes the existence of any solution
u
to (
1.2
) with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\beta
. Although we believe this result is well known, we include here its proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy (
4.8
) and let
\lambda>0
. Then there is no nonnegative solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
1.2
) with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\beta
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a nonnegative solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
1.2
) with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\beta
. Define
v\coloneq\beta-u
. Using (
4.8
), we obtain that
v\geq 0
satisfies the problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}v+\lambda Mv^{p-1}&\geq 0\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
v&\geq 0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Since
v\not\equiv 0
(because
v=\beta
in
\partial\Omega
), the strong maximum principle (
[
27
]
) implies that
v>0
in
\Omega
. This shows that
u<\beta
in
\overline{\Omega}
, contradicting
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\beta
.
∎

The extension of this result to problem (
1.1
) is not immediate. Taking advantage of Proposition
2.3
, the strategy is to relate
f
and
\tilde{f}
(defined in (
2.4
)) through a condition like (
4.9
), and then apply Lemma
4.4
to problem (
2.3
).

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
f,g\in C(\mathbb{R})
. If
f
satisfies (
4.8
), then there is no nonnegative solution
u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}
to (
1.1
) with
\|u\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\beta
.

Remark 4.6
.

When
1<p\leq 2
, condition (
4.8
) holds whenever
f(\beta)=0
and
f
is Lipschitz continuous in
[\beta-\delta,\beta]
for some
\delta>0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.3
, we are done if we prove that problem (
2.3
) has no nonnegative solution
{v\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}}
with
\|v\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}=\Psi(\beta)
. Thanks to Lemma
4.4
, it suffices to show that

(4.10)

\limsup_{s\to\Psi(\beta)^{-}}\frac{\tilde{f}(s)}{(\Psi(\beta)-s)^{p-1}}<\infty,

Taking into account the definition of
\tilde{f}
(see (
2.4
)) and performing a change of variables, this limit can be rewritten as

(4.11)

\limsup_{s\to\Psi(\beta)^{-}}\frac{\tilde{f}(s)}{(\Psi(\beta)-s)^{p-1}}=\limsup_{s\to\beta^{-}}\frac{f(s)e^{-G(s)}}{(\Psi(\beta)-\Psi(s))^{p-1}}=e^{-G(\beta)}\limsup_{s\to\beta^{-}}\frac{f(s)}{(\Psi(\beta)-\Psi(s))^{p-1}}.

Now, we use Taylor’s Theorem to write

\Psi(s)=\Psi(\beta)-\Psi^{\prime}(\beta)(\beta-s)+o(\beta-s)=\Psi(\beta)-e^{-\frac{1}{p-1}G(\beta)}(\beta-s)+o(\beta-s),\ \forall s\in[\beta-\delta,\beta],

where
\delta>0
and
o(\beta-s)
is such that
\frac{o(\beta-s)}{\beta-s}\to 0
as
s\to\beta
. Thus, we obtain that

(4.12)

\begin{split}\limsup_{s\to\beta^{-}}\frac{f(s)}{(\Psi(\beta)-\Psi(s))^{p-1}}&=\limsup_{s\to\beta^{-}}\frac{f(s)}{\left(e^{-\frac{1}{p-1}G(\beta)}(\beta-s)-o(\beta-s)\right)^{p-1}}\\
&=\limsup_{s\to\beta^{-}}\frac{f(s)}{(\beta-s)^{p-1}}\left(\frac{\beta-s}{e^{-\frac{1}{p-1}G(\beta)}(\beta-s)-o(\beta-s)}\right)^{p-1}\\[5.69054pt]
&=e^{G(\beta)}\limsup_{s\to\beta^{-}}\frac{f(s)}{(\beta-s)^{p-1}}.\end{split}

Joining (
4.11
) and (
4.12
), we deduce that

\limsup_{s\to\Psi(\beta)^{-}}\frac{\tilde{f}(s)}{(\Psi(\beta)-s)^{p-1}}=\limsup_{s\to\beta^{-}}\frac{f(s)}{(\beta-s)^{p-1}}.

Since (
4.9
) holds (because it is equivalent to (
4.8
)), it follows that (
4.10
) also holds, as desired.
∎

Finally, we note that when
f(s)\approx C(\beta-s)^{k-1}
near
s=\beta
for some
0<k<p-1
, the strong maximum principle does not apply and solutions to (
1.1
) having a flat core may appear. These are solutions
{u\in{C^{1}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})}}
such that
\{x\in\Omega:u(x)=\beta\}\neq\emptyset
. We refer the reader to
[
15
,
18
]
for a more detailed explanation of this phenomenon.

4.4.
Regularizing effect as
p\to 1^{+}

Some of the results proved in Section
3
remain valid if we fix
L
and we tend
p
to 1. Observe that the functions
\Psi_{L}(s)
(defined in (
3.7
)) and
H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s,L)
(defined in (
3.8
)) can be rewritten as

\Psi_{p,L}(s)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}e^{-C_{1}(p,L)G(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta,\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R},

and

H_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(s,p,L)\coloneqq\int_{s}^{\gamma_{2}}f(\eta)e^{-C_{2}(p,L)G(\eta)}\ \mathrm{d}\eta,\ \forall(s,L)\in[0,\gamma_{1}]\times\mathbb{R},

where
C_{1}(p,L)=\frac{1}{p-1}L
and
C_{2}(p,L)=\frac{p}{p-1}L
. The key is that if
L<0
, then
C_{1}(p,L)
and
C_{2}(p,L)
tend to
-\infty
as
p\to 1^{+}
; whereas if
L>0
, then
C_{1}(p,L)
and
C_{2}(p,L)
tend to
\infty
as
p\to 1^{+}
. This allows us to reproduce some of the arguments of Section
3
.

If we fix
L=-1
, then the following regularizing effect appears. The proof is analogous to that of Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
be positive. Given
\lambda>0
, there is some
p_{\lambda}>1
such that the maximal solution
\overline{u}_{p}
in the interval
[0,\beta]
to problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u&=g(u)|\nabla u|^{p}+\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

exists for every
p\in(1,p_{\lambda}]
, and

\|\overline{u}_{p}\|_{{C(\overline{\Omega})}}\to\beta\text{ as }p\to 1^{+}.

On the other hand, when
L=1
and
f
changes sign in
[0,\beta]
, tending
p
to 1 causes the area condition (
1.4
) to be lost. Reasoning as in Proposition
3.5
, we obtain the following.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(s)>0
for
s\in(\alpha,\beta)
, where
0<\alpha<\beta
are two zeros of
f
, and let
g\in C(\mathbb{R})
be positive. If
f
changes sign in
[0,\beta]
, there is some
\tilde{p}>1
such that problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{p}u+g(u)|\nabla u|^{p}&=\lambda f(u)\quad&&\mbox{in}\;\;\Omega,\\
u&=0\quad&&\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

has no nonnegative solution with maximum in
[\alpha,\beta]
for any
p\in(1,\tilde{p}]
and any
\lambda>0
.
