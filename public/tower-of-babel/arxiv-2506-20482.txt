Rabi oscillations with close-range quantum vortex states
Sam Patrick

Abstract
Quantum vortices separated through distances much larger than their core size interact via their long-range velocity field. At smaller separations, however, the influence of the core’s compressibility strongly influences the vortex dynamics. Using the example of a compact ring of five vortices, it is shown that close-range effects lead to a new (slower) state of orbital motion which is not predicted by the usual long-range theory. This secondary state can be created starting from the usual orbital state by modulating the interaction strength to induce Rabi oscillations between the states.



Introduction.
—Vortices are key components of a wide variety of systems, examples of which include the ocean and atmosphere, optical devices, superconductors and neutron stars

;

;

;

In quantum fluids (such as atomic Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs)

, superfluid
4
He

\Psi
with quantised circulation
\oint d\bm{l}\cdot\mathbf{v}=2\pi\hbar\ell/M
, where
\mathbf{v}=\bm{\nabla}\mathrm{arg}(\Psi)
is the fluid velocity,
M
is the mass of the condensate particles, and
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
is the number of times the phase of

The existence of quantum vortices (QVs) in these systems gives rise to important phenomena such as mutual friction

Ryzhov et al. 2017
and quantum turbulence in 2D
Neely et al. 2013
and 3D
Kozik and Svistunov 2009

At low energy, the separation between QVs is much larger than the size of the vortex core.
Hence, the core structure is neglected and QVs are treated as infinitesimal filaments (in 3D) or points (in 2D) interacting only through their long-range velocity field.
In 2D, the corresponding model is known as the point-vortex (PV) model which, without dissipation, takes the form,

\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbf{v}_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(1)

where
\mathbf{v}_{j}
is the velocity field of the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
vortex whose centre is at the position
\mathbf{x}_{j}(t)
, overdot denotes time differentiation and a physical boundary can be accounted for by including image vortices.
Eq. (
1
) states that each vortex simply moves with a velocity given by summing up the velocity fields of all the others.
Although the PV model captures the salient features of the vortex dynamics, it can fail quantitatively in simple scenarios where the fluid compressibility (density variations) cannot be neglected
Groszek et al. 2018
.
Furthermore, the model does not account for important phenomena arising for vortices at close-range, i.e. when the inter-vortex distance becomes small.
Indeed, Eq. (
1

;
Barenghi et al. 2001
.

QV splitting instabilities are another crucial effect in this category, since they cause QVs with

There is a rich literature on QV splitting patterns, especially in the context of atomic BECs.
The simplest case involves the splitting of
\ell=2

At higher

The

Experiments on

In holographic superfluids (studied using the AdS/CFT correspondence) temperature is an additional control parameter

Further novelties can be observed in two-component BECs when the species are miscible, including
\ell=1

\ell>1

Overall, there is a huge interest in understanding the splitting of large QVs and the ensuing dynamics.
In most cases discussed above, the vortex motion following the splitting is captured by Eq. (
1

The purpose of letter is to illustrate that a new class of vortex states (with no counterpart in the long-range PV theory) can arise for QVs at close-range, and that these states can be accessed starting from the usual states by modulating the interaction strength to induce Rabi oscillations.

Vortex states.
—We consider the (dissipative) Gross-Pitaevskii equation (GPE),

i\hbar\partial_{t}\Psi=(1-i\gamma)\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}\nabla^{2}+V(\mathbf{x})+g|\Psi|^{2}-\mu\right)\Psi,

(2)

describing the condensed fraction of a cold atomic gas, e.g
23
Na or
87
Rb
Shin et al. 2004
;
Okano et al. 2007
, near
T=0\,\mathrm{K}
.
Variants of the GPE describe polariton condensates and quantum fluids of light
Carusotto and Ciuti 2013
.
We assume a quasi-2D condensate in the
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)
plane, with tight confinement in the vertical direction.

V(\mathbf{x})
is the trapping potential,
g
is the interaction strength,
\mu
is the chemical potential and
\gamma
is a phenomenological dissipation parameter
Cockburn and Proukakis 2009
.
The system is described by the condensate wavefunction
\Psi=\sqrt{\bar{n}}e^{i\Phi}
, where
\bar{n}
is the number density of atoms in the condensate which move with velocity
\mathbf{v}=\frac{\hbar}{M}\bm{\nabla}\Phi
.
The ratio of the nonlinear term to the kinetic term defines the healing length
\xi=\hbar/\sqrt{M\mu}
.
The speed of sound (density waves) in the uniform state
\bar{n}=\mu/g
is given by
c=\sqrt{\mu/M}
.
In what follows we set
\hbar,M,\mu=1
, which amounts to measuring distance and time in units of
\xi
and
\xi/c
respectively.

Eq. (
2
) has stationary vortex solutions given by
\Phi=\ell\theta
, where
\ell
is the winding number mentioned in the introduction and
\mathbf{x}=(r,\theta)
are polar coordinates. We fix
\ell>0
without loss of generality and consider a vortex at the centre of a circular trapping potential with a hard wall at
r=r_{B}
. The density
\bar{n}(r)
(determined numerically by evolving (
2
) in imaginary time) vanishes exponentially outside
r_{B}
and scales as
\bar{n}{\sim}r^{2\ell}
inside the vortex core, which has characteristic width
\ell\xi
.
When the vortex is excited, the full order parameter becomes
\Psi=e^{i\ell\theta}(\sqrt{\bar{n}}+\psi)
.
Small excitations can be expanded in modes
\psi=\sum_{m}(u_{m}e^{im\theta}+v_{m}^{*}e^{-im\theta})
with
m\in(-\infty,\infty)
, where
u_{m}
and
v^{*}_{m}
are the positive and negative frequency components of
\psi
respectively. These obey the linear equation,

\begin{split}&i\partial_{t}|\psi_{m}\rangle=\mathcal{L}|\psi_{m}\rangle,\quad\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{D}_{+}&g\bar{n}\\
-g\bar{n}&-\mathcal{D}_{-}\end{pmatrix},\\
&\mathcal{D}_{\pm}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}_{r}+\frac{(\ell\pm m)^{2}}{2r^{2}}+2g\bar{n}+V-1,\end{split}

(3)

where
|\psi_{m}\rangle=(u_{m},v_{m})^{\mathrm{T}}
.
In the stationary state,
|\psi_{m}\rangle=\sum_{n}a_{n}e^{-i\omega_{n}t}|\psi_{mn}\rangle
where
a_{n}
are the mode amplitudes and
n\in[0,\infty)
indexes the different eigenmodes.
These are normalised in the inner product,

\langle\psi_{\lambda}|\psi_{\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}\equiv\int d^{2}\mathbf{x}(u_{\lambda}^{*}\ v_{\lambda}^{*})\sigma_{3}\begin{pmatrix}u_{\lambda^{\prime}}\\
v_{\lambda^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}},

(4)

where
\sigma_{3}
is the third Pauli matrix,
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}=\pm 1
is the sign of the norm, and we write
\lambda=(m,n)
for shorthand.
The eigenfrequencies
\omega_{\lambda}
are determined by the eigenvalue equation
\omega_{\lambda}|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle=\mathcal{L}|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle
and fall into two categories.
Modes which are localised in the vortex core (with
\omega^{\mathrm{vor}}
) encode the vortex dynamics, whereas those which extend outside the vortex core are sound waves (phonons with
\omega^{\mathrm{ph}}
).
If
\omega^{\mathrm{vor}}
and
\omega^{\mathrm{ph}}
are sufficiently close, they hybridise to produce a dynamical instability describing the transfer of energy between the central vortex and a surrounding phonon
Patrick et al. 2022b
;
Patrick et al. 2022a
.
By selecting a small value of
r_{B}
, this instability can be avoided since
\omega^{\mathrm{ph}}\sim r_{B}^{-1}
at low frequency whilst
\omega^{\mathrm{vor}}
is roughly
r_{B}
independent.

In the following, we will be primarily interested in the dynamics of the vortex modes.
Those with
m>0
have
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}=-1
and describe negative energy excitations, that is, states which lower the total energy of the vortex.
The different
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}=-1
states correspond to distinct configurations of vortices as the cluster loses energy.
They are approximately determined (when the density varies smoothly) by a resonance condition
\cos S(\omega^{\mathrm{vor}}_{\lambda})=0
, where
S
is the phase integral of the mode over the vortex core (see
Patrick et al. 2022b
;
Patrick et al. 2022a
for details).
S
increases with the size of the vortex core, hence there will be an increasing number of states for larger
\ell
. In Fig.
1
(a), we plot the zeros of
\cos S(\omega^{\mathrm{vor}}_{\lambda})
for
\ell=2,3,4,5

In general, there are negative energy vortex modes in the band
2\leq m\leq 2\ell-2

For
\ell=2
, the only such mode occurs for
m=2
and encodes the splitting into two vortices with
\ell=1
, whereas for
\ell=3
, there are states for
m=2,3,4
, corresponding to a linear/triangular/square arrangement of
\ell=1

\ell=-1
vortex at

.
A new phenomenon occurs for
\ell=4
, namely, the occurrence of a secondary (lower frequency) mode with
m=4

\ell
.
These secondary modes become more numerous for larger
\ell

\ell=5
possesses secondary modes for
m=4,5,6
.
From here on, we focus on the case
\ell=m=5
due to numerical artefacts when working with a fourfold symmetric Cartesian grid.

The secondary modes correspond to a distinct state of orbital motion with no counterpart in the PV dynamics.

For the interaction parameter, we take

A small amount of damping (

Since the PV orbital frequency smoothly merges with

Conversely, a seed secondary mode does not cause splitting and the vortex cores remain overlapping (see Fig.

Any attempt to push the secondary mode to larger vortex separations (i.e. increasing the seed amplitude

The secondary mode appears, therefore, to be a unique feature of close-range vortex dynamics without a counterpart in the long-range (PV) theory.

Exciting the secondary mode.
—Since the primary mode is more unstable, it is natural to wonder whether there is any realistic scenario where the secondary mode can be observed. We show now that the secondary mode can be accessed starting from the primary by modulating one of the system parameters at a frequency
\Omega=\zeta(\omega^{\mathrm{vor}}_{\lambda_{p}}-\omega^{\mathrm{vor}}_{\lambda_{s}})
, where
\zeta
is close to
1
, to induce Rabi oscillations between the states. We choose to vary the interaction parameter,

g(t)=1+\delta g,\qquad\delta g=\delta g_{0}\sin\Omega t,

(5)

which could be achieved with a Feshbach resonance
Timmermans et al. 1999
. Another option would be to work with a harmonic trapping potential and modulate the trap frequency.
The following treatment shares similarities with
Caradoc-Davies et al. 1999
, where a periodic stirring potential was used to drive nonlinear transitions between the ground state and single vortex state.

The first effect of this modulation is to alter background (symmetric) vortex state.
Provided
|\delta g_{0}|\ll 1
, this shift can be treated as a linear perturbation which obeys,

i\partial_{t}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\mathcal{L}|\psi_{0}\rangle+\delta g(t)\bar{n}^{3/2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\end{pmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}},

(6)

with
|\psi_{0}\rangle=(\psi_{0}\ \psi_{0}^{*})^{\mathrm{T}}
and
\psi_{0}=\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}(\Psi e^{-i\ell\theta}-\sqrt{\bar{n}})
.
This
\psi_{0}
will modulate the parameters in the
m\neq 0
equation, which to linear order in
\delta g_{0}
is,

\begin{split}&i\partial_{t}|\psi_{m}\rangle=(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L})|\psi_{m}\rangle,\quad\delta\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
-B^{*}&-A^{*}\end{pmatrix},\\
&A=2\bar{n}\delta g+4\sqrt{\bar{n}}\mathrm{Re}[\psi_{0}],\quad B=\bar{n}\delta g+2\sqrt{\bar{n}}\psi_{0}.\end{split}

(7)

All the
\omega_{\lambda}
are assumed real, which occurs provided we don’t have a dynamical instability (i.e. for small trap sizes as explained above).
Applying basic techniques, the state is projected onto the basis of the linear equations
|\psi_{m}\rangle=\sum_{n}a_{\lambda}(t)e^{-i\omega_{\lambda}t}|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle
with amplitudes
a_{\lambda}
which vary slowly relative to
\omega_{\lambda}^{-1}
(recall that
\lambda=(m,n)
).
Since the background is symmetric in
\theta
and Eq. (
7
) is linear, the different
m
do not couple.
We therefore work with a single mode (
m=5
in our case) and drop the index
m
.
The equation for each mode amplitude becomes,

i\mathcal{N}_{n}\dot{a}_{n}=\sum_{n^{\prime}}\rho_{nn^{\prime}}(t)e^{i(\omega_{n}-\omega_{n^{\prime}})t},

(8)

where
\rho_{nn^{\prime}}(t)=\langle\psi_{n}|\delta\mathcal{L}|\psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}
.
We now make the simplifying assumption that the system behaves as a two level system, which is satisfied in the usual way provided the background modulation
\Omega
is close to the level difference.
We take the two levels to be the primary and secondary vortex modes, which for small traps (
r_{B}\lesssim 10.5
) correspond to
n=1
and
n=0
respectively.
If modulations in
g
are switched on abruptly,
\delta\mathcal{L}
will contain multiple frequencies.
There will be a dominant component at
\omega=\pm\Omega
and subdominant components at the
m=0
eigenfrequecies, provided
\Omega
is not on resonance with these.
Focussing on only the dominant contributions, we can approximate
\rho_{nn^{\prime}}\simeq\tilde{\rho}^{+}_{nn^{\prime}}e^{-i\Omega t}+\tilde{\rho}^{-}_{nn^{\prime}}e^{i\Omega t}
, where
\tilde{\rho}^{\pm}_{nn^{\prime}}
are the Fourier components of
\rho_{nn^{\prime}}
at
\omega=\pm\Omega
.
Writing
a_{n}(t)=b_{n}(t)\exp(i\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{nn}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime})
, the
b_{n}
obey,

\begin{split}i\dot{b}_{0}=&\ -\tilde{\rho}^{+}_{01}e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}\Delta^{+}dt^{\prime}}b_{1}-\tilde{\rho}^{-}_{01}e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}\Delta^{-}dt^{\prime}}b_{1},\\
i\dot{b}_{1}=&\ -\tilde{\rho}^{+}_{10}e^{i\int_{0}^{t}\Delta^{-}dt^{\prime}}b_{0}-\tilde{\rho}^{-}_{10}e^{i\int_{0}^{t}\Delta^{+}dt^{\prime}}b_{0},\end{split}

(9)

with
\Delta^{\pm}=\omega_{1}-\omega_{0}-\rho_{11}+\rho_{00}\pm\Omega
.
The terms
\rho_{00}
and
\rho_{11}
are oscillatory and small compared to the remaining terms which lead to secular evolution of the phase.
Neglecting them, the
\Delta^{\pm}
become constants.
Furthermore, in the rotating wave approximation (RWA), the terms containing
\Delta^{+}
oscillate much more rapidly than the ones with
\Delta^{-}
and tend to average out.
Hence, writing
\tilde{\rho}\equiv\tilde{\rho}^{-}_{01}=\tilde{\rho}^{+*}_{10}
and
\Delta\equiv\Delta^{-}
, the equations become,

i\dot{b}_{0}=-\tilde{\rho}e^{-i\Delta t}b_{1},\qquad i\dot{b}_{1}=-\tilde{\rho}^{*}e^{i\Delta t}b_{0}.

(10)

The solution for an initial amplitude
b_{1}(0)=\alpha
in the primary mode and
b_{0}(0)=0
in the secondary is,

\begin{split}b_{1}(t)=&\ e^{\frac{i\Delta t}{2}}\alpha\left(\cos\kappa t-\frac{i\Delta}{2\kappa}\sin\kappa t\right),\\
b_{0}(t)=&\ e^{-\frac{i\Delta t}{2}}\frac{i\tilde{\rho}\alpha}{\kappa}\sin\kappa t,\quad\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+4|\tilde{\rho}|^{2}}}{2}.\end{split}

(11)

These describe out-of-phase oscillations with period
2\pi/\kappa
of the mode occupation numbers.
Using
\Omega=\zeta(\omega_{1}-\omega_{0})
, we have
\Delta=(\zeta-1)(\omega_{1}-\omega_{0})
.
Hence, when the parameter
\zeta
is close to 1, the primary mode amplitude
|b_{1}|
can be brought close to zero at
t=\pi/2\kappa
, where the secondary mode amplitude
|b_{0}|
reaches a maximum of around
|\alpha|
.

\kappa
.
Crucially, optimal conversion of the primary into the secondary mode is achieved when
\Omega
matches the level difference
\omega_{1}-\omega_{0}
.

Discussion.
—We have demonstrated the existence of new (secondary) states of vortex motion not captured by the point-vortex model in Eq. (
1
). It was shown that these states can be excited by modulating the interaction strength to induce Rabi oscillations starting from the usual (primary) states of vortex motion, which are extensions of the dynamics in Eq. (
1
) to small inter-vortex distances.
The main feature which led to these new states is the widened vortex core at large winding numbers
|\ell|
.
These states may be relevant for the dynamics of larger vortices in a variety of scenarios beyond the simple Gross-Pitaevskii model we have studied here, e.g. thin superfluid
4
He films
Sachkou et al. 2019
, exciton-polariton condensates
Alperin and Berloff 2021
, intertype superconductors
Vagov et al. 2016
and superconductor-ferromagnet structures
Aladyshkin et al. 2009
.

In atomic BECs, the splitting patterns which have received most attention are the ones corresponding to the primary states.
By contrast, the secondary states (which have lower frequencies) seem to be a consequence of overlapping vortex cores and do not extend to the regime of large vortex separations.
For giant vortices (with even larger
\ell
) an increasing number of these modes per
m
can fit inside the vortex core.
A better understanding of them could therefore lead to improved modelling of compact vortex clusters in a wide variety of systems
Stockdale et al. 2020
;
Alperin and Berloff 2021
;
Geelmuyden et al. 2022
;
Delhom et al. 2024
;
Patrick 2024
;
Švančara et al. 2024
.

Whilst we have focussed on
\ell=5
(for numerical purposes), the secondary mode first appears for
\ell=4
with
m=4
and
\omega\sim 0.1c/\xi
.

\ell=4
vortices have been the subject of various experimental studies
Okano et al. 2007
;
Isoshima et al. 2007
;
Kuwamoto et al. 2010
;
Shibayama et al. 2016
, firstly, because they are large enough that the competition of multiple unstable modes leads to rich dynamics and, secondly, since they can be induced by reversing the trap’s magnetic field
Kumakura et al. 2006
.
Future experiments could therefore study these secondary states using the Rabi oscillation technique suggested here, either by modulating the interaction strength with a Feshbach resonance or the trap size.
It is feasible that a similar Rabi oscillation technique could also induce transitions between the threefold and fourfold symmetric splitting patterns of the
\ell=4
vortex, where the latter is difficult to access due to its slow decay
Shibayama et al. 2016
;
Lan et al. 2023b
.
Secondary states may also be relevant for dynamics of vortex filaments in 3D, for example, in the turbulent cascade induced by the decay of
\ell=4
vortices
Cidrim et al. 2017
.

Close-range effects may also be relevant for the statistical behaviour of large numbers of vortices – so-called vortex gases.
In kinetic theories of vortices, the system can become frozen in a metastable state before reaching statistical equilibrium if only two-body collisions are considered, and collisions involving
\geq 3
vortices are required to unfreeze the system on longer timescales
Chavanis 2001
;
Chavanis 2012
.
Furthermore, three-body collisions are known to play an important role in the asymptotic scaling of the vortex density when it becomes small at late times
Sire and Chavanis 2000
;
Sire et al. 2011
, highlighting the importance of collisions between multiple vortices.
These studies, typically based on the PV model, need to implement ad hoc rules which take effect at close-range
Carnevale et al. 1991
.
In quantum fluids, vortex clustering occurs when suitable forcing, e.g. mechanical stirring, is used to overcome the effects of dipole decay
Reeves et al. 2013
;
Gauthier et al. 2019
, in which case the inverse cascade can become the dominant mechanism in energy transport
Bradley and Anderson 2012
, even leading to the formation of point-like giant vortex structures in the high energy limit
Yu et al. 2016
.
Compact clusters are also relevant when there is a large vortex in the initial condition
Stockdale et al. 2020
, and for quantum fluids with localised heating
Sachkou et al. 2019
or particle losses
Delhom et al. 2024
.
Dynamics beyond the PV model, such as secondary states or superpositions of primary states, may then arise as transient configurations during collisions involving
\geq 3
vortices.
Formally, the linear theory used in this work is valid when the intervortex distance goes to zero, whereas the PV model holds as this quantity tends to infinity.
Developing models that connect these two distinct regimes could therefore improve modelling of vortex dynamics across a wide scope of physical scenarios.
