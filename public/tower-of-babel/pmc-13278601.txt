Simulation-based training: Assessment of impact on medical students’ performance, confidence in CPR, and patient outcomes – A single-center study
Authors: J Rizwana Fathima, K Senthil Kumar, T Aarthi, K Gaayathri, Saandhiya Raja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is an essential life-saving procedure. Traditional training methods may not sufficiently prepare medical students for real-world emergencies. Simulation-based training offers a high-fidelity, immersive approach to teaching CPR skills. To assess the effectiveness of simulation-based CPR training on medical students’ performance and confidence, and to evaluate its impact on real patient outcomes.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A single-center, prospective observational study was conducted at Chettinad Academy of Research and Education (CARE) from January 2022 to June 2024. A total of 1000 medical students underwent AHA-certified BLS and ACLS training. Performance was assessed pre- and post-training using standardized AHA checklists. Student confidence was measured using structured surveys developed by the Department of Community Medicine. Patient outcome data from 103 real-life resuscitation cases involving these students were analyzed.RESULTS:Post-training, mean BLS and ACLS scores improved from 65% to 85% and 60% to 80%, respectively. Significant gains were observed in chest compression (70% to 90%), ventilation (60% to 85%), defibrillation (55% to 80%), and protocol adherence (60% to 85%). Student confidence rose from 40% to 85%. Patient outcomes improved notably, with return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) rates increasing from 60% to 78%.CONCLUSION:Simulation-based CPR training significantly enhanced both skill performance and confidence among medical students, and contributed to improved patient survival during clinical resuscitation events.

Introduction

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is a critical life-saving intervention and remains a cornerstone in the management of cardiac arrest. Timely and high-quality CPR has been directly linked to improved survival and neurological outcomes in both in-hospital and out-of-hospital settings.[1] Consequently, training medical professionals to deliver effective CPR is a key component of undergraduate medical education.

Traditional methods of CPR training, often based on didactic lectures and low-fidelity mannequins, may not sufficiently prepare students for the high-pressure dynamics of real-world resuscitation scenarios.[2] These methods lack realism, emotional stress, and hands-on exposure, which can lead to poor retention of skills and low confidence in clinical settings.[3]

Simulation-based medical education has emerged as an innovative and evidence-based approach to overcome these limitations.[4] By replicating clinical environments with high- or medium-fidelity manikins and structured feedback systems, simulation creates a safe yet realistic platform for practicing critical procedures. This study was designed to evaluate the effectiveness of AHA-guided CPR simulation training on the technical performance and confidence of undergraduate medical students, and its potential correlation with patient outcomes, particularly the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).

To evaluate the effectiveness of American Heart Association (AHA)-certified BLS and ACLS training in improving undergraduate medical students’ CPR performance, self-reported confidence, and associated real-life patient outcomes in clinical resuscitation events.

The primary outcome was the improvement in CPR performance scores among medical students, measured through standardized AHA checklists administered before and after the training. Secondary outcomes included changes in self-reported confidence, domain-specific skill performance (e.g., chest compressions, ventilation, defibrillation), and patient-related outcomes such as return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) in real-life resuscitation events.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, observational study was conducted at the Chettinad Academy of Research and Education (CARE), Tamil Nadu, India, over a 30-month period from January 2022 to June 2024. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating students prior to inclusion.

Study participants and sampling

Sample size calculation

Drawing on an article published by Silva et al. (2023), who used approximately 20 participants per group to detect a 0.6 SD difference with 80% power (α = 0.05), we applied the paired t-test formula:

n = [(Z₁₋α/2 + Z1-β)/(Δ/σₑ)]² = [(1.96 + 0.84)/0.6]² ≈22

Therefore, 22 participants per group would suffice. However, acknowledging the broader aims of subgroup analysis and real-world variability, we enrolled 1000 medical students, ensuring high statistical power and generalizability.

Sampling method

A convenience sampling technique was employed to recruit participants. All eligible undergraduate medical students undergoing scheduled AHA-certified Basic Life Support (BLS) and Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) training at Chettinad Academy of Research and Education (CARE) between January 2022 and June 2024 were included. No randomization or exclusion criteria were applied beyond training eligibility, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of training impact across the full student cohort.

A total of 1000 undergraduate medical students from CARE were enrolled in the study. Inclusion criteria were:

Completion of AHA-certified Basic Life Support (BLS) and Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) provider coursesConsent to participate in both the training and follow-up assessmentsParticipation in clinical resuscitation efforts during rotations.

Patient outcomes and clinical correlation

To evaluate real-world impact, clinical data were retrospectively reviewed for 103 resuscitation events in which trained students actively participated. Data were sourced from Emergency Department, ICU, and General Ward case records between 2022 and 2024. To reduce observer bias during clinical performance assessments—particularly in real-life resuscitation scenarios—evaluations were conducted by faculty physicians who were blinded to the students’ pre- and post-training status. These evaluators used a standardized checklist during each code event without knowledge of which students had completed simulation training. Additionally, the assessors were not involved in delivering the training, thereby maintaining objectivity. Case records and outcome data were anonymized prior to review to further minimize potential bias.

Outcome measures included:

Ethical consideration

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of CARE. Written informed consent was obtained from all student participants prior to their inclusion in the study. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained during all phases of data collection and analysis. The use of AI in this study has been very limited; iThenticate was used to measure plagiarism, and Canva was used to generate figures.

Statistical analysis method

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS Version 26.0. Pre- and post-training scores were compared using paired t-tests, with a significance level set at P < 0.05. Confidence levels were compared using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Categorical outcomes like ROSC and protocol adherence were analyzed using Chi-square tests. For all key outcomes, 95% confidence intervals were calculated to provide estimates of effect size precision, alongside p values. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study evaluated the impact of AHA-certified simulation-based CPR training on undergraduate medical students’ performance, self-reported confidence, and observed patient outcomes. Quantitative and qualitative analyses revealed significant improvement across all measured parameters.

Discussion

This study provides robust evidence that simulation-based training, when implemented in alignment with established protocols such as those from the American Heart Association (AHA), significantly improves undergraduate medical students’ CPR performance, confidence, and contribution to patient outcomes. Our findings are consistent with the growing consensus that traditional didactic or low-fidelity CPR training alone is insufficient for long-term retention and real-life applicability of resuscitation skills.[5]

The observed 20% increase in BLS and ACLS scores across all domains is in line with findings from Issenberg et al., who noted that high-quality simulation with feedback is among the most effective modalities for enhancing clinical skills.[6] Our data corroborate this, especially in areas like chest compression quality, where student performance improved from 70% to 90%, similar to the results reported by Wayne et al. in internal medicine residents.[2]

Moreover, improvements in defibrillation timing and ventilation technique echo the results of studies by Perkins et al. and Hansen et al., both of which emphasized the benefits of immersive, scenario-based learning in strengthening psychomotor skills and clinical decision-making under pressure.[47] These enhancements are not just statistically significant—they reflect real-world readiness to respond to cardiac emergencies.

A notable contribution of this study is the substantial increase in student confidence, with 85% of participants reporting high confidence post-training compared to 25% pre-training. This mirrors findings from Sawyer et al., who introduced the LSPPDM (Learn, See, Practice, Prove, Do, Maintain) model as a foundation for building self-efficacy in clinical learners.[8] As Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory posits, confidence (self-efficacy) is a major driver of performance in high-stakes scenarios.[9] Our qualitative feedback affirms this, with students noting increased emotional readiness to initiate resuscitation and lead teams.

Critically, our study moves beyond the classroom by exploring real-life clinical outcomes. Among 103 in-hospital cardiac arrests were trained students participated; ROSC was achieved in 78% of cases, an 18% improvement over baseline. While causality remains multifactorial and difficult to prove, our findings support the meta-analysis by Cheng et al., which concluded that simulation training improves both adherence to resuscitation guidelines and short-term patient outcomes.[10] These findings have been echoed in more recent reviews emphasizing the correlation between simulation exposure and increased ROSC and survival rates in clinical settings.[11]

Protocol adherence improved dramatically in our study (from 45% to 82%), consistent with results from Nishiyama et al., who found that simulation-trained learners were more likely to follow algorithmic sequences and maintain CPR quality.[12] Similarly, Edelson et al. demonstrated that simulation-trained teams initiate defibrillation faster, a finding that our study replicates-time to defibrillation within 2 minutes improved from 38% to 70%.[13] These time-sensitive metrics are strongly correlated with improved survival and neurologic outcomes, which are key benchmarks for modern health policy frameworks and emergency care accreditation.

Beyond technical skills, simulation proved valuable in cultivating non-technical competencies such as communication, teamwork, and leadership. This holistic training is essential for code situations, where shared mental models and effective team coordination are paramount. Fernandez Castelao et al. and Hunziker et al. emphasized that team-based simulation enhances situational awareness and crisis resource management—benefits echoed by our participants.[1415] More recent studies have further validated this by demonstrating improved team dynamics and task execution under stress in simulation-trained cohorts.[16]

A study by Brazilian researchers using a just-in-time teaching approach for BLS training among healthcare students found significant knowledge improvement immediately after training, with substantial retention even after one month.[17] This supports our findings of sustained skill acquisition and protocol adherence among students post-simulation. A study done by Chellaiyan et al. emphasized the impact of the educational environment in assessing the perception of medical students regarding their learning environment and academic performance.[20]

Our findings align with competency-based medical education (CBME) frameworks, where clinical readiness, communication, and confidence are key indicators of preparedness. Furthermore, the outcomes resonate with the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) and European Resuscitation Council’s 2021 guidelines, which endorse simulation-based learning as essential to high-quality resuscitation training and workforce development.[1618] Policy-makers and accreditation bodies should thus view simulation not as an optional educational tool but as a strategic requirement for patient safety and emergency preparedness.

Silva et al. (2023) conducted a randomized, prospective study comparing three instructional approaches for first-semester medical students: dialogic expository lecture, expository with demonstration, and these two combined with simulated practice. The results showed a notable 20 % increase in theoretical knowledge across all groups, with 80 % retention at the 3-month checkpoint. Crucially, the group that experienced simulated practice outperformed the others (P = 0.007), maintaining a mean posttest performance of 90 %—though retention for infant BLS skills dipped by 10 %. Participants also rated the training highly, reinforcing the effectiveness of simulation-based methods for durable BLS education.[19] This was in accordance with our study.

The outcomes of this study can be further classified using Kirkpatrick’s Four Levels of Training Evaluation, which provide a structured framework for analyzing educational effectiveness. Level 1 (Reaction) was reflected in students’ positive feedback and perceived training relevance. Perception of the educational environment by the student as a study was done previously in our institute. 20 This helps us asses gaps in knowledge obtained by the students. Level 2 (Learning) was evidenced by the significant improvements in CPR performance scores post-training. Level 3 (Behavior) was demonstrated by enhanced confidence and observed application of skills in real-time clinical scenarios. Finally, Level 4 (Results) was captured through improved patient outcomes, including return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), timely defibrillation, and survival to ICU transfer. This multi-level alignment reinforces the robust impact of simulation-based CPR education on learner competence and patient care.

This study lacked a randomized control group, limiting the ability to establish causality. Patient outcomes may have been influenced by external clinical factors beyond student performance. The retrospective nature of resuscitation data introduces potential observer and selection bias. Long-term retention of CPR skills and impact on survival to discharge were not assessed. Observer bias during real-life performance assessments cannot be fully excluded, although efforts were made to standardize evaluations. Additionally, the use of medium-fidelity manikins may not fully replicate real patient anatomy or stress conditions. Future research should include randomized controlled trials, multi-center validation, and analysis of long-term outcomes, including neurologically intact survival and cost-effectiveness of simulation programs

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. The absence of a randomized control group restricts the ability to draw definitive causal inferences regarding the impact of the simulation-based CPR training. Furthermore, patient outcomes observed during the study may have been influenced by confounding clinical variables like comorbidities, severity of illness, time of arrest, and variations in team composition could have influenced outcomes independent of student performance. Additionally, variations in physician oversight, resuscitation environment, and documentation quality may have acted as confounding factors unrelated to student performance. The retrospective design of resuscitation data collection introduces potential selection and observer biases, despite efforts to standardize the evaluation process. The study did not assess long-term retention of CPR skills or outcomes such as survival to hospital discharge, which are crucial indicators of training effectiveness. Additionally, while medium-fidelity manikins were used to deliver a consistent training experience, they may not fully replicate human anatomy or the psychological stress of real-life resuscitation scenarios. Future investigations should incorporate randomized controlled trials, involve multiple centers for broader validation, and evaluate long-term outcomes, including neurologically intact survival and the cost-effectiveness of simulation-based training programs.

Despite its limitations, this study possesses several notable strengths. It involved a large sample size of undergraduate medical students, enhancing the generalizability of the findings within the academic setting. The use of both quantitative and qualitative measures allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of training outcomes, including objective improvements in CPR performance and self-reported confidence. The structured, AHA-certified simulation-based training ensured adherence to international guidelines and standardized protocols, enhancing the reliability of the intervention. Furthermore, real-life resuscitation performance was assessed in clinical settings, providing a practical perspective on the transfer of learned skills to actual patient care. These aspects collectively strengthen the study’s contribution to the growing evidence base supporting simulation-based CPR education in undergraduate medical curricula.

Conclusion

Simulation-based CPR training, when aligned with standardized AHA protocols, demonstrably enhances medical students’ technical proficiency, confidence, and adherence to resuscitation guidelines—core competencies emphasized by national and international health education standards. The improvement in clinical outcomes, including higher rates of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and timely defibrillation, underscores the training’s effectiveness in advancing patient safety benchmarks set by organizations like WHO and the Joint Commission.

From a health policy perspective, these findings support the integration of structured simulation modules into undergraduate medical curricula as a mandated supplement to traditional instruction. This aligns with the goals of competency-based medical education (CBME), aiming to produce a clinically prepared and emotionally resilient healthcare workforce. Moreover, the development of leadership, communication, and teamwork skills through simulation-based education addresses broader healthcare system priorities, including interprofessional collaboration and high-reliability care models.

Institutional and regulatory bodies should consider simulation-based CPR training not as an optional enhancement, but as a strategic investment in emergency care preparedness and system-wide quality improvement.
