CSyMR: Benchmarking Compositional Symbolic Music Reasoning with MIR Tool Integration
Boyang Wang Yash Vishe Xin Xu Zachary Novack Julian McAuley Junda Wu

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are leveraged in symbolic music reasoning, yet existing benchmarks emphasize isolated knowledge or atomic analyses rather than the integrative compositional reasoning needed to connect musical structures. To address this, we present the Compositional Symbolic Music Reasoning Benchmark (CSyMR-Bench) 1 1 1 Dataset available at: https://huggingface.co/datasets/wangby76/CSyMR-Bench , a curated multiple-choice dataset of 126 questions derived from expert forums and professional examinations. Each item involves combining several atomic analyses to arrive at the final answer. Furthermore, we introduce a tool-augmented agent framework that leverages symbolic music analysis tools from the music21 library to address the challenges posed by CSyMR-Bench. Experiments validate that CSyMR-Bench poses a non-trivial challenge across both community-sourced and exam-style questions, while our tool-augmented agent consistently outperforms all baselines, achieving 5–7% absolute accuracy gains.

2
The CSyMR Benchmark

2.1
Task Definition

We define a
atomic analysis
, as the act of inferring one implicit musical element directly from the score, which cannot be further decomposed into simpler analytical operations. A task that requires chaining multiple such atomic analyses into an evidence trail is regarded as a compositional task.

In this work, the score input is represented in the symbolic format of Humdrum kern notation
[
2
]
. We formally define our target as
compositional symbolic music reasoning (CSyMR)
: answering questions about a symbolic score by combining multiple atomic analyses into a coherent reasoning chain.

2.2
Data Sources and Curation

The Reddit section of the benchmark was constructed from posts collected on r/musictheory (2012 to 2022). Each entry consisted of the original post, its first-level comments, and associated symbolic score images. Score images were filtered using a YOLO-based
[
3
]
detector trained on manually annotated samples to retain only high-quality images. This process resulted in a curated set of 807 consistently high-quality examples. To further increase the benchmark’s rigor, we performed an additional manual filtering stage to preserve only the most challenging questions, those that pose substantial difficulty for large language models in the domain of symbolic music reasoning. Symbolic score images were converted into Humdrum kern format using commercially available music-notation software. The final set of 85 questions was then paired with the highest upvoted comment, which was manually verified by a human expert, as the ground truth. We subsequently generated three nuanced distractor options, similar in form to the ground truth yet incorrect, using GPT-4o-mini. The result is a community-grounded multiple-choice question dataset.

The exam section of the benchmark was collected from multiple openly available online sources of college- and graduate-level professional music theory examinations, followed by the same OMR-based conversion. Open-ended items were manually adapted by domain experts into multiple-choice format to ensure consistency. In addition, we incorporated a small number of distinctive musician- or genre-specific scores, reformulated in the style of the exam questions, yielding nine supplementary items that expand coverage of the
Genre/Musician Judgment
category. The source of each exam item is explicitly documented within the benchmark.

2.3
Categories and Tags

Compositional music reasoning tasks typically go beyond analyzing a single type of musical element. Instead, they often require combining multiple types of analyses, or chaining together different atomic tasks within a given analytical dimension. Therefore, rather than classifying questions purely by musical element type as is common in prior work, we organize our benchmark according to practical problem scenarios. Specifically, we define six categories. Names and representative examples of each category are shown in Figure
2
.

In addition, to more precisely capture the analytical dimensions involved in each question, we assign tags that denote the relevant musical element analyses. We define six tag types: Pitch & Interval Analysis, Chord & Harmony Analysis, Key & Scale Analysis, Score Structural Statistics Analysis, Rhythm & Meter Analysis, and Performance & Expression Analysis. A compositional reasoning task may involve multiple such tags, reflecting the integration of different analytical perspectives.

3
Tool-Augmented Agent

As musical passages become more complex and longer, the symbolic representation expands rapidly in textual form, which severely constrains an LLM’s capacity for precise parsing. Meanwhile, most compositional symbolic music reasoning problems can naturally be decomposed into a sequence of atomic analyses or simple reasoning steps. Previous works show that agentic frameworks with tool usage perform good in multiple tasks
[
10
,
18
]
that requires a solving chain. We therefore introduce a ReAct-style
[
14
]
agent framework, where domain-specific MIR tools based on
music21
are invoked to resolve critical analytic steps. Symbolic analysis functions from the
music21
library are integrated to ensure higher accuracy in atomic analyses and to ease the contextual demands placed on the LLM.

3.1
Framework Overview

Figure 3:
Overview of the proposed Music21-Tool-Augmented Agent framework. The diagram highlights the Planner, the Thinker (LLM), and the Tooler as the key components of the reasoning loop.

As illustrated in Fig.
3
, the agent adopts a ReAct-style controller(thinker) alternating reasoning and tool calls. Most of the symbolic analysis is handled by a compact toolset, while the LLM makes some logical reasoning and synthesizes the final answer.

The action space consists of one generic search tool and 16 symbolic analysis tools implemented with
music21
. These cover chord/root extraction, sliding-window key estimation, cadence detection, duration and meter calculation, as well as global score-structural features statistics. Together, they span the six analytical dimensions used in our benchmark.

3.2
Implementation Details

We use a four-role agent (Planner
\rightarrow
Thinker/Tool-user loop
\rightarrow
Reviewer). Tools are defined via a YAML registry with schema validation, and each call is checked for robustness before being returned to the Thinker in a standardized format. The Thinker selects tools from given names and descriptions, while a separate Tooler instantiates concrete calls. All symbolic analysis tools take the score in Humdrum kern format plus possible positional arguments (e.g., measure range, event index, part index), and return natural-language summaries that can be directly consumed by the Thinker.

4
Experiments

4.1
Setup

We evaluate six prompting and reasoning methods: Direct GPT (directly selecting the answer letter), GPT Few-Shot, Chain-of-Thought (CoT), CoT Few-Shot, ReAct without musical tools, and our ReAct-style
music21
tools-augmented agent framework. All prompting baselines and agent controllers are implemented with
GPT-4.1-mini
to ensure comparability.

Beyond these controlled comparisons, we further test five recent LLMs from different families—
GPT-4.1-mini
,
GPT-4.1
,
DeepSeek-V3.1
,
Gemini 1.5 Pro
, and
Claude 3.7 Sonnet
—on the Direct GPT setting to assess commercial model performance on CSyMR-Bench.

All models receive the same multiple-choice inputs, consisting of symbolic score excerpts, a natural-language question, and four answer options. Decoding parameters (e.g., temperature, max tokens) are kept consistent across runs, and the controller step budget for ReAct/Tool-Agent is fixed at 12 steps.

4.2
Main Results

Table
1
summarizes per‐category results across prompting and reasoning methods. Few‐shot prompting and explicit reasoning improve over direct prompting, but ReAct (without musical tools) is only comparable to CoT. Our method achieves the best overall accuracy, consistently outperforming all baselines.

At the category level, musical tool augmentation brings clear gains on harmonically demanding tasks, while improvements are smaller—or even negative—for style or metadata–driven categories such as genre judgment. Most of the improvements concentrate on problems that require fine-grained, score-grounded analysis. By contrast, for questions that depend more on background knowledge or conceptual definitions, our approach may underperform—likely because the search tool was not sufficiently leveraged or because the model’s reasoning focus shifted away from these aspects.

Table
2
further reports direct LLM baselines across model families. Somewhat surprisingly,
GPT-4.1-mini
achieves the highest accuracy under the direct setting, surpassing other models. However, in our additional experiments with CoT prompting,
GPT-4.1
reaches 69.41%, substantially higher than
GPT-4.1-mini
with CoT. This suggests that while the smaller model is more “plug-and-play” for direct answering, the larger model benefits more from structured reasoning in those complex music reasoning questions.

4.3
Case Study

Fig.
4
illustrates how our framework successfully solves a compositional reasoning problem over a 25-measure passage.
This question couples
key modulation
with
roman numeral analysis
: the solver must first recognize a local modulation, then extract the target chord and identify its harmonic function under the correct key.
The Chain-of-Thought baseline produces ten reasoning steps but fails early due to hallucination—fabricating non-existent pitches and a chord—which leads to a misidentification of the target harmony and an incorrect global analysis.
By contrast, our agent initially adopts an incorrect global key but self-corrects by (i) using the input parameters to precisely restrict analysis to the specified measure span, (ii) invoking a windowed key-estimation tool to detect the true modulation, and (iii) applying a roman-numeral analysis tool for stable, rule-consistent labeling.
This tool-assisted analysis enables recovery from initial assumptions and yields the correct final answer; although both methods produce seemingly coherent rationales, the accuracy of symbolic, tool-based analyses ultimately determines success.

5
Limitations and Future Work

Our benchmark remains modest in scale compared to large NLP or music analysis datasets. The current toolset, built on a manually curated subset of
music21
functions, also covers only part of the symbolic analysis space. Future work will focus on enlarging the dataset with more diverse sources and question types, enriching the toolset with higher-level analytical and retrieval modules, and exploring automatic discovery or learning of new tools
[
11
]
.

6
Conclusion

We presented CSyMR-Bench, the first benchmark explicitly targeting compositional symbolic music reasoning, together with a MIR tool-augmented agent that integrates
music21
analyses into the reasoning loop. Our experiments highlight that verifiable tool use substantially improves performance over pure prompting and reasoning baselines, especially on harmonically demanding tasks. These findings underscore the importance of combining symbolic-domain expertise with LLM reasoning. This work serves as a foundation for advancing research on symbolic music reasoning and inspires broader exploration of tool-augmented AI systems in specialized domains.
