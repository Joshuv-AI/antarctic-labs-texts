Environmental drivers of CVD DALYs: 20–year macro–level evidence from China’s administrative data
Authors: Yonghua Li, Qinchuan Ran, Hezhou Jiang, Song Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular diseases (CVD) account for 40% of deaths in China, with increasing prevalence associated with rapid urbanization and aging populations. Current research lacks comprehensive analysis of macro-scale environment-socioeconomic interactions. This study establishes a framework analyzing five environmental determinants of CVD disability-adjusted life years (DALYs): air quality (M1), green space accessibility (M2), public service facilities (M3), natural conservation status (M4), and transportation infrastructure (M5). Using 2000–2019 national and provincial data, we applied partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to quantify direct/mediated effects, complemented by spatial heatmaps. Results reveal: (1) urbanization indirectly reduces CVD burden through improved transportation infrastructure (β = – 1.396, p < 0.1); (2) natural reserves provide the strongest protection (β = – 1.235, p < 0.01) with time-lagged effects; (3) significant synergy between green spaces and public services (r = 0.69); (4) high-risk provinces (e.g., Yunnan, Fujian) require geographically tailored strategies. The results can provide evidence-based planning strategies for CVD-mitigating urban development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12942-025-00421-x.Keywords: CVD DALYs, Built environment, PLS-SEM, Macro-scale analysis, Environmental health framework, Administrative data

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has become China’s most critical public health challenge, constituting 40% of national mortality (2 in every 5 deaths). From 1990 to 2019, the age-standardized incidence rate among Chinese increased from 646.2 to 652.2 per 100,000 person-years, reflecting a growing disease burden [1, 2]. This trend stems from dual urbanization-aging drivers at the macro level: (1) built environment-mediated lifestyle changes (reduced physical activity, increased processed food consumption); (2) aggravated environmental exposure; and (3) elevated physiological susceptibility from aging. Current interventions show limited efficacy in controlling these compound risks, while urban-rural health resource disparities (fold higher tertiary hospital density in urban areas) further limit effectiveness [3–8].

Understanding and addressing this complex challenge requires a systemic perspective that moves beyond traditional biomedical frameworks. The Social-Ecological Systems Theory (Sallis et al., 2006) and Urban Ecosystem Theory (Barton, 2009) jointly establish the theoretical foundation for this study. These theories posit that individual health is nested within multi-level, interacting environmental systems, ranging from micro-level individual behaviors to meso-level community environments and macro-level socioeconomic policies, all dynamically interacting to shape health outcomes [9–12]. Guided by this framework, substantial empirical evidence has converged to confirm three interdependent pathogenic pathways for cardiovascular disease (CVD): (1) Environmental exposure pathway: A robust body of evidence confirms the direct pathophysiological effects of atmospheric particulates (PM2.5/SO2/NOX) on CVD incidence and mortality through mechanisms such as systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and autonomic nervous system dysfunction (Shang et al., 2013; Qin et al., 2011; Wan et al., 2011). Recent research is further elucidating the cumulative effects of long-term, low-concentration exposure and its interaction with individual susceptibility (Beelen et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2022). (2) Behavioral pathway: The built environment (e.g., land-use mix, street connectivity, park access) mediates CVD risk indirectly by influencing physical activity levels, dietary patterns (e.g., accessibility to healthy food), and psychological stress (e.g., noise, stress mitigation by green space). For instance, multiple systematic reviews have concluded that high walkability neighborhoods are significantly associated with lower risks of cardiovascular events (Frank et al., 2004; Nieuwenhuijsen, 2018); and (3) Healthcare resource pathway: The accessibility, quality, and equity of healthcare services constitute the critical last line of defense in CVD management. Structural disparities exist not only between urban and rural areas but also across socioeconomic groups, directly affecting early diagnosis, treatment adherence, and prognosis (Li et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2022).

Despite these advancements, a critical limitation persists in the current literature: most studies focus on analyzing a single pathway or a single scale (Fig. 1) [13–20]. For example, environmental epidemiology often focuses on exposure-response relationships, health geography examines built environment-behavior links, and health services research concentrates on resource allocation. This “siloed” approach fails to capture the complex synergistic or antagonistic effects among environmental, socioeconomic, and healthcare determinants at the macro level. The health benefits of a park-rich neighborhood (a built environment advantage) could be offset by surrounding severe air pollution (an environmental disadvantage); similarly, high-quality healthcare resources (a medical advantage) might be inaccessible to those most in need due to transportation barriers or high costs (a socioeconomic disadvantage). Consequently, the lack of an integrative analytical framework capable of incorporating multi-dimensional determinants is a key reason why existing research struggles to generate policy-actionable evidence to guide cross-sectoral collaboration [21, 22].

Scale and elements of built environment impact on public health research

To address these critical gaps, this study constructs a comprehensive theoretical and analytical framework that simultaneously examines and quantifies the synergistic effects of built environmental, socioeconomic, and healthcare determinants on CVD burden. We employ a Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) approach, a technique suited for handling multicollinearity and testing complex mediating relationships, to empirically test this framework using national and provincial data from 2000 to 2019. This integrated strategy aims not only to uncover complex mediating pathways but also to generate actionable insights for targeted urban health interventions, thereby providing precise guidance for urban planning, environmental protection, and public health policy.

Materials and methods

Theoretical framework and research design

We develop a theoretical framework for CVD DALYs’ environmental health determinants, integrating: (1) multidisciplinary synthesis combining biomedical and urban planning perspectives; (2) Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) to analyze complex systems with limited data [23, 24], which accommodates formative latent variables while providing superior explanatory power for CVD DALYs determinants; and (3) policy-responsive outputs generated through SmartPLS-StataMP integrated analysis of national time-series data (2000–2019) and multi-period provincial cross-sectional data (2015, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2023). The framework incorporates two complementary analytical tiers: The national-level analysis aims to uncover the long-term impact mechanisms and causal mediation pathways of environmental factors on CVD DALYs from a macro-temporal dimension. The provincial-level analysis is designed to capture the spatial heterogeneity of the disease burden, identify persistent high–risk regions (e.g., Yunnan, Fujian), and examine the spatial manifestations of the macro-level mechanisms. This framework overcomes prevailing single-factor analysis limitations by simultaneously evaluating built environment, socioeconomic, and healthcare determinants, identifying critical intervention nodes while elucidating their dynamic interactions and exploring underlying mechanistic pathways (Fig. 2).

Research methodology framework

The model controls for biological factors (aging rate) as a critical confounding variable. Having accounted for this, the study proposes an environment-society-economy tri-dimensional coupling framework and tests the following three core hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1

Higher urbanization rate (X) and per capita GDP are positively associated with increased CVD DALYs (Y) (β > 0, p < 0.05).

Hypothesis 2

Improved environmental indicators (air quality, green space availability, etc.) will reduce CVD DALYs (β < 0, p < 0.05).

Hypothesis 3

Socioeconomic development (X) indirectly influences CVD DALYs (Y) through its modification of built environment conditions (M1–M5), with expected mediation effects accounting for > 30% of total variance (p < 0.1).

The study area and indicator selection

The study area

China (73°40′–135°05′E, 18°10′–53°33′N) is situated in eastern Asia and ranks as one of the most populous countries in the world. With a land area of approximately 9.6 million square kilometers, it is the third largest country by territory. China has undergone rapid urbanization, with the urbanization rate increasing from 17.92% in 1978 to 66.16% in 2023. Economic development, population migration, and urban expansion have brought about profound transformations across the nation. Alongside accelerated urbanization and shifts in lifestyle, the prevention and control of chronic diseases—particularly cardiovascular diseases (CVD)—have become increasingly urgent public health challenges.

Indicator selection

The built environment varies significantly across population groups in China, due to variations over time in economic development, spatial structure, and resource allocation, as well as differences in individual socioeconomic attributes and self-selection. Therefore, a multidimensional assessment of cardiovascular disease (CVD) determinants is essential [25]. To address this complexity, the environmental dimension of our theoretical framework was operationalized through a systematic synthesis of multi-factorial determinants operating across macro, meso, and micro scales (Fig. 1). This integration distills key environmental processes into a cohesive set of five formative indicators, selected not only for their established roles in CVD pathways but also for their function as potential mediators between socioeconomic development and health outcomes, thereby facilitating the examination of Hypothesis 3.

Air Quality (M1) and Natural Conservation Status (M4) represent macro-scale atmospheric and ecological processes, capturing population-level exposures to pollutants and the protective ecosystem services provided by nature reserves [26–31]. Public Service Facility (M3) and Transportation Infrastructure (M5) reflect meso-scale urban form and resource distribution, influencing accessibility to healthcare and shaping mobility patterns that affect physical activity and pollution exposure [31–37]. Green Space Availability (M2) captures micro-scale immediate environmental exposures and opportunities for recreation and stress reduction [30–39].

The selection of specific measurement variables for each latent construct adhered rigorously to two criteria: (1) scientific validity, as supported by prior literature, and (2) data availability of standardized longitudinal metrics at the national and provincial levels in China. This pragmatic approach ensures our model is both theoretically comprehensive and empirically actionable. The composition and measurement metrics of the indicator system are shown in Table 1.

Data preprocessing

All integrated datasets underwent standardized preprocessing, which comprised four sequential steps: (1) temporal alignment into a balanced annual panel (2000–2019); (2) outlier treatment using the boxplot method (± 3IQR criterion); (3) Min-Max normalization; and (4) data integration and export. Data consistency was ensured through triple–verification against primary sources.

The specific procedures were as follows. First, data from disparate sources were temporally aligned to construct a consistent annual panel from 2000 to 2019. Second, potential extreme values were identified and winsorized using the boxplot method with a ± 3 interquartile range (IQR) criterion to mitigate their undue influence on model estimation. Third, and most critically, Min-Max normalization was applied. This step was essential due to the significant heterogeneity in measurement units, value ranges, and distribution characteristics across the variables (Table 3). PLS-SEM framework

This study employed partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) for empirical analysis, with the complete methodological workflow illustrated in Fig. 3. The framework consists of two core components designed to uncover the drivers of CVD burden at both national and provincial levels.

Comprehensive research and methodology application flowchart

First, based on the theoretical framework outlined in Sect. 2.1, national time–series data from 2000 to 2019 were used to construct, validate, and test the PLS-SEM hypotheses (H1–H3), thereby determining the influence weights (path coefficients) of various environmental factors. Both the measurement and structural models underwent a series of rigorous evaluations. Subsequently, bootstrapping was applied to assess the significance of path coefficients for hypothesis testing and to decompose direct and mediating effects, so as to elucidate the mechanisms through which socioeconomic factors influence CVD burden via built environmental factors. On the other hand, the weights derived from the national model were applied to provincial cross–sectional data. A comprehensive environmental risk index for each province was calculated through weighted linear combination, followed by spatial visualization to identify high-risk regions across five selected cross-sectional years (2015, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2023). This integrated workflow ensures a holistic analytical chain—from macro-level mechanism detection to spatial heterogeneity characterization. Validation protocol

The measurement model was rigorously validated through three dimensions: (1) Reliability assessed via composite reliability (CR > 0.7 threshold); (2) Validity evaluated using both convergent validity (average variance extracted, AVE > 0.5) and discriminant validity (Fornell-Larcker criterion); and (3) Predictive power examined through R2 effect sizes and Stone-Geisser’s Q2 (blindfolding procedure). All analyses were performed in SmartPLS 4.0 with 5,000 bootstrap samples to ensure robust parameter estimates.

Provincial risk spatial analysis

The provincial-level analysis aimed to spatially apply the outcomes of the national PLS-SEM model to elucidate the geographical distribution patterns of the CVD burden. The specific procedures were as follows:

First, statistically significant standardized path coefficients (β) for the environmental factors (M1–M5) were extracted from the PLS-SEM model fitted with national time–series data. These coefficients (e.g., for paths X→M and M→Y) represent the relative importance of each environmental factor’s impact on CVD DALYs and were, therefore, employed as weights for constructing the provincial risk index.

Subsequently, the corresponding annual data for each environmental factor (M1–M5) at the provincial level were retrieved from the same comprehensive database used for the national model. The theoretical model was validated, yielding indicator loadings and path coefficients for the latent variables. The established model elucidates the relationships between environmental factors (as latent variables) and their manifest variables with cardiovascular disease burden from 2000 to 2019, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Our analyses revealed significant effect heterogeneity among environmental mediators: (1) Ambient air quality (M1) exhibited marginal initial association (β = 0.209, p < 0.01) that became non-significant in fully adjusted models, indicating its secondary importance relative to other environmental determinants; (2) Comprehensive modeling showed effect size amplification for green space accessibility (M2) (Δβ = + 0.124), while coefficients attenuated for public service infrastructure (M3) (Δβ = – 0.191) and transportation networks (M5) (Δβ = – 0.082), implying potential specification bias in prior unitary assessments; (3) Natural conservation areas (M4) manifested a paradoxical protective transition (β inversion: + 0.269 to – 0.370, p < 0.05), underscoring its disease-mitigating potential.

This study identified three temporal dimensions of influence mechanisms: (1) Exposure-response lag: Health effects of environmental exposure often manifest after months/years; (2) Policy implementation inertia: Urban green space development and infrastructure upgrades require multi-year cycles; (3) Behavioral adaptation period: The assessment of cardiovascular disease determinants must account for lagged behavioral changes following exposure.

When exploring the relationship between cardiovascular diseases and the built environment, time lag is an important consideration. These cross-scale temporal dynamics (ranging from physiological responses to urban planning cycles) necessitate differentiated time-lag specifications in analytical models-a finding with dual implications for research validity and policy formulation.

Mediation analysis

This study systematically examined how environmental factors mediated the relationship between urbanization, aging, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) burden in China.

As presented in Table 8, the analysis revealed that collectively demonstrated the complex interaction mechanisms among these variables. The baseline model (Model 1) established a significant positive correlation between urbanization rate (X) and CVD burden (β = 0.452, ***p < 0.001), with all models demonstrating strong explanatory power (R2 > 0.871). However, when environmental mediating variables were introduced, this association showed progressive attenuation (β range: 0.452–0.616), indicating that environmental factors substantially accounted for urbanization’s health impacts. Further analysis demonstrated that while population aging (C) initially exhibited an independent effect on CVD burden (β = 0.926, ***p < 0.001), this effect became statistically insignificant (β = – 0.040, p > 0.1) after controlling for environmental factors, confirming their complete mediating role. Notably, differential mediation patterns were observed: air quality (M1) not only showed direct protective effects (β = – 0.240, *p < 0.05) but also displayed significant interaction with urbanization (β = 0.213, *p < 0.05). Natural conservation (M4) demonstrated a significant effect in reducing CVD burden DALYs (β = – 1.235, **p < 0.01), though this protective effect was attenuated in urban environments. These findings collectively indicated that environmental factors not only served as mediators but also modulated the health impacts of urbanization and aging.

Through comprehensive analysis using SmartPLS and StataMP, this study identified five key environmental mediators: air quality, green space availability, public service facilities, natural conservation status, and transportation infrastructure. These factors collectively formed an integrated framework for understanding environmental influences on CVD burden. The robust model fit (R2 up to 0.998) and rigorous controls for temporal dynamics and multicollinearity (VIF < 3.0) enhanced the validity of these findings. These results provided important evidence for public health policymaking, particularly offering valuable insights into how targeted environmental interventions could mitigate the cardiovascular health impacts of urbanization and population aging in China.

Supported hypothesis results

Our longitudinal analyses (2000–2019) yielded statistically robust findings regarding cardiovascular disease (CVD) burden determinants (Tables 9, 10 and 11). Both Hypotheses H1 (urbanization to environmental factors) and H2 (environmental factors to CVD burden) were strongly supported (all coefficients p < 0.01 after FDR correction). For Hypothesis H3 (mediation effects), we observed significant mediation through air quality (M1), public services (M3), natural conservation (M4), and transport infrastructure (M5) (mediation β = 0.664–0.958, p < 0.05), while green space availability (M2) showed only direct effects (direct β = 0.296, p < 0.01). All models met PLS-SEM quality criteria with excellent fit (mean adjusted R2 = 0.927).

The analysis revealed particularly noteworthy dual effects of transport infrastructure (M5). While increased road density showed direct adverse effects (β = +1.489, 95%CI: 1.236–1.742), urbanization-mediated improvements demonstrated protective benefits (β = – 1.396, 95%CI: – 1.628 to – 1.164). Natural conservation status (M4) consistently showed protective effects (β = – 1.235, p < 0.01), though the magnitude varied by urban context. These complex patterns underscore the importance of considering both direct and indirect pathways in environmental health assessments.

Our findings advance environmental health research in three key aspects: First, they confirm the socio-ecological framework through demonstrated cross-scale interactions. Second, they identify specific environmental factors with the strongest health associations. Third, they highlight the need for integrated urban planning approaches that account for these complex relationships. Study limitations, including potential exposure misclassification, suggest directions for future research while not diminishing the current findings’ validity.

Robustness checks

To assess whether our results are driven by extreme values, we winsorized all continuous variables (Y, C, X, and M1–M5) at the 1st and 99th percentiles and re-estimated the models using the winsorized dataset. The full robustness check results are provided in the Supplementary Materials and compared with the original analyses (Tables 6, 7 and 8).

Across all model specifications, the path coefficients, significance levels, and explanatory power (R2 values) remained highly consistent with the original analyses. For example, the coefficients linking urbanization (X) to the environmental mediators (M1–M5) remained within the range of 0.95–1.30 (p < 0.01), and the explanatory power of the models remained high (R2 between 0.87 and 0.99). Similarly, the main relationships among population aging (C), the environmental mediators (M1–M5), and cardiovascular disease burden (Y)—including key interaction terms—showed virtually no change in direction or statistical significance.

These results indicate that our primary findings are robust to potential outliers, thereby enhancing the stability and reliability of our conclusions.

Discussion

Importance of variables influencing CVD DALYs

This study reveals that economic development and urbanization processes impact cardiovascular health by directly and indirectly modifying environmental conditions. Spatially, urbanization rates demonstrate high concordance with CVD burden [40], though their relationship is not simply linear. Through empirical research at the provincial level incorporating economic-environmental factors from 34 Chinese provinces (2015, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2023), we generated annual CVD risk distribution maps (warmer colors indicate higher risk, i.e., poorer cardiovascular health status), as shown in Fig. 5.

Cardiovascular disease risk distribution map. (a) 2015; (b) 2017; (c) 2019; (d) 2021; (e) 2023. (This figure is based on the Standard Map Service Website of the Ministry of Natural Resources, as approved under Survey-and-Mapping Approval No. GS(2022)4308.)

Key findings include: (1) Marked spatial heterogeneity exists in China’s CVD risk. Frontier provinces (e.g., Yunnan, Fujian, Heilongjiang) consistently exhibit the highest risk, while central regions (Henan, Shandong, Hebei) show lower and declining trends. (2) High-risk clusters (e.g., Yunnan, Fujian) demonstrate divergent drivers. Although Yunnan consistently ranks poorly in the China CDC Weekly (2022) CHI healthcare dimension, Fujian’s higher CHI ranking may reflect the index’s exclusion of environmental factors like PM2.5 exposure (Yin et al., 2022), potentially underestimating composite risks. Our study addresses this gap by quantitatively accounting for environmental pollution’s counteracting effects on healthcare outcomes, advocating geographically targeted prevention strategies (Liu et al., 2019). (3) The risk maps show strong spatial alignment with China’s cardiovascular mortality distribution, confirming the model’s predictive accuracy for provincial CVD burden and its utility in elucidating geographical disparities.

Policy implications

From the perspective of reducing cardiovascular disease burden (DALYs), future planning priorities should focus on:

Prioritizing transportation infrastructure upgrades

Under urbanization effects, transportation infrastructure shifts from positive to negative correlation with CVD DALYs (β = – 1.396, p < 0.1) (Table 8). Mediation analysis reveals that urbanization mitigates the negative health impacts of economic development through improved transportation metrics (e.g., buses per 10,000 population), demonstrating synergistic health benefits.

Implementing tiered green space initiatives

Natural reserves show significant protective effects against CVD DALYs (β = – 1.235, p < 0.01) (Table 8), while green space accessibility exhibits weaker impacts-potentially due to limited consideration of pedestrian connectivity metrics. Evidence from Shanghai’s 15-minute living circle plan (mandating 14% green space coverage) and Qingdao’s 2035 “3-minute greenspace” target supports institutionalizing Health Impact Assessments (HIAs) with pilot “15-minute green space access” programs as essential for healthy city development.

Developing province–specific strategies employing geographic targeting

Discrepancies between China Cardiovascular Health Index (CHI) rankings and our DALYs-based risk mapping (e.g., Fujian’s high-risk status despite favorable CHI scores) underscore the need for dominant risk factor analysis. Environmental determinants like PM2.5 exposure—excluded from CHI—must be incorporated for accurate regional assessments.

Coordinating green space-public service synergies while managing transport-air quality tradeoffs

Interaction analysis reveals that significant M2–M3(green space and public facilities) and (air quality and transport infrastructure) M1–M5 interactions (Fig. 7). This highlights the necessity of integrated planning to optimize green space-service facility colocation and mitigate transport-induced pollution through clean mobility solutions.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that also inform future research priorities. First, regarding the methodological approach, the sample size for the national-level analysis, comprising 20 annual data points (2000–2019), is limited. While Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) is recognized for its efficacy in analyzing complex models with small samples due to its predictive orientation and minimal distributional assumptions, the generalizability of findings can be constrained by data scarcity. To mitigate this concern and enhance the robustness of our estimates, we employed a rigorous bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 subsamples. The consistency of the results derived from this resampling technique, coupled with the strong and theoretically sound path coefficients obtained, lends confidence to the identified relationships despite the temporal data limitation.

Other constraints include: (1) Temporal granularity: Annual data may obscure seasonal variations; integration of monthly China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data is recommended. (2) Individual behaviors: Street-level PM2.5-activity pattern interactions should be captured via wearable devices. (3) Dynamic feedback: Agent-based modeling should utilize the derived coefficients for policy simulation.

Conclusions

This study establishes a comprehensive framework for environmental determinants of CVD burden in China, revealing dynamic mechanisms through which socioeconomic and environmental factors interact from 2000 to 2019. Four principal findings emerge:

Significant mediation effects of environmental factors

Air quality (M1), public service distribution (M3), natural reserve coverage (M4), and transportation infrastructure (M5) exhibited dual direct and indirect effects on CVD burden with temporal lags. Urbanization indirectly reduced disease burden (β = – 1.396) through improved transit infrastructure (e.g., buses per 10,000 population), demonstrating synergistic potential between development and health objectives.

Unique role of green space accessibility

While green space availability (M2) showed direct protective effects, its significance attenuated in comprehensive models. This underscores the need for targeted green space planning: (1) Short-term: Pilot “15-minute green space access” programs in high-risk provinces (e.g., Yunnan Province); (2) Long-term: Institutionalize Health Impact Assessments (HIAs) in urban approval processes.

Spatial heterogeneity and tiered policy responses

Provincial analyses revealed distinct risk drivers (e.g., environmental exposure vs. healthcare access) requiring geographically tailored strategies. Transportation infrastructure (M5) and air quality (M1) emerged as priority intervention and monitoring targets respectively, while natural reserves (M4) demonstrated significant health co–benefits.

Environmental factor interactions

Synergistic enhancement was observed between green spaces and public services (r = 0.53–0.69), whereas persistent transportation-air quality correlations (r = 0.51–0.59) necessitate differentiated policies for conventional vs. clean transit systems.
