Size-structured populations with growth fluctuations: Feynman–Kac formula and decoupling
Ethan Levien, Yair Heïn, Farshid Jafarpour

Abstract
We study a size-structured population model of proliferating cells in which biomass accumulation and binary division occur at rates modulated by fluctuating internal phenotypes. We quantify how fluctuations in internal variables that influence both growth and division shape the distribution of population phenotypes. We derive conditions under which the distributions of size and internal state decouple. Under this decoupling, population‑level expectations are obtained from lineage‑level expectations by an exponential tilting given by the Feynman–Kac formula. We further characterize weaker (ensemble‑specific) versions of decoupling that hold in the lineage or the population ensemble but not both. Finally, we provide a more general interpretation of the tilted expectations in terms of the mass-weighted phenotype distribution.

Introduction

In modern biology single-cell data are abundant, owing largely to microfluidic devices such as the
mother machine

[
50
]
, which enable precise measurements of gene expression and morphology within single-cell lineages. Traditionally, phenotype measurements (e.g., cell size, gene expression levels) were predominantly conducted in growing populations. Consequently, understanding how single-cell growth and division dynamics shape phenotype distributions in large populations has become essential. Specifically, in growing populations (figure
1
C), selection biases samples toward faster-growing cells, causing systematic differences between lineage-level and population-level distributions. Understanding the relationship between these distributions is thus critical, as it (1) reveals how populations respond to selective pressures and (2) allows inference of selection strength by comparing single-cell with population-level data

[
29
,
25
,
30
]
.

There is a vast literature on these questions, which can be traced back to foundational work by Euler
[
3
]
. In the mathematics literature, work on these topics falls under the umbrella of structured population models. Sharpe and Lotka were the first to study branching models with age dependence
[
45
]
. Powell later explicitly connected these models with experimental measurements of microbial population growth
[
40
]
. Many others have explored mathematical questions, such as existence and uniqueness, primarily using tools from semigroup theory
[
13
,
51
,
52
]
. The importance of size regulation
[
47
]
for explaining experimental measurements was only recently appreciated
[
31
]
and has motivated considerable efforts in the biophysics community
[
31
,
37
,
54
,
23
,
39
,
18
]
. These studies focus separately on the dynamics of a fluctuating phenotype or on the mechanisms of cell-size regulation.

In this paper, we study a model of size-regulated growth in which the growth rate is determined by internal variables
{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(e.g. expression levels) that evolve independently of cell size between divisions, and may be affected at division. We further assume that
{\boldsymbol{X}}
may affect the division rate, denoted
\beta
. Within this model, we explore (1) the structure of the stationary distribution of phenotypes in both the lineage and population ensemble, and (2) representations of population statistics in terms of lineage statistics, which are based on the Feynman–Kac formula. Previous work on Feynman–Kac representations has focused on models lacking the size variable
[
35
]
, but a basic fact is that the division rate
\beta
cannot be independent of size. This is most easily seen in a model where
\lambda
is fixed (see section
4.1
). In the absence of any feedback from size to the division rate, the log size will undergo a random walk, and therefore the variance in sizes will diverge
[
1
]
. Meanwhile, the dependence of
\lambda
and
\beta
on
{\boldsymbol{X}}
generates a potentially complex correlation structure between size and the phenotype
{\boldsymbol{X}}
within a population. This makes the problem of determining the distribution of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
difficult, because it requires marginalization over the size distribution.

Interestingly, recent work
[
20
]
has shown that in certain cases the size and growth rate are asymptotically decoupled, which means that they are independent variables in a snapshot of the population or a collection of lineages. This phenomenon was also observed in
[
48
]
, in which the authors study the fitness effects of gene expression noise. When the growth rate and cell size decouple, we can study the growth rate distribution in a simpler model without a size variable. This has many practical advantages, such as making inference procedures robust to the model of cell size and making simulations more straightforward. To show that size and growth decouple, in
[
20
]
, the authors derive a lineage representation of population statistics. This has the form of a tilted expectation, which, as we will discuss, is closely related to the Feynman–Kac formula.

In the model studied in
[
20
]
, division is prescribed by a random threshold that may depend on the previous cell sizes.
Our motivation was to understand more generally the relationship between the size and phenotype distributions. This is achieved by building upon the adder-sizer model in
[
53
,
2
,
24
]
by combining it with the fluctuating growth rate model studied in
[
20
,
28
,
48
,
21
]
.

We specifically sought to answer the following questions: When do the size and phenotype asymptotically decouple in either the lineage or population distributions? When decoupling occurs, it is straightforward to represent the population statistics as a tilted expectation, which does not involve the size phenotype. This motivates another question: What is the interpretation of this tilted expectation when decoupling does not occur? This leads us to a generalized Feynman–Kac relationship, which relates lineage statistics to a mass-weighted phenotype distribution.

2
Size and phenotype structured population model

Here, we describe a class of single cell growth models in which a cell’s phenotype at any instant can be decomposed into
growth phenotype

{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
size phenotype

{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t)=(Y_{c}(t),Y_{b}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The growth phenotype, which could represent the concentration of growth-limiting cellular components such as ribosomes or proteins involved in antibiotic resistance, evolves independently of size between cell divisions according to some Markov process as described below. The size phenotype consists of the normalized (log) current cell size and size at the most recent division. Specifically, if
M_{b}(t)
is the size at birth of a cell at time
t
, we set
{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t)=(Y_{c}(t),Y_{b}(t))
where
Y_{c}=\ln(M(t)/\mathbb{E}[M_{b}])
and
Y_{b}(t)=\ln(M_{b}(t)/\mathbb{E}[M_{b}])
, and
\mathbb{E}[M_{b}]
is the mean steady-state birth size.
Meanwhile, cells accumulate size (e.g. mass)
M(t)=e^{Y_{c}(t)}
exponentially at a rate
\lambda({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))
and divide at a rate
\beta({\boldsymbol{X}}(t),{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t))
yielding two newborn cells whose phenotype is sampled from a distribution
h
.

Note that the initial size is expressed as a function of time because it changes during cell division, but remains constant within a cell cycle. The motivation for keeping track of both the current size and size at birth is that many cells exhibit size-regulation mechanisms in which the growth increment between divisions depends on the initial size. To accommodate such regulatory strategies within our modeling framework, it is necessary to include both absolute cell size and initial size.

2.1
Growth phenotype dynamics

The dynamics of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
within the cell-cycle are characterized by a generator
\mathcal{L}
, which is a linear operator acting on a dense domain of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
\mathcal{L}
defines a size-independent
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(t)
whose forward equation (known as the
Fokker-Planck equation
for a diffusion process) is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nu({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\mathcal{L}\nu({\boldsymbol{x}},t)

(1)

In the mathematics literature, one usually begins by defining the backward generator acting on test functions. In our notation this operator is
\mathcal{L}^{*}
, defined by

\mathcal{L}^{*}f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(t+h))-f(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(t))|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(t)={\boldsymbol{x}}].

(2)

Recall that solutions to the backward equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\mathcal{L}^{*}g^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t),

(3)

propagate expectations; that is,
g^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(t))|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}]
with
g^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},0)=f({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
We use the convention that
\mathcal{L}
(without the adjoint notation) propagates the probability density because this object will appear more often in our presentation. As explained later, we will allow the possibility that
\mathcal{L}=0
, in which case
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(t)=\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(0)
.

2.2
Size and division dynamics

We now discuss the joint process
({\boldsymbol{X}}(t),{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t))_{t\geq 0}
which is built upon the size-independent process
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(t)
as follows.
For a cell born at time
t=0
with growth phenotype
{\boldsymbol{X}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}
, the growth phenotype distribution at time
t
before division is obtained by propagating equation (
1
) with initial data
\nu=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
. The division time
\tau
is determined by a division rate function
\beta
, defined as the per unit time probability to divide given that the cell has not yet divided:

\beta({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})dt=\mathbb{P}\left(\tau\in[t,t+dt)|({\boldsymbol{X}}(t),{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t))=({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}),\{\tau>t\}\right),

(4)

After division, the distribution of newborn growth and size phenotypes is sampled from a conditional density

\displaystyle\begin{split}&h({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{c}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime})\prod_{i=1}^{d}dx_{i}dy_{c}\\
&=\mathbb{P}\left(\{{\boldsymbol{X}}(t_{k})\in[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}+d{\boldsymbol{x}})\}\cap\{Y_{c}(t_{k})\in[y_{c},y_{c}+dy_{c})\}|({\boldsymbol{X}}(t_{k}^{-}),Y_{c}(t_{k}^{-}))=({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime})\right)\end{split}

(5)

where
t_{k}
is the
k
th division event,
({\boldsymbol{X}}(t_{k}^{-}),Y_{c}(t_{k}^{-}))
are the growth and size phenotypes immediately before division and
[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}+d{\boldsymbol{x}})
is shorthand for the rectangle
[x_{1},x_{1}+dx_{1})\times\cdots\times[x_{d},x_{d}+dx_{d})
.

In summary, the model is defined by a quadruple
(\mathcal{L},\lambda,\beta,h)
containing:

•

A linear operator
\mathcal{L}
which is the generator of a continuous time Markov process (including the trivial case
\mathcal{L}=0
).

•

Growth and division rate functions
\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\beta:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
\lambda
is bounded below by a constant, while
\beta
is an increasing function of the added size
Y_{c}-Y_{b}
and vanishes as
Y_{c}\to Y_{b}
.

•

A transition kernel
h:(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
gives the phenotypes of a cell following a division event.
h(\cdot,\cdot|{\boldsymbol{x}},y_{c})
is a probability density which respects the conservation of size at division– that is, the sum of the two newborn daughter cell sizes must equal the mother cell size immediately before division. Said another way, if
y_{c}^{\prime}
is the log of the size of the mother cell before division and
y_{c}
of a daughter cell, then
e^{y_{c}^{\prime}}-e^{y_{c}}
(the mass of the other daughter cell) is equal in distribution to
e^{y_{c}}
.

We note that without the growth phenotype, our model reduces to the adder-sizer model studied in references
[
53
,
14
]
. An additional discussion of the relationship to other models will be provided in section
8
.

2.3
Lineage distribution

The lineage distribution is obtained when we consider the distribution of phenotypes over a sequence of cells, where at each division, one of the daughter cells is discarded.
The evolution of the joint density
\rho_{\rm l}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
of growth and size phenotypes,
({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{t})=({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
along a lineage obeys the PDE

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho_{\rm l}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)&=\mathcal{A}\rho_{\rm l}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)\end{split}

(6)

where

\mathcal{A}p({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\mathcal{L}p({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})-\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})\partial_{y_{c}}p({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})-\beta({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})p({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})

(7)

The boundary conditions are derived from examining the flux in the
y_{c}
direction across the boundary at zero added size (
y_{c}=y_{b}
).
Assuming
\beta({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
does not diverge at
y_{c}=y_{b}
(cells do not divide at the instant of division), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})\rho_{\rm l}^{b}({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{b},t)=\int_{y_{b}}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{y_{c}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{b}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime})\beta({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime},y_{b}^{\prime})\rho_{\rm l}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime},y_{b}^{\prime},t)d{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}dy_{b}^{\prime}dy_{c}^{\prime}\end{split}

(8)

where

\rho_{\rm l}^{b}({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{b},t)=\rho_{\rm l}({\boldsymbol{x}},(y_{b},y_{b}),t).

(9)

Throughout this paper, we will use the superscript
b
to denote the distribution over newborn cells.

We are solely interested in the steady-state distributions

\rho_{{\rm l},\infty}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho_{\rm l}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)

(10)

We remove the subscript
∞
when it is clear that we are referring to the stationary density.

The calculation of
\rho_{\rm l}
is greatly simplified if the PDE equation (
6
) can be solved using separation of variables, which means that the stationary solution of the form
\rho_{\rm l}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\nu({\boldsymbol{x}})w({\boldsymbol{y}})
. In this case the
{\boldsymbol{x}}
-factor
\nu({\boldsymbol{x}})
lies in the kernel of
\mathcal{L}
. Intuitively, we expect such a factorization to exist when
\beta
and
h
factorize, but as we explain below, this is not sufficient.

2.4
Population distribution

In a population in which cells undergo binary fission, we study the number density

n({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)d{\boldsymbol{x}}d{\boldsymbol{y}}=\mathbb{E}[\#\{\text{Cells with $({\boldsymbol{X}}(t),{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t))\in[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}+d{\boldsymbol{x}})\times[{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{y}}+d{\boldsymbol{y}})$}\}].

(11)

The expectation here is taken over different realizations of the population tree, which we call the
population ensemble
.
The number density is un-normalized, but it has a very similar structure to
\rho_{\rm l}
.
This is because, as long as
y_{c}>y_{b}
, the number of cells in the rectangle
[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}+d{\boldsymbol{x}})\times[{\boldsymbol{y}},{\boldsymbol{y}}+d{\boldsymbol{y}})
is not influenced by the division events. Meanwhile, the flux of cells in and out of this region due to the evolution of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
, the accumulation of biomass, and the division events are all unaffected by the fact that we are dealing with a growing population
[
53
]
. Therefore
n({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)
obeys equation (
6
). The growth only manifests at the boundary where, instead of producing one cell, a division event produces two. As a result, the boundary conditions for
n
are

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})n^{b}({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{b},t)=2\int_{y_{b}}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{y_{c}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{b}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime})\beta({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime},y_{b}^{\prime})n({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime},y_{b}^{\prime},t)d{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}dy_{b}^{\prime}dy_{c}^{\prime}.\end{split}

(12)

We take for granted that
n({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)
exists and exhibits balanced exponential growth, meaning

n({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)\sim e^{\Lambda t}\rho_{\rm p}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}).

(13)

Previous studies have rigorously derived conditions under which structured population models exhibit balanced exponential growth – specifically, see references
[
35
,
34
,
51
]
.
The normalized density

\rho_{\rm p}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)=\frac{n({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)}{\int\int n({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)d{\boldsymbol{x}}d{\boldsymbol{y}}},

(14)

will be referred to as the population distribution.
The density of population phenotypes
\rho_{\rm p}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
can also be interpreted as the density of phenotypes in a chemostat, where cells are diluted at a rate
\Lambda

[
28
]
.
By plugging equation (
13
) into equation (
6
) and equation (
12
), obtain an eigenvalue problem for
\Lambda
. Just as with the lineage distribution, it is much easier to solve this problem when
\rho_{\rm p}
can be factored. As we will see, when this is the case, the eigenvalue problem for the
{\boldsymbol{x}}
component is given by an eigenvalue problem for a
tilted
version of the generator, which is closely related to the Feynman–Kac formula.

3
Summary of results

3.1
Decoupling

We will state our main result concerning decoupling.
In words:
under biologically reasonable factorization and independence assumptions, the long‑time joint phenotype distribution splits into the product of a size‑phenotype distribution and a growth‑phenotype distribution,

\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)\to\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\nu({\boldsymbol{x}})w({\boldsymbol{y}}),\quad t\to\infty,

(15)

where
\rho
is either the lineage or population distribution.
The precise statement of this result is given in Theorem
1
below.
We impose two structural conditions:

(
Condition 1
)

\beta({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})\varphi({\boldsymbol{y}})

(
Condition 3
)

h({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{c}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime})=r({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})u(y_{c}|y_{c}^{\prime})

Condition
(Condition 1)
states that the division rate is scaled by the growth rate. As we will show below, this implies cells have no intrinsic age other than their size. Condition
(Condition 3)
asserts that growth and size are independently inherited between newborn cells. Biologically, this means that noise in the partitioning of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is independent of noise in size partitioning.
Note that compared to Condition
(Condition 3)
, Condition
(Condition 1)
is much stronger – not only must
\beta
factor, but the
{\boldsymbol{x}}
contribution must be exactly the growth rate.

Together, Conditions
(Condition 1)
and
(Condition 3)
imply that the integral on the right-hand side of the boundary condition (equation (
8
)) can be factorized into a product of functions depending separately on
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}
, provided that
\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
is of the separable form
\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\nu({\boldsymbol{x}})w({\boldsymbol{y}})
. Both assumptions are necessary for this factorization because
\beta
appears in the boundary conditions.

However, even when both conditions hold,
\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
does not necessarily separate. This is because, in our model, cell-cycle duration is determined by size. Thus, the time over which
{\boldsymbol{X}}
evolves between divisions depends on size, and consequently, the distribution of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
at division does too. Only when the distribution of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is invariant under the boundary conditions can we expect to have some form of decoupling. We distinguish between strong and weak forms:

•

Strong Decoupling (SD):

the boundary conditions leave
{\boldsymbol{X}}
unchanged (
r({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})=\delta({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})
) and
\mathcal{L}
generates a stationary ergodic Markov process. In this case
\nu_{\rm p}
obeys

\mathcal{L}\nu_{\rm p}+\lambda\nu_{\rm p}=\Lambda\nu_{\rm p}.

(16)

•

Weak Decoupling in lineage (WDl):

The iteration

\nu_{k+1}=R_{\lambda}\nu_{k}

(17)

converges, where

(R_{\lambda}\nu)({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int r({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})\frac{\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})}{\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})}\nu({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})d{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}

(18)

and
\nu=\lim_{k\to\infty}\nu_{k}\in{\rm ker}\mathcal{L}
.

•

Weak Decoupling in population (WDp):

The iteration in equation (
17
) converges to the unique solution to the eigenvalue problem in equation (
16
).

In the case of
SD
, equation (
15
) holds for both
\rho_{\rm l}
and
\rho_{\rm p}
. The size distributions
w_{\rm l}
and
w_{\rm p}
obey the same equations as the size-only model, while
\nu_{\rm l}\in{\rm ker}(\mathcal{L})
and the population growth rate is determined by the eigenvalue problem, equation (
16
).
This is, in fact, the equation for the population density and asymptotic growth rate we would arrive at if we began with a model where there was no size regulation and cells divided at a rate
\lambda
. While it is a strong assumption that
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is unaffected by division events, recent analysis of high-resolution biomass data from cancer cells has shown this to be a good model of their growth rate fluctuations
[
27
]
.

For
WDl
(resp.
WDp
), equation (
15
) holds only for the lineage (resp. population) distribution. Basically, when
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is not continuous across division, we need the evolution of its probability density to be preserved in order to have some form of decoupling. However, since this evolution differs between the population and lineage distributions by a dilution term, we cannot have that both distributions decouple in the same model (except for the trivial case of constant
\lambda
).

Note that the operator
R_{\lambda}
is not the stochastic operator,
R_{1}
, corresponding to the integral kernel
r({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})
; however, it is similar to
R_{1}
in the sense that
R_{\lambda}=\lambda^{-1}R_{1}\lambda
(here
\lambda
is interpreted as the multiplication operator which multiples densities on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})
).
The existence of a stable, positive fixed point to the iteration equation (
17
) is guaranteed when
R_{1}
is the generator of discrete-time, irreducible, ergodic Markov chain. Moreover, if
\pi
is probability density satisfying
R_{1}\pi=\pi
, the normalized fixed point of
\nu
satisfying
R_{\lambda}\nu=\nu
satisfies
\nu({\boldsymbol{x}})=\pi({\boldsymbol{x}})/\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})/\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[1/\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})]
. This mapping between densities is known as the Esscher Transform of
\pi
(see
[
12
]
) and is usually written in terms of an energy function
E({\boldsymbol{x}})=\ln\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})
. This transformation is also related to the Feynman–Kac formula, which interestingly emerges separately from the population statistics, as we describe below.

3.2
Lineage representation of population statistics

In addition to the decoupling results, we describe the relationship between lineage and population observables via path integral representations. These representations are also known as the “Many-to-one” formula, as they relate the population process (the many body theory in physics terminology) to the lineage process
[
35
,
34
]
.
For strong decoupling, we can obtain expectations with respect to the population distribution by averaging, yielding

\mathbb{E}_{\rm p}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))]\sim\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))e^{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda({\boldsymbol{X}}(s))ds}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda({\boldsymbol{X}}(s))ds}]}.

(19)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}
is used to represent averages with respect to the lineage sample path distribution on
[0,t)
(rather than over
\nu({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
).

Additionally, we will show that when division is symmetric, but the internal variable and size phenotype do not necessarily decouple, the right-hand side of equation (
19
) has an interpretation as an average with respect to a mass-weighted distribution (section
6
).

Examples

4.1
Size-only process

A special case of the model described above is the size-only process obtained by setting
\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})=\lambda_{0}
.
In this case, the time-homogeneous solution to equation (
6
),
\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
factors out into a growth distribution
\nu({\boldsymbol{x}})
and a size distribution
w({\boldsymbol{y}})
. The latter must now satisfy

\partial_{y_{c}}w_{\rm l}(y_{c},y_{b})=-\varphi(y_{c},y_{b})w_{\rm l}(y_{c},y_{b}).

(20)

The full proof of this relationship is given in section
7
, but one can easily verify that it follows from equation (
6
) when growth is constant.

The size distribution of non-newborn cells can now be solved as

w_{\rm l}(y_{c},y_{b})=e^{-\int_{y_{b}}^{y_{c}}\varphi(z,y_{b})dz}w_{\rm l}(y_{b},y_{b}).

(21)

We introduce the marginal density of birth sizes

w_{\rm l}^{b}(y)=Z^{-1}w_{\rm l}(y,y),

(22)

where the normalization constant
Z=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w_{\rm l}(y,y)dy
ensures this is a density in
y
. Using the boundary conditions given in equation (
8
), we find that
\omega^{b}_{\rm l}
satisfies the integral equation

w_{\rm l}^{b}(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{y_{b}^{\prime}}^{\infty}u(y|y_{c}^{\prime})f(y_{c}^{\prime}|y_{b}^{\prime})w_{\rm l}^{b}(y_{b}^{\prime})\,dy_{c}^{\prime}dy_{b}^{\prime},

(23)

where

f(y_{c}|y_{b})=\varphi(y_{c},y_{b})e^{-\int_{y_{b}}^{y_{c}}\varphi(z,y_{b})dz}

(24)

is the density of the final log-fold change in size,
y_{c}
, conditioned on the initial cell-size
y_{b}
.

To obtain similar equations for the population birth size distribution, we need to start with the exponential growth ansatz for the size number density
n_{\rm p}({\boldsymbol{y}},t)\sim w_{\rm p}({\boldsymbol{y}})e^{\Lambda t}
where
\Lambda
is the asymptotic exponential growth rate. To find the population growth rate
\Lambda
, we follow
[
31
]
and look at the dynamics of the total mass of the cells in the population:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bar{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{d}{dt}M_{i}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}M_{i}(t)=\lambda_{0}\bar{m}

(25)

where
\bar{m}
is the total mass of the population,
M_{i}(t)
is the mass of the
i
th cell, and
N
is the total number of cells. The population growth rate is therefore given by
\Lambda=\lambda_{0}
and is independent of the size-related parameters
\beta
and
h
. Plugging
w_{\rm p}({\boldsymbol{y}})e^{\lambda_{0}t}
into equation (
6
) and solving for the time-homogeneous solution yields

w_{\rm p}(y_{c},y_{b})=w_{\rm p}(y_{b},y_{b})e^{-\int_{y_{b}}^{y_{c}}[\varphi(z,y_{b})+1]dz}.

(26)

We note that the
+1
that appears in the exponent comes from the fact that, as the population grows, the flux of newborn cells leads to the dilution of cells at any given size. This leads to the population version of equation (
23
):

w_{\rm p}^{b}(y)=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{y_{c}^{\prime}}u(y|y_{c}^{\prime})f(y_{c}^{\prime}|y_{b}^{\prime})e^{-(y_{c}^{\prime}-y_{b}^{\prime})}w_{\rm p}^{b}(y_{b}^{\prime})dy_{b}^{\prime}dy_{c}^{\prime}.

(27)

In the special case of symmetric division, where
u(y_{b}|y_{c})=\delta(y_{b}-y_{c}+\ln(2))
, one can check that for any solution
w_{\rm l}(y_{c},y_{b})
to the lineage size distribution equations (
20
) and (
23
), the lineage distribution divided by cell mass
w_{\rm l}(y_{c},y_{b})e^{-y_{c}}
must satisfy the population distribution equations (
26
) and (
27
). We therefore have the proportionality
w_{\rm p}(y_{c},y_{b})\propto w_{\rm l}(y_{c},y_{b})e^{-y_{c}}
. In
A
, we discuss the cell size distributions marginalized over birth size and show how they reduce to known forms that appear in other literature.

Notice that in order to obtain similar expressions to those in this section for the newborn size distribution in the general case, we need to marginalize over the sample path distribution of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
. This is the fundamental problem we encounter in models with a fluctuating growth phenotype. The idea underlying our decoupling results is that when Condition
(Condition 1)
holds, cells have no intrinsic notion of age other than their size, allowing us to bypass the calculation of this path integral (see section
5
).

As an example, consider

\varphi(y_{c},y_{b})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{Y}}\frac{\phi\left(\frac{y_{c}-y_{b}(1-\alpha)-\ln(2)}{\sigma_{Y}}\right)}{1-\Phi\left(\frac{y_{c}-y_{b}(1-\alpha)-\ln(2)}{\sigma_{Y}}\right)}

(28)

where
\phi
and
\Phi
are, respectively, the pdf and cdf of standard Gaussian distributions. Then the distribution of the final size conditioned on the initial size is approximately
{\rm Normal}(y_{b}(1-\alpha)+\ln(2),\sigma_{Y})
, provided
\sigma_{Y}\ll 1
. This is the commonly used linear regression model for size dynamics
[
20
,
10
,
1
]
. The approximation comes from the fact that

is supported on
y_{c}\geq y_{b}
, whereas in the exactly Gaussian model
y_{c}
may be less than
y_{b}
. We include a brief discussion of this autoregressive model and the biological significance of
\alpha
in
B
. Note that if we take
u(\cdot|y_{c}^{\prime})
to be Gaussian with mean
y_{c}^{\prime}
and variance
\sigma_{d}^{2}
, then we can absorb the variance introduced by division into the parameter
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
, thus we can set
u(y|y_{c}^{\prime})=\delta_{y_{c}^{\prime}-\ln(2)}
, without loss of generality. As long as
\alpha\in(0,2)
, the size distribution will remain stable.

4.2
OU Growth rate dynamics (strong decoupling)

As an example where the growth rate fluctuates, consider an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process for the growth dynamics within the cell-cycle, which has previously been studied in
[
48
,
20
,
29
,
49
,
11
,
9
,
27
]
. For simplicity we take
d=1
(the multivariate version of this process is discussed in
C

is given by

dX(t)=-\theta X(t)dt+\sigma dW(t),

(29)

where
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and

is a standard Brownian motion, with the backward generator

\mathcal{L}^{*}=-x\theta\partial_{x}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}.

(30)

The stationary density (which is the normalized Perron eigenvector of
\mathcal{L}
) of the size-independent process is a
{\rm Normal}(0,\sigma^{2}/\theta)
.

We consider this model division rate
\beta=\lambda\varphi
, where
\lambda(x)=\lambda_{0}+x
and
\varphi
is given by equation (
28
). Although
\lambda
is not strictly positive,
we work in the small noise regime where the negative growth rates are exponentially unlikely, and therefore this can be considered as an approximation to
(\lambda_{0}+x)1_{x>-\lambda_{0}}

As was shown in
[
20
]
, if
r(x|x^{\prime})=\delta(x-x^{\prime})
(implying
SD

and

are independent in both the lineage and population ensemble. Note that technically, because
\lambda
is not positive, we can obtain non-physical solutions where the growth rate becomes negative, but these have a negligible effect in the small noise regime.
Figure

(top row) illustrates this numerically. Here, we have computed the empirical correlation coefficients

\widehat{{\rm corr}}_{T}(X^{2},Y_{c}-y_{b})
computed from averages over a time window
T
. These show a clear convergence to zero as we looked over longer time windows (lineage dynamics) and larger population sizes (population dynamics).

Within this model, we can also obtain an analytical formula for the population growth rate:
\Lambda=\lambda_{0}+\mathbb{E}_{p}[x]=\lambda_{0}+\sigma^{2}/(2\theta^{2})
.
As claimed in section
3
(see equation (

\mathcal{L}+\lambda

We computed these correlations specifically (rather than correlations with

Division noise

We add noise in

Strong decoupling and Feynman–Kac formula

In this section, we discuss the eigenvalue problem in equation (
16
) from the perspective of the Feynman–Kac formula. We do not go into the technical details of the Feynman–Kac theory. The general theory of Feynman–Kac semigroups can be found in
[
36
]
, while applications to structured population models are discussed in
[
35
,
6
]
.

5.1
Time-changed process

Under Condition
(Condition 1)
, the size dynamics can be transformed, via a random time-change, to a process
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(t)
with the same size distribution but a constant growth rate. Hence
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(t)
is exactly the size-only dynamics of section
4.1
.

Recall that divisions occur at a rate
\varphi(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(t))
upon which
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(t)
is resampled according to the density
u({\boldsymbol{y}}|\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(t))
.
We define the stochastic time
T(t)
by (see
[
20
]
)

T(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda({\boldsymbol{X}}(s))ds,\qquad\frac{dT}{dt}=\lambda({\boldsymbol{X}}(t)).

(31)

The marginal size process generated by the model described in section
4
is then given by

{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t)=\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(T(t)).

(32)

Assuming
\lambda
is positive,
T(t)
is invertible and we can write
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(s)={\boldsymbol{Y}}(T^{-1}(s))

This is because each time increment in the stochastic time variable corresponds to an increment
\lambda({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})dt
in the original time variable.

To derive equation (
32
), consider the probability
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(t)
dividing (meaning it is resampled from
u
) in an interval
[T,T+dT)
, which by definition of
\varphi
is

\mathbb{P}\left(\text{$\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(T)$ divides in $[T,T+dT)$}\Big|\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(T)\right)=\varphi(\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(T))dT.

(33)

The equivalent probability for
{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t)
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\text{${\boldsymbol{Y}}(t)$ divides in $[t,t+dt)$}\Big|{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t),{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)\right)\\
&=\mathbb{P}\left(\text{$\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(T)$ divides in $[T(t),T(t)+T^{\prime}(t)dt)$}\Big|\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}(T),{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)\right)\\
&=\lambda({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))\varphi({\boldsymbol{Y}}(t))dt.\end{split}

(34)

This transformation procedure is shown in figure
3
(A). In figure
3
(B), we have shown trajectories of a process with OU growth rate. Note that the time change can be performed even when
{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)
depends on
{\boldsymbol{Y}}(t)
, because by the definition of
T(t)
,
T^{\prime}(t)|{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)
is always deterministically equal to
\lambda({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))
.

5.2
The Feynman–Kac formula

When (
SD
) holds, the population distribution may be obtained via expectations involving
e^{T(t)}
.
Recall that in this case
r({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})=\delta({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{L}
generates a stationary ergodic Markov process. Let

g_{\rm p}^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}\left[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))e^{T(t)}\Big|{\boldsymbol{X}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}\right].

(35)

We use the subscript
{\rm p}
in anticipation of the fact that
g_{\rm p}^{f}
will represent population expectations, in contrast to the expectations appearing in equation (
3
).
Importantly, the expectation is over the lineage path distribution, since
T(t)
depends on the entire trajectory of
{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)
from
0
to
t
.
According to the Feynman–Kac (FK) formula,
g_{\rm p}^{f}
is the solution to a linear evolution equation
[
49
]

\partial_{t}g_{\rm p}^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}^{*}\,g_{\rm p}^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t),

(36)

with
tilted generator

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}=\mathcal{L}+\lambda,

(37)

and
g_{\rm p}^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},0)=f({\boldsymbol{x}})
. Equation (
36
) can be derived for SDEs using Itô’s lemma (see
[
38
]
), but it can also be derived for a more general process by looking directly at the forward variation of
g_{\rm p}^{f}
and Taylor expanding, similar to textbook derivations of the Kolmogorov backward equations
[
17
]
.

A consequence of the Krein–Rutman theorem (a generalization of the Perron-Frobenius theorem for operators
[
43
]
) is that the non–conservative operator
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}
has a dominant eigenvalue
\Lambda
corresponding to a strictly positive, normalized right eigenfunction
\nu_{\rm p}
(the population density); that is,

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}\nu_{\rm p}=\Lambda\nu_{\rm p},\qquad\langle 1,\nu_{\rm p}\rangle=1.

(38)

Under mild assumptions on
\mathcal{L}
(positivity and irreducibility),
\Lambda
is algebraically simple and strictly dominates the rest of the spectrum, thus any solution
g_{\rm p}^{f}
to equation (
36
) has the large-time asymptotics

g_{\rm p}^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\;=\;e^{\Lambda t}\,\langle f,\nu_{\rm p}\rangle+o(e^{\Lambda t}),\quad t\to\infty,

(39)

where the coefficient
\langle f,\nu_{\rm p}\rangle
is the projection of the initial data
f
onto the right eigenspace. In particular, for
f\equiv 1
,

g_{\rm p}^{1}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\;=\;e^{\Lambda t}+o(e^{\Lambda t}).

(40)

Taking the ratio for large
t
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{g_{\rm p}^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}{g_{\rm p}^{1}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}=\frac{\langle f,\nu_{\rm p}\rangle}{\langle 1,\nu_{\rm p}\rangle}=\int f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,\nu_{\rm p}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(41)

Notice that

\frac{g_{\rm p}^{f}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}{g_{\rm p}^{1}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))e^{T(t)}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}[e^{T(t)}]}

(42)

is the ratio appearing in equation (
19
). Therefore, as claimed, the population-level expectations can be obtained as the
t\to\infty
limit of weighted lineage expectations.

Equation (
19
) can also be stated as a formula for the bias between the population and lineage distributions as

\mathbb{E}_{\rm p}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))]-\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))]={\rm Cov}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}\left(f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t)),\frac{e^{T(t)}}{\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}\left[e^{T(t)}\right]}\right).

(43)

These relationships connect the quantities of interest in microbial population models to importance sampling, where one weights samples by an exponential factor
[
46
]
to sample more efficiently from a different distribution. In effect, exposing a population to selection is one procedure to “importance sample” the phenotypes.

5.3
Numerical example

To numerically illustrate equation (
19
), we obtain population expectations from lineage data using the OU process example from section
4
. In this case, we have an analytical formula for the population growth rate and population expectation of
\mathbb{E}_{p}[x]
. Equation (
19
) says that we can in principle retrieve this quantity from
m
independent realization of the lineage process,
\{({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}(s),{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{i}(s))_{0\leq s\leq t}\}_{i=1,\dots,m}
. Note that the stochastic time
T_{i}(t)
for each lineage can be obtained directly from the size measurements using

T(t)=Y_{c}(t)-Y_{b}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{K(t)}(Y_{c}(t_{i}^{-})-Y_{b}(t_{i}^{-})),

(44)

where
t_{i}^{-}
is the time just before the
i
-th division along the lineage with
t_{0}=0
.

The estimator
\hat{f}_{p}(t)
of
\mathbb{E}_{\rm p}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))]
is

\hat{f}_{p}(t)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}f({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}(t))e^{T_{i}(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{T_{i}(t)}}.

(45)

Intuitively, we expect this to give a good approximation to
\mathbb{E}_{\rm p}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))]
when
both

t
and
m
are sufficiently large; however, as shown in refs
[
26
,
19
,
42
]
, estimating expectations of this form is difficult in practice. In particular, it is shown that there are two sources of error, a finite time bias and a sampling bias, the interplay of which leads to non-monotonic convergence in
t
.
These works focus on the estimation of the dominant growth rate
\Lambda=\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\ln\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}[e^{T(t)}]
, rather than the expectations with respect to
\nu_{\rm p}
, but we expect
\hat{f}_{\rm p}(t)
to be plagued by the same issues.
Nonetheless, we have found that the Feynman–Kac estimator does provide some information about the selection bias as shown in figure
4
. For any given time value, the estimator is too noisy to detect differences between the lineage and population expectation, yet the time-average gives good agreement. We leave it to future work to explore the practical issues surrounding these estimates.

6
Symmetric division and the mass-weighted phenotype density of path expectations

This section provides a more general interpretation of the tilted expectation equation (
35
), which holds when the size and growth phenotypes do not decouple, but division is symmetric. The argument is based on previously developed lineage representations of the population growth rate given in references
[
26
,
37
,
20
]
, which we review in the following subsection.

6.1
The population tree argument and population distribution

Consider a population starting from one cell at time
t=0
. Suppose one selects a lineage from a tree by choosing either daughter cell at random at division. Let
K(t)
denote the number of divisions along a lineage occurring before time
t
. Due to the symmetry of divisions, the distribution of
K(t)
should be identical for each lineage in a growing tree. Now fix the number of generations
k
(assuming the first cell is of generation zero for simplicity). The population tree contains
2^{k}

k
-th generational cells, each of which can be associated with one of
2^{k}
lineages that are unique at the
k
-th generation. These lineages are indexed by
j\in\{1,\dots 2^{k}\}
and have a division count function
K_{j}(t)
. The number of cells in generation
k
at time
t
can be obtained by counting the number of lineages
j
that have
K_{j}(t)=k
, and hence it is given by the sum
\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}1_{\{K_{j}(t)=k\}}
. Although the different lineage division counts are highly dependent on each other, the expected number of
k
-th generational cells
n(k,t)
can be computed via

n(k,t)=\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}1_{\{K_{j}(t)=k\}}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(K_{j}(t)=k\right)=2^{k}\mathbb{P}\left(K(t)=k\right)

(46)

The expected total number of cells at time
t
is therefore

n(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}n(k,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k}\mathbb{P}(K(t)=k)=\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}\left[2^{K(t)}\right]

(47)

This automatically gives a lineage representation of the long-term growth rate
[
26
]
.

We make a similar argument to obtain expectations with respect to the population phenotype distribution,
\mathbb{E}_{\rm p}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))]
. If we say a population at time
t
consists of cells
i,\dots,N(t)
, with
N(t)
being a random variable, then the population distribution can be given as

\mathbb{E}_{\rm p}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))]=\frac{1}{n(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}f(X_{i}(t))\right].

(48)

For any population, the summation over
\{1,\dots,N(t)\}
can be broken down into a summation over all possible cells in the entire population tree, each cell given by its generation
k\in\{1,\dots\}
and lineage index
j\in\{1,\dots,2^{k}\}
, where cells are only counted if they are actually present in the population at time
t
. This condition is satisfied only if
K_{j}(t)=k
. Hence, we write

\mathbb{E}_{\rm p}\left[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))\right]=\frac{1}{n(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}f({\boldsymbol{X}}_{j}(t))1_{\{K_{j}(t)=k\}}\right].

(49)

Given the generation of a cell
k
, the distribution of
X_{j}(t)
and
K_{j}(t)
are the same for all cells
j
, hence we may drop the index
j
and evaluate the sum as

\frac{1}{n(t)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}\left[f({\boldsymbol{X}}_{j}(t))1_{\{K(t)=k\}}\right]=\frac{1}{n(t)}\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}\left[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))2^{K(t)}\right].

(50)

By using
n(t)=\mathbb{E}_{l}[2^{K(t)}]
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\rm p}\left[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}\left[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))2^{K(t)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}^{\rm path}\left[2^{K(t)}\right]}.

(51)

This is a general formula that holds irrespective of the division and growth dynamics. For example, even if
\mathcal{L}
depends on the size variable, we can still obtain population expectations in this way. Next, we will show more generally, in the case of
SD
of section
3
, this same argument can be repeated with mass instead of division numbers to derive equation (
41
).

6.2
Connecting Feynman–Kac expectation to mass-weighted statistics

We want to interpret equation (
41
) within the context of a model where decoupling does not occur. In the case of symmetric division,
Y_{b}(t_{i}^{-})=Y_{c}(t_{i-1}^{-})-\ln(2)
. Replacing this relation in the sum given by equation (
44
), canceling out all the intermediate
Y_{c}(t_{i}^{-})
s, as well as using
Y_{b}(t)=Y_{b}(t_{K(t)}^{-})
simplifies equation (
44
) to

T(t)=Y_{c}(t)+\ln(2)K(t).

(52)

Hence, the exponential weight
e^{T(t)}
from equation (
35
) splits into a mass term
e^{Y_{c}(t)}
for the current cell and a multiplicity term
2^{K}(t)
for the number of cells present

\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))e^{T(t)}]=\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))e^{Y_{c}(t)}2^{K(t)}].

(53)

In particular,
\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}[e^{Y_{c}(t)}2^{K(t)}]
is the expected total biomass of the population at time
t
.

Just as we have a number density
n({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
, we can introduce a mass-weighted population density of the phenotype
{\boldsymbol{X}}

m({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\int e^{y_{c}}n({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}},t)d{\boldsymbol{y}}.

(54)

With this notation, the identity above reads

\mathbb{E}_{\rm l}[f({\boldsymbol{X}}(t))\,e^{Y_{c}(t)}2^{K(t)}]=\int f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,m({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(55)

Then, in general, tilted expectations of the form given in equation (
35
) can be understood as averages with respect to this size-weighted density, and the finite-time version of equation (
41

f({\boldsymbol{X}})
. Asymptotically, only when we have the decoupling condition, we can pull out the expected value of

from both the numerator and the denominator of equation (

Setup

Here, we provide a formulation of the model in terms of linear operators between certain function spaces, without assuming Conditions
(Condition 1)
and
(Condition 3)

whereby the operators describing growth and division factor when applied to the dominant eigenvector.
We begin by introducing some notation.
Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}=\{(y_{c},y_{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:y_{c}\geq y_{b}\},\quad\mathbb{Y}_{b}=\{(y_{c},y_{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:y_{c}=y_{b}\}\subset\mathbb{Y}.

Representing the space of size phenotypes and the space of newborn cells (note
\mathbb{Y}_{b}\equiv\mathbb{R}

The linear operators will act on subspaces of the function spaces

\mathcal{M}_{y}=L^{1}(\mathbb{Y})

Lineage equations

We will start with the lineage distribution and omit their subscript
l

The boundary conditions in our model define a linear operator
B:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}^{b}
which maps the joint density of growth phenotype, size, and initial size to the density at

acts on a function

\displaystyle\begin{split}(B\rho)({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{b})&=\frac{1}{\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})}\int_{y_{b}}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{y_{c}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h({\boldsymbol{x}},y_{b}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime})\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\beta({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime},y_{b}^{\prime})\rho({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},y_{c}^{\prime},y_{b}^{\prime})d{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}dy_{b}^{\prime}dy_{c}^{\prime}\end{split}

This is simply the right-hand side of equation (

({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})

8
Discussion

The dynamic interplay of growth (i.e., biomass accumulation) and the cell cycle is a distinctive feature of models of cell growth. Much has been done to understand how the details of these processes influence size homeostasis and population dynamics. Here, we generalized work on decoupling, lineage–population relations, and pathwise Feynman–Kac formulations
[
20
,
16
,
26
,
7
,
31
,
32
]
to describe the structure of the phenotype distribution under different decoupling scenarios, as summarized in table
2
. We worked within a general model, accommodating most existing models of size regulation and cell growth. Within this model, a cell’s phenotype is separated into two distinct phenotypes: a growth phenotype, which determines the biomass accumulation rate, and a size phenotype, which contains information about a cell’s absolute size and its newborn size. We have demonstrated that, in the lineage distribution, even when growth is controlled by a phenotype perturbed at division events, the size distribution remains insensitive to the details of how this phenotype evolves. When the growth phenotype is “blind” to division events, the size distribution and growth phenotype distribution are independent in both distributions.

The decoupling of the growth and size phenotypes emerges due to a type of operator splitting, where the propagation of phenotypes from the boundary of newborn cells and the division dynamics act separately on the steady-state distribution (even though these operations do not commute).
In the most general case, we have shown that a size-biased sampling yields a generalized Feynman–Kac representation of population expectations. Prior work has rigorously proved Feynman–Kac formula for structured population models (see reference
[
35
]
), but not in the context of size-structured models with fluctuating growth rates. Practically, these formula allow us to use lineage-tracked data to obtain population statistics, and they motivate importance‑sampling approaches for estimating population expectations from lineage paths.

Our results have potential applications and extensions: First, they could be used to predict population distributions in liquid culture based on lineage averages. Second, they can help understand how size-biased sampling affects statistical analysis of single-cell data. Third, from a mathematical perspective, they introduce novel questions in stochastic processes about generalizations of the Feynman–Kac formula for non-diffusion processes and their potential application, e.g., in importance sampling.

Finally, we believe that the operator formulation of the steady-state dynamics (resulting in equations (
60
) and (
63
)) is of interest outside of the decoupling context. This could potentially be a starting point for constructing spectral representations of the population and lineage distributions, which contain information about the transient dynamics. It is also interesting to carry out a more rigorous analysis, which provides precise conditions under which these equations have unique solutions, since our Theorem has assumed the existence of such solutions.

Appendix A
Cell size distribution

Another interesting distribution is simply the distribution of cell sizes randomly selected from a single lineage, which the marginal density of cell size
y_{c}
is defined as

w_{\rm l}^{1}(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{y}w_{\rm l}(y,y_{b})dy_{b}.

(72)

This can be shown to be the difference between the cumulative distribution functions of birth size and division size

w_{\rm l}^{1}(y)=\int^{y}_{-\infty}dy_{b}\omega^{b}_{l}(y_{b})-\int_{-\infty}^{y}dy_{d}\omega_{l}^{d}(y_{d})

(73)

where the division size density distribution is defined as

\omega_{l}^{d}(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{y}dy_{b}f(y|y_{b})\omega_{l}^{b}(y_{b})

(74)

In the case of symmetric division, where
u(y_{b}|y_{c})=\delta(y_{b}-y_{c}+\ln(2))
, it follows from equation (
23
) and equation (
74
), that
\omega_{l}^{d}(y)=\omega_{l}^{b}(y-\ln(2))
. Consequently, the lineage size distribution can be simplified even further as

w_{\rm l}^{1}(y)=\int_{y-\ln(2)}^{y}dy_{b}\omega_{l}^{b}(y_{b})=F_{b}(y)-F_{b}(y-\ln(2)),

(75)

Where
F_{b}(y)
is the cumulative birth size distribution along a lineage. Similarly, the marginal density of cell size in a population is defined as

w_{\rm p}^{1}(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{y}w_{\rm p}(y,y_{b})dy_{b}.

(76)

For symmetric division, we argued that there is a proportionality
w_{\rm p}(y_{c},y_{b})\propto w_{\rm l}(y_{c},y_{b})e^{y_{c}}
, hence the population cell size distribution is

w_{\rm p}^{1}(y)\propto e^{-y}\left[F_{b}(y)-F_{b}(y-\ln(2))\right],

(77)

which matches the result from reference
[
20
]
.

Here, we give the formula for the dominant eigenfunction

The generator of the process is

\mathcal{L}^{*}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Theta_{i,j}x_{i}\partial_{x_{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}D_{i,j}\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}

where

\lambda({\boldsymbol{x}})=\lambda_{0}+{\bf b}^{T}{\boldsymbol{x}}+{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}A{\boldsymbol{x}}.

It is well known that the lineage density of

\Sigma\Theta^{T}+\Theta\Sigma=2D.

(81)

The population distribution
