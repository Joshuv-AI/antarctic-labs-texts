The effects of caffeinated chewing gum on sprint swimming performance and upper-body strength: a randomized crossover study
Authors: Marília Cavalcante Serpa, Jean de Souza dos Santos, Mariana Fernandes de Oliveira, Fernando Klitzke Borszcz, Tiago Turnes, Ricardo Dantas de Lucas, Luiz Guilherme Antonacci Guglielmo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of caffeinated chewing gum on 50-m freestyle swimming performance, as well as on pre- and post-performance measures of maximal isometric shoulder flexion strength and blood lactate concentration in male swimmers.MethodsIn a randomized, double-blind, crossover, placebo-controlled design, ten male swimmers (mean ± SD; age 27 ± 10 years; body mass 82.7 ± 13.6 kg; height 179 ± 6 cm; body fat 11.9 ± 7.3%) completed two experimental trials. Participants ingested either caffeinated (300 mg) or placebo chewing gum followed by a 10-minute absorption period. Swimmers then performed a 50-m freestyle time trial in a pool. Maximal isometric shoulder flexion strength and blood lactate concentrations were measured before and after the swim.ResultsCompared to the placebo, caffeinated gum reduced the 50-m swim time from 28.70 ± 1.21 to 28.18 ± 1.31 s, resulting in a performance improvement of -1.83% [95% confidence interval: -2.69%, -0.63%] (p = 0.013). However, there were no significant effects of caffeine supplementation on maximal strength (p = 0.261) or blood lactate levels (p = 0.586), nor significant interactions between supplementation and time.ConclusionIngesting caffeinated chewing gum just 10 min prior to exercise led to a significant enhancement (-1.83%) in 50-m freestyle swimming performance time, independent of changes in maximal shoulder flexion strength or blood lactate concentrations. This suggests that rapid caffeine absorption can benefit sprint swimming performance without affecting muscle strength or lactate metabolism in male swimmers.Clinical trial numberBrazilian Clinical Trial Registry (https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br: RBR-3GX2H7F). Introduction

Caffeine (1,3,7-trimethylxanthine) is a widely used nutritional ergogenic aid in sport [1]. Its primary mechanisms of action during exercise involve the blockade of adenosine receptors and a subsequent increase in neural drive to active muscles at the central nervous system level [2]. Additionally, caffeine (CAF) has been shown to attenuate the rating of perceived exertion (RPE) during strenuous exercise [3]. At the peripheral level, caffeine may enhance excitation–contraction coupling by increasing calcium availability, thereby improving muscle contractile force, and potentially enhance muscle contractility [4–6]. Given these multi-level effects, CAF ingestion is commonly used to enhance performance across various exercise modalities [7–9], including short-duration, high-intensity efforts, such as sprint swimming or running, where the window for performance enhancement is narrow and dependent on maximal neuromuscular activation [10, 11].

Despite this well-established mechanistic basis, the effects of caffeine on swimming performance remain inconclusive, especially when considering different administration modes (e.g., capsules, liquids, or drinks) [10, 12, 13]. For example, Acar et al. [13] showed that CAF ingestion via capsules (6 mg/kg) or energy drinks (3 mg/kg) improved 50-meter freestyle performance in (~ 4.5%) in recreational female swimmers (50.7 ± 6.4 vs. 48.4 ± 5.8 s, p = 0.03). Additionally, Lara et al. [10] reported more modest (~ 1%), but still relevant, improvements in young male swimmers (27.8 ± 3.4 vs. 27.5 ± 3.2 s, p = 0.01). Similarly, Vanata et al. [14] observed positive effects in 50-yard sprint swimming (27.28 ± 3.65 vs. 27.51 ± 3.74 s, p = 0.009) with 3 mg/kg of CAF ingested via capsules 30 min prior to the test in collegiate swimmers. In contrast, Pruscino et al. [15] observed that consumption of CAF in tablet form (6.2 ± 0.3 mg/kg) 45 min before the task did not enhance 200-meter swimming performance. It is important to note that the cited studies exhibit notable methodological differences, particularly regarding participants’ training status and performance levels. The markedly slower performance reported by Acar et al. [13] indicate a lower level of swimming proficiency compared to the trained athletes evaluated by Lara et al. [10]. Such disparities suggest that lower-level participants may exhibit different sensitivity to ergogenic aid, which could partially explain the variability in performance gains [16]. This distinction is vital in a sport where margins are reduced. For example, at the 2024 Olympic Games, the difference between first and second place and between third and fourth place in the men’s 50-meter freestyle final was 0.24% (21.25 vs. 21.30 s) and 0.09% (21.56 vs. 21.58 s), respectively. In addition to participant-related factors, the mode of caffeine administration may further contribute to these inconsistent findings [17].

Caffeine administration mode can impact its influence on performance [18]. As a strategic alternative, caffeinated chewing gum has emerged as an increasingly popular delivery method [19], offering distinct pharmacokinetic advantages over traditional methods [19, 20]. Crucially, CAF delivery via gum is highly efficient, with up to 85% of the dose becoming bioavailable within just five minutes of chewing [12]. In contrast, the peak action for capsules or energy drinks typically occurs around 60 min after ingestion [12, 21]. Furthermore, CAF chewing gum facilitates direct absorption into the bloodstream through the oral mucosa, which may reduce abdominal discomfort compared to traditional methods [12]. These practical benefits allow for precise “top-up” timing in the call room shortly before swimming competition, where unpredictable delays often make the 60-minute window of traditional capsules difficult to manage [12]. This flexibility is a practical necessity for athletes in the high-intensity environment of competitive sprinting. In this sense, given these potential advantages, CAF chewing gum may represent a more effective strategy for optimizing caffeine timing in sprint swimming events.

Considering that sprint swimming perfomance is highly dependent on neuromuscular factors, particulary linked to upper-body power [22], investigating the effects of CAF chewing gum on specific muscle strength is crucial. Currently, a divergence exists regarding CAF and strength; while some studies report positive effects [15, 20], others have found no benefits for one-repetition maximum strength [23] In swimmers specifically, CAF increased right-hand grip strength but not left-hand grip or vertical jump height [10, 13]. This gap is particularly relevant because understanding whether CAF can attenuate fatigue-induced declines in force production may provide mechanistic insight into performance maintenance during sprint events. Notably, to our knowledge, no previous study has investigated the effects of CAF in chewing gum on isometric shoulder flexor strength, a movement that directly mimics the propulsive phase of swimming crawl stroke [24]. Evaluating this parameter under fatigued conditions (pre-and post-sprint) provides a novel neuromuscular insight into whether CAF in chewing gum can attenuate the decline in force output in swimmers.

Thus, combining measurements of upper limb strength before and immediately after a 50-meter sprint performance could be an interesting approach to better understand and elucidate the influence of CAF delivered by chewing gum on swimming sprint performance associated to neuromuscular performance. Therefore, this study aimed to analyse the effects of caffeinated chewing gum on 50-meter swimming performance, and pre- and post-performance measures of isometric shoulder flexion strength in swimmers. It was hypothesized that caffeinated chewing gum would enhance 50-meter sprint performance and attenuate the decline in shoulder strength after the sprint compared to a placebo chewing gum (PLA).

Materials and methods

This study employed a randomized, double-blind, crossover, and placebo-controlled design (Fig. 1). Participants visited the research center on four occasions, separated by a period of at least 48 h. During the first visit, anthropometric measurements. Habitual CAF intake was assessed using a 3-day food record (two weekdays and one weekend day), which is a recognized method for evaluating dietary patterns in athletic populations [25]. Additionally, a structured questionnaire was used to identify specific CAF sources and consumption habits (Available as a supplementary file). All dietary data were analyzed by a certified nutritionist to ensure the accuracy of the daily CAF intake estimation. In the second visit, participants were familiarized with the strength test on the swimming bench. During the third and fourth visits, each swimmer took part in two experimental trials conducted at the same swimming facility and under the same experimental conditions (Fig. 2). Water temperature was measured using a digital scientific thermometer (Thermo-Hygro-Anemometer, Homis, Model 492 A, Brazil) to ensure that the temperature was similar in both experimental conditions.

To decrease the risk of bias regarding the effect of CAF ingestion, the usual CAF intake was recorded. All participants were instructed to maintain their habitual dietary intake throughout the study, with the exception of abstaining from CAF intake for at least 24 h before all trials. The same pre-exercise meal pattern was maintained for each participant on all trial days to avoid within-subject differences in exercise performance. Participants were also instructed to discontinue the use of any nutritional ergogenic aids during the study period.

Subjects

The sample size for this study was determined by available resources and data collection time, constrained by the scheduling of the swimmers’ clubs [26]. Consequently, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the smallest effect size, given the obtained sample size, alpha level, and statistical power, which are detailed in the results section.

Ten male swimmers participated in the study (mean ± SD; age 27 ± 10 years; body mass 82.7 ± 13.6 kg; height 179 ± 6 cm; body fat 11.9 ± 7.3%; wingspan 187 ± 8 cm). The swimmers presented a mean World Aquatics score of 352 points (range: 300–430) for 50-m freestyle performance. The eligibility criteria for participants were as follows: at least 5 years of swimming experience, training at least 6 days per week during the previous year, and competition experience. The average training volume during data collection was 19.875 ± 11.789 m per week. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the principles of good clinical practice, and the CONSORT guidelines.

Procedures

Caffeine administration

Participants chewed each type of gum for 10 min of passive rest before the 50-meter test and were required to dispose of the chewed gum in a trash receptacle. CAF-containing chewing gums from the commercial brand Military Energy (Ford Gum & Machine Co., New York, USA) were used. Each gum contains 100 mg of CAF, with each experimental protocol in the CAF condition providing an absolute and total dose of 300 mg of the substance (mean ± SD [range]: 3.71 ± 0.50 [2.61 to 4.31] mg/kg body mass). The PLA gums were designed to be as similar as possible in texture, flavor, and smell, and to provide the same amount of carbohydrates (Trident XFresh Crystal Mint, Smaków, Poland).

The preparation of the gum was carried out by a researcher who was not involved in data collection or analysis. This researcher was responsible for preparing the gum doses (CAF or PLA) corresponding to the day’s protocol for the subject (randomized) and recording them on a sheet that other participants did not have access to until the end of the data collection period. The preparation of the gums involved cutting them into small pieces within a small, cylindrical, and opaque container to make it difficult for other participants to perceive the size, shape, and color of the gums. At the time of supplementation, the subject was required to open the container with their eyes closed and chew the gum for 5 min. After this time, without looking, the subject discarded the gums in the trash wrapped in a paper towel.

Strength measurement

The strength test was based on the protocol proposed by [27]. For this test, participants lay prone on a swim bench and replicated the upper-body movement during the front crawl swim stroke, measured outside the pool on an adapted swimming ergometer (Schwimm Ergometer, Webba Sport, Vienna, Austria) using a load cell for tension and compression (SDS200KG, Miotec, Porto Alegre, Brazil) fixed to the equipment and connected to the MiotecSuite system (Miotec, Porto Alegre, Brazil). They were connected to the measurement device solely by the palm of the right hand. The trunk and arm were aligned at a 90º angle, with the right hand placed on the palmar surface. In this position, participants performed a maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) of shoulder flexion, compressing the load cell for 5 s, replicating the force application during part of the upper limb movement in the front crawl swim stroke. The strength test was measured at three time points: (1) before CAF/PLA administration, (2) after CAF/PLA administration, and (3) after the 50-meter swimming test. The time before and after each strength test was controlled, and the intervals consisted of participants passively resting in a chair. A 2-minute interval was given between the first and second strength tests at all three time points. Strength variables were analyzed using the acquisition system (SDS200KG, Miotec, Porto Alegre, Brazil), with the highest value being considered for analysis. All volunteers were verbally encouraged during the MVIC and were previously instructed to produce maximum strength as quickly as possible.

Fifty-meter freestyle swimming test

The protocol began with a controlled warm-up lasting 20 min and covering a total distance of 1000 m, based on the warm-up strategy proposed by [28]. This was followed by 10 min of passive recovery. During this recovery period, the 5-second strength tests were conducted twice, with a 2-minute interval between them. Five minutes before the 50-meter test, CAF or PLA gums were administered. One minute after this test, the strength measurements were repeated. Blood lactate concentrations (BLC) were measured after the warm-up and immediately following the 50-meter freestyle swimming test. Capillary blood samples (25 µl) were obtained from the earlobe of each subject, and BLC was measured using an electrochemical analyzer (YSI 2700 STAT, Yellow Springs, OH, USA).

The tests were conducted individually in a 25-meter indoor swimming pool, with swimmers beginning the 50-meter performance from a push-off at the wall. Before all the tests, they were strongly encouraged to give their best effort to perform maximally by the same investigator. However, they did not receive any additional encouragement during the performance. The 50-meter performance was started with an acoustic signal, and the 50-meter swimming time was measured by a digital video camera (Nikon model D5000, Tokyo, Japan) placed at the start and end of the test to record the time. The 50-meter time was recorded by analysing the video using Kinovea video editor software (Kinovea Open-Source software). Additionally, the time was registered by two experienced trainers using 0.01 chronometers (Ultrak model 495, Gardena, USA), as recommended by the International Swimming Federation. The test was performed in the central swimming lane, and the pool was isolated during the test.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data are presented as mean ± SD, statistical data as mean point estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The 50-m time was analysed using a paired mean difference test for repeated measures between PLA and CAF. The p-value of the two-sided permutation t-test, along with the effect size and its 95% CI, were calculated using 5000 bootstrap samples [29]. The results of the MVIC and BLC were analysed using a two-way ANOVA mixed model. The fixed effects of the model were supplementation (CAF and PLA), time (before supplementation, after supplementation, and after 50 m), and a supplementation × time interaction. Tukey’s post hoc corrections were applied when the ANOVA main effects yielded p-values below 0.05. Data were analyzed using the R software (version 1.2.5, RStudio, Boston, MA, USA) and in the Estimation Stats https://www.estimationstats.com/#/. GraphPad Prism software (version 8 - GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used to produce the graphs. A statistical significance level of less than 5% (p < 0.05) was considered. To determine the minimum detectable effect size for the 50-m swim sprint, a sensitivity analysis was performed in G*Power (version 3.1.97, Germany). Given a sample size of 10, an alpha level of 5%, and a statistical power of 80%, the analysis yielded a Cohen’s d of 0.996.

Results

The 50-m swimming performance mean ± SD of CAF and PLA trials were 28.18 ± 1.31 and 28.70 ± 1.21 s, respectively. The CAF presented a statistically significant ergogenic effect on 50-m performance with a paired mean difference between PLA and CAF of -0.524 s [95% CI -0.771, -0.182], which represents a percentage effect of -1.83% [95% CI -2.69%, -0.63%] (p = 0.013) (Fig. 3). The observed Cohen’s d between PLA and CAF was 1.090, exceeding the minimum detectable effect size of 0.996 determined by the sensitivity analysis. Therefore, the study achieved the desired statistical power.

For strength and BLC measures, there were no significant main effects of supplementation (F(1, 42) = 1.30, p = 0.261; F(1, 44) = 0.30, p = 0.586, respectively) and interaction supplementation × time (F(2, 42) = 0.528, p = 0.594; F(2, 44) = 1.48, p = 0.238, respectively), but there was a significant main effect of time (F(2, 42) = 8.10, p = 0.001; F(2, 44) = 145.3, p < 0.0001, respectively).

Discussion

This study aimed to analyze the effects of caffeinated chewing gum on 50-meter swimming performance, and pre- and post-performance measures of isometric shoulder flexion strength in male swimmers. It was hypothesized that caffeinated chewing gum would enhance 50-meter sprint performance and attenuate the decline in shoulder strength following the sprint, compared to PLA, in competitive regional-level male swimmers. Although caffeinated chewing gum has been investigated in a variety of sports [8, 30, 31], the novelty of the present study lies in evaluating this specific administration route during sprint swimming and its potential role in attenuating the decline in isometric strength. The results of the present study revealed that use of caffeinated chewing gum (i.e., 300 mg or 3.7 ± 0.5 mg/kg body mass of CAF) enhanced the 50-meter freestyle performance compared to PLA gum in male swimmers. However, these improvements occurred without any attenuation in MVIC of shoulder flexion or alterations in BLC. Despite the benefits of CAF on sprint swimming performance not being novel [10, 13, 32], these improvements originated from modes requiring at least 60 min between CAF intake and the start of the performance. Therefore, the current findings revealed for the first time a positive impact of caffeinated chewing gum ingested just 10 min before the 50-meter test in swimmers. The benefits of caffeinated chewing gum on sprint swimming performance align with the positive effects of CAF observed in previous research [10, 13, 32, 33]. However, these studies administered CAF via capsules or energy drinks, typically requiring a 60-minute interval between ingestion and exercise. In contrast, the present protocol allowed exercise to begin within 10 min of ingestion, as chewing gum facilitates rapid CAF absorption through the oral mucosa [12]. The rapid onset of action of CAF in chewing gum provides a practical advantage and serves as an effective delivery method for athletes [8]. This can be particularly interesting for swimmers who may be required to perform maximal efforts repeatedly throughout a competition day, especially when competing in more than one event where the interval between trials is not very long. While the mechanisms that explain the current benefits are not entirely clear, it is well established that CAF exerts its effects on the central nervous system primarily by blocking adenosine receptors, thereby altering central nervous system functioning [34], resulting in increased concentrations of neurotransmitters such as dopamine and norepinephrine, and ultimately enhancing neural activity [35]. Thus, the performance improvements observed in this study may be attributed to these mechanisms.

Given that CAF reduces exercise-induced fatigue [6] and improves lower limb strength and power variables [36], it was hypothesized that CAF chewing gum could mitigate the reduction in muscle strength in swimmers following the 50-meter freestyle swim. Despite the improvements in swimming performance, CAF ingestion did not attenuate the MVIC of shoulder flexors, partially refuting the hypothesis of the present study. To our knowledge, this is the first study to combine the assessment of caffeinated chewing gum on sprint and strength performance in male swimmers. Previous studies have reported contradictory results regarding CAF and strength-related variables in swimmers using different ingestion methods [10, 13]. For instance, ingestion of 6 mg/kg of CAF in a capsule did not impact the vertical jump height in female swimmers [13]. On the other hand, a caffeinated energy drink improved right-hand grip strength by 3.5% in swimmers, but not left-hand grip strength, along with benefits in 50-meter freestyle performance [10]. In other sports, CAF chewing gum did not attenuate the magnitude of lower limb strength decline measured by MVIC or dynamic (i.e., cycling all-out sprint) tests, which were performed after a sequence of two 6-minute severe-intensity cycling exercises [37]. In addition, MVIC assessed after a time-to-exhaustion running test at a heavy-intensity exercise domain, specifically the Δ50% intensity (PLA: 33 ± 7 min; CAF: 41 ± 9 min) in runners [3]. Finally, despite the 50-meter swimming test reducing upper body MVIC during both CAF and PLA conditions, demonstrating that the measurement detected the expected strength loss in swimmers, these results are consistent with those reported in the literature and suggest that CAF may enhance short-duration performance through mechanisms distinct from those directly related to strength.

A possible explanation for the ergogenic effect of CAF is that it influences the autonomic nervous system, where the increased concentration of adrenaline may affect glycolytic metabolism [38, 39]. Several studies have shown an increase in BLC after CAF ingestion during short-duration exercise [38, 40, 41], although this is not always associated with performance improvements [38, 40]. However, in the present study, caffeinated chewing gum did not result in any significant changes in BLC compared to PLA ingestion. Regardless of the potential mechanisms, it was observed that caffeinated chewing gum led to a significant improvement in 50-meter freestyle performance compared to the PLA. These findings suggest that CAF gum is a practical and strategic alternative for sprint swimming, particularly due to its faster absorption rate compared to capsule consumption. Therefore, future studies could investigate the influence of caffeinated chewing gum supplementation on training and competition routines, especially in short-duration events.

An original aspect of this study was the administration of CAF via chewing gum. This method appears to be an effective and convenient means of CAF ingestion for swimmers, given its rapid onset of action and lack of gastrointestinal distress. Administering CAF through chewing gum is particularly beneficial for swimmers competing in single sprint events and may be especially advantageous for athletes participating in multiple-event competitions, as some competition events require swimmers to perform with incomplete recovery. For example, in competitive swimming, athletes frequently participate in multiple events with recovery intervals inferior of one hour. A common scenario involves competing in a race, performing an active recovery (warm-down), and returning to the start blocks for subsequent events, sometimes totaling three races within a two-hour window. Given these time constraints, caffeinated chewing gum emerges as a viable ergogenic strategy due to its rapid absorption kinetics compared to traditional capsules or energy drink. Thus, caffeinated chewing gum presents an appealing alternative during breaks between events for athletes needing a quick performance boost during the competition [18]. Although examining the effects of caffeinated chewing gum during successive swimming events was not the focus of the current study and remains to be further explored, some recommendations can be drawn for CAF use among athletes competing in 50-meter freestyle trials based on our findings. In summary, modest but significant performance improvements of approximately 2% can be expected with the ingestion of a moderate CAF dose (300 mg or 3.7 mg/kg body mass) administered via chewing gum at least 10 min before exercise.

Despite the novel aspects, the current study is not absent of limitations, which should be acknowledged. Firstly, although caffeinated chewing gum was used as a rapid delivery method, no direct assessment of caffeine pharmacokinetics was performed. Although the timing of caffeine availability when using chewing gum is rapid, with a bioavailability of 85% in 5 min [12]. Consequently, our findings regarding the time of the ergogenic effects should be interpreted with caution [12]. Furthermore, the 50-m time trial was performed using an in-water start rather than a starting block, removing the contribution of reaction time and the dive phase, which may limit ecological validity. However, this approach improved experimental control by reducing variability associated with start technique. Future studies should consider block starts and additional performance metrics. Regarding strength measurements, isometric shoulder flexion strength was selected as a sport-relevant measure of upper limb force production. However, this assessment does not fully capture the complexity of swimming-specific movements, which involves coordinated, multi-joint actions under dynamic conditions, and may not be entirely reflected by isolated isometric testing.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results of this study demonstrate that CAF administration via chewing gum just 10 min before exercise can improve 50-meter freestyle swimming performance in competitive regional-level male swimmers. However, these improvements were not accompanied by an attenuation in maximal voluntary isometric contraction of shoulder flexors or alterations in blood lactate concentrations.
