Cost-Utility Analysis of Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide Monoclonal Antibodies for Patients with Episodic Migraine Headache in the USA
Authors: Ariel Weiming Dong, Mark Bounthavong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate the cost effectiveness of eptinezumab and erenumab as a preventive treatment of migraine in patients with episodic migraine with different routes of administration, from the US healthcare payer perspective.MethodsA hybrid decision tree-Markov model was constructed to assess the cost effectiveness of eptinezumab and erenumab as a preventive treatment for migraines compared to topiramate among patients with episodic migraine from the US healthcare payer’s perspective. A decision tree model simulated the probability of patients experiencing adequate responses to treatments in the first 6 months, which fed into a Markov model using a 2-year time horizon. The Markov model consisted of three states: “Off Preventive Treatment,” “Preventive Treatment,” and death. Total costs, quality-adjusted life-years, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios were estimated for each strategy followed by sensitivity analyses. An annual discount rate of 3% was applied to both costs and utility values, and costs were reported in 2025 US dollars.ResultsIn the base-case results, total costs of eptinezumab and erenumab were $21,767 and $21,739, respectively. Total quality-adjusted life-years for eptinezumab and erenumab were 1.32 and 1.30, respectively. Base-case incremental cost-effectiveness ratios of eptinezumab and erenumab compared to topiramate were $117,626 and $155,259 per quality-adjusted life-year gained, respectively. Erenumab was eliminated because of extended dominance; thus, eptinezumab was selected for additional sensitivity analyses. In the one-way sensitivity analysis, treatment response, utility values, and drug costs were influential in changing the study’s conclusions. In the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, eptinezumab was cost effective in 1.8%, 33.6%, and 78.6% of simulations when the willingness-to-pay threshold was $50,000, $100,000, and $150,000 per quality-adjusted life-year gained, respectively.ConclusionsOur analysis suggests that eptinezumab might be cost effective at a willingness-to-pay threshold of $150,000 per quality-adjusted life-year gained compared with topiramate, with uncertainties on health state utilities and pharmaceutical costs. Policymakers should carefully consider the tradeoffs between costs and benefits when making decisions regarding formulary coverage for the episodic migraine population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40273-026-01624-1.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Eptinezumab may be a cost-effective strategy compared with topiramate from a US healthcare payer perspective over a 2-year time horizon; however, results are associated with uncertainties around treatment effects and pharmaceutical costs.Erenumab 70 mg may not be cost effective at any willingness-to-pay threshold examined; however, increasing the dose to 140 mg may be a cost-effective strategy based on the scenario analysis.Policymakers should carefully consider the context in which they intend to use calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibodies when assessing formulary coverage for episodic migraine.

Introduction

Migraine is a debilitating neurological disorder associated with severe headaches lasting from 4 to 72 hours and generally accompanied by nausea and vomiting, sensitivity to light and sound, and/or visual neurologic symptoms (e.g., aura) [1, 2]. Migraine headache is one of the most prevalent neurological disorders among adults aged between 18 and 44 years, affecting approximately one in six people in the USA, with women having twice the likelihood as men [3]. Additionally, migraine is the second largest cause of disability worldwide with significant social and economic burdens [4]. In 2016, migraine was responsible for approximately 4.3 million provider office visits and 1 million emergency department visits in the USA, corresponding to 0.8% and 0.5% of total visits, respectively [3]. Patients who experience migraine attacks miss an average of 4.2 workdays per year [5]. Moreover, the economic burden of individuals with migraine was approximately $8924 greater in terms of healthcare and other indirect costs when compared with demographically similar individuals without migraine [6].

Migraine headaches are categorized as episodic and chronic based on the frequency of events. Episodic migraine (EM) is defined as experiencing less than or equal to 14 monthly headache days and is more prevalent than chronic migraine (CM) [7, 8]. According to the American Headache Society consensus statement, current pharmacological treatments for EM include acute treatments for symptom relief and preventive treatments focused on the reduction of migraine days [9]. All patients with a confirmed diagnosis of EM should be offered acute treatments, and they should be offered preventive treatments if they experience more than six monthly migraine days (MMDs) or have a significant impact on daily activities [9]. The goal of preventive treatment is to reduce the frequency, severity, and duration of migraine attacks, reduce the use of acute medications, and improve function in patients with EM [9]. A clinically successful outcome of prevention treatment is defined as a 50% or greater reduction in MMDs [9].

Conventional preventive treatments of EM include divalproex, metoprolol, propranolol, timolol, and topiramate, which are administered orally [9]. However, these treatments have been associated with more severe side effects and discontinuation and are less effective than newer pharmaceuticals that have recently entered the market [10]. The introduction of novel agents with greater efficacy and minimal side effects offers potential alternatives.

Eptinezumab (Vyepti®) and erenumab (Aimovig®) are calcitonin gene-related peptides (CGRP) monoclonal antibodies with a US Food and Drug Administration-approved indication for EM prevention. Both CGRP monoclonal antibodies have been reported to significantly reduce MMDs in patients with EM with mild adverse event profiles [11, 12]. According to the American Headache Society position statement update in 2024, CGRP monoclonal antibodies were included as one of the first-line preventive treatment options, without requiring prior failure, intolerance, or contraindications to conventional treatments [13]. However, both medications are costly with monthly costs greater than $500 according to the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Federal Supply Schedule (FSS) [14].

Given the high costs of eptinezumab and erenumab and increasing healthcare expenditure in the USA, evaluating the cost effectiveness of these CGRP monoclonal antibodies is important to inform healthcare decision making. Currently, there is a lack of economic evidence comparing eptinezumab and erenumab to existing and less costly first-line treatments such as topiramate, particularly between CGRP monoclonal antibodies with different routes of administration. To address this gap, we conducted a value assessment from the US healthcare payer perspective to evaluate the cost effectiveness of CGRP monoclonal antibodies with different routes of administration (eptinezumab [intravenous], erenumab [self-injectable]) compared with topiramate for individuals with EM.

Methods

Model Structure

A hybrid decision tree-Markov model was constructed to evaluate the cost effectiveness of eptinezumab and erenumab compared to topiramate in patients with EM from the perspective of the US healthcare payer over a 2-year time horizon (Fig. 1). This model was constructed using R Statistical Software (Version 4.5.3; R Core Team, 2025), and the model code is available in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).Fig. 1Model schema of eptinezumab and erenumab in patients who did not respond to prior preventive therapies. The decision tree model started with a hypothetical cohort of adults (aged >18 years) with EM who received eptinezumab, erenumab, and topiramate for the first 6 months. The decision tree model was constructed to simulate the probability of patients having a clinically successful response to eptinezumab, erenumab, and topiramate, which was defined as experiencing a 50% or greater reduction of MMDs over the first 6 months of therapy according to the treatment guidelines of EM published by the American Headache Society [9, 15]. Afterwards, the cohort would enter the Markov model for the remaining 2 years.

The Markov model consisted of three states: “On Preventive Treatment,” “Off Preventive Treatment,” and “Death.” The “On Preventive Treatment” state was defined as receiving migraine preventive treatment and acute treatments for migraine attacks. The “Off Preventive Treatment” state was defined as discontinuing preventive treatments and only receiving acute treatments for migraine attacks. The “Death” state was the absorbing state and was defined when an individual experienced all-cause mortality. Each cycle was 4 weeks in duration, and the time horizon was 2 years.

In the Markov model, individuals who had successful treatment outcomes with the three strategies in the decision tree (achieve a 50% or greater reduction in MMDs) might continue with treatment in the “On Preventative Treatment” state in the first cycle. They would then be able to remain in the “On Preventive Treatment” state, transition to the “Off Preventive Treatment” state, or transition to the “Death” state. Individuals who did not have a successful treatment outcome (did not achieve a 50% or greater reduction in MMDs) would discontinue treatment and enter the “Off Preventive Treatment” state in the first cycle. They would then be able to remain in the “Off Preventive Treatment” state or transition to the “Death” state as we assumed that preventive treatments would not be restarted after subjects had inadequate responses to the initial treatments.

Interventions

We simulated the initial effectiveness of eptinezumab 100 mg, erenumab 70 mg, and topiramate 100 mg with the decision tree model. Eptinezumab was selected because of its intravenous route of administration, whereas erenumab was selected because of its high prevalence of use in the US healthcare sector [16]. Eptinezumab was administered intravenously over 30 minutes every 12 weeks at an outpatient clinic, infusion center, or through home infusion services [17]. In this study, we assumed that individuals on eptinezumab would receive their intravenous infusion at an infusion center. Erenumab, however, was a self-injectable CGRP monoclonal antibody administered every 4 weeks with a prefilled syringe [18]. We assumed that individuals on erenumab would receive their injection through community or specialty pharmacies and would self-administer the medication.

Topiramate was selected as the comparator as it had one of the most established bodies of evidence among conventional migraine prevention treatments, according to the American Headache Society [13]. Moreover, the Italian Society for the Study of Headache and the International Headache Society guidelines determined topiramate as a “strong in favor” treatment [19]. Consistent with guideline recommendations, randomized clinical trials have demonstrated that topiramate reduces migraine frequency and improves quality of life in patients with EM [20]. Topiramate tablets were taken by mouth daily. We assumed that individuals on topiramate would be adherent to the treatment regimen and refill their medication every 90 days.

Clinical Trial Selection

Studies were included if they were randomized controlled trials conducted in patients with EM that reported the required model outcomes (percentage of patients achieving a 50% or greater reduction in MMDs from months 4–6, mean MMD reduction, and discontinuation rate because of adverse events). Based on those criteria, three clinical trials were selected as primary data sources: STRIVE for erenumab; PROMISE-1 for eptinezumab; and HER-MES for erenumab and topiramate, the only head-to-head trial directly comparing erenumab and eptinezumab and reporting endpoints using migraine days [11, 12, 21, 22]. Although HER-MES enrolled both patients with EM and patients with CM, the trial population was predominantly patients with EM, as only 11.0% of participants had 15 or greater baseline MMDs [22]. Therefore, it was considered generally consistent with the EM patient population. Model inputs of topiramate were based on HER-MES [22]. Erenumab model inputs were derived as sample-sized weighted averages of STRIVE and HER-MES to include evidence of effectiveness from both placebo-controlled and head-to-head trials [11, 22]. Model inputs of eptinezumab were based on PROMISE-1 and the PROMISE-1 1-year extension study [12, 21]. Historical randomized controlled trials of topiramate were excluded because migraine frequency was used to report efficacy outcomes [23].

Cohort

A hypothetical cohort of 1000 US adults with a diagnosis of EM of at least 12 months was simulated in this cost-effectiveness analysis. The cohort was assumed to have a distribution of 84% female (16% male) and an average age of 39.8 years based on the PROMISE-1 clinical trial of eptinezumab [12]. The baseline MMD was calculated as a sample size-weighted average of baseline MMDs from pivotal clinical trials of eptinezumab and erenumab in patients with EM [11, 12] (Table S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). Based on the existing literature, we assumed that patient demographics in this model represented the population of patients with EM [24].

Transition Probabilities

In the decision tree portion of the model, the probabilities of patients experiencing successful treatment outcomes were based on the rates of patients experiencing a 50% or greater MMD reduction from baseline over months 4–6 reported in the clinical trials of topiramate, eptinezumab, and erenumab [11, 12, 21, 22]. We assumed that the rates over months 4–6 could be used to estimate the rate of patients achieving successful treatment outcomes at month 6 based on the observation that the percentages of patients achieving a 50% or greater reduction in MMDs were stable across months 4–6 [11].

In the Markov model, we assumed that treatment discontinuation occurred because of adverse events only, as discontinuation because of lack of efficacy was accounted for in the preceding decision tree phase, where only patients achieving a 50% or greater reduction in MMDs entered the “On Preventive Treatment State.” The probability of discontinuing therapy was derived from the discontinuation rate due to adverse effects from clinical trials of erenumab, eptinezumab, and topiramate [11, 12, 21, 22] (Table S4 of the ESM).

We used the 2021 US Life Table to derive the baseline age-specific mortality rate [25]. The annual mortality rate was converted to a monthly probability of death (Table S5 of the ESM). We assumed that EM did not alter the all-cause mortality rate based on a literature review conducted by Schürks et al. [26]. Although this literature review was completed from a global perspective, multiple studies performed in the USA were included, and we assumed that any variations would be minor and would not impact the findings of the model.

According to a prospective cohort study on patients with migraine, MMDs returned to baseline after discontinuing CGRP receptor monoclonal antibodies for 16 weeks [27]. Thus, MMDs were assumed to return to baseline levels upon discontinuation. All transition probabilities were converted to the length of each cycle, which was 4 weeks [28].

Cost

Total expected costs included pharmaceuticals, pharmaceutical administration, migraine rescue medication, and migraine-related healthcare utilization such as hospitalizations, provider office visits, and urgent care visits. All costs were reported in 2025 US dollars. Pharmaceutical costs were based on the US VA FSS pricing, which was recommended by the Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine as a publicly available source estimating pharmaceutical costs [29]. As eptinezumab is administered intravenously, the administration cost of eptinezumab was based on the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2025 Physician Fee Schedule using HCPCS code 96365 (intravenous infusion, for therapy, prophylaxis, or diagnosis) [30]. We assumed there would be no administration cost for erenumab as it is a self-administered subcutaneous injection. Similarly, topiramate is an oral tablet that did not incur any additional administration costs.

Once the hypothetical cohort entered the model, we assumed that individuals who experienced a migraine attack would self-administer a migraine rescue medication such as triptans and analgesics [31]. The cost of rescue medication per migraine attack was based on the existing literature [32]. We use the Clinical Classification Software Refined codes for the Nervous System category (NVS010G) to estimate the cost of migraine-related hospitalization, provider office visits, and emergency department visits from the 2021 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey data [33]. All costs were adjusted to 2025 US dollars using the Consumer Price Index. Probabilities of hospitalization, provider office visits, and emergency department visits per migraine attack were based on a previous study from 2001 (Table 1) [34]. Costs were discounted at a rate of 3% per year. Utility

Utility scores were calculated based on the MMDs of patients, the primary endpoint of pivotal clinical trials of eptinezumab and erenumab [11, 12]. The MMD reductions in treatments were synthesized from the PROMISE-1 trial of eptinezumab, the STRIVE trial of erenumab, and a head-to-head trial comparing erenumab with topiramate [11, 12, 21, 22]. The baseline MMD was defined as the weighted average of the baseline characteristics of those three clinical trials. The utility scores for patients on each treatment were calculated using the formula developed by Jönsson et al. to address the relationship between MMDs and utility scores [35]. This study derived utility scores from Migraine-Specific Quality of Life scores mapped to EQ-5D-3L using the algorithm published by Gillard et al. with the UK value set [36] (Table S6 of the ESM). Utility scores were discounted at a rate of 3% per year.

Statistical Analysis

The discounted total expected cost and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) were estimated for eptinezumab, erenumab, and topiramate over a 2-year horizon. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated by dividing the incremental total costs by the incremental total QALYs of the two CGRP monoclonal antibodies (eptinezumab and erenumab) compared to topiramate. The willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds of this cost-effectiveness analysis were $50,000, $100,000, and $150,000 per QALY gained, as this approach was adopted by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review report on CGRP inhibitors and other economic analyses on migraine prevention treatments [37–39]. Incremental net monetary benefit (INMB) was estimated across the WTP thresholds.

Sensitivity Analyses

In the one-way sensitivity analyses, the base-case pharmaceutical costs, utility scores of “On Preventive Treatment” state in the Markov model, discontinuation rates, and the probabilities of achieving a 50% or greater reduction in MMDs were varied to test for the robustness of the model’s findings. The high and low ranges of these parameters were estimated using ±15% of the base-case values (Table 1). A tornado diagram was generated to visualize the impact of each parameter on the INMB.

A probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) with 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations was performed to assess the impact of parameter uncertainty on our model’s findings. Parameters were assigned a distribution based on the data type. Costs were assigned a gamma distribution; transition probabilities were assigned a beta distribution; MMD reductions were assigned a normal distribution. We used the standard errors to generate the distributions for the parameters in our models when available (e.g., MMD reduction). Distributions of MMD reduction for each treatment strategy were derived from clinical trial data [11, 12, 21, 22]. As the distribution of MMD reduction of eptinezumab was not reported in the 1-year extension of PROMISE-1, we used the distribution from week 1 to 12 as a proxy [12]. The standard errors of the remaining parameters were estimated to be 25% of the parameter values. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis results were presented as the average ICER with its corresponding 95% confidence interval. A cost-effectiveness acceptability curve was created to evaluate the probability of each treatment’s cost effectiveness across a WTP threshold of $0–$300,000 per QALY gained.

Scenario Analyses

Given that the listed price was not the actual price paid by payers, we performed a scenario analysis where we estimated that the actual price of brand-name medications would be lower than the base-case price. According to a report by the Congressional Budget Office, the estimate actual acquisition costs of a brand name drug is approximately 41% off the VA FSS price, which was the base-case price used in our model [40]. In the scenario analysis, we adjusted the cost of eptinezumab and erenumab to reflect 41% off the FSS listed price, provided that they remained a branded product without biosimilar competitors during the study time horizon. We calculated the INMB, repeated the one-way sensitivity analyses (displayed findings in a tornado diagram), and repeated the PSA. For the one-way sensitivity analyses, we followed the same methods previously described. For the PSA, the parameters were assigned using the same distributions as previously described.

We conducted another scenario analysis, considering disutilities related to subcutaneous injections and intravenous infusions. However, we did not identify any literature reporting disutility associated with an intravenous infusion for migraine prevention. As an alternative, the disutilities were based on a time trade-off interview that estimated the impact of different treatment modalities of bisphosphonates on patients with cancer [41]. The average disutilities associated with a monthly 30-minute infusion and subcutaneous injection were 0.023 and 0.004, respectively [41]. As eptinezumab was administered every 3 months, we assumed that the disutility would be 0.008, which was one third of the disutility reported in the literature. We applied those utilities to the Markov state “On Preventive Treatment” for eptinezumab and erenumab. For the one-way sensitivity analysis, the same methods as the base case were applied, except that the disutility was modified by ±50%.

The third scenario analysis considered the disutilities of adverse effects due to the interventions. Matza et al. studied the disutilities of adverse events associated with migraine preventive medications [42]. Common adverse events associated with CGRP monoclonal antibodies included upper respiratory tract infection, injection site pain, and pruritus. The disutilities of these adverse events were 0.005, 0.015, and 0.001, respectively, according to Matza et al. [42]. The most common adverse reactions of topiramate 100 mg were paresthesia, anorexia, and weight loss [43]. The only disutility available in the literature was 0.013 for paresthesia [42]. The average disutilities were applied to each intervention. For the one-way sensitivity analysis, the same methods as the base case were applied, except that the disutility was varied by ±50%.

As alternative dosing regimens are available for CGRP monoclonal antibodies, a scenario analysis was conducted using eptinezumab 300 mg every 3 months and erenumab 140 mg monthly. The modified parameters are reported in Table S11 of the ESM. To assess the impact of longer time horizons, scenario analyses were conducted using 5- and 10-year time horizons. All model inputs and assumptions remained the same as the base case with an increased number of model cycles. Additional scenario analyses were also performed by varying baseline MMDs between the minimum and maximum values reported in the selected clinical trials to investigate the impact of the baseline MMD on model outcomes [11, 12, 22].

Model Validation

Internal validation of the model was evaluated for logical inconsistencies and errors. Using extreme ranges, we generated a model output that we compared to expected outcomes. For example, we maximized the utility scores of the utility parameters to generate an expected QALY of 2 (maximum possible) given the 2-year time horizon in the base case.

To assess external validity, model-predicted discontinuation rates were compared with available real-world evidence. Our model predicted that the 1-year treatment persistence rate was 19.8% for topiramate, 46.7% for erenumab, and 58.9% for eptinezumab (including the initial 6-month decision tree phase). Recently published US claims data analyses reported that the 12-month persistence for oral migraine preventives was 14–22%, and the modeled topiramate persistence was consistent with available real-world evidence [44, 45]. The modeled erenumab persistence of 46.7% was broadly consistent with US real-world claim data reporting 40.1% persistence at 12 months [46]. Direct real-world persistence data for eptinezumab at 12 months were unavailable. However, Charleston et al. found that subcutaneous CGRP monoclonal antibodies had a significantly higher treatment discontinuation hazard than eptinezumab [47]. Thus, the higher modeled persistence of eptinezumab (58.9%) than erenumab (46.7%) was consistent with this finding. Overall, the model-derived persistence was in line with published real-world data in the USA and supported the validity of the model.

Results

Deterministic Findings

Table 2 summarizes the base-case results of the cost-effectiveness analysis, including discounted total expected cost, total expected QALYs, and ICERs,. The total expected costs of eptinezumab were $21,767 over a 2-year time horizon, which were $28 more than the total expected cost of erenumab. Pharmaceutical costs of eptinezumab and erenumab were 51.8% and 52.3% of the total expected costs, respectively. However, the pharmaceutical cost of topiramate only accounted for 0.7% of the total expected costs. The total expected QALYs gained for the eptinezumab, erenumab, and topiramate groups were 1.32 QALYs, 1.30 QALYs, and 1.25 QALYs, respectively. When compared to topiramate, eptinezumab had an ICER of $117,626 per QALY gained, and erenumab has an ICER of $155,259 per QALY gained. Erenumab was excluded from the cost-effectiveness frontier by extended dominance, as it had a high ICER versus topiramate compared to eptinezumab (Fig. S1 of the ESM). The base-case INMB of eptinezumab compared to topiramate was – $4853, – $1265, and $2323 at a WTP threshold of $50,000, $100,000, and $150,000 per QALY gained, respectively. Costs associated with high-dose erenumab were similar to base-case pharmaceutical costs with an ICER of $137,350 per QALY gained, meeting the WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained.

Eptinezumab was associated with ICERs of $78,878 and $67,394 per QALY gained with 5- and 10-year time horizons, respectively, compared with topiramate, and was cost effective at a WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained. Erenumab was associated with ICERs of $99,940 and $84,588 per QALY gained with 5- and 10-year time horizons, respectively, compared with topiramate. Erenumab became a cost-effective strategy with 5- and 10-year horizons. Scenario analyses using alternative baseline MMD values showed a minimal impact on the ICER, as incremental differences between treatments remained consistent across different baseline MMDs.

Discussion

Summary of Findings

Despite reductions in the frequency of migraine attacks and healthcare utilization in a simulated population with EM by both eptinezumab and erenumab, our findings suggest that eptinezumab was the only CGRP monoclonal antibody that was considered cost effective compared to topiramate at a WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained. Erenumab was not a cost-effective strategy because of elimination by extended dominance (more costs and less benefits generated compared with eptinezumab). However, our findings were sensitive to the assumptions and parameters used in our model. In the scenario analysis of exploring alternative doses, the ICER for eptinezumab 300 mg increased substantially, which was primarily driven by higher pharmaceutical costs, with a modest incremental improvement in MMD. After increasing the dose of erenumab from 70 mg to 140 mg, the ICER of erenumab was less than $150,000 per QALY gained, driven by the increase in efficacy. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution and careful consideration of the context in which they are applied.

One of the goals for performing this cost-utility analysis was to compare migraine prevention treatments with different routes of administration. Eptinezumab is an intravenous infusion that is administered every 3 months. To receive this medication, patients would need to schedule an appointment with an infusion center or home infusion service. This can have a substantial impact on the outcomes because eptinezumab would be associated with a higher burden on society, which would result in productivity loss due to missed work. In contrast, erenumab is a monthly self-administered medication, and its administration would not have as large an impact on society. Despite being an intravenous infusion, eptinezumab was cost effective when compared to topiramate whereas erenumab was not. This was likely due to lower drug costs and greater benefits (increased QALYs) associated with eptinezumab compared with erenumab. These findings likely would change when considering the productivity loss due to infusion treatment associated with eptinezumab, which was not part of our base-case analysis. However, the potential lost productivity due to infusion treatment may be offset by the reduction in absenteeism and presenteeism generated by eptinezumab [48]. Further work will need to be conducted to understand the impact intravenous treatment with eptinezumab would have on the study’s outcomes.

Comparison with the Existing Literature

During the time this investigation was performed, no other study, to our knowledge, comprehensively evaluated the cost effectiveness of eptinezumab and erenumab compared to topiramate in the USA. However, several value assessments evaluated these CGRP monoclonal antibodies independently. For instance, one study compared eptinezumab with placebo in patients with EM and CM from the Taiwan health payer’s perspective and concluded that eptinezumab is a cost-effective strategy. [49] Another economic evaluation in patients who did not respond to previous preventive migraine treatments reported that eptinezumab is a cost-effective strategy compared to supportive care from a societal perspective in the UK [50]. Although these studies included a mix of patients with EM and CM, our findings were broadly similar despite the focus on EM.

Regarding erenumab, published value assessments reported ICER values between $180,012 and $395,000 per QALY gained, suggesting a wide range of factors contributed to this variability. For instance, Sussman et al. reported that erenumab has an ICER of $180,012 per QALY gained compared to no preventive treatment in patients with EM from the US payer’s perspective [38]; whereas, the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review reported that erenumab has an ICER of $395,000 per QALY gained compared to other first-line oral medications for EM prevention [39]. Compared with these studies, our finding comparing erenumab to topiramate was on the lower end of this range (ICER = $155,259 per QALY gained). The discrepancies in ICERs between this current analysis and published studies are likely attributed to differences in comparators and sources of discontinuation rates. First, different comparators were used in our study and published studies. For instance, the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review compared erenumab with active preventive treatments (amitriptyline, propranolol, and topiramate), and Sussman et al. compared erenumab with no preventive treatment; whereas, we compared erenumab to topiramate. Second, treatment discontinuation was handled differently in our study and published studies. Both Sussman et al. and the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review applied a single all-cause discontinuation rate derived from clinical trials, capturing discontinuation because of lack of efficacy and adverse events; whereas our model, captured discontinuation because of lack of efficacy in the decision tree phase. This resulted in a larger absolute difference in the probability of discontinuation between erenumab and topiramate, which better reflected real-world data as evident in our external validation [44–46]. Despite these methodological differences, our results aligned with the two studies by ultimately concluding that erenumab was not cost effective from a US healthcare payer perspective.

Although our study focused on eptinezumab (because of it being administered intravenously) and erenumab (which is a self-injectable), other CGRP monoclonal antibodies have been Food and Drug Administration approved for EM but were not evaluated in this study. For instance, Takeshima et al. concluded that fermanezumab is associated with improved outcomes and reduced healthcare utilization compared with standard of care from a Japanese public healthcare payer perspective in patients with CM and EM [51]. Canada’s Drug Agency reimbursement review found that galcanezumab has an ICER of $273,560 per QALY gained compared to best supportive care over a 5-year time horizon in EM from a Canadian public payer perspective [52]. We opted to use erenumab to represent the self-injectable CGRP monoclonal antibodies owing to its high prevalence of use in the USA [16]. However, policymakers should consider these other self-injectable CGRP monoclonal antibodies assuming their effectiveness is similar to erenumab. Moreover, future studies should consider performing a comprehensive cost-effectiveness analysis of all CGRP monoclonal antibodies to better inform formulary decision making for migraine prevention treatments.

Implications in Practice and Other Considerations

Our analysis provides much-needed information for policymakers on determining coverage of migraine prevention medications for patients with EM and decisions related to formulary management and price negotiations. Decision makers may be able to use our findings to assist with price negotiations for these CGRP monoclonal antibodies for EM.

Other considerations, such as route of administration, patient preferences, and provider preferences, are vital in making coverage and reimbursement decisions. Additionally, individual patient-level response and the potential discounts and rebates for each medication are key elements to consider for decision makers, particularly as there were high degrees of uncertainty associated with health-state utilities for both strategies and the pharmaceutical cost of eptinezumab.

Limitations

Our findings were based on modeling future costs and outcomes, which inherently included limitations that require discussion. This analysis relied on treatment efficacy estimates derived from separate clinical trials and therefore involved naïve cross-trial comparison. Differences in baseline characteristics across trials, particularly MMDs, may act as treatment-effect modifiers and influence observed treatment outcomes. Although indirect treatment comparison or network meta-analyses could help account for variabilities between clinical trials, available evidence was limited and reported class-level estimates of CGRP monoclonal antibodies rather than drug-specific estimates [53]. As a result, inputs from those studies were not incorporated, and this remained a source of uncertainty in this model.

Erenumab model inputs were derived as a sample size-weighted average of STRIVE and HER-MES [11, 22]. HER-MES used a flexible dosing approach with a mean erenumab dose of 119 mg rather than the 70-mg dosing [22]. This approach might overestimate efficacy of erenumab 70 mg and reduce its ICER. The base-case conclusion that erenumab was not cost effective at a WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained further supported our conservative approach and the robustness of our findings. Additionally, HER-MES used investigator-titrated dosing for topiramate, with a mean dose of 92 mg rather than the 100-mg dose [22]. Dose titration reflected real-world prescribing practice and the mean dose was considered sufficiently reflective of the 100-mg dose.

Moreover, uncertainties in future biosimilars and increasing market competition contribute to the challenges of modeling with a longer time horizon. Data for hospitalizations, provider visits, and ED visits per migraine attack were based on data from 2001. Since then, treatment of EM likely impacted the practice and thereby the probabilities of resource utilization. Further studies using real-world data will be needed to update these parameters to accurately reflect current practice.

The model structure also involves certain assumptions that should be considered when interpreting the results. Treatment switching is possible, but this was not modeled in our study. However, given the short time horizon of 2 years, we speculate that switching’s impact on the model’s conclusion may be minimal. Further studies using real-world switching data will need to be generated to determine the impact of switching on a value assessment.

In this model, health state utilities were estimated using a mapping function developed by Jönsson et al. [35]. Alternative methodologies, such as assigning a utility score to different MMD frequency categories, may provide different utility estimates [36]. However, these MMD categories may not be as sensitive to capturing changes in MMDs that might impact the utility outcomes. Thus, we opted to adopt the mapping function by Jönsson et al. to capture utility differences with different MMDs.

This cost-effectiveness analysis only modeled the cost effectiveness of erenumab and eptinezumab in patients with EM. Future studies investigating the cost effectiveness of those two medications in patients with CM need to be done to provide health policy decision makers with the full landscape of the migraine disease state. This study adopted a US healthcare payer perspective pooled across multiple payer types. However, variations in reimbursement rates and healthcare resource utilization across different US healthcare payers and the presence of confidential rebates may limit the generalizability of results to individual payers. Uncertainty associated with pharmaceutical costs was explored in the scenario analysis reflecting potential price negotiations.

Conclusions

A cost-utility analysis was conducted to evaluate the value of CGRP monoclonal antibodies (eptinezumab and erenumab) compared to topiramate in individuals with EM from the US payer perspective across a 2-year time horizon. Eptinezumab, erenumab, and topiramate were able to reduce the frequency of migraine attacks in patients with EM and improve their quality of life, but erenumab was eliminated because of having a higher ICER than the next best alternative. The findings from this value assessment indicated that eptinezumab might be a cost-effective strategy to EM prevention compared to topiramate. However, these findings were sensitive to the uncertainty in treatment effects and pharmaceutical costs. Policymakers should carefully consider these factors when making decisions regarding formulary coverage for the EM population.
