Three-Dimensional Morphometric Analysis of the Maxillary Sinus-Nasal Complex in Adolescents With Unilateral Cleft Lip and Palate Using Cone‑Beam Computed Tomography
Authors: Man Kin Charlotte Lam, Ying-Qi Liu, Yi-Man Wang, Xiao-Le Wang, Yiu Yan Leung, Kuo Feng Hung, Jian Li, Jie-Gang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUnilateral cleft lip and palate (UCLP) often causes craniofacial asymmetry and maxillary growth disturbance, but differences in the maxillary sinus-nasal complex between cleft and noncleft sides remain unclear. This study used cone‑beam computed tomography (CBCT) to evaluate three‑dimensional features of the maxillary sinus, nasal septum, and nasolacrimal duct in adolescents with UCLP.MethodsPreoperative CBCT scans of 50 nonsyndromic adolescents with UCLP aged 9 to 15 years were retrospectively analysed. The maxillary sinus height, width, depth, volume, primary ostium opening diameter, mucosal thickness and morphology, nasal septal deviation angle, and nasolacrimal duct dimensions and patency were assessed by two observers. Paired comparisons were performed between the cleft and noncleft sides.ResultsThe cleft side demonstrated significantly reduced maxillary sinus height (26.10 ± 4.42 vs 28.60 ± 3.57 mm; P < .0001) and volume (12.50 ± 3.92 vs 13.09 ± 3.74 cm³; P = .0079) compared with the noncleft side. In contrast, no significant interside differences were observed in sinus width, depth, or primary ostium diameter. Nasal septal deviation toward the cleft side was identified in all patients. No significant differences were found between sides in sinus mucosal thickness, mucosal morphology, or nasolacrimal duct patency.ConclusionsAdolescents with UCLP exhibit asymmetry of the maxillary sinus-nasal complex, characterized primarily by reduced maxillary sinus height and volume on the cleft side together with consistent nasal septal deviation toward the cleft side. These findings provide further insight into craniofacial developmental characteristics in UCLP and may support more targeted multidisciplinary treatment planning during adolescence.Key words: Unilateral cleft lip and palate, CBCT, Maxillary sinus, Nasal septal deviation, Craniofacial asymmetry

Introduction

Cleft lip and/or palate (CLP) is one of the most common congenital craniofacial anomalies, with a global prevalence of approximately 1.7 per 1000 live births.1 It affects the oral and maxillofacial regions in varying combinations and degrees of severity and is commonly associated with disturbed maxillary growth, resulting in midfacial hypoplasia and potential alterations in adjacent anatomical structures, including the maxillary sinus, nasal cavity, nasolacrimal system and dentoalveolar complex.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Among its clinical subtypes, unilateral cleft lip and palate (UCLP) is particularly associated with asymmetrical craniofacial development in addition to the abovementioned cleft deformity.10,11 As a result, patients with UCLP often exhibit craniofacial asymmetry that extends beyond the cleft site itself.

Previous anatomical and radiographic studies in UCLP have mainly focused on abnormalities directly related to the cleft, such as labial, alveolar, and nasal deformities.2,4,10,12, 13, 14 Whereas the effects of UCLP on other maxillofacial structures remain insufficiently characterized. Given that maxillofacial development is an integrated biological and structural process, alterations in adjacent anatomical structures may not only contribute to the development or clinical presentation of the primary deformities, but may also carry important aesthetic and functional implications. Particularly, upper airway-related symptoms observed in patients with UCLP may be associated with impaired sinus drainage, altered nasal airflow, and an increased susceptibility to rhinosinusitis.15, 16, 17, 18

Despite increasing imaging-based studies on maxillary sinus morphology in UCLP, characterization of sinus asymmetry between the cleft and noncleft sides remains inconclusive.12,19,20 Some investigations have reported reduced sinus volume and altered morphology on the cleft side,19,21, 22, 23 whereas others have found minimal interside differences.24,25 Moreover, many previous studies relied on relatively limited or nonstandardised imaging analyses, potentially restricting spatial accuracy and comprehensive evaluation of adjacent anatomical structures. Current literature still lacks an integrated three-dimensional assessment of the maxillary sinus-nasal complex in UCLP. These inconsistencies may therefore be partly attributable to methodological heterogeneity, differences in imaging precision and demographic variability across studies, highlighting the need for further standardized morphometric evaluation.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is an essential imaging modality for three-dimensional evaluation of the maxillofacial region, offering high spatial resolution and accurate visualization of complex anatomical structures without the superimposition inherent to conventional two-dimensional imaging.26,27 This approach is particularly well suited for the analysis of highly irregular maxillofacial anatomy and provides a reliable method for investigating asymmetrical deformities in UCLP.28,29 Accordingly, the present study applied a standardized CBCT-based three-dimensional morphometric approach to comprehensively evaluate the maxillary sinus-nasal complex and related anatomical structures in adolescents with UCLP. Using reproducible anatomical landmarks and measurement protocols, this approach enabled a more integrated characterization of craniofacial asymmetry and anatomical variation associated with UCLP, consistent with the increasing application of quantitative CBCT-based morphometric analyses in cleft research.14,30,31

Taken together, the present study therefore aimed to clarify the anatomical characteristics and interside asymmetry of the maxillary sinus-nasal complex in adolescents with UCLP. A better understanding of these developmental alterations may contribute to more refined multidisciplinary treatment planning during adolescence, including alveolar bone grafting, orthodontic and orthognathic intervention, airway management, and implant rehabilitation.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study included preoperative CBCT scans of 50 patients aged between 9 and 15 years who were diagnosed with UCLP. The scans were obtained from Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University between 2020 and 2024. All included patients had previously undergone primary cleft lip and palate repair at an appropriate early age but had not yet received alveolar cleft repair at the time of imaging. According to the institutional treatment protocol, primary cleft lip repair was routinely performed between 3 and 6 months of age, followed by primary palate repair between 8 and 12 months of age. All patients were managed according to a standardized institutional cleft treatment protocol, thereby minimizing variability related to the timing and principles of primary surgical repair.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 9 and 15 years at the time of CBCT acquisition; (2) nonsyndromic UCLP, without repair of alveolar cleft; (3) completion of primary cleft lip and palate repair; and (4) availability of high-quality preoperative CBCT data suitable for morphometric analysis. Patients with bilateral clefts or associated syndromic conditions were excluded to achieve reliable evaluation. To minimize selection bias, all consecutive patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were retrospectively screened and included in the analysis.

All CBCT examinations were performed as part of routine preoperative assessment for alveolar cleft repair and were not acquired specifically for research purposes. Therefore, no additional radiation exposure was incurred for this study. The indication for imaging was clinically justified in accordance with current radiographic guidelines and the principles of radiation protection principles. Ethical approval for this retrospective study was obtained from the Ethical Committee of Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University (Approval No.: WDKQ2024-B98). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ legal guardians, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Analysis of CBCT image

All CBCT scans were obtained using NewTom 5G CBCT scanner (NewTom, Verona, Italy). Imaging was performed with a field-of-view of 18 cm × 16 cm covering the entire bilateral maxillary sinuses and a voxel size of 0.3 mm3, a tube voltage of 110 kV, and tube current of 4 mA, with an exposure time of 16 seconds. All patients were positioned according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol to ensure consistent and reproducible head orientation during image acquisition.

The image datasets were exported in Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine (DICOM) format and analysed using ITK-SNAP software (version 4.4.0; Cognitica, Philadelphia, USA). Before analysis, all patient identifiers were removed to ensure examiner blinding and data confidentiality. The deidentified datasets were then independently evaluated by two examiners with clinical experience in maxillofacial imaging and cleft-related anatomical assessment. Any disagreement was resolved through consultation with a third senior examiner until consensus was reached prior to final data analysis.

All measurements were performed using standardized multiplanar reconstruction views. Prior to measurement, each dataset was reoriented according to reproducible anatomical reference planes to ensure consistency and comparability across cases. Standardized anatomical landmarks and measurement protocols were applied throughout the analysis to improve reproducibility.

Anatomical measurement

The examiners independently assessed all CBCT scans for the following parameters to evaluate interobserver agreement. They were allowed to navigate freely through axial, coronal, and sagittal planes and to adjust image contrast and brightness as needed. All measurements were conducted based on following predefined anatomical landmarks (Figure 1) and standardized protocols to ensure consistency and reproducibility across cases. To assess intraobserver agreement, one examiner repeated all assessments on a randomly selected 20% of the scans after a washout period of 2 weeks.

To ensure reproducible measurement of maxillary sinus height, the primary maxillary sinus ostium was selected as the superior landmark rather than a subjectively chosen point on the sloping sinus roof. The primary ostium is located on the superior medial wall of the maxillary sinus, at or just inferior to the level of the orbital floor and can be reproducibly identified on CBCT images. Maxillary sinus height was measured on the coronal plane as the vertical distance from the inferior margin of the primary ostium to the sinus floor (Figure 1A). Sinus width and depth were measured on the axial plane at the level corresponding to the infraorbital canal (Figure 1B). The maximum mediolateral (width) and anteroposterior (depth) dimensions were recorded for each sinus.

2. Maxillary sinus volumetric analysis

2. Maxillary sinus volumetric analysis

Maxillary sinus volume was determined using a semiautomated segmentation protocol described in previous studies.32,33 On each CBCT scan, the maxillary sinus cavity was segmented using a region-growing approach. Presegmentation was performed in edge-attraction mode with smoothing selected by visual assessment of sinus borders on multiplanar images. A bubble was initialized within the sinus cavity and evolved until the cavity was filled. Segmentation was confirmed after assessing morphological consistency, with manual contour adjustments performed when necessary. Eventually, the total sinus volume was automatically calculated by the software (Figure 1C). Mucosal thickening within the sinus cavity was included in the volumetric measurement and was not digitally subtracted.

3. Primary ostium opening

3. Primary ostium opening

The diameter of the primary maxillary sinus ostium was measured on the coronal plane as the maximum horizontal distance across the ostium opening (Figure 1D).

4. Nasal septal deviation

4. Nasal septal deviation

Nasal septal deviation was assessed by measuring the maximum perpendicular displacement of the nasal septum from the defined midline. The midline was established by a line connecting the midpoint of the ethmoid bone crista galli to the junction between the vomer and palatal bone on the noncleft side, as the palatal bone on the cleft side may be discontinuous or absent (Figure 1E). Both bony and mucosa components of septal deviations were recorded.

5. Mucosal thickening

5. Mucosal thickening

Maxillary sinus mucosal thickening was evaluated on the coronal plane. The maximum perpendicular thickness of the Schneiderian membrane was measured at its greatest dimension within the sinus cavity (Figure 1E). In accordance with previous studies,34,35 the mucosal status of each maxillary sinus was classified according to radiographic thickness and morphological appearance on the CBCT images as follows: (1) inconspicuous or up to 4 mm thickening; (2) flat, shallow thickening greater than 4 mm; (3) semispherical thickening suggestive of a mucous retention cyst; (4) complete sinus opacification; (5) mixed flat and semispherical thickening; and (6) other findings, such as bone destruction, cystic lesion, foreign body, or suspected neoplasia. Each sinus was labelled according to the corresponding classification category for further analysis.

6. Nasolacrimal duct dimensions and patency

6. Nasolacrimal duct dimensions and patency

The mediolateral (coronal) and anteroposterior diameters of the nasolacrimal duct were measured at the level of the orbital floor on the coronal plane (Figure 1F). Measurements were obtained at the widest visible portion of the duct lumen. Nasolacrimal duct patency was assessed at the same level. A visible luminal space with a measurable diameter (>0 mm) was considered patent, whereas complete luminal soft tissue filling with no measurable lumen (0 mm) was classified as nonpatent.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. A P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. As measurements of the cleft and noncleft sides were obtained from the same individuals (n = 50), paired comparisons were conducted. The normality of paired differences for each continuous variable was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to statistical analysis. Variables satisfying the assumption of normality were analysed using the paired t-test, whereas variables that did not meet this assumption were analysed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test. The ordinal variable of maxillary sinus mucosal classification was also compared between the cleft and noncleft sides using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test, whereas the binary variable of nasolacrimal duct patency was analysed using McNemar’s test. All statistical tests were two-tailed. All measurements were independently performed by two calibrated examiners. Inter and intraobserver reliability for continuous variables were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), whereas agreement for categorical variables was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ). As each morphometric parameter represented a predefined anatomical outcome of independent clinical interest, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

Results

CBCT scans from a total of 50 patients with UCLP were included and analysed in the study, including 39 males and 11 females. The mean age at the time of CBCT acquisition was 11.1 ± 1.4 years, with an age range of 9 to 15 years. Among them, right-sided clefts were observed in 22 patients, whereas 28 patients had left-sided clefts (Table 1). Excellent measurement reproducibility was observed throughout the study. Inter-rater agreement for quantitative measurements ranged from ICC = 0.757 to 0.988, while categorical assessments demonstrated Cohen’s κ values of 0.889 for maxillary sinus mucosal classification and 0.915 for nasolacrimal duct patency. Similarly, intraobserver agreement was excellent for all quantitative measurements (ICC = 0.884-0.989), with Cohen’s κ values of 0.913 and 0.977 for maxillary sinus mucosal classification and nasolacrimal duct patency, respectively.

Comparison of maxillary sinus morphology between the cleft and noncleft sides

Paired comparisons demonstrated that the maxillary sinus height on the cleft side (26.10 ± 4.42 mm) was significantly smaller than that of the noncleft side (28.60 ± 3.57 mm) (P < .0001) (Figure 2A, B and Table 2). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two sides in terms of sinus width (26.3 ± 2.7 mm vs 26.2 ± 3.6 mm; P = .7683) or depth (35.2 ± 3.0 mm vs 35.3 ± 2.8 mm; P = .9599) (Figure 2C, D and Table 2). Similarly, the diameter of the primary ostium opening did not differ significantly between the cleft and noncleft sides (1.16 ± 0.52 mm vs 1.19 ± 0.52 mm; P = .6185) (Figure 2E and Table 2).

Nasal septal deviation

All patients presented with varying degrees of nasal septal deviation. Septal deviation was identified in all patients and was consistently directed toward the cleft side. The mean deviation angle of the bony nasal septum was 13.90° ± 5.05° (range: 2.70°-22.70°), while the mean deviation angle including both bony and mucosal tissue components was 18.00° ± 5.46° (range: 6.70°-30.40°) (Figure 4 and Table 3).

No significant difference was observed in the maxillary sinus mucosal thickness between the cleft side and noncleft side (6.12 ± 6.68 mm vs 6.64 ± 6.94 mm; P = .4498) (Figure 5A and Table 2).

The majority of maxillary sinuses exhibited either normal mucosa or mild thickening (≤4 mm), accounting for 54% of cases on the cleft side and 60% on the noncleft side (Table 2, Table 4). The remaining cases were distributed among different morphological patterns, including flat, semispherical, and mixed types of mucosal thickening, as well as complete sinus opacification. Notably, complete sinus opacification was identified in 4 maxillary sinuses (8%) on the cleft side and 7 (14%) on the noncleft side (Table 2, Table 4). Nasolacrimal duct morphology and patency

In the bony nasolacrimal duct measurements, the coronal width on the noncleft side was significantly greater than that on the cleft side (4.02 ± 0.76 mm vs 4.35 ± 0.78 mm; P = .0004) (Figure 6A, B and Table 2), while no significant difference was observed in the sagittal depth between the two sides (Figure 6C and Table 2) (P = .6606). Regarding soft tissue patency of the nasolacrimal duct, the patency rate was 40% on the cleft side and 44% on the noncleft side, with no statistically significant difference (P = .5690) (Table 2).

Discussion

The present study provided a comprehensive three-dimensional radiographic assessment of the maxillary sinus-nasal complex and related structures in adolescent patients with UCLP. The present study showed distinct anatomical differences between the cleft and noncleft sides of the maxillary sinus-nasal complex, primarily reflected by reduced maxillary sinus height and volume on the cleft side. In addition, nasal septal deviation was observed in all patients, and the severity of deviation was quantitatively assessed. By contrast, no significant interside differences were found in mucosal status, including mucosal thickness and morphology, or in nasolacrimal duct patency. Together, these findings suggest that anatomical differences between the cleft and noncleft sides are not uniformly distributed across the craniofacial complex, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of the developmental characteristics associated with UCLP.

It has been suggested that the development and physiological function of the maxillary sinus in cleft patients may be impaired due to altered craniofacial growth patterns, discontinuity of the alveolar process and hard palate, and abnormal nasal airflow dynamics.36,37 In healthy populations, previous CBCT studies have reported an average maxillary sinus volume of approximately 15 cm³ in adults and around 12 cm³ in adolescents,20,38,39 while age-related changes in maxillary skeletal dimensions during adolescence have also been well documented.40 According to a recent systematic review, the pooled maxillary sinus volume on the cleft sides of patients with cleft lip and palate was found to be 9.43 cm3 (95% confidence interval (CI), 7.45-11.41), which was significantly smaller than that of healthy individuals.12 The mean maxillary sinus volumes observed in the present study fall within the broad range reported in the literature and provide further support for the presence of reduced sinus volume on the cleft side, while also illustrating the considerable variability among individual cohorts. However, these studies also highlighted the considerable anatomical complexity and heterogeneity of maxillary sinus morphology among cleft patients, suggesting that published normative values should primarily serve as contextual reference rather than direct benchmarks for comparison.

Therefore, although several studies have attempted to evaluate interside differences in maxillary sinus morphology in patients with UCLP, conclusions remain controversial.12,19,21, 22, 23, 24, 25 Rodrigues ML et al22 reported a tendency for the maxillary sinus volume on the cleft side to be smaller than that on the contralateral side, whereas Yassaei et al19 found no significant volumetric differences between the cleft and noncleft sides. In the present study, we revealed that maxillary sinus volume was significantly reduced on the cleft side compared with the noncleft side. The discrepancies among previous studies may be related to differences in sample size, ethnic and demographic composition, cleft severity, age distribution, imaging modality, and segmentation methodology. In particular, variations in imaging protocols and measurement approaches may substantially influence volumetric assessment and contribute to the inconsistent findings currently reported in the literature.

Compared with previous studies primarily focusing on maxillary sinus volume, the present study additionally assessed sinus width, height, and depth. Clinically, unilateral cleft deformity is commonly associated with sagittal maxillary hypoplasia and facial asymmetry.41 However, despite the reduced sinus volume observed on the cleft side, no significant difference in anteroposterior sinus depth was identified between the cleft and noncleft sides. Instead, the volumetric reduction was primarily associated with decreased sinus height rather than alterations in width or depth. These findings suggests that the sagittal asymmetry commonly observed in UCLP may not reflect a reduction in sinus depth, but rather a posterior displacement of the sinus on the cleft side. Although these observations were derived from morphometric measurements, they may have several clinically relevant implications for the multidisciplinary management of patients with UCLP. First, the reduced sinus height on the cleft side may reflect impaired vertical development of the ipsilateral maxilla and therefore warrants consideration during preoperative CBCT assessment. This anatomical feature may provide useful information for surgical planning of alveolar bone grafting, sinus floor augmentation, implant rehabilitation, and orthognathic surgery by improving the understanding of local bone morphology and potential anatomical limitations.40,42 Likewise, recognition of vertical asymmetry may assist orthodontic treatment planning, particularly when individualized vertical control strategies are required in patients with severe maxillary asymmetry. Second, the present findings may provide additional anatomical rationale for emerging treatment strategies such as adolescent trans-suture distraction osteogenesis (TDSO). Rather than suggesting that sagittal maxillary deficiency results from reduced sinus depth, our results indicate that the cleft-side maxillary sinus is more likely displaced posteriorly while maintaining a comparable anteroposterior dimension. This observation supports the concept that correction of maxillary asymmetry may be achieved through repositioning of the posterior maxillary and pterygomaxillary complex rather than expansion of the maxillary sinus itself. Furthermore, the magnitude of sinus asymmetry may provide useful anatomical reference during virtual surgical planning by facilitating individualized repositioning of the cleft-side maxillary segment according to its inherent developmental characteristics. Third, pronounced vertical sinus asymmetry was consistently observed in adolescents aged 9 to 15 years, despite previous primary lip and palate repair. This finding suggests that early soft-tissue reconstruction alone may not completely restore congenital disturbances in maxillary skeletal development and highlights the importance of continued craniofacial monitoring throughout adolescence. Mechanistically, the reduced sinus height may be associated with disruption of the structural continuity between the alveolar process and piriform aperture, leading to altered biomechanical support and vertical growth patterns, while asymmetric occlusal loading may further contribute to these developmental alterations. Nevertheless, these mechanisms remain speculative and require further experimental validation. Future longitudinal studies correlating sinus morphometric findings with orthodontic, surgical, and functional outcomes are warranted to determine whether CBCT-derived morphometric parameters may serve as clinically useful imaging biomarkers for individualized treatment planning. Nevertheless, the present findings already provide clinically relevant anatomical information for multidisciplinary cleft care. Improved characterization of vertical maxillary asymmetry may assist orthodontists in treatment planning and surgeons in evaluating anatomical constraints before alveolar bone grafting, orthognathic surgery, and implant rehabilitation, while objective CBCT-derived morphometric information may facilitate communication and coordinated decision-making among members of the cleft care team throughout adolescence.

Previous studies evaluating maxillary sinus volume in patients with UCLP have reported inconsistent findings. Some investigations, particularly those employing semiautomated segmentation methods using ITK-SNAP, have demonstrated reduced sinus volume on the cleft side, consistent with the present results.22,23 In contrast, other studies have reported no statistically significant interside differences.19,21,24 These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in sample characteristics, particularly age distribution, as well as variations in imaging protocols, segmentation techniques, and examiner-related factors. According to our measurement, the maxillary sinus volume on the cleft side was approximately 10% smaller than that on the noncleft side. This reduction in maxillary sinus volume may be functionally relevant, as decreased sinus pneumatization could influence sinus physiology and potentially predispose patients to sinonasal symptoms. Previous studies have also reported a higher incidence of sinusitis in children with cleft lip and palate compared with healthy controls, with prevalence rates reaching up to 44.1%.18 This increased susceptibility has traditionally been attributed to anatomical abnormalities, including nasal septal deviation, maxillary sinus hypoplasia, and impaired sinus drainage. However, despite the presence of structural asymmetry, no significant interside differences were observed in primary ostium diameter, mucosal thickness, or radiographic mucosal classification according to our results. Notably, more than half of the maxillary sinuses on both sides exhibited no or only minimal mucosal thickening, and this proportion approached nearly 80% when mild thickening was included. Taken together, these findings indicate that although maxillary sinus development is altered in UCLP, differences in sinus volume and morphology alone may not be the primary drivers of upper airway symptoms in this population.

Although morphological differences of the maxillary sinus itself may not be the primary driver of upper airway symptoms in patients with UCLP, nasal septal deviation appears to represent a more consistent and clinically relevant anatomical factor. In the present study, septal deviation was observed in all included adolescents and, without exception, was directed toward the cleft side. Moreover, the degree of deviation was often pronounced. The mean deviation angle including the soft tissue component reached 18.00° ± 5.46°, with the greatest value approaching 23° for mucosal deviation. The finding that the combined soft tissue deviation angle was consistently greater than the bony deviation angle suggests that, in addition to skeletal displacement, mucosal thickening may further aggravate airway narrowing. Previous studies have suggested that septal deviation leads to narrowing of the cleft-side nasal passage, whereas increased airflow through the relatively unobstructed noncleft side may induce compensatory turbinate mucosal hypertrophy. The combined effect of these changes may increase nasal resistance on both sides and contribute to chronic nasal symptoms.8,9 This may partly explain the high prevalence of rhinitis and other upper airway complaints reported in patients with UCLP.

Regarding the nasolacrimal duct, the present study demonstrated a significantly smaller coronal width of the bony nasolacrimal duct on the cleft side, indicating that the developmental alterations associated with UCLP extend beyond the maxillary sinus to involve adjacent components of the maxillary-nasal complex. This finding is consistent with a previous study published in 2023, which likewise reported a significantly reduced transverse diameter of the nasolacrimal duct in patients with UCLP compared with controls.43 The present findings therefore suggest that nasolacrimal duct narrowing represents another manifestation of the craniofacial asymmetry associated with unilateral cleft development. Despite this anatomical difference, no significant interside difference was observed in nasolacrimal duct patency. Taken together, these results suggest that the observed narrowing remains within the range of physiological compensation, whereby luminal patency can be maintained provided that the mucosal lining and the function of the terminal Hasner valve remain intact.44 Consequently, morphological narrowing of the bony nasolacrimal duct should not be interpreted as evidence of functional obstruction in the absence of impaired duct patency. Nevertheless, awareness of this anatomical variation may be valuable during radiological assessment and surgical planning in patients with UCLP, particularly when procedures involve the nasolacrimal drainage pathway or adjacent maxillary structures.

The present study has several strengths. First, a standardized three-dimensional CBCT-based reconstruction protocol with paired intraindividual comparison was applied, allowing precise evaluation of anatomical asymmetry between the cleft and noncleft sides in patients with UCLP. Second, by combining linear and volumetric measurements, the study clarified that the reduction in maxillary sinus volume on the cleft side was primarily associated with decreased sinus height rather than alterations in width or depth, thereby providing a more refined understanding of cleft-related maxillary hypoplasia. Third, the analysis extended beyond sinus morphology to include mucosal status, primary ostium dimensions, nasal septal deviation, and nasolacrimal duct characteristics, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of the maxillary sinus-nasal complex. Importantly, the present analysis focused on adolescents, a stage during which craniofacial structures retain active growth potential. This allows better characterization of developmental patterns at a clinically relevant time point and may provide additional insight for early multidisciplinary intervention aimed at optimizing treatment outcomes.

This study also has several limitations. First, the absence of a healthy control group precluded direct comparison of the measured parameters with unaffected individuals. Therefore, the anatomical differences observed in the present study should be interpreted as relative differences between the cleft and noncleft sides rather than direct comparisons with normal craniofacial development. Nevertheless, the primary objective of this study was to characterize intraindividual anatomical asymmetry associated with UCLP using a paired comparison design, in which each participant served as his or her own internal control. This approach minimized interindividual variability and provided clinically relevant information regarding the asymmetry that underlies contemporary management of UCLP. Furthermore, the retrospective single-centre design may have introduced selection bias and may limit the generalizability of the present findings. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts, refined phenotypic stratification, and appropriately matched healthy controls are warranted to further determine whether the noncleft side also demonstrates developmental alterations compared with unaffected individuals. Second, male adolescents were more frequently represented than females in the present cohort. However, this distribution reflected the characteristics of consecutive patients available during the study period rather than selective bias in patient recruitment. No obvious differences were identified between male and female patients with respect to cleft severity, cleft laterality, or age range. Although sex-related differences in maxillary sinus pneumatization have been reported previously, the present study primarily adopted a paired intraindividual comparison design, thereby minimizing the influence of sex on the interpretation of interside anatomical asymmetry. Furthermore, an additional sensitivity analysis demonstrated no significant sex-related difference in the magnitude of interside asymmetry for either maxillary sinus volume or sinus height. Nevertheless, studies including larger cohorts with a more balanced sex distribution remain warranted to further validate these findings. Third, the present measurements were obtained during adolescence, and it is possible that some anatomical parameters may continue to change into adulthood. However, assessment at this stage is also a strength of the study, because it captures craniofacial characteristics at a time when patients still retain growth potential that may be clinically utilized, thereby providing a more relevant basis for early treatment planning and intervention. In addition, current domestic expert consensus in China generally recommends management of alveolar cleft during adolescence. As a result, it is not practical to obtain imaging data from untreated adult patients with alveolar clefts. More importantly, we did not wish to include patients who had already undergone alveolar cleft repair, as such treatment could alter the morphology of the anatomical structures evaluated in this study. Finally, maxillary sinus volume was segmented based on the entire anatomical sinus cavity, including the sinus mucosa, rather than the aerated sinus cavity alone. Although this approach is unlikely to have materially influenced the paired interside comparison because no significant differences in mucosal thickness or mucosal classification were observed, it may limit direct comparison of absolute volumetric values with studies adopting different segmentation protocols.

Conclusion

In conclusion, paired intraindividual CBCT analysis demonstrated distinct anatomical differences between the cleft and noncleft sides of the maxillary sinus-nasal complex in adolescents with UCLP, primarily reflected by reduced maxillary sinus height and volume on the cleft side and consistent nasal septal deviation toward the cleft side. In contrast, mucosal status, ostium dimensions, and nasolacrimal duct patency were largely similar between sides. These findings refine current understanding of craniofacial development in UCLP and may support more targeted multidisciplinary treatment planning and communication among members of the cleft care team during adolescence.
