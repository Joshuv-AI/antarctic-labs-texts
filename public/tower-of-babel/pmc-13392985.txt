Epilepsy as a predictor of subjective memory and executive dysfunction in American children and adults, with sleep as a relevant factor
Authors: Danielle A. Wallace, Nigel P. Pedersen, Daniel L. Drane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
People with epilepsy (PWE), including adults with epilepsy (AWE) and children with epilepsy (CWE), may experience problems with attention and memory. We hypothesized that epilepsy and sleep disturbances are associated with subjective memory and executive dysfunction. Using cross-sectional investigation of two nationally-representative U.S. surveys, the 2021–2023 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) and the 2016–2023 National Survey of Children’s Health (NSCH), we tested if subjective concentration or memory difficulty differed between children or adults with (n = 2675 children; n = 1570 adults) and without (n = 217,579 children; n = 84,763 adults) epilepsy. Analyses examined epilepsy (comparisons of no epilepsy, inactive epilepsy, and active epilepsy) as a predictor of memory, concentration, or decision-making difficulties. Data were analyzed in survey-weighted adjusted logistic regression models. Exploratory analyses evaluated seizure frequency, sleep health, physical activity, depression, and anxiety, as well as socialization in children. CWE had > 7 times higher odds of subjective memory, concentration, or decision-making difficulty compared to children without epilepsy (ORadj = 7.3; 95%CI: 6.21, 8.56; p < 0.001), and AWE had > 3.5 times higher odds of memory or concentration difficulty compared to people without epilepsy (ORadj = 3.7; 95%CI: 3.2, 4.2; p < 0.001). Odds were higher for PWE with active epilepsy. Reported memory or concentration difficulty were associated with worse sleep health in both AWE and CWE (p < 0.05). This large epidemiological study evaluated the prevalence of memory and executive dysfunction among 4,245 PWE, highlighting the ubiquity of memory and attention complaints, regardless of age or disease status. Memory and attention difficulties, particularly among CWE, may be an important factor in epilepsy-related disparities.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological condition that affects 1.8% of all Americans and 1% of American children [1]. While many people with epilepsy (PWE) have normal cognitive abilities, PWE often report cognitive problems, including difficulty with attention and memory, possibly due to epileptiform discharges, sleep disruption, or other disease-related factors [2]. Epilepsy during childhood and adulthood poses unique challenges. Deficits have been reported in memory, executive function, language, visuo-perception, processing speed, and attention in both CWE and AWE, affecting social functioning and contributing to disparities in education and overall health among PWE [3–5]. In addition to the overt medical challenges posed by seizures, epilepsy-related comorbidities, and treatments such as anti-seizure medications (ASMs), children (CWE) and adults with epilepsy (AWE) may also face disease-related stigma, lack of appropriate accommodations, and difficulty with learning or socialization [6–8]. In addition to the added challenges directly posed by epilepsy, PWE may also be less likely to have protective factors, such as healthy sleep, physical activity, and social support [7,9–12]. The multifaceted effects of these stressors and lack of adequate protective factors may contribute to poor health outcomes among PWE (Fig. 1).

A rich literature documents the subjective and objective cognitive effects of epilepsy, as well as discrepancies between self-report and standardized neuropsychological test results [13]. The low concordance between these two measurement modalities has been attributed to the possible effects of mood and psychiatric variables to self-report [13,14], yet more recent studies have demonstrated that subjective and objective measures may be capturing both shared and distinct aspects of cognitive health [15–18]. More specifically, there may be instances when standardized tests are simply failing to capture elements of memory or broader cognition that are causing subjective distress, such as the absence of tests for measuring rapid long-term forgetting, emotional contributions to memory, consolidation of memory during sleep, multimodal binding of information, and the temporal and spatial context of episodic memory (i.e., most standardized tests are unimodal in nature and devoid of context) [19–21]. For example, Lemesle et al. (2022) demonstrated that temporal lobe epilepsy patients who had subjective complaints but normal performance on standard neuropsychological tests exhibited deficits on a task of rapid long-term forgetting. Overall, subjective and objective memory measures may show better alignment when broader aspects of memory are considered.

Both subjective and objective cognitive outcomes have been found to correspond to sleep health [14,18,22–25], which is relevant given the high prevalence of sleep disturbance and sleep disorders [26,27] among PWE and the importance of sleep and circadian alignment for memory and executive function in general [28–32]. In addition to sleep, physical activity may also affect cognitive health[29,33,34]. While prior studies have evaluated these relationships in small or modestly-sized samples of epilepsy patients, the prevalence and extent of cognitive difficulty and relationship to factors such as sleep and physical activity has not been well-established in large studies.

Therefore, the goal of this nationally-representative analysis of US children and adults is to determine the prevalence of subjectively-reported cognitive difficulties among PWE compared to people without epilepsy and to examine possible modifying factors such as sleep. Exploratory subgroup analyses examine the associations of seizure frequency, social difficulty, sleep health, physical activity, depression, or anxiety with cognitive difficulties in the pediatric and adult PWE population. These analyses can be used to guide more detailed prospective investigations of these topics, with the ultimate goal of improving care and quality of life for PWE.

Methods

Description of Pediatric Epilepsy Dataset: National Survey of Children’s Health (NSCH)

Cross-sectional data on children with epilepsy (CWE) were collected by nationwide mail and web-based survey conducted by the US Census Bureau, the National Survey of Children’s Health (NSCH), as previously described [35]. Briefly, every year the NSCH collects national and state-level information about the health of children from randomly sampled US households that have at least one non-institutionalized resident under 18 years old. In a household with multiple children, one child is randomly selected for the survey. An adult proxy respondent living in the household and familiar with the child’s health (usually a parent) completes questions about the child [36]. The NSCH collected self-reported information about the household and demographics, as well health information about the sampled child. Respondents reported child’s age (years), sex, family income ratio, education, and health-related measures (described in more detail below). NSCH derived family poverty ratio from family income, household size, and federal poverty guideline information with multiple imputation using the sequential regression approach. It was categorized as the percentage of family income to federal poverty level (<100%, 100–199%, 200–399%, and 400%+) [37]. This analysis included NSCH data from all available cycles from 2016 onwards (2016–2023) from the enhanced data files that were released in 2023 [38]. While the response rate for the 2016–2023 NSCH data was ~40%, nonresponse bias analyses by the Census Bureau found no strong or consistent evidence of bias, although respondents were more likely to have higher household income; sample weights reduce this bias, suggesting that income-related response bias is minimal[39]. Detailed information about survey design, sample size and response rates, and weighting are documented and publicly available on the NSCH website (https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nsch.html). Participation is based on the voluntary consent of respondents with the U.S. Census Bureau's Notice and Consent Warning.

Description of Adult Epilepsy Dataset: National Health Interview Survey (NHIS)

The self-reported adult data on epilepsy and cognitive health were collected by the 2021–2023 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) from respondents aged 18 years and older. The NHIS is a large, nationwide cross-sectional survey that collects demographic and health information every year from non-institutionalized US civilians to assess prevalence, trends, and associations of chronic medical conditions and disability for population-based research. Similar to NSCH, NHIS derived family income to poverty ratio for the survey respondent's family; as for NSCH, NHIS ratios were grouped into percentage categories (<100%, 100–199%, 200–399%, and 400%+). The adult response rate for the 2021–2023 NHIS data was ~48%. Detailed information about the survey design, sample size and response rates, and weighting are documented and publicly available on the CDC NHIS website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhis/documentation/index.html). The 2021, 2022, and 2023 cycles of adult NHIS data were used in this analysis because they were the only survey cycles with questions on epilepsy and memory and concentration. The 2022 survey also included questions on sleep health and was used for the secondary analyses on sleep. NHIS does not collect epilepsy information for children, unlike NSCH. The NHIS study was approved by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board.

Definition of Exposure: Epilepsy Status

Epilepsy categories were defined using the methods by Tian et al [26] and Zack and Kobau [40]. Briefly, participants who responded “Yes” when asked “Has a doctor or other health care provider ever told you that this child has epilepsy or seizure disorder?” or (for adult PWE) “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have a seizure disorder or epilepsy?” were considered to have epilepsy. In NHIS, adult PWE were also asked to report their seizure frequency over the past 12 months, with the following options: 0, 1, 2 or 3, or 4 or more. Among those with epilepsy, participants who responded “Yes” when asked “If yes, does this child currently have the condition?” or (for adult PWE) “Are you currently taking any medicine to control your seizure disorder or epilepsy?” or adult PWE who responded 1 or more when asked “About how many seizures of any type have you had during the past 12 months?” were considered to have active epilepsy. Otherwise, adult or pediatric PWE who were not currently taking epilepsy medication and (for adult PWE) reported 0 seizures in the past year were considered to have inactive epilepsy.

Definition of Outcomes: Subjective Cognitive Difficulty – Memory, Concentration, or Decision-Making

NSCH collected information on memory, concentration, and decision-making for children 6–17 years old. To ascertain cognitive difficulty, adult proxies were asked to respond “Yes” or “No” to a question of whether the child being surveyed had “Serious difficulty concentrating, remembering, or making decisions because of a physical, mental, or emotional condition”. If the response was affirmative, the child was considered to have difficulty.

For adults 18 and older, NHIS questions related to cognitive health were adapted from the Washington Group on Disability Statistics Short Set on Functioning [41]. Participants who responded that they had “Some difficulty”, “A lot of difficulty” or “Cannot do at all” when asked “Do you have difficulty remembering or concentrating?” were considered to have difficulty remembering or concentrating, compared to participants who responded, “No difficulty”. In supplemental NHIS analyses that evaluated results by executive dysfunction subgroup, adult participants who responded, “Difficulty remembering only” or “Difficulty with both remembering and concentrating” were considered to have memory difficulty, while participants who responded, “Difficulty concentrating only” or “Difficulty with both remembering and concentrating” were considered to have concentration difficulty when asked “Is that a difficulty with remembering, concentrating, or both?”.

Measures of Sleep Health, Physical Activity, Socialization, and Depression and Anxiety

The NSCH also asked questions about the surveyed child’s sleep regularity and sleep duration (further details provided in Supplemental Table 1). A categorical variable for healthy sleep duration by age group was created using the American Academy of Sleep Medicine consensus statement for recommendations for pediatric sleep duration [42]; if sleep duration was within age group guidelines (9–12 hours of sleep per 24 hours for children 6–12 years old; 8–10 hours per 24 hours for teenagers 13–18 years old), sleep duration was considered “Appropriate for age group”, otherwise if sleep duration was outside recommended guidelines it was considered “Inappropriate for age group”. Proxy respondents were also asked to report on the physical activity patterns and ease of making friends for the surveyed child. Children were considered to have depression or anxiety, respectively, if NSCH proxy respondents answered “Yes” when asked “Has a doctor or other health care provider EVER told you that this child has”: “…depression?” or “…anxiety problems”.

The NHIS 2022 cycle included measures of sleep health and physical activity (for further details, see Supplemental Table 2). Measures included questions on sleep duration, trouble falling or staying asleep, and sleep medication use. Physical activity questions asked about time spent doing moderate or vigorous physical activity. NHIS respondents who answered “Yes” when asked “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had”: “…any type of depression?” or “…any type of anxiety disorder” were considered to have depression or anxiety, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Children or adults with missing information on epilepsy status, age, sex, or difficulty remembering or concentrating (n=2,924 children, 1.3% of pediatric sample; n=322 adults, 0.4% of adult sample) were excluded from the analysis. Data were analyzed in survey-weighted logistic regression models adjusted for age (years), sex (male/female), highest level of education among adult respondent (didn’t graduate high school or GED/high school graduate/associates, some college/ college graduate or more), participant’s race (American Indian or Alaska Native alone/Asian alone/Black or African American alone/Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander alone/White alone/Two or more races), participant’s Hispanic ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino origin/Not Hispanic or Latino origin (NH)), health insurance coverage (yes/no), and family income ratio category (<100%, 100–199%, 200–399%, 400+% to federal poverty level), with epilepsy diagnosis as the predictor and presence or absence of difficulty as the outcome. Age in the adult NHIS analysis was modeled with natural cubic splines (df=7), the number of knots chosen by comparing models (df=1–9) and selecting the result with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Race and ethnicity are social, not biological, constructs and were included as covariates in this analysis to attempt to account for confounding from the effects of racism on health. Exploratory analyses among the pediatric and adult PWE subgroup examined associations between seizure frequency or cognitive difficulty with sleep health or physical activity in survey-weighted Chi-square tests. Exploratory analyses also tested the interaction between epilepsy status (No/Yes) with binarized sleep and physical activity factors (Supplemental Table 1) in separate fully adjusted survey-weighted logistic regression models. For the NHIS adult data, only the 2022 data cycle was used for exploratory analyses, as survey items on sleep were not available for the 2021 or 2023 cycle. To examine the influence of depression and anxiety as co-morbid factors, sensitivity analyses excluded CWE and AWE with depression or anxiety. Subgroup analyses were also performed to test associations among PWE with depression or anxiety. Percentages and model output are shown with applied sample weights. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.0) using the “survey” package [43] and considered statistically significant if p<0.05.

Results

This analysis included 220,254 children and 86,333 adult participants, among whom 2,675 children and 1,570 adults reported a history of epilepsy, with a sample-weighted epilepsy prevalence of 1.1% in NSCH and 1.8% in NHIS (Table 1, Supplemental Table 3). The prevalence of difficulty remembering or concentrating was 10.4% and 19.9% among all child and adult respondents, respectively, but rose to 45.3% and 50.5% among CWE and AWE.

In analyses adjusted for possible confounders (age, sex, education, race, ethnicity, health insurance coverage, and family income ratio), CWE had >7 times higher odds of difficulty remembering, concentrating, or decision-making (adjusted OR=7.29; 95%CI: 6.21, 8.56; p<0.001) and AWE had >3.7 times higher odds of difficulty remembering or concentrating compared to peers without epilepsy (adjusted OR=3.65; 95%CI: 3.21, 4.15; p<0.001; Table 2, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). PWE with active epilepsy had higher odds of cognitive difficulties compared to PWE with inactive epilepsy. Compared to 3.3 greater odds of children with inactive epilepsy (ORadj=3.29; 95%CI: 2.58, 4.19; p<0.001), children with active epilepsy had 11.9 greater odds of cognitive difficulty (ORadj=11.9; 95%CI: 9.57, 14.73; p<0.001) compared to children without epilepsy. Similarly, adults with inactive epilepsy had 2.9 greater odds (ORadj =2.87; 95%CI: 2.30, 3.58; p<0.001) and adults with active epilepsy had 4.8 greater odds (ORadj=4.16; 95%CI: 3.53, 4.90; p<0.001) of cognitive difficulty compared to adults without epilepsy. Results in adults were similar when assessing memory difficulty separately from concentration difficulty, with larger effect sizes for memory difficulty (Supplemental Table 4). In sensitivity analyses excluding participants with depression or anxiety, effect sizes were generally magnified in children and attenuated in adults, but remained similar to the primary results (Supplemental Table 5).

In the exploratory subgroup analysis of CWE (Fig. 4), CWE with memory, concentration, or decision-making difficulty were more likely to have sleep duration either too long or too short for their age group (χ2=37.8, df=1, p=0.007), lower physical activity (χ2=169.3, df=3, p<0.001), and greater difficulty making friends (χ2=842.5, df=2, p<0.001). Weeknight sleep regularity did not significantly differ by cognitive difficulty among CWE; however, CWE were more likely to have irregular bedtimes compared to children without epilepsy (χ2=39.5, df=4, p=0.04), and, among all children, more irregular bedtimes were strongly associated with cognitive difficulty (χ2=2788, df=4, p<0.001; Supplemental Fig. 1). Similarly, in the exploratory analysis of the adult PWE subset, adult PWE with difficulty remembering were more likely to have frequent seizures (χ2= 74, df=9, p<0.001; Fig. 3) and sleep medication use (χ2=14.9, df=3, p<0.05), problems falling asleep (χ2=23.5, df=3, p<0.001), and problems staying asleep (χ2=13.6, df=3, p<0.05) (Fig. 4). However, the NHIS data did not support a relationship with physical activity in adults (see Fig. 4).

In the exploratory analysis of interaction between sleep and physical activity factors with epilepsy status, NSCH results suggested an interaction between epilepsy status and bedtime regularity on cognitive difficulty in children (Table 3). However, the data did not support an interaction between sleep duration or physical activity. Among adults, the NHIS data supported an interaction between epilepsy status and insomnia-related symptoms of difficulty falling asleep or difficulty staying asleep on cognitive difficulty, but not sleep duration or sleep medication use. The interaction terms represent a ratio of odds ratios and are all <1, suggesting that the multiplicative effect of having epilepsy and poor sleep or activity is smaller than expected when compared to the magnitude of the main effects.

Discussion

This is the first large epidemiological study to evaluate the prevalence of subjective memory and executive dysfunction among PWE and association with sleep health, physical activity, and socialization across the lifespan. Like prior work in smaller clinic and community-based samples, our nationally-representative findings suggest that difficulty with memory and concentration are common among PWE. Model results were modestly attenuated after adjustment for potential confounders, with epilepsy status remaining a strong predictor. Results supported greater prevalence of cognitive complaints among PWE with active epilepsy, although odds remained elevated among PWE with inactive epilepsy compared to those without epilepsy. These findings align with prior work showing that disruptions to brain activity by seizures and epileptiform activity and adverse effects from ASMs [44] can disrupt cognition and contribute to neuropsychological dysfunction in PWE [45,46]. Our results are also supported by a growing appreciation for epilepsy as a multifaceted, multimorbidity condition that encompasses more than the occurrence of seizures; interictal discharges and other network dysfunction may still occur in the absence of seizures or among those considered to have “inactive” epilepsy [47–50]. This network-level dysfunction may contribute to cognitive issues such as memory problems and executive dysfunction, as well as sleep disturbances (which may mechanistically affect memory and cognition) [51,52], and explain the lingering presence of these morbidities in PWE who are not on medication and have not had a recent seizure. For example, research suggests that interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs), transient cortical hyperexcitability that can occur independently of seizures, affect spindle formation and could influence cognition and neuropsychiatric function [31,53–55].

Our assessment of this large dataset of epidemiological survey data is supportive of a relationship between subjective sleep and cognition, suggesting that greater subjective sleep disruption is related to greater subjective cognitive difficulty among AWE and CWE. Sleep disruption is common among PWE [26,27], and sleep disturbance has been shown to impair cognitive performance in healthy individuals and other patient populations [29,33,34,56–58]. Memory has been shown to be particularly vulnerable to sleep disturbance, as sleep plays a key role in memory consolidation processes [32], and memory is a salient patient-reported outcome [59,60]. In the setting of epilepsy, however, data about sleep consolidation has been lacking and studies have been inconclusive due to a variety of limitations, such as small sample sizes and the use of heterogenous study paradigms and memory assessment methods (see recent review by Latrielle et al[61]).

The best studies to date relating sleep disturbance to cognition have occurred in children[22,23]. In these studies, recent onset CWE were compared to their healthy siblings on sleep, behavioral rating scales, and neuropsychological measures. The CWE experienced a higher prevalence of sleep issues, and there were significant associations between parent-reported sleep problems and greater neuropsychological dysfunction, including restricted IQ scores and limitations in language and verbal memory. In these samples, greater behavioral problems were associated with lower cognitive performance and more depression symptomology, all of which correlated with sleep [22,23]. In a different, cross-sectional study, more behavioral problems were found among CWE (n=90) with worse (parent-reported) sleep health, even after controlling for age, sex, seizure frequency, and number of ASMs(4); a case-control study (n=48 CWE) also reported an association between behavioral problems and sleep difficulties, but did not report differences between CWE and controls(6). To date, similar studies are lacking in AWE samples, and studies attempting to relate cognition directly to objectively measured sleep characteristics (e.g., using polysomnography or actigraphy) are even more lacking. Disturbed bedtime sleep patterns were negatively correlated with most assessed neuropsychological measures in these studies in CWE. In our assessment of survey data, reports of irregular bedtimes in CWE were similarly related to increased subjective reports of cognitive dysfunction, and our results also suggest that CWE with sleep durations deemed to be either too long or too short also expressed greater cognitive complaints. For our adult sample of PWE, those reporting greater difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep, and those reporting the use of sleep medications were more likely to report cognitive difficulty. Our exploratory analysis among all children (with or without epilepsy) also supported a relationship between decreased weeknight sleep regularity and cognitive difficulty, with irregular bedtimes occurring more frequently among CWE. While our analyses were limited to subjective measures, this sample represents a very large set of survey data, and appears consistent with trends seen in existing, smaller studies of PWE that have explored self-reported sleep parameters with either subjective or objective neuropsychological and behavioral assessment methods [22–24]. We believe our findings point to additional aspects of sleep function that should be explored and highlight the potential pervasiveness of problems in this domain.

In addition to sleep, physical activity may be another important protective factor for cognitive functioning, but PWE are less likely to participate in sports and more likely to have low physical activity [12]. In our analysis, more frequent physical activity was associated with decreased presence of cognitive difficulty among CWE. However, this is not necessarily a causal relationship, as it is possible that CWE with greater health challenges may have less opportunity for physical activity, such as limitation by caregivers worried about safety, stigma, and other barriers to participation. Side effects of ASMs or disease characteristics can also make it more difficult to exercise (e.g., low energy levels or depressed mood due to medication or sleep disruption). However, among the adult PWE subset, low physical activity was not associated with cognitive difficulty. Of note, one recent study exploring the effect of exercise on hippocampal health and memory in PWE demonstrated a positive effect of physical exercise as an intervention[62]; however, a small (n=10) pilot study of a remotely-delivered exercise intervention for people with drug-resistant epilepsy encountered multiple challenges, including decreased participation among persons with greater co-morbid depression or anxiety[63].

Neuropsychological deficits in learning and memory, attention/executive function, reaction time, processing speed, and visuomotor tasks have been observed in both adults and CWE at the time of their first recognized seizures, as well as problems with mood, anxiety, and elevated fatigue, even in drug-naïve patients[3,5,64,65]. As an example, impairments in attention and executive function (49.4%) and memory (47.8%) domains of a brief testing battery were reported in a fairly large sample of newly diagnosed epilepsy patients (n=247), with another quarter of this sample reporting subjective cognitive concerns not noted on testing[66]. These percentages are grossly similar to the self-reported survey results in this manuscript, which were not measured through formal testing. Academic difficulties were also observed in 50% of children with new onset epilepsy[3]. Moreover, our group has championed the inadequacy of current neuropsychological testing to fully measure cognition, and it is likely that the subjective concerns of many PWE represent valid, but unassessed, areas of function (e.g., rapid long-term forgetting, memory consolidation, emotional contribution to memory)[5,20,21,67]. Ongoing work is demonstrating that PWE who perform normally on standard neuropsychological measures, yet have subjective complaints, often demonstrate deficits on novel tasks that assess neglected areas of function[19–21,68]. Standard memory measures, for example, which involve the encoding and recall of unimodal stimuli (e.g., auditory vs. visual) for brief spans of time (typically only 20–30 minutes duration), will not be able to detect problems with consolidation of information over a span of days or the ability to associate stimuli across sensory modalities (e.g., putting a name with a face). We also acknowledge that the mismatch between standard testing and functional outcome is sometimes affected by psychiatric status, which can contribute to under and over-reporting of symptoms, some of which are transient symptoms related to mood disturbance rather than the direct effect of seizures[13,14]. Finally, standard neuropsychological tests have been criticized for decades for lacking ecological validity, as they frequently do not reflect the patient’s functional status in the real world (i.e., performance on neuropsychological measures may not reflect performance on vocational or academic tasks or specific skills, such as driving an automobile)[69–71]. To address these complexities, future work may seek to integrate tools, such as combined clinical-patient-reported outcome frameworks, novel tasks created to address assessment gaps, or ecological momentary assessment (EMA), which also carefully measure mood and performance validity to capture a more holistic appraisal of cognitive health.

Research has started to explore the manner in which cognitive dysfunction may hinder social development for children and adults with epilepsy[72–74]. Problems with memory, attention, and executive functioning can contribute to problems with fluent communication and affect social inclusion and adaptation; these communication deficits can exceed other social barriers related to seizures (e.g., the inability to drive). Of note, even transient dysfunction may hinder social development and engagement[73]. One study demonstrated that people with early onset epilepsy had poorer autobiographical memories than those with later, adult-onset seizures; moreover, patients with poor autobiographical memories and early age of onset had a less well-formed sense of self, and were thought to be less socially connected than those with good memories[75]. Finally, independent of problems with traditional cognitive domains (e.g., memory, language, processing speed), these patients are also demonstrating greater dysfunction in the area of socioemotional function (e.g., recognizing emotion in themselves and others, labeling emotion, understanding social etiquette, understanding intention/theory of mind, managing their feelings appropriately, multimodal binding of emotion)[74,76–79]. Such factors may further hinder social connection and development and seem to occur as part of the disease process, as well as a possible side effect of potential curative treatments [80].

Depression and anxiety, which are commonly comorbid with epilepsy[64,81,82] and explain some of the discordance between subjective and objective measures of cognition in PWE[13,14], were also associated with both subjective sleep and cognitive outcomes in our analyses; prior research suggests a bidirectional relationship, with sleep as a stronger predictor of depression than vice versa, and that improving sleep can improve mood outcomes[83–87]. However, the primary study findings are not wholly attributable to these co-morbidities, as sensitivity analyses excluding people with depression or anxiety attenuated, but did not mitigate, results. Research to better understand the mechanisms and interactions between these factors may allow us to develop improved treatment approaches for addressing mood, sleep, or cognitive problems in this patient group.

This study has several strengths and limitations. The results are representative of the non-institutionalized US child and adult population, respectively, and highlight the prevalence of memory impairment and cognitive difficulty among PWE. However, all analyses are cross-sectional in nature, and causality cannot be determined. Additionally, the classification of “active” and “inactive” epilepsy is not the terminology used by epileptologists, clinicians, or the recommended terminology developed by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE). We were not able to classify according to ILAE guidelines due to limitations of the survey questions. However, the classification methods used in this study were previously developed and applied by the Epilepsy Program at the CDC specifically for these datasets. This analysis also relied on crude measures of subjectively-reported cognitive and comorbid outcomes, which can be affected by recall bias, under- or over-reporting, or other forms of bias; comprehensive, longitudinal studies with neuropsychological testing are needed. For example, people experiencing cognitive or memory difficulties may inaccurately assess their own memory problems, potentially underreporting them. In this case, the NSCH results may be less affected by this issue because cognitive difficulties were reported by a proxy representative who knew the child well, rather than relying on self-assessment. This difference in proxy-report vs. self-report for the pediatric vs. adult data may also explain some of the differences in effect size between these age groups.

In summary, compared to people without epilepsy, children with epilepsy have approximately 3.3–11.9 times and adults with epilepsy have 2.7–4.3 times higher odds of difficulty remembering, concentration, and/or decision-making compared to peers without epilepsy. Notably, these results highlight the ubiquity of memory and concentration complaints among PWE regardless of active or inactive status, although PWE taking medication or who had a seizure in the past year report greater difficulty. Future clinical and pre-clinical research should investigate the roles of epilepsy characteristics, sleep health, physical activity, co-morbidities, and other factors in this relationship with prospectively collected, longitudinal, co-occurring and acquired, and objective measures of sleep and cognitive health to better understand causality and reduce measurement error, with the ultimate goal of developing tailored interventions to improve memory and cognitive health among PWE.
