Long-Read Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Reveals Dynamic Isoform Changes During Corneal Epithelial Wound Healing in Cynomolgus Monkeys
Authors: Le-Chun Ou, Ming Zhou, Hu Chen, Zhi-Bo Huang, Yuan-Xia Yang, Shi-Da Chen, Feng Zhang, Ying-Feng Zheng, Zhuo-Xing Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCorneal epithelial wound healing is essential for visual recovery after injury, yet the molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Different RNA isoforms from the same gene may have distinct functions not captured by gene-level analyses. This study characterized isoform-level transcriptional changes during corneal epithelial wound healing in cynomolgus monkeys using long-read single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq).MethodsWe profiled corneal limbal tissues from cynomolgus monkeys under uninjured conditions and at 1 day and 3 days post-injury using HIT-scISOseq, a long-read single-cell approach detecting full-length transcripts. Bioinformatic analyses examined differential isoform expression, usage, diversity, and switching across epithelial cell populations. Key isoforms were validated by RT-qPCR in scratch-wounded HCE-T cells and RNA-FISH in monkey corneal tissue.ResultsAnalysis revealed widespread isoform-level changes during corneal epithelial repair that were not evident from gene expression alone. Cytoskeletal remodeling genes (TPM1 and DST) and stress-response genes (ERICH5 and CTSL) showed altered isoform usage despite relatively stable total expression. ARID5B and GSN exhibited cell-type-specific isoform switching across repair stages. We identified 27 genes with dynamic isoform diversity changes and 62 genes with significant isoform switching. RT-qPCR and RNA-FISH validation confirmed injury-induced expression dynamics and cell-type-specific localization of key isoforms.ConclusionsIsoform-level regulation represents an important and independent layer of transcriptional control during corneal epithelial wound healing. This single-cell isoform atlas provides a resource for identifying injury-associated transcripts and candidate regulatory pathways, offering a framework for future functional studies and isoform-focused therapeutic strategies.

The corneal epithelium forms the eye’s frontline defense, maintaining the optical clarity essential for vision. Corneal injuries affect millions annually and can lead to permanent vision loss if healing fails.1,2 Epithelial regeneration relies on coordinated limbal stem/progenitor cell (LESC) proliferation, migration,3,4 and differentiation5–7 to restore the stratified barrier.8–12 Understanding the molecular programs regulating these cellular transitions is essential for developing strategies to promote corneal repair after injury or surgery.

Short-read single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has mapped cellular heterogeneity in the cornea during homeostasis and disease,13–20 but primarily quantifies gene-level expression, providing limited information about full-length transcript structures. As a result, RNA isoforms, which are alternative transcripts generated through alternative splicing, transcription start sites, or polyadenylation, which can differ in stability, localization, and function, may be overlooked. Isoform-level dynamics during corneal epithelial wound healing thus remain largely unexplored.

To address this gap, we previously developed HIT-scISOseq,16 a high-throughput long-read single-cell RNA-seq method that captures full-length transcript structures while preserving single-cell barcode information with high accuracy at single-cell resolution. This approach enables cell-type-specific isoform identification not achievable with short-read sequencing.

Here, we applied HIT-scISOseq to profile corneal limbal tissues from cynomolgus monkeys under homeostatic and at 1 and 3 days after corneal epithelial injury. We constructed a single-cell RNA isoform atlas of the limbal epithelium and systematically analyzed cell-type–specific isoform expression, usage, diversity, and switching during early epithelial repair. Our results reveal extensive isoform-level remodeling in limbal epithelial cells (LEpcs), basal epithelial cells (BEpcs), and suprabasal epithelial cells (SEpcs) after injury, with injury-associated isoform changes linked to extracellular matrix remodeling, epithelial migration, proliferation, and differentiation. Together, this study provides an isoform-resolved view of early corneal epithelial wound healing and a resource for future functional studies of post-transcriptional regulation in corneal repair.

Methods

Ethical Statement

All experiments involving animals were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-Sen University (Acceptance Number: 2019-044, Guangzhou, People's Republic of China) and were conducted in accordance with the ARVO Statement on the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The study used 4-year-old female cynomolgus monkeys, which were provided and maintained by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the center. All procedures adhered to strict animal welfare standards.

Corneal Epithelial Wounds

Corneal epithelial wounds in cynomolgus monkeys were induced as previously described.15 Briefly, monkeys were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine, followed by topical application of 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride (Alcaine; Alcon) for local ocular anesthesia. A 360-degree superficial keratectomy was performed on one eye using an AlgerBrush II, creating a standardized circular wound approximately 6 mm in diameter, located 2 mm from the limbus (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Six monkeys were randomly assigned to 3 groups: an uninjured control (day 0) and 2 wounded groups euthanized at day 1 or day 3 post-injury, representing early and late healing stages, respectively.

Tissue Dissociation

A 2 mm × 2 mm limbal tissue area was resected via layer-by-layer dissection to a depth of 100 µm, transferred to Advanced DMEM/F-12 and disaggregated using Dispase II (Sigma) and collagenase IV (Sigma) at 37°C under constant rotation. The epithelial layer was separated from the underlying stroma and digested separately at 37°C for 2 hours using 1 mg/mL collagenase A (Sigma-Aldrich) in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 50 µg/mL gentamicin, and 1.25 µg/mL amphotericin B. Cell clusters were further dissociated using 0.25% trypsin and 1 mM EDTA, filtered through a 30-µm strainer and resuspended in PBS containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA) to a final concentration of 1000 cells/µL for capture on the 10 × Genomics Chromium Controller.

Immunofluorescence Staining and Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization

Uninjured cynomolgus monkey corneal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C, equilibrated in 30% sucrose/PBS overnight, and embedded in Optimal cutting temperature compound. Cryosections (10 µm) underwent RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) using a FISH Detection Kit (GF006; Servicebio) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, sections were pretreated with Proteinase K, hybridized with custom-designed isoform-specific probes (Supplementary Table S6) at 40°C for 16 hours, and processed for signal amplification and detection. Sections were then immunostained with anti-Keratin 12 antibody (ab185627; Abcam; 1:100) overnight at 4°C, followed by a secondary antibody (A-21428; Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:500) and DAPI counterstaining. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Corneal Epithelial Cell Culture and Scratch Assay

HCE-T cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 complete medium with 15% FBS, insulin (5 µg/mL), human epidermal growth factor (10 ng/mL), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO₂, with passaging every 3 days. For scratch-wound assay, cells were grown to approximately 95% confluence in 6-well plates, scratched with a sterile P1000 tip, and collected at 0, 6, and 12 hours after scratch injury for RNA extraction.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, USA) and reverse-transcribed using EasyScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Transgene Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using isoform-specific primers designed to distinguish target isoforms from other isoforms of the same genes. Relative expression levels were normalized to GAPDH using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

10 × Genomics Single-Cell Capture

Single-cell suspensions were processed on the GemCode Single-Cell Platform according to the manufacturer's instructions, using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ GEM, Library, and Gel Bead Kit version 3 (10 × Genomics; PN-1000075). Approximately 2000 cells were recovered per sample.

Data Processing and Downstream Analysis

Raw HIT-scISOseq data were processed using the scISA-Tools16 software to assign barcodes to full-length non-chimeric (FLNC) reads. FLNC reads were mapped and isoforms were identified using IsoQuant.21 An isoform count matrix was generated based on the read-to-isoform mappings output by IsoQuant.

The count matrix was subsequently imported into Seurat (version 4.3.0)22 for downstream single-cell RNA-seq analysis. Each sample was normalized using the “LogNormalize” method. To correct for batch effects, data integration was performed using the canonical correlation analysis (CCA) method. For clustering based on the isoform count matrix, the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA). The first 15 principal components were used for graph-based clustering via the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) algorithm in the “FindClusters” function. Cell-type-specific marker isoforms were identified using the “FindAllMarkers” function, with thresholds set at |avg_logFC| > 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05.

Differentially Expressed Analysis in Major Cell Populations at Day 0, Day 1, and Day 3

Differential expression analysis at the isoform level was performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test in Seurat's “FindMarkers” function. Isoforms with |avg_log2FC| > 0.50 and an adjusted P value < 0.05 were classified as differentially expressed isoforms (DEIs).

Gene Ontology Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for marker genes, marker isoform-derived genes, wound-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and DEI-associated genes was conducted using Metascape23 (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html). Results were visualized with the ggplot2 R software package (version 3.3.5).

Gene Set Score Analysis

Gene set scores were calculated using the “AddModuleScore” function in Seurat. The score for a specific gene set in a cell subpopulation was defined as the total sum of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) for all isoforms in the set expressed by those cells, divided by the total sum of all UMIs expressed in the same cells.

Isoform Usage Analysis

Percent Spliced-In (PSI) analysis was conducted using raw counts per million (CPM) values. To ensure robustness, lowly expressed isoforms were filtered out, retaining only those with CPM > 1 and detected in over 80% of samples per condition. The PSI value for each isoform was calculated as (isoform CPM / total gene CPM) × 100%, with the sum of PSI values for all isoforms of a gene equaling 100%.

Significant isoform usage events were defined by a dual-threshold criterion: a minimum |ΔPSI| > 20% between conditions and a coefficient of variation (CV) of PSI across biological replicates of <0.2. Statistical significance was assessed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (P < 0.05). The PSI matrix was visualized by heatmap and subjected to cluster analysis to identify condition-specific splicing patterns. Complete results are provided in Supplementary Data S1. The correspondence between the isoform IDs and their abbreviations used in this study is provided in Supplementary Data S2.

Isoform Diversity Analysis

Isoform diversity24 was assessed at the pseudo-bulk level to minimize the impact of dropout events. For each gene, isoform counts were aggregated per group to calculate relative proportions. To mitigate bias from variable isoform numbers, diversity was evaluated using only the top two most abundant isoforms per gene. Shannon entropy was applied as follows: DI=-∑i=12pilnpi

Where DI represents the diversity index and pi is the relative proportion of the i-th isoform. Novel isoforms were excluded from this analysis. Genes were included only if they had a minimum of 15 total UMIs in the respective cell type.

Isoform Switch Analysis

We used IsoDiffR,25 a method based on expression trend similarity, to identify genes exhibiting significant isoform usage changes. This analysis compared the expression trends of different isoforms across time points (0 days, 1 day, and 3 days) within each cell type.

All sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE278797. The reference genome and annotation file for Macaca fascicularis (version Macaca_fascicularis_5.0.99) were obtained from Ensembl (https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-99/fasta/macaca_fascicularis/).

Results

Single-Cell Isoform Atlas Reveals Cell-Type-Specific Transcript Diversity During Corneal Repair

To investigate isoform regulation during corneal wound healing, we generated a single-cell isoform atlas from cynomolgus monkey limbal tissues at three time points: steady state (0 days), early response (1 day), and active remodeling (3 days) post-injury (see Figs. 1A, 1B), capturing initial cell activation/migration (1 day), and subsequent proliferation/stratification (3 days).

Using HIT-scISOseq, we generated full-length transcript data from limbal tissues with two biological replicates per time point (see Fig. 1A). After quality control, we retained 9526 high-quality cells detecting an average of 902 isoforms per cell (Supplementary Tables S1–S5). Nine distinct cell clusters were identified based on isoform expression patterns (Supplementary Figs. S1B, S1C), consistent with previously defined cell types.15 Subsequent analyses focused on four major corneal epithelial cell types: SEpcs, BEpcs, LEpcs, and conjunctival epithelial cells (CoCs), totaling 8781 cells (Fig. 1C). Classical marker genes exhibited distinct isoform-level expression patterns across cell types (Figs. 1D, 1E) including markers previously considered uniformly expressed, revealing regulatory information not captured by gene-level analysis alone. No evident batch effects were observed across timepoints (Supplementary Fig. S2), confirming that cell clustering reflected biological rather than technical variation.

Isoform-Level Analysis Uncovers Wound Healing Genes Missed by Conventional Sequencing

To address whether isoform-level analysis reveals biological information invisible to standard gene expression analysis, we compared differential expression using gene-level versus isoform-level expression matrices at 1 and 3 days post-injury. Gene-level analysis detected 676 and 463 DEGs at 1 day and 3 days, respectively, whereas isoform-level analysis identified 797 and 711 DEIs (Supplementary Data S3, S4). UpSet plot analysis revealed that a substantial proportion of differentially regulated genes were uniquely detected at the isoform level (Fig. 2A), indicating that isoform-resolution analysis captures biological information inaccessible to conventional gene-level expression analysis.

GO enrichment analysis showed that terms uniquely enriched in the isoform-level matrix were more closely associated with corneal repair processes than those from gene-level analysis, including “wound healing,” “cell population proliferation,” and “epidermis development” (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. S3A, S3B). Notably, “protein-RNA complex organization” was exclusively identified in the isoform matrix, suggesting that isoform regulation controls post-transcriptional machinery during repair.

Cell-type–specific analysis of these GO modules revealed distinct temporal patterns. “Protein-RNA complex organization” genes showed the most pronounced enrichment in BEpc at 1 day post-injury (Fig. 2C). “Wound healing” associated genes were persistently activated in SEpcs throughout the 3-day period, whereas LEpcs exhibited delayed activation (Fig. 2D). Analysis of growth-related GO terms revealed cell-type–specific regulation, with BEpcs showing early activation at 1 day followed by rapid down-regulation, contrasting with a gradual increase in LEpcs (Fig. 2E). The “epithelial cell differentiation” pathway displayed the most complex temporal regulation, with SEpcs and BEpcs exhibiting opposing temporal patterns (Fig. 2F).

These functional changes were accompanied by specific isoform switching events. For instance, EZR, controlling cytoskeletal organization and cell migration,26,27 showed isoform changes in SEpcs during late-stage repair, whereas SGK1 and NET1, which regulate cell survival, migration, and growth, exhibited isoform switches in BEpcs and LEpcs.28–31 These coordinated isoform changes illustrate how isoform-level regulation enables precise spatiotemporal control of corneal epithelial repair.

Experimental Validation of Representative Injury-Associated Isoforms

To further assess the reliability of the isoform-level signals identified by long-read single-cell RNA-seq, we performed independent experimental validation using two complementary approaches: isoform-specific RNA-FISH to evaluate spatial localization and isoform-specific RT-qPCR to assess injury-associated expression dynamics. For spatial validation, we selected the DSG1 isoform ENSMFAT00000035199, which showed high expression and a well-defined suprabasal epithelial distribution, as a positive target for evaluating the specificity of our isoform-specific RNA-FISH strategy. We also selected the novel, unannotated transcript Transcript16407.8 to determine whether a transcript-specific signal corresponding to a previously unrecognized isoform could be detected directly in corneal tissue (Fig. 3A). RNA-FISH probes were designed against transcript-specific regions rather than sequences shared among isoforms. Consistent with the sequencing results, both targets showed clear localization in the suprabasal epithelial layers of uninjured cynomolgus monkey corneas, as confirmed by co-staining with keratin 12 (Fig. 3B).

We next performed isoform-specific RT-qPCR on three isoforms upregulated at the post-injury stage (Fig. 3C). Given the limited availability of monkey wound-healing tissue, we used the HCE-T scratch-wound assay as a complementary in vitro corneal epithelial injury model. Selected isoforms were confirmed to contain conserved isoform-specific regions or exon-exon junctions at the corresponding human orthologous loci before primer design. All 3 isoforms were upregulated at 6 and/or 12 hours after scratch injury relative to baseline (Fig. 3D), consistent with sequencing-based injury-associated trends. Together, these experiments support the spatial and injury-responsive dynamics of representative isoforms identified in this study.

Critical Wound Healing Genes Switch Isoforms Without Changing Expression Levels

Many genes showed differential isoform expression without changes in overall gene abundance. To investigate this phenomenon, we performed PSI analysis on genes with multiple detectable isoforms, which identified four genes with significant isoform usage changes during repair: TPM1, ERICH5, DST, and CTSL (Fig. 4A; see Supplementary Data S1). Strikingly, these genes showed no significant differences in overall expression before and after injury (Fig. 4B), yet their isoform usage and splicing events were markedly altered (Figs. 4C–H; Supplementary Fig. 4). TPM1 and ERICH5, which showed the most pronounced changes, were selected for detailed characterization.

The TPM1 isoform ENSMFAT00000066053 was significantly upregulated in BEpcs at 3 days (Fig. 4C). As TPM1 regulates cytoskeletal dynamics and cell migration via actin filament stability, this isoform switch may support migration and regeneration during late repair.32,33 The ERICH5 isoform ENSMFAT00000024382 showed significantly increased usage in BEpcs, LEpcs, and SEpsc at 1 day post-injury (Fig. 4D). Given ERICH5’s role in DNA damage response and stress response, this isoform upregulation may reflect transcriptional adaptation to injury-induced stress.

Cells Modulate Isoform Diversity to Fine-Tune Repair Responses

Beyond isoform switching, cells can also modulate isoform diversity. This reflects a shift between expressing a single dominant transcript and multiple co-expressed variants, which may indicate functional flexibility. To quantify this, we calculated a DI based on Shannon entropy24 for each gene, measuring how evenly expression is distributed among its isoforms (Figs. 5A, 5B). Genes with one dominant isoform have low DI (monoform), whereas those with multiple co-expressed isoforms have high DI (polyform).

Polyform genes accounted for approximately 7% of genes in LEpcs, BEpcs, and SEpcs (Fig. 5C). Isoform diversity was dynamically regulated during repair, with significant differences between steady-state and injured tissue in LEpcs and SEpcs (Fig. 5D).We identified 27 genes showing substantial diversity changes (DI difference >0.2) during repair (Figs. 5E, 5F; Supplementary Data S5), including GMPPB (membrane protein synthesis), HLA-E (immune regulation),34 NAA38 (protein acetylation),35,36 and PRMT2 (transcriptional control),37,38 which showed the largest changes. These genes exhibited cell-type and stage-specific expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. S5). For example, GMPPB and NAA38 were more involved in early repair processes such as cell growth and migration, HLA-E functioned primarily during immune regulation, and PRMT2 supported functional recovery and proliferation through regulation of gene expression. Increased isoform diversity may generate protein variants with distinct functional properties, enabling cells to fine-tune their responses to the changing wound environment.

Cell-Type–Specific Isoform Switching Coordinates Distinct Phases of Epithelial Regeneration

To assess whether isoform-level expression matrices could identify cell-type–specific isoform switching events across repair stages, we systematically analyzed isoform expression trends in LEpcs, BEpcs, and SEpcs at 0 days, 1 day, and 3 days using IsoDiffR, identifying 62 isoforms showing clear isoform switches without changes in overall gene expression (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Data S6). Critically, many of these genes showed clear isoform switches without changes in overall gene expression (Supplementary Fig. S8), confirming that isoform regulation acts independently of transcriptional control.

We highlight 4 representative genes spanning distinct functional categories and repair phases: ARID5B, GSN, SRP54, and MFSD14A (Figs. 6B–E; Supplementary Figs. S6, S7). The switching isoforms displayed markedly different splicing structures (see Fig. 6B; Supplementary Figs. S6C, S6D), suggesting functionally distinct variants. ARID5B, a chromatin remodeling regulator of cell proliferation and migration,39,40 showed significant isoform switching in LEpcs from 0 days to 1 day (see Figs. 6D, 6E). GSN, an actin-binding protein implicated in cell migration and corneal stromal dystrophy41; switched isoforms primarily in SEpcs over the same period (see Figs. 6D, 6E). SRP54 and MFSD14A, involved in protein targeting and secretion,42,43 showed prominent switching in LEpcs between 1 day and 3 days (see Supplementary Figs. S6A–F). Notably, these genes exhibited clear isoform usage switching without significant changes in overall gene expression levels (see Supplementary Fig. S8), demonstrating that isoform switching captures critical information masked in gene-level analysis.

Together, these findings suggest that gene-level and isoform-level regulation act together during corneal repair. Broad transcriptional changes drive the overall process, whereas isoform switching fine-tunes cell-specific functions to support precise tissue restoration.

Discussion

Corneal epithelial wound healing is essential for preserving vision after injury, yet the molecular programs coordinating this complex process remain incompletely understood. Using the long-read single-cell sequencing method HIT-scISOseq, we constructed a single-cell RNA isoform atlas of early corneal epithelial wound healing in cynomolgus monkeys, and identified widespread isoform-level remodeling including altered isoform usage, dynamic isoform diversity, and 62 significant isoform-switching events that were not captured by gene expression alone.

Previous single-cell studies of the cornea, including our own short-read analysis of wound healing in cynomolgus monkeys and studies of mouse corneal injury responses,13–15 have characterized cellular heterogeneity and gene-level transcriptional dynamics during repair, but cannot resolve isoform-level regulation. Rodent models have also provided valuable insights into corneal wound healing, but anatomic and physiological differences from primates may limit translational relevance. Isoform-level dynamics have recently been recognized as an independent regulatory layer in other tissues, such as the heart,44 brain,45 and immune cells,46 yet remain largely unexplored in the cornea. By applying long-read sequencing in a clinically relevant nonhuman primate model, the study addresses this gap.

Several isoforms highlighted in this study warrant further biological investigation. Although RPS5 and RPL35A are traditionally regarded as constitutive components of the translational machinery, accumulating evidence indicates that ribosomes may be compositionally heterogeneous and that individual ribosomal proteins may contribute to context-dependent translational regulation.47 RPS5 and RPL35A have also been associated with cellular proliferation and migration in several experimental systems.48–52 Their isoform-level changes during corneal repair may therefore reflect alterations in translational or extra-ribosomal regulation associated with epithelial regeneration. However, because most available functional evidence derives from tumor models, the isoform-specific functions of RPS5 and RPL35A in corneal wound healing cannot be inferred from transcriptomic data alone and require further experimental validation.

CTSL, classified here as a stress-response-associated gene, encodes a lysosomal cysteine protease that can also participate in extracellular-matrix degradation and remodeling.53 These processes may support changes in cell adhesion and epithelial migration during tissue repair. The altered CTSL isoform usage observed in this study may therefore represent a transcriptional adaptation linking the early injury response with subsequent matrix-remodeling processes, although the functions of the individual CTSL isoforms remain to be experimentally determined.

Future studies combining isoform-specific RNA-FISH with migration-associated markers, such as vimentin,54 in injured corneal tissues will be important for determining whether migration-related isoforms, including CTSL and TPM1 isoforms, are spatially associated with actively migrating epithelial cells. In contrast, Transcript16407.8 represents a novel, unannotated transcript with no currently established biological function. Detection of its transcript-specific signal by RNA-FISH supports its biological presence and spatial localization in the corneal epithelium. Its identification highlights the ability of long-read sequencing to uncover previously unrecognized transcript diversity that may be difficult to resolve using conventional short-read approaches. Further studies will be required to determine its coding potential and physiological function. To support the reliability of these findings, we performed independent experimental validation using RNA-FISH and isoform-specific RT-qPCR, providing orthogonal support for the sequencing results. These experiments validate expression patterns rather than establish direct functional roles. Protein-level validation, although desirable, was not feasible, as many identified isoforms are novel and lack isoform-specific antibodies, whereas conventional gene-level antibodies cannot distinguish between transcript isoforms. Future studies using isoform-specific knockdown or splice-switching perturbation will be needed to determine casual contributions to epithelial migration, proliferation, and wound closure. Additionally, sample size was constrained by ethical considerations and resource availability. Our study focused on the early repair phases, leaving later remodeling processes such as those occurring approximately at day 7 for future investigation.

Conclusions

This study provides a single-cell isoform atlas revealing widespread isoform-level remodeling during early corneal epithelial repair, establishing RNA isoform regulation as an important and previously underexplored layer of post-transcriptional control, offering a foundation for future functional and therapeutic studies of corneal wound healing.
