Decreasing cardiovascular events after kidney transplantation: a Finnish analysis covering 1990–2019
Authors: Salla Helve, Jaakko Helve, Mika Gissler, Tuomo Nieminen, Mika Laine, Patrik Finne, Ilkka Helanterä
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCardiovascular prognosis has improved in the general population during the past decades, but it is unclear if this trend explains the better cardiovascular outcomes in kidney transplant recipients.MethodsWe obtained data of all first kidney transplant recipients (n = 4942) in Finland 1990–2019 from the Finnish Transplantation Registry and the Finnish Care Register for Health Care. We analyzed post-transplant non-fatal cardiovascular events [acute myocardial infarction (AMI) or stroke] and compared the risk in patients transplanted in 1990–99, 2000–09, and 2010–19. We also compared the incidences of cardiovascular events in kidney transplant patients with the general population of Finland using age and sex-adjusted standardized incidence ratios.ResultsThere were 811 non-fatal cardiovascular events during the follow-up time (median 7.4 years, interquartile range 3.5–13.4), with 137 events within the first post-transplant year. Long-term cardiovascular-event risk was significantly higher in adjusted analyses in patients transplanted in 1990–99 than in 2000–09 (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.63–0.89) or 2010–19 (HR 0.78 95% CI 0.63–0.97). The standardized incidence ratio compared with the general population declined from 4.61 to 1.65 for AMI and from 3.62 to 1.13 for stroke between 1990–94 and 2015–20.ConclusionsThe adjusted risk of post-transplant cardiovascular events has decreased after the 1990s. The incidence of cardiovascular events, especially AMI, has declined more in kidney transplant patients than in the general population. Therefore, better cardiovascular outcomes of kidney transplant patients are not only explained by the improved prognosis of the general population.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease is a major risk factor for the development of cardiovascular disease [1]. Although kidney transplantation improves cardiovascular prognosis [2], cardiovascular disease remains common and is the leading cause of death in kidney transplant recipients [3, 4]. Kidney transplant recipients are nowadays older and have more comorbidities [5, 6], but at the same time, treatment strategies for both cardiovascular disease and transplantation have improved over the past decades. Therefore, it is important to examine how these trends affect the risk of post-transplant cardiovascular events. Age-standardized incidence of cardiovascular disease has decreased in the general population in Europe over the last decades [7], and there are some trend reports on the cardiovascular events in kidney transplant recipients, but they do not reach to the more recent era and the study periods are shorter [6, 8]. These studies have reported that the risk of cardiovascular events has not increased despite the current higher risk profile of kidney transplant recipients. Previous studies, however, do not answer the question of whether the changes in the incidence of cardiovascular events in kidney transplant recipients reflect only the positive development in the general population, or whether the transplant-specific risks have decreased.

The objective of this study was to determine the trends in post-kidney transplant major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients transplanted in 1990–2019 in Finland. We also compared the incidence of MACE in kidney transplant recipients to that in the general population to find out if the trends seen in the kidney transplant population reflect the changes in the general population or if there are unique factors specific to this group.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study data were extracted from the Finnish Transplantation Registry and the Finnish Care Registry for Health Care (HILMO). The national transplantation registry contains information about all transplantations, baseline characteristics, immunosuppression, complications, and causes of death. Information about hospital admissions and cardiac interventions after kidney transplantation was acquired from the HILMO registry, which is a national health care registry maintained by the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare. It includes all inpatient care since 1967 and outpatient care in public hospitals since 1998. The register collects information on hospitalizations (day of admission and discharge), outpatient visit (day of visit), diagnoses given during the inpatient care (International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes since 1996 and ICD-9 codes in 1987–95) and surgical procedure codes (NOMESCO Classification of Surgical Procedures since 1997 and national classifications until 1996). About 1.7 million persons are reported to the registry annually, with >1.5 million periods of care and >6 million outpatient visits every year. Reporting to both registries is mandatory and obliged by law.

All patients (n = 4942) who underwent their first kidney transplantation in Finland during 1990–2019 were included in the study. Data on cardiovascular events in the Finnish general population were obtained from the Finnish Care Registry for Health for each calendar year, stratified by sex and five age categories: 0–19, 20–44, 45–64, 65–74, and 75 years or more.

The primary endpoint was the first non-fatal MACE after kidney transplantation. If the patient experienced more events during the follow-up period, only the first one was included. MACE was determined as acute myocardial infarction (AMI) (defined as 410 in ICD-9 or I21-I22 in ICD-10) or ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke (430–434 in ICD-9 or I60-64 in ICD-10), as defined in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) health care quality outcomes indicators [9]. We have previously reported trends in cardiovascular mortality in this same patient population [3], and therefore we concentrated on non-fatal MACE in this study. MACE was considered non-fatal if the patient did not die on the first or second day of admission. However, we did sensitivity analyses where we included cardiovascular deaths in MACE. In addition, we included fatal AMIs and strokes in the comparison with the general population because the data on the general population also contained fatal events. Data on coronary revascularizations (percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass graft) were not systematically collected to the registries in the 1990s so we could do comparisons regarding them only between 2000–09 and 2010–19.

Patients were followed until MACE, death, 3 months after graft failure (return to dialysis or re-transplantation) or until the end of the follow-up period 31 December 2020. As we wanted to examine events in patients with a functioning graft and returning to dialysis or receiving another kidney transplant after graft failure can significantly affect the cardiovascular prognosis, we excluded events that occurred three months after graft failure. Three-month period was chosen due to the definition of end-stage kidney disease and permanent graft loss at 3 months.

The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board of Helsinki University Hospital Abdominal Center (HUS/115/2020) and the Finnish Social and Health Data Permit Authority Findata (THL/6825/14.02.00/2020). Findata was responsible for the pseudonymization of the data and ensuring the anonymity of the results. The clinical and research activities being reported are consistent with the Principles of the Declaration of Istanbul as outlined in the Declaration of Istanbul on Organ Trafficking and Transplant Tourism.

Categorical data were presented as percentages and continuous data as medians with interquartile range (IQR). The χ2 and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to calculate between-group differences in the distributions of categorical and continuous variables, respectively. We used univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses to compare the differences in the risk of MACE between the three decades. Proportional hazards assumptions were met. Based on established cardiovascular risk factors and available data, other covariates in the regression analyses were age at the time of transplantation, sex, pre-transplant dialysis vintage, and the cause of kidney disease (glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, type 1 or 2 diabetes, or other). All these covariates were also individually associated with MACE risk in an unadjusted Cox regression analysis. In addition, we examined the interaction between transplantation era and age in the multivariable Cox regression model. We conducted sensitivity analyses including immunosuppression (tacrolimus vs cyclosporine) and acute rejection as covariates in the Cox regression. We also used Fine–Gray competing risk regression to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios for cardiovascular events, treating death as a competing event. The frequency of missing values was very low (0.8% of patients had a missing value in any of the variables), and these cases were excluded, as values were estimated to be missing at random. As a sensitivity analysis, we imputed these missing values using multiple imputations (five imputations), and the results of the analyses remained unchanged. We used standardized incidence ratio to compare the kidney transplant population with the general population of Finland. Standardized incidence ratios for AMI and stroke were calculated using the indirect standardization method with AMI and stroke incidence in the general population stratified by sex and the five age groups as references (expected vs observed) [10]. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS release 27 for Windows (SPSS Inc, Chicago, Illinois, USA) and R version 4.4.1 (cmprsk package).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

During the study period from 1990 to 2019, 4942 patients underwent first kidney transplantation in Finland. The median follow-up time was 7.4 years (IQR 3.5–13.4). Transplant recipients were older, more often male and with longer dialysis duration in the later decades. Glomerulonephritis as the cause of kidney disease became less common and type 2 diabetes and polycystic kidney disease more common in the later eras. Peritoneal dialysis before transplantation was more frequent in the 1990s and there has been an increase in pre-emptive transplantations in the more recent years. Tacrolimus instead of cyclosporine and mycophenolate instead of azathioprine were used as baseline immunosuppression more often in the later eras. The number of living donors was low but increased in 2010–19 (Table 1).

MACE after kidney transplantation

The incidence of 1-year MACE after transplantation was quite low (2.8%) and remained stable during the study period. There were no statistically significant differences in the risk of 1-year MACE between the transplantation eras in unadjusted or adjusted Cox regression analyses. Almost half (48.9%) of the 1-year events occurred during the first post-transplant month. In unadjusted Cox regression analyses, no differences in long-term risk of MACE between the transplantation eras were observed, but adjusted analyses showed a decreased risk of long-term MACE after the 1990s (Table 2). Other covariates associated with higher risk of long-term MACE were older age, male sex, longer time on dialysis before transplantation, and diabetes (type 1 or 2) as the cause of kidney disease. Older age and diabetes were the strongest predictors of 1-year MACE.

We performed a sensitivity analysis including cardiovascular deaths in MACE and the results were similar. The risk of long-term MACE was significantly lower in patients who were transplanted after the 1990s in the adjusted Cox regression analysis [HR 0.68 (95% CI 0.59–0.78) and HR 0.66 (95% CI 0.55–0.79) for transplantation years 2000–09 and 2010–19, respectively]. There were no differences between the transplantation eras regarding 1-year MACE in adjusted or unadjusted analyses. In further sensitivity analyses including immunosuppression as a covariate, the risk reduction of long-term non-fatal MACE was no longer significant in 2010–19 vs 1990–99. Adding immunosuppression did not change the results of 1-year MACE or 1-year or long-term AMI or stroke. Adjusting for acute rejection did not change the results in any of the analyses (Supplementary Table S1).

In a Fine-Gray competing risk analysis with all-cause death as a competing event, risks of long-term non-fatal MACE, AMI, and stroke declined significantly after the 1990s. There was no significant difference between the transplantation eras regarding the risk of 1-year MACE, AMI or stroke in Fine-Gray analyses (Supplementary Table S2).

MACE risk in older patients (>65 years)

In Kaplan-Meier analysis, the risk of 5-year MACE was lower in older patients transplanted in 2000–09 in comparison to 1990–99, but no significant differences in 1-year MACE risk or in younger patients were observed (Fig. 1). The 5-year MACE risk was lower in the older patients transplanted in 2000–09 than in 1990–99 also in an interaction analysis (P = .033) in Cox regression. In addition, the decreased 5-year MACE risk of older patients transplanted in 2000–09 was also seen in an interaction analysis with AMI (P = .044) as an endpoint but not with stroke.

Coronary revascularizations

Coronary revascularizations were rare after kidney transplantation. Less than 1% of transplant recipients underwent coronary revascularization within 1 year of transplantation and 6.4% until the end of the follow-up time. The incidence and risk of revascularization remained constant when comparing patients transplanted in 2000–09 and 2010–19 (Supplementary Table S3).

Effect on long-term mortality

Long-term all-cause mortality was significantly greater in patients who had non-fatal MACE or revascularization during the first year after transplantation than patients who did not. First-year MACE or revascularization was also associated with higher long-term mortality risk in unadjusted and adjusted Cox regression analyses (Supplementary Table S4).

Comparison with the general population

The incidences of AMI and stroke were significantly higher in kidney transplant recipients than in the general population when compared by sex and age groups. However, there was a declining trend in the standardized incidence ratio for both AMI and stroke during the last three decades (Fig. 2), showing that the incidences of stroke and AMI have decreased more in kidney transplant patients than in the general population. The standardized incidence ratio for AMI decreased from 4.6 to 1.7 and for stroke from 3.6 to 1.1 during the study period (Supplementary Table S5). Therefore, the additional risk of AMI and stroke associated with kidney transplantation is lower in the most recent decades.

DISCUSSION

This comprehensive study covering all patients receiving first kidney transplant in Finland from the past three decades showed that the adjusted risk of long-term post-transplant MACE has decreased after the 1990s in patients with a functioning graft. The incidence of MACE and coronary revascularizations during the first year after transplantation remained low throughout the study period and most of the 1-year events occurred already during the first post-transplant month. In addition, the incidence of AMI and stroke has declined more in kidney transplant patients than in the general population. Therefore, the better outcomes of kidney transplant patients are not only explained by the trends in the general population.

Our low incidence of 1-year MACE (2.8%) aligns with previous studies. This incidence has ranged between 2.6% and 5.6%, being lower in the more recent studies [11–14]. The adjusted risk of 1-year MACE remained similar during the last three decades in contrast to the decline seen in the adjusted long-term risk. Most of the first-year events occurred already during the first post-transplant month which could be due to perioperative reasons. Improved long-term risk-factor management and secondary prevention may have lowered long-term atherosclerotic risk more than perioperative risk with hemodynamic and electrolyte changes. First-year cardiovascular events and coronary interventions were also associated with higher long-term mortality risk. Therefore, it is important to identify these high-risk patients and effectively manage their cardiovascular risk factors. Previous studies have also reported worse long-term survival in patients with post-transplant MACEs [11, 15]. AMI and stroke were roughly as common during the first year after transplantation. Long-term adjusted risk of AMI decreased after the 1990s, whereas long-term stroke risk did not. One explanation could be the older age of transplant recipients nowadays. Older age is a risk factor for atrial fibrillation which is a common cause of stroke. Improvements in the prevention and care of traditional risk factors such as blood pressure and lipids may decrease the risk of AMI more than the risk of stroke. However, in competing risk analysis, the risk of both long-term AMI and stroke declined significantly. On the other hand, diagnostic possibilities for both AMI and stroke have improved during the last decades with improved imaging techniques and high-sensitivity troponins, which would further emphasize our finding of lower cardiovascular event incidence in the more recent years.

Other studies have also reported declining trends in MACE in kidney transplant patients. Agrawal et al. estimated using their data and the United States Renal Data System (USRDS) that the annual incidence of AMI in kidney transplant recipients reduced by 29% from 2003 to 2011 [16]. Lam et al. reported that the cumulative 3-year incidence of MACE (AMI, ischemic stroke, or revascularization) remained similar from 1994–97 to 2006–09 even though transplant recipients were older and had more comorbidities in the later eras [6]. In another study, the absolute risk of perioperative MACE remained relatively stable from 2004 to 2013 despite the higher risk profile of the transplant recipients [8]. We observed similar trends in our study population over the extensive 30-year study period. Although cardiovascular prognosis seemed more favorable in 2000–09, especially for older transplant patients, there were no significant differences between transplantation periods 2000–09 and 2010–19 in any of the analyses. In the future, cardiovascular outcomes of kidney transplant population may be improved even more with novel medications with positive cardiovascular impact. For example, there are promising results of the cardioprotective effects of SGLT2-inhibitors in kidney transplant recipients with diabetes [17, 18].

Our goal was to find out whether this improved cardiovascular prognosis of kidney transplant recipients was explained by the decline in the incidence of cardiovascular events in the general population. Kidney transplant recipients are at higher risk of MACE in comparison with the general population, but the risk is substantially lower than in the dialysis population [6, 19, 20]. De la Mata et al. showed that kidney transplant recipients had a significantly higher incidence of stroke deaths in comparison to the general population (standardized mortality rates, SMR) but excess stroke deaths decreased significantly from 11.6 in 1988 to 3.1 in 2013 [20]. In our study, the declining trend in standardized incidence ratios shows that the risk of cardiovascular events has decreased more in kidney transplant recipients than in the general population. This could stem from more active treatment of cardiovascular risk factors in kidney transplant patients and changes in the immunosuppressive protocols. After adjusting for immunosuppression, the risk reduction of long-term MACE was no longer significant in transplantation years 2010–19. The use of tacrolimus instead of cyclosporine has increased substantially and that could partly explain the improved cardiovascular outcomes. Tacrolimus is associated with better lipid and blood pressure profile but higher risk of diabetes than cyclosporine [21]. Overall, advances in immunosuppression may have led to better graft survival and thus also improved cardiovascular prognosis. However, there was a strong correlation between transplantation era and immunosuppression (the use of tacrolimus instead of cyclosporine has increased significantly) causing possible collinearity. Because of collinearity, we did not include azathioprine versus mycophenolate in the models because mycophenolate has almost replaced azathioprine after the 1990s. In our previous study with the same population, we showed that blood pressure and LDL levels of kidney transplant patients were lower in patients transplanted after the year 2000 in comparison with 1990–99. In addition, graft survival improved, and steroid withdrawal became more common in the more recent transplant years [3]. The higher risk of cardiovascular events in kidney transplant patients is not only explained by the accumulation of traditional risk factors. Recent years have brought more understanding about the cardiovascular risks associated with chronic kidney disease and therefore these risk factors are probably managed more effectively. Screening for cardiovascular disease is recommended, although the usefulness of extensive screening has been questioned in the recent years [22]. Screening practices for cardiovascular disease before transplantation have evolved during time also in Finland, and although unlikely in our opinion, could be associated with the reduced risk seen in our study. Longer dialysis vintage before transplantation is a known risk factor for cardiovascular disease [23, 24], and this was seen also in our results. Although median duration of dialysis before transplantation increased over the study period, dialysis duration was still relatively short and did not lead to a higher incidence of MACE.

The number of coronary revascularizations after kidney transplantation was low in our study. The results are in line with previous studies reporting 0.4% one-year incidence of coronary revascularization [11] and 1.4% three-year incidence of coronary revascularization with no significant changes from 1994 to 2009 [6]. Low incidence of coronary revascularizations might reflect the nature of ischemic heart disease in kidney transplant patients as microvascular disease is more common.

Our study has several strengths. It is a population-based study of all kidney transplant recipients in Finland from the last three decades. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report such a long-ranging comparison of the MACE risk in kidney transplant patients and comparison to the general population. Being an observational registry study, however, it cannot prove causalities. There might be residual confounding, because we could use only variables available in the registries for adjustments in the analyses. The registries contain no data about previous cardiovascular diseases, other comorbidities, medications, or laboratory values, most importantly kidney function. Nevertheless, the adjusted risk of MACE would probably have decreased even more if we could have included previous cardiovascular disease in the analyses as covariates, because the frequency of cardiovascular comorbidities has increased among kidney transplant recipients (Finnish Registry for Kidney Diseases). We included only the first MACE after kidney transplantation in the analyses because the registries might repeat previous diagnoses in the later admissions. The data on the general population may contain multiple events per patient in the same year, so the absolute standardized incidence ratios may be underestimated. However, this bias remains consistent throughout the study period allowing us to examine the trends seen in the standardized incidence ratios. Our results are from a single country with quite a homogenic and almost exclusively non-Hispanic White population and typically short waiting times before transplantation, so our results may not be generalizable to other transplant populations. In addition, the relatively small number of events should be considered when interpreting the results.

In conclusion, the adjusted risk of long-term MACE after kidney transplantation has decreased in the last decades. Moreover, this decline is not explained only with the trends in the general population, but the event-free prognosis has improved more in kidney transplant patients than in the general population. Early cardiovascular events and coronary interventions after kidney transplantation are rare but associated with worse long-term survival.
