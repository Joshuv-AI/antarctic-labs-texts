Gaussian approximation for non-linearity parameter estimation in perturbed random fields on the sphere
Claudio Durastanti

Abstract
The nonlinear parameter f NL f_{\mathrm{NL}} measures the amplitude of primordial non-Gaussianity in the cosmic microwave background radiation (CMB), offering a crucial test of early-universe models. While standard single-field inflation predicts nearly Gaussian fluctuations, more complex scenarios yield subtle non-Gaussian signals, particularly captured by the CMB bispectrum. In the local model, these signals arise through a quadratic correction to a Gaussian field. To estimate f NL f_{\mathrm{NL}} , we adopt a Komatsu–Spergel–Wandelt (KSW)-type estimator, based on spherical harmonics and Wigner 3j-symbols, and adapted to narrow-band configurations that depend on the range of multipoles considered.In this paper, we rigorously study its asymptotic properties by applying fourth-moment theorems from Wiener chaos theory. More in detail, we establish a quantitative central limit theorem for the KSW estimator, with an explicit convergence rate controlled by number of admissible multipoles. Our results establish both theoretical guarantees and practical robustness for high-resolution CMB analyses. Keywords: Spherical random fields, Primordial non-Gaussianity, spherical harmonics, Komatsu-Spergel-Wandelt (KSW) estimator, Spherical Bispectrum, central limit theorem. Introduction

The parameter
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
, defined as the amplitude of the second-order corrections in the local model of inflationary non-Gaussianity, plays a fundamental role in modern cosmology by quantifying deviations from Gaussianity in the primordial density fluctuations that seeded cosmic structure. A precise measurement of
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
allows us to test beyond the simplest single-field inflationary scenarios, where fluctuations are nearly Gaussian and fully described by the two-point correlation function, against more complex models (e.g. multi-field dynamics or non-standard kinetic terms) that naturally generate non-Gaussian signatures (see, e.g.,
Meerburg et al. 2019
,
Planck 2016
). These primordial non
-
Gaussian imprints are most directly probed via the temperature anisotropies of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation, which deliver a high
-
precision map of fluctuations at the surface of last scattering.

The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation, observed as small temperature anisotropies on the sky, provides a precise provides of the primordial density fluctuations at the surface of last scattering. Therefore, to analyze these anisotropies and infer non-Gaussian signatures, we model the observed temperature field
T(x)
on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
as a realization of an isotropic spherical random field, decomposed in spherical harmonics:

T(x)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell,m}(x),

where the
Y_{\ell,m}
describe an orthonormal basis on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and the corresponding random harmonic coefficients
a_{\ell,m}
contain the field’s statistical properties (see for example
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
). The assumption of isotropy, that is, statistical invariance under rotations, is well motivated by the cosmological principle and greatly simplifies inference by decoupling different harmonic modes.
If we additionally assume Gaussianity, motivated by the fact that simplest single-field inflationary models predict nearly Gaussian fluctuations, we establish a clear baseline where the field
T_{G}
is defined by

T_{G}=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell,m;G}Y_{\ell,m}(x),

where the Gaussian harmonic coefficients
\{a_{\ell,m;G}\}
satisfy
\mathbb{E}\left[a_{\ell,m;G}\bar{a}_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime};G}\right]=C_{\ell;G}\delta_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}}
. The angular power spectrum
\{C_{\ell;G}\}
encodes all the stochastic information of
T_{G}
, while its covariance function depends only on the angular separation

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{G}(x)\bar{T}_{G}(y)\right]=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}C_{\ell;G}P_{\ell}(\langle x,y\rangle).

Under these assumptions, all odd-order moments of the harmonic coefficients vanish.
Hence, the full spectral bispectrum, which captures third
-
order correlations in harmonic space, defined by

B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}=\mathbb{E}\left[a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\,a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\,a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}\right],

vanishes under the assumption of a Gaussian, isotropic field (see e.g.
Durastanti and Patschkowski 2019
,
Planck 2015
).

Realistic inflationary physics often induces small but measurable fluctuations from Gaussianity and isotropy. Introducing a perturbed field of the form

T(x)=T_{G}(x)+f_{\mathrm{NL}}\left(T_{G}^{2}(x)-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{G}(x)\right]^{2}\right),

where
T_{G}
is a mean-zero Gaussian isotropic field, provides a minimal, theoretically motivated framework to capture these non-Gaussian features (cf.
Marinucci and Peccati 2010b
,
Planck 2016
). Such perturbations are necessary both to reflect the imprint of interactions during inflation and to enable observational searches for new physics beyond the simplest models.
To extract a rotation-invariant signature of third-order correlations, one compresses the full bispectrum via the so-called Wigner-3j symbols into the reduced bispectrum, given by

B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left[a_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\,a_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}\,a_{\ell_{3},m_{3}}\right].

Under the local non-Gaussian model, this reduced bispectrum is proportional to
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
.
The Komatsu–Spergel–Wandelt (KSW) estimator, see for example
Komatsu et al. 2005
,
Münchmeyer et al. 2014
,
Planck 2016
, exploits this fact to construct an optimal, unbiased estimate of
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
from observed maps. Denoting by

\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}a_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\,a_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}\,a_{\ell_{3},m_{3}}

the observed reduced bispectrum, the estimator reads

\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{rL\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\ell_{3}\leq L}B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\,C_{\ell_{1};G}^{-1}C_{\ell_{2};G}^{-1}C_{\ell_{3};G}^{-1}}{\displaystyle\sum_{rL\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\ell_{3}\leq L}\left(B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\right)^{2}\,C_{\ell_{1};G}^{-1}C_{\ell_{2};G}^{-1}C_{\ell_{3};G}^{-1}}.

Here,
L
denotes the maximum multipole considered, which is determined by the experimental resolution or computational constraints, to ensure the estimator remains tractable in practice, while
r\in(0,1/2)
encodes a narrow-band strategy, commonly adopted to reduce the estimator’s sensitivity to low-frequency multipoles. This choice guarantees that the number of terms retained in the numerator is still asymptotically large (i.e., of order
L^{3}
), while effectively focusing the estimation on the high-frequency content of the field. In this paper, we develop a refined asymptotic analysis, valid in the limit as the maximum multipole cutoff
L
grows to infinity, of this KSW-type estimator in the high-frequency regime. More in detail, we establish a quantitative central limit theorem (CLT) for the KSW estimator of the primordial non-Gaussianity parameter
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
by employing fourth-moment central limit theorem techniques, by
Nourdin and Peccati 2012
, stating that a sequence of random variables living in a given Wiener chaos is characterized by a central limit theorem if and only if the sequence of their fourth cumulants converges to zero, providing also a rate of convergence. In particular, we prove

d_{\operatorname{TV}}\left(\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}-f_{\mathrm{NL}},Z\right)\leq\tilde{C}L^{-2},

where
d_{\operatorname{TV}}
is the total variance distance, and
\tilde{C}>0
is a positive constant to be discussed below.
This quantitative approach not only solidifies the theoretical underpinnings of the KSW estimator but also ensures its robustness for analyzing CMB data, offering a rigorous foundation for interpreting
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
constraints in both current and future cosmological surveys.

An intuitive way to see the
L^{-2}
rate is to note that the number of admissible multipole triplets grows like
N_{L}=O(L^{3})
. Hence
L^{-2}=O(N_{L}^{-2/3})
, which outpaces the usual
O(N_{L}^{-1/2})
decay of a standard CLT. This super
-
parametric exponent reflects the cubic volume growth of permissible modes combined with the boundary
-
dominated error in approximating sums by integrals, much like the classical Gauss circle problem where lattice-point counting yields a boundary-dominated error. Similar behaviors appear across discrepancy theory, lattice-point enumeration, and empirical process theory (see, e.g.,
Kuipers and H. 1974
).

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we present key results on spherical Gaussian and perturbed random fields, along with a discussion of both theoretical and sample bispectra. Section
3
is devoted to the construction and motivation of the estimator for
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
. In Section
4
, we state our main result, a quantitative central limit theorem for the estimator of
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
, and collect the pivotal auxiliary results necessary for its proof. Finally, Section
5
collects all the proofs.

2
Preliminary results

This section collects some results concerning harmonic analysis on the sphere and the construction of non-Gaussian random fields over the sphere when they can be considered as perturbed Gaussian ones. We begin by examining the harmonic expansion of these fields with an emphasis on their stochastic properties. Next, we revisit the angular bispectrum, which quantifies the three-point correlation function of spherical harmonics and serves as a crucial measure of non-Gaussianity, effectively capturing deviations from purely Gaussian behavior in the underlying field. For further details and discussions, the reader is referred, for example, to
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
,
Stein and Weiss 1971
,
Yadrenko 1983
and the references therein.

2.1
Gaussian random fields and their stochastic properties

Let us start by introducing some notation. From now on,
x=\left(\vartheta,\varphi\right)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
denotes the generic spherical coordinate. Also, let
\,\mathrm{d}x=\sin\vartheta\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta\,\mathrm{d}\phi
be the spherical Lebesgue measure, while
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2}\right)=L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2},\,\mathrm{d}x\right)
is the space of square-integrable functions over the sphere with respect to the measure
\,\mathrm{d}x
. As well-known in the literature (see, for example,
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
,
Stein and Weiss 1971
,
Vilenkin and Klimyk 1991
), the set of spherical harmonics
\left\{Y_{\ell,m}:\ell\geq 0;m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell\right\}
provides an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2}\right)
. The spherical harmonic can be expressed as the normalized product of the associated Legendre function
P_{\ell,m}:\left[-1,1\right]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
of degree
\ell
and order
m
, and a complex exponential function. Following for example
Szego 1975
, the representation is given as as follows

Y_{\ell,m}\left(x\right)=Y_{\ell,m}\left(\vartheta,\varphi\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{\left(\ell-m\right)!}{\left(\ell+m\right)!}}P_{\ell,m}\left(\cos\vartheta\right)e^{im\varphi},

where

P_{\ell,m}\left(u\right)=\frac{1}{2^{\ell}\ell!}\left(1-u^{2}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{\ell+m}}{\,\mathrm{d}u^{\ell+m}}\left(u^{2}-1\right)^{\ell+m},\quad u\in\left[-1,1\right].

Let
T_{G}\left(x\right)
denote a centered, isotropic, mean square continuous, Gaussian random field over the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. A random field
T_{G}
is isotropic if it is invariant in distribution with
respect to the so-called group of rotations
SO\left(3\right)
,

\displaystyle T_{G}\left(Rx\right)\overset{d}{=}T_{G}\left(x\right),

where
R\in SO\left(3\right)
and
\overset{d}{=}
denotes equality in distribution.
The harmonic expansion of
T_{G}
is given by

T_{G}\left(x\right)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell,m;G}Y_{\ell,m}\left(x\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

(1)

where the term corresponding to
\ell=0
vanishes due to the centering of
T_{G}
.
The set of harmonic coefficients
\left\{a_{\ell,m;G}:\ell\geq 1,m=-\ell,\dots,\ell\right\}
contains all the stochastic information related to
T_{G}
and are computed by

a_{\ell,m;G}=\langle Y_{\ell,m},T_{G}\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}T_{G}\left(x\right)\overline{Y}_{\ell,m}\left(x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
\overline{z}
denotes its complex conjugate.
Also, as a consequence of the centering of
T_{G}
, it directly follows that for any
\ell\geq 1
,
m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell

\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell,m;G}]=0.

Also, since
Y_{00}(x)=\left(4\pi\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for any
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, it follows that
a_{00}=0
for centered random fields, so that Equation (
1
) reduces to

T_{G}\left(x\right)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell,m;G}Y_{\ell,m}\left(x\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

In view of the isotropy, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the covariance function of
T_{G}
is given by

\mathbb{E}[T_{G}(x)\overline{T_{G}}(y)]=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}C_{\ell;G}P_{\ell}\left(\langle x,y\rangle\right),

where
P_{\ell}:\left[-1,1\right]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
\ell
,
\left\{C_{\ell;G}:\ell\geq 0\right\}
are the angular power spectrum coefficients associated to
T_{G}
, and
\langle x,y\rangle
is the great circle distance between
x
and
y
, the arc length of the great circle connecting the two points. The angular power spectrum is the collection of the coefficients for the Legendre expansion of the covariance function of the field. Roughly speaking, it gives the expected variance of the field at angular scale roughly
\pi/\ell

such that higher
\ell
’s correspond to finer angular features; it is related to the harmonic coefficients by the following formula

\mathbb{E}\left[a_{\ell,m;G}\overline{a}_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime};G}\right]=C_{\ell;G}\delta_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}},

(2)

where
\delta_{\cdot}^{\cdot}
is the Kronecker symbol.
We assume a mild regularity condition on the angular power spectrum

C_{\ell}
, consistent with standard models used in applications such as cosmology and spatial statistics, see for example
Marinucci and Peccati 2010a
,
Durastanti et al. 2014
.

Condition 1
.

Let
C_{\ell;G}
be defined as in (
2
). For any
\ell\geq 0
, there exist an amplitude
A>0
, and a spectral index
\alpha>2
so that

C_{\ell;G}=A\left(1+\ell\right)^{-\alpha}.

Heuristically, Condition
1
imposes a form of regular variation on the asymptotic behavior of
C_{\ell;G}
, reflecting features that are central to many practical applications of spherical random fields. For instance, this condition aligns with the behavior of the Sachs-Wolfe power spectrum, which serves as a leading model for the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation. The Sachs-Wolfe spectrum describes the fluctuations in the primordial gravitational potential (see, for example,
Dodelson 2003
). In this framework, the constant
A
quantifies the overall intensity of the random field, while the spectral index
\alpha

encodes the scale invariance properties of the field, offering insight into its underlying structure and physical characteristics. This balance between intensity and scale invariance is critical for understanding and modeling phenomena such as the CMB.
You can smoothly add these important statistical properties of the harmonic coefficients in a dedicated paragraph after you introduce the angular power spectrum and its relation to the harmonic coefficients via (
2
).
Under the assumption of isotropy and Gaussianity, the harmonic coefficients
\{a_{\ell,m;G}:\ell\geq 1,m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell\}
inherit a particularly convenient probabilistic structure. First, they are mutually independent, and, considering also that
T_{G}
is centered, each
a_{\ell,m;G}
is a centered complex Gaussian random variable with variance
C_{\ell;G}
:

a_{\ell,m;G}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\ell;G}),\quad\mbox{independently for all }\ell\geq 1,;m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell,

where
\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\ell;G})
denotes a complex Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance
C_{\ell;G}
(see e.g.,
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
,
Lang and Schwab 2015
).
Then, under Gaussianity and isotropy, the harmonic coefficients
\{a_{\ell m;G}:\ell\geq 1,m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell\}
form a collection of mutually independent, centered complex Gaussian random variables with variances given by (
2
). Following for example
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
[Chapter 5], we can also show that all odd-order joint moments vanish, that is, for any integer
k
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{2k+1}a_{\ell_{j},m_{j};G}\right]=0;

while all even-order joint moments decompose as sums over pairwise contractions:

\mathbb{E}\left[a_{\ell_{1},m_{1};G}\ldots a_{\ell_{2k}m_{2k};G}\right]=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2k}}\prod_{(i,j)\in\pi}\mathbb{E}\left[a_{\ell_{i},m_{i};G}\bar{a}_{\ell_{j},-m_{j};G}\right],

where the sum runs over all pairwise partitions
\pi
of the index set
\{1,\ldots,2k\brace
, and each contraction term vanishes unless
\ell_{i}=\ell_{j}
and
m_{i}=-m_{j}
.
This formula is a complex version of Wick’s theorem, valid for centered complex Gaussian variables (see
Marinucci and Peccati 2010b
). It plays a central role in the analysis of high-order statistics, bispectra and polyspectra of isotropic fields on the sphere.

2.3
Perturbed random fields and their stochastic properties

With perturbed Gaussian random fields, we denote those random fields, where non-Gaussianity, arises as a “small” perturbation of the Gaussian random field. This model finds relevant applications in Cosmology and Astrophysics, among other research fields. Indeed, most of the inflationary models predict the fluctuations of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation to be close to but not exactly Gaussian (see, for example,
Planck 2016
and the references therein). From now on, we will focus on the so-called “slow-roll” inflationary model
Dodelson 2003
. Thus, the perturbed Gaussian random field can be parametrized in the simplest form by the following model

\displaystyle T\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=T_{G}\left(x\right)+f_{\mathrm{NL}}\left(T_{G}^{2}\left(x\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{G}^{2}\left(x\right)\right]\right)\text{ , }x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}

\displaystyle=T_{G}\left(x\right)+f_{\mathrm{NL}}H_{2}\left(T_{G}\left(x\right)\right)\text{ , }x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

where
T(x)
describes the primordial curvature perturbation field at the end of inflation and
T_{G}
is its Gaussian part, while
H_{2}
is the Hermite polynomial of order
2
. The non-Gaussian random field is obtained by summing to the Gaussian random field
T_{G}
a perturbation whose amplitude is given by the parameter
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
. The perturbation itself depends on
T_{G}
. It corresponds indeed to the second-order Hermite polynomial
H_{2}\left(u\right)=u^{2}-1
taking as argument
T_{G}
itself.

The dimensionless parameter
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
describes the amplitude of the non-Gaussian perturbation and, in general, depends only upon physical constants (see again
Dodelson 2003
). The term
f_{\mathrm{NL}}\mathbb{E}\left[T^{2}_{G}\left(x\right)\right]
is expected to be very small, more precisely of order
10^{-3}/10^{-4}
(see, for instance,
Bartolo et al. 2004
).

Let us now focus on the harmonic expansion of
T
. The following equality holds, in the
L^{2}
-sense,

\begin{split}T\left(x\right)&=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell,m}\left(x\right)\\
&=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\left(a_{\ell,m;G}\left(x\right)+f_{\mathrm{NL}}a_{\ell,m;2}\right)Y_{\ell,m}\left(x\right),\end{split}

(12)

where the harmonic coefficients
\left\{a_{\ell,m}:\ell\geq 1,m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell\right\}
are defined by

a_{\ell,m}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}T\left(x\right)\overline{Y}_{\ell,m}\left(x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=a_{\ell,m;G}+f_{\mathrm{NL}}a_{\ell,m;2}.

(13)

The Fourier expansion of

2.4
The angular bispectrum and its statistical properties

The angular bispectrum is a pivotal tool in the analysis of cosmological data, offering valuable insights into the statistical properties of fields on the celestial sphere. It characterizes the three-point correlation function of spherical harmonics and serves as a key measure of non-Gaussianity, capturing deviations from purely Gaussian behavior in the underlying field. Analogous to how the angular power spectrum acts as the Laplace transform of the two-point angular correlation function, the angular bispectrum can be regarded as the harmonic transform of the three-point angular correlation function. More in detail, let
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and

\widetilde{T}\left(x\right)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\widetilde{a}_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell,m}\left(x\right)

be a perturbed random field defined as in (
16
). Also, we introduce some auxiliary notation that will be central to organizing the structure of our estimator.

•

Symmetric covariance product
\Gamma_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
.
For any pair of distinct indexes
i\neq j=1,2,3
, define the normalized covariance product

\gamma_{\ell_{i},\ell_{j}}=2\frac{C_{\ell_{i}}C_{\ell_{j}}}{\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}},

which captures the contribution of each pairwise interaction between the multipoles
\left(\ell_{1},\ell,2,\ell_{3}\right)
relative to the geometric mean of the full triplet. This allows us to define the combined weight

\Gamma_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}=\gamma_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}+\gamma_{\ell_{1},\ell_{3}}+\gamma_{\ell_{2},\ell_{3}},

which serves as a compact way to encode the total interaction strength between all three legs of the bispectrum.

•

Azimuthal Multiplicity Index
h_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
.
To account for the dimensional multiplicity of the spherical harmonics at each multipole

\ell

h_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}=\sqrt{\frac{\left(2\ell_{1}+1\right)\left(2\ell_{2}+1\right)\left(2\ell_{3}+1\right)}{\left(4\pi\right)^{3}}}.

Finally, we combine the formerly defined elements with the Wigner
3j
-symbol
\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
to define:

•

Angular Bispectrum Weight.

\begin{split}\eta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}&=2\zeta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}+C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{3}}+C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}{\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}}\\
&\quad\frac{\sqrt{\left(2\ell_{1}+1\right)\left(2\ell_{2}+1\right)\left(2\ell_{3}+1\right)}}{\left(4\pi\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\\
&=2\zeta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}h_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\Gamma_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}},\end{split}

(17)

where
\zeta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}=1+\delta_{\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{2}}+\delta_{\ell_{2}}^{\ell_{3}}+3\delta_{\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{3}}
(see for example
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
).

The three point correlation is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{T}\left(x_{1}\right)\widetilde{T}\left(x_{2}\right)\widetilde{T}\left(x_{3}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\ell_{1}\geq 1}\sum_{\ell_{2}\geq 1}\sum_{\ell_{3}\geq 1}\sum_{m_{1}=-\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{1}}\sum_{m_{2}=-\ell_{2}}^{\ell_{2}}\sum_{m_{3}=-\ell_{3}}^{\ell_{3}}B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}Y_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\left(x_{1}\right)Y_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}\left(x_{2}\right)Y_{\ell_{3},m_{3}}\left(x_{3}\right),

(18)

where the full bispectrum
B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}
is given by

B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{3},m_{3}}\right].

(19)

If we consider the non-perturbed normalized random field
\widetilde{T}_{G}=T_{G}/\sqrt{C_{\ell;G}}
,
it holds that

B_{G;\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1};G}C_{\ell_{2};G}C_{\ell_{3};G}}}\mathbb{E}\left[a_{\ell_{1},m_{1};G}a_{\ell_{2},m_{2};G}a_{\ell_{3},m_{3};G}\right]=0,

since the expectation of the product of an odd number of harmonic coefficients in a Gaussian field is always zero.
As a result, the three-point correlation function is also zero, expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T_{G}\left(x_{1}\right)T_{G}\left(x_{2}\right)T_{G}\left(x_{3}\right)\right]=0,

see for example
Marinucci 2006
,
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
.

Assume now that the considered random field
T
is isotropic; in this case, both the sides of (
18
) are not modified by rotations of the coordinate system. it implies that the structure of
B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}
ensures that
\mathbb{E}\left[T\left(x_{1}\right)T\left(x_{2}\right)T\left(x_{3}\right)\right]
remains unaltered if
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, and
x_{3}

are rotated by the same angles. Following
Marinucci 2006
, the angular bispectrum of an isotropic field can be not-null only if the following conditions hold:

1.

\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
, and
\ell_{3}
satisfy the triangle rule, that is,
\left|\ell_{j}-\ell_{k}\right|\leq\ell_{i}\leq\ell_{j}+\ell_{k}
, for all choices of

i,j,k=1,2,3
;

2.

\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}
is even;

3.

m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}=0
.

These selection rules directly mirror the properties of the Wigner
3j
-symbols, which encode the angular momentum coupling conditions. In particular, the bispectrum coefficient
B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}
can be nonzero only if the corresponding Wigner
3j
-symbol is nonvanishing, which precisely requires the triangle condition, the sum
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}
to be even, and
m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}=0
(see Section
2.2
).

The full bispectrum is rarely used directly in cosmological analyses due to its detailed nature; instead, rotationally invariant forms are preferred. The rotationally invariant version of the bispectrum is obtained by summing over all
azimuth indices the full bispectrum weighted by the related Wigner

3j
-symbol to enforce rotational symmetry:

\begin{split}B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{3},\ell_{3}}&=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\\
&=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{3},m_{3}}\right].\end{split}

(20)

The rotationally invariant bispectrum encodes the overall strength of mode coupling in harmonic space; it still
satisfies the triangle condition for
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
, and
\ell_{3}
, and includes both physical information and geometric factors via the Wigner
3j
-symbol. The dependence on
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}
, which does not carry any physical information if the field is isotropic, is removed. This representation is widely used in theoretical and observational cosmology to study non-Gaussianities and their relation to physical processes.

Finally, the reduced bispectrum is defined to isolate the physical signal by factoring out the geometric terms (e.g., Wigner
3j
-symbol and related factors) from the rotationally invariant bispectrum
B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{3},\ell_{3}}
.
More in detail, as shown by
Hu 2001
, a necessary and sufficient condition for
B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}
to represent the angular bispectrum of an isotropic random field
is that there exists a real function
b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}
, symmetric with respect to the permutations of the three indexes
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{3}
, such that the following identity holds:

\displaystyle B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}.

The function
b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}
is called reduced bispectrum and it features the advantage to be independent on the indexes
m_{1},m_{2}
and
m_{3}
.
Using the properties of the Wigner
3j
symbols composing the Gaunt integral, yields the consequence that
\mathcal{G}\left(\ell_{1},m_{1};\ell_{2},m_{2};\ell_{3},m_{3}\right)
is not null only if the three conditions listed above are fulfilled.

As outlined in
[
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
, Section 9.2.2]
, the full bispectrum can similarly be expressed in terms of the other two representations as follows:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{3},m_{3}}\right]&=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}},\end{split}

(21)

the reader is also referred for further details to
Marinucci 2006
,
Marinucci and Peccati 2010b
and the references therein.

T

Remark 6

(The choice of the index set)
.

From now on, we restrict attention to ordered indices
\left(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right)
satisfying
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\ell_{3}
rather than considering all possible combinations. This restriction is justified by the symmetry of the bispectrum: due to the permutation invariance of the Wigner
3j
-symbols and the isotropy of the field, the bispectrum remains unchanged under any reordering of the multipole indices. As a result, considering all permutations leads to redundant information. By focusing on the ordered case, one selects a unique representative from each equivalence class of index triples, thereby avoiding overcounting and significantly reducing computational complexity. Furthermore, this restriction simplifies statistical analysis by ensuring that distinct bispectrum configurations are non-redundant and more easily treated as statistically independent or weakly correlated. This approach is both analytically and computationally efficient, without any loss of generality.

These results collected and presented in the following proposition have been originally shown in
Marinucci 2006
(see also
[
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
, Theorem 9.7]
).

Proposition 7
.

For any triple
\left(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
such that
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\ell_{3}
, let
\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}
be defined as in Equation (
23
). Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\right]=B_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}};;

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{2}\right]=1+O\left(f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{2}\right);

(25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cum}_{4}\left(\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\right)=6\left(\begin{Bmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\end{Bmatrix}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{2\ell_{i}+1}\right)+O\left(f_{\mathrm{NL}}\right).

(26)

Also, it holds that

\mathrm{Cum}_{4}\left(\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\right)\leq\frac{12}{2\ell_{1}+1}.

Remark 8

(Bispectrum and spherical white noise)
.

We recall also an alternative derivation for the bispectrum based the definition
of spherical stochastic processes (see, for example,
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
,
Yadrenko 1983
). The spherical
white noise on the sphere
W\left(\,\mathrm{d}x\right)
is a stochastic process featuring
the following properties:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W\left(\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\right]=0;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W\left(\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\right)\overline{W}\left(\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\right)\right]=\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\delta\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right),

where
\delta\left(\cdot\right)
is the Dirac delta function. The
component of a random field corresponding to the multipole
\ell
is given
by

\begin{split}T_{\ell}\left(x\right)&=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\widetilde{a}_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell,m}\left(x\right)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}\left(\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\right)W\left(\,\mathrm{d}y\right).\end{split}

For
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
T_{\ell}
is centered, that is,
\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell}\left(x\right)\right]=0,

while the two-point covariance matrix is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell}\left(x\right)\overline{T}_{\ell^{\prime}}\left(y\right)\right]=\frac{\left(2\ell+1\right)}{4\pi}P_{\ell}\left(\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\right)\delta_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}.

Now, notice that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}T_{\ell_{1}}\left(x\right)T_{\ell_{2}}\left(x\right)T_{\ell_{3}}\left(x\right)dx=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},m_{2,}m_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}\widetilde{a}_{\ell_{3},m_{3}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{\ell_{1},m_{1}}\left(x\right)Y_{\ell_{2},m_{2}}\left(x\right)Y_{\ell_{3},m_{3}}\left(x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}h_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}.

It follows that

\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}h_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}T_{\ell_{1}}\left(x\right)T_{\ell_{2}}\left(x\right)T_{\ell_{3}}\left(x\right)dx\text{ ,}

that is, sample bispectrum is essentially a scaled version of the sample third moment of the Fourier components of the random field, computed as an average over the sphere. Also, as stated in
[
Marinucci and Peccati 2011
, Lemma 9.6, Section 9.2]
, the sample bispectrum of a Gaussian, centered, isotropic random field is equal in distribution to a random variable lying ont the third Wiener chaos associated to a standard Brownian motion.

3
The construction of the OLS estimator for the nonlinearity parameter

Let us assume the following experimental set-up. We fix a parameter
r\in\left(0,1/2\right)
and a maximum angular resolution
L\in\mathbb{N}
, corresponding to the upper multipole level; we also define
L_{0}=rL
. Without losing generality, we consider also
L_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that the array

\left\{\widetilde{a}_{\ell,m}:\ell=L_{0},\ldots,L;m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell\right\}

is evaluated from the observed
field
T
. Furthermore, we assume that the set of harmonic coefficients from
L_{0}
to
L
can be reconstructed without numerical error or observational noise. The value of
L
clearly grows larger depending on the precision of the considered experiment. For instance,
L
is in the order of
500/600
,
1500/2000
, or
10000\textendash 12000
for data collected from WMAP, Planck, South Pole Telescope SPT and the Simons’ Observatory SO/SO4, respectively.

In what follows, we restrict our attention to the set of ordered triples

\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}=\{\left(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right):L_{0}\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\ell_{3}\leq L;\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}\leq\ell_{3}\leq\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\}

(27)

when analyzing the bispectrum. This choice is motivated by both mathematical and computational considerations. The bispectrum is invariant under permutations of the multipole indices due to the symmetry of the Wigner
3j
-symbols and the isotropy of the underlying random field, meaning that unordered configurations yield redundant information. By focusing on strictly ordered triples, we avoid this redundancy, reduce the computational cost, and simplify the statistical treatment of the bispectrum. Also, no generality is lost, as the full information content is preserved. The additional triangle inequality constraint ensures the non-vanishing of the associated Wigner coefficients, thereby identifying only admissible configurations.

Remark 9

(The cardinality of
\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}
)
.

Let
N_{L,L_{0}}
be the cardinality of
\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}
. For large
L
the dominant behavior of the sum is cubic in
L
, but the triangle inequality excludes many triples.
Indeed the total number of triples is

\binom{L(1-r)+1}{3}\approx\frac{L^{3}\left(1-r\right)^{3}}{6}.

The triangle inequality cuts off roughly about half of them, since in each random triple, the sum of the two smaller indices doesn’t always exceed the third, such that

N_{L,L_{0}}\approx\frac{L^{3}\left(1-r\right)^{3}}{12}.

Indeed, rescaling
x_{i}=\ell_{i}/L

x_{i}=\left(r,1\right)

and look at the region. The region where

3.1
Ordinary least squares method and nonlinearity parameter

In our approach, we treat the non-Gaussian amplitude
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
as the slope in simple linear regression of the observed bispectrum against its theoretical counterpart, and we estimate it by ordinary least squares (OLS). Concretely, for any
\left(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right)\in\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}
, we stack the measured, filtered bispectrum coefficients
\{\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}:\left(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right)\in\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}\}
into a data vector and the corresponding model predictions
\{\eta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}:\left(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right)\in\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}\}

into a design vector; then the OLS estimator
\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}
is exactly the best linear unbiased estimator under Gaussian noise assumptions, recovering the Komatsu–Spergel–Wandelt (KSW) estimator structure (see
Komatsu et al. 2005
), making high-resolution analyses feasible, inheriting its
O(L^{3})
scaling and optimal variance.

Remark 10
.

In our model, we will assume a slight dependence of
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
on

, namely,
f_{\mathrm{NL}}=f_{\mathrm{NL}}\left(L\right)
so that

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}f_{\mathrm{NL}}=0.

This condition reflects the framework of local alternatives, where one studies the behavior of estimators under increasingly subtle deviations from Gaussianity; this is well motivated in cosmology, where leading inflationary models predict a nearly Gaussian primordial curvature field, and any nonzero
f_{\mathrm{NL}}

is expected to be small—potentially vanishing in the high-resolution limit.
Scale-dependent

naturally arises in inflationary scenarios, with running couplings or multi-field interactions, so that as observational resolution

see for example
Liguori et al. 2010
and references therein.

In what follows, we review the construction of this nonlinear estimator, and we prove a central limit theorem for

in Wasserstein distance by the Fourth Moment Theorem of Nourdin–Peccati

More in detail, we consider the linear model for the measured bispectrum coefficients,

\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}=f_{\mathrm{NL}}\eta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}+\varepsilon_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}},

(28)

where the experimental noise

independent and identically distributed random variables, assumed to be centered, with variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
, uncorrelated to
\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}

We can now define the following vectors of dimension

based on
\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}

and

\begin{split}&{Y}=\left[\widehat{B}_{L_{0},L_{0}+1,L_{0}+2},\ldots,\widehat{B}_{L_{0},L_{0}+1,L},\widehat{B}_{L_{0},L_{0}+2,L_{0}+3},\ldots,\widehat{B}_{L_{0},L-1,L},\ldots,\widehat{B}_{L-2,L-1,L}\right]^{T}\\
&H=\left[\eta_{L_{0},L_{0}+1,L_{0}+2},\ldots,\eta_{L_{0},L_{0}+1,L},\eta_{L_{0},L_{0}+2,L_{0}+3},\ldots,\eta_{L_{0},L-1,L},\ldots,\eta_{L-2,L-1,L}\right]^{T}\\

Applying the ordinary least squares (OLS) method to the estimation of the non-Gaussianity parameter

f_{\mathrm{NL}}
leads to the construction of an estimator
\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}
. In this framework, we aim to minimize the squared residuals between the observed bispectrum and its theoretical expectation under a linear model. Specifically, the OLS estimator is defined by the solution to the optimization problem:

\underset{f_{\mathrm{NL}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\operatorname{argmin}}E^{T}E.

Using standard results from linear regression theory, the OLS solution can be explicitly written as:

\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}=\left(H^{T}H\right)^{-1}H^{T}{Y}.

In our context, this expression becomes explicitly:

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\left(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right)\in\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}}\eta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}^{2}\right)^{-1}\sum_{\left(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right)\in\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}}\eta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}.

(30)

f_{\mathrm{NL}}
a manner analogous to an OLS regression where

is the regressor and
Y
is the response variable.
Thus, using the bispectrum, we can write an OLS estimator for
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
that leverages the linear relation between the observed three-point statistics and the underlying non-Gaussianity parameter.

4
Asymptotic properties of the estimator: Gaussianity

As mentioned in Remark
8
, since
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
is a linear combination of elements of the sample bispectrum, which belongs to the third Wiener chaos, also the estimator
\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}
inherits this chaotic structure and can be expressed as an element in the third Wiener chaos. This observation is crucial because it allows us to apply the Fourth Moment Theorem to study its asymptotic behavior.

In this section, we establish a quantitative central limit theorem for
\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}
in total variation distance. The total variation distance between two probability measures
P
and
Q
on a measurable space
\Omega,\mathcal{F}
is defined as

d_{\operatorname{TV}}\left(P,Q\right)=\underset{A\in\mathcal{F}}{\sup}\left|P(A)-Q(A)\right|.

In particular, we will use the celebrated results known as the “fourth moment theorem" (see, for example,
[
Nourdin and Peccati 2012
, Theorem 5.2.6]
). If F belongs to the
q
-th Wiener chaos, then

d_{\operatorname{TV}}\left(F,Z\right)\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{q-1}{3q}\frac{\mathrm{Cum}_{4}\left(F\right)}{\left(\mathrm{Var}\left(F\right)\right)^{2}}},

where
Z
is a standard normal random variable.

In light of Remark
8
, since
\widehat{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}
belongs to the third Wiener chaos (i.e.,
q=3
), any linear combination of such terms also lies in the third chaos. This leads to the following bound for the centered estimator
\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}-f_{\mathrm{NL}}
:

d_{\operatorname{TV}}\left(\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}-f_{\mathrm{NL}},Z\right)\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Cum}_{4}\left(\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}\right)}{\left(\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}\right)\right)^{2}}},

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
.

Remark 16

(Some heuristics on the convergence rate)
.

1.

The key to understanding the speed
L^{-2}
of our total variation bound concerns how the estimator improves as the number of available bispectrum modes grows. More precisely, recall that the cardinality of elements in
\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}
, given by (
27
), the set of all admissible multipole-triplets satisfying the triangle and parity constraints is propositional to
N_{L,L_{0}}=O\left(L^{3}\right)
. Indeed, as stated in Remark
9
, each of the multipoles
\ell_{1},

\ell_{2}
, and
\ell_{3}
ranges over an

O\left(L\right)
window (up to constant factors), and the triangle inequalities (
3
) cut out only an
O\left(1\right)
-fraction of the full set. We can rewrite the bound in Equation (
37
) in Theorem
15
, we may re-express that rate in terms of
N_{L,L_{0}}
as

d_{\operatorname{TV}}\left(\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}-f_{\mathrm{NL}},Z\right)\leq O\left(N_{L,L_{0}}^{-\frac{2}{3}}\right),

When comparing this to a classical CLT rate
O\left(N_{L,L_{0}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)
, here we see a strictly faster decay exponent, since
2/3>1/2
. This super-parametric exponent is a direct consequence of the cubic growth of admissible multipole-triplets combined with the bispectral weighting structure.

2.

A similar result concerning objects belonging to the third Wiener chaos can be derived from a canonical example studied by
[
Nourdin and Peccati 2015
, Section 4]
, in the spirit of the Breuer–Major theorem. The example considers the normalized sum
F_{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}H_{3}\left(X_{k}\right)
, where
H_{3}
is the third Hermite polynomial and
\{X_{k}:k=1,\ldots,N\}
is a stationary Gaussian sequence with covariance
\rho{j}=\left|j\right|^{-\beta}
. This sequence lives in the third Wiener chaos, and the total variation distance between
F_{N}
and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
converges at the rate
O\left(N^{1-4\beta}\right)
. This provides an optimal benchmark for third-chaos bispectrum-type estimators. Also, for the critical decay rate
\beta=\frac{2}{3}
, the convergence rate become
O\left(N^{-\frac{2}{3}}\right)
, as in our case.

3.

It is possible to draw an analogy with the classic Gauss circle problem. Let

\nu_{R}=\operatorname{card}\{\left(j,k\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:j^{2}+k^{2}=R^{2}\}
be the number of integer lattice points in the disk of radius

R
. According for example to
Hardy and Wright 1979
, it is possible to show that

\nu(R)=\pi R^{2}+\epsilon(R),

where
\epsilon(R)
is the error term, of order
O(R^{2}/3)
. The relative error in approximating the disk’s area
\pi R^{2}
by lattice points is given by

\frac{\epsilon(R)}{\nu(R)}=\left(R^{-\frac{4}{3}}\right)=\left(N^{-\frac{2}{3}}\right).

In our estimator, we sum over indices in
\Lambda_{L,L_{0}}
, which form a
3
-dimensional simplex of integer points in
\mathbb{N}^{3}
, and approximate this discrete sum by an integral of a third-Wiener-chaos functional over the corresponding continuous region. Gauss’s circle problem makes the exact same kind of approximation in two dimensions—counting lattice points inside a disk by its area
\pi R^{2}
, with the error coming entirely from points near the circular boundary. In both cases the dominant contribution to the error comes from the
d-1
-dimensional boundary (here

d=3
for the tetrahedron, there
d=2
for the circle), which forces a convergence rate of order
N^{-\frac{d}{d-1}}
.
Geometrically, whenever one approximates a
d
-dimensional volume by discrete sampling, the surface–to–volume ratio governs the error, yielding this universal
d/(d-1)
exponent. In our case, looking at a relative error in total variation distance for
\widehat{f}_{\text{NL}}
versus the relative error in Gauss’s problem, the
d/(d-1)
exponent falls out of exactly the same boundary-vs-volume balance. This boundary-dominated phenomenon recurs throughout discrepancy theory, lattice-point enumeration, and empirical process theory (see, e.g.,
Kuipers and H. 1974
).

(39)

On the other hand, recall the Stirling approximation for the factorials (see, for example,
[

, Eq. 6.1.28]
): for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

n!\simeq\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}

(40)

Combining Eqq. (

) we can approximate
\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
with

\begin{split}\left(-1\right)^{\frac{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2e}{\pi}}\left(\frac{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}}{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}+1}\right)^{\frac{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}-1}{2}}\\
