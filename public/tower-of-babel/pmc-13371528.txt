Association between platelet/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and incidence of coronary heart disease: insights from the UK Biobank
Authors: Chen Chen, Pengfei Chen, Changgeng Fu, Dazhuo Shi, Ming Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe platelet-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (PHR) integrates platelet count and HDL-C concentration and may reflect thrombo-inflammatory and lipid-related cardiovascular risk. However, its association with incident coronary heart disease (CHD) and its incremental value beyond its individual components remain uncertain.MethodsWe included 326,803 UK Biobank participants without baseline CHD. PHR was calculated as platelet count (×109 cells/L) divided by HDL-C concentration (mmol/L) and analyzed in quartiles and as a log-transformed continuous variable. Incident CHD was defined as the first occurrence of angina pectoris, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), or chronic ischemic heart disease. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates. Restricted cubic spline models were applied to examine non-linearity. Discriminatory performance and incremental model information were evaluated using ROC/C-index analyses and likelihood ratio tests. Exploratory mediation-oriented analyses examined hs-CRP, HbA1c, and neutrophil count.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 13.0 years, 20,340 incident CHD events occurred. Compared with participants in the lowest PHR quartile, those in the highest quartile had a higher hazard of incident CHD in the fully adjusted model (HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.31–1.42; P < 0.01). The association was also observed for CHD subtypes and appeared stronger for AMI (Q4 vs. Q1: HR 1.68, 95% CI 1.56–1.81) than for chronic ischemic heart disease (Q4 vs. Q1: HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.32–1.45). Restricted cubic spline analysis suggested a non-linear association between log-transformed PHR and incident CHD (P for non-linearity < 0.001), with a relatively flat association at lower values and a steeper increase at higher values. PHR showed moderate discrimination for incident CHD (AUC 0.716, 95% CI 0.713–0.720), and adding PHR to the fully adjusted clinical model produced a small increase in C-index (0.714 to 0.720), larger than that observed when platelet count or HDL-C was added separately. Exploratory analyses suggested that hs-CRP, HbA1c, and neutrophil count statistically accounted for part of the observed association, although these findings may reflect shared inflammatory and metabolic background rather than causal mediation.ConclusionHigher PHR was associated with increased risk of incident CHD in this large prospective cohort, particularly for AMI. PHR may serve as a simple adjunctive marker for CHD risk stratification when interpreted alongside established clinical risk factors.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD), a predominant driver of global morbidity and mortality, continues to exert substantial pressure on healthcare systems worldwide [1, 2]. Although interventional techniques such as percutaneous coronary intervention and coronary artery bypass grafting have markedly improved acute-phase treatment, patient outcomes remain heterogeneous due to complex clinical and biochemical determinants [3]. Accordingly, the stratification of high-risk individuals and the implementation of timely, evidence-based therapeutic strategies are pivotal for improving the long-term management of CHD [4].

The Platelet-to-High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Ratio (PHR), calculated as the ratio of platelet count to HDL-C concentration [5], has gained recognition as a promising biomarker for a range of cardiometabolic conditions, including hypertension [6], hyperuricemia [7], diabetes [8], nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [9], and heart failure [10]. Functionally, circulating platelets play a central role in hemostatic regulation, whereas HDL-C facilitates reverse cholesterol transport and is implicated in the stabilization and regression of atherosclerotic plaques. Elevated platelet counts and low levels of HDL-C are central players in atherothrombosis, leading to adverse thrombotic events that obstruct blood vessels and impair blood flow [11].

Current CHD risk assessment relies mainly on established clinical and biochemical factors, whereas simple composite markers derived from routine blood tests may provide additional risk-related information. However, whether PHR is associated with incident CHD and whether it provides incremental information beyond its individual components remain insufficiently clarified [12–14]. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between PHR and CHD in the UK Biobank.

Methods

Study sample and participants

This study utilized data from the UK Biobank (UKB), a prospective population-based cohort comprising over 500,000 individuals aged 40 to 69 at enrollment [15]. Baseline assessments included self-administered questionnaires, physical measurements, biological specimen collection, and integration with healthcare databases. Longitudinal health outcomes are monitored via linkage to national electronic health records. Ethical approval for the UK Biobank was granted by the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

A total of 326,803 participants with complete data on the PHR, and with no prior history of CHD or loss to follow-up, were initially included. The study sample selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

PHR Measurement

At baseline, peripheral blood specimens were processed within 24 h of collection at the UK Biobank central laboratory using standardized protocols. Platelet counts were measured using an automated hematology analyzer, and HDL-C concentrations were obtained from baseline biochemistry assays. PHR was calculated by dividing platelet count (×109 cells/L) by HDL-C concentration (mmol/L). For regression analyses, PHR was natural-log transformed to reduce skewness and was also categorized into quartiles according to its baseline distribution.

Incidence of CHD

The primary outcome was CHD events, including angina pectoris (AP, I20), acute myocardial infarction (AMI, I21–I22), and chronic ischemic heart disease (CIHD, I25). Each participant contributed only one event to the composite CHD endpoint. Secondary outcomes were the first occurrence of each CHD subtype analyzed separately. These subtype-specific outcomes were not mutually exclusive; therefore, participants who developed more than one subtype during follow-up could contribute to more than one subtype-specific analysis, but only once to the composite CHD analysis. Participants were censored at death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first.

Assessment of covariates

Covariates encompassed demographic characteristics such as age, sex (male vs. female), ethnicity (White vs. non-White), and socioeconomic indicators including the Townsend Deprivation Index (higher scores indicating greater socioeconomic deprivation) and educational attainment (college/university degree, other qualifications, no formal qualifications). Lifestyle-related variables included sleep duration, alcohol consumption status (daily, 3–4 times per week, 1–2 times per week, 1–3 times per month, only on special occasions, or never), and smoking frequency (current, former, or never), and physical activity, classified by total weekly energy expenditure (< 600, 600–<3000, or ≥ 3000 metabolic equivalent of task (MET)-minutes/week). Medical history variables encompassed physician-diagnosed diabetes and hypertension. Anthropometric and clinical measurements included body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Laboratory biomarkers included fasting glucose, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), serum uric acid (SUA), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and serum creatinine (SC).

Hypertension was defined according to standard clinical criteria: SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, a self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was defined by fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a self-reported physician diagnosis, or the use of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were stratified according to CHD status, with categorical variables summarized as proportions and continuous variables expressed as means with standard deviations. To evaluate associations, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Log-transformed PHR was analyzed both as a categorical variable, grouped into quartiles (Q1 to Q4) based on its distribution, and as a continuous variable, with risk assessed per one-unit increase in the transformed value. Three models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally included ethnicity, socioeconomic status, education, alcohol use, smoking, sleep status, and physical activity; and Model 3 was further adjusted for diabetes, hypertension, BMI, triglycerides, LDL-C, and hs-CRP. HDL-C and total cholesterol were not included in Model 3 to avoid collinearity and overadjustment related to the denominator of PHR and overlapping cholesterol information.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles were used to examine potential non-linear dose–response associations between log-transformed PHR and incident CHD. Non-linearity was assessed by comparing models with and without the non-linear spline terms. Any spline-derived turning point was considered exploratory rather than a prespecified clinical threshold.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed as an exploratory assessment of the discriminatory performance of PHR, platelet count, and HDL-C for incident CHD status during follow-up. The area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CI were reported. Because conventional ROC analysis does not fully account for censoring in time-to-event data, C-index and likelihood ratio tests from Cox models were also used to assess incremental model information. Platelet count, HDL-C, and log-transformed PHR were separately added to the same fully adjusted clinical model, and model improvement was evaluated relative to that baseline model. These analyses were not interpreted as a formal test of superiority over a model including platelet count and HDL-C simultaneously as separate predictors.

Exploratory mediation analyses were performed to assess whether hs-CRP, HbA1c, and neutrophil count statistically accounted for part of the association between PHR and incident CHD. These analyses were adjusted for the same baseline covariates where appropriate, excluding the mediator under evaluation. Given the observational design and baseline-only measurements, these results were interpreted as statistical decomposition rather than evidence of a definitive causal pathway.

To test the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were performed: first, participants with less than two years of follow-up were excluded to minimize reverse causality; second, propensity score matching was applied to balance covariates between groups, enhancing the reliability of association estimates; and third, age was used as the timescale instead of follow-up duration to mitigate residual confounding by age.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Baseline characteristics stratified by CHD status are presented in Table 1. The overall mean age was 55.15 years, with females comprising 57.6% of the cohort and individuals of White ethnicity accounting for 94.7%. Participants who developed CHD tended to be older and predominantly male, with a higher likelihood of current smoking, increased alcohol intake, reduced physical activity, and shorter sleep duration. They also exhibited lower educational attainment and greater socioeconomic disadvantage. Additionally, this group showed a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and excess body weight, including overweight and obesity.

Association between PHR and risk of CHD

Over a median follow-up period of 13.0 years, 20,340 participants (6.22%) developed coronary heart disease (CHD), including 8,600 cases of angina pectoris (AP; 2.63%), 6,700 cases of acute myocardial infarction (AMI; 2.05%), and 17,204 cases of chronic ischemic heart disease (CIHD; 5.26%). The cumulative count of these subtypes exceeds the total number of primary CHD events because individual participants may have been diagnosed with multiple subtypes, either concurrently or sequentially, during the follow-up period.

Although the overall relationship between PHR and CHD risk showed significant evidence of non-linearity, for the purpose of standardized estimation, we first examined the association using a linear assumption. As illustrated in Table 3, participants in higher PHR quartiles demonstrated significantly increased hazards of developing CHD compared to those in the lowest quartile, consistent across all three multivariable models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each one-unit increase in log-transformed PHR was linked to a 48% higher risk of CHD (HR 1.48, 95% CI: 1.41–1.55; P < 0.01). Similarly, elevated log-transformed PHR was associated with an increased risk of AP (HR 1.62, 95% CI: 1.51–1.74), a 93% higher risk of AMI (HR 1.93, 95% CI: 1.78–2.08), and a 52% increased risk of CIHD (HR 1.52, 95% CI: 1.45–1.60), all with P < 0.01.

RCS analysis was used to explore the association between PHR and CHD. RCS analysis showed a statistically significant non-linear association between log-transformed PHR and incident CHD (P for overall association < 0.001; P for non-linearity < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The curve suggested a relatively flat association at lower log-PHR levels, followed by a steeper increase in CHD risk at higher log-PHR levels. Because the spline-derived turning point was exploratory and not prespecified, it should not be interpreted as a clinically actionable threshold.

RCS analysis of the association between PHR and CHD incidence

ROC analysis suggested that PHR had moderate discriminatory performance for incident CHD status during follow-up, with an AUC of 0.716 (95% CI: 0.713–0.720). The AUCs for platelet count and HDL-C were 0.5212 (95% CI: 0.5170–0.5253) and 0.5951 (95% CI: 0.5910–0.5991), respectively (Fig. 3). The optimal threshold was determined to be 0.6102 by maximizing the Youden index. At this threshold, the sensitivity was 71.06%, the specificity was 60.89%, the positive predictive value was 10.76%, and the negative predictive value was 96.94%. To further evaluate whether PHR provides additional information beyond its individual components, we compared models in which platelet count, HDL-C, or log-transformed PHR was separately added to the fully adjusted clinical model. Compared with Model 3 alone, the addition of platelet count modestly improved model fit, with an likelihood ratio test (LRT) χ² of 31.24 and a C-index of 0.715. The addition of HDL-C resulted in an LRT χ² of 129.24 and a C-index of 0.716. In comparison, the addition of log-transformed PHR produced the largest improvement in model fit, with an LRT χ² of 393.15 and a C-index of 0.720 (Table 4). These findings indicate that, among the three single-marker extensions examined, log-transformed PHR produced the largest improvement in model fit and a modest increase in C-index.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses (Table 5) demonstrated a consistently positive association between elevated PHR and CHD incidence across all examined strata. Notably, this relationship was more pronounced among participants under the age of 60 (HR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.56–1.79) and those of non-White ethnicity (HR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.53–2.23), suggesting potential effect modification by age and ethnicity.

To evaluate the stability of the observed associations, multiple sensitivity analyses were performed. First, excluding individuals with a follow-up duration of less than two years yielded comparable results (Table 6), with PHR remaining significantly associated with CHD risk (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.39–1.53, P < 0.01). Second, after covariate balancing between comparison groups (Table 6), the association persisted (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.37–1.50, P < 0.01). Third, modeling age as the time scale in the survival analysis (Table 6) continued to demonstrate a significant relationship (HR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.40–1.53, P < 0.01), reinforcing the robustness of the main findings.

Mediation analyses

Exploratory mediation analysis suggested that hs-CRP, HbA1c, and neutrophil count statistically accounted for part of the observed association between PHR and incident CHD. Specifically, hs-CRP accounted for 8.2% (95% CI: 7.0%–9.2%), HbA1c for 12.1% (95% CI: 11.1%–13.4%), and neutrophil count for 15.9% (95% CI: 13.6%–17.9%) of the observed association (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). These results should be interpreted cautiously because the apparent mediation may reflect shared inflammatory or metabolic background rather than a directional causal pathway.

Discussion

In this large-scale, prospective cohort study of middle-aged adults, elevated PHR levels were independently associated with an increased risk of developing CHD. This relationship persisted even after controlling for established cardiovascular risk factors. In the fully adjusted model, each one-unit increase in log-transformed PHR was associated with a 48% higher hazard of CHD, whereas participants in the highest PHR quartile had a 36% higher hazard compared with those in the lowest quartile.

PHR serves as a novel marker reflecting systemic inflammation and lipid metabolism [16]. Platelet-driven chronic inflammation within the vascular wall contributes to the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic plaque formation and atherothrombosis. In contrast, HDL-C plays a key role in endothelial health through its anti-inflammatory effects, anti-oxidative properties, and reduction of atherosclerosis by facilitating reverse cholesterol transport and inhibiting monocyte formation [17, 18]. Given their distinct mechanisms, the ratio of these serum markers has shown promise in CHD or its subtypes. In 2021, Jialal et al. identified a link between PHR levels and the severity of metabolic syndrome, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for assessing atherosclerotic thrombosis risk [19]. Building on this, Zhou et al. in 2024 demonstrated that elevated PHR was associated with an increased incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with myocardial infarction and non-obstructive coronary arteries, underscoring its prognostic relevance in cardiovascular pathology [20]. In the same year, Wu et al. reported a positive association between PHR and unfavorable long-term outcomes in individuals with coronary artery disease complicated by type 2 diabetes [14]. Collectively, accumulating evidence supports PHR as a promising circulating indicator for evaluating cardiometabolic risk [10, 21], which may complement conventional inflammatory or metabolic markers in identifying individuals with heightened atherosclerotic risk. In light of these findings, our study aimed to explore the association between PHR and incident CHD risk within a larger population-based cohort. While our mediation analysis suggests that inflammatory and metabolic markers, such as hs-CRP, HbA1c, and neutrophil count, may partially account for the observed association, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Given the observational nature of this study and the reliance on baseline-only measurements, these results indicate potential pathways rather than definitive causal mechanisms. The observed “mediation” may also reflect a shared inflammatory etiology. In this scenario, systemic cytokines act as common upstream drivers for both PHR components and the identified mediators, rather than representing a direct directional causal chain. Future research utilizing longitudinal data and dynamic monitoring is required to confirm whether these factors truly sit on the causal chain between PHR and CHD.

This study revealed that individuals with elevated PHR levels were typically older, more likely to be male, and exhibited a higher prevalence of coexisting chronic conditions compared to those with lower PHR values. This reflects the well-established link between PHR, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation. Aging is often associated with low-grade inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, and men typically have higher proinflammatory cytokines and metabolic syndrome than women [22]. Consequently, PHR levels are higher in older and male populations. As anticipated, an independent association was identified between elevated PHR levels and heightened CHD risk among middle-aged and older adults. These results are consistent with the findings of Manoochehri et al., who reported that higher PHR values were significantly related to the extent of coronary artery stenosis and the severity of coronary artery disease, as assessed by the Gensini score. Zhang et al. reported that individuals in the highest PHR quartile faced a 49% greater risk of stroke compared to those in the lowest quartile (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.13–1.96, p = 0.004) [23]. Additionally, our study expands on the existing knowledge of the relationship between PHR and cardiovascular disease (CVD). While previous studies have focused on the relationship between PHR and CVD prognosis in various conditions, such as major adverse cardiovascular events and mortality, our results suggest that PHR may serve as an early, low-cost marker associated with subclinical atherosclerosis and the progression of cardiac and cerebrovascular events. For instance, prior research has demonstrated that elevated PHR levels correlate with increased CVD mortality among stroke survivors [16, 23], and individuals with depression [24]. Moreover, a recent prospective cohort study involving 7,063 participants revealed that both log-transformed PHR and cumulative log PHR were independently linked to higher CVD risk [25]. Notably, the association between PHR and CHD appeared stronger for AMI than for chronic ischemic heart disease. This pattern is biologically plausible, as platelet activation is central to acute arterial thrombosis following plaque rupture, whereas chronic ischemic heart disease may more strongly reflect long-term atherosclerotic plaque burden. Therefore, PHR may be particularly informative for thrombotic or unstable manifestations of coronary disease. However, because CHD subtypes were not mutually exclusive in the present analysis, this interpretation should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires validation in studies with adjudicated and mutually exclusive coronary endpoints. By focusing specifically on CHD, our findings further support the potential utility of PHR as a practical, inexpensive biomarker associated with elevated risk of developing atherosclerotic cardiovascular complications.

This study benefits from several key strengths, including access to comprehensive biological and clinical data from nearly 500,000 participants in the UK, a prospective cohort design, extended follow-up duration, and meticulous adjustment for a wide range of cardiovascular risk factors. The use of standardized assessment protocols and stringent quality control measures further enhanced the reliability of both association and mediation analyses. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Primarily, PHR was derived from a single baseline blood measurement, limiting the ability to evaluate temporal variations or longitudinal trends. Second, one methodological limitation is that PHR, as a ratio-based index, mathematically combines platelet count and HDL-C into a single variable. Therefore, it may impose a constraint on the relative contribution of its numerator and denominator. Although PHR provided a larger improvement in model fit than platelet count or HDL-C when each marker was added separately to the same fully adjusted model, this analysis does not establish that the ratio model is superior to a model including platelet count and HDL-C simultaneously as separate predictors. Therefore, PHR should be interpreted as a convenient composite marker rather than as a mathematically superior representation of its components. Future studies should directly compare ratio-based models with separate-component models to determine whether PHR offers predictive advantages beyond its mathematical components. Third, although our findings highlight the strong clinical association of PHR, we did not compare it directly with other established inflammatory or lipid-related indices (e.g., neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio or triglyceride-to-HDL-C ratio). Future studies should examine whether PHR provides incremental predictive value beyond these markers. Fourth, the UK Biobank cohort does not reflect a nationally representative sample, potentially introducing a “healthy volunteer” bias. However, despite this limitation, the observed associations are expected to be broadly applicable, as the internal validity of exposure–outcome relationships remains robust regardless of representativeness. Finally, as PHR is easily obtainable from routine blood tests, its integration into existing CHD risk prediction models should be evaluated, along with the impact of interventions such as statins, antiplatelet therapy, and lifestyle modification on PHR trajectories and cardiovascular outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, elevated PHR was associated with a higher risk of incident CHD in this large prospective cohort. The association appeared particularly evident for AMI, supporting the hypothesis that PHR may capture thrombo-inflammatory risk related to acute coronary events. However, the incremental discrimination was modest, and the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. PHR may be useful as a simple adjunctive marker for risk stratification when combined with established clinical risk factors, but further validation and direct comparison with component-based and established risk models are warranted.
