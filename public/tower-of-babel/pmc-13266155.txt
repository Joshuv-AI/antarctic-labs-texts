Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System v2025: Key Updates in US
Authors: Jung Min Chang, Su Min Ha, Heera Yoen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS®) US lexicon is a well-established and comprehensive guide for image acquisition, lesion description, characterization, and reporting. BI-RADS® v2025 includes multiple updates reflecting recent advances and practice changes in breast US. The BI-RADS® v2025 edition includes an assessment of glandular tissue component in the tissue composition section and fine-tunes the US lexicon. The addition of a new category of non-mass lesion aids in the characterization of subtle findings that often correlate with imaging findings detected on other imaging modalities, and the discussion of lymph nodes has expanded in depth and scope. This article provides an overview of the changes introduced in BI-RADS® v2025 US.

INTRODUCTION

The U.S. Congress has enacted federal legislation mandating the reporting of breast density, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has updated its regulations to require patient notifications regarding breast density. This requirement became effective on September 10, 2024, in all 50 states. As reporting of breast density has become mandatory, supplemental screening with US is being performed more frequently, whether through handheld US conducted by technologists or physicians or via automated breast US systems (ABUS).

The Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS®) US lexicon is a well-established and comprehensive guide to image acquisition, lesion description, characterization, and reporting. BI-RADS® v2025 is the third edition of the US BI-RADS® lexicon and incorporates multiple updates reflecting recent advances and evolving clinical practices in breast US (1). The BI-RADS® v2025 edition includes assessment of glandular tissue component (GTC) in the tissue composition section and fine-tunes the US lexicon in the section for masses, calcifications, and associated features. A new category of non-mass lesion was included, and the discussion on lymph nodes (LNs) expanded in depth and scope. This review article provides an overview of the changes made to this version of the BI-RADS® US.

CHANGES IN BREAST TISSUE AND LESION DESCRIPTION

TISSUE COMPOSITION (TISSUE PATTERN/GLANDULAR TISSUE COMPONENT)

The wide normal variability in tissue composition seen on mammograms can also be observed in US images. In the 2003 BI-RADS® 4th edition, background echotexture (BE) on breast US was classified as homogeneous–fat, homogeneous–fibroglandular, or heterogeneous for whole-breast screening US (2). Heterogeneous BE may affect the sensitivity of breast US for lesion detection. The 2013 BI-RADS® 5th edition introduced the term “tissue composition” to align mammographic density and breast fibroglandular tissue (FGT) on MRI while preserving the three categories of BE (3).

In BI-RADS® v2025, GTC is a new qualifier for fibroglandular BE (Table 1) (1). Breast US distinguishes between glandular (relatively hypoechoic terminal duct lobular units) and stromal (hyperechoic interlobular fibrous tissue) components of the breast based on echogenicity, despite both appearing radiopaque on mammographic images. A qualitatively appraised percentage volume of GTC <25% of the overall FGT is considered minimal, 25% to 49% as mild, 50% to 74% as moderate, and ≥75% as marked GTC (1). Lee et al. (4) reported that GTC can be qualitatively assessed with US and classified as low (minimal or mild) or high (moderate or marked) in women with dense breasts; women with high GTC had a higher risk of developing breast cancer than women with low GTC, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.5 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.05–2.1; p = 0.03) after adjusting for other breast cancer risk factors, such as age and breast density (Fig. 1) (4). A recent study also showed that in patients with early stage breast cancer, heterogeneous BE (HR 11.24 [95% CI: 2.82–44.92]; p = 0.001), BRCA mutation (HR 15.94 [95% CI: 2.47–102.97]; p = 0.004), and pathologic index cancer size (HR per 1-cm increase 1.91 [95% CI: 1.13–3.23]; p = 0.02) were independently associated with recurrence-free survival (RFS) (5). In patients with dense breasts, heterogeneous BE (HR 14.17 [95% CI: 2.69–74.60]; p = 0.002) and high GTC (HR 10.32 [95% CI: 2.35–45.28]; p = 0.002) on ABUS, BRCA mutation (HR 24.34 [95% CI: 2.75–215.06]; p = 0.004), and pathologic cancer size (HR per 1-cm increase 2.62 [95% CI: 1.50–4.59]; p = 0.001) were independently associated with RFS (5).

MASSES

A mass is a three-dimensional (3D) space-occupying lesion that is visible in two different planes; with volumetric acquisition, it should be seen in three planes. These masses can be either benign or malignant. A few changes have been made to the mass description. In the 2013 BI-RADS® 5th edition, the term “lobular” was removed from the lexicon as a shape descriptor (3). However, because many sonographic masses that are not strictly oval, round, or irregular, the term “lobulated” has been reintroduced. The term “lobulated” was chosen instead of “lobular” to avoid potential confusion with lobular carcinoma. A lobulated mass generally has an oval shape, but shows one or more indentations, resulting in an undulating contour. In the echo pattern category, “complex cystic and solid” has been changed to “mixed solid and cystic” because the solid component of such a mass is likely to be of greater clinical significance than the cystic component, and the word “complex” may be confused with “complicated cyst.” In the posterior feature category, the combined pattern was removed to reflect the recognition that any degree of shadowing arising from the mass should be considered significant. Minor terminology revisions have also been made in this update, such as changing “not parallel” to “non-parallel” and “not circumscribed” to “non-circumscribed” (Table 2) (1).

NON-MASS LESIONS

Most breast abnormalities detected on US present as 3D masses and are characterized by features such as shape, margin, orientation, echogenicity, and posterior acoustic characteristics (1). Nevertheless, in clinical practice, certain focal findings do not satisfy the criteria for a mass, which led to the introduction of the US concept of “non-mass lesions” (67). Uematsu (8) classified these findings as “ductal hypoechoic areas or non-ductal hypoechoic areas.” Various terms have been used, including non-mass like lesion, non-mass lesion, non-mass image-forming lesion, ductal changes, duct-like structures and focal shadowing (9). Technological advancements and the growing use of US to evaluate subtle abnormalities identified on mammography and MRI have likely contributed to the development and formal inclusion of this category in the BI-RADS® lexicon (1).

Among the various sonographic features of non-mass lesions, the distribution, echo pattern, and posterior features can be used to characterize lesions. However, shapes/margins are not applicable to non-mass lesions (Table 3) (1). Non-mass lesions that demonstrate a linear or segmental pattern of distribution, accompanied by abnormal ductal alterations, calcifications, and/or posterior acoustic shadowing, should be considered suspicious findings because they are related to malignancy, including ductal carcinoma in situ and invasive ductal and lobular carcinoma (10). In a multicenter retrospective study (6), associated calcifications, posterior shadowing, segmental distribution, and mixed echogenicity showed positive predictive values (PPVs) of 44%, 22%, 22.9%, and 16.6%, respectively, for non-mass lesions on US. Having a negative mammogram was associated with a lower malignancy rate (2.8% vs. 28.8%) and lower PPVs for sonographic features (0.7%–10.4% vs. 24%–55%) than those with a positive mammogram. In contrast, non-mass lesions with small interspersed cysts without suspicious features have been described as representing benign fibrocystic changes, and the focal or regional asymmetry of otherwise normal FGT can resemble a true non-mass lesion. However, normal glandular tissue can be distinguished from the true pathology by its bilateral, symmetric distribution, tendency to blend with the surrounding tissue on multiplanar imaging, and absence of vascularity on color Doppler US (Fig. 2) (610).

CALCIFICATIONS

With advances in US technology, high-frequency, high-resolution transducers can depict both microcalcifications and macrocalcifications. Accordingly, the “calcifications” section in this version has been updated to distinguish between macrocalcifications and microcalcifications (Table 2). Macrocalcifications are sufficiently large to attenuate the US beam and are typically associated with posterior shadowing. Common macrocalcifications identified on US may be caused by degenerating fibroadenomas, oil cysts, or dystrophic calcifications. Microcalcifications appear as tiny echogenic foci on US and are most conspicuous when located within a hypoechoic mass (11).

With the introduction of the new category of “non-mass lesion,” as noted earlier, the lexicon now refers to calcifications located in a mass or non-mass lesion, and calcifications located outside a mass or non-mass lesion. Calcifications outside a mass may also reside within a non-mass lesion (1).

ASSOCIATED FEATURES

In the findings of associated features, “Echogenic rind” and “Echogenic pseudocapsule” were added (Table 4). An echogenic rind is a thick, non-uniform band that surrounds a lesion of any shape (Fig. 3). It is a feature predictive of malignancy but may also be associated with benign causes, such as fat necrosis (12). In a cohort of 137 breast lesions, the presence of a hyperechoic zone demonstrated a sensitivity of 87% and a specificity of 81% for the detection of malignancy. The hyperechoic zone inversely correlated with tumor differentiation (p = 0.002), suggesting that a larger peritumoral zone may be associated with poorer tumor differentiation (13). A retrospective reader study by Nguyen et al. (14) involving 583 biopsied suspicious masses demonstrated that an echogenic rind is a more specific than sensitive indicator of breast malignancy. Furthermore, among malignant lesions, this finding is associated with invasive cancer. Incorporating the echogenic rind into imaging assessments reduces the underestimation of tumor size compared with surgical pathology.

In contrast to the echogenic rind, the echogenic pseudocapsule is thin and uniform and usually surrounds an oval mass (Fig. 4). Oval or lobulated masses surrounded by a circumferential echogenic pseudocapsule and lacking suspicious features are highly likely to be benign; however, interval growth should be considered. Even in the presence of an echogenic pseudocapsule, a biopsy should be considered if the mass has a round or irregular shape (1).

As a minor change, a terminology revision was made for vascularity, with “Vessels in the rim” changed to “Peripheral hypervascularity” (Table 4).

SPECIAL CASES

Minor revisions to the terminology have been made in the section on special cases. The descriptions of the implants have been reorganized into sub-findings. In addition, “Post-traumatic (non-surgical) changes” and “Abscess” have been newly added (Table 4) (1). LNs have been reclassified into a new category, which is discussed in greater detail below.

LYMPH NODES

The discussion of LNs has been greatly expanded in this new version (Table 5) (1). According to the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system, ipsilateral regional nodal basins—including axillary levels I, II, and III, the internal mammary chain, and the supraclavicular region—are classified as locoregional rather than metastatic (15). The assessment of LNs on US plays a key role in determining the stage of breast cancer and guiding treatment decisions (16).

The following parameters were used to characterize LNs on US: size, shape, cortical thickening, margin, hilum, echogenic foci, symmetry, interval change, and clinical correlation (Fig. 5). Cortical thickening larger than 3 mm and a focal, eccentric, hypoechoic cortical bulge may be observed in nodal metastasis. LNs with non-circumscribed margins may be a sign of extranodal extension. A compressed or displaced hilum or complete absence of a fatty hilum are suspicious findings of nodal metastasis (116).

US enables a detailed assessment of nodal morphology and can provide real-time guidance for biopsy, allowing a definitive histopathological diagnosis of malignancy (16).

REPORT ORGANIZATION

The report should be concise and organized, including indications for the examination, comparison with previous examination(s), technique, tissue composition, findings, composite reports (if applicable), assessment, and management recommendations. These key points are described in detail below.

1) This version of BI-RADS® introduces structured categories for the major clinical indications for breast imaging, with optional subcategories and relevant clinical histories, for use in reports across all modalities (Table 6). The most common indications for breast US are the confirmation and characterization of focal signs/symptoms or abnormalities on other imaging modalities, guidance of interventional procedures, and its use as the initial imaging technique for young, pregnant, or lactating patients. Supplemental US screening can be performed in asymptomatic women with dense breasts.

2) If a lesion is evaluated with color or power Doppler or with strain or shear-wave elastography, these techniques should be reported. This should also be considered when automated whole-breast scanning is used.

3) For important findings, a sequential description of lesions is needed, and the size should be reported in three dimensions. Multiple bilateral circumscribed masses are typically considered benign (17). However, in unusual circumstances that require separate descriptions of each mass, they should be reported in a list format according to breast, location within the breast, and size. If a US finding correlates with a finding on another imaging modality (mammography or MRI) or with a clinical finding, the correlation should be reported. Similarly, if a US finding is incidental and does not correlate with findings from another imaging modality or clinical examination, its incidental nature should be reported.

4) When more than one imaging modality examination is performed concurrently (on the same day), it is preferable that the examinations be reported together. The examination of each modality is described in separate paragraphs. The assessment should be unified into an overall assessment with combined management recommendations that incorporate the conclusions from all imaging modalities. If the two assessments differed, the more abnormal assessment was assigned. However, an exception exists when the characteristically benign features of a finding on one imaging modality supersede the less specific benign features of the same finding on another examination. For example, an oval circumscribed mass on mammography is not specifically benign; however, if concurrent US characterizes the same mass as a simple cyst, it may be classified as benign (category 2).

The report should be succinct, using terminology from the latest approved lexicon without embellishments. Definitions of lexicon terms should not be included in the report (1).

SUMMARY

An accurate description of imaging findings is of paramount importance, particularly for conveying precise information about a patient’s breast characteristics and lesion features to both clinicians and patients. In alignment with this objective, the current update incorporates expanded descriptors made possible by advancements in US technology. Collectively, these revisions enhance clarity, improve diagnostic consistency, and support more precise communication, ultimately contributing to more accurate assessment and optimized patient management.
