From Detection to Decision Support: Advancing AI-Assisted Serum Quality Assessment
Authors: Sunghwan Shin, Sollip Kim
Published: 2026

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are rapidly reshaping laboratory medicine, with predictive models and operational applications growing in diversity [1, 2]. This momentum is reflected in a recent Korean Society for Laboratory Medicine survey, in which 32% and 31% of respondents identified AI and big data, respectively, as the most important emerging technologies [3].

Reliable laboratory results fundamentally depend on specimen quality. Despite continued efforts to improve specimen collection and handling, preanalytical problems, including hemolysis, clotting, and insufficient specimen volume, still account for an estimated 60–70% of laboratory errors [4]. Among these, hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia (HIL) warrant particular attention as they interfere with multiple analytical measurements, prompt specimen rejection or recollection, and delay the reporting of results. Their operational footprint exceeds their frequency: in the 21,000-specimen cohort reported in this issue, HIL abnormalities affected only 2.4%, 1.4%, and 3.6% of specimens for hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia, respectively, and approximately 0.4% exceeded rejection limits for hemolysis and for lipemia [5]. However, hemolysis alone accounts for more than 60% of specimen rejections [6]. Consequently, automated HIL assessment has become a compelling target for AI-assisted specimen quality management [7].

Automated HIL assessment has evolved along two complementary paths. The first is upstream image-based tube screening, which can replace subjective visual inspection and permit rapid pre-testing detection and early specimen rerouting [8]. Some such systems can classify segmented serum images through reference-based color-space comparison, including RGB-based approaches, but provide categorical rather than quantitative or analyte-specific information, and their performance may be influenced by image-acquisition conditions and specimen presentation [8, 9]. The second path utilizes spectrophotometric HIL indices generated using chemistry analyzers. While offering objective, quantitative and/or semiquantitative values, these generally become available only after the specimen has entered the analytical platform, limiting their use for upstream rerouting; moreover, the values and decision thresholds of different analyzer systems are not standardized [6].

Recent AI-based studies have sought to retain the upstream advantages of image screening while extracting richer information and linking it to analyzer-based assessment. Yang et al. [8] first demonstrated the potential of this approach by applying their multitask deep-learning system (Inception-ResNet-v2) to 16,427 images of centrifuged blood specimens. Inception-ResNet-v2 aided in identifying unqualified specimens with 97.2% sensitivity and 89.4% specificity, with areas under the ROC curve of 0.983 for unqualified specimens and above 0.98 for HIL individually. The system also estimated numerical HIL indices separately (Pearson correlation coefficients, 0.840–0.963) and rendered 30.8% of serum-index tests unnecessary on preliminary application [8]. Man et al. shifted the emphasis toward operational utility [10]. Using a streamlined ResNet18 binary classifier, they selected the decision threshold by balancing missed HIL specimens against the proportion of specimens for which serum-index measurement could be avoided. Across 112,121 routine specimens prospectively collected over four consecutive months, the false-negative rate ranged from 1.32% to 3.04% (overall 2.56%, corresponding to a derived sensitivity of 97.4%), 86.48% of specimens passed without serum-index measurement, and only 3 of 133 false negatives exceeded manufacturer interference limits [10]. These figures illustrate the value of intended-use-based threshold selection and real-world temporal evaluation.

In this issue of Annals of Laboratory Medicine, Chen et al. extend this progression with a two-step framework in which YOLOv5 localizes the serum region and ResNet-50 classifies HIL type and severity [5]. The model was evaluated in a Roche-based image-capture and preprocessing environment, with HIL indices measured via Cobas c 701 (Roche Diagnostics, Shanghai, China) serving as the principal reference for classification, and a physician reviewed the images to exclude image interference and support image annotation. A notable feature is the dedicated learned detector, whose localization performance was evaluated separately, which may reduce the influence of nonserum features such as tube labels, caps, cellular layers, and background. The investigators further assessed the model using a large-scale routine dataset with pronounced class imbalance, including independent test subsets exceeding 15,000 images for hemolysis and lipemia, reported binary and severity-level performance, compared the model with results from nine trained laboratory professionals, and reported a mean inference time below 100 ms per specimen. In the independent test set, sensitivities and specificities ranged from 87% to 100% and 93% to 97%, respectively, whereas the precisions only ranged from 15% to 23%, a profile consistent with preanalytical screening designed to minimize false negatives.

These developments shift the central question from whether AI can recognize HIL abnormalities to how its output should be translated into safe and efficient laboratory action. In a balanced set of 100 specimens read by nine trained professionals, the model achieved 96% and 92% sensitivity and specificity, respectively, with higher exact-match consistency than manual reading (72% vs. 42–68%), although its specificity did not exceed the range achieved by the readers [5]. Such sensitivity is suitable for screening when alerts are confirmed using photometric HIL measurement or expert review. The false-positive burden, however, cannot be inferred from a balanced comparison: in the independent test set, precision was only 15–23%, despite the specificity of 93–97%; therefore, 77–85% of positive alerts were false [5]. Such low precision is an expected property of screening for events occurring in only 1%–4% of specimens rather than a defect of the model, and the trade-off is intrinsic—Yang et al. reported that, at sensitivity thresholds of > 95%, > 99%, and 100%, 6.26%, 18.47%, and 48.14% of qualified specimens would be misclassified, respectively [8]. Whether such a burden is acceptable depends on what the alert triggers. Because any specimen that proceeds to analysis yields a quantitative HIL index, the incremental value of image-based screening lies in earlier rerouting rather than in detection itself and must be weighed against the additional visual review, redundant index testing, and turnaround delay that false alerts generate, which can paradoxically slow down the workflow that screening is meant to streamline. Experience with potassium testing demonstrates that stringent H-index policies can delay replacement results without clear clinical benefit [11]. Reporting the pass rate alongside the false-negative rate, as performed by Man et al. [10], makes this balance explicit.

Clinical value will also depend on moving from specimen-level labels to assay-specific action. As HIL effects vary with the analyte, method, and concentration [12–14], a single specimen-level “hemolysis alert” is clinically insufficient. Moreover, icterus and lipemia often reflect the underlying state of the patient, and hemolysis may occur in vivo; therefore, recollection does not invariably resolve the interference. Future systems should merge initial image-based flags with confirmatory analyzer indices and patient-level clinical data, such as known diagnoses of hemolytic anemia or cholestasis, to guide tailored actions, such as whether to report with a comment, confirm via alternative methodology, or request recollection.

Clinical translation will also depend on how reference labels are established. As analyzer-derived HIL indices may be affected by coexisting interferences, mixed-HIL specimens may require adjudication using additional laboratory measurements and expert review, rather than reliance on a single index [15]. Ultimately, successful implementation demands robust external validation across diverse institutions, tube types, cameras, and analyzer systems. The International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC) recommendations and Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD)-AI provide useful guidance on defining intended use, separating development and test data, reporting exclusions and failure modes, and evaluating generalizability [16, 17]. Practical deployment will also require local verification, version control, performance monitoring, and reliable integration with laboratory automation, middleware, and information systems [18].

In this context, Chen et al. represent a timely step toward severity-aware, high-throughput image assessment [5]. The next stage will be connecting recognition with confirmatory measurement and individualized laboratory action, allowing automated serum quality assessment to progress from detection toward decision support.
