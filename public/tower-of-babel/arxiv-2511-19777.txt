Canonical order spectra in topological dynamical systems
F. Ciavattini, A. Della Corte, C. Lucamarini

Abstract
In a compact topological dynamical system ( X , f ) (X,f) , we associate to every pair ( x , y ) (x,y) a canonical order-theoretic invariant: its emergent order spectrum Ω ⁡ ( x , y ) \Omega(x,y) . We first prove that one can always build families of nested ε n \varepsilon_{n} -chains ( ε n → 0 \varepsilon_{n}\to 0 ) whose linear orders are eventually compatible under inclusion. Ω ⁡ ( x , y ) \Omega(x,y) is then defined as the set of countable linear order-types obtained as direct limits of order-compatible nested ε n \varepsilon_{n} -chains (empty if and only if x x is not in chain relation with y y ). The order spectrum is independent of the compatible metric and of the vanishing sequence, and invariant under topological conjugacy. Moreover, it reveals tight connections with the underlying dynamics and discriminates recurrence phenomena that are indiscernible via Conley’s decomposition or Auslander’s prolongational hierarchy. KEYWORDS: Topological Dynamics; Chain Recurrence; Emergent Order Spectrum; Conjugacy Invariants. MSC2020: 37B20, 37C15, 37B35, 06A05.



1
Introduction

Many questions in dynamics, from structural stability to metastable states, concern what can reliably be said about the structure of dynamics when one allows small perturbations. One way to formalize this is to replace exact orbits by
\varepsilon
–chains (or pseudo-orbits) and study the relations they generate. The notion of an
\varepsilon
–chain thus plays a central role in dynamics. From a purely theoretical point of view, it provides the foundation for both C. Conley’s decomposition theory of dynamical systems
[
4
]
and E. Akin’s structural description of attractors (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
, Theorem 2.68, p. 82).

A point
x
is chain related to a point
y
(and one writes
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
) if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, it is possible to go from
x
to
y
by repeating finitely many times the operation of “taking the
f
-image and applying a correction smaller than
\varepsilon
”. The finite sequence of points

x_{0}=x,\,x_{1},\dots,\,x_{n}=y

such that
d(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})<\varepsilon
, for every
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, is called an
\varepsilon
-chain.

The starting point of this work is the observation that the chain relation hides much more structure than the bare existence of arbitrarily fine pseudo-orbits. Between two chain-related points
x,y
there are in general many
\varepsilon
–chains, and each of these chains comes equipped with a natural linear order (given by the indices along the chain). At first sight, the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
seems to remember only whether two points belong to the chain relation. The definition itself gives no immediate indication that the finite-
\varepsilon
chains should leave behind any structurally invariant “track”, and one might even suspect that no such track exists at all. Our main result shows that, in the compact case, there is indeed a canonical track encoded in the invariant structure of the chains.

This latent structure can be made explicit once one realizes that it is always possible to pass to families of chains that are
nested and eventually order-compatible
, and then record only the
order-types
of the resulting limit.
This produces, for each pair
(x,y)
, a collection of countable linear order-types, the
emergent order spectrum

\Omega(x,y)
(EOS).

Our main result is the following:

Theorem
.

Let
(X,f)
be a topological dynamical system, with
X
a compact metric space and
f:X\longrightarrow X
continuous. Given any pair of chain-related points
x,y
, there is a countable family
\{C_{n}\}
of
\varepsilon_{n}
–chains (with
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
) such that:

1.

For every
n
,
C_{n}\subseteq C_{n+1}
;

2.

The linear orders
\leq_{n}
of the chains
C_{n}
(given by the indices) are eventually compatible under inclusion.

Any such sequence of nested and order-compatible
\varepsilon_{n}
-chains produces a unique limit order-type supported on
\bigcup_{n}C_{n}
. These order-types are independent of the sequences
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}
and of the compatible metric, and invariant under conjugacy.

Therefore, the usual chain relation coincides, for compact systems, with the order-enriched relation defined via nested, order-compatible chains. Thus what is usually treated as a purely existential relation (“there exist arbitrarily fine pseudo-orbits from
x
to
y
”) encodes in fact a significantly finer description of the recurrence.

The main result is proven in three steps. We prove point 1. in Theorem
3.6
, through a projection on the Hausdorff limit of a Hausdorff-convergent sequence of
\varepsilon_{n}
-chains. The existence of nested chains for chain-related points is false, in general, in non-compact spaces (an example is provided). Point 2. is proven in Theorem
5.2
through a transfinite iteration of the Hausdorff projection arriving at a stabilizing closed, invariant limit supporting nested and order-compatible chains.

Finally, to prove the last statement, we extract asymptotic information from the nested, order-compatible chains
\{C_{n}\}
. This leads to the notion of EOS introduced above, which captures the types of recurrence exhibited by every pair of chain related points. The EOS is canonical, in the sense that it is:

•

independent of the vanishing sequences
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}
(Remark
6.6
);

•

independent of the metric (Theorem
6.7
);

•

invariant under topological conjugacy (Theorem
6.8
).

In the last section, we show that the EOS contains quite comprehensive
information on the invariant recurrence structure of the system.
A structural property of the EOS is that it canonically refines Conley’s
decomposition: for each pair of chain-related points
(x,y)
and each

\beta\in\Omega(x,y)
, the corresponding limit linear order on the union
of the chains decomposes into convex blocks indexed by the chain components
encountered by the chains, and the induced order on these blocks is precisely
the Conley partial order restricted to those components
(Theorems
7.2
and
7.3
). Moreover, we provide examples
in which the EOS refines Auslander’s prolongational sets, distinguishing
recurrence patterns in cases in which prolongational ranks coincide.

The EOS can thus be viewed as a complexity invariant for transitions under small perturbations. It allows for a meaningful comparison between dynamical models (for instance in metastable or noisy settings) that share the same coarse chain structure but exhibit different patterns of recurrence between their basic pieces.

Preliminaries

By
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}
we mean, respectively, the set of positive integers and of non-negative integers,
while
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{R}
indicate, respectively, the set of integers, rational and real numbers. We denote by
\mathbb{S}^{1}
the standard unit circle.

By compact system we mean a pair
(X,f)
, where
X
is a metric space, with metric
d
, and

f:X\longrightarrow X

is a continuous map. We say that the system
(X,f)
is compact if
X
is a compact space. Given a point
x\in X
, we denote by
\mathcal{O}(x)
its orbit, that is the set

\mathcal{O}(x)=\{f(x),f^{2}(x),\dots\}.

For
S\subseteq X
, we write
\partial S
for the topological boundary of
S
, and
\operatorname{int}(S)
for the topological interior of
S
, respectively, and we denote by
\overline{S}
the topological closure of
S
. We indicate the open (closed) ball of radius
\rho
centered at
x
by
B_{\rho}(x)
(
\overline{B}_{\rho}(x)
).

Let us review the well-known topological dynamical relations
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\subseteq X^{2}
, as introduced by E. Akin in
[
1
]
for the more general case of closed relations of
X
. In the case of a continuous map, they can be defined as follows (see for instance [
[
8
]
, Def. 2.2, p. 47]).

Definition 2.1
.

We introduce the following standard topological dynamical relations:

1)

Orbit relation:

x\,\mathcal{O}\,y
if and only if
\exists k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{k}(x)=y
;

2)

Recurrence relation:

x\,\mathcal{R}\,y
if and only if
\forall\,\varepsilon>0\,\exists k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{k}(x)\in B_{\varepsilon}(y)
;

3)

Non-wandering relation:

x\,\mathcal{N}\,y
if and only if
\forall\,\varepsilon>0\,\exists z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)
and
\exists k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{k}(z)\in B_{\varepsilon}(y)
;

Let us also recall the notion of
\varepsilon
-chain and chain relation.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system. Given two points
x,y\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
, an
\varepsilon
-chain from
x
to
y
is an indexed, finite sequence of points of
X
, that is a map

C:\{0,1,\dots,m\}\longrightarrow X,

with
m\geq 1
, such that, setting
x_{i}=C(i)
, we have:

i)

x_{0}=x
and
x_{m}=y
,

ii)

d(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})<\varepsilon
, for every
i=0,1,\dots,m-1
.

By
\widehat{C}
, we denote the support of the chain
C
, that is,

\widehat{C}=C(\{0,1,\dots,m\})=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{m}\}.

With an abuse of notation, we may indicate a chain
C
by writing simply

C:x_{0},\,x_{1},\dots,\,x_{m}.

We say
x,y\in X
are in
chain recurrence relation
(or simply in chain relation), and we write
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
, if and only if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-chain from
x
to
y
.

The relation
x\equiv_{C}y
defined as
x\equiv_{C}y
if and only if
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
and
y\,\mathcal{C}\,x
is an equivalence relation. Its equivalence classes are called
chain components
. The chain component containing
x
will be denoted by
[x]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and
K,K^{\prime}\subseteq X
be two chain components. We set

K\leq_{\mathrm{Conley}}K^{\prime}\iff\ \exists x\in K\,,\,y\in K^{\prime}\,:\,y\mathcal{C}x.

The relation
\leq_{\mathrm{Conley}}
is a partial order on the chain components.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a linear order
(S,\preceq)
, we say that
M\subseteq S
is
convex
with respect to
\preceq
(or simply convex if no confusion may arise) if, for every
x,y\in M
, we have
x\preceq z\preceq y\implies z\in M
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system. We say that
(X,f)
is
transitive
if and only if there exists
x\in X
such that
\overline{\mathcal{O}(x)}=X
.

Let us also recall some standard definitions of basic concepts in topological dynamics (see for instance
[
8
]
).

Definition 2.6
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system.

•

The
\omega
-limit set of
x\in X
is defined as:

\omega(x):=\bigcap_{N\geq 0}\overline{\{f^{k}:k\geq N\}}.

•

A closed set
U\subseteq X
is
called an
inward set
if
f(U)\subseteq\operatorname{int}(U)
.

•

An
attractor
is a set
A\subseteq X
for which there exists
an inward set
U
such that

A=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}f^{n}(U).

•

The
basin
of an attractor
A
is

B(A):=\{x\in X:\omega(x)\subseteq A\}.

•

The
dual repeller
of an attractor
A
is

R:=X\setminus B(A).

Definition 2.7
.

Given two closed sets
F_{1},F_{2}\subseteq X
, the Hausdorff distance
d_{\mathcal{H}}(F_{1},F_{2})
between them is given by (see for instance
[
10
]
, Section 45):

d_{\mathcal{H}}(F_{1},F_{2}):=\inf\{\varepsilon>0\,:\,F_{1}\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(F_{2})\text{ and }F_{2}\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(F_{1})\}.

Remark 2.8
.

If
d_{\mathcal{H}}(F_{1},F_{2})=\varepsilon
, then
F_{1}\subseteq\overline{B}_{\varepsilon}(F_{2})
and
F_{2}\subseteq\overline{B}_{\varepsilon}(F_{1})
.
Let
\mathfrak{F}
be the collection of nonempty closed sets of
X
. It is known that the metric space
(\mathfrak{F},d_{\mathcal{H}})
is compact if and only if
X
is compact. Thus, assuming that
X
is compact, for every sequence of closed sets
\{F_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, there is a subsequence
\{F_{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and a closed set
F_{\infty}\subseteq X
such that
d_{\mathcal{H}}(F_{n_{k}},F_{\infty})\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}0
.

Remark 2.9
.

We recall that, in a compact space, we always have:

A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\dots\subseteq A_{n}\subseteq\dots\implies A_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\overline{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}}

(1)

where convergence is meant with respect to the Hausdorff distance (see Corollary 5.32 in
[
13
]
).

Definition 2.10
.

Let
(P,\leq_{P})
and
(Q,\leq_{Q})
be posets. An
order-isomorphism
between
P
and
Q
is a bijection
f:P\to Q
such that for all
x,y\in P
,

x\leq_{P}y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(x)\leq_{Q}f(y).

We write
(P,\leq_{P})\cong(Q,\leq_{Q})
if such a map exists. This defines an equivalence relation on the class of all posets.

The
order-type
of a poset
(P,\leq_{P})
is the equivalence class

\operatorname{otp}(P,\leq_{P})\;=\;\{(Q,\leq_{Q}):(Q,\leq_{Q})\cong(P,\leq_{P})\}.

Remark 2.11
.

Of course the collection
\operatorname{otp}(P,\leq_{P})
is in general not a set
but a proper class in ZF.
One common way to avoid this size issue is to work inside a

universe of sets
(for example, the cumulative hierarchy
V

in von Neumann’s construction (see for instance
[
7
]
, p.63)), and to take order-types with respect to
the posets contained in that universe.

Let us now define two binary relations
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq},\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}\subseteq X^{2}
that are for sure not weaker than the chain relation
\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system. For
x,y\in X
, we say that
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,y
if and only if, for a sequence
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of positive real numbers converging to 0, there is a collection
\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of maps
C_{n}:\{0,1,\dots,m_{n}\}\longrightarrow X

,

\varepsilon_{n}
-chain from
x
to
y
and
\widehat{C_{n}}\subseteq\widehat{C_{n+1}}
.

We will call
\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
a
family of nested chains for the sequence
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.

Remark 2.13
.

Def.
2.12

\{\delta_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
of positive real numbers converging to 0, there exists a subsequence
\{C_{n_{m}}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
of nested
\delta_{m}
-chains. For this reason, in the following we may consider collections of nested chains for a certain pair of points in
X
without specifying the sequence of real numbers.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system.
For
x,y\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
let
C:x_{0},\,x_{1},\dots,\,x_{m}
be an
\varepsilon
-chain from
x=x_{0}
to

. We say that
C
contains a

if one of the following conditions holds:

•

for some
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}
such that

, we have
x_{i}=x_{j}
;

•

for some
i\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}
, we have
x_{i}=x_{0}
or
x_{i}=x_{m}
.

We say that
C
is

if it does not contain any cyclic sub-chain.

It is easily seen that any
\varepsilon

with a cyclic sub-chain can be modified (by suitably eliminating some points) to get an
\varepsilon
-chain
C^{\prime}
with no cyclic sub-chain and such that
\widehat{C^{\prime}}\subseteq\widehat{C}
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system.
Given two points
x,y\in X
, such that
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}\,y
. We say that
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}\,y
if the sequence of nested chains
\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
can be chosen so as to make all its chains without cyclic sub-chains and, for every
z,w\in(\bigcup_{n}\widehat{C_{n}})\setminus\{x,\,y\}
, exactly one of the following conditions holds:

i)

there exists a natural number
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
z
appears before
w
in the chain
C_{n}
, for every
n>N
, or,

ii)

there exists a natural number
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w
appears before
z
in the chain
C_{n}
, for every
n>N
.

We say in this case that
\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of
ordinately nested chains
.

Remark 2.16
.

Also here, if
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}\,y
, then the collection of ordinately nested
\varepsilon
-chains
\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
works for every positive sequence
\varepsilon_{n}
converging to zero. Thus, the fact that
x
is in
\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}
-relation with

, is independent of the specific sequence
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. For this reason, in the following we may consider collections of ordinately nested chains for a certain pair of points in
X
without specifying the sequence of real numbers.

Definition 2.17
.

Let
(X,f)
be a dynamical system.
Assuming that, for
x,y\in X
, we have
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
, we say that the sequence
\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
\varepsilon_{n}
-chains from
x
to
y
is a
complete sequence of chains
if
\varepsilon_{n}
converges to 0 monotonically.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system, and let
d
be the metric on
X
.
Consider
x,y\in X
such that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
, and let

\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a complete sequence of chains from
x
to
y
.
Then, for every other metric
d^{\prime}
on
X
that is equivalent to
d
,
the sequence
\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is again a complete sequence of chains
from
x
to
y
.

Proof.

Since
X
is compact and
d,d^{\prime}
are equivalent,
the identity map
\operatorname{id}:(X,d)\to(X,d^{\prime})
is uniformly continuous,
and similarly for
\operatorname{id}:(X,d^{\prime})\to(X,d)
. Hence there exist
monotone functions (moduli)
\alpha,\beta:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
with

\alpha(\varepsilon)\downarrow 0
and
\beta(\varepsilon)\downarrow 0
as

\varepsilon\downarrow 0
such that

d(u,v)<\varepsilon\ \Rightarrow\ d^{\prime}(u,v)<\alpha(\varepsilon),\qquad d^{\prime}(u,v)<\varepsilon\ \Rightarrow\ d(u,v)<\beta(\varepsilon).

Since
\{C_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a complete sequence of chains from
x
to
y

for the metric
d
, there exists a sequence of positive reals

\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converging monotonically to zero such that
each

C_{n}:\ x_{0}^{n}=x,\ x_{1}^{n},\dots,x_{m_{n}-1}^{n},\ x_{m_{n}}^{n}=y

is an
\varepsilon_{n}
-chain for
d
, i.e.

d\bigl(f(x_{i}^{n}),x_{i+1}^{n}\bigr)<\varepsilon_{n}\quad\text{for }i=0,1,\dots,m_{n}-1.

Define
\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}:=\alpha(\varepsilon_{n})
. Since
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0

and
\alpha
is monotone with
\alpha(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
,
the sequence
\{\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}\}
also converges monotonically to
0
.
Moreover, by the defining property of
\alpha
,

d\bigl(f(x_{i}^{n}),x_{i+1}^{n}\bigr)<\varepsilon_{n}\ \Rightarrow\ d^{\prime}\bigl(f(x_{i}^{n}),x_{i+1}^{n}\bigr)<\alpha(\varepsilon_{n})=\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}

for all

and
n
. Thus each
C_{n}
is an
\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}
-chain for
d^{\prime}
,
so
\{C_{n}\}
is a complete sequence of chains from
x
to
y
with respect to
d^{\prime}
.
∎

In the following, we will prove that in fact
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}
coincide with
\mathcal{C}
in compact systems. Recalling Remarks
2.13
and
2.16
and Lemma

, this identifies
\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}
as canonical (i.e., independent of the metric and of the vanishing sequence) refinements of the relation

, yielding a finer description of the recurrence properties.

It may seem, at first sight, that the challenging part in proving the equalities
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}=\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}
is to build nested chains, while the compatibility of their linear orderings, boiling down to avoid cycles in each chain, is a technical nuisance one can take care of easily. As we will see when we will address the problem in Section

, things are different.

3
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\subseteq}

In this section, we prove that, in the compact case, we can always build nested chains for two points in chain relation (Theorem
3.6
). We will refine later the construction to achieve nested chains with compatible linear orderings. in the meantime, we will develop some results concerning what can be said in some particularly simple cases.

Let
\{C_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a complete sequence of chains. Starting from
\{C_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, we want to obtain a complete sequence of nested chains
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
whose supports are contained in the Hausdorff limit of a converging subsequence of
\{\widehat{C_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
.

Since
\widehat{C_{k}}
is a finite set for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\{\widehat{C_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of closed sets. Up to considering subsequences, we can assume that

\widehat{C_{k}}\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}C_{\infty},

(2)

where
C_{\infty}
is a closed set and the convergence is in the Hausdorff metric.

Setting
\eta=4\,\cdot\,\mbox{diam}(X)
and
\varepsilon_{n}=\frac{\eta}{2^{n}}
, we will prove that we can find a sequence of nested chains
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, where, for every
n
,
S_{n}
is an
\varepsilon_{n}
-chain from
x
to
y
such that
\widehat{S_{n}}\subseteq\widehat{S_{n+1}}\subseteq C_{\infty}
.
We proceed by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
x,y\in\widehat{C_{n}}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
x,y\in C_{\infty}
and, therefore,
S_{1}:x_{0}=x,\,x_{1}=y
is an
\varepsilon_{1}
-chain.

Assume now that we have an
\varepsilon_{n}
-chain
S_{n}:x_{0}=x,\dots,x_{m}=y
between
x
and
y
such that
\widehat{S_{n}}\subseteq C_{\infty}
. We want to build an
\varepsilon_{n+1}
-chain
S_{n+1}
from
x
to
y
such that
\widehat{S_{n}}\subseteq\widehat{S_{n+1}}\subseteq C_{\infty}
.

Since
f
is uniformly continuous, we can pick a real number
\delta_{n}
such that:

•

0<\delta_{n}<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{6},\min\left\{\frac{d(x_{i},x_{j})}{2}\,\Bigg|\,i,j=0,\dots,m,\text{ and }x_{i}\neq x_{j}\right\}\right\};

(3)

•

for every
u,v\in X
, if
d(u,v)<\delta_{n}
, then
d(f(u),f(v))<\varepsilon_{n}/6
.

Since
\{C_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a complete sequence of chains and by (
2

k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the chain

C_{k_{n}}:\,x_{0}^{(k_{n})},\,x^{(k_{n})}_{1},\dots,\,x_{m_{k_{n}}}^{(k_{n})}

\varepsilon_{n}/6-

d_{\mathcal{H}}(\widehat{C_{k_{n}}},C_{\infty})<\delta_{n}/2

Thus, since

C_{\infty}\subseteq\overline{B}_{\delta_{n}/2}(\widehat{C_{k_{n}}}),

every point
x_{0},\dots,\,x_{m}\in\widehat{S_{n}}
has a “close enough” point in
\widehat{C_{k_{n}}}
. More precisely,
for every
i=0,\dots,m
, since
\widehat{C_{k_{n}}}
is finite, there is a point
x_{j_{i}}^{(k_{n})}\in\widehat{C_{k_{n}}}
such that

d(x_{j_{i}}^{(k_{n})},x_{i})=\min_{z\in\widehat{C_{k_{n}}}}d(z,x_{i}),

(4)

5
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}

In this section we prove that
\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}
always coincides with
\mathcal{C}
.

To achieve our goal, we refine the construction of nested chains by means of a
transfinite pruning procedure, by which we build a family of closed sets

\{C^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda<\omega_{1}}
and associated nested chains

\{S_{n}^{\lambda}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Heuristically,
C^{\lambda}
is the region
where cyclic behaviour still survives after
\lambda
rounds of pruning.
At each successor stage we remove cycles inside
C^{\lambda}
and project again
onto a Hausdorff limit
C^{\lambda+1}\subseteq C^{\lambda}
, while at limit
ordinals we intersect. This process stabilizes at some countable ordinal

\lambda
such that
C^{\lambda}=C^{\lambda+1}
. At that stage the Hausdorff projection
can no longer identify distinct chain points, so acyclicity is preserved and we
obtain nested, order-compatible chains that witness
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}\,y
.

The following lemma, ensuring stabilization of closed, nonempty, nested sets
at some countable level, is folklore in the setting of second-countable
spaces. We include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 5.1
.

\lambda<\omega_{1}

Proof.

Take

to be a countable base for the topology. Towards a contradiction, let us assume

. Thus, for every
\beta<\omega_{1}

Theorem 5.2
.

Let

be a compact topological dynamical system. Then

Since

is a compact metric space, it is separable and second countable, too. Then, by Lemma

, we know there is a countable ordinal
\lambda

. Therefore, the sequence of chains

(8)

so that the Hausdorff projection at ordinal level

Let the chain
{S^{\lambda}_{k}}^{\prime}
consist of the points

x_{0}^{(k)},\,x_{1}^{(k)},\dots,\,x^{(k)}_{m_{k}}.

We now show that we can take a Hausdorff projection
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}=\mathcal{H}(\{{S^{\lambda}_{n}}^{\prime}\}_{n=1}^{\infty})

\varepsilon_{n}
-chain from

to

. We prove that we can define an

and such that it does not contain any cyclic sub-chain.

Since

is uniformly continuous, we can pick a real number

such that:

•

0<\delta_{n}<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{6},\min\left\{\frac{d(x_{i},x_{j})}{2}\,\Bigg|\,i,j=0,\dots,m,\text{ and }x_{i}\neq x_{j}\right\}\right\};

(9)

•

if

By (

Thus, since

, for every

As done when defining the Hausdorff projection, we now want to find points in

•

•

for every

is acyclic by inductive hypothesis;

•

for every

is an

By construction, we have

\displaystyle d(f(x_{i}^{\prime}),x_{i+1}^{\prime})

6
The Emergent Order Spectrum

Definition 6.1
.

We indicate by:

i)

\omega
the first infinite ordinal;

ii)

\omega^{*}
the reverse order-type of
\omega
, that is the order type of non-positive integers under the usual
\leq
relation;

iii)

\zeta=\omega^{*}+\omega
the order type of the integer numbers
\mathbb{Z}
;

iv)

\eta
the countable dense order-type without extrema;

v)

\text{Lin}_{\aleph_{0}}
the set of all countable linear order-types.

We will use standard ordinal addition and multiplication throughout; e.g.
\zeta\cdot\omega
denotes the (countable) sum of
\omega
many copies of
\zeta
.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
(X,f)
be a topological dynamical system. Consider two points
x,y\in X
such that
x\,\mathcal{C}_{\preceq}\,y
. We let
\Omega_{f}(x,y)
be the set of all the order-types
\beta
such that there is a complete sequence of ordinately nested chains
\{S_{n}\}_{n}
from
x
to
y
such that, setting
S:=\cup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
, we have that
(S\setminus\{x,y\},\leq_{\infty})
has order type
\beta
. We will omit the subscript and write simply
\Omega(x,y)
if the map is clear from the context.

We will call
\Omega(x,y)

the Emergent Order Spectrum
(EOS) of
(x,y)
.
The map:

\Omega_{f}:X^{2}\ni(x,y)\mapsto\Omega_{f}(x,y)\in\mathcal{P}(\text{Lin}_{\aleph_{0}})

(12)

will be called the
EOS map
of the dynamical system
(X,f)
.

Given a countable order-type
\xi
and
x\in X
, we set

[\xi](x):=\{y\in X\,|\,\xi\in\Omega(x,y)\}.

Remark 6.3
.

Notice that
\Omega(x,y)=\varnothing
here means
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{C}
, whereas
\Omega(x,y)\ni\varnothing
indicates the fact that
f(x)=y
so that we can define a complete sequence of ordinately nested chains
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
setting, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

S_{n}:\,x,\,y.

In fact, in this case, we have
S\setminus\{x,y\}=\varnothing
.

The following are elementary properties of the EOS. The first one is built in the definition, the last one is an immediate consequence of 5. and 6. The other ones are proven below.

1.

(Chain cut)
\Omega(x,y)=\varnothing
iff
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{C}
.

2.

(Metric independence)
\Omega
is independent of the choice of compatible metric on
X
.

3.

(Conjugacy invariance) If
h
topologically conjugates
f
to
g
, then

\Omega_{f}(x,y)=\Omega_{g}(h(x),h(y))
.

4.

(Orbit detection) A finite ordinal
k
lies in
\Omega(x,y)

iff
f^{k+1}(x)=y
.

5.

(Periodicity detection) There are two finite ordinals
k
and
k^{\prime}
such that
k\in\Omega(x,y)
and
k^{\prime}\in\Omega(y,x)
iff
x
is a periodic point (if
x=y
is a fixed point, then
k
and
k^{\prime}
are the empty order).

6.

(Recurrence detection) The first infinite ordinal
\omega
lies in
\Omega(x,y)
iff
(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

7.

(Limit set detection)

y\in\omega(x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Big(\,\omega\in\Omega(x,y)\,\Big)\ \ \text{or}\ \ \Big(\exists\,m,n\in\mathbb{N}:\ m\in\Omega(x,y)\ \text{and}\ n\in\Omega(y,x)\Big).

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact metrizable dynamical system, and let
x,y\in X
be such that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
. Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a complete sequence of ordinately nested
\varepsilon_{n}
-chains from
x
to
y
such that, setting

, the ordered limit set
(S\setminus\{x,y\},\leq_{\infty})
has order type
\beta
. Then, for every sub-family
\{S_{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
that is a sequence of ordinately nested
\delta_{k}
-chains from
x
to
y
, and setting

, we have that

and the ordered limit set

We now prove

. Trivially, we have
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
. Take now

. Thus, since

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system. Then, for every
x,y\in X
, the ordering
\beta
is in the set
\Omega(x,y)
if, for every sequence of positive real numbers
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
that goes to zero monotonically, there exists a complete sequence of
\varepsilon_{n}
-chains
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that, setting
S=\bigcup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
, the ordered limit set
(S\setminus\{x,y\},\leq_{\infty})
has order type
\beta
.

Proof.

Take
x,y\in X
. If
\Omega(x,y)=\varnothing
there is nothing to prove.
Let thus
x,y\in X
be such that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
and let
\beta\in\Omega(x,y)
. Thus, there exist a sequence of positive real numbers
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n}
that tends to zero monotonically and a complete sequence of ordinately nested chains
\{S_{n}\}_{n}
from
x
to
y
for the sequence
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n}
such that the ordered limit set
(S\setminus\{x,y\},\leq_{\infty})
has order type
\beta
, where
S=\bigcup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
.

Take now another sequence of positive real numbers
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
that tends to zero monotonically. By Remark
2.16
, we know that we can extract a sub-sequence
\{S_{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
\{S_{n_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a complete sequence of ordinately nested
\varepsilon_{k}
-chains from
x
to
y
for the sequence
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. By Lemma
6.4
we also have that the order type of the ordered limit set

\left(\left(\bigcup_{k}\widehat{S_{n_{k}}}\right)\setminus\{x,y\},\leq_{\infty}\right)

\beta
, so we are done.
∎

Remark 6.6
.

By theorem
6.5
, given two points

in a compact dynamical system

, the set
\Omega(x,y)
is independent of the sequences

to obtain a limit set

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact metrizable dynamical system, and let

\Omega_{d}(x,y)\;=\;\Omega_{d^{\prime}}(x,y).

Proof.

Pick

\varepsilon_{n}
-chains

S_{n}:\ x=x^{(n)}_{0},\,x^{(n)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(n)}_{m_{n}}=y,\qquad d\big(f(x^{(n)}_{i}),x^{(n)}_{i+1}\big)<\varepsilon_{n},\quad\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0.

By Lemma

, we know that there exists a sequence of positive reals
\{\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that the
same

are
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{n}
-chains for

.

Because we have not changed any points or their indices, nestedness and the “appears-before” relation are preserved, hence the induced limit order on

is the same. Thus

. The reverse inclusion follows by symmetry using
\beta
. Therefore
\Omega_{d}(x,y)=\Omega_{d^{\prime}}(x,y)

∎

Let us now explore the interplay between the EOS of a pair of points and the kind of recurrence that may occur between them. As we will see, in certain cases the knowledge of
\Omega(x,y)
alone can tell something also about the map
f
on the whole.

Let us start by a straightforward invariance result.

Theorem 6.8
.

The EOS map
\Omega
is invariant under topological conjugacy.

Precisely, if
(X,f)
and
(Y,g)
are topologically conjugate through the homeomorphism
h:X\to Y
, then, setting

H=h\times h:X^{2}\ni(x,y)\mapsto(h(x),h(y))\in Y^{2},

we have

\Omega_{f}=\Omega_{g}\circ\ H\text{ \emph{and} }\ \Omega_{g}=\Omega_{f}\circ H^{-1}.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in X
be such that
x\,C\,y
and let a countable order-type
\beta
such that
\beta\in\Omega_{f}(x\,,y)
. We would like to prove that
\beta\in\Omega_{g}(h(x),h(y))
. In fact, consider
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
a sequence of positive real numbers converging to zero and, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, since
h
is a uniformly continuous function, there exists
\varepsilon_{n}
such that, for every
z_{1}\,,z_{2}\in X
,

d_{X}(z_{1}\,,\,z_{2})<\varepsilon_{n}\Rightarrow\,d_{Y}(h(z_{1})\,,\,h(z_{2}))<\delta_{n}.

Since it is always possible to take
\varepsilon_{n}<\delta_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, also
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of positive real numbers converging to zero. Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n}
be a complete sequence of ordinately nested
\varepsilon_{n}
-chains from
x
to
y
such that, setting
S:=\cup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
, the ordered limit set
(S\setminus\{x,y\},\leq_{\infty})
has order type
\beta
. If
S_{n}:x_{0},\,x_{1},\dots,\,x_{m}
, setting
D_{n}=h\circ S_{n}
, we have trivially
\widehat{D_{n}}\subseteq\widehat{D_{n+1}}
. In addition,
\{D_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of ordinately nested
\delta_{n}
-chains from
x
to
y
. In fact, we have
D_{n}:\,h({x_{0}}),h({x_{1}}),\dots,h({x_{m}})
and, for every
i=0,\dots,m-1
,

d_{Y}(g(h({x_{i}})),h(x_{i+1}))=d_{Y}(h(f({x_{i}})),h(x_{i+1}))<\delta_{n}

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
d_{X}(f({x_{i}}),x_{i+1})<\varepsilon_{n}.
Then, the limit ordered set
(D\setminus\{h(x),h(y)\},\leq_{\infty})
, where
D=\bigcup_{n}\widehat{D_{n}}
, has order type
\beta
. Finally, performing the same construction and using the fact that

\Omega_{g}=\Omega_{f}\circ H^{-1}

∎

Remark 6.9
.

In general
\Omega
is not functorial under factor maps.
A factor map
h:(X,f)\twoheadrightarrow(Y,g)
may glue far–apart points and thereby create
“shortcuts” that did not exist upstairs. Since
\Omega(x,y)
records the fine interleaving
of pseudo–orbits via ordinately nested
\varepsilon
–chains and their induced linear orders,
such coarse–graining can collapse order–compatible chains, introduce cycles, and even create
chainability absent in
(X,f)
. Thus
\Omega
is invariant under conjugacy but it is simply too fine to be functorial with respect to arbitrary factor maps: non-injective identifications can create shortcuts or cycles, so no canonical push–forward
\Omega_{X}(x,y)\to\Omega_{Y}(hx,hy)
generally exists.

\mathbb{Q}
. First let us recall that a classical representation theorem by Hausdorff provides a general expression of linearly ordered sets in terms of scattered sets (see
[
11
]
, Theorem 4.9):

Theorem 6.16

(Hausdorff)
.

Any linear ordering
L
is a dense sum of scattered linear orderings; that is, there is a dense linear ordering
L_{*}
and a map
h
from
L_{*}
to scattered linear orderings such that

L=\sum\{h(i)\mid i\in L_{*}\}.

We record here, without proof, a result from
[
3
]
, which is obtained leveraging on the above classical theorem and describes the EOS for transitive homeomorphisms in more detail. Nothing in the present paper depends on this fact.

Fact
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system with
\text{card}(X)=\mathfrak{c}
. If
f
is a transitive homeomorphism, then
\Omega(X^{2})
contains every countable scattered ordering and the countable dense ordering.

More precisely:

1.

There exists a co-meagre set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq X^{2}
such that, for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}
, the family of orderings
\Omega(x,y)
contains every scattered countable infinite ordering;

2.

\Omega(x,y)
contains the finite ordinal
K
if and only if
x\,\mathcal{O}\,y

3.

\Omega(x,y)
contains the dense countable ordering for every
x,y\in X
.

The proof is quite long and proceeds by distinguishing, within a transfinite induction, a certain number (8) of distinct cases that may occur when gluing together orders from the previous inductive steps. We refer the reader to
[
3
]
for the proof.

Now we want to analyze the order spectrum in terms of dual attractor/repeller pairs. For this, we need some preliminary results describing the behavior of nested chains on the gradient-like part of the system. We will use indeed the following result, which is stated (for flows) in
[
6
]
(p. 2). To get the result for discrete iteration of maps it is enough to observe that
xCy
implies
f(x)Cy
unless
f(x)=y
.

Theorem 6.17
.

Suppose that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
, Then exactly one of the
following alternatives holds:

(1)

y
lies on the forward orbit of
x
, i.e.
y=f^{k}(x)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
;

(2)

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

f^{k}(x)\,\mathcal{C}\,y
.

We also recall two well-known facts concerning attractors (see for instance
[
8
]
, p. 80-83.)

Lemma 6.18
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and
x\in X
.
If
V\subseteq X
is an open set such that
\omega(x)\subseteq V
, then
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(x)\in V
for every
n\geq N
.

Lemma 6.19
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and let
A\subseteq X
be an
attractor with inward set
U
, i.e.
U
is closed,

f(U)\subseteq\operatorname{int}(U)
, and

A=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}f^{n}(U)
. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
every
\varepsilon_{0}
-chain
C:x_{0},\dots,x_{m}
with
x_{0}\in U
satisfies

x_{i}\in U
for all
i=0,\dots,m
.

We now prove that the chain relation, in the basin of an attractor, coincides in fact with the orbit relation (this result is folklore, but we prefer to give a full proof).

Theorem 6.20
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and let
A
be an attractor with
inward set
U
and basin
B(A)
. Suppose
x,y\in B(A)\setminus A
and

x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
. Then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

y=f^{k}(x)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.17
, since
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
, either

(1)

y=f^{k}(x)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, or

(2)

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
f^{k}(x)\,\mathcal{C}\,y
.

If (1) holds we are done. Thus, for a contradiction, assume that (2) holds
and
y
is not on the forward orbit of
x
.

Since
A
is an attractor and

y\notin A
, we can choose a closed inward set
U
such that
A\subseteq\operatorname{int}(U)
,
A=\cap_{k\to\infty}f^{k}(U)
and
y\notin U
.
As
x\in B(A)
, we have
\omega(x)\subseteq A\subseteq\operatorname{int}(U)
. By
Lemma
6.18
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

f^{n}(x)\in\operatorname{int}(U)\subseteq U\qquad\text{for every }n\geq N.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be given by Lemma
6.19
for the inward
set
U
. Then any
\varepsilon_{0}
-chain starting in
U
stays in
U
. In
particular, for each
n\geq N
there can be no
\varepsilon_{0}
-chain from

f^{n}(x)
to
y
, because
f^{n}(x)\in U
while
y\notin U
.

This contradicts alternative (2) of Theorem
6.17
, which
asserts that
f^{n}(x)\,\mathcal{C}\,y
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e. for
every
\varepsilon>0
(hence for
\varepsilon_{0}
in particular) there should
exist an
\varepsilon
-chain from
f^{n}(x)
to
y
. Therefore alternative (2)
cannot occur, and we must be in case (1). Hence there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}

with
y=f^{k}(x)
.
∎

Theorem 6.21
.

Let
x,y\in X
be such that
x\,\mathcal{R}\,y
and
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{O}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{O}(y)
is a stable, attractive periodic orbit with period
K
. Then,
\Omega(x,y)=\{\omega,\,\omega+1,\dots,\,\omega+K-1\}
.

Proof.

First of all, let us prove
\{\omega,\,\omega+1,\dots,\,\omega+K-1\}\subseteq\Omega(x,y)
. Let
j\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
and take
z\in\mathcal{O}(y)
to be such that
f^{j}(z)=y
.
Since
\mathcal{O}(y)
is an attractor and
x\,\mathcal{R}\,y
, there exists a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers
\{k_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
f^{k_{n}+1}(x)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}z
and
f^{k_{n}}(x)\neq z
. Then, in order to define a sequence of ordinately nested chains
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, it is enough to take
S_{n}
as

S_{n}:\,x,\,f(x),\dots,\,f^{k_{n}}(x),\,z,\,f(z),\dots,\,f^{j-1}(z)=y.

Now, we will show that
\omega+j
, for
j\in\{0,\dots,\,K-1\}
, are the only possible order types in
\Omega(x,y)
.
Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a complete sequence of ordinately nested chains from
x
to
y
for a sequence
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and set
S=\bigcup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
. We want to show that the order type of the ordered limit set
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
, where
S^{\prime}=S\setminus\{x,y\}
, is
\omega+j
for some
j\in\{0,\dots,\,K-1\}
. The set
S^{\prime}
decomposes in

S^{\prime}=S_{1}\sqcup S_{2},

where
S_{1}=S^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{O}(y)
and
S_{2}=S^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{O}(y)
.
By Lemma
5.3
, we have that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,z
, for every
z\in S_{1}
, and, by Theorem
6.20
, we have
x\,\mathcal{O}\,z
. Thus
S_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{O}(x)
. On the other hand, it is not difficult to see that
\mathcal{O}(x)\subseteq S_{1}
.

Indeed, suppose there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{k}(x)\in S_{1}
. We now show that
f^{k+1}(x)\in S_{1}
, too. Since
\omega(x)=\mathcal{O}(y)
, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that

B_{\varepsilon}(f^{k+1}(x))\cap(\mathcal{O}(x)\cup\mathcal{O}(y))=\{f^{k+1}(x)\}.

Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{k}(x)\in\widehat{S_{n}}
and
\varepsilon_{n}<\varepsilon
, the point
f^{k}(x)
must be followed by
f^{k+1}(x)
in the chain
S_{n}
and, hence,
f^{k+1}(x)\in S_{1}
.
Therefore, the order type of
(S_{1},{\leq_{\infty}}_{|})
is
\omega
.

To have the statement, it is enough to note that
\operatorname{card}(S_{2})<K
. In fact, otherwise the chains
S_{n}
would contain cyclic sub-chains.
∎

We now use Theorem
6.20
to prove that, in the basin of an attractor, but outside the attractor itself, ordinately nested chains can
only
be supported on full orbits exiting from one chain component and entering into the other one. Let us make precise the statement giving some needed definitions.

Definition 6.22
.

We call a
full orbit
of
f
a bi-infinite sequence

(z_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
of points in
X
such that

f(z_{n})=z_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. We call a
negative semi full orbit
of
f
a one-sided infinite sequence
(z_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{-1}
of points in
X
such that
f(z_{n})=z_{n+1}
for all
n<-1
.

Lemma 6.23
.

Let
A\subseteq X
be an attractor and let
R:=X\setminus B(A)
be its dual repeller. Let
x\in R
,
y\in A
be such that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
and let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a complete sequence of ordinately nested chains from
x
to
y
for a sequence of positive real numbers
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Then, setting

S=\bigcup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad S^{\prime}=S\setminus(A\cup R),

one of the following holds:

1.

there exists a full orbit
\{z_{n}\}_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
such that
S^{\prime}=\{z_{n}\}_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
;

2.

there exists a negative semi full orbit
\{z_{n}\}_{n=-\infty}^{-1}
such that
S^{\prime}=\{z_{n}\}_{n=-\infty}^{-1}.

Proof.

Since
R
is invariant, we have
f(x)\in R
, so
f(x)\neq y
and thus
S^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
. Then, pick some point
z_{0}\in S^{\prime}
. If
S^{\prime}=\{z_{0}\}
, it means that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a point
w\in R
such that
d(f(w),z_{0})<\varepsilon
which is in contradiction with the fact that
R
is a repeller.

Let
w\in S^{\prime}
be arbitrary,
w\neq z_{0}
. Since
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is
ordinately nested, there exists
N
such that for all
n\geq N
both

z_{0}
and
w
appear in
S_{n}
and always in the same relative order.
Thus, exactly one of the following holds:

(a)

z_{0}
appears before
w
eventually;

(b)

w
appears before
z_{0}
eventually.

In case (a), Lemma
5.3
gives

z_{0}\,\mathcal{C}\,w
. Since
z_{0},w\in B(A)\setminus A
, by Theorem
6.20
, we have

w=f^{k}(z_{0})

for some
k>0
.

In case (b), Lemma
5.3
gives
w\,\mathcal{C}\,z_{0}
.
Again, arguing as above and using Theorem
6.20
, we have

z_{0}=f^{k}(w)

for some
k>0
.

Summarizing, for every
w\in S^{\prime}
there exists an integer

k(w)>0
such that either

w=f^{k(w)}(z_{0})\quad\text{or}\quad z_{0}=f^{k(w)}(w).

Hence, for every
z,w\in S^{\prime}
, we have
z\,\mathcal{O}\,w
if and only if
z\leq_{\infty}w
.

We now prove that every
z\in S^{\prime}
is the exact
f
-image of some point in
S^{\prime}
, i.e. there exists
w\in S^{\prime}
with
f(w)=z
. In fact, if there was a point
z\in S^{\prime}
that is not the exact
f
-image of any point
w\in S^{\prime}
, we would have that
z
is the minimal element in
(S^{\prime},{\leq_{\infty}}_{|S^{\prime}})
, which implies that
S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{O}(z)\cup\{z\}
and in general
S\subseteq R\cup\mathcal{O}(z)\cup\{z\}\cup A
.

Since
z\in B(A)\setminus A
,
R
is closed and since
\omega(z)\subseteq A
, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that

B_{\varepsilon}(z)\cap(R\cup\mathcal{O}(z)\cup A)=\varnothing.

Now, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\varepsilon_{n}<\varepsilon
, we have
z\notin\widehat{S_{n}}
, because, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\widehat{S_{n}}\setminus\{z\}\subseteq R\cup\mathcal{O}(z)\cup A
and
f(R\cup\mathcal{O}(z)\cup A)\subseteq R\cup\mathcal{O}(z)\cup A
. Thus, we found a contradiction and, therefore, every element
w
in
S^{\prime}
has a predecessor with respect to
\leq_{\infty}
in
f^{-1}(w)\cap S^{\prime}
.

We first show that this predecessor is unique. Suppose there exist
p,q\in S^{\prime}
with
p\neq q
and

f(p)=f(q)=z.

Then

\mathcal{O}(p)=\{f(p)=z,f(z),f^{2}(z),\dots\}\qquad,\qquad\mathcal{O}(q)=\{f(q)=z,f(z),f^{2}(z),\dots\}.

In particular,

q\notin\mathcal{O}(p)\qquad,\qquad p\notin\mathcal{O}(q),

so
p
and
q
are not orbit-related, because otherwise
z
would be a periodic point in
B(A)\setminus A
. Thus, we found a contradiction, because we proved that, for any two points
z,w\in S^{\prime}
, we have
z\,\mathcal{O}\,w
or
w\,\mathcal{O}\,z
. Hence each
z\in S^{\prime}
has exactly one predecessor in
S^{\prime}
. It follows that
f|_{S^{\prime}}
is injective on
S^{\prime}
.

Therefore, we have

z\leq_{\infty}w\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z=w\text{ or }w\in\mathcal{O}(z).

For any
z,w\in S^{\prime}
we have
z\leq w
or
w\leq z
, so the relation is total. It is obviously also transitive and, since there are no periodic points in
S^{\prime}\subseteq B(A)\setminus A
, it is antisymmetric, too. Thus
\leq
is a linear order on
S^{\prime}
. Denote

z<_{\infty}w\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z\leq_{\infty}w,\;z\neq w.

Let
w,z\in S^{\prime}
with
f(w)=z
. We claim that there is no
t\in S^{\prime}
with

w<_{\infty}t<_{\infty}z.

Assume by contradiction that such
t
exists. By linearity of the order,
w<_{\infty}t
implies
t\in\mathcal{O}(w)
, and
t<_{\infty}z
implies
z\in\mathcal{O}(t)
. Thus there exist
m,k>0
such that

t=f^{m}(w),\qquad z=f^{k}(t).

On the other hand we also know
z=f(w)
, hence

f(w)=f^{k}(t)=f^{k}(f^{m}(w))=f^{m+k}(w).

Since
f_{|S^{\prime}}
is injective, we deduce
f^{m+k-1}(w)=w
, and so
w
is a periodic point living in
B(A)\setminus A
, which is absurd.

Thus, no such
t
exists, and
z
is the immediate successor of
w
with respect to the order
\leq_{\infty}
. In particular, for each
z\in S^{\prime}
, there is a unique
w\in S^{\prime}
such that
f(w)=z
, and this
w
is the immediate predecessor of
z
.

We have shown that
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
is a linearly ordered set such that:

•

every element has a unique immediate predecessor (coming from the unique
w
such that
f(w)=z
),

•

there is no minimal element.

There are two cases.

Case 1: there is no maximal element in
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
.
Then, the ordered set
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
is such that every element
z\in S^{\prime}
has a unique immediate successor
w\in S^{\prime}
; moreover, we have
w=f(z)
.
Then, the ordered set
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
is order-isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
. Hence, there exists a bijection

\varphi:\mathbb{Z}\to S^{\prime},\qquad k\mapsto z_{k},

such that

k<\ell\;\Longleftrightarrow\;z_{k}<_{\infty}z_{\ell},

and the immediate successor of
z_{k}
in
S^{\prime}
is
z_{k+1}
. By construction of the order, this successor relation coincides with the dynamics, so

f(z_{k})=z_{k+1}\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z},

and

S^{\prime}=\{z_{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Case 2: there is a maximal element.
Then, there exists a bijection
\psi
between the negative integers
\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
S^{\prime}

\psi:k\mapsto z_{k}

such that

k<\ell\iff z_{k}<_{\infty}z_{\ell}.

Also in this case, by the construction of the order, we have

f(z_{k})=z_{k+1}\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0},

and so

S^{\prime}=\{z_{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0}\}.

∎

Theorem 6.24
.

Let
A
be an attractor and
R
its dual repeller. Let
x\in R
and
y\in A
be such that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
. Then every
\tau\in\Omega(x,y)
admits the decomposition:

\tau=\beta+\eta+\beta^{\prime}

where:

•

\beta,\beta^{\prime}
correspond to the order type of nested chains supported on
R
and
A
respectively;

•

\eta
is equal to
\omega^{*}
or
\zeta
and corresponds to the order-type of nested chains supported on
B(A)\setminus A
.

Proof.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a complete sequence of ordinately nested chains from
x
to
y
such that the ordered limit set
(S\setminus\{x,y\},\leq_{\infty})
, where
S=\bigcup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
, has order-type
\tau
. Then, the set
S
decomposes in

S=S_{R}\sqcup S^{\prime}\sqcup S_{A},

where

S_{R}=S\cap R\quad,\quad S_{A}=S\cap A\quad\text{and}\quad S^{\prime}=S\setminus(A\cup R).

By Lemma
6.23
, there exists either a full orbit
O=\{z_{k}\}_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}
or a negative semi full orbit
O=\{z_{k}\}_{k=-\infty}^{-1}
such that
S^{\prime}=O
. Then, clearly, the order type of
(S^{\prime},\leq^{*}_{\infty})
, where
\leq^{*}_{\infty}
is the order relation
\leq_{\infty}
restricted to
S^{\prime}
, is, respectively,
\zeta
or
\omega^{*}
.

Thus, to have the statement, it is enough to set
\beta
as the order type of
\left(S_{R}\setminus\{x\},\leq^{R}_{\infty}\right)
, where
\leq^{R}_{\infty}
is the order relation
\leq_{\infty}
restricted to
S_{R}\setminus\{x\}
, and
\beta^{\prime}
as the order type of
\left(S_{A}\setminus\{y\}),\leq_{\infty}^{A}\right)
, where
\leq_{\infty}^{A}
is the order relation
\leq_{\infty}
restricted to
S_{A}\setminus\{y\}
.
∎

7
Refinement of Conley decomposition and prolongational hierarchy

For a compact system
(X,f)
, the emergent order spectrum
\Omega
detects trivially both the chain recurrent and the gradient parts:
x,y
lie in the same chain component iff
\Omega(x,y)\neq\varnothing\neq\Omega(y,x)
. If
x,y
are chain related but lie along the gradient portion between distinct components, then
\Omega(x,y)\neq\varnothing
while
\Omega(y,x)=\varnothing
.

If
K
is a chain component, the spectra observed inside
K
are intrinsic, as we prove in the following result, to the subsystem
(K,f|_{K})
, independent of the ambient system, and provide a canonical decoration of each Conley component.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical systems, let
K\subseteq X
be a chain component and consider the dynamical system
(K,f|_{K})
. Then,

\big(\Omega\big)_{f_{|_{K}}}=\big(\Omega_{f}\big)_{|_{K^{2}}}

Proof.

Trivially, for every
(x,y)\in K^{2}
, we have
\Omega_{f_{|K}}(x,y)\subseteq\Omega_{f}(x,y)
, because any
\varepsilon
-chain from
x
to
y
within
K
is an
\varepsilon
-chain in the dynamical system
(X,f)
, too.

To prove the other inclusion,
we show that, given
(x,y)\in K^{2}
, any ordinately nested sequence
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
from
x
to
y
is such that
\widehat{S_{n}}\subseteq K
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From this fact, it follows that for every ordering
\beta\in\Omega_{f}(x,y)
, we have
\beta\in\Omega_{f_{|K}}(x,y)
, too. Moreover, by Lemma
5.3
, for every
z\in\bigcup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
, we have
x\,\mathcal{C}\,z
and
z\,\mathcal{C}\,y
, and since
x
and
y
belong to the same chain component
K
, we have
z\in K
, too.
∎

In the next two results, we will see that the limit order
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
attached to a complete sequence of ordinately
nested chains from
x
to
y
induces a natural refinement of Conley’s partial order.
Indeed, the components that are
actually visited by the chains appear as contiguous “blocks” in
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
, and
the quotient that collapses each such block
to a single point recovers exactly the Conley order on the corresponding set of
components.
The next two results make this precise: first we show that the intersection of
each chain component with
S^{\prime}
is convex in
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
, and then that the
induced Conley order on the corresponding set of components is in fact linear.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and let
x,y\in X
be such that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
. Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a complete sequence of ordinately nested chains and set
S=\bigcup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
and
S^{\prime}=S\setminus\{x,y\}
. Then, for every chain component
K\subseteq X
, the set
K\cap S^{\prime}
is convex in the ordered limit set
(S^{\prime},\leq_{\infty})
.

Proof.

Pick
x_{1},x_{2}\in K\cap S^{\prime}
such that
x_{1}\leq_{\infty}x_{2}
and take
x_{3}\in S^{\prime}
such that
x_{1}\leq_{\infty}x_{3}\leq_{\infty}x_{2}
. Then, we need to prove that
x_{3}\in K
. In fact, by Lemma
5.3
, we have
x_{1}\,\mathcal{C}\,x_{3}
and
x_{3}\,\mathcal{C}\,x_{2}
and, since
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
, we have also
x_{2}\,\mathcal{C}\,x_{1}
. Therefore,
x_{3}\,\mathcal{C}\,x_{1}
, because
\mathcal{C}
is a transitive relation, and thus,
x_{3}\in K
.
∎

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
(X,f)
be a compact dynamical system and let
x,y\in X
be such that
x\,\mathcal{C}\,y
. Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a complete sequence of ordinately nested chains and set
S=\bigcup_{n}\widehat{S_{n}}
and
S^{\prime}=S\setminus\{x,y\}
. Let
H
be the set of all the chain components
K\subseteq X
such that
K\cap S\neq\varnothing
.
Then,
(H,\leq_{\mathrm{Conley}})
is a linearly ordered set.

Proof.

It is known that
(H,\leq_{\mathrm{Conley}})
is a partially ordered set. It remains to prove that for every
K,K^{\prime}\in H
one of the following holds:
K\leq_{\mathrm{Conley}}K^{\prime}
or
K^{\prime}\leq_{\mathrm{Conley}}K
.
We distinguish two cases.
If
x=y
, it means that there is a chain component
K
such that
S\subseteq K
, so
H=\{K\}
and so there is nothing to prove. In fact, let
z\in S\setminus\{x\}
and let
S_{n}:\,x_{0}^{(n)}=x,\,x_{1}^{(n)},\dots,\,x_{m_{n}}^{(n)}=x
. Thus, there exists a sequence of points
\{x_{i_{n}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, with
i_{n}\in\{1,\dots,m_{n}-1\}
, such that
x_{i_{n}}^{(n)}=z
. Then

\{S_{n}^{x\to z}:\,x_{0}^{(n)},\,x_{1}^{(n)},\dots,\,x_{i_{n}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\quad\text{and}\quad\{S_{n}^{z\to x}:\,x_{i_{n}}^{(n)},\,x_{i_{n}+1}^{(n)},\dots,\,x_{m_{n}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

are two complete sequences of ordinately nested chains respectively from
x
to
z
and from
z
to
x
, and therefore
z\in K
.

If
x\neq y
, we can have that
S\subseteq K
and so
H=\{K\}
and the thesis is trivially valid. Instead, if
x\neq y
and there are at least two different chain components
K
and
K^{\prime}
such that both
K\cap S
and
K^{\prime}\cap S
are not empty, we
take two distinct points
z\in K\cap S
and
w\in K^{\prime}\cap S
. Suppose that, for all sufficiently large
n
,
z
appears before
w
in
S_{n}
. Then, by Lemma
5.3
, we have that
z\,\mathcal{C}\,w
and thus
K\leq_{\mathrm{Conley}}K^{\prime}
. Similarly, if
w
appears before
z
, we can conclude that
K^{\prime}\leq_{\mathrm{Conley}}K
.
∎

The EOS of the pair
(x,y)
are of course related to other transfinite structures used to describe recurrence. The most important ones, among them, are probably the prolongational sets
J_{\alpha}(x)
introduced by Auslander (see
[
2
]
). We recall that the prolongational set of
x
of order
\alpha
is defined transfinitely, in the discrete-time case, as (see
[
12
]
):

J_{1}(x)=\{y\in X\,|\,x\,\mathcal{N}\,y\}\quad,\quad J_{\alpha}(x)=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\overline{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}(J_{\beta})^{n}(B_{\varepsilon}(x))},

(14)

where
(J_{\beta})^{1}=J_{\beta}
and
(J_{\beta})^{n}=(J_{\beta})((J_{\beta})^{n-1})
.

We want to show some elementary examples to demonstrate that the EOS provides a finer description of recurrence properties than the prolongational sets. Indeed, the fact that any emergent order in
\Omega(x,y)
is anchored to a precise sequence of ordinately nested chains makes it possible to describe the recurrence in distinct ways, each independent of the others, whereas different transfinite levels of prolongational sets (that concern only the existence of certain sets of points, and not their realization) are strictly nested: if
y\in J_{\alpha}(x)
then it automatically belongs to
J_{\beta}(x)
for every countable
\beta>\alpha
. For instance, if a point
x
is in recurrence relation with
y
, then clearly
y\in J_{1}(x)
(and thus to
J_{\alpha}
for every
\alpha
). This may happen, to mention two extreme cases, either if:

•

x
is in the basin of the attracting fixed point
y
,

or if

•

x
is a transitive point.

From the point of view of the EOS, we have in the two cases:

•

\Omega(x,y)
coincides with the unique order-type
\{\omega\}
;

•

\Omega(x,y)
contains every scattered (infinite) countable ordering.

More generally, prolongational sets only “count” the needed
\varepsilon
-
corrections
, while emergent orders directly count
iterations
, and this can provide a finer description of recurrence. We now discuss some examples of dynamical systems
(X,f)
in which we have three points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in X
where we cannot distinguish
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
from the point of view of prolongational set of
x_{1}
, while the EOS discriminates the recurrence properties between
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
and between
x_{1}
and
x_{3}
.

Theorem 7.4
.

There exists a dynamical system
(X,f)
and three points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in X

x_{3}\in J_{\alpha}(x_{1})\quad,\quad x_{3}\in J_{\alpha}(x_{2})

\alpha=\min\{\beta\mid x_{3}\in J_{\beta}(x_{1})\}=\min\{\beta\mid x_{3}\in J_{\beta}(x_{2})\}

Let us now show another instance of the refinement we are describing, this time using the classical Denjoy homeomorphism (
[
5
]
).

Definition 7.5

(Denjoy circle homeomorphism)
.

An orientation–preserving homeomorphism
f:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
with irrational rotation number
\rho(f)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
(defined via a lift to
\mathbb{R}
) is called a
Denjoy homeomorphism
if it has a nontrivial
wandering interval
: an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that
f^{m}(I)\cap f^{n}(I)=\varnothing
for all
m\neq n
. We set the notation

I_{n}:=f^{n}(I)\qquad(n\in\mathbb{Z}).

Fact
.

Let
f
be a Denjoy homeomorphism and
I
a wandering interval, with
I_{n}=f^{n}(I)
. Then:

1.

There exists a continuous, surjective, degree–one monotone map
h:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
with

h\circ f\;=\;R_{\rho(f)}\circ h,

where
R_{\rho(f)}
is the rigid rotation by angle
\rho(f)
. The map
h
collapses each component of

\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{n}
to a point; hence
f
is semi–conjugate but not conjugate to
R_{\rho(f)}
.

2.

The closed set

K\;:=\;\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{n}

is a perfect, totally disconnected, nowhere–dense (Cantor)
f
–invariant set, and
f|_{K}
is minimal. All wandering intervals are exactly the family
\{I_{n}\}_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
, and their lengths tend to
0
.

Theorem 7.6
.

There exists a dynamical system
(X,f)
and three points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in X
such that

x_{2},x_{3}\in J_{\alpha}(x_{1}),

for some ordinal
\alpha=\min\{\beta\mid x_{2}\in J_{\beta}(x_{1})\}=\{\beta\mid x_{3}\in J_{\beta}(x_{1})\}
, and

\Omega(x_{1},x_{2})\neq\Omega(x_{1},x_{3}).

Proof.

We will prove the statement by exhibiting two instances of the phenomenon.

1.

Consider the dynamical system
(X,f)
, where
X=[-1,1]
and

f(x)=\begin{cases}2^{-\lfloor\log_{2}x\rfloor}\bigl(x-2^{\lfloor\log_{2}x\rfloor}\bigr)^{2}+2^{\lfloor\log_{2}x\rfloor}&\text{if }0\leq x\leq 1\\
(x+1)^{2}-1&\text{if }-1\leq x\leq 0\end{cases}.

Then, setting
x_{1}=1
and
x_{2}=-1
and picking
x_{3}\in(-1,0)
, we have

x_{2},x_{3}\in J_{3}(x_{1})\setminus J_{2}(x_{1}),

and

\zeta\cdot\omega+\zeta\in\Omega(x_{1},x_{2})\setminus\Omega(x_{1},x_{3})\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta\cdot\omega+\omega^{*}\in\Omega(x_{1},x_{3})\setminus\Omega(x_{1},x_{2}),

and thus,
\Omega(x_{1},x_{2})\neq\Omega(x_{1},x_{3})
.

2.

Let
f:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a Denjoy homeomorphism and consider the dynamical system
(\mathbb{S}^{1},f)
. Pick
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
and
x_{3}\in I_{0}
.
Then, since
\{I_{-h}\}_{h\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense, in every neighbourhood of
x_{1}
we can find a point
z
such that
z\,\mathcal{O}\,x_{3}
, thus
x_{1}\,\mathcal{N}\,x_{3}
and
x_{3}\in J_{1}(x_{1})
. On the other hand, no point in the circle has
x_{3}
in its
\omega
-limit set (no forward orbit accumulates at
x_{3}
), because
x_{3}
belongs to the wandering set. So
\zeta\notin\Omega(x_{1},x_{3})
.
On the other hand, we have
x_{2}\in J_{1}(x_{1})
and
\Omega(x_{1},x_{2})
contains
\zeta
(it contains
\omega^{*}
, in fact).

∎

Remark 7.7
.

The previous example shows that the implication in Theorem
6.13
cannot be inverted in general.

Note that
\Omega
also elementarily discriminates between the two dynamical systems
(\mathbb{S}^{1},f)
, where
f
is the Denjoy homeomorphism, and
(\mathbb{S}^{1},R_{\alpha})
, where
R_{\alpha}
is the irrational rotation. In fact, for instance,
\Omega_{R_{\alpha}}(x,x)
is always non trivial, while
\Omega_{f}(x,x)=\varnothing
if
x
is in a wandering interval
I
.

In conclusion, Section 8. shows that the topological invariant provided by the EOS discriminates finely the type of recurrence exhibited by pairs of points, and furnishes a high-resolution order-theoretic refinement of the invariant recurrence structure of a compact dynamical system.
