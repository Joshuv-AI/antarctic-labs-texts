Curve Stitching and Dancing Planets
Frances Herr

Abstract
Curve stitching is a classic educational activity where one constructs elegant curves from a family of straight lines. We perform curve stitching around a circle to make a modular stitch graph . Take m m points equally spaced around a circle, choose an integer multiplier a a , and draw a chord from point p p to a ​ p mod m ap\mod m . What design will appear as the envelope of these chords? We connect these discrete objects to a continuous-time dynamical system and apply a topological perspective to understand the answer to this question.

1.1
Some first patterns

What do we mean by the “design” of a modular stitch graph? One candidate for a concrete mathematical notion is an
envelope

Definition 1.1

(Envelope)
.

An
envelope
of a collection of lines
L

C
such that each
\ell\in L
is tangent to
C
at some point.

By this definition, a collection of lines can possibly have many envelopes. Sometimes, an envelope curve for a modular stitch graph can strongly resemble its design. Other times, however, it is not a good representation of the graph’s appearance. We can see this phenomenon with some examples.

1.2
Envelopes

An
epicycloid
is a curve given by rolling one circle on the outside of another fixed circle and tracking a point on the boundary of the outer circle. To construct the epicycloid with
a-1
petals, take the moving circle to have radius
\frac{1}{a+1}
and the fixed circle to have radius
\frac{a-1}{a+1}
. See Figure
4
.

Figure 4
:
Constructing an epicycloid and a hypocycloid by rolling circles. (left) This epicycloid is formed by a circle of radius
\frac{1}{5}
rolling outside a circle of radius
\frac{3}{5}
. (right) This hypocycloid is formed by a circle of radius
\frac{4}{3}
rolling inside a circle of radius
\frac{5}{3}
.

A
hypocycloid
is similar to an epicycloid but the rolling circle is on the inside of the fixed circle. We can realize both these curves with the following parametric equations.

\displaystyle\begin{split}x(t)&=\frac{\alpha\cos(\beta t)+\beta\cos(\alpha t)}{\alpha+\beta}\\
y(t)&=\frac{\alpha\sin(\beta t)+\beta\sin(\alpha t)}{\alpha+\beta}\end{split}

(1)

If
\alpha
and
\beta
are both positive real numbers, then equations (
1
) describe an epicycloid. By a change of variables, we can assume without loss of generality that
0<\beta\leq\alpha
. Then (
1
) corresponds with a circle of radius
\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}
rolling around a circle of radius
\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha+\beta}
. If
\alpha>0
but
\beta<0
, then equations (
1
) describe a hypocycloid with fixed circle radius
\frac{\alpha-\beta}{|\alpha+\beta|}
and rolling circle radius
\frac{|\beta|}{|\alpha+\beta|}
.

In this way, we can realize epicycloids and hypocycloids using the same set of parametric equations. For our purposes, we will only be concerned with cases where
\alpha
and
\beta
are integers because we are interested in periodic curves.

Epicycloids and hypocycloids are the relevant envelopes for modular stitch graphs. In a quantifiable sense, understanding the design of a modular stitch graph amounts to finding the most “natural” epicycloid or hypocycloid which fits as an envelope. To accomplish this task, we construct a new object.

2
Dancing Planets

The second century Ptolemaic model for the solar system involved planets traversing “epicycles” over the course of their circular orbit around the Earth. Although we no longer view the universe as heliocentric, and we know that orbits are not circular, the beauty of this model endures. Even today, epicycloids from the ratios of planetary orbits make a common appearance in science-inspired art (see
[
4
]
). This history lends us a convenient metaphor to introduce our next mathematical object.

We denote a

When the planet dance

\mathcal{P}(1,0)

. If

Proposition 2.3

(Envelope of a planet dance)
.

The curve (epicycloid or hypocycloid) given by equations (
1
) for integers
\alpha
and
\beta
will be an envelope for the family of lines given by
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Proposition
2.3
generalizes Fact
1.2
as will be explained in the remainder of this section. Since we want to focus on extending this result, we will not prove it here but refer to “The Trochoid as a Tack in a Bungee Cord” by Simoson
[

Notice that
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
is a

\MMT(m,a)
.

Definition 2.4

(
m
-sampling of a planet dance)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
be a planet dance and
m
be a positive integer. An
m
-sampling
of
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
is the finite set of chords for
t=0,\frac{1}{m},\frac{2}{m},\dots,\frac{m-1}{m}
. We denote this set
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)
.

A sampling of a planet dance looks rather like a modular stitch graph (see Figure
6
for an example). But does this visual correlation point to an actual mathematical correspondence?

Question 2
.

For every
\MMT(m,a)
are there
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
\MMT(m,a)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)
? And, for each
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)
, can we find
a
such that
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)=\MMT(m,a)
?

The first part of Question
2
has an immediate affirmative answer. The modular stitch graph
\MMT(m,a)
is exactly the same set of chords as
\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)
. When the initial end of the chord has traveled distance
p
, the terminal end will have traveled a distance of
ap
. Thus, each chord in
\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)
can be constructed by multiplying the initial point
p
by
a
. We will call planet dances of the form
\mathcal{P}(1,\beta)

integral dances
.

Lemma 2.5

(Fundamental Correspondence)
.

The modular stitch graph
\MMT(m,a)
is an
m
-sampling of the integral planet dance
\mathcal{P}(1,a)
.

Together, Lemma
2.5
and Proposition
2.3
imply Fact
1.2
from the previous section. A modular stitch graph is a discrete sampling of a planet dance, and the envelope for a planet dance is an envelope for its discrete sampling. However, a given modular stitch graph may be a discrete sampling of multiple planet dances, just as it might have multiple envelopes.

The answer to the second part of Question
2
is not so simple. If we have a planet dance
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha>1
and
\gcd(\alpha,\beta)=1
, the multiplier of the corresponding modular stitch graph should be
\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
. But in our definition of modular stitch graphs, we do not allow non-integral multipliers. Of course, we could change the definition, but we will see that there is a way forward without this restructuring.

3
Introducing Topology

3.1
Planet dances as paths on a torus

A directed chord on the circle is uniquely determined by the position of the two endpoints—planet A and planet B. Hence, the space of all such possible chords is a circle times a circle… a torus! We denote the circle by
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and the torus by
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Figure 8
:
A standard embedding of a torus in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Instead of imagining the familiar doughnut shape as in Figure
8
, we will be working with the flat torus.
3
3

3

Thanks to Elliot Kienzle for the beautiful torus drawing.
Parameterize the circle with
e^{2\pi it}
for
t\in[0,1]
. Then the product of two circles
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
is parameterized by the unit square
[0,1]\times[0,1]
. We can then represent
\mathbb{T}^{2}
as the unit square in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with opposite sides identified or, equivalently,
\mathbb{T}^{2}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

A point on the torus gives a single chord on the circle where the coordinates determine the endpoints of the chord. A path on the torus gives a continuous family of chords. For a planet dance
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
, we know planet A and planet B are moving at constant speeds
\alpha
and
\beta
respectively. So all chords in
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
form a linear closed path on the flat torus. These loops are commonly used to represent torus knots. Figure
9
shows two examples of these linear loops on the torus.

Figure 9
:

which corresponds with
\mathcal{P}(2,3)
and the orange lines depict

\mathcal{P}(1,9)
. The black dots indicate that
\mathcal{P}(1,9)
is sampled at a rate of

\MMT(25,9)
.

The line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which corresponds to
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
is given by

x=\alpha t,\hskip 10.0pty=\beta t,\hskip 10.0pt\text{ or, if }\alpha\neq 0,\hskip 10.0pty=\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}x.

We will identify the planet dance
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
with this line on the torus, and depending on the context, refer to this line as
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Remark 3.1

(On direction)
.

Swapping the values of
\alpha
and
\beta
in a planet dance will not make a difference in the picture, but it does change the direction of each chord and change the slope of the corresponding line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Moving forward, we will maintain the direction of chords as this inherently matters for modular stitch graphs. In a modular stitch graph, we think of the initial point of the chord being multiplied by
a
to get the terminal point of the chord. That is, we would like
\MMT(m,a)
to correspond with
\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)
and not
\mathcal{S}_{m}(a,1)
.

If a planet dance is a linear loop on the torus, then a sampling of the planet dance is a set of equally spaced points along the loop. As the sampling becomes more frequent, the set of points more closely approximates the loop and the discrete sampling picture becomes a better representation of the continuous-time planet dance.

Not only do planet dances and sampled planet dances have a geometric interpretation, but they also have an algebraic one. We can write
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
as a collection of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)=\left\{\left(\alpha t,\beta t\right)\>|\>t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

This set forms a rank-one continuous subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with component-wise addition.

(\alpha t,\beta t)+(\alpha s,\beta s)=\left(\alpha(t+s),\>\beta(t+s)\right)\in\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)

Likewise,
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)
is a rank-one discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We will use this group structure in the subsequent sections.

3.2
Aliasing in planet dances

We have observed that sometimes samplings of planet dances can appear in disguise. Like how
\MMT(100,34)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,34)
looks much more like
\mathcal{P}(3,2)
than
\mathcal{P}(1,34)
. It’s possible the reader has seen this kind of phenomena before.

Aliasing
is a pervasive topic in signal processing. The idea is best demonstrated with sine waves. Suppose that a computer is storing discrete-time samples of a musical note, represented as a sine wave. If the sampling rate is too low, then when the computer plays back the note from its discrete samples, the listener hears it as a lower note, i.e. with a lower frequency. This happens because there is a more “natural” wave that fits the samples. The two waves—the original sine wave and the lower frequency alias—intersect exactly at the sample points. An example is shown in Figure
10
. Common examples of aliasing are Moiré patterns and the “wagon wheel” effect in cinema. In signal processing, aliasing is often seen as an unfortunate reality, and research into “anti-aliasing filters” is common. However, this phenomenon is why we see such a large variety of designs among modular stitch graphs. This will be the focus for the rest of the paper.

Figure 10
:
When a higher frequency sine wave is under-sampled, it can appear as a lower frequency sine wave where the sampling points are exactly the points of intersection.

Just as one sine wave can alias another when we take a discrete sampling, one planet dance can alias another. The key to this aliasing is the points of intersection.

Suppose that the lines corresponding to planet dances
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
intersect
m
times in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. These intersection points occur at regular intervals because both lines have constant rational slopes. Then the
m
-sampling of
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and the
m
-sampling of
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are the same set of chords and will look identical. Since we know how to achieve
m
-samplings of
integral
planet dances with modular stitch graphs, this gives us a way to sample
any
planet dance with a modular stitch graph by finding an integral alias.

Lemma 3.2

(Planet dance intersection)
.

Let lines
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be given by

\ell_{1}(t)=(\alpha t,\beta t)\hskip 10.0pt\text{and}\hskip 10.0pt\ell_{2}(t)=(\gamma t,\delta t)

such that
\gcd(\alpha,\beta)=\gcd(\gamma,\delta)=1
. Then
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
will intersect
|\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma|
times and will do so at regular intervals.

This is a standard result often applied to intersection numbers of torus knots in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. The proof follows by doing a change of basis to send the vectors
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(\gamma,\delta)
to
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
respectively. Then one can apply Pick’s Theorem to relate the number of integer lattice points inside the relevant parallelogram to the determinant of the change of basis matrix. By restating Lemma
3.2
, we can relate non-integral planet dances to modular stitch graphs.

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
and
\mathcal{P}(\gamma,\delta)
are two planet dances in reduced form and let
m=|\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma|
. Then
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(\gamma,\delta)
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Given a planet dance
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
in reduced form and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
m=|\alpha a-\beta|
. Then the modular stitch graph
\MMT(m,a)
will be an
m
-sampling of
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Although we arrived at Proposition
3.4
geometrically, there is also an algebraic way to view this result. Consider that for
\alpha,\beta,a
, and
m
as above, we have the relation
\beta=\alpha a\pm m
. Then we note that
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\alpha a\pm m)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\alpha a)
. This follows from the definition of a sampling. We can then rephrase Proposition
3.4
as
\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\alpha a)
.

Note that this is only true because
\gcd(\alpha,m)=1
. To see this, write
\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)
and
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\alpha a)
as subsets of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)=\left\{\left(\frac{k}{m},\frac{ak}{m}\right)\>\Big|\>0\leq k<m\right\}\hskip 30.0pt\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\alpha a)=\left\{\left(\frac{\alpha k}{m},\frac{a(\alpha k)}{m}\right)\>\Big|\>0\leq k<m\right\}

In order for these to be the same set of points, we need that the map
k\mapsto\alpha k
on
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
is an isomorphism of the group. This happens if and only if
\alpha
is invertible in
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
. There are then
|(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}|
possible choices for
\alpha
given
m
and
a
.

This brings to light a way to describe the most “visually natural” envelope for a set of chords
\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)
. We use the metric on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to determine the sample point
(\frac{\alpha}{m},\frac{a\alpha}{m})
closest to the origin. If
\gcd(\alpha,m)=1
, then the line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
connecting
(0,0)
to
(\frac{\alpha}{m},\frac{a\alpha}{m})
will pass through all the sample points. This determines a planet dance alias for
\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)
. If
\gcd(\alpha,m)=d>1
, then we have some interesting behavior which will be explained in Section
4
.

To address an inverse of Proposition
3.4
, a sampling of a planet dance
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)
can be realized as a modular stitch graph exactly when
m=|\alpha a-\beta|

for some positive integer
a
. This has a solution if and only if
m\equiv\pm\beta\mod\alpha
. If we start with a general sampled planet dance
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)
, we may not be able to represent it as a modular stitch graph with the exact sample rate
m
. We can instead choose the nearest
m^{\prime}
to
m
such that
m^{\prime}\equiv\pm\beta\mod\alpha
and then we can realize
\mathcal{S}_{m^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta)
as an modular stitch graph with multiplier
m^{\prime}
. The size of
\alpha
will determine how far we may need to roam to find a solution.

3.3
Returning to our questions

Looking back at Question
2
, we now have an answer. Given any planet dance
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
, we can find a corresponding family of modular stitch graphs which are all samplings of
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
. But given a specific sampling
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)
, we may not be able to realize it as a modular stitch graph. This also reveals why certain modular stitch graphs exhibit designs that are very different from the integral planet dance given by the Fundamental Correspondence Lemma
2.5
. When
\MMT(m,a)
is viewed as an
m
-sampling of
\mathcal{P}(1,a)
, if
m
is an “under-sampling” then
\MMT(m,a)
will alias as a sampling of another planet dance. But this correspondence is only the tip of the iceberg.

Theorem 4.2

(Overlaying planet dances)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
be a planet dance in reduced form with
\alpha\neq 0
and let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
be any integer. For
m=|\alpha a-\beta|
we have
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)

3.4

consists of
d

\mathcal{S}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{d-1}

k

|\mathcal{S}_{k}|=m
and
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is contained in a copy of
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
rotated by
\frac{k}{d(\alpha-\beta)}
around the circle.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
m
and
b
be positive integers such that
b<m
and
b\nmid m

for

\MMT(m,\lceil\frac{m}{b}\rceil)

•

For
\MMT(m,\lceil\frac{m}{b}\rceil)

Proof.

We prove this result for a graph of the form
\MMT(m,\lceil\frac{m}{b}\rceil)
. The proof for
\MMT(m,\lfloor\frac{m}{b}\rfloor)
follows similarly.

Note that
d=\gcd(r,b)=\gcd(m,b)
so we can write
m=dm^{\prime}
for
m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{>0}
. Then we rewrite
a=\lceil\frac{m}{b}\rceil
.

a=\frac{dm^{\prime}+(b-r)}{b}=\frac{m^{\prime}+\frac{b-r}{d}}{\frac{b}{d}}

So we can write the relation

a\left(\frac{b}{d}\right)-\left(\frac{b-r}{d}\right)=m^{\prime}

where all terms are integers. Next, we apply Corollary
4.1

\mathcal{S}_{m^{\prime}}(1,a)=\mathcal{S}_{m^{\prime}}(\frac{b}{d},\frac{b-r}{d})
. Then we apply Theorem
4.2
to get the desired result for
\mathcal{S}_{dm^{\prime}}(1,a)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(1,a)=\MMT(m,a)
.

5
Wrapping up and moving forward

We will take a moment to recall where we have been and fit the pieces together. In Section 1, we introduced modular stitch graphs and asked the Big Question:

Given values for
m
and
a
, what design will
\MMT(m,a)
create?

In Section 2 we defined planet dances and we saw that modular stitch graphs are discrete samplings of these objects. We established a fundamental correspondence in Lemma
2.5
which gives a planet dance for each modular stitch graph. However, sometimes this correspondence doesn’t capture the visual design of the modular stitch graph. We solved this conundrum with planet dance intersection in Section 3. We saw that if two planet dances intersect
m
times, they will do so at regular intervals and an
m
-sampling of each will look identical. At this point, we have reframed our Big Question.

A modular stitch graph is most naturally sampled from which planet dance?

By drawing planet dances on a flat torus, we find the answer. After graphing the chords of
\MMT(m,a)
as points in the torus, we look for the closest sample point to
(0,0)
given by
(\frac{\alpha}{m},\frac{a\alpha}{m})
with coordinates taken modulo 1. Then we connect
(0,0)
to this point with the shortest path on the square torus and extend the line. This line will have a rational slope given by
\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
for some
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
so it will form a closed loop and it will pass through all the points
(\frac{\alpha k}{m},\frac{a(\alpha k)}{m})
by construction. As we saw in Section
3.2
, if
\gcd(\alpha,m)=1
, then these will be all the points of
\MMT(m,a)
. However, if
\gcd(\alpha,m)=d>1
, then it will pass through exactly
\frac{m}{d}
of them. Letting
m=dm^{\prime}
, they will be the points of the form
(\frac{dk}{m},\frac{a(dk)}{m})=(\frac{k}{m^{\prime}},\frac{ak}{m^{\prime}})
. Now we have reached the discussion from Section
4
in Theorem
4.2
. The
m^{\prime}
-sampling
\mathcal{S}_{m^{\prime}}(1,a)
lies on the line
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. So the original modular stitch graph
\MMT(m,a)=\mathcal{S}_{dm^{\prime}}(1,a)
is composed of
d
sets of chords which each lie on a copy of
\mathcal{P}(\alpha,\beta)
rotated around the circle. With this, we have a complete answer to our Big Question.

All this mathematical theory may obscure the simplicity of the answer. At the end of the day, we have only to connect the dots. Given a modular stitch graph
\MMT(m,a)
, we can simply graph the set of chords as points in the square torus. And then, the most natural way to connect the dots will produce the planet dance that most closely visually represents the design of
\MMT(m,a)
.
