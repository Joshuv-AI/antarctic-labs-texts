Phosphorus (P)-magnesium (Mg) interaction in citrus growth: high P promotes new shoot growth while accelerating old leaf symptoms in young navel orange plants under Mg deficiency
Authors: Yawei Hu, Yuanyuan Zhang, Fengxian Yao, Guidong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe interaction between phosphorus (P) and magnesium (Mg) significantly influences citrus productivity, especially under Mg deficiency. This study investigated how P availability affects growth, physiological performance, and nutrient partitioning in young navel orange plants under different Mg supplies.MethodsA factorial experiment was conducted using sand-cultured navel orange plants subjected to two Mg levels (0 mM as deficient and 1 mM as sufficient) and three P levels (0.2, 1, and 5 mM, supplied as KH2PO4/NaH2PO4, representing low, moderate, and high supply, respectively). Growth parameters, leaf chlorophyll content, photosynthetic performance, oxidative stress markers, and mineral concentrations in old and new leaves were measured.ResultsMg deficiency induced chlorosis and corky veins in old leaves, symptoms markedly accelerated by high P supply. Under Mg deficiency, High P reduced the old leaf biomass but promoted new shoot growth. These responses correlated with accelerated chlorophyll degradation, inhibited photosynthesis, enhanced oxidative damage, and altered nutrient levels (decreased nitrogen but increased potassium and P) in the old leaves, alongside a higher Mg concentration in the new leaves. Adequate Mg nutrition completely mitigated the negative effects of high P to the old leaves.ConclusionsHigh P supply under Mg deficiency triggers a tissue-specific physiological trade-off, promoting old leaf senescence while stimulating new shoot growth via nutrient reallocation. Avoiding high-P inputs in Mg-limited orchards is crucial to prevent aggravating Mg deficiency and ensure sustainable citrus production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08758-6.

Background

As a globally significant fruit crop, citrus requires balanced mineral nutrition for optimal productivity and fruit quality [1, 2]. Among essential macronutrients, magnesium (Mg) and phosphorus (P) play pivotal yet interconnected roles in fundamental plant physiological processes [3, 4]. The physiological indispensability of Mg stems from its role as the central atom of chlorophyll and its function in activating numerous enzymes involved in photosynthesis, carbohydrate partitioning, and P utilization itself [5, 6], while P is fundamental for energy metabolism (ATP), nucleic acid synthesis, and membrane integrity [7].

Despite their critical importance, imbalances in P and Mg availability are common in the primary citrus-growing regions [8–10]. Mg deficiency is becoming increasingly widespread and serious in the farming system, particularly in highly weathered acidic soils of southern China, also the main area of citrus cultivation, which are prone to Mg deficiency due to intense leaching and cation imbalance [8, 11]. Mg deficiency significantly impairs photosynthetic efficiency, carbohydrate translocation, and overall plant vigor [12]. It was reported that Mg deficiency induced interveinal chlorosis, vein enlargement, and corkiness in Citrus sinensis leaves, along with alterations to photosynthesis and related parameters, and the severity of these symptoms progressively increased with leaf age [3]. Compounding this challenge, P availability in these soils exhibits extreme spatial variability [13]. Furthermore, excessive P fertilization, driven by historical practices aiming for rapid growth, has been documented in major citrus-producing regions, and contributes to environmental eutrophication [14].

Critically, emerging evidence suggests significant P-Mg interactions influence nutrient acquisition, physiological efficiency, and stress tolerance in plants [15–18]. Previous study suggested that inadequate P availability induces Mg deficiency in grapevines grown on acidic soils, while this deficiency can be mitigated by P fertilization, which enhances the translocation of Mg from roots to shoots of grapevines [19]. Conversely, research indicates that while plant growth promotion by arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis occurred under low P status, high Mg supply enhanced the efficiency of AM symbiosis under these low P conditions and alleviated the inhibition of AM symbiosis caused by high P availability [16]. It was also reported that optimal Mg status can enhance P use efficiency in rapeseed plants [20]. All these results reveal a key physiological interaction between P and Mg nutrition. However, the expression pattern of this bidirectional interaction mechanism in perennial woody plants such as citrus is not yet clear.

Navel orange (Citrus sinensis) is one of the most important citrus varieties and is widely cultivated in southern China. Previous studies often focus on single nutrient deficiency or sufficiency in citrus [21–23], overlooking the dynamic interplay between these elements [17]. Nutrient imbalances are one of the major constraints to citrus productivity, especially excess P fertilization and Mg deficiencies in tropical and subtropical areas [8, 24, 25]. However, the combined impact of P supplies, under both Mg-deficient and adequate Mg conditions, on key physiological and performance traits in young citrus plants (during their critical establishment phase) remains inadequately explored. In the present study, we designed a controlled factorial experiment combining Mg deficiency/sufficiency with low, moderate, and high P supplies to investigate their interactive effects on young ‘Newhall’ navel orange plants. Systematic analyses included growth dynamics, photosynthetic parameters, oxidative stress, and ionomic profiles. We hypothesized that low and/or high P levels would critically modulate plant responses to Mg deficiency, with these interactions mediated by coordinated changes in photosynthesis, redox balance, and nutrient partitioning.

The integrated approach aims to elucidate the synergistic or antagonistic interplay between P and Mg, providing a mechanistic understanding essential for developing refined, sustainable nutrient management strategies. The findings will advance the theoretical framework of nutrient interaction in perennial crops and provide actionable insights for citrus orchard fertilization management in subtropical acidic soils.

Methods

Plant material and treatments

Nine-month-old plants of ‘Newhall’ navel orange (Citrus sinensis) grafted on trifoliate orange (Citrus trifoliata) rootstock were cultivated in a controlled greenhouse environment under a sand culture system. The quartz sand substrate (particle size 0.2–1 mm) was acid-washed and rinsed thoroughly with deionized water prior to use to eliminate residual nutrients. All plants were uniform in stem diameter (7–9 mm), height (35–40 cm) and number of leaves (14–18 fully expanded leaves). A factorial design comprising two Mg levels (0 and 1 mM as MgSO4) and three P concentrations (0.2, 1 and 5 mM as KH2PO4 and NaH2PO4) was implemented. This resulted in six treatment combinations: Mg0P0.2 (0 mM Mg + 0.2 mM P), Mg0P1 (0 mM Mg + 1 mM P), Mg0P5 (0 mM Mg + 5 mM P), Mg1P0.2 (1 mM Mg + 0.2 mM P), Mg1P1 (1 mM Mg + 1 mM P), Mg1P5 (1 mM Mg + 5 mM P), which arranged in a completely randomized design with three biological replicates per treatment and two plants per replicate. The treatments started at the end of March 2022 and lasted 17 weeks. During this period and every 2 days, plants were irrigated with a modified Hoagland’s nutrient solution, adjusted to pH 5.8–6.2, and maintained under natural sunlight with daytime temperatures of 25–35 °C and relative humidity of 60–85%. The basal nutrient solution contained: 2 mM Ca(NO3)2, 3 mM KNO3, 50 µM Fe-EDTA, 20 µM H3BO3, 9 µM MnCl2, 1.6 µM ZnSO4, 0.32 µM CuSO4, 0.2 µM Na2MoO4. Mg and P concentrations were adjusted with MgSO4 and KH2PO4 according to treatment specifications while maintaining ionic balance through compensatory adjustments of K2SO4 and NaH2PO4.

Dynamic determination of relative chlorophyll content (SPAD values)

SPAD values, representing relative chlorophyll content, were monitored weekly on the same set of tagged leaves using a SPAD-502Plus chlorophyll meter (Konica Minolta, Japan). Old leaves (pre-existing at treatment initiation) and new leaves (newly expanded leaves after treatment) were non-destructively tracked to assess temporal chlorophyll dynamics. For old leaves, the measured leaves were the third and fourth ones retained from top to bottom before treatment. For new leaves, two fully unfolded leaves were selected for the first measurement and marked for subsequent long-term monitoring. For each labeled leaf, four measurements were taken at the mid-section of the leaf blade, avoiding major veins, and the average value was recorded.

Analysis of chlorophyll content and photosynthetic measurements

Chlorophyll was extracted from both old and new leaves using 95% ethanol as the extraction solvent, following the method of Hiscox and Israelstam [26]. The absorbance of the extracts was measured at 663 nm and 645 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Mapada UV-1800, Shanghai Mapada Instruments Co, China). The contents of chlorophyll a and b were then determined according to the formulae established by Lichtenthaler and Wellburn [27].

Photosynthetic parameters [net photosynthetic rate (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), and stomatal conductance (Gs)] were measured per unit leaf area on both old and new healthy leaves with a portable photosynthesis system (Li-6400, Li-Cor Inc, Lincoln, NE, USA). Measurements were conducted between 09:00 and 11:00 h on a sunny day under photosynthetic active radiation (PAR) controlled at 1000 µmol m− 2 s− 1. The leaf chamber air flow was maintained at 500 µmol s− 1 and temperature at 30 ± 2 °C. For each treatment, measurements constituted 24 replicates (4 leaves × 6 individual trees).

Histochemical detection of superoxide (O2∙–) and H2O2 accumulation

Histochemical localization of O2∙– and H2O2 in both old and new leaves was assessed using nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB), respectively. Detached leaves were vacuum-infiltrated; for O2∙– detection, 0.2% (w/v) NBT in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) was used, followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 8–12 h until blue formazan deposits became visible. For H2O2 detection, leaves were infiltrated with 1 mg/mL DAB-HCl (pH 3.8) and incubated at room temperature for 8–12 h until visible brown polymerization products formed. All stained leaves were subsequently cleared by boiling in ethanol, rinsed, mounted in 60% (v/v) glycerol, and visualized under a light microscope to determine the relative accumulation and distribution of O2∙– and H2O2 based on staining intensity and localization [28].

Determination of mineral element concentrations

Old and new leaves were harvested separately and were dried at 60 °C until constant weight was reached. After grinding, 0.2 g of each dried sample was ashed in a muffle oven at 500 °C for 6 h and digested with 10 mL of 5% HNO3. In the obtained extract, the concentrations of P, K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, B, and Mo were measured by Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7900, USA). Nitrogen was digested with H2SO4-H2O2 and determined by Auto Discrete Analyzer (Smartchem200, Alliance Instruments, France) [29].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, USA) to compute means and standard deviations, and IBM SPSS Statistics 21 (IBM Corp, Armonk, USA) for analysis of variance (ANOVA). A two-way ANOVA was performed to assess the effects of Mg levels, P supplies, and their interaction (Mg × P), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) for post-hoc comparisons. Ionomic responses were further examined using hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA). These multivariate analyses were performed using SPSS and MeV software. HCA was carried out based on Ward’s linkage method and Euclidean distance, after standardizing elemental concentrations by z-score transformation.

Results

Plant growth and symptoms

At the 10th week of cultivation, plants subjected to Mg-deficient/low-P (Mg0P0.2) and Mg-deficient/moderate-P (Mg0P1) conditions exhibited partial yellowing of old leaves in some replicates, while all plants in the Mg-deficient/high-P group (Mg0P5) displayed chlorosis in old leaves. By the 13th week, all Mg-deficient treatments displayed chlorosis in old leaves regardless of P levels (data not shown). Upon harvest at the 17th week, the symptom of corky split vein was observed in old leaves in all Mg-deficient plants besides chlorosis (Fig. 1a). However, there were no obvious visible symptoms in the old leaves in Mg-sufficient plants, except for a slight yellowing in the old leaves in Mg1P0.2 plants (Fig. 1a). The severity of old leaf impairment was quantitatively confirmed by biomass data (Table 1). Under Mg deficiency, high P supply (Mg0P5) significantly reduced old leaf dry weight by 25.1% compared to the Mg-sufficient/moderate-P control (Mg1P1).

At the end of the experiment, the overall growth performance of the plants in different treatments is shown in Fig. 1b. No obvious visible symptoms were observed in all new leaves across various treatments. Conversely, biomass allocation showed a distinct pattern favoring new in the Mg0P5 treatment. While Mg-sufficient plants showed reduced new leaf and total biomass under low- and high-P conditions compared to moderate-P, Mg-deficient plants exhibited a progressive increase in new leaves, stem of scion, and total biomass with rising P supply (Table 1). Notably, the dry weight of new leaves in the Mg0P5 treatment was comparable to that in the Mg1P1 control, indicating that high P under Mg deficiency supported new leaf growth to a level similar to that under Mg sufficiency.

Dynamic changes of the relative chlorophyll content in different treatments

The initial SPAD values of old leaves were similar in all treatments (Fig. 2a). Throughout the following 13 weeks of cultivation, all Mg-deficient treatments regardless of P levels only exhibited marginally lower relative chlorophyll content (SPAD values) in old leaves compared to Mg-supplied plants. However, Mg-deficient plants showed accelerated chlorophyll loss in old leaves after 13 weeks of cultivation, with severity directly proportional to P supply levels (Fig. 2a). By harvest (week 17), Mg-deficient treatments displayed pronounced declines in SPAD values relative to baseline measurements: 21.4% in the low-P group (Mg0P0.2), 27.3% in moderate-P (Mg0P1), and 34.7% in high-P (Mg0P5). Conversely, low-P treatment showed lower chlorophyll content (72.1) in old leaves compared to moderate- (82.8) and high-P (80.7) groups under Mg sufficiency condition (Fig. 2a).

While the old leaves exhibited progressive chlorophyll degradation under Mg deficiency, new leaves displayed inverse trends across all treatments. From week 10 onward, the relative chlorophyll content in newly developed leaves gradually increased, reaching relative stable levels by week 13 that persisted until harvest (Fig. 2b). In addition, although the relative chlorophyll content of new leaves was lower in Mg-deficient treatment than that in Mg-supplied group, it was not as evident as the decrease in old leaves. We also determined the pigment concentrations in the old and new leaves at harvest through chemical extraction and colorimetric method, and the results were consistent with the relative chlorophyll content (Fig. S1).

Effect of Mg and P on photosynthetic parameters in leaves

Mg deficiency significantly reduced net photosynthetic rate (A) in old leaves across all P levels compared to Mg supply, with the highest value in the Mg1P1 and lowest value in the Mg0P5 plants (Fig. 3a). In contrast, Mg-deficient plants exhibited no significant differences in intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) relative to Mg-supplied counterparts, with the lowest value in the Mg1P1 plants (Fig. 3b). Mg-sufficient plants showed maximal stomatal conductance (gs) and transpiration rate (E) of old leaves in the moderate-P treatment (Mg1P1), while Mg-deficient plants displayed progressive Gs and Tr reduction with increasing P levels (Fig. 3c and d).

Under Mg-sufficient conditions, the A of new leaves increased with rising P supply levels. However, Mg deficiency altered this response, resulting in higher A in both high- and low-P treatments relative to the moderate-P level (Fig. 3e). The Ci showed no significant differences among P treatments under Mg sufficiency, but under Mg deficiency, Ci was significantly higher in the low-P treatment (Fig. 3f). Under adequate Mg supply, the high-P treatment resulted in significantly higher gs and E than both the moderate- and low-P treatments. In contrast, under Mg-deficient conditions, the low-P treatment showed the highest values for both parameters, while the moderate-P treatment showed the lowest (Fig. 3g and h).

Effect of Mg and P on O2∙–, H2O2 contents

Magnesium deficiency appeared to increase the levels of superoxide anions (O2∙–) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the old leaves of young navel orange plants, as visualized by histochemical staining, with a similar trend observed across different P levels (Fig. 4a and b). In contrast, the staining intensity for O2∙– and H2O2 in the new leaves showed no evident change under Mg deficiency compared to Mg-sufficient conditions (Fig. 4c and d).

Effect of Mg and P on nutrient concentrations in the old and new leaves

The concentrations of 5 macroelements (Mg, P, N, K, and Ca) and 6 microelements (B, Mn, Fe, Cu, Zn, and Mo) in the leaves of young navel orange plants treated with Mg and P are demonstrated in Table 2 and Table S1, respectively. Mg deficiency decreased Mg concentrations in both old and new leaves compared to Mg-supplied plants regardless of P levels. Under Mg-sufficient conditions, low- and high-P plants had lower Mg concentrations than moderate-P plants both in the old and new leaves. In contrast, high-P plants had the highest Mg concentrations in the new leaves under Mg-deficient conditions. The P concentrations in both old and new leaves increased along with P treatment concentration under Mg-deficient conditions, while moderate-P plants had the highest P concentrations in both old and new leaves under Mg-sufficient conditions. The N concentrations in the old leaves of navel orange plants were severely affected by Mg deficiency, whereas it had little effect on new leaves. Regardless of P levels, Mg deficiency significantly increased K concentrations in both old and new leaves (Table 2).

Effect of Mg and P on the ionome differential in the old and new leaves

The ionomic responses of navel orange plants to Mg and P were characterized by hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principle component analysis (PCA). HCA was performed separately on elements (variables) and samples to identify homogeneous subgroups. The clustering results, including the generated two-way dendrograms, are presented as heatmaps in Fig. 5. The elements were categorized into two main clusters: Cluster 1 contains Mg, P and Mo, while Cluster 2 was further divided into two subclusters 2a (K, Ca and B) and 2b (N, Mn, Fe, Zn and Cu). The row dendrogram showed a clear separation between the old leaves and new leaves. In both the old and new leaves Clusters, the samples were classified into two main groups which were mainly associated with Mg supplied or not.

Discussion

P and Mg are two critical macronutrients that are required for many functions in plants, including photosynthesis, energy generation, nucleic acid metabolism, enzyme activity regulation, redox reactions, and carbohydrate metabolism [7, 21, 30, 31]. The interaction of P and Mg has been reported in both directional growth of primary roots in Arabidopsis and mycorrhizal symbiosis in soybean [16, 32]. However, little is known about the interaction of P and Mg in citrus growth. In this study, we showed that P supply levels significantly modulate Mg deficiency responses in citrus plants, revealing a complex antagonistic interaction between these two essential nutrients. The most significant finding is the dual effect of high-P supply: while it stimulated bud sprouting and increased biomass accumulation in newly developing leaves under Mg-limited conditions, it simultaneously accelerated Mg-deficiency symptoms in older leaves (Fig. 1). This distinct divergence in tissue-specific responses underscores the complexity of P-Mg interplay in perennial fruit trees. The phenomenon of nutrient reallocation from older to younger tissues under deficiency stress has been observed in many plants [33, 34]. However, the specific role of high P in accelerating this source-sink reprogramming under Mg deficiency in citrus, as clearly demonstrated here, provides a new mechanistic insight.

Previous studies have suggested that Mg deficiency induces enlargement, cracking and lignification of citrus old leaf veins, finally affecting normal plant growth [35]. In this study, high P supply exacerbates senescence in old leaves under Mg deficiency, as evidenced by the earliest visible chlorosis symptom and accelerated decline in SPAD values (Figs. 1 and 2). This phenomenon arise from the intensified disruption of the photosynthetic machinery and oxidative balance induced by high P under Mg-limited conditions (Figs. 3 and 4). Mg deficiency alone impairs chlorophyll biosynthesis and destabilize photosystem II, leading to reduced photosynthetic efficiency [36]. Our results indicate that high P amplifies these impairments. The accelerated chlorophyll degradation likely involves the upregulation of senescence-associated genes (SAGs) and key chlorophyll catabolic enzymes (e.g., pheophorbide a oxygenase, PAO), propelling the loss of photosynthetic capacity [37, 38]. This not only limits light harvesting but also increases excess excitation energy, promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction. The accumulated ROS act as potent signals, potentially activating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades and senescence-promoting transcription factors (e.g., NAC, WRKY), which further orchestrate the expression of SAGs and suppress photosynthesis-related genes [39–43]. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle where reduced photosynthesis weakens ROS-scavenging capacity, and elevated ROS further damages photosynthetic components, collectively accelerating the breakdown of cellular integrity in old leaves.

Another critical mechanism underlying this exacerbation could be the altered nutrient reallocation and ion imbalance triggered by high P under Mg deficiency. At the root level, the active uptake of phosphate, mediated by H+-coupled symporters (e.g., PHT1 family), may compete for membrane electrochemical gradients and energy (ATP) with Mg2+ uptake systems, potentially intensifying Mg2+ deprivation under high P supply. This foundational competition sets the stage for systemic ionomic changes. Nitrogen (N) remobilization from old leaves to young tissues is a well-documented adaptive strategy during leaf senescence [44, 45]. Our findings suggest that high P intensifies this process in Mg-deficient plants, as reflected by the more pronounced decrease in N content in the old leaves (Table 2). This enhanced N translocation likely accelerates the functional decline of the old leaves, as N is irreplaceable for maintaining chloroplast structure and enzyme activity, and its depletion directly compromises the leaf’s metabolic vitality, driving senescence [7]. Concurrently, the observed imbalance between K and Mg under high P may exacerbate metabolic disorders. The altered K/Mg ratio could disrupt stomatal regulation and impair the activity of Mg-dependent enzymes [46, 47]. Furthermore, the altered nutrient status may be perceived by nutrient sensors, such as SPX-domain proteins for P sensing [48]. These signals could integrate with phytohormone pathways, potentially accelerating abscisic acid (ABA) or ethylene biosynthesis/signaling in aging leaves [49]. This may create a metabolic environment that accelerates the degradation of cellular components, culminating in more severe senescence symptoms in the old leaves.

Contrary to the observed senescence in old leaves, high P supply significantly promotes new shoot growth under Mg deficiency in comparing with moderate and low P levels, which provides novel insights into the interactive effects of P and Mg on plant growth regulation. Phosphorus is a key component of ATP, nucleic acids, and phospholipids, which are essential for cell division and elongation in developing shoots [50]. Our results showing increased shoot biomass and higher leaf P concentrations under high P plus Mg-deficient conditions support the idea that high P supply enhances energy metabolism and biosynthetic capacity, partially compensating for the reduced Mg-dependent photosynthetic output [51]. Additionally, P might modulate nutrient uptake and distribution. Mg deficiency often induces imbalances in cation uptake (e.g. K+ and Ca2+), leading to ion toxicity or deficiency in meristematic tissues [46]. High P could alleviate such imbalances by stabilizing membrane structures or regulating ion transporters, ensuring sufficient nutrient flux to developing shoots [32]. For instance, P-dependent proton pumps in root cells may enhance the uptake of remaining Mg2+ or other essential ions, indirectly supporting new shoot growth [52]. Our findings align with previous studies highlighting the role of P in alleviating nutrient stress. For example, increased P supply has been shown to improve growth under Zn or Fe deficiency by enhancing root exploration and nutrient mobilization [53, 54].

The present study also reveals that both low and high P levels disrupt the growth of navel orange plants under adequate Mg supply conditions. This growth disruption was associated with distinct physiological perturbations, specifically manifested in altered chlorophyll content and photosynthetic parameters in both old and new leaves (Figs. 3 and S1). These findings confirm the critical importance of P homeostasis for normal plant development and, importantly, reveal differential underlying pathways responsible for growth inhibition under low-P versus high-P conditions. Notably, the response to high-P application under Mg sufficiency differed markedly from the typical response observed under Mg deficiency. Specifically, while Mg deficiency often adversely affects older leaves, high-P treatment under adequate Mg did not reduce SPAD values (chlorophyll content) or photosynthetic capacity in old leaves. Its effects on new shoots were minor and potentially even beneficial. This pattern is consistent with the work of Niu et al. [32], who observed that the effect of high-P on directional primary root growth in Arabidopsis was more pronounced under low Mg than under high Mg. Importantly, these results collectively underscore that adequate Mg nutrition confers significant resilience against stress induced by P imbalance, particularly P excess. Moreover, the absence of synergistic oxidative damage in plants supplied with sufficient Mg further suggests that Mg sufficiency prevents P-overload toxicity, likely by enabling efficient antioxidant buffering mechanisms.

These findings underscore the significant risks associated with excessive P fertilization in Mg-deficient navel orange orchards. It is important to note, however, that this research was conducted under controlled pot conditions using young navel orange plants. Consequently, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results directly to mature trees in orchard settings, where complex soil dynamics and the demands of fruit production may alter nutrient interactions. While our data indicate that elevated P levels may transiently stimulate new shoot growth in young plants, this stimulation was consistently accompanied by accelerated senescence in older leaves. This premature senescence critically threatens the tree’s core photosynthetic capacity and essential nutrient reserves, as these are fundamental prerequisites for sustained fruit production over multiple seasons. Therefore, management strategies designed for orchards must prioritize achieving a balanced P and Mg input regimen. This specifically entails reducing P application in soils known to be Mg-deficient to mitigate potential antagonism, while simultaneously supplementing Mg to preserve the function and longevity of older leaves, particularly during critical phenological stages like fruit set and ripening. To build upon these insights and address the limitations of the current pot study, future research should first investigate the long-term impacts of accelerated old leaf senescence on fruit yield and quality in field-grown bearing trees. Additionally, elucidating the molecular basis underlying imbalanced mineral partitioning should be a priority, focusing on tissue-specific expression profiles of key nutrient transporters and senescence-associated genes under conditions of P and Mg stress in orchard environments. This molecular characterization could reveal crucial targets for developing strategies to improve nutrient use efficiency in commercial citrus production facing such nutritional imbalances.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that P availability critically modulates Mg deficiency responses in young navel orange plants, exerting opposing effects on different tissues. High P supply under Mg deficiency accelerated chlorophyll degradation, photosynthetic impairment, and oxidative damage in old leaves, leading to reduced biomass and early senescence. Conversely, under the same conditions, high P promoted new shoot biomass accumulation. This tissue-specific trade-off was mitigated by adequate Mg nutrition (Fig. 7). Collectively, the findings reveal that high P magnifies Mg-deficiency costs in aging organs while concurrently subsidizing new growth. From an agronomic perspective, this indicates that high-P inputs in orchards with suboptimal Mg status risk accelerating functional leaf loss and long-term canopy decline, despite potentially stimulating short-term shoot vigor. Therefore, ensuring adequate Mg supply is fundamental for sustainable citrus production, particularly when P fertilization is considered.
