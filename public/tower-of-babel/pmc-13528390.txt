Retrospective evaluation of a machine learning model to facilitate pharmacogenetic testing

ABSTRACT

Background

Many paediatric patients require medications to treat acute or chronic conditions. In fact, about half of all children and adolescents in Canada will require at least one prescription medication annually.1 Pharmacogenetics addresses genetic contributions to medication response including impacts on efficacy, toxicity and safety in individual patients and is a key step towards precision medicine.2 As our understanding of the genetic landscape and its impact on medication efficacy and toxicity has grown, indications for pharmacogenetic testing and associated clinical practice guidelines have accelerated over the last decade.3 However, testing is not yet standard for the majority of medications with pharmacogenetic guidance, and the number of such medications continues to grow.4 5

One of the challenges with pharmacogenetic testing is the time required between ordering the test and receipt of the results. Pharmacogenetic test results need to be available at the time the medication is initially prescribed to inform medication selection and to individualise the medication dose. To avoid ineffective or harmful dosing, knowledge of pharmacogenetic variants prior to the first administration of the medication is required. However, the time required between sample collection and receipt of pharmacogenetic results is typically 3–4 weeks.

New tools are needed to identify patients who will be prescribed a medication where pharmacogenetic testing may influence choice or dosing weeks before the medication is required given the long turnaround time for testing results. Early identification will allow test results to be available at the time the drug is prescribed.

We hypothesised that machine learning (ML) based on data in the electronic health records (EHR) can identify patients with a high probability of requiring a medication with pharmacogenetic implications in the future. Consequently, objectives were to describe the epidemiology of nine medications with pharmacogenetic guidance and frequency of pharmacogenetic testing and to develop a retrospective ML model to predict prescription of targeted medications within 3 and 6 months of admission.

Methods

The setting was The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids) in Toronto, Canada. SickKids is a large academic paediatric centre that has over 15 000 inpatient admissions per year. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board (REB) at SickKids. The need for informed consent and assent was waived by the REB, given the retrospective nature of the study. This study was conducted by Pediatric Real-world Evaluative Data sciences for Clinical Transformation (PREDICT), a deployment-aware ML programme at SickKids.

Selection of targeted medications with a pharmacogenetic indication

Specific medications where pharmacogenetic testing is indicated were identified and prioritised based on frequency of prescribing and level of evidence for modifying prescribing practices by two investigators with expertise in this area (IC and RHJV). Evidence for modifying prescribing practices was based on those offered by the Clinical Pharmacogenomics Implementation Consortium.3 Selection also considered medications used in the acute setting where there is typically insufficient time to arrange for pharmacogenetic testing and those where variants are associated with a high risk of toxicity or treatment failure. Drugs where testing is already uniformly performed at our institution prior to administration (eg, TPMT/NUDT15 gene testing performed with a targeted panel prior to thiopurine administration) were not considered. These preselected medications are referred to as ‘targeted medications’. The targeted medications selected were azathioprine, brivaracetam, clobazam, clopidogrel, flecainide, phenytoin, tacrolimus, voriconazole and warfarin. Only systemically administered (oral, subcutaneous, intramuscular or intravenous) medications or prescriptions (eg, not topical, intrathecal or intravitreal) were included. Phenytoin was only considered if given orally (to exclude emergency administration without a plan for ongoing treatment).

Data source

The data source was the SickKids Enterprise-wide Data in Azure Repository (SEDAR).6 SEDAR is a curated and validated data warehouse derived from the SickKids Epic Clarity database. It is suitable for developing and validating both retrospective and prospective ML models. For this study, we used SEDAR’s ‘Curated Schema’, which consolidates the SEDAR data into 18 usable tables. The data model is updated once daily.

Eligibility

Two cohorts were created: an epidemiology cohort and an ML cohort. The epidemiology cohort was used to describe the historic local practices as they relate to the prescribing of targeted medications and ordering of pharmacogenetic tests. The ML cohort was used to train and evaluate an ML model among inpatient admissions to predict targeted medication prescribing.

For the epidemiology cohort, all patients (both inpatients and outpatients) who received a targeted medication between 2 June 2018 and 16 July 2024 were included and the unit of analysis was at the order level. No patients were excluded from this cohort. For the ML cohort, we included all patients admitted to SickKids between 2 June 2018 and 13 January 2024. The end dates for the two cohorts differed because, for the ML cohort, we excluded the last 6 months of data used in the epidemiology cohort to account for censoring when labelling the longest prediction window. Each admission served as the unit of analysis and individual patients could be included in the cohort multiple times. This was felt to be appropriate as it mimics our intended deployment environment. Patients were excluded if they: (1) had prior pharmacogenetic testing performed, (2) had received a targeted medication previously, (3) were <6 months of age at admission, (4) were admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) or (5) died on or before prediction time (8:30 morning following admission). Patients <6 months or admitted to the NICU were excluded as most of the pharmacology genes of interest are not fully expressed early in life and interpretation is uncertain in this age group. To address the potential for biased performance by repeated admissions for the same patient, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis on the test set after excluding patients also present in the training or validation sets.

Epidemiology of targeted medication administration and pharmacogenomic testing

To describe the epidemiology of targeted medication administrations, both hospital administrations and outpatient prescriptions for the targeted medications were evaluated. The number of orders and the number of patients who received these medications were determined. Multiple prescriptions for the same medication were only counted once. From these patients, we also described those who had comprehensive pharmacogenetic testing performed and whether testing occurred before or after the first medication order. Pharmacogenetic testing was defined as comprehensive pharmacogenetic panel testing and not single variant testing.

ML model development and evaluation

The outcomes (or labels) of the prediction were two binary (yes/no) variables indicating if at least one targeted medication was prescribed within a 3-month or a 6-month period postprediction time (ie, the prediction window). The prediction time was 8:30 AM on the morning after admission as clinical decisions are typically made during morning rounds. Three-month and 6-month prediction windows were selected to allow sufficient time for clinicians to order and receive test results before they are needed for prescribing decisions.

The PREDICT programme uses a standardised pipeline that has been previously described.7 In brief, the pipeline starts with extraction of features such as demographic information and clinical observations from SEDAR. Data are binned into three time intervals prior to prediction time: 0–1 days, 1–7 days and prior to 7 days. Data are excluded if less than 25 observations exist, or if no observations exist in the last 90 days of the dataset. Categorical elements, such as medication administrations, are counted within each interval. For continuous variables such as heart rate, we compute the mean. For measurements recorded more than two times on average across patients, we also compute the minimum and maximum within each time interval.

The data are split temporally into training (80%), validation (10%) and test sets (10%). Data preprocessing includes standardising age (and count values, for logistic regression models only) and transforming measurement features into quintiles before one-hot encoding. The final output is a large sparse feature matrix. All preprocessing steps are fit on the training set and applied to the validation and test sets.

The training set is used to train L2-regularised logistic regression using Sci-kit Learn8 and gradient boosting machines (GBM) using LightGBM9 and XGBoost.7 For hyperparameter selection, fivefold cross-validation is performed optimising for area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). For feature reduction, we recursively remove feature groups as previously described, except in the LightGBM models, where we use split-based importance for feature selection.10 The model architecture (logistic regression, LightGBM or XGBoost) with the best AUROC in the validation set is then selected for each prediction window (3 months and 6 months). Threshold-dependent metrics (such as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value) and threshold-independent metrics (such as AUROC) are then described in the held-out test set. The 95% bootstrapped CIs are derived using 2000 bootstrapping iterations.

ML model threshold selection and fairness evaluation

Clinical champions (IC and RHJV) were presented with three threshold options for the selected model and asked to choose their preferred threshold. The threshold is the cut-point used to define positive and negative predictions. These thresholds reflected three optimisation strategies on the validation set: maximising true predictions or achieving number needed to alert values of 2 or 3. For each threshold, champions were presented with threshold-dependent metrics and total number of alerts per month. Selecting a threshold requires champions to define the intervention triggered by a positive prediction, consider the resources needed to implement it and weigh the impact of both false negatives and false positives. The PREDICT team (APY, SEA and LS) facilitated a discussion with the clinical champions to help them select a threshold that reflected their preferences, values and available resources. The proposed intervention for a positive alert would be for the clinical pharmacology team to approach the patient or their family to request consent for comprehensive pharmacogenetic testing. The two champions independently selected a threshold and then reached consensus through iterative discussion with the PREDICT team.

Fairness evaluation (addressing algorithmic bias) was conducted across sex, age group,11 non-English language flag, neighbourhood income quintile and the four dimensions of the Canadian Index of Multiple Deprivation based on the 2021 census12: residential instability, economic dependency, situational vulnerability and ethnocultural composition. The clinical champions determined if there were concerns with these analyses.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Machine learning model

A consort diagram of patient inclusion in the ML aim is shown as figure 2. Demographic characteristics of the cohort are presented in table 1; 57 368 admissions were included. Among these admissions, 1784 (3.1%) had a targeted medication prescribed within 3 months, and 2293 (4.0%) had a targeted medication prescribed within 6 months of the admission.

Consort diagram showing inclusion and exclusion of patients in the retrospective machine learning model to predict targeted medication ordering. NICU, neonatal intensive care unit.

The LightGBM architecture was the best-performing model in the validation set for the 3-month and 6-month models. Hyperparameters for the selected 3-month model are available in online supplemental appendix 2. For the 3-month model, recursive feature selection decreased the number of features from 54 301 to 7851, and the AUROC was 0.926 (95% CI 0.911 to 0.939). The area-under-the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) was 0.477 (95% CI 0.462 to 0.495). For the 6-month model, recursive feature selection decreased the number of features from 54 301 to 5068 and the AUROC was 0.922 (95% CI 0.911 to 0.932). The AUPRC was 0.450 (95% CI 0.411 to 0.494).

Table 3 presents model metrics for the three threshold determination approaches. At this point, the champions and the PREDICT team chose to focus on the 3-month model because of the shortened period required for prospective validation. The selected threshold approach aimed at optimising true detections. For the 3-month prediction window model, the PPV was 0.36 and specificity was 0.97. At this threshold, 36 alerts would be triggered per month across the hospital, representing an additional workload deemed manageable by the pharmacogenetics team. The false-positive results generated were felt to be acceptable due to the potential that these patients might receive a medication with a pharmacogenetics indication at another time point beyond the 3-month window evaluated, or receive a pharmacogenetically-guided medication not on our targeted medication list. Among the false-positive group, 21/155 received a targeted medication after the 3-month time period. Clinical champions then reviewed the fairness evaluation stratified by sociodemographic characteristics at this threshold (online supplemental appendix 3). No concerns with the fairness evaluation were identified by the clinical champions that would impede future clinical implementation. Online supplemental appendix 4 presents the feature importance of the top 20 features. In the sensitivity analysis, we excluded patients from the test set who were present in the training or validation sets for the 3-month prediction window. This resulted in a test set comprised of 3825 samples with the following performance metrics that were similar to the initial approach: AUROC 0.915 (95% CI 0.889 to 0.939), AUPRC 0.404 (95% CI 0.307 to 0.507), PPV 0.35 and specificity 0.98.

Discussion

In this study, we found that only 1% of patients receiving a targeted medication, where pharmacogenetic testing could influence initial selection or dosing, underwent testing prior to medication ordering. We were successful in developing an ML model to predict ordering of a targeted medication in the next 3 months with an AUROC of 0.926. This is a novel application of ML that can facilitate precision child health.

Using historical data on pharmacogenetic testing and targeted medication prescribing at our institution, only 12.2% of patients for whom testing was ordered went on to receive a targeted medication. In contrast, with our model, 36.5% of predictions corresponded to patients who were prescribed a targeted medication within 3 months. The greater performance of our model compared with historical clinician performance suggests that our model would be an effective tool to ensure that the patients most likely to benefit from pharmacogenetic testing receive access to this service. Notably, only 1% of patients prescribed one of these medications had testing performed prior to the initial order. There is clearly room to improve this metric and ML is a promising approach.

Our finding that we can develop an ML model to predict future receipt of specific drugs is consistent with prior studies evaluating the use of ML for predicting receipt of a drug in different settings. For example, Rough et al. trained an ML model on hospital inpatients to predict patient-specific medication orders and found that 55% of medications ordered were ranked in the model's top 10 predictions.13 Similarly, models have been designed to predict future benzodiazepine prescriptions with good accuracy.14 In both of these studies, there is not a clearly delineated plan for how clinical teams would use these predictions to make meaningful changes to a patient’s care. In contrast, we have designed our prediction task to align with a known gap in clinical care, the ability to identify patients needing pharmacogenetic testing prior to prescribing of a medication with pharmacogenetic guidance.

The setup of our ML framework underestimates potential clinical benefits. Given that we perform a single comprehensive pharmacogenetic panel, it is possible that some patients identified as false positives will receive medications where pharmacogenetic testing would be useful but were not included on our targeted medication list. Second, the model only predicts medication orders within the next 3 months. Some of the false positives will go on to receive targeted medications beyond this 3-month period and thus may also benefit from pharmacogenomic testing. These considerations led champions to accept a PPV of 0.36, recognising that false positives could still benefit from testing with minimal downside. The AUPRC was also relatively modest although reasonable given the low prevalence of positive labels as a random classifier is expected to have an AUPRC equal to the prevalence of the positive outcome (0.03).

Future work will include conducting a prospective silent trial of the best-performing retrospective model utilising the threshold selected by clinical champions in this work.15 If the model maintains performance during the silent trial, we plan to deploy the model prospectively and evaluate its performance in a clinical trial. In addition, to ensure that the model is integrated effectively into clinical workflows, we plan to conduct usability testing of various clinical decision support tools to facilitate delivery of predictions and ordering of the required pharmacogenomic testing.

A strength of this study is that the model was developed in a deployment-aware framework, allowing for future prospective evaluation and deployment. In addition, most ML-related work in paediatrics has used a small number of discrete data elements, which limits the performance. This model leveraged all data available in the EHR at the time of prediction, allowing us to harness the richness and complexity of longitudinally collected data. The majority of ML models in paediatrics have focused on a limited number of prediction tasks, such as sepsis,16 clinical deterioration,17 acute care visits18 and mortality.19 To our knowledge, this is the first ML model developed to support pharmacogenomic test ordering.

This study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, we did not evaluate all possible medications with pharmacogenomic guidance, but instead chose to focus on a targeted group of high-yield medications. Although champions with pharmacogenetic expertise selected these medications, some subjectivity is involved, and their choices may not reflect the priorities of all institutions for pharmacogenetic testing. Furthermore, this list will likely change over time. Second, the model makes a single prediction per admission in order to minimise alert burden for clinical teams. However, given dynamic changes in a patient’s status over the course of an admission, a prediction later in a patient’s admission may be more informative of the patient’s risk of requiring a targeted medication. Finally, the model predicts receipt of a medication within the next 3 months, and it is possible that some patients will receive the medication prior to the pharmacogenetic testing results being available.

In summary, only 1% of patients receiving a targeted medication had pharmacogenetic testing before the medication order. We developed an ML model with acceptable performance to predict targeted medication administration with the goal of facilitating earlier pharmacogenomic testing. Prospective evaluation is required to validate its performance and to evaluate its impact on pharmacogenomic test ordering once deployed clinically.
