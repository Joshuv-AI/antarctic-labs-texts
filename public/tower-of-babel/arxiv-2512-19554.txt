Care What Fails: Contrastive Anchored‑REflection for Verifiable Multimodal Reasoning
Yongxin Wang, Zhicheng Yang, Meng Cao, Mingfei Han, Haokun Lin, Yingying Zhu, Xiaojun Chang, Xiaodan Liang

Abstract
Group-relative reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) often wastes the most informative data it already has—the failures. When all rollouts are wrong, gradients stall; when one happens to be correct, the update usually ignores why the others are close-but-wrong, and credit can be misassigned to spurious chains. We present CARE ( Contrastive Anchored REflection ), a failure-centric post-training framework for multimodal reasoning that turns errors into supervision. CARE combines: (i) an anchored-contrastive objective that forms a compact subgroup around the best rollout and a set of semantically proximate hard negatives, performs within-subgroup z z -score normalization with negative-only scaling, and includes an all-negative rescue to prevent zero-signal batches; and (ii) Reflection-Guided Resampling (RGR), a one-shot structured self-repair that rewrites a representative failure and re-scores it with the same verifier, converting near-misses into usable positives without any test-time reflection. CARE improves accuracy and training smoothness while explicitly increasing the share of learning signal that comes from failures. On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, CARE lifts macro-averaged accuracy by 4.6 points over GRPO across six verifiable visual-reasoning benchmarks; with Qwen3-VL-8B it reaches competitive or state-of-the-art results on MathVista and MMMU-Pro under an identical evaluation protocol.

Overview of
Care
.
Given a multimodal prompt, the policy samples a fixed number of rollouts.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have advanced text comprehension, generation, and reasoning, catalyzing the development of Large Multimodal Models (MLLMs) that integrate vision and language. Early MLLMs (e.g., GPT-4V
(
27
)
, LLaVA
(
18
)
) demonstrate strong abilities in captioning and conversational visual-question answering (VQA), but the frontier has shifted toward complex, compositional reasoning in domains such as mathematics, science, and engineering.
Recent benchmarks MathVista
(
22
)
, MathVerse
(
49
)
, MATH-Vision
(
38
)
, and MMMU
(
25
)
reveal persistent gaps: while strong systems can exceed 70–80% on
MathVista
, errors concentrate on visually grounded, compositional reasoning, underscoring that outcome‑only supervision still provides weak guidance for long‑horizon reasoning.

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) offers a promising alternative by using programmatic verifiers (e.g., answer checkers) to provide deterministic, scalable rewards
(
26
;
7
)
. Group-based methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
33
)
make RLVR practical by comparing multiple rollouts per query
(
33
)
. However, two limitations remain salient under small rollout budgets: (1) high gradient variance and training instability, (2) flawed credit assignment, which can reinforce spurious chains of reasoning when a correct answer is reached by chance.

We introduce
CARE
(
Contrastive Anchored REflection
), a verifier-driven post-training framework that is explicitly failure-centric. CARE turns informative errors into learning signal in two complementary ways. First, an
anchored-contrastive objective
treats the best rollout as an anchor and normalizes rewards
within
a curated subgroup of hard negatives, yielding a stable, scale-aware contrast that teaches the policy to separate correct solutions from plausible failures while assigning credit in a fail-aware manner. Second, a lightweight
Reflection-Guided Resampling
step performs a one-shot, error-corrective resample on a selected hard negative whenever a group contains a successful rollout, so representative mistakes are actively converted into improvements rather than passively ignored.

We validate on MathVista, MathVerse, MATH-Vision, and MMMU-Pro. Under identical inference-time budgets,
CARE
achieves state-of-the-art performance on MathVista and MMMU-Pro, and consistently surpasses strong RLVR baselines such as GRPO
33
, DAPO
(
44
)
and GSPO
(
50
)
on all benchmarks, indicating a stable and effective path to training reliable multimodal reasoners.

Contributions.
Our contributions are threefold:

•

Anchored-contrastive objective.
We anchor advantages to the best rollout and normalize
within
a subgroup of hard negatives, producing a stable, contrastive, and scale-aware learning signal with improved, fail-aware credit assignment.

•

Reflection-Guided Resampling.
We introduce a single-shot guided resample on a selected hard negative in success-containing groups, turning representative failures into improvements rather than discarding them.

•

Empirical gains on visual reasoning.
CARE sets new SOTA on MathVista and MMMU-Pro and delivers consistent improvements on MathVerse and MATH-Vision over established RLVR baselines.

Multimodal Large Reasoning Models

Methodology

Overview.

We optimize under
verifiable
rewards while keeping test-time decoding unchanged (
single decode, no reflection
).

CARE
combines an anchored-contrastive objective with a
training-only
reflection step when a success exists.
(1) An
anchored-contrastive objective
anchors sequence-level advantages to the best rollout and normalizes them within a hard-negative subgroup, with negative-penalty scaling and an all-negative rescue.
(2)
Reflection-Guided Resampling
(RGR) augments training by selecting one hard negative, inserting a brief repair cue into its rationale, and resampling exactly one reflected response. The policy is then updated on the augmented group; reflected successes replace the original failure, and reflected failures receive a reduced penalty. An overview is shown in

3.1
Problem Setting and Notation

We study verifiable multimodal reasoning over inputs
x=\langle\mathbf{I},\mathbf{q}\rangle
, where
\mathbf{I}
denotes one or more images and
\mathbf{q}
denotes a text query.
The policy
\pi_{\theta}[y\mid x]
emits
<think>

\hat{r}
</think>
followed by
<answer>

\hat{y}
</answer>
.
A programmatic verifier
\mathcal{V}
parses only the answer span and returns two bounded signals
\mathrm{acc}[x,y]\in[0,1]
and
\mathrm{fmt}[x,y]\in[0,1]
.
We define the overall reward

r_{i}\;=\;[1-\lambda]\cdot\mathrm{acc}[x,y_{i}]\;+\;\lambda\cdot\mathrm{fmt}[x,y_{i}],\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda\in[0,1].

(1)

For each prompt we draw
G
rollouts
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{G}\}
with rewards
r_{i}=r[y_{i}]
.
Let
T_{i}
be the total number of response tokens,
T_{i}^{\text{ans}}
those inside
<answer>...</answer>
, and
T_{i}^{\text{think}}
those inside
<think>...</think>
.
We denote by
\pi_{\text{old}}
the frozen sampling policy for the current batch, and by
\pi_{\text{ref}}
a fixed reference policy used only for KL regularization.

3.2
CARE
: Anchored-Contrastive Subgroup

Anchor.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\,i:\ \mathrm{acc}[x,y_{i}]=1\,\}
be the positive set under the verifier.
If
\mathcal{P}\neq\varnothing
, choose the anchor

y^{+}\;=\;\arg\min_{i\in\mathcal{P}}\,T_{i}^{\text{think}},\vskip-8.53581pt

(2)

breaking ties toward shorter answers
T_{i}^{\text{ans}}
.
If
\mathcal{P}=\varnothing
, anchoring is bypassed and the group is handled by the all-negative rescue in
Section

3.7
.

Hard negatives.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the hard negatives returned by the selector in
Section

3.3
.
When the number of negatives is fewer than the target subgroup size
K
, we shrink to fit:

\displaystyle K^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\,\min\!\bigl(K,\ |\{i:\ \mathrm{acc}[x,y_{i}]=0\}|\bigr),

(3)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\,\{y^{+}\}\ \cup\ \{y^{-}_{1},\ldots,y^{-}_{K^{\prime}}\}.

If
K^{\prime}=0
, we skip the update to avoid arbitrary drift under saturated rewards.

3.3
Negative Selection

We seek negatives that are
close in rationale
yet wrong in outcome, so the contrast focuses on near misses rather than heterogeneous error types.
We therefore use verifier correctness only as a binary gate and rank failures by cosine proximity to the anchor.
Let the failure pool be
\mathcal{F}=\{\,i:\ \mathrm{acc}[x,y_{i}]=0\,\}
.
For each
i\in\mathcal{F}
, form a rationale embedding by mean-pooling final-layer hidden states over the
<think>
span and
\ell_{2}
-normalizing; denote the anchor’s embedding by
\tilde{h}_{+}
.
We do
not
backpropagate through these embeddings (stop-gradient).

d_{\cos}(i)\;=\;1-\tilde{h}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{h}_{+}.\vskip-2.84526pt

(4)

We rank
\mathcal{F}
by ascending
d_{\cos}
and take the top
K^{\prime}
items as hard negatives.
To curb redundancy, we first preselect the top
M\!>\!K^{\prime}
nearest and apply a brief farthest-first pass within this subset before returning the final
K^{\prime}
; hyperparameter details for
M
are provided in the Appendix.
The selected set supplies the subgroup
S
used by the within-group normalization in Eqs. (
5
)–(
7
) and the negative considered for reflection in
Section

3.6
.

3.4
Group-Normalized Advantages

Within-group normalization.

Given the current subgroup
S
, define

\displaystyle\mu_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{y\in S}r[y],

(5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{S}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{y\in S}\bigl(r[y]-\mu_{S}\bigr)^{2}}\;+\;\varepsilon.

(6)

We first form
raw
(pre-scaling) sequence-level advantages

A_{\mathrm{raw}}[y]\,=\,\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{r[y]-\mu_{S}}{\sigma_{S}},&y\in S,\\[6.0pt]
0,&y\notin S.\end{cases}

(7)

A robust variant replaces
\mu_{S}
by a trimmed mean and
\sigma_{S}
by a median absolute deviation with the same
\varepsilon
.

Negative-penalty scaling and update-size equalization.

To mitigate over-sharpening we down-weight only negatives and leave the anchor unchanged:

\displaystyle\forall\,y_{j}^{-}\in S:\hskip 10.00002ptA[y_{j}^{-}]

\displaystyle\leftarrow-\,s\,\bigl\lvert A_{\mathrm{raw}}[y_{j}^{-}]\bigr\rvert,

(8)

\displaystyle\phantom{\forall\,y_{j}^{-}\in S:\hskip 10.00002pt}A[y^{+}]

\displaystyle\leftarrow A_{\mathrm{raw}}[y^{+}].

with
s\in(0,1]
.
When
K^{\prime}\!<\!K
, a global rescale
A\leftarrow\sqrt{K/K^{\prime}}\,A
equalizes update magnitude across groups of different
K^{\prime}
without changing direction.

Mechanistic signature.

Assume a near two-level pattern inside
S
with one anchor at
r^{+}
and negatives around
m_{-}
, and let
\Delta=r^{+}-m_{-}>0
.
z-score normalization yields

A_{\mathrm{raw}}[y^{+}]\approx\alpha\,\sqrt{K^{\prime}},\hskip 20.00003pt\overline{A}_{\mathrm{raw}}[y_{j}^{-}]\approx-\,\alpha\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{K^{\prime}}},

(9)

with attenuation
\alpha=\bigl(1+\tfrac{(K^{\prime}+1)\operatorname{Var}_{-}}{\Delta^{2}}\bigr)^{-1/2}\in(0,1]
.
For strictly binary rewards and
\Delta=1
this reduces to

A_{\mathrm{raw}}[y^{+}]\,=\,\sqrt{K^{\prime}},\hskip 20.00003ptA_{\mathrm{raw}}[y_{j}^{-}]\,=\,-\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{K^{\prime}}}.

(10)

Note.
The
final
advantages used by the optimizer are obtained from
A_{\mathrm{raw}}
by applying negative-penalty scaling
s
(negatives only) and, when
K^{\prime}\!<\!K

\sqrt{K/K^{\prime}}
. The proof is shown in Appendix
Section

7

3.5
Token-Weighted Policy Objective

Let
\rho_{i,t}=\exp\!\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}[y_{i,t}\mid x]-\log\pi_{\text{old}}[y_{i,t}\mid x]\bigr)

Define region weights
w_{i,t}
as follows:
tokens inside the answer span receive weight
1

tokens inside the rationale span receive weight
\gamma^{+}
if

is a positive sample and
0
if
i
is a negative sample.
Normalize per-token advantages

a_{i,t}\;=\;A[y_{i}]\cdot\frac{w_{i,t}}{\sum_{u=1}^{T_{i}}w_{i,u}+\epsilon_{w}}.

(11)

We use
\gamma^{+}=0.005
in all main results, and set
\epsilon_{w},\varepsilon>0
as small constants for numerical stability.
The clipped surrogate is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{|S^{\star}|}\sum_{i\in S^{\star}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\min\Bigl\{\rho_{i,t}\,a_{i,t},

(12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{clip}\bigl(\rho_{i,t},\,1-\epsilon_{\text{low}},\,1+\epsilon_{\text{high}}\bigr)\,a_{i,t}\Bigr\},

where
S^{\star}=\{\,i\,:\,A[y_{i}]\neq 0\,\}
.
We add a regularizer

\mathcal{L}\,=\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{PG}}\;+\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr].

3.6
Reflection-Guided Resampling

RGR augments the anchored-contrastive training when a verifier-positive anchor exists.
Select exactly one positive
y^{+}
and one hard negative
y^{-}
according to
Section

3.3
.
Insert a brief repair cue inside the rationale span and resample:

<think>

Your previous reasoning was incorrect. Identify the failing operation, correct it, and re-derive the necessary quantities.

Keep this section concise. Do not restate the question.

</think>

<answer> FINAL_ANSWER_HERE </answer>

Decode exactly one additional rollout for
y^{-}
with the same decoding hyperparameters and rescore it with the same verifier.
Run the anchored-contrastive update on the augmented group and apply two safeguards:
if the reflected sample succeeds, replace the original failed rollout from the subgroup;
if it fails, keep it as a negative and use a reduced negative-penalty scaling for that item (use
Eq.

8
with a smaller factor
s_{\text{refl}}\in(0,s]
).
RGR is disabled in all–negative groups.

Default settings.
We set
s{=}0.5
and
s_{\mathrm{refl}}{=}s/2
across all main results.

3.7
All-Negative Rescue with Partial Balance

If
\max_{i}r_{i}
is near zero, the group-relative gradient nearly vanishes.
We inject a small zero-sum pseudo-contrast on a subset
S=\{t\}\cup\mathcal{N}
where
t
is a pseudo-anchor (chosen as the failure with the highest
\log\pi_{\text{old}}(y\!\mid\!x)
) and
\mathcal{N}
contains
K^{\prime}=\min(K,|\mathcal{F}|)
representative negatives.
On
S
only, assign

\displaystyle r^{\prime}[t]

\displaystyle=\delta,

(14)

\displaystyle r^{\prime}[j]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta}{K^{\prime}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }j\in\mathcal{N},

\displaystyle r^{\prime}[u]

\displaystyle=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }u\notin S.

Then compute
Eq.

7
on
S
using
r^{\prime}
, apply
Eq.

8
to negatives, and proceed with
Eqs.

11
,
12
and
13
.
This reproduces
Eq.

Default magnitude.
We fix the pseudo-contrast to
\delta{=}0.1
for all main experiments.

5
Conclusion

We recast verifiable‑reward post‑training as
learning from failures
. Under an unchanged single‑decode evaluation protocol,
CARE
turns near‑miss errors into supervision through three lightweight ingredients: (i) an anchored, subgroup‑normalized contrast with negative‑only scaling that separates correct solutions from plausible failures; (ii) a
training‑only
, success‑conditioned Reflection‑Guided Resampling that attempts a single structured repair on a representative hard negative; and (iii) an all‑negative, zero‑sum rescue together with region‑weighted token credit. This design preserves inference cost while stabilizing group‑relative updates.

Across MathVista, MathVerse, MATH‑Vision, MMMU, and MMMU‑Pro,
CARE
improves exact‑match accuracy and training smoothness—e.g., +4.62 macro points over GRPO on Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B—and attains the strongest numbers in our reproducible setting on MathVista and MMMU‑Pro with Qwen3‑VL‑8B. We defer limitations and future directions to Appendix
Section

10
.

10
Future Directions and Limitations

10.1
Limitations

While
CARE
demonstrates strong robustness on verifiable reasoning tasks, its current reliance on binary correctness signals restricts its primary application to domains with objective ground truths, such as mathematics and coding, leaving the extension to open-ended generation contingent on the development of reliable reward models. Additionally, although the all-negative rescue mechanism effectively mitigates gradient collapse in sparse-reward scenarios, the framework operates most efficiently when the policy can discover at least one successful rollout to anchor the contrastive update, implying that extremely hard tasks with near-zero pass rates may still benefit from initial warm-up supervision. Finally, our heuristic preference for the shortest successful rationale is a deliberate design choice to optimize inference efficiency; while this successfully curbs verbosity, it entails a trade-off that may theoretically discourage extended reasoning chains in specific edge cases where exhaustive self-verification is strictly necessary.

10.2
Future Directions

Step‑level verification and credit.

Augment the reward with
intermediate
verifiers that check sub‑goals (e.g., variable extraction, unit conversion, geometric equivalences), enabling token‑ or span‑level advantages on
<think>
without relying on a global scalar.

Multi‑anchor or anchor ensembles.

Replace the single anchor with a
set
of diverse positives (e.g., by clustering successful rationales) and distribute positive credit across them.
This can mitigate brevity bias, preserve solution diversity, and improve stability on multi‑modal prompts with several correct styles.

Learned negative selection.

Move beyond cosine heuristics by training a selector that predicts “fixability” or causal proximity to the anchor (e.g., via contrastive learning on success/failure edits).
Hybrid selectors could balance semantic proximity with coverage of distinct error families.

Adaptive subgroup sizing and scaling.

Learn
K^{\prime}
and the negative scaling
s
from data (or schedule them dynamically) to maintain a target signal‑to‑noise ratio inside the subgroup.
This could reduce sensitivity to class imbalance and reward variance across prompts.
