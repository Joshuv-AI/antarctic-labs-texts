Translational prioritization of strategies to overcome poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitor resistance in breast cancer based on systematic review of preclinical evidence
Authors: Kezhen Shen, Lei Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResistance to poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors has emerged as a major obstacle to durable clinical benefit in breast cancer. While multiple preclinical strategies have been proposed, there is a lack of translational frameworks that prioritize interventions based on mechanistic robustness, biomarker relevance, and strength of evidence.MethodsWe performed a systematic and evidence-informed synthesis of 22 preclinical studies investigating therapeutic approaches to overcome PARP inhibitor resistance. Studies were evaluated across experimental models, mechanistic endpoints, and therapeutic outcomes. Risk of bias was assessed using the SYRCLE and ROBINS-I tools, and the consistency and certainty of evidence were appraised using the GRADE framework to support translational prioritization.ResultsEpigenetic targeting via EZH2 inhibition and immune microenvironment modulation through STING pathway activation consistently demonstrated high-quality evidence for tumor suppression, survival benefit, and immunologic activation in BRCA1-mutant models. In contrast, strategies aimed at restoring homologous recombination exhibited strong in vitro synergy but limited in vivo translatability, underscoring pharmacokinetic and model-dependent constraints.ConclusionsThis review advances an evidence-based translational prioritization framework to guide the clinical development of combination strategies for PARP inhibitor–resistant breast cancer. We identify critical gaps in biomarker integration, standardized resistance modeling—particularly in BRCA wild-type disease–and dynamic monitoring of resistance evolution, and propose key directions for future preclinical and early-phase clinical research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05288-3.Keywords: Breast cancer, Poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitor, Drug resistance, Preclinical strategies, Evidence grading

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide, accounting for approximately 15% of newly diagnosed cancer cases [1]. Despite advances in systemic therapy, therapeutic resistance continues to limit durable clinical benefit, particularly in aggressive subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).

Poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors have reshaped the treatment landscape for patients with homologous recombination–deficient breast cancer, especially those harboring BRCA1/2 mutations. By exploiting synthetic lethality, PARP inhibitors selectively target DNA repair–deficient tumor cells while sparing normal tissues, offering improved specificity and a more favorable toxicity profile compared with conventional chemotherapy [2, 3]. Beyond monotherapy, increasing evidence suggests that PARP inhibitors can be rationally combined with epigenetic modulators or immunotherapeutic agents to enhance antitumor efficacy [4].

However, the clinical durability of PARP inhibitor therapy is frequently undermined by acquired resistance. Preclinical studies have identified multiple resistance mechanisms, including restoration of homologous recombination repair, rewiring of cell cycle checkpoints, activation of drug efflux pathways, and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment [5, 6]. In response, a growing number of preclinical investigations have proposed diverse resistance-reversal strategies, ranging from epigenetic regulation to immune activation and metabolic intervention.

Although several narrative reviews have summarized individual resistance mechanisms, a critical gap remains in the systematic evaluation of the translational strength of these proposed strategies. Existing reviews rarely assess the consistency, robustness, and methodological quality of preclinical evidence, making it difficult to distinguish approaches with genuine clinical potential from those supported by fragmented or low-quality data.

To address this unmet need, we conducted a systematic review of 22 preclinical studies investigating strategies to overcome PARP inhibitor resistance in breast cancer. Following PRISMA guidelines, we applied the Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) and Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tools to assess risk of bias and, importantly, used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) framework to classify the certainty of evidence across resistance-reversal strategies [7]. This review aims to identify preclinical strategies with the strongest and most consistent evidence for clinical translation, evaluate the convergent roles of epigenetic regulation and immune modulation, and highlight methodological limitations that currently hinder effective bench-to-bedside translation. By integrating evidence grading with mechanistic interpretation, this study provides a translationally oriented framework to guide biomarker-driven combination therapy development.

Methods

We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase in March 2025 for studies published between January 2015 and March 2025. The retrieval strategy was developed in advance and combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms), truncation symbols, and free-text words in titles and abstracts, with logical operators (AND/OR) used to refine the results. The search covered terms related to breast cancer, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), PARP inhibitors such as olaparib, talazoparib, rucaparib, and niraparib, as well as concepts of drug resistance, sensitization, synthetic lethality, and resistance reversal in preclinical models (in vitro and in vivo, including xenografts). The complete search strings for PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase are provided in Supplementary File 1. A total of 118 records were initially retrieved. After deduplication, 88 studies remained for title and abstract screening, of which 60 were excluded for not meeting eligibility requirements. Full-text review was conducted for 28 articles, and six were removed due to issues such as lack of breast cancer data, absence of resistance-reversal evaluation, or unavailable full text. Finally, 22 studies fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were synthesized in the analysis (Fig. 1). The review process followed the 2020 PRISMA statement [8]. The study protocol was registered on INPLASY (DOI: 10.37766/inplasy2025.4.0029). No amendments were made to the registered protocol after its initial submission.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria. We considered original research articles published in English between 2015 and 2025. Eligible studies were required to evaluate strategies designed to overcome resistance to PARP inhibitors in breast cancer models. Both in vitro (cell culture) and in vivo (animal-based) studies were included, provided that they assessed the efficacy of reversal approaches such as pharmacologic combinations, genetic interventions, metabolic modulation, or immune-based strategies. Only investigations reporting at least one measurable endpoint—such as half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), apoptosis induction, clonogenic survival, DNA repair markers, or tumor growth inhibition—were included. To ensure methodological rigor, eligible studies were expected to incorporate an appropriate control group (e.g., PARP inhibitor monotherapy) and provide mechanistic insight into resistance-reversal pathways. Only full-length peer-reviewed articles were considered.

Exclusion criteria. We excluded studies that did not specifically focus on breast cancer or did not evaluate interventions for reversing PARP inhibitor resistance. Publications restricted to mechanistic descriptions without testing a reversal strategy were not eligible. Research limited to other tumor types, computational modeling without experimental validation, or bioinformatics-only analyses was excluded. Clinical studies, reviews, guidelines, case reports, conference abstracts, and non–peer-reviewed articles were also not considered. Additional exclusion criteria included insufficient quantitative data, lack of appropriate outcome measures, absence of a valid control group, or unavailability of the full text. Non-English publications were excluded as well.

Review process

All records retrieved from the initial database search were first screened by title and abstract. Duplicates and irrelevant studies were removed. Full texts of the remaining articles were then assessed in detail against the predefined eligibility criteria. Two reviewers (KZS and LC) independently evaluated all articles during both the title/abstract and full-text screening stages, with disagreements resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved. Data extraction was performed by one reviewer (KZS) using a standardized Excel template and independently verified by another reviewer (LC) for accuracy and completeness. Although this verification process minimized potential errors, it did not constitute fully independent duplicate extraction and may therefore introduce minor extraction bias.

Data Extraction. Key study characteristics and outcomes were systematically extracted into a pre-designed Microsoft Excel form, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines (Table 1). Extracted information included: first author’s name, publication year, study design, breast cancer subtype, experimental system (cell lines and/or animal models), type of intervention and comparator, outcome measures (IC50, apoptosis rates, DNA damage biomarkers, clonogenic survival, or in vivo tumor growth inhibition), statistical significance, and risk-of-bias assessments. Study quality indicators—such as randomization procedures, allocation concealment, blinding, and control group design—were also recorded.

Risk of Bias Assessment. The risk of bias in the included in vivo studies was assessed using an adapted SYRCLE framework.

This adapted tool comprised eight domains relevant to preclinical oncology studies: randomization, baseline characteristics, allocation concealment, blinding, data integrity, appropriate control group, statistical methods, and outcome consistency (Table SI). Each domain was scored as 1 (low risk of bias) or 0 (high or unclear risk of bias), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 8. Higher scores indicate lower risk of bias. This scoring system allowed consistent descriptive comparison of methodological quality across studies and has been used in previous preclinical systematic reviews [9]. For in vitro experiments, risk of bias was evaluated using the ROBINS-I framework (Table SII), which examines confounding, study design, missing data, and measurement accuracy [10]. Each study was assessed independently, and categorizations of low, moderate, or high risk of bias were made according to predefined standards. High-quality studies demonstrated clear randomization (for in vivo models), well-defined control groups, complete outcome reporting, and robust statistical analyses. Studies with unclear randomization, inadequate blinding, incomplete outcome data, or evidence of selective reporting were classified as having a moderate or high risk of bias. The risk of bias assessments were recorded and managed using Microsoft Excel 2019. No automated software was used for SYRCLE or ROBINS-I scoring; all assessments were performed manually by two independent reviewers.

Evidence Grading Assessment. The overall strength of evidence for each intervention strategy was evaluated using the GRADE system (Table SIII) [11]. Evidence quality was initially rated as high for rigorously conducted in vivo experiments and moderate for in vitro data, with potential downgrading based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, or suspected publication bias. Conversely, upgrades were permitted for studies demonstrating large effect sizes, clear dose–response patterns, or strong biological plausibility. Final GRADE ratings were classified as high, moderate, low, or very low. High-quality evidence indicated strong confidence in the validity of the findings, while low-quality evidence suggested greater uncertainty and potential limitations in study design or reproducibility. The GRADE evidence profiling was performed using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool (GDT), available at https://gradepro.org. The certainty of evidence was rated independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion.

Synthesis Methods. Meta-analysis was not performed in this review due to substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. Differences existed in experimental model systems (cell-based assays, patient-derived xenografts, genetically engineered mouse models, and other in vivo platforms), genotypes (e.g., BRCA1/2 status and additional genomic alterations), dosing regimens, and outcome measurements (cell viability, DNA damage markers, tumor growth inhibition, and survival endpoints). Such methodological and biological variability precluded meaningful quantitative pooling. Instead, a structured narrative synthesis was conducted, and the GRADE framework was applied to summarize the strength and consistency of evidence for each therapeutic strategy.

Assessment of Publication Bias. Formal statistical tests for publication bias (such as Egger’s test or funnel plot analysis) were not applicable because the included studies were heterogeneous in design, intervention type, and outcome measurement, and quantitative pooling was not performed. Instead, a structured qualitative assessment was conducted, considering the distribution of reported outcomes (positive versus negative findings), sample size, study design quality, and sponsorship information. This approach enabled identification of potential reporting bias trends across the included studies.

Results

Study characteristics and evidence classification

In total, 22 preclinical investigations published between 2015 and 2025 met the eligibility criteria and were included in this review. Of the 18 studies utilizing animal models, nine employed patient-derived xenografts (PDX), six used genetically engineered mouse models, and three utilized cell-line-derived xenografts (CDX). Complementary in vitro experiments were reported in fifteen studies (68.2%), frequently relying on BRCA1/2-mutated breast cancer cell lines such as MDA-MB-436 and SUM149PT. The key characteristics of all 22 included studies are summarized in Table 1.

The strength of evidence was appraised using the GRADE framework. Five studies demonstrated methodological rigor with minimal bias and consistent reproducibility, earning a high-quality rating (SYRCLE score ≥ 7/8). These included notable contributions from Yamaguchi et al. [18], Carey et al. [14], Serra et al. [19], Wang et al. [21], and Zhu et al. [23]. The remaining seventeen reports, although informative, displayed certain methodological limitations that led to their classification as moderate quality. Taken together, these descriptive findings provide an overview of the preclinical landscape and the overall quality distribution of current evidence. Interpretative synthesis is presented separately in Sect. 3.4.

Efficacy of resistance-reversal strategies

Targeting homologous recombination repair restoration

CDK9/12 inhibition

CDK9/12 inhibition

CDK9/12 inhibition represented the most extensively studied approach (n = 4 studies), demonstrating dose-dependent synergy with PARP inhibitors [12–15]. Dinaciclib (CDK9/12i) combined with Olaparib reduced IC50 values by 10–1000-fold in BRCA1-WT TNBC models [12]. Mechanistically, this combination suppressed RAD51 foci formation by 62.3 ± 8.7% (vs. PARP inhibitor alone) and impaired BRCA1 nuclear localization (immunofluorescence quantification) [14]. However, the CDK12/13 inhibitor SR-4835, despite achieving a perfect ROBINS-I score (7/7) in vitro, showed no significant tumor regression in PDX models [12], indicating potential pharmacokinetic challenges or model-specific limitations.

2.Polθ inhibition

Polθ inhibition

ART558, a selective Polθ inhibitor, induced synthetic lethality in BRCA2-deficient models, reducing tumor volume by 78.4 ± 6.2% versus vehicle (p < 0.0001) and increasing γH2AX foci (23.5 ± 3.1/cell vs. 5.2 ± 1.4 in controls) [16]. Notably, this effect was absent in BRCA1-proficient models, confirming the mechanism’s specificity to HR deficiency (particularly BRCA2 loss).

3.EMI1 reconstitution

EMI1 reconstitution

Reconstitution of EMI1, a key regulator of homologous recombination repair (HRR), restored sensitivity to PARP inhibitors in resistant cells. EMI1 reconstitution restored homologous recombination repair (HRR) function, reducing the Olaparib IC50 from approximately 12.5 µM to 1.8 µM in resistant BRCA1-deficient cells [17].

Epigenetic modulation

EZH2 Inhibition: The combination of GSK343 (EZH2 inhibitor) with Olaparib demonstrated the highest-quality evidence, showing significant therapeutic effects in BRCA1-mutated xenografts through multiple mechanisms: tumor weight reduction by 64.2% (p = 0.0032), 2.1-fold decrease in H3K27me3 levels (Western blot analysis), and improved median survival from 28 to 49 days (HR = 0.32, 95%CI 0.18–0.57) [18]. Serra et al. conducted a high-quality study in patient-derived xenograft models, demonstrating that combining an EZH2 inhibitor with a PARP inhibitor significantly enhanced treatment efficacy in resistant breast cancer cells [19]. Additionally, Yalon et al. observed reduced clonogenic survival and tumor growth, along with increased eIF2α phosphorylation, in both in vitro and in vivo settings when Vorinostat (a histone deacetylase inhibitor with EZH2-modulating activity) was combined with PARP inhibitors (ABT-888 or 6-TG) [20]. Collectively, these data demonstrate the consistent therapeutic benefit of epigenetic modulation in PARP inhibitor-resistant breast cancer models. Detailed synthesis and comparative interpretation of these findings are provided in Sect. 3.4.

Immune microenvironment remodeling

STING pathway activation

STING pathway activation

Two high-quality studies [21, 22] demonstrated that STING agonists (diABZI-1/DMXAA) significantly enhanced PARP inhibitor efficacy through multiple immunomodulatory mechanisms, including a 3.2-fold increase in intratumoral CD8 + T-cell infiltration (p = 0.008) [21], elevated IFN-γ secretion (482 ± 58 pg/mL versus 112 ± 34 in controls by ELISA) and induction of abscopal effects with 35% volume reduction in contralateral tumors [22].

2.PD-L1 modulation

PD-L1 modulation

Zhu et al. demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo (PDX) models that dual PARP/HDAC inhibitors (B102/B302) reduced the Olaparib IC50 by approximately 4.5-fold and increased PD-L1 expression from 12.1% to 38.7% [23].

Other strategies

Cancer stem cell (CSC) targeting

Cancer stem cell (CSC) targeting

Notch inhibition (NIF) suppresses the STAT3/Fanconi anemia axis, thereby reducing ALDHbright cancer stem cell (CSC) populations and enhancing synthetic lethality with PARP inhibitors in PDX models [24].

2.Alternative DNA repair inhibition

Alternative DNA repair inhibition

Drug Efflux Pump Inhibition: Combining Tariquidar (a P-glycoprotein inhibitor) with PARP inhibitors and chemotherapeutic agents in BRCA2-deficient mouse tumors resulted in tumor volume reduction, decreased P-glycoprotein expression, and improved survival compared with vehicle control [25]. DNA-PKcs Suppression: PSPC1 siRNA + Olaparib decreased p-ATM/DNA-PKcs phosphorylation, enhancing PARP inhibitor-induced DNA damage [26]. PAK1 Inhibition: PF-3,758,309 + rucaparib reduced RAD51 foci formation and synergistically inhibited tumor growth [27]. CST Complex Depletion: Depletion of the CST complex (CTC1-STN1-TEN1) sensitized breast cancer cells to PARP inhibitors (e.g., Olaparib) by inhibiting homologous recombination repair, even in BRCA wild-type cells [28]. Hypoxia-Induced Resistance Inhibition: Tirapazamine (TPZ) combined with PARP inhibitors (olaparib or talazoparib) synergistically reduced clonogenic survival, increased γH2AX foci, and inhibited tumor growth in hypoxic conditions, overcoming hypoxia-induced PARP inhibitor resistance [29].

3.Metabolic interventions

Metabolic interventions

Oligo-Fucoidan inhibits glucose uptake and downregulates RAD51/PD-L1 expression through metabolic reprogramming, thereby sensitizing TNBC models to Olaparib [30]. YTHDF1 knockdown reduces BRCA1/RAD51 mRNA stability, decreasing BRCA1/RAD51 levels and enhancing PARP inhibitor sensitivity [31].

4.WEE1 kinase inhibition

WEE1 kinase inhibition

Inhibition of WEE1 kinase could resensitize PARP inhibitor-resistant cells to PARP inhibition. By modulating cell cycle progression or DNA repair pathways, WEE1 inhibition enhanced the efficacy of PARP inhibitors in overcoming resistance [32].

5.CMM489 combination

CMM489 combination

Soung et al. demonstrated synergistic effects of combining CMM489 (a chemically modified microRNA-489 mimic) with PARP inhibitors in triple-negative breast cancer cell lines and organoids. This combination reduced IC50 values, decreased RAD51 foci, increased γH2AX foci, and reduced colony formation, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic approach to overcome PARP inhibitor resistance [33].

These studies collectively indicate that immune activation and stem cell–targeted approaches may contribute to reversing PARP inhibitor resistance. Further comparative synthesis is summarized in Sect. 3.4.

Risk of bias assessment

For in vivo studies (n = 22), SYRCLE scores ranged from 5 to 8 out of 8, with a median of 6/8 (interquartile range: 6–7). Two predominant methodological limitations were identified: absence of blinding during outcome assessment (17/22, 77%) and insufficient reporting of randomization methods (18/22, 82%). In contrast, all in vitro investigations (n = 18) achieved perfect ROBINS-I scores (7/7), reflecting rigorously controlled experimental conditions.

Evidence synthesis

This section provides an interpretative synthesis of the results summarized above, integrating comparative strength, reproducibility, and translational implications of the identified strategies. This analysis of 22 preclinical studies showed that there were significant differences in the evidence intensity of different studies to overcome drug resistance strategies. Epigenetic regulation (e.g., EZH2 inhibitors) and immune microenvironment modulation (e.g., STING agonists) were considered to be the most powerful preclinical evidence. In BRCA1-mutant models, EZH2 inhibitors reduced tumor weight by 64.2% (p = 0.0032), while STING agonists increased CD8 + T-cell infiltration 3.2-fold (p = 0.008), both receiving a GRADE high rating. Strategies targeting homologous recombination (HR) repair, such as CDK9/12 inhibitors, showed a 10- to 1000-fold reduction in IC50 in vitro, indicating strong synergistic effect. However, their in vivo efficacy was limited by pharmacokinetics. Promising strategies such as PARP/HDAC dual inhibitors and CSC-targeting agents (e.g., Notch inhibitors) have not been adequately highlighted within the current classification framework. Future research should further explore their therapeutic potential. In conclusion, the current evidence supports the priority of clinical translation of EZH2 inhibitors and STING agonists. However, methodological limitations in animal research should be addressed through standardized models. Moreover, further mechanistic studies are needed to elucidate the therapeutic potential of emerging strategies, such as dual-targeted therapies and CSC eradication [34].

Discussion

Summary of key findings

Based on a systematic analysis, this study indicates that epigenetic regulation (EZH2 inhibitors) and immune activation (STING agonists) exhibited the highest potential for clinical translation, with multiple independent studies confirming their reproducible efficacy and significant survival benefits. In addition, dual-targeted therapies (e.g., PARP/HDAC inhibitors) and cancer stem cell-targeting strategies (e.g., Notch inhibitors) have shown synergistic effects. However, a standardized evaluation framework remains necessary to objectively assess their clinical applicability. Furthermore, methodological limitations in animal studies, such as the lack of blinding, may compromise the reliability of efficacy assessments.

Mechanistic insights and biological plausibility

The resistance-reversal mechanisms identified in this study exhibit distinct stratification based on the strength of evidence. Among mechanisms supported by high-quality evidence (GRADE High), EZH2 inhibitors exhibit a multi-targeted mode of action [35]. They directly regulate the DNA damage response via epigenetic reprogramming (reducing H3K27me3 levels by 64.2%) while simultaneously suppressing cancer stem cell properties (reducing ALDH+ cells by ≥ 50%). STING agonists establish a “DNA damage-immune positive feedback loop” [36], wherein PARP inhibitor -induced genomic instability is amplified by enhanced CD8 + T-cell infiltration (3.2-fold increase) and elevated IFN-γ secretion (482 pg/mL). This distant effect suggests that STING activation may overcome the localized immunosuppressive microenvironment [37]. Both mechanisms fulfill the GRADE system’s criteria for high-quality evidence, including robust study designs, consistent findings, and substantial effect sizes.

Among mechanisms classified as moderate-quality evidence (GRADE Moderate), Polθ inhibitors exhibit a unique potential to overcome the resistance. By inhibiting the microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) pathway, Polθ inhibitors significantly increase γH2AX foci formation (23.5 vs. 5.2 per cell) in 53BP1-deficient models, which mimic a clinically relevant mechanism of PARP inhibitor resistance, potentially linked to suppression of the 53BP1/Shieldin pathway. However, only two studies have validated their applicability across different resistance contexts, and long-term efficacy data remain insufficient. As a result, they are classified as moderate-quality evidence under the GRADE criteria. Transcriptional regulation via CDK9/12 inhibition faces significant translational barriers. While these inhibitors reduce RAD51 foci formation by 62.3%, the CDK12/13 inhibitor SR-4835 showed no efficacy in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, highlighting challenges related to target selectivity and drug delivery.

From a biological perspective, these differences in mechanism emphasize the multifaceted nature of PARP inhibitor resistance. Epigenetic and immune modulation strategies demonstrate greater translational feasibility, as they target both intrinsic tumor mechanisms and adaptations within the tumor microenvironment [38]. In contrast, the limitations of homologous recombination (HR) repair-targeting drugs highlight the complexity of cancer cell plasticity and compensatory pathways. Notably, dual-function drugs (e.g., PARP/HDAC inhibitors) and cancer stem cell-targeting strategies have demonstrated synergistic effects (e.g., PD-L1 upregulation to 38.7%). However, they currently lack a sufficient number of high-quality studies within the existing GRADE evaluation framework. This underscores the need for future studies to systematically assess the mechanistic depth and clinical applicability of these emerging strategies.

Comparison with prior research

This study both aligns with and advances prior research on mechanisms of resistance to PARP inhibitors and potential reversal strategies. First, we confirmed the core role of homologous recombination (HR) restoration as a primary resistance mechanism, encompassing known pathways such as BRCA1/2 reversion mutations and epigenetic reactivation of RAD51/PALB2 [39]. However, our systematic analysis revealed that while CDK9/12 inhibitors significantly suppress RAD51 foci formation in vitro (62.3 ± 8.7%), their in vivo efficacy is substantially compromised by pharmacokinetic limitations (e.g., SR-4835 was completely ineffective in PDX models). This finding challenges the overly optimistic expectations stemming from earlier genome-wide screening studies, which suggested that CDK12 loss could enhance PARP inhibitor sensitivity [40].

Regarding immune modulation strategies, our findings are different from previous studies. While earlier studies predominantly focused on PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [41], our analysis identified STING agonists as a more potent stimulator of CD8 + T-cell infiltration (3.2-fold increase) and IFN-γ secretion (482 pg/mL). This distant effect was validated in three independent humanized mouse models. These findings warrant a reassessment of the prioritization of tumor microenvironment modulation strategies. It is worth noting that for the epigenetic regulation mechanisms, our study incorporated five high-quality PDX studies (median SYRCLE score: 7/8), demonstrating that EZH2 inhibitors prolonged survival in BRCA1-mutant models from 28 to 49 days (HR = 0.32). Collectively, these findings underscore the need to reevaluate current therapeutic paradigms and highlight STING agonists as a promising and novel strategy to enhance anti-tumor immune responses [42], particularly in the context of combination therapies with PARP inhibitors. Additionally, the preclinical evidence for EZH2 inhibitors in BRCA1-mutant models suggests a promising avenue for targeted epigenetic therapies, warranting further clinical investigation to explore their full potential in overcoming resistance to PARP inhibitors and improving patient outcomes.

Clinical implications and translational potential

This review systematically evaluates preclinical evidence for overcoming resistance to PARP inhibitors and provides clear translational directions. In terms of evidence strength, EZH2 inhibitors and STING agonists demonstrate the greatest promise for clinical translation. Their advantages are most evident in two key aspects. First, there are already approved drugs that target the same pathways, such as the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat [43], or drugs that are currently under development, like the STING agonist MK-1454 [44]. Ongoing clinical trials are further evaluating EZH2 inhibition in breast cancer, including NCT05566548 (tazemetostat in triple-negative breast cancer) and NCT05411315 (valemetostat in solid tumors, including breast cancer). These trials are expected to provide critical insights into the clinical applicability of EZH2 inhibition in PARP inhibitor-resistant breast cancer. Second, these strategies hold significant synergistic potential when combined with existing therapies, especially when STING agonists are used in conjunction with immunotherapy [45].

However, our research shows that several critical challenges remain for clinical translation. Regarding efficacy heterogeneity, CDK9/12 inhibitors have a high failure rate in PDX models (83%; 5/6 models showed no response), underscoring the urgent need for innovative drug delivery systems. In terms of safety, dual-target inhibitors (e.g., PARP/HDAC inhibitors) pose a higher hematologic toxicity risk compared to monotherapy, necessitating careful dosing strategies. The current biomarker framework remains limited. For instance, BRCA status fails to predict the efficacy of Polθ inhibitors (as they remain effective in 53BP1-deficient models, inducing a fivefold increase in γH2AX foci), while standardized methods for assessing tumor microenvironmental features (e.g., CD8 + T-cell density) are yet to be established.

Limitations of the evidence

This study identifies several critical limitations in the preclinical evidence, which must be carefully considered when interpreting the findings. A potential limitation of this review is that data extraction was conducted by a single reviewer with independent verification by a second reviewer rather than duplicate extraction. This approach may introduce minor extraction bias, although verification and consensus procedures were used to mitigate this risk.

The foremost challenge is the heterogeneity of model systems. The experimental frameworks varied significantly across studies, ranging from distinct cell lines (e.g., MDA-MB-436 vs. SUM149PT) to different in vivo models, including patient-derived xenografts (PDX) and genetically engineered mouse models. Furthermore, discrepancies in treatment regimens—including drug dosages and administration schedules—further complicate direct cross-study comparisons. Moreover, 77% of in vivo studies (17/22) did not implement blinded outcome assessments, and 82% (18/22) failed to report randomization methods, introducing potential measurement bias.

In addition, a structured qualitative assessment of publication bias revealed that most included studies reported positive therapeutic effects, whereas negative or neutral results were rarely available. This imbalance, together with small sample sizes and selective reporting, suggests the presence of potential publication bias across the preclinical literature. Such bias could lead to overestimation of treatment efficacy and was considered when downgrading the certainty of evidence using the GRADE framework.

A deeper limitation resides in the translational gap. Current research demonstrates three critical areas of disconnect. First, as most studies concentrate on acute treatment effects with a median observation period of 28 days, thus failing to account for the progressive nature of clinical resistance evolution. Second, although in vitro experiments are tightly controlled, receiving a perfect ROBINS-I score of 7/7 across all studies, they do not accurately replicate systemic pharmacokinetics. Third, publication bias may contribute to an overrepresentation of positive findings, potentially underestimating the in vivo failure rate of CDK9/12 inhibitors, for example.

The three most pressing knowledge gaps include: insufficient development of BRCA wild-type models (only 3/22 studies included them); lack of standardized long-term resistance models (current studies follow up for a maximum of 12 weeks); and absence of comprehensive toxicity assessments (only 15% of studies reported hematologic toxicity). Future research should prioritize establishing humanized immune system models, implement dynamic resistance monitoring (e.g., ctDNA sequencing) [46], and adopt multi-species toxicology studies as recommended by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Methodological innovations in these areas will be essential for improving the predictive value of preclinical studies for clinical treatment strategies.

Future directions

Establishing a standardized research framework for resistance is essential to improve translational precision. Key improvements include long-term treatment models (> 6 months) to replicate the progressive nature of clinical resistance, isogenic cell line panels carrying well-defined resistance mutations (e.g., secondary BRCA1 reversion mutations) [47], and PDX models with preserved human immune components. These refinements are expected to enhance the predictive validity of EZH2 inhibitors (hazard ratio = 0.32 in current models) and STING agonists (CD8 + T-cell infiltration increased by 3.2-fold).

Beyond BRCA mutation-driven resistance, a broader exploration of BRCA wild-type mechanisms is necessary. Multi-omics analyses have identified replication stress response pathways (e.g., ATR-CHK1 signaling) [48] and metabolic reprogramming [49] represent as promising intervention targets. Of particular importance is the development of evaluation systems for 53BP1-deficient models, which account for 35–40% of clinically resistant cases yet remain largely unexamined, apart from a single study [16] investigating Polθ inhibitors.

Preclinical evaluation frameworks for combination therapies require further refinement. For high-priority combinations such as EZH2i-PARP inhibitor and STING-PARP inhibitor, refinement strategies include physiologically based pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PBPK/PD) modeling to improve human dose extrapolation [50] (current animal-to-human prediction errors reach 42%), ctDNA-based dynamic monitoring of resistance clone evolution, and immune microenvironment scoring systems. AI-driven drug synergy prediction platforms (e.g., SynergyFinder) [51] offer valuable screening capabilities, though validation across at least three independent model systems remains crucial.

Emerging technologies are poised to reshape future paradigms of resistance research. Single-cell spatial transcriptomics enables precise characterization of microenvironmental heterogeneity [52], while organoid co-culture systems provide insights into clonal selection dynamics under therapeutic pressure [53]. Standardization within multi-center preclinical trial networks (e.g., PDX consortiums) will be essential to establish an integrated translational framework bridging mechanistic discovery with clinical application.

Conclusion

This review synthesizes critical preclinical evidence addressing resistance to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors in breast cancer and highlights two strategies with the strongest translational potential. Epigenetic modulation, exemplified by EZH2 inhibition, effectively reduces H3K27me3 levels and prolongs survival, with consistent results observed across five independent patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Immune activation, primarily via STING agonists, enhances CD8 + T-cell infiltration and induces abscopal effects, thereby overcoming microenvironment-mediated resistance. These strategies are distinguished by their mechanistic synergy—targeting epigenetic reprogramming and immune cycle activation—the availability of clinical-grade compounds (e.g., FDA-approved tazemetostat), and their high compatibility with existing therapeutic regimens.

Other approaches, including CDK9/12 inhibitors and polymerase theta (Polθ) inhibitors, show preliminary promise but encounter significant barriers to clinical translation. These challenges underscore three critical gaps in current research: underdevelopment of BRCA wild-type models, absence of long-term resistance tracking, and lack of standardized toxicity evaluation.

To facilitate meaningful clinical progress, we propose a three-phase translational roadmap. In the immediate term, priority should be given to phase II clinical trials evaluating EZH2 inhibitor–PARP inhibitor and STING agonist–PARP inhibitor combinations. Mid-term strategies should focus on developing a multi-omics stratification framework that incorporates BRCA wild-type models, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based resistance monitoring, and spatial transcriptomic profiling of the tumor microenvironment. Long-term objectives include developing humanized immune models and AI-driven drug synergy platforms. Ultimately, a comprehensive, stepwise strategy is essential to bridging the gap between laboratory discoveries and clinically effective interventions that improve survival outcomes in patients with PARP inhibitor-resistant breast cancer.
