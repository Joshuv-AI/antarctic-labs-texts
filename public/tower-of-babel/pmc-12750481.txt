REporting Guidelines for aCupuncture‐Related AdverSe Event Case Reports (RECASE): Elaboration and Explanation
Authors: Ye‐Seul Lee, Tae‐Hun Kim, Jung‐Won Kang, Lin Ang, Jeremy Y. Ng, Stephen Birch, Terje Alræk, Lin Yu, Yuting Duan, Zhirui Xu, Myeong Soo Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCase reports represent the earliest form of scientific literature to identify and describe adverse events (AEs) associated with medical interventions, and they still represent important educational resources for ensuring the safe practice of such interventions. Although case reports on AEs related to acupuncture continue to be published, detailed information about the acupuncture procedure is often insufficiently reported in these studies, which may introduce an overstatement or exaggeration of the harm of acupuncture. Consequently, these reports fall short of evaluating causality and achieving the educational purpose of preventing future AEs. To help address these limitations, we developed the “REporting guidelines for aCupuncture‐related AdverSe Event case reports” (RECASE) based on the CARE (CAse REports) reporting guidelines for case reports using the expert Delphi methodology. This guideline contains essential items for case reports on acupuncture‐related AEs. We anticipate that this reporting guideline will encourage greater transparency, fairness, and comprehensiveness in future case reports on acupuncture‐related AEs.

Introduction

Adopting the general definition of adverse events (AEs) for medical devices, acupuncture‐related AEs can be defined as any “untoward medical occurrence, unintended disease or injury, or untoward clinical signs,” which develop following acupuncture treatment [1]. Case reports on acupuncture‐related AEs that have been published to date have played a significant role in identifying potential risks associated with acupuncture [2]. However, acupuncture‐related AEs have characteristics distinct from those of conventional drugs or medical devices. For example, it is unclear whether minor bleeding inevitably occurs as an AE when a needle is removed following its insertion. Moreover, complications may arise due to a patient's specific characteristics, underlying conditions, or individual risk factors, and may also result from the practitioner's lack of competence or inadequate training [3]. These factors make it difficult to assess the inherent risks associated with acupuncture. In this regard, case reports often lack a reasonable evaluation of whether acupuncture was indeed the definitive cause of the reported event, and fail to provide sufficient data for such an evaluation. Consequently, published case reports often fall short of achieving the educational purpose of preventing similar future AEs, and may instead contribute to an overestimation of the risks of acupuncture as a medical procedure [2].

Many case reports addressing AEs related to acupuncture indicated that they followed the reporting guideline for case reports (the CARE guidelines). However, it is frequently observed that important information is still missing. An analysis of case reports published between 2010 and 2023 that described acupuncture‐related AEs suggested that key items were often omitted, including an assessment of other potential causes of the AEs, an evaluation of patient risk factors relevant to the case, and sufficient information to judge the causal relationship between acupuncture and the event. In addition, most case reports lacked information necessary to assess whether the acupuncture procedure itself had been appropriate [2]. This situation arises because the CARE guidelines were originally developed for general case reports and does not include items that are essential for reporting AEs and specific characteristics of acupuncture treatment. Therefore, using solely on the CARE statement is insufficient for case reports describing acupuncture‐related AEs.

To address these limitations, we developed the “REporting guidelines for aCupuncture‐related AdverSe Event case reports” (RECASE) based on the CARE guidelines [4]. This guideline contains the essential items needed to promote transparent and sufficient reporting of acupuncture‐related AEs. Each item in the RECASE addresses aspects not covered in the CARE guidelines, and is therefore appropriate for use in conjunction with the CARE checklist when preparing case reports (or other article types, such as academic letters or image‐based reports) dealing with acupuncture‐related AEs.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a multistage Delphi consensus process to develop reporting guidelines (RECASE) for case reports of acupuncture‐related AEs. The methodology followed the EQUATOR Network recommendations for reporting guideline development, combining evidence synthesis, structured expert consensus, and iterative refinement. The study protocol and survey instruments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University (approval no. KHSIRB‐24‐395(RA)) prior to initiation. The project was registered in the Reporting Guidelines under the Development for Observational Studies in the Equator Network (https://www.equator‐network.org/library/reporting‐guidelines‐under‐development/reporting‐guidelines‐under‐development‐for‐observational‐studies/#RECASE).

Systematic Review and Item Generation

A systematic review of case reports on acupuncture‐related AEs published between 2010 and 2023 was used as the initial pool of reporting items. This review identified recurring gaps in reporting, including insufficient details on causality assessment, appropriateness of treatment, and patient risk factors [2]. Based on this, 26 preliminary items were drafted and organized into four domains: AE‐related information (8 items), acupuncture treatment details (12 items), patient‐related information (3 items), and authorship (3 items).

Development Group and Recruitment of Delphi Experts

An international development group comprising 11 members from five countries (Korea, China, Canada, the Netherlands, and Norway) was established. The group included eight senior clinicians and researchers with extensive expertise in acupuncture, and three methodologists with experience in reporting guideline development and consensus methodology. The members were responsible for designing the Delphi survey, reviewing evidence, analyzing results, and approving the final checklist [5].

Individuals recommended by acupuncture societies were selected as experts for the Delphi process. Potential experts for the Delphi process were either directly nominated by the national acupuncture society of each country or recruited through email invitations sent to society members. Eligible participants were limited to healthcare professionals who performed acupuncture or had enough knowledge about acupuncture‐related AEs, clinicians, or researchers with experience in writing case reports (including those related to acupuncture AEs), safety committee members, and methodological experts in reporting guidelines.

Delphi Procedure

The Delphi survey was conducted across three rounds between March and September 2025. Rounds 1 and 2 were administered online using survey links (Google Forms), distributed via email to expert panelists. Panelists rated each item on a 9 point Likert scale (1 = very non‐essential; 9 = very essential). Items rated 7–9 by 80% or more of the respondents were accepted, while items that did not reach a consensus were retained. In Round 1 (March 2025), 62 experts responded, and 19 items reached a consensus. In Round 2 (July 2025), 20 experts responded, and six additional items reached a consensus.

For Round 3 (September 2025), a virtual consensus meeting was convened to discuss the single remaining unresolved item (“1.7. Suggested treatment option for the reported AE”). Panelists agreed to retain it with the qualifier “if possible,” thereby finalizing the checklist (Figure 1).

Delphi Results

The preliminary draft checklist presented to the Delphi panel comprised 26 items organized into four thematic domains: (1) 8 items related to information on adverse events (AEs); (2) 12 items concerning acupuncture treatment details as the potential cause of the reported event; (3) 3 items addressing patient information; and (4) 3 items related to the authors of the case report. Each item was rated on a 9‐point Likert scale (1 = very non‐essential to 9 = very essential), with scores ranked as follows: 7–9, essential; 4–6, preferable; and 1–3, nonessential. Consensus was defined as ≥80% of participants rating an item within either the 1–3 or 7–9 range; items that reached consensus were excluded from subsequent rounds.

Three rounds of testing were conducted. After Round 1, seven items did not achieve consensus and were retained for Round 2, during which all items were presented in their original form. Following Round 2, six items reached a consensus and were included in the current form, whereas a single item remained below the consensus threshold and was therefore carried forward to Round 3. The third round consisted of an online discussion meeting in which experts deliberated on the remaining items, proposed revisions, and reached a final consensus on its modification. According to the conclusion of Round 3, a consensus was reached for all 26 items, yielding a finalized version of the checklist.

Item That Was Modified

One item, “Suggest treatment options for the reported AE,” from the category Information on the AEs, did not reach consensus following Round 2. An online discussion was therefore held for Round 3, during which the experts debated whether this item should be deleted or modified. The experts agreed that this item was important in that it encourages authors to demonstrate their clinical reasoning in response to adverse events, but also acknowledged that it may not always be feasible to propose treatment options when the report is purely observational or when the original care provider is unavailable. The experts emphasized that retaining this item would enhance their understanding of both the clinical consequences of acupuncture treatment and the medical decision‐making involved in managing AEs. Ultimately, they agreed to retain the item with the phrase “if possible” added to reflect this flexibility.

Final Checklist Guide and Explanation

1. Information on AEs

AE‐related information includes details regarding the signs or symptoms related to the event, diagnosis, treatment, and causality assessment between AE and acupuncture treatment.

Item 1.1. Describe the type of reported AE.

1. Explanation

The item “type of AE” refers to the types of diseases or symptoms experienced by the patient during or after acupuncture, as reported in the case study. While a classification system for AEs of acupuncture has not yet been established, this item refers to categorization based on factors such as whether the events are systemic or localized, type of injury (organ injury or infection), and cause (whether the issue stems from the acupuncture itself or from practice‐related problems).

2. Examples

“We report a case of 59‐year‐old Korean woman who developed M. abscessus cutaneous infection after multiple acupunctures” [6].

“Medulla oblongata injury caused by an acupuncture needle; warning for serious complications due to a common method of alternative medicine” [7].

Item 1.2. Suggest the final diagnosis of the reported AE, including its etiology, if possible. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

A diagnosis of acupuncture‐related AE should be made. At this point, it is advisable to present an appropriate etiology that can support the diagnosis and help determine whether the AE was indeed caused (or to a certain degree related to) by acupuncture. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“The initial diagnosis was erysipelas of the right arm complicated by toxic shock. When the condition of the patient deteriorated necrotising fasciitis was considered which was confirmed with surgical exploration” [8].

Item 1.3. Suggest the severity of the described AE. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

The severity of AEs can be classified as mild (no or minimal difficulty conducting usual activities), moderate (greater than minimal difficulty), severe (inability to perform usual activities), or life‐threatening (medical treatment necessary, persistent disability, or death). Alternatively, cases that require no treatment and recover spontaneously or with minimal treatment can be classified as mild, those requiring active treatment as moderate, and those that result in death or permanent disability as severe. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“Infectious complications from acupuncture are extremely rare; we present a case of severe necrotizing Fasciitis (type 1) in an elderly nondiabetic male” [9].

Item 1.4. Clearly report the time relation between acupuncture and the described AE. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

The temporal relationship between acupuncture and associated AEs is an important factor in assessing causality. It is necessary to clearly describe how far apart the acupuncture treatments and events occurred, as well as their chronological order. In addition, it is advisable to determine whether other potential causal events occur between acupuncture treatment and AE. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“During recuperation, the patient received acupuncture for shoulder and neck pain treatment. … A few minutes after the removal of the needle, the patient experienced slight chest tightness and discomfort” [10].

Item 1.5. Conduct a causality assessment and provide sufficient information to allow judgment of the results and reasoning processes. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

An important item that is often overlooked in case reports dealing with acupuncture AEs is the assessment of causality regarding the cause of the AE. Causality assessment refers to the proper evaluation of the causal relationship between the acupuncture treatment and the AE experienced by the patient. The causality assessment described by the World Health Organization‐Uppsala Monitoring Center causality assessment system considers factors such as the temporal relationship between the intervention and the occurrence of AE, whether the event cannot be explained by factors other than the intervention, whether the reaction disappears after stopping the treatment, or whether the reaction reoccurs upon re‐administration of the same intervention [11]. Based on these different factors, causality can be categorized as Certain, Probable, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional, or Unassessable. It is not necessary to judge causality strictly, but an appropriate assessment of whether there is sufficient causality between the acupuncture treatment and the event is essential. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“After further investigation of the medical history, the patient said he had received lumbar acupuncture treatment in the local clinics one week prior to the onset of the disease. Therefore, we inferred that the acupuncture had led to the development of a paravertebral abscess, which eventually entered the blood and caused blood‐stream infection, and that multiple migrating lesions were formed in the lungs” [12].

Item 1.6. Other possible causes of the reported AE or underlying medical conditions predisposed the patient to the reported AE. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

For acupuncture to be considered causally related to the AE, other factors that may have contributed to the event should be ruled out. In addition, whether the patient had any medical conditions that could have increased the possibility of an event occurring due to acupuncture also needs to be assessed to determine causality. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“An epidural abscess due to Nocardia is extremely rare…9 of these 10 patients had potential comorbidities or histories of previous nocardial infections. Alcoholism was present in 3 patients, diabetes mellitus and renal transplant were present in 1 patient, intravenous heroin abuse occurred in 1 patient, and long‐time prednisone use occurred in 1 patient. Our patient also had a comorbidity of diabetes mellitus….” [13].

Item 1.7. If possible, suggest the treatment options for the reported AE. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

In typical case reports of acupuncture‐related AEs, the described events are often temporally distant from the acupuncture treatment session, and it is therefore likely that the report was written by another healthcare professional who diagnosed and treated the events. In such cases, the lack of appropriate management in the early stages of acupuncture‐related AEs may have been problematic, leading to more serious complications. Therefore, from an educational perspective, it is helpful for case reports to suggest measures of treatment options that should have been applied by acupuncturists after the occurrence of AE. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“After performing the procedure several times on his own, the acupuncture needles were accidentally broken and remained in his neck. His local doctor instructed him to consult a spinal surgeon, but the patient chose not to do so and continued with several self‐treatments. A few days after his final self‐treatment, he presented to another hospital because of neck pain and weakness in the lower extremities. He was then referred to our hospital, and 5 days later he visited our department…Because of the reported delayed onset, the risk of infection, and hematoma, improperly embedded acupuncture needles should be removed as soon as possible before symptoms appear or become severe…” [14].

Item 1.8. Describe the clinical outcomes after treatment. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Case reports of acupuncture‐related AEs, which follow the CARE guidelines commonly provide sufficient information on the clinical course after treatment. Presenting clinical outcomes makes it possible to assume the clinical course of various acupuncture‐related AEs of distinct severities, thereby providing educational value. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“We report a case of 59‐year‐old Korean woman who developed M. abscessus cutaneous infection after multiple acupunctures…We treated the patient with a combination of clarithromycin and ciproproxacin for 3 months and surgical drainage, and she made a good recovery” [6].

2. Information related to the acupuncture treatment

Item 2.1. Describe the practitioners' certifications. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

The qualifications, educational backgrounds, training, and clinical experience of acupuncture practitioners are critical for assessing the appropriateness of acupuncture treatment. However, these are not reported in most case reports on acupuncture‐related AEs. It is essential that case reports include appropriate details about the practitioner who performed the acupuncture in the case reports. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“A sterilised single‐use acupuncture needle (0.3 × 30 mm, Dongbang medical, Korea) was inserted into the acupuncture point large intestine 4 (LI4, Hapgok) of right hand of participant approximately in depth of 1.8 cm by licensed KMD (Korean Medicine Doctor)…” [15].

Item 2.2. Describe the practitioners’ medical background. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Because the qualifications of practitioners vary depending on the setting of acupuncture treatment, and the detailed methods of acupuncture differ according to the practitioner's educational background, identifying the practitioner's medical background can be helpful for understanding acupuncture procedures related to AE. It may be easier for the authors of case reports for AE to consult the acupuncture association that the acupuncturist is a member of, as these usually have a minimum stated standard in relation to training requirements to be a member of the association.

Item 2.3. Describe the acupuncture training history of the practitioner. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Similar to other invasive interventions, the safety of acupuncture procedures tends to improve proportionally with the practitioner's level of training and clinical experience. Information on the extent of a practitioner's experience with acupuncture is useful for determining the minimum level of training required to ensure the safe practice of acupuncture. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“…who has a 10 year's career and 4 year of the career was as an assistant teacher of acupuncture practice at the university” [15].

Item 2.4. Describe the needling sites or acupuncture points. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Acupoints or sites of acupuncture needling are among the most important factors for assessing the causality between acupuncture and AE. For example, pneumothorax, a serious acupuncture‐related injury, is associated with needling of the sternum or thoracic cage. Providing the exact location of acupuncture needling (or at least the names of the acupuncture points) offers essential information for evaluating causal relationships, and serves as valuable data for future practice guidelines for acupuncture to prevent such events. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“According to her description, a sudden sharp painful sensation occurred in the left eye when the acupuncturist inserted a needle at the midpoint of the eyebrow, presumably the acupuncture point Ex‐HN‐4 (Yuyao)” [16].

Item 2.5. Describe whether sterile needles were used or not. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Clean needle technique is critical for ensuring the safe practice of acupuncture, “Use only single‐use sterile needles” is classified as critical or strongly recommended to prevent infection and broken needles [17]. In cases involving infections or broken needles after acupuncture, the use of disposable needles must be clearly stated. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“Four disposable, sterilized, and individually wrapped stainless‐steel needles (0.25 mm in diameter, 50‐mm length) were inserted into the tender area of the right calf” [18].

Item 2.6. Describe the frequency and duration of acupuncture treatment before the reported AE. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Acupuncture treatment is usually not completed in a single session, but is instead provided over multiple sessions. Information such as the weekly frequency and total duration of acupuncture treatment helps to understand the stage during which AE occurs. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“The details of the acupuncture were provided by the patient, his family and his acupuncturist: seven sessions of acupuncture were performed in the lower back around the L4−5 disc” [19].

Item 2.7. Describe the depth of insertion of acupuncture needles. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

The depth of the inserted needle is important in identifying acupuncture‐related AEs. In particular, without knowing the depth of insertion, it is difficult to assess causality in cases of pneumothorax or peripheral nerve injury. Therefore, the depth of the needle inserted at the acupoint suspected to have caused AE needs to be reported. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“On the day of his death, the patient had undergone electroacupuncture therapy; 22 needles each with a total length of approximately 6.8 cm were inserted sequentially into his left shoulder and scapular region at a depth of approximately 0.5 cm, and into his lower back at a depth of approximately 1.0 cm” [20].

Item 2.8. Describe the needle type. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Most needles currently in use are thin disposable stainless steel needles that are generally coated with silicone. However, the length and thickness of the needle body can vary depending on the type of needle used. In some acupuncture types, non‐inserted needles are also used. Because the type of needle used in acupuncture may influence the development of AEs, information on the type of needle used should be provided. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“Four disposable, sterilized, and individually wrapped stainless‐steel needles (0.25 mm in diameter, 50 mm length) were inserted into the tender area of the right calf” [18].

Item 2.9. Describe the stimulation method of acupuncture. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

The method used to stimulate the needle should be described. When electroacupuncture is applied, details such as the frequency, intensity, and mode of electrical stimulation should be reported. It should also be stated whether the treatment involved simple needle insertion or whether manual techniques were employed to elicit a deqi sensation. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“The needles were inserted to a depth where the patient can typically experience a sensation known as de qi (a subjective soreness, fullness, and numb feeling around the acupuncture points)” [18].

“At the end of the session the therapist tried to ‘deactivate’ a trigger point which he located by palpating the elevator scapulae muscle. He used a dry needling technique (intramuscular stimulation) by manipulating a needle that was already in place. According to the patient, he used intense manipulation. At this point the patient felt an acute local and intense pain which remained unchanged even after the needle was removed” [21].

“The manual technique consisted of 5 seconds stimulation repeated every 3–4 min in every session. The stimulation consisted of a mixture of twisting, pushing, pulling and shaking methods to aim for de qi status. This mixture of methods was applied until de qi status was obtained” [22].

Item 2.10. Describe the settings of acupuncture practice. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Stating whether acupuncture was performed in a properly equipped clinic (or hospital), or in an inadequate setting lacking appropriate facilities is important, as this factor could also be closely related to the occurrence of acupuncture‐related AE. Therefore, providing information about the environment in which acupuncture is administered is essential for evaluating the appropriateness of the procedure in relation to the event. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“She then received one session of needle acupuncture treatment at a Traditional Chinese Medicine clinic” [18].

Item 2.11. Describe the disinfection procedure. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

According to the clean needle technique, the skin preparation guidelines recommend conducting alcohol swabbing of the areas to be treated with intradermal or subcutaneous methods using 70% isopropyl or ethanol to ensure safe practice [17]. In cases of infection after acupuncture, it must be clearly stated whether the disinfection procedure is appropriate. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“The acupuncture was performed by a traditional Chinese medical doctor using alcohol swabs (containing isopropyl alcohol 70%) locally over the skin of the right calf” [18].

Item 2.12. Provide an overall appraisal of whether the acupuncture treatment described in the case report was appropriate for the patient. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Assessment of the appropriateness of acupuncture treatment needs to be based on a balanced and objective appraisal of all the information obtained, in order to correctly evaluate whether the treatment is suitable for the patient's specific condition. This requires presenting whether the appropriate acupoints were selected in accordance with the patient's symptoms or diseases, whether the treatment was performed safely considering the patient's characteristics (such as an obese or thin body type), and whether the treatment environment was adequate. Therefore, it is necessary to determine the appropriateness of acupuncture treatment by integrating these factors.

To reduce bias, authors should avoid making judgments based on personal opinions regarding acupuncture. Instead, referring to the relevant literature, including clinical guidelines, institutional protocols, and expert opinions, is recommended. Given that there are multiple styles of acupuncture practice, the authors need to note any relevant style or tradition followed (e.g., traditional Korean, Chinese, Japanese, or ear acupuncture), so that the appraisal reflects the norms of that style. When the authors are unable to judge appropriateness, this limitation should be explicitly stated.

3. Patient information

Patient‐related information refers to information that can help clinicians to assess whether a patient has any conditions that may have contributed to the occurrence of AE. Some acupuncture‐induced AEs may depend on the patient's condition. For example, thin and tall patients may have a shallower depth from the skin to the thoracic cavity, making them more susceptible to pneumothorax than overweight individuals.

Item 3.1. Describe preceding medical conditions before the AE. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

To consider the possibility that a patient's medical condition contributes to an AE, it is necessary to provide detailed patient information. The risk of infection after acupuncture increases if the patient has an abnormal immune function or an impaired skin barrier. Providing sufficient information about a patient's pre‐existing medical conditions allows for a fair evaluation of the causality between acupuncture and AE. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“A 44‐year‐old woman with aplastic anemia experienced right calf pain after running. She had received immunosuppressive treatment (prednisolone 5 mg per day) for 4 months and had stopped treatment for 1 month prior to this event” [18].

Item 3.2. Provide reasons why the patient seeks acupuncture or the acupuncture practitioner recommends acupuncture. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Information on why patients seek acupuncture is important when assessing the appropriateness of the treatment. The authors must specify the primary reason for the visit, and this information should be provided to determine whether the procedure was performed in accordance with the patient's needs. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“A 21‐year‐old female college student visited the Chinese Medicine Outpatient Clinic of our hospital because of an abnormal menstrual cycle…During recuperation, the patient received acupuncture for shoulder and neck pain treatment. The bilateral acupoints GB21 (Jianjing) and GB20 (Fengchi) were used” [10].

Item 3.3. Suggest assessments of the patient's risk factors for AEs. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

The term “patient risk factor” here refers to whether the patient had any preexisting conditions that may have made them more susceptible to AEs before receiving acupuncture treatment. It is necessary to describe whether the patient had any conditions that could have contributed to the occurrence of the AE, such as the presence of a sternal foramen, or the patient's body type (underweight or overweight), considering the type or cause of the AEs. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“A 37‐year‐old woman presented with acute thoracic and neck pain to a private practice acupuncture practitioner. The patient was healthy with no other known significant medical conditions, although she had been a heavy smoker for the previous 15 years (2–3 cigarette packs per day) and marginally underweight with a body mass index of 18.2… We suggest that high‐risk patients are those with a thin body status, thin chest wall, atrophic neck and thoracic muscles, a history of chronic respiratory diseases, and heavy long‐time smokers” [21].

4. Additional information

Item 4.1. Describe authors' areas of expertise. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Case reports of acupuncture‐related AEs are generally written by the clinicians who identify and treat patients’ problems. Therefore, these case reports tend to emphasize the author's own treatment techniques or outcomes within the area of expertise, instead of focusing on exploring the cause of the AE. Accordingly, information on the authors’ field of specialization provides an essential context for understanding the perspectives from which case reports were written.

Item 4.2. Consider involving experts in acupuncture treatment in the case analysis and as authors of the case report. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

It is common for the list of authors of case reports on acupuncture‐related AEs to include no acupuncture specialists. The authors reporting AEs of acupuncture are predominantly medical doctors who treat the AEs. Consequently, they often report cases without sufficient knowledge of acupuncture.

Item 4.3. Refer to citations of similar AEs. 1.Explanation

1.Explanation

Explanation

Analyzing the existing literature to determine whether similar reported cases have already been published can provide scientific evidence to support the causality of the case being reported. 2. Example

2. Example

Example

“Orbital hemorrhage, globe perforation with retinal injuries such as retinal holes, subretinal tracks, retinal hemorrhages, and retinal detachment caused by penetration of an acupuncture needle have been reported [3,4]… [3] Fielden M, Hall R, Kherani F, Crichton A, Kherani A. Ocular perforation by an acupuncture needle. Can J Ophthalmol 2011; 46:94e5. [4] You TT, Youn DW, Maggiano J, Chen S, Alexandrescu B, Casiano M. Unusual ocular injury by an acupuncture needle. Retin Cases Brief Rep 2014;8:116e9” [16].

Discussion

The existing CONSORT‐harm guidelines report AEs in randomized controlled trials, while the CARE guidelines provide a general reporting checklist for case reports [4, 23]. However, these guidelines do not offer a checklist of various acupuncture AE‐specific items that should be included in acupuncture AE‐related case reports. This reporting guideline aims to recommend necessary items in the case reports which enable readers to undersatnd the causal relationship between acupuncture and AEs and to assess sufficient information on the risk factors associated with patients or acupncture practice. As a result, case reports can serve an educational purpose by preventing future AEs, and ensure that the risk of acupuncture are assessed fairly without exaggeratig the harm of acupuncture.

Case reports of acupuncture‐related AEs are important educational resources for problem‐based medical education [24]. Although clean needle technique guidelines have already been well‐established, actual case reports allow novices to understand AEs linked to acupuncture and, acquire essential clinical knowledge based on these live examples that can help avoid AEs and improve the safety of acupuncture practice [17]. For this to be meaningful for education, case reports on acupuncture‐related AEs must include sufficient information. High‐quality case reports that provide the necessary information can contribute to the establishment of safer acupuncture practices and ultimately serve as the foundation for delivering higher‐quality acupuncture treatments.

This reporting guideline provides authors of case reports with instructions on how to ensure accurate and transparent reporting. It offers readers sufficient information on acupuncture‐related AEs and will help reviewers and journal editors assess whether AEs have been adequately described and appraised.

Nevertheless, this reporting guideline has some limitations. Firstly, it was developed based on expert opinions using the Delphi method. However, a validity test to confirm its applicability to actual case report writing has not yet been conducted. Future studies applying this reporting guideline to the preparation of case reports should be conducted to evaluate its applicability, identify any issues, and revise the guidelines accordingly. Moreover, as acupuncture is often combined with other interventions such as moxibustion, cupping, pharmacopuncture (or bee venom acupuncture), electroacupuncture, or guasha, these adjunctive therapies may fall outside the scope of this guideline. Therefore, further studies are warranted.

Finally, when using this RECASE guideline, it is recommended to use it with the CARE guidelines together. For case reports on acupuncture‐related AEs, the basic items of the case report should be described according to the CARE guidelines, while the items of the RECASE guideline should be applied to individual CARE items including items 6 (Clinical findings), 7 (Timeline), 8 (Diagnostic assessment), 9 (Therapeutic intervention), 10 (Follow‐up and outcomes), and 11 (Discussion), following the checklist provided in Table 2.
