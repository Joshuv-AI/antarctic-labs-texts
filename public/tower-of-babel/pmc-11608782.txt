Total Arthroscopic Resection of the Talocalcaneal Coalition in Adults: Key Points of Surgical Technique
Authors: Yiyuan Sun, Wufeng Cai, Xihao Huang, Jian Li, Qi Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePotential disadvantages of open technique for talocalcaneal coalition (TCC) include a risk of wound infection, opioid drug dependence, and prolonged hospitalization. The purpose of this study was to retrospectively evaluate the effectiveness and safety of endoscopic resection of the TCC.MethodsA retrospective study from June 2019 to February 2023was conducted on 16 consecutively admitted patients who were diagnosed by imaging to have TCC for whom conservative treatment had failed and who undergone arthroscopic resection. The mean age of this cohort was 31.56 ± 10.39 years (range, 16–57 years). The mean follow‐up period was 38.93 ± 15.69 months (range, 11–59 months). The site of the coalition, comorbidities, satisfaction with treatment, visual analog scale (VAS), and American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle–Hindfoot scale scores were evaluated pre‐ and postoperatively. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were used to categorize the coalition according to the Lim classification. Postoperative computed tomography (CT) scans were used to assess recurrence. The distribution of TCC cases according to the Lim classification was type I in one case, type II in four cases, and type III in 11 cases.ResultsThe site of coalition involved the middle facets in seven patients, the posterior facets in three patients, and both the above sites in six patients. All patients underwent total arthroscopic resection of TCC. An auxiliary mini‐incision was made for three patients due to serious tibial nerve compression. Radiographics showed that the coalition disappeared and pain was relieved postoperatively. The mean VAS score decreased from 4.31 ± 1.54 to 1.81 ± 0.98 points (p < 0.001). The mean AOFAS ankle‐hindfoot score improved from 65.56 ± 5.82 to 87.31 ± 6.30 points (p < 0.001). Fifteen patients were satisfied with the procedure, and one patient experienced numbness after surgery. No recurrence was reported based on CT scan and clinical results up to the end of the study.ConclusionSurgical reconstruction employing total arthroscopic resection of TCC can achieve significant functional and radiographic improvements and symptom relief in selected patients with TCC. Auxiliary mini‐incisions were necessitated in complex situations.Keywords: Ankle‐Hindfoot Scale, Endoscopic Resection, Mini‐Incision, Surgical Reconstruction, Talocalcaneal Coalition

Introduction

The incidence of congenital dysplasias of the foot tarsal bones accounts for 1%–6%, 1 and the talocalcaneal coalition (TCC) accounts for 53% of these conditions. 2 TCC was first described by Zuckerkandl 3 in 1877 as an abnormal connection between the talus and calcaneus bones. It frequently presents as foot fatigue, vague pain around the hindfoot, and reduced range of motion (ROM) of the subtalar joint. The most common site of coalition is the posterior facets, accounting for 100% of TCC, followed by the middle facets (73.1%) and anterior facets (4.6%). 4 Most patients have no obvious symptoms. Some patients present with pain and subtalar joint dysfunction. A long‐term existence of this condition may lead to secondary arthritis of adjacent joints, collapse of the foot arch, peroneal tendon contracture, flat foot deformity, and an increased risk of fractures. The well‐known ‘C' sign on a lateral radiograph can indicate a TCC. Moreover, three‐dimensional (3D) multiplanar reformatted computed tomography (CT) images improved evaluation of the bony anatomy and the delineation of TCCs that help in surgical planning. 5 Generally, most patients with TCC are asymptomatic and serendipitous. 6

Operative treatment is recommended for the management of painful coalitions after failure of conservative treatment. Different and controversial techniques, such as open or arthroscopic surgery, have been described for TCC excision or arthrodesis, with or without interposition of material (bone wax, fat, muscle, and tendon grafts) and adjuvant for 3D printing. 7 Many factors should be considered for surgical intervention, such as size and location of the coalition, and the presence of degenerative changes. Harris 8 first treated symptomatic TCC by talocalcaneal or talonavicular arthrodesis. Currently, arthrodesis is only indicated in TCC when there have been repeatedly failed operations and severe osteoarthritis, particularly if it is associated with severe deformity or revised complex surgery. 9 Open resection provides excellent exposure of coalitions. However, potential disadvantages of the open technique include a risk of wound infection, opioid drug dependence, and prolonged hospitalization.

To our knowledge, arthroscopic excision has been described for different kinds of TCC, including the anterior, middle, and posterior facet coalitions that has resulted in high rates of patient satisfaction. Notably, most of these cases involved simple and small sized TCCs without comorbidities such as osteoarthritis, nerve entrapment, and synovitis. In this study, we adopted a slicing technique under arthroscopy, using a bone knife to slice the TCC into smaller pieces and then smoothing the joint surfaces, which reduced surgical trauma and nerve damage, yielding good results.

The purpose of this study was to (i) describe the technique of arthroscopic resection of TCC that involved anterior or posterior portals and (ii) to report the outcomes and safety of resecting different types and sites of TCC.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective study was conducted in the Department of Orthopedics, West China Hospital, Sichuan University, China, from June 2019 to February 2023. All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (Ethical Approval Number: 2024204). We enrolled 16 patients who were diagnosed by imaging to have TCC, and arthroscopic resection was performed on them. All patients had exhibited a poor response to conservative treatment, such as orthopedic footwear, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and physical therapy. The inclusion criteria were symptomatic patients with TCC of the primary operation and coalitions involving less than 50% of the joint. The exclusion criteria were severe hindfoot valgus deformity (>20°) and severe osteoarthritis (K‐LGrade > III). The Kellgren‐Lawrence (K‐L) classification was used to assess the degree of osteoarthritis. 10 All study participants signed informed consent forms for surgery and research prior to surgery.

Study Procedure

We measured subtalar motion (calcaneus variation after the tiptoe standing test, as well as dorsal and plantar foot flexion) pre‐ and postoperatively. We performed 3D CT scans and MRI of the ankle before surgery and 6 months after the operation. MRI scans were used to categorize the coalition according to the Lim classification. 11 We determined the site, size, and shape of the TCC and presence of injured surrounding soft tissues; localized the coalition on the surface of the ankle; provided a safe surgical approach; and determined the degree of hindfoot valgus. Intra‐ and postoperative surgical complications were also recorded until the last follow‐up. The pre‐ and postoperative assessment and surgical treatment of all patients was conducted by one of the authors (Q.L.). Patient‐rated assessments, including the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS), Ankle‐Hindfoot scale score, visual analog scale (VAS) score, ROM evaluation, and an ordinal 3‐point scale score for treatment satisfaction (not satisfied, 0; satisfied, 1; and extremely satisfied, 2) were administered. Pre‐ and postoperative pain intensity was assessed with the AOFAS numeric scale.

Operative Procedure

General anesthesia was administered to the patient. A tourniquet was positioned at the proximal thigh and the patient was flipped into a prone position with the feet hanging off the operating table. A tourniquet was attached to the root of the afflicted side of the thigh. Using conventional procedures, the surgical team disinfected the drape, drained blood from the afflicted lower extremities, inflated the tourniquet, secured the posterior and posterolateral channels of the ankle, and executed arthroscopy. The continuity, quality, and tension of the anterior talofibular ligament, posterior talofibular ligament, and calcaneofibular ligament were investigated by using arthroscopic cleaning and ablation of the proliferating joint synovium. Ligament injuries of the third degree were mended or rebuilt. The tibial nerve was isolated and exposed, and the nerve was released to avoid harming the blood vessels and nerves around the bone bridge. The tibial nerve is squeezed and clings close to the bone bridge, making separation difficult. Switching to an incision is suggested. Silk thread was used to retract the tibial neurovascular system and explore the posterior tibial arteries and veins. The hyperplastic bone bridge was removed with the bone knife groove until the joint surface was visible, and the joint edge was polished with a planar drill. The soft tissue covering the calcaneus and talus joint was reduced after resection of the calcaneus and talus bridge; thus, a tissue fascia flap was performed. The subtalar joint ROM on the affected side was examined, and this was markedly improved after the procedure. The site of incision was disinfected, stitched, dressed, pressurized, and bandaged. The tourniquet was relaxed. Examples of surgical procedures can be found in the supplemental video that recorded the detailed method of TCC slice resection.

In one example, the tibial nerve was contused near the coalition, the epineurium was lacerated, and the nerve bundle was exposed. The epineurium was healed with sutures. For individuals who had cartilage damage on the lateral side of the knee, microfractures were used. If a cyst is explored, it should be removed and referred to a pathologist for diagnosis. A power burr is then used to smooth the rough bone edges. Once the coalition is removed, the subtalar joint ROM can be directly observed to ensure adequate resection. We used electrocautery along the osseous margins of the remaining bone, followed by bone wax application, and endoscopic excision of the bar through two posterior proper assessments of the quality of the articular surfaces. The amount of synovitis within the talocalcaneal joint can be assessed through the standard middle and anterior ports. A classic case is presented in Figure 1.

Rehabilitation Program

Enoxaparin sodium injection of 4000 IU aXa was administered 6 h after the operation. Ankle joint dorsiflexion and plantar flexion exercises were performed the same day. Weight‐bearing walking on the affected side was strictly prohibited for 3 weeks after the operation and gradually transitioned from partial weight‐bearing to full weight‐bearing 3–6 weeks after the operation.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Based on whether the data conform to a normal distribution, a two‐tailed paired t‐test was used to compare pre‐ and postoperative AOFAS scores, and the Wilcoxon matched pairs test was used to compare preoperative and the last available follow‐up VAS scores. Qualitative data are expressed as numbers and percentages. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Seventeen feet in 16 patients consecutively admitted to our institution and diagnosed with TCC were involved. The average patient age at the time of operation was 31.56 ± 10.39 years (range, 16–57 years). Ten patients were men, and six patients were women. Operations were performed on the right foot in 11 cases and on the left foot in 5 cases. Only the right foot in the bilateral TCC case was symptomatic. The mean follow‐up period was 38.93 ± 15.69 months (range, 11–59 months). One case exhibited osteochondral lesions of the talus, eight cases involved merged osteoarthritis (level of K‐L I or II), one case was combined with flexor hallucis longus tendon tenosynovitis, while four cases displayed anterior talofibular ligament injury that was imaged via MRI and detected under arthroscopy. All cases exhibited merged synovitis, two cases showed merged gouty arthritis, one case was combined with flexor hallucis longus tendon cyst, two cases showed rigid flatfoot deformity, one case involved merged arthrochalasis, and an ankle cyst was checked by MRI in three cases (Table 1).

Based on the various sites of coalition and synovial lesions, anterior, posterior, or combined arthroscopic techniques for TCC excision were applied. Auxiliary mini‐incisions were needed for three patients because 30%–40% of the joint was involved and adhered to the neurovascular bundle. In addition to TCC excision, microfracture surgery was conducted in one case, neurolysis of the tibial nerve was performed in eight cases, and cystectomy was performed in three cases. Synovitis debridement was performed in all cases.

Postoperative Follow‐Up Results

The average AOFAS Ankle‐Hindfoot score improved from 65.56 ± 5.82 to 87.31 ± 6.30 (p < 0.001). The average VAS score was reduced from 4.31 ± 1.54 to 1.81 ± 0.98 (p < 0.001). We followed 12 “very satisfied” patients, three “satisfied” patients, and one “unsatisfied” patient. An absence of recurrence was followed up with a postoperative CT scan up to the end of the study.

Only one patient aged 19 years had traumatic tibial nerve injury during the operation. Postoperative numbness of the footplate persisted for 3 months but greatly improved after 12 months. No superficial or deep infection, revision surgery or thrombotic events occurred up to the last follow‐up.

Discussion

Arthroscopic resection of TCC has been suggested for symptomatic patients who fail to benefit from conservative treatment, have normal subtalar joints, and whose TCC surface area is less than half. In this retrospective study of patients with TCC displaying the above characteristic, we found that arthroscopy for TCC excision was safe and allowed rapid recovery with patient satisfaction. Additionally, auxiliary mini‐incisions were necessitated in complex cases, such as tibial nerve injury, where this approach is more thorough and can effectively protect the tibial nerve.

Technique of Arthroscopic Resection of TCC

TCC excision or fusion has been described as a workable plan for patients when conservative treatment has failed. 12 However, some researchers have reported unsatisfactory results in up to 50% of cases after resection. Inadequate or excessive coalition resection may result in poor talocalcaneal matching. The most important technical issue is inadequate exposure of the posteromedial aspect. Single methods, such as only open or arthroscopic methods cannot solve radical excision. Arthroscopic methods have been reported to increase recently with different levels of satisfaction. Therefore, an auxiliary mini‐incision was applied as an alternative plan in the present study. Additionally, ROM examination of the subtalar joint was important for prognosis. Up to 95.4% of coalitions involved the posterior facets.4, 11 All coalitions involved the medial or posterior facets in this report. Routine posterolateral and posteromedial portals were applicable. However, Lim reported only two cases among 70 feet involving the posterior joint. Preoperative assessment of the site, size, and shape of the coalition and degeneration of the joint affected the operation. Bonasia 13 concluded with the following indications to choose a surgical technique: (1) symptomatic and posterior facet; (2) nonarthritic subtalar and tarsal joints; (3) <50% of the subtalar joint involved; and (4) failure of adequate conservative treatment. In contrast, various levels of osteoarthritis of the subtalar joint were detected in five cases in this study. Poor talocalcaneal matching resulted in joint wear and tears. Patients are more likely to experience sprains and injuries to the anterior talofibular ligament. Therefore, effective polishing and smoothing of the edge of the articular surface isa critical procedure. Surgeons need to explore the anterior talofibular ligament as well and repair or reconstruct it if necessary. Anterior talofibular ligament injury was detected in four cases, and conservative treatment was applied. The main symptom associated with this condition is increased activity.

It has been reported that synovitis, resulting from tarsal coalitions, makes diagnosis difficult and contributes to postresection symptomatology. Additionally, extensive synovitis and inflammation within the talocalcaneal area results in pain. 14 Resection of the coalition and debridement of the associated synovitic tissue under arthroscopy helps to improve postoperative recovery.

Technique Tips

Arthroscopic resection is recommended as a primary choice of treatment for symptomatic patients after failure of conservative treatment according to our experience and past literature. There are four key parameters for consideration in determining surgical resection by arthroscopy. The first relates to the coalition being bony in origin that can be scanned by CT images. The second parameter is whether the surgeon is acquainted, via his clinical experience, with the anatomic complexity exhibited by patients. The third attribute point is the presence or absence of serious secondary arthritic changes. 15 The fourth characteristic is whether it is a re‐excision or is combined with infection. The site of coalition determines the operative route of arthroscopy. The presence of extensive cartilage or fibrous tissue coalition determines the operative time.

Arthroscopic excision of talar calcaneal coalition (TCC) requires careful consideration of several technical aspects. Firstly, adequate exposure of the posteromedial aspect is crucial, as inadequate exposure can lead to poor talocalcaneal matching and subsequent joint wear and tears. Single methods, such as open or arthroscopic approaches alone, may not achieve radical excision, necessitating the use of auxiliary mini‐incisions. Preoperative assessment, including evaluation of the site, size, and shape of the coalition, as well as the degree of joint degeneration, is essential for planning the operation. Effective polishing and smoothing of the articular surface edge is a critical step to prevent joint wear and tears. Surgeons must also inspect the anterior talofibular ligament and repair or reconstruct it if necessary, as injury to this ligament can occur in TCC cases.

Outcomes and Safety of Resecting Different Types and Sites of TCC

All 16 patients were categorized as “extremely satisfied” (69%) or “satisfied” (23%), but one patient still complained of plantar numbness, and sural nerve dysesthesia failed to resolve this complaint until the last follow‐up. A bone knife was used to cut the patient's coalition into small pieces so that it could be easily removed. Even though the tibial nerve had been retracted, a small sharp piece of bone may have damaged it. Adequate preoperative planning and careful intraoperative procedures may avoid or reduce similar situations. Preoperative assessment, including the site, proportion and tissue of coalition, and classification was a matter of great concern for choosing a diagnosis and treatment plan. Arthroscopic resection of coalitions had good outcomes in all 16 patients. This may be attributed to the selection of the right patients for the right interventions. The classification of TCCs has been discussed in many studies. Rozansky 16 classified TCC based on CT findings into five types and provided helpful information for surgical planning. Wang 4 devised a classification based on the shape and nature of the coalitions and the articular facets revealed by CT images. It involves articular facets and helps in planning the resection approach. Utilizing three‐dimensional (3D) reconstructions, a report categorized the TCC in children into five distinct types: linear, linear with a posterior hook, shingled, completely osseous, and posterior. 16 While aiming to offer beneficial insights for surgical planning, it was observed that the imaging of adult TCC does not entirely align with the above classification. Meanwhile, Lim 11 classified coalitions into four types based on MRI and CT that were easy to distinguish. MRI can not only show the calcaneal‐talar coalition but also help determine whether the cartilage or the surrounding nerves and blood vessels are damaged and provide technical support for surgical plans.

The possible complications after arthroscopic resection of TCC include recurrence of symptoms, possible damage to the tibial neurovascular bundle and flexor hallucis longus, increased surgical time, and deep venous thrombosis. 13 A longer learning curve makes the technique difficult to popularize. Aldahshan 17 reported that one patient had hyperesthesia over the medial aspect of the calcaneus. Jagodzinski 18 found that one patient relapsed requiring subsequent fusion where one posterior tibial nerve had been damaged, and there was persistent loss of sensation. This study also found that tibial nerve contusions occurred in young patients. Fortunately, nerve symptoms were ameliorated after trophic nerve treatment was administered for 3 months.

Limitations and Strengths

The primary limitation of this study is the limited number of cases that impacts the accuracy and reliability of the research outcomes. A larger sample size is necessary to achieve statistically significant results, a factor that can elevate both the cost and complexity of the research. Further, within this case series, a complete osseous coalition was notably absent. Most type I TCC cases were asymptomatic, and no cases of Type IV were gathered during this study. These shortcomings in the diversity and completeness of samples may undermine the representativeness and broader applicability of the research results.

One of the key strengths of our study is (i) the utilization of the Lim classification for preoperative evaluation and planning, which significantly reduces the risk of neurovascular injury; (ii) collecting all patient‐reported outcomes in a structured manner ensures that no patient is lost to follow‐up; and (iii) all cases were consecutively operated on by the same senior chief physician, maintaining the study's homogeneity.

Conclusions

Arthroscopic resection of TCC is an effective and useful treatment for TCCs. Auxiliary mini‐incisions were necessitated for complex cases, such as tibial nerve injury. It is essential for preoperative planning to know the vascular and nerve distribution around TCCs by MRI and clinical physical examination. We recommend using the Lim classification for preoperative evaluation. The protection of surrounding nerves, blood vessels, and tendons requires cautious manipulation. Arthroscopic debridement in synovitis is helpful for relieving pain. Degeneration of the arthritic subtalar joint may not be a contraindication for arthroscopic surgery.
