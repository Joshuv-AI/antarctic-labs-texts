The higher order partial derivatives of Okamoto’s function with respect to the parameter

Abstract

1.
Introduction

In 2005, Okamoto
[
23
]
introduced a one-parameter family of self-affine functions
\{F_{a}:0<a<1\}
defined on the interval
[0,1]
as the unique continuous solution of the functional equation

(1.1)

F_{a}(x)=\begin{cases}aF_{a}(3x)&\mbox{if $0\leq x\leq 1/3$},\\
(1-2a)F_{a}(3x-1)+a&\mbox{if $1/3<x\leq 2/3$},\\
aF_{a}(3x-2)+1-a&\mbox{if $2/3<x\leq 1$}.\end{cases}

Special cases of this family had been considered before: Most famously,
F_{1/2}
is the ternary Cantor function. Furthermore,
F_{5/6}
is Perkins’ continuous but nowhere differentiable function
[
24
]
, and
F_{2/3}
was studied independently by Bourbaki
[
9
]
and Katsuura
[
18
]
. However, Okamoto
[
23
]
was the first to systematically study the differentiability of
F_{a}
for all
a\in(0,1)
. He proved the following surprising result:

Theorem 1.1

(Okamoto)
.

Let
a_{0}\approx.5592
be the unique real root of
54a^{3}-27a^{2}=1
.

(a)

F_{a}
is nowhere differentiable if
2/3\leq a<1
;

(b)

F_{a}
is nondifferentiable almost everywhere but differentiable at uncountably many points if
a_{0}<a<2/3
;

(c)

F_{a}
is differentiable almost everywhere but nondifferentiable at uncountably many points if
0<a<a_{0}
and
a\neq 1/3
.

Note the exclusion of
a=1/3
in part (c). The reason is, that
F_{1/3}(x)=x
. Thus,
F_{1/3}
is the only member of the family
\{F_{a}\}
that is differentiable everywhere.
Kobayashi
[
19
]
later showed that the conslusion of case (
b
) also applies for
a=a_{0}
. Graphs of
F_{a}
for several values of
a
are shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Graph of
F_{a}
for several values of
a
. Top left:
a=5/6
(Perkins’ function); top right:
a=a_{0}\approx.5592
; bottom left:
a=1/2
(the Cantor function); bottom right:
a=1/5
(a “slippery devil’s staircase”).

Various authors have subsequently studied Okamoto’s function. For instance, Allaart
[
2
]
computed the Hausdorff dimension of the sets of exceptional points of measure zero in (
b
) and (
c
) of the above theorem, and described the set of points where
F_{a}
has an infinite derivative. McCollum
[
22
]
used the self-affine structure of
F_{a}
to show that its graph has box-counting dimension
1+\log_{3}(4a-1)
for
a\geq 1/2
, and claimed (with an incorrect proof) that this is also the Hausdorff dimension. Only very recently have Bárány and Prokaj
[
7
]
shown that for Lebesgue-almost all
a\in(1/2,1)
the Hausdorff dimension of the graph of
F_{a}
is indeed equal to
1+\log_{3}(4a-1)
. (For
a\leq 1/2
the function
F_{a}
is increasing, and so the dimension of its graph is trivially
1
.) Seuret
[
25
]
was the first to determine the multifractal structure of
F_{a}
.

In this article, we are interested in the partial derivatives of
F_{a}
with respect to the parameter
a
. That is, we define for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
0<a<1
the function

M_{k,a}(x):=\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial a^{k}}F_{a}(x),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},\quad x\in[0,1].

(In particular,
M_{0,a}=F_{a}
.) These functions are well defined, because
F_{a}(x)
is real analytic in
a
for every fixed
x
, as shown in
[
19
]
. Figure
2
shows some of their graphs.

We observe that the function
M_{1,1/3}
(first graph in second row of Figure
2
) was investigated by Dalaklis et al.
[
10
]
, who showed that it is continuous but nowhere differentiable and characterized the set of points where it has an infinite derivative. (By “nowhere differentiable” in this article, we shall mean “not possessing a finite derivative at any point”.) The nowhere-differentiability of
M_{1,1/3}
is perhaps surprising because
F_{1/3}
is simply the identity function on
[0,1]
, and is therefore differentiable everywhere.

Motivated by the article
[
10
]
and the interesting structures of the graphs in Figure
2
, we study here the partial derivative
\partial{F_{a}}(x)/{\partial a}
for other values of
a
, as well as the higher order partial derivatives. These functions are no longer strictly self-affine, but are still approximately self-affine in some sense. We will compute the box-counting dimension of the graph of
M_{k,a}
, characterize its differentiability, and investigate in detail the set of points where
M_{k,a}
has an infinite derivative. While some of our results are similar to the known facts about Okamoto’s function, there are also some notable differences and surprising new phenomena that arise when considering the higher order partial derivatives of
F_{a}
. We hope that this article, while highlighting a specific family of functions, will serve as a blueprint for studying functions that are only approximately self-affine.

While many papers (e.g.
[
11
,
14
,
17
,
26
]
) have studied points of non-differentiability or infinite derivatives of monotone functions, there is considerably less literature on analogous questions for non-monotone functions. Nonetheless, as was shown in
[
2
,
3
,
5
]
, especially the study of infinite derivatives for non-monotone self-affine functions can lead to surprising results and reveal intricate structures that are not encountered in the monotone case. The functions considered in this paper exhibit even finer and more complex behavior than those in
[
2
,
3
]
.

2.
Statement of results

We first address the continuity of
M_{k,a}
. From the functional equation (
1.1
) it follows easily that
F_{a}
is Hölder continuous with exponent

\gamma:=-\log_{3}\max\{a,|1-2a|\}.

This means that there is a constant
C
such that
|F_{a}(x)-F_{a}(y)|\leq C|x-y|^{\gamma}
for all
x,y\in[0,1]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For each
k
,
M_{k,a}
is Hölder continuous with exponent
\alpha
for any
0<\alpha<\gamma
. More precisely, there is a constant
C_{k}
such that

|M_{k,a}(x)-M_{k,a}(y)|\leq C_{k}|x-y|^{\gamma}\left(\log_{3}\frac{1}{|x-y|}\right)^{k}\qquad\forall x,y\in[0,1].

Our next result gives the box-counting dimension of the graph of
M_{k,a}
. We denote the box-counting dimension of a set
E
by
\dim_{B}E
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in(0,1)
, we have

\dim_{B}\mathrm{Graph}(M_{k,a})=\dim_{B}\mathrm{Graph}(F_{a})=\begin{cases}1+\log_{3}(4a-1)&\mbox{if $a\geq 1/2$},\\
1&\mbox{if $a\leq 1/2$}.\end{cases}

2.1.
Finite derivatives

To state our results on differentiability, we need some further notation. Let
(x_{i})_{i}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
denote the ternary expansion of a point
x\in(0,1)
, so that
x_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
for each
i
and
x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}3^{-i}
. If
x
has two such representations, we choose the one ending in all zeros. For
x\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

l_{n}(x):=\#\{i\leq n:x_{i}=1\}

denote the number of
1
’s in the first
n
ternary digits of
x
. Define the function

(2.1)

\phi(a):=\frac{\log(3a)}{\log a-\log|1-2a|},\qquad a\in\left(0,2/3\right]\backslash\left\{1/3,1/2\right\}.

We extend
\phi
continuously to
[0,2/3]
by setting
\phi(0):=1
,
\phi(1/3):=1/3
, and
\phi(1/2):=0
. It can be shown that
\phi
is strictly decreasing on
[0,1/2]
, and strictly increasing on
[1/2,2/3]
; see Figure
3
.
For each
a\in(0,1)\backslash\{1/3,1/2\}
, we also define the constant

C_{0}:=C_{0}(a):=\frac{1}{\log a-\log|1-2a|}.

Note that
C_{0}<0
for
a<1/3
, whereas
C_{0}>0
for
a>1/3
. Furthermore,
C_{0}(a)
tends to
-\infty
as
a\nearrow 1/3
, to
+\infty
as
a\searrow 1/3
, and to
0
as
a\to 1/2
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
a\in(0,1)
. If
M_{k,a}
is differentiable at a point
x
, then
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
a\in(0,1)
. For
x\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

r_{n}(x):=l_{n}(x)-n\phi(a).

(a)

If
0<a<1/3
, then
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
if and only if
r_{n}(x)+k|C_{0}|\log n\to-\infty
as
n\to\infty
;

(b)

If
1/3<a<2/3
and
a\neq 1/2
, then
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
if and only if
r_{n}(x)-kC_{0}\log n\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
;

(c)

If
2/3\leq a<1
or
a=1/3
, then
M_{k,a}
is nowhere differentiable.

The case
a=1/2
is a bit special; we characterize the differentiability of
M_{k,1/2}
in Proposition
5.5
below.

Defining the set

D_{k,a}^{0}:=\{x\in(0,1):M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0\},

we obtain the following consequence of the last theorem, which makes precise that the functions
M_{k,a}
become progressively “less differentiable” as
k
increases.

Corollary 2.5
.

For each
0<a<2/3
except
a=1/3
, the sequence of sets
(D_{k,a}^{0})_{k\geq 0}
is strictly decreasing; that is,

D_{0,a}^{0}\supsetneq D_{1,a}^{0}\supsetneq D_{2,a}^{0}\supsetneq\dots.

Moreover, for each
k\geq 0
,
D_{k,a}^{0}\backslash D_{k+1,a}^{0}
has positive Hausdorff dimension.

\phi
(left) and
h
(right)

Another consequence of Theorem
2.4
is that we obtain for
k\geq 1
nearly the same trichotomy as in Okamoto’s theorem. We include in the next result also the Hausdorff dimensions of the exceptional sets. To this end, define the functions

h(p):=\frac{-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)+(1-p)\log 2}{\log 3},\qquad 0\leq p\leq 1,

where, following standard convention, we set
0\log 0\equiv 0
; and

(2.2)

d(a):=h(\phi(a)),\qquad 0\leq a\leq 2/3.

The significance of the function
h
is that it gives the Hausdorff dimension of the set of points
x
in whose ternary expansion the digit
1
appears with a given frequency, namely

\dim_{H}\left\{x\in(0,1):\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{l_{n}(x)}{n}=p\right\}=h(p),

where
\dim_{H}E
denotes the Hausdorff dimension of a set
E
.
Depending on
p
, the limit may also be replaced with a
\liminf
or
\limsup
and the equality with a weak or strict inequality. The function
h
is hence maximized at
p=1/3
, with
h(1/3)=1
; see Figure
3
. We can further interpret
\phi(a)
as the “critical value” of the frequency of
1
’s at which the differentiability behavior changes. This makes the dimension statements in the next theorem plausible.

Finally, let
N_{k,a}
denote the set of points at which
M_{k,a}
has neither a finite nor an infinite derivative.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
a_{0}
be as in Theorem
1.1
. Then for each
k\geq 1
,

(a)

M_{k,a}
is nowhere differentiable if
2/3\leq a<1
or
a=1/3
;

(b)

M_{k,a}
is nondifferentiable almost everywhere but
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{0}=d(a)>0
if
a_{0}\leq a<2/3
;

(c)

M_{k,a}
is differentiable almost everywhere but
\dim_{H}N_{k,a}=\dim_{H}\big((0,1)\backslash D_{k,a}^{0}\big)=d(a)>0
if
0<a<a_{0}
and
a\neq 1/3
.

(The dimensions of the exceptional sets are equal to
d(a)
also for Okamoto’s function (i.e.
k=0
); see
[
2
]
.) Note the exceptional role of the parameter value
a=1/3
in the above theorem: it is an isolated point in the set of values of
a
for which
M_{k,a}
is nowhere differentiable. We contrast this with Okamoto’s function itself, which for
a=1/3
is the identity function and hence differentiable everywhere.

The graph of
d
is shown in Figure
4
. Note that, since
\phi(a_{0})=1/3
,
d(a)
attains its maximum value of
1
at both
a=1/3
and
a=a_{0}
.

d(a)
. Note that
d(0)=0
and
d(1/2)=\log_{3}2
.

2.2.
Infinite derivatives

A large part of the paper is devoted to the infinite derivatives of
M_{k,a}
. We will be interested in the sets

D_{k,a}^{+\infty}:=\{x:M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=+\infty\},\qquad D_{k,a}^{-\infty}:=\{x:M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=-\infty\},

and

D_{k,a}^{\pm\infty}:=D_{k,a}^{+\infty}\cup D_{k,a}^{-\infty}.

Here we find fundamentally different behavior between the parameter regions
0<a<1/2
and
1/2<a<1
, but also between even and odd
k
. Again the case
a=1/2
is special; we state some precise results in Section
6
.

For the case
0<a<1/2
, an important difference between
M_{k,a}
for
k\geq 1
and Okamoto’s function is that the latter is monotone increasing and can hence only have an infinite derivative of
+\infty
, whereas for
k\geq 1
, both
+\infty
and
-\infty
occur as infinite derivatives. However, the sets of points where these values occur have different Hausdorff dimensions. Precisely, we prove the following, in which
d(a)=h(\phi(a))
as defined in (
2.2
), and

\tilde{d}(a):=h(1-2a)=-\frac{2a\log a+(1-2a)\log(1-2a)}{\log 3}.

Essentially, if
M_{k,a}
has an infinite derivative at
x
, its sign depends on whether the frequency of
1
’s in the ternary expansion of
x
is greater or less than
1-2a
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
0<a<1/2
. Then
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{\pm\infty}=d(a)
. Furthermore, if
k
is even, then
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}=d(a)
. On the other hand, if
k
is odd, we have the following:

(a)

If
0<a<1/3
, then

\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}=d(a)>\tilde{d}(a)=\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{-\infty}.

(b)

If
a=1/3
, then

\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}=\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{-\infty}=1.

(c)

If
1/3<a<1/2
, then

\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}=\tilde{d}(a)<d(a)=\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{-\infty}.

Theorem
2.7
shows that derivatives of
+\infty
dominate when
a<1/3
, whereas derivatives of
-\infty
dominate when
a>1/3
. Note that (b) was proved in
[
10
]
for the case
k=1
.

We do not know the Hausdorff dimension of
D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
for even
k
, but we show in Section
7
that this set is uncountable even then, and derive a fairly high lower bound for its dimension in the case
k=2
. We suspect that the Hausdorff dimension of
D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
is positive when
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, but zero when
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. In Section
7
we also give some fairly sharp explicit conditions, in terms of the frequency of
1
’s in the ternary expansion of
x
, for
M_{k,a}
to have an infinite derivative at
x
.

As stated above, the number
1-2a
plays the role of a critical frequency of
1
’s which separates points in
D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
from points in
D_{k,a}^{+\infty}
, at least for odd
k
. When the frequency of
1
’s is exactly
1-2a
, the sign of the infinite derivative at
x
depends in a subtle way on the deviation of
l_{n}(x)
from
(1-2a)n
, and a surprising alternating pattern emerges.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
0<a<1/2
and
k\geq 1
. There is a polynomial
q_{k}
of degree
k
, depending only on
k
and not on
a
, having a set of
k
distinct real roots
t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{k}
which is symmetric about
0
, such that the following holds: Suppose the limit

(2.3)

\delta(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(1-2a)n-l_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{n}}

exists and is finite. Put
t_{0}:=-\infty
and
t_{k+1}:=+\infty
. For
i=0,1,\dots,k
, if

t_{i}\sqrt{2a(1-2a)}<\delta(x)<t_{i+1}\sqrt{2a(1-2a)},

then

M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}+\infty&\mbox{if $k-i$ is even},\\
-\infty&\mbox{if $k-i$ is odd}.\end{cases}

The polynomials
\{q_{k}\}
are given by a simple recursion; see (
7.1
) below. For ease of presentation we have assumed the existence of the limit
\delta(x)
in (
2.3
), but this assumption can be weakened. A full statement is given in Theorem
7.3
.

We next consider the case
a>1/2
. Before stating the result, we first recall some facts about unique
\beta
-expansions. Given a number
1<\beta<2
, a
\beta
-expansion
of a number
x
is an expression of the form

x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}}{\beta^{n}},

where
c_{n}\in\{0,1\}
for all
n
. Such an expansion exists if and only if
x\in I_{\beta}:=[0,\frac{1}{\beta-1}]
, but it is in general not unique. Let
\mathcal{U}_{\beta}
denote the set of all
x\in I_{\beta}
having a unique expansion.

Theorem 2.9
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in(1/2,1)
, we have

\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}=\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{-\infty}=\frac{\log\beta}{\log 3}\dim_{H}\mathcal{U}_{\beta},

where
\beta:=1/a
.

For the case
k=0
(i.e.
F_{a}
), this result was established in
[
2
]
. Comparing with the previous theorem, we observe that for
a>1/2
the sets
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}
and
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
have the same dimension, whereas for
a<1/2
they do not (unless
a=1/3
).

The size of the univoque set
\mathcal{U}_{\beta}
was described in the celebrated theorem of Glendinning and Sidorov
[
16
]
. Let
\varphi:=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
be the golden ratio, and recall that the
Thue-Morse sequence
is the sequence
(t_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
of
0
’s and
1
’s given by
t_{j}=s_{j}\mod 2
, where
s_{j}
is the number of
1
’s in the binary representation of
j
. Thus,

(2.4)

(t_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}=0110\ 1001\ 1001\ 0110\ 1001\ 0110\ 0110\ 1001\ \dots

Let
\hat{a}\approx.5595
be the unique root in
(0,1)
of the equation
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}t_{j}a^{j}=1
. Its reciprocal
\hat{\beta}:=1/\hat{a}\approx 1.787
is known as the
Komornik-Loreti constant
, introduced in
[
21
]
. (It is the smallest base in which the number 1 has a unique expansion.)

Theorem 2.10

(Glendinning and Sidorov)
.

The set
\mathcal{U}_{\beta}
is:

(i)

empty if
1<\beta\leq\varphi
;

(ii)

countably infinite if
\varphi<\beta<\hat{\beta}
;

(iii)

uncountable but of Hausdorff dimension
0
if
\beta=\hat{\beta}
;

(iv)

of positive Hausdorff dimension if
\hat{\beta}<\beta\leq 2
.

It was shown furthermore in
[
20
]
(with a corrected proof in
[
6
]
) that the function
\beta\mapsto(\log\beta)\dim_{H}\mathcal{U}_{\beta}
is an increasing devil’s staircase. Thus, we obtain the following consequences for the set
D_{k,a}^{\infty}
:

Corollary 2.11
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
D_{k,a}^{+\infty}
is:

(i)

empty if
1>a\geq 1/\varphi\approx 0.6180
;

(ii)

countably infinite, containing only rational points, if
\hat{a}<a<1/\varphi
;

(iii)

of Hausdorff dimension
0
if
a=\hat{a}
;

(iv)

of positive Hausdorff dimension if
0<a<\hat{a}
.

Moreover, on the interval
1/2<a<\hat{a}
, the function
a\mapsto\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}
is continuous and nonincreasing in the manner of a devil’s staircase; that is, there is a countable family
(I_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of disjoint subintervals of
(1/2,\hat{a})
, whose union has full Lebesgue measure in
(1/2,\hat{a})
, such that
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}
is constant on
I_{j}
for each
j
.

(The same statements hold also for
D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
.)

Remark 2.12
.

We do not know if
D_{k,a}^{\pm\infty}
is uncountable when
a=\hat{a}
. For
k=0
(i.e. Okamoto’s function
F_{a}
) this question was answered in the affirmative in
[
3
]
. But for
k\geq 1
the situation is more complicated.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In Section
3
, we give functional equations for
M_{k,a}
, describe a useful symmetry property, prove Proposition
2.1
, and develop crucial expressions for the increments and oscillation of

3.
Preliminaries

For a number
x\in[0,1]
, we identify
x
with its ternary expansion. Thus we write simply
M_{k,a}(x_{1}x_{2}\dots)
instead of
M_{k,a}(0.x_{1}x_{2}\dots)
, where
x_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
for all
i
. We also write
\sigma(x)
, or simply
\sigma x
, to denote
3x\!\!\mod 1
, so
\sigma
acts as the left shift on the ternary expansion of
x
. If
(x_{i})
is the ternary expansion of
x
, we express this by
x=(0.x_{1}x_{2}\dots)_{3}
, i.e.
x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}/3^{i}
.

Lemma 3.1

(Functional equations)
.

For
x=(0.x_{1}x_{2}\dots)_{3}
, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle F_{a}(x)=\begin{cases}aF_{a}(\sigma x)&\mbox{if $x_{1}=0$},\\
(1-2a)F_{a}(\sigma x)+a&\mbox{if $x_{1}=1$},\\
aF_{a}(\sigma x)+1-a&\mbox{if $x_{1}=2$},\end{cases}

(3.2)

\displaystyle M_{1,a}(x)=\begin{cases}aM_{1,a}(\sigma x)+F_{a}(\sigma x)&\mbox{if $x_{1}=0$},\\
(1-2a)M_{1,a}(\sigma x)-2F_{a}(\sigma x)+1&\mbox{if $x_{1}=1$},\\
aM_{1,a}(\sigma x)+F_{a}(\sigma x)-1&\mbox{if $x_{1}=2$},\end{cases}

and for
k\geq 2
,

(3.3)

M_{k,a}(x)=\begin{cases}aM_{k,a}(\sigma x)+kM_{k-1,a}(\sigma x)&\mbox{if $x_{1}=0$ or $2$},\\
(1-2a)M_{k,a}(\sigma x)-2kM_{k-1,a}(\sigma x)&\mbox{if $x_{1}=1$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The functional equation for
F_{a}
is a restatement of (
1.1
). The others follow from it by repeated differentiation of (
3.1
) with respect to
a
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Symmetry)
.

Let
I=[x_{l},x_{r}]=[j3^{-n},(j+1)3^{-n}]
be a ternary interval. Then for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(3.4)

M_{k,a}(x_{l}+u)-M_{k,a}(x_{l})=M_{k,a}(x_{r})-M_{k,a}(x_{r}-u)\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in[0,3^{-n}]$}.

In particular, for each
k\geq 1
we have

M_{k,a}(1-x)=-M_{k,a}(x)\qquad\mbox{for all $x\in[0,1]$}.

Proof.

Note that
M_{0,a}=F_{a}
satisfies (
3.4
). Hence, by differentiating repeatedly with respect to
a
, we see that
M_{k,a}
satisfies this property for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The second statement follows from the first since, by the functional equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) and using
F_{a}(0)=0
and
F_{a}(1)=1
, it follows easily by induction that
M_{k,a}(0)=M_{k,a}(1)=0
for all
k\geq 1
.
∎

The following explicit expression for
F_{a}(x)
was first given in
[
19
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
x=(0.x_{1}x_{2}\dots)_{3}
, we have for
F_{a}(x)
the explicit expression

(3.5)

F_{a}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^{n-l_{n}(x)}(1-2a)^{l_{n}(x)}q(x_{n+1}),

where
q(0)=0,q(1)=a
and
q(2)=1-a
.

Define the closed intervals

I_{n,j}:=[j3^{-n},(j+1)3^{-n}],\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad j=0,1,\dots,3^{n}-1.

Further, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in(0,1)
, let
I_{n}(x)
denote the ternary interval
I_{n,j}
containing
x
. (If
x=j/3^{n}
, we choose
I_{n}(x):=[j/3^{n},(j+1)/3^{n}]
for definiteness.)

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Write
l_{n}:=l_{n}(x)
for brevity. Differentiating (
3.5
)
k
times with respect to
a
using Leibniz’ generalized product rule gives

(3.6)

M_{k,a}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}=k}\binom{k}{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}\frac{\partial^{i_{1}}}{\partial a^{i_{1}}}a^{n-l_{n}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{i_{2}}}{\partial a^{i_{2}}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{i_{3}}}{\partial a^{i_{3}}}q(x_{n+1}),

where we sum over triples
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})
of nonnegative integers summing to
k
. Assume first that
a\neq 1/2
. Since
|\partial^{i}q(x_{n+1})/\partial a^{i}|\leq 1
for all
i\geq 0
, we can estimate

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}=k}\binom{k}{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}\frac{\partial^{i_{1}}}{\partial a^{i_{1}}}a^{n-l_{n}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{i_{2}}}{\partial a^{i_{2}}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}\cdot\frac{\partial^{i_{3}}}{\partial a^{i_{3}}}q(x_{n+1})\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}=k}\binom{k}{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}\frac{(n-l_{n})!}{(n-l_{n}-i_{1})!}a^{n-l_{n}-i_{1}}\cdot\frac{l_{n}!}{(l_{n}-i_{2})!}|1-2a|^{l_{n}-i_{2}}2^{i_{2}}\cdot 1

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}=k}\binom{k}{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}(n-l_{n})^{i_{1}}a^{n-l_{n}-i_{1}}\cdot l_{n}^{i_{2}}|1-2a|^{l_{n}-i_{2}}2^{i_{2}}\cdot 1^{i_{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad=a^{n-l_{n}}|1-2a|^{l_{n}}\left(\frac{n-l_{n}}{a}+\frac{2l_{n}}{|1-2a|}+1\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq Ca^{n-l_{n}}|1-2a|^{l_{n}}n^{k}\leq Cb^{n}n^{k},

where
C
is a sufficiently large constant,
b:=\max\{a,|1-2a|\}
, and the second-to-last inequality holds for all
n
, since
l_{n}\leq n
.

Now let
x,y\in[0,1]
with
x<y
and take
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
3^{-m-1}\leq|x-y|<3^{-m}
. Suppose first that
x
and
y
belong to the same ternary interval
I_{m,j}
of order
m
. Then
x
and
y
agree in their first
m
ternary digits. Since the terms in the summation (
3.6
) for
n<m
depend only on the first
m
ternary digits of
x
, it follows from the above that

|M_{k,a}(x)-M_{k,a}(y)|\leq 2\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}Cb^{n}n^{k}\leq C^{\prime}b^{m}m^{k}

for some constant
C^{\prime}
depending only on
b
. If instead,
x
and
y
belong to adjacent ternary intervals, say
I_{m,j}
and
I_{m,j+1}
, we let
z=(j+1)3^{-m}
denote the common endpoint of
I_{m,j}
and
I_{m,j+1}
and apply the above estimate to the pairs
(x,z)
and
(z,y)
to get the same inequality but with
2C^{\prime}
in place of
C^{\prime}
. Note that, although
x
and
z
do not have their first
m
ternary digits in common, we have by the symmetry property of Lemma
3.2
that

M_{k,a}(x)-M_{k,a}(z)=M_{k,a}(j3^{-m})-M_{k,a}(j3^{-m}+z-x),

and the points
j3^{-m}
and
j3^{-m}+z-x
do agree in their first
m
digits. Now observe that

b^{m}m^{k}=b^{-1}(3^{m+1})^{\log_{3}b}m^{k}<b^{-1}|x-y|^{-\log_{3}b}\left(\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}\right)^{k}.

Recalling that
\gamma=-\log_{3}b
, this completes the proof for the case
a\neq 1/2
.

When
a=1/2
, we have that

\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial a^{i}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}\bigg|_{a=1/2}=\begin{cases}l_{n}!(-2)^{l_{n}}&\mbox{if $i=l_{n}$},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, the
n
th term in (
3.6
) vanishes when
l_{n}>k
, while for
l_{n}\leq k
we can estimate its absolute value by

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}+i_{3}=k-l_{n}}

\displaystyle\binom{k}{i_{1},l_{n},i_{3}}(n-l_{n})^{i_{1}}\left(1/2\right)^{n-l_{n}-i_{1}}l_{n}!2^{l_{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(1/2\right)^{n}2^{2l_{n}}\frac{k!}{(k-l_{n})!}\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{k-l_{n}}\binom{k-l_{n}}{i_{1}}\big(2(n-l_{n})\big)^{i_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq k!2^{2k}\left(1/2\right)^{n}\big(2(n-l_{n})+1\big)^{k-l_{n}}\leq C_{k}\left(1/2\right)^{n}n^{k}

for a suitably large constant
C_{k}
. The rest of the proof proceeds in the same way as before.
∎

We next develop some explicit expressions for the increments of
M_{k,a}
over ternary rational intervals, as well as bounds on the oscillation over such intervals.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
j=0,1,\dots,3^{n}-1
, define

\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j}):=M_{k,a}\left(\frac{j+1}{3^{n}}\right)-M_{k,a}\left(\frac{j}{3^{n}}\right).

In what follows, we also let
l(i)
denote the number of
1
’s in the ternary representation of
i
for any non-negative integer
i
. We write
0^{\infty}
for the infinite sequence of all
0
’s.

We first consider the case
a\neq 1/2
. The description of the increments of
M_{k,a}
over ternary rational intervals given in the next lemma is central to many of the proofs in this paper.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume
a\neq 1/2
. There is a polynomial
P_{k}(n,l)
in
n
and
l
of the form

P_{k}(n,l)=\{(1-2a)n-l\}^{k}+R_{k}(n,l),

where
R_{k}(n,l)
is a polynomial of degree at most
k-1
in
n
and
l
, such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq i<3^{n}
,

\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,i})=a^{n-l(i)-k}(1-2a)^{l(i)-k}P_{k}(n,l(i)).

(By the degree of a polynomial
R(n,l)
, we mean the maximum value of
p+q
such that
R(n,l)
contains a term
c_{p,q}n^{p}l^{q}
. Although
P_{k}(n,l)
and
R_{k}(n,l)
also depend on the parameter
a
, we suppress this dependence in the notation.)
For example,

\displaystyle R_{1}(n,l)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle R_{2}(n,l)

\displaystyle=-(1-2a)\{(1-2a)n-l\}-2al,

etc.

Proof.

Note that

\Delta_{0,a}(I_{n,i})=a^{n-l(i)}(1-2a)^{l(i)}.

Write
l(i)=l
for short. Differentiating
k
times with respect to
a
gives, using Leibniz’s rule,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial a^{j}}\big(a^{n-l}\big)\cdot\frac{\partial^{k-j}}{\partial a^{k-j}}\big((1-2a)^{l}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-l-j)!}a^{n-l-j}\frac{l!}{(l-k+j)!}(1-2a)^{l-k+j}(-2)^{k-j}

\displaystyle=a^{n-l-k}(1-2a)^{l-k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-l-j)!}a^{k-j}\frac{l!}{(l-k+j)!}(1-2a)^{j}(-2)^{k-j}.

Now

\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-l-j)!}=(n-l)^{j}+\ \mbox{lower order terms}

and similarly,

\frac{l!}{(l-k+j)!}=l^{k-j}+\ \mbox{lower order terms}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,i})

\displaystyle=a^{n-l-k}(1-2a)^{l-k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(n-l)^{j}l^{k-j}(-2a)^{k-j}(1-2a)^{j}+R_{k}(n,l)\right)

\displaystyle=a^{n-l-k}(1-2a)^{l-k}\left(\big\{(n-l)(1-2a)-2al\big\}^{k}+R_{k}(n,l)\right)

\displaystyle=a^{n-l-k}(1-2a)^{l-k}\left(\{(1-2a)n-l\}^{k}+R_{k}(n,l)\right),

for some polynomial
R_{k}(n,l)
of degree at most
k-1
.
∎

For an interval
I
and function
f
, denote

\mathrm{osc}(f;I):=\sup_{x,y\in I}|f(x)-f(y)|.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume
a\neq 1/2
. There is for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
a constant
C_{k}
such that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
j=0,1,\dots,3^{n}-1
,

(3.8)

\mathrm{osc}(M_{k,a};I_{n,j})\leq C_{k}a^{n-l(j)}|1-2a|^{l(j)}n^{k}.

Proof.

This is immediate from the proof of Proposition
2.1
.
∎

For
a=1/2
we have the following explicit expression for the increments over ternary intervals.

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume
0\leq l\leq k\leq n
, and let
j\in\{0,1,\dots,3^{n}-1\}
be such that
l(j)=l
. Then

\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{n,j})=\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\cdot\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-k)!}\left(1/2\right)^{n-k}(-2)^{l}.

Proof.

Starting from (
3.7
), set
a=1/2
and note that since
l\leq k\leq n
, only the term with
j=k-l
does not vanish.
∎

In the next lemma and its proof, we write
M_{k}:=M_{k,1/2}
to avoid clutter.

Lemma 3.7
.

Fix
n\geq 2k-1
, and let
I=[x_{l},x_{r}]=I_{n,j}
be a ternary interval of order
n
. Then

\min\{M_{k}(x_{l}),M_{k}(x_{r})\}\leq M_{k}(x)\leq\max\{M_{k}(x_{l}),M_{k}(x_{r})\}\qquad\forall\,x\in I.

In other words, the graph of
M_{k}
over
I
lies inside the rectangular ‘box’ with opposite vertices
(x_{l},M_{k}(x_{l}))
and
(x_{r},M_{k}(x_{r}))
and edges parallel to the coordinate axes.

Note the conclusion of the lemma fails for
n\leq 2k-2
, as in that case the functional equation shows that
M_{k}(0)=M_{k}(3^{-n})=0
.

Proof.

Since
M_{k}
is continuous, the conclusion will follow by induction if we show that for each
m\geq 2k-1
and each
j\in\{0,1,\dots,3^{m}-1\}
,
M_{k}\big((3j+1)3^{-m-1}\big)
and
M_{k}\big((3j+2)3^{-m-1}\big)
lie between
M_{k}(j3^{-m})
and
M_{k}((j+1)3^{-m})
. By symmetry (cf. Lemma
3.2
), it is enough to show this for
(3j+1)3^{-m-1}
.
Let
l:=l(j)
. Note that

M_{k}\left(\frac{j+1}{3^{m}}\right)-M_{k}\left(\frac{j}{3^{m}}\right)=\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{m,j})

and

M_{k}\left(\frac{3j+1}{3^{m+1}}\right)-M_{k}\left(\frac{j}{3^{m}}\right)=\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{m+1,3j}).

Since
l(3j)=l(j)=l
, Lemma
3.6
shows that
\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{m,j})
and
\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{m+1,3j})
have the same sign, and

\frac{|\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{m+1,3j})|}{|\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{m,j})|}=1/2\cdot\frac{m+1-l}{m+1-k}\leq 1,

since
m\geq 2k-1\geq 2k-l-1
. Hence,
M_{k}\big((3j+1)3^{-m-1}\big)
lies between
M_{k}(j3^{-m})
and
M_{k}((j+1)3^{-m})
, as desired.
∎

4.
Box-counting dimension of the graph

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let
G_{k,a}
denote the graph of
M_{k,a}
.

Step 1: Upper bound.
Assume first that
a\neq 1/2
. For fixed
n
, the number of words
\mathbf{i}\in\{0,1,2\}^{n}
with
l
zeros is

\binom{n}{l}2^{n-l}.

Lemma
3.5
implies that the number of
3^{-n}
-mesh cubes intersecting
G_{k,a}
is bounded above by

\displaystyle N_{3^{-n}}(G_{k,a})

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\left(3^{n}+2\right)n^{k}\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}2^{n-l}a^{n-l}|1-2a|^{l}

\displaystyle=C_{k}\left(3^{n}+2\right)n^{k}\big(2a+|1-2a|\big)^{n},

where
C_{k}
is the constant from Lemma
3.5
.
The stated upper bound follows upon observing that

2a+|1-2a|=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $a<1/2$},\\
4a-1&\mbox{if $a>1/2$}.\end{cases}

If
a=1/2
, then by Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
, we have, for an interval
I_{n,j}
with
l(j)=l
,

\mathrm{osc}(M_{k,1/2};I_{n,j})=|\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{n,j})|=\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\cdot\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-k)!}\left(1/2\right)^{n-k}2^{l},

for
0\leq l\leq k\leq n
. Furthermore, if
l(j)>k
, then
M_{k,1/2}
is constant over
I_{n,j}
and so the graph of
M_{k,1/2}
over
I_{n,j}
intersects only one
3^{-n}
-mesh square. Thus, for
n\geq k
,

\displaystyle N_{3^{-n}}\left(G_{k,1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 3^{n}+\left(3^{n}+2\right)\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{n}{l}2^{n-l}\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\cdot\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-k)!}\left(1/2\right)^{n-k}2^{l}

\displaystyle=3^{n}+\left(3^{n}+2\right)2^{k}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}

\displaystyle\leq 3^{n}+\left(3^{n}+2\right)2^{2k}n^{k},

yielding the desired upper bound of 1.

Step 2: Lower bound.
The lower bound is trivial for
a\leq 1/2
, so let
a>1/2
. Then
1-2a<0
, and so
|(1-2a)n-l|\geq|1-2a|n=(2a-1)n
for all
0\leq l\leq n
. By Lemma
3.4
, there exists therefore a constant
\delta>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,

|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j})|\geq a^{n-l(j)}(2a-1)^{l(j)}\delta n^{k},\qquad\mbox{for all $0\leq j<3^{n}$}.

(Here we have included the factors
a^{-k}
and
(2a-1)^{-k}
in the constant
\delta
.) We may further assume
n
is so large that
\delta n^{k}\geq 1
.
Since the oscillation of
M_{k,a}
over
I_{n,j}
is at least
|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j})|
, the number of
3^{-n}
-mesh squares that intersect the graph of
M_{k,a}
over
I_{n,j}
is at least

3^{n}|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j})|\geq 3^{n}a^{n-l(j)}(2a-1)^{l(j)}.

Summing over
j=0,1,\dots,3^{n}-1
and noting once more that for each
l\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
there are precisely
\binom{n}{l}2^{n-l}
values of
j
with
l(j)=l
, we get

N_{3^{-n}}\left(G_{k,a}\right)\geq 3^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}2^{n-l}a^{n-l}(2a-1)^{l}=3^{n}\big(2a+(2a-1)\big)^{n}=3^{n}(4a-1)^{n}

for all sufficiently large
n
, yielding the desired lower bound.
∎

5.
Finite derivatives

In this section we consider the set of points where
M_{k,a}
has a finite derivative. Throughout,
P_{k}(n,l)
denotes the polynomial from Lemma
3.4
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume
a\neq 1/2
. Let
x\in(0,1)
and put
l_{n}:=l_{n}(x)
. Then, for
k\geq 1
, the sequence
(P_{k}(n,l_{n}))_{n}
cannot have a finite limit as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose, to the contrary, that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{k}(n,l_{n})
exists and is finite. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\big[P_{k}(n+1,l_{n+1})-P_{k}(n,l_{n})\big]=0.

From the functional equations in Lemma
3.1
(or, alternatively, from the precise expressions in the proof of Lemma
3.4
), we can derive the difference equations

(5.1)

\displaystyle P_{k}(n+1,l)-P_{k}(n,l)

\displaystyle=k(1-2a)P_{k-1}(n,l_{n}),

(5.2)

\displaystyle P_{k}(n+1,l+1)-P_{k}(n,l)

\displaystyle=-2kaP_{k-1}(n,l_{n}).

Since
l_{n+1}\in\{l_{n},l_{n}+1\}
, it follows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{k-1}(n,l_{n})=0
.
By induction, we then conclude that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{0}(n,l_{n})=0
. However, by definition,
P_{0}(n,l_{n})=1
and we have a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{l_{n}(x)}{n}-(1-2a)\right|>0.

Then there exists
\delta>0
such that
|P_{k}(n,l_{n}(x))|\geq\delta n^{k}
for all sufficiently large
n
. In particular, this holds when
a>1/2
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Recall that
I_{n}(x)
denotes the ternary interval
I_{n,j}
containing
x
.
Throughout this section, we use the following well-known fact: If
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=d\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\frac{\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))}{|I_{n}(x)|}=3^{n}\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))\to d\qquad\mbox{as $n\to\infty$},

Recall the definition of
\phi(a)
from (
2.1
). It is not difficult to verify that

(5.3)

\displaystyle 0<a<\frac{1}{3}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{1}{3}<\phi(a)<1-2a,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}<a<\frac{1}{2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{1}{3}>\phi(a)>1-2a.

We first collect a few special cases.

Proposition 5.3
.

(a)

If
a\geq 2/3
, then
M_{k,a}
does not have a finite derivative at any point.

(b)

If
a=1/3
and
k\geq 1
, then
M_{k,a}
does not have a finite derivative at any point.

Proof.

Let
x\in(0,1)
and write
l_{n}:=l_{n}(x)
.

(a) Suppose
a\geq 2/3
. Then
3^{n}a^{n-l_{n}}|1-2a|^{l_{n}}\geq 1
for all
n
, and by Lemma
5.2
,
|P_{k}(n,l_{n})|\to\infty
. So by Lemma
3.4
,
3^{n}\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))
does not have a finite limit for any
x
. Hence,
M_{k,a}
does not have a finite derivative at any point.

(b) Suppose
a=1/3
and
k\geq 1
. Then by Lemma
3.4
we have that

3^{n}\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j})=3^{-2k}P_{k}(n,l_{n}),

but this does not have a finite limit by Lemma
5.1
. Hence,
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)
does not exist as a finite number.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

For
k=1
, the conclusion of Proposition
5.3
(b) was proved in
[
10
]
. We observe that this result is quite different from the situation for
k=0
, as

is the identity function.

The case

is also special:

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
a=1/2
and
x\in(0,1)
. Then
M_{k,a}
has a finite derivative at
x
if and only if there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
l_{n}(x)=k+1
and
3^{n}x\not\in\mathbb{Z}
. In that case,
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
.

Proof.

Let
x=(0.x_{1}x_{2}\dots)_{3}
, and first assume the stated condition. It follows from the functional equation (Lemma
3.1
) that the graph of
M_{k,1/2}
is flat on
I_{n}(x)
, and since
x
lies in the interior of this interval,
M_{k,1/2}^{\prime}(x)=0
.

Next, suppose
l:=\sup_{n}l_{n}(x)\leq k
. Then Lemma
3.6
implies that

3^{n}|\Delta_{k,1/2}(I_{n}(x))|=\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\cdot\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-k)!}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{n}2^{k+l}\to\infty,

so
M_{k,1/2}^{\prime}(x)
does not exist as a finite real number.

Finally, if
l_{n}(x)=k+1
and
3^{n}x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we can assume, by choosing
n
minimal with this property, that
x_{n}=1
. Put
\bar{x}:=1-x
. Then the terminating ternary expansion of
\bar{x}
contains exactly
k

1
’s, because we can alternatively write
x=(0.x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}02^{\infty})_{3}
. So by the previous case,
M_{k,1/2}
does not have a finite derivative at
\bar{x}
. But then, by symmetry (Lemma
3.2
), it does not have one at
x
either.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

For each
k\geq 1
,
M_{k,1/2}
is differentiable almost everywhere.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.5
,
M_{k,1/2}^{\prime}(x)=0
whenever the ternary expansion of
x
contains infinitely many
1
’s. This is the case for Lebesgue-almost all
x\in(0,1)
.
∎

We now get to the main case:
0<a<2/3
and
a\not\in\{1/3,1/2\}
. We divide this case in two parts. For both, we introduce the constant

(5.5)

C_{0}:=C_{0}(a):=\frac{1}{\log a-\log|1-2a|}.

Note that
C_{0}<0
for
a<1/3
, whereas
C_{0}>0
for
a>1/3
. Furthermore,
C_{0}(a)
tends to
-\infty
as
a\nearrow 1/3
, to
+\infty
as
a\searrow 1/3
, and to
0
as
a\to 1/2
.

The next two propositions show that the only possible finite value of
M_{k,a}^{\prime}
is
0
, and characterize exactly the set of points where this happens.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
1/3<a<2/3
and
a\neq 1/2
. For
x\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, set

r_{n}:=l_{n}(x)-n\phi(a).

Then
M_{k,a}
has a finite derivative at
x
if and only if

(5.6)

r_{n}-kC_{0}\log n\to\infty\qquad\mbox{as $n\to\infty$},

in which case
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
.

Proof.

First assume (
5.6
). Let
y\neq x
be arbitrary and choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that
3^{-(n+1)}<|x-y|<3^{-n}
. Let
j
be the integer such that
x\in I_{n,j}
; then
y\in I_{n,j-1}\cup I_{n,j}\cup I_{n,j+1}
. Since
l(j-1)=l(j)\pm 1
and
l(j+1)=l(j)\pm 1
, Lemma
3.5
and the triangle inequality yield that

(5.7)

\left|\frac{M_{k,a}(x)-M_{k,a}(y)}{x-y}\right|\leq 3^{n+1}(2B_{k})a^{n-l_{n}(x)}|1-2a|^{l_{n}(x)}n^{k},

where
B_{k}:=C_{k}a/|1-2a|
. The definition of
\phi(a)
is equivalent to
3a^{1-\phi(a)}|1-2a|^{\phi(a)}=1
, and so

(5.8)

3^{n}a^{n-l_{n}(x)}|1-2a|^{l_{n}(x)}n^{k}=\left(\frac{|1-2a|}{a}\right)^{r_{n}}n^{k}=e^{-r_{n}/C_{0}}n^{k}\to 0,

as can be seen by taking logarithms. Hence,
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
.

Conversely, suppose
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=d\in\mathbb{R}
. Then certainly
3^{n}\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))\to d
. Write
l_{n}:=l_{n}(x)
for short. We consider two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose
\liminf\big|\frac{l_{n}}{n}-(1-2a)\big|>0
. Then by Lemma
5.2
there exists
\delta>0
such that for all large enough
n
,
|P_{k}(n,l_{n})|\geq\delta n^{k}
. It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|P_{k}(n+1,l_{n+1})|}{|P_{k}(n,l_{n})|}=1,

since the difference equations (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) imply that
P_{k}(n+1,l_{n+1})-P_{k}(n,l_{n})=o(n^{k-1})
. Since
|1-2a|<1/3
for
1/3<a<2/3
, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that

3|1-2a|(1+\varepsilon)<1<3a(1-\varepsilon),

and then choose
n_{0}
so large that

1-\varepsilon<\frac{|P_{k}(n+1,l_{n+1})|}{|P_{k}(n,l_{n})|}<1+\varepsilon\qquad\forall n\geq n_{0}.

Now

\frac{3^{n+1}|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n+1}(x))|}{3^{n}|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))|}=3a^{1-(l_{n+1}-l_{n})}|1-2a|^{l_{n+1}-l_{n}}\cdot\frac{|P_{k}(n+1,l_{n+1})|}{|P_{k}(n,l_{n})|},

and since
3a^{1-(l_{n+1}-l_{n})}|1-2a|^{l_{n+1}-l_{n}}=3a
or
3|1-2a|
, it follows that the above ratio cannot converge to
1
. Hence,
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=d=0
. But then, for all large enough
n
,

\delta\left(\frac{|1-2a|}{a}\right)^{r_{n}}n^{k}=3^{n}a^{n-l_{n}}|1-2a|^{l_{n}}\delta n^{k}\leq 3^{n}\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))\to 0,

and taking logarithms yields (
5.6
).

Case 2.
Suppose alternatively that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{l_{n}}{n}=1-2a
. This is of course only possible if
a<1/2
.
Note by (
5.4
) that
1-2a<\phi(a)
, and since
a>|1-2a|
, it follows that

3^{n}a^{n-l_{n}}|1-2a|^{l_{n}}\to\infty.

Further, by Lemma
5.1
,
P_{k}(n,l_{n})
cannot tend to
0
. Therefore,
3^{n}|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))|
cannot have a finite limit as
n\to\infty
, contradicting that
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=d\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

.

Let
0<a<1/3
. let

The proof is analogous to that of Proposition

Together, Propositions

Corollary 5.9
.

Let

and define

\lambda_{*}(x):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{l_{n}(x)}{n},\qquad\lambda^{*}(x):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{l_{n}(x)}{n}.

(a)

If
0<a<1/3
, then

\{x:\lambda^{*}(x)<\phi(a)\}\subset D_{k,a}^{0}\subset\{x:\lambda^{*}(x)\leq\phi(a)\}.

(b)

If
1/3<a<2/3
, then

\{x:\lambda_{*}(x)>\phi(a)\}\subset D_{k,a}^{0}\subset\{x:\lambda_{*}(x)\geq\phi(a)\}.

Proof.

Let
0<a<1/3
and
\lambda^{*}<\phi(a)
. Then
r_{n}=l_{n}(x)-\phi(a)n\leq-\delta n
for some fixed
\delta>0
and all large enough

, so certainly
r_{n}+k|C_{0}|\log n\to-\infty
. On the other hand, if
\lambda^{*}>\phi(a)
, then there is a subsequence
(n_{i})
and a
\delta>0
such that

for all

. Hence (a) follows from Proposition

. The proof of (b) is analogous.

Theorem 5.10
.

For
a\in(0,2/3)\backslash\big\{1/3\big\}
and any
k\geq 1
there exist uncountably many points
x
such that
M^{\prime}_{k,a}(x)=0
but
M_{k+1,a}
does not have a finite derivative at
x
. Moreover, the set of such points has positive Hausdorff dimension.

Proof.

We first consider the case
a=1/2
. For
x\in(0,1)
, let
l(x):=\sup_{n}l_{n}(x)
. If
x
is not a ternary rational point, then the condition of Proposition
5.5
simplifies to
l(x)\geq k+1
. Thus, the set difference
D_{k,1/2}^{0}\backslash D_{k+1,1/2}^{0}
is, up to a countable set of endpoints, equal to the set of points
x
such that
l(x)=k+1
. This set has Hausdorff dimension
\log_{3}2
, as it is a countable union of affine copies of the ternary Cantor set.

Next, we assume
0<a<1/3
. Recall that
C_{0}<0
in this case.
Fix an arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1)
. We construct a point
x
with the desired property by prescribing the positions of the 1’s in its ternary expansion. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

b_{n}:=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\phi(a)}-\frac{k+\delta}{\log 3a}\log n\right\rfloor,

and put
x:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}d_{j}3^{-j}
, where

(5.9)

d_{j}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $j=b_{n}$ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}$},\\
0\ \mbox{or}\ 2\ \mbox{arbitrarily}&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We first verify that
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
. For all large enough
n
,

\displaystyle l_{b_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle-\phi(a)b_{n}+k|C_{0}|\log b_{n}=n-\phi(a)b_{n}+k|C_{0}|\log b_{n}

\displaystyle\leq n-\phi(a)\left(\frac{n}{\phi(a)}-\frac{k+\delta}{\log 3a}\log n-1\right)+k|C_{0}|\log\left(\frac{n}{\phi(a)}-\frac{(k+\delta)}{\log 3a}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\phi(a)\left(\frac{k+\delta}{\log 3a}\log n+1\right)+k|C_{0}|\log\left(\frac{2n}{\phi(a)}\right)

\displaystyle=-(k+\delta)|C_{0}|\log n+\phi(a)+k|C_{0}|\{\log n+\log 2-\log\phi(a)\}

\displaystyle=-\delta|C_{0}|\log n+\phi(a)+k|C_{0}|\{\log 2-\log\phi(a)\}\to-\infty.

Here the second-to-last step follows from the definitions of
\phi(a)
and
C_{0}
.
Now if
j\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary, choose
n
so that
b_{n-1}<j\leq b_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle l_{j}(x)-\phi(a)j+k|C_{0}|\log j

\displaystyle\leq l_{b_{n}}(x)-\phi(a)b_{n-1}+k|C_{0}|\log b_{n}

\displaystyle\leq l_{b_{n}}(x)-\phi(a)b_{n}+k|C_{0}|\log b_{n}+\phi(a)\{b_{n}-b_{n-1}\}

\displaystyle\to-\infty,

because
b_{n}-b_{n-1}
is bounded in
n
. Thus, we have verified the condition of Proposition
5.8
. A similar calculation, which we omit, shows that
l_{j}(x)-\phi(a)j+(k+1)|C_{0}|\log j\to+\infty
. Hence,
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
but
M_{k+1,a}^{\prime}(x)
does not exist as a finite real number.

It is not difficult to show that the set of points
x
whose ternary expansion is given by (
5.9
) has Hausdorff dimension
(1-\phi(a))\log_{3}2>0
. Hence, the set of points for which
M_{k,a}^{\prime}(x)=0
but
M_{k+1,a}
does not have a finite derivative at
x
has at least this dimension. We leave the details to the interested reader.

Finally, the case where
1/3<a<2/3,a\neq 1/2
can be dealt with in a similar way; we omit the details.
∎

Proof of Corollary
2.5
.

That the differences
D_{k,a}^{0}\backslash D_{k+1,a}^{0}
are descending in
k
for each

follows immediately from Propositions

,
5.7
and

.

6.
Infinite derivatives:
a=1/2

In this section we describe the infinite derivatives of
M_{k,1/2}
. Since the parameter
a
will be fixed at
1/2
, we drop it from the notation and write simply
M_{k}
instead of
M_{k,1/2}
.

For
x\in[0,1]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\in\{0,2\}
, let

(6.1)

\rho_{n}(x;d):=\max\{m\geq 0:x_{n+1}=\dots=x_{n+m}=d\}

be the run length of consecutive digits
d
starting at the
(n+1)
th ternary digit of
x
, where we make the convention that the maximum of the empty set is zero.

Theorem 6.1
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
x\in(0,1)
, and let
l:=l(x):=\sup_{n}l_{n}(x)
be the total number of
1
’s in the ternary expansion of
x
.

(a)

If
l>k
, then
M_{k}
does not have an infinite derivative at
x
.

(b)

If
l=k
, then
M_{k}
has an infinite derivative at
x
if and only if Eidswick’s condition holds (see
[
13
]
):

cn-\rho_{n}(x;d)\to\infty,\qquad d=0,2,

where
c:=\log_{2}3-1
. If this is the case,
M_{k}^{\prime}(x)=(-1)^{k}\cdot\infty
.

(c)

If
l<k
, then sufficient for
M_{k}
to have an infinite derivative at
x
is that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\rho_{n}(x;d)\leq\log_{2}n-\log_{2}(k-l)-2\qquad\forall n\geq N,\forall d\in\{0,2\};

and necessary is that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\rho_{n}(x;d)\leq\log_{2}n-\log_{2}(k-l)\qquad\forall n\geq N,\forall d\in\{0,2\}.

Moreover, if
M_{k}
has an infinite derivative at
x
, then
M_{k}^{\prime}(x)=(-1)^{l}\cdot\infty
.

(An exact characterization would involve the entire sequence
(\rho_{n}(x;d))
of run lengths in a complicated way, and even though one can be written down, it is not enlightening and therefore we omit it here. On the other hand, the conditions given above are simple to understand, and are sharp to within a small constant.)

Corollary 6.2
.

For all
l\leq k
, let
F_{k,l}^{\infty}
be the set

F_{k,l}^{\infty}:=\{x\in[0,1]:l(x)=l\ \mbox{and}\ M_{k}^{\prime}(x)=\pm\infty\}.

Then
F_{k,l}^{\infty}
has Hausdorff dimension
s:=\log_{3}2
. Furthermore, letting
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)
denote the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of a set
E
, we have that
\mathcal{H}^{s}(F_{k,l}^{\infty})=0
for
l<k
, whereas
\mathcal{H}^{s}(F_{k,k}^{\infty})>0
. As a result,

\mathcal{H}^{s}(D_{k,1/2}^{+\infty})>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{H}^{s}(D_{k,1/2}^{-\infty})=0\quad\mbox{when $k$ is even},

and

\mathcal{H}^{s}(D_{k,1/2}^{-\infty})=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{H}^{s}(D_{k,1/2}^{+\infty})>0\quad\mbox{when $k$ is odd}.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

(
a
) and (
b
): If
x_{1}\dots x_{n}
contains exactly
k
1’s, including at the
n
th digit, then the graph of
M_{k}
over the interval
[0.x_{1}\dots x_{n},0.x_{1}\dots x_{n}+3^{-n}]
is an affine copy of the Cantor devil’s staircase
F_{1/2}
. This follows by setting
a=1/2
in the functional equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) and iterating: eventually, all higher order partial derivatives will vanish. Thus, (
b
) follows from Eidswick’s theorem
[
13
]
. Moreover, if
(x_{i})
contains at least
k+1
1’s, then
x
lies in one of the flat segments of a devil’s staircase (possibly at an endpoint), so the right derivative of
M_{k}
vanishes at
x
, and this gives (
a
).

(
c
) We assume
l
is even and explore when
M_{k}^{\prime}(x)=+\infty
. Note that
M_{k}^{\prime}(x)
cannot equal
-\infty
because for all sufficiently large
n
(namely, all
n
beyond the position of the last 1), the secant slope of
M_{k}
over the interval
I_{n}(x)=[0.x_{1}\dots x_{n},0.x_{1}\dots x_{n}+3^{-n}]
is positive. Note further that
x
cannot be a ternary rational point, because
M_{k}
has local extrema at such points. Thus,
(x_{i})
does not end in
0^{\infty}
or
2^{\infty}
, and hence
(x_{i})
contains both infinitely many 0’s and infinitely many 2’s.

We first consider whether
M_{k}
has an infinite
right
derivative at
x
. We denote the right derivative by
(M_{k})_{+}^{\prime}
. Let
n_{0}
be the position of the last 1 in
(x_{i})
, and define the set
E:=\{n>n_{0}:x_{n}=0\}
. For each
n\in E
, put
z_{n}:=0.x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}20^{\infty}
. Then
z_{n}>x
and
3^{-n}<z_{n}-x<2\cdot 3^{-n}
. We certainly need that

(6.2)

\frac{M_{k}(z_{n})-M_{k}(x)}{z_{n}-x}\to\infty,

but this is also sufficient: For arbitrary
y>x
, let
n
be the index such that
x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}=y_{1}\dots y_{n-1}
and
x_{n}<y_{n}
. If
y
is close enough to
x
, then
n>n_{0}
, and hence
x_{n}=0
; i.e.
n\in E
. Since
y_{n}=1
or
2
, Lemmas
3.7
and
3.6
imply that
M_{k}(y)\geq M_{k}(z_{n})
, recalling that
l
is even. Thus, if (
6.2
) holds, then
M_{k}^{\prime}(x)=+\infty
.

Take
n>n_{0}
and assume also that
n\geq 2k
. Let
r:=\rho_{n}(x;2)
be the run length of consecutive
2
’s starting with
x_{n+1}
; see (
6.1
). Since the ternary expansion of
x
contains no more 1’s after the
n
th digit,
x
lies between
0.x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}02^{r}0^{\infty}
and
0.x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}02^{r}10^{\infty}
, and so

(6.3)

M_{k}(x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}02^{r}0^{\infty})\leq M_{k}(x)\leq M_{k}(x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}02^{r}10^{\infty})

by Lemma
3.7
, since
l
is even. Hence,

\frac{M_{k}(z_{n})-M_{k}(x)}{z_{n}-x}<3^{n}\big\{M_{k}(x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}20^{\infty})-M_{k}(x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}02^{r}0^{\infty})\big\}.

To calculate the difference in square brackets, we first compute the corresponding difference for
F_{a}
and then differentiate
k
times. Set
\mathbf{i}:=x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}
. Note that

F_{a}(\mathbf{i}20^{\infty})-F_{a}(\mathbf{i}0^{\infty})=a^{n-l-1}(1-2a)^{l}(1-a)\qquad\mbox{for all $n>n_{0}$},

and so

\displaystyle F_{a}(\mathbf{i}20^{\infty})-F_{a}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}0^{\infty})

\displaystyle=a^{n-l-1}(1-2a)^{l}(1-a)-\sum_{j=n+1}^{n+r}a^{j-l-1}(1-2a)^{l}(1-a)

\displaystyle=a^{n-l-1}(1-2a)^{l}\big(1-a-a(1-a^{r})\big)

\displaystyle=a^{n-l-1}(1-2a)^{l+1}+a^{n-l+r}(1-2a)^{l}.

Differentiating
k
times and substituting
a=1/2
gives, after some calculations,

(6.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}M_{k}(\mathbf{i}&20^{\infty})-M_{k}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}0^{\infty})\\
&=\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}(-2)^{l}\left(1/2\right)^{n-k+r}\left[\frac{(n-l+r)!}{(n-k+r)!}-2^{r+1}(k-l)\frac{(n-l-1)!}{(n-k)!}\right].\end{split}

Suppose
r>\log_{2}n-\log_{2}(k-l)
. Then
2^{r+1}(k-l)>2n
, and we claim this implies that the difference in square brackets is negative for all sufficiently large
n
. First we estimate

(6.5)

\frac{(n-l+r)!}{(n-k+r)!}-2^{r+1}(k-l)\frac{(n-l-1)!}{(n-k)!}<(n+r)^{k-l}-2n(n-k+1)^{k-l-1}.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that

(1+\varepsilon)^{k-l}<2(1-\varepsilon)^{k-l-1}.

If
r\geq\varepsilon n
, then for large enough
n
the factor
2^{r+1}
in (
6.5
) will dominate and make the left-hand side negative. So assume
r<\varepsilon n
. Then, for
n
so large that

,

(n+r)^{k-l}<(1+\varepsilon)^{k-l}n^{k-l}<2(1-\varepsilon)^{k-l-1}n^{k-l}<2n(n-k+1)^{k-l-1},

as required. Thus, an infinite derivative at
x
is impossible. This proves the necessity statement in (

).

For the sufficiency statement, observe that
M_{k}(x)\leq M_{k}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}10^{\infty})
, by (
6.3
). Lemma
3.6
gives

M_{k}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}10^{\infty})-M_{k}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}0^{\infty})=(-2)^{l}\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\cdot\frac{(n+r+1-l)!}{(n+r+1-k)!}\left(1/2\right)^{n+r+1-k}.

Subtracting this from (
6.4
) yields, after some manipulation,

M_{k}(\mathbf{i}20^{\infty})-M_{k}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}10^{\infty})=(-2)^{l}\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\left(1/2\right)^{n-k+r+1}A_{k}(n,l,r),

where

A_{k}(n,l,r):=\frac{(n-l+r)!}{(n-k+r+1)!}(n+r+1+l-2k)-2^{r+2}(k-l)\frac{(n-l-1)!}{(n-k)!}.

Since
l
is even, it follows that
M_{k}(\mathbf{i}20^{\infty})-M_{k}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}10^{\infty})>0
if and only if
A_{k}(n,l,r)>0
. We can estimate
A_{k}(n,l,r)
by

A_{k}(n,l,r)>(n-k+r+2)^{k-l-1}(n+r+1+l-2k)-2^{r+2}(k-l)(n-l-1)^{k-l-1}.

Assume
r\leq\log_{2}n-\log_{2}(k-l)-2
. Then
2^{r+2}(k-l)\leq n
. If, in fact,
r\leq 2k-l
, then
2^{r+2}(k-l)(n-l-1)^{k-l-1}=O(n^{k-l-1})
whereas
(n-k+r+2)^{k-l-1}(n+r+1+l-2k)
is of order
n^{k-l}
. Otherwise, we have

\displaystyle(n-k+r+2)^{k-l-1}(n+r+1+l-2k)

\displaystyle>(n+k-l+2)^{k-l-1}(n+1)

\displaystyle>n(n-l-1)^{k-l-1}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{r+2}(k-l)(n-l-1)^{k-l-1}.

Hence, in either case,
A_{k}(n,l,r)>0
,
at least for large enough
n
. Since
A_{k}(n,l,r)
is an integer (recall

and

), it follows that

M_{k}(\mathbf{i}20^{\infty})-M_{k}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}10^{\infty})\geq 2^{l}\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\left(1/2\right)^{n-k+r+1}.

But this implies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty,n\in E}\frac{M_{k}(z_{n})-M_{k}(x)}{z_{n}-x}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{M_{k}(\mathbf{i}20^{\infty})-M_{k}(\mathbf{i}02^{r}10^{\infty})}{2\cdot 3^{-n}}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}3^{n}2^{l-1}\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\left(1/2\right)^{n-k+\rho_{n}(x;2)+1}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}3^{n}2^{l-1}\frac{k!}{(k-l)!}\left(1/2\right)^{n+\log_{2}n}=+\infty.

Hence,
(M_{k})_{+}^{\prime}(x)=+\infty
.

If
l
is odd, all the signs are reversed and we get
(M_{k})_{+}^{\prime}(x)=-\infty
.

Finally, the symmetry property of Lemma
3.2
, applied to the full unit interval (that is,

for all

) yields that
(M_{k})_{-}^{\prime}(x)=(M_{k})_{+}^{\prime}(1-x)
, so the conditions for

to have an infinite

derivative at

derivative at

. Since the expansion of

is obtained from that of

’s.
∎

Proof of Corollary

.

Let
l\leq k
. By Theorem
6.1
,
M_{k}
has an infinite derivative at

whenever

and the run lengths

and

are bounded. Let

be the set of points

for which this is the case, and for

, let

be the set of points

and it follows that

On the other hand, each

is contained in a countable union of affine copies of the ternary Cantor set

, hence

.

Next, observe that

(see
[

), so

is a probability measure on

. In fact, it is the measure that makes the ternary digits of

independent and identically distributed, with digits

and

each having probability

. Let first

. By a refinement of the Borel-Cantelli lemma (see

), the set of

-measure in

\mathcal{H}^{s}(F_{k,l}^{\infty})=0
.

If

The statements about

∎

Lemma 7.2

For each

Proof.

The second statement is immediate from the definition and induction. It follows that the graph of

We now prove inductively that

and

and another to the right of

is even, so

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
(l_{n})

We will need the following linear recursion for

For

)

\displaystyle=a(1-2a)\frac{\partial^{j+1}P_{k}}{\partial a^{j+1}}(n,l)+\{(1-2a)n-l-(1-4a)(k-j)\}\frac{\partial^{j}P_{k}}{\partial a^{j}}(n,l)

By the symmetry argument outlined at the end of the proof of Theorem

Observe that

is unimodal over
(t_{1},t_{2})
. Hence, Lemma
7.8
yields
P_{k}(n,l_{n})\to\infty
, and in fact,

(7.7)

P_{k}(n,l_{n})\geq\delta n^{k/2}\qquad\mbox{for all large enough $n$},

where
\delta>0
is a constant depending only on
a
and
\varepsilon
.

Observe that, if
l_{n}
is replaced with
l_{n}^{\prime}:=l_{n}+c_{n}
for some bounded sequence
(c_{n})
, then (
7.6
) holds also for
l_{n}^{\prime}
in place of
l_{n}
, so the same argument shows that
P_{k}(n,l_{n}+c_{n})\to\infty
as well. Even more is true, namely,

(7.8)

P_{k}(n,l_{n}+c_{n})\sim P_{k}(n,l_{n}).

This is clear if
g(n)\to\pm\infty
. But when
k
is odd,
(g(n))
cannot have a bounded subsequence in view of (
7.6
) since
(t_{1},t_{2})
does not contain
0
. (When
k
is even,
(t_{1},t_{2})
can contain
0
and so
(g(n))
can have a bounded subsequence, but even then (
7.8
) still holds because
q_{k}(0)\neq 0
.)

In fact, the sequence
(c_{n})
may even tend to
\pm\infty
, as long as it does so more slowly than
\sqrt{n}
. In that case, we have, instead of (
7.6
),

\left(\tilde{t}_{1}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\sqrt{n}\leq g(n)\pm c_{n}\leq\left(\tilde{t}_{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\sqrt{n}

for all large enough
n
, and we conclude (
7.8
) in the same way. We will need this fact later on in the proof.

Now let
h>0
be given, and take
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
3^{-n+1}\leq h<3^{-n+2}
. Let
j
and
j^{\prime}
be the integers such that
x\in I_{n,j}
and
x+h\in I_{n,j^{\prime}}
. Then
3\leq j^{\prime}-j\leq 9
. For
i\in\mathbb{N}
, let
l(i)
denote the number of
1
’s in the ternary representation of
i
. Since
|l(i+3^{\nu})-l(i)|=1
for all
i
and
\nu=0,1,2,\dots
, it follows that

(7.9)

|l(i)-l(j)|\leq 4\qquad\mbox{for $i=j,j+1,\dots,j^{\prime}$}.

Define constants

b:=\max\{a,1-2a\},\qquad r:=\min\left\{\frac{a}{1-2a},\frac{1-2a}{a}\right\}.

By Lemma
3.4
, we may write

\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,i})=a^{n-l(i)-k}(1-2a)^{l(i)-k}P_{k}(n,l(i)).

By (
7.9
) and (
7.8
), there is an
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

P_{k}(n,l(i))\geq\frac{1}{2}P_{k}(n,l_{n})\qquad\mbox{for all $j\leq i\leq j^{\prime}$ and all $n>n_{0}$}.

Again using (
7.9
), it follows from (
7.7
) that

(7.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,i})&\geq\frac{1}{2}r^{4}a^{n-l_{n}-k}(1-2a)^{l_{n}-k}P_{k}(n,l_{n}),\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}r^{4}a^{n-l_{n}-k}(1-2a)^{l_{n}-k}\delta n^{k/2},\qquad i=j,\dots,j^{\prime}.\end{split}

We have established that the increments over the ternary intervals of order
n
between
x
and
x+h
are large. Furthermore,
x
and
x+h
are separated by at least one full ternary interval of order
n
. Next, we must make sure that the oscillations of
M_{k,a}
over
I_{n,j}
and
I_{n,j^{\prime}}
are not too much bigger than the increments over these intervals, so that
M_{k,a}(x)
is not too much bigger than
M_{k,a}((j+1)3^{-n})
and
M_{k,a}(x+h)
is not too much smaller than
M_{k,a}(j^{\prime}3^{-n})
. Unfortunately, we do not have an analogue of Lemma
3.7
for
a<1/2
, so the analysis is by necessity somewhat more technical.

Let
m:=m_{n}:=\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor
, and note that for any
y\in J:=\bigcup_{i=j}^{j^{\prime}}I_{n,i}
, we have
|l_{n+m_{n}}(y)-l_{n+m_{n}}(x)|\leq m_{n}
. Hence, by (
7.8
) applied to
n+m_{n}
in place of
n
, we see that

P_{k}(n+m_{n},l_{n+m_{n}}(y))>\frac{1}{2}P_{k}(n+m_{n},l_{n+m_{n}}(x))>0\qquad\forall y\in J.

Thus,

(7.11)

\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n+m,\nu})\geq 0\qquad\mbox{for $\nu=3^{m}j,\dots,3^{m}(j^{\prime}+1)-1$},

i.e., the increments over the ternary intervals of order
n+m
in
J
are nonnegative.
Now we use Lemma
3.5
to estimate, for
0\leq\nu<3^{m}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{osc}(M_{k,a};I_{n+m,3^{m}j+\nu})

\displaystyle\leq a^{n+m-l(3^{m}j+\nu)}(1-2a)^{l(3^{m}j+\nu)}C_{k}(n+m)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}b^{m}C_{k}(n+m)^{k},

where we used that
l(3^{m}j+\nu)\geq l(j)=l_{n}
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathrm{osc}(M_{k,a};I_{n+m,3^{m}j^{\prime}+\nu})

\displaystyle\leq a^{n+m-l(3^{m}j^{\prime}+\nu)}(1-2a)^{l(3^{m}j^{\prime}+\nu)}C_{k}(n+m)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq a^{n-l(j^{\prime})}(1-2a)^{l(j^{\prime})}b^{m}C_{k}(n+m)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq r^{-4}a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}b^{m}C_{k}(n+m)^{k},

where the last inequality used (
7.9
). Since
m\leq n
and
x
lies in one of the intervals
I_{n+m,3^{m}j+\nu}
for
0\leq\nu<3^{m}
, it follows by (
7.11
) that

M_{k,a}(x)\leq M_{k,a}\left(\frac{j+1}{3^{n}}\right)+b^{m}a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}C_{k}2^{k}n^{k},

and likewise, since
x+h
lies in one of the intervals
I_{n+m,3^{m}j^{\prime}+\nu}
for
0\leq\nu<3^{m}
,

M_{k,a}(x+h)\geq M_{k,a}\left(\frac{j^{\prime}}{3^{n}}\right)-r^{-4}b^{m}a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}C_{k}2^{k}n^{k}.

Hence,

\displaystyle M_{k,a}(x+h)-M_{k,a}(x)

\displaystyle\geq M_{k,a}\left(\frac{j^{\prime}}{3^{n}}\right)-M_{k,a}\left(\frac{j+1}{3^{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-(1+r^{-4})b^{m}a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}C_{k}2^{k}n^{k}

\displaystyle\geq\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j+1})-(1+r^{-4})b^{m}a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}C_{k}2^{k}n^{k},

where we used that
j^{\prime}\geq j+3
and
\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,i})\geq 0
for
i=j,\dots,j^{\prime}
. Finally, applying (
7.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle M_{k,a}(x+h)-M_{k,a}(x)

\displaystyle\geq a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}\left\{1/2r^{4}\delta n^{k/2}-(1+r^{-4})b^{m}C_{k}2^{k}n^{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}n^{k/2}\cdot\frac{1}{4}r^{4}\delta,

for all sufficiently large
n
, since
b^{m_{n}}n^{k/2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. (Recall
m_{n}=\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor
.)
Note that (
7.2
) holds in view of (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), because the hypothesis
\tilde{t}_{1}<\delta_{*}\leq\delta^{*}<\tilde{t}_{2}
implies that
l_{n}(x)/n\to 1-2a
.
Recalling that
h<3^{-n+2}
, we conclude that

(7.12)

\frac{M_{k,a}(x+h)-M_{k,a}(x)}{h}\geq 3^{n-2}a^{n-l_{n}}(1-2a)^{l_{n}}n^{k/2}\cdot\frac{1}{4}r^{4}\delta^{k}\to\infty

as
n\to\infty
. This proves (
b
). For (
a
) and (
c
), observe that the hypotheses do not imply that
l_{n}(x)/n\to 1-2a
, so we additionally assume (
7.2
) to ensure (
7.12
).

It remains to prove (
d
). Suppose
\delta_{*}<\tilde{t}_{i}<\delta^{*}
for some
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. Then, as can be seen from the above argument,
P_{k}(n,l_{n})
oscillates around
0
infinitely many times, and hence, so does
\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))
. Therefore,
M_{k,a}
cannot have an infinite derivative at
x
.
∎

7.1.
The dimension of
D_{k,a}^{\pm\infty}

We can now prove Theorem
2.7
. Recall that
d(a)=h(\phi(a))
and
\tilde{d}(a)=h(1-2a)
. It follows from (
5.3
), (
5.4
), and the graph of
h
that
\tilde{d}(a)\leq d(a)
, with equality if and only if
a=1/3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

We sketch the main idea of the proof. Suppose
0<a<1/3
. By Theorem
7.1
(
a
),
D_{k,a}^{+\infty}
is contained in the set
\{x:\lambda_{*}(x)\geq\phi(a)\}
, and since
\phi(a)>1/3
, this set has dimension
h(\phi(a))=d(a)
by
[
2
, Lemma 4.2]
, which generalizes a well-known theorem of Eggleston
[
12
]
. On the other hand, for each sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
,
D_{k,a}^{+\infty}
contains the set
\{x:l_{n}(x)/n\to\phi(a)+\varepsilon\}
, which has dimension
h(\phi(a)+\varepsilon)
. So, letting
\varepsilon\to 0
we find that
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}\geq h(\phi(a))
. If in addition
k
is odd, then
D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
can similarly be compared to the sets
\{x:\lambda_{*}(x)\geq 1-2a\}
and
\{x:l_{n}(x)/n\to 1-2a+\varepsilon\}
, so in the same way it follows that
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{-\infty}=h(1-2a)
. (We note that the sets

\{x:\phi(a)<\lambda_{*}(x)\leq\lambda^{*}(x)<1-2a\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\{x:\lambda_{*}(x)>\phi(a)\}

have the same Hausdorff dimension. The reason is, that the dimension is already determined by the set of points for which the frequency of
1
’s is “near”
\phi(a)
, so the extra condition
\lambda^{*}(x)<1-2a
is irrelevant.)

The proofs of (b) and (c) are similar.
∎

We do not know the dimension of
D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
when
k
is even, but we know from Theorem
7.3
that this set is not empty. For
k=2
we sketch a proof here that
D_{2,a}^{-\infty}
actually has fairly large dimension. We suspect this to be the case whenever
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, whereas for
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
the set
D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
seems to be much smaller.

An upper bound for
\dim_{H}D_{2,a}^{-\infty}
is easy to come by: It follows from Example
7.4
that

D_{2,a}^{-\infty}\subset\left\{x:\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|(1-2a)n-l_{n}(x)|}{\sqrt{n}}\leq c_{0}\right\}\subset\left\{x:\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{l_{n}(x)}{n}=1-2a\right\},

and so
\dim_{H}D_{2,a}^{-\infty}\leq h(1-2a)=\tilde{d}(a)
. To obtain a lower bound, observe that by Example
7.4
,
D_{2,a}^{-\infty}
contains for each
c<c_{0}
the set

E_{c}:=\{x:|l_{n}-(1-2a)n|\leq c\sqrt{n}\ \mbox{for all $n$}\}.

Let

E:=\bigcup_{c<c_{0}}E_{c}=\bigcup_{c<c_{0},c\in\mathbb{Q}}E_{c}.

We will now construct a lower estimate for the dimension of
E
. When
a=1/3
, an obvious estimate can be obtained by considering the set of points
x
whose ternary expansion has a
1
precisely in every third place, and
0
’s or
2
’s arbitrarily everywhere else. This set has Hausdorff dimension
\log 4/\log 27
, and on this set,
|l_{n}-(1-2a)n|
is in fact bounded. This can be extended to arbitrary rational
a
. To get a better estimate, and also cover the case of irrational
a
, we need to introduce more randomness, though not “too much”. More precisely, we look for a probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
with large Hausdorff dimension that gives full measure to
E
. A natural candidate is the measure
\mu
which makes the ternary digits
\{x_{i}\}
of a point
x\in[0,1]
independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables taking the values
0,1
and
2
with probabilities
a,1-2a
and
a
, respectively. By the Strong Law of Large Numbers,
l_{n}(x)/n\to 1-2a
with
\mu
-probability 1. Put
\xi_{i}=1
if
x_{i}=1
, and
\xi_{i}=0
otherwise; then
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. random variables under
\mu
with mean
1-2a
and variance
\sigma^{2}:=2a(1-2a)
, and
l_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
. The Law of the Iterated Logarithm (LIL - see
[
8
, Theorem 9.5]
) implies that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{l_{n}-(1-2a)n}{\sigma\sqrt{2n\log\log n}}=1,\qquad\mu-\mbox{a.s.},

and so, with probability one,
l_{n}-(1-2a)n>2\sqrt{a(1-2a)n}>c_{0}\sqrt{n}
infinitely often. Hence
\mu(E)=0
, which is not what we wanted. The problem is, essentially, that the i.i.d. digit model contains “too much” randomness. To overcome this, we replace
\mu
with a Markov measure
\mu_{p}
, where
p
is a parameter whose value we will choose later. Unfortunately, in what follows we need to impose a restriction on
a
and assume
1/8\leq a\leq 3/8
.

Consider the Markovian matrix

\mathcal{P}=[p_{ij}]_{i,j=0}^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{1-r}{2}&\displaystyle r&\displaystyle\frac{1-r}{2}\\
\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{2}&\displaystyle p&\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{2}\\
\displaystyle\frac{1-r}{2}&\displaystyle r&\displaystyle\frac{1-r}{2}\end{bmatrix},

where
p,r\in[0,1]
. We consider
p
an arbitrary parameter and choose
r
so that the vector
\mathbf{a}:=[a_{0}\ \ a_{1}\ \ a_{2}]:=[a\ \ 1-2a\ \ a]
is an invariant measure; that is,
\mathbf{a}\mathcal{P}=\mathbf{a}
. This gives

(7.13)

r=\frac{(1-2a)(1-p)}{2a},

assuming this quantity lies between
0
and
1
. We now let
\mu_{p}
be the Markov measure such that
\mu_{p}(x_{1}=i)=a_{i}
and
\mu_{p}(x_{n+1}=j|x_{n}=i)=p_{ij}
, for
i,j=0,1,2
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By standard theory,
\mu_{p}
has entropy

\displaystyle H(a,p):

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=0}^{2}a_{i}\sum_{j=0}^{2}p_{ij}\log p_{ij}

\displaystyle=-2a(1-r)\log\left(\frac{1-r}{2}\right)-2ar\log r

\displaystyle\qquad-(1-2a)(1-p)\log\left(\frac{1-p}{2}\right)-(1-2a)p\log p,

and so
\mu_{p}
has Hausdorff dimension

\dim_{H}\mu_{p}=\frac{H(a,p)}{\log 3}.

(We recall that the Hausdorff dimension of a probability measure
\mu
is defined by

\dim_{H}\mu:=\inf\{\dim_{H}F:F\ \mbox{is a Borel set with}\ \mu(F)=1\}.)

We wish to choose
p
so that
\mu_{p}
gives full measure to
E
. Note that under
\mu_{p}
, the random variables
\{\xi_{n}\}
are no longer independent, so the process
\{l_{n}\}
is not a classical random walk, but rather, a
correlated random walk
. We derive a LIL for
\{l_{n}\}
by modifying the proof of
[
1
, Proposition 6.3]
, as follows. Put

\displaystyle\tau_{1}:=\inf\{n\in\mathbb{N}:x_{n}=1\},

\displaystyle\tau_{2m}:=\inf\{n>\tau_{2m-1}:x_{n}\neq 1\},\qquad m\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\tau_{2m+1}:=\inf\{n>\tau_{2m}:x_{n}=1\},\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
T_{m}:=\tau_{m}-\tau_{m-1}
and
U_{m}:=\tau_{2m}-\tau_{2m-2}=T_{2m-1}+T_{2m}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For
m\geq 2
,
T_{m}
has a geometric distribution with parameter
1-p
if
m
is even, and with parameter
r
if
m
is odd. Furthermore,
T_{1},T_{2},\dots
are independent. Thus, for
m\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T_{2m})=\frac{1}{1-p},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(T_{2m})=\frac{p}{(1-p)^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T_{2m-1})=\frac{1}{r},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(T_{2m-1})=\frac{1-r}{r^{2}},

where all expectations and variances are with respect to
\mu_{p}
. From this,

(7.14)

\mathbb{E}(U_{m})=\frac{1}{1-p}+\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{(1-2a)(1-p)}.

Now observe that
l_{n}-(2a-1)n
attains its local maxima at the times
n=\tau_{2m}-1
, and
l_{\tau_{2m}}-l_{\tau_{2m}-1}
is bounded. Thus, it suffices to develop a LIL for the process
S_{m}:=l_{\tau_{2m}}
. We can express
S_{m}-\mathbb{E}(S_{m})
as a sum of i.i.d. random variables by
S_{m}=Z_{1}+\dots+Z_{m}
, where

\displaystyle Z_{m}:

\displaystyle=l_{\tau_{2m}}-l_{\tau_{2m-2}}-(1-2a)(\tau_{2m}-\tau_{2m-2})

\displaystyle=\tau_{2m}-\tau_{2m-1}-(1-2a)(\tau_{2m}-\tau_{2m-2})

\displaystyle=2a(\tau_{2m}-\tau_{2m-1})-(1-2a)(\tau_{2m-1}-\tau_{2m-2})

\displaystyle=2aT_{2m}-(1-2a)T_{2m-1}.

This shows that
\mathbb{E}(Z_{m})=0
and, after some calculation,

\mathrm{Var}(Z_{m})=4a^{2}\mathrm{Var}(T_{2m})+(1-2a)^{2}\mathrm{Var}(T_{2m-1})=\frac{2a(4a-1+p)}{(1-p)^{2}}=:s^{2},

where we used the independence of
T_{2m-1}
and
T_{2m}
and substituted the expression (
7.13
) for
r
. It follows from the classical LIL that

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{l_{\tau_{2m}}-(1-2a)\tau_{2m}}{\sqrt{2s^{2}m\log\log m}}=1\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

Let
v(m):=\sqrt{2m\log\log m}
. Since
\tau_{2m}=U_{1}+\dots+U_{m}
and
U_{2},\dots,U_{m}
are i.i.d. with mean given by (
7.14
), the SLLN implies

\frac{v(\tau_{2m})}{v(m)}\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-2a)(1-p)}}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

Hence,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{l_{n}-(1-2a)n}{v(n)}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{l_{\tau_{2m}}-(1-2a)\tau_{2m}}{v(\tau_{2m})}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{l_{\tau_{2m}}-(1-2a)\tau_{2m}}{sv(m)}\cdot\frac{sv(m)}{v(\tau_{2m})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2a(1-2a)(4a-1+p)}{1-p}}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

The corresponding
\liminf
can be dealt with in a similar way.
Therefore, in order that
\mu_{p}(E)=1
, we need to choose
p
so that

\sqrt{\frac{4a(1-2a)(4a-1+p)}{1-p}}<c_{0}=\sqrt{2a(1-2a)},

i.e.

p<p_{c}(a):=1-\frac{8a}{3}.

This is possible if and only if
a<3/8
. Furthermore, substituting
p=p_{c}(a)
into (
7.13
), the requirement that
r\leq 1
is equivalent to

. Under these restrictions on

8.
Infinite derivatives:
a>1/2

The situation regarding infinite derivatives is very different when
a>1/2
. The reason is, that if the ternary expansion of
x
has infinitely many
1
’s, then the increments
\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))
change sign infinitely many times in view of Lemma
3.4
, because
1-2a<0
. Therefore, the only points at which there can possibly be an infinite derivative are those whose ternary expansion has only a finite number of
1
’s. This condition is necessary, but not sufficient, as we will see below. Contrary to the case
a<1/2
studied in the previous section, the polynomial
P_{k}(n,l)
behaves much more regularly here: Depending on the parity of
k
, there is an integer
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that either
P_{k}(n,l)>0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all
l\leq n
, or
P_{k}(n,l)>0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all
l\leq n
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Assume
a>1/2
. There is an integer
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n>n_{0}
, the signs of
\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j})
alternate: that is,

\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j+1}))=-\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j})),\qquad j=0,1,\dots,3^{n}-2.

Furthermore, for each
n>n_{0}
and
0\leq j<3^{n}
, we have that

(8.1)

|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n+1,3j})|=|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n+1,3j+2})|>|\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n+1,3j+1})|.

Thus, for all sufficiently large
n
, the
n
th piecewise linear approximation of
M_{k,a}
follows the same ‘zig zag’ pattern as Okamoto’s function itself.

Proof.

Observe that
(1-2a)n-l<0
for all
n
and
l
, and its absolute value increases with
l
. Thus,
|(1-2a)n-l|^{k}\geq n^{k}(2a-1)^{k}
. Furthermore, there is a constant
C>0
such that
R_{k}(n,l)\leq Cn^{k-1}
for all
n
and all
l\leq n
, where
R_{k}(n,l)
is the polynomial from Lemma
3.4
. Put

n_{0}:=\frac{C}{(2a-1)^{k}}.

Then
|(1-2a)n-l|^{k}>R_{k}(n,l)
for all
n>n_{0}
and all
l\leq n
, so for
n>n_{0}
the sign of
P_{k}(n,l)
is the same as the sign of
\{(1-2a)n-l\}^{k}
; that is,
P_{k}(n,l)>0
if
k
is even, and
P_{k}(n,l)<0
if
k
is odd. Therefore, the sign of
\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j})
is equal to
(-1)^{k}
times the sign of
(1-2a)^{l(j)}
. Since
|l(j+1)-l(j)|=1
for all
j
, this proves the alternating signs statement. In addition, we see that
|P_{k}(n,l)|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, uniformly in
l
.

Next, since
a>1/2
implies
a>2a-1=|1-2a|
, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
so that

\frac{a}{|1-2a|}>1+\varepsilon,

and then we can find
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}<\frac{P_{k}(n,l+1)}{P_{k}(n,l)}<1+\varepsilon\qquad\forall n>n_{1},\ \forall l\leq n.

Together with Lemma
3.4
, this establishes (
8.1
), because

\frac{\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n+1,3j+1})}{\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n+1,3j})}=\frac{|1-2a|}{a}\cdot\frac{P_{k}(n+1,l(j)+1)}{P_{k}(n+1,l(j))}.

∎

We see from the above proof that for all large enough
n
,
P_{k}(n,l)
has the same sign as
(1-2a)^{-k}
. Hence,
\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n,j})
has the same sign as
(1-2a)^{l(j)}
for all large enough
n
.

For
d,\xi\in\{0,1,2\}
, define

\delta_{d}(\xi):=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $\xi=d$},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proposition 8.2
.

Assume
a>1/2
. Let
x=(0.x_{1}x_{2}\dots)_{3}
, and assume that
l:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}l_{n}(x)<\infty
. Put

L_{a}^{+}(x):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a^{j}\delta_{2}(x_{n+j}).

In order that
M_{k,a}
have an infinite right derivative at
x
it is sufficient that
L_{a}^{+}(x)<1
, and it is necessary that
L_{a}^{+}(x)\leq 1
. Furthermore, if
M_{k,a}
has an infinite right derivative at
x
, then
(M_{k,a})_{+}^{\prime}(x)=(-1)^{l}\cdot\infty
.

Proof.

Let
N_{0}
be the position of the last 1 in
(x_{i})
, and put
E:=\{n>N_{0}:x_{n}=0\}
. For each
n\in E
, put
z_{n}:=0.x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}20^{\infty}
. Then
z_{n}>x
and
3^{-n}<z_{n}-x<2\cdot 3^{-n}
. As in the proof of Theorem
6.1
, in order that
M_{k,a}
has an infinite right derivative at
x
, it is necessary and sufficient that

\frac{M_{k,a}(z_{n})-M_{k,a}(x)}{z_{n}-x}\to\pm\infty.

This is a consequence of Lemma
8.1
; we refer to the argument in the above-mentioned proof.

Take
n\in E
. Then Lemma
3.3
implies

F_{a}(z_{n})-F_{a}(x)=a^{n-l-1}(1-a)(1-2a)^{l}-\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}a^{j-l-1}(1-a)(1-2a)^{l}\delta_{2}(x_{j}).

Differentiating
k
times with respect to
a
gives

\displaystyle M_{k,a}

\displaystyle(z_{n})-M_{k,a}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial a^{i}}(a^{n-l-1}-a^{n-l})\cdot\frac{\partial^{k-i}}{\partial a^{k-i}}(1-2a)^{l}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial a^{i}}(a^{j-l-1}-a^{j-l})\cdot\frac{\partial^{k-i}}{\partial a^{k-i}}(1-2a)^{l}\delta_{2}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\left(\pi_{n-l-1,i}a^{n-l-i-1}-\pi_{n-l,i}a^{n-l-i}\right)\pi_{l,k-i}(-2)^{k-i}(1-2a)^{l-k+i}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\left(\pi_{j-l-1,i}a^{j-l-i-1}-\pi_{j-l,i}a^{j-l-i}\right)\pi_{l,k-i}(-2)^{k-i}(1-2a)^{l-k+i}\delta_{2}(x_{j}),

where, for nonnegative integers
n
and
m
with
n\geq m
, we denote

\pi_{n,m}:=\frac{n!}{m!}=n(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1).

After reindexing, the summation over
j
can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}

\displaystyle\left(\pi_{n+m-l-1,i}a^{n+m-l-i-1}-\pi_{n+m-l,i}a^{n+m-l-i}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\pi_{l,k-i}(-2)^{k-i}(1-2a)^{l-k+i}\delta_{2}(x_{n+m}).

Thus,
M_{k,a}(z_{n})-M_{k,a}(x)
takes the form

M_{k,a}(z_{n})-M_{k,a}(x)=a^{n-l-k-1}(1-2a)^{l}\left(c_{k}^{(n)}n^{k}+c_{k-1}^{(n)}n^{k-1}+\dots+c_{1}^{(n)}n+c_{0}^{(n)}\right),

for certain coefficients
c_{k}^{(n)},\dots,c_{0}^{(n)}
that depend on
n
only through
\{\delta_{2}(x_{n+m}):m\in\mathbb{N}\}
and are therefore bounded. In particular,

c_{k}^{(n)}=(1-a)\left(1-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a^{m}\delta_{2}(x_{n+m})\right),

as only the terms with
i=k
include a term
n^{k}
. If
L_{a}^{+}(x)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}a^{m}\delta_{2}(x_{n+m})<1
, then
c_{k}^{(n)}
is bounded away from
0
, and so the sign of
M_{k,a}(z_{n})-M_{k,a}(x)
is eventually determined by the dominant term
c_{k}^{(n)}n^{k}
, so there is a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that

|M_{k,a}(z_{n})-M_{k,a}(x)|\geq a^{n-l-k-1}(1-a)|1-2a|^{l}\varepsilon n^{k}

for all sufficiently large
n\in E
. Since furthermore
z_{n}-x<2\cdot 3^{-n}
and
(3a)^{n}\to\infty
, it follows that
(M_{k,a})_{+}^{\prime}(x)=(-1)^{l}\cdot\infty
.

On the other hand, suppose
L_{a}^{+}(x)>1
. Then there is a subsequence
(n_{i})
and a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that
c_{k}^{(n_{i})}<-(1-a)\varepsilon
for all
i
, which implies that
M_{k,a}(z_{n_{i}})-M_{k,a}(x)
has the opposite sign to
(1-2a)^{l}
for all sufficiently large
i
. If
M_{k,a}
were to have an infinite derivative at
x
, say
+\infty
, we would need to have the secant slope
3^{n}\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n}(x))=3^{n}a^{n-l-k}(1-2a)^{l-k}P_{k}(n,l)
tending to
+\infty
as well. But the sign of
M_{k,a}(z_{n_{i}})-M_{k,a}(x)
is the opposite of the sign of
\Delta_{k,a}(I_{n_{i}}(x))
. Hence,
M_{k,a}
does not have an infinite derivative at
x
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

We prove the statement for
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}
; the proof for
\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{-\infty}
is essentially the same.
Recall that
M_{k,a}
can only have an infinite derivative at
x
if
l:=\sup_{n}l_{n}(x)<\infty
. When this is the case, Proposition
8.2
gives both a necessary and a sufficient condition for
M_{k,a}
to have an infinite right derivative at
x
. Analogously, define

L_{a}^{-}(x):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a^{j}\delta_{0}(x_{n+j}).

Then, in order that
M_{k,a}
have an infinite
left
derivative at
x
, sufficient is that
L_{a}^{-}(x)<1
, and necessary is that
L_{a}^{-}(x)\leq 1
. This follows from Proposition
8.2
and the symmetry
M_{k,a}(1-x)=-M_{k,a}(x)
, or can be proved from scratch in the same way as Proposition
8.2
. Define now the sets

E_{a}:=\left\{x\in(0,1):\sup_{n}l_{n}(x)\in 2\mathbb{N},\ L_{a}^{+}(x)<1\ \mbox{and}\ L_{a}^{-}(x)<1\right\},\qquad\frac{1}{2}<a<1.

Since
L_{a}^{+}(x)
and
L_{a}^{-}(x)
are clearly decreasing in
a
, we have

E_{a}\subset D_{k,a}^{+\infty}\subset\bigcap_{1/2<a^{\prime}<a}E_{a^{\prime}}.

Hence
\dim_{H}E_{a}\leq\dim_{H}D_{k,a}^{+\infty}\leq\dim_{H}E_{a^{\prime}}
for all
1/2<a^{\prime}<a
. Since the function
\beta\mapsto\dim_{H}\mathcal{U}_{\beta}
is continuous (see
[
20
,
6
]
), it therefore suffices to show that

(8.2)

\dim_{H}E_{a}=\frac{-\log a}{\log 3}\dim_{H}\mathcal{U}_{1/a}\qquad\mbox{for all $\frac{1}{2}<a<1$}.

The proof of this fact is given in
[
2
, Section 5]
. (The idea is to apply a transformation
\phi:C\to[0,a/(1-a)]
, where
C
is the ternary Cantor set, by

\phi(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a^{n}\cdot\frac{x_{n}}{2},

where
x_{1}x_{2}\dots
is the ternary expansion of
x
which does not contain the digit 1. Then the condition that
L_{a}^{+}(x)<1
and
L_{a}^{-}(x)<1
is very close to the condition that
\phi(x)\in\mathcal{U}_{1/a}
, so
\phi(C\cap E_{a})
is “close” to
\mathcal{U}_{1/a}
– close enough to have the same Hausdorff dimension. Furthermore, it can be shown that the map
\phi
, restricted to
C\cap E_{a}
, is bi-Hölder continuous with exponent
-\log a/\log 3
. Making these statements precise yields (
8.2
), since
E_{a}
is the countable union of affine images of
C\cap E_{a}
.)
∎
