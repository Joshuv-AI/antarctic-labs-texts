APOE Lipoprotein Particles: Pathophysiology, Therapy, and the Crosstalk in Alzheimer’s Disease and Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Chan Liu, Juan Liu, Yan-Yang Wang, Shang-Fu Xu, Li-Mei Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe APOE4 variant was the strongest genetic risk factor for sporadic Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Individuals with APOE4 have an increased risk of developing the disease at an early age of onset. Similarly, APOE4 carriers are predisposed to high cholesterol levels and tend to have an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD). The global allele frequency of APOE4 was 13.7%, underlining its widespread impact on global human health. Conversely, the relatively rare APOE2 allele was a genetic protective factor against AD and CVD. However, the mechanisms underlying this association remain to be elucidated. The apolipoprotein E (APOE) protein coats lipoprotein particles and mediates lipid transport and metabolism in the peripheral circulation and central nervous system (CNS). Although initial studies causally linked APOE lipoprotein particles (APOE particles) with lipid homeostasis, our understanding of the physiological and pathological effects of APOE particles has extended to amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation and spread, as well as neuroinflammation in AD initiation and progression. Moreover, the most examined functions of APOE particles are reverse cholesterol transport, anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidation, and improvement of endothelial dysfunction in atherosclerotic CVD. This review outlines what is known about the structure and functions of APOE particles, emphasizing their involvement in AD and CVD pathogenesis, while also considering the crosstalk between the peripheral circulation and CNS. In addition, we discuss how these APOE particles act as therapeutic targets.Keywords: APOE, Lipoprotein particle, Alzheimer’s disease, Cardiovascular disease

Introduction

Human ΑPOE is polymorphic with three alleles, ε2, ε3, and ε4, encoding the apolipoprotein E (APOE) isoforms APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4. Compared to the most common APOE3 genotype, the ΑPOE4 variant is the strongest monogenetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (AD), whereas the APOE2 allele has been associated with a lower risk of AD [1, 2]. Similarly, the APOE4 allele predisposes individuals to high cholesterol and has a slightly higher incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) than APOE3 carriers [3], while APOE2 carriers have a 20% lower risk [4]. However, the mechanisms underlying this association of APOE genotype and AD and CVD remain to be elucidated.

In the mammalian central nervous system (CNS), APOE is the primary apolipoprotein produced by astrocytes under physiological conditions and within neurons, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and vascular cells under pathological or certain injury conditions [5]. Approximately 90% of APOE in the plasma is synthesized by liver hepatocytes and to a much lesser extent by macrophages [6, 7]. Notably, APOE is the most abundant apolipoprotein in the CSF and is secreted as high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-like lipoprotein particles [8]. Although about 50% of plasma APOE is on very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), APOE-containing VLDL comprises < 10% of total VLDL [9]. APOE-containing HDL and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) are also minor subspecies of the total plasma HDL and LDL, and they are inversely associated with incident atherosclerotic CVD events [10, 11]. Functionally, APOE lipoprotein particles (APOE particles) were initially identified as triacylglycerol and cholesterol transporters between different cells and as a major ligand for the low-density lipoprotein receptors (LDLRs), which play a role in lipid metabolism. Therefore, this review is the first to outline what is known about the biogenesis, structure, and functions of APOE particles. APOE particles represent protective transport vehicles for small RNAs, lipids, and proteins, protecting them from degradation in the circulating blood and brain [12]. Recent reports have revealed that these cargoes are the basis for mediating cell-to-cell communication using APOE particles [13, 14]. Thus, we then focused on APOE particle cargoes by multi-omics strategies, which contribute to providing mechanistic insights into APOE genotype risk and disease.

We speculated on the molecular mechanisms by which APOE particles of different isoforms may play an essential role in the risk of AD and CVD. APOE2 and APOE3 proteins have a higher affinity towards smaller, more phospholipid-enriched HDL, while APOE4 protein preferentially binds to larger, triglyceride-rich VLDL and LDL [15–17]. Relative to APOE3, APOE4-containing VLDL have higher lipid affinity leading to inhibition of lipolysis, which gives rise to increased proatherogenic lipoprotein–cholesterol and the risk for atherosclerosis [18]. In addition, there are several well-documented functions of APOE particles in the peripheral circulation, such as anti-oxidation and anti-inflammatory properties and improvement of endothelial dysfunction, which may explain the ability of these particles to protect against atherosclerosis and CVD [19–24]. The role of APOE particles in increasing the risk of AD is complex and multifactorial. Mechanistically, an extensive body of literature indicates that APOE4 particles increase the risk of AD by enhancing amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation, promoting tau hyperphosphorylation and spread, exacerbating gliosis, disrupting the neurovascular unit, and causing abnormal lipid metabolism [14, 25–28]. For APOE particle-targeted treatment to be effective, it is important to understand the differential and cooperative roles of APOE particles in peripheral and central pathogenesis, as well as the impact of different APOE isoforms. Importantly, it remains to be determined whether these APOE particles cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) to mediate indispensable molecular exchange in the circulation and CNS.

At last, this review focuses on the current and emerging therapeutic strategies targeting APOE particles, including APOE genome editing therapy, APOE4 small-molecule structure correctors, liver X receptors (LXR)/retinoid X receptors (RXR) agonists, reconstituted or mimetic APOE particles, and anti-APOE4 antibody and antisense oligonucleotides. These will aid in the development of novel therapeutics for AD and CVD in the future.

APOE Particles: Biogenesis, Structure, and Biological Functions

The Biogenesis and Structure of APOE Particles

In the brain, approximately 60% of astrocytic APOE is secreted into the extracellular space, whereas most APOE synthesized in neurons remains intracellular [29, 30]. In the first step of APOE particle biosynthesis, newly translated APOE is first translocated into the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where APOE forms lipid-poor lipoprotein particles via an unknown mechanism and traffics to the Golgi apparatus. In the Golgi apparatus, lipid-poor APOE particles are glycosylated and secreted [31]. Next, on the extracellular surface of the plasma membrane, lipid-poor APOE particles acquire the majority of their lipids extracellularly through interactions with ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transport proteins, primarily ABCA1, ABCA7, and ABCG1 [32, 33]. Nascent APOE particles secreted by astrocytes expressing wild-type mouse or human APOE are discoidal in shape rather than spherical due to a lack of neutral lipid core in the form of triacylglycerol or cholesterol esters [34–36]. Subsequently, lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT) esterifies free cholesterol on nascent APOE discoidal particles to convert them into the spherical particles observed in the human cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [37] (Fig. 1A). Indeed, the brain exhibits the second highest LCAT activity after the liver in rats and humans [38, 39]. Human CSF primarily contained spherical particles, while mouse and canine CSF were a mixture of discoidal and spherical particles [40], perhaps because of not fully active LCAT.

Structurally, APOE particles contain a hydrophobic core (cholesteryl ester and triacylglycerol) surrounded by a hydrophilic bilayer composition of phospholipids [35]. APOE binds to the polar head groups of lipid molecules and extends over the surface rather than being inserted into the hydrophobic lipid core of the particles. Specifically, two APOE molecules are aligned as antiparallel dimers on nascent discoidal particles and a helical hairpin or a discrete horseshoe-shaped structure on the surface of spherical particles [8, 41, 42]. APOE, composed of a 299-amino acid glycoprotein, contains an N-terminal domain and a C-terminal domain linked by a protease-sensitive hinge region [5]. The N-terminal elongated domain forms a 4 α-helix bundle and presents a region rich in basic Arg and lysine residues (residues 135–150) that creates the LDLRs’ binding region, while the lipid-binding region (residues 244–272) is in the C-terminal domain [43]. The subtle differences in the side-chain conformations and salt-bridge arrangements of the three APOE isoforms substantially affect APOE’s properties in forming lipoprotein particles and receptor binding [44]. The Biological Functions of APOE Particles

As dietary cholesterol cannot penetrate the BBB, the brain contains 23% of the total body cholesterol and produces all of its own cholesterol on-site [45]. The neuronal demand for cholesterol in the adult brain is mainly fulfilled by cholesterol-rich APOE particles from astrocytes and only partly by neuronal synthesis [46]. Neurons then take up available cholesterol by internalizing APOE particles via neuron-specific LDL receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) at both the presynapse and postsynapse (Fig. 1B) [47]. The APOE particle-mediated flux of hydrophobic molecules (cholesterol and phospholipids) supports neuronal plasticity, repair, and remodeling of membranes, organelle biogenesis, and synaptogenesis [48, 49]. On the other hand, neurons have the minimum capacity to catabolize fatty acids (FAs); fine regulation and timely elimination of neuronal free FAs are vital to protect against neurodegeneration. Toxic FAs are stored in lipid droplets (LDs) and are metabolized in the mitochondria by β-oxidation in astrocytes. APOE particles secreted from neurons and mediate neuron-astrocyte coupling of FAs metabolism [14, 25]. Recently, Haynes et al. found that the mitochondrial lipid metabolic cycle between neurons and glia, mediated by APOE particles, reflects a fundamental function of sleep that is relevant for brain energy homeostasis [50].

APOE serves as a high-affinity ligand for hepatic APOE receptors, which are involved in the hepatic clearance of cholesterol-rich or triglyceride-rich VLDL, chylomicron remnants, and a subset of HDL from circulation, thus reducing plasma lipid levels [51]. Furthermore, APOE particles have been proposed to promote cholesterol efflux from lipid-loaded macrophages in the artery wall and participate in the reverse cholesterol transport process, which plays a role in the attenuation of atherosclerosis [7, 52] (Fig. 1C).

The Composition of APOE Particle

Lipids

APOE particles contained a similar mass of APOE and lipids, mainly covering esterified cholesterol, free cholesterol, phospholipids, and triglycerides. Apoe−/− × Apoa1−/−double-deficient mice were infected with APOA1- or APOE3-expressing adenoviruses; APOA-I and APOE3 particles were isolated from plasma, respectively. Surprisingly, APOE3 particles recruit different apolipoproteins and lipids compared to APOA-I particles, leading to structural and functional distinctions. For example, high levels of phosphatidylcholine and ceramide d18:2 were found in APOE3 particles [59]. Lidgard et al. noted that ceramides, sphingomyelins with long FAs, and saturated and monounsaturated phosphatidylcholines were present on the surface of APOE particles from plasma [60, 61]. Moreover, the delivery of long-chain saturated free FAs and phosphatidylcholines with very-long-chain FAs acyl chains contained in APOE and APOJ particles mediates neurotoxic astrocyte-induced toxicity in neurodegenerative disease [62] (Table 2). Notably, hiPSC-derived astrocytes secrete abundant APOE4 particles with a lower lipidation status compared to APOE3 particles, leading to APOE4/4 genotype astrocytes being less effective in supporting neurotrophic functions than those with APOE3/3 genotype [63].

microRNAs (miRNAs)

A large portion of circulating miRNAs is protected by encapsulation in various lipid-containing vesicles, including exosomes, supermeres, exomeres, and lipoprotein particles, which may aid in their protection from abundant RNase in the extracellular space [64–66]. miR-30c-5p has been shown to be enriched in small, dense APOE particles, and pravastatin therapy significantly increased circulating miR-30c-5p expression in addition to the pleiotropic dimensions of statins [67]. Neuron-derived APOE particles transmit miR-124-3p and miR-9-5p to microglia within a novel intercellular communication network, which favors the acquisition of a specific phenotype under the CNS microenvironment [68]. Astrocytic APOE particles vector miRNA species (miR-126-5p, miR-1960-5p, and miR-183-3p [13]) that silence the rate-limiting enzyme of cholesterol biosynthesis, resulting in the accumulation of the pathway-initiating substrate acetyl-CoA, histone acetylation, transcription in neurons, and consequently, enhanced memory consolidation in mice. Notably, APOE4 particles display smaller sizes and vector significantly lower levels of miRNAs than APOE3 particles, and therefore are less capable of metabolic and epigenetic regulation in neurons [13].

Proteins

LCAT and cholesterol-ester transfer protein (CETP) are located on the surface of APOE particles. APOE particles have a greater ratio of LCAT activity to mass, signaling a greater degree of LCAT activation, which favors cholesterol esterification [69]. APOE3 particles particularly recruit apolipoprotein C1 compared to those recruited by APOA-I particles, suggesting a close interaction of apolipoprotein C1 with particles in the presence of APOE3 [59]. Unlike plasma HDL, APOE is the primary scaffold protein on CSF-derived lipoprotein particles [70, 71]. Mass spectrometric analysis identified 303 proteins on CSF-derived APOE particles, including kallikrein-related peptidase, secretogranin, and neurosecretory protein VGF, over half of which have not been reported in plasma. Further computational analysis revealed that these APOE particles were enriched in proteins important for wound healing, inflammation, immune response, and both neuron generation and development [72, 73]. In view of the above studies, the distinct miRNA, lipid, and protein compositions of APOE particles have been shown to be responsible for the diversity of function. Therefore, the molecular profile dynamics during the progression of AD or CVD need to be explored deeply in APOE particles by multi-omics strategies.

The Role of APOE Particles in AD Pathophysiology

APOE Genotype-Dependent Expression Differences in the Brain

The brain is the second most common site of APOE production, contributing approximately 10–20% of that in the plasma [74]. The concentrations of APOE in the plasma and CSF are estimated to be 40–70 µg/ml and 3–10 µg/ml, respectively [75–77]. As well known, the APOE protein levels are under strong genetic influence by the APOE polymorphism. APOE4 circulates at lower levels than APOE2 or APOE3 in human CSF [76] and in the brain lysates and CSF of humanized APOE-targeted replacement mice [78]. However, previous studies have reported no association between APOE genotype and CSF APOE levels [79, 80]. For instance, Martínez-Morillo et al. found that CSF APOE levels did not vary among APOE genotypes, did not correlate with age or cognitive status, and did not differ between AD and non-AD subjects [81]. One of the reasons for this lack of association may be the small sample size used in those studies. Another possible explanation is that previous studies have used different antibodies to quantify APOE protein levels. Specifically, Cruchaga et al. confirmed a strong correlation measured by mass spectrometry [76] but not by ELISA in the same individuals [79, 80]. Moreover, some earlier studies have pointed out that CSF APOE messenger RNA (mRNA) expression varies independently of the APOE genotype [78, 81], implying that the altered levels of APOE are post-translational; however, the mechanism is not fully understood.

The Mechanism

The frequency of the APOE4 allele is 13.7% in the general population and as high as 40% in patients with AD. In addition to increasing the prevalence of AD, the presence of the APOE4 allele also lowers the age of onset of AD in a dose-dependent manner. However, the role of APOE particles in increasing the risk of AD is complex and multifactorial.

Aβ

APOE4 allele carriers may predispose to AD because they have been suggested to differentially regulate Aβ production, aggregation, and clearance in an APOE isoform-dependent manner [82] (Fig. 2A). Earlier studies have demonstrated that recombinant human APOE robustly stimulated amyloid precursor protein (APP) transcription and recycling and enhance Aβ production in cultured neurons with a relative effect size that parallels APOE variants as risk factors on AD pathogenesis [83, 84]. Concomitantly, it has been shown that reconstituted APOE particles can enhance the activity and level of β-site APP-cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1), thereby promoting Aβ production and oligomerization in the order APOE4 > APOE3 > APOE2 [26]. APOE isoform-specific physical interactions with Aβ (APOE/Aβ) may modulate oligomerization and clearance of Aβ. APOE4 bound Aβ with a higher affinity than APOE3 [85]. However, this result is reversed using physiologically relevant APOE particles; levels of the APOE3 particle/Aβ complex are significantly greater than the APOE4 particle/Aβ complex [86, 87]. Indeed, Tokuda et al. have proved that native APOE3 particles bind to Aβ with a 2 ~ threefold higher affinity than APOE4 particles [88]. Isoform binding efficiency correlates inversely with the risk of developing late-onset AD, further supporting the notion that APOE3 might be more proficient in carrying Aβ for cellular clearance.

The interaction of Aβ, either alone or in complex with APOE, with cell surface receptors enables its clearance via drainage of interstitial fluid (ISF), transport across the BBB, or cellular uptake by glial cells [82]. The APOE/Aβ40 complex reduces its efflux rate at the BBB by 5.7-fold compared to Aβ alone [89]. The APOE isoform can further determine the preferred route of clearance. Specifically, APOE2/Aβ and APOE3/Aβ complexes were cleared efficiently across the BBB via LRP-1 and VLDL receptor (VLDLR), whereas APOE4/Aβ complexes were predominantly internalized via VLDLR, which mediated significantly slower endocytosis, indicating that APOE4 expression induces a lower rate of Aβ clearance from the brain. APOE particle/Aβ complexes are cleared at the BBB by similar mechanisms but at 2~threefold slower rates [90]. LRP1, LDLR, and heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG) are the major APOE receptors that mediate the cellular uptake of Aβ. It has also been suggested that APOE particles, irrespective of the isoform, may impair Aβ clearance by competing for receptor-mediated internalization [91, 92]. However, it was found that the APOE particle/Aβ complex was cleared more efficiently than Aβ alone, as a result of enhanced astrocytic and microglial Aβ uptake and degradation by facilitating the lysosomal transport [93].

Tau

Patients with AD who are APOE4 carriers have a greater extent of tau pathology than non-carriers. APOE4 potentiates neuronal tau phosphorylation and the release of phosphorylated-tau compared with the APOE3 isoform in a TauP301S transgenic mouse background [94, 95]. Nevertheless, other studies have highlighted a paradoxical situation in which APOE2 was identified to increase hyperphosphorylated tau species, tau aggregates, and behavioral abnormalities in TauP301L mice and human progressive supranuclear palsy patients, thereby increasing the risk and progression of tauopathy [96]. Furthermore, the presence of APOE4 and APOE2 was not associated with paired helical filament tau (PHF-tau) tangles in the absence of Aβ from 2 clinical-pathologic cohort studies of aging and AD [97]. LRP1 is a cell surface endocytic receptor that interacts strongly with APOE and is involved in neuronal uptake, seeding, and propagation of tau [98]. On one hand, LRP1-mediated endocytosis of tau represents a physiological pathway by which physiologically secreted tau is catabolized. On the other hand, LRP1 participates in mediating the uptake of pathological forms of tau, leading to tau seeding and spreading. APOE could be due to direct competition of binding to LRP1 with tau; thus, all isoforms of APOE inhibit the uptake of physiological and pathological forms of tau. Interestingly, APOE4 has the greatest impact, consistent with its higher affinity for LRP1 [27] (Fig. 2B).

Neuroinflammation

Multiple observations have associated the APOE4 allele with greater neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress responses than the APOE2 or APOE3 [99, 100]. APOE4 koockin (KI) mice exhibited glial activation and greater and more prolonged increases in cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) than APOE2 and APOE3 mice, which are related to dysregulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [101, 102]. Notably, APOE4 induces a lipid-accumulated state that renders astrocytes and microglia a pro-inflammatory phenotype, and thus, could significantly exacerbate neuroinflammation [103, 104].

25-hydroxycholesterol (25-HC), a known modulator of both inflammation and lipid metabolism, promotes neuroinflammation in an APOE isoform-dependent manner (E4 > E2, E3) [105, 106]. Astrocytes take up 25-HC secreted from microglia and respond to altered lipid metabolism, resulting in the increased release of APOE particles into the extracellular milieu. However, astrocyte-secreted APOE4 particles markedly decreased after exposure to 25-HC compared with APOE3 particles, which led to cholesterol accumulation and reactive astrogliosis [28] (Fig. 2C). In future, more attention should be given to therapies for AD that modulate lipid metabolism, immune responses, and the interplay between these.

Lipid Transport and Metabolism

APOE4 is a key pathological promoter and driver that can induce lipid deposition, lipid peroxidation damage, lipotoxicity, cellular steatosis, and death in the brain, ultimately leading to the occurrence and progression of AD [107, 108]. APOE particles act as metabolic regulatory links between neurons and glial cells. On one hand, APOE particle transports lipids from glial cells to neurons to support neuronal activity, remodeling, and repair [50]. On the other hand, excitotoxic neurons secrete excess lipids and metabolic toxic products in the form of APOE particles, which are transported to astrocytes for catabolism [14, 25] (Fig. 2D). Owing to their lower lipidation ability, APOE4 particles impair the transport of excess cholesterol and FAs from neurons to glial cells, leading to abnormal accumulation and distribution of lipids in neurons [14, 109]. Furthermore, glia endocytose these APOE4 particles; intracellular cholesterol or FAs catabolism is impaired, and excess lipid accumulates in glial cells, thereby attenuating the metabolic support effect of glia on neurons [104, 110, 111]. In contrast, APOE3 particles exhibit a higher lipidation ability and transfer efficiency for cholesterol, triglycerides, and FAs [14]. Neighboring astrocytes more easily take up APOE3 particles because of their higher affinity for LDLRs on the cell surface, consume these lipids via mitochondrial β-oxidation, and activate a detoxification gene expression program [25]. Collectively, APOE particles are key carriers that provide support and detoxification mechanisms for neuronal cholesterol and FAs metabolism and may become a novel means for intervention in AD by regulating central lipid metabolism.

The Role of APOE Particles in CVD

APOE Genotype-Dependent Expression Differences in the Periphery

Plasma APOE is predominantly synthesized in the hepatocytes and to a lesser extent in macrophages and adipocytes. The APOE polymorphism is associated with variation in plasma APOE levels; the concentration of APOE2 is higher than that of APOE3, and the concentration of APOE4 is lower than that of APOE3. The mechanisms leading to the difference are related to the contrasting kinetics of the isoforms; APOE2 catabolic rates are lower relative to APOE3, and APOE4 production rate is lower than that of APOE3 and APOE2 [112]. High plasma levels of APOE are associated with atherogenic lipids and an increased risk of ischemic heart disease and CVD [113, 114], while low plasma APOE levels are associated with a high risk of AD and dementia [115, 116]. Hence, plasma levels of APOE may be a new, easily accessible preclinical biomarker, independent of APOE genotype.

The Mechanism

There are several well-documented functions of APOE particles, such as anti-oxidative activity, anti-inflammatory properties, and improvement of endothelial dysfunction, which may explain the ability of the particles to promote or protect against atherosclerosis and CVD.

Lipid Transport and Metabolism

Atherosclerosis is a metabolic and inflammatory disease characterized by the accumulation of cholesterol-rich lipoprotein particles and inflammatory cells in the arterial wall, which is the pathological basis of CVD. Endogenous APOE particles mediate the efflux of cholesterol from macrophage foam cells in atheromatous blood vessels, which are thought to be central to their anti-atherogenic mechanism and represent the first step in the overall process of reverse cholesterol transport [20, 21]. Furthermore, APOE uniquely facilitates reverse cholesterol transport by allowing cholesterol ester-rich core expansion in HDL [21] (Fig. 3A). It has been proved that in reconstituted APOE particles, APOE resides in a superficial location among the phosphorylcholine head groups of phospholipids in contrast to more deeply embedded APOA-I molecules, and this may endow flexibility in the surface of HDL, permitting cholesterol ester-rich core expansion as the LCAT reaction proceeds[41]. APOE3 possesses both the requisite lipid-binding ability and affinity for the LDLRs to mediate the endocytosis of triglyceride/cholesterol and lipolytic processing. APOE3 operates optimally in promoting the clearance of TG-rich lipoprotein particles (e.g., HDL) and is associated with normal plasma lipid levels. However, APOE2, due to the altered structure and impaired function of the receptor-binding region, results in delayed clearance of APOE2 particles and thereby type III hyperlipoproteinemia. Although APOE4 binding of the LDLRs is unaffected, it has enhanced lipid-binding ability, and so it is associated with a more pro-atherogenic cholesterol-rich lipoprotein particles distribution [117, 118]. The lipid cargoes delivered to hepatocytes via receptor-mediated endocytosis of APOE particles are catabolized, whereas the majority of APOE remains in the peripheral recycling pool. Rather, APOE recycling is also isoform-specific. APOE3 recycling is associated with concomitant cholesterol efflux and thereby contributes to the formation of APOE3 particles, whereas APOE4 accumulates within endosomal compartments and is connected to an impaired cholesterol efflux [119].

Anti-Oxidative Activity

In vitro studies have established that the APOE protein acts as an anti-oxidant in the Cu2+-induced LDL oxidation model, with the rank order of efficacy being E2 > E3.> E4 [120, 121]. Concomitantly, APOE3 particles protect lipids from peroxidation more than APOE4 particles when a strong oxidative stress is applied in vitro [122]. Serum paraoxonase (PON) is an antioxidant interfacially activated lipo-lactonase that was shown to bind HDL carrying APOA-I. Reconstituted APOE3 or APOE4 particles associate with PON in a manner and affinity similar to those of APOA-I. APOE particles also stabilize the enzyme and stimulate the lipo-lactonase activity, although to a > tenfold lesser extent compared to APOA-I particles (Fig. 3B) [22, 59].

Anti-Inflammatory Properties

It is now well-accepted that atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disorder characterized by an accumulation of macrophages and T lymphocytes in the arterial intima and an increased plasma concentration of several inflammatory markers. Reconstituted APOE3 and APOE4 particles’ interaction with memory regulatory T lymphocytes (mTreg) and effector memory Treg (emTreg) protected them from apoptosis. This mechanism could thus contribute to the atheroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties of APOE particles through their effect on Treg homeostasis (Fig. 3C). However, reconstituted APOE2 particles showed no effect. Importantly, APOE-depleted mice failed to rescue emTregs, suggesting a unique role of the APOE protein [19]. Apoe−/− mice are a well-established model for atherosclerosis; the lack of this apolipoprotein increases cholesterol levels, plaque formation, severe Treg defects, which in turn causes a global hyper-inflamed status [123, 124]. Furthermore, serum APOE3 particles reduced the enrichment of lipid rafts and cholesterol in the membrane of APOE4 genotype dendritic cells (DCs), compared to cells treated with APOE4 particles. Reduced lipid rafts and cholesterol accumulation were associated with decreased expression of HLA-DR and antigen presentation ability, which resulted in decreased CD4+ emTreg development and the expression of inflammatory genes [125] (Fig. 3C). Additionally, APOE particles secreted locally at lesion sites by macrophages may be anti-inflammatory by suppressing vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) expression in human umbilical vein endothelial cells [23].

Moreover, APOE3 particles attenuated LPS-induced production of TNFα in RAW264.7 cells [59]. Taken together, these findings imply that the two separate anti-inflammatory processes of APOE particles, immunomodulatory properties and Treg anti-inflammatory function, may work in concert to dampen inflammation and protect against CVD.

Improvement of Endothelial Dysfunction

Endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS)–derived NO plays a major role in cardiovascular protection by promoting the integrity of the endothelial cell monolayer and attenuating endothelial cell–leukocyte adhesion [126]. It has been shown that APOE particle secreted by macrophages suppresses the activation of human umbilical vein endothelial cells by stimulating the endothelium to release NO [23]. APOE particles enhance endothelial NO production by disrupting the inhibitory interaction of eNOS with caveolin 1 and significantly improve vasodilation in resistance arteries [127] (Fig. 3D). Surprisingly, APOE3 particles secreted from recombinant CHO cells stimulate more NO release in cultured human EA.hy926 endothelial cells than APOE2 or APOE4 particles (141% more than controls vs. 61 or 11%) [24]. The contrasting endothelial actions of APOE4 vs. APOE3 are related to APOE receptor2 (ApoER2) interaction. APOE3/ApoER2 tandem promotes endothelial NO production, endothelial repair, and endothelial anti-inflammatory properties, and it prevents neointima formation. In contrast, APOE4 does not stimulate eNOS or endothelial cell migration or dampen cell adhesion, and alternatively, it selectively antagonizes APOE3/ApoER2 actions [128]. DeKroon et al. further demonstrate that APOE4 particles inhibit the anti-apoptotic activity of HDL in endothelial cells by the activation of SHIP2 and the subsequent inhibition of the Akt pathway [129].

Is There a Crosstalk of APOE Particles in the Systemic Circulation and the CNS?

Whether APOE particles cross the BBB in vivo has been controversial. Previously, studies have suggested that peripheral APOE particles do not traffic through the BBB, resulting in two distinct pools of APOE. The most compelling evidence for the existence of two compartments of APOE was from a liver transplantation study. It was found that in patients after liver transplantation, the CSF APOE phenotype did not change to the donor’s phenotype, clearly indicating that APOE in the CSF cannot originate from the plasma pool and must be synthesized locally [6]. Further, the composition of the APOE particle in the brain is different from that in circulation. For example, glycosylation and sialylation of APOE occur in the Golgi and are uniformly present in APOE particles secreted from astrocytes [130]. In contrast, APOE particles in plasma are largely devoid of these carbohydrates because they are partially removed in circulation by glycosidases [131, 132], which suggests that the extent of human APOE glycosylation and sialylation is heterogeneous, reflecting not only its tissue/cell type origin, but also its extracellular environment.

Although peripheral and CNS APOE are separate pools that are differentially regulated, emerging evidence indicates that peripheral APOE also contributes to AD risk. For instance, Lane–Donovan et al. found that genetic restoration of peripheral APOE KO to wild-type levels improved learning and memory impairment [133]. Similarly, a recent study demonstrated that liver-expressed APOE4 impairs synaptic plasticity and cognitive behaviors likely by compromising cerebrovascular integrity, whereas APOE3 plasma from young mice reduced aging-related BBB damage and improved cognition in aged mice [134]. In some prospective studies, higher APOE plasma levels have been associated with better cognitive function and lower dementia risk [115, 135]. APOA-I particles could interact with apolipoprotein receptors (e.g., scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI)) at the BBB and result in their endocytic uptake into endothelium and possibly transcytosis into the brain [136, 137]. This suggestion is supported by the observation that nanoparticles with adsorbed or covalently bound APOE3 are internalized into the brain capillary endothelial cells. This is followed by transcytosis and release into brain parenchyma and the subsequent appearance of these particles in the cytoplasm of neurons [138, 139]. It is, therefore, possible that drug delivery to the brain is enabled by the interaction of APOE particles with the SR-BI expressed by the brain vessels endothelial cells.

APOE4 Genome Editing Therapy

Gene correction therapeutics are important potential avenues for targeting APOE4. Injection of adeno-associated virus (AAV)-GFAP-APOE2 into the lateral ventricles of postnatal day 2 APOE4-targeted replacement (TR) mice enhanced APOE particles’ lipidation and decreased Aβ accumulation (Fig. 4A) [140]. Similarly, other groups have reported that AAV-mediated delivery and expression of APOE2 in 7-month-old APP/PS1 mice decreased Aβ oligomerization and deposition, enhanced plaque clearance, and, more importantly, attenuated Aβ-mediated synapse toxicity [158]. As the next step in moving APOE2 gene therapy for AD to the clinic, Rosenberg and coworkers have proved intracisternal delivery of AAVrh.10hAPOE2 was identified as the optimal strategy for delivering human APOE2 to the CNS in a non-human primate model system [142].

Furthermore, the application of CRISPR/Cas9 editing technology to switch the APOE allele has been successful in neurons and glial cells or organoids derived from hiPSCs (Fig. 4A). Brookhouser et al. utilized genome editing to convert APOE3 to APOE2, greatly reducing the production of Aβ peptides in early onset familial AD (fAD) hiPSC-derived neuronal–astrocytic cultures [159]. Interestingly, no difference in Aβ levels was observed in cells derived from the non-demented control hiPSCs. This suggests that the protective effects of APOE2 are observed only in fAD cultures with elevated Aβ species. Consistently, converting APOE4 to APOE3 in hiPSC-derived brain cells and cerebral organoids is sufficient to attenuate multiple AD-related pathologies [103, 160]. Remarkably, these results indicate that the APOE4 mutation results in loss of function or gain of toxic functions, which is critical for tailoring appropriate therapeutic interventions for APOE4 carriers. Advances in gene editing have made this an increasingly intriguing prospect, but much remains to be determined regarding the safety and feasibility of this new approach for the prevention or treatment of human diseases.

APOE4 Small Molecule Structure Correctors

Interaction between the N- and C-terminal domains, called domain interaction, is a molecular basis for APOE4’s detrimental effects in AD pathogenesis. In APOE4, domain interaction occurs as a result of the formation of a salt bridge between Arg61 and Glu255, owing to the effect of Arg112 [15]. In contrast, APOE3, especially APOE2, displays fewer domain interactions and is visualized as having a more open structure [15, 118]. Small-molecule structure correctors convert APOE4 to an APOE3-like tertiary structure, which has been shown to rescue APOE4-associated neuropathology [145] (Fig. 4B).

GIND-25 and GIND-105, by abolishing APOE4 intramolecular domain interactions, modulate APP processing and reduce Aβ production in cultured neurons [83]. Likewise, a potent APOE4 structure corrector, CB9032258, rescued the impairment of mitochondrial motility and neurite outgrowth in overexpressing APOE4 neuronal cells [161]. Moreover, the impairment of intracellular trafficking of APOE4 in the ER and Golgi was rescued with GIND25 and PH002. In fact, rescuing the impaired intracellular trafficking of APOE4 with GIND25 and PH002 abolished its detrimental effects on neurite outgrowth and dendritic spine development [143]. PH002, which renders APOE4 to APOE3-like both structurally and functionally, significantly reduced APOE4 fragmentation, GABAergic neuron degeneration/loss, tau phosphorylation, and Aβ production in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, the treatment of APOE−/−-hiPSC-derived neurons with PH002 did not show any significant changes, indicating that the effect of PH002 depends on the presence of APOE4 [144]. More importantly, newly synthesized APOE4 appears to be the structure corrector target because APOE4 only displays domain interactions when it is non- or poor-lipidation, as would occur during or soon after synthesis, whereas the helical structure of APOE4 is reorganized when it is fully lipidated [162].

LXR/RXR Agonists

APOE expression is transcriptionally regulated by ligand-activated nuclear receptors, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), and liver X receptors (LXRs) in coordination with retinoid X receptors (RXRs). The obligate heterodimer of PPARγ/RXR or LXR/RXR interacts with DNA elements near the promoters of ABCA1, ABCG1, and APOE and upregulates their expression and subsequent lipidation. It has recently been reported that the lipidation of APOE governs its functions, including its conformation, interactions with membrane receptors, and Aβ. Thus, many studies have focused their efforts on LXR/RXR agonists as targets for AD treatment (Fig. 4C) [163].

Currently, there is an extensive body of evidence demonstrating the efficacy of nuclear receptor agonists in ameliorating AD-related pathologies in animal models. The LXRs’ agonist GW3965 enhanced APOE expression and lipidation, stimulated Aβ degradation, and reversed contextual memory deficits in aged Tg2576 mice [147]. Oral administration of the highly specific RXR agonist bexarotene to APP/PS1 mice stimulated clearance of soluble Aβ within hours, rapid reversal of cognitive, social, and olfactory deficits, and improved neural circuit function [164]. Interestingly, APP/PS1 mice treated with GW3965 showed a reduced Aβ burden in the hippocampus and whole brain, whereas APP/PS1-treated mice lacking ABCA1 failed to display reduced Aβ load. This further suggests that the capacity of nuclear receptors to ameliorate AD-associated pathology is dependent on increasing the expression of ABCA1 and APOE, promoting the creation of highly lipidated APOE particles [148]. In addition, the initiation and progression of AD have been linked to cholesterol metabolism and inflammation, which can be regulated by PPARγ/LXR [165]. The LXR activator T0901317 has been shown to enhance cerebral cholesterol turnover and restore impaired memory independent of Aβ plaque load [149]. Thus, we speculated that modulating the lipidation status of APOE in the brain may serve as a therapeutic approach to alleviate APOE4-associated dysregulation.

Reconstituted APOE Particles

Mimicking the HDL found in vivo, reconstituted APOE particles were prepared using 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) and unesterified cholesterol in a 1:90:5 molar ratio (human recombinant APOE: POPC: cholesterol) and employing the sodium cholate dialysis method (Fig. 4D) [34, 166]. Reconstituted APOE particles interact with SR-BI and promote cholesterol efflux in cells [167]. Surprisingly, reconstituted APOE3 particles, but not APOA-I particles and APOE3 protein, presented marked anti-oxidant potential and anti-inflammatory properties, suggesting that the effect of lipoprotein particles is dependent on APOE expression but not limited to it [59]. This is in line with a recent study showing that APOE protein supplementation alone cannot replace the inhibitory effect of APOE particles on de novo cholesterol biosynthesis in neurons, depending on APOE3 particles-carried miRNA [13]. Nakayama et al. produced recombinant APOA-I and APOE3 particles as a delivery vehicle for cholesterol-conjugated siRNAs and found that delivery of APOE3 particles was considerably more potent than APOA-I particles [152]. Furthermore, Kreuter and his group designed human serum albumin nanoparticles with covalently bound APOE attached on the surface. After intravenous administration, APOE-albumin nanoparticles are internalized into the brain capillary endothelial cells followed by transcytosis and release into brain parenchyma and the subsequent appearance of these particles in the cytoplasm of neurons [138, 139]. Future studies may aim to customize reconstituted APOE particles to deliver anticancer drugs, modified siRNAs, and enriched miRNAs for the effective treatment of brain diseases.

Anti-APOE4 Antibody and Antisense Oligonucleotides

Intraperitoneal administration of the anti-APOE antibody HJ6.3 dramatically decreased amyloid deposition by 60–80%, significantly reduced insoluble Aβ40 and Aβ42, and improved spatial learning performance in APP/PS1 mice [153, 168]. Recently, Holtzman’steam has shown that an antibody specific for human APOE4 was able to reduce Aβ accumulation when infused directly into the brain or delivered by intraperitoneal injection starting at the time of plaque onset in APP/PS1 × APOE4 mice (Fig. 4E). Moreover, HAE-4 selective for nonlipidated, aggregated APOE can preferentially bind to APOE in plaques and have very similar effects to anti-Aβ antibodies that also bind to and decrease Aβ accumulation via a microglial-mediated clearance mechanism [169].

Accumulating evidence has also shown that lowering APOE4 levels by intra-cerebroventricular injection of antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), which bind specifically to APOE4 messenger RNA (mRNA) and reduce human APOE4 gene expression, can be effective (Fig. 4E). Huynh et al. found that anti-APOE ASOs treatment starting at the onset of Aβ deposition resulted in an elevation in plaque size and a reduction in plaque-associated neuritic dystrophy without changing the overall plaque load in APP/PS1 × APOE4 mice [154]. Another study indicated that an approximately 50% reduction in APOE4 protein levels with ASOs in P301S × APOE4 mice not only protected against tau pathology but also significantly reduced brain atrophy and synapse loss and inhibited neuroinflammation [155]. Collectively, we speculate that lowering APOE4 levels in the CNS might serve as a promising therapeutic approach for reducing AD-associated pathologies.

Conclusion

Given the important function of APOE particles in the periphery and CNS, a better understanding of their involvement in AD and CVD initiation and progression may clarify the contribution of different APOE alleles to disease risk and provide insights into prevention and treatment approaches. The studies described earlier identified clusters of common phenotypes associated with APOE4 genotype in AD and CVD. However, a clear mechanistic explanation is lacking for how APOE particles prevent and cause these phenotypes. A better way to further exploit the field and identify these mechanisms depends on diverse cargoes of APOE particles based on multi-omics strategies. It is likely that APOE particles rescue and drive these phenotypes through multiple parallel mechanisms, rather than a single point in the pathway.

It is also important to consider that APOE particles may have different functions in different pools (e.g., circulation vs. CNS). Emerging evidence indicates that peripheral APOE also contributes to AD risk. To date, however, the mechanisms by which circulatory APOE might contribute to cognitive decline and AD pathology are poorly understood. Hence, further studies are needed to elucidate whether plasma APOE particle mirrors in the brain tissue and CSF are easily accessible biomarkers for individual AD risk assessment. Second, future studies will be aimed at determining whether specific APOE particles mediate their exchange in the BBB and developing approaches to exploit APOE particles for therapeutic purposes.

Several APOE particle-directed therapeutic approaches, including APOE genome editing, APOE4 structure correctors, LXR/RXR agonists, and reconstituted APOE particles, have been shown to be effective in mouse models and hold promise for translation into human clinical trials. As our understanding of APOE particles in AD and CVD deepens, the development of precise and targeted therapies with real disease-modifying capacities may not only enhance patient outcomes but also alleviate the broader societal and emotional burden.

Limitation

APOE is the most abundant apolipoprotein in the CSF and is secreted as an HDL-like lipoprotein particle. However, the APOE particle is a minor subspecies of the plasma HDL, VLDL, and LDL population. So most studies have focused on the mechanism and treatment of AD, and less on CVD. (ii.) A long-standing question has been whether there is a crosstalk of APOE particles in the systemic circulation and the CNS. Most studies suggested that peripheral APOE particles do not traffic through the BBB, resulting in two distinct pools of APOE. Although some reconstituted APOE particles were uptaken into the endothelium and transcytosed into the brain, endogenous APOE particles have not been found to cross the BBB. (iii.) Therapeutic strategies targeting APOE particles have shown promise in cultured neurons and AD mouse models but lack testing in CVD and in clinical trials (Table 3). Only LXR/RXR agonists were applied in the periphery and CNS. Furthermore, LXR/RXR agonists increased APOE levels; however, the anti-APOE4 antibody and ASOs reduced APOE levels. Thus, controlling for APOE levels in plasma vs brain during any APOE-targeting therapy will be important to prevent disruption of plasma lipid metabolism.
