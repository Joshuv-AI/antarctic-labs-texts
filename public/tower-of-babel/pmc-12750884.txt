Biomechanical characteristics of medial plantar load and rearfoot valgus in young female adults with hallux valgus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tong-Hsien Chow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven that previous studies on hallux valgus (HV) have predominantly concentrated on elderly and flatfoot populations with multifactorial etiologies, while studies addressing young populations with relatively simple causes of HV remain limited. As a result, considerable uncertainty and potential bias may exist when inferring the congenital foot characteristics of HV patients from existing literature.PurposeThis study aimed to explore the characteristics of plantar load distribution, center of pressure (COP), foot postures, and correlations between these parameters in Taiwanese female collegiate students with HV.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted involving 40 healthy and 32 HV-affected participants. Indicative parameters including arch index, plantar load distribution, COP and hallux angle were measured using the JC Mat optical plantar pressure analyzer. Rearfoot angular characteristics and correlation with hallux angle were also assessed through rearfoot postural alignment.ResultsParticipants in the HV group had normal arches, with plantar loads predominantly concentrated on the medial aspects of the metatarsals and heels of both feet. The COP distribution was symmetrical and balanced across both feet, while the rearfoot posture exhibited a valgus alignment positively correlated with ipsilateral HV angles (β = 0.503, p < 0.01 for the left foot; β = 0.639, p < 0.01 for the right foot).ConclusionTaiwanese female collegiate students with HV exhibited normal arches, with plantar loads predominantly concentrated on the medial aspects of both feet. Their rearfoot valgus angles were positively correlated with the ipsilateral HV angles. The findings may serve as an intuitive and predictable diagnostic reference for assessing potential rearfoot eversion changes related to a pronounced HV appearance. Additionally, the results offer new insights into the widely held belief that flatfoot predispose individuals to HV, as normal arches were also observed in the young population studied.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Hallux valgus (HV) is a progressive deformity of the forefoot characterized by the medial displacement of the first metatarsal, lateral deviation of the hallux, and bony enlargement of the first metatarsal head [1, 2]. This condition is associated with symptoms such as forefoot pain, an imbalanced center of gravity, impaired gait patterns, and an increased risk of falls during walking and standing [2, 3]. Research from various countries consistently indicates that HV is more prevalent among females and the elderly [2–5], with the highest incidence rate among females occurring in the age group of 17 to 20 years [5]. In school-age patients, females are affected 2 to 3 times more frequently than males [6]. Furthermore, recent studies have shown a significant correlation between HV and flatfoot in adolescents, suggesting that flatfoot may act as a predisposing factor for the development of HV [5–8]. Specifically, Dygut et al. highlighted that individuals with transverse flatfoot may have an increased risk of developing HV, particularly in populations such as dancers and sprinters [6]. Therefore, based on these studies, it can be inferred that HV deformity exhibits distinct developmental tendencies in specific age and gender populations, while its association with flatfoot still requires further validation through comprehensive foot data from diverse population groups.

Additionally, in the study of the association between HV and foot posture, Yokozuka found that young females with HV showed a decrease in plantar load on the lateral aspect of the foot during walking, indicating a tendency toward pronation [9]. These findings can be explained by those of Kawakami et al. who attributed the lack of stability and coordination between the forefoot and midfoot due to excessive rearfoot eversion of the foot, which makes the rearfoot become hypermobile and reduces the rigidity of the foot, which in turn leads to HV deformity and impairs gait pattern [10].

From the perspective of plantar load distribution in patients with hallux valgus (HV), a recent study by Menzildzic et al. found that patients with severe HV exhibited a statistically significant increase in pressure distribution at the first metatarsal head, while those with mild deformities demonstrated higher pressure distribution at the first toe region [11]. Similarly, Chang et al. noted a significant shift in plantar load from the second metatarsal to the first as the degree of HV deformity increased, further emphasizing the concentration of pressure on the medial forefoot [12]. Furthermore, regarding studies on the relationship between HV and plantar pressure during dynamic activities, Hida et al. found that HV patients walked with lower peak forces, contact times, contact areas, and force-time integrals at the hallux, while their second metatarsal experienced significantly higher peak pressure and force [13]. This redistribution of plantar load to the second metatarsal increases the risk of metatarsalgia due to inhibited hallux function during walking [13]. Yokozuka et al. extended this observation by identifying a positive correlation between the hallux valgus angle (HVA) and lateral heel pressure, suggesting that HV patients experience greater lateral heel loading during walking compared to those without HV [9]. These dynamic findings highlight the complex interplay between hallux function, forefoot loading, and rearfoot pressure in HV patients. In addition, other biomechanical factors that may contribute to plantar load redistribution in HV patients include increased forefoot abduction, as suggested by Okonkwo et al., which results in non-physiological loading on the plantar medial aspect of the hallux during heel lifting, potentially exacerbating the deformity [5]. Conversely, Geng et al. presented a different perspective, proposing that first metatarsal elevation, forefoot supination, and sesamoid subluxation could lead to transfer metatarsalgia, thereby redistributing plantar loads to adjacent areas [14]. These findings indicate that the biomechanical alterations in HV patients are multifactorial and may vary depending on the severity and specific characteristics of the deformity.

Previous research on the relationship between HV and balance ability has revealed that individuals with HV often experience balance impairments during both standing and walking activities [2, 3, 15]. These impairments are primarily linked to excessive rearfoot pronation, which compromises the coordination and stability between the forefoot and midfoot, resulting in reduced foot rigidity and increased rearfoot mobility, ultimately leading to gait abnormalities [2]. The severity of HV deformity has been shown to significantly influence walking patterns, particularly affecting hallux abduction and plantar flexion capabilities in moderate to severe cases [2]. Such conditions predispose individuals to mediolateral sway during single-limb stance, thereby weakening foot functional activity and increasing the risk of falls [16]. However, conflicting findings have emerged from studies examining various severities of HV and different populations. For instance, Hurn et al. found that patients with mild to moderate HV maintained adequate balance control, with significant deficits only observed in severe cases characterized by pronounced mediolateral oscillation [16]. Similarly, Kavlak’s study indicated that neither static nor dynamic balance abilities were compromised in elderly individuals with HV [17]. These discrepancies suggest that the biomechanical relationship between HV deformity and balance control may vary based on the severity of the deformity, age, and other individual factors. Given these conflicting results and the predominant focus on elderly populations with multifactorial etiologies, there exists a critical knowledge gap regarding balance characteristics in young adults with HV. The center of pressure (COP) analysis has been extensively validated as a sensitive and reliable tool for quantifying neuromuscular control strategies during both static posture and dynamic gait [18, 19]. While plantar pressure measurement systems or force plates are effective tools for analyzing the CoP. Specifically, COP analysis provides quantitative insights into the distribution of plantar loads and the dynamic shifts in balance, which are essential for understanding the biomechanical alterations associated with HV. It provides critical insights into balance control, foot functionality, and the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions [20]. Consequently, COP has been validated as a clinical assessment tool for various foot conditions, including HV, hallux rigidus, metatarsalgia, and lower limb amputations [21].

The relationship between foot posture, plantar load distribution, and balance performance in patients with HV has been widely studied. However, research findings regarding HV conditions associated with age-related factors remain inconsistent. Previous studies have reported that congenital HV cases are rare, with limited information available on this topic and conflicting descriptions of its etiology in the literature [22]. Most current studies have focused on middle-aged and elderly populations, who frequently present with multifactorial etiologies including degenerative changes, comorbidities, and long-term biomechanical adaptations [2, 11–17, 23, 24]. These confounding factors complicate the interpretation of results and may fail to accurately reflect the characteristics of adolescent HV deformities or its early-stage foot features. Furthermore, meta-analyses and systematic reviews have consistently concluded that the prevalence of HV in females is approximately 2 to 2.5 times higher than in males [25]. The predisposing factors may be related to the fact that women are more likely to wear high-heeled shoes when entering university relative to men who wear flat shoes, as well as other possible factors such as biomechanical or metabolic differences [25], inadequate footwear and family history of HV [26]. Although the exact etiology of HV in females is still unclear, female gender is considered a key factor in the prevalence or likelihood of HV [25].

Considering the significant differences in the prevalence of HV across age and gender, early biomechanical changes may influence the progression of this condition. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the foot conditions of young adults with HV, as this demographic represents a critical developmental stage for the deformity. Studying HV in this population is essential for identifying early foot characteristics and biomechanical patterns that may contribute to the advancement of the condition. This study aimed to investigate the characteristics of plantar load and CoP distributions, as well as foot postures in Taiwanese female collegiate students with HV. Additionally, the correlation between the hallux angle and rearfoot posture alignment, COP, and arch index was further explored. As a result, it was hypothesized that female students with HV would exhibit low-arched, pronated feet, characterized by plantar loads predominantly distributed across the medial regions of the feet. Their COP was expected to focus on the limb with the smaller HV angle, and their HV angles were anticipated to correlate with the ipsilateral rearfoot valgus angles.

Methods

Participants

A total of 72 female collegiate students in Taiwan were recruited for this cross-sectional study. To minimize confounding factors that could influence foot structure and posture, participants were required to meet specific eligibility criteria. These included having no professional sports background or training in specialized sports disciplines, and maintaining a body mass index (BMI) within the normal range (18.5–23.9) as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) and Asia-Pacific guidelines [27]. Additionally, participants were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) incomplete participation in experimental procedures; (2) documented history of lower extremity surgeries, dislocations, or fractures; (3) ligament or cartilage injuries in the lower extremities within the past six months; or (4) diagnosis of other podiatric conditions such as plantar fasciopathy, metatarsalgia, calcaneal spurs, osteoarthritis, or lower extremity neuropathies. The participants were divided into two groups, one with 32 female students with hallux valgus (HV group) and the other with 40 age-matched healthy asymptomatic female students (control group). Both groups were homogeneous in terms of age, BMI, ethnicity, academic background and level, and physical activity levels, with the only distinguishing factor being the presence of HV in the HV group.

Before the commencement of the experiment, participants in the HV group were primarily screened based on either self-provided pre-existing hospital diagnostic records or the results of their hallux valgus angles (HVA) measured by the researcher using a goniometer (A 12-inch plastic goniometer featuring a 360-degree rotating head with three measurement scales and 15-centimeter arms, King Life Technology Co., Ltd, Taipei City, Taiwan) on the day of the study. Goniometry is the simplest and widely accepted clinical and research measurement for assessing the degree of lateral deviation of the hallux [28, 29], therefore, it was used as a convenient tool for the initial diagnosis and screening of target participants in this study. Eligible participants proceeded to the plantar load distribution measurement procedure. This procedure simultaneously assessed plantar load distribution across different regions of the foot and the hallux angle. Consequently, this study primarily defines the hallux angle of participants based on previous literature [4, 30] using the intersection angle measurement of footprint images. The details of measuring the intersection angle of the footprint image are described in the measurement of the hallux angle.

Study design

The study collected a certain amount of targeted samples over the course of one year. All measurements were conducted in empty classrooms at the participants’ respective schools. In order to ensure the consistency and reliability of this study, the time for each participant to conduct the experiment was set from 9 am to 12 pm on the day. At the beginning of the experiment, each participant completed the measurement of height and weight under the guidance of the researcher, and recorded their primary physiological conditions and past medical history. The basic demographic surveys for all participants are summarized in Table 1. This was helpful for serving as a threshold for target participant recruitment. Those who meet the qualifications can continue to conduct subsequent research. After completing the demographic surveys, measurements of plantar load distribution, arch index and COP were conducted, as well as assessments of hallux angle and rearfoot postural alignment. Each participant took approximately 15 min to complete the entire procedure. All experimental procedures in this study were conducted in accordance with the research guidelines of the National Taiwan University Ethics Committee and in compliance with the recommended norms of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Instruments

The JC Mat optical plantar pressure analyzer (View Grand International Co., Ltd, New Taipei City, Taiwan) employed in this study has been extensively validated for its reliability, validity, and reproducibility in previous studies focusing on plantar load distribution in specific populations [31–33]. This device is equipped with 13,600 embedded sensors on each side of its sensing pad (dimensions: 32 × 17 cm), enabling precise pressure measurements during both static and dynamic foot assessments. Before each application, the device undergoes a standardized calibration process to ensure measurement sensitivity and accuracy. This involves testing pressure linearity using proportional weights. The JC Mat captures barefoot plantar pressure data and converts it into color-coded footprint images via its built-in FPDS-Pro-V2 software. These images provide detailed visual and quantitative information on plantar load intensity and distribution across different regions of the foot. The system is capable of analyzing key parameters such as plantar load distribution, arch index, COP, hallux angle, foot dimensions, and gait trajectory. Its versatility and precision make it an ideal tool for comparative analyses of foot biomechanics across diverse demographic groups.

Measurement of plantar load distribution and arch index

The measurement procedure for plantar load distribution is to guide each participant to wear shorts, take off shoes and socks, and stand comfortably on the sensing cushion with the specific marks and measuring range of the JC Mat, then, under the guidance of the experiment instructor, each participant stampeded for six to eight steps at their own pace and then stood in a standing position. Their arms hung naturally, balanced the COP by naturally standing with their feet shoulder-width apart, and looked directly at the instructor’s eyes in front of them. Keep the body balanced and stationary until their bipedal plantar load values measured by the JC Mat have no obvious changes. Meanwhile, another researcher immediately pressed the capture button to capture the participant’s plantar load distribution measurement data. Each participant had to repeat this protocol three times to obtain an average value.

The indicator parameters assessed in this study, such as plantar load distribution, arch index, COP and hallux angle are all directly analyzed through the JC Mat built-in FPDS-Pro-V2 program. Based on the protocol of previous studies, these values could be obtained directly from fixed-point connection analysis of the color footprint images [31–33]. In terms of plantar load distribution and arch index estimation, the researcher accurately identified two points located at the base of the second toe and the central base of the heel from the captured color footprint images. Once these two points are positioned, the program automatically generates a vertical line as well as four horizontal tangent lines with the same spacing between the forefoot and rearfoot (excluding the toes). On the basis of these tangents, the footprint image can be categorized into either five main regions or six subregions. The six subregions are defined sequentially from anterolateral to posteromedial of the footprint as (1) the lateral metatarsal (L.M), (2) the lateral longitudinal arch (L.LA), (3) the lateral heel (L.H), (4) the medial metatarsal (M.M), (5) the medial longitudinal arch (M.LA), and (6) the medial heel (M.H). Furthermore, each of the five major regions is individually composed of these six subregions, they are defined in order from front to back and from lateral to medial as (A) forefoot (subregion 1 and 4), (B) midfoot (subregion 2 and 5), (C) rearfoot (subregion 3 and 6), (D) lateral foot (subregion 1, 2 and 3), and (E) medial foot (subregion 4, 5 and 6) [31–33].

For calculating the arch index (AI), the study also follows the calculation formula proposed by Cavanagh and Rodgers, which divides the midfoot region of a footprint by its total region except for toes, that is, AI = B/(A + B + C). According to their definition, high arches are defined as AI values below 0.21, normal arches as AI values between 0.21 and 0.26 and flat arches as AI values above 0.26 [34].

Measurement of hallux angle

The hallux angle is calculated by intersecting two straight lines on the footprint image. Based on the method described in past literature, the first straight line is generated through two fixed points: the center point of the innermost edge of the heel and the center point of the innermost edge of the first metatarsal head. The other line that intersects the first straight line is formed by connecting the center point of the innermost edge of the first metatarsal head and the center point of the innermost edge of the first toe [35]. Several hallux postures can be identified by analyzing the angle change resulting from the intersection of these two straight lines, including varus (−1° to −5°), straight (0°), valgus I (from 1° to 5°), valgus II (from 6° to 10°), valgus III (from 11° to 15°) and valgus IV (≥ 16°) [4, 30].

Measurement of rearfoot postural alignment

Rearfoot postural alignment analysis is primarily used to determine the amount of change in the participant’s heel deflection. The measurement procedure has been described in past studies [31–33]. Based on optical photographs taken at a fixed point from the dorsal aspect of the participant’s lower limbs to the heel, the Biomech 2019 posture analysis software was then used to estimate the intersection angle formed by the lines connecting the fixed points of the lower limbs to determine the changes in the static rearfoot postural alignment of each participant. Different types of rearfoot posture can be identified by heel angle variations, such as varus (< 0°), neutral (from 0° to 5°), and valgus (> 5°) [36].

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was determined using a priori power analysis conducted with the statistical power analysis software G*Power (version 3.1.9.7, Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) [37, 38]. The effect size Cohen’s d was calculated based on the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for each group (HV group: 50.90 ± 2.93; control group: 48.59 ± 3.81). By setting the significance level (alpha) at 0.05, statistical power (1-beta) at 0.80, and the effect size at 0.68 for independent samples t-tests, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 56 participants was required, with 28 subjects allocated to each group [39–41].

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from each experiment depended on normality and were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), while statistics were summarized as p < 0.05 (indicated by *) and p < 0.01 (indicated by **) representing statistically significant differences. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to examine the normality of the data, where p > 0.05 indicates normally distributed data. Indicative parameters such as plantar load distribution and COP were presented as percentages, while hallux angle and rearfoot postural alignment were displayed as angles. Statistical analyses of these parameters between the two groups were conducted by independent sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. For participants with HV, the study further used multiple linear regression analysis to elucidate the associations between hallux angle and other variables. Hallux angle was dependent variable, while rearfoot angles, COP and arch index were independent variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficients for hallux angle with rearfoot angles, COP and arch index were calculated. In the multiple linear regression analysis, the adjusted R square value was determined for the model. The statistically significant was set as p < 0.05 for all determinations. Statistical analyses were performed by the same investigator using the SPSS 27.0 statistical software for Windows (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

Based on the comprehensive results of this study, we found that participants in the HV group exhibited normal arch structures. This finding contrasts with previous literature that associates HV deformities with flatfoot conditions [5–8]. Recent studies have also clearly indicated that approximately 36% of HV patients suffer from flatfoot [42, 43], and a correlation has been identified between HV deformities and the reduction of the medial longitudinal arch [44]. The discrepancies observed in this study compared to those findings may be attributed to the focus on a relatively young and homogeneous cohort, which consisted of individuals with healthy body weights. This suggests that the duration of their HV deformities is relatively short, and their foot conditions may still be in a developmental stage, which means they have not yet experienced sufficient time for secondary arch collapse to become a contributing factor in the progression of HV. Therefore, despite the presence of HV, they may still retain a normal arch structure. Furthermore, the etiology of HV is multifactorial; while flatfoot is recognized as a known risk factor, it may not be the sole cause of HV development in this study population. Similarly, a study conducted in neighboring South Korea reported results akin to those of this study, indicating no association between flatfoot and HV [45]. Consequently, the findings of this study provide new insights into the general relationship between HV and flatfoot. Further research is recommended to include a broader and more diverse population and to conduct longitudinal studies to validate these conclusions and explore causal relationships.

As for the observation from the results of plantar load distributions, participants in the HV group received the majority of plantar loads at the bipedal medial aspects of the metatarsals and the heels, whereas the plantar loads were relatively decreased at the lateral aspects of the right heel. The plantar load distributed at the medial aspects of the metatarsals in the present study is in agreement with previous findings that have mentioned that HV patients experience more plantar load at the metatarsals and produce greater peak pressure and peak force specifically at the second metatarsal, resulting in increased forefoot loading and possible metatarsalgia [13]. The study by Chang et al. clearly noted that patients with mild to severe HV had higher impulses and longer contact durations at their second and third metatarsals, and lower loads at their fourth and fifth metatarsals [12]. Menzildzic et al. also documented the fact that patients with severe HV deformity had a significant pressure distribution above the 500 kPa threshold at the first metatarsal head [11]. The most recent study by Martínez-Nova et al. also reconfirmed that increased plantar loading below the first to third metatarsals in patients with HV is often associated with painful hyperkeratosis of the first metatarsal head or the interphalangeal joint of the big toe [46]. As for the situation where the plantar loads are located at the medial aspects of the heels, a recent clinical study found that when patients with HV wear customized foot orthoses with both medial longitudinal and transverse arch supports, the plantar pressure and contact area of the midfoot and rearfoot are redistributed effectively, improving the functional support of the midfoot [47]. Further, the use of foot orthoses resulted in an immediate and significant reduction in peak pressure and pressure-time integral in the rearfoot (i.e., medial and lateral heel), which maintained a decreasing trend over a one-month follow-up period [47, 48]. According to the aforementioned literature, it is evident that HV patients generally experience uneven plantar pressure, which may be exacerbated as the severity of the deformity progresses. This imbalance may lead to alterations in heel eversion angles, thereby affecting postural balance and stability [49]. Consequently, these findings highlight the importance of targeted interventions, such as customized foot orthotics designed to effectively redistribute plantar loads while enhancing the structural support of the foot arch and stabilizing the rearfoot. These strategies are crucial for alleviating functional impairments and improving postural stability in individuals affected by HV.

As for the results of COP, participants with HV in the study were consistent with those healthy controls who exhibited a symmetrical and balanced state for the feet. One report by Hurn et al. stated that those with mild to moderate HV did not lose their balance, while those with severe HV experienced significant mediolateral oscillation [16]. It was claimed in the study reports by Javaid and Sun et al. that individuals with this condition may be prone to falls when walking and standing due to an impaired gait pattern caused by an imbalance in the center of gravity [2, 3]. Therefore, such differences may be attributed to the age of the subjects and the severity of the thumb deformity.

Furthermore, in terms of rearfoot postural alignment analysis, the results showed that those with HV also experienced statistically significant increases in rearfoot valgus angles. Such a rearfoot pattern echoes the observation in one study that young females with HV typically have reduced lateral plantar pressure and exhibit a tendency to pronate the foot during walking [9]. As well, Coşkun et al. found that the majority of 32 females diagnosed with HV (aged 20 to 54 years) had excessive pronation of the rearfoot in a weight-bearing position [50]. Another study proposed an opinion of ​​reverse causation and suggested that the development of HV deformity is primarily based on rearfoot valgus and a lack of stability and coordination between the forefoot and midfoot that affects gait pattern [10]. Nevertheless, several studies from the past have provided sufficient evidence to suggest that extended or increased pronation of the rearfoot is one of the main intrinsic factors in the development of HV deformity [50–55]. However, the biomechanical interaction and clear causal relationship between the two still require confirmation through investigations involving a large number of subjects across various age groups.

Based on these results, the study further calculated the average plantar load values across six subregions for each foot of participants with HV to obtain the representative footprint image, which provides a visual depiction of the plantar load distribution and foot postural characteristics associated with HV, particularly highlighting the concentration of loads on the medial aspects of the metatarsals and heels. This visual evidence complements the quantitative findings and further substantiates the conclusion that HV deformities are linked to specific plantar load patterns and rearfoot valgus alignment. Furthermore, the results of the multiple linear regression analysis revealed that the bipedal hallux angles of participants with HV were positively correlated with the rearfoot angles of their ipsilateral foot, while no significant correlations were observed with COP or arch index. The findings reaffirmed the fact that there is a correlation between increased HV angle and weight-bearing subtalar pronation [50–55].

Based on the above comprehensive discussion, as this study primarily focused on Taiwanese female collegiate students, its generalizability to other populations is limited. Therefore, it is necessary to include more diverse samples to enhance the applicability of the results. Additionally, since this study employed a cross-sectional design, the causal relationship between HV deformity and the observed biomechanical characteristics remains to be investigated through longitudinal studies to trace the progression of HV and its long-term impact on plantar load distribution and rearfoot posture. Nevertheless, this study initially identified the characteristics of plantar load and COP, as well as changes in rearfoot posture in Taiwanese female collegiate students with HV. The findings provide significant insights into the foot biomechanics and foot pattern characteristics of a young population with relatively simple HV etiology, addressing a gap in the existing literature that primarily focuses on middle-aged and elderly individuals, as well as those with flat feet. While the study population was selected based on inclusion and exclusion criteria to minimize the influence of intrinsic confounding factors (e.g., BMI, aging, degenerative conditions, musculoskeletal injuries, and foot disorders) on foot biomechanics, it is unavoidable that the impact of extrinsic factors (e.g., footwear type and habits), which may lead to biomechanical adaptations, cannot be entirely eliminated. A notable finding is that HV can also occur in individuals with normal arches, suggesting that the development of HV may not be solely attributed to changes in arch morphology but could also be influenced by demographic characteristics, weight distribution patterns, footwear choices, and lifestyle factors. Moreover, HV patients in this study demonstrated symmetrical and balanced CoP, indicating that age and the severity of HV deformity may play a role in balance impairments. Additionally, the study identified a positive correlation between rearfoot valgus angles and ipsilateral HV angles, highlighting the biomechanical interaction between these two conditions. Ultimately, these findings not only provide intuitive and predictive diagnostic references for assessing potential rearfoot eversion changes associated with pronounced HV deformity but also serve as a foundation for considering the use of foot orthoses. Such orthoses, designed to support the foot arch and calcaneus, aim to redistribute plantar loads or increase the contact area of the midfoot and rearfoot, thereby improving foot function support in HV patients [47].

Conclusion

Based on the preliminary findings, Taiwanese female collegiate students with HV in the present study exhibited normal arches, with increased plantar loads concentrated on the medial aspects of the metatarsals and heels. Their COP were symmetrically distributed and balanced across both feet. Furthermore, the rearfoot posture of both feet exhibited a valgus alignment, which was positively correlated with the ipsilateral HV angles. These results highlight the biomechanical interaction between hallux valgus and rearfoot valgus, as well as provide an intuitive and predictable diagnostic reference for assessing potential rearfoot eversion changes linked to a pronounced HV appearance.
