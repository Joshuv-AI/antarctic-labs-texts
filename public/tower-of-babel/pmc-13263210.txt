Conversion from failed antireflux surgery to RefluxStop: A multicenter feasibility, technical evaluation, safety, and effectiveness study with 30 consecutive cases
Authors: Thorsten G Lehmann, Joerg Zehetner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study evaluates the feasibility of RefluxStop conversion after failed antireflux surgery (ARS). Failed ARS necessitating re-surgery is common (comprising explantation, dysphagia, migration/erosion, hernia and reflux recurrence) and technicalities of original ARS may restrict subsequent options.MethodsA two-center retrospective study of 30 consecutive subjects was conducted evaluating feasibility of RefluxStop conversion after failed ARS. Other clinical outcomes included safety, symptomatology, GERD Health-Related Quality-of-Life (GERD-HRQL), and proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use.ResultsThirty (N = 30) subjects underwent RefluxStop conversion from MSAࣧ—43.3%; fundoplicationࣧ—36.7%; EndoStimࣧ—10%; and Otherࣧ—10%. Revision with RefluxStop was straightforward in all cases. Baseline characteristics included: median age 55 years; 33.3% had large hiatal hernia (HH) ≥4 cm in size. At a mean follow-up of 22.3 months, median total score improved by 87.2% (pre- and post-GERD-HRQL available in n = 28/30). Symptoms improved from baseline to 1-year: general reflux symptoms (n = 26 vs. n = 3); regurgitation (n = 11 vs. n = 2); dysphagia (n = 10 vs. n = 2), respectively, and PPI use decreased from n = 28 at baseline to n = 3. Two reoperations for HH recurrence (preoperatively both 4 cm) due to total collapse of the hiatal repair, one second hernia recurrence post-MSA and one third hernia recurrence post-two Toupets, reoperated with hiatoplasty and plication extension, and hiatoplasty alone, respectively.ConclusionConversion to RefluxStop surgery was feasible regardless of prior ARS. Conversion from both fundoplication and MSA were straightforward, although fundoplication required careful handling of vagus nerves, hiatal repair, and plication dissection; MSA being even simpler. At a mean follow-up of 22.3 months, median total GERD-HRQL score improved by 87.2%.Keywords: Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), RefluxStop, Antireflux surgery, Revisional surgery, Hiatal hernia recurrence

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) treated by antireflux surgery (ARS) results in satisfactory outcomes in 85–90%, however, up to 15% experience treatment failure by persistent symptomatology, recurrence, or complications like dysphagia [1]. Reoperation is broadly indicated along a spectrum of cases ranging from severe symptom recurrence (despite an adequate anatomical outcome) to asymptomatic recurrence of abnormal esophagogastric anatomy [1]. Morbidity and mortality are elevated with revisional surgery, being less successful in overall treatment as compared to primary ARS [2, 3]. The poorer success of re-surgery, with the consideration that primary treatment likely failed due to severe or complex disease, shows the dire prognosis that a plurality of patients may face with options being limited. Treatment guidance may also be quite arduous given the technical limitations imposed by prior intervention, persistence of symptoms despite redo of index surgery, and unmet need in this group of challenging cases without adequate relief.

Reoperation after ARS occurs variably depending on intervention selected but nevertheless seems to increase with time. The combined rate of reoperations and PPI use as indication of failure reaches 19% at 1 year in a systematic literature review of Nissen fundoplication [4]. Reoperation after Nissen fundoplication poses technical challenges and is associated with more complication and morbidity as compared to primary surgery, stemming from scarring and herniation (of the fundus into the thorax) that severely affects gastric anatomy and suitability of stomach tissue for esophageal reconstruction [1, 5–7]. Partial fundoplication techniques ostensibly pose less of a challenge for revisional surgery due to less esophageal manipulation and resultant scarring.

Magnetic sphincter augmentation (MSA), an emergent technology, also results in significant rates of reoperation often in two-digit number occurring at a higher rate than the standard of care (as per propensity-matched analysis), where even MSA with complete dissection provided no benefit in dilatation, reoperation, or hernia recurrence as compared to Nissen fundoplication [8]. However, reoperation after MSA is suggested to provide specific advantages over fundoplication due to its technical features; namely, it is feasible to safely remove the MSA device while leaving gastric anatomy intact [9]. Fundoplication techniques and MSA are the most utilized ARSs globally but are nonetheless at risk of reoperation in complicated disease with esophageal dysmotility or large hiatal hernia (HH) [10–12], common in real-world practice. When failure of these conventional surgeries occurs, the arsenal of subsequent options is limited, and novel approaches may help mitigate this treatment gap.

RefluxStop™ (Implantica, Zug, Switzerland) was first introduced in 2018 with CE mark for Europe and is currently in submission with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in the United States. Clinical data from its CE mark trial have been published with follow-up of 1–5 years [13–17]. These reports demonstrated excellent safety and effectiveness with 90% improvement in total GERD Health-Related Quality of Life (GERD-HRQL) score and 90.4% improvement in acid exposure time with no reoperations at 5 years [15, 16]. Real-world evidence has supported these favorable outcomes even in those with esophageal dysmotility and/or large HH (> 3 cm), albeit at a shorter follow-up [18–23].

Given RefluxStop’s aptitude for effectively treating GERD with few adverse events (AEs) based on the literature and our experience, even in those with difficult-to-treat and complex disease, as well as its pioneering methodology for esophagogastric junction reconstruction/stabilization that avoids exacerbation of morbidity (by not encircling the esophagus), RefluxStop surgery may be a suitable candidate for revisional surgery in those with failure of other ARS techniques. The purpose of this study is to report the feasibility and outcomes of converting to RefluxStop after failed ARS and discuss surgical considerations of conversion.

Materials & methods

Study design

Retrospective analysis of 30 consecutive cases was conducted, evaluating the feasibility and clinical outcomes of conversion from failed ARS to the RefluxStop procedure. RefluxStop surgery was performed laparoscopically at two institutions (Germany and Switzerland) where pre- and postoperative evaluations were performed as per country-specific norms. All patients with failure of prior ARS were included contingent on agreement for study participation with signed informed consent. All were counseled individually in terms of operative and non-operative options. General contraindications included pregnancy, known silicone allergy, and history of malignancy, but none were excluded. Data was collected and analyzed, and technical features of surgical conversion were noted. Outcome measures included GERD-HRQL score, PPI use, symptoms (as binary yes/no), and AEs.

Analysis

Categorical data was presented as counts (n) and percentages (%). Continuous data was reported as counts (n), mean, standard deviation (SD), median, interquartile range (IQR), minimum, and maximum. Clinical data included commonly reported baseline characteristics, previous history of ARS, regular daily PPI use, reflux symptomatology, and post-conversion AEs. Quality of life was evaluated by total GERD-HRQL score using the 10-item questionnaire for possible scores of 0–50.

Ethics approval

All subjects provided written and informed consent for surgery and publication of anonymized data. Ethical approval was provided by the institutional review board of the University of Bern, Switzerland (No. 2018 − 01827) and was not required for the German center due to no deviation from institutional standard of care; the study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Safety

In two large hernia cases that had already been reoperated prior to RefluxStop conversion for HH recurrence one and two times, respectively, new recurrent HH occurred after RefluxStop revision, managed with fundic repositioning after extended dissection (with the device intact in its pouch) and new hiatal repair. In the case following MSA, plication extension was also performed. One death occurred after 10 months from vascular disease and was unrelated to the RefluxStop procedure.

Discussion

Redo surgery in the treatment of GERD is generally associated with higher complication rates and limited effectiveness. One of the greatest challenges in surgical management of GERD is deciding appropriate next steps should treatment fail, where patients are ideally offered the best options based on history of illness, demographic factors, and patient priorities. Surgical failure may limit selection of future options and re-surgery with the same surgical technique may not improve outcomes. Certain features of conventional ARS limit subsequent courses of action, should they be necessary, by way of consequences like tissue fibrosis among others. As a result, morbidity and mortality are more pronounced with revisional cases using traditional techniques, with only 70% treatment success as compared to primary ARS [2, 3].

Since a considerable proportion of ARS cases require revision/reoperation, of which various techniques may result in different limitations, a treatment that is ubiquitously applicable as a secondary option is required to eliminate or minimize decision-making hesitance to reoperative measures. This feasibility evaluation showed that RefluxStop surgery is a possible candidate for revisional surgery of prominent ARS types, similarly straightforward (i.e., redo cases were executable to achieve favorable intraoperative results in comparison to early RefluxStop cases on the general population) and effective regardless of previously employed technique, based on this case series of 30 subjects with short- to mid-term follow-up. We present the surgical considerations determining the feasibility of conversion to RefluxStop as the focus of this study, with longer term follow-up and larger samples of benefit to further explore this facet of foregut surgery, particularly given late recurrences are relevant. To better understand the complicating features of ARS failure, we must first refer to the literature.

Failure of ARS is denoted by disease persistence or recurrence, often necessitating revisional surgery. Common indications for reoperation are recurrent reflux (41.7%) often involving recurrence of HH, dysphagia (16.6%), and recurrent reflux with dysphagia (4.0%) [1], postoperative outcomes that arise either from poor/diminished treatment effect [24] or as sequelae due to surgical manipulation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) [4, 25–27]. Of the acid reflux sufferers that undergo ARS, up to 15% experience treatment failure and revisional surgery is required for alleviation of symptoms despite poorer treatment success rates [1]. For instance, reoperation performed after the gold standard, Nissen fundoplication, is associated with more complication and morbidity as compared to index operation [1, 5–7]. The resulting treatment gap consists of patients with severe disease that are not adequately managed by conventional ARS and have few options. Study of revisional ARS outcomes is limited to symptom recurrence with scarce anatomical and functional evaluation of the esophagogastric junction [1]. The morbidity of reoperation is pronounced most often due to direct esophageal or gastric injury during re-surgery, which may be because of the complexity of adhesions after primary surgery [1].

The incidence of reoperation after primary ARS is conceivably higher than ideal according to the literature: Nissen fundoplication (up to 16.3%) [4], Toupet fundoplication (3.1-5%) [28–30], and MSA (10%) [8], as prominent surgical treatment options. RefluxStop as primary surgery, by comparison, had a much lower reoperation rate (1.99% or n = 12/602) according to the multicenter (i.e., 602 patients) real-world safety study from 22 European centers conducted by the RefluxStop Evaluation Group [31], and may be a suitable candidate for revisional surgery since only two reoperations occurred after previous surgical failures in this analysis. However, longer follow-up with larger sample sizes is targeted since late recurrence is relevant for the topic of revisional surgery. Decision-making for subsequent treatment must consider anatomical and physiologically reactive consequences of primary surgery like regional fibrosis, weakened musculature, and disturbance of gastric anatomy [1, 5–7, 32]. RefluxStop aims to restore all attributes of the antireflux barrier in parallel, with diversified reliance on all components of the esophagogastric junction and plication necessitating only limited (90–120°) esophageal circumference, thereby avoiding encircling or compressing the distal esophagus.

This study evaluated the feasibility of RefluxStop conversion from previously failed fundoplication, MSA, BICORN, and EndoStim. A large majority (80%) of conversions to RefluxStop were from previous fundoplication or MSA, and 16.7% of cases overall were converted after two previous procedures. Reoperation with RefluxStop was feasible in all (N = 30) cases regardless of primary ARS type: GERD-related quality of life (0–50) improved by median 87.2%, 82.1% of patients experienced ≥50% improvement in GERD-HRQL score, PPI use improved from n = 28/30 at baseline to n = 3/30 at follow-up, reflux symptoms improved from baseline after RefluxStop (i.e., general reflux symptomsࣧn = 26/30 vs. n = 3/30, regurgitationࣧn = 12/30 vs. n = 2/12, and dysphagiaࣧn = 10/30 vs. n = 2/30, respectively), and two reoperations for reherniation occurred in patients with large hernia that had already underwent one and two reoperations, respectively, for re-herniation(s) before RefluxStop redo. Both cases were resolved with fundic repositioning with RefluxStop unaffected in its pouch and new hiatoplasty (plication extension in one case).

RefluxStop redo of existing ARS procedures was straightforward with both fundoplication and MSA, the most used ARS techniques worldwide, although conversion from fundoplication may be slightly more challenging in comparison because of presupposing tissue fibrosis. Based on experience from these 30 redo cases, we see RefluxStop surgery as a promising candidate for revisional surgery particularly in cases of hernia recurrence, supported by real-world data [18, 19, 21–23]. Exposition of clinical context for each conversion type is provided below.

Conversion from Nissen, Toupet, and Dor fundoplication

Failure of Nissen fundoplication, the surgical gold standard, requires reoperation in 6.7% of patients by 1 year, according to a systematic literature review of 63 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and increases with time to 8.7% (i.e., 5 years) [4]. Partial wrapping techniques like Toupet fundoplication result in lower reoperation rates of 3.1-5% [28, 30], presumably due to less gastroesophageal interaction.

Fundoplication may result in treatment failure due to anatomical abnormalities like intrathoracic wrap migration (27.9%), wrap disruption (22.7%), telescoping (14.1%), paraesophageal hiatal herniation (6.1%), hiatal disruption (5.3%), tight wrap (5.3%), and stricture (1.9%) [1]. Thus, the integral wrapping mechanism of fundoplication may have a paradoxical and counterproductive effect that is either therapeutic or a source of treatment failure. Since creation of a wrap that is overly tight and disruption of hiatal repair are more likely after laparoscopic surgery [1], higher rates of reoperation should be expected from this type of ARS in its modern iteration. Furthermore, there are technical challenges associated with revision of Nissen fundoplication due to scarring, fundus herniation, and severe disruption of gastric anatomy, thus making the stomach unsuitable for esophageal reconstruction [1, 5–7]. Importantly, infirmity of fundus wall musculature may occur from prior plication, acting as a potential impediment to subsequent surgical intervention. In our experience, such cases with very thin fundus wall were perceived with caution and RefluxStop implantation was considered prudently.

In this study, one reoperation for reherniation occurred in a patient with two previous Toupet fundoplications. This patient had large HH before conversion and two prior operations, RefluxStop being the third in sequence to signify the difficulty of this case. This case was reoperated with fundic repositioning with the implant unaffected and intact in its invagination and new hiatal repair. It is possible that total collapse of hiatal repair in cases of large hernia may permit RefluxStop-pouch (i.e., ~ 4 cm apparatus) herniation, but its unique mechanism as an abdominal anchor [18, 33] may reduce the incidence even in such difficult cases.

Conversion to RefluxStop from Toupet (20%), Nissen (10%), both Nissen and Toupet (3.3%), and Dor (3.3%) fundoplication was similar, however, partial fundoplication (i.e., Toupet and Dor fundoplication) was slightly more facile a priori plication dissection. In all cases, total and partial fundoplication alike required careful handling of vagus nerves, hiatus repair, and plication dissection of gastroesophageal adherences to varying degrees. Nonetheless, conversion to RefluxStop surgery after Nissen, Toupet, and Dor fundoplication was feasible in all cases and subjects experienced favorable treatment effectiveness.

Conversion from MSA

Cases of MSA were the most common (43.3%) primary surgery considered for conversion to RefluxStop surgery in our study, perhaps due to larger market share, higher need for reoperation, or both. The literature suggests that the post-surgical period of MSA-operated subjects may be more cumbersome than the gold standard in terms of sequelae, mostly due to food passageway-related side effects where postoperative dysphagia is reportedly more severe and lasting longer as compared to Nissen fundoplication [34]. In terms of re-intervention, dilatation is evidently often insufficient and more drastic measures (i.e., reoperation) are required. Although sphincter augmentation as a primary surgery is technically simpler than fundoplication due to technique standardization [35], device-related factors like correct sizing and capsular fibrosis may be associated with odious postoperative dysphagia and even erosion [32]. In fact, reoperation after MSA occurs in up to 10% of patients generally due to postoperative dysphagia and other side effects [8, 36]. Moreover, fibrotic encapsulation from MSA implantation even after the device has been removed/explanted contributes to an integrated relaxation pressure that remains elevated, and thus outflow resistance and postoperative dysphagia persist [36].

Fibrotic device encapsulation is to be expected with implantation of any device, and with MSA this may disturb esophagogastric function and result in revisional surgery [32, 33]. Fibrotic encapsulation of the RefluxStop implant is adjacent to the esophagogastric junction (i.e., cranial fundic pouch fibrosis) and likely mitigates direct functional insult to the distal esophagus, while hanging in a tear-drop fashion into the stomach and not interacting with any other structures. Furthermore, fibrosis of the invagination pouch serves to buttress the platform of antireflux barrier reconstruction in addition to acting as an anchor preventing reherniation [18, 33]. These functional assertions are extrapolated through surgical logic and observation, requiring verification via structural and functional assessment.

Ultimately, RefluxStop conversion from MSA was feasible in all (N = 13) cases and was generally unchallenging. The MSA devices were removed by 8 to 4 o’clock dissection of encapsulation and RefluxStop surgery was performed as per protocol. One case of reherniation (i.e., baseline HH size 4 cm) occurred after MSA-to-RefluxStop conversion in this study, which was repaired by uncomplicated hiatoplasty with the device left intact in its fundic pouch as well as extension of esophageal plication.

Conversion from the BICORN procedure

The BICORN procedure is not commonly used and is reported only in the research context. It has similarities to Dor fundoplication, including a limited circumferential plication, and involves reconstruction of original anatomy [37]. In one study, the BICORN procedure is reported to provide patient satisfaction in 76.7% of patients despite PPI consumption remaining high, up to ~ 50% mid- to long-term [37], but overall there is a paucity of clinical data regarding safety and effectiveness. Furthermore, the BICORN procedure does not include device implantation, that in the case of RefluxStop ostensibly provides regional stability of reconstructed anatomy (with a fibrotic and implant anchor). Thus, esophagogastric anatomy is logically more likely disrupted or herniated after BICORN, but data is lacking on this currently experimental procedure. The stabilizing mechanism of RefluxStop is supported by multiple publications showing excellent safety and effectiveness in its pivotal trial for up to 5 years [13–17] and reherniation was even prevented in cases of large HH (>3 cm) in real-world practice [18, 19, 21–23]. In fact, fibrotic encapsulation of the RefluxStop implant may be sufficient in supporting anatomical stability, even in rare cases of asymptomatic early device erosion (exiting uneventfully via digestive tract per implant design) where fundic scarring alone strengthens the antireflux barrier and effectively treats acid reflux.

The rate of reoperation after the BICORN procedure is not readily available from the literature to the best of our knowledge and overall clinical data is scarce. Conversion from the BICORN procedure shared similarities to Dor fundoplication but was easier to perform. After dissection of gastroesophageal attachment was accomplished, RefluxStop surgery was performed as per protocol with no postoperative complications and subjects were effectively treated.

Conversion from EndoStim

Conversion to RefluxStop from EndoStim was straightforward as abdominal structures were limitedly affected by prior intervention. The EndoStim device was simply removed and RefluxStop implantation was performed as recommended. Esophageal neurostimulation of the LES by implantation of an electrical neuromodulator (electrodes placed on the sphincter) is the mechanism of EndoStim therapy [38]. The efficacy of this approach remains uncertain [39] and uncontrolled study has shown reduction of symptoms and acid exposure despite incomplete response to PPIs at ≥12 months, also potentially resulting in serious device- or procedure-related events (i.e., bowel perforation and erosion) [40]. Reporting on the reoperation rates after EndoStim is lacking potentially from its minimally invasive nature and ease of removal, but factors prompting removal presumably include persistent symptoms, implant site pain, localized infection, and postoperative nausea [41]. Since EndoStim is minimally invasive, esophagogastric junction anatomy was unaffected by surgical manipulation and conversion was quite straightforward. As such, the subjects converted to RefluxStop from EndoStim were well-treated and experienced no AEs.

Strengths and limitations

A limitation of this study is the small sample size (N = 30) of subjects undergoing revisional surgery with RefluxStop. However, fundoplication techniques and MSA together are the most utilized antireflux procedures globally [38] (prominent in our studyࣧ—80%) and this series reflected such real-world cases. Moreover, outcomes were based on symptoms and patient-reported measures without objective postoperative assessments like pH-impedance, barium swallow, or endoscopy, limiting confirmation of physiological or anatomical improvement. However, the primary purpose of this study was the feasibility of conducting the RefluxStop conversion after failed ARS, unreported until now, and effectiveness evaluation with a low burden (i.e., questionnaire versus uncomfortable testing) to the patient seemed most appropriate. More comprehensive clinical outcome evaluation with a larger sample size and longer follow-up would be of benefit. The patient population consisted completely of complex cases in which treatment failure had occurred and demographics (i.e., 33.3% with HH ≥4 cm) posed more risk of adverse outcome. Despite the reasonable doubt of treatment success in such cases, RefluxStop surgery was successfully performed in all subjects with clinical outcomes comparable to results in previously unoperated patients, albeit at a short-term follow-up. Although this analysis included follow-up of almost 2 years on average, recurrence after ARS may emerge later and longer-term follow-up is important to confirm durability but was secondary in the scope of this feasibility study. Longer term results and additional inclusion of subjects for follow-up study would strengthen the analysis.

This study assessed the feasibility of RefluxStop surgery as a potential candidate for revisional surgery and additional investigation would be of benefit. RefluxStop surgery is a novel (2018) ARS in which long-term data is published from its CE mark trial and real-world evidence is still being generated. Several investigator-initiated studies have been published reflective of real-world settings, including those with reoperation risk due to comorbid ineffective esophageal motility [20, 21] and large HH [18, 19, 22, 23] with results up to 4 years. This article contributes to this corpus of real-world evidence and RefluxStop surgery seems to be a favorable candidate for revisional surgery of previously failed ARS despite the potentially challenging features of reoperation; however, head-to-head comparison with conventional techniques is necessary, although a less prioritized area of research in the current adoption curve phase of this emerging technology. Our report is of novelty for describing conversions to RefluxStop, but further clinical study is required to provide more detail as to clinical outcomes of RefluxStop revisional surgery. RefluxStop conversion was feasible and generally unchallenging across all primary ARS types while effectively treating GERD with minimal AEs and only two reoperations for repair of HH recurrence transpired in cases that had already been reoperated for this event before RefluxStop redo, in one case even twice. Both HH recurrence redo procedures were managed with RefluxStop unaffected in situ.

Conclusion

Conversion of failed ARS to the RefluxStop procedure was feasible in all (N = 30) subjects regardless of procedure type: MSA (43.3%), fundoplication (36.7%), BICORN (10%), and EndoStim (10%). Total GERD-HRQL score (0–50) improved by median 87.2%, PPI use decreased to n = 3/30, and regurgitation were reduced to n = 2/30 patients at follow-up and two reoperations occurredࣧ—HH recurrence in large hernia cases already reoperated for such recurrence (in one case even twice) before RefluxStop conversion, the device was not involved, unaffected in its fundal pouch during revision. Conversion from fundoplication and MSA were both straightforward, although a minor challenge may be posed with fibrotic tissue dissection after fundoplication. RefluxStop is a potential candidate for revision of ARS after treatment failure, common in real-world practice and further evaluation is targeted with larger samples and longer follow-up.
