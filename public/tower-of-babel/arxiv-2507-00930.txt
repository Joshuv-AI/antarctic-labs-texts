Inverse Matroid Optimization Under Subset Constraints
Kristóf Bérczi, Mirabel Mendoza-Cadena, José A. Soto

Abstract
In the Inverse Matroid problem, we are given a matroid, a fixed basis B B , and an initial weight function, and the goal is to minimally modify the weights – measured by some function – so that B B becomes a maximum-weight basis. The problem arises naturally in settings where one wishes to explain or enforce a given solution by minimally perturbing the input. We extend this classical problem by replacing the fixed basis with a subset S 0 S_{0} of the ground set and imposing various structural constraints on the set of maximum-weight bases relative to S 0 S_{0} . Specifically, we study six variants: (A) Inverse Matroid Exists , where S 0 S_{0} must contain at least one maximum-weight basis; (B) Inverse Matroid All , where all bases contained in S 0 S_{0} are maximum-weight; and (C) Inverse Matroid Only , where S 0 S_{0} contains exactly the maximum-weight bases, along with their natural negated counterparts. For all variants, we develop combinatorial polynomial-time algorithms under the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -norm. A key ingredient is a refined min-max theorem for Inverse Matroid under the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -norm, which enables simpler and faster algorithms than previous approaches and may be of independent combinatorial interest. Our work significantly broadens the range of inverse optimization problems on matroids that can be solved efficiently, especially those that constrain the structure of optimal solutions through subset inclusion or exclusion. Keywords: Inverse optimization, Matroids, Min-Max theorem, Partial inverse problem, Subset constraints

Introduction

In inverse optimization problems, the goal is, broadly speaking, to modify certain parameters of an optimization problem such that the resulting optimal aligns with a given fixed feasible set. Due to their applicability in both theory and practice, inverse problems have garnered significant interest in past decades; we refer the interested reader to
[
28
,
18
,
13
,
10
]
for surveys.

Let
S
be a ground set and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{S}
be the set of feasible solutions. Given weights
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, we denote by
(S,\mathcal{F},w)
the optimization problem of finding a maximum-weight member of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
w
. By an
objective function

\|\cdot\|
, we mean a function from
\mathbb{R}^{S}
to
\mathbb{R}
that measures the magnitude of a vector, such as a norm. Then, a classical inverse optimization problem can formally be described as follows.

Inverse Optimization (IO)

Input:
A finite ground set
S
, a collection
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{S}
of feasible solutions for an underlying optimization problem, an input solution
F^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
, a weight function
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, an objective function
\|\cdot\|
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
, and an oracle
\mathcal{O}
for the maximization problem
(S,\mathcal{F},w^{\prime})
for any weight function
w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

Goal:
Find a new weight function
w^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
such that

(a)

F^{*}
is a maximum-weight member of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
w^{*}
, and

(b)

\|w-w^{*}\|
is minimized.

A weight function that has property
(a)
is called
feasible
, and it is called
optimal
if additionally
(b)
holds. For an optimal weight function
w^{*}
, its
optimal value
is
\delta^{*}\coloneqq\|w-w^{*}\|
.

Motivated by applications in dynamic pricing and partial inverse problems, we introduce several extensions of
IO
where, instead of a fixed solution, a subset
S_{0}\subseteq S
is specified. The goal is to modify the weight function so that at least one, all, or exactly the feasible solutions contained in
S_{0}
become maximum-weight. We also consider the corresponding negated variants, resulting in six distinct problems that capture different requirements on the structure of optimal solutions.

1.1
Our results and techniques

In this paper, we focus on the
Inverse Matroid
(
IM
) problem under
\ell_{\infty}
-norm – a special case of
IO
where the feasible set
\mathcal{F}
consists of the bases of a matroid and the goal is to make a given basis maximum-weight while minimizing the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of the deviation from the original weights.

In Section
3
, we revisit a result by Bérczi, Mendoza-Cadena, and Varga
[
7
]
on general inverse optimization problems. We present a simple algorithm for
IM
, achieving better running time than earlier methods (Theorem
3.4
). At the same time, we give a refinement of the min-max theorem of
[
7
]
in the matroid setting (Corollary
3.5
).

Then we turn our attention to inverse matroid problems under subset constraints, where instead of a fixed basis, a subset
S_{0}\subseteq S
of the ground set is given. In Section
4
, we study the
Inverse Matroid Exists (IM-Exists)
problem, where the goal is to modify the weight function so that
S_{0}
contains a maximum-weight basis. Revealing structural properties of optimal solutions (Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
), we present two algorithms: one based on binary search (Theorem
4.5
) and another via reduction to
IM
(Theorem
4.6
).

Section
5
studies the
Inverse Matroid All (IM-All)
and
Only (IM-Only)
problems. In
IM-All
, the goal is to modify the weights so that all bases in
S_{0}
become maximum-weight;
IM-Only
additionally requires that no other basis is optimal. Using a structural result on optimal solutions (Lemma
5.4
), we give a two-step algorithm for
IM-All
involving a homogenization phase to equalize the weights of bases in
S_{0}
, followed by a maximization step to ensure optimality (Theorem
5.5
). A similar strategy yields a solution for
IM-Only
(Theorem
5.7
); here, we consider integral weight functions only to ensure that an optimal solution exists. These results are somewhat surprising, as controlling the weight of a fixed set of bases appears difficult at first glance – yet it becomes tractable when the set includes all bases contained in a given subset.

The remainder of the paper is devoted to the negated versions of these problems, where we again add integrality constraints to ensure the existence of optimal solutions. Section
6
focuses on
Inverse Matroid Not Exists
, where the goal is to modify the weights so that no maximum-weight basis is contained in
S_{0}
. We approach the problem in multiple steps: first, we handle simple base cases (Theorem
6.2
), then introduce a relaxed intermediate problem that might be of independent interest (Theorem
6.5
), which ultimately leads to a full solution (Theorem
6.8
).

Section
7
introduces the
Inverse Matroid Not All (IM-Not-All)
and
Not Only (IM-Not-Only)
problems. In
IM-Not-All
, the goal is to modify the weights so that not all bases in
S_{0}
are maximum-weight. The
IM-Not-Only
problem relaxes this requirement by allowing either some basis in
S_{0}
to be non-maximum-weight or the existence of a maximum-weight basis that is not contained in
S_{0}
. To solve these problems, we first analyze the structure of optimal solutions (Lemma
7.3
), and then deduce efficient algorithms for both problems (Theorem
7.4
and Corollary
7.5
).

Finally, we conclude the paper with a list of open problems in Section
8
. Our algorithms rely on several intermediate subproblems, such as finding a weight function that assigns the same weight to all bases of a matroid, or finding one that ensures a maximum-weight basis is not contained in a given set. While these problems are not difficult, they serve as key building blocks for efficient algorithms and are thus of particular interest. Table

1.2
Further implications

The six problems presented in the previous section explore different aspects of the inverse matroid problem under subset constraints. It is worth noting, however, that the framework we introduce applies to a much wider range of problems. Let
M
be a matroid over ground set
S
, and let
S_{0}\subseteq S
be a subset. Depending on whether we apply the problems to
M
or its dual
M^{*}
, and whether we consider
S_{0}
or its complement
S\setminus S_{0}
, different problem variants arise.

For example, applying
IM-Exists
to the dual matroid
M^{*}
and the set
S\setminus S_{0}
allows us to find a weight function for which
M^{*}
has a maximum-weight basis contained in
S\setminus S_{0}
; this is equivalent to
M
having a minimum-weight basis containing
S_{0}
. Similarly, applying
IM-All
to
M^{*}
and the set
S_{0}
lets us find a weight function so that all bases of
M^{*}
contained in
S_{0}
are maximum-weight; equivalently, all bases of
M
containing
S\setminus S_{0}
have minimum weight.

The six base problems (
IM-Exists, IM-All, IM-Only, IM-Not-Exists, IM-Not-All, IM-Not-Only
), combined with the choice of
M
or
M^{*}
and exchanging the roles of
S_{0}
and its complement
S\setminus S_{0}
, yield 24 different problems – all of which can be solved by one of our algorithms. In particular, our framework can be used for solving partial inverse problems; see Section
1.3
for further details.

1.3
Related work and motivation

Inverse matroid problems under subset constraints are closely related to several areas in combinatorial optimization. In the following, we provide a brief overview of previous work and related topics.

Inverse matroid problem.

The
Inverse Matroid
problem was first introduced by Cai
[
9
]
, who studied matroid intersection under the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm. Dell’Amico, Maffioli, and Malucelli
[
12
]
later considered the problem under the
\ell_{1}
-norm and proposed an auxiliary matroid construction. Variants of the problem have since been explored under other distance measures, including weighted Hamming
[
3
]
and Chebyshev distances
[
29
]
. More generally, inverse optimization under
\ell_{\infty}
and
\ell_{1}
norms has been studied for 0-1 linear programs
[
30
,
2
,
31
]
, which include
Inverse Matroid
as a special case.

Beyond classical matroid structures, recent work has considered inverse optimization over general set systems. Bérczi, Mendoza-Cadena, and Varga
[
7
,
6
]
studied inverse problems under the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm and the span objectives, where the feasible solutions are not necessarily defined by matroids. This framework includes matroids as a special case and applies to a wider range of combinatorial problems.

Inverse problems with forced and forbidden elements.

Partial inverse optimization problems aim to adjust weights so that a desired subset of elements is either included in or excluded from an optimal solution. In the matroid setting, Li, Zhang, and Lai
[
21
]
and Zhang, Li, Lai, and Du
[
32
]
considered the problem of modifying weights (only decreasing or only increasing, respectively) so that a maximum-weight basis contains a given independent set. More generally, similar problems have been studied for spanning trees, cuts, and matchings
[
14
,
22
,
25
,
24
,
23
]
, with different complexities depending on the structure of the constraints and the norm used as an objective
[
17
,
20
,
16
]
. In the exclusion variant, where certain elements must be avoided in any optimal solution, many well-known problems become
\mathsf{NP}
-hard even under simple norms
[
20
,
16
]
. Ahmadian, Bhaskar, Sanità, and Swamy
[
1
]
considered a related problem where a subset
S_{0}\subseteq S
is given, and the goal is to find a weight function with small
\ei
-norm (instead of minimizing the deviation from an initial weight function) for which the bases contained in
S_{0}
are exactly the maximum-weight bases of the matroid.

Inverse matroid problems with forced and forbidden elements fit naturally into our subset-constrained framework. Given a matroid
M
, an independent set
I
, and a set of forbidden elements
F
, the goal is to modify the weight function so that there exists a maximum-weight basis
B
satisfying
I\subseteq B\subseteq S\setminus F
. This condition can be rephrased using the dual matroid
M^{*}
: we seek a basis
B^{*}
such that
F\subseteq B^{*}\subseteq S\setminus I
. Hence, the problem reduces to an instance of
Inverse Matroid Exists
on the matroid obtained from
M^{*}
by contracting
F
, with
S\setminus I
as the target set
S_{0}
.

Dynamic pricing schemes.

A combinatorial market consists of a set of indivisible items and a set of agents, where each agent has a valuation function assigning a value to each subset of items. Cohen-Addad, Eden, Feldman, and Fiat
[
11
]
and independently Hsu, Morgenstern, Rogers, Roth, and Vohra
[
19
]
observed that Walrasian prices alone may not be enough to achieve optimal social welfare based solely on buyers’ decisions. To address this,
[
11
]
introduced the concept of dynamic pricing schemes. Unlike static models, the dynamic approach allows prices to be updated between buyer arrivals based on the remaining items and agents. A central open question posed in
[
11
]
asked whether every market with gross substitutes valuations admits a dynamic pricing scheme that achieves optimal social welfare. Progress has been made in several special cases: the problem was resolved positively for matching markets
[
11
]
, bi-demand markets
[
5
]
, multi-demand markets with up to three
[
8
]
and four
[
27
]
agents, and matching markets with fairness constraints
[
4
]
.

Since matroid rank functions form a subclass of gross substitutes valuations, a natural first step toward understanding the general case is to investigate whether a pricing scheme exists for markets with matroid rank valuations
[
5
]
. In this setting, the problem can be reduced to the following inverse-type formulation: given
k
matroids
M_{1},\dots,M_{k}
over the same ground set, find a weight function
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
such that, for any maximum
w
-weight basis
B
of
M_{i}
, the complement
S\setminus B
is a basis of the sum of the remaining matroids. This problem resembles the setting where optimal bases are required to lie within a specified subset.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

We denote the sets of
real
and
integer
numbers by
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{Z}
, respectively, and add
+
as a subscript whenever non-negativity is assumed. For a positive integer
k
, we use
[k]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,k\}
. Let
S
be a ground set of size
n
. Given subsets
X,Y\subseteq S
, the
symmetric difference
of
X
and
Y
is denoted by
X\triangle Y\coloneqq(X\setminus Y)\cup(Y\setminus X)
. If
Y
consists of a single element
y
, then
X\setminus\{y\}
and
X\cup\{y\}
are abbreviated as
X-y
and
X+y
, respectively. Given a weight
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, the weight of a set
F\in\mathcal{F}
is
w(F)\coloneqq\sum_{s\in F}w(s)
. By convention, we define
\min\emptyset=+\infty
and
\max\emptyset=-\infty
. Given a vector
p\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
and
1\leq q<\infty
, its
\ell_{\infty}
-norm
is
\|p\|_{\infty}\coloneqq\sum_{s\in S}p(s)
. The
characteristic vector
of a set
Z\subseteq S
is
\chi_{Z}(s)=1
if
s\in Z
and
0
otherwise.

2.2
Matroids

Here we give a brief summary of the basic definitions from matroid theory; for further details, we refer the reader to
[
26
]
.

A
matroid

M=(S,\mathcal{I})
is defined by its
ground set

S
and its
family of independent sets

\mathcal{I}\subseteq 2^{S}
that satisfies the
independence axioms
: (I1)
\emptyset\in\mathcal{I}
, (I2)
X\subseteq Y,\ Y\in\mathcal{I}\Rightarrow X\in\mathcal{I}
, and (I3)
X,Y\in\mathcal{I},\ |X|<|Y|\Rightarrow\exists e\in Y\setminus X\ s.t.\ X+e\in\mathcal{I}
. The
rank

r(X)
of a set
X
is the maximum size of an independent set in
X
. The
rank
of the matroid is
r(S)
. The maximal independent subsets of
S
are called
bases
and their family is usually denoted by
\mathcal{B}
. If the matroid is given by its rank function or by its family of bases instead of its independent sets, then we write
M=(S,r)
or
M=(S,\mathcal{B})
, respectively.

An inclusionwise minimal non-independent set is called a
circuit
. A subset
X\subseteq S
is said to
span
or
generate
a subset
Y\subseteq S
if
r(X\cup Y)=r(X)
. The
closure
of
X
is defined as
\operatorname{cl}(X)=\{s\in S\mid r(X+s)=r(X)\}
. The closure of
X
is also called the set
generated
or
spanned
by
X
.

Let
M_{1}=(S_{1},\mathcal{I}_{1}),\dots,M_{k}=(S_{k},\mathcal{I}_{k})
be matroids on disjoint ground sets and let
S=S_{1}\cup\dots\cup S_{k}
. The
direct sum

M_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus M_{k}
is the matroid
M=(S,\mathcal{I})
, where
\mathcal{I}=\{I\subseteq S\mid I\cap S_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\ \text{for $i\in[k]$}\}
. For a matroid
M=(S,r)
, let
S=S_{1}\cup\dots\cup S_{k}
be the partition of its ground set into the connected components of the circuit hypergraph of
M
. Then the matroids
M_{i}=M|S_{i}
for
i\in[k]
are called the
connected components
of
M
and
M=M_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus M_{k}
. We say that
M
is
connected
if
k=1
, or in other words, if for any two elements
x,y\in S
there exists a circuit containing both of them. The connected components of
M
can be computed in polynomial time by taking any basis
B
and computing the connected components of the hypergraph of fundamental circuits
C(B,e)
for
e\in S\setminus B
.

Given a matroid
M=(S,\mathcal{B})
, the
dual of
M
is the matroid
M^{*}=(S,\mathcal{B}^{*})
where
\mathcal{B}^{*}\coloneqq\{X\mid S\setminus X\in\mathcal{B}\}
, that is, the bases of
M^{*}
are the complements of the bases of
M
. For a matroid
M=(S,\mathcal{I})
, its
restriction
to a subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
is the matroid
M|{S^{\prime}}=(S^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
where
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}\mid I\subseteq S^{\prime}\}
.

The following observation gives an extension of the exchange property, see e.g.
[
15
, Theorem 5.3.4]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any two bases
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
of a matroid, there exists a bijection
\varphi\colon B_{1}\setminus B_{2}\to B_{2}\setminus B_{1}
such that
B_{1}-e+\varphi(e)
is a basis for every
e\in B_{1}\setminus B_{2}
.

A well-known characterization of a basis having maximum weight is as follows, see e.g.
[
15
, Thm. 5.5.3]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

A basis
B
is of maximum weight if and only if
w(f)\leq w(e)
for every pair of elements
f\in S-B
and
e\in C(B,f)
.

As usual in matroid algorithms, we assume that the matroid is given by an independence oracle, and we measure the running time by the number of oracle calls and other elementary operations. For ease of discussion, by a “polynomial number” of oracle calls we mean polynomial in the size of the ground set of the matroid.

3
Inverse Matroid

In this section, we focus on
IM
, where the underlying optimization problem is to find a maximum-weight basis of a matroid. In Section
3.1
, we recall some of the results presented in
[
7
]
that we will rely on in our proofs. Section
3.2
then presents our algorithm that is significantly simpler and faster than previous approaches. Furthermore, our technique leads to a refinement of the min-max theorem from
[
7
]
. Formally,
IM
is defined as follows.

Inverse Matroid (IM)

Input:
A matroid
M=(S,\mathcal{I})
, a fixed basis
B^{*}
, a weight function
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, and an objective function
\|\cdot\|
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

Goal:
Find a weight function
w^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
such that

(a)

B^{*}
is a maximum-weight basis of
M
with respect to
w^{*}
, and

(b)

\|w-w^{*}\|
is minimized.

Then a weight function is called
feasible
if condition
(a)
holds. Note that
IM
is always feasible: set
w^{*}(s)=0
for all
s\in S
.

Remark 3.1
.

Given a weight function
w^{\prime}
, we can verify in polynomial time whether
w^{\prime}
is feasible for
IM
. To do so, compute a maximum
w^{\prime}
-weight basis of
M
, and compare its weight to that of
B^{*}
.

3.1
Previous results

IO
under the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm was studied by Bérczi, Mendoza-Cadena, and Varga
[
7
]
. A key step in their proof was showing that for the
\ei
-norm,
IO
always admits an optimal weight function
w^{*}
with a special structure.

Proposition 3.2

(Bérczi, Mendoza-Cadena, and Varga
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\delta^{*}
be the optimum value of an
IO
instance
(S,\mathcal{F},F^{*},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
. Then, for any
\delta\geq\delta^{*}
,

w_{\delta}(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}w(s)+\delta&\text{if $s\in F^{*}$},\\
w(s)-\delta&\text{if $s\in S\setminus F^{*}$}\end{cases}

is a feasible weight function. In particular,
w_{\delta^{*}}
is an optimal solution.

Furthermore, using this observation, the following min-max characterization was also provided in
[
7
]
.

Proposition 3.3

(Bérczi, Mendoza-Cadena, and Varga
[
7
]
)
.

Consider an instance
(S,\mathcal{F},F^{*},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
of
IO
. Then

\displaystyle\min\big\{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\bigm|w^{\prime}\text{ is a feasible weight function}\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\biggl\{0,\max\Bigl\{\frac{w(F)-w(F^{*})}{|F\triangle F^{*}|}\Bigm|F\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{F^{*}\}\Bigr\}\biggr\}.

3.2
Algorithm and min-max theorem

The goal of this section is to design an algorithm for
IM
and to prove a refined version of Proposition
3.3
for
IM
. Our algorithm requires a single maximum-weight basis computation, improving upon the
O(|S|)
calls required by the algorithm in
[
7
]
, and being simpler and faster than those of
[
9
,
2
,
31
,
30
,
21
,
32
]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
B_{\max}
be a maximum
w
-weight basis of
M
, and let
B^{*}
be a fixed basis. If
w(B_{\max})>w(B^{*})
, then there exist elements
f\in B_{\max}
and
e\in B^{*}
with
w(f)>w(e)
such that

(a)

\delta^{*}=(w(f)-w(e))/2
is the optimum value of the IM instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),B^{*},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, and

(b)

B^{\prime}=B^{*}+f-e
is a basis attaining the maximum in Proposition
3.2
.

Moreover, if both
B_{\max}
and
B^{*}
are given, then the value of
\delta^{*}
can be found using
O(r\cdot\log r)
elementary operations and independence oracle calls, where
r
denotes the rank of
M
.

Proof.

Label
B^{*}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{r}\}
so that
w(e_{1})\geq\dots\geq w(e_{r})
. For each
f\in B_{\max}\setminus B^{*}
, we define
\operatorname{index}(f)=\min\{i\mid f\in\operatorname{cl}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i}\}\}
and
\gamma_{f}=\max\{w(f)-w(e_{\operatorname{index}(f)}),0\}
. Finally, let
\delta^{*}=\max\{\gamma_{f}/2\mid f\in B_{\max}\setminus B^{*}\}
.

We claim that
\delta^{*}
is the optimum value of the
IM
instance considered. To prove this, define a modified weight function
w^{*}
as in Proposition
3.2
, namely

w^{*}(s)=\begin{cases}w(s)+\delta^{*}&\text{if $s\in B^{*}$},\\
w(s)-\delta^{*}&\text{if $s\notin B^{*}$}.\end{cases}

We define a total order
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
on the ground set
S
, sorted in decreasing order of
w^{*}
-value. To break ties, elements in
B^{*}
come before those in
B_{\max}\setminus B^{*}
, which in turn come before elements in
S\setminus(B^{*}\cup B_{\max})
. That is, if
w^{*}(s_{i})=w^{*}(s_{j})
, then
s_{i}
precedes
s_{j}
whenever
s_{i}\in B^{*}
and
s_{j}\notin B^{*}
, or when
s_{i}\in B_{\max}
and
s_{j}\notin B^{*}\cup B_{\max}
. Ties not resolved by these rules are broken using any fixed total order on
S
.

We claim that the greedy algorithm applied to
S
under this order returns
B_{G}=B^{*}
. To see this, note that since
B_{\max}
is a maximum
w
-weight basis, any element
g\in S\setminus(B_{\max}\cup B^{*})
satisfies

g\in\operatorname{cl}\bigl(\{f\in B_{\max}\mid w(f)\geq w(g)\}\bigr)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\bigl(\{s_{j}\in S\mid j<i\}\bigr),

where
s_{i}=g
is its position in the ordering. Hence,
g\notin B_{G}
. Furthermore, for every
f\in B_{\max}\setminus B^{*}
,

f\in\operatorname{cl}\bigl(\{e_{1},\dots,e_{\operatorname{index}(f)}\}\bigr)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\bigl(\{e\in B^{*}\mid w(e)\geq w(f)-\gamma_{f}\}\bigr)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\bigl(\{s_{j}\in S\mid j<i\}\bigr),

where
s_{i}=f
is its position in the ordering. Hence,
f\notin B_{G}
. Therefore, no element outside
B^{*}
is selected by the greedy algorithm, which implies that
B_{G}\subseteq B^{*}
. Since
B_{G}
and
B^{*}
are both bases, it follows that
B_{G}=B^{*}
.

This shows that
w^{*}
is feasible for
IM
on the instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),B^{*},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, and hence the optimal value is at most
\delta^{*}
. To show that it cannot be strictly smaller, suppose that we set
\delta^{*}<\max\{\gamma_{f}/2\mid f\in B_{\max}\setminus B^{*}\}
. This implies that there exists
f\in B_{\max}\setminus B^{*}
with
2\cdot\delta^{*}<\gamma_{f}
. Let
e=e_{\operatorname{index}(f)}\in B^{*}
. By the basis exchange property,
B^{*}+f-e
is also a basis and

\displaystyle w^{*}(B^{*}+f-e)

\displaystyle=w^{*}(B^{*})+w^{*}(f)-w^{*}(e)

\displaystyle=w^{*}(B^{*})+(w(f)-w(e))-2\cdot\delta^{*}

\displaystyle=w^{*}(B^{*})+\gamma_{f}-2\cdot\delta^{*}

\displaystyle>w^{*}(B^{*}),

showing that
w^{*}
is not feasible for smaller values of
\delta^{*}
.

The above proof is algorithmic. We start by sorting
B^{*}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{r}\}
according to
w
in a decreasing order. For each
f\in B_{\max}\setminus B^{*}
, we can compute
\operatorname{index}(f)
in
O(\log r)
oracle calls by testing independence over sets of the form
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{s}\}
using binary search over
s
. In total, this gives
O(r\cdot\log r)
oracle calls and elementary operations.
∎

As a corollary, we get that for
IM
, the maximum in Proposition
3.3
is attained on a basis that is close to the fixed basis
B^{*}
in terms of symmetric difference, thus giving a refinement of the min-max result in
[
7
]
in the matroidal setting. We also read out a similar statement that we will use later on.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
M=(S,\mathcal{B})
be a matroid,
B^{*}\in\mathcal{B}
be a fixed basis, and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
be a weight function.

(a)

If
B^{*}
is not of maximum
w
-weight, then there exists
B^{\prime}\in\arg\max\big\{\frac{w(B)-w(B^{*})}{|B\triangle B^{*}|}\big|B\in\mathcal{B}\big\}
such that
|B^{\prime}\triangle B^{*}|=2
.

(b)

If
B^{*}
is not of minimum
w
-weight, then there exists
B^{\prime}\in\arg\min\big\{\frac{w(B^{*})-w(B)}{|B^{*}\triangle B|}\big|B\in\mathcal{B}\big\}
such that
|B^{*}\triangle B^{\prime}|=2
.

Proof.

Part
(a)
is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.4
, and
(b)
follows from
(a)
by multiplying
w
by
-1
.
∎

4
Inverse Matroid Exists

From now on, we introduce several extensions of
IM
in which, instead of a fixed basis
B^{*}
, a subset
S_{0}
is given, and various constraints are imposed on the set of optimal solutions depending on
S_{0}
. Our first goal is to find a weight function
w^{*}
close to
w
that ensures
S_{0}
contains a maximum
w^{*}
-weight basis.

Inverse Matroid Exists (IM-Exists)

Input:
A matroid
M=(S,\mathcal{I})
, a subset
S_{0}\subseteq S
that contains a basis, a weight function
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, and an objective function
\|\cdot\|
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

Goal:
Find a weight function
w^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
such that

(a)

there exists a basis contained in
S_{0}
that has maximum
w^{*}
-weight, and

(b)

\|w-w^{*}\|
is minimized.

Similarly as in
IM
, we say that a weight function is
feasible
if condition
(a)
holds. Note that
IM-Exists
is always feasible: set
w^{*}(s)=0
for all
s\in S
.

Remark 4.1
.

Given a weight function
w^{\prime}
, we can verify in polynomial time whether
w^{\prime}
is feasible for
IM-Exists
. To do so, it is enough to find a maximum
w^{\prime}
-weight basis
B_{0}
of
M|S_{0}
and a maximum
w^{\prime}
-weight basis
A
of
M
. Then,
w^{\prime}
is feasible if and only if
w^{\prime}(B_{0})=w^{\prime}(A)
.

4.1
Preparations

First, we give a characterization of feasible weight functions.

Lemma 4.2
.

A weight function
w^{\prime}
is feasible for an
IM-Exists
instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
if and only if
e\in\operatorname{cl}(\{f\in S_{0}\mid w^{\prime}(e)\leq w^{\prime}(f)\})
for all
e\in S\setminus S_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
w^{\prime}
is feasible and that
B_{0}\subseteq S_{0}
is a basis of maximum
w^{\prime}
-weight. Then, by running the greedy algorithm with weight function
w^{\prime}
, while breaking ties in favour of elements in
S_{0}
, no element
e\in S\setminus S_{0}
is selected, since the algorithm outputs
B_{0}
. This means that each
e\in S\setminus S_{0}
is spanned by the set
\{f\in B_{0}\mid w^{\prime}(e)\leq w^{\prime}(f)\}
. Therefore,
e\in\operatorname{cl}\left(\{f\in S_{0}\mid w^{\prime}(e)\leq w^{\prime}(f)\}\right)
, as claimed.

To see the other direction, assume that
e\in\operatorname{cl}(\{f\in S_{0}\mid w^{\prime}(e)\leq w^{\prime}(f)\})
holds for all
e\in S\setminus S_{0}
. Then the greedy algorithm for weight function
w^{\prime}
, where ties are broken by giving priority to elements in
S_{0}
and arbitrarily otherwise, returns a basis of maximum
w^{\prime}
-weight contained in
S_{0}
. Indeed, for every
e\in S\setminus S_{0}
, the set of elements considered before
e
– that is, those with weight greater than or equal to that of
e
– already generate
e
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Similarly to
IM
,
IM-Exists
admits an optimal weight function
w^{*}
that has a special structure.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\delta^{*}
be the optimum value of an
IM-Exists
instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
. Then

w^{*}(s)=\begin{cases}w(s)+\delta^{*}&\text{if $s\in S_{0}$},\\
w(s)-\delta^{*}&\text{if $s\in S\setminus S_{0}$}\end{cases}

is an optimal weight function.

Proof.

As
\|w-w^{*}\|_{\infty}=\delta^{*}
, it suffices to show that
w^{*}
is feasible. Let
w^{\text{opt}}
be an optimal weight function. By Lemma
4.2
, we know that
e\in\operatorname{cl}(\{f\in S_{0}\mid w^{\text{opt}}(e)\leq w^{\text{opt}}(f)\})\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(\{f\in S_{0}\mid w^{*}(e)\leq w^{*}(f)\})
for all
e\in S\setminus S_{0}
, since
w^{*}
increases the weight of each element in
S_{0}
and decreases the weight of each element in
S\setminus S_{0}
at least as much as
w^{\text{opt}}
. Therefore, by Lemma
4.2
,
w^{*}
is also feasible.
∎

Let
w^{*}
be an optimal solution for an
IM-Exists
instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and
B_{0}\subseteq S_{0}
be a maximum
w^{*}
-weight basis of
M
. Then, by Proposition
3.3
and Corollary
3.5
(a)
, either
B_{0}
has maximum
w
-weight in
M
, or
\|w-w^{*}\|=\arg\max\big\{\frac{w(B)-w(B_{0})}{2}\big|B\in\mathcal{B},|B\triangle B_{0}|=2\big\}
. However, at this point it is not clear whether
B
attaining the maximum is a subset of
S_{0}
or not. Next we show that the element in
B\setminus B_{0}
can be assumed to be in
S\setminus S_{0}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
w^{*}
be an optimal solution for an
IM-Exists
instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and let
\delta^{*}=\|w-w^{*}\|_{\infty}
. Then either
\delta^{*}=0
, or there exists a triple
(B_{0},e,f)
such that

(a)

B_{0}\subseteq S_{0}
is a basis,

(b)

f\in S\setminus S_{0}
,

(c)

e\in S_{0}
,
e\in C(B_{0},f)
, and

(d)

w(f)-w(e)=2\delta^{*}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\delta^{*}>0
. We call a triple
(B_{0},e,f)
is
valid
if conditions
(a)
–
(c)
hold. Note that a valid triple exists:
S_{0}
contains a basis
B_{0}
by assumption and
\delta^{*}>0
implies
S\setminus S_{0}\neq\emptyset
, therefore for any
f\in S\setminus S_{0}
and
e\in C(B_{0},f)
the triple
(B_{0},e,f)
is valid.

Suppose indirectly that
w(f)-w(e)<2\delta^{*}
for all valid triples
(B_{0},e,f)
. Let
\delta^{\prime}=\max\{\frac{w(f)-w(e)}{2}\mid(B_{0},e,f)\text{ is a valid triple}\}
, and let
(B_{0},e,f)
be a maximizer of
\delta^{\prime}
.
Consider the weight function

w^{\prime}(s)=\begin{cases}w+\delta^{\prime}&\text{if $s\in B_{0}$},\\
w-\delta^{\prime}&\text{if $s\in S\setminus B_{0}$}.\end{cases}

We claim that
w^{\prime}(B)\leq w^{\prime}(B_{0})
for all basis
B\not\subseteq S_{0}
. To see this, let us denote
B_{0}\setminus B=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\}
and
B\setminus B_{0}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\}
, where
B_{0}-e_{i}+f_{i}
is a basis of
M
for all
i\in[k]
– such orderings exist by Proposition
2.1
. By definition of
\delta^{\prime}
, we have
w(f_{i})-w(e_{i})\leq w(f)-w(e)=2\delta^{\prime}
for all
i\in[k]
. Thus,

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(B)-w^{\prime}(B_{0})

\displaystyle=w(B)+\delta^{\prime}\cdot|B\cap B_{0}|-\delta^{\prime}\cdot|B\setminus B_{0}|-w(B_{0})-\delta^{\prime}\cdot|B_{0}|

\displaystyle=w(B)-w(B_{0})-\delta^{\prime}\cdot|B\triangle B_{0}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big(w(f_{i})-w(e_{i})-2\cdot\delta^{\prime}\big)

\displaystyle\leq 0

as stated. Thus
w^{\prime}
is a feasible weight function with
\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=\delta^{\prime}<\delta^{*}=\|w-w^{*}\|_{\infty}
, contradicting the optimality of
w^{*}
.
∎

.

w^{*}

.

If

5
Inverse Matroid All
and
Only

In this section we consider two extensions of
IM
with constraints on all bases contained in
S_{0}
. In the first one, the goal is to find a weight function that makes all the bases contained in
S_{0}
to be of maximum weight.

Inverse Matroid All (IM-All)

Input:
A matroid
M=(S,\mathcal{I})
, a subset
S_{0}\subseteq S
, a weight function
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, and an objective function
\|\cdot\|
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

Goal:
Find a weight function
w^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
such that

(a)

all bases contained in
S_{0}
have maximum
w^{*}
-weight, and

(b)

\|p\|
is minimized.

We say that a weight function is
feasible
if condition
(a)
holds. Note that
IM-All
is always feasible, even if
S_{0}
does not contain a basis of
M
: set
w^{*}(s)=0
for all
s\in S
.

Remark 5.1
.

Given a weight function
w^{\prime}
, we can verify in polynomial time whether
w^{\prime}
is feasible for
IM-All
. To do so, it is enough to find a minimum
w^{\prime}
-weight basis
B^{1}_{0}
of
M|S_{0}
, a maximum
w^{\prime}
-weight basis
B^{2}_{0}
of
M|S_{0}
, and a maximum
w^{\prime}
-weight basis
A
of
M
. Then,
w^{\prime}
is feasible if and only if
w^{\prime}(B^{2}_{0})=2^{\prime}(B^{1}_{0})\geq w^{\prime}(A)
.

In the second variant,
S_{0}
is required to contain exactly the bases of maximum weight. Ahmadian, Bhaskar, Sanità, and Swamy
[
1
]
proposed an LP-based approach for a related problem, where the goal is to find a weight function rather than modify one. Our problem differs from previous ones in that an optimal solution does not necessarily exist. Indeed, for a given cost function, it may happen that every basis contained in
S_{0}
has maximum weight, yet there also exists a maximum-weight basis not contained in
S_{0}
. In such cases, by increasing the weights of elements in
S_{0}
by an arbitrarily small value
\varepsilon
and decreasing the weights of elements outside
S_{0}
accordingly, one can obtain a feasible solution. To avoid this, we consider integer-valued weight functions.

Inverse Matroid Only (IM-Only)

Input:
A matroid
M=(S,\mathcal{I})
, a subset
S_{0}\subseteq S
that contains at least one basis, a weight function
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{S}
, and an objective function
\|\cdot\|
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

Goal:
Find a weight function
w^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}^{S}
such that

(a)

the bases contained in
S_{0}
are exactly the ones of maximum
w^{*}
-weight, and

(b)

\|w-w^{*}\|
is minimized.

We say that a weight function is
feasible
if condition
(a)
holds. Note that
IM-Only
is always feasible: set
w^{*}(s)=1
for all
s\in S_{0}
and
w^{*}(s)=0
for all
s\in S\setminus S_{0}
.

Remark 5.2
.

Given a weight function
w^{\prime}
, we can verify in polynomial time whether
w^{\prime}
is feasible for
IM-Only
. First find a minimum
w^{\prime}
-weight basis
B_{0}^{1}
of
M|S_{0}
and a maximum
w^{\prime}
-weight basis
B_{0}^{2}
of
M|S_{0}
, and check if
w^{\prime}(B_{0}^{2})=w^{\prime}(B_{0}^{1})
. If no, then
w^{\prime}
is not feasible. Otherwise, run the greedy algorithm while breaking ties in favour of elements in
S\setminus S_{0}
. If the resulting basis is not contained in
S_{0}
, then
w^{\prime}
is not feasible, otherwise it is feasible.

5.1
Preparations

One of the key tools in solving
IM-Exists
was Lemma
4.3
, which enabled the search for an optimal weight function in a special form. Unfortunately,
IM-All
and
IM-Only
do not necessarily admit an optimal solution with such a simple structure, which results in more involved algorithms. Our approach relies on the folklore result that if all bases of a matroid have the same weight, then the weight function must be constant on its connected components. For completeness, we include a proof to make the paper self-contained.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
M=(S,\mathcal{I})
be a matroid and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
be a weight function. Then, every basis has the same
w
-weight if and only if
w
is constant on the connected components of
M
.

Proof.

Let
S=S_{1}\cup\dots\cup S_{k}
be the partition of the ground set into the connected components of the circuit hypergraph of
M
, and let
M_{i}=M|S_{i}
for
i\in[k]
. Since
M=M_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus M_{k}
, we get that
B\subseteq S
is a basis of
M
if and only if
B_{i}=B\cap S_{i}
is a basis of
M_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
.

Assume first that
w
is a constant on every
S_{i}
. Then, every basis of
M_{i}
has the same weight, and so the same holds for their direct sum, proving the ‘if’ direction.

Now suppose that there exists
i\in[k]
and
e,f\in S_{i}
such that
w(e)\neq w(f)
; we may assume without loss of generality that
i=1
. Since
e
and
f
are in the same component of
M
, there exists a circuit
C
containing both of them. Let
B
be a basis of
M
containing
C-f
. Then
C(B,f)=C
, and so
B^{\prime}=B-e+f
is a basis with
w(B^{\prime})\neq w(B)
, proving the ‘only if’ direction.
∎

Another simple but useful observation is the following.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
w_{\textsc{Feas}}
be a feasible solution for the
IM-All
or
IM-Only
instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\allowbreak\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, and let
\delta=\|w-w_{\textsc{Feas}}\|_{\infty}
. Then there exists a feasible solution
w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}
satisfying the following:

(a)

\|w-w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}\|_{\infty}=\delta
,

(b)

for every component
M_{i}=(S_{i},\mathcal{I}_{i})
of
M|S_{0}
, there exists
e\in S_{i}
with
w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(e)-w(e)=\delta
, and

(c)

w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(e)-w(e)=-\delta
for all
e\in S\setminus S_{0}
.

Proof.

If
w_{\textsc{Feas}}(e)-w(e)<\delta
for all
e\in S_{i}
, then we can further increase the weights on
S_{i}

For the second part, observe that it is worth decreasing the weights as much as possible on elements outside

.
∎

5.2

Our algorithm for

and

Phase 1: Homogenization.

for all

and
w^{*}(s)=w_{H}(s)-\Delta
for all
s\in S\setminus S_{0}
.

5.3
Algorithm for
IM-Only

We show that
IM-Only
can be solved by first computing an optimal weight function for the corresponding
IM-All
instance, and then slightly modifying it, if needed, so that the bases in
S_{0}
are exactly the set of maximizers.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{S}
be an optimal solution to the
IM-All
instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
under integrality constraints, and suppose it satisfies conditions
(a)
–
(c)
of Lemma
5.4
. Then either
w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}
or
w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}+\chi_{S_{0}}-\chi_{S\setminus S_{0}}
is an optimal solution to the
IM-Only
problem for the same instance.

Proof.

Let
w^{*}_{\textsc{Only}}
be an optimal weight function for the
IM-Only
instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and let
\delta=\|w-w^{*}_{\textsc{Only}}\|_{\infty}
. Since
w^{*}_{\textsc{Only}}
is also a feasible solution for the
IM-All
problem on the same instance, we may assume that it satisfies conditions
(a)
–
(c)
of Lemma
5.4
.

First, we show that if
\|w-w^{*}_{\textsc{Only}}\|_{\infty}=\|w-w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}\|_{\infty}
, then
w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}
is feasible for
IM-Only
, and hence it is optimal. This follows immediately from conditions
(b)
and
(c)
, along with the fact that both
w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}
and
w^{*}_{\textsc{Only}}
are constant on the connected components of
M|S_{0}
. Together with the equality
\|w-w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}\|_{\infty}=\|w-w^{*}_{\textsc{Only}}\|_{\infty}
, this implies that the two weight functions in fact coincide.

Otherwise
\|w-w^{*}_{\textsc{Only}}\|_{\infty}\geq\|w-w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}\|_{\infty}\|+1
by the integrality constraints. Consider the weight function

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(s)=\begin{cases}w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}(s)+1&\text{if }s\in S_{0},\\
w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}(s)-1&\text{if }s\in S\setminus S_{0}.\end{cases}

Since every basis contained in
S_{0}
has maximum
w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}
-weight, the same holds under
w^{\prime}
. Moreover, for any pair of bases
B\not\subseteq S_{0}
and
B_{0}\subseteq S_{0}
, we have
w^{\prime}(B_{0})=w_{\textsc{All}}(B_{0})+|B_{0}|>w_{\textsc{All}}(B)+|B\cap S_{0}|=w^{\prime}(B)
. Thus,
w^{\prime}
is a feasible solution for
IM-Only
with
\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=\|w-w^{*}_{\textsc{All}}\|_{\infty}+1
, and therefore it is also optimal.
∎

6
Inverse Matroid Not Exists

We turn to the negated versions of the problems studied in Sections
4
and
5
, starting with
Inverse Matroid Exists
. Recall that in the negated variant,
Inverse Matroid Not Exists
, the goal is to modify the weight function as little as possible so that
S_{0}
contains no maximum weight basis of
M
with respect to the modified weights. A similar problem was considered in
[

,
32
]
where
S_{0}
is an independent set, the weight function can be only increased or only decreased, and the objective is to minimize
\|w-w^{*}\|_{\infty}
. In contrast, we consider arbitrary subsets
S_{0}
of

, and permit both increases and decreases to the weights. For similar reasons as in the case of

, we consider integer-valued weight functions.

Inverse Matroid Not Exists (IM-Not-Exists)

Input:
A matroid

, a subset

\|\cdot\|
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

Goal:
Find a weight function
w^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}^{S}
such that

(a)

none of the bases contained in
S_{0}
is of maximum
w^{*}
-weight, and

(b)

\|w-w^{*}\|
is minimized.

w
is
feasible
if condition

such that

for all

and
w^{\prime}(s)=1
for all
s\in S\setminus S_{0}
.

Remark 6.1
.

Given a weight

, we can verify in polynomial time whether

. To do so, run the greedy algorithm while breaking ties in favour of elements in
S_{0}
. Then

is feasible if and only if the resulting basis is not contained in

.

6.1
Base cases

The problem has two easy cases. Clearly, if
S_{0}
does not contain a maximum
w
-weight basis, then we are done. The second is stated below.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
be an instance of
IM-Not-Exists
and assume that
M
has a maximum
w
-weight basis contained in
S_{0}
, and also one not contained in
S_{0}
. Then
w^{*}=w-\chi_{S_{0}}+\chi_{S\setminus S_{0}}
is an optimal solution for the problem.

Proof.

By the assumption of the theorem and the integrality constraints, we know that the optimum value of any feasible solution is at least
1
. Since
\|w-w^{*}\|_{\infty}=1
, it suffices to show that
w^{*}
is feasible. To see this, let
B_{0}\subseteq S_{0}
be an arbitrary basis of
M
, and let

be a maximum
w
-weight basis of

not contained in
S_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle w^{*}(B_{0})

\displaystyle=w(B_{0})-|B_{0}|

\displaystyle\leq w(B_{\max})-|B_{\max}|

\displaystyle<w(B_{\max})-|B_{\max}\cap S_{0}|+|B_{\max}\setminus S_{0}|

\displaystyle=w^{*}(B_{\max}),

concluding the proof.
∎

Preparations

Similarly to the case of

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
w_{\textsc{Feas}}
be a feasible solution for the
IM-Not-All
or
IM-Not-Only
instance
(M=(S,\mathcal{I}),S_{0},w,\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, and let
\delta=\|w-w_{\textsc{Feas}}\|_{\infty}
. Then there exists a feasible solution
w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}
satisfying the following:

(a)

\|w-w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}\|_{\infty}=\delta
, and

(b)

w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}=w+\delta\cdot(\chi_{S\setminus B_{0}}-\chi_{B_{0}})
for some
B_{0}\subseteq S_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
B_{0}\subseteq S_{0}
be a basis that is not of maximum
w_{\textsc{Feas}}
-weight and let
B_{\max}
be a basis of maximum
w_{\textsc{Feas}}
-weight – such bases exist since
w_{\textsc{Feas}}
is feasible. For
w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}=w+\delta\cdot(\chi_{S\setminus B_{0}}-\chi_{B_{0}})
, we get

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{0})

\displaystyle=w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{0}\setminus B_{\max})+w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{0}\cap B_{\max})

\displaystyle\leq w_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{0}\setminus B_{\max})+w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{0}\cap B_{\max})

\displaystyle<w_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{\max}\setminus B_{0})+w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{0}\cap B_{\max})

\displaystyle\leq w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{\max}\setminus B_{0})+w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{0}\cap B_{\max})

\displaystyle=w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{\max}),

where the strict inequality holds by
w_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{0})<w_{\textsc{Feas}}(B_{\max})
. That is,
w^{\prime}_{\textsc{Feas}}
is a feasible solution that satisfies the conditions of the lemma.
∎

8
Discussion

In this paper, we presented a thorough analysis of various extensions of the inverse matroid problem under subset constraints. Given a fixed set
S_{0}
, we provided algorithms for problems in which the goal is to modify the weight function so that the matroid has at least one, all, or exactly the bases contained in
S_{0}
as maximum-weight bases. We also examined the negated versions of these problems, resulting in efficient algorithms for six naturally arising variants.

It is worth noting that our results extend to the more general setting where lower and upper bounds are given on the coordinates of the modified weight function. However, the corresponding algorithms become more involved due to numerous case distinctions, which would detract from the main structural insights that are more easily presented in the unconstrained setting. Moreover, some of the problems remain easy to solve under the weighted
\ell_{\infty}
-norm
\|p\|_{\omega}=\max\{|p(s)|\cdot\omega(s)\mid s\in S\}
, where
\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{S}
represents the “cost” of modifying each coordinate of
w
.

We conclude the paper by mentioning some open questions:

1.

Throughout the paper, we used the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm to measure the distance between the original and modified weight functions. It would be interesting to investigate the complexity landscape of the considered problems under alternative “distance measures”.

2.

We introduced six variants of the
Inverse Matroid
problem, all of which can also be formulated for the intersection of two matroids. Designing efficient algorithms for this setting would be particularly interesting due to its applications to bipartite matchings and arborescences.

3.

Subset-constrained variants of
Inverse Optimization
can be considered in general, although certain settings may lead to NP-hard problems. An intriguing direction is to identify structural assumptions on
\mathcal{F}
or the underlying optimization problem under which the proposed variants remain tractable.
