HHV-6–induced hydrocephalus with ventricular septa formation in an immunocompetent adult: illustrative case
Authors: Umika Paul, Constance J. Mietus, Brittany Owusu-Adjei, Omar Sorour, Paramesh V. Karandikar, Mark D. Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDHuman herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) is a neurotropic virus known to induce encephalitis in immunocompromised patients as well as a subset of pediatric patients. However, ventriculitis secondary to HHV-6 is a rare occurrence among immunocompetent adults. Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) is a neurotropic virus that has been linked to several disorders including infectious mononucleosis-like syndrome, roseola, hemophagocytic syndrome, viral myocarditis, and others.1,2 In the CNS, HHV-6 infections have been associated with seizures, encephalitis (including necrotizing encephalitis),3 mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, and multiple sclerosis, especially in immunocompromised patients.4–6 HHV-6 encephalitis typically presents in pediatric or immunocompromised patients with altered mental status or seizures, and less often with anterograde amnesia or hyponatremia.7 Encephalitis-like symptoms have also been observed in infants younger than 2 years of age.7,8 HHV-6 encephalitis is far more rare in immunocompetent adults9–14 and presents with a range of symptoms including high fever, altered consciousness, and acute psychosis and seizures.12–14

Previous reports have described a connection between HHV-6 and hydrocephalus, principally in infants and children. Neonatal HHV-6 meningitis has been observed in association with congenital hydrocephalus.15 Acquired hydrocephalus has also been observed in pediatric patients with HHV-6 infection.16–18 Progressive neurological symptoms and hydrocephalus were observed in HHV-6A–related diffuse leptomeningeal oligodendrogliomatosis in a 2-year-old child.16 In another child receiving craniotomy for removal of a fourth ventricular pilocytic astrocytoma and decompression of hydrocephalus, the postoperative course was complicated by persistent fevers and CSF pleocytosis. Detection of HHV-6 prompted initiation of ganciclovir, which resulted in resolution of CSF leukocytosis, fevers, and elevated intracranial pressure (ICP), but the hydrocephalus persisted. Pathological analysis of the tumor tissue demonstrated no HHV-6 genome positivity, suggesting postoperative HHV-6 innoculation.17 Notably, there is only 1 reported case of an adolescent with hydrocephalus and CSF findings of both HHV-6 and coagulase-negative staphylococcus.18 A related herpesvirus, herpes simplex virus (HSV), has been implicated in a possible atypical presentation of acute hydrocephalus in adolescents.19 To the best of our knowledge, HHV-6–induced hydrocephalus has not previously been reported in immunocompetent adults.

Here we present a case of acquired hydrocephalus with intraventricular membranous septa formation arising in an immunocompetent adult with isolated HHV-6 positivity detected by CSF analysis. Intraventricular septations can result in obstructive hydrocephalus and present distinct challenges in CSF diversion. We propose a mechanism by which the neuroinflammatory properties of viruses, including HHV-6, can promote hydrocephalus.

Illustrative Case

A 34-year-old male with a history of intravenous drug use, alcohol use disorder, tricuspid regurgitation endocarditis, hepatitis C, and prior COVID-19 infection presented with 1 month of progressive headaches, generalized weakness, malaise, urinary incontinence, and acute altered mental status. CT imaging of the head revealed severe communicating hydrocephalus (Fig. 1). A lumbar puncture (LP) demonstrated decreased glucose (32 mg/dl), elevated protein (217 mg/dl), and lymphocytosis (141 cells/mm3) in the CSF. A Gram stain and CSF culture were negative. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing of the CSF was positive for HHV-6 but negative for HSV 1 and 2, cryptococcal antigen, Eastern equine encephalitis virus IgM/IgG, lyme DNA, West Nile IgG, Treponema pallidum, COVID, Haemophilus influenzae, Listeria monocytogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, cytomegalovirus, human parechoviruses, varicella zoster, and bacterial or fungal growth. The patient was started on vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and acyclovir for broad-spectrum treatment of suspected meningitis. Given the positive HHV-6 in the CSF, there was concern that the patient was immunocompromised. Serological screening was performed to assess for sources of immunocompromise, but all serum studies were negative, including HIV1, HIV2, and blood serology. Flow cytometry revealed polytypic B cells and immunophenotypically normal T cells utilizing an 18-marker panel. The patient had taken no immunosuppressive agents. Three days after initial presentation, the patient became less responsive and was transferred to the intensive care unit. Antiepileptic agents were administered and a large-volume LP was performed. Opening pressure was found to be significantly elevated to 35 cm H2O. A new S1 dermatomal vesicular rash was noted at the time of the LP. The patient’s neurological status rapidly improved after the LP. Electroencephalography was performed and revealed mild cortical irritability and asymmetrical bilateral temporal dysfunction, suggestive of encephalopathy. The patient was switched from acyclovir to ganciclovir and foscarnet for better HHV-6 coverage (Fig. 2A). Given the robust neurological improvement after LP, serial LPs and CSF PCR analysis were performed to trend HHV-6 positivity and monitor the effects of treatment. CSF studies continued to show low glucose and significantly elevated protein levels ranging from 50 to 220. As HHV-6 copy number declined, CSF glucose rose and CSF protein and both serum and CSF white blood cell (WBC) counts declined (Fig. 2B–F), which supports treatment response to ganciclovir and foscarnet. Later during the patient’s admission, a similar relationship occurred when HHV-6 levels rose (297 copies/ml), CSF glucose declined, and CSF protein, serum, and CSF WBC counts increased. Opening pressures remained normal on repeat LPs, but an interval head CT demonstrated worsening ventriculomegaly and serial neurological examinations became more variable. A right frontal external ventricular drain (EVD) was placed for continuous CSF drainage. ICP was continuously low (< 10 cm H2O). Despite aggressive CSF drainage, the patient’s neurological examination continued to fluctuate between mild confusion and lethargy. Proteinaceous obstruction of EVD catheters required multiple replacements and prevented placement of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt due to a high likelihood of obstruction. After 2 months in the hospital, a head CT revealed a collapsed right lateral ventricle and increased volume of the left lateral ventricle with 4 mm of rightward midline shift. Given the apparent poor communication between the left and right lateral ventricles, a left-sided EVD was placed. MRI was performed and demonstrated multiple intraventricular septations; trapping of the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles (Fig. 3A and B); and scattered ependymal enhancement with diffusion restriction in the lateral and third ventricles consistent with persistent ventriculitis (Fig. 3C and D). Over the next month, enlargement of the left lateral ventricle persisted and the right lateral ventricle remained collapsed despite bilaterally functioning EVDs. Subsequent changes to the EVD to further increase the CSF drainage rate demonstrated radiographic improvement of the ventriculomegaly, but there was no clinical improvement in the patient’s poor neurological status. Three weeks later, the patient’s family requested that the patient’s care be transitioned to hospice, and the patient subsequently died.

Discussion

Observations

The neurotropic properties of HHV-6 are particularly apparent in children or immunocompromised individuals, where it can lead to encephalitis at the time of infection or secondary to reactivation.20 However, HHV-6 encephalitis rarely occurs in immunocompetent adult patients. Rare cases of HHV-6 encephalitis with acquired hydrocephalus have been reported and are limited to pediatric and immunocompromised patients. We present a novel case of intraventricular membranous septa formation and hydrocephalus in an immunocompetent adult with HHV-6 encephalitis. Despite attempts at CSF diversion, the patient exhibited persistent pan-ventricular enlargement, likely due to the development of multiple septated loculations throughout the ventricular system.

Extensive CSF and serum analysis excluded alternative infectious etiologies for the patient’s encephalopathy and hydrocephalus. Although HHV-6–induced neuroinflammation is uncommon in immunocompetent adults, negative bacterial cultures and blood serologies suggested that a neuroinflammatory response to HHV-6 was responsible for this patient’s worsening condition. Furthermore, progressive septa formation within the ventricular system resulted in loculated compartments, producing a noncommunicating hydrocephalus. As HHV-6 levels peaked, low opening pressures on serial LPs were observed despite ventricular enlargement. These findings suggest the development of low-pressure hydrocephalus. The development of multiple septations and the subsequent loss of communication between the lateral ventricles required placement of a second EVD. Despite both lateral ventricles demonstrating low ICPs throughout the patient’s course, the third and fourth ventricles remained enlarged. Fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition (FIESTA) MRI sequences showed numerous, dense septations throughout all four ventricles. Septa formation was likely induced by the neuroinflammatory response to the HHV-6 infection. The addition of ganciclovir and foscarnet resulted in a significant decline in HHV-6 copy number as well as a concomitant decline in CSF WBC and CSF protein levels and normalization of CSF glucose. Later during the hospitalization, HHV-6 copy number mildly increased along with increases in CSF WBC and protein levels. Elevated CSF protein, possibly from inflammation and diminished CSF clearance, may have also contributed to the development of the patient’s hydrocephalus.

Approximately 70% of confirmed cases of encephalitis are viral in origin. The most common causes of viral encephalitis within the United States are HSV, West Nile virus, and enteroviruses. Additionally, infection with other viruses (e.g., varicella zoster, cytomegalovirus, coronavirus, measles, equine, dengue, and rabies) can produce encephalitis21 and potentially hydrocephalus. Murine studies of CNS viral tropism suggest that the choroid plexus is a site of early viral infection and is likely involved in the pathogenesis of meningitis.22,23 In humans, the choroid plexus has been identified as a potential reservoir for HIV infection within the CNS and may function as a site of hematogenous dissemination to the brain and spinal cord.24,25

HHV-6 infection of the CNS sets the stage for a robust neuroinflammatory response that is characterized by autoreactivity, molecular mimicry, and cytokine dysregulation.4 HHV-6 establishes infection within neural cells and astrocytes26 and can also infect microglial cells in vitro.27 Furthermore, HHV-6 infects oligodendrocytes in vivo and may contribute to the pathogenesis of multiple sclerosis, a neuroinflammatory disorder.28,29 HHV-6 can cause upregulation of cell adhesion proteins in the brain’s endothelium, thereby increasing permeability of the blood-brain barrier and the activity of autoreactive lymphocytes.4 Inflammatory responses may contribute to virus-induced hydrocephalus. Choroid plexus epithelium has been shown to have an NF-κB–dependent inflammatory response that resulted in an approximately threefold increase in CSF secretion.30 Additionally, cytokine upregulation damages the ependymal lining of the ventricles,31 resulting in reactive gliosis.32 Neuroinflammation has also been shown to induce a fibroblastic reticular network in the meninges,29 and a similar network may be induced within the choroid plexus. Sclerosis of the choroid plexus stroma is a feature of chronic hydrocephalus,4,33 and it is likely that fibrotic scarring of the choroid plexus and/or ventricle contribute to the development of hydrocephalus.34 Glial protrusions, emerging as an outcome of the dynamic neuroinflammatory environment, could establish a primary structural framework for septa formation. The combination of glial protrusions and extracellular matrix formation in the background of neuroinflammation can lead to the accumulation of cellular debris, protein aggregates, fibroblasts, and immune cells.34 One study in neonates with hydrocephalus from bacterial meningitis reported similar processes. Neutrophils in inflammatory ventricular exudate were replaced by fibroblasts, leading to septation formation and intraventricular compartmentalization. This resulted in CSF outflow obstruction at the cerebral aqueduct and foramina of Luschka and Magendie.34 In the case of the patient described here, septa growth led to CSF loculation of the ventricular system. We suspect that the patient’s immunocompetent status led to a persistent and robust neuroinflammatory response that promoted the formation of numerous septations within the ventricles.

Our case demonstrates that HHV-6 can lead to ventriculitis and hydrocephalus in immunocompetent adults. HHV-6 seropositivity is nearly ubiquitous, with 95% of individuals older than 2 years of age showing evidence of exposure to HHV-6A or HHV-6B.35 Traditional signs of encephalitis such as focal or diffuse cerebral changes in intensity, cerebral edema, enhancement, hemorrhage, or necrosis36 were not evident on this patient’s imaging studies, although electroencephalography was consistent with encephalopathy. The predominant clinical and radiological findings of this case were related to ventriculitis and hydrocephalus. Viral etiologies, including HHV-6, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients with radiological findings of hydrocephalus and ventricular septations. Ventricular septa formation can be caused by other neurotropic viruses that induce neuroinflammation, such as other herpesviruses, enteroviruses, arboviruses, rhabdoviruses, paramyxoviruses, and retroviruses.37 Treatment approaches should be tailored to address the viral infection as well as the hydrocephalus, although CSF diversion in the setting of extensive membranous loculation and proteinaceous debris may make CSF drainage challenging.

Lessons

This report highlights a case of HHV-6–induced hydrocephalus in an immunocompetent adult. The formation of extensive intraventricular septations resulting in trapping of the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles was the result of a robust inflammatory ventriculitis. The process of virus-induced hydrocephalus in the context of neuroinflammation, glial and fibroblast activation, choroid plexus hypersecretion, and intraventricular septation formation offers a new opportunity for further exploration of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying postinfectious hydrocephalus. Clinicians should be aware of the risk of ventricular septa formation and obstructive hydrocephalus in immunocompetent patients with HHV-6 ventriculitis and the potential for severe neurological consequences in such patients.
