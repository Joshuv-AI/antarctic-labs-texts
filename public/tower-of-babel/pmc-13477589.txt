A preliminary study on the radiomic assessment of the surrounding trabecular bone microenvironment in taurodontism: Is it more than a dental anomaly?
Authors: Tulin Tasdemir, Sultan Uzun, Melek Tassoker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate whether taurodontism is associated with localized alterations in the surrounding trabecular bone microenvironment using quantitative radiomic analysis of panoramic radiographs in children.MethodsPanoramic radiographs of 24 children (6 taurodontism cases; 18 age-matched controls) were retrospectively evaluated. Due to incomplete root development, severity was qualitatively assessed via Shaw’s criteria. Trabecular bone was analyzed across four topographic regions (interradicular epicenter, mesial apex, distal apex, and gonial angle) using Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI)-compliant 3D Slicer. Diagnostic performance was preliminarily evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsRadiomic alterations demonstrated a distinct spatial gradient, with the most pronounced differences localized to the interradicular region and progressively diminishing toward the mesial, distal, and gonial regions. Four higher-order radiomic features showed significant discriminatory performance in the interradicular regions of interest (ROI) (all p < 0.05). GLSZM_ZoneVariance achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.824; 95% CI: 0.617–0.948), with 100.0% sensitivity and 72.2% specificity, followed by GLSZM_ZoneEntropy (AUC = 0.815).ConclusionThis proof-of-concept study provides preliminary evidence that taurodontism is associated with localized radiomic alterations in the surrounding trabecular bone microenvironment, particularly within the interradicular region. These findings suggest that quantitative radiomic analysis of routinely acquired panoramic radiographs may provide complementary imaging features for investigating developmental dental phenotypes. Further prospective multicenter studies with external validation are required to confirm the biological and clinical significance of these observations.Keywords: Bone, Children, Panoramic radiography, Radiomics, Taurodontism

Introduction

Taurodontism, morphologically characterized by an apically displaced furcation floor, an abnormally enlarged pulp chamber, and the absence of a normal constriction at the cementoenamel junction, has traditionally been classified as an isolated developmental anomaly restricted to the dental hard tissues [1, 2]. Although considered a relatively rare condition with a global prevalence ranging from 0.1% to 3% in non-syndromic populations [3], recent deep learning models and automated screening algorithms have demonstrated an increasing capability to detect this subtle morphological trait in large-scale panoramic datasets [4]. The etiology of this unique condition is widely attributed to the delayed invagination or failure of Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath to fuse at the proper horizontal level during odontogenesis [5]. Historically, clinical and radiographic evaluations have focused almost exclusively on the internal morphometric proportions of the affected teeth to anticipate complex endodontic challenges [2, 6].

Emerging evidence suggests that some developmental dental anomalies may not be confined exclusively to dental hard tissues, as alterations in the surrounding trabecular bone have been reported in conditions such as amelogenesis imperfecta, hypodontia, and molar incisor hypomineralization (MIH) [7–9]. Collectively, these findings provide a rationale for investigating developmental dental anomalies within a broader osteodental framework, considering both dental morphology and the surrounding alveolar bone. However, objectively characterizing subtle trabecular alterations on conventional two-dimensional radiographs remains challenging. Fractal Analysis (FA) has been widely used in pediatric dental imaging to quantify trabecular bone complexity [7–9]. However, while FA is a valuable computational tool that quantifies structural complexity using a single fractal dimension [10, 11], radiomic analysis enables the extraction of multiple complementary features describing spatial heterogeneity. Consequently, radiomics has emerged as a promising quantitative imaging approach for investigating subtle tissue microenvironment changes that may not be visually appreciable on conventional radiographs [10, 12].

Although CBCT provides three-dimensional information and is generally considered more suitable for radiomic analysis [12], its use in pediatric patients is appropriately restricted because of radiation protection principles, including ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) and ALADA (As Low As Diagnostically Acceptable) [13]. Consequently, maximizing the diagnostic information obtained from routinely acquired panoramic radiographs is of particular interest in pediatric dental imaging. Recent studies have demonstrated that radiomic analysis of panoramic images can successfully identify imaging patterns associated with jawbone conditions, including the prediction of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) before clinical onset [14]. Beyond MRONJ, radiomics has also been successfully applied to several dentomaxillofacial conditions, including odontogenic cysts, temporomandibular joint disorders, and pediatric dental trauma [15–18]. However, these applications have largely focused on established pathologic conditions, whereas developmental dental anomalies have received comparatively little attention. Accordingly, investigating whether quantitative radiomic analysis can detect subtle trabecular alterations associated with developmental dental anomalies represents an important unexplored research direction.

Pilot and proof-of-concept studies play an important role in the early evaluation of emerging imaging biomarkers by assessing methodological feasibility and generating hypotheses for subsequent validation in larger cohorts [19–21]. In pediatric dental imaging, where CBCT cannot be routinely justified solely for research purposes, determining whether routinely acquired panoramic radiographs contain meaningful quantitative imaging information is of particular clinical interest. Building upon previous fractal analysis studies of developmental dental anomalies [7–9], the present study extends this concept by applying high-dimensional IBSI-compliant radiomic analysis to characterize the trabecular bone surrounding taurodont teeth. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has investigated the trabecular bone surrounding taurodont teeth using panoramic radiomics, leaving this aspect of taurodontism largely unexplored. Therefore, this proof-of-concept study aimed to investigate whether taurodontism is associated with quantitative radiomic alterations in the surrounding alveolar trabecular bone on routinely acquired panoramic radiographs in children. The findings are intended to provide preliminary imaging evidence and to inform the design of future multicenter validation studies.

Materials and methods

Study design, open-access dataset, and patient selection

This preliminary proof-of-concept case-control study was conducted using digital panoramic radiographs obtained from the publicly available Children’s Dental Panoramic X-ray machine learning dataset hosted on Kaggle. As detailed in the dataset’s foundational paper by Zhu et al. [4], this comprehensive repository aggregates pediatric panoramic radiographs sourced from various internet databases and multicenter clinical settings. Specifically, it includes foundational contributions from the highly curated open-access cohort published by Zhang et al. [22] (China), as well as real-world clinical acquisitions from the Dental Bauhinia Clinic (China). This diverse origin provides a heterogeneous anatomical baseline for computational imaging analysis.

As the imaging data were retrieved from an open-access database lacking complete demographic records (such as chronological age and gender), the control subjects were matched to the study group strictly based on dental age to ensure comparability. Accordingly, the present study was designed as an exploratory proof-of-concept study to evaluate the feasibility of panoramic radiomics rather than to establish causal biological relationships. Nevertheless, to minimize potential confounding within the available dataset, stringent exclusion criteria were applied. Radiographs presenting any of the following conditions were excluded: (1) missing or impacted adjacent permanent teeth, (2) severe structural enamel-dentin anomalies (e.g., amelogenesis imperfecta), (3) presence of large cystic or periapical lesions overlapping the trabecular regions, (4) orthodontic appliances or severe panoramic positioning distortions; and (5) radiographic manifestations suggestive of systemic syndromes or genetic disorders (e.g., ectodermal dysplasia) known to be associated with taurodontism.

Following this rigorous filtration, a total of 6 patients presenting with isolated, bilateral taurodontism in the mandibular first permanent molars were identified. Radiomic analysis was performed exclusively on the mandibular first permanent molars in both the taurodontism and control groups. Because isolated pediatric taurodontism is relatively uncommon in open-access pediatric imaging datasets, and because the primary objective of this pilot study was to evaluate methodological feasibility rather than definitive validation of radiomic features, a 1:3 case-control matching strategy was adopted [23, 24]. For the 6 affected cases, 18 healthy pediatric controls were meticulously selected. To eliminate the confounding effects of biological growth on trabecular bone density, the control group was rigorously matched to the exact dental age of the study group, resulting in a final matched cohort of 24 children. Image pre-processing and standardization

Given the diverse clinical and multicenter origin of the open-access dataset, the panoramic radiographs were inherently acquired using various imaging devices, resulting in distinct exposure parameters, spatial resolutions, and pixel intensities. To mitigate these scanner-dependent variations and improve the reproducibility and comparability of radiomic features, a standardized image pre-processing pipeline was applied prior to feature extraction.

Following the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) guidelines [25], all digital radiographs were maintained at their original 16-bit depth to preserve high-dynamic-range texture information. The images were spatially resampled using B-spline interpolation to achieve a uniform isotropic pixel size. Furthermore, image intensity normalization was performed using a standard z-score transformation (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) to reduce contrast and brightness variations between scanners and improve radiomic feature stability. Finally, gray-level discretization was applied using a fixed bin width of 32 to maintain a constant intensity resolution across all samples, thereby reducing scanner-related variability and improving the consistency and comparability of the extracted radiomic features.

Qualitative morphological assessment

Due to the incomplete root development (open apices) inherent to the 6–8 year dental age, traditional biometric indices for diagnosing taurodontism (such as the Shifman and Chanannel index) which rely on final root length, could not be reliably applied because incomplete root formation mathematically biases the pulp-to-root length ratios [2, 6]. Therefore, to overcome this limitation, the diagnosis and severity of taurodontism were determined qualitatively according to the classic morphological criteria proposed by Shaw [26], which are better suited to this developmental stage than conventional quantitative indices.

To improve diagnostic consistency and reduce subjective interpretation, this assessment was initially performed independently by a calibrated expert panel consisting of one pediatric dentist and two dentomaxillofacial radiologists. The observers were blinded to all radiomic analysis results during diagnostic assessment and regions of interest (ROI) delineation. To establish a definitive diagnosis without relying on isolated assessments, any initial diagnostic discrepancies among the observers were thoroughly discussed and resolved through a joint review session to reach a unanimous consensus. Ultimately, the panel confirmed the presence of true taurodontism in all selected cases, classifying the affected teeth entirely within the mild-to-moderate spectrum (hypo- and mesotaurodont), explicitly noting the absence of extreme hypertaurodont presentations.

ROI segmentation and radiomic feature extraction

Following the image preprocessing steps, ROI segmentation was performed on the standardized images. For each panoramic radiograph, bilateral segmentation was performed, resulting in four ROIs per side and a total of eight ROIs per patient. The segmentation workflow and representative ROI locations are illustrated in Figs. 2 and 3. All ROIs were manually delineated using the Segment Editor module of the open-source 3D Slicer software (version 5.11, http://www.slicer.org).

First ROI: mesial periradicular trabecular region

The first ROI was defined in the trabecular bone located mesial to the mandibular first permanent molar (tooth 36/46). Specifically, the ROI encompassed the trabecular bone between the mesial lamina dura of the first permanent molar and the developing tooth germ of the permanent second premolar (tooth 35/45). The inferior boundary of this ROI was defined by a line connecting the root apex of the first permanent molar and the distal apex of the developing premolar tooth germ. The superior boundary depended on the anatomical relationship between structures in the panoramic image. In cases where superimposition was present, the ROI was limited to the trabecular region located inferior to the overlap between the lamina dura and the premolar tooth germ. When superimposition was absent, the superior boundary was extended up to the level corresponding to the distal root apex of the primary second molar.

Second ROI: distal periradicular trabecular region

The second ROI was defined in the trabecular bone distal to the mandibular first permanent molar, corresponding anatomically to the area between the distal root of the first permanent molar and the developing tooth germ of the permanent second molar (tooth 37/47). The same anatomical principles used for the mesial ROI were applied here, with the trabecular bone area confined between the distal lamina dura of the first permanent molar and the mesial aspect of the developing second molar germ.

Third ROI: interradicular trabecular region

The third ROI corresponded to the interradicular trabecular bone located between the mesial and distal roots of the mandibular first permanent molar. This ROI included only the trabecular bone within the interradicular space, while excluding the surrounding lamina dura and root structures. The superior boundary was defined by the furcation area, ensuring that only trabecular bone structures were included in the analysis.

Fourth ROI: gonial trabecular region

The fourth ROI was selected to determine whether the observed trabecular alterations were localized to the periradicular environment or represented a generalized mandibular bone pattern. For this purpose, a trabecular bone region was delineated within the gonial angle of the mandible.

The trabecular bone area enclosed within these anatomical boundaries was used as the systemic reference region.

All ROIs were manually delineated as free-form regions rather than predefined geometric shapes, allowing the segmentation process to follow the natural trabecular bone contours and avoid the inclusion of adjacent non-trabecular anatomical structures. Prior to the formal segmentation phase, an observer calibration session was conducted. Both observers jointly established consensus regarding the anatomical landmarks and boundaries of the mesial, distal, interradicular, and gonial trabecular ROIs to standardize the manual segmentation protocol before image analysis. ROI segmentation was then performed independently by two dentomaxillofacial radiologists with 9 and 15 years of experience in dental radiology. During the ROI segmentation process, both observers were blinded to the patients’ group allocation (taurodontism or control), clinical information, and the extracted radiomic features. To assess intra- and inter-observer reproducibility, the entire segmentation procedure was independently repeated by both observers using the full dataset after a two-week interval to minimize recall bias. Reproducibility was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Intra-observer agreement demonstrated excellent reliability, with ICC values ranging from 0.89 to 0.95. Inter-observer agreement was also excellent across all ROI locations (mesial ROI: ICC = 0.88, distal ROI: ICC = 0.91, interradicular ROI: ICC = 0.86, gonial ROI: ICC = 0.94).

Following segmentation, radiomic feature extraction was performed using the PyRadiomics extension within the 3D Slicer platform. Given the exploratory proof-of-concept nature of the study, all IBSI-compliant radiomic features were initially extracted to provide a comprehensive quantitative characterization of trabecular texture, after which statistical analyses were performed to identify potentially informative radiomic features. All radiomic feature extraction parameters were implemented in accordance with the IBSI guidelines, ensuring standardized and reproducible radiomic measurements [25]. All extracted radiomic features were exported as tab-separated value (TSV) files. Subsequently, the TSV files generated for each ROI were merged using RStudio software, and the consolidated dataset was converted into Microsoft Excel format for statistical analysis. After organizing the dataset, the radiomic values obtained from the right and left sides for each corresponding ROI were averaged, resulting in four final mean ROI values per patient representing the mesial, distal, interradicular, and gonial regions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for primary modeling and MedCalc (version 24.1; MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium) for advanced Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) comparisons. Intra-observer reproducibility of manual ROI segmentation was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) following repeated segmentation of the same images after a two-week interval. Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed separately for each radiomic feature using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the radiomic features did not follow a normal distribution, comparisons between the taurodontism and control groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test across the four predefined ROIs. In addition to p-values, effect sizes (r) were calculated for all Mann–Whitney U test comparisons using the standardized test statistic (Z/√N). Effect sizes were interpreted according to Cohen’s criteria as small (0.10), medium (0.30), and large (0.50) [27].

ROC curve analysis was performed to provide a preliminary assessment of the discriminatory performance of candidate radiomic features. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden Index, corresponding to the threshold that maximized the sum of sensitivity and specificity. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure implemented in R (version 4.6.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and the adjusted values (q-values) were reported alongside the unadjusted p-values. Given the exploratory nature of this proof-of-concept study, both unadjusted and FDR-adjusted results are reported transparently. The statistical analyses were intended to identify potentially informative radiomic features for future validation rather than to establish clinically applicable diagnostic thresholds.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

The study sample comprised a total of 24 pediatric patients, all strictly matched to a dental age range of 6–8 years. The study group included 6 patients presenting with bilateral taurodontism in the mandibular first permanent molars, yielding a total of 12 affected teeth. Following the qualitative morphological assessment by the expert consensus panel, these cases were equally distributed as 3 hypotaurodont and 3 mesotaurodont patients. The matched control group consisted of 18 healthy individuals, providing a total of 36 unaffected mandibular first molars. Consequently, 48 teeth were successfully segmented and subjected to radiomic feature extraction.

While structural differences persisted towards the mesial and distal periradicular regions, both their statistical significance and effect sizes gradually decreased. Among the evaluated radiomic features, GLSZM_ZoneVariance demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy with an AUC of 0.824 (95% CI:0.617–0.948). At the optimal cut-off value of > 4339.00, this feature achieved a sensitivity of 100.0% and a specificity of 72.2%. While the higher-order radiomic features from the NGTDM class (Coarseness and Contrast) also demonstrated discriminatory ability (Table 3), the GLSZM-based radiomic features reflecting structural heterogeneity and randomness (Zone Variance and Entropy) showed the most consistent performance and emerged as promising candidate radiomic features for future validation. These findings suggest that localized trabecular heterogeneity in the interradicular region may represent a potential quantitative imaging feature associated with taurodontism, particularly in mild-to-moderate anatomical variants.

Discussion

Historically, taurodontism has been defined primarily according to its internal tooth morphology, including apical displacement of the furcation and enlargement of the pulp chamber [28, 29]. The present study provides preliminary radiomic evidence suggesting that taurodontism may also be associated with localized alterations in the surrounding trabecular bone microenvironment rather than representing only an isolated dental morphological variation. The observed radiomic changes demonstrated a distinct spatial pattern, with the most pronounced alterations occurring in the interradicular region and gradually decreasing toward the mesial and distal periradicular bone before no features remained significant after FDR correction in the gonial region. This localized distribution suggests that the identified radiomic alterations are closely associated with the immediate osseous environment of taurodont teeth rather than reflecting generalized mandibular changes. One possible explanation for this localized pattern is the altered biomechanical environment created by taurodont morphology. Apical displacement of the furcation shifts the center of resistance and may modify physiological load distribution, concentrating non-axial stresses toward the interradicular and apical bone [30]. Whether the observed radiomic heterogeneity reflects primary developmental disturbances, secondary biomechanical adaptation in accordance with Wolff’s law, or a combination of both remains unknown [31, 32].

Traditional taurodontism classification (hypo-, meso- and hypertaurodontism) is based on the degree of pulpal floor displacement [26]. However, no universally accepted diagnostic classification exists, and both subjective radiographic assessment and several quantitative indices have been described in the literature. In children, ongoing root development may further complicate radiographic assessment of the final internal tooth morphology, making the diagnosis of taurodontism more challenging [2]. Therefore, diagnosis in the present study was established by expert consensus using Shaw’s qualitative classification. To minimize the risk of misclassification, only cases with unanimous agreement among the observers were included. Although this approach cannot completely eliminate diagnostic uncertainty, it represents a pragmatic and rigorous strategy for evaluating developing permanent molars in pediatric populations. Despite including only mild-to-moderate taurodont cases, GLSZM_ZoneVariance demonstrated promising discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.824) with high sensitivity. These findings suggest that quantitative radiomic analysis may detect trabecular alterations associated with taurodontism even in relatively mild anatomical variants. Nevertheless, the absence of internal and external validation requires that these findings be interpreted cautiously, and further validation is necessary before any diagnostic application can be considered.

Taurodontism is recognized as a phenotypic feature of several systemic developmental disorders, including Osteogenesis Imperfecta, Klinefelter syndrome, Down syndrome, and Tricho-Dento-Osseous syndrome [33]. At the molecular level, normal root development depends on tightly coordinated Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath (HERS) function and conserved signaling pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin and EDA/EDAR, disruptions of which have been implicated in taurodontism [33, 34]. This concept is consistent with the broader principles of craniofacial development, in which tooth morphogenesis and alveolar bone formation are coordinated through reciprocal epithelial–mesenchymal interactions and highly conserved developmental signaling pathways [35]. Consequently, developmental dental phenotypes may not always represent isolated alterations confined to dental hard tissues but may reflect broader biological processes influencing the surrounding osseous microenvironment. This concept is further supported by previous imaging studies reporting trabecular bone alterations in developmental dental conditions such as amelogenesis imperfecta, hypodontia, and molar–incisor hypomineralization [7–9]. Furthermore, altered eruption patterns associated with taurodontism may prolong interactions between the developing tooth, dental follicle, and surrounding alveolar bone during craniofacial growth [36]. Although the present study did not investigate these biological mechanisms directly, the observed radiomic alterations are consistent with the hypothesis that shared developmental pathways may also influence the surrounding trabecular bone microenvironment. Whether common molecular mechanisms underlie these imaging phenotypes remains to be determined.

An important aspect of the present study is that all analyses were performed using routinely acquired panoramic radiographs, which remain the primary imaging modality in pediatric dental practice [37]. Although panoramic radiographs provide only two-dimensional information and have lower spatial resolution than CBCT [38], the present findings suggest that they may still contain quantitative imaging information beyond conventional visual assessment. In pediatric patients, where CBCT should be reserved for situations with a clear clinical indication in accordance with radiation protection principles [13, 39], panoramic radiomics may represent an exploratory quantitative imaging approach for extracting additional information from routinely acquired radiographs without additional radiation exposure. However, it is not intended to replace three-dimensional imaging or directly characterize trabecular microarchitecture.

Furthermore, the utilization of an open-access database stands as a significant methodological advantage. By promoting the ethical reuse of existing pediatric clinical data, it eliminates the need for de novo radiation exposure strictly for research purposes. In addition, it fosters high scientific transparency and reproducibility, allowing independent research groups to access the data and validate the proposed radiomic pipeline. Finally, the rigorous application of IBSI-compliant preprocessing protocols ensures that this reproducible data is fully standardized against device-induced artifacts.

The present study has several important strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first study to quantitatively characterize radiomic alterations in the trabecular bone surrounding taurodont teeth rather than focusing exclusively on tooth morphology. The strict 1:3 age-matched case-control design within a narrow developmental window (6–8 years) minimized the potential influence of skeletal maturation and mixed-dentition variability. Although panoramic images were obtained from an open-access database with inherent variability in acquisition hardware and imaging protocols, this variability was mitigated through rigorous case selection, standardized image pre-processing according to IBSI recommendations, and a predefined ROI segmentation protocol that demonstrated excellent observer agreement (ICC > 0.85). Collectively, these methodological measures enhanced the reproducibility and robustness of the extracted radiomic features.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the retrospective proof-of-concept design and the relatively small sample size, reflecting the rarity of isolated bilateral taurodontism in pediatric populations, limited statistical power and precluded subgroup analyses according to taurodontism severity. Second, neither external nor internal validation procedures were performed because of the limited number of available cases. Consequently, the reported diagnostic performance and radiomic associations should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Although FDR adjustment attenuated the statistical significance of several comparisons, the persistence of large effect sizes together with biologically plausible spatial patterns supports the exploratory identification of potentially informative radiomic features. Third, panoramic radiographs provide two-dimensional representations of trabecular bone and therefore cannot directly validate the underlying bone microarchitecture. Although IBSI-compliant preprocessing was applied to reduce scanner-related variability, residual variability related to differences in acquisition hardware, image processing algorithms, and patient positioning cannot be completely eliminated. Furthermore, no independent reference standard, such as CBCT, histological assessment, or bone density measurements, was available to verify the biological significance of the identified radiomic alterations. Because no clinical outcomes were evaluated, any potential implications for endodontic, orthodontic, periodontal, or surgical management remain speculative and require dedicated investigation. Finally, because the study was based on a publicly available pediatric database, complete demographic information, including chronological age and sex, was unavailable. Consequently, matching was performed using dental age only, which may not have fully controlled for all potential confounding factors.

In conclusion, the present proof-of-concept study demonstrates the feasibility of panoramic radiomics for quantitatively investigating the trabecular bone microenvironment surrounding developmental dental anomalies in children without additional radiation exposure. These preliminary findings suggest that developmental dental phenotypes may reflect broader biological processes extending beyond the affected tooth and into the surrounding osseous microenvironment. Future prospective multicenter studies integrating quantitative radiomics with developmental genetics, molecular biology, and longitudinal clinical outcomes will be essential to validate these candidate radiomic features and clarify their biological basis and potential clinical significance in craniofacial development.
