Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease and Multimorbidity: Insights from a National Claims Administrative Database
Authors: Álvaro Javier Burgos-Cárdenas, Rodolfo J Dennis, Carlos A Torres-Duque, Alejandro Casas, Mauricio Gonzalez-Garcia, Giancarlo Buitrago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo estimate the prevalence of diagnosed chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and characterize the frequency and distribution of multimorbidity among individuals with and without COPD in Colombia’s contributory health system.Patients and MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study using administrative healthcare data from the Capitation Unit Sufficiency Database (Unidad de Pago por Capitación, UPC) and the Unique Enrollees Database (Base de Datos Única de Afiliados, BDUA). An electronic algorithm was used to identify individuals with a COPD diagnosis and to estimate diagnosed COPD prevalence among all affiliates during 2018–2019. We also estimated the prevalence of selected health conditions among individuals with and without COPD. Differences between groups were assessed in two steps. First, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for prevalence estimates. Second, conditions with SMDs of approximately 0.1 or greater were further evaluated using prevalence ratios (PRs) adjusted for sex, region, age, insurer, and prior healthcare service utilization estimated with Poisson regression with robust variance and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe study included 6,865,420 individuals. The nationwide prevalence of diagnosed COPD was 2.21% (95% CI, 2.20–2.22), with the highest prevalence observed in the Central region (2.77%; 95% CI, 2.74–2.79). In adjusted analyses, several conditions were more prevalent among individuals with COPD, including asthma (PR = 3.88; 95% CI, 2.88–5.23), sleep apnea (PR = 2.62; 95% CI, 2.05–3.35), heart failure (PR = 1.89; 95% CI, 1.29–2.78), ischemic heart disease (PR = 1.60; 95% CI, 1.26–2.03), hypertension (PR = 1.08; 95% CI, 1.02–1.15), and osteoporosis (PR = 2.34; 95% CI, 1.52–3.58), among others, were more prevalent among individuals with COPD. Lung cancer (PR = 6.41; 95% CI, 5.89–6.97) and breast cancer (PR = 1.17; 95% CI, 1.08–1.25) were also more prevalent in this population.ConclusionIndividuals with diagnosed COPD in Colombia’s contributory health system had a substantial burden of multimorbidity, particularly respiratory and cardiovascular conditions. These findings support interdisciplinary care models and locally tailored strategies to address regional differences in diagnosed COPD.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide; in 2021, it caused an estimated 3.7 million deaths globally.1 COPD-related mortality shows marked socioeconomic gradients, with nearly 90% of deaths among people younger than 70 years occurring in low- and middle-income countries.1,2 COPD is strongly linked to cumulative exposure to inhaled noxious agents—including tobacco smoke, biomass smoke, and ambient air pollution—and its frequency increases with age as structural and functional respiratory changes accrue over the life course.3

As populations age, the clinical and health-system impact of COPD is increasingly shaped by multimorbidity. Multimorbidity is commonly defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic or long-standing conditions in an individual.4,5 This concept differs from comorbidity and complications: comorbidity refers to additional diseases or conditions occurring in relation to an index disease, whereas complications are health conditions that arise as a consequence of that index disease.6 Previous studies have shown that individuals with COPD have a greater burden of accompanying conditions than individuals without COPD, with respiratory, cardiovascular, metabolic, and mental health conditions frequently reported.7–11

This multimorbidity burden can amplify symptoms and healthcare utilization and may complicate treatment through polypharmacy, drug–drug interactions, and adverse events, with substantial effects on quality of life and mortality. In a large cohort of patients with COPD, each additional disease was associated with higher 3-year mortality (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.10–1.40).

COPD burden and multimorbidity patterns may also vary by environmental, geographic, and social context. Geographic differences in cigarette smoking, biomass fuel use,12 and ambient air pollution—such as higher exposure in lowland or valley regions—may contribute to variation in COPD prevalence and related conditions.13–15

Colombia is geographically and socioeconomically diverse, with regions that differ in altitude, climate, geographic accessibility, and levels of development. These contextual factors are structural determinants of health outcomes, and regions may therefore exhibit distinct patterns of chronic conditions and multimorbidity.16 COPD is strongly associated with socioeconomic conditions and social inequities related to race/ethnicity, income, and sexual orientation, underscoring the importance of characterizing its distribution across heterogeneous settings.17

Cross-sectional studies at the national and regional levels have been described in the literature.18,19 Specifically for Colombia, the PREPOCOL study provided population-based estimates of COPD prevalence in five major cities using multiple diagnostic approaches.19 However, as with studies in other settings, it did not capture nationwide patterns—including rural areas and smaller cities—and it did not characterize multimorbidity at the national level. This evidence gap is particularly relevant because current COPD pathogenic and care pathways within the health system emphasize disease-specific management and referral processes, while systematic assessment and management of multimorbidity is not explicitly prioritized. Consequently, describing the distribution of other conditions in people with and without COPD is relevant to clinical decision-making and should inform health policy aimed at integrated chronic care.

Administrative health care databases offer an opportunity to generate national estimates of COPD disease burden and to characterize multimorbidity patterns at scale. Such data can support planning of chronic disease services and help identify gaps in integrated care delivery, provided that case-identification strategies are clearly specified.

Using administrative health care databases, the objectives of this study were to estimate the prevalence of diagnosed COPD and to estimate and characterize the prevalence and distribution of other morbid conditions among individuals with and without COPD in the Colombian population affiliated to the contributory regime (a mandatory, employment-based health insurance regime for workers and their dependents, for formally employed workers, for independent workers with reported income, and for pensioners).

Material and Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fundación Cardioinfantil under approval code CEIC-093-2026, Act No. 09-2026. Because this was a retrospective study based on an anonymized administrative database, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Access to the BDUA and UPC datasets was formally requested by Dr. Giancarlo Buitrago (co-author, Instituto de Investigaciones Clínicas, Universidad Nacional de Colombia; request No. 201842301411402) and authorized by the Colombian Ministry of Health and Social Protection, which granted delivery of the anonymized datasets to the Instituto de Investigaciones Clínicas, Universidad Nacional de Colombia (official authorization letter No. 201913000267381).

Design

We conducted a cross-sectional study with retrospective data collection using administrative health care data from two databases of the Colombian Ministry of Health and Social Protection (MHSP): the UPC and the BDUA. For the 2018–2019 period, we estimated the period prevalence of diagnosed chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), assessed the frequency of multimorbidity among individuals with COPD, and described differences between individuals with and without COPD. Both databases are managed by the MHSP, anonymized, and linked through a unique identifier.

Data Source and Study Population

The Base for the Study of Capitation Unit Sufficiency (UPC database) is part of the Integrated Social Protection Information System (Sistema Integrado de Información de la Protección Social, SISPRO) and is routinely used by the MHSP to calculate the per-capita premium paid to health insurance entities (Health Promoter Enterprises; Empresas Promotoras de Salud, EPS) responsible for guaranteeing access to healthcare services for individuals enrolled in the contributory insurance regime.

The contributory regime in Colombia includes formally employed workers and their dependents and covered approximately 50% of the Colombian population in 2018. The UPC database contains individual-level records of all reimbursable healthcare services provided to this population during the study period. The database is highly standardized, as it follows nationally mandated reporting structures, coding systems, and quality rules defined by the MHSP, which ensure internal consistency and comparability across insurers, providers, and geographic regions.20 In addition, the UPC database has been used in prior studies supporting its usefulness, including evaluations of its prognostic performance.21

Each record in the database corresponds to a healthcare service provided to a specific individual and includes a unique, longitudinally stable anonymized identifier, date of birth, and sex. Service-level information includes the date of service, healthcare setting (outpatient, home-based care, inpatient hospitalization, or emergency care), healthcare provider institution, and geographic location. Information about clinical procedures was identified using the Clasificación Única de Procedimientos en Salud (CUPS), which assigns a unique standardized code to each healthcare procedure.

Information about prescribed medications was identified using the Clasificación Única de Medicamentos (CUM), which provides standardized medication codes. For each healthcare service, the database also includes the reimbursed cost paid by the insurer, allowing the estimation of direct healthcare costs from the payer perspective.

To define the denominator population of individuals affiliated with the contributory regime—including both healthcare users and non-users during the study period— we used BDUA. The BDUA contains records of all individuals affiliated with the Colombian health system and includes information on insurance regime, insurer, sex, and date of birth. We linked the UPC and BDUA databases using an anonymized unique individual identifier to identify all individuals enrolled in the contributory insurance regime between 2018 and 2019. Individuals with any recorded COPD diagnosis code who did not meet the prespecified COPD case-definition algorithm were excluded from the non-COPD denominator population. This approach allowed accurate ascertainment of the study population.

COPD Definition and Study Population

We identified individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) using an electronic case-identification algorithm implemented in Colombian administrative healthcare data. To select an algorithm appropriate for this setting, we specified and evaluated four alternative algorithms that combined: (i) COPD-related ICD-10 diagnosis codes, (ii) evidence of spirometry testing, and (iii) records of prescriptions for inhaled bronchodilators. To improve specificity, each candidate algorithm required at least one criterion beyond a COPD or ICD-10 code, including a spirometry procedure recorded through a CUPS code and/or dispensing of inhaled bronchodilators (eg, salbutamol, ipratropium bromide, or theophylline). The ICD-10 codes used and CUPS are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

We developed the candidate algorithms based on criteria used in previously published systematic reviews and administrative-database studies of COPD case definitions.22 These studies have commonly combined diagnosis codes with additional evidence, such as repeated healthcare encounters, respiratory medication records, and pulmonary function testing, to improve the specificity and overall accuracy of COPD case ascertainment in routinely collected data.22 Consistent with this literature, we incorporated specificity-enhancing criteria, including repeated COPD-coded encounters in different months when applicable. These criteria were intended to reduce misclassification from isolated coding errors, rule-out diagnoses, or single acute respiratory encounters, and to increase the likelihood that identified individuals had persistent or clinically recognized COPD.23 The operational definitions for each candidate algorithm are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

We selected the final algorithm through an author consensus process. All authors participated in the consensus. Four authors (AC, CT, RD and MG) are board-certified pulmonologists with extensive clinical and research experience in COPD. One author (GB) is an epidemiologist with doctoral training in health economics and expertise in administrative healthcare data. One author (AB) is a physician trained in internal medicine and clinical epidemiology.

We used the PREPOCOL study as an external epidemiological benchmark to assess the plausibility of the COPD prevalence generated by each candidate algorithm in the Colombian context. PREPOCOL was a cross-sectional, population-based study that used probabilistic sampling in five Colombian cities and estimated COPD prevalence using three definitions: (i) spirometric COPD, defined by an obstructive pattern on pre- and post-bronchodilator spirometry; (ii) medical COPD, defined by self-report of a prior physician diagnosis of COPD; and (iii) clinical COPD, defined by reported chronic bronchitis. The PREPOCOL definitions are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. PREPOCOL was not used as an individual-level validation standard, because the present administrative database could not be linked to PREPOCOL participants, clinical records, spirometry reports, or physician-confirmed diagnoses.

A confusion matrix could not be constructed because no individual-level clinical reference standard was available. Therefore, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value could not be directly estimated. The administrative database was fully anonymized and could not be linked back to medical records or pulmonary function test results. For this reason, the algorithm-selection process relied on structured expert consensus, clinical plausibility, comparison with external Colombian prevalence estimates, and consistency with prior administrative-database approaches.

In all algorithms, we defined the index date as the first healthcare encounter carrying a COPD ICD-10 code recorded between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2019. We restricted eligibility to individuals aged 40 to 89 years at the index date (ie, age ≥40 and <90 years at the time of the first COPD-coded encounter). The comparator group — individuals without COPD — was defined as active enrollees in the contributory health insurance scheme, aged 40 to 89 years, affiliated with the selected insurers during 2018–2019, who had no recorded healthcare encounter carrying a COPD-related ICD-10 code (J41–J44) at any point during the 2016–2019 observation window. Eligible comparators were identified from the national beneficiary registry (BDUA) and linked to the service-use database; individuals with at least one COPD-coded encounter were excluded. Prevalence Calculation

We estimated the period prevalence of diagnosed COPD for the 2018–2019 period. To identify prevalent cases, we searched for individuals who met the selected COPD case-definition algorithm at any time between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2019. We used a look-back period starting in 2016 to capture individuals with a prior COPD diagnosis who were likely to remain prevalent cases during 2018–2019, even if the qualifying COPD-related event occurred before the start of the prevalence period.

During 2016–2019, we identified health insurance entities that consistently reported data to the UPC database. To ensure completeness and temporal consistency over time, we restricted analyses to individuals affiliated with these entities, as identified in both the UPC and BDUA databases.

For the numerator, we then included all individuals who satisfied the selected COPD case-identification algorithm and who were enrolled in the contributory insurance regime during 2018–2019, as evidenced by at least one recorded healthcare service during this period.

For the denominator, we included all individuals affiliated with the contributory regime during the same period, as identified in the BDUA database, regardless of healthcare utilization. All individuals in the denominator were aged 40 to 89 years.

Morbidities Measured and Health Services

We assessed multimorbidity using a predefined list of 35 chronic conditions commonly included in administrative data–based multimorbidity studies.24 For each condition, we classified an individual as having the condition if at least two healthcare services related to that condition were recorded in different calendar months within the same year. We adopted this criterion to reduce misclassifications due to rule-out diagnoses or isolated coding errors.

We determined the conditions associated with multimorbidity using healthcare records from 2016 to 2019. We estimated the prevalence of each condition among individuals with COPD and compared it with the prevalence observed among individuals without COPD. For all the individuals in the study, we measured multimorbidity at the time of the most recent health care service during 2018 to 2019 record and during the preceding year.

We calculated standardized mean differences (SMDs) to quantify differences in morbidity prevalence between individuals with and without COPD. We used SMDs rather than p-values because SMDs are less dependent on sample size and are therefore more informative in large administrative datasets, where statistically significant p-values may reflect large sample size rather than clinically or epidemiologically meaningful differences. Morbidities with an SMD ≥0.10 were carried forward to the multivariable analysis. We estimated prevalence ratios using Poisson regression. The adjustment variables were region, age, sex, insurer, and prior healthcare service utilization. We defined prior healthcare service utilization as the number of healthcare services used by each individual during the year before study entry.

Prevalence

The overall national prevalence of COPD was 2.21% (95% CI, 2.20–2.22). Prevalence was higher among women (2.28%; 95% CI, 2.26–2.29) than among men (2.13%; 95% CI, 2.12–2.15; SMD vs the female reference category, −0.009). Regional variation was observed, with the highest prevalence in the Central region (2.77%; 95% CI, 2.74–2.79) and the lowest prevalence in the Amazonia region (0.08%; 95% CI, 0.072–0.09; SMD vs the Central region reference category, −0.14) and the Caribbean region (1.27%; 95% CI, 1.24–1.29; SMD vs the Central region reference category, −0.10). COPD prevalence increased consistently with advancing age. Additional details on prevalence estimates stratified by sex, age group, and region are presented in Table 1. Multimorbidity

Except for dyslipidemia, the most common morbidities were consistently more prevalent among individuals with COPD than among those without COPD. The largest standardized mean differences (SMDs) between groups were observed for arterial hypertension (75.37% vs 33.93%; SMD = 0.90), asthma (16.35% vs 0.78%; SMD =0.55), sleep apnea (16.37% vs 1.57%; SMD = 0.51), ischemic heart disease (17.89% vs 2.92%; SMD = 0.47), chronic kidney disease (18.94% vs 4.80%; SMD = 0.43), and heart failure (10.24% vs 0.69%; SMD = 0.37).

Regarding neoplasms, lung cancer (1.10% vs 0.07%; SMD = 0.13), breast cancer (4.40% vs 1.84%; SMD = 0.14), and prostate cancer (1.60% vs 0.51%; SMD = 0.17) were more prevalent among individuals with COPD, whereas colon and stomach cancer showed no meaningful between-group differences. Discussion

In this national claims-based, cross-sectional analysis of the contributory insurance regime in Colombia, in addition to estimating a prevalence of diagnosed COPD of 2.21% (95% CI, 2.20–2.22) with wide regional and by sex variation, we found a very high burden of multimorbidity associated with the diagnosis of COPD.

Prevalence was highest in the central regions (including Medellín and the Coffee-Growing Axis) and in Bogotá, whereas—except for the Amazon region—the lowest prevalence was observed in the Pacific and Caribbean regions. COPD prevalence was higher in women than in men and increased with age.

Regarding the very high burden of multimorbidity associated with COPD, respiratory conditions showed the strongest associations, particularly asthma and obstructive sleep apnea, which may reflect shared pathophysiology, diagnostic overlap, care pathways that increase diagnostic clustering in administrative data.

After adjustment for age, sex, geographic region, and health care services used cardiovascular conditions remained more prevalent among individuals with COPD, underscoring the clinical importance of integrated chronic disease management. We also observed higher prevalence of musculoskeletal and chronic pain conditions, endocrine disorders (including hypothyroidism and osteoporosis), and hearing impairment.

Our findings appear to be aligned with previously published studies. The high prevalence of asthma among individuals with COPD has been previously reported in cross-sectional studies. Depending on the ascertainment method, up to 13% of patients with COPD have reported that a physician told them they had asthma. Similarly, the asthma–COPD overlap phenotype may be present in up to one-third. In our setting, given data limitations, we cannot determine whether these records reflect true asthma, asthma–COPD overlap, or coding and diagnostic practices in which both conditions are recorded by clinicians.

With respect to sleep apnea syndrome, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis showed a global prevalence of 28.3% (95% CI, 20.8–36.4%) among patients with COPD.25 Lower airway obstruction shares risk factors with sleep apnea, such as obesity and gastroesophageal reflux.26 Additionally, sleep apnea has also been associated with higher concentrations of inflammatory cytokines in bronchoalveolar samples, such as those seen in COPD.27 In our study, these findings could also be related to a higher detection rate in patients diagnosed with COPD rather than a true association between these diseases. On the other hand, a high proportion of our population lives at high altitude (>2,500 m) where desaturation during sleep is more frequent, making the achievement of hypopnea criteria more probable and, consequently, the diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome probably more frequent.28

In a large US self-reported survey, participants who reported arthritis-related conditions (including osteoarthritis, arthritis, fibromyalgia, and gout) had a higher COPD prevalence than those without these conditions (PR, 1.5; 95% CI, 1.4–1.5).29 A case–control study reported that patients with COPD who underwent spinal computed tomography had a fivefold higher likelihood of low back pain and were more likely to present thoracic spine abnormalities than controls.30 To explain these findings, dysregulation of the osteoprotegerin (OPG)/receptor activator of NF-κB (RANK)/RANK ligand (RANKL) pathway has been described in COPD and may contribute to osteoporosis; this pathway may also be linked to rheumatoid arthritis.31–33

With respect to endocrine disorders, the prevalence of hypothyroidism has been reported to be as high as 25% among patients with COPD.34 Despite the role of thyroid hormone in respiratory muscle function and respiratory drive,35 thyroid hormone deficiency has not been consistently associated with COPD severity.34 On the other hand, the prevalence of diabetes mellitus has been reported to correlate with COPD severity, with higher diabetes prevalence at lower FEV1. Diabetes in COPD has been linked to glucocorticoid exposure and to an excess of proinflammatory cytokines.36 However, in our study, after adjustment, diabetes prevalence did not differ between patients with COPD and individuals without COPD.

Regarding neoplasms, COPD was associated with a higher prevalence of lung cancer and, among women, breast cancer. The COPD–lung cancer association is plausibly explained by shared risk factors—especially tobacco exposure—and potentially shared biological pathways, including chronic systemic and airway inflammation.37,38 The higher breast cancer prevalence among women with COPD should be interpreted cautiously. Prior cohort evidence has suggested that, after adjustment, emphysema may be associated with breast cancer (OR, 2.6; 95% CI, 1.25–5.41).39 Other studies have not shown a clear linear association with cigarette consumption; however, earlier smoking initiation and longer smoking duration have been linked to estrogen receptor–positive breast cancer.40

The high cardiovascular burden among people with COPD appears consistent with prior literature. Heart failure showed one of the strongest associations with COPD in our analysis (PR, 1.89; 95% CI, 1.29–2.78). This association may reflect both shared risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms,41 including chronic hypoxemia and pulmonary vascular changes that increase right ventricular afterload and may contribute to right-sided cardiac dysfunction.42

Given the cross-sectional design and the reliance on administrative databases, causal relationships should not be inferred for any of the conditions evaluated. The observed associations may be influenced by misclassification bias, diagnostic and coding practices, and differential healthcare utilization. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as patterns of recorded multimorbidity rather than evidence that the conditions identified represent direct complications of COPD. Based on these data, it is not possible to determine whether the observed conditions are direct COPD-related complications or part of a broader pattern of multimorbidity.

The analysis by sex of our study suggests higher COPD prevalence among women than among men. The PREPOCOL study and other studies in Colombia43,44 and elsewhere, show that other risk factors besides cigarette smoking, such as biomass fuels for cooking, differentially affect women more than men. Additionally, in PREPOCOL, women more frequently reported a prior COPD diagnosis, although the difference was not statistically significant. Men show higher prevalence when spirometry-based criteria are considered,19 suggesting that sex differences may vary depending on whether COPD is identified through clinical diagnosis versus objective lung-function assessment. One plausible explanation is differential healthcare utilization, supported by prior studies indicating that women more frequently report chronic conditions45 despite lower mortality and use outpatient services more often than men.46 A cohort in Brazil likewise reported that women had more health conditions and more primary care visits, whereas men had higher mortality.47 Women have also reported poorer health-related quality of life in both high- and middle-/low-income countries and may be more likely to undergo routine health check-ups.47 Accordingly, the higher COPD prevalence observed among women in some data sources may partly reflect greater case detection related to differential healthcare utilization, rather than a true difference in underlying disease frequency, potentially leading to differential misclassification.

Our regional findings are consistent with prior Colombian evidence, including PREPOCOL and the regional analyses by Ospina et al indicating a higher COPD burden in central areas.18 Geographic heterogeneity may be influenced by differences in tobacco exposure and household air-pollution patterns, including biomass fuel use and indoor cooking practices.48 Nonetheless, national survey data from the Colombian government suggest substantial regional variability without a simple, consistent altitude gradient.49 Differences in exposure intensity, duration, and fuel type likely contribute to this heterogeneity and warrant further investigation. In support of the tobacco-exposure hypothesis, the Encuesta Nacional de Consumo de Sustancias Psicoactivas (ENCSPA) reported a higher prevalence of smoking in the departments comprising the Central region compared with those in the Caribbean and Pacific regions.50

This study has several strengths. We used a large administrative database with standardized, reproducible records previously used in epidemiologic research and applied a COPD case-identification algorithm intended to improve specificity. Our denominator included the insured population in the contributory regime regardless of healthcare utilization, reducing restriction to healthcare users only. We also restricted analyses to insurers with consistent reporting quality to the UPC database, improving internal consistency, and focused on a pre–COVID-19 period to avoid pandemic-related disruptions in healthcare utilization.

Several limitations should be considered. First, our analysis was restricted to individuals enrolled in Colombia’s contributory regime, which may limit generalizability to individuals in the subsidized regime. The subsidized regime predominantly covers socioeconomically vulnerable individuals without formal employment, who often face greater barriers to accessing health services; thus, COPD and multimorbidity may be differentially diagnosed and recorded in the latter population.

Second, we restricted analyses to EPSs with consistent reporting to reduce information bias; however, this decision may introduce selection and detection bias. If these insurers have stronger case detection and coding completeness than other EPSs, the prevalence of COPD and other conditions may be overestimated. Nonetheless, these EPSs cover most of the population within the contributory regime, which partially mitigates concerns about representativeness within that segment of the health system.

Third, the operational definition of the denominator population involved a methodological trade-off. To increase the specificity of the non-COPD comparator group, we excluded individuals with any recorded COPD diagnosis code from the non-COPD population. This approach likely increased the specificity of the comparator definition and reduced misclassification within the non-COPD reference population. However, it may also have amplified contrasts between the COPD and non-COPD groups and yielded lower prevalence estimates for some evaluated conditions than would have been observed under a broader comparator definition.

Fourth, limitations related to COPD case identification should be considered. The selected algorithm was designed to identify individuals with administratively documented COPD rather than to establish a clinical diagnosis de novo. Because no individual-level clinical reference standard was available, we could not construct a confusion matrix or directly estimate the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, or negative predictive value of the algorithm. The database was fully anonymized and could not be linked to medical records, spirometry reports, or physician-confirmed diagnoses. Therefore, the algorithm-selection process relied on clinical plausibility, expert consensus, consistency with prior administrative-database approaches, and comparison with external Colombian prevalence estimates. Nevertheless, in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD)51 for studies using routinely collected health data, we transparently described the algorithm-selection process, the data elements used, and the operational definitions applied to identify COPD cases.

Finally, as in all claims-based studies, misclassification and ascertainment bias are possible because diagnoses depend on health care contact and coding practices.46,52 The strong associations between COPD and other respiratory diagnoses (eg, asthma and obstructive sleep apnea) may be partly influenced by specialist-care pathways and coding proximity. In addition, some asthma codes may reflect overlap phenotypes (eg, asthma–COPD overlap) or diagnostic uncertainty in routine practice.

Conclusion

This study, based on an administrative healthcare database, suggests that administratively documented COPD may be associated with a high recorded burden of multimorbidity, particularly respiratory and cardiovascular conditions, with apparent heterogeneity in COPD prevalence across regions and demographic groups. These findings may inform integrated, region-sensitive chronic care strategies and risk stratification approaches that account for multimorbidity patterns in routine care. Future studies should evaluate longitudinal outcomes, externally validate COPD coding algorithms across healthcare settings, and include populations from both contributory and subsidized regimes to improve national representativeness.
