Inference in Spreading Processes with Neural-Network Priors
Davide Ghio, Fabrizio Boncoraglio, Lenka Zdeborová

Abstract
Stochastic processes on graphs are a powerful tool for modelling complex dynamical systems such as epidemics. A recent line of work focused on the inference problem where one aims to estimate the state of every node at every time, starting from partial observation of a subset of nodes at a subset of times. In these works, the initial state of the process was assumed to be random i.i.d. over nodes. Such an assumption may not be realistic in practice, where one may have access to a set of covariate variables for every node that influence the initial state of the system. In this work, we will assume that the initial state of a node is an unknown function of such covariate variables. Given that functions can be represented by neural networks, we will study a model where the initial state is given by a simple neural network – notably the single-layer perceptron acting on the known node-wise covariate variables. Within a Bayesian framework, we study how such neural-network prior information enhances the recovery of initial states and spreading trajectories. We derive a hybrid belief propagation and approximate message passing (BP-AMP) algorithm that handles both the spreading dynamics and the information included in the node covariates, and we assess its performance against the estimators that either use only the spreading information or use only the information from the covariate variables. We show that in some regimes, the model can exhibit first-order phase transitions when using a Rademacher distribution for the neural-network weights. These transitions create a statistical-to-computational gap where even the BP-AMP algorithm, despite the theoretical possibility of perfect recovery, fails to achieve it.



I
Introduction

Spreading dynamics on networks model many important systems, such as information diffusion in social networks
Vespignani 2012
, epidemic processes
Pastor-Satorras et al. 2015
;
Keeling and Eames 2005
, and gene regulatory networks
Karlebach and Shamir 2008
. There are many studies in the literature that tackle the interplay between the network topology and the dynamical process which takes place on top of it
Hwang 2006
.

In this work we study Bayesian inference for this class of models, aiming to recover some information about a realisation of the dynamics from partial observations. In such Bayesian setting, this problem reduces to computing posterior expectations, where the spreading process itself defines the prior and observations form the likelihood.
Statistical physics has yielded several inference algorithms for spreading models on random sparse graphs, including dynamic message passing (DMP)
Lokhov et al. 2014
and belief propagation (BP)
Altarelli et al. 2014a
. Our work builds on the BP formulation to derive a new algorithm.

Spreading models often assume a separable prior over initial sources, ignoring node-specific information. However, node features are often available in practice and can enhance inference. To leverage such data, recent work in signal processing uses generative priors, often based on neural networks that map features to problem variables
Aubin et al. 2019
;
Aubin et al. 2020
.

We introduce the Neural Sources Spreading (NSS) model, where the unknown initial state of the spreading process is given by the output of a neural network with unknown weights acting on known node-wise variables. The NSS model’s posterior corresponds to a hybrid factor graph. It combines a sparse graph on which the spreading dynamics takes place - suited for BP on locally tree-like structures
Altarelli et al. 2014a
;
Mezard and Montanari 2009
- with a dense graph for the neural-network prior, which is effectively handled by approximate message passing (AMP) in high dimensions
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
;
Barbier et al. 2019
. Using the cavity method, we merge these components to derive a hybrid BP-AMP algorithm that handles both the sparse spreading interactions and the dense Neural-Network prior
Manoel et al. 2017
;
Duranthon and Zdeborová 2023
.

This paper is structured as follows. Section
II
defines the NSS model. Section
III
details the Bayesian inference framework and performance metrics. Section
IV
presents the cavity equations and our BP-AMP algorithm. We then analyse its performance in a setting showing evidence of Bayes-optimality (Sec.
V
) and another exhibiting a statistical-to-computational gap (Sec.
VI
).

I.1
Related works

This study is situated within the field of spreading processes on graphs, which examines how these dynamics relate to the underlying graph structure, particularly in domains such as epidemiology
Newman 2003
;
Kiss et al. 2017
. Building on established compartmental models
Kermack et al. 1932
;
Paré et al. 2020
, we focus on the algorithmic challenges of inference, such as identifying the initial sources of an epidemic or recovering entire infection trajectories.

A large body of work in different scientific domains have studied the problem of inference of spreading processes on graphs. Probably the most important example is the one of source detection, or equivalently of finding the patient-zero of the epidemic, which has attracted many researchers after the seminal papers
Shah and Zaman 2010a
;
Shah and Zaman 2011
, where the authors, restricting to the SI model, showed rigorous results for regular trees using the concept of rumour centrality as maximum likelihood estimator. Later, in
Antulov-Fantulin et al. 2014
the authors extended the study to general trees, and in
Dong et al. 2013
the SIR model was considered, in both cases choosing as partial information a snapshot of the epidemic, i.e. the state of all the nodes at a particular time. At the same time in
Pinto et al. 2012a
the authors moved to consider localised observations, or sensors, as source of information and described the optimal maximum likelihood estimator for trees.

In
Karrer and Newman 2010
for the first time a message-passing approach was introduced to analyse epidemic models, enabling the computation of average properties on arbitrary random graphs ensembles. Later, two different works were able to design a message-passing algorithm operating on single graphs: in
Lokhov et al. 2014
the authors introduced a “Dynamical Message Passing” (DMP) algorithm, which is exact on trees but gives results for a fixed initial condition, while in
Altarelli et al. 2014b
the “Belief Propagation” (BP) equations for this problem were derived for the first time, and were used to infer the origin of epidemics with multiple patients zero and various types of partial information. These techniques were later used for other related problems, as the reconstruction of the model’s parameters
Lokhov 2016
or epidemic mitigation studies
Baker et al. 2021
.

The methodology we employ connects to the idea of merging graphical models, an approach that was formulated in
Manoel et al. 2017
;
Gabrié et al. 2018
to extend single-layer AMP to multiple layers. Closer to the present setting,
Duranthon and Zdeborová 2023
derived a BP-AMP algorithm for community detection with a neural network prior on node attributes.

Finally, we note a recent line of work conjecturing the existence of replica symmetry breaking in spreading processes at very low source densities
Braunstein et al. 2023
;
Braunstein et al. 2025
. This paper, in contrast, focuses on scenarios with higher source densities where this phenomenology is not observed, leaving the low-density regime with a neural-network prior for future study.

II
The Neural Sources Spreading Model

II.1
Spreading models on graphs

We consider processes on a graph
G(V,E)
with
N
nodes.
Each node of the graph is assigned a variable
x_{i}^{t}
at discrete times
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
, taking values among a finite number
p
of possible states.
We focus on unidirectional dynamics, where a node’s transition times between the possible states of the nodes
\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\}_{i=1}^{N}=\{t^{1}_{i},\dots,t_{i}^{p-1}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, which we define as the last time in which a node is in a state before it transitions, fully specify its evolution. This static representation reduces the system’s variables from
O(N\times T)
to
O(N)
. This restriction can be relaxed to models where nodes revisit states (e.g., SIS) by treating backward steps as new state transitions
Ghio et al. 2023
;
Pastor-Satorras et al. 2015
.

Assuming local spreading dynamics, where transitions depend only on nearest-neighbour states, the transition time probability factorizes as:

P(\,\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\}_{i=1}^{N}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm spread}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Psi_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}_{j\in\partial_{i}},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})\,,

(1)

where
Z_{\rm spread}
is the normalization,
\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
are model parameters, and
\partial_{i}
is the set of neighbours of node
i
. Importantly, the factor
\Psi_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}_{j\in\partial_{i}},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})
is a node factor which depends on its transition time and the ones of its nearest neighbours.

We will illustrate the method on two examples of the dynamical model: the Susceptible-Infected (SI) model and the deterministic Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (dSIR) model
Ghio et al. 2023
.

•

In the SI model, each node is either susceptible (S) or infectious (I), so the dynamics is described by a single transition time
t_{i}
for each node
i
. We say that node
i
is a
source
of the spreading if it is infectious at time
t=0
, and in this case we have
t_{i}=-1
. At time
t
, a susceptible node
i
has a probability
\lambda_{ji}^{t}\in[0,1]
to get infected from an infectious node
j\in\partial_{i}
.
In mathematical terms, the probability for node
i
of being infected at time
s
is given by

{p}^{\rm inf}_{i}(s,\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\partial_{i}})=1-\prod_{k\in\partial_{i}}(1-\lambda_{ki}\mathds{I}[s>t_{k}])\,.

(2)

The total factor for this node is then

\Psi_{i}(\,t_{i},\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\partial_{i}}\mid x_{i}^{0}\,)=\begin{cases}{p}^{\rm inf}_{i}(t_{i},\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\partial_{i}})\prod_{s=0}^{t_{i}-1}\left(1-{p}^{\rm inf}_{i}(s,\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\partial_{i}})\right)&\text{if }x_{i}^{0}=S,\ t_{i}<T\\
\prod_{s=0}^{T-1}\left(1-{p}^{\rm inf}_{i}(s,\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\partial_{i}})\right)&\text{if }x_{i}^{0}=S,\ t_{i}=T\\
\delta_{t_{i},-1}&\text{if }x_{i}^{0}=I\,,\end{cases}

(3)

where we assign
t_{i}=T
to nodes which are still susceptible at
t=T-1
, the last simulation time.

•

In the dSIR model, S-to-I transitions are still stochastic, and follow the same rule as the SI model. However, an additional recovery process takes place and it is deterministic: a node remains infectious for a fixed time
\Delta_{i}
before recovering permanently. The resulting factor for node
i
can still be written as in Eq. (
3
), but with the modified infection probability

{p}^{\rm inf}_{i}(s,\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\partial_{i}})=1-\prod_{k\in\partial_{i}}(1-\lambda_{ki}\mathds{I}[t_{k}<s\leq t_{k}+\Delta_{k}])

(4)

We can recover the SI model by fixing
\Delta_{i}>T\;\ \forall\,i
, such that all nodes remain infectious until the end.

Throughout, we assume the form of the spreading kernel in Eq. (
1
) is known, as well as all parameters
\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
of the spreading model. In the SI model, these include the graph
G
and all the associated infectivity parameters
\lambda_{ij}\;\forall\,(i,j)\in E
, while for the dSIR model we also have the recovery delays
\Delta_{i}\;\forall\,i\in V
. Moreover, we will focus on studying inference on graphs generated through random sparse ensembles, such as Erdos-Renyi (ER) and Random Regular (RR) graphs.

II.2
Neural-network priors on the spreading sources

Spreading models often assume that the nodes that originate the spreading, i.e. the sources, are single
Pinto et al. 2012b
;
Shah and Zaman 2010b
;
Lokhov et al. 2014
or multiple
Luo et al. 2013
;
Altarelli et al. 2014a
uniformly random nodes. Realistically, the source prior is often non-trivial and can be informed by known node-wise covariates
F\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, namely each node
i\in V
has
M
features, contained in the vector
\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, representing some contextual information about each node. For instance, in epidemiology, covariates like age or mobility can determine a node’s propensity to act as an initial source. Such features can be incorporated to improve inference
Fan et al. 2015
. This implies the initial state is a function of its covariates,
x_{i}^{0}=f(\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}})
. In the SI and dSIR models described before, this variable is binary: node
i
can either be a source of the spreading, and we conventionally assign
x_{i}^{0}=-1
, or it is susceptible at the start, and in this case
x_{i}^{0}=+1
.

Since generic functions can be parametrised using a multi-layer neural network, it is reasonable to consider the covariates
\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}}
to be an input and the initial states
\bm{\mathbf{x_{i}^{0}}}
to be an output of a multi-layer fully-connected neural network:

x_{i}^{0}=\varphi^{(L)}\left(W^{(L)}\varphi^{(L-1)}\left(W^{(L-1)}\dots\varphi^{(1)}\left(W^{(1)}\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}}\right)\dots\right)\right)

(5)

where
\varphi^{(l)}(\cdot)
are component-wise non-linearities and
\{W^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
are the network weights. We call this the Neural Sources Spreading (NSS) model.

In order to obtain a model where the information contained in the prior can be quantified precisely, we will consider in what follows a single-layer network with random weights
\bm{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, following a known, component-wise prior
u_{a}\sim P_{u}
,
\forall\,a\in\{1,\dots,M\}
. The initial state is then sampled according to

x_{i}^{0}\sim P^{\rm out}(\,x_{i}^{0}\mid\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}},\bm{\mathbf{u}}\,)\equiv\delta\left(x_{i}^{0}-\varphi\left(\sum_{a}F_{ia}u_{a}\right)\right)\,.

(6)

Here,
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta. For modelling purposes we also choose
F\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
as a matrix of i.i.d. Gaussian components
F_{ia}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/M)
. We will consider the two cases of Gaussian and Rademacher weights
\bm{\mathbf{u}}
. We set
\varphi(x)=\text{sign}(x-\kappa)
, yielding the perceptron model
Gardner 1988
;
Gardner and Derrida 1988
;
Krauth and Mézard 1989
, where the threshold
\kappa
controls the source density. We call this specific version the Perceptron Sources Spreading (PSS) model.

In order to be in an analytically tractable setting, we study the limit where
N,M\to\infty
with a finite constant ratio
\alpha=N/M=\mathcal{O}(1)
. In this regime,
\alpha
acts as a signal-to-noise ratio controlling correlations between initial states. For large
\alpha
(small
M
), the
x_{i}^{0}
are strongly correlated via
\bm{\mathbf{u}}
. As
\alpha\to 0
, the terms
\sum_{a=1}^{M}F_{ia}u_{a}
become independent Gaussians, and we recover a uniform prior
P(x_{i}^{0}=I)=\delta
for all
i
, with
\delta=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\text{erf}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)
.

III
Bayesian inference framework

In this section, we define in detail the inference problem.
We assume we are given the covariates
F\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, the graph structure
G
and the edge-wise transmission probabilities
\lambda_{ij}
. We also know the constant
\kappa
from Eq. (
6
), and all
\Delta_{i}
for the dSIR model. We are not given the vector
\bm{\mathbf{u}}
, and consequently the sources are also unknown, as well as the transition times
{\bf t}_{i}
. The task is to recover the initial state
{\bf x}^{0}
and the transition times
{\bf t}_{i}
based on additional partial observation of the state of each node
\{\mathcal{O}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
We assume the availability of node-wise observations
\bm{\mathcal{O}}=\{\mathcal{O}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,

P(\,\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\},\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P(\,\mathcal{O}_{i}\mid\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)

(7)

where
\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\}
is the set of all transition times. This implies that the observation
\mathcal{O}_{i}
is conditionally independent of other nodes’ transition times given
\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}
.
In the following, we will focus on two classes of observations: the first are
sensors

Pinto et al. 2012b
;
Spinelli et al. 2017
, in which we choose randomly a fraction
\rho
of the nodes, and we observe their full time trajectory, such that an observation on node
i
acts as a delta function on the transition time
t_{i}
. The second framework, known as
snapshots

Lokhov et al. 2014
;
Altarelli et al. 2014b
, is when the entire system state
\bm{\mathbf{x^{t=T_{\rm obs}}}}
is observed at a single time
T_{\rm obs}
, and thus an observation on node
i
does not fix
t_{i}
, but gives either a lower bound
t_{i}\geq T_{\rm obs}
or an upper bound
t_{i}<T_{\rm obs}
.

Using Bayes’ rule, the posterior distribution of the model becomes:

\displaystyle P(\,\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\},\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}},\bm{\mathbf{u}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P(\,\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mid F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)}P(\,\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\},\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}},\bm{\mathbf{u}},F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)P(\,\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\},\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}},\bm{\mathbf{u}}\mid F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P(\,\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mid F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)}P(\,\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mid\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\},\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)P(\,\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)P(\,\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{u}},F\,)P(\bm{\mathbf{u}})

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}_{j\in\partial_{i}},x_{i}^{0},\mathcal{O}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})\Psi_{i}^{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}})\prod_{a=1}^{M}\psi_{a}(u_{a})\,,

(10)

where

\psi_{a}(u_{a})=P^{u}(u_{a})
is the prior distribution of the weights,
\Psi_{i}^{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}})=P^{\rm out}(\,x_{i}^{0}\mid\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}},\bm{\mathbf{u}}\,)
the output distribution of the initial epidemic state of the nodes given the external context, and
\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}_{j\in\partial_{i}},x_{i}^{0},\mathcal{O}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})\equiv\Psi_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}_{j\in\partial_{i}},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})P(\,\mathcal{O}_{i}\mid\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\,)
, which contains the information regarding the epidemic and the observations provided. Finally,

\displaystyle Z\left(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\left(\prod_{a=1}^{M}\differential{u}_{a}\psi_{a}(u_{a})\right)\sum_{\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},x_{i}^{0}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}_{j\in\partial_{i}},x_{i}^{0},\mathcal{O}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})\Psi_{i}^{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}})

(11)

is the partition function, and we define

\Phi\left(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\log Z\left(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F,\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\right)

(12)

the associated free entropy.

III.1
Sources retrieval and optimal inference

A Bayesian approach unifies various inference tasks, as they all reduce to estimating marginals of initial states
\{x_{i}^{0}\}
or transition times
\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\}
. For concreteness, we focus on retrieving the spreading sources. An analysis of retrieving the full trajectory, which shows similar phenomenology, is in Appendix
C.3
.

To quantify source recovery performance, since the initial states
x_{i}^{0}
are binary (susceptible or source), a natural metric is the overlap between the ground-truth state
\bm{\mathbf{x^{*,0}}}
and an estimator
\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0}}}
. The overlap is defined as:

\rm{O}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{y}})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i},y_{i}}\,,

(13)

where
\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}
are two generic vectors and
\delta_{x_{i},y_{i}}
is the Kronecker delta.

The Bayes-optimal strategy uses both observations and features to compute the posterior
P(\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)
and leads to an estimator
\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0}}}
that maximises the mean overlap with the true state:

\rm{MO}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0}}})\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F}\left[\rm{O}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0}}},\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}})\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}}P(\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\hat{x}_{i}^{0},x_{i}^{0}}\,.

(14)

It is known
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
that this quantity is maximised by the estimator

\hat{x}_{i}^{0,\rm{MMO}}=\underset{x_{i}^{0}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\,\mu(x_{i}^{0}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)\quad\forall\,i\in[1:N]

(15)

where
\mu(x_{i}^{0}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)
is the posterior marginal for node
i
. This is the Maximum Mean Overlap (MMO) estimator. We analyse its performance via the overlap
{\rm O}_{t=0}\equiv\rm{O}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0,\rm{MMO}}}},\bm{\mathbf{x^{*,0}}})
and mean overlap
{\rm MO}_{t=0}\equiv{\rm MO}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0,{\rm MMO}}}})
.

In a Bayes-optimal setting, the Nishimori conditions have to hold
Nishimori 2001
;
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
. Since we will resort to approximations, verifying the validity of the Nishimori conditions is a useful self-consistency check.
In a Bayes-optimal setting, indeed, the estimated prior and likelihood match the true distributions, making samples from the posterior statistically indistinguishable from the ground truth. This implies that the expected overlap
{\rm O}_{t=0}
and the expected mean overlap
{\rm MO}_{t=0}
must coincide in expectation. The expected overlap over the disorder is:

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{x^{*,0}}},\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F}\left[\rm{O}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0,\rm{MMO}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F),\bm{\mathbf{x^{*,0}}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{F}\Big[\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{x^{*,0}}},\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}}\rm{O}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0,\rm{MMO}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F),\bm{\mathbf{x^{*,0}}})P^{*}(\bm{\mathbf{x^{*,0}}})P^{*}(\,\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x^{*,0}}}\,)\Big]

(16)

where
P^{*}
denotes the ground-truth distributions. Similarly, the disorder-averaged mean overlap is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F}\left[\rm{MO}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0,\rm{MMO}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F}\Big[\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}}\rm{O}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0,\rm{MMO}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F),\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}})P(\,\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F\,)\Big]=

(17)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{F}\Big[\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}},\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}}\rm{O}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{x}^{0,\rm{MMO}}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F),\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}})P(\,\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}\,)P(\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}})\Big]

(18)

Bayes-optimality implies
P(\cdot)=P^{*}(\cdot)
, so Eq. (
16
) and Eq. (
18
) coincide. Substituting the MMO estimator yields:

\mathbb{E}[{\rm O}_{t=0}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}[\argmax_{x_{i}^{0}}\mu(\,x_{i}^{0}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F\,)=x_{i}^{*,0}]]

(19)

and

\mathbb{E}[{\rm MO}_{t=0}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\max_{x_{i}^{0}}\mu(\,x_{i}^{0}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F\,)]\,,

(20)

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function. In our numerical experiments, we utilise the equivalence of the empirical estimates in Eq. (
19
) and Eq. (
20
) to assess the consistency of the approximations made in the algorithm with the expected behaviour of the Bayes-optimal estimator.

IV
The algorithm

In this section we present the inference algorithm we are going to use to estimate the posterior marginals of the problem. This is derived using the cavity method from statistical physics
Mezard and Montanari 2009
and combines approximate message passing (AMP) for Generalized Linear Models
Barbier et al. 2019
;
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
with belief propagation (BP) for spreading models
Altarelli et al. 2014b
;
Ghio et al. 2023
.
This approach of merging dense and sparse graphical models has been successfully applied in related contexts
Manoel et al. 2017
;
Gabrié et al. 2018
;
Duranthon and Zdeborová 2023
;
Duranthon and Zdeborová 2024
.

) is shown in Fig.

\begin{cases}m_{i\rightarrow j}(t_{i},t_{j})&=\frac{1}{Z_{i\rightarrow j}}\sum_{x_{i}^{0}}\eta_{i}(x_{i}^{0})\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{t_{k\in\partial i\backslash j}}}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}(t_{i},\bm{\mathbf{t_{k\in\partial i}}},\mathcal{O}_{i},x_{i}^{0})\prod_{k\in\partial_{i}\backslash j}m_{k\rightarrow i}(t_{k},t_{i})\\

\eta_{i}(x_{i}^{0})&=\frac{1}{Z_{i}^{\eta}}\prod_{a}\left(\int{\rm d}u_{a}\bar{\chi}_{a\rightarrow i}(u_{a})\right)\Psi_{i}^{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}})\\
\chi_{i\rightarrow a}(u_{a})&=\frac{1}{Z_{i\rightarrow a}}\sum_{x_{i}^{0}}\nu_{i}(x_{i}^{0})\int\left(\prod_{b\neq a}{\rm d}u_{b}\bar{\chi}_{b\rightarrow j}(u_{b})\right)\Psi_{i}^{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}})\\
\bar{\chi}_{a\rightarrow i}(u_{a})&=\frac{1}{\bar{Z}_{a\rightarrow i}}\psi_{a}(u_{a})\prod_{j\neq i}\chi_{j\rightarrow a}(u_{a})\end{cases}

(21)

These equations allow to compute the probability marginals exactly when the factor graph associated to the posterior is acyclic
Yedidia et al. 2003
. In our setting, we will consider spreading graphs which are locally tree-like, i.e. with loops of typical length scaling as
\log N
, for which BP is expected to estimate the marginals with an error which goes to zero as
N
goes to
\infty
, if the replica symmetric (RS) assumption holds. We will refer to this property as the asymptotic optimality of the algorithm. Recently, some evidence was shown
Braunstein et al. 2023
;
Braunstein et al. 2025
towards the existence of a replica symmetry broken (RSB) phase in spreading models for very low values of the fraction of sources, where these assumptions break down and the BP equations stop converging. Here, we focus on cases where the density of sources is high enough that these issues are not observed, thus remaining in the regime where the RS assumption is valid.

Eq. (
21

Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
. These simplifications yield key denoising functions, for which we postpone the detailed derivation in Appendix
A

The output denoising function is:

where the last step uses

Finally, we comment on the relevance of these phase transitions to algorithmic hardness. While proving statements about all polynomial-time algorithms is beyond the scope of this work, significant results have established the asymptotic optimality of AMP among large classes of algorithms for various inference tasks
Celentano et al. 2020
;
Montanari and Wein 2024
. Motivated by this line of research, we conjecture that our BP-AMP algorithm is also asymptotically optimal within these classes. Consequently, the hardness phenomena identified here likely represent fundamental computational hurdles.

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a model for local spreading on graphs where sources are generated by a neural-network prior rather than being sampled uniformly. Using statistical physics techniques, we derived a Bayesian inference algorithm which we conjecture to be asymptotically optimal among polynomial-time algorithms, when considering locally tree-like graphs and i.i.d. Gaussian features.
We have analysed the performance of the resulting BP-AMP algorithm against estimators that use only information from the spreading (BP-only) or that use only the covariate variables (AMP-only), finding a great advantage overall in combining the two techniques.
For binary weights, our analysis revealed a statistical-to-computational gap, driven by first-order phase transitions to perfect recovery, as a function of the problem’s signal-to-noise ratio. This phenomenology, absent in models with uniform priors, highlights how a neural network model for the sources’ prior can drastically alter the inference problem’s hardness.

Several future directions stem from this work. We assumed all model parameters were known; a natural extension is to study the more realistic case where these parameters must be learned.

Future work could also explore more complex neural-network priors, such as multi-layer architectures
Aubin et al. 2020
. This would technically require combining results from
Manoel et al. 2017
and
Ghio et al. 2023
, to study the influence of network depth. Alternatively, the neural prior could be applied directly to the transition times, which would necessitate a multi-class output AMP algorithm as derived in
Cornacchia et al. 2023
.

Finally, developing rigorous mathematical proofs for the more general hardness conjectures presented here remains an important open question.

. We adopt the notation of
Ghio et al. 2023
, which is more directly adaptable to the present case.

The variables
\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{(i,j)}
in Fig.
1
compactly represent the pair of variables
\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}
on a modified factor graph that preserves the local tree-like structure. The detailed derivation can be found in
Ghio et al. 2023
; here we give the final form of the message-passing equations:

m_{i\rightarrow j}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}})=\frac{1}{Z_{i\rightarrow j}}\sum_{x_{i}^{0}}\sum_{\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{k}}}\}_{k\in\partial_{i}\backslash j}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}_{j\in\partial_{i}},x_{i}^{0},\mathcal{O}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})\prod_{k\in\partial_{i}\backslash j}m_{k\rightarrow i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{k}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}})\,,

(31)

from which one can compute the marginal for each variable as

\displaystyle b_{i}\left(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{i}}\sum_{x_{i}^{0}}\sum_{\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{k}}}\}_{k\in\partial_{i}}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}\}_{j\in\partial_{i}},x_{i}^{0},\mathcal{O}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}})\prod_{k\in\partial_{i}}m_{k\rightarrow i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{k}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}})=

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{i\rightarrow j}}{Z_{i}}\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}}m_{j\rightarrow i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}})m_{i\rightarrow j}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}})\,,

(33)

where
Z_{i}
is the normalization. The free entropy for a single instance can then be written as

\frac{1}{N}\log Z=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log Z_{i}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\log Z_{(i,j)}\,,

(34)

where
Z_{(i,j)}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}}}m_{j\rightarrow i}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}})m_{i\rightarrow j}(\bm{\mathbf{t_{i}}},\bm{\mathbf{t_{j}}})
.

A.2
Approximate message passing for the Perceptron prior

Approximate message passing (AMP) is a message-passing algorithm tailored for inference on dense graphical models, such as the Generalized Linear Models (GLMs)
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
that include the one-layer perceptron prior used here. We sketch its derivation, adapting the notation to our setting.

The GLM prior generates the initial states
\bm{\mathbf{x^{0}}}
from the vector of weights
\bm{\mathbf{u}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{M}
(with prior
P^{u}
) and a random matrix
F\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
(with
F_{ia}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(0,1/M)
) via a perceptron channel:

P_{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0}|z_{i})=\delta\Bigl(x_{i}^{0}-\mathrm{sign}(z_{i}-\kappa)\Bigr)\qquad\text{where}\qquad z_{i}\;=\;\sum_{a=1}^{M}\!F_{ia}\,u_{a}\,.

(35)

The factor graph for this prior is dense. In the thermodynamic limit (
M,N\to\infty
with
\alpha=N/M=O(1)
), the cavity fields acting on any single variable are sums of many weakly correlated terms. By the central-limit theorem, these fields concentrate and their distributions become Gaussian, allowing them to be characterised by just their first two moments.

The key messages in the dense sub-graph of Fig.
1
are
\chi_{i\to a}(u_{a})
(from node
i
to the weight
a
) and
\bar{\chi}_{a\to i}(u_{a})
(from weight
a
to node
i
). Due to the concentration argument, these messages can be approximated as Gaussian distributions. Specifically, the message
\bar{\chi}_{a\to i}(u_{a})
takes the form:

\bar{\chi}_{a\to i}(u_{a})\;\propto\;P^{u}(u_{a})\,\exp\!\Bigl[\!-\,\tfrac{A}{2}\,u_{a}^{2}+B_{a\to i}\,u_{a}\Bigr],

(36)

where
A
and
B_{a\to i}
are effective parameters representing the variance and mean of the cavity field on
u_{a}
. This Gaussian approximation is the foundation of the AMP algorithm.

The algorithm proceeds by iteratively updating estimates for the means (
a_{a}
) and variances (
v_{a}
) of the weights, and the effective fields (
\omega_{i}
) acting on the output nodes. This is achieved through scalar denoising functions for the input (
f_{a},f_{v}
) and output (
g_{o}
) channels. The standard AMP updates for a GLM take the form:

\displaystyle V^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{a}v_{a}^{(t)},

(37)

\displaystyle\omega_{i}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}F_{ia}a_{a}^{(t)}-V^{(t+1)}g_{o,i}^{(t)},

(38)

\displaystyle g_{o,i}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=g_{o}\bigl(\omega_{i}^{(t+1)},x_{i}^{0},V^{(t+1)}\bigr),

(39)

\displaystyle A^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i}\bigl(g_{o,i}^{(t+1)}\bigr)^{2},

(40)

\displaystyle B_{a}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}F_{ia}g_{o,i}^{(t+1)}+a_{a}^{(t)}A^{(t+1)},

(41)

\displaystyle a_{a}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=f_{a}\!\bigl(A^{(t+1)},B_{a}^{(t+1)}\bigr),\;v_{a}^{(t+1)}=f_{v}\!\bigl(A^{(t+1)},B_{a}^{(t+1)}\bigr).

(42)

Here, the terms
g_{o,i}^{(t)}
in the update for
\omega_{i}
and
a_{a}^{(t)}A^{(t+1)}
in the update for
B_{a}
are the crucial Onsager reaction terms. They correct for the “self-interaction” that would otherwise arise from using a variable’s estimate to compute the field acting back on it, ensuring the algorithm’s dynamics can be tracked accurately by State Evolution
Barbier et al. 2019
. The functions
g_{o}
,
f_{a}
, and
f_{v}
are the scalar denoisers defined in the main text.

A.3
Putting the two together

In the NSS model, the posterior factorizes into two sub-problems: a dense GLM part coupling
(\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{0})
and a sparse spreading part coupling
(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{0},\{\bm{\mathbf{t}}_{i}\})
. This structure motivates a hybrid message-passing architecture: AMP handles the dense GLM part, providing prior messages
\eta_{i}(x_{i}^{0})
to BP, which in turn computes refined beliefs
\nu_{i}(x_{i}^{0})
that act as effective likelihoods for AMP.

Concretely, at each iteration
t
we perform two steps:

•

BP-step
: Update all epidemic messages
m_{i\to j}^{(t)}(\bm{\mathbf{t}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{t}}_{j})
using Eq. (
31
), where the source term is now weighted by the message
\eta_{i}^{(t)}(x_{i}^{0})
from AMP. From the updated messages, compute the new marginals on the initial states:

\nu^{(t+1)}_{i}(x_{i}^{0})=\sum_{t_{i},\bm{\mathbf{t_{k\in\partial i}}}}\widetilde{\psi}_{i}(t_{i},\bm{\mathbf{t_{k\in\partial i}}},\mathcal{O}_{i},x_{i}^{0})\prod_{k\in\partial i}m^{(t)}_{k\rightarrow i}(t_{k},t_{i})\,.

(43)

•

AMP-step
: Use the BP marginals
\nu_{i}^{(t+1)}(x_{i}^{0})
as effective, data-dependent likelihoods in the GLM–AMP update. The resulting field, to be passed back to BP, is computed as:

\eta^{(t+1)}_{i}(x_{i}^{0})\propto\int dzP_{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0}|z)\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(z-\omega_{i}^{(t+1)})^{2}}{2V^{(t+1)}}\Bigr]\,.

(44)

To merge the two algorithms, we only need to modify the output denoising function
g_{o}
to incorporate the prior knowledge
\nu_{i}(x_{i}^{0})
from the BP step:

g_{o}(\omega,\nu,V)\;=\;\frac{\displaystyle\int\!dz\sum_{x^{0}}\nu(x^{0})\,P_{\rm out}(x^{0}|z)\,(z-\omega)\;e^{-\frac{(z-\omega)^{2}}{2V}}}{\displaystyle V\int\!dz\sum_{x^{0}}\nu(x^{0})\,P_{\rm out}(x^{0}|z)\;e^{-\frac{(z-\omega)^{2}}{2V}}}\,.

(45)

With this modification, the AMP updates in Eq. (
37
) and the BP updates in Eq. (
31
) (now weighted by
\eta_{i}
) are iterated, forming the complete Algorithm
1
. This construction is inspired by
Duranthon and Zdeborová 2023
;
Ghio et al. 2023
. The limit
F\to 0
recovers the BP-only inference from
Ghio et al. 2023
, while the limit
\lambda_{ij}\to 0
collapses to the standard perceptron-AMP from
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
. In practice, damping and asynchronous updates are used to ensure convergence.

Appendix B
Free-Entropy

Given a probability distribution, and the associated factor graph, the Bethe approximation of the log–partition function is given by

\log Z_{\rm Bethe}=\sum_{a}\phi_{a}-\sum_{(a,b)}\phi_{ab}\,,

(46)

where the sum over
a
runs over all factor nodes and variable nodes, and
(a,b)
runs over all edges in the factor graph. We can decompose the free entropy into three pieces:

\phi_{\rm RS}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\log Z_{\rm Bethe}=\phi_{\rm spread}+\phi_{\rm GLM}+\phi_{\rm inter}\,,

(47)

with

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm spread}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{\rm spread}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\phi_{(i,j)}^{\rm spread}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\phi_{i,(i,j)}^{\rm spread}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\phi_{j,(i,j)}^{\rm spread}\,,

(48)

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm GLM}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{\rm out}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{a=1}^{M}\phi_{a}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{a=1}^{M}\phi_{ia}\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm inter}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{0}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{0,{\rm out}}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{0,{\rm spread}}\,.

(50)

As done in
Ghio et al. 2023
, one can show that
\phi_{i,(i,j)}^{\rm spread}=\phi_{j,(i,j)}^{\rm spread}=\phi_{(i,j)}^{\rm spread}
, so that for the spreading part we have

\phi_{\rm spread}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{\rm spread}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\phi_{(i,j)}^{\rm spread}\,,

(51)

where

\phi_{i}^{\rm spread}=\log\sum_{x_{i}^{0},t_{i},\{t_{j}\}_{j\in\partial i}}\eta_{i}(x_{i}^{0})\widetilde{\psi}_{i}\Bigl(x_{i}^{0},t_{i},\{t_{j}\}_{j\in\partial i},\mathcal{O}_{i},\bm{\lambda},F_{i},\mathbf{u}\Bigr)\prod_{j\in\partial i}m_{j\to i}(t_{j},t_{i})\,,

(52)

and

\phi_{(i,j)}^{\rm spread}=\log\sum_{t_{i},t_{j}}m_{i\to j}(t_{i},t_{j})\,m_{j\to i}(t_{j},t_{i})\,.

(53)

By the same argument, one can show that
\phi_{i}^{0}=\phi_{i}^{0,{\rm out}}=\phi_{i}^{0,{\rm spread}}
and thus

\phi_{\rm inter}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{0}\,,

(54)

in which

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{0}

\displaystyle=\log\sum_{x_{i}^{0}}\eta_{i}(x_{i}^{0})\,\nu_{i}(x_{i}^{0})

(55)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1}{Z_{i}^{\nu}}\sum_{x_{i}^{0}}\eta_{i}(x_{i}^{0})\sum_{t_{i},\bm{\mathbf{t_{k\in\partial i}}}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}(t_{i},\bm{\mathbf{t_{k\in\partial i}}},x_{i}^{0},\mathcal{O}_{i})\prod_{k\in\partial i}m_{k\rightarrow i}(t_{k},t_{i})\right)

(56)

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}^{\rm spread}-\log Z_{i}^{\nu}\,,

(57)

where we made the message
\nu_{i}
explicit. It is easy to see that in the total free entropy in Eq. (
47
), the first term in Eq. (
57
) cancels the first term in Eq. (
51
).

Finally, for the GLM part we have

\displaystyle\phi_{a}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\mathrm{d}u_{a}\,\psi_{a}(u_{a})\prod_{j}\chi_{j\rightarrow a}(u_{a})\right)

(58)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\mathrm{d}u_{a}\,\psi_{a}(u_{a})\prod_{j}\left(\frac{1}{Z_{j\rightarrow a}}\sum_{x_{j}^{0}}\nu_{j}(x_{j}^{0})\int\left(\prod_{b\neq a}{\rm d}u_{b}\right)\Psi_{j}^{\rm out}(x_{j}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{F_{j}}})\prod_{b\neq a}\bar{\chi}_{b\rightarrow j}(u_{b})\right)\right)

(59)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}\log Z_{j\rightarrow a}+\log Z_{a}\,,

(60)

and

\phi_{i}^{\rm out}=\log\left(\sum_{x_{i}^{0}}\nu_{i}(x_{i}^{0})\int\left(\prod_{b}{\rm d}u_{b}\right)\Psi_{i}^{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}})\prod_{b}\bar{\chi}_{b\rightarrow i}(u_{b})\right)\,,

(61)

while

\displaystyle\phi_{ia}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int\differential u_{a}\bar{\chi}_{a\rightarrow i}(u_{a})\chi_{i\rightarrow a}(u_{a})\right)

(62)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1}{Z_{i\rightarrow a}}\sum_{x_{i}^{0}}\nu_{i}(x_{i}^{0})\int\left(\prod_{b}{\rm d}u_{b}\right)\Psi_{i}^{\rm out}(x_{i}^{0},\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{F_{i}}})\prod_{b}\bar{\chi}_{b\rightarrow i}(u_{b})\right)

(63)

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}^{\rm out}-\log Z_{i\rightarrow a}\,.

(64)

Putting all together, we get

\phi_{\rm RS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log Z_{i}^{\nu}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\phi_{(i,j)}^{\rm spread}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{a=1}^{M}\log Z_{a}+\frac{1-M}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{\rm out}\,.

(65)

One can show, see for example
(
Duranthon and Zdeborová 2023
, Appendix B)
, that

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{M}\log Z_{a}=M

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{\rm out}+\sum_{a=1}^{M}\log\int\mathrm{d}u_{a}\,P^{u}(u_{a})\,\exp\Bigl[-\frac{A_{a}}{2}u_{a}^{2}+B_{a}u_{a}\Bigr]

(66)

\displaystyle+\sum_{a=1}^{M}\Bigl[\frac{A_{a}}{2}(a_{a}^{2}+v_{a})-B_{a}\,a_{a}\Bigr]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Bigl(\omega_{i}-\sum_{a=1}^{M}F_{ia}a_{a}\Bigr)^{2}}{2V_{i}}\,.

(67)

To conclude, the final formula for the RS free entropy reads

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm RS}=\frac{1}{N}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log Z_{i}^{\nu}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\phi_{(i,j)}^{\rm spread}+\sum_{a=1}^{M}\log\int\mathrm{d}u_{a}\,P^{u}(u_{a})\,\exp\Bigl[-\frac{A_{a}}{2}u_{a}^{2}+B_{a}u_{a}\Bigr]

(68)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{\rm out}+\sum_{a=1}^{M}\Bigl[\frac{A_{a}}{2}(a_{a}^{2}+v_{a})-B_{a}\,a_{a}\Bigr]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Bigl(\omega_{i}-\sum_{a=1}^{M}F_{ia}a_{a}\Bigr)^{2}}{2V_{i}}\,,

(69)

and it can be computed in linear time by using the BP messages and AMP variables in Algorithm
1
.

Finally, we discuss the calculation of the free entropy at the informative fixed point,
\phi_{\mathrm{info}}
. For the non-stochastic SI model (

\lambda\neq 1

\phi_{\mathrm{info}}

\phi_{\mathrm{info}}

C.3
Mean squared error

In the main text, we focused on the problem of retrieving the sources of the spreading, for convenience. However, using a Bayesian approach allows us to characterise the performance of our algorithm in very generic tasks, just by using the marginals estimated through the belief propagation and approximate message passing algorithms.
For example, it is interesting to look at how well the algorithm is able to recover the entire trajectory of each individual, which for
\lambda<1
, where the spreading is stochastic, is a problem strictly harder than source recovering. If we restrict to models in which there is a single transition time, like the SI and dSIR models we described in the main text, we can use as performance parameter the Squared Error between the ground truth transmission times
\bm{\mathbf{t^{*}}}\equiv\{t_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the ones estimated through the algorithm
\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}}\equiv\{\hat{t}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, defined as

\rm{SE}(\bm{\mathbf{t^{*}}},\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{t}_{i}-t_{i}^{*})^{2}\,.

(71)

As for the overlap estimator, we assume that the algorithm has additional information on the process through partial observations and feature covariates, so that the Bayes-optimal estimator uses the posterior probability distribution
P(\bm{\mathbf{t}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)
and evaluates the Mean Squared Error, defined as

\rm{MSE}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}})\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{t}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F}\left[\rm{SE}(\bm{\mathbf{t}},\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}})\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{t}}}P(\bm{\mathbf{t}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{t}_{i}-t_{i})^{2}\,.

(72)

In this case, in order to maximise this quantity over
\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}}
, one can show
Zdeborová and Krzakala 2016
that we have to consider the minimum mean-squared error estimator:

\hat{t}_{i}^{\rm{MMSE}}=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{t}}}t_{i}P(\bm{\mathbf{t}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)=\sum_{t_{i}}t_{i}P_{i}(t_{i}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)\quad\forall\,i\in[1:N]

(73)

where we have defined the marginal probability
P_{i}(t_{i}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)\equiv\sum_{\{t_{j}\}_{j\neq i}}P(\bm{\mathbf{t}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\mathcal{O}}},F)
. In this section, we will use as performance parameters the rescaled squared error

\rm{R}_{\rm SE}=\frac{\rm{SE}(\bm{\mathbf{t^{*}}},\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}}^{\rm RND})-\rm{SE}(\bm{\mathbf{t^{*}}},\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}}^{\rm MMSE})}{\rm{SE}(\bm{\mathbf{t^{*}}},\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}}^{\rm RND})}\,,

(74)

and the associated rescaled mean squared error

\rm{R}_{\rm MSE}=\frac{\rm{MSE}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}}^{\rm RND})-\rm{MSE}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}}^{\rm MMSE})}{\rm{MSE}(\bm{\mathbf{\hat{t}}}^{\rm RND})}\,,

(75)

where
\hat{t}_{i}^{\rm RND}=\sum_{t_{i}}P_{i}(\,t_{i}\mid\emptyset,F\,)t_{i}
is the random estimator.

Notice that these parameters are equal to zero if the MMSE estimator and the RND estimator have the same performance, and they are equal to one if the MMSE estimator recovers all the trajectories perfectly. As done for the overlap, we can probe the optimality of the algorithm by checking the Nishimori conditions, i.e. the difference between
\rm{R_{SE}}
and
\rm{R_{MSE}}
, since from the Nishimori conditions we know that they coincide on average in the Bayes-optimal setting.

C.4
Finite size study for Rademacher variables

In this section, we discuss the behaviour of the Nishimori conditions when considering Rademacher variables and values of
\rho
near the transition to perfect recovery.

In Fig.
12
, we consider the setting analysed in Sec.
VI
, looking at the SI model with
\lambda=1
, and fixing as an example
\alpha=10
and
\kappa=0
. We compute the values of the rescaled overlap and mean overlap as a function of
\rho
, looking at different sizes
N
. We see that the more we increase
N
, the more the transition gets sharp, signifying that the model presents strong finite size corrections even at moderately large sizes. In the inset plot we look at the difference between overlap and mean overlap, to investigate the Nishimori conditions. We see that taking
N
larger shrinks the region where the conditions are not satisfied, leading us to conjecture that in the large
N
limit, when the transition is very sharp, the Nishimori conditions are satisfied for all values of
\rho
.

C.5
Other graph ensembles

In the main text, we have fixed the ensemble of graphs in which the spreading happens to be Random Regular Graphs (RRGs) of degree
d=3
, for convenience. Here we present results for some other variants of locally tree-like random graphs, to show that the phenomenology we describe in the paper remains unchanged. Specifically, we change the degree of the graphs to
d=5
, and we compare the ensemble of RRGs to Erdos-Renyi (ER) graphs
Erdös and Rényi 1959
, where each couple of nodes is randomly connected with probability
p
. To make the two ensembles of graphs comparable, we set
p=\frac{d}{N-1}
, such that in both cases the average degree of the nodes is
d
. Moreover, if the graph is composed by multiple disconnected components, we consider only the biggest one and neglect the others (for
d=5
and
N
large enough, the largest component comprehends almost all the nodes with high probability).
