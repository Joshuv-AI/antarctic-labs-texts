Spike-and-Slab Posterior Sampling in High Dimensions
Syamantak Kumar, Purnamrita Sarkar, Kevin Tian, Yusong Zhu

Abstract
Posterior sampling with the spike-and-slab prior [ MB88 ] , a popular multimodal distribution used to model uncertainty in variable selection, is considered the theoretical gold standard method for Bayesian sparse linear regression [ CPS09 , Roc18 ] . However, designing provable algorithms for performing this sampling task is notoriously challenging. Existing posterior samplers for Bayesian sparse variable selection tasks either require strong assumptions about the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) [ YWJ16 ] , only work when the measurement count grows at least linearly in the dimension [ MW24 ] , or rely on heuristic approximations to the posterior. We give the first provable algorithms for spike-and-slab posterior sampling that apply for any SNR, and use a measurement count sublinear in the problem dimension. Concretely, assume we are given a measurement matrix 𝐗 ∈ ℝ n × d {\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d} and noisy observations 𝐲 = 𝐗 ​ 𝜽 ⋆ + 𝝃 {\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}+\boldsymbol{\xi} of a signal 𝜽 ⋆ \boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star} drawn from a spike-and-slab prior π \pi with a Gaussian diffuse density and expected sparsity k k , where 𝝃 ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 𝟎 n , σ 2 ​ 𝐈 n ) \boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{n},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}) . We give a polynomial-time high-accuracy sampler for the posterior π ( ⋅ ∣ 𝐗 , 𝐲 ) \pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}}) , for any SNR σ − 1 > 0 \sigma^{-1}>0 , as long as n ≥ k 3 ⋅ polylog ​ ( d ) n\geq k^{3}\cdot\textup{polylog}(d) and 𝐗 {\mathbf{X}} is drawn from a matrix ensemble satisfying the restricted isometry property. We further give a sampler that runs in near-linear time ≈ n ​ d \approx nd in the same setting, as long as n ≥ k 5 ⋅ polylog ​ ( d ) n\geq k^{5}\cdot\textup{polylog}(d) . To demonstrate the flexibility of our framework, we extend our result to spike-and-slab posterior sampling with Laplace diffuse densities, achieving similar guarantees when σ = O ⁡ ( 1 k ) \sigma=O(\frac{1}{k}) is bounded.

Introduction

Sparse linear regression is a fundamental, well-studied problem in high-dimensional statistics. In this problem, there is an unknown sparse signal
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that we are trying to estimate, and we are given a measurement matrix
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, as well as noisy observations

{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}+\boldsymbol{\xi},\text{ where }\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{n},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}).

(1)

This problem has been studied for decades by statisticians from the perspective of
variable selection
, see e.g.,
[
Hoc76
]
and references therein, where
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})
models the “important features” for a regression problem, and potentially
|\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})|\ll d
. More recently, due to the increasing dimensionality of data in modern statistics and learning tasks, there has been significant interest in understanding the
computational complexity
of sparse linear regression.

One major algorithmic success story in sparse linear regression is a line of work building off breakthrough results of
[
CR05
,
CT05
,
Don06
,
CT06
,
CRT06
]
for compressed sensing, which we refer to collectively as “sparse recovery” methods (some of which are reviewed in Section
2.3
). These algorithms take a frequentist viewpoint of (
1
), and aim to return an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
such that
\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\rVert
for an appropriate norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
, e.g.,
\ell_{2}
or
\ell_{\infty}
, often via penalized maximum likelihood methods such as the Lasso
[
Tib96
]
. Impressively, despite the nonconvexity of the problem (i.e., modeling the constraint that
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
is sparse), the aforementioned line of work showed that as long as
{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies the
restricted isometry property
(RIP, cf. Definition
1
), a sparse vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
can be recovered up to a function of the noise level
\sigma
in polynomial time. In fact, sparse recovery is tractable even in the
sublinear measurement
regime
n\ll d
, as long as
n
is sufficiently larger than the sparsity
|\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})|
. This regime is natural for high-dimensional sparse linear regression, because its underconstrained nature necessitates making a structural assumption on the signal
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
, e.g., sparsity.

A potential shortcoming of sparse recovery methods is their inability to model uncertainty in variable selection. In particular, these frequentist algorithms commit to a single estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and do not return multiple plausible candidate supports. This issue is particularly manifest in the setting of moderate signal-to-noise ratios, where some coordinates of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
have magnitude at or around the noise level
\sigma
. In such settings, it is more appropriate to consider a distribution over estimates to reflect our uncertainty of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
. This motivates the problem of
Bayesian sparse linear regression
, the main problem studied in this paper. In this problem, for some prior distribution
\pi
that is mostly supported on sparse vectors, the task is to return a sample from the posterior distribution,

\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi\left(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}}\right)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(2)

[

In such regimes, the posterior distribution either collapses to a single candidate support (as all signal coordinates are above the noise level), or the posterior effectively reduces to the prior (as the noise overwhelms the signal). Both settings appear to be at odds with a major motivation behind Bayesian sparse linear regression, i.e., modeling partial uncertainty in variable selection. Conversely, while

\begin{gathered}\textit{Is there a polynomial-time spike-and-slab posterior sampler}\\
\textit{that uses $n=o(d)$ measurements and works for arbitrary SNRs $\sigma^{-1}>0$?}\end{gathered}

(4)

1.1
Our results

Gaussian prior.
Our primary contribution is to resolve (
4
) when
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a Gaussian, and the expected sparsity
k
is sufficiently small.
For simplicity in this introduction, we only state our results in the case where the measurement matrix
{\mathbf{X}}
has i.i.d. Gaussian entries. Our formal theorem statements extend the statements here broadly to any
{\mathbf{X}}
drawn from any commonly-used RIP matrix ensemble (cf. Section
2.3

{\mathbf{X}}
is a subsampled Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) matrix.

Theorem 1

(informal, see Theorem
3

Let

{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
has i.i.d. entries

,
\delta\in(0,1)

{\mathbf{q}}\in[0,1]^{d}

\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
in (
2
).

1.

If

, there is an algorithm that returns
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi^{\prime}
such that
D_{\textup{TV}}(\pi^{\prime},\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{X}}))\leq\delta
with probability
\geq 1-\delta

,

, and

If

\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi^{\prime}
such that
D_{\textup{TV}}(\pi^{\prime},\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{X}}))\leq\delta

\geq 1-\delta
over the randomness of
{\mathbf{X}}

We briefly remark on the form of Theorem
1
. There are two sources of failure: the random model instance
({\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star},\boldsymbol{\xi})
, and the inaccuracy of our sampler itself. With positive probability, the model will produce an instance with an intractable posterior sampling task (e.g., if
{\mathbf{X}}
is not RIP or
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
is not sparse). Assuming the success of the model, our sampler gives high-accuracy convergence guarantees to the true posterior (achieving total variation
\delta
with a
\textup{polylog}(\frac{1}{\delta})

Our required sample complexity, or the number of random measurements

in Theorem

, scales sublinearly in

for sufficiently small

and results in the sparse recovery literature, which also estimate

(via optimization instead of sampling) from

4

n\approx k
observations. However, this would likely require fundamentally new techniques, which we leave as an exciting open question.

1.2
Overview of approach

In this section, we overview the pieces of our algorithm and its analysis. A straightforward calculation based on Bayes’ theorem (see Lemmas
8
and
10
) shows that when
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in (
3
), the posterior distribution (
2
) is a mixture of Gaussians:

\begin{gathered}\pi\left(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}w(S)\,\mathcal{N}\left({\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}}_{S},{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}\right),\\
\text{where }w(S)\propto\left(\prod_{i\in S}\frac{{\mathbf{q}}_{i}}{1-{\mathbf{q}}_{i}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det{\mathbf{A}}_{S}}},\\
{\mathbf{A}}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times S}:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right]_{S\times S}+\mathbf{I}_{S},\;{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}},\end{gathered}

(5)

and
w
forms a distribution over
\{S\mid S\subseteq[d]\}
. The key challenge is that, even if one can establish that
\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
is mostly supported on
\approx k
-sparse subsets
S
(where RIP holds and estimation is easy), there are still
\approx d^{k}
candidate small subsets to enumerate over. Thus, a natural starting point is to correct an easier-to-sample proposal distribution that approximates (
5
).

Approximating with a product mixture.
Our approach is inspired by first-order frequentist methods for solving sparse linear regression (
1
), when
{\mathbf{X}}
is RIP. This condition means that for bounded-size subsets
S\subseteq[d]
, we have
[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S}\approx\mathbf{I}_{S}
is an approximate isometry, so
{\mathbf{A}}_{S}
in (
5
) is close to a multiple of the identity (see Definition
1
for a formal statement). Many sparse recovery methods simulate well-conditioned gradient descent, a method for optimizing convex functions, on the naturally nonconvex sparse linear regression objective by enforcing sparsity of iterates
[
BD09
,
NT09
]
or by projecting onto convex proxy sets for sparsity
[
ANW10
]
. Thus, a natural approach to sampling from (
2
), at least restricted to components
S\subseteq[d]
with small
|S|
, is to approximate each corresponding Gaussian in (
5
) with an isotropic Gaussian (i.e., a product density).

Fortunately, there is precedent: for the closely-related problem of sampling from the
sparse mean model
, where we receive observations of the form (
1
) but
{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
,
[
CVDV12
]
provided a simple dynamic programming-based algorithm for drawing a candidate subset
S\sim w
. The key property enabling the
[
CVDV12
]
algorithm is that both the prior and mixture components are product distributions. Thus, our starting point is to approximate (
1
) with a sparse mean model.

Denoising the observations.
One candidate for simulating the sparse mean model is

{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}+{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\approx\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}+{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi},

where the above approximation holds due to RIP, assuming the signal
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
is sparse. At least for the natural setting of small
\sigma
, this calculation suggests
{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}
(which we can recover from our observations) is a good estimate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
. Unfortunately, the coordinates of
{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}
are highly non-uniform in magnitude, due to randomness inherent in the diffuse density
\mu
. This causes difficulties when setting up a rejection sampling scheme for sampling from
w
in (
5
), because the estimate

\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}\right)\approx\exp\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2(1+\sigma^{2})}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right),\text{ where }{\mathbf{b}}:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}

does not hold, even though
{\mathbf{A}}_{S}\approx(\frac{1+\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}})\mathbf{I}_{S}
is true for small
S
. This is simply because the scale of
{\mathbf{b}}_{S}
is too large, due to the aforementioned nonuniformity in
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\sim\pi
.

We make the crucial observation that we can “denoise” the proportionality constants in (
5
), using a vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that meets a certain criterion, as follows. Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
have
T:=\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, and let
T\subseteq S,S^{\prime}\subseteq[d]
. Then, a straightforward calculation (Lemma
5
) shows that

\frac{\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)}=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}_{S^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)},\text{ where }{\mathbf{z}}:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

(6)

Thus, as long as almost all high-weight
S
in (
5
) are supersets of some
T\subseteq[d]
, we have freedom in choosing
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
supported in
T
, to shrink the scale of our proportionality constants using (
6
).

Constructing a posterior estimator.
The next step in our plan is to construct an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
, for which we can show
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\supseteq\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
with high probability over
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
.

We propose to use
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
obtained using sparse recovery with
\ell_{\infty}
guarantees. To draw a connection between
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\approx\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\approx\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
, we extend a powerful result of
[
JAD+21
]
(Proposition
4
) that characterizes posterior sampling as an estimation tool.
Specifically, it shows that posterior sampling achieves estimation guarantees within a constant factor of
any other estimator
in any metric, that is learned from noisy measurements of a signal. Due to the existence of sparse recovery guarantees in
\ell_{\infty}
, we show that thresholding the coordinates of a frequentist estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
yields a sufficient
T
for our framework. This can be intuitively viewed as learning the “obvious” coordinates of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
above the noise level
\sigma
, which almost all posterior samples should also contain.

After applying this denoising, we are able to show that our new proportionality constants in (
6
) are much more closely approximated by a sparse mean model, for small
S\subseteq[d]
. However, we are left to prove that
\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
is mainly supported on small
S\subseteq[d]
. While this is an intuitive property (as the prior is sparse), we were previously only aware of proofs in specific cases. For example,
[
CSHVdV15
]
showed sparsity of the spike-and-slab posterior when
\mu=\mathrm{Lap}(0,1)
, but their proof technique required the negative log-density to be a norm, and hence did not apply to
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. By a very simple application of Proposition
4
(cf. Corollary
1
), we prove such a sparsity concentration result for all densities
\mu
, which is potentially of independent interest.

Our final sampler first learns a frequentist estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with large coordinates
T
, and draws
S\subseteq[d]
from a product distribution restricted to
S\supseteq T
and
|S|\lesssim k
. It then applies a denoised rejection sampling step to correct this proposal. Our proposal distribution is sampled via
conditional Poisson sampling
(Lemma
4
), based on a dynamic programming strategy. Interestingly, the product distribution inducing our proposal is actually not concentrated on small subsets, so it only approximates our posterior (
5
) over a high-probability region of
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
where
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
.

Intuitively, our algorithm reflects uncertainty in the denoised sparse linear regression problem: we do not know the location of the remaining small signal coordinates, but we know they are few.

Extending to a Laplace prior.
We now illustrate how to extend our findings to the Laplace prior setting (Theorem
2
). Unlike the Gaussian case, the
\ell_{1}
norm arising from the Laplace prior precludes a closed-form expression for the density over the posterior support. Consequently, we rely on the triangle inequality to perform approximations, and subsequently establish in Lemma
13
that for noise level
\sigma=O\bigl(\frac{1}{k}\bigr)
, it is still possible to construct an approximate density that is both efficient to sample from and closely approximates the true posterior. A further challenge in designing an efficient algorithm is computing the rejection ratio, again due to the lack of a closed-form expression for the posterior density. To overcome this, we use an annealing-based algorithm (Proposition
6
) combined with an approximate sampler for composite densities developed in
[
LST21
]
to estimate normalizing constants in polynomial time, enabling us to bypass the need for an exact closed-form density while still ensuring efficient and accurate sampling from the posterior.

1.3
Related work

Over the years, there has been a huge amount of work on high dimensional sparse regression in both the Bayesian and frequentist communities
[
MB88
,
GM93
,
EHJT04
,
Wai09
,
BP20
,
MW24
]
.
Under the Bayesian paradigm, some works focus on finding the mode of the posterior distribution in (
2
)
[
RG14
,
RG18
]
, but such an estimate ignores the uncertainty of the posterior. Directly sampling from the posterior, on the other hand, could address such drawbacks. While there is an active line of research in this direction
[
BCM16
,
NH19
,
JB20
]
, computationally efficient algorithms with rigorous theoretical guarantees have been few, due to the key challenge that the posterior resulting from the spike-and-slab prior is high-dimensional and may be very far from unimodal.

MCMC-based methods.
One line of work on posterior sampling exploits MCMC-based methods
[
BC09
,
RBR10
,
SFCM15
,
YWJ16
]
.
Motivated by the Bernstein-von Mises theorem,
[
BC09
]
assume that the posterior is close to a normal density and design a sampler based on this assumption. Closer in spirit to our work,
[
YWJ16
]
assumes their measurement matrix
{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies a condition similar to RIP, and shows that the posterior on the set of “influential coordinates” (i.e., those larger than the noise level) is unimodal, if the SNR is very high or very low. However, their work does not capture a broad range of intermediate SNRs (see discussion after Eq. (10)), for which the posterior can display multimodal behavior. Moreover, Theorem 6 of
[
CSHVdV15
]
shows that under some mild conditions, the posterior converges to a
mixture of Gaussians
, and such a mixture will collapse into a single Gaussian only if the signal dominates the noise. Such a result uncovers a limitation of current MCMC-based methods: their conclusions are based on the premise that posterior is approximately a unimodal distribution, which reduces the difficulty of mixing time analyses.

Finally, recent work
[
BH24
]
studies the use of diffusion methods for posterior sampling in linear inverse problems. We think this is an interesting approach that merits further investigation, e.g., for our specific case of spike-and-slab posterior sampling. However, the aforementioned result is not analyzed in discrete time, and its techniques do not readily apply to our setting; in particular, their parameter
\chi_{t}(\pi)
in Eq. (14) is unbounded for the spike-and-slab prior
\pi
.

Approximating the posterior.
Another line of work on posterior sampling uses variational Bayesian methods, which obtain the closest approximation of a complex posterior distribution from a more tractable family
[
JGJS99
,
WJ08
,
BKM17
]
. The most popular approximating family consists of product distributions, and the corresponding approximation is known as a naïve mean-field approximation. For example,
[
RS22
]
and
[
MS22
]
show that the mean-field approximation has strong theoretical properties. However, the mean-field optimization problem has been shown to be highly nonconvex with spurious local optima in some simple learning settings
[
MS22
]
. Moreover,
[
GJM18
]
show the failure of naïve mean-field for a simple high-dimensional problem. In some sense, our paper also obtains a variational approximation by sampling from an appropriate product distribution. However, instead of choosing the “best” candidate (requiring nonconvex optimization), we choose one guided by fine approximations of the true posterior and the RIP condition.

Moderate dimension.
Recently,
[
MW24
]
gave a computationally-efficient posterior sampler in a setting where the posterior can be highly multimodal. Their algorithm is motivated by decompositions in statistical physics, and introduces a latent variable that is easily sampleable and induces the correct marginal distribution on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. However,
[
MW24
]
assume that their design matrix
{\mathbf{X}}
has a number of linear measurements which grows at least linearly in the feature dimension
d
. This can be a costly assumption in practical high-dimensional settings.

In our paper, we remove the aforementioned constraints, i.e., we operate in the
high-dimensional regime
of
n=o(d)
and under a range of SNRs
\sigma^{-1}
where the posterior is potentially
multimodal
.

2.2
Spike-and-slab posterior sampling

In this section, we formally define our problem. We begin by defining the
spike-and-slab
density, originally introduced by
[
MB88
]
. The spike-and-slab density is a simple product density that is parameterized by
{\mathbf{q}}\in[0,1]^{d}
and a
diffuse density

\mu
over
\mathbb{R}
, given as follows:

\pi:=\bigotimes_{i\in[d]}\left((1-{\mathbf{q}}_{i})\delta_{0}+{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\mu\right).

(7)

In other words, the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi
is nonzero with probability
{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
, for all
i\in[d]
. Finally, if
\mu
is unspecified we assume
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian.

There is an alternative characterization of the distribution in (
7
) which follows by first describing the distribution
\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}
of the support of
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi
, and then sampling
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from the conditional distribution (i.e., via Bayes’ theorem). We describe this alternative characterization as follows.

1.

First, a support
S\subseteq[d]
is sampled from
\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}
, where

\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}(S)=\left(\prod_{i\in S}{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\right)\left(\prod_{i\in S^{c}}(1-{\mathbf{q}}_{i})\right)\text{ for all }S\subseteq[d].

(8)

2.

Second,
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}_{S^{c}}
is set to
\mathbf{0}_{S^{c}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}_{S}
is sampled from

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}_{S}\sim\mu^{\otimes S}.

(9)

We now define our posterior sampling problem.

Model 1

(Spike-and-slab posterior sampling)
.

Let
{\mathbf{q}}\in[0,1]^{d}
,
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
k=\left\lVert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rVert_{1}
, and
\sigma>0
be known, and let
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Let
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a known measurement matrix, and suppose that we observe
({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
where, following the notation (
7
),
{\mathbf{y}}
is generated via

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\sim\pi,\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}),\quad{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}+\boldsymbol{\xi},

(10)

and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
are independent. Our goal is to sample from the posterior
\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},y)
.

That is,
{\mathbf{y}}
in (
10
) follows a standard noisy linear model, so our posterior task is a Bayesian linear regression problem with spike-and-slab prior (
7
). In light of our earlier viewpoint (
8
), (
9
) of sampling from
\pi
, there is similarly a two-stage process that characterizes sampling
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
. We now provide an explicit description of this process in the case when
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

1.

First, a support
S\subseteq[d]
is sampled from
\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
,
where

\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}(S\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})\propto\frac{(\prod_{i\in S}{\mathbf{q}}_{i})(\prod_{i\in S^{c}}(1-{\mathbf{q}}_{i}))}{(2\pi)^{\frac{|S|}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{S}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\textup{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S},

(11)

and the integral extends each
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
to
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
via padding by zeroes.

2.

Second,
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sampled from
\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}},S)
, where

\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}},S)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\subseteq S}.

(12)

In (
11
), we used the following standard calculation to compute the normalizing constants of the relevant multivariate Gaussian densities, which will be used several times throughout.

Fact 1

(Gaussian normalization)
.

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{S}_{\succeq\mathbf{0}}^{d\times d}
be non-singular and let
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,

\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)\textup{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}=(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{2}\right)\det(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(13)

2.3
Sparse recovery

In this section, we state several preliminaries on noisy sparse recovery in the following model. In particular, Model
2
differs from Model
1
primarily in that

is not assumed to be drawn from a fixed prior distribution, but is instead a “worst-case” sparse signal to be estimated. We defer all proofs from this section to Appendix

Model 2

(Sparse recovery)
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in[1,d]
, and
\sigma>0
be known. Let
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a known measurement matrix, and let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy
\mathrm{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})\leq k
. Suppose we observe
({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
where

{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}+\boldsymbol{\xi},\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0}_{n},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}).

Our goal is to output an estimate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}
.

For a survey of techniques and known results in sparse recovery, we refer the reader to Chapter 7 of
[
Wai19
]
and references therein. We provide two recovery results under Model
2
under assumptions on the pair
({\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\xi})
. To state our assumptions, we require the following standard definition.

Definition 1

(Restricted isometry property)
.

We say
{\mathbf{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
satisfies the
(\epsilon,s)
-restricted isometry property, or
{\mathbf{X}}
is
(\epsilon,s)
-
\mathrm{RIP}

\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
\mathrm{nnz}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq s

(1-\epsilon)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\epsilon)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

An equivalent condition is that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}([{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S})\in[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]^{s}
for all
S\subseteq[d]
with
|S|\leq s
.

Intuitively, RIP implies
{\mathbf{X}}
acts as an
approximate isometry when restricted to sparse vectors, so
[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S}
is well-conditioned for any sparse support
S
.
Various random matrix ensembles have been shown to satisfy Definition
1
; we recall two such standard constructions below.

Proposition 1

(Theorem 9.2,
[
FR13
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}
be an

{\mathbf{M}}
are independent mean-zero sub-Gaussian random variables with variance
1
.
There exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
s\in[d]
,
{\mathbf{X}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbf{M}}
is
(\epsilon,s)
-RIP with probability
\geq 1-\delta
if

n\geq C\cdot\frac{s\ln\frac{d}{s}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

Proposition 2

(Theorem 4.5,
[
HR17
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d\times d}
be a discrete Fourier matrix whose elements are given by

{\mathbf{M}}_{jk}=d^{-1/2}\cdot\exp(2\pi\iota\cdot\frac{jk}{d})
where
\iota:=\sqrt{-1}
. Let
{\mathbf{X}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times d}
be a matrix whose

rows are chosen uniformly and independently from the rows of
{\mathbf{M}}
multiplied by
\sqrt{d/n}
.
There exists a constant

such that for any

{\mathbf{X}}
is
(\epsilon,s)
-RIP with probability

For more constructions of RIP matrix ensembles, including sampling bounded orthonormal systems and real trignometric polynomials, we refer the reader to Chapter 12 of
[

2.4
Performance of posterior sampling via estimation

We next introduce a key technical tool from that shows posterior sampling performs nearly as well at estimation as
any other procedure
, in a precise sense. To our knowledge, this fact was first stated in
[
JAD+21
]
. We use this in several places to prove properties of our posterior distributions. Here, we state a slight generalization of the result from
[
JAD+21
]
and defer a proof to Appendix
B.1
.

Proposition 4

(Generalization of Theorem 3.4,
[
JAD+21
]
)
.

Let
\mathrm{m}(\cdot,\cdot)
be an arbitrary metric over
{\mathcal{K}}\times{\mathcal{K}}
and suppose
\mu
is a distribution over
{\mathcal{K}}
. Let
\theta^{\star}\sim\mu
, let
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{K}}\to\Omega
be an arbitrary (possibly randomized) forward operator, and let
\varphi={\mathcal{F}}(\theta^{\star})
. Suppose there is any (possibly randomized) algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}:\Omega\to{\mathcal{K}}
such that for
\epsilon>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{m}(\widehat{\theta},\theta^{\star})>\epsilon\right]\leq\delta,\text{ for }\widehat{\theta}\sim\mathcal{A}(\varphi),

(14)

where probabilities are taken over the joint distribution of
(\theta^{\star},\varphi,\widehat{\theta})
.
Then, letting
\theta\sim\mu(\cdot\mid\varphi)
, i.e., the posterior distribution of
\theta^{\star}
given observations
\varphi
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{m}(\theta,\theta^{\star})>2\epsilon\right]\leq 2\delta.

To demonstrate the power of Proposition
4
, we prove a support size concentration result for spike-and-slab posteriors. A similar result was shown in Theorem 1,
[
CSHVdV15
]
, which did not give a quantitative convergence rate. Moreover, the strategy in
[
CSHVdV15
]
is somewhat more ad hoc, as it was specialized to Laplace priors (for example, it relied on the negative log-density obeying the triangle inequality, which does not hold for e.g., the Gaussian prior). In contrast, we prove a non-asymptotic result for arbitrary product priors, using an arguably simpler proof.

Corollary 1

(Sparsity of posterior)
.

In the setting of Model
1
, let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
. Then

{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left|\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right|\leq 6\left(k+\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)\right)\right]\geq 1-\delta,\text{ for all }\delta\in(0,1),

(15)

Moreover, (
15
) holds for any choice of
\mu
in Model
1
.

Proof.

We apply Proposition
4
to the distribution
\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
defined in (
8
), (
11
). Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be the set of subsets of
[d]
, and let
\varphi=({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
(so
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
). Moreover, let
\mathrm{m}(S_{1},S_{2})
be the Hamming distance between
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq[d]
, i.e.,

\mathrm{m}(S_{1},S_{2})=\left|\left\{i\in[d]\mid\left(i\in S_{1}\wedge i\not\in S_{2}\right)\vee\left(i\in S_{2}\wedge i\not\in S_{1}\right)\right\}\right|.

It is well-known that
\mathrm{m}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a metric on
{\mathcal{K}}
. We let
\mathcal{A}(\varphi)
always return the empty set
\emptyset
. By a Chernoff bound, letting
S^{\star}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}
, since
|S^{\star}|
is the sum of variables
\sim\textup{Bern}({\mathbf{q}}_{i})
for all
i\in[d]
,

\mathbb{P}\left[|S^{\star}|\geq 2\left(k+\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)\right)\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{m}(S^{\star},\mathcal{A}(\varphi))\geq 2\left(k+\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)\right)\right]\leq\frac{\delta}{3}.

Thus, (
14
) holds with
\epsilon=2(k+\log(\frac{3}{\delta}))
so that, for
S\sim\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{m}\left(S,S^{\star}\right)\geq 4\left(k+\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)\right)\right]\leq\frac{2\delta}{3}.

Finally, by the triangle inequality on
\mathrm{m}(\cdot,\cdot)
,

|S|=\mathrm{m}(S,{\mathcal{A}}(\varphi))\leq\mathrm{m}(S,S^{\star})+\mathrm{m}(S^{\star},{\mathcal{A}}(\varphi))=\mathrm{m}(S,S^{\star})+|S^{\star}|.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[|S|\leq 6\left(k+\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left[|S^{\star}|+m(S,S^{\star})\leq 6\left(k+\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left[|S^{\star}|\leq 2\left(k+\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)\right)\land m(S,S^{\star})\leq 4\left(k+\log\left(\frac{3}{\delta}\right)\right)\right].

The conclusion follows from a union bound over the above two events.
∎

Proposition
4
’s conclusion holds over the randomness of both
(\theta^{\star},\varphi)
(collectively, defining the “model” we are given in the posterior sampling problem) and the sample
\theta\sim\mu(\cdot\mid\varphi)
. This guarantee is not quite compatible with our applications, as we would only like to reason about the failure probability of our sampler for a given posterior distribution, holding the model fixed.

We give a simple reduction showing that an event which holds with very high probability over the randomness of
(\theta^{\star},\varphi,\theta)
also holds with high probability over
\theta\sim\mu(\cdot\mid\varphi)
, for “most” models
(\theta^{\star},\varphi)
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)
be random variables, and let
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\beta}
be an event that depends on their realizations. Suppose for some
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
that
\mathbb{P}_{(\alpha,\beta)}[\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\beta}]\geq 1-\delta_{1}\delta_{2}
. Then, letting
g(\alpha):=\mathbb{P}_{\beta}\left[\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\beta}\mid\alpha\right]
,

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}[g(\alpha)\leq 1-\delta_{1}]\leq\delta_{2}.

Our final theorem statements use Lemma
2
by applying it with
\alpha\leftarrow(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star},\boldsymbol{\xi})
(i.e., the randomness used to generate observations
{\mathbf{y}}
in Model
1
), and
\beta\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mu(\cdot\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})
. We refer to this usage by qualifying that statements hold “with probability
\geq 1-\delta_{1}
over the randomness of Model
1
.”

Our posterior sampler is based on

Lemma 3
.

Let
\pi
,

be distributions over the same domain

and
\mu\propto Q
for unnormalized densities
P,Q
. Moreover, suppose that for all

\displaystyle\frac{P(\omega)}{Q(\omega)}\leq C,\;\frac{Q(\omega)}{P(\omega)}\leq C.

There is an algorithm

that, for any

, outputs a sample within total variation distance

from

. The algorithm uses
O(C^{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta}))

\frac{P(\omega)}{Q(\omega)}

O(C^{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta}))
times.

Spike-and-slab posterior sampling with Gaussian prior

In this section, we give our posterior sampler under Model

Lemma 5

In the setting of Model

Proof.

First, observe that since

\left[{\mathbf{A}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right]_{S}=\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}+\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right]_{S}={\mathbf{A}}_{S}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S},

and similarly
[{\mathbf{A}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]_{S^{\prime}}={\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S^{\prime}}
. The result follows by directly expanding:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}_{S^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{S}{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}}_{S}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{A}}_{S}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}}^{2}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S^{\prime}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}}_{S^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}}^{2}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S}^{\top}{\mathbf{b}}_{S}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S^{\prime}}^{\top}{\mathbf{b}}_{S^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{S}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S}-\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S^{\prime}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}}^{2},

where in the last equality, we used our support set assumption.
∎

To explain how Lemma
5
is used, a direct calculation (combining (

) with Fact
1
, see Lemma

) shows that up to a term depending only on
{\mathbf{q}}_{S}
, we have

\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}(S\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}})\propto\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}_{S}\right\rVert_{{\mathbf{A}}_{S}^{-1}}^{2}\right).

We would like to sample from this distribution using rejection sampling (Lemma

) on top of a product distribution. However, some coordinates of

We instead use Lemma

with an estimated “hint” vector

that has two properties:

In Section

. To obtain the result using the
\ell_{2}
estimator, we first set

m=O(k^{\star}\log(d))

which is enough to guarantee the precondition of Theorem
3
on
\sqrt{m/n}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}_{[m]:}
holds, by Proposition
1
. We substitute this into our choice of
\epsilon
and obtain our bound on

, again through Proposition
1
. In both cases, we simplified by dropping all non-dominant runtime terms.
∎

[

]
(see also
[

4.1
Annealing

In this section, we consider the following computational task, parameterized by an approximation tolerance
\Delta\in(0,1)
, a failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, a matrix
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\succeq\mathbf{0}}^{k\times k}
satisfying
\mu\mathbf{I}_{k}\preceq{\mathbf{A}}\preceq L\mathbf{I}_{k}
, and a vector
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Our goal is to produce an estimate
\widetilde{Z}
, such that with probability
\geq 1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\int\exp\left(-f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)\textup{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\in\left[(1-\Delta)\widetilde{Z},(1+\Delta)\widetilde{Z}\right],

(30)

\displaystyle\text{where }f\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}.

This general question was studied by
[
GLL20
]
, who gave the following framework based on an annealing scheme, patterned in part off of prior work by
[
LV06
,
CV18
,
BDM18
]
.

Proposition 6

(Section 3, Appendix B,
[
GLL20
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\mu
-strongly convex, let
Z:=\int\exp(-f(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\textup{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and fix an approximation tolerance
\Delta\in(0,1)
and a failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
. Let
\sigma_{1}>0
be a parameter satisfying

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\leq\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\exp\left(-f_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\leq\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta},

(31)

where, for a sequence of increasing
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i\in[M]}
specified in
[
GLL20
]
, we let

f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=f(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}},\text{ for all }i\in[M].

Further, let

M=\Theta\left(\sqrt{k}\log\left(\frac{k}{\mu\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)\right),\;N=\Theta\left(\frac{M^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right),

for appropriate constants. Finally, let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm that takes as input
i\in[M]
, and produces a sample within total variation distance
\frac{1}{8N}
from the density on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
that is
\propto\exp(-f_{i})
. There is an algorithm that queries
\mathcal{A}

O(N\log(\frac{1}{\delta}))
times, and produces an estimate
\widetilde{Z}
satisfying

Z\in\left[(1-\Delta)\widetilde{Z},(1+\Delta)\widetilde{Z}\right],\text{ with probability }\geq 1-\delta.

Proof.

This is almost the exact derivation carried out in
[
GLL20
]
, except for two differences. First, they assume that the relevant negative log-density
f
is
L
-smooth in addition to being strongly convex. However, one can check that the only place this assumption is used in their analysis is in Lemma 3.1 to choose
\sigma_{1}
satisfying (
31
), which we replace with by isolating the sufficient condition (
31
). Second, the
[
GLL20
]
result is stated for an algorithm which produces a correct estimate
Z
with probability
\geq\frac{3}{4}
, using exactly
N
calls to
\mathcal{A}
. However, by repeating
\log(\frac{1}{\delta})
times and taking a median, Chernoff bounds show that we can boost the failure probability as stated.
∎

We next provide a sufficient choice of
\sigma_{1}
that satisfies (
31
) in the setting of (
30
).

Lemma 12
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\succeq\mathbf{0}}^{k\times k}
, let
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq R
, let
\Delta\in(0,1)
, and let

\displaystyle\lambda\geq\frac{8R\sqrt{k}}{\Delta}+\frac{192k}{\Delta^{2}}\left(k+\log\left(\frac{4}{\Delta}\right)\right).

(32)

Denoting
{\mathbf{M}}:={\mathbf{A}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)Z_{{\mathbf{M}},{\mathbf{b}}}\leq\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\leq Z_{{\mathbf{M}},{\mathbf{b}}}

(33)

where
Z_{{\mathbf{M}},{\mathbf{b}}}:=\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Proof.

The right-hand side of (
33
) is immediate from
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}\geq 0
for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. In the rest of the proof, we prove the left-hand side of (
33
). Define the set
\Omega:=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}:\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\Delta}{4\sqrt{k}}\}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\sup_{\Omega}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}\right)\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta}{4}\right)\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta},

(34)

where the last line used
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{k}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}
. Moreover, we claim that

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{M}}^{-1})}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Omega\right]\geq 1-\frac{\Delta}{4},

(35)

which completes the proof when combined with (
34
), since

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta}{4}\right)\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{4}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta}{4}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)\textup{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\geq\left(1-\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)Z_{{\mathbf{M}},{\mathbf{b}}},

as
\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the unnormalized density corresponding to
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{M}}^{-1})
. We now prove (
35
). First, observe that for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{M}}^{-1})
, we can write

\boldsymbol{\theta}={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}}+{\mathbf{M}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{z}},\text{ where }{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{k},\mathbf{I}_{k}).

Since
{\mathbf{M}}\succeq\lambda\mathbf{I}_{k}
, we have

\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{R}{\lambda}\leq\frac{\Delta}{8\sqrt{k}},\;\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{z}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda}},

for our range of
\lambda
. Returning to (
35
), this implies the desired

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{b}},{\mathbf{M}}^{-1})}\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}\not\in\Omega\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{k},\mathbf{I}_{k})}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{z}}\right\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{\Delta}{8\sqrt{k}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{k},\mathbf{I}_{k})}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}\right\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{\Delta\sqrt{\lambda}}{8\sqrt{k}}\right]\leq\frac{\Delta}{4},

where we used Fact
3
with our choice of
\lambda
.
∎

To facilitate our annealing scheme, we use a sampler from
[
LST21
]
for composite densities.

Proposition 7

(Corollary 2,
[
LST21
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
be
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex, let
\kappa:=\frac{L}{\mu}
, and let
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
have the form
g({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{i\in[k]}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})
for scalar functions
\{g_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{i\in[k]}
. There is an algorithm which runs in
O(\kappa k\log^{3}(\frac{\kappa k}{\epsilon}))
iterations, each querying
\nabla f
and performing
O(k)
additional work, and obtains
\epsilon
total variation distance to the density over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\pi\propto\exp(-f-g)
.

Proof.

This is almost the statement of Corollary 2 in
[
LST21
]
; we briefly justify the differences here. First, if
g
is coordinatewise-separable, then the RGO access required by
[
LST21
]
takes
O(k)
time, under our assumption of
O(1)
-time integration and sampling over
\mathbb{R}
. Next, Corollary 2 in
[
LST21
]
requires access to the minimizer of
f+g
, but the tolerance of the algorithm to inexactness is discussed in Appendix A of that paper, and it is justified why the cost of computing an approximate minimizer for composite functions does not dominate the sampler’s gradient complexity. Finally, the runtime in
[
LST21
]
is in expectation, but can be bounded with probability
1-O(\delta)
by using standard Chernoff bounds over the complexity of rejection sampling steps. The failure probability of the runtime being bounded can be charged to the total variation distance, via a union bound.
∎

We put together the pieces to obtain our desired estimator for normalizing constants.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose that
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\succeq\mathbf{0}}^{k\times k}
satisfies
\mu\mathbf{I}_{k}\preceq{\mathbf{A}}\preceq L\mathbf{I}_{k}
, and that
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
satisfies
\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq R
. Fix an approximation tolerance
\Delta\in(0,1)
and a failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
. There is an algorithm for computing
\widetilde{Z}
satisfying (
30
), with probability
\geq 1-\delta
, using time

O\left(\frac{Lk^{4}}{\mu\Delta^{2}}\log^{4}\left(\frac{LRk}{\mu\Delta}\right)\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

Proof.

We instantiate Proposition
6
with
\sigma_{1}^{-2}\leftarrow\lambda
satisfying (
32
), and
\mathcal{A}\leftarrow
the sampler from Proposition
7
. In particular, for any
i\in[M]
, the density
\propto\exp(-f_{i})
has the structure required by Proposition
7
, where in the statement of Proposition
7
, we set

f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},\;g(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leftarrow\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}.

Observe that for any
i\in[M]
, the parameter
\kappa
in Proposition
7
satisfies

\kappa\leq\frac{L+\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}}{\mu+\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}}\leq\frac{L}{\mu}.

Thus, the time complexity of each call to
\mathcal{A}
is at most

O\left(\frac{Lk^{3}}{\mu}\log^{3}\left(\frac{NLk}{\mu}\right)\right),\text{ for }N=\Theta\left(\frac{k}{\Delta^{2}}\log\left(\frac{k}{\mu\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)\right)=\Theta\left(\frac{k}{\Delta^{2}}\log\left(\frac{kR}{\mu\Delta}\right)\right),

since the cost of computing
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is
O(k^{2})
time. The conclusion follows from Proposition
6
.
∎

Lemma 13
.

In the setting of Model

, take

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}\left(S\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}},S\in\Omega_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},k^{\star}}\right)

{\mathbf{X}}
is

Proof.

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{supp}}\left(S\mid{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{y}},S\in\Omega_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},k^{\star}}\right)

\alpha
-
\mathrm{MI}
over
S
for any
S\subseteq[d]
with
|S|\leq s
, and
\alpha=\sqrt{2s\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)}
.

Proof.

Fix
S\subseteq[d]
such that
|S|\leq k
, and fix
j\in S^{c}
. We define a projection operator:

\mathbf{P}_{S}:={\mathbf{X}}_{:S}[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]^{-1}_{S\times S}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}.

It is well known that
\mathbf{P}_{S}
is the projection matrix of the linear subspace:
\mathrm{span}\{{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid j\in S\}
.
Let
T:=S\cup{j}
. Since
{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies
(\epsilon,s+1)
-RIP, for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:T}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S}+{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=(1-\epsilon)(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}^{2}).

(42)

By the properties of projection operators, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{P}_{S}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S}){\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

(43)

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S}){\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S}+{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq 1-\epsilon,

(44)

where the inequality in (
44
) follows from (
42
) with
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}=1
and
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\geq 0
.
Inserting (
44
) into (
43
),

\left\lVert\mathbf{P}_{S}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-(1-\epsilon).

Next, let
{\mathbf{x}}_{j}:=[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
. Then applying RIP again, we have

\left\lVert\mathbf{P}_{S}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq(1-\epsilon)\left\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Thus, we have by combining the above two displays that

\left\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{1-\epsilon}-1.

Moreover, notice that
{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{S}
, so the above display implies

\left\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{s}\left\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{s}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{1-\epsilon}-1}.

Finally, by RIP applied with the
1
-sparse test vector
{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
,

\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\in[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]\implies\left\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2s\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}.

Because our choices of
S
and
j\in S^{c}
were arbitrary, we have the desired MI parameter bound.
∎

A.2
Solving the Lasso to high precision

Here, we consider the (Lagrangian) Lasso problem, a.k.a.
\ell_{1}
-penalized linear regression, defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1},

(45)

where
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
.
We provide a polynomial-time algorithm achieving
\ell_{\infty}
-norm convergence guarantees to the optimal solution. The reason this is nontrivial using off-the-shelf convex optimization tools, e.g., cutting-plane methods, is because (
45
) is not strongly convex, so function approximation guarantees do not transfer to distance bounds.

Throughout the section, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 4
.

The columns of
{\mathbf{X}}
are in general position.

We now restate a result from
[
Tib13
]
that shows Assumption
4
implies uniqueness of the Lasso solution (
45
), and characterizes the solution. The former fact is attributed to
[
OPT00
]
.

Lemma 16

(Lemma 4,
[
Tib13
]
)
.

Under Assumption
4
,
(
45
)
has a unique solution,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S}=[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S}^{-1}({\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}-\lambda{\mathbf{s}}),\;\;\;\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S^{C}}=\mathbf{0},\;\;\;{\mathbf{s}}:=\mathrm{sign}({\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}({\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})),

where
S:=\{i\in[d]\mid|{\mathbf{X}}_{i:}^{\top}({\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|=\lambda\}
.

We next derive a constrained dual problem for (
45
).

Lemma 17

(Lasso dual)
.

The dual problem for (
45
) is given as

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}=\argmin_{\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,1}\;\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{a}}\right)^{\top}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{a}}\right),\;\;{\mathbf{a}}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}.

(46)

Furthermore, if Assumption
4
holds, then (
46
) has a unique minimizer satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S}=[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S}^{-1}({\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}-\lambda\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{S}),\;\;\;\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{S^{c}}=\mathbf{0},\;\;\;\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}={\mathbf{a}}-\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the unique minimizer of (
45
) and
S=\left\{i\in[d]\mid|\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}|=1\right\}
.

Proof.

First, by the standard identity
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{1}=\max_{\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,1}{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, (
45
) is equivalently

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max_{\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,1}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}.

(47)

Note that the set
\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,1
is a compact set, and
f(\boldsymbol{\theta},z):=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
is convex-concave. Therefore, using Sion’s minimax theorem,
[
Sio58
]
, strong duality holds so (
47
) equals

\displaystyle\max_{\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,1}\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}.

(48)

Solving the inner minimization shows that for fixed
{\mathbf{z}}
, we should choose

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}-\lambda{\mathbf{z}}).

Substituting this back into (
48
) we have for the optimal solution,
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
, of the dual (
48
), that

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,1}\;-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{a}}\right)^{\top}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{a}}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}{\mathbf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\right){\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,1}\;-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{a}}\right)^{\top}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{a}}\right)=\argmin_{\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,1}\;\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{a}}\right)^{\top}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}\left({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{a}}\right).

This proves the first conclusion. For the second conclusion, since strong duality holds, by using KKT conditions, we have for primal and dual optimal solutions
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
respectively,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}-\lambda\hat{{\mathbf{z}}},\;\;\left\lVert\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1.

Note that
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
is fixed given
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Under Assumption
4
, we conclude uniqueness of
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
. Next, we have

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{\top}({\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\implies\max_{i\in[d]}|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{\top}({\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|\leq\lambda.

Let
S:=\left\{i\in[d]:|\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}|=1\right\}
, which by the above is consistent with the definition in Lemma
16
. We have the remaining conclusions due to Lemma
16
.
∎

Our strategy is now to show that restricted to
\mathrm{range}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top})
, the dual problem (
46
) is strongly convex, and consequently, we can suboptimality guarantees to distance bounds on the parameter
{\mathbf{z}}
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
satisfy Assumption
4
with
n\leq d
, with singular value decomposition

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{\Sigma}{\mathbf{V}}^{\top},{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\mathbf{\Sigma}=\textbf{{diag}}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)\text{ for }\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0},\;{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}.

Consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}=\argmin_{\left\lVert{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{w}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\;({\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{b}})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-2}({\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{b}}),\;\;{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{b}}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}.

(49)

Then,
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}={\mathbf{V}}\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}
, where
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
is the unique optimal dual solution as defined in Lemma
17
.

Proof.

By Lemma
17
, the optimal solution to (
46
) is of the form

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}={\mathbf{a}}-\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}({\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

where
{\mathbf{a}}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}
.
Therefore,
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}\in\mathrm{range}\left({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\right)
and
\exists{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}={\mathbf{V}}\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}
. Similarly,
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathrm{range}\left({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\right)
. Therefore,
\exists{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{b}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}
.

Thus, if we were to restrict the dual problem to solutions of the form

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{w}}

(50)

then we would restrict our constraint set to a subset of the original set in (
46
),
\left\{{\mathbf{z}}\mid\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}
. However, since
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}={\mathbf{V}}\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}
holds, this restriction does not affect the problem. The uniqueness of
\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}
implies the uniqueness of
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
. The conclusion of the lemma follows by using (
50
).
∎

Lemma
18
shows that (
49
), our reformulation of (
46
), is strongly convex, with a convex constraint set. Consequently, a standard convex optimization algorithm can now be used to achieve function approximation guarantees, which we formalize, using a result from
[
NN94
]
, restated below.

Lemma 19

(Eq. (8.1.5),
[
NN94
]
)
.

Consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min\psi({\mathbf{x}}):=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{c}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}},\;\text{subject to }{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},-{\mathbf{c}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i\in[d],

where
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\succeq\mathbf{0}}^{n\times n}
and
\left\{{\mathbf{c}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in[d]}\cup\{{\mathbf{c}}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define the sets

{\mathcal{G}}:=\left\{{\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{c}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i\in[d]\right\},\;{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}:=\left\{{\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{c}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{r}}_{i}>0\text{ for all }i\in[d]\right\}.

If
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
is non-empty, then for any
\epsilon>0
, there is an algorithm which returns
{\mathbf{x}}_{\epsilon}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
satisfying

\displaystyle\psi({\mathbf{x}}_{\alpha})-\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\psi({\mathbf{x}})\leq\epsilon\left(\max_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\psi({\mathbf{x}})-\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\psi({\mathbf{x}})\right),

initialized at
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\in{\mathcal{G}}
, and runs in time

\displaystyle O\left(d^{1.5}n^{2}\log\left(\frac{d}{\alpha\left(G,{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right)\epsilon}\right)\right)

where
\alpha\left({\mathcal{G}},{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right):=\max\left\{\alpha\mid{\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\alpha({\mathbf{x}}_{0}-{\mathcal{G}})\subset{\mathcal{G}}\right\}
.

We next bound the asymmetry coefficient
\alpha({\mathcal{G}},{\mathbf{x}}_{0})
for our quadratic program (
49
).

Lemma 20
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}:=\left\{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\lVert{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{w}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}
where
{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
satisfies
{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\alpha({\mathcal{G}},\mathbf{0}_{n}):=\max\left\{\alpha\mid\mathbf{0}_{n}+\alpha(\mathbf{0}_{n}-{\mathcal{G}})\subset{\mathcal{G}}\right\}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{G}}
is symmetric about the origin and nonempty,
-\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{G}}=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{G}}\subset{\mathcal{G}}
as claimed.
∎

Finally, we combine these results to provide a fast algorithm for approximating (
45
).

Proposition 9
.

Let Assumption
4
hold. Following the notation in (
45
), there is an algorithm which returns
\boldsymbol{\theta}
satisfying
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rVert_{2}\leq\epsilon
,
in time

O\left(d^{1.5}n^{2}\log\left(\frac{(\lambda+\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2})d}{\epsilon}\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}({\mathbf{X}})}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}({\mathbf{X}})}\right)\right).

Proof.

We first claim it suffices to solve (
49
) to function error

\Delta:=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}({\mathbf{X}})^{4}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}({\mathbf{X}})^{2}}.

To see this, let
{\mathbf{w}}
satisfy
f({\mathbf{w}})-f(\hat{{\mathbf{w}}})\leq\Delta
, where
\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}
minimizes (
49
). By strong convexity of (
49
),

\left\lVert{\mathbf{w}}-\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{2}\Delta=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}({\mathbf{M}})^{2}\Delta.

Moreover, letting
{\mathbf{z}}:={\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{w}}
and
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}:={\mathbf{V}}\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}
, so that
\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}
minimizes (
46
) by Lemma
18
, we have
\left\lVert{\mathbf{w}}-\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}-\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}\right\rVert_{2}
since
{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
. Finally, by Lemma
17
, letting
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}-\lambda\hat{{\mathbf{z}}})
optimize (
45
) and
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}-\lambda{\mathbf{z}})
, we have

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\rVert_{2}=\lambda\left\lVert({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{\dagger}({\mathbf{z}}-\hat{{\mathbf{z}}})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}({\mathbf{X}})^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{z}}-\hat{{\mathbf{z}}}\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{\lambda}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}({\mathbf{X}})^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{w}}-\hat{{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}.

It remains to bound the complexity of achieving
\Delta
function error. Letting
{\mathbf{A}}:=2\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-2}
and
{\mathbf{c}}:=2\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-2}{\mathbf{b}}
in the setting of Lemma
19
,

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\psi({\mathbf{w}})-\min_{{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\psi({\mathbf{w}})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathcal{G}}}({\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{b}})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-2}({\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{b}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}({\mathbf{X}})^{2}}\max_{\left\lVert{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{w}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\left\lVert{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{b}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}({\mathbf{X}})^{2}}\cdot\left(\max_{\left\lVert{\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{w}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}}2\left\lVert{\mathbf{w}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{2(d+\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2})}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}({\mathbf{X}})^{2}}.

The conclusion follows from Lemma
19
using
\left\lVert{\mathbf{b}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}
by our choice of
{\mathbf{b}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}
.
∎

A.3
Proof of Proposition
3
:
\ell_{\infty}
recovery

In this section, we prove the first statement in Proposition
3
. Our main tool is the following known result on the performance of the Lasso for sparse recovery under MI (Definition
2
).

Lemma 21

(Theorem 7.21,
[
Wai19
]
)
.

In the setting of Model
2
, let
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})=S
and
|S|=k
. If
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\min}([{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S})>0
and
{\mathbf{X}}
is
\alpha
-MI over
S
, then for any regularization parameter
\lambda
such that

\lambda\geq\frac{2}{1-\alpha}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{S^{c}:}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{S^{\perp}}({\mathbf{X}})\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty},

(51)

where
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{S^{\perp}}({\mathbf{X}}):=\mathbf{I}_{n}-{\mathbf{X}}_{:S}[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}_{S:}
,
the Lasso solution

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left\{\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\theta}-{\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\rVert_{1}\right\}

(52)

satisfies

\left\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert\left[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right]^{-1}_{S\times S}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\lambda\max_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in S}\left|\left[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right]^{-1}_{ij}\right|.

(53)

We next give a bound on the parameters in (
51
), (
53
) under RIP.

Lemma 22
.

In the setting of Model
2
, let
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})=S
and
|S|=k
. If
{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies
(\epsilon,k+1)
-RIP, then for any regularization parameter
\lambda
such that

\lambda\geq\frac{2(1+\alpha)}{1-\alpha}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty},

(54)

the Lasso solution (
52
) satisfies

\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}+\frac{2\sqrt{k\epsilon}}{1-\epsilon^{2}}\right)\left(\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\lambda\right).

(55)

Proof.

Since
{\mathbf{X}}
is
(\epsilon,k+1)
RIP, we have by Lemma
15
that
{\mathbf{X}}
is
\alpha
-MI over any
S\subseteq[d]
with
|S|\leq k
, for
\alpha=\sqrt{2k\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)}
. We begin by bounding the right-hand side of (
51
). By the definition of
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{S^{\perp}}({\mathbf{X}})
and the triangle inequality, we have that

\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}_{S^{c}:}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{S^{\perp}}({\mathbf{X}})\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}_{S^{c}:}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert\left(\left[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right]_{S\times S}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:S^{c}}\right)^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

(56)

Also by the definition of
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\left[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right]_{S\times S}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:S^{c}}\right)^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\max_{j\in S^{c}}\left\lVert\left[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right]_{S\times S}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}_{:j}\right\rVert_{1}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{S:}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty},

(57)

where the last inequality used MI. Combining (
56
) and (
57
), we have shown

\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}_{S^{c}\times S^{c}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{S^{\perp}}({\mathbf{X}})\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq(1+\alpha)\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty},

proving that (
54
) is a sufficient condition for (
51
) to apply. Next we bound the right-hand side of (
53
).
Let
{\mathbf{M}}:=[{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}]_{S\times S}^{-1}
. Because
\lVert{\mathbf{M}}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}=\max_{i\in S}\lVert{\mathbf{M}}_{i:}\rVert_{1}
, we have from (
53
) that

\left\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}\left(\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\lambda\right),

(58)

so it is enough to provide a bound on
\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}
. Because
{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies
(\epsilon,k+1)
-RIP, we have

\boldsymbol{\lambda}({\mathbf{M}}^{-1})\in[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]^{k}\implies\boldsymbol{\lambda}({\mathbf{M}})\in\left[\frac{1}{1+\epsilon},\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\right]^{k}.

(59)

Thus, because
\left\lVert{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}=1
,

{\mathbf{M}}_{ii}={\mathbf{e}}^{\top}_{i}{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\in\left[\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\min}({\mathbf{M}}),\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\max}({\mathbf{M}})\right]=\left[\frac{1}{1+\epsilon},\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\right].

(60)

Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S\\
