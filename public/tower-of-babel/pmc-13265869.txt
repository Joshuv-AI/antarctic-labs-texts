Rutin ameliorates cold stress-induced impairment of growth performance, intestinal barrier function and cecal microbiota in broiler chickens
Authors: Jiaqi Dong, Xinyan Li, Zhongyu Li, Peiyue Guan, Xiao Liu, Haoyang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the effects of rutin on growth performance, intestinal barrier function, and gut microbiota in broilers under cold stress. A total of 360 one-day-old male broilers with an average body weight of 39 ± 2 g were randomly allocated into four groups. (6 replicates per treatment, 15 birds per replicate): CON (thermoneutral, basal diet), CS (cold stress control), CSR500 (500 mg rutin/kg diet), and CSR1000 (1000 mg rutin/kg diet). On day 28, one male bird per replicate, with body weight within ±5% of the group mean, was selected to represent the replicate, fasted for 12 hours, and then sampled. Serum, ileal tissue, and cecal content samples were collected for further analysis. Dietary supplementation of rutin significantly improved average daily gain (ADG) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) (p < 0.05) in cold-stressed broilers. It also enhanced immune organ indices and serum immunoglobulin levels (p < 0.05). Rutin significantly enhanced antioxidant capacity of ileal tissue, markedly increasing the activities of total superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) (p < 0.05), while reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) content. Rutin alleviated intestinal injury, which may be associated with modulation of intestinal permeability and inflammatory signaling pathways, repairing the intestinal mucosal and mechanical barriers associated with the Notch-1 signaling pathway, suppressing the activation of the NF-κB pathway and the expression of inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, rutin optimized the gut microbial structure by reducing the abundance of harmful bacteria (e.g., Proteobacteria) and increasing beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bacteroides). In conclusion, rutin alleviates the adverse effects of cold stress on broiler growth performance and improves intestinal health through specify mechanisms, offering a novel feed additive strategy for mitigating cold stress in poultry production.

Introduction

The husbandry environment critically influences livestock production. While moderate environmental challenges may enhance immune function and longevity, extreme temperatures induce stress responses that surpass animals' adaptive capacities, causing pathological damage. Prolonged exposure to intense stressors suppresses growth performance, compromise immunity, and increase mortality (Scazzocchio et al., 2020). Low temperature is a major environmental stressor in northern China. In broilers, cold stress triggers adverse responses that compromise health and productivity (Gao et al., 2025). Studies show cold stress significantly reduces the average daily gain (ADG) and increases the feed conversion ratio (FCR) of broilers (Zhang et al., 2021; Moreira et al., 2024; Vasconcelos et al., 2024). This is particularly critical during the starter phase, when chicks' immature thermoregulation and incomplete feathering force them to allocate substantial energy to maintain body temperature, heightening susceptibility to cold stress (Hoesel and Schmid., 2013). This predisposes birds to enteritis and respiratory infections. Cold stress damages the intestinal mucosal barrier and disrupts gut microbiota, causing immunosuppression and metabolic disorders (Pelaseyed et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2022; Aribigbola et al., 2024).

Mitigating cold stress is essential for sustainable poultry production. Genetic selection has improved broiler growth rates and efficiency. This progress demands precise environmental and nutritional management. In the era of antibiotic-free feeding, developing additives that enhance immunity and growth performance is crucial for efficient, healthy production (Tyagi and Prasad., 2019). This has accelerated the search for safe antibiotic alternatives, with phytogenic feed additives gaining attention for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and gut‑health properties (Liu et al., 2019). Rutin (quercetin-3-rutinoside), a natural flavonoid abundant in buckwheat and jujube, exhibits potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (Chen et al., 2018). Notably, rutin protects the digestive system; for example, it alleviates gut microbiota disruption and intestinal inflammation in broilers (Chen et al., 2022), and dietary rutin supplementation has also been shown to enhance growth performance, intestinal health, and cecal microbiota in broilers (Liu et al., 2026). The intestinal barrier is essential for preventing pathogen translocation and maintaining homeostasis (Ren et al., 2018). As the primary site for nutrient absorption, the intestine is vital for maintaining metabolic health. Cold stress disrupts intestinal morphology, impairing integrity and reducing digestive and absorptive capacity. This is evidenced by decreased villus height and increased crypt depth (Stanley et al., 2014). Despite existing evidence on the beneficial effects of rutin (Chen et al., 2023; Hafez et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025), its protective role against cold stress-induced intestinal injury in broilers and the underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored.

Therefore, We hypothesized that dietary rutin supplementation would improve growth performance, intestinal barrier function, and cecal microbiota in broilers subjected to chronic cold stress, thereby alleviating cold stress‑induced impairment. Our findings aim to provide a theoretical basis for the potential application of rutin as a dietary supplement to mitigate the adverse effects of cold stress in poultry production.

Experimental design, animals, and housing

A total of 360 one-day-old healthy male Arbor Acres broiler chicks were randomly allocated into four dietary treatments with 6 replicates per treatment and 15 birds per replicate. The four groups were: 1) CON group (control, raised under normal temperature and fed a basal diet); 2) CS group (cold-stressed, raised under cold stress at 12 °C and fed the basal diet); 3) CSR500 group (cold-stressed at 12 °C and fed the basal diet supplemented with 500 mg/kg rutin); and 4) CSR1000 group (cold-stressed at 12 °C and fed the basal diet supplemented with 1000 mg/kg rutin). Birds were housed in two climate-controlled rooms, with each replicate kept in an independent cage at a stocking density of 10 birds per square meter. Detailed environmental conditions are provided in Table 2. For the first 7 days, all chicks were maintained under the same thermoneutral conditions: 34 °C from day 1 to 3 and 33 °C from day 4 to 7. From day 8 onwards, the cold stress regimen was initiated for the CS, CSR500, and CSR1000 groups. The temperature for these groups was gradually reduced by 3 ± 1 °C per day, reaching 12 °C on day 14, and maintained at this temperature until day 28. The CON group was kept at a constant normal temperature of 26 ± 2 °C throughout the experiment (days 1–28), with relative humidity maintained at 55-65%. A lighting schedule of 23 hours of light and 1 hour of darkness (23L:1D) was provided throughout the 28-day experimental period. Sample collection

On the morning of day 28, one male bird per replicate was randomly selected after a 12-h fasting period and humanely euthanized for sample collection. The selected birds had body weights within ±5% of the group mean to avoid extreme values. Ileal tissue (2 cm segment proximal to the ileocecal junction) was rapidly excised, gently flushed with ice-cold saline to remove luminal contents, and then divided into two portions. One segment was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for histological analysis, and another segment was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent RT-qPCR and Western blotting analysis. Blood samples (approximately 10 mL) were collected from the jugular vein into heparinized tubes, then centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The separated plasma was stored at -40 °C for ELISA analysis. For each treatment (CON, CS, CSR500, CSR1000), there were six replicates, so one bird per replicate resulted in six birds per treatment. The cecal content was aseptically collected and immediately stored on dry ice for 16S rRNA gene sequencing of the gut microbiota.

Measurement of growth performance and immune organ indices

Birds were weighed after a 12‑hour fasting period on day 1, day 28, and at several intermediate time points. To evaluate the overall cumulative effect of cold stress, only body weights on day 1 and day 28 were used to calculate the average daily gain (ADG) and feed conversion ratio (FCR).Daily feed intake was recorded to calculate the average daily feed intake (ADFI) and FCR. After slaughter, the thymus, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius were dissected, cleared of adhering fat, and weighed. The immune organ index was calculated as follows:Immuneorganindex(%)=Immuneorganfreshweight(g)Livebodyweight(kg)*100%

Intestinal morphology and permeability assessment

Intestinal contents were thoroughly rinsed with physiological saline, and the intestines were then laid flat on a sterile surgical tray. Segmental lengths were measured using vernier calipers with 0.01 cm precision: duodenum (pylorus to duodenojejunal flexure), jejunum (duodenojejunal flexure to Meckel's diverticulum), and ileum (Meckel's diverticulum to ileocecal junction). Total small intestinal length was summed, and the length index (cm/kg) was calculated as total length divided by live body weight (kg). For morphological analysis, ileal segments fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (75%, 85%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in a xylene‑ethanol mixture (1:1) followed by pure xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. The paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). After dehydration and clearing, the sections were mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured at 100× magnification using a Nikon Eclipse Ci‑L microscope. Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured from well‑oriented villus‑crypt units using Image‑Pro Plus 6.0 software, and the VH/CD ratio was calculated. Intestinal permeability was assessed by determining the serum levels of diamine oxidase (DAO) and D-lactate using commercial ELISA kits (Shanghai Hengyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

RNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from ileal tissues using Trizol reagent. RNA purity and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit. RT-qPCR was performed using TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ on a quantitative PCR system. The mRNA expression levels of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Claudin-1, Occludin), mucin (MUC2), Notch signaling pathway-related genes (Notch-1, Jagged1, Hes-1), and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-4, IFN-γ) were analyzed. The β-actin gene was used as an internal control for normalization, The 2^(-ΔΔCt) method was used to calculate the relative mRNA expression levels of target genes. For each sample, three technical replicates were performed, with the coefficient of variation (CV) of Ct values required to be < 5%. The mean value of the replicates was used for statistical analysis. In this study, we focused on genes related to intestinal barrier function and inflammatory responses; therefore, only the above-mentioned targets were analyzed in the results section. Determination of antioxidant capacity in ileal tissue

Tissues (0.1 g) were homogenized in 0.9 mL of ice-cold physiological saline to prepare a 10% (w/v) homogenate. The homogenate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. The activities of total superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), as well as the content of malondialdehyde (MDA), were measured using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Ileal mucosal samples were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.4) at a mass/volume ratio of 1:9. The homogenate was centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min. The concentrations of secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), immunoglobulin M (IgM), and immunoglobulin G (IgG) in the supernatant were determined using specific commercial ELISA kits (Shanghai Hengyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Western blotting

Analysis Ileal tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1 mmol/L phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) to extract total protein. The protein concentration of the lysates was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Equal amounts of protein (typically 30 - 50 μg per lane) were separated by electrophoresis on 10% or 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels, as appropriate for the target protein's size. The separated proteins were then electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Beyotime Biotechnology) using a semi-dry transfer system. Following transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 hours at room temperature. The membranes were subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies against β-actin, NF-κB, and TLR-4 (all from Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).After incubation, the membranes were washed three times (10 min each) with TBST and then probed with corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at 37°C. Following another three TBST washes, the protein bands were visualized using a BeyoECL Star chemiluminescent substrate kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). Signal detection and image capture were performed using an Essential V6 imaging system (UVITEC, Cambridge, UK). Band intensity was quantified with the system's native software, and the relative expression level of each target protein was calculated by normalizing its intensity to that of β-actin. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and gut microbiota analysis

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from the cecal content. The hypervariable V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using specific primers (e.g., 338F/806R) and subjected to high-throughput sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform (or similar). Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered, trimmed, and merged using QIIME2 or USEARCH software. High-quality sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold. Alpha diversity indices, including the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices, were calculated to assess microbial community richness and diversity. Beta diversity was evaluated using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis distance metric to visualize structural differences between groups. Taxonomic composition was analyzed against the SILVA or Greengenes database. Differences in the relative abundance of specific taxa between groups were identified using the Kruskal-Wallis H test or Mann-Whitney U test, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 21.0). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene's test. All variables were found to satisfy both the normality assumption (p > 0.05 for all) and the homogeneity of variance assumption (p > 0.05 for all). Therefore, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple range test to compare differences among groups. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effects of rutin on immune organ indices in cold-stressed broilers

Compared with the CON group, the CS group showed a significantly lower thymus index (p < 0.05). Both the thymus and spleen indices in the CSR500 group were significantly higher than those in the CS group (p < 0.05). Discussion

This study systematically investigated the effects of rutin on growth performance, immune organ development, intestinal morphology and permeability, intestinal antioxidant capacity, intestinal barrier function, NF-κB signaling pathway, inflammatory factors, and intestinal flora in cold-stressed broilers. The following discussion interprets the results in light of recent advances in this field.

Effect of rutin on growth performance of cold-stressed broilers

In the present study, ADG was significantly lower and FCR significantly higher in the CS group than in the CON group (Liu et al., 2020). Supplementation with 500 mg/kg and 1000 mg/kg rutin significantly reduced FCR. Moreover, ADG in the 1000 mg/kg rutin group recovered to a level comparable with the thermoneutral control group. These results align with reports that dietary rutin at 500 and 1000 mg/kg improves growth performance in broilers under stress conditions (Li et al., 2023), likely through regulation of key energy metabolism genes and reduction of stress-related energy losses (Walk et al., 2019).

Effect of rutin on immune organ indices of cold-stressed broilers

In the current study, cold stress reduced the thymus index in the CS group (Fu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). After adding 500 mg/kg rutin, the thymus index and spleen index of broilers were significantly increased, suggesting that rutin can alleviate the damage of cold stress to immune organs, possibly through modulation of immune-related signaling pathways (Tarkhan et al., 2020). The duodenal, jejunal and ileal indices were significantly higher in the CS group, likely due to inflammatory hyperplasia triggered by cold stress (Lu et al., 2023). However, after rutin addition, the intestinal indices returned to normal in both rutin‑supplemented groups, with the 1000 mg/kg dose showing a slightly better normalization effect. This further confirming the protective effect of rutin on the intestines of cold-stressed broilers (Rysman et al., 2023).

Effect of rutin on intestinal morphology and permeability of cold-stressed broilers

In this study, cold stress led to decreased ileal villus height, increased crypt depth, and lowered the VH/CD ratio in broilers, while significantly elevating serum diamine oxidase (DAO) and D-lactic acid levels, indicating that the low-temperature environment destroys the integrity of the intestinal mucosal mechanical barrier (Fang et al., 2024). Rutin supplementation at both 500 and 1000 mg/kg significantly increased intestinal villus height, decreased crypt depth, and reduced serum DAO and D-lactate levels. Notably, crypt depth was lowest in the 500 mg/kg rutin group, while the 1000 mg/kg group also showed a significant reduction compared to the CS group Indicating that rutin reverses cold stress-induced morphological damage to the intestinal mucosa by promoting proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells (Basumatary et al., 2024). Rutin reduced intestinal permeability markers (DAO and D‑lactic acid), supporting its protective effect on the intestinal barrier (Wu et al., 2023).

Effect of rutin on intestinal antioxidant capacity of cold-stressed broilers

In this study, the ileal activities of SOD, GSH-Px, and catalase (CAT) were significantly decreased in the CS group while the MDA content was significantly increased (Chen et al., 2021). Rutin supplementation at both 500 and 1000 mg/kg, particularly at 1000 mg/kg, significantly enhanced ileal total antioxidant capacity (T‑AOC) and increased the activities of CAT, SOD, and GSH‑Px, while reducing MDA content. Rutin improves intestinal antioxidant capacity in cold‑stressed broilers by activating the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway to enhance antioxidant enzyme expression (Zhu et al., 2022). Meanwhile, research found that flavonoids can alleviate stress-induced oxidative damage by scavenging ROS and reducing the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products(Zhang et al., 2020), which further explains the molecular mechanism by which rutin improves the intestinal antioxidant status of cold-stressed broilers.

Effect of rutin on intestinal barrier function of cold-stressed broilers

In this study, cold stress led to significantly decreased mRNA expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and MUC2, and significantly increased mRNA expression levels of Notch-1, Jagged, and Hes-1 in the ileum of broilers, indicating cold stress-induced damage to the intestinal mucosal barrier (Zhang et al., 2023). After rutin addition, the mRNA expression levels of ZO-1 and MUC2 were significantly increased in both rutin-supplemented groups, and the mRNA expression levels of genes related to the Notch signaling pathway were significantly decreased, suggesting that rutin repairs the intestinal mucosal mechanical barrier and mucus barrier by regulating the Notch signaling pathway. The 1000 mg/kg dose tended to have a stronger effect on restoring ZO-1 and MUC2 expression. Rutin improves intestinal barrier function by inhibiting excessive Notch pathway activation (Wang et al., 2024b). Cold stress increased sIgA, IgM, and IgG, indicating intestinal inflammation‑related immune disorders (Yang et al., 2021). Rutin restored the abnormal contents to normal, with both 500 and 1000 mg/kg doses being effective, thereby regulating intestinal immune balance and repairing cold stress-induced intestinal barrier damage both structurally and immunologically via the Notch signaling pathway (Dai et al., 2022).

Effect of rutin on NF-κB signaling pathway and inflammatory factors of cold-stressed broilers

In this study, the mRNA and protein expression levels of TLR4, NF-κB, and MYD88 in the ileum of the CS group were significantly increased, and the mRNA expression levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-4, and IFN-γ were significantly increased, indicating that cold stress activates the NF-κB pathway to induce intestinal inflammation (Wang et al., 2024a). After rutin addition, the expression levels of molecules related to the NF-κB signaling pathway were significantly decreased, and the expression levels of pro-inflammatory factors were significantly reduced, indicating that rutin inhibits cold stress-induced intestinal inflammation by blocking the TLR4/NF-κB pathway (Ringseis and Eder, 2022). Both 500 and 1000 mg/kg rutin were effective, with the 1000 mg/kg dose showing a stronger suppressive effect on the expression of NF-κB-related molecules and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Cold stress disrupts Th1/Th2 balance by altering IFN-γ and IL-4 expression (Liu et al., 2024). Rutin regulates IFN-γ and IL-4 expression to maintain Th1/Th2 balance, thereby improving intestinal immune function (Attri et al., 2018). The 1000 mg/kg supplementation showing greater efficacy in restoring the Th1/Th2 balance.

Effect of rutin on intestinal flora of cold-stressed broilers

In this study, cold stress led to a significant increase in the abundance of Proteobacteria (a phylum of harmful bacteria) and a significant decrease in the abundance of Bacteroides (a genus of beneficial bacteria) in the cecum of broilers, indicating that cold stress changes the intestinal flora structure of broilers (Ayob et al., 2023). After rutin addition, the abundance of Proteobacteria was significantly reduced, and the abundance of Bacteroides was significantly increased. Both 500 and 1000 mg/kg rutin showed beneficial effects, with the higher dose resulting in a more pronounced increase in Bacteroides abundance. Rutin optimizes the intestinal flora structure of cold-stressed broilers by serving as a fermentation substrate for beneficial bacteria and inhibiting adhesion of harmful bacteria (Wang et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2018). In addition, Faecalibacterium, as a key genus producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), its abnormal increase in abundance may be related to intestinal metabolic disorders caused by cold stress (Zhao et al., 2013). Rutin (especially at 1000 mg/kg) regulates its abundance, supporting its modulatory effect on intestinal microecology, as flavonoids improve host health by regulating gut flora metabolism (Zhou et al., 2021).

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrate that rutin alleviates the adverse effects of cold stress in broilers through the following mechanisms: improving growth performance and immune organ development, enhancing intestinal antioxidant capacity, restoring the intestinal mucosal barrier via modulation of the Notch-1 and NF-κB signaling pathways, and optimizing gut microbiota structure by increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the application of rutin as a feed additive to mitigate cold stress in broiler production. Future studies should further elucidate the specific mechanisms through which rutin alleviates cold stress in broilers.
