Application of a food trigger score to identify common dietary triggers among Saudi adults with irritable bowel syndrome
Authors: Sarah M. Ajabnoor, Lara Tashkandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a prevalent functional gastrointestinal disorder in which diet plays a significant role in modulating symptom severity. This study aims to identify common food triggers in Saudi individuals with IBS and examine how patient characteristics relate to food trigger scores.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 98 adults in Saudi Arabia diagnosed with IBS. Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire that captured sociodemographic variables, symptom severity, and responses to predefined dietary triggers. A food trigger score was computed to quantify both the number and severity of responses.Results:The most frequently reported dietary triggers were spicy foods (64.4%), legumes (62.0%), cabbage (48.2%), milk (47.7%), and fried foods (43.3%). Multiple linear regression analysis demonstrated that older age (β = 5.835, P = 0.041) and IBS symptom severity (β = 0.035, P = 0.039) were significant predictors of higher total food trigger scores. In subgroup models, chronic medical conditions were strongly associated with increased trigger scores for fruits and vegetables (β = 15.798, P = 0.002) and protein foods (β = 13.226, P = 0.008). Meanwhile, IBS severity remained a significant predictor for protein foods (β = 0.046, P = 0.026) and caffeinated beverages (β = 0.080, P = 0.004).Conclusion:Specific foods are strongly linked to worse IBS symptoms. Individual factors such as symptom severity, history of chronic illness, and employment status appear to modulate dietary sensitivity. These results highlight the need for tailored dietary interventions in the management of IBS.

INTRODUCTION

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a widespread condition defined by abnormal bowel movement and frequent abdominal discomfort.[1] The most common functional gastrointestinal disorder (FGID),[2] IBS is characterized by recurrent abdominal pain and bloating, with altered bowel habits in the form of constipation, diarrhea, or both.[3] Non-gastrointestinal symptoms, such as fatigue and psychological distress, are also frequently observed.[4] Key proposed pathophysiological factors include altered motility, impaired barrier function, immune activation, high visceral activity, and central nervous system dysfunction, while environmental and individual predisposing factors—such as stress, diet, infections, and antibiotic use—have been associated with the condition.[1] IBS is responsible for more than 12% of primary care visits and 28% of gastroenterology consultations, resulting in considerable healthcare expense and extensive resource demand.[5] The global incidence of IBS is estimated at 3.2%–9.2%, with variation depending on the application of Rome III and Rome IV diagnostic criteria.[6] A recent systematic review found the overall prevalence of IBS in Saudi Arabia to be 26.3%, significantly higher than the global average.[7] However, prevalence estimates vary widely depending on region, population type, and diagnostic criteria used. Previous research in Saudi Arabia has identified several factors associated with IBS, including female sex, certain living conditions, poor sleep quality, lifestyle behaviors such as smoking and physical inactivity, family history, and psychological stress; however, no studies to date have specifically assessed dietary patterns or food triggers in this population.[8]

IBS is diagnosed primarily through symptom-based assessment—predominantly the Rome IV criteria—alongside clinical history and minimal testing to exclude other conditions, as no definitive biomarker currently exists. The Rome IV criteria define IBS by recurrent abdominal pain occurring at least one day per week over the past three months, associated with two or more features: pain related to defecation, a change in stool frequency, or a change in stool form.[9] A comprehensive clinical assessment—comprising medical, surgical, dietary, and psychological history—is essential, with the absence of alarm features such as anemia, rectal bleeding, unintentional weight loss, or a family history of colorectal cancer supporting IBS diagnosis.[10] Basic laboratory tests, such as complete blood count, C-reactive protein, and celiac serology, may be used to rule out other conditions, particularly in patients with diarrhea or concerning symptoms.[11] Extensive investigations, like colonoscopy or imaging, are generally unnecessary in patients under 50 years of age unless alarm features are present.[12] IBS is categorized into subtypes based on bowel habit changes: constipation-predominant (IBS-C), diarrhea-predominant (IBS-D), mixed (IBS-M), and unclassified (IBS-U).[12] Current guidelines support a positive diagnosis based on clinical presentation and limited testing, moving away from the traditional diagnosis of exclusion.[13]

A large proportion of individuals with IBS identify certain foods as triggers that worsen their symptoms, with approximately 85%–90% reporting aggravated symptoms following the intake of specific items.[14] These intolerances often exhibit a dose-response relationship, whereby larger portions increase the likelihood of triggering symptoms.[14] Frequently implicated foods include those rich in fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols—commonly referred to as FODMAPs—such as wheat, select fruits and vegetables, dairy, legumes, and artificial sweeteners.[15] These foods may lead to increased gas formation and water retention in the colon, contributing to discomfort, bloating, and disruptions in bowel habits.[16] Although actual food allergies are uncommon in IBS, non-allergic sensitivities, and individual intolerances are often significant contributors.[16] Dietary interventions, especially the low-FODMAP diet, have been shown to alleviate symptoms in many IBS patients; however, individual responses differ, and the long-term outcomes remain under investigation.[17] Meanwhile, this diet presents some challenges relating to complexity, risk of nutritional deficiencies, and impact on gut microbiota.[18] Personalized nutrition counseling is therefore advised to assist patients in identifying and managing their specific food triggers, while ensuring adequate nutrient intake.[19]

Specific tools, such as food diaries and questionnaires, are commonly used to identify patient-reported dietary triggers in IBS. Recognizing these triggers is essential for implementing personalized dietary strategies to achieve better clinical outcomes.[202122] While assessments of these tools have been conducted in various populations, they are yet to be explored among Saudi patients with IBS. This study aims to identify the most reported food triggers among individuals living in Saudi Arabia, with IBS diagnosed using the Rome IV criteria. It also seeks to explore the associations between patient characteristics and their food trigger scores, offering a clearer understanding of how individual factors may influence dietary sensitivities in IBS.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April 2022 and January 2023. Data were collected by using a self-administered online survey distributed via SurveyMonkey. The survey aimed to assess self-reported IBS symptom severity and dietary triggers.

A purposive sampling approach was used to recruit eligible participants, targeting adults with IBS-related symptoms or a prior clinical diagnosis. Designated data collectors—licensed dietitians from different regions of Saudi Arabia, some of whom worked directly with patients diagnosed with IBS—facilitated participant recruitment. The survey link was disseminated via social media platforms, primarily WhatsApp. Participants were required to be at least 18 years of age and to report IBS-related symptoms consistent with the Rome IV diagnostic criteria.[12] This requires recurrent abdominal pain (on average at least one day per week in the past 3 months) associated with two or more of the following: pain related to defecation; a change in stool frequency or form; or prior clinical diagnosis of IBS. Participants were excluded if they did not meet the Rome IV criteria for IBS diagnosis, or if they provided incomplete responses to the questionnaire.

Data collection and instruments

Data were gathered through a structured online questionnaire comprising three primary sections: sociodemographic characteristics, IBS severity, and dietary triggers. Sociodemographic variables also included previous diagnoses of chronic diseases.

Symptom severity was assessed using the IBS Symptom Severity Score (IBS-SSS), developed by Francis et al.,[23] which measures the frequency and intensity of abdominal pain, abdominal bloating, satisfaction with bowel habits, and the extent to which symptoms interfere with daily life. Scores range from 0 to 500 and are categorized as mild (75–175), moderate (175–300), or severe (>300). A formal license agreement was obtained from the Rome Foundation for the use of the validated Arabic version of the Rome IV Diagnostic Questionnaire and the IBS Symptom Severity Score (IBSSSS), and the study adhered to the foundation’s recommended procedures for administration and scoring.[24]

The dietary trigger section consisted of 30 items reflecting common food-related IBS triggers identified in previous research.[2025] These items were grouped into four categories:(1) fruits and vegetables, (2) dairy products, (3) protein-based foods, and (4) caffeinated beverages. The study subjects reported their food trigger score on a three-point Likert scale (0 = no symptoms, 1 = mild symptoms, 2 = severe symptoms). An additional option, “I do not consume this food,” was available and was recorded as missing data to prevent underestimation of symptom scores.

Each participant’s total food trigger score was calculated by summing all reported scores across the 30 items and converting the total to a 0–100 scale, based on the methodology of Simrén et al.[25] This normalization accounted for missing items by dividing the score by the maximum possible (number of items answered (30) – number of missing items × 2) and multiplying by 100. Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 25; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyze the data. Categorical variables are shown as frequencies (percentages), while continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution for food trigger scores was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and histograms. The reliability of the food trigger questionnaire was assessed by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to examine associations between participants’ characteristics and their total food trigger score, adjusted for potential confounders including age and sex. Additional subgroup analyses were conducted using the four-food group sub-scores as outcome variables to explore category-specific associations. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The research adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Research and Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Applied Medical Sciences at King Abdulaziz University (Approval No. FAMS-EC2022-06). The initial page of the online survey featured a participant information sheet and included a mandatory consent question to confirm voluntary participation.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics and symptom severity

The study included 98 participants diagnosed with IBS. Most participants were female (86.7%) and of Saudi nationality (94.9%). The most prevalent age group was 18–29 years, representing 45.9% of the sample. The largest percentage of participants resided in the Western region (69.4%), and approximately half of the participants were employed (50%). A high percentage of patients reported suffering from chronic conditions (78.6%). Participants’ demographic data are presented in Table 1.

Reliability of food trigger items

Table 2 presents the reliability statistics for the food items included in the food trigger score. The overall Cronbach’s Alpha for all items was 0.935, indicating excellent internal consistency of the scale. Cronbach’s Alpha values, if individual items were deleted, ranged from 0.929 (Nuts) to 0.938 (Coffee), suggesting that no single food item significantly compromised the overall reliability of the questionnaire. This consistency across items confirms that the questionnaire reliably measures perceived food-related symptom triggers among individuals with IBS.

Frequency of reported food triggers

Figure 2 presents participants’ self-reported symptom responses to 30 commonly consumed food triggers. The most frequently identified triggers included spicy foods (64.4%), legumes (62.0%), cabbage (48.2%), milk (47.7%), and fried foods (43.3%); most respondents experienced moderate to severe symptoms after intake. Conversely, foods such as chicken (0%), potatoes (3.2%), bananas (4.7%), and apples (5.9%) were the least associated with symptoms, with most individuals reporting no adverse effects. These findings indicate that high-fat, gas-producing, and spicy foods were the principal dietary triggers among the study participants.

Determinants of food trigger scores

The mean total food trigger score was 36.3 ± 16.9. Among food groups, dairy products had the highest score (49.4 ± 26.9), followed by caffeinated beverages (39.0 ± 28.1), fruits and vegetables (31.4 ± 21.3), and protein-based foods (29.1 ± 20.8). Overall, lactose- and caffeine-containing foods were perceived as the most symptom-provoking.

Multiple linear regression analyses, adjusted for age and sex, were performed to explore the relationships between participant characteristics and food trigger scores [Table 3]. In the overall analysis, both increased age (β = 5.835, P = 0.041) and greater IBS symptom severity (β = 0.035, P = 0.039) were significantly linked to higher total food trigger scores, suggesting heightened dietary sensitivity among older individuals and those with more severe symptoms. Regarding specific food categories, higher scores for fruit and vegetable triggers were significantly associated with age (β = 9.223, P = 0.009) and the presence of chronic conditions (β = 15.798, P = 0.002). Employment status was the only significant predictor for dairy product sensitivity (β = 14.062, P = 0.009), while both chronic conditions (β = 13.226, P = 0.008) and IBS severity (β = 0.046, P = 0.026) were linked to increased sensitivity to protein-rich foods. For caffeinated beverages, IBS severity emerged as the sole significant factor (β = 0.080, P = 0.004).

DISCUSSION

This study explored food-related symptom triggers among adults with IBS in Saudi Arabia. Our findings offer novel insights into the relationship between Saudi culture and eating habits and the perception of IBS symptoms. Several foods commonly consumed by the Saudi population—particularly spicy foods, legumes, cabbage, milk, and fried foods—were the most frequently reported symptom triggers, while others, such as chicken, potato, and certain fruits, were rarely associated with discomfort. General symptom severity, alongside certain characteristics of the participants including age, employment status, and the presence of chronic medical conditions, was linked to higher food trigger sensitivity. These patterns highlight that food–symptom relationships in IBS are complex and shaped by both individual physiological differences and cultural dietary habits.

The predominance of spicy foods as major triggers aligns with previous reports of capsaicin-rich foods aggravating IBS symptoms. Capsaicin activates transient receptor potential vanilloid-1 (TRPV1) receptors, inducing heightened visceral sensitivity and faster gastrointestinal transit.[26] IBS patients demonstrate upregulation of TRPV1-positive sensory fibers, which correlates with abdominal pain intensity.[2728] Although Saudi cuisine typically emphasizes aromatic and mildly spiced dishes rather than intensely hot foods, the frequent reporting of spicy foods as triggers may indicate a combination of both cultural dietary patterns and individual differences in neural sensitivity.

Legumes were also frequently reported triggers, consistent with evidence that FODMAPs in legumes, particularly galacto-oligosaccharides, can produce gas and distension through colonic fermentation.[29] This mechanism underlies the rationale for FODMAP restriction in some IBS patients. However, legumes form a key part of traditional Middle Eastern diets,[30] which may indicate the need for moderation and selective substitution rather than complete avoidance. Dairy products were also identified as moderate triggers. Although lactose malabsorption may contribute to symptoms, self-reported dairy intolerance does not always correspond to objective lactose intolerance.[31] Fat content, casein peptides, or hypersensitivity may lead some patients to experience symptoms, rather than enzyme deficiency. High-fat foods were also commonly reported triggers, reflecting established associations between dietary fat, delayed gastric emptying, and colonic hypersensitivity.[32]

This study demonstrates that structured food trigger scores can reliably quantify symptom sensitivity to different foods in IBS patients. The tool showed strong internal reliability and has potential as a culturally relevant assessment instrument in both clinical and research settings. Developing and validating such tools in target populations is essential for several reasons, including accuracy in identifying dietary triggers, promoting consistency in clinical management, supporting tailored treatment strategies, and facilitating scientific research. These factors contribute to better patient care and more robust research findings.[22] Moreover, a validated score provides a structured, evidence-based method for identifying food sensitivities without relying on overly restrictive elimination diets. While a low-FODMAP diet can improve symptoms in some patients with IBS, it can be difficult to maintain—particularly in regions where FODMAP-rich foods are central to the local cuisine—and risks nutritional imbalance.[33] Using a scoring tool to recognize personalized triggers allows clinicians to target the most relevant foods for individual patients, improving adherence and minimizing unnecessary restriction.

The observed associations between higher food trigger scores and factors such as IBS severity, chronic illnesses, and employment status indicate that symptom perception is influenced by both physiological and lifestyle factors. Patients with more severe IBS symptoms likely exhibit increased visceral sensitivity and immune activation, mechanisms linked to mast cell degranulation and histamine-mediated nociceptive signaling.[3435] Chronic medical conditions may further modify gastrointestinal motility or microbiota composition, exacerbating sensitivity to dietary stimuli.[36] Meanwhile, lifestyle factors, such as employment status and daily routine, may contribute through an influence on eating patterns, stress levels, and symptom reporting. In this study, employment status emerged as the sole significant predictor of dairy-product sensitivity, which suggests that lifestyle factors and environmental exposures may play a more substantial role than physiological mechanisms alone. Employment may influence dietary habits—such as frequency of eating out, irregular meal schedules, and reliance on specific dairy-containing foods like those found in fast food or workplace cafeterias. Also, such findings may reflect work-related factors like higher stress levels, time-pressured eating patterns, and greater reliance on commercially prepared foods and beverages that often contain dairy. Although existing literature linking employment status directly to dairy sensitivity in IBS is scarce, it is well-documented that individuals with IBS frequently report dairy intolerance, even when not associated with verified lactose malabsorption.[37]

Key strengths of this study include the use of the Rome IV diagnostic criteria alongside a culturally relevant questionnaire, and the statistical adjustments made for possible confounding variables. The food trigger scoring tool also showed high internal reliability, indicating strong psychometric properties; yet, further validation, including test–retest reliability and convergent validity, is warranted in the Saudi population. This is the first study to evaluate food trigger scores specifically among Saudi individuals with IBS, contributing novel regional insights. Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered. This study relied on self-reported dietary triggers, which may introduce recall bias. No single validated objective diagnostic test exists for IBS, and diagnosis is primarily based on symptom-based criteria and no objective measure is yet available to verify specific food sensitivities. Future studies might consider incorporating validation methods, such as prospective food diaries and clinical correlation, to improve the reliability of findings. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the current study limits the ability to infer causal relationships between dietary factors and symptom occurrence. Also, the relatively small sample size and over-representation of participants from the Western region may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly those from other regions or with lower digital literacy. While females were over-represented in the study sample, this is consistent with the higher reported prevalence of IBS among women.[7] A formal power calculation and multicollinearity assessment of predictors were not formally assessed in the present study, which should be considered when interpreting regression findings. However, this study represents an initial exploratory investigation in the Saudi population to identify dietary triggers and inform the development of a food trigger score for IBS. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger, adequately powered, and stratified samples are warranted. Finally, the lack of separate analysis by IBS subtypes (e.g., IBS-D, IBS-C) may obscure subtype-specific trigger patterns and may provide additional clinical insights; however, a previous study by Rijnaarts et al.[20] reported no significant differences in self-perceived food intolerance across subtypes. This supports the exploratory nature of the current study for examining the overall trigger patterns.[38]

In conclusion, the findings of the present study underscore the need for personalized dietary approaches in IBS management that consider each patient’s symptoms, comorbidities, and lifestyle. Following this approach, the development of culturally tailored and validated food trigger score systems can support clinicians to more accurately recognize food-related symptoms and guide patients toward targeted, less restrictive dietary modifications. Future research should focus on validating such tools across diverse populations and integrating them with objective biomarkers—including gut microbiota composition and immune responses—to enhance the precision and sustainability of diet-based therapy in IBS. Furthermore, future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to better elucidate the causal relationships between dietary triggers and symptom severity in IBS.
