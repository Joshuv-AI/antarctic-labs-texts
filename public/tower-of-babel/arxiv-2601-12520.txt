Quasigeodesic languages are not context-free in some non-hyperbolic groups
Arya Saranathan

Abstract
. We study the full language of quasigeodesics in Cayley graphs with fixed error constants. We show that, given a non-virtually-cyclic nilpotent group or Baumslag–Solitar group, and any finite generating set, such languages fail to be context-free for sufficiently large error constants. In fact, this conclusion holds for any finitely generated group which contains one of these groups as an undistorted subgroup. This strengthens a recent theorem of Hughes, Nairne, and Spriano, who showed that such languages fail to be regular in any non-hyperbolic group, for sufficiently large error constants.



1.
Introduction

Given a group
G
and finite generating set
S
, we can form its Cayley graph
\Gamma(G,S)
, which has a vertex for every group element, and edges labelled by generators.
In this paper, we are interested in the quasigeodesics of
\Gamma(G,S)
, which are shortest paths in
\Gamma(G,S)
up to some fixed additive and multiplicative error. In particular, since edges are labelled by generators from
S
, every path corresponds to a word in the generators
S
, and we consider the formal language of words described by the quasigeodesics (with fixed error constants). Geodesic and quasigeodesic languages have been studied extensively. For example,
Groves showed that there is no regular set of geodesic normal forms
for words in
BS(1,n)

[
9
]
, and Elder has shown that the
geodesic language of
BS(1,2)
in the standard generating set is not counter
[
5
]
.
Moreover, it was shown by Cleary, Elder, and Taback that the full language of geodesics of the lamplighter group
is context-free, with respect to the standard wreath-product generating set
[
3
]
. It happens that the algorithmic properties of these quasigeodesic languages are closely connected to the geometry of the group in a striking way. The bridge is the fundamentally geometric notion of a hyperbolic group, originally introduced by Gromov in
[
7
]
; a recurring theme is that the class of hyperbolic groups seems to draw a boundary between ‘tame’ groups with simple quasigeodesic languages, and ‘wild’ groups with complex quasigeodesic languages. To make this precise, the following definition was introduced in
[
11
]
and is of particular interest to this paper.

Definition 1.1
.

A finitely generated group
G
is
\mathbb{Q}
REG
if for all rational

\lambda\geq 1
, real
\epsilon\geq 0
and finite generating sets
S
, the
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesics in
the Cayley graph
\Gamma(G,S)
form a regular language.

Holt and Rees showed in
[
11
]
that hyperbolic groups are
\mathbb{Q}
REG
. Some intuition for this result comes from the Morse lemma for hyperbolic groups. Quasigeodesics in the Cayley graph between two elements of a hyperbolic group fellow-travel, so an automaton recognizing the quasigeodesic language need only check that an input word does not deviate from a given quasigeodesic by too much.
Remarkably, Hughes, Nairne, and Spriano proved the converse in
[
12
]
,
establishing that the regularity of rational quasigeodesic languages

characterizes
hyperbolicity.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
12
,
11
]
)
.

A finitely generated group is hyperbolic if and only if it is
\mathbb{Q}
REG
.

Theorem

1.2
lends credence to the idea that hyperbolic groups form a very natural class that can be defined in many different ways. Indeed, the result adds to
a growing list of diverse characterizations of hyperbolicity:
thin triangles condition
[
7
]
,
vanishing of
\ell^{\infty}
-cohomology
[
6
]
,
all asymptotic cones being
\mathbb{R}
-trees
[
7
]
,
and as strongly geodesically automatic groups
[
18
]
.

In this paper we explore the question of whether a stronger characterization is possible in the same vein. Define the
analogous property
\mathbb{Q}
CF
by replacing ‘regular’ with ‘context-free’ in
Definition

1.1
. While context-freeness is weaker than regularity, it still places
limits on the memory available to an automaton recognizing the quasigeodesic languages, and would thus impose constraints on the geometry of the group.
This suggests the following audacious conjecture.

Conjecture 1.3
.

A finitely generated group is hyperbolic if and only if it is
\mathbb{Q}
CF
.

Such a strengthening of
Theorem

1.2
would be an enthralling surprise, as a linguistic gap would appear:
1.3
implies that a finitely generated group that is
\mathbb{Q}
CF
is actually
\mathbb{Q}
REG
.
This would be an interesting parallel to the behaviour of the Dehn function, where a similar gap appears: hyperbolic groups
are precisely those finitely presented groups with linear Dehn function, and any group with subquadratic Dehn function must
be hyperbolic and therefore have linear Dehn function
[
16
]
.
To show the strength of the conjecture we remark that the behaviour of context-free languages is really not well understood under a change of generating sets. Indeed, even the following significantly weaker statement is currently unknown: if a group admits a finite generating set for which rational quasigeodesic languages in the Cayley graph are context-free, must rational quasigeodesic languages be context-free for any choice of finite generating set? Nevertheless, motivated by these considerations, in this paper we verify
1.3
for various classes of groups. First, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

A finitely generated nilpotent group is
\mathbb{Q}
CF
if and only if it is virtually cyclic.

This fits with
1.3
because the hyperbolic nilpotent groups are exactly the virtually cyclic ones. Secondly, we also verify
1.3
for Baumslag–Solitar groups, which are
not
hyperbolic.

Theorem 1.5
.

Baumslag–Solitar groups
BS(m,n)
are not
\mathbb{Q}
CF
.

In this paper, we do not work directly with the definition of
\mathbb{Q}
CF
, as it poses some challenges.
For example, as mentioned,
a priori

there could be a group which has context-free rational quasigeodesic languages for one choice of generating set, but not another. It is also not clear how this property interacts with undistorted subgroups.
To remedy this, we opt to introduce the notion of a
chaotic
group, a direct strengthening of the not-
\mathbb{Q}
CF
property. This reframing is important to the proofs of the above theorems, and it ultimately allows us to lift to overgroups in which these groups sit as undistorted subgroups,
significantly expanding the class of groups for which we can
confirm
1.3
. Our key findings can thus be summarised as follows.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group which contains an undistorted
subgroup which is either non-virtually-cyclic and nilpotent, or a Baumslag–Solitar group.
Then
G
is chaotic, and in particular not
\mathbb{Q}
CF
.

An important tool used to obtain our results is Ogden’s lemma from
[
15
]
, which provides a strong necessary condition for a language to be context free, and hence a way to prove that a given language is not context free. Then, the general proof strategy is to construct a family of words in a
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesic language of a group, and to argue that this family of words has properties violating the necessary condition given by Ogden’s lemma, showing that the
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesic language is not context-free. Along the way we rely on Osin’s theorem on subgroup distortion in nilpotent groups
[
17
]
, and later use results by Burillo and Elder
[
2
]
on normal forms and lower bounds on displacement for words in Baumslag–Solitar groups.

Outline

In §2 we set up notation and establish a useful corollary of Ogden’s lemma, which will be used several times to prove that certain languages are not context-free.
In §3 we introduce the notion of a
chaotic
group,
which is a stronger property than not-
\mathbb{Q}
CF
, and
is easier to work with. In §4 we prove that
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is chaotic. We then extend
the methods of §4 to show that non-virtually-cyclic virtually nilpotent groups are chaotic,
in §5. Finally, in §6 we use a slightly more involved construction to
show that Baumslag–Solitar groups are chaotic.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation and terminology

Consider a group
G
with finite generating set
S
.
In this paper we
deal exclusively with finitely generated groups and finite generating sets,
which are also assumed symmetric (closed under inverses).
We may then form the

Cayley Graph

\Gamma(G,S)
, which is a
geometric picture of the group’s combinatorial
structure. It has vertex set
G
, with directed edges
\{g,gs\}

for all
g\in G
and
s\in S
. By considering edges to all have unit length, distances in
\Gamma(G,S)

induce a metric
d_{S}
on
G
, and in this way
G
is a discrete metric space.
Left-multiplication by an element of
G
is an isometry of
\Gamma(G,S)
,
so
d_{S}(h,g)=d_{S}(e,h^{-1}g)
(here
e\in G
is the identity element). Therefore, it will be convenient
to treat
d_{S}
as a unary operator, and we will write
d_{S}(g)
to mean
d_{S}(e,g)
.
When the generating set
S
is clear we may write
d=d_{S}
.

A
word

w
in
S
is an element of
S^{*}
, i.e. a finite sequence of generators,
whose length is denoted by
|w|
. We will frequently abuse notation
and use
w
to refer also to the corresponding element of
G
(yielded by
multiplying together its letters in
G
). A word is understood to trace a discrete path in
the Cayley graph
\Gamma(G,S)
starting from the identity
e\in G
,
which gives rise to the following geometric notions.
A word
w
is
geodesic
if it describes a shortest path
in
\Gamma(G,S)
, or equivalently
|w|=d(w)
. We will be
especially interested in a coarse version of this property. The word

w
is
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesic
if for any subword
u
of
w
,
we have

|u|\leq\lambda d(u)+\epsilon.

By ‘subword’ we mean any contiguous subsequence of letters of
w
.
Geodesics and quasigeodesics can be defined in much greater generality,
but this discrete viewpoint will suffice for our purposes.
The collection of all geodesic words for
(G,S)
is a subset of
S^{*}

known as the
geodesic language
, and the collection of all

(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesics is the
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesic language
.
Note that these languages depend on the generating set
S
.

We will also need the notion of a quasiisometrically embedded (QI-embedded, or undistorted) subgroup.
The subgroup
H\leq G
is QI-embedded roughly when distances in
H
are distorted
only by a uniform constant compared to distances in
G
. That is, if
T,S
are generating sets for
H,G
respectively,
then we require constants
(A,B)
such that
for all
h\in H
,

\tfrac{1}{A}d_{T}(h)-B\leq d_{S}(h)\leq Ad_{T}(h)+B.

However, it is straightforward to see that
changing generating sets only affects
distances by a unform constant, so the property of QI-embeddedness
is independent of generating sets.

2.2.
Context-free languages

A language
L\subseteq S^{*}
is context-free (or is a CFL) if it is the
language recognized by a nondeterministic pushdown automaton,
or equivalently if it the language of a context-free grammar
(see any textbook on formal languages, for example
[
19
]
).

A particularly useful necessary condition for a language to be context-free
is given by the following theorem of Ogden from
[
15
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Ogden’s lemma)
.

Let
L
be a context-free language. Then there exists a constant
p

such that for any word
w\in L
with at least
p
marked positions,
there is a decomposition
w=uxzyv
such that:

•

xzy
contains at most
p
marked positions,

•

z
contains at least one marked position,

•

u,x
both contain marked positions, or
y,v
both contain marked positions, and

•

for all
n\geq 0
, the word
ux^{n}zy^{n}v\in L
.

We call this
p
the Ogden constant of
L
.
We will make use of this theorem several times to prove that certain langauges are
not context-free. We record a corollary tailored to our use.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
L
be context free with Ogden constant
p
, and

\alpha\beta\gamma\in L
with
|\beta|\geq p
.
Then there is a decomposition
\alpha\beta\gamma=uxzyv
such that:

•

At least one of
x
or
y
is contained within
\beta
, and

•

for all
n\geq 0
, the word
ux^{n}zy^{n}v\in L
.

Proof.

Apply Ogden’s lemma to
\alpha\beta\gamma
, marking all positions of
\beta
.
There is a decomposition
\alpha\beta\gamma=uxzyv
such that either both
u,x
or both
y,v
contain marked positions.
Without loss of generality, suppose
u,x
contain marked positions. Then
\alpha
must be contained entirely in
u
. But
z
also must
contain a marked position, so
ux
is contained in
\alpha\beta
. It follows that
x
is contained in
\beta
.
∎

3.
Chaotic groups

The property of a group
G
with generating set
S
having non-context-free
quasigeodesic languages is not necessarily well-behaved. For example, it may
a priori
be
sensitive to the choice of generating set, and may not be inherited by overgroups in which

G
is QI-embedded. This makes it hard to prove ’not-
\mathbb{Q}
CF
’ type results which generalize to large classes of groups. We therefore introduce a
stronger
version of the not-
\mathbb{Q}
CF
property that is
stable with respect to the above operations.

Definition 3.1

(Chaotic group)
.

A group
G
with finite generating set
S
is
chaotic
if there
exist
\lambda_{0}\geq 1
and
\epsilon_{0}\geq 0
such that
for every context–free language

L\subseteq S^{*}
that contains all
(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
–quasigeodesic
words of
\Gamma(G,S)
, the following holds: for all
\lambda\geq 1
and

\epsilon\geq 0
, the language
L
contains a word that
is not
(\lambda,\epsilon)
–quasigeodesic in
\Gamma(G,S)
.
Then we say
(G,S)
is chaotic, or
(G,S)
is chaotic at
(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
.

This definition seems to depend both on
G
and
S
.
But as we shall see, the chaotic nature of a group is preserved
when changing generating sets, so we may speak of a group
G
itself being
chaotic. We emphasize that if
G
is chaotic, then it is not
\mathbb{Q}
CF
.

Let
H\leq G
be finitely generated with
finite generating sets
T
for
H
and
S
for
G
.
Assume
H\hookrightarrow G
is a
(A,B)
–quasiisometric embedding:
for all
h\in H
,

(1)

\tfrac{1}{A}d_{T}(h)-B\leq d_{S}(h)\leq Ad_{T}(h)+B.

For each
t\in T
, choose a word
w_{t}\in S^{*}
, geodesic in
\Gamma(G,S)
,
representing
t
in
H\leq G
, and define
the monoid homomorphism
h:T^{*}\to S^{*}
by extending
h(t)=w_{t}
for
t\in T
.
Let
\ell_{\max}:=\max_{t\in T}|w_{t}|
and
write

R:=(w_{t_{1}}\mid\cdots\mid w_{t_{m}})^{*}\subseteq S^{*}

where
T=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}
.

Lemma 3.2

(Block replacement preserves quasigeodesicity)
.

(a)

If
w\in T^{*}
labels a
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesic
in
\Gamma(H,T)
, then
h(w)
labels a

(\Lambda,\mathsf{E})
–quasigeodesic in
\Gamma(G,S)
with

(2)

\Lambda\;=\;A\,\lambda\,\ell_{\max},\qquad\mathsf{E}\;=\;2(\Lambda+1)\ell_{\max}+\Lambda B+\ell_{\max}\epsilon.

(b)

If
h(w)
labels a
(\Lambda,\mathsf{E})
–quasigeodesic in
\Gamma(G,S)
, then
w
labels a
(\lambda,\epsilon)
–quasigeodesic in
\Gamma(H,T)
with

(3)

\lambda\;=\;A\,\Lambda,\qquad\epsilon\;=\;\Lambda\,B+\mathsf{E}.

Proof.

To prove (a) we need to verify the
(\Lambda,\mathsf{E})
-quasigeodesic inequality for all subwords of
h(w)
.
Subwords of
h(w)
can be decomposed into blocks
w_{t_{i}}
, upto error at the
start and end. Concretely, any subword
v
of
h(w)
is of the form
v=ah(u)b
, where
u
is a subword of
w
,

a
is a proper suffix of some
w_{t_{i}}
, and
b
is a proper prefix of some
w_{t_{j}}
.

We have
d_{S}(h(u))\geq\tfrac{1}{A}d_{T}(u)-B

from (
1
), since
h(u)
represents the same element of
H
as
u
.
Also,
|h(u)|\leq|u|\ell_{\max}
since each of the
|u|
blocks in
h(u)
has
length at most
\ell_{\max}
. Further, since
w
is
(\lambda,\epsilon)
–quasigeodesic,
and
u
is a subword of
w
,
we have
|u|\leq\lambda d_{T}(u)+\epsilon
.
We account for the terms
a,b
via estimates
|v|\leq|h(u)|+2\ell_{\max}

and
d_{S}(v)\geq d_{S}(h(u))-2\ell_{\max}
.
Combining these shows that

\displaystyle|v|

\displaystyle\leq|h(u)|+2\ell_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq|u|\ell_{\max}+2\ell_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq(\lambda d_{T}(u)+\epsilon)\ell_{\max}+2\ell_{\max}

\displaystyle=A\lambda\ell_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{A}d_{T}(u)-B-2\ell_{\max}\right)+2(\Lambda+1)\ell_{\max}+\Lambda B+\ell_{\max}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda(d_{S}(h(u))-2\ell_{\max})+\mathsf{E}

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda d_{S}(v)+\mathsf{E}.

The proof of (b) is similar. Let

u
be a subword of
w
. Then
h(u)
is a subword of
h(w)
, so we have

|h(u)|\leq\Lambda d_{S}(h(u))+\mathsf{E}
. From (
1
) we
have
d_{S}(h)\leq Ad_{T}(h)+B
. Lastly
|u|\leq h(u)
since all
blocks
w_{t_{i}}
have positive length. Combining these gives

\displaystyle|u|

\displaystyle\leq|h(u)|\leq\Lambda d_{S}(0,h(u))+\mathsf{E}

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda(Ad_{T}(0,u)+B)+\mathsf{E}

\displaystyle=A\Lambda d_{T}(0,u)+\Lambda B+\mathsf{E}

\displaystyle=\lambda d_{T}(0,u)+\epsilon,

which shows that
w
is
(\lambda,\varepsilon)
-quasigeodesic.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(Inheriting from an undistorted subgroup)
.

If
(H,T)
is chaotic, where
T
is some finite generating set, then
(G,S)
is chaotic for all finite
generating sets
S
of
G
.

Proof.

Let
(H,T)
be chaotic at
(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
. Choose
constants
(\Lambda_{0},\mathsf{E}_{0})
according to (
2
) with

(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
in place of
(\lambda,\epsilon)
,
and let
L\subset S^{*}
be some CFL containing all
(\Lambda_{0},\mathsf{E}_{0})
-quasigeodesics in
\Gamma(G,S)
.
Define
L^{\prime}:=h^{-1}(L\cap R)\subseteq T^{*}
.
CFLs are closed under intersection with regular languages and inverse homomorphisms (see for example
[
19
]
), so
L^{\prime}
is a CFL.
By construction,
L^{\prime}
contains all
(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
-quasigeodesics in
\Gamma(H,T)
.
Let
\Lambda\geq 1,\mathsf{E}\geq 0
be arbitrary. Using (
3
),
choose
(\lambda,\epsilon)
so that
(\Lambda,\mathsf{E})
-quasigeodesics in
\Gamma(G,S)

are sent to
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesics in
\Gamma(H,T)
by
h^{-1}
.
Since
(H,T)
is chaotic at
(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
,

L^{\prime}
contains a word
w\in T^{*}
that is not

(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesic
in
\Gamma(H,T)
. Then
h(w)\in L
is not
(\Lambda,\mathsf{E})
-quasigeodesic in
\Gamma(G,S)
,
by the contrapositive of the reverse direction of
Lemma

3.2
. Since
\Lambda,\mathsf{E}
were arbitrary,
(G,S)
is chaotic at
(\Lambda_{0},\mathsf{E}_{0})
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

If
(H,T)
is chaotic, then so is
(H,T^{\prime})
for any other

.

Proof.

Clearly
H
is QI-embedded in itself. Then this follows immediately from

.
∎

The next technical lemma allows us to be more flexible with the generating set, which will be useful later.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
S
be a finite generating set for
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
and
a,b\in S
be any two independent generators.
For some
\lambda\geq 1
, and for
q\geq 1
,

w_{q}=(b^{-q}a^{2q}b^{q})a^{-q}(b^{q}a^{2q}b^{-q})

5.
Nilpotent groups

The goal is to prove that non-virtually-cyclic virtually
nilpotent groups are chaotic. This class of groups
is much bigger than the class for which the results of §4 apply: many nilpotent groups contain no undistorted
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
For example,
the discrete Heisenberg group of
matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}1&x&z\\
0&1&y\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
for
integer

is nilpotent but nonabelian, and contains no undistorted
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Our basic tool will be similar:
a spiral word that can be pumped using Ogden’s lemma
to give arbitrarily ‘bad’ quasigeodesics. We will
however need some results on the structure and metric distortion of nilpotent groups.

We will use the convention
[a,b]=a^{-1}b^{-1}ab
. If
G
is a group,
and
A,B\subseteq G
are subsets, then
[A,B]
denotes the subgroup generated
by
\{[a,b]:a\in A,b\in B\}
.
Let
G
be a group, set
G_{1}=G
,
and recursively define
G_{i+1}=[G_{i},G]
. Then we have

G=G_{1}\triangleright G_{2}\triangleright\cdots

which is the
lower central series
for
G
.
If eventually the
G_{i}
are all trivial,
G
is
nilpotent
.
In that case, the
nilpotency class
of

is the least

such that

G_{c+1}
is trivial.
The following is a standard fact:
if
H\leq G
is a finite index subgroup, then
H
is QI-embedded in
G
.
This implies that it suffices to consider nilpotent groups, and chaoticity
will immediately extend to virtually nilpotent groups.
Denote

and write

for the abelianization of
G
.
The next technical lemma arises from a MathOverflow post of Yves Cornulier
[
4
]
,
and is proven in more detail in
[
1
]
;
see Appendix B, Lemma 7 and discussion just preceding it.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

be nilpotent, where

for
some torsion abelian group

.
Then there is a nilpotent, finite-index subgroup

.

Theorem 5.2

If

Proof.

If

with trivial abelianization, i.e.

is virtually trivial and hence finite.
If

has free rank

Then

gives a finite-index nilpotent subgroup

In other cases

So from now on we assume that

is a finitely generated nilpotent group

has free rank at least

Let

Lemma 5.3

.

From

and

is freely reduced.

An arbitrary word in

can be reduced to this normal form
by performing the following rewritings:

•

Lemma 6.7

(
[
2
]
)
.

There exist constants
C,D>0
such that for every

written in
the normal form

, we have

d(g)\geq C(|w|+\log_{n/m}(|N|+1))-D.

In particular, we can take
C=1/(n+1)
and
D=\log_{n/m}(\tfrac{mn}{n-m})
.

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
g=a^{N^{\prime}}w(a,t)
where
w(a,t)
has the form described in
6.6
and
the letters
t,t^{-1}
appear
T

w(a,t)
. Then

d(g)\geq C_{1}|w|+C_{2}\log\left(1+\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{T}|N^{\prime}|\right)-D_{1}

for some constants
C_{1},C_{2},D_{1}>0
. If
N^{\prime}\geq 0
, these are given by

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n(n+1)},\qquad C_{2}:=\frac{1}{(n+1)\log(m/n)},

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle:=\log_{n/m}(\tfrac{nm}{n-m})+\frac{1}{n+1}\log_{n/m}(1+\tfrac{n^{2}}{n-m}).

Proof.

We give a proof for the case
N^{\prime}\geq 0
.
The proof for
N^{\prime}<0
is identical, save for some extra bookeeping
of constants. We can convert
g=a^{N^{\prime}}w(a,t)
to the normal form from
Lemma

6.6

by moving the large power
a^{N^{\prime}}
to the right via a sequence of rewrites.
Consider the generic rewrites
a^{X}t\to a^{r}ta^{X^{\prime}}
and
a^{X}t^{-1}\to a^{s}ta^{X^{\prime}}
.
In the first case we have
X=r+dn
and
X^{\prime}=dm
for some
r,d
,
which gives
X^{\prime}\geq(X-n)(m/n)=\tfrac{Xm}{n}-m
, and similarly

X^{\prime}\geq(X-m)(n/m)=\tfrac{Xn}{m}-n
for the second case. This gives

X^{\prime}\geq\min(\tfrac{Xn}{m}-n,\tfrac{Xm}{n}-m)\geq\tfrac{Xm}{n}-n.

Note that
a^{N^{\prime}}
may interact with intermediate powers of
a
in the word

w(a,t)
as it is pushed to the right; however, since these intermediate powers
are always positive by the definition of the normal form, the lower bound
for
X^{\prime}
still holds (here we use that
N^{\prime}\geq 0
).

Thus, once
a^{N^{\prime}}
has been pushed through
T

t
’s, we have

a^{N^{\prime}}w(a,t)\to w^{\prime}(a,t)a^{N^{\prime\prime}}
. After summing the geometric series resulting
from iterating the lower bound for
X^{\prime}
, we get

\displaystyle N^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\geq N^{\prime}\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{T}-\frac{n^{2}}{n-m}\left(1-\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)\right)^{T}

\displaystyle\geq N^{\prime}\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{T}-\frac{n^{2}}{n-m}.

Also, trivially
N^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
.
Now applying
Lemma

6.7
to
w^{\prime}(a,t)a^{N^{\prime\prime}}
, we have

d(g)=d(w^{\prime}(a,t)a^{N^{\prime\prime}})\geq\frac{1}{n+1}\left(|w^{\prime}|+\log_{n/m}(1+N^{\prime\prime})\right)-\log_{n/m}(\tfrac{mn}{n-m}).

Since at most
n

a
’s follow a
t
or
t^{-1}
in
w^{\prime}(a,t)
,

|w^{\prime}|\geq T\geq|w|/n
. We also have
N^{\prime\prime}\geq\max(0,N^{\prime}\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{T}-\frac{n^{2}}{n-m})
,
and using the concavity of the logarithm we get

\displaystyle d(g)\geq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{n(n+1)}|w|+\frac{1}{n+1}\log_{n/m}\left(1+\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{T}N^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n+1}\log_{n/m}(1+\tfrac{n^{2}}{n-m})-\log_{n/m}(\tfrac{nm}{n-m}).\qed

Lemma 6.9

such that for all subwords

•

Conclusion

We can combine the main feature of the chaoticity property (

)

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group,
containing an undistorted subgroup isomorphic to a
non-virtually-cyclic nilpotent group, or a Baumslag–Solitar group.
Then
G
is chaotic, and in particular, is not
\mathbb{Q}\emph{\bf{CF}}
.

The techniques in this paper do not extend well to the general case of

1.3
,
as we have made explicit use of particular structural properties of nilpotent and Baumslag–Solitar groups.
The crux of the problem lies in the fact that there seems to be little to link the algebraic and geometric structure of an arbitrary finitely generated group to the combinatorial structure of its quasigeodesic words.

Lastly, arguments in this paper say nothing about the minimal

(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
for which groups fail to have context-free
(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
-quasigeodesic
languages, because the
\mathbb{Q}\bf{CF}
property
is too coarse for this question.
We therefore end with the following more ambitious conjecture.

Conjecture 7.2
.

There exists a universal constant
\lambda_{0}
such that if
G
is a non-hyperbolic finitely generated group with generating set
S
,
then for all

\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, the

(\lambda_{0},0)
-quasigeodesics in
\Gamma(G,S)
do not
form a context-free language.
