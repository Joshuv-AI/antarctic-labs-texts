Conic Formulations of Transport Metrics for Unbalanced Measure Networks and Hypernetworks
Mary Chriselda Antony Oliver, Emmanuel Hartman, Tom Needham

Abstract
The Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) variant of optimal transport, designed to compare probability densities defined over distinct metric spaces, has emerged as an important tool for the analysis of data with complex structure, such as ensembles of point clouds or networks. To overcome certain limitations, such as the restriction to comparisons of measures of equal mass and sensitivity to outliers, several unbalanced or partial transport relaxations of the GW distance have been introduced in the recent literature. This paper is concerned with the Conic Gromov-Wasserstein (CGW) distance introduced by Séjourné, Vialard, and Peyré [ 35 ] . We provide a novel formulation in terms of semi-couplings, and extend the framework beyond the metric measure space setting, to compare more general network and hypernetwork structures. With this new formulation, we establish several fundamental properties of the CGW metric, including its scaling behavior under dilation, variational convergence in the limit of volume growth constraints, and comparison bounds with established optimal transport metrics. We further derive quantitative bounds that characterize the robustness of the CGW metric to perturbations in the underlying measures. The hypernetwork formulation of CGW admits a simple and provably convergent block coordinate ascent algorithm for its estimation, and we demonstrate the computational tractability and scalability of our approach through experiments on synthetic and real-world high-dimensional and structured datasets.

Introduction

Optimal transport (OT) techniques have emerged as a fundamental tool in applied mathematics
[
41
,
34
,
31
]
. Broadly speaking, OT theory encompasses methods for comparing probability distributions by determining the best way to allocate mass from one distribution to the other, where optimality is measured by a chosen objective function. Classical OT is defined in the setting of probability distributions over a fixed metric space, where the objective involves an integral of the metric function. The present paper is primarily concerned with the
Gromov-Wasserstein
(GW) variant of OT
[
28
,
29
]
, which allows comparison of distributions defined on distinct metric spaces, by replacing the linear objective of classical OT with a quadratic objective defined in terms of both of the relevant metrics. In particular, we focus on a recently introduced extension of GW distance—the
Conic Gromov-Wasserstein distance
(CGW) —which allows comparisons between general positive measures (i.e., not necessarily probability distributions) on distinct metric spaces
[
35
]
.

In our study of the CGW distance, we provide a new formulation, leading to extensions which enable comparison in more general classes of objects. Our formulation allows us to derive theoretical properties of the metric, including a precise characterization of how the classical GW distances arises as a variational limit of CGW distances, and a result which shows that the CGW distance is robust to noise. The novel formulation and extension also suggests an efficient numerical scheme for approximating CGW distance.

With a view toward precisely describing the contributions of this paper, we now briefly discuss some details of the OT framework and its extensions. Let
(X,\mathsf{d}_{X})
be a Polish metric space. For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the
p
-Wasserstein distance
between Borel probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
is

\mathsf{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\|\mathsf{d}_{X}\|_{\textrm{L}^{p}(\pi)},

(1)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the space of
measure couplings
of
\mu
and
\nu
(see Section
2.1
for details). This family of metrics is canonical, due to its well-understood theoretical
[
41
]
and computational
[
31
]
properties. However, it has some obvious shortcomings. First, it is unable to compare distributions with different total masses, which may be unnatural from the perspective of modeling in real applications. Second, due to its requirement of exactly coupling masses between
\mu
and
\nu
, it is sensitive to noise in the form of outlier contamination of the measures. There has been significant interest in extensions of this framework which overcome these deficiencies—see
[
21
,
20
,
8

11
,
32
]
, to name a few. Of particular interest to this paper is the

14
,
25

.

Now, consider the GW setting of distinct metric spaces. Let
\mathcal{M}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\mathsf{d}_{X})
and

where

is considered as a function

. Similar to the situation in classical OT, the GW distance is limited by its restriction to comparison of probability measures, and by its sensitivity to noise; several approaches have been introduced to mitigate these issues, e.g.,

2.1
Notation

We begin by recalling standard terminology and fixing general conventions and notation.

•

Let
X
and
Y
be Polish spaces, i.e., separable, completely metrizable topological spaces.

•

Metric spaces
(X,\mathsf{d}_{X})
will be assumed to be separable and complete.

•

Let
\mathbb{M}(X)
denote the set of positive Radon measures on
X
.

•

Absolute continuity of a measure
\mu
with respect to a measure
\nu
is denoted
\mu\ll\nu
.

•

We use
\mathbb{P}(X)
to denote the set of Borel probability measures on
X
.

•

Let
\mu_{X}\in\mathbb{P}(X)
be a probability measure and
T:X\to Y
be a Borel-measurable function. The
pushforward
of
\mu_{X}
by
T
is the measure
T_{\#}\mu_{X}\in\mathbb{P}(Y)
defined by
T_{\#}\mu_{X}(B)=\mu_{X}(T^{-1}(B))
, for all measurable sets
B\subset Y
.

•

We generically use
\operatorname{Pr}_{0}
and
\operatorname{Pr}_{1}
to denote projections from a product of two sets to its left and right factors, respectively. That is, given
X
and
Y
,
\operatorname{Pr}_{0}:X\times Y\to X
and
\operatorname{Pr}_{1}:X\times Y\to Y
are the obvious maps.

•

Given two probability measures
\mu_{X}\in\mathbb{P}(X)
and
\mu_{Y}\in\mathbb{P}(Y)
, we denote the set of
couplings
as
\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
. That is,
\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
if
\pi\in\mathbb{P}(X\times Y)
and
\operatorname{Pr}_{0}\#\pi=\mu
,
\operatorname{Pr}_{1}\#\pi=\nu
.

2.2
Conic Formulations for Unbalanced Optimal Transport

As we described in the introduction, the classical Wasserstein distance (
1
) between probability distributions over a fixed metric space has been extended in several ways to allow comparison of measures which may have unequal total mass. This type of extension is generally referred to as
unbalanced optimal transport
. We now describe the details of the particular approach taken in, e.g.,
[
13
,
14
,
35
]
.

In unbalanced optimal transport,
cone spaces
offer a natural geometric setting to define and compute transport distances. Topologically, the
cone
over a metric space
(X,\mathsf{d}_{X})
is simply
(X\times\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0})/(X\times\{0\})

[
7
]
. However, in the context of unbalanced mass transport, it is important to consider how the cone inherits a distance from the base space
X
. We will consider a general class of distances defined on the cone defined as follows.

Definition 1
(Cone Space).

Given a metric space
(X,\mathsf{d}_{X})
and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
the
cone over
X
with angle
\delta
, denoted
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]
, is defined to be the set
(X\times\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0})/(X\times\{0\})
, equipped with a distance inherited from the metric on
X
given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}:\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]\times\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}

(3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}([x,r],[y,s])^{2}

\displaystyle=4\delta^{2}\left(r^{2}+s^{2}-2rs\Omega\left(\frac{\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,y)}{2\delta}\right)\right)

(4)

where
\Omega:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
is a function satisfying the axioms

•

\Omega(0)=1
;

•

for all
\varepsilon>0
,
\Omega(\varepsilon)<1
;

•

\Omega(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime})\leq\Omega(\varepsilon)\Omega(\varepsilon^{\prime})
.

Convention 2
.

For simplicity in our notation, if
\delta=1/2
(a typical choice) we denote the cone and the associated distance by
(\mathcal{C}[X],\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}[X]})
.

Also note that we suppress the dependence on the function
\Omega
from the notation
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}
. In general, various quantities defined below which depend on a choice of
\Omega
will have this dependency suppressed from notation. On the other hand, the choice of
\delta
is typically represented in the notation, as the value of
\delta
will play a direct role in the analysis at various points.

We note that
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}[X]}
, in general, does not define a true metric since it may not satisfy the triangle inequality; however, it is always a positive, symmetric and definite. Another ingredient for comparing measures of different total mass is to relax the classical notion of couplings between measures. Instead of requiring both marginals of a single joint distribution to match the input measures, we fix only one marginal of two separate joint distributions. This is made precise in the following definition.

Definition 3
(Semi-couplings
[
14
]
).

Let
(X,\mu_{X})
,
(Y,\mu_{Y})
be two measure spaces. The set of all
semi-couplings
from
\mu_{X}
to
\mu_{Y}
is defined as,

\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})=\left\{(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\mathbb{M}(X\times Y)^{2}|(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma_{0}=\mu_{X},(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma_{1}=\mu_{Y}\right\}.

where
\operatorname{Pr}_{0}
and
\operatorname{Pr}_{1}
are the projections onto the first and second factor respectively.

Using cone geometry and semi-couplings, we can now formulate a generalized transport cost that accounts for both spatial displacement and mass variation defining a distance between measures with unequal mass.

Definition 4
(Conic Formulation of Unbalanced Optimal Transport
[
25
]
).

Given a metric space
(X,\mathsf{d}_{X})
, two Radon measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{M}(X)
,
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
, and choice of cone metric
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}
, the
unbalanced optimal transport distance
between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as,

\mathsf{UOT}_{\delta}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf\limits_{(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\mathbf{J}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),

where, given
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
,

\mathbf{J}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\int_{X\times X}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}\left(\left[x,\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)}\right],\left[y,\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)}\right]\right)^{2}\textnormal{d}\gamma.

(5)

This is well defined, as the value of (
5
) does not depeond on the choice of absolutely continuous reference measure
\gamma
. We note that, if
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}
satisfies the triangle inequality, then
\mathsf{UOT}_{\delta}
defines a metric for Radon measures on
X
. Different choices of the function
\Omega
yield different notions of unbalanced optimal transport, some of which correspond to well-known metrics. Below we provide two example of such UOT metrics.

•

Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao (or Hellinger-Kantorovich) Distance.

Let

\Omega(z)=\overline{\cos}\left(z\right):=\cos(\min(z,\pi/2)).

Then the corresponding cone distance is given by

\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}([x,r],[y,s])^{2}=4\delta^{2}\left(r^{2}+s^{2}-2rs\overline{\cos}\left(\frac{\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,y)}{2\delta}\right)\right).

(6)

This defines a metric on
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]

[
7
, Definition 3.6.16]
. Moreover, the associated unbalanced optimal transport distance is the Wassertein-Fisher-Rao (or Hellinger Kantorovich) distance which was introduced independently by
[
25
]
and
[
14
]
.

•

Gaussian Hellinger Distance.

Let
\Omega(z)=\exp(-z^{2})
. Then the cone distance takes the following form

\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}([x,r],[y,s])^{2}=4\delta^{2}\left(r^{2}+s^{2}-2rs\exp\left(-\frac{\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,y)^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}\right)\right).

(7)

Similarly, this gives a metric on the cone and the associated UOT distance was proposed in
[
25
]
.

2.3
Gromov-Wasserstein Distances

The Wasserstein distance was adapted by Mémoli
[
28
,
29
]
to allow for comparisons between probability measures defined over distinct metric spaces, via the formula (
2
). In fact, this formula makes sense even when the functions
\mathsf{d}_{X}
and
\mathsf{d}_{Y}
are not assumed to be metrics. This led a generalized notion of Gromov-Wasserstein distance, introduced in
[
15
]
, whose details we now recall.

Definition 5
(Measure Network
[
15
]
).

A
measure network
is a triple of the form

\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X}),

where
X
is a compact Polish space,
\mu_{X}\in\mathbb{M}(X)
with
\mu_{X}(X)<\infty
, and
\omega_{X}:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
is a measurable, bounded function. If
\mu_{X}(X)=1
, we refer to
\mathcal{N}_{X}
as a
probability measure network
.

In the case that
\omega_{X}
is a metric on
X
, the above recovers the notion of a
metric measure space
. On the other hand, this more general setting allows for kernels which appear naturally in applications; e.g.,
X
is the node set of a graph and
\omega_{X}
is a (weighted) adjacency function.

The definition of Gromov-Wasserstein distance extends without change to compare probability measure networks. That is, if
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
are probability measure networks, the
p
-Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) distance
between them is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{GW}_{p}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}\|_{\textrm{L}^{p}(\pi\otimes\pi)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\left(\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|^{p}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)^{1/p},

where the integral expression is valid in the
p<\infty
case. It is shown in
[
15
]
that
\mathsf{GW}_{p}
still defines a pseudometric on the space of probability measure networks, and the distance-zero equivalence relation is completely characterized (cf. Theorem
11
below).

2.4
Conic Gromov-Wasserstein Distance

For the rest of the paper, a
metric measure space (mm-space)

\mathcal{M}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\mathsf{d}_{X})
is allowed to have arbitrary (non-negative, finite) total mass
\mu_{X}(X)
. Analogous to the conic unbalanced optimal transport constructions described above, a similar trick can be applied to the GW distance to extend it to a distance between mm-spaces with distinct total masses. Such a construction was introduced in
[
35
]
, and we recall the definition here.

Definition 6
(Conic Gromov-Wasserstein
[
35
]
).

Let
\mathcal{M}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\mathsf{d}_{X})
and
\mathcal{M}_{Y}=(Y,\mu_{Y},\mathsf{d}_{Y})
be mm-spaces and let
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}[\mathbb{R}]}
be a metric on the (
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
) cone over
\mathbb{R}
. The
Séjourné-Vialard-Peyré conic Gromov Wasserstein distance
is defined as

\mathsf{CGW}_{\mathrm{SVP}}(\mathcal{M}_{X},\mathcal{M}_{Y})\coloneqq\inf\limits_{\alpha\in\mathrm{A}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\mathbf{K}(\alpha)^{1/2},

where:

•

\mathrm{A}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
is the space of measures

\begin{split}\mathrm{A}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})\coloneqq&\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{C}[X]\times\mathcal{C}[Y])\mid\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}r^{2}\,\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\alpha(\cdot,r)=\mu_{X},\right.\\
&\hskip 144.54pt\left.\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}s^{2}\,\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\alpha(\cdot,s)=\mu_{Y}\right\};\end{split}

(8)

•

the
SVP loss

\mathbf{K}:\mathrm{A}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(\alpha)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathcal{C}[X]\times\mathcal{C}[Y])^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}[\mathbb{R}]}\left([\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),rr^{\prime}],[\mathsf{d}_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}),ss^{\prime}]\right)^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt\textnormal{d}\alpha([x,r],[y,s])\textnormal{d}\alpha([x^{\prime},r^{\prime}],[y^{\prime},s^{\prime}]).

Observe that, although it also relies on cone metrics, this definition has a rather different flavor than the UOT distance construction in Definition
4
. The first goal of this paper is to reformulate CGW distance in terms of semicouplings. In doing so, we also extend it to the measure network setting.

[

3.1
Conic Gromov-Wasserstein Distance

The main construction that we study in this paper is defined as follows.

Definition 7
(Conic Gromov-Wasserstein Distance).

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}=(Y,\mu_{Y},\omega_{Y})
be measure networks,
\delta
a positive real number, and
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}
a metric on the cone over
\mathbb{R}
, as in Definition
1
. We define the associated
conic Gromov Wasserstein distance
(
\mathsf{CGW}
) as

\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})\coloneqq\inf\limits_{(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})^{1/2},

where:

•

\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
is the space of semicouplings, as in Definition
3
;

•

the
\mathsf{CGW}
loss

\mathbf{H}_{\delta}:\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(p^{\gamma_{0},\gamma}_{X}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),p^{\gamma_{1},\gamma}_{Y}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)^{2}\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

(10)

with
p^{\gamma_{0},\gamma}_{X},p^{\gamma_{1},\gamma}_{Y}:X\times Y\times X\times Y\to\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]
the functions defined by

p^{\gamma_{0},\gamma}_{X}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\left[\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right]

and

p^{\gamma_{1},\gamma}_{Y}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\left[\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}),\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right];

•

\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
is any absolutely continuous measure on
X\times Y
. The value of
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
does not depend on the choice of
\gamma
, due to 1-homogeneity (cf.
[
14

\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\omega_{X},\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma},\omega_{Y},\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\right)
the quantity

\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\left[\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right],\left[\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}),\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right]\right).

In particular, this notation suppresses function arguments, and will be used when doing so doesn’t cause confusion. With this convention, the CGW loss can be expressed as

\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\omega_{X},\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma},\omega_{Y},\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\right)^{2}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}.

3.2
Metric Properties

A certain equivalence relation for measure networks, called
weak isomorphism
, was introduced by Chowdhury and Mémoli in
[
15
]
, and an appropriate generalization will be useful in the conic setting. We give the definition below in the context of measure networks without any mass constraint, whereas
[
15
]
specifically considered
probability
measure networks; this generalization is only superficial.

Definition 10
(Weak Isomorphism).

Measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}=(Y,\mu_{Y},\omega_{Y})
are called
weakly isomorphic
if there exists a Polish space
Z
equipped with a Borel measure
\mu_{Z}
and measurable maps
f:Z\to X
and
g:Z\to Y
such that the following holds:

•

f_{\#}\mu_{Z}=\mu_{X}
,

•

g_{\#}\mu_{Z}=\mu_{Y}
,

•

and
\|f^{\#}\omega_{X}-g^{\#}\omega_{Y}\|_{\textrm{L}^{\infty}(\mu_{Z}\otimes\mu_{Z})}=0
.

where
f^{\#}\omega_{X}:Z\times Z\to\mathbb{R}
is the pullback weight function
(z,z^{\prime})\mapsto\omega_{X}(f(z),f(z^{\prime}))
, and
g^{\#}\omega_{Y}
is defined analogously.

A key result of Chowdhury and Mémoli is that the Gromov-Wasserstein distance
\mathsf{GW}_{p}
defines a metric on the space of probability measure networks, considered up to weak isomorphism
[
15
, Theorems 2.3 and 2.4]
. The following result for conic Gromov-Wasserstein is analogous.

Theorem 11
.

The conic Gromov-Wasserstein distance
\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}
is a metric on the space of measure networks, considered up to weak isomorphism.

The proof uses the following lemmas.

Lemma 12
.

Given two compact measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}=(Y,\mu_{Y},\omega_{Y})
there exists an optimal semi-coupling
(\gamma_{0}^{*},\gamma_{1}^{*})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
that attains
\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})
.

Proof.

This proof follows exactly as in
[
14
]
. In particular,
[
14
, Lemma 2.9]
gives general conditions for weak* lower semi-continuity for functionals on spaces of measures, and it applies in our case to give weak* lower semi-continuity of
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}
. Furthermore, since
X,Y
are compact and
\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}
have finite measure, we conclude
\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
is weak* compact (see
[
14
, Proposition 3.4]
for more details). Thus, we can conclude the existence of minimizers.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
be measure networks,
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
a semicoupling and
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
an absolutely continuous measure. The
\mathsf{CGW}
loss
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
is equal to

\displaystyle 4\delta^{2}\big(\mu_{X}(X)^{2}+\mu_{Y}(Y)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad-8\delta^{2}\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\,\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

where
\Omega
is the function defining
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}
(Definition
1
).

Proof.

Using the structure of
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}
(see Definition
1
), and recalling the definitions of the functions
p_{X}^{\gamma_{0},\gamma}
and
p_{Y}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma}
(see Definition
7
), we have that the integrand in (
10
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}(p_{X}^{\gamma_{0},\gamma}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),p_{Y}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=4\delta^{2}\left(\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)

(11)

\displaystyle\qquad-8\delta^{2}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}.

(12)

Integrating the term (
12
) against
\gamma\otimes\gamma
obviously yields the second term in the proposed formula, so consider the integral of the term (
11
):

\displaystyle 4\delta^{2}\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\;\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=4\delta^{2}\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+4\delta^{2}\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=4\delta^{2}\mu_{X}(X)^{2}+4\delta^{2}\mu_{Y}(Y)^{2},

where we have applied the marginal conditions of the semicoupling
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
. This gives the first term of the claimed formula, so the proof is complete.

We now proceed with the proof of Theorem
11
, which follows similarly to the analogous result for
\mathsf{GW}_{p}
. However, a key step leveraging the AM-GM inequality offers an insight into the behavior of the
\mathsf{CGW}
distance, so we provide necessary details in the positive-definiteness part of the proof.

Proof of Theorem
11
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
be measure networks. Non-negativity of
\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})
is obvious. Symmetry of
\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}
follows from the observations that
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
implies
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0})\in\Gamma(\mu_{Y},\mu_{X})
and that
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}
is symmetric.

To derive the triangle inequality, let
\mathcal{N}_{X}
,
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
, and
\mathcal{N}_{Z}
be measure networks. Then, by the gluing lemma for semi-couplings proved in
[
14
, Theorem 3.7]
, a standard argument (cf.
[
15
, Theorem 2.3]
for the analogous proof in the Gromov-Wasserstein setting, which follows easily from the classical gluing lemma) shows

\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},N_{Z})\leq\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})+\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{Y},N_{Z}).

It only remains to establish the vanishing condition for
\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
are weakly isomorphic and let
(Z,\mu_{Z})
be a metric space with
f:Z\to X
and
g:Z\to Y
satisfying the conditions of Definition
10
. Let
(f,g):Z\to X\times Y
denote the map
z\mapsto(f(z),g(z))
and set
\gamma\coloneqq(f,g)_{\#}\mu_{Z}
. Then
(\gamma,\gamma)\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
, whence we obtain (using notational Convention
9
)

\displaystyle\mathsf{CGW}^{2}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}([\omega_{X},1],[\omega_{Y},1])^{2}\textnormal{d}\gamma\textnormal{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{Z^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}([f^{\#}\omega_{X},1],[g^{\#}\omega_{Y},1])^{2}\textnormal{d}\mu_{Z}\textnormal{d}\mu_{Z}=0.

Next, assume
\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})=0
. Let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
be an optimal semi-coupling (guaranteed to exist, by Lemma
12
), so that, by Lemma
13
and the calculations in its proof, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=4\delta^{2}\int_{X\times Y}\int_{X\times Y}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt-2\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 90.3375pt\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\,\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Since the integrand is always non-negative and
\Omega(x)\leq 1
, this implies that,
\gamma\otimes\gamma
-almost everywhere,

\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}(x,y)\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

By the AM-GM inequality,
\gamma\otimes\gamma
-almost everywhere,

\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

So,
\gamma
-almost everywhere, we have

\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)=\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y).

Indeed, if
(x,y)\in\mathrm{supp}(\gamma)
, then
\big((x,y),(x,y)\big)\in\mathrm{supp}(\gamma\otimes\gamma)
, hence

\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)^{2}=\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)^{2},

and the claim follows by non-negativity of the densities.
Therefore,
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{1}
. Let
Z=X\times Y
,
\mu_{Z}=\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{1}
and
f:X\times Y\to X
and
g:X\times Y\to Y
the obvious projection maps. Thus,
f_{\#}\mu_{Z}=\mu_{X}
,
g_{\#}\mu_{Z}=\mu_{Y}
. Moreover, Lemma
13
gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})=4\delta^{2}\left(2\mu_{Z}(Z)^{2}-2\int_{Z^{2}}\Omega\left(\frac{|f^{\#}\omega_{X}-g^{\#}\omega_{Y}|}{2\delta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{Z}\mathrm{d}\mu_{Z}\right).

Since
\Omega\leq 1
, this is only possible if the integrand is equal to one
\mu_{Z}\otimes\mu_{Z}
-almost everywhere. This implies that the argument of
\Omega
is equal to zero
\mu_{Z}\otimes\mu_{Z}
-almost everywhere; that is,
\|f^{\#}\omega_{X}-g^{\#}\omega_{Y}\|_{\textrm{L}^{\infty}(\mu_{Z}\otimes\mu_{Z})}=0
. It follows that
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
are weakly isomorphic.

3.3
Equivalence to the Formulation of Séjourné, Vialard and Peyré

A short argument shows that that the optimization problems defining
\mathsf{CGW}_{1/2}
(as in Definition
7
) and the original formulation of Conic Gromov Wasserstein distance
\mathsf{CGW}_{\mathrm{SVP}}
(as in Definition
6
) are, in fact, equivalent.

Proposition 14
(Equivalence to the Formulation of
[
35
]
).

For mm-spaces
\mathcal{M}_{X}
and
\mathcal{M}_{Y}
,

\mathsf{CGW}_{1/2}(\mathcal{M}_{X},\mathcal{M}_{Y})=\mathsf{CGW}_{\mathrm{SVP}}(\mathcal{M}_{X},\mathcal{M}_{Y}).

(13)

Proof.

Let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
and let
\gamma\in\mathbb{M}(X\times Y)
be such that
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\ll\gamma
.
Consider the map

\displaystyle T:X\times Y

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{C}[X]\times\mathcal{C}[Y]

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\left[x,\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)}\right],\left[y,\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)}\right]\right),

then define
\alpha:=T_{\#}\gamma\in\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{C}[X]\times\mathcal{C}[Y])
. Note that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}r^{2}\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\alpha(\cdot,r)=\int_{X\times Y}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(\cdot,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma(\cdot,y)=\mu_{X}

and similarly,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}s^{2}\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\alpha(\cdot,s)=\mu_{Y}.

So,
\alpha\in\mathrm{A}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}).

By definition,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{1/2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\bigg[\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt-2\,\Omega\big(|\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\mathsf{d}_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 83.11005pt\cdot\left.\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x,y)}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 216.81pt\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathcal{C}(X)\times\mathcal{C}(Y))^{2}}\bigg[(rr^{\prime})^{2}+(ss^{\prime})^{2}-2\,rr^{\prime}ss^{\prime}\,\Omega\big(|\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\mathsf{d}_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 155.38005pt\mathrm{d}\alpha([x,r],[y,s])\,\mathrm{d}\alpha([x^{\prime},r^{\prime}],[y^{\prime},s^{\prime}])

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}(\alpha),

where the second line follows by the change of variables, hence

\mathsf{CGW}_{1/2}(\mathcal{M}_{X},\mathcal{M}_{Y})\geq\mathsf{CGW}_{\mathrm{SVP}}(\mathcal{M}_{X},\mathcal{M}_{Y}).

(14)

To prove the other inequality, let
\alpha\in\mathrm{A}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
.
Define

\gamma_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}r^{2}\textrm{d}\alpha([\cdot,r],[\cdot,s])\in\mathbb{M}(X\times Y)

and

\gamma_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}s^{2}\textrm{d}\alpha([\cdot,r],[\cdot,s])\in\mathbb{M}(X\times Y).

Note that

(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma_{0}=\int_{Y}\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}(\cdot,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times Y\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}r^{2}\textnormal{d}\alpha([\cdot,r],[y,s])=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}r^{2}\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\alpha(\cdot,r)=\mu_{X}

and

(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma_{1}=\int_{X}\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}(x,\cdot)=\int_{X\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}s^{2}\textnormal{d}\alpha([x,r],[\cdot,s])=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}s^{2}\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\alpha(\cdot,s)=\mu_{Y}.

Therefore,
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
.
A similar argument to the one above shows that
\mathbf{H}_{1/2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\mathbf{K}(\alpha)
, so that

\mathsf{CGW}_{1/2}(\mathcal{M}_{X},\mathcal{M}_{Y})\leq\mathsf{CGW}_{\mathrm{SVP}}(\mathcal{M}_{X},\mathcal{M}_{Y}).

(15)

4
Properties of the Conic Gromov-Wasserstein Distance

This section establishes various theoretical properties of the Conic Gromov-Wasserstein distance.

4.1
Scaling Properties

When generalizing transport metrics to the unbalanced case, it is crucial to understand how the metric behaves with respect to scaling in the total mass of the networks. To do so, we leverage the following result from
[
23
]
.

Lemma 15
(
[
23
, Lemma 2.1]
).

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and consider the cone over
X
,
\mathcal{C}[X]
equipped with a distance
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}
from Definition
1
. Then

\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}^{2}([x,tt^{\prime}],[y,ss^{\prime}])=t^{\prime}s^{\prime}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}^{2}([x,t],[y,s])+(t^{\prime 2}-t^{\prime}s^{\prime})t^{2}+(s^{\prime 2}-t^{\prime}s^{\prime})s^{2}.

Directly following from this, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}^{2}([x,tr],[y,sr])

\displaystyle=r^{2}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}^{2}([x,t],[y,s])+(r^{2}-rr)s^{2}+(r^{2}-rr)t^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle=r^{2}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}^{2}([x,t],[y,s])

and

\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}^{2}([x,t^{\prime}],[x,s^{\prime}])=t^{\prime}s^{\prime}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[X]}^{2}([x,1],[x,1])+(t^{\prime 2}-t^{\prime}s^{\prime})+(s^{\prime 2}-t^{\prime}s^{\prime})=(t^{\prime}-s^{\prime})^{2}.

(17)

We now show how the CGW distance behaves under measure scaling.

Proposition 16
.

Given a measure network
\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
. Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}^{s}=(X,s\cdot\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
, and
\mathcal{N}_{X}^{r}=(X,r\cdot\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
where
r,s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Then we have

\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X}^{s},\mathcal{N}_{X}^{r})\leq|r-s|\mu_{X}(X).

(18)

Proof.

Consider the diagonal map
\bigtriangleup:X\to X\times X
. Define
\gamma_{0}=r\cdot\bigtriangleup_{\#}\mu_{X}
,
\gamma_{1}=s\cdot\bigtriangleup_{\#}\mu_{X}
, and
\gamma=\bigtriangleup_{\#}\mu_{X}
.
Thus,
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(r\cdot\mu_{X},s\cdot\mu_{X})
and
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
with
\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}=r
and
\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}=s
.
We then have (using notational Convention
9
)

\displaystyle\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X}^{s},\mathcal{N}_{X}^{r})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(X\times X)^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\omega_{X},\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma},\omega_{Y},\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\right)^{2}\textnormal{d}\gamma\textnormal{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{(X\times X)^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\omega_{X},r,\omega_{X},s\right)^{2}\textnormal{d}\gamma\textnormal{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{(X\times X)}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\left[\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),r\right],\left[\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),s\right]\right)^{2}\textnormal{d}\mu(x)\textnormal{d}\mu(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(r-s)^{2}\mu_{X}(X)^{2}.

Note that the final equality follows directly from (
17
).

Proposition 17
.

Given measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
, and
r\geq 0
, we have

\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X}^{r},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{r})=r\cdot\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}).

(19)

Proof.

Let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
be a semicoupling attaining
\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})
, and let
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
. Define
\pi_{0}=r\cdot\gamma_{0}
and
\pi_{1}=r\cdot\gamma_{1}
. Thus,
(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})\in\Gamma(r\cdot\mu_{X},r\cdot\mu_{Y})
and
\gamma\ll\pi_{0},\pi_{1}
with
\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}=r\cdot\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}
and
\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}=r\cdot\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X}^{r},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{r})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(X\times X)^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\omega_{X},\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma},\omega_{Y},\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\right)^{2}\textnormal{d}\gamma\textnormal{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{(X\times X)^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\omega_{X},r\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma},\omega_{Y},r\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\right)^{2}\textnormal{d}\gamma\textnormal{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=r^{2}\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})^{2}

Note that if
r=0
the equality (
19
) holds trivially. For
r>0
, the inequality established above gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}((\mathcal{N}_{X}^{1/r})^{r},(\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{1/r})^{r})

\displaystyle\leq r\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X}^{1/r},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{1/r})\leq\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}).

The inequalities in this string are therefore forced to be equalities, which proves (
19
).

Putting the results of this subsection together and applying the triangle inequality for CGW distance, the following corollary is immediate.

Corollary 18
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}
be measure networks and let
r,s\geq 0
. Then

\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X}^{r},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{s})\leq\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})+|1-r|\mu_{X}(X)+|1-s|\mu_{Y}(Y).

4.2
Variational Convergence

We now recall the notion of
\Gamma
-convergence for functions on a metric space
(X,d)
.

Definition 19
(
\Gamma
-Convergence).

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
\{\mathcal{E}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of functionals
\mathcal{E}_{n}:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
. We say that
\mathcal{E}_{n}

\Gamma
-converges
to a functional
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
as
n\to\infty
, denoted
\mathcal{E}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\to}}\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
, if for all
u\in X
we have:

1.

(
Lim-Inf Inequality
) for every
u\in X
and every sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
X
such that
u_{n}\to u
, it holds that
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(u)\leq\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u_{n})
;

2.

(
Lim-Sup Inequality
) for every
u\in X
, there exists a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
X
with
u_{n}\to u
such that
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(u)\geq\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u_{n})
.

This concept was introduced by De Giorgi and Franzoni in 1970’s to study limits of variational problems
[
17
]
; we refer to
[
5
,
6
]
for comprehensive introductions to
\Gamma
-convergence. Rather than requiring pointwise or uniform convergence,
\Gamma
-convergence ensures that the limiting behavior of minimizers is preserved: any sequence of approximate minimizers of the original functionals will converge (under suitable compactness conditions) to a minimizer of the limiting functional.

The main theorem for this subsection treats variational convergence of the CGW cost functional. Its statement requires some additional definitions and notation. For measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
, consider the augmented functional

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}:\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})&\to\mathbb{R}\\
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})&\mapsto\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})-4\delta^{2}\left(\mu_{X}(X)-\mu_{Y}(Y)\right)^{2},\end{split}

where
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}
is as in (
10
). We note that, applying Lemma
13
, this functional can be conveniently rewritten as

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})&=8\delta^{2}\Bigg(\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{Y}(Y)\\
&\qquad\qquad\left.-\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|}{2\delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma\right),\end{split}

(20)

where the function arguments in the integrand are suppressed to save space, but should agree with those in Lemma
13
.
We assume throughout the rest of this subsection that the function
\Omega:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies both a global lower bound and a local upper bound. Specifically, there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that

\Omega(z)\geq 1-Cz^{2}\qquad\text{for all }z\geq 0,

(21)

and constants
C,C^{\prime}\geq 0
together with a small parameter
a_{1}>0
such that

\Omega(z)\leq 1-Cz^{2}+C^{\prime}z^{4}\qquad\text{for all }z\in\left[0,\frac{M}{a_{1}}\right].

(22)

The upper bound apply uniformly for
z:=\frac{|\omega_{X}(x,y)-\omega_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|}{2\delta}
,
where
\delta>a_{1}
, and
\omega_{X},\omega_{Y}
are measurable bounded functions satisfying
|\omega_{X}(x,y)-\omega_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|\leq M
for all
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in X\times Y
.

These bounds are satisfied, for instance, by the following choices of
\Omega(z)
:

1-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\leq\Omega(z)=\overline{\cos}(z)\leq 1-\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\frac{z^{4}}{24},

(23)

1-z^{2}\leq\Omega(z)=\exp(-z^{2})\leq 1-z^{2}+\frac{z^{4}}{2}.

(24)

Finally, we introduce the functional

\mathbf{H}_{\infty}:\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},

which is defined according to the following cases:

•

If
\gamma_{0}=0
or
\gamma_{1}=0
(equivalently,
\mu_{X}=0
or
\mu_{Y}=0
), then
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\coloneqq 0
.

•

If
\gamma_{1}=\alpha\gamma_{0}
for
\alpha\coloneqq\mu_{Y}(Y)/\mu_{X}(X)
(assuming
\mu_{X}(X)>0
), then

\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\coloneqq 2C\alpha\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|^{2}\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

where
C
is as in (
21
) and (
22
).

•

If neither of the previous conditions apply, then
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\coloneqq\infty
.

With the definitions and assumptions above, the main result of this subsection is stated as follows.

Theorem 20
(
\Gamma
-Convergence for CGW).

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
be measure networks, and let
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}
be the functionals defined above. Then we have
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\to}}

\mathbf{H}_{\infty}
.

The strategy of the proof is inspired by that of
[
14
, Theorem 5.10]
, which considers variational convergence in the WFR setting. We will use the following preliminary results, which both assume fixed measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
and the polynomial estimates on
\Omega
described above.

Lemma 21
.

For sufficiently large
\delta
, the adapted CGW loss
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}
can be expressed as

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=8\delta^{2}U(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})+2CV(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})-\frac{C^{\prime}}{2\delta^{2}}W(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),

(25)

where

•

C
and
C^{\prime}
are as in (
21
) and (
22
), respectively;

•

U
is a lower semi-continuous function such that
U(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
\gamma_{0}=0
,
\gamma_{1}=0
, or
\gamma_{0}=\frac{\mu_{Y}(Y)}{\mu_{X}(X)}\gamma_{1}
;

•

V
is the lower semi-continuous function defined by

V(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\coloneqq\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}(\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y})^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma

(where function arguments in the integrands are supressed for space);

•

W
is a non-negative, bounded function.

Proof.

Using the bounds (
21
) and (
22
), and the expression
20
, there must be a number
I(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=

\displaystyle 8\delta^{2}\left[\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{Y}(Y)-\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma\right]

\displaystyle+2C\left[\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}(\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y})^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{C^{\prime}}{2\delta^{2}}\left[I(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}(\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y})^{4}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma\right].

The functions
U
,
V
and
W
are then defined accordingly by the bracketed formulas in the respective lines of this expression. It remains to show that these functions satisfy the claimed properties.

The function
U
is lower semi-continuous by
[
14
, Lemma 2.9]
. Moreover, Cauchy-Schwarz applied to the functions

(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\mapsto\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})},\qquad i\in\{0,1\},

(26)

implies that

\displaystyle\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma\right)^{1/2}=\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{Y}(Y),

with equality if and only if the
i=0
and
i=1
instances of the function (
26
) are linearly dependent. An argument similar to the one used in the proof of Theorem
11
shows that this implies that
\gamma_{0}=0
or
\gamma_{1}=0
or
\gamma_{1}=\alpha\gamma_{0}
for some constant
\alpha
, which is then forced to be
\alpha=\mu_{Y}(Y)/\mu_{X}(X)
.

The function
V
is lower semi-continuous, also by
[
14
, Lemma 2.9]
. Finally,
W
is bounded due to the fact that
I(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in[0,1]
and
\omega_{X}
and
\omega_{Y}
are assumed to be bounded kernels.

Corollary 22
.

Fix natural numbers
\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}
with
\delta_{1}
sufficiently large, in the sense of Lemma
21
. Let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
and let
\{(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\}_{\delta\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
which converges weakly to
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
. Then there exists a constant
K
(depending on the measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
, and the polynomial constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
) such that

\liminf_{\delta\to\infty}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\geq\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta_{1}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})+8(\delta_{2}^{2}-\delta_{1}^{2})U(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})-\frac{K}{\delta_{1}^{2}},

with
U
as in Lemma
21
.

Proof.

For
\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}\leq\delta
, with
\delta_{1}
sufficiently large, the expression from Lemma
21
yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})-\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta_{1}}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=8(\delta^{2}-\delta_{1}^{2})U(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})-\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\right)W(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta}).

We now assume without loss of generality that
\delta\geq\delta_{1}^{2}
, so that, also invoking the boundedness of
W
, we may choose
K
satisfying

-\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right)W(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\geq-\frac{K}{\delta_{1}^{2}}.

The work above then gives way to the bound

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\geq\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta_{1}}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})+8(\delta_{2}^{2}-\delta_{1}^{2})U(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})-\frac{K}{\delta_{1}^{2}}.

The desired inequality follows by taking the lim-inf and applying lower semi-continuity of the functions
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta_{1}}
and
U
.

We proceed with the proof of the main result of this subsection.

Proof of Theorem
20
.

We begin with the
lim-sup inequality
. Let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
and consider the constant sequence
\{(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\}_{\delta\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\gamma_{0,\delta}=\gamma_{0}
,
\gamma_{1,\delta}=\gamma_{1}
for all
\delta
. It is obvious that
(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\to(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
, so we wish to show that
\limsup\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\leq\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
. We do so by checking the cases in the definition of
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}
.

First, consider the case that
\gamma_{0}=0
or
\gamma_{1}=0
. Then it follows easily from the expression (
20
) that

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})=0=\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),

and the lim-sup inequality follows.

Next, consider the case where
\gamma_{1}=\alpha\gamma_{0}
, for
\alpha=\mu_{Y}(Y)/\mu_{X}(X)
. Using the expression (
20
) and the bound (
21
), we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\alpha\gamma_{0})

\displaystyle=8\delta^{2}\left(\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{Y}(Y)-\alpha\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|}{2\delta}\right)\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 8\delta^{2}\left(\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{Y}(Y)-\alpha\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}1-C\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|}{2\delta}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 8\delta^{2}\left(\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{Y}(Y)-\alpha\mu_{X}(X)^{2}+\frac{C\alpha}{4\delta^{2}}\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\left(\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=2C\alpha\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\left(\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}=\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}).

The lim-sup inequality in this case then follows.

In the last case of the definition of
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}
(where
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}=\infty
), the lim-sup inequality is obvious. The proof of the lim-sup inequality is therefore complete.

Next, we prove the
lim-inf inequality
. Let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
be the limit of a sequence
\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
,
\{(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\}_{\delta\in\mathbb{N}}
. We establish the inequality by checking cases.

To check the first case of
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}
, assume without loss of generality that
\gamma_{0}=0
. Then
\mu_{X}=0
, so that
\gamma_{0,\delta}=0
for all
\delta
. In turn, it follows easily that
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})=0
for all
\delta
, whence the lim-inf inequality follows.

Next, suppose that
\gamma_{1}=\alpha\gamma_{0}
, for
\alpha=\mu_{Y}(Y)/\mu_{X}(X)
(
\mu_{X}(X)\neq 0
). By Corollary
22
and the fact that
U(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=0
(from Lemma
21
), we have

\liminf_{\delta\to\infty}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\geq\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta_{1}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})-\frac{K}{\delta_{1}^{2}}

for all sufficiently large
\delta_{1}
. In particular, Lemma
21
yields

\displaystyle\liminf_{\delta\to\infty}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\delta_{1}\to\infty}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta_{1}}(\gamma_{0},\alpha\gamma_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\delta_{1}\to\infty}8\delta_{1}^{2}\cdot 0+2CV(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})-\frac{C^{\prime}}{2\delta_{1}^{2}}W(\gamma_{0},\alpha\gamma_{0})

\displaystyle=2CV(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=2C\alpha\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}=\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

Finally, consider the case where
\mathbf{H}_{\infty}=\infty
. By Corollary
22
,

\liminf_{\delta\to\infty}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})\geq\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta_{1}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})+8(\delta_{2}^{2}-\delta_{1}^{2})U(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})-\frac{K}{\delta_{1}^{2}},

for all sufficiently large
\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}
. Our assumptions on
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
imply, by Lemma

, that
U(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})>0
. Since we can take
\delta_{2}
arbitrarily larger than
\delta_{1}
, it follows that

\liminf_{\delta\to\infty}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0,\delta},\gamma_{1,\delta})=\infty=\mathbf{H}_{\infty}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),

and this completes the proof.

Next, we state a corollary linking our CGW metric defined in (

Corollary 23
.

For probability measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}

,

\lim_{\delta\to\infty}\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}\right)=\sqrt{2C}\cdot\mathsf{GW}_{2}\left(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}\right),

4.3
Bounds on
\mathsf{CGW}

Next, we establish bounds for the
\mathsf{CGW}
metric. The first is an upper bound by the
\mathsf{GW}_{2}
distance, which is intuitively and formally obvious, as the
\mathsf{CGW}
distance allows additional flexibility in probabilistic matching between the spaces.

Proposition 24
(Upper Bound of
\mathsf{CGW}
metric).

Let
\Omega(z)=\overline{\cos}(z)
or
\Omega(z)=e^{-z^{2}}
. For probability measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
, we have

2\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})\geq\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}).

Proof.

Recall by (
23
) and (
24
), we have
x^{2}\geq 1-\Omega(z)
. Substituting
x=\frac{z}{2\delta}
we have,
\frac{z^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}\geq 1-\Omega\left(\frac{z}{2\delta}\right)
. Therefore
2z^{2}\geq 4\delta^{2}\left(2-2\Omega\left(\frac{z}{2\delta}\right)\right)
. Let
\pi
be an optimal coupling that attains
\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})
. Then

\displaystyle 4\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}|\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\mathsf{d}_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|^{2}d\pi d\pi

\displaystyle\geq 4\delta^{2}\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}2-2\Omega\left(\frac{|\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\mathsf{d}_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)d\pi d\pi

\displaystyle\geq 4\delta^{2}\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}([\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),1],[\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}),1])d\pi d\pi

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})^{2}.

Remark 25
.

If
\Omega(z)=\overline{\cos}(z)
by (
23
),
\frac{x^{2}}{2}\geq 1-\Omega(x)
. Following the change in constant, we can make our estimate sharper. In particular, we have

\sqrt{2}\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})\geq\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}).

Next we establish a lower bound by the UOT metric. This is analogous to the bounds on

Proposition 26
(Lower Bound of
\mathsf{CGW}

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
be measure networks. Define
\nu_{X}
and
\nu_{Y}
as pushforward measures on

\nu_{X}\coloneqq(\omega_{X})_{\#}(\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{X})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nu_{Y}\coloneqq(\omega_{Y})_{\#}(\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{Y}).

If
\delta>0

\mathsf{UOT}_{\delta}(\nu_{X},\nu_{Y})\leq\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}).

Proof.

Let us define
g:X\times Y\times X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
via

(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\mapsto(\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}))
.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{UOT}_{\delta}(\nu_{X},\nu_{Y})^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle=\nu_{X}(\mathbb{R})+\nu_{Y}(\mathbb{R})

(27)

\displaystyle-2\sup_{(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})\in\Gamma(\nu_{X},\nu_{Y})}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\Omega\left(\frac{|u-v|}{2\delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\pi}(u,v)}\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\pi}(u,v)}\textnormal{d}\pi

\displaystyle=\mu_{X}(X)^{2}+\mu_{Y}(Y)^{2}

\displaystyle-2\sup_{(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})\in\Gamma(\nu_{X},\nu_{Y})}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\Omega\left(\frac{|u-v|}{2\delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\pi}(u,v)}\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\pi}(u,v)}\textnormal{d}\pi

Meanwhile,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{CGW}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})^{2}}{4\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{X}(X)^{2}+\mu_{Y}(Y)^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle-2\sup_{(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})

with

\int_{(X\times Y)^{2}}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)\\
\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\Omega\left(\frac{|u-v|}{2\delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}g_{\#}(\gamma_{0}\otimes\gamma_{0})}{\textnormal{d}g_{\#}(\gamma\otimes\gamma)}(u,v)}\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}g_{\#}(\gamma_{1}\otimes\gamma_{1})}{\textnormal{d}g_{\#}(\gamma\otimes\gamma)}(u,v)}\textnormal{d}g_{\#}(\gamma\otimes\gamma)(u,v)\\
\leq\sup_{(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})\in\Gamma(\nu_{X},\nu_{Y})}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\overline{\cos}\left(\frac{|u-v|}{2\delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\pi}(u,v)}\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\pi_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\pi}(u,v)}\textnormal{d}\pi.

(29)

Thus,
\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})\geq\mathsf{UOT}_{\delta}(\nu_{X},\nu_{Y})
.

4.4
Robustness Guarantees

We analyze the robustness of the
\mathsf{CGW}
metric under contamination by outliers. This is motivated by the known sensitivity of several optimal transport-based distances, including the Wasserstein
\mathsf{W}_{2}

[
2
, Section Discussion]
, Gromov-Wasserstein (
\mathsf{GW}_{2}
)
[
22
]
, and Co-Optimal Transport (
\mathsf{COT}
)
[
38
, Proposition 2]
distances to perturbations in the marginal distributions. Even a small fraction of outliers can induce significant distortions, making such distances unstable in practice.

To mitigate this sensitivity, several robust variants have been introduced. For example, the Robust Gromov-Wasserstein distance (
\mathrm{RGW}
)
[
22
]
was explicitly designed to prevent divergence of the
\mathsf{GW}_{2}
distance as the separation between inliers and outliers increases. Related approaches include Unbalanced Optimal Transport
[
19
, Lemma 1.1]
, Robust Wasserstein
[
32
, Theorem 3.1]
, Unbalanced Gromov-Wasserstein
[
22
, Remark 2.4]
, Partial and Robust Gromov-Wasserstein
[
12
, Corollary 4.13]
, and Unbalanced Co-Optimal Transport
[
38
, Theorem 1]
distances, for which lower bounds on robustness have been established.

In the following, we use
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
to denote the total variation norm on the space of measures over a given measurable space. The main result quantifies the effect on CGW distance of perturbing the measure of a measure network.

Theorem 27
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
and
\mathcal{N}_{X}^{\prime}=(X,\mu_{X}^{\prime},\omega_{X})
be measure networks over the same underlying set
X
, with the same network kernel
\omega_{X}
, and let
\varepsilon=\|\mu_{X}-\mu_{X}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
. Then

\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{X}^{\prime})\leq 2\cdot\delta\cdot\mu_{X}(X)\cdot\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+4\varepsilon}.

Proof.

We have

(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{X}\leq\mu_{X}^{\prime}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mu_{X}.

(30)

Define a semi-coupling
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
between
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{X}^{\prime}
by

\gamma_{0}=(\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id})_{\#}\mu_{X}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\gamma_{1}=(\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id})_{\#}\mu_{X}^{\prime}.

By (
30
),
\gamma_{1}\ll\gamma_{0}
, so that the
\mathsf{CGW}
distance can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{X}^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 4\delta^{2}\big(\mu_{X}(X)^{2}+\mu_{X}^{\prime}(X)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-8\delta^{2}\int_{(X\times X)^{2}}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

(31)

\displaystyle\qquad=4\delta^{2}\big(\mu_{X}(X)^{2}+\mu_{X}^{\prime}(X)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-8\delta^{2}\int_{X\times X}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}(x,x)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})}\textnormal{d}\mu_{X}(x)\textnormal{d}\mu_{X}(x^{\prime})

(32)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 4\delta^{2}(1+(1+\varepsilon)^{2})\mu_{X}(X)^{2}-8\delta^{2}\int_{X\times X}(1-\varepsilon)\textnormal{d}\mu_{X}(x)\textnormal{d}\mu_{X}(x^{\prime})

(33)

\displaystyle=4\delta^{2}\mu(X)^{2}\big(1+(1+\varepsilon)^{2}-2(1-\varepsilon)\big)

\displaystyle=4\delta^{2}\mu(X)^{2}(\varepsilon^{2}+4\varepsilon).

The steps are justified as follows: (
31
) follows by the suboptimality of our choice of semicoupling and by Lemma
13
, where we have used
\gamma=\gamma_{0}
as the reference measure in the loss
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}
; (
32
) follows by the observation that
\gamma_{0}
is supported on the diagonal, where
\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}(x,x)=\textnormal{d}\mu_{X}(x)
, as well as the fact that
\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}(x,x)=1
; (
33
) applies (
30
), together with the fact that
\Omega(0)=1
; the rest of the calculations are basic simplifications. This completes the proof.

The next corollary shows that the CGW distance is robust to perturbations of the measures of the measure networks being compared. Its proof follows easily from Theorem
27
and the triangle inequality for CGW distance.

Corollary 28
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
be measure networks and let

\mathcal{N}_{X}^{\prime}=(X,\mu_{X}^{\prime},\omega_{X})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\prime}=(Y,\mu_{Y}^{\prime},\omega_{Y}),

where
\mu_{X}^{\prime}
and
\mu_{Y}^{\prime}
be
\varepsilon
-perturbations of
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
, respectively, in the sense that

\|\mu_{X}-\mu_{X}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{TV}},\|\mu_{Y}-\mu_{Y}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\varepsilon,

for some
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Then

|\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})-\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X}^{\prime},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\prime})|\leq 2\delta\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+4\varepsilon}(\mu_{X}(X)+\mu_{Y}(Y)).

It is natural to ask whether classical GW distance happens to enjoy a similar robustness. However, it is not hard to construct a counterexample, as we do in the following proposition (cf.
[
12
, Proposition 4.15]
).

Proposition 29
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotonically increasing function with
f(0)=0
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist probability measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}
,
\mathcal{N}_{Y}
and
\varepsilon
-perturbations
\mathcal{N}_{X}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\prime}
such that

|\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})-\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})|\geq f(\varepsilon).

(34)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{Y}=\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\prime}
be the one-point probability metric measure space. Let
\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mathsf{d}_{X},\mu_{X})
with
X=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
,

\mathsf{d}_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{(1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon}}f(\varepsilon),

and
\mu_{X}
supported on
x_{1}
. Let
\mu_{X}^{\prime}
be the
\varepsilon
-perturbation with
\mu_{X}^{\prime}(x_{1})=1-\varepsilon
and
\mu_{X}^{\prime}(x_{2})=\varepsilon
. Then
\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})=0
and (cf.
[
29
, Theorem 5.1(f)]
)

\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X}^{\prime},\mathcal{N}_{Y})=\sqrt{\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon}{2}}\mathsf{d}_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=f(\varepsilon),

so that (
34
) follows.

5
Conic Co-Optimal Transport for Unbalanced hypernetworks

In this section, we define a notion of conic GW distance—dubbed
conic Co-Optimal transport
—which operates on generalized structures called
measure hypernetworks
. The section begins with an explanation of the hypernetwork and co-optimal transport terminology.

5.1
Hypernetworks and Co-Optimal Transport

Roughly, the Gromov-Wasserstein framework is a method for comparing square-shaped kernels
\omega_{X}
and
\omega_{Y}
, while the co-optimal transport framework of
[
33
]
adapts this idea to provide a comparison method for rectangle-shaped kernels. We recall the mathematical formalism for this framework, developed in
[
16
]
.

Definition 30
(Measure Hypernetwork and Co-Optimal Transport).

A
measure hypernetwork
is a structure of the form
\mathcal{H}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},X^{\prime},\mu_{X}^{\prime},\omega_{X})
, where
(X,\mu_{X})
and
(X^{\prime},\mu_{X}^{\prime})
are compact Polish measure spaces and
\omega_{X}:X\times X^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
is a measurable, bounded function. If
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{X}^{\prime}
are probability measures, we refer to
\mathcal{H}_{X}
as a
probability measure hypernetwork
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the
p
-Co-Optimal Transport distance
between probability measure hypernetworks
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
is

\mathsf{COT}_{p}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}),\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\mu_{X}^{\prime},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})}\|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}\|_{\textrm{L}^{p}(\pi\otimes\pi^{\prime})},

(35)

where
\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}
is considered as a function
(X\times Y)\times(X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime})\to\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 31
.

The
measure hypernetwork
terminology is in reference to the
measure network
terminology of
[
15
]
; the idea is that measure hypernetworks serve as natural, general models for weighted hypernetworks. Examples of hypernetworks which arise in practice include feature matrices (the original motivation for developing the framework in
[
33
]
) and general cost functions, say, in the context of optimal transport (see
[
30
]
).

Remark 32
.

One can consider a probabilistic measure network
\mathcal{N}_{X}
as a probabilistic measure hypernetwork
\mathcal{H}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
. Given two such measure hypernetworks, the COT formula is almost the same as the one of GW, with the difference being that the couplings being optimized over are ‘de-coupled’: we integrate over
\pi\otimes\pi^{\prime}
rather than
\pi\otimes\pi
. In this setting, one has
\mathsf{COT}_{p}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})\leq\mathsf{GW}_{p}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})
, in general, and sufficient conditions for equality are provided in
[
33
]
. We consider similar questions for conic versions of these distances below.

Theoretical and computational properties of
\mathsf{COT}_{p}
were studied in
[
33
,
16
]
. In particular,
\mathsf{COT}_{p}
defines a pseudometric on the space of probability measure hypernetworks, considered up to a certain equivalence relation, called
weak isomorphism
. The following section extends this framework to the unbalanced setting and proves an analogous result.

5.2
Conic Co-Optimal Transport

The distance we study in this section is defined as follows.

Definition 33
(Conic Co-Optimal Transport Distance).

For measure hypernetworks
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
,
\delta
a positive real number, and
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}
a metric on the cone over
\mathbb{R}
, the associated
Conic Co-Optimal Transport (CCOT) distance
is

\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})\coloneqq\inf\limits_{(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}),(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})^{1/2},

where:

•

the
CCOT loss

\mathbf{L}_{\delta}:\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})\times\Gamma(\mu_{X}^{\prime},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\begin{split}&\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\coloneqq\\
&\quad\int_{X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}\times X\times Y}\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(p^{\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}_{X}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),p^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}_{Y}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)^{2}\\
&\hskip 216.81pt\cdot\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y)\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime});\end{split}

(36)

•

the functions
p^{\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}_{X},p^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}_{Y}:X\times Y\times X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}\to\mathcal{C}_{\delta}[\mathbb{R}]
are defined by

p^{\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}_{X}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\left[\omega_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right]

and

p^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}_{Y}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\left[\omega_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}),\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right];

•

\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma^{\prime}\ll\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
are any absolutely continuous measures on
X\times Y
and
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
, respectively.

The following lemma gives a useful alternative expression for the CCOT loss. The proof is analogous to that of Lemma
13
.

Lemma 34
.

The CCOT loss (
36
) can be equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad=4\delta^{2}\big(\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{X}^{\prime}(X^{\prime})+\mu_{Y}(Y)\mu_{Y}^{\prime}(Y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-8\delta^{2}\int_{X\times Y\times X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}}\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|}{2\delta}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}}}\textnormal{d}\gamma\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime},

where
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma^{\prime}\ll\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
.

As in the case of measure networks, we will consider the proposed distance up to weak isomorphism of hypernetworks. This is a superficial generalization of the notion of weak isomorphism originally proposed in
[
16
]
, reformulated analogously to Definition
10
.

Definition 35
(Weak Isomorphism of hypernetworks,

[
16
]
).

Measure hypernetworks
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
are called
weakly isomorphic
if there exists a pair of Polish spaces
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
equipped with Borel measures
\mu_{Z}
and
\mu_{Z}^{\prime}
, respectively, and measurable maps
f:Z\to X
,
f^{\prime}:Z^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
,
g:Z\to Y
, and
g^{\prime}:Z^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
such that

•

f_{\#}\mu_{Z}=\mu_{X}
,

•

f^{\prime}_{\#}\mu_{Z}^{\prime}=\mu_{X}^{\prime}
,

•

g_{\#}\mu_{Z}=\mu_{Y}
,

•

g^{\prime}_{\#}\mu_{Z}^{\prime}=\mu_{Y}^{\prime}
,

•

and
\|(f,f^{\prime})^{\#}\omega_{X}-(g,g^{\prime})^{\#}\omega_{Y}\|_{\textrm{L}^{\infty}(\mu_{Z}\otimes\mu_{Z}^{\prime})}=0
,

where
(f,f^{\prime})^{\#}\omega_{X}:Z\times Z^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
is the pullback function given by the map
(z,z^{\prime})\mapsto\omega_{X}(f(z),f^{\prime}(z^{\prime}))
, and
(g,g^{\prime})^{\#}\omega_{Y}
is defined analogously.

The next result says that the conic Co-Optimal transport distance does in fact define a distance up to weak isomorphisms of measure hypernetworks. The proof is the same as that of Theorem
11
, up to adapting the arguments with more involved notation, so we omit it. The analogous result for co-optimal transport distance is proved in detail in
[
16
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 36
.

For
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
,
\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}
is a psuedometric on the space of measure hypernetworks and, in particular,
\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})=0
if and only if
\mathcal{H}_{X}
is weakly isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
.

In particular, the proof of Theorem
36
would make use of the following lemma, which is analogous to Lemma
12
and which is proved via similar arguments. We include the statment of the lemma here, because it will be used in other arguments below.

Lemma 37
.

The infium defining
\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}
is always realized.

5.3
Relationship with UCOT

We now examine the relationship between the conic Co-Optimal transport (CCOT) distance and the Unbalanced Co-Optimal transport (UCOT) introduced in
[
38
]
, whose definition we restate in the context of hypernetworks below.

Definition 38
(Unbalanced Co-Optimal Transport
[
38
]
).

The
Unbalanced Co-Optimal transport (UCOT) distance
between hypernetworks
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{UCOT}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{M}(X\times Y),\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{M}(X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime})}\int_{X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}\times X\times Y}|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|^{2}\operatorname{d}\gamma\operatorname{d}\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\operatorname{KL}((\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma\otimes(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma^{\prime}|\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{X}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\operatorname{KL}((\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma\otimes(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma^{\prime}|\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{\prime}),

where
\operatorname{KL}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
denotes Kullback-Leibler divergence.

We can now state a comparison result between UCOT and CCOT. This result mirrors the relationship between the
\mathsf{CGW}
and the
unbalanced Gromov-Wasserstein distance
, defined similarly to UCOT in
[
35
]
. The proof follows a similar argument.

Proposition 39
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
be measure hypernetworks and let the function defining
\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}[\mathbb{R}]}
be
\Omega(z)=\exp(-z^{2})
. Then

\mathsf{UCOT}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})\geq\mathsf{CCOT}_{1/2}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y}).

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{M}(X\times Y)
and
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{M}(X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime})
attain the infimum defined by
\mathsf{UCOT}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})
—the existence of these minimizers is guaranteed by adapting the argument of
[
35
, Proposition 2]
to the co-optimal transport setting. We define densities

\displaystyle f=\frac{\textnormal{d}\mu_{X}}{\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma}:X\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\qquad f^{\prime}=\frac{\textnormal{d}\mu_{X}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma^{\prime}}:X^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle g=\frac{\textnormal{d}\mu_{Y}}{\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma}:Y\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\qquad g^{\prime}=\frac{\textnormal{d}\mu_{Y}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma^{\prime}}:Y^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}.

Using these functions we can define semi-couplings
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
and
(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X}^{\prime},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})
by, for any
U\subseteq X\times Y
and
U^{\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}(U)=\int_{U}f(x)\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y),

\displaystyle\qquad\gamma_{0}^{\prime}(U^{\prime})=\int_{U^{\prime}}f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(U)=\int_{U}g(y)\textnormal{d}\gamma(x,y),

\displaystyle\qquad\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(U^{\prime})=\int_{U^{\prime}}g^{\prime}(y^{\prime})\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Then we have
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma^{\prime}\ll\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
, with
\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)=f(x),\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}(x,y)=g(y),\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
, and
\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=g^{\prime}(y^{\prime})
.
Finally, we define
(\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{X}^{\prime})^{\perp}
and
(\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{\prime})^{\perp}
to be the measures satisfying

\displaystyle\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{X}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=f(x)f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma\otimes(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma^{\prime}+(\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{X}^{\prime})^{\perp},

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=g(y)g^{\prime}(y^{\prime})(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma\otimes(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma^{\prime}+(\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{\prime})^{\perp}.

From Lemma 1 of
[
35
]
, we have the equality

\displaystyle\int|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|^{2}\operatorname{d}\gamma\operatorname{d}\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad+\operatorname{KL}((\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma\otimes(\operatorname{Pr}_{0})_{\#}\gamma^{\prime}|\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{X}^{\prime})+\operatorname{KL}((\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma\otimes(\operatorname{Pr}_{1})_{\#}\gamma^{\prime}|\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|^{2}ff^{\prime}gg^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad+\operatorname{KL}((\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{X}^{\prime})^{\perp}|\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{X}^{\prime})+\operatorname{KL}((\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{\prime})^{\perp}|\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{\prime}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{UCOT}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\int|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|^{2}ff^{\prime}gg^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\int\mathsf{d}_{\mathcal{C}[\mathbb{R}]}\left(\left[\omega_{X},\sqrt{ff^{\prime}}\right],\left[\omega_{Y},\sqrt{gg^{\prime}}\right]\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\gamma\operatorname{d}\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}_{1/2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\geq\mathsf{CCOT}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})^{2}.

5.4
Relationship with CGW Distances

Consider measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
and observe that we may construct a hypernetwork from
\mathcal{N}_{X}
as follows
\mathcal{H}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},X,\mu_{Y},\omega_{X})
. In the following section we will investigate the relationship between
\mathsf{CCOT}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})
and
\mathsf{CGW}(\mathcal{N}_{Y},\mathcal{N}_{Y})
(cf. Remark
32
). First, we observe that conic Co-Optimal transport lower bounds the conic Gromov-Wasserstein distance, in general.

Proposition 40
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}
be measure networks with associated measure hypernetworks
\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y}
. Then

\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})\leq\mathsf{CGW}_{\delta}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}).

Proof.

Let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
and note that
\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=\inf\limits_{(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle\geq\inf\limits_{(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})}\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}).

We now provide sufficient conditions under which this lower bound becomes an equality, i.e., when CCOT and CGW coincide.

Theorem 41
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}
be measure networks and consider the associated hypernetworks,
\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y}
respectively. Suppose that
\omega_{X}
,
\omega_{Y}
, and
\Omega
have the property that the map
k:(X\times Y)\times(X\times Y)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

k\coloneqq\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|}{2\delta}\right)

defines a positive-definite kernel. Then for
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
,
(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
such that
\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})
, we have

\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}).

It follows that

\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})=\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})=\mathsf{CGW}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}).

We note that this result is closely related to
[
35
, Theorem 3]
, which is stated in terms of a bi-convex relaxation of CGW distance, rather than co-optimal transport. One approach to proving the theorem would be to show that the CCOT distance agrees with the bi-convex relaxation of CGW presented in
[
35
]
when the measure hypernetworks
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
are induced by metric measure spaces (this would be analogous to Proposition
14
), and to then apply
[
35
, Theorem 3]
, checking that the arguments do not depend on the assumption that the functions
\omega_{X}
and
\omega_{Y}
are metrics. We instead give a more direct proof below.

Proof.

Assume that
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
such that
\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})
. By optimality, we have

\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\leq\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\leq\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}),

so that

\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})+\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})-2\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\geq 0.

(37)

Choosing the reference measure
\gamma
so that
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
, it follows from the expression for
\mathbf{L}_{\delta}
given in Lemma
34
and some simplification that (
37
) implies

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}\int_{X\times Y}k\left(\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}-\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}\right)\textnormal{d}\gamma\left(\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}-\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}\right)\textnormal{d}\gamma\leq 0.

(38)

Since
k
is positive-definite, this implies

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}\int_{X\times Y}k\left(\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}-\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}\right)\textnormal{d}\gamma\left(\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}-\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}\right)\textnormal{d}\gamma=0,

(39)

and, in turn, that

\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}=\sqrt{\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}\frac{\textnormal{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\textnormal{d}\gamma}}.

Therefore,

\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime}).

The work above, plus the fact that the infimum in the definition of
\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}
is always realized (Lemma
37
), proves the second statement of the theorem. That is, suppose
\mathsf{CCOT}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})
is realized by
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
and
(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\geq\mathsf{CGW}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}).

The reverse inequality always holds, by Proposition
40
, so this completes the proof.

For examples of kernels satisfying the hypotheses of the theorem, see
[
35
]
; in particular, the discussion surrounding Theorem 3 and Proposition 11 therein.

5.5
Algorithm for CCOT

In this section, we examine the conic Co-Optimal transport (CCOT) problem between discrete hypernetworks and develop a computational method for finding the optimal pair of semi-couplings. Our method closely follows that of
[
4
]
(in the context of the WFR distance), employing a cyclic block coordinate ascent algorithm to compute optimal semi-couplings, with a closed form for the optimal solution on each block while keeping the other blocks fixed. To consider this algorithm we first require the notion of a discrete semi-coupling from
[
4
, Definition 2.4]
.

Definition 42
(Discrete Semi-Couplings
[
4
]
).

Let
(X,\mu_{X}),(Y,\mu_{Y})
be measure spaces with finitely-supported measures expressed as
\mu_{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\mu_{Y}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
. A
discrete semi-coupling
of
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
is a pair of
m\times n
matrices
(A,B)
satisfying the properties:

•

for all
i,j
,
A_{ij}\geq 0
and
B_{ij}\geq 0
;

•

for
1\leq i\leq m
,
a_{i}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}
;

•

for
1\leq j\leq n
,
b_{j}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}B_{ij}
.

We denote the set of all discrete semi-couplings of
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{X}
by
\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
.

Remark 43
.

The space of matrix pairs
\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
naturally corresponds to a subset of the space of measure pairs
\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
, justifying our notation. In general, this subset is proper: even though
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
are finitely supported, measure couplings
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
can be non-finitely supported.

In light of Remark
43
, it is not apparent that the CCOT distance can be written as an optimization over the space of discrete semi-couplings, even between two measure hypernetworks whose measures are finitely supported. Our first result of this section will be to show that in fact, it is sufficient to optimize over these spaces.

Proposition 44
.

For measure hypernetworks
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
with finitely supported measures

\mu_{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}},\,\mu_{X}^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}a_{k}^{\prime}\delta_{x_{k}^{\prime}},\,\mu_{Y}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}\delta_{y_{j}},\,\mu_{Y}^{\prime}=\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}b_{l}^{\prime}\delta_{y_{l}^{\prime}},

the CCOT distance between them is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\min_{A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}4\delta^{2}\Bigg(\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{Y}(Y)+\mu_{X}^{\prime}(X^{\prime})\mu_{Y}^{\prime}(Y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega_{ijkl}^{\delta}\sqrt{A_{ik}B_{ik}A^{\prime}_{jl}B^{\prime}_{jl}}\Bigg),

where the minimum is over
(A,B)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}),(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X}^{\prime},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})
and the distortion cost tensor is defined by
\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega(x_{i},x_{j}^{\prime})-\omega^{\prime}(y_{k},y_{l}^{\prime})|}{2\delta}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
and
(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X}^{\prime},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})
and let
\gamma\ll\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma^{\prime}\ll\gamma^{\prime}_{0},\gamma^{\prime}_{1}
.
Construct
(A,B)
and
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
as follows:

A_{ik}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x_{i},y_{k}),\,B_{ik}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}(x_{i},y_{k}),\,A^{\prime}_{jl}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma^{\prime}}(x_{j}^{\prime},y_{l}^{\prime}),\,B^{\prime}_{jl}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma^{\prime}}(x_{j}^{\prime},y_{l}^{\prime}).

Then it is easy to verify that the quanitity
\mathbf{L}_{\delta}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})/(4\delta^{2})
is given by

\mu_{X}(X)\mu_{Y}(Y)+\mu_{X^{\prime}}(X^{\prime})\mu_{Y^{\prime}}(Y^{\prime})-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega_{ijkl}^{\delta}\sqrt{A_{ik}B_{ik}A^{\prime}_{jl}B^{\prime}_{jl}}.

The result follows by Lemma
34
.

The following computationally convenient fact is immediate.

Corollary 45
.

In the case of finitely supported measures, computing
\mathsf{CCOT}_{\delta}
is equivalent to finding the maximizer of the function

\begin{split}F:\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X},\mu_{X}^{\prime})\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{Y},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})&\to\mathbb{R}\\
(A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})&\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}\sqrt{A_{ik}B_{ik}A^{\prime}_{jl}B^{\prime}_{jl}}.\end{split}

(40)

We propose to approximate the CCOT distance via block coordinate ascent, that is, by iteratively maximizing functions of the form

F(-,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime}),\,F(A,-,A^{\prime},B^{\prime}),\,F(A,B,-,B^{\prime}),\,F(A,B,A^{\prime},-)

(41)

(i.e., holding all but one argument constant), where
F
is as in (
40
). The rest of this subsection is devoted to technical results which show that this is well posed and numerically feasible. These results all assume finitely-supported hypernetworks with the same notation as Proposition
44
.

Lemma 46
.

Consider the subset
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
of
\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X},\mu_{X}^{\prime})\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{Y},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})
consisting of pairs of discrete semi-couplings
(A,B)
and
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
satisfying the conditions:

1.

A_{ik}=B_{ik}=0
whenever
\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0

2.

A^{\prime}_{jl}=B^{\prime}_{jl}=0
whenever
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0

3.

for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{ik}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0\\
a_{i}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

4.

for
k=1,\ldots,m
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{ik}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0\\
b_{k}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

5.

for
j=1,\ldots,n^{\prime}
,

\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}A^{\prime}_{jl}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0\\
a^{\prime}_{j}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

6.

for
l=1,\ldots,m^{\prime}
,

\sum_{j=1}^{m}B^{\prime}_{jl}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0\\
b^{\prime}_{l}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The function
F
(
40
) obtains its maximum on
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
.

Proof.

Let
(A,B)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X},\mu_{X}^{\prime})
and
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{Y},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})
be arbitrary discrete semi-couplings. We perform a two step construction of
\big((\hat{A},\hat{B}),(\hat{A}^{\prime},\hat{B}^{\prime})\big)\in\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
such that
F(\hat{A},\hat{B},\hat{A}^{\prime},\hat{B}^{\prime})>F(A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
. For the first step, we construct
\big((\tilde{A},\tilde{B}),(\tilde{A}^{\prime},\tilde{B}^{\prime})\big)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})\times\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X}^{\prime},\mu_{Y}^{\prime})
as follows:

•

For
i=1,\dots,n
and
k=1,\dots,m
, define:

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0\\
A_{ik}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0\\
B_{ik}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

•

For
j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
and
l=1,\dots,m^{\prime}
, define:

\displaystyle\tilde{A}^{\prime}_{jl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0\\
A^{\prime}_{jl}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\tilde{B}^{\prime}_{jl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0\\
B^{\prime}_{jl}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

For the second step, we construct
(\hat{A},\hat{B}),(\hat{A}^{\prime},\hat{B}^{\prime})
by:

•

For
i=1,\dots,n
and
k=1,\dots,m
, define:

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{a_{i}\tilde{A}_{ik}}{\sum_{r=1}^{m}\tilde{A}_{ir}}&\text{if }\sum_{r=1}^{m}\tilde{A}_{ir}>0\\
\tilde{A}_{ik}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{b_{k}\tilde{B}_{ik}}{\sum_{r=1}^{n}\tilde{B}_{rk}}&\text{if }\sum_{r=1}^{n}\tilde{B}_{rk}>0\\
\tilde{B}_{ik}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

•

For
j=1,\dots,n^{\prime}
and
l=1,\dots,m^{\prime}
, define:

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\prime}_{jl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{a^{\prime}_{j}\tilde{A}^{\prime}_{jl}}{\sum_{r=1}^{m^{\prime}}\tilde{A}^{\prime}_{jr}}&\text{if }\sum_{r=1}^{m^{\prime}}\tilde{A}^{\prime}_{jr}>0\\
\tilde{A}^{\prime}_{jl}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{\prime}_{jl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{b^{\prime}_{l}\tilde{B}^{\prime}_{jl}}{\sum_{r=1}^{n^{\prime}}\tilde{B}^{\prime}_{rl}}&\text{if }\sum_{r=1}^{n^{\prime}}\tilde{B}^{\prime}_{rl}>0\\
\tilde{B}^{\prime}_{jl}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

By construction,
(\hat{A},\hat{B},\hat{A}^{\prime},\hat{B}^{\prime})\in\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
and
F(\hat{A},\hat{B},\hat{A}^{\prime},\hat{B}^{\prime})>F(A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
. Thus
F
obtains its maximum on the subset
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
.

Our computational approach for determining the CCOT distance employs a straightforward cyclic block coordinate ascent method. However, the key to our method is that when three of the blocks are fixed, the optimal solution for the remaining block can be expressed in a closed-form. In the following lemma, we demonstrate how to compute this optimal solution for each block while keeping the others fixed.

Lemma 47
.

The block coordinate ascent functions (
41
) have unique maximizers on the interior of
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
, which are described explicitly as follows. For
\big((A,B),(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\big)
in the interior
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
, the
n\times m
matrix,
P
, is defined by

P_{ik}=\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}\sqrt{A^{\prime}_{jl}B^{\prime}_{jl}}

and the
n^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}
matrix,
Q
, is defined by

Q_{jl}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}\sqrt{A_{ik}B_{ik}}.

The functions (
41
) satisfy:

1.

\displaystyle\arg\max F(-,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=E

\displaystyle\text{where }E_{ik}=\begin{cases}a_{i}\dfrac{B_{ik}\left(P_{ik}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{r=1}^{m}B_{ir}\left(P_{ir}\right)^{2}}&\text{if }\sum_{r=1}^{m}B_{ir}\left(P_{ir}\right)^{2}>0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

2.

\displaystyle\arg\max F(A,-,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=E

\displaystyle\text{where }E_{ik}=\begin{cases}b_{k}\dfrac{A_{ik}\left(P_{ik}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{r=1}^{n}A_{rk}\left(P_{rk}\right)^{2}}&\text{if }\sum_{r=1}^{n}A_{rk}\left(P_{rk}\right)^{2}>0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

3.

\displaystyle\arg\max F(A,B,-,B^{\prime})=E

\displaystyle\text{where }E_{jl}=\begin{cases}a_{j}^{\prime}\dfrac{B^{\prime}_{jl}\left(Q_{jl}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{r=1}^{m^{\prime}}B^{\prime}_{jr}\left(Q_{jr}\right)^{2}}&\text{if }\sum_{r=1}^{m^{\prime}}B^{\prime}_{jr}\left(Q_{jr}\right)^{2}>0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

4.

\displaystyle\arg\max F(A,B,A^{\prime},-)=E

\displaystyle\text{where }E_{jl}=\begin{cases}b_{l}^{\prime}\dfrac{B^{\prime}_{jl}\left(Q_{jl}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{r=1}^{n^{\prime}}B^{\prime}_{rl}\left(Q_{rl}\right)^{2}}&\text{if }\sum_{r=1}^{n^{\prime}}B^{\prime}_{rl}\left(Q_{rl}\right)^{2}>0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Here, maximization of
F(-,B,C,D)
is over the set

\{E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\mid\big((E,B),(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\big)\in\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}\},

(42)

with the domains of the other functions defined similarly.

Proof.

We give the proof for
1.
, with the remaining claims following by symmetric arguments. With
E
as defined in
1.
, we wish to show that
F(A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\leq F(E,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
for all
A
in the set (
42
). We will prove the following

Claim:
For all
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sqrt{A_{ik}B_{ik}}P_{ik}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sqrt{E_{ik}B_{ik}}P_{ik}.

Assuming the claim, the proof that
E
is a maximizer follows easily, as

F(A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sqrt{A_{ik}B_{ik}}P_{ik}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sqrt{E_{ik}B_{ik}}P_{ik}=F(E,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime}).

To prove the claim, first suppose that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}=0
. Then, for all
k=1,\ldots,m
, either
B_{ik}=0
or
P_{ik}=0
, in which case

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sqrt{A_{ik}B_{ik}}P_{ik}=0=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sqrt{E_{ik}B_{ik}}P_{ik}.

It remains to prove the claim for the case that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}>0
. By Cauchy-Schwarz, and the definition of
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
, we
have,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}\sqrt{A_{ik}B_{ik}}P_{ik}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}A_{ik}\right)\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}\right)}=\sqrt{a_{i}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{a_{i}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}}{\sqrt{\left(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}\right)}}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}\sqrt{\frac{a_{i}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}}{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}}B_{ik}}P_{ik}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sqrt{E_{ik}B_{ik}}P_{ik}.

This completes the proof of the claim, hence that
E
is a maximizer.

It remains to prove uniqueness. The established inequality
F(A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\leq F(E,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is strict unless, for each
1\leq i\leq n
such that
\sum_{r=1}^{m}B_{ir}P_{ir}^{2}>0
,
the vector
\{A_{ik}\}_{k}
is a scalar multiple of
\{B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}\}_{k}
. Since we know
\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{ik}=a_{i}
, equality holds if and only if

\{A_{ik}\}=\left\{\frac{a_{i}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}}{\sum_{r=1}^{m}B_{ir}P_{ir}^{2}}\right\}=\{E_{ik}\}.

This proves that a maximizer must agree with
E
on indices
i
with
\sum_{k=1}^{m}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}>0
. We now consider those indices
i
where
\sum_{k=1}^{m}B_{ik}P_{ik}^{2}=0
. By Definition of
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
, since
\big((A,B),(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\big)
is in the interior of
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
,
B_{ik}=0
if and only if
P_{ik}=0
. Thus, for all
k
,
B_{ik}=0
. Once again using that
\big((A,B),(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\big)
is interior, this implies that, for all
k
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0
. Furthermore, this implies that
A_{ik}=0
, for all
k
; in particular,
\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{ik}=0
. However, since
(A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is in the interior of
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
, this is true if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\Omega^{\delta}_{ijkl}=0
. Thus, by definition of
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
we must have
\sum_{k=1}^{m}E_{ik}=0
, so that
E_{ik}=0
for all
k
, hence
A_{ik}=E_{ik}
, and we have completed the proof of uniqueness.

Corollary 48
.

When initialized on the interior of
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
he block coordinate ascent algorithm described above converges to a stable point of
F
.

Proof.

When
F
is restricted to
\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{d}}
it attains a unique maximum in each coordinate block. Therefore, by
[
39
, Theorem 4.1]
(c.f.
[
27
]
) the cyclic block coordinate ascent algorithm will converge to a stable point of
F
.

Remark 49
.

Recall from Theorem
41
that
\mathsf{CCOT}(\mathcal{H}_{X},\mathcal{H}_{Y})=\mathsf{CGW}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y})
for discrete networks
\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}
such that
\Omega\left(\frac{|\omega_{X}-\omega_{Y}|}{2\delta}\right)
defines a positive definite kernel. Therefore, we can also use our algorithm to approximate the
\mathsf{CGW}
distance between discrete measure networks satisfying this property.

We demonstrate the use of the CCOT metric (as defined in
33
) and COT metric (as defined in (
35
)) to align single-cell multi-omics datasets in scenarios where cellular populations partially overlap across modalities. Such incomplete overlap arises due to technical limitations (e.g., assay throughput or capture efficiency) and biological constraints (e.g., cell-type exclusivity in sampling), which prevent comprehensive multi-omics profiling of individual cells. We utilize a benchmark CITE-seq dataset
[
37
]
, which is closely related to the dataset used in
[
38
]
. While the experimental setups are similar, slight differences exist due to the random sampling of the biological entities such as cells, genes, and proteins. Nonetheless, this allows for an approximate comparison of our qualitative results using CCOT against the UCOT (as defined in
38
) results in
[
38
]
.

This dataset contains profiles of gene expression and surface protein abundance simultaneously in 1,000 human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) each profiled for 17,014 genes and 10 surface proteins. This dataset is chosen because it contains known biological correspondences between features (i.e., genes and their encoded proteins, such as the CD4 gene and CD4 protein), allowing us to rigorously evaluate CCOT’s ability to jointly align both cells and features. Although gene expression and protein abundance are distinct biological measurements with imperfect correlation, these known correspondences serve as ground truth for assessing feature alignment.

Here, we denote the gene expression modality by the measure hypernetwork
\mathcal{H}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},X^{\prime},\mu_{X}^{\prime},\omega_{X})
, where
X
corresponds to the set of cells profiled for gene expression (samples) and
\mu_{X}
is the empirical measure over these cells. The set of features
X^{\prime}
represents the genes expressed (features), with
\mu_{X}^{\prime}
their associated empirical measure. The kernel
\omega_{X}
encodes the pairwise relationships between genes. Similarly, the protein modality is represented by the hypernetwork
\mathcal{H}_{Y}=(Y,\mu_{Y},Y^{\prime},\mu_{Y}^{\prime},\omega_{Y})
, where
Y
denotes the set of cells profiled for surface proteins (samples) with corresponding measure
\mu_{Y}
. The protein cells (features)
Y^{\prime}
and measure
\mu_{Y}^{\prime}
are defined analogously, with
\omega_{Y}
capturing similarity between proteins.

Pre-processing utilizes the Muon package to handle each modality appropriately: the RNA data undergoes quality filtering, normalization, log-transformation, and selection of highly variable genes, while protein data is normalized using centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation to adjust for technical variability. Dimensionality reduction is performed independently on each modality to obtain low-dimensional embeddings suitable for alignment.

To evaluate alignment quality, we use the Fraction of Samples Closer to the True Match (FOSCTTM) metric for cells
[
10
,
26
,
18
]
, where a lower score indicates better cell-level alignment, and the proportion of correctly matched gene-protein pairs for feature-level alignment. To test robustness, we simulate three experimental conditions: a balanced scenario with equal numbers of cells (fixed at 1,000) and matched features (10 genes paired with their corresponding 10 proteins); an unbalanced scenario (features) with equal numbers of cells (1,000), but unequal numbers of features (5 genes versus 10 proteins), simulating missing or unmatched features; and unbalanced scenario (cells) where the number of cells differs across modalities to mimic partial cellular overlap.

Balanced Scenario (Features)

We select equal numbers of cells (1000 fixed) and features (10 genes and their corresponding 10 proteins). Both COT and CCOT accurately align features (Figures
2
(a)-(b)), but CCOT achieves better cell alignment with a lower FOSCTTM score (0.0059 vs. 0.0234). CCOT’s improved performance stems from its robustness to noise, resulting in a clearer diagonal (darker red) alignment pattern relative to COT distance (as demonstrated theoretically in Theorems
41
and
27
).

Unbalanced Scenario (Features)

We select equal number of cells (1000) and align ten proteins against five genes, creating an unbalanced setting. COT fails to recover correct feature correspondences, dispersing alignment away from the diagonal (Figure
2
(d)). In contrast, CCOT’s relaxation of the mass conservation constraint allows down-weighting of unmatched protein cells, improving alignment quality (Figure
2
(c)).

Balanced and Unbalanced Scenario (Samples)

We also consider the case when there are equal and unequal number of cells across both the modalities. For the balanced case, we align 1000 cells for both the modalities. For the unbalanced case, we downsample the protein modality by 25% and perform alignment with the full set of cells in the gene modality. We calculate the FOSCTTM score for all cells with known true matches in the dataset and report the average values. CCOT maintains a low FOSCTTM score (0.0073 versus 0.0059 in the balanced scenario), indicating robust performance, whereas COT had a more pronounced decline (0.1104 compared to 0.0234 in the balanced scenario). To further illustrate the alignment quality, we visualize the aligned cells using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plots (Figure
2
(e)-(h)), which reveal how well cells from both modalities cluster together after alignment.

Finally, in Figure
3
, we aim to align the unequal number of features (5 genes and 10 proteins) by varying the number of cells fixed for both modalities, we notice a decreasing trend in convergence diagnostics, including both the CCOT distance and the relative log errors in iterations. This behavior indicates that the solver progressively stabilizes its solution as the number of iterations increases. The observed reduction in these metrics reflects increasingly consistent and diminishing updates to the solver variables, providing evidence that the algorithm is approaching a stable point (see Corollary
48
). Consequently, these results support the reliability and robustness of the solver and the metric in achieving a meaningful and stable alignment between the datasets.

6.2
Equality of Semi-Couplings for CCOT for Measure Networks

Figure 4
:
Figure of the logarithm of average Frobenius distance between the semi-couplings at each iteration of the proposed algorithm for 20 random pairs of point clouds with
n
points.

In Remark
49
, we discuss how our algorithm can be applied to compute the conic Gromov Wasserstein distance between measure networks under certain conditions. Our method converges to a valid solution when the optimal pair of semi-couplings
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})\in\Gamma(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
are equal—that is,
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=(\gamma_{0}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime})
. In the discrete setting, for discrete semi-coupling pairs
(A,B),(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{d}}(\mu_{X},\mu_{Y})
, this condition translates to the Frobenius norm identity:

\|A-A^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}+\|B-B^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}=0.

Figure
4
shows the evolution of the Frobenius norm of the difference between successive semi-couplings during iterations of the block coordinate ascent algorithm and the Frobenius norm between them converges toward zero. The results are averaged over networks constructed from point clouds in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, using the squared Euclidean distance.
We present results using
\Omega(z)=\exp(-z^{2})
and
\Omega(z)=\overline{\cos}(z)
. Although no theoretical guarantee ensures equality of the optimal semi-couplings, the Frobenius norm between them still converges toward zero over iterations of the block coordinate ascent algorithm, offering empirical evidence of convergence of the algorithm to conic Gromov Wasserstein distance.

6.3
Digit Classification Comparison

Figure 5
:
(a). 2-D representation of the MNIST dataset. (b)-(c) Comparison of test error on the MNIST digit dataset by varying the number of training labels for a

-NN classifier with different values of
k
. Experiments were conducted using two kernel choices:
\Omega(z)=\overline{\cos}(z)

We address a binary classification task using the MNIST dataset
[
24
]
, which contains 70,000 grayscale images of handwritten digits from
0
to
9
, each sized
28\times 28
pixels. For our experimental setup, we focus exclusively on the digits 1 and 7, selecting
N=1000
samples from each class to form a dataset of size
2N=2000
. To enhance variability within the data, additive Gaussian noise is introduced to the pixel intensities of half the images (see Figure
5
(a)).

Each image is modelled as a measure network
\mathcal{N}=(V,\mu,\omega)
, where the set of nodes
V=\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{784}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
corresponds to fixed pixel coordinates, with
v_{i}
denoting the spatial location of pixel
i
. The discrete probability measure
\mu
on
V
is defined by normalizing pixel intensities:

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{784}p_{i}\,\delta_{v_{i}},\quad\text{where}\quad p_{i}=\frac{I(v_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{784}I(v_{j})},

and
I(v_{i})
denotes the grayscale intensity at pixel
v_{i}
.

The adjacency kernel
\omega=(\omega_{ij})\in\{0,1\}^{784\times 784}
represents the 8-neighborhood connectivity structure between pixels denoted by,

\omega_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }v_{j}\text{ is one of the 8 nearest neighbors of }v_{i}\text{ based on Euclidean distance},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here,
\mathcal{N}
denotes a generic measure network representing any image in the dataset.

More precisely, measure networks
\mathcal{N}_{X}
and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{(m)}
for
m=1,\dots,2000
are all constructed according to the general definition of
\mathcal{N}
above. In particular,
\mathcal{N}_{X}=(X,\mu_{X},\omega_{X})
corresponds to the first image which is digit 1 as a reference, and each
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{(m)}=(Y,\mu^{(m)}_{Y},\omega^{(m)}_{Y})
is constructed analogously from the
m
-th image in the dataset.

We compute the
\mathsf{CGW}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{(m)})
as defined in (
9
), yielding the vector of distances:

d_{\mathsf{CGW}}:=\bigl(\mathsf{CGW}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{(m)})\bigr)_{m=1}^{2000}\in\mathbb{R}^{2000}.

For comparison, the standard
\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{(m)})
is computed similarly:

d_{\mathsf{GW}_{2}}:=\bigl(\mathsf{GW}_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{X},\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{(m)})\bigr)_{m=1}^{2000}\in\mathbb{R}^{2000}.

Both
d_{\mathsf{CGW}}
and

serve as feature vector (inputs) for subsequent classification using a

-nearest neighbors (k-NN) classifier, with
k\in\{10,20,30\}
and varying training label rates from 20% to 80%. For each training label rates, 100 independent trials are conducted by randomly subsampling the training data, and the average test error is reported. Results are summarized in Figure
5
(b)-(c) for different choice of the kernel
\Omega(z)
. We note that changing the reference image to digit 7 and following the same process outlined to compute the feature vector yields similar classification accuracy, with a difference well within the standard error margin of approximately
\pm 1.5\%
. This indicates that the classification performance is robust to the choice of reference measure network.

Three salient observations emerge from Figure
5
(c). Firstly, the
\mathsf{CGW}
metric is robust to outliers than the
\mathsf{GW}_{2}
metric (see Theorem
27
), since it takes into account the variation in pixel intensity and the spatial coordinates, whilst the
\mathsf{GW}_{2}
only handles the latter. We observe that as the number of training labels increases, the test error decreases. Secondly, the feature vector corresponding to the
\mathsf{CGW}
metric serve as a better latent space for applying state-of-the-art machine learning algorithms for better classification performance. Thirdly, from a graph perspective
[
3
,
1
]
, this can be interpreted as the error decreasing with the reduction in the graph’s length scale (choice of
k

[
9
, Theorem 2.1, Theorem 2.4]
).
