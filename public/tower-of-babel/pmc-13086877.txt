mTOR-driven integrin β4-enriched extracellular vesicles from lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma fuel lung metastasis via fibroblast-niche formation
Authors: Tiantian Zou, Xufeng Wang, Haoting Sun, Beiyuan Hu, Jianhua Li, Yu Guo, Zilu Tian, Hao Xu, Yan Zheng, Yinghan Su, Chak Lam Chan, Le Zhu, Keyu Shen, Xiaotian Shen, Qiongzhu Dong, Lun-Xiu Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) play crucial roles in facilitating the colonization and growth of metastatic cancer cells in distant organs. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which EVs contribute to therapy-resistant cancer dissemination remain poorly understood. In the present study, we aim to investigate how EVs derived from lenvatinib-resistant (LR) hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) drive lung metastasis and identify potential therapeutic targets for both inhibiting metastasis and overcoming lenvatinib resistance. Using LR HCC models and omics analysis, we demonstrated that LR HCC cells exhibited an enhanced metastatic potential towards the lungs owing to an increased release of EVs. Aberrant activation of mTOR signaling drove EVs secretion from LR cells by impeding the autophagic degradation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs). Furthermore, EVs derived from LR cells exhibited an enrichment of ITGβ4, thereby fostering the pre-metastatic niche (PMN) formation by activating lung fibroblasts via the ITGβ4-laminin interaction and the PI3K-AKT-p65 signaling pathway. Elevated levels of plasma EV-ITGβ4 were observed in LR HCC patients and associated with dismal prognosis. Moreover, inhibition of mTOR signaling using rapamycin impeded lung metastasis and restored the sensitivity to lenvatinib. These findings highlight the role of ITGβ4-enriched EVs released from LR HCC cells in promoting lung metastasis and propose a potential target for combating lung metastasis and overcoming lenvatinib resistance.Subject terms: Metastasis, Gastrointestinal cancer

Introduction

Primary liver cancer, the majority of which is hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally.1 Patients with HCC are commonly diagnosed at an advanced stage, offering limited therapeutic avenues. Over the past decade, the therapeutic landscape for advanced HCC has undergone a significant transformation with the advent and clinical validation of systemic therapies. Recently, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) alone or their combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors has been demonstrated to be helpful in extending survival of advanced HCC patients.2,3 Lenvatinib, a first-line TKI for advanced HCC, is an oral multi-kinase inhibitor that targets vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 1, 2, 3 (VEGFR1-VEGFR3), fibroblast growth factor receptor 1, 2, 3, 4 (FGFR1-FGFR4), platelet-derived growth factor receptor α (PDGFRα), KIT and RET.4 However, the clinical efficacy of lenvatinib is invariably limited by the development of acquired resistance. It not only leads to treatment failure but also is frequently accompanied by a more aggressive disease phenotype characterised by enhanced metastatic dissemination, which remains the primary cause of death in HCC patients. Thus, it is urgent to elucidate the mechanisms underlying lenvatinib resistance and metastasis of HCC, as well as develop effective strategies to combat metastasis and overcome lenvatinib resistance.5

Intercellular communication has emerged as a fundamental process underpinning tumor adaptation, therapeutic evasion, and systemic spread. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are a heterogeneous population of endogenous, lipid bilayer-enclosed nanoparticles secreted by virtually all cell types. They have emerged as pivotal messengers between cancer cells and the local/distant microenvironment, which is indispensable for tumor growth and systemic dissemination.6–8 Tumor-derived EVs have been shown to play indispensable roles in multiple stages of cancer progression. They can remodel the tumor microenvironment, suppress immune surveillance, and critically, prepare distant organs for metastatic colonization—a process known as pre-metastatic niche (PMN) formation.9–11 By delivering their oncogenic cargo to resident stromal cells, such as fibroblasts and endothelial cells, or to bone marrow-derived cells, EVs can reprogramme these recipient cells to create a supportive, pro-inflammatory, and pro-angiogenic milieu conducive to the subsequent seeding and outgrowth of circulating tumor cells. While these studies have focused on cataloguing the molecular contents of EVs from various cancers, the upstream regulatory mechanisms that govern the biogenesis, selective cargo loading, and enhanced secretion of EVs, particularly from therapy-resistant cancer cells, remain incompletely understood. Therefore, exploring the regulatory mechanisms underlying EVs production and elucidating the contents they transport from tumor cells are of paramount importance.

Integrins, a diverse family of heterodimeric transmembrane receptors consisting of non-covalently associated α and β subunits, play crucial roles in mediating cell-cell and cell-matrix interaction.12 Emerging evidence indicates that integrins drive various stem cell functions, including tumor initiation, epithelial plasticity, metastasis, and resistance to targeted therapies.13,14 Moreover, integrins constitute the most highly expressed receptors on the surface of EVs and have been shown to play functional roles in guiding EVs to specific sites, acting as the primers of the PMN to support cancer cells settlement in specific organs.15 In particular, lung-tropic cancer cells were found to secrete α6β1 and α6β4 integrin-enriched EVs, whereas liver-tropic cancer cells shed predominantly αvβ5 integrin-positive EVs. These integrins are thought to facilitate PMN formation by mediating EV docking to specific ECM components (e.g., laminin for α6β4) in the target organ, subsequently activating resident stromal cells through inside-out and outside-in signaling. These observations suggest that integrins carried by EVs potentially contribute to the development of organ-specific cancer metastasis. Despite these insights, the precise role, regulatory mechanisms of loading, and downstream signaling cascades triggered by specific integrin-bearing EVs, particularly in the context of HCC and its acquired resistance to targeted therapies like lenvatinib, are still poorly defined and represent a significant gap in knowledge.

Building upon this conceptual framework, we hypothesised that lenvatinib-resistant (LR) HCC cells may undergo adaptive changes that enhance EV secretion and alter EV cargo, including integrins, to foster a pro-metastatic microenvironment. The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway is a central regulator of cellular metabolism, growth, and autophagy, and its dysregulation is common in cancer and therapy resistance. Given its role in controlling lysosomal function and autophagic degradation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs), we further hypothesised that mTOR signaling might be a key regulator of EV secretion in LR HCC. In this study, we demonstrated that lenvatinib-resistant (LR) HCC cells induced EVs secretion by inhibiting the mTOR-dependent autophagic degradation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs). EV-carried ITGβ4 actively promoted interaction with laminin and PI3K-AKT-p65-dependent activation of lung fibroblasts, thus facilitating the establishment of PMNs and lung metastasis. Consequently, targeting mTOR signaling with rapamycin effectively blocked EVs-induced lung metastasis and overcame lenvatinib resistance. Therefore, our findings not only unveil a novel mTOR-ITGβ4-EV-fibroblast axis that drives metastasis in LR-HCC but also propose a rational cotreatment strategy—combining mTOR inhibition with lenvatinib—to suppress EV-mediated metastasis and overcome therapeutic resistance.

Results

Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells exhibit increased lung metastasis capacity

In our previous study, we reported that acquired LR HCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b) presented increased invasion and migration abilities, indicating a potential association between the acquired lenvatinib resistance and the onset of lung metastasis.16 To further investigate this correlation, in the present study, we established orthotopic implantation mouse models using LR and parental HCC cells expressing luciferase. One week after implantation, in vivo imaging using an IVIS 100 Imaging System (Xenogen) was conducted to evaluate the tumor volume. Only the mice whose bioluminescence values surpassed 1.7 × 107 were included in this study (Fig. 1a). The results revealed that both the primary tumor growth (Fig. 1b, c) and lung metastasis (Fig. 1d–g) in the LR group were obviously enhanced compared with those bearing the parental strains. In addition, the survival time of the mice in the LR group was significantly shorter than that of the control group (Fig. 1h). These results suggest that acquired LR HCC cells are associated with an increased capability for lung metastasis.

EVs derived from lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells promote lung metastasis in mice

Accumulating studies have highlighted the pivotal roles of tumor-derived EVs in modulating the PMN to facilitate tumor metastasis.10,17 To investigate the possible mechanism underlying the enhancement of lung metastasis in LR HCC, we isolated EVs from the cell culture supernatants of both LR HCC cells and their parental strains via ultracentrifugation. The EVs were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Western blot analyses were performed to validate the EVs purity. The presence of canonical EV markers (CD63, CD9, and TSG101) was confirmed, whereas the absence of common contaminants was verified using negative markers for endoplasmic reticulum (Calnexin), Golgi apparatus (GM130), and serum proteins (Albumin) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Furthermore, significantly elevated levels of EV proteins were detected in EVs derived from LR strains via BCA assays after normalization to the number of cells (Fig. 2a). In addition, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed increased release of EVs from LR strains compared with the equivalent numbers of the parental HCC cells (Fig. 2b). However, no significant variation in EV size was observed (Supplementary Fig. 2c). These findings indicate that, compared with parental cells, LR HCC cells exhibit increased EV release without altering the size of EVs.

To further investigate the biodistribution of EVs, we intravenously injected 10 μg of Dil-labeled EVs isolated from both the parental and LR strains into the nude mice. After 48 hr, we quantified EV biodistribution and uptake in distant organs through immunofluorescence analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2d). The predominant localizations of EVs were observed in the lungs, bones, and livers of the mice. Notably, the EV-associated fluorescence intensity notably increased in the lungs of the mice injected with EVs derived from the LR strains compared with those derived from the parental strains (Fig. 2c, d). These findings suggest a preference for the accumulation of EVs released from the LR HCC cells in the lungs.

We next established an EV education mouse model comprising repeated EV injections prior to the intravenous injection of HuH7 and PLC/PRF/5 cells to investigate the potential functional consequences of extended EV accumulation in lung metastasis (Fig. 2e). The number of lung metastases in the mice administered with EVs derived from the LR group (LR-EVs) were significantly increased compared with those treated with EVs derived from the control group (Ctrl-EVs) (Fig. 2f, g; Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). To further validate this finding, we preconditioned the nude mice with either LR-EVs or Ctrl-EVs before the orthotopic implantation of HCC tumor seeds in the liver (Fig. 2h). Consistently, LR-EVs significantly enhanced lung metastasis in the orthotopic xenograft model compared with Ctrl-EVs (Fig. 2i, j; Supplementary Fig. 2g, h). Taken together, these findings indicate that the accumulation of EVs released from LR HCC cells significantly facilitates the lung metastasis of HCC.

Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells increase EVs secretion via the mTOR-dependent autophagic degradation of MVBs

The aforementioned findings indicated increased release of EVs from LR strains (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f), prompting deeper investigation into the underlying molecular mechanisms involved. Given that prior studies highlighted autophagy dysfunction as a critical driver of EV secretion,18–20 we postulated that dysregulated MVB autophagic degradation contributed to the increased EV secretion observed in the LR strains. First, we examined the levels of the autophagy markers including LC3B I/II and SQSTM1, CD63 (a MVB marker) and LAMP1 (a lysosomal marker) using Western blotting and revealed that the levels of LC3B-II, SQSTM1 and CD63 were obviously elevated whereas the LAMP1 level was decreased in LR strains compared with the parental cells (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3a). However, there were no discernible differences in the mRNA expression of these markers between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Next, the LR and parental cells were treated with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX), and the degradation levels of LC3B I/II, SQSTM1, CD63 and TSG101 (an EV marker) were assessed at designated time intervals. Compared with that in the parental strains, their degradation in the LR strains was obviously delayed (Supplementary Fig. 3c–f). To further investigate the potential involvement of the autophagic degradation process in the LR strains, HCC cells were transfected with the RFP-GFP-LC3B plasmid. An increased number of RFP+-GFP+ puncta (depicted as yellow) were observed specifically in LR strains (Fig. 3b), which further supported that the accumulated autophagic flux in LR strains was primarily due to impaired degradation of autophagic proteins. Moreover, to gain insight into the MVBs accumulation in LR strains, electron microscopic analysis was performed to examine the quantity and morphology of MVBs. A notable increase in the number of MVBs was observed in LR strains (Fig. 3c, d). Immunofluorescence assays further confirmed the MVBs accumulation in LR strains (Fig. 3e, f). These results indicate that LR strains possess more MVBs due to the inhibition of the autophagic degradation process of MVBs.

Previous studies have underscored the pivotal role of mTOR as a crucial regulator that governs various stages of the autophagy process, including nucleation, elongation, maturation, and termination.21 Therefore, to elucidate the mechanisms of MVB autophagic degradation impairment in LR strains, we analyzed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data in our previous study16 via gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and the results revealed activation of the mTOR signaling pathway in the LR strains (Fig. 3g), which was further validated via Western blot analyses (Fig. 3h). Rapamycin, an inhibitor of the mTOR pathway, attenuated the release of EVs from the LR strains, as demonstrated by NTA and Western blot analyses of the EV markers (Fig. 3i, j, Supplementary Fig 4a–c). These findings demonstrate that the mTOR signaling pathway mediates EV release in LR strains.

To determine whether mTOR inhibition promoted MVBs autophagic degradation in the LR strains, the parental and LR strains were treated with rapamycin for 48 hr, and alterations in p-mTOR/mTOR, LC3B, SQSTM1 and CD63 expression were assessed via Western blotting. The findings revealed that rapamycin, rather than lenvatinib alone, notably suppressed the expression of p-mTOR/mTOR, autophagy markers, and CD63 in LR HCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). Rapamycin treatment induced a reduction in the number of GFP-RFP puncta (depicted in yellow) in the LR strains transfected with the GFP-RFP-LC3B plasmid but not in parental HCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 4f–h). Concomitantly, rapamycin attenuated the elevated CD63 expression in LR cells but had no significant effect on parental cells, indicating that inhibition of mTOR signaling promoted the autophagic degradation of MVBs in the LR strains (Supplementary Fig. 4i–k). Taken together, these findings suggest that LR HCC cells regulate the secretion of EVs by activating mTOR signaling, which inhibits the autophagic degradation of MVBs (Fig. 3k).

EV-associated ITGβ4 facilitates lung colonization in HCC

To comprehensively delineate the biological activity of LR-EVs, label-free proteomic analysis of HuH7-EVs and HuH7 LR-EVs was conducted via mass spectrometry. A total of 1453 proteins were identified in HuH7-EVs, 937 proteins in HuH7 LR-EVs, with an overlap of 780 proteins between them (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Quantitative analyses of the proteomic data revealed that 124 proteins displayed significant differences between HuH7-EVs and HuH7 LR-EVs, with 51 proteins showing upregulation in HuH7 LR-EVs (Fig. 4a). The top 5 up-regulated candidates included histone H2B (H2BC15), proteasome chain 13 (PMSB3), 60S ribosomal protein L12 (RPL12), integrin β4 (ITGβ4) and t-complex protein 1 subunit α (TCP1). Among these proteins, ITGβ4, a member of the integrin family, has emerged as a key protein with potential pro-metastatic function. The increased levels of ITGβ4 in LR cells and their corresponding EVs were further validated by Western blotting (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig 5b).

To further explore the functional role of ITGβ4 in LR strains, we utilized short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) to knock down ITGβ4 expression in LR strains (Supplementary Fig. 5c) and established HuH7 and PLC/PRF/5 cells with stable overexpression of ITGβ4 (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Subsequently, we isolated EVs from both the ITGβ4-WT and ITGβ4-KD LR strains to investigate whether altering the cargo of ITGβ4 packaged into EVs could prevent lung metastasis in mice. According to the results of TEM and NTAs, the absence of ITGβ4 had no significant effect on the morphological characteristics, modal size distribution, or secretion patterns of EVs (Supplementary Fig. 5e–g). Furthermore, quantitative assessment through BCA assays revealed that the total protein content of EVs remained unchanged under ITGβ4-deficient conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5h). To further assess the impact of EV-ITGβ4 on lung metastasis, we preconditioned nude mice with EVs obtained from the ITGβ4-WT and ITGβ4-KD LR strains before the intravenous injection of HCC cells (Fig. 4c). Both bioluminescence (Fig. 4d, e) and histological analyses (Fig. 4f, g) demonstrated that the depletion of ITGβ4 in LR-EVs led to a reduction in the incidence of lung metastasis. Collectively, these findings suggest that ITGβ4-enriched EVs derived from LR strains facilitate the lung metastasis of HCC.

EV-ITGβ4 binding to laminin receptors on lung fibroblasts triggers PMN formation via the PI3K-AKT-p65 pathway

Tumor-derived EVs are recognized to integrate with lung cells during the formation of the PMN.22 Therefore, to further elucidate the mechanisms by which EV-ITGβ4 derived from LR strains contributes to lung metastasis, we aimed to identify the specific cell types targeted by these EVs. mCherry-labeled EVs were injected intravenously into mice and after 14 days, lungs were harvested and digested. Various cell populations including CD31+ endothelial cells, CD140a+ fibroblasts, CD326+ epithelial cells, CD45+CD11b+ F4/80+ macrophages and CD45+CD11b+ Ly6G+ neutrophils were analyzed for the uptake of CD63-mCherry EVs using flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 6a). We observed that approximately 25% of fibroblasts (CD45-CD140a+), 23% of epithelial cells (CD45-CD326+), 10% of endothelial cells (CD45-CD31+), 6% of macrophages (CD45+CD11b+F4/80+) and 5% of neutrophils (CD45+CD11b+ Ly6G+) displayed EV-associated fluorescence, indicating that EVs were taken up predominantly by CD140+ fibroblasts and CD326+ epithelial cells (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 6b). In addition, EVs labeled with Dil (red) were incubated with HFL1s and 16HBEs in vitro. Immunofluorescence imaging after 30 min revealed the presence of red spots in HFL1s whereas no significant red spots were detected in 16HBEs (Supplementary Fig. 6c). These findings indicate that although fibroblasts and epithelial cells are the primary cell types that take up HCC-derived EVs in the lungs, fibroblasts exhibit faster and more substantial incorporation of EVs. Therefore, lung fibroblasts are considered as the primary recipient cell type within the PMN, warranting our subsequent focus on them.

The high targeting efficiency of EVs is determined by specific interactions between membrane-proteins of EVs and plasma membrane receptors on recipient cells.23 To identify the cognate receptor for ITGβ4 on fibroblast membranes, RNA-seq was performed to compare the lung fibroblasts cultured with EVs derived from ITGβ4-overexpressing HuH7 cells and control cells (Fig. S6d). Pathway enrichment analyses, including Reactome, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and GSEA, revealed significant enrichment of laminin interactions and extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor signaling pathways in HFLs treated with EVs from ITGβ4-overexpressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 6e–g), suggesting that EV-ITGβ4 may promote tumor progression through interactions with laminin and the modulation of ECM signaling. Next, we systematically investigated whether EV-ITGβ4 interacts with laminin to medicate its uptake by lung fibroblasts. Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays revealed a direct physical interaction between ITGβ4 and laminin (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, immunofluorescence studies demonstrated that genetic knockdown of laminin in lung fibroblasts significantly impaired EV-ITGβ4 internalization, providing functional evidence that laminin serves as the primary receptor mediating EV-ITGβ4 uptake in lung fibroblasts (Fig. 5c, d).

On the basis of the findings above, we focused on the potential impact of EV-ITGβ4 derived from LR strains on the behavior of lung fibroblasts. Western blot analyses revealed that exposure of HFL1s and adult lung fibroblasts to LR-EVs led to an increase in the expression of α-SMA (a biomarker for fibroblast activation expression) (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 6h). However, this increase in α-SMA expression was notably attenuated by the treatment with EVs derived from ITGβ4-knockdown LR strains (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 6h). On the other hand, ITGβ4 overexpression further increased α-SMA expression (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Fig. 6i). Similar results were observed both in vitro (Fig. 5h, Supplementary Fig. 7a) and in vivo (Fig. 5i, Supplementary Fig. 7b) via immunofluorescence assays. In the migration assays, the migration of HFL1s was greater when they were cocultured with LR-EVs than when they were cocultured with LS-EVs or the control group (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Conversely, their migratory capacity was impeded following treatment with EVs derived from shITGβ4 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). Furthermore, given the pivotal role of inflammatory factors in fibroblast activation, we analyzed the expression levels of proinflammatory genes and chemokines in fibroblasts treated with EVs from HCC cells. Increased mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TGF-β, and CXCL12 were observed in fibroblasts exposed to LR-EVs. However, these effects were abrogated by ITGβ4 silencing (Supplementary Fig. 7g).

To explore the specific mechanisms underlying the impact of EVs on the phenotype of HFL1s, we performed RNA-seq on HFL1s following 24-hour treatment with HuH7-EVs and HuH7 LR-EVs derived from an equal number of cells. A total of 93 upregulated genes and 77 downregulated genes were identified (Fig. 5j). KEGG pathway analysis identified the pivotal involvement of the PI3K-AKT and NF-κB signaling pathways in the activation of HFL1s (Fig. 5k). Western blot analyses further confirmed that LR-EVs induced the phosphorylation of AKT and NF-κB expression (Fig. 5l, Supplementary Fig. 7h) whereas ITGβ4 silencing notably attenuated the activation of PI3K-AKT-p65 pathway in lung fibroblasts (Fig. 5m, Supplementary Fig. 7i). The activation of HFL1s induced by EV-ITGβ4 was effectively hindered by treatment with ipatasertib, an AKT inhibitor, whereas SB202190, a p65 inhibitor, exhibited no obvious effect on α-SMA expression (Fig. 5n, Supplementary Fig. 8a). Similarly, transient siRNA-mediated silencing of AKT and p65 in EV-treated HFL1 fibroblasts yielded results consistent with our pharmacological profile (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Furthermore, phosphorylation of both AKT and p65 following EV treatment was significantly attenuated in lamininβ1/2 KD fibroblasts compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. 8c), demonstrating that laminin is required for the efficient activation of the AKT-p65 signaling pathway by tumor-derived EVs.

In addition, immunofluorescence assays demonstrated that compared with no treatment or treatment with Ctrl-EVs, treatment with LR-EVs promoted the nuclear translocation of p65 in HFL1s (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Notably, treatment with ITGβ4-enriched EVs increased the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TGF-β, and CXCL12, whereas these effects were attenuated upon the inhibition of either AKT or p65 (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Taken together, these findings strongly indicate that EV-ITGβ4 derived from LR strains promotes fibroblast activation through the PI3K-AKT-p65 signaling activation.

The expression levels of ITGβ4 and CD63 are associated with lenvatinib response and prognosis of patients with HCC

To ascertain the clinical relevance of the key modulators, we detected the EV-ITGβ4 levels in the plasma samples obtained from HCC patients. Both Western blot analyses and the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) revealed increased expression levels of ITGβ4 in EVs isolated from the plasma of patients resistant to lenvatinib treatment (Fig. 6a–c). Furthermore, significant increases in the expression levels of p-mTOR, CD63, and ITGβ4 were observed in the nonresponders compared with those in the lenvatinib-sensitive patients (Fig. 6d). Consistent results were demonstrated in HCC tissues via immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining analyses (Fig. 6e, f).

Given the pivotal roles of both EV-ITGβ4 and EV secretion in the lung metastasis of HCC, we first stratified a patient cohort (n = 105) based on EV-ITGβ4 expression into a low-expression group (n = 56) and a high-expression group (n = 49). The results indicated that the high-expression group presented a significantly greater incidence of lung metastasis (18.4% vs. 7.1%) (Fig. 6g). Consequently, these patients also suffered from significantly poorer survival outcomes (Fig. 6h, i). Furthermore, we investigated the prognostic significance of ITGβ4 and CD63 in 188 patients who underwent liver resection for HCC. The upregulation of ITGβ4 and CD63 expression was detected in HCC specimens via IHC staining (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c). Of note, patients in the ITGβ4-high or CD63-high groups presented notably shorter overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates compared than did those with low expression levels of ITGβ4 and CD63 (Supplementary Fig. 9d–h). When considering the co-expression status of ITGβ4 or CD63, we observed that the ITGβ4-high/CD63-high group presented the shortest OS and RFS (Supplementary Fig. 9i, j). Moreover, retrospective analyses revealed a correlation between the ITGβ4-high subgroup and advanced TNM stage, whereas patients in the CD63-high subgroup tended to exhibit poorer tumor differentiation (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). Taken together, these results suggest that ITGβ4 and CD63 expression levels are associated with the lenvatinib response and poor prognosis in patients with HCC.

Blockade of mTOR signaling with rapamycin inhibits HCC metastasis and overcomes lenvatinib resistance

Finally, we investigated whether the inhibition of mTOR-mediated EV secretion could suppress the EV-ITGβ4 / PI3K-AKT-p65 signaling axis in lung fibroblasts, thereby preventing lung metastasis and restoring lenvatinib sensitivity in LR strains. First, we treated LR HCC cells with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin and observed a significant reduction in ITGβ4 + EV release in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 10a). To further determine whether rapamycin could counteract the pro-metastatic impact of LR-EVs, we preconditioned nude mice with EVs derived from parental and LR strains treated with either DMSO or rapamycin, followed by intravenous injection of HuH7 or PLC/PRF/5 cells (Fig. 7a). Strikingly, EVs derived from the rapamycin-treated LR strains markedly suppressed lung metastasis compared with those derived from the DMSO-treated LR strains (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 10b). These results demonstrated that blocking mTOR signaling-dependent ITGβ4 + EVs secretion from LR strains attenuated lung metastasis.

Compared with either lenvatinib or rapamycin treatment alone, the combination of lenvatinib (Lenva) and rapamycin (Rapa) resulted in more substantial inhibition of the proliferation and colony formation of the LR strains (Fig. 7c–e, Supplementary Fig. 10c–e). Importantly, many more apoptotic tumor cells were detected via TUNEL assays under the combination treatment than under either lenvatinib or rapamycin treatment alone (Fig. 7f, g, Supplementary Fig. 10f, g). Moreover, the combination treatment induced a more significant suppression of tumor growth in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent subcutaneous xenografts compared with the monotherapy (Fig. 7h, i, Supplementary Fig. 10h–k). Additionally, we performed electron microscopy to assess the number and morphology of MVBs in mouse subcutaneous tumors following treatment with lenvatinib, rapamycin, or their combination. A marked reduction in MVB number was observed in LR HCC models treated with either rapamycin alone or in combination with lenvatinib, suggesting that rapamycin treatment reduced MVB abundance in LR HCC models (Supplementary Fig 10l, m). These findings provide both in vitro and in vivo evidence to support that targeting the mTOR signaling pathway using rapamycin could effectively restore the responsiveness of LR HCC to lenvatinib.

To further explore the potential of rapamycin in inhibiting the metastasis of HCC, we established orthotopic HCC mouse models using LR strains and treated them with PBS, lenvatinib (20 mg/kg/d), rapamycin (2 mg/kg/d) or their combination on day 7 after tumor implantation (Fig. 7j). Compared with lenvatinib treatment alone, combination therapy significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 7k, l; Supplementary Fig. 11a, b) and lung metastasis (Fig. 7m–p; Supplementary Fig. 11c–f). Moreover, the combination therapy significantly prolonged the OS of the mice (Fig. 7q, Supplementary Fig. 11g).

To more directly elucidate the effects of rapamycin on EV-mediated changes in the PMN, we employed a preconditioned nude mouse model and found that EVs derived from rapamycin-treated LR strains significantly attenuated lung fibroblast activation compared with EVs derived from DMSO-treated LR strains (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Next, we conducted in vitro co-culture experiments and found that EVs derived from rapamycin-treated LR strains impeded the activation of HFL1s and the PI3K-AKT-p65 pathway (Supplementary Fig. 12c). Furthermore, we constructed orthotopic HCC mouse models with resistant HCC cells and administered PBS, lenvatinib (20 mg/kg/d), rapamycin (2 mg/kg) or their combination as described above. After two weeks of treatment, we collected plasma EVs and lung tissues from the mice in each group to further detect changes in EV-ITGβ4 expression, as well as the expression of a-SMA in the lungs. We found that rapamycin treatment alone or in combination with lenvatinib significantly inhibited ITGβ4 expression on EVs (Supplementary Fig. 12 d) and α-SMA expression (Supplementary Fig. 12e, f) in the lungs, suggesting that rapamycin treatment alone or in combination with lenvatinib could inhibit PMN formation by blocking ITGβ4-enriched EV release. Taken together, these findings collectively demonstrate that blocking mTOR signaling with rapamycin effectively inhibits HCC lung metastasis and overcomes the lenvatinib resistance in combination with lenvatinib.

Discussion

Lung metastasis represents the predominant site of HCC dissemination, occurring in approximately 4.8% of all HCC patients and accounting for 30–50% of metastatic cases.24 Lenvatinib is the first-line treatment for advanced HCC. Despite its impressive objective response rate (ORR), acquired drug resistance and its potential role in promoting lung metastasis are substantial challenges for its clinical application. While the development of therapy resistance is a dynamic process involving the entire tumor microenvironment,25 the precise mechanism by which the tumor microenvironment driving lenvatinib resistance is largely unknown. In the present study, we revealed that increased secretion of EVs from LR HCC cells led to lung metastasis. EVs enriched with ITGβ4 fostered PMN formation by activating lung fibroblasts. Notably, LR HCC cells exhibited activated mTOR signaling and suppressed the autophagic degradation processes of MVBs. Consequently, inhibiting mTOR signaling with rapamycin reduced the release of pro-metastatic EVs, thus blocking HCC metastasis and overcoming lenvatinib resistance (Fig. 8).

Anti-angiogenic therapy triggers an adaptive-evasive response that could potentially induce invasion or metastasis. Combining anti-angiogenic therapies with anti-metastatic treatments could improve overall efficacy.26,27 Previous studies on sorafenib, another anti-angiogenic drug used in advanced HCC treatment, have suggested its potential to enhance invasiveness and metastasis in preclinical studies, involving both tumor-dependent and host-mediated mechanisms.28–30 Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying the potential role of lenvatinib in promoting lung metastasis are still under investigation.15,31 In addition, there is a debate regarding whether resistance mechanisms to anti-angiogenic therapy might contribute to increased metastatic potential. In the present study, we focused on EVs to elucidate the mechanism underlying both lenvatinib resistance and lung metastasis. Our findings revealed that ITGβ4, known for its involvement in the targeted delivery of EVs to the lung, is significantly enriched in EVs derived from LR strains.

Lung fibroblasts play a pivotal role in orchestrating the intricate framework of the PMN in the lung22,32 Upon exposure to signals derived from primary tumor cells, activated lung fibroblasts undergo phenotypic alterations and release various factors, including cytokines, growth factors, and chemokines.33 Consequently, these molecular cues remodel the ECM, promote angiogenesis, and recruit immune cells, collectively fostering an environment conducive to the colonization and growth of tumor cells.34–36 Thus, lung fibroblasts are pivotal in shaping the PMN, providing a critical cellular framework that facilitates the initiation and progression of lung metastasis. Our study demonstrated that exposure of lung fibroblasts to ITGβ4-enriched EVs from LR HCC cells activated the PI3K-AKT-p65 signaling pathway, which contributed to the establishment of a conducive microenvironment that supported the lung metastasis of HCC. Furthermore, pharmacological intervention targeting the PI3K-AKT-p65 pathway effectively attenuated this process, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic strategy to block lung fibroblast activation and HCC metastasis.

The process of EV production is gradually being elucidated. EVs are generated as intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) by budding within early endosomes and MVBs. The MVBs either fuse with lysosomes for autophagic degradation or with the plasma membrane (PM) for EV secretion.37 Previous reports have highlighted a correlation between autophagy and EV secretion,20,38,39 suggesting an interplay between EV release and autophagic flux.40 Impairment in autophagic degradation impedes the lysosomal pathway, thereby prompting MVBs to fuse with the PM for EV release.19,20,41 In addition to mTOR signaling activation and impairment of the autophagic‒lysosomal pathway in LR cells, MVBs accumulation and increased EV release from LR strains. Several studies have investigated the effect of mTOR signaling on EV production, yet their findings often present contradictory outcomes.19,39 These discrepancies may arise from diverse experimental models or conditions, leading to varied effects of mTOR signaling on EV release. Therefore, further research should explore the mechanisms through which mTOR signaling mediates EV release under distinct conditions.

Recent investigations have revealed the pivotal role of integrin-related signaling in various cellular functions, including tumor initiation, epithelial plasticity, metastatic reactivation, and resistance to targeted therapies.13,14 Thus, innovative strategies targeting integrins are emerging as promising avenues. Based on our findings, blockade of ITGβ4 has emerged as a potentially promising approach for impeding metastasis and overcoming lenvatinib resistance. A recent study explored ITGβ4-targeted immunotherapy, including ITGβ4-DC vaccination and adoptive transfer of anti-CD3/anti-ITGβ4 bispecific antibody-armed T cells, and demonstrated that immunological targeting of ITGβ4 enhanced the effectiveness of anti-PD-L1 checkpoint blockade.42 However, at present, no reliable ITGβ4-neutralizing antibody or site-specific inhibitor has gained clinical approval, limiting its clinical translational value and underscoring the necessity for further investigation. Nevertheless, we propose that EV-ITGβ4 could serve as a promising biomarker for predicting the response to lenvatinib. The validation of this hypothesis warrants a comprehensive investigation in a large-scale HCC cohort in the future.

Given the inhibitory effect of mTOR on autophagy induction,21 the inhibition of mTOR signaling represents an appealing therapeutic avenue to combat metastasis by inducing the autophagic degradation of MVBs. Moreover, mTOR signaling is increasingly recognized as a significant compensatory pathway contributing to resistance to targeted cancer therapies, suggesting the potential of mTOR inhibitors as adjunctive co-therapies.43,44 Rapamycin, also known as sirolimus, is a mTOR inhibitor derived from the fermentation broth of Streptomyces absorbent and has exhibited anti-tumor effects in recent research.45,46 Combining rapamycin with other agents has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome drug resistance. A recent study confirmed the synergistic inhibitory effect of the mTOR inhibitor temsirolimus in combination with lenvatinib in HCC organoids and patient-derived xenograft models.47 Another study demonstrated that the combination of cabozantinib and mTOR inhibitors represents a novel therapeutic approach against HCC characterized by activated c-MET and AKT/mTOR cascades.48 Our study emphasized that inhibiting mTOR signaling with rapamycin could combat lung metastasis and overcome resistance to lenvatinib. Nevertheless, owing to its potent immunosuppressive effects, frequent use and high doses of rapamycin can create an immunosuppressive microenvironment, compromising the body’s immune surveillance and anti-tumor effects, thus leading to tumor recurrence and metastasis.49 Hence, optimizing the beneficial survival effects of rapamycin by adjusting its dosage, frequency, and sequential administration is an urgent issue that requires resolution.

In summary, our investigation elucidated the pivotal role of EV-ITGβ4 from LR HCC cells in the PMN establishment by activating lung fibroblasts, facilitating the colonization of HCC cells in the lungs. Our findings strongly suggest that the combination treatment with rapamycin and lenvatinib is a promising strategy to combat HCC metastasis and overcome lenvatinib resistance. Furthermore, the assessment of plasma EV-ITGβ4 levels in HCC patients may serve as a valuable biomarker to identify individuals who are more likely to benefit from lenvatinib-based therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The human HCC cell lines (HuH7 and PLC/PRF/5), HEK293T cells, human fetal lung fibroblast 1 (HFL1) cells, human bronchial epithelial (16HBE) cells, and the mouse HCC cell line Hepa1-6 were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cell lines were authenticated through Short Tandem Repeat (STR) analysis to confirm their identity and to ensure the absence of mycoplasma contamination. HCC cell lines and HEK293T cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone), 2 mM glutamine and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco). HFL1 cells were cultivated in F12K (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). 16HBE cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS + and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco). All the cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The LR HCC cell lines named HuH7 LR and PLC/PRF/5 LR were established according to our previous study.16

Patients and clinical specimens

A tissue microarray was constructed from HCC samples (n = 188) obtained from patients who underwent curative liver resection for HCC at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China) from September 2016 to December 2018. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the duration between the surgery and either the date of death or the last follow-up visit. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was determined as the duration from the surgery to disease recurrence/metastasis or the last follow-up visit. Recurrence was diagnosed on the basis of characteristic imaging features on gadoxetate disodium-enhanced MRI (arterial phase hyperenhancement with washout) with corresponding elevated AFP levels. Blood samples (n = 105) were collected from another cohort of HCC patients including 13 of them with lung metastasis. The serum was isolated and subsequently preserved at -80 °C for further investigation.

Additional, twenty HCC patients who received lenvatinib treatment at the authors’ institution were included. All patients had a pathological diagnosis of HCC without evidence of extrahepatic or lymph node metastasis. Treatment response was evaluated in 3 months after treatment commenced via the mRECIST criteria.50,51 Patients with disease progression (PD) were categorized as LR patients, whereas those who achieved partial response (PR) or stable disease (SD) were classified as lenvatinib-sensitive (LS) patients. All procedures were carried out with the approval of the Ethical Committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, and were conducted in compliance with relevant regulations and guidelines (approval number: 2023-HSYY-289-JZS). Written informed consent was obtained prior to the start of the study.

EV purification

For EV isolation from cell culture supernatants, cells were cultured in media supplemented with 10% EV-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 48 hr, prepared by subjecting FBS to overnight ultracentrifugation at 134,000× g. EV isolation from the conditioned medium was performed via a sequential ultracentrifugation method as previously described.52 Briefly, the cell culture supernatant was centrifuged at 500× g for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 3000× g for 20 min, and then centrifugation at 12,000× g for 20 min to remove cellular debris. EVs were collected by ultracentrifugation at 100,000× g for 70 min. The resulting pellet containing EVs was washed by resuspending it in PBS and reultracentrifuging it at 100,000× g for 70 min. The protein concentration and number of EVs in PBS were measured via a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227).

For EV isolation from human plasma, venous citrated blood from HCC patients was initially centrifuged at 1500× g for 30 min to obtain cell-free plasma. Subsequently, 1 ml of plasma was centrifuged at 16500× g for 45 min to pellet the large vesicles. Plasma-derived EVs were finally isolated from the collected supernatants by centrifugation at 100, 000× g for 2 hr.

Orthotopic implantation model of HCC

5 ×;106 luciferase-transfected LR HCC cells were implanted into the right flank of mice. Once the tumor volume reached 200 mm3 mice were euthanized, and tumor samples were collected, finely minced into 1 mm3 cubes, and subsequently implanted into the liver capsule of 6-8-week-old BALB/c nude mice. One week after implantation, in vivo imaging via an IVIS 100 Imaging System (Xenogen) was conducted to evaluate the tumor volume. Only mice displaying bioluminescence values surpassing 1.7 ×107 were included in this study, yielding a successful engraftment rate of 75% in parental cells and 80% in LR cells. These selected mice were then randomly divided into four groups (5 mice per group) for subsequent drug intervention, following a similar approach as the subcutaneous tumor model. Upon completion of the experiment, ex vivo bioluminescence imaging of lungs was performed and dissected lungs were subjected to standard histological examination. The specific criteria for euthanasia were as follows: (1) Tumor Burden: a primary tumor volume exceeding 1.5 cm³; or (2) Clinical deterioration: evidenced by signs such as significant ulceration, necrosis, or severe impairment of normal functions including mobility or feeding. Animals were monitored daily, and any subject meeting one or more of these criteria was promptly euthanized, with that date recorded as the event for survival analysis. These animal experiments were conducted following approval from both the Shanghai Medical Experimental Animal Care Commission and the Institutional Animal Care of Fudan University.

Lung colonization assays

For experimental metastasis assays, 6-week-old nude mice underwent intravenous injection with 5 × 105 luciferase-labelled HCC cells following EVs pre-conditioning, as detailed previously. The occurrence and extent of lung metastasis were assessed using the IVIS® Spectrum Imaging System in 60 days after injection. Following the imaging evaluation, lung tissues were harvested and systematically sectioned at 200 μm intervals. From this series, 4–5 μm thick sections were collected at each interval and stained with H&E for analysis. All H&E-stained sections were evaluated by an observer blinded to the experimental groups, and metastatic nodules were counted based on standardized morphological criteria. The total number of nodules identified across all systematically sampled sections from one lung was divided by the number of sections analyzed to yield the mean number of metastases per section, which was used to represent the metastatic burden for each animal.

Mouse xenograft studies

All mice experiments adhered to approved protocols from both the Shanghai Medical Experimental Animal Care Commission and the Institutional Animal Care of Fudan University. To investigate the combinational effect of lenvatinib (Lenva) and rapamycin (Rapa), suspensions of LR cells were prepared in a 1:10 matrigel (Corning) mixture, and subsequently, 5 × 106 cells were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of each 6-8-week-old BALB/c nunu mice (male, n = 20). These groups were subjected to five-day-per-week treatments administered via oral gavage, consisting of PBS, Lenva (10 mg/kg), Rapa (2 mg/kg), and a combination treatment (Lenva 10 mg/kg + Rapa 2 mg/kg). The treatment regimen spanned over a period of 4 weeks. Tumor volume was assessed every week with a caliper and calculated using the formula: (a × b2)/2, where “a” represents the longest tumor diameter and “b” represents the shortest tumor diameter. At the conclusion of the experiment, all mice were euthanized for the collection and processing of tumor samples. The drugs lenvatinib (S1164) and rapamycin (S1039) were purchased from Selleckchem for use in this study.

Proteomics analysis

The label-free proteome analysis was performed at Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, both LS- and LR-EVs underwent lysis using SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM DTT, pH7.6) followed by trypsin digestion based on the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) procedure. Subsequently, the generated peptides were directly subjected to analysis by LC-MS/MS employing a Q Exactive mass spectrometer manufactured by Thermo Scientific. The MS raw data obtained from each sample were integrated and processed using the MaxQuant 1.5.3.17 software for identification and quantitation analysis.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

For RNA sequencing of HFL1s, total RNA was extracted from cells via the TRIZOL reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) from Thermo Fisher Scientific, followed by DNase treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. After RNA quality was assessed via an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, the mRNA was used for RNA-seq library preparation with the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit. Subsequently, the RNA libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq machine and paired-end reads were generated. The Python package HTSeq was used to generate read counts for each gene, which were analyzed via the R package DESeq2 (v.1.28.0). The edgeR R package v3.4.2 was used to calculate the significance of expression differences. Gene expression levels were estimated via fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) by StringTie v1.3.4 d. The criteria for identifying significant differences included | log2 Fold change | ≥ 1 and P value < 0.05. Pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed via DAVID functional annotation clustering. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed via KOBAS (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/index.php).

Statistical analysis

Analysis of the data was performed via GraphPad Prism 7.0 software. Data were provided as mean ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical significance between two groups was assessed via a two-sample t-test accounting for unequal variances. Multiple group comparisons were accomplished via one- or two-way ANOVA as indicated. Survival curves were generated via the Kaplan-Meier method, and the equality between these curves was evaluated via the log-rank test. Statistical significance levels are represented as follows: ****P < 0.0001; ***P < 0.001; **P < 0.01; *P < 0.05; ns: no significance. Statistical details of each experiment are described in the relevant figure legends. No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size.
