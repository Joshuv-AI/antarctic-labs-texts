Surfactant-enhanced Cuδ+ and induced electrostatic forces promote the electrocatalytic deuteron-dechlorination of trichloroacetic acid
Authors: Mengmei Qin, Meng He, Chuanqi Cheng, Rui Li, Cuibo Liu, Bin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrocatalytic deuteron-dechlorination of trichloroacetic acid (TCAA) in D2O provides a green route to synthesize commercial acetic-d3 acid-d (AA-d4). Synthesizing AA-d4 with a high Faradaic efficiency (FE) and reaction rate is highly challenging because of the difficult C−Cl bond deuteration of the 2-monochloroacetic-2,2-d2 acid-d (MCAA-d3) intermediate. Here, a quaternary ammonium salt surfactant-modified low-coordination copper electrocatalyst is designed, achieving TCAA-to-AA-d4 with a 91% selectivity, 91% FE and 0.59 mmol h−1 reaction rate at −100 mA cm−2. Mechanistic and kinetic studies reveal that the surfactant enhances the adsorption of MCAA-d3 through electrostatic forces and increases the electron deficiency of Cuδ+ sites, which accelerates electron transfer and promotes C−Cl bond activation, increasing the AA-d4 selectivity. Surfactant-induced low D2O coverage suppresses D2 formation, improving the FE. AA-d4 electrosynthesis (1.84 g) with a 30 mmol h−1 reaction rate and 65% FE at 600 mA cm−2 and deuterated drug applications demonstrate promising potential.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Sustainability

Introduction

Owing to the kinetic isotope effect (KIE), deuterium (D)-labeling has important applications in organic synthesis, the pharmaceutical industry, etc1–5. Usually, the incorporation of a larger number of D at one position will be of practical use in pharmaceutical studies6,7. Among the deuterated compounds, acetic-d3 acid-d (AA-d4) is a widely used and commercially available chemical8–10. Typically, AA-d4 is produced through the H/D exchange of acetic acid (AA, CH3COOH) or acetic anhydride with D2O or expensive acetone-d611–13. To achieve high D incorporation, noble metals, high temperatures, and repeated H/D exchange processes (3 to 5 or more cycles) are usually required to promote the activation of the relatively inert C(sp3)−H bond. Therefore, it is highly important to search for a mild and expedient deuteration method for the synthesis of AA-d4 with a high D ratio using easily accessible starting materials.

Electrocatalytic C−X bond deuteration of halides with D2O can achieve deuteration with precise D numbers and sites and high D ratios at room temperature14–16. Compared with tribromoacetic acid and triiodoacetic acid, trichloroacetic acid (TCAA, CCl3COOH) is less expensive and dissolves well in alkaline solutions. Thus, the electrocatalytic full deuteration of TCAA in D2O provides a green and alternative route toward AA-d4 synthesis. Although the electrocatalytic hydrogenation of TCAA, which proceeds through the TCAA→dichloroacetic acid (DCAA, CHCl2COOH)→monochloroacetic acid (MCAA, CH2ClCOOH)→AA processes, has been studied, these processes are used mainly to remove the TCAA waste from chlorinated water17–19. Additionally, MCAA is often obtained because the activation of the C−Cl bond in MCAA is more difficult than that in TCAA and DCAA (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and Supplementary Notes 1, 2), resulting in its hard hydrogenation to AA. Recently, more electronegative Pd was used to modify Cu (CuPd0.52) to obtain Cuδ+ sites that promote the C−Cl deuteration of MCAA with D2O20. However, owing to the easy coordination of Cl− with the vacant orbital of Pd to form stable Pd−Cl bonds21, it is difficult to stabilize highly active Cuδ+ at high Cl− concentrations or for long-term electrosynthesis. Moreover, the preparation of CuPd0.52 needs precise control and noble metals, increasing the complexity and cost. Furthermore, the rapid synthesis of AA-d4 at high current densities with high Faradaic efficiency (FE) is significant, but it is limited by severe D2 evolution reactions. Aqueous electrochemical reactions usually occur at the electrode−electrolyte interface, and interfacial microenvironment modulation by surfactants is an effective way to suppress H2/D2 formation and improve the FE22–26. However, current studies have focused mainly on the effects of surfactants on the interfacial water structure, whereas their effects on the electronic structure of catalysts and their interactions with reactants have often been ignored27. Therefore, developing a simple strategy to modify both the catalytic sites and the interface microenvironment to facilitate the dechlorination of the MCAA-d3 intermediate and suppress D2 formation to achieve a high FE of AA-d4 at high current densities and clarify the influence of surfactants on the electronic structure of the catalysts, the reactants, and the dechlorination kinetics are highly important.

In this work, an octyltrimethylammonium bromide (OTAB)−modified low-coordination copper (LC-Cu) electrocatalyst with abundant Cuδ+ sites was designed for the electrocatalytic full deuteration of TCAA in D2O. The AA-d4 product with a 91% selectivity, 91% FE, and 0.59 mmol h−1 reaction rate at −100 mA cm−2 (Fig. 1a) is achieved, far surpassing those of coordination-saturated Cu (CS-Cu) without the addition of OTAB (26% selectivity and 21% FE). Theoretical and experimental results show that (i) the downshift of the antibonding state of the C−Cl interaction of MCAA toward the Fermi level (Ef) on the low-coordination Cuδ+ sites promotes C−Cl bond adsorption and activation and that (ii) the N+ head of OTAB adsorbed on LC-Cu can enhance the oxidation state of the Cuδ+ sites and form electrostatic forces between the N+ head and the COO− of MCAA-d3, which prolongs the residence time of MCAA-d3 on the catalyst surface, enhances the adsorption of MCAA-d3, accelerates electron transfer, and decreases the activation energy of the C−Cl bond. (Fig. 1b). Additionally, the orderly arrangement of OTAB molecules under bias potentials reduces the interface D2O coverage and suppresses D2 formation. Furthermore, the quick and gram-scale preparation of AA-d4 with a 65% FE at 600 mA cm−2 and deuterated drug synthesis demonstrate the potential utility of our method.

Results

Reaction process analysis and theoretical calculation assisted design of a LC-Cu electrocatalyst

The electrocatalysts significantly influence the reaction activity and/or product selectivity of an electrochemical reaction28,29. An electrode screen for the electrocatalytic hydrogenation of TCAA in a 0.5 M K2CO3 solution at −100 mA cm−2 was performed, and the Cu foil had the highest selectivity and FE of the AA product (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Note 3). Nevertheless, modifying the electronic structure of the Cu catalytic sites is necessary to further improve the performance of AA. A KIE value of kH/kD = 1.43 reveals that water electroreduction is not involved in the rate-determining step of the TCAA deuteration reaction (Supplementary Fig. 4). Thus, to better understand the dechlorination process of TCAA, the hydrogenation pathway of TCAA with H2O over Cu(111) is theoretically simulated. As shown in Fig. 2a, all three hydrogen-addition steps (blue line) are exothermic, but the dechlorination of adsorbed MCAA (*MCAA) to *CH2COOH requires much higher energy than the other two dichlorination steps (red line, 0.77 eV vs. 0.12 and −0.69 eV), suggesting greater difficulties in the dechlorination of MCAA than TCAA and DCAA (Supplementary Figs. 5a, b and Supplementary Note 4). Additionally, the highest overpotential of MCAA-d3 electroreduction from linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves (Fig. 2b) and the sluggish conversion of MCAA to AA from proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR) validations (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Note 5) indicate that the deuteration of MCAA-d3 is the most difficult. Thus, exploring an efficient Cu electrocatalyst is crucial for promoting the deuteration of MCAA-d3.

Cu+/2+ is usually the active species in the Cu-catalyzed coupling reaction30,31. Therefore, increasing the oxidation state of the Cu sites may be helpful in promoting the C−Cl bond activation of MCAA-d3. Creating low-coordination structures in Cu materials to generate abundant Cuδ+ sites is beneficial for increasing the intrinsic activity of the electrochemical reactions (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Note 6)32–36. Compared with those of CS-Cu, the projected crystal orbital Hamilton population (−pCOHP) analyses reveal a downshift relative to Ef and an increased area of the antibonding state of the C−Cl interaction on low-coordination Cu (LC-Cu), indicating enhanced *MCAA adsorption on LC-Cu (Fig. 2c). In addition, the integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population (ICOHP) value, which can be used for quantitative analysis of C−Cl bond activation, also increases after MCAA adsorbs at the LC-Cu sites, suggesting that LC-Cu can effectively weaken the C−Cl bond of MCAA, thereby promoting its cleavage. Moreover, the charge density difference reveals stronger electronic coupling between MCAA and LC-Cu than between MCAA and CS-Cu, which promotes electron transfer from the electrode to MCAA and accelerates the reaction (Fig. 2d). The above theoretical results encouraged us to synthesize the LC-Cu electrocatalyst for the electrocatalytic deuteration of TCAA with D2O.

Synthesis and performance of LC-Cu for the electrocatalytic deuteration of TCAA to AA-d4

The LC-Cu electrocatalysts were prepared via in situ electroreduction of CuO nanorod (NR) precursors in a 0.5 M K2CO3 electrolyte at −1.0 V vs. Hg/HgO (Supplementary Fig. 8). The disappearance of the CuO peak from the LSV curves and the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) results confirm the full conversion of CuO to metallic Cu (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10a, b and Supplementary Note 7). Furthermore, the local coordination environment of the reduced Cu sample was examined via X-ray absorption of the near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses. The XANES curves show that the Cu absorption edge position of the sample is located between the Cu foil and CuO, suggesting a positively charged Cuδ+ state (Supplementary Fig. 11). The Fourier transformed k3-weighted Cu-K edge EXAFS spectra show that the average Cu-Cu coordination shell of the sample is lower than that of the Cu foil, indicating the presence of abundant low-coordination Cuδ+ sites (Fig. 2e). These results demonstrate the successful synthesis of the LC-Cu electrocatalyst.

Next, we tested its performance in the electrocatalytic hydrogenation of TCAA at −100 mA cm–2. Despite similar conversions of TCAA over LC-Cu and CS-Cu, LC-Cu can substantially promote the conversion of the MCAA intermediate to AA, resulting in ~5.2- and 5.7-fold increases in AA selectivity (14%→72%) and FE (11%→62%) (Fig. 2f). Additionally, the activation energy is lower for the hydrogenation of MCAA-d3 over LC-Cu than for that over CS-Cu (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Figs. 12, 13a, b and Supplementary Note 8). These results demonstrate the facilitating effect of Cuδ+ sites on the formation of AA, rationalizing our speculations and the theoretical results.

Surfactant-assisted selectivity and FE enhancement and unveiling the origin of promotion

Despite the acceptable selectivity and FE of AA over LC-Cu, further improving these two metrics is necessary for practical utilization. As TCAA deuteration continued, the FE of AA decreased gradually after 40 min, and the reaction rates increased slowly (Fig. 3a). This may be because the concentration of MCAA near the electrode surface decreased, which led to a high H2O: MCAA ratio, increasing H2 formation. A plot of the reaction rate against the concentration of MCAA-d3 suggests that MCAA-d3 deuteration is consistent with a first-order kinetics model (Supplementary Figs. 14a, b and Supplementary Note 9). These findings indicate that increasing the local MCAA-d3 concentration can increase the conversion of MCAA-d3 to AA-d4. In addition, decreasing the interface D2O coverage is also helpful for improving the FE by inhibiting D2 evolution. Thus, we plan to modulate the LC-Cu−electrolyte interface to further improve the FE of AA-d4 at high current densities, which is important for achieving a high reaction rate and D2O utilization.

The addition of surfactants efficiently regulates the interface microenvironment to improve the electrochemical performance37–41. Long-chain quaternary ammonium salt surfactants (QASSs) are a good choice to meet the above requirements because the electrostatic force between the N+ head of the QASSs and the O− of the carboxylate of MCAA-d3 can concentrate MCAA-d3, and the hydrophobic alkyl tail can repel the D2O at the interface. Thus, four types of QASSs with the same head groups but different tail chains were added to the cathodic electrolyte under other identified conditions for the hydrogenation of TCAA (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 15). The AA selectivity and FE both increased after the addition of different QASSs (1.0 mM), which reached their highest values (92.5% and 87.0%) when the optimal amount of 0.75 mM OTAB was used (Supplementary Fig. 16). In addition, a 0.56 mmol h−1 yield rate was achieved, which was 1.3 times faster than that obtained over LC-Cu without OTAB (Supplementary Fig. 17). However, a higher concentration of OTAB was detrimental to the reaction, which might be attributed to the excess OTAB coverage on the electrode surface impeding TCAA adsorption. There was no obvious decrease in the conversion of TCAA or the FEs of AA after 10 cycles at −100 mA cm−2, indicating the good durability of the OTAB-modified LC-Cu (Supplementary Fig. 18). With the optimal amount of OTAB, AA-d4 with 91% selectivity, 91% FE, and 0.59 mmol h−1 was achieved at −100 mA cm−2, which was much higher than that obtained over CS-Cu without the addition of OTAB (26% selectivity, 21% FE, and 0.14 mmol h−1) (Supplementary Fig. 19).

As MCAA-d3 deuteration is confirmed as the most difficult step for the electrocatalytic deuteration of TCAA to AA-d4 over LC-Cu, a series of in situ and ex experiments and theoretical calculations were performed to investigate the effect of this process on promoting the deuteration of TCAA.

Proving the enhancement of the MCAA-d3 concentration and adsorption by OTAB

Potential-dependent in situ attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and the N 1 s XPS spectra of LC-Cu after electrolysis indicate the adsorption of OTAB on the LC-Cu surface (Supplementary Figs. 20a, b and Supplementary Note 10), which is arranged in an orderly manner with the head group on the electrode surface and the tail alkyl chain group reaching the electrolyte37–39. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to explore the underlying mechanism of the interaction between surfactants and reactants and/or intermediates. The simulated distance between N+ in OTAB and O− in MCAA is ~4.72 Å (Supplementary Figs. 21a–c, Supplementary Note 11 and Supplementary Movies 1 and 2)42,43. Meanwhile, the electron localization function pattern displays a higher electron density at the N+ of OTAB and a lower electron density around the carboxylate group of MCAA in comparison with MCAA adsorbed on LC-Cu (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Figs. 22, 23 and Supplementary Notes 12, 13). These results indicate the existence of electrostatic interactions between OTAB and MCAA-d3, which can concentrate MCAA-d3 on the LC-Cu surface (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Note 14). Furthermore, XPS analysis reveals a positive shift in the Cu 2p3/2 binding energy after OTAB is adsorbed on LC-Cu (Fig. 3d), indicating greater electron deficiency at the Cuδ+ sites. Therefore, a strong interaction between MCAA-d3, OTAB, and LC-Cu formed (Supplementary Fig. 25), which further enhanced the adsorption of MCAA-d3 on LC-Cu. Thus, a greater adsorption energy of MCAA on LC-Cu (Fig. 3e) and an obvious redshift of the C−Cl bond vibration (from 778 to 764 cm‒1) from in situ Raman spectroscopy are observed (Fig. 3f), contributing to the activation and deuteration of the C−Cl bond.

Validating the promoting effect of OTAB on the kinetics of MCAA-d3 deuteration

Electrochemical kinetic analyses were carried out to understand the promoting effect of OTAB on the kinetics of electrocatalytic deuteration. A Tafel slope of 129 ± 5 mV/decade implies that the rate-determining step of MCAA-d3 deuteration involves a 1e– transfer process; thus, accelerating the electron transfer rate is expected to improve the reaction kinetics (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 26 and Supplementary Note 15). The charge transfer resistance (Rct) obtained via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements reveals a smaller Rct after the addition of OTAB (Supplementary Figs. 27a, b). Additionally, the transfer coefficient (α) was calculated according to the following criteria to distinguish whether the electrochemical dehalogenation of halides is a concerted mechanism (α ≪ 0.5, usually ≈ 0.3) or a stepwise mechanism (α > 0.5)44–46, of which electron transfer to halides is the first step.

where Ep, ν, R, and T are the cathode peak potential, scan rate, gas constant, and thermodynamic temperature, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 28 and Supplementary Note 16). As shown in Fig. 4b, α was calculated as 0.316 and 0.406 for LC-Cu without and with OTAB, respectively, suggesting a concerted mechanism of MCAA-d3 electroreduction for both cases. However, the larger α value and the smaller Rct after adding OTAB suggest the rapid electron transfer process between LC-Cu and MCAA-d3 (Supplementary Figs. 29, 30a, b and Supplementary Notes 17, 18)46–48. Furthermore, the significantly reduced activation energy (Ea) and the larger apparent reaction rate constant k in the presence of OTAB demonstrate the lower energy of C−Cl bond dissociation and the improved reaction rate for the deuteration of MCAA-d3 (Fig. 4c, d, Supplementary Figs. 31–33 and Supplementary Notes 19, 20). These results showed that the addition of OTAB can promote dechlorination kinetics, thereby promoting the deuteration of MCAA-d3 to AA-d4.

Uncovering the role of OTAB in the suppression of D2 formation

Apart from the OTAB-accelerated dechlorination kinetics of TCAA, the modulation of interface D2O by the OTAB, which can further balance the deuteration of TCAA and D2 formation, is another key factor for the satisfying FE (91%) of AA-d4 at −100 mA cm−2. In situ Raman spectroscopy was used to investigate the dynamic changes in the interface D2O structure of the OTAB-modified system (Supplementary Figs. 34, 35 and Supplementary Note 21). With increasing bias potential, the intensities of these peaks (4−DB·D2O, 2−DB·D2O, and K+·D2O) gradually increase at the LC-Cu−electrolyte interface but clearly decrease after the addition of OTAB. This might be attributed to OTAB being adsorbed in an orderly manner on the LC-Cu surface, which creates a highly deuterophobic microenvironment, reducing the interface D2O coverage.

Furthermore, at the negative bias potential, K+·D2O migrates to the electrode surface to dissociate to form D* for D2 evolution and deuteration reactions49–52. However, the amount of K+·D2O far exceeds that of the reactants; thus, reducing the amount of K+·D2O may inhibit D2 evolution. Figure 5a shows that the ratio of K+·D2O increases on LC-Cu as the bias potential becomes more negative, whereas it decreases for LC-Cu with OTAB, thereby efficiently suppressing D2. In addition, the redshift of the K+·D2O peak is obviously suppressed at the negative bias potential in the system containing OTAB, which may be attributed to the positively charged −R4N+ group of OTAB adsorbed on the electrode that repels K+·D2O via electrostatic repulsion, resulting in a weak interaction between K+·D2O and the electrode, thus inhibiting the dissociation of D2O (Supplementary Figs. 36a, b and Supplementary Note 22). Moreover, a larger contact angle further illustrated the deuterophobicity of the OTAB-modified LC-Cu interface (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 37). Additionally, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations reveal that the density distribution of water molecules at the electrified electrode−electrolyte is less than 1.0 g cm–3 with OTAB (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 38). The number of hydrogen bonds also significantly decreases in the presence of OTAB according to the AIMD simulations (Fig. 5d), which is conducive to reactant migration to increase the concentration near the electrode surface. Therefore, the reduced coverage of interface D2O and increased local concentration of MCAA-d3 lead to an increase in the ratio of MCAA-d3: D2O, contributing to a high FE at −100 mA cm−2.

On the basis of the above results, OTAB adsorbed on LC-Cu can effectively optimize the crucial MCAA-d3 deuteration process to increase the performance of the electrochemical deuteration of TCAA to AA-d4. The key factors include (1) enhancing the adsorption of MCAA-d3 via N+-enhanced Cuδ+ sites and N+-induced electrostatic forces, (2) promoting the deuteration kinetics of MCAA-d3 by increasing the local concentration of MCAA-d3 around the electrode surface and accelerating electron transfer, and (3) inhibiting D2 evolution by reducing D2O coverage.

Scalable and quick electrosynthesis of AA-d4 and deuterated drug applications

The large-scale and fast synthesis of AA-d4 is highly appealing for practical applications. Flow electrolysis shows great potential because of its higher working current and better mass transfer efficiency53–55. Owing to the sluggish kinetics of the anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and low economic value of O2, the more thermodynamically favorable glycerol electrooxidation reaction (GOR) is used to replace the OER with the deuteration of TCAA56. Therefore, a two-electrode flow reactor was assembled by using LC-Cu as the cathode and Ni foam as the anode, which was separated by a membrane (Fig. 6a–c). The FEs of AA-d4 all exceed ~65% at currents ranging from 2.5 A (200 mA cm−2) to 7.5 A (600 mA cm−2), and the highest yield rate reaches 30 mmol h–1 at 7.5 A (Fig. 6c). After electrolysis for 55 min at 7.5 A, 1.84 g of AA-d4 with >98% NMR purity is obtained through simple acidification and distillation processes (Fig. 6b, d, e). In addition, the deuteration of TCAA can be powered by a solar panel to generate AA-d4, providing a more sustainable route to the synthesis of AA-d4 (Supplementary Fig. 39).

The deuteration of the methyl group (−CH3→−CD3) has been proven to efficiently improve the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of drugs57–59. Thus, the obtained AA-d4 was used as the building block for the synthesis of deuterated drugs and late-stage modification of drugs. For example, CH3-deuterated aspirin-d3 and paracetamol-d3 are easily synthesized through one-pot, two-step procedures involving phenol precursors and AA-d4 (Fig. 6f). In addition, the well-known dihydroartemisinin, which has antimalarial and anticancer properties, celecoxib, which is used to treat pain or inflammation, and oseltamivir, which is used for the treatment of the flu, are well functionalized (Fig. 6g) by AA-d4, indicating the potential utility of AA-d4. The introduction of −CD3 into these drugs is anticipated to alter their metabolism or promote new drug discovery.

Discussion

In summary, OTAB-modified low-coordination Cu was designed to enable the electrocatalytic deuteration of TCAA with D2O to form AA-d4 with 91% selectivity, 91% FE, and 0.59 mmol h−1 yield rate at −100 mA cm−2. The DFT calculations reveal that LC-Cu with abundant Cuδ+ sites enhances the adsorption of the MCAA-d3 intermediate and lowers the activation energy of the C−Cl bond, promoting MCAA-d3 deuteration and improving the AA-d4 selectivity. In addition, the theoretical and experimental results demonstrate that the electrostatic force between the N+ head of OTAB and the O− of the carboxylate of MCAA-d3 can concentrate MCAA-d3 on the LC-Cu surface and that the oxidation of the Cuδ+ sites increases, which further enhances the adsorption of MCAA-d3. Moreover, the charge transfer resistance is markedly reduced at the OTAB-modified interface. These factors significantly accelerate the deuteration kinetics of MCAA-d3, thus increasing the yield of AA-d4. Furthermore, in situ Raman spectra and AIMD simulations reveal that the adsorbed OTAB creates a deuterophobic interface microenvironment that reduces D2O coverage, thereby suppressing D2 evolution and improving the FE. Moreover, our method is used for the rapid synthesis of gram-scale AA-d4 at 7.5 A with a 65% FE, and further applications of AA-d4 in the synthesis of deuterated drugs imply its potential utility. This work provides not only profound insights into the kinetic regulatory mechanisms of a multistep reaction but also a green and efficient way to prepare AA-d4.

Methods

Reagents and chemicals

Tetramethylammonium bromide (TMAB, 98%), butyltrimethylammonium bromide (BTAB, 98%), octyltrimethylammonium bromide (OTAB, 98%), and dodecyl trimethylammonium bromide (DTAB, 98%) were purchased from Heowns. NaOH (99%), (NH4)2S2O8 (99%), K2CO3 (99%), KOH (99%), Na2SO4 (99%), tetrahydrofuran (THF, 99.5%), dichloromethane (DCM, 99.5%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%), and glycerol (99.5%) were purchased from Aladdin. Trichloroacetic acid (TCAA, 99%), dichloroacetic acid (DCAA, 99%), 2-monochloroacetic-d2 acid-d (MCAA-d3, 99%), and monochloroacetic acid (MCAA, 99%) were purchased from Macklin.

Synthesis of CuO nanorods (NRs)

The self-supported CuO NRs were synthesized according to previous methods60. First, the commercial Cu foam was cut to a size of 1.0 cm × 1.5 cm and treated with acetone, 3.0 M HCl, and deionized water (DIW) for 15 min. The cleaned Cu foam was subsequently immersed in an aqueous solution containing 2.5 M NaOH and 0.1 M (NH4)2S2O8 for 3 h at 0 °C. After that, the blue Cu(OH)2 NRs/Cu foam was removed from the solution, washed with DIW and ethanol, and dried at room temperature. Finally, the Cu(OH)2 NRs/Cu foam was annealed (60 °C for 2 h, 120 °C for 4 h, and finally 180 °C for 6 h) in an Ar environment to obtain the CuO NRs structure.

Fabrication of the LC-Cu electrode

LC-Cu was synthesized via in situ electroreduction of CuO NRs in a three-electrode system using a 0.5 M K2CO3 solution as the electrolyte at −1.0 V vs. Hg/HgO for 40 min to ensure the complete disappearance of the reduction peak. The CuO NRs served as the working electrode and were exposed with a surface area of 1.0 cm2; a carbon rod was used as the counter electrode, and Hg/HgO was used as the reference electrode.

Fabrication of the CS-Cu electrode

Two milligrams of Cu powder (10−30 nm, 99.9%, Meryer) was suspended in a mixture containing 10 μL of Nafion solution (5 wt%), 600 μL of isopropanol and 100 μL of DIW via ultrasonication for 15 min to obtain a homogeneous dispersion. The above homogeneous dispersion was subsequently dropped on a piece of carbon fiber (CF) (1 cm × 1 cm).

Characterizations

The morphology of the catalysts was determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Apreo S LoVac) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 F20). The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) of the Cu K-edge were collected at the 1W1B beamline of the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) and analysed via ATHENA software. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer. The NMR spectra were recorded on a BRUKER Ascend 400 (400 MHz) and AVANCE III (600 MHz) for 1H NMR and 101 MHz for 13C NMR with DMSO-d6 or CDCl3-d as the deuterated solvents. Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) downfield from internal tetramethylsilane. The multiplicity of peaks is indicated as follows: s (singlet), d (doublet), t (triplet), and m (multiplet). The coupling constants are reported in hertz (Hz). The accurate mass of each product was determined on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive HF hybrid quadrupole−Orbitrap mass spectrometer (ESI, positive mode for deuterated drugs, negative mode for AA-d4). High−performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed on a SHIMADZU CBM-20A instrument with a Shim-pack GIST C18-AQ column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm).

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical tests were carried out on a CS310MA (CorrTest) electrochemical workstation. The H-type cell was equipped with a three-electrode system and separated by a Nafion 117 membrane. The geometric area of the working electrode was 1 × 1 cm2, the reference electrode was a Hg/HgO electrode (which was periodically calibrated with respect to a reversible hydrogen electrode in a high-purity hydrogen-saturated electrolyte), and a graphite rod served as the counter electrode. Both the cathodic and anodic cells used 0.5 M K2CO3 (pH = 12 ± 0.3) in D2O or H2O (7.0 mL) as the electrolyte. Before LSV, the resistance (R) was measured firstly using CS310MA electrochemical workstation and the R values were 0.6 ± 0.3 Ω. Then, 0.5 mmol of reactants was added to the cathodic cell under stirring (880 rpm) to form a homogeneous solution, and chronoamperometry was used to carry out electrolysis at −100 mA cm−2 (all the potentials in this work were referred to as Hg/HgO unless otherwise stated). When the electrolysis finished, NMR tests were conducted to identify and quantify the products, with 0.0167 mmol maleic acid used as the internal standard. HPLC was used to determine the AA-d4 product. The column was operated at 30 °C with a flow rate of 1.0 mL min–1 using acetonitrile and a pH = 2.5 H3PO4 aqueous mixture (1:99, V/V) as the mobile phase.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements were conducted in H-type cells with and without 0.75 mM OTAB in 0.5 M K2CO3 in D2O electrolytes in the frequency range from 105 to 0.01 Hz with an amplitude of 5 mV. Rct represents the interfacial charge transfer resistance, and CPE represents the constant phase element, which can be derived from the fitting of Nyquist plots via ZView 2 software.

Electrochemical measurements in a flow electrolyzer

Flow electrolyzer electrochemical tests were carried out on a 25 cm2 two-electrode flow cell separated by a Nafion 117 membrane at room temperature with an IT6722A workstation. LC-Cu and Ni foams (5.0 cm × 5.0 cm) were used as the cathode and anode, respectively. Then, 0.5 M K2CO3 and 3.0 M KOH served as the catholyte and anolyte, respectively. Six millimoles of trichloroacetic acid were added to 40 mL of catholyte, and 0.2 M glycerol was added to 40 mL of anolyte. After that, galvanostatic electrolysis was conducted at a flow rate of 10 mL min−1 by using peristaltic pumps (Baoding Lead Fluid Technology).

Electrochemical in situ ATR-FTIR spectra measurements

In situ ATR-FTIR was performed on a Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer equipped with a mercury−cadmium−telluride (MCT) detector with silicon as the prismatic window. First, CuO ink dispersed in ethanol was carefully dropped on the surface of the Au film, which was chemically deposited on the surface of the silicon prism before each experiment. The 0.5 M K2CO3 in D2O solution was employed as the electrolyte. The electrolyte was degassed by bubbling with N2 for 20 min before the measurement. Before spectral acquisition, the working electrode was pretreated at a potential of −1.0 V vs. Hg/HgO for the in situ formation of LC-Cu. The background spectrum was acquired at an open-circuit voltage (OCP) before each systemic measurement. The spectra of 0.5 M K2CO3 in D2O solution containing 0.75 mM OTAB were recorded with a continuous change in applied potential from −0.7 to −1.3 V vs. Hg/HgO.

Electrochemical in situ Raman spectra measurements

In situ electrochemical Raman spectroscopy was performed with a Renishaw inVia Reflex microscope using a 633 nm laser source according to our previous work61. The system was coupled with an electrochemical workstation (CS310MA) for potential control. A custom three-electrode cell, machined from Teflon and sealed with a circular quartz window, allowed optical access while protecting the objective lens. The working electrode was oriented perpendicular to the laser beam. A circular Pt wire counter electrode was positioned around the working electrode, and a Hg/HgO reference electrode (with 1.0 M KOH internal electrolyte) was used to complete the circuit. Spectra were acquired at the electrolyte (D₂O)/electrode interface over a potential range of –0.8 to –1.3 V vs. Hg/HgO. Additionally, reactant spectra were collected under galvanostatic conditions at different times.

Computational details

All computational work in this study, including density functional theory (DFT) calculations and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations62, was performed via the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)63. The electron‒ion interactions were described via the projector augmented wave (PAW) method64, whereas the exchange‒correlation functional was treated within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)65 via the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation. A plane-wave kinetic energy cut-off of 500 eV was employed, and a Monkhorst-Pack mesh of 3 × 3 × 1 was used in K-sampling in the adsorption energy calculation. A (3 × 3√3) supercell of the Cu model was constructed with four atomic layers. During structural optimization, the bottom two layers were held fixed to mimic bulk behavior, while the upper layers were allowed to fully optimize. A vacuum region of at least 15 Å was introduced normal to the surface to eliminate periodic interactions. Dispersion corrections were incorporated via the DFT-D3 empirical scheme, and solvation effects were implicitly introduced via the VASPsol66 Poisson–Boltzmann model with a dielectric constant of εr = 78.4 to represent water. Electronic relaxations were considered converged when the energy change fell below 10⁻⁵ eV per atom, and ionic steps were optimized until the maximum force on any atom was less than 0.05 eV Å−1. AIMD trajectories were propagated in the NVT ensemble via a Nosé–Hoover thermostat set to 300 K, with a time step of 1 fs. Each simulation was equilibrated for at least 10 ps before analysis. To model the electrochemical interface, 90 water molecules were arranged atop the copper slab, corresponding to a near-experimental density of ∼1 g cm−3 for the LC-Cu model without OTAB. In simulations of the LC‒Cu model with OTAB, one OTAB molecule replaced 13 water molecules while conserving the total number of hydrogen atoms to maintain system comparability. Hydrogen bonds were identified via geometric criteria implemented in VMD67, with a donor–acceptor distance threshold of 3.5 Å and an angle cut-off of 30°.
