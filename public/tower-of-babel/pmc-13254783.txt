Inflammation, fronto-amygdala connectivity, and negative affective reactivity to daily stress in adolescents
Authors: Eugenia Giampetruzzi, Justin P Yuan, Jessica P Uy, Chase Antonacci, Yoonji Lee, Ian H Gotlib
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaladaptive affective responses to daily stress in adolescence are a robust risk factor for the development of psychopathology. Importantly, however, adolescents vary widely in the magnitude of their affective reactivity to stress. Although peripheral inflammation and fronto-amygdala circuitry during emotion regulation are both plausible biological contributors to this variability, no study has yet integrated inflammatory markers, neural metrics of emotion regulation, and real-world assessments of stress and affect to examine how these systems interact to shape affective reactivity to stress in adolescents’ daily life.MethodsAdolescents (N = 124; 60% female) aged 13.07-20.10 years (M = 16.20) completed a 14-day ecological momentary assessment (EMA) protocol assessing stressor occurrence, stress severity, and momentary affect. A subset of participants provided peripheral blood samples assayed for C-reactive protein (CRP) to index systemic inflammation (N = 85), and a further subset completed an fMRI affect-labeling task to quantify task-related functional connectivity between the amygdala and lateral prefrontal cortex (LPFC; N = 68). Multilevel models tested whether higher CRP predicted stronger within-person coupling of stress and negative affect (NA), and whether LPFC-amygdala connectivity moderated this association.ResultsHigher stress severity predicted higher NA, and greater inflammation potentiated these stress-related increases in NA. A three-way interaction with DLPFC-amygdala connectivity may further moderate this association, but the effect did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, and the sample was underpowered to test it reliably.ConclusionsInflammation was linked to stronger coupling of daily stress and negative affect in adolescents. Whether fronto-amygdala connectivity adds to this remains untested at adequate power.Keywords: Adolescence, Ecological momentary assessment, Affective reactivity, Inflammation, Fronto-amygdala connectivity

Introduction

Adolescence is characterized by heightened affective reactivity (Somerville, 2013). Normative increases in affective reactivity – the magnitude of change in affective states in response to stressors – have been posited to reflect responses to both novel stressors and social demands in the context of biological maturation (Guyer et al., 2016; Romeo, 2010). Maladaptive affective responses to everyday stressors, however, which are often manifested as high levels of negative affect (NA) and, in some youth, blunted levels of positive affect (PA), have been found to be associated with the onset and severity of psychopathology (Beauchaine, 2015; Steinberg, 2005). Although adolescents vary widely in the magnitude of their affective responses to daily stressors, we do not yet understand the factors that contribute to this variability.

Researchers have implicated peripheral physiological systems, particularly inflammatory signaling, in adolescents' affective responses to stress. For example, studies using daily diary methodologies have shown that adolescents who experience more frequent daily interpersonal stressors have higher levels of CRP and interleukin-6 (Fuligni et al., 2009). Moreover, longitudinal investigations have demonstrated that adolescents with a history of childhood adversity who experience more frequent acute stressors have elevated and sustained trajectories of inflammatory markers, such as interleukin-6 and interleukin-10, across adolescence (Kautz et al., 2023; Chiang et al., 2022). In adults, levels of inflammatory markers have been associated with the frequency and severity of daily stressors (Reed et al., 2022), and greater affective reactivity to daily stressors has likewise been linked to higher levels of inflammation (Sin et al., 2015) highlighting the likely bidirectional nature of this relationship.

In addition to inflammation, individual differences in the strength and direction of fronto-amygdala connectivity during emotion processing and regulation may help to explain variability in adolescents’ affective reactivity to daily stress. Within this network, the lateral prefrontal cortex (LPFC) and its subregions play a key role in emotion regulation (Toenders et al., 2024). While the dorsolateral PFC (DLPFC) supports cognitive control strategies such as attentional reallocation and distraction, the ventrolateral PFC (VLPFC) supports semantic reappraisal and implicit emotion regulation, often measured using a task involving labeling of affective facial expressions (Lieberman et al., 2007; Ochsner and Gross, 2005). Although attentional reallocation, distraction, and semantic reappraisal rely on distinct regulatory mechanisms, affect labeling produces overlapping activation in bilateral VLPFC and DLPFC alongside comparable amygdala deactivation, suggesting shared neural mechanisms of affect regulation (Burklund et al., 2014). In adults, more positive LPFC-amygdala coupling in response to negative stimuli has been interpreted as reflecting greater regulatory dysfunction, indicating less efficient prefrontal downregulation of the amygdala. Consistent with this formulation, Poon et al. (2022) found that adolescents who exhibited greater LPFC-amygdala connectivity while viewing negative images reported more emotion regulation difficulties in daily life, which in turn predicted greater internalizing and externalizing symptoms two years later. It is important to recognize, however, that most studies of adolescent stress reactivity rely on laboratory paradigms that do not capture the dynamic nature of real-world stress responses (Del Piero et al., 2016).

While fMRI tasks provide controlled assessment of neural mechanisms underlying emotion processing and regulation (Ochsner and Gross, 2005), ecological momentary assessment (EMA) measures naturally occurring affective responses to stressors throughout the day (Shiffman et al., 2008); few studies, however, have integrated these approaches to link task-based neural function with daily affective reactivity. Emerging evidence supports such links: in adults, stronger fronto-amygdala connectivity during emotional conflict has been found to be related to greater daily affective instability (Hua et al., 2021), and in adolescents, fronto-amygdala connectivity during peer rejection predicts greater naturalistic reactivity to social threat (Sequeira et al., 2021).

In adolescents, peripheral inflammation has been directly linked to altered amygdala–striatum and fronto-parietal connectivity (Swartz et al., 2021), lower connectivity within emotion-regulation and central-executive networks (Nusslock et al., 2019) and altered fronto-cingulate white matter (Ho et al., 2022). We posit here that systemic inflammation and fronto-amygdala connectivity interact to influence adolescents' affective reactivity to daily stress. Specifically, the functional significance of inflammatory signaling for daily stress reactivity may depend on the strength and direction of fronto-amygdala connectivity during emotion processing. Supporting this framework, Yuan et al. (2025) reported that neural activity during affective processing can moderate the association between inflammation and concurrent self-reported depression in adolescents. In adults, inflammation has been associated with anomalous fronto-amygdala connectivity, and inflammatory markers have been found to mediate associations between early adversity and fronto-amygdala functioning (Kraynak et al., 2019).

The DLPFC and VLPFC are regions rich in dopamine receptors (Joyce et al., 2024; Mo et al., 2023). Inflammatory cytokines can affect both regions by altering dopamine metabolism and glutamate signaling (Miller and Raison, 2016). Indeed, higher levels of systemic inflammation have been associated with altered VLPFC activation and fronto-amygdala connectivity during implicit regulation of negative emotions in adolescents (Yuan et al., 2022), although the direction of these relations may differ across regulatory contexts and developmental stage. Combined with evidence that fronto-amygdala connectivity during emotion regulation is associated with affective reactivity in daily life (Hua et al., 2021; Sequeira et al., 2021), these findings suggest that inflammation is associated with daily affective reactivity most strongly in adolescents whose strength and direction of fronto-amygdala connectivity reflects less efficient regulatory functioning (e.g., more positive connectivity).

In this study, we integrated three levels of analysis – peripheral physiology (systemic inflammation), neural function (task-based fMRI during implicit emotion regulation), and real-world stress exposure and affect (EMA) – to test whether FC between the lateral prefrontal cortex and amygdala moderates the association between systemic inflammation and daily affective stress reactivity in adolescents. We hypothesized, first, that higher levels of systemic inflammation will be associated with greater affective reactivity to daily stressors. Second, we hypothesized that the association between inflammation and affective reactivity will be stronger in adolescents with more positive fronto-amygdala connectivity during implicit emotion regulation. Finally, given the distinct but complementary roles of the DLPFC and VLPFC in emotion regulation and their sensitivity to inflammatory processes, we expected that FC between the amygdala and both DLPFC and VLPFC will moderate the association between inflammation and affective reactivity to daily stress.

Methods

Overview

In the present study we analyzed data from the Early Life Stress (ELS) study at Stanford University. A cohort of 224 youth was recruited at ages 9–13 years from the San Francisco Bay Area community and completed assessments across four timepoints at approximately two-year intervals. All study procedures were approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board. Data for the current analyses were drawn from participants who completed a 14-day EMA protocol at the third (T3) or fourth (T4) timepoint; for all participants, the EMA, CRP, and fMRI measures were drawn from the same assessment wave to ensure within-wave alignment.

Specific samples were defined through a staged process. Of the 161 adolescents who initiated the EMA protocol, 157 were retained after quality control. The behavioral sample comprised participants with sufficient EMA stress variability to estimate affective reactivity, defined as having both ≥1 event-endorsed prompt and ≥1 no-event prompt; 33 were excluded for lacking either prompt type (precluding estimation of a within-person stress slope), yielding N = 124 (3416 prompts; M = 27.5 per person). The inflammation sample comprised participants in the behavioral sample who provided blood samples to assay CRP during an in-person visit at the same wave, with EMA beginning within the next five days (N = 85; 2352 prompts; M = 27.7 per person). The inflammation-fMRI sample comprised inflammation-sample participants with useable FC data from a task-based fMRI session completed during the same in-person visit preceding EMA; of the 85 participants with CRP, 69 completed a scan at the matched wave and 1 was excluded at functional-connectivity quality control for insufficient amygdala-seed coverage, yielding N = 68 (1910 prompts; M = 28.1 per person).

At each prompt, participants reported whether they had experienced a significant event since the prior prompt and rated their current affect. Stress Assessment. For the event assessment, participants responded to a screening item asking whether they had experienced a significant event since the last prompt (“Have you experienced a significant event since the last beep?”). Participants were instructed that events did not need to be major life events but could be any event that affected them in any way, either positively or negatively. If participants endorsed experiencing an event, they rated its stressfulness on a continuous scale from 1 (not at all stressful) to 100 (extremely stressful). Prompts without an endorsed event were treated as non-event occasions.

Affect Assessment. Participants rated their current affect using nine emotion items on a continuous scale from 0 (not at all) to 100 (extremely). Negative Affect (NA) was calculated as the mean of five items (sad, annoyed/angry, grouchy/cranky, worried, anxious), and Positive Affect (PA) was calculated as the mean of four items (happy, cheerful, excited, energetic). These items were adapted from validated scales assessing affect in youth (Laurent et al., 2012; Klonsky et al., 2019; Stringaris et al., 2012). NA and PA composites were computed at the prompt level (the mean of the relevant items within each EMA prompt), and prompt-level NA served as the Level 1 outcome in all multilevel models. Multilevel reliability, estimated using a Cranford-style decomposition, was high between persons (NA RKF = .98; PA RKF = .97) and acceptable within persons (NA alpha = .73; PA alpha = .84).

Data Processing. Detailed documentation is available for data collection, pre-processing, and quality assessment of EMA (https://github.com/eugiampetruzzi/els_ema_data; See Supplemental Methods). Briefly, prompts were excluded from analysis if they met any of the following criteria: (1) a missing event-occurrence response; or (2) an incomplete stressfulness rating when an event was endorsed (Jaso et al., 2021; Portillo-Van Diest et al., 2024). Affect composites were computed from all available items at each prompt, and a prompt was excluded for affect missingness only when the entire affect block was absent (Supplement Table 1). Across all criteria, 145 of 4204 prompts were excluded (81 for a missing affect block, 41 for a missing event response, and 23 for failing consistency checks).

C-reactive protein (CRP)

Systemic inflammation was indexed using levels of CRP obtained from finger-prick dried blood spot (DBS) samples and assayed at the Stanford Human Immune Monitoring Center using a singleplex assay on the Luminex FlexMap3D Instrument System. CRP concentrations are quantified as median fluorescence intensity (MFI) values and converted to ng/mL. CRP values were natural log-transformed to reduce skewness (log-transformed MFI: M = 4.41, SD = 1.17; range: 2.64–8.16). For clinical context, CRP levels were low in this sample, with a mean well below the 3.0 mg/L clinical threshold (inflammation sample N = 85: M = .16 mg/L, SD = .30, range <.01–1.30 mg/L). Log-transformed MFI was z-scored to place regression coefficients and the ±1 SD values used in simple-slopes and interaction probing on a standardized, interpretable scale.

Blood spots taken at T3 (N = 52) were assayed in singlets across four batches. For blood spots taken at T4 (N = 16), CRP values were assayed in duplicate within a single batch and averaged. Batch effects were statistically controlled for in all analyses using a categorical covariate. The intra-assay coefficients of variation for CRP were M ± SD = 5.7 ± 5.4% and 5.4 ± 4.1% for the two timepoints, respectively. For additional details about the DBS protocol, see Yuan et al. (2022).

Implicit emotion regulation fMRI task

Affect Labeling Task. At each timepoint, participants completed an affect labeling task during fMRI scanning to assess task-related fronto-amygdala functional connectivity (FC) during implicit emotion regulation (Lieberman et al., 2007). For the present study, we used fMRI data from the same assessment wave in which participants completed EMA (see Overview). Data from this affect labeling task in the larger cohort have been reported in prior work in relation to early life stress (Colich et al., 2017), systemic inflammation (Yuan et al., 2022), the COVID-19 pandemic and pollution burden (Uy et al., 2024). The task had two main conditions: Label (selecting corresponding emotion words) and Match (selecting corresponding facial expressions) in response to emotional target faces displayed at the top of the screen. Facial stimuli were emotional expressions from the NimStim picture set (Tottenham et al., 2009), depicting 10 actors (5 females, 5 males) showing positive or negative emotional expressions. Participants indicated their choice by a button press to select between two options located at the bottom left and right of the screen. The correct response was the option that matched the target face: in the Label condition, the two options were words describing the emotion; in the Match condition, the two options were images of facial expressions. Stimuli were grouped into positive-valence (happy, calm, excited) and negative-valence (fearful, angry, sad) blocks. A sensorimotor control condition (Shape Match) was also included, resulting in five separate conditions per run: Positive Match, Positive Label, Negative Match, Negative Label, and Shape Match. Each block contained 10 trials with each stimulus presented for 5 s. Blocks were interspersed with 15-s fixation rest period during which participants focused on a cross in the center of the screen. A single run consisted of one block of each task condition presented in randomized order. Participants completed two runs of the task.

MRI Acquisition & Preprocessing. Neuroimaging data were acquired at the Stanford Center for Cognitive and Neurobiological Imaging using a 3T General Electric scanner. During data collection, the scanner was upgraded from a Discovery MR750 (N = 38) to an Ultra High Performance (UHP) system (N = 30); consequently, a categorical scanner covariate was included in all neuroimaging analyses. Both systems used the same 32-channel head coil (Nova Medical). Functional imaging was acquired using a T2-weighted gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 2000 ms; TE = 30 ms; voxel size = 3.2 × 3.2 × 3.0 mm; 43 axial slices). All neuroimaging data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep (version 20.2.1; Esteban et al., 2019, 2020). Motion outliers were identified (framewise displacement >.5 mm or DVARS >1.5) and censored using single-TR censor regressors (Power et al., 2014; Siegel et al., 2014). Additional acquisition and preprocessing details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Fronto-Amygdala Functional Connectivity. Preprocessed data were analyzed using FSL FEAT (Smith et al., 2004; Woolrich et al., 2001). Task-related fronto-amygdala connectivity was estimated using psychophysiological interaction (PPI) analyses, with the psychological regressor defined as the Negative Label > Negative Match contrast to isolate the regulation-related signal specific to labeling negative affect while holding constant the visual and motor demands shared by the two conditions. Bilateral amygdala seed regions were defined using Harvard-Oxford atlas masks (50% threshold). Prefrontal regions of interest included bilateral DLPFC and VLPFC, defined as 10-mm spheres centered on published coordinates (Burklund et al., 2014; Pozzi et al., 2021; See Fig. 1). Connectivity values were extracted for left and right amygdala with each of the four prefrontal ROIs (eight values total). Full model specification is provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.5.2) with RStudio environment (version 2025.9.2.418).

Sample Differences. To evaluate whether participants differed across analytic samples, we compared mutually exclusive groups defined by staged inclusion criteria: adolescents in the behavioral sample who did not provide CRP data (behavioral-only), adolescents with CRP data who did not have useable fMRI connectivity data (behavior-inflammation), and adolescents with both CRP and useable fMRI connectivity data (inflammation-fMRI). We compared groups on demographic characteristics, mean affect levels, daily stress, and EMA compliance. For continuous variables (age, mean negative affect, mean positive affect, mean daily stress, and retained prompt compliance), we used one-way ANOVA when distributional assumptions were met and Kruskal-Wallis tests otherwise. For categorical variables (sex and race/ethnicity), we used chi-square tests. Given small cell sizes in some race/ethnicity categories, race/ethnicity was dichotomized as White versus non-White (all other categories combined, including Hispanic/Latino) for group comparisons.

Behavioral Stress-Affect Analyses. Affective reactivity was modeled using two-level linear mixed-effects models in R (lme4; Bates et al., 2015), with EMA prompts (Level 1) nested within individuals (Level 2). Separate models were estimated for NA and PA. Intraclass correlation coefficients indicated substantial clustering of prompts within individuals (NA ICC = .63; PA ICC = .52), supporting the use of multilevel models with random intercepts. Random slopes for momentary stress were included to capture individual differences in stress-related affective reactivity.

Momentary experience of stress was operationalized using a continuous rating (1–100) of the stressfulness of each endorsed event, with non-event prompts (78% of all observations) coded as 0 to retain each participant's full within-person stress distribution; these prompts represent genuine low-stress occasions and anchor each participant's within-person baseline, and excluding them would bias the within-person stress range upward. For numerical stability in model estimation, stress ratings were rescaled to 10-point units (divided by 10), such that coefficients reflect the effect of a 10-point increase in stress severity. To distinguish momentary (within-person) fluctuations in stress from stable (between-person) differences in typical stress experience, stress severity was decomposed into (1) each adolescent's average stress across prompts (between-person mean stress); and (2) each prompt's deviation from that adolescent's own average (within-person stress deviation). We examined changes in levels of NA and PA as a function of the severity of the reported stress.

For each outcome (NA, PA), models included fixed effects for within-person stress deviations, between-person mean stress, age (mean-centered), and sex. Random intercepts captured stable individual differences in baseline affect, and random slopes for within-person stress deviations captured individual differences in affective reactivity to stress. Models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). CRP as a Moderator of the Within-Person Association Between Momentary Stress and Negative Affect. To test whether higher systemic inflammation is associated with greater negative affective reactivity to daily stress, we estimated multilevel models in the inflammation sample (N = 85). We predicted prompt-level NA using the same modeling framework, stress decomposition, and scaling procedures described in the Behavioral Stress-Affect Analyses section above. In these models, we added person-level C-reactive protein (CRP; log-transformed and z-scored) and the cross-level interaction between within-person stress deviations and CRP to test moderation of within-person stress reactivity. Models included the covariates of age (mean-centered), sex, BMI (mean-centered), and CRP assay batch (categorical). We then conducted simple slopes analyses to estimate the within-person stress-NA association at low (−1 SD), mean, and high (+1 SD) CRP, and used Johnson-Neyman analyses to identify the range of CRP values for which within-person stress reactivity was statistically significant.

LPFC-Amygdala Connectivity × CRP Moderation of Association Between Momentary Stress Severity and Negative Affect. To test whether DLPFC- and VLPFC-amygdala connectivity during implicit emotion regulation moderates the association between inflammation and stress reactivity, we conducted hierarchical multilevel modeling in the inflammation-fMRI sample (N = 68). Given that hemisphere-specific connectivity estimates within the DLPFC and VLPFC were highly intercorrelated (DLPFC: Pairwise r = .68-.84, Cronbach's α = .93; VLPFC: Pairwise r = .42-.84, α = .85), we computed region-level composites to reduce measurement error and multicollinearity. DLPFC connectivity was operationalized as the mean of four z-scored amygdala-DLPFC connectivity values (L amygdala-L DLPFC, L amygdala-R DLPFC, R amygdala-L DLPFC, R amygdala-R DLPFC); VLPFC connectivity was computed analogously.

We fit linear mixed-effects models predicting prompt-level negative affect (NA), with random intercepts and random slopes for within-person stress deviations nested within participant. Fixed effects included within-person stress deviations, CRP (log-transformed and standardized), the DLPFC and VLPFC composite connectivity measures, and covariates (age, sex, BMI, CRP assay batch, scanner, and mean framewise displacement). Hierarchical model building proceeded as follows: (1) a covariate-only model; (2) addition of main effects (stress, CRP, DLPFC, VLPFC); (3) addition of all corresponding two-way interactions (stress × CRP; stress × DLPFC; CRP × DLPFC; stress × VLPFC; CRP × VLPFC); and (4) addition of both three-way interactions (stress × CRP × DLPFC and stress × CRP × VLPFC). Nested models were compared using likelihood ratio tests (maximum-likelihood estimation; REML = FALSE). Following the omnibus three-way test, we conducted planned nested comparisons adding each three-way interaction individually (DLPFC three-way first, then VLPFC; and the reverse order) to quantify the incremental contribution of each LPFC subregion. Significant interactions were probed using simple slopes analyses estimating the association between stress and NA at low (−1 SD) and high (+1 SD) levels of CRP and connectivity.

Multiple comparisons and power analysis. For multiple comparisons, confirmatory analyses followed an a priori, hierarchically structured model-building plan in which nested models were compared using likelihood-ratio tests, constraining the number of independent inferential tests. The one point at which a family of comparable tests arises is the pair of LPFC-subregion three-way interactions (DLPFC– and VLPFC–amygdala); for these, we report Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR)- and Bonferroni-corrected p-values alongside uncorrected values. We also conducted a simulation-based sensitivity power analysis for the Hypothesis 2 three-way interaction using the simr package (Green and MacLeod, 2016), treating the fitted DLPFC–amygdala model in the inflammation-fMRI sample (N = 68) as the data-generating model and varying only the three-way coefficient across a grid of values (1000 simulations per value).

Results

Participant characteristics

Participant demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1 for the behavioral (N = 124), inflammation (Hypothesis 1; N = 85), and inflammation-fMRI (Hypothesis 2; N = 68) samples. In the behavioral sample, participants completed an average of 66.6% of the 42 scheduled EMA prompts (SD = 24.9%), and 51.6% exceeded the 70% compliance threshold. The final inflammation-fMRI sample (N = 68) had a mean age of 16.24 years (SD = 1.73, range = 13.07–20.10). There were no significant group differences across samples in demographic characteristics, CRP, inflammation-related covariates, affect levels, daily stress, or EMA compliance (all ps ≥ .18; Supplement Table 2). Bivariate correlations among key person-level aggregates of EMA variables, CRP, and LPFC-amygdala connectivity in the inflammation-fMRI sample are presented in Fig. 2. Cross-sectional associations between CRP and LPFC-amygdala connectivity were not significant in this sample (CRP–DLPFC r = −.03, p = .84; CRP–VLPFC r = −.02, p = .87; N = 68). The association between VLPFC-amygdala connectivity and the EMA-derived within-person stress-NA slope was also not significant (r = −.01, p = .95), and the corresponding DLPFC-reactivity correlation was small and nonsignificant (r = .20, p = .11).

Behavioral Stress-Affect Analyses. We first examined the main effect of stress severity on levels of affect. Both higher average stress severity across prompts (B = 8.97, SE = 1.36, t = 6.60, p < .001) and within-person increases in stress severity (B = 1.48, SE = .16, t = 9.31, p < .001) predicted higher NA. Similar findings were obtained in the inflammation (N = 85) and inflammation-fMRI (N = 68) samples (see Fig. 3). In contrast, neither average levels of nor increases in stress severity were associated significantly with levels of PA in follow-up analyses (all ps > .21); therefore, primary moderation models focused on NA as the outcome with subsequent PA models reported as exploratory.

CRP as a Moderator of the Within-Person Association Between Momentary Stress Severity and Negative Affect. We next tested whether higher systemic inflammation is associated with stronger within-person coupling of daily stress and NA in the inflammation sample (N = 85; 2352 prompts). As shown in Table 2, both the within-person stress slope (B = 1.50, SE = .12, p < .001) and the between-person mean stress (B = 6.45, SE = 1.78, p < .001) predicted prompt-level NA, with the within-person slope reflecting moment-to-moment stress reactivity and the between-person term reflecting trait-level differences in average reported stress. The interaction between within-person stress and CRP was significant, such that higher levels of inflammation were associated with stronger within-person coupling of stress and NA; significant covariates were between-person mean stress, sex, and BMI (Supplement Table 3). The fixed effects accounted for a marginal R2 of .21 and the full model a conditional R2 of .64, with the within-person stress × CRP interaction contributing an incremental ΔR2 = .004 relative to an otherwise identical model omitting it. Simple-slopes analyses confirmed that the within-person stress-NA association strengthened as a function of inflammation (Fig. 4): the slope was positive and significant at low (−1 SD, B = 1.14, p < .001), mean (B = 1.50, p < .001), and high (+1 SD, B = 1.85, p < .001) CRP. Johnson-Neyman analysis indicated that CRP significantly moderated stress reactivity across the entire observed range of CRP in this sample.

LPFC-Amygdala Connectivity × CRP Moderation of Association Between Momentary Stress Severity and Negative Affect. We next tested whether LPFC-amygdala connectivity moderated the extent to which inflammation potentiates within-person stress reactivity in the inflammation-fMRI sample (N = 68; 1910 prompts). Hierarchical model comparisons indicated that adding LPFC-amygdala connectivity and its interactions with stress and CRP significantly improved prediction of NA relative to the CRP-only model, χ2(9) = 20.73, p = .014. Further, adding the interaction terms among stress, CRP, and LPFC-amygdala connectivity significantly improved model fit relative to a model including only main effects, χ2(7) = 18.57, p = .010. In the full model, the three-way interaction among within-person stress deviations, CRP, and DLPFC-amygdala connectivity was significant before correction (B = .33, SE = .16, df = 41.5, t = 2.12, uncorrected p = .04), indicating that the within-person stress–NA association became stronger at higher CRP when DLPFC–amygdala connectivity was more positive. The corresponding three-way interaction involving VLPFC–amygdala connectivity was not significant (uncorrected p = .65; Supplement Table 4). The marginal R2 was .24, with the three-way term contributing ΔR2 = .001 and the full interaction block ΔR2 = .01. Simple-slopes analyses were consistent with the DLPFC interaction: when connectivity was high (+1 SD), higher CRP was associated with a steeper stress–NA slope (i.e., greater stress reactivity), whereas when connectivity was low (−1 SD), the stress–NA slope did not differ as a function of CRP.

Power and multiple comparisons. Given that the two LPFC-subregion three-way interactions (DLPFC- and VLPFC-amygdala) constitute a family of comparable tests, we applied corrections across the pair. The DLPFC three-way interaction did not survive correction (FDR-corrected p = .08; Bonferroni-corrected p = .08), and the VLPFC interaction remained non-significant (FDR-corrected p = .65). A simulation-based sensitivity analysis further indicated that the inflammation-fMRI design (N = 68) was only marginally powered to detect the three-way interaction: at the observed effect (B = .33), power was approximately 65%, increasing with effect size (B = .30: 52%; B = .40: 76%; B = .50: 88%), and the conventional 80% threshold was reached only at a coefficient roughly 30-40% larger than observed (B ≈ .43–.45). We therefore interpret the three-way interaction as preliminary and in need of replication in a larger sample.

Sensitivity analyses. All analyses remained significant controlling for additional confounding variables that could affect inflammation levels, including COVID infection prior to assessment, recent illnesses, and use of antibiotic, corticosteroid, and psychiatric medications. In stepwise sensitivity models, the stress × CRP interaction (Hypothesis 1) remained significant in Hypothesis 1 (Bs = .33-.36, ps = .005-.002), and the uncorrected stress × CRP × DLPFC-amygdala connectivity interaction (Hypothesis 2) remained significant (Bs = .35, p = .04 across models). Results were also unchanged when adding parental education and income as covariates (Supplement Table 5; Hypothesis 1 B = .40, p = .03; Hypothesis 2 three-way B = .35, p = .03) and when winsorizing CRP at the 99th percentile (Bs = .40 and .35). EMA compliance was unrelated to CRP (r = .20, p = .07) and adding it as a covariate did not change the Hypothesis 1 interaction (B = .39, p = .02), indicating the moderation is not driven by differential data quality.

Exploratory analyses. Parsing NA into anger/irritability, anxiety, and sadness sub composites (within-prompt rs = .52–.57; between-person rs = .74–.81), no sub composite-specific stress × CRP interaction reached significance (ps = .06–.23), consistent with reduced power relative to the full composite (Supplement Table 3); the uncorrected DLPFC three-way interaction was significant only for anxiety (B = .47, p = .045; sadness p = .08, anger/irritability p = .50; Supplement Table 4). For positive affect, the stress × CRP interaction was significant (B = −.46, p = .02), indicating higher CRP was associated with greater stress-related PA reductions, though neither three-way interaction reached significance (DLPFC p = .15, VLPFC p = .51; Supplement Table 6).

Discussion

In the present study we integrated a peripheral inflammatory marker (CRP), task-based fronto-amygdala FC, and real-world assessments of stress and affect to examine multilevel contributors to affective reactivity to daily stress in adolescents. We found that prompts at which adolescents reported experiencing greater stress were associated with higher levels of NA. Importantly, however, the magnitude of this affective reactivity was not uniform across individuals; rather, consistent with our hypotheses, higher levels of systemic inflammation, indexed by CRP, were associated with stronger negative affective reactivity to stress. In a preliminary, uncorrected analysis, this potentiating effect of inflammation on stress-related NA appeared to be further moderated by task-based DLPFC-amygdala FC during implicit emotion regulation, such that adolescents with more positive DLPFC-amygdala FC exhibited a steeper CRP-related increase in affective reactivity to stress. VLPFC-amygdala FC did not independently moderate this association. Together, these findings suggest a multilevel pathway through which inflammatory and, preliminarily, neural processes interact to shape adolescents’ negative affective responses to daily stressors.

Our use of EMA in this study is critical in capturing covariations in stress and affect with the natural rhythm of daily life, providing ecological validity that laboratory paradigms cannot; the fact that CRP significantly moderated within-person stress reactivity across the full range of CRP values in this sample underscores the robustness and advantage of this methodology over a simpler contrast of high versus low levels of inflammation. Our primary finding, that higher CRP was associated with greater affective reactivity to daily stressors, extends previous work linking systemic inflammation to summative measures of affective reactivity in adults (Sin et al., 2015), and is the first to do so using momentary assessments of naturally occurring stress and affect in adolescents. A parallel exploratory analysis suggested this potentiating effect was not limited to negative affect: higher CRP was also associated with greater stress-related reductions in positive affect, consistent with inflammation broadly amplifying the affective impact of daily stress across both valences. Inflammation may potentiate affective reactivity to stress through several interacting mechanisms. For example, pro-inflammatory signaling alters the function of cortico-limbic circuits implicated in threat detection and affective appraisal, including sensitizing amygdala responses to aversive and ambiguous cues (Haroon et al., 2012). Inflammatory cytokines also disrupt dopamine and serotonin neurotransmission in ways that alter hedonic tone and increase threat salience (Miller and Raison, 2016). These effects are likely to be particularly important in adolescence given the protracted developmental reorganization of cortico-limbic systems during this period (Casey et al., 2019; Tottenham and Galván, 2016).

We also found, in a preliminary analysis that did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, that stress-related affective reactivity in adolescents was moderated by CRP and DLPFC-amygdala FC. Adolescents with stronger DLPFC-amygdala coupling during the affect-labeling task showed a more pronounced relation between CRP and stress reactivity; that is, higher CRP was significantly associated with a steeper within-person slope of NA in response to stress only for adolescents with high DLPFC-amygdala FC. Thus, more positive DLPFC-amygdala FC might reflect a less efficient regulatory configuration in which prefrontal control resources are co-activated with, rather than inhibited by amygdala response (Hare et al., 2008; Poon et al., 2022). Importantly, the DLPFC is metabolically demanding, characterized by dense dopaminergic innervation and high catecholamine turnover (Arnsten, 2015). In this context, inflammatory processes may disrupt the efficiency of this regulatory circuit, given that pro-inflammatory cytokines can reduce dopamine synthesis and increase dopamine reuptake in prefrontal circuits (Miller and Raison, 2016). Therefore, adolescents who are already reliant on a less efficient fronto-amygdala circuit may be particularly susceptible to inflammation-related impairment of regulatory capacity in the face of daily stressors.

To the extent that the DLPFC findings are replicated in larger samples, the absence of a comparable moderation effect for VLPFC-amygdala connectivity may reflect functional differences between the DLPFC and VLPFC in regulating affective responses to stress. Whereas the DLPFC supports more deliberate, top-down regulation of emotional responses through working memory and cognitive control (Ochsner and Gross, 2005), the VLPFC is more closely associated with semantic reappraisal and affect labeling, processes that may be less relevant to the moment-to-moment stress reactivity captured in daily life (Lieberman et al., 2007; Burklund et al., 2014). To the extent that inflammation disrupts prefrontal regulation of amygdala activity, its effects may be expressed preferentially through circuits that support sustained cognitive control rather than circuits involved in labeling or reinterpreting affective states.

The associations among CRP, DLPFC connectivity, and stress-related affective reactivity may be especially salient in adolescence, when fronto-amygdala FC is undergoing substantial reorganization, shifting from subcortically driven reactivity toward more integrated cortico-subcortical regulation (Casey et al., 2019; Gabard-Durnam et al., 2014; Gee et al., 2013). During adolescence, inter-individual variability in the strength of positive fronto-amygdala connectivity and the heightened sensitivity of developing neural circuits to inflammatory perturbation may be at their peak. Given that emotion regulation in everyday life often occurs without deliberate effort (Mauss et al., 2007), the use of implicit regulation paradigms such as the affect labeling task are particularly informative for understanding naturalistic stress reactivity. In this context, therefore, fronto-amygdala connectivity during implicit regulation may index individual differences in automatic regulatory capacity that are reflected in affective responses to daily stressors. This multilevel approach strengthens ecological and construct validity over studies that rely on retrospective self-report measures or a single level of analysis (Toenders et al., 2024).

We should note several limitations of this study. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow us to establish the temporal ordering of inflammatory changes, fronto-amygdala connectivity, and daily stress reactivity, and thereby limits the strength of causal inference. Longitudinal studies in which all assessments are repeated across development are needed to clarify whether inflammation-related disruption of fronto-amygdala circuitry precedes or follows the emergence of heightened stress reactivity, or, alternatively, whether these associations are bidirectional. Second, the final inflammation-fMRI sample of 68 participants, while deeply phenotyped, is modest in size for testing higher-order moderation effects; our sensitivity power analysis indicated that the design was only marginally powered to detect the three-way interaction at the observed effect size, and this interaction did not survive correction across the two LPFC subregions examined. We therefore interpret the neural moderation result as preliminary, a finding that motivates rather than settles the question of how prefrontal-amygdala connectivity shapes the effects of inflammation on daily stress reactivity, and one that requires replication in larger samples. Third, because CRP is a broad-spectrum marker of systemic inflammation, we cannot distinguish among specific inflammatory pathways; future work should examine whether cytokines with more direct central nervous system access, such as interleukin-6 or tumor necrosis factor-α, have comparable or even stronger moderation effects. Fourth, higher CRP was associated with slightly lower average NA across the week even as it was associated with stronger moment-to-moment stress-NA coupling; because these reflect distinct between- and within-person estimands, the between-person pattern does not alter interpretation of the within-person moderation, but we interpret it cautiously given the single CRP assessment. Fifth, although CRP was not directly associated with LPFC-amygdala connectivity in this sample, inflammation may shape stress-related NA through alternative pathways (e.g., anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate salience circuits, ventromedial prefrontal-amygdala regulation, or striatal dopaminergic systems) that warrant investigation in future work. Finally, the sample was predominantly female; given evidence that stress-inflammation coupling may be stronger in female than male adolescents (Jiang et al., 2026), the observed association may not generalize to predominantly male samples.

Despite these limitations, the present findings are important in extending research assessing inflammation and affective reactivity to the level of individual by examining naturalistic daily stress encounters in adolescents, a population for whom elevated stress-related affective reactivity has substantial developmental consequences. We also provide preliminary evidence that DLPFC-amygdala FC may serve as a neural moderator of the effects of inflammation on daily affective functioning in adolescents, offering an initial hypothesis about the cortico-limbic circuitry through which inflammation shapes real-world stress reactivity. Further, we demonstrated the value of integrating EMA with peripheral biomarkers and task-based neuroimaging, a multilevel approach that may help to explain why some adolescents are more vulnerable than others to the adverse mental health consequences of daily stress. Clinically, these findings suggest that adolescents who are characterized by both elevated inflammation and stronger DLPFC-amygdala coupling represent a subgroup at heightened risk for the development of stress-related psychopathology and, further, that interventions targeting either inflammatory processes or fronto-amygdala regulatory efficiency might attenuate the adverse effects of inflammation on daily stress reactivity (Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 2010; Hölzel et al., 2011).
