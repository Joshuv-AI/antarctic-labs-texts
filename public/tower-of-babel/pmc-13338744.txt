Assessment of artificial intelligence in detecting errors on panoramic radiographs
Authors: Tarek Abdallah Abdel Salam, Ahmed Montaser Abdelsalam, Nada Reda Sholkamy, Ahmed Sebaq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the ability of artificial intelligence (AI) to detect common errors in panoramic radiographs.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective study utilized a dataset of 2,888 anonymized panoramic radiographs obtained from multiple dental imaging units. Three annotators classified the images into 3 categories: “uneven magnification,” “tongue space,” and “normal.” Three deep learning architectures—Attention Dental X-ray, ResNet50, and MobileNet—were developed and evaluated. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, precision, sensitivity, area under the curve, and F1 score with 5-fold cross-validation. Statistical analysis was performed using Python version 3.9 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, USA) with the Scikit-learn, Matplotlib, Seaborn, and PyTorch libraries for model development and evaluation. McNemar’s test was used for pairwise model comparisons, and Cohen’s kappa was used to assess intra-rater and inter-rater reliability.ResultsThe Attention Dental X-ray model demonstrated superior performance among all evaluated models. It achieved the highest performance in classifying tongue space errors, with an average accuracy of 82.7%, whereas the ResNet50 and MobileNet models achieved accuracies of 56.9% and 64.1%, respectively.ConclusionArtificial intelligence models, particularly those incorporating attention mechanisms, show strong potential as supplementary tools for detecting errors on panoramic radiographs, improving image quality, and reducing radiation exposure. This study extends the application of AI beyond disease detection to quality assurance in dental radiography. Future research should focus on real-time integration for immediate operator feedback and on the development of automated error correction methods.

Introduction

Dental radiographs are an integral component of modern clinical dentistry. Panoramic radiography is a specialized tomographic technique that produces a flattened image of the dental arches for the evaluation of diagnostic concerns.123 However, panoramic radiographs (PRs) have inherent limitations, including relatively low resolution and the presence of radiopaque and radiolucent structures representing anatomical features, soft tissues, and air spaces.245

Low-quality PRs may lead to misinterpretation, resulting in incorrect diagnoses, inappropriate treatment planning, and repeated imaging, thereby increasing radiation exposure. These issues are not solely attributable to inherent equipment limitations but are often caused by operator-related errors during patient positioning and image acquisition.46

Although numerous types of PR errors exist, including patient movement, improper posture, and imaging artifacts, certain errors are particularly common in clinical practice. Among these, uneven magnification and tongue space errors directly affect image interpretation and exhibit distinct radiographic characteristics. Addressing these issues places an additional burden on clinicians, who must ensure that PRs are free from positioning errors during radiographic acquisition.378

The application of artificial intelligence (AI) in maxillofacial radiology has the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and treatment outcomes. When used as a supplementary tool, AI can assist in the detection of dental conditions and in the evaluation of teeth and surrounding structures in both 2-dimensional and 3-dimensional radiographic images.8910

Maxillofacial radiology has been substantially advanced by the integration of AI technologies, particularly in tasks such as image classification, detection, segmentation, registration, generation, and enhancement. AI systems in this field are used for radiographic diagnosis, image analysis, forensic applications, and image quality improvement.111213141516 Therefore, this study aimed to assess the ability of AI to detect common panoramic radiograph errors.

Materials and Methods

Radiographic dataset collection, evaluation, and annotation

This retrospective observational study was conducted after approval by the Research Ethics Committee under No. DCSR-02025-47. The study utilized a radiographic dataset of 2,888 anonymized PRs collected from the archive of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology at the College of Dentistry, Deraya University, as well as from 2 private radiology centers. The PRs were acquired using 3 panoramic dental imaging units: the Orthophos SL machine (Sirona Dental Systems GmbH, Bensheim, Germany), operated at 63 kVp, 6 mA, and 14.1 seconds with H 403 Prog; the RAYSCAN α+ (Ray Co, Seoul, Korea), operated at 80 kVp, 13 mA, and 13.9 seconds with standard prog; and the Planmeca unit (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland), operated at 66 kVp, 10 mA, and 14.9 seconds with standard M SmartPan Prog.

Ground-truth annotations for all images were provided by 1 oral and maxillofacial radiologist with more than 15 years of experience and 2 oral radiology residents. The images were categorized into 3 main classes relevant to this study: “uneven magnification,” “tongue space,” and “normal images.” Images in the “uneven magnification” category were identified by asymmetric mandibular ramus width or other dimensional distortions, whereas “tongue space” images were identified by a radiolucent band across the apices of the maxillary teeth. In contrast, “normal” images were defined as PRs without uneven magnification or tongue space technical errors (Fig. 1).

The 2 residents independently annotated the entire dataset, and a third senior radiologist was consulted to reach consensus when disagreements occurred. In addition, the senior radiologist repeated the annotation after a 1-month interval for 288 PRs, representing a random 10% subset of the total dataset.

Image analysis identified 2 categories. The first consisted of single-label images (n = 2,206), each containing a single technical error or no error, and was distributed as follows: normal (n = 829) (Fig. 1A), tongue space only (n = 688) (Fig. 1B), and uneven magnification only (n = 689) (Fig. 1C). The second category consisted of multi-label images (n = 682) (Fig. 1D), which exhibited both uneven magnification and tongue space simultaneously. All images were then organized into training, validation, and testing sets containing 2,080 (72.02%), 404 (13.99%), and 404 (13.99%) images, respectively.

Image standardization, preprocessing, and augmentation

Image standardization included uniform resizing of all radiographs to 224 × 224 pixels, normalization using ImageNet statistics for consistency, and conversion to RGB 3-channel format despite the grayscale source images. The training augmentation pipeline included horizontal flipping, shifting, scaling, rotation, and brightness/contrast adjustment. Validation and test preprocessing were limited to resizing and normalization, with no augmentation, to ensure realistic evaluation conditions.

Several strategies were implemented to mitigate class imbalance, including class-specific optimization and a weighted loss function that assigned the normal class a 2.4-fold higher penalty for misclassification. Data splitting was performed using 5-fold stratified cross-validation to preserve proportional class representation in each fold and to promote learning of robust features that were invariant to common acquisition-related variations while maintaining evaluation integrity on unaugmented test data (Table 1).

Model architecture, training, and evaluation

The development and evaluation of the AI models followed a structured pipeline that included data collection, model selection, training, and rigorous performance assessment. This study explored and compared multiple model architectures—MobileNet, ResNet50, and Attention Dental X-ray—with particular emphasis on the performance of the Attention Dental X-ray model. The codebase also included several additional architectures, reflecting a broad approach to model selection.

The Attention Dental X-ray model was built on a ResNet50 backbone and was trained without pretrained weights. A key component of this model was an attention mechanism designed to highlight the most relevant regions within the extracted features. For interpretability, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was used to visualize the decision-making process of the attention mechanism. Grad-CAM identifies the pixels with the strongest influence on the final output and generates a heatmap in which red or warm colors indicate high attention, whereas blue or cool colors indicate regions receiving less attention (Fig. 2).

ResNet50 is a widely used convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture known for its depth and its use of residual, or skip, connections. These connections help mitigate the vanishing gradient problem and enable the training of much deeper networks. In this study, ResNet50 was trained without pretrained weights, meaning that all features were learned from scratch using the provided dental X-ray dataset.

The MobileNet model used the MobileNetV2 architecture as its backbone. MobileNetV2 is recognized for its computational efficiency and suitability for mobile and embedded vision applications. In this study, MobileNet was also trained without pretrained weights because, in preliminary experiments, models initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights consistently underperformed models trained from scratch by 7%–9% for this specific application.

All models were trained on an NVIDIA RTX 3070 GPU (NVIDIA Corp, Santa Clara, USA) with 8 GB of VRAM. For the attention model, all images were processed in batches of 16, and training was performed for a maximum of 25 epochs with early stopping after 5 epochs without improvement. The AdamW optimizer was used with a learning rate of 1 × 10-4, a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler, dropout, and a weight decay of 1 × 10-4. A binary cross-entropy loss function was used with class weights to address class imbalance. Comprehensive preprocessing and augmentation pipelines were applied, and a random seed of 42 was set to improve reproducibility.

Model performance was evaluated using key metrics for each class and then averaged to provide an overall assessment, including the following:

Accuracy (overall correctness of predictions):

Accuracy=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FN

Precision (the proportion of correct identifications):

Precision=TPTP+FP

Sensitivity / recall (the proportion of actual positives that were correctly identified).

Sensitivity/Recall=TPTP+FN

F1 score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced measure).

F1=2×Precision×RecallPrecision+Recall

Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) (a summary of how well different models can discriminate between the positive class and the negative class across all possible classification thresholds) (Fig. 3):

AUC=∑Ri−np(np+1)2np×nn

where TP denotes true positives, TN indicates true negatives, FP denotes false positives, FN corresponds to false negatives, ∑Ri refers to the sum of the ranks of all positive samples, np is the number of positive samples, and nn is the number of negative samples.

Detailed confusion matrices were generated for each class to illustrate the counts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. These matrices provided a granular view of the attention model’s classification performance for each error type (Fig. 4).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses, including calculation of performance metrics, McNemar’s test for pairwise model comparisons across all classes, and plot generation, were performed in Python version 3.9 (Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, USA) using the following libraries: Scikit-learn for metric computation, Matplotlib and Seaborn for data visualization, and PyTorch for model development and training. Cohen’s kappa was used to assess both intra-rater and inter-rater reliability.

Results

The results demonstrated that the Attention Dental X-ray model outperformed the ResNet50 and MobileNet models, achieving an average accuracy of 75.4%, an AUC of 0.828, and an F1 score of 0.709. Its strongest performance was observed in the classification of tongue space errors.

According to Cohen’s kappa results, inter-rater reliability was almost perfect across all categories, with kappa values of 0.951, 0.995, and 0.942 for the uneven magnification, tongue space, and normal classes, respectively, and an overall value of κ = 0.976. Intra-rater reliability also showed strong agreement, with an overall value of κ = 0.895 and class-specific values of 0.878, 0.705, 0.797, and 0.990 for the uneven magnification, tongue space, multi-label, and normal classes, respectively.

The 5-fold cross-validation approach demonstrated stable model performance across all folds, yielding a mean validation F1 score of 0.702. This consistency suggests that the AI system can reliably identify PR errors across different subsets of clinical data and supports the robustness of the classification approach (Table 1).

According to the McNemar test results for pairwise comparisons among the evaluated models, P > 0.05 indicated no statistically significant differences in overall classification performance. Thus, despite numerical differences in observed accuracy and AUC values, the models were statistically comparable, and these differences may have reflected random variation rather than reliable differences in error rates (Table 2).

The ROC curves demonstrated strong, nonrandom performance across all 3 classification tasks, with all reported AUC values exceeding 0.5. All models showed robust diagnostic performance across the 3 categories. Performance was highest for the tongue space class, for which the attention model achieved the highest AUC (0.905). By contrast, uneven magnification was the most challenging class, with all models showing their lowest AUC values for this category (all ≤ 0.713) (Fig. 3).

Table 2 presents a detailed comparison of the 3 deep learning models across key performance metrics and diagnostic classes. The Attention Dental X-ray and MobileNet models generally outperformed ResNet50 in most categories, particularly for the uneven magnification and tongue space classes.

The confusion matrices provided a granular view of the attention model’s classification performance across all classes. The model performed best in classifying tongue space, followed by the normal class, and showed its weakest performance in the uneven magnification class, with a substantial number of false positives (46). This finding indicates difficulty in correctly rejecting negative cases and a tendency to generate false alarms (Fig. 4).

A comparative analysis of 3 deep learning models highlighted the superior performance of the Attention Dental X-ray and MobileNet models relative to ResNet50 in terms of average accuracy, F1 score, and AUC (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Despite its widespread use, PR is prone to positioning errors that may lead to misdiagnosis and increased radiation exposure because of retakes.615171819 An AI model capable of accurately classifying PR errors could provide immediate feedback to operators, enabling correction of positioning errors in real time. This could reduce the frequency of low-quality PRs, minimize the need for retakes, and consequently decrease patient radiation exposure.912131415202122 The primary objective of this study was to assess the ability of AI to detect specific PR errors and thereby address this important clinical problem by demonstrating the feasibility of an AI-driven solution.

The inclusion of data from multiple sources and multiple dental imaging units likely enhanced model robustness, generalizability, and dataset variability, while also better reflecting real-world clinical diversity and reducing equipment-specific bias.2324 In addition, the use of multiple annotators supported a consensus-based approach and strengthened the reliability and validity of the ground-truth labels.3925 Comparing multiple model architectures is also a sound practice in deep learning research because it enables a broader assessment of model strengths and weaknesses and helps identify the architecture best suited to the specific task of detecting PR errors.2627

Previous studies have used attention maps to localize tongue-positioning errors and to identify subtle artifacts, reporting AUC values as high as 0.94, whereas the overall AUC of the attention model in the present study was 0.829. However, attention-based approaches may require extensive training data and substantial computational resources.2328

The consistent results across the 5 folds are encouraging because they suggest that the attention model behaved stably across repeated training and validation cycles. This stability may, in part, reflect the use of early stopping and other regularization strategies, which appear to have limited overfitting (Table 1).

In this study, the attention-based architecture demonstrated the strongest overall performance, achieving average F1 score, AUC, and accuracy values of 0.709, 0.828, and 0.754, respectively, across the evaluated classes. This model was particularly effective in classifying tongue space errors, for which it achieved an AUC of 0.905 and a sensitivity of 80.9% (Table 2). Several previous studies have reported findings consistent with these results.141525 The performance of the ResNet50 and MobileNet models, although lower than that of the attention-based model overall, still provides a useful baseline and supports the broader applicability of CNN-based methods for this task.29

The superior performance of the Attention Dental X-ray model may be attributable to its integrated attention mechanism, which likely directed the model toward the most relevant image regions. This capability may have been particularly important for distinguishing subtle features indicative of technical errors (Fig. 2). Previous research in medical image analysis has highlighted the benefits of attention mechanisms in improving diagnostic accuracy by enabling models to prioritize salient image features.61417181928

Preliminary experiments in this study showed that attention models initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights underperformed models trained from scratch by approximately 7%–9% for this specific application. The model trained from scratch achieved 82.7% accuracy and an F1 score of 0.821 for tongue space classification. This finding may reflect the substantial domain gap between natural images and dental radiographic images, as well as differences in feature characteristics and the presence of technical artifacts in dental radiography.

Particular attention was given to tongue space errors because they commonly occur during PR examinations and can adversely affect radiographic interpretation and diagnostic accuracy. Zadrozny et al.3 and several other studies reported low sensitivity of CNN-based automatic protocols, which may be partly attributable to technical errors, such as tongue space, that interfere with diagnostic interpretation.57

For the uneven magnification class, the attention model outperformed ResNet50 across all the key metrics. However, it underperformed relative to MobileNet for nearly all evaluated metrics (Table 2). This pattern suggests that the attention model had greater difficulty handling the visual distortions associated with uneven magnification or separating this class within a complex classification setting.

The Attention Dental X-ray and MobileNet models achieved higher accuracies for the normal class (77.8% and 75.2%, respectively) than ResNet50 (63.4%). However, the attention model showed relatively low sensitivity but excellent specificity for this class (Fig. 4).

Although inter-rater reliability for the normal class was high in this study (κ = 0.942), variability in radiologists’ interpretations may still have contributed to these findings. In clinical practice, the spectrum of normality is broad, and some radiologists may consider minor positioning variations clinically acceptable, whereas others apply stricter quality thresholds.15 In addition, the normal class is defined largely by the absence rather than the presence of distinctive error patterns, which makes classification inherently more challenging. Further strategies, such as advanced data augmentation or synthetic data generation for the normal class, may improve detection.30

Building on the present findings and addressing the identified limitations, future work should expand the dataset to include a larger and more diverse collection of PRs from multiple institutions and imaging units to improve generalizability. Future studies should also aim to detect a broader range of PR positioning errors, including those related to patient movement, head tilt, and other technical artifacts.

Finally, attention maps derived from deep learning models may support the development of novel approaches for identifying and quantifying positioning errors, such as blurring or anatomical distortion. Such information could facilitate training of models designed to correct these errors automatically, for example, by deblurring images to produce diagnostically improved results. This type of automated solution could enhance image clarity and reduce the need for repeat exposures, thereby benefiting patients, operators, and clinicians alike.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the promising performance of attention-based deep learning architectures for detecting specific common PR errors. The findings provide empirical support for the value of incorporating attention mechanisms to improve diagnostic accuracy in complex radiographic images. This work extends the application of AI beyond general disease detection to quality assurance in dental radiography. It also offers useful insights for both theoretical advances in medical imaging and practical applications aimed at improving patient care. Despite its limitations, this study provides a solid foundation for future investigations and supports the continued integration of AI into high-quality dental diagnostics.
