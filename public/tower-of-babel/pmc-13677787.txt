Ribo-seq rRNA reads reveal co-predicted signatures of ribosome heterogeneity and collisions
Authors: Edwin S Kyei-Baffour, William J Faller, Ferhat Alkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRibosome heterogeneity is increasingly recognized as a key factor in various biological contexts, including development, cancer, and immune responses. To enable systematic exploration of this diversity, we previously developed a bioinformatics method that utilized rRNA reads from Ribo-seq data, detecting changes in ribosomal protein composition (dripARF) and ribosome collisions (dricARF) between conditions simply and efficiently. The method works by projecting differences in rRNA read abundance onto the 3D-structure of the ribosome, following which we perform enrichment tests on predefined heterogeneity-specific rRNA position sets. In this study, we apply this tool to publicly available Ribo-seq datasets to understand the impact of experimental protocol variations and to identify shared biological signals across studies. Our analyses show that the rRNA regions assigned to our prediction sets are consistently enriched in rRNA reads, supporting the core assumptions of our approach. We further examine how various protocol factors influence rRNA read distribution and implement a single-sample rRNA position set enrichment analysis-based weight to limit false positives. Finally, we highlight consistent ribosomal changes across these studies, including prominent co-predictors such as ribosome collisions, eS25, and uS19.Keywords: ribosome heterogeneity, ribosome collisions, rRNA reads, Ribo-seq

Introduction

Although long regarded as homogeneous complexes without inherent regulatory capacity, accumulating evidence demonstrates that ribosomes can exhibit considerable variability [1]. This “ribosome heterogeneity” exists in different forms, with numerous examples now defined [2]. For example, some ribosomes have varied compositions associated with differential ribosome localization [3] and differential translation of transcripts involved in metabolism [4], while others respond to cytokine exposure to adapt the translatome [5]. Ribosomes can also collide when stalled by external stressors or translation defects, forming higher-order structures such as disomes and trisomes that can regulate cell fate [6, 7]. It is clear that variations in ribosome composition and higher-order structure are functionally critical for the cell, and warrant further investigation to clarify their roles in both homeostasis and disease.

Experimental detection of heterogeneity in ribosomal protein (RP) stoichiometry involves the isolation of ribosomal fractions by sucrose density gradient centrifugation, or using a tagged ribosome affinity purification approach, followed by mass-spectrometry-based identification and quantification of RPs [8]. Collisions can be detected using similar approaches, or using ribosome (disome/trisome) profiling [9, 10]. These are laborious and expensive, and are not conducive to high-throughput analysis.

We recently developed the analysis of rRNA fragments (ARF) approach, which repurposes rRNA reads typically discarded during Ribo-seq to predict RP composition changes (dripARF) and collision changes (dricARF) [11, 12]. Positions in the rRNA are assigned to RPs and collision interfaces using resolved ribosome structures from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) [13]. Position counts are normalized and analyzed differentially between conditions, after which predefined position sets are tested using rRNA position set enrichment analysis (RPSEA) to derive an enrichment score (ES2) identifying candidate RPs with compositional changes or altered collision levels.

As ARF can be applied to previously published studies, the large number of pre-existing datasets provides an immense resource to understand the impact of heterogeneity and collisions on biology. However, differences in protocols may introduce biases that affect dripARF predictions [14–16]. To exploit this wealth of existing data, we first map how protocol choices such as RNase type, rRNA depletion strategy, fragment size, and ribosome isolation method shape rRNA read distributions across 166 publicly available human and mouse Ribo-seq studies. This reveals a level of variability that calls for tighter control on predictions, which we address through a single-sample RPSEA-based weighting that sharpens ARF’s predictive accuracy. We then ask which ribosomal changes are co-predicted across diverse biological conditions and phenotypes, correcting for structural similarity between rRNA position sets and reveal a co-prediction network anchored by ribosome collision, eS25, eS19, and uS7.

Materials and methods

Ribo-seq datasets and rRNA read analysis

Human and mouse Ribo-seq data were obtained from NCBI and manually annotated for protocol factors including RNase type, rRNA depletion strategy, fragment size selection, ribosome isolation method, cell or tissue type, immobilization agent, and biological system (in vitro, in vivo, or ex vivo) (Supplementary Table 1). Raw reads were retrieved and converted to FASTQ with sra-tools (v3.0.3) and trimmed for adapters and unique molecular identifiers with cutadapt (v4.2) [17]. Trimmed reads were mapped to rRNA sequences using tophat (v2.1.1) [18]. Mapped read counts, percent rRNA reads, and read size distributions were determined for each sample (Supplementary Fig. 1). Samples with at least 1 million reads and 10% rRNA reads were retained.

To analyze multiple Ribo-seq datasets using downstream statistical methods, mapped read counts were normalized within studies using median-of-ratios implemented by the DESeq2 (v1.38.3) R package [19]. Mapped read counts were also normalized across studies (for each organism), using the vsn (v3.66.0) package [20] and a transformation.

Ribosome structure and rRNA modification analysis

Structural analysis of rRNA positions was based on the PDB ribosomal structure 4V6X [21]. Surface rRNA positions were defined with the Convex Hull of all phosphorus (P) atoms in each rRNA nucleotide (Supplementary Fig. 2A) [22], using the geometry (v0.5.2) R package (https://github.com/davidcsterratt/geometry). The minimum Euclidean distance between all rRNA positions and the nearest surface positions was determined as a measure of the depth of the rRNA position within the ribosome structure (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

The association between rRNA read counts and the rRNA position depth and their assignment to specific RPs and collisions (as a measure of protection of these rRNA positions) was tested with a fixed-effects negative binomial regression with the fixest (v1.01.2) R package [23]. An additive model was used to test the effect of assignment and depth on read counts for each sample to absorb all between-sample differences in experimental conditions and sequencing depth. The depth variable was a natural spline with two degrees of freedom. Separate models were additionally fitted with only assigned or only unassigned positions, and a natural spline with 2 and 1 degree(s) of freedom, respectively. All models were estimated by maximum likelihood and independently for human- and mouse-related samples. Predictions were computed across samples with marginaleffects (v0.32.0) [24] with 95% uncertainty around the median estimated.

Modified rRNA positions were annotated based on previously published modification data [25]. Positions with a modification rate >50% were classified as modified (Supplementary Fig. 2C and D). Read end frequencies were compared between modified and unmodified positions using the Wilcoxon rank test. A negative binomial GLMM was used to compare read counts between modified and unmodified positions across samples, controlling for assignment status [glmmTMB v1.1.14 [26]; fixed effects: modification status and assignment; random effects: modification and sample (1 + modification | sample)]. To test whether the modification effect is sample-specific, a reduced model (1 | sample) was fitted and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) compared.

Assessing protocol factors on rRNA reads

Protocol factor contributions were assessed by comparing variance explained by technical factors against study-level variation using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA). The adonis2 function of the vegan (v2.7-3) R package [27] was used to fit a linear model of all protocol factors, and another model was fitted on the study variable with vst-normalized read counts as the response variable. Factor-specific variance was estimated with variancePartition (v1.28.0) [28]. For human datasets, a full model including all protocol factors was fitted. For mouse datasets, due to redundancy, separate models were fitted for cell/tissue type, RNase, and maximum fragment size individually. The dominant factor at each rRNA position was defined as that explaining the most variance, tallied across positions, organisms, and rRNA position sets.

Single-sample rRNA position set enrichment analysis-based weighting of predictions

To reduce predictions attributable to technical remnants, the single-sample rRNA position set enrichment analysis (ssRPSEA) metric was introduced as an orthogonal measure of confidence in ARF predictions. Whereas the original ARF framework relies on RPSEA derived from differential analysis between conditions, ssRPSEA evaluates enrichment patterns at the level of individual samples and is subsequently used to support or correct the pairwise RPSEA-based predictions. For each sample, a single-sample enrichment score was calculated for every rRNA position set, conceptually equivalent to single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [29]. These scores, termed ssRPSEA scores, were computed using the GSVA (v1.46.0) R package [30] with the ssGSEA method after library normalization with DESEQ2 count factors [19] and natural log transformation (log1p function). ssRPSEA scores were z-scaled, and changes across pairwise comparisons were determined using the lmFit function of the limma (3.54.0) R package [31]. Since these sets originate from a single ribosomal structure and are correlated, the Bayesian moderation of the variance was employed with the eBayes function of the limma package. The fold change and P-value were used in the scoring function (Formula 1) with a 0–1 boundary.

Analysis of rRNA fragments predictions and co-prediction analysis

RP and collision changes were predicted with dripARF and dricARF for human and mouse studies containing replicate samples per comparison group. As mouse datasets show stronger protocol effects and mouse rRNA position sets are derived from the human ribosomal structure, co-prediction analysis only focused on the human datasets. The threshold for prediction was set at 1 from our earlier publication [11], and enrichment score 2 (ES2)/RPSEA_NES_randZ score or weighted ES2 (ES2 × w) of sets above these thresholds were counted across studies and compared. Highly predicted RP sets were visualized with ChimeraX (1.11.1) [32], using the 4V6X structure [21].

For co-prediction analysis, pairwise counts of sets predicted together in a single comparison were generated from the predictions and visualized with the ComplexHeatmap (v2.14.0) R package [33]. Pairwise 3D Euclidean distances between all sets (capped at 100, then z-scaled) were compared with co-prediction counts to assess proximity-driven co-prediction. Co-prediction counts were normalized for rRNA position overlap using a Poisson GLM (log link, R v4.2) with log(overlapping positions) as an offset. Fitted values represent the overlap-expected co-prediction rate; observed-to-fitted ratios were log-transformed, and a threshold of 1 was used to retain genuine co-predictions.

To further analyze the co-prediction results, co-predictions were built into a graph with the igraph (v2.1.4) R package [34] and visualized the network with the ggraph R package [35]. Betweenness and pageranks were computed for the network to identify fundamental rRNA position sets for the co-prediction network.

Visualization and statistical tests

All statistical test details about each experiment can be found in the figure legends, figures, or results. Unless stated explicitly, plots were made with the ggplot2 (v3.3.6) R package [36].

Results

Ribo-seq rRNA reads originate from all parts of the ribosome

Although considered “junk,” we have shown that rRNA reads contain information about the components of the ribosome [11] and their higher order structure [12]. A major implication of this is that all previously published datasets also contain this information, providing a rich source of information about ribosomes in many different contexts.

In order to understand the dataset-to-dataset variability that exists, we downloaded and reanalyzed 166 previously published studies, from both human- (95 studies with 1153 samples) and mouse-derived (71 studies with 951 samples) samples from different cells and tissues (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1A and B). The predominant fragment size sequenced across the studies ranged from 26 to 35 nucleotides (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

On average, studies had a large number of reads (median > 10 M) that originated from rRNA (Fig. 1B). This number tended to be higher in human studies than in mouse studies (median of 55.7% compared with 48.1% of total Ribo-seq reads) (Fig. 1C). rRNA fragment sizes peaked at 32 (human) and 31 (mouse) nucleotides, consistent with ribosome footprint sizes (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 1C). Further analysis showed that although rRNA reads originate from all parts of the ribosome, there was significant variability in the number of reads across the rRNA (Fig. 1E), allowing us to use ARF to identify changes in the ribosome.

rRNA regions protected by ribosomal proteins show higher read counts

The ARF approach (dripARF [11] and dricARF [12]) aims to capture the changes in the total abundance of proteins in the ribosome (dripARF) or changes in the abundance of collisions (dricARF) during translation. Underlying this approach is the assumption that rRNA regions will be protected from degradation by the presence of a protein, and this can be detected in the rRNA reads. To test this, ARF determines the enrichment of specific rRNA positions in the ribosome, grouped into rRNA sets, between two conditions by utilizing rRNA fragments from Ribo-seq data. Through the analysis of available ribosomal structures, these positions are assigned to proteins (dripARF) or positions with altered digestion in a disome compared with a monosome (dricARF). We assessed the occurrence of assigned and unassigned rRNA regions, previously determined through structural analysis [11], in the data gathered from the 166 Ribo-seq studies. As predicted, assigned rRNA positions had higher read counts than unassigned positions in both mouse and human datasets (Fig. 2A), suggesting that these positions are protected from digestion by their associated proteins.

We next tested whether surface accessibility independently influences read counts, as solvent-exposed positions may be more prone to digestion [37]. Using surface distance and assignment as predictors in a fixed-effects negative binomial regression (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B), assigned positions had 300% and 11% more reads in both human- (exp(β) = 3.045, se = 0.017, z = 401.6, P<.0001) and mouse-related (exp(β) = 1.114, se = 0.007, z= 17.8, P<.0001) datasets (Fig. 2B), buttressing the earlier analysis of assigned and unassigned rRNA positions (Fig. 2A).

Within the assigned positions (Fig. 2C, orange lines), read counts decreased monotonically (all splines were negative) as the rRNA positions were buried deeper in the ribosome in both organisms (Human: spline 1 β = −0.722, se = 0.004, spline 2 β = −1.538, se = 0.004, P<.0001; Mouse: spline 1 β = −1.786, se = 0.004, spline 2 β = −2.125, se = 0.005, P<.0001) (Fig. 2C). This suggests that assigned positions buried deep in the ribosome have reduced read recovery, reflecting the compact and restrictive buried areas that are less accessible to nuclease digestion, leading to the generation of larger fragment sizes that are usually not selected for sequencing.

Unassigned rRNA positions were closer to the surface (Supplementary Fig. 2B, 0–14.7 Å range of distance from the surface), and within this surface-restricted range, an increase in this distance was associated with increased read counts (Human: β = 2.902, se = 0.016, P<.0001; Mouse: β = 1.450, se = 0.02, P<.0001) (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that as the ribosome is traversed from the surface to buried rRNA positions, rRNA shielding, defined in ARF, and the depth of these positions contribute to read counts.

Finally, we checked for the association between rRNA modifications and changes in observed rRNA fragments, as modification is known to affect RNase digestion efficiency and reverse transcription (RT) process at such rRNA positions [25, 38]. For this, we quantified the frequency of read ends of uniquely mapped reads in each sample and assessed the frequencies across all samples (Supplementary Fig. 2D). There was no detectable difference between modified and unmodified positions (Fig. 2D), suggesting that modified sites are not preferentially enriched or depleted with respect to cleavage occurrence. Additionally, we assessed the association between modification status and rRNA position counts. A generalized linear mixed model (negative binomial), while controlling for structural effects (assigned and unassigned positions), was used to test whether modified positions differ in counts compared to unmodified positions across samples. In contrast to the frequency-based analysis, modified positions had lower counts compared with unmodified positions in both human (β = −1.1336, se = 0.0301, z= −37.6, P<.00001) and mouse (β = −1.1917, se = 0.0308, z= −38.7, P<.00001) samples.

Model comparison also showed that this effect varies across samples (Fig. 2E). This was ascertained by observing whether allowing modification effect to differ across samples (rRNA modification random effect model) improved the model compared with a fixed, across-sample effect (full mixed-effect model: Human BIC = 184445913, Mouse BIC = 141369625; reduced mixed-effect model: Human BIC = 184495771, Mouse BIC = 141406225). This suggests that digestion can occur across the ribosome, but modified positions tend to have fewer reads, indicating that modification could affect rRNA position counts. This is consistent with the possibility that rRNA modifications can reduce cleavage efficiency and rRNA read counts.

Different protocol factors can affect rRNA read counts

Beyond structural determinants of rRNA read counts, Ribo-seq protocols differ substantially in RNase choice, ribosome immobilization agent (i.e. cycloheximide or harringtonine), fragment size selection, and rRNA depletion strategy [16, 39], each of which could independently distort the read distributions that ARF relies on. PERMANOVA revealed significant technical variation across studies, higher in mouse () than human () datasets (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 3A and B). Variance partitioning across individual rRNA positions [28] showed that cell or tissue type was the dominant factor in the human dataset (Fig. 3B), with RNase type and maximum fragment size also explaining a significant portion of variance (Supplementary Fig. 3C). When we assessed the same protocol variations on the rRNA positions in each set separately (as opposed to across all the rRNA as above), the same factors are largely found to be dominant, with some exceptions. In the human data, for example, maximum fragment size did not affect any of the rRNA positions in the uL11 set, while in eS12 and uL3, the type of RNase type had little or no effect on the set (Fig. 3C), suggesting that read counts in these proximal regions may be less sensitive to some library preparation choices. In the mouse dataset, redundancy among cell/tissue type, RNase, and maximum fragment size required separate models for each factor. Despite this, the same three factors dominated, with RNase type and maximum fragment size explaining a higher proportion of variance than cell/tissue type (Fig. 3B and D). Together, these analyses show that variations in protocol can introduce significant biases to the rRNA read distributions observed during Ribo-seq. Direct cross-study comparisons of rRNA counts are therefore inadvisable, as samples processed under different protocols are not comparable. Crucially, within-study comparisons remain valid, as all samples within a study share the same protocol. However, as different protocols produce different noise profiles in rRNA read distributions, the behavior of ARF’s ES2 scores will differ across studies, resulting in variable false positive and false negative rates across within-study comparisons from different studies.

Single-sample rRNA position set enrichment analysis-based weighting limits overestimated prediction scores

The variable false positive rates arising from different protocol noise profiles present a significant challenge when drawing consistent conclusions about ribosome heterogeneity across the 166 datasets. Although ARF predictions have been benchmarked to correctly identify expected RP heterogeneity and collision events with no obvious false negatives [11, 12], additional predictions consistently accompany these expected findings, whose validity remains uncertain. Predicted heterogeneity even in control-to-control comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 4A) further suggests that prediction scores are systematically overestimated, inflating the false positive rate. To reduce this false positive rate without increasing the false negative rate, we introduced additional single-sample rRNA position set enrichment analysis (ssRPSEA).

To highlight the effectiveness of this constraint, we analyzed a study in which the authors showed an increase in collisions between ribosomes upon either Syh1 knockout or the use of a construct containing repeats of the nonoptimal CGA codon (syh1_cga_mono) [40]. In addition to this were two controls: the normal reporter and a condon-optimized version. As expected, we detect a change in collisions (via the Rib.Col. set) as the top hit using the raw ES2. However, in addition to a change in collisions, this analysis suggested that many RPs were also changing between the conditions (Fig. 4C, left panel). Furthermore, comparison of the two control groups also resulted in the prediction that several RPs were changing (Fig. 4C, right panel), highlighting the detrimental role of false positives. To assess the effectiveness of our approach to minimize this issue, we reanalyzed these data using ssRPSEA-derived weighting. While we still detected changes in ribosome collisions, the number of predicted changes was vastly reduced (Fig. 4C, left panel), and no predictions were made in the control:control comparison (Fig. 4C, right panel), demonstrating that ssRPSEA retains the true collision signal while eliminating spurious RP predictions and resolving the false positive control-to-control comparisons. We next applied it to two additional studies to confirm our observations. In the first, the authors promoted ribosome collisions in HeLa cells by removing glutamine from the media [41]. ARF predicted a change in collisions using both the raw and weighted ES2. However, while the raw ES2 predicted five additional RPs as altered following starvation, the weighted score only makes one additional prediction (Supplementary Fig. 4C). In the second study, cells were treated with 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3AT), leading to an increase in ribosome collisions [9]. As before, the raw ARF approach resulted in a large number of predictions (18), which was drastically reduced using the weighted score (Supplementary Fig. 4D). These results confirm that ssRPSEA-derived weighting consistently reduces spurious prediction scores across datasets with different protocol noise profiles.

Weighted ribosome heterogeneity and collision prediction scores identify commonly predicted ribosomal changes

With this validated weighting approach, we next applied it across human datasets to identify consistently predicted ribosomal changes. We focused on the human dataset because they are less affected by protocol factors compared with the mouse dataset (Fig. 3A). Additionally, the mouse rRNA position sets are derived from the human ribosomal structure [11, 12], which introduces additional uncertainty in their positional assignments and reduces our confidence in mouse-specific predictions. We ran ARF on 88 of the 95 human studies, comprising 1646 distinct comparisons. The remaining seven studies were excluded for lacking adequate comparisons, defined as at least two conditions with two replicates after sample quality filtering. We subsequently applied the previously defined threshold of 1 [11, 12], which we previously showed to reflect structural changes in the ribosome in ARF-defined positions.

Using the weighted ES2 score, >50% of the predictions made using the raw ES2 score were discarded (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 5A), suggesting that the majority of raw predictions are not supported by within-sample positional relationships, consistent with artifacts arising from differential test ranking and enrichment testing [11]. Collisions/Rib. Col. (53.5% removed), eS25 (64.4% removed), and eS19 (64.5% removed) had the least of their predictions removed, while all predictions of eL38 were discarded (Supplementary Fig. 5A). With these predictions removed, the most predicted sets were collisions/Rib. Col. (278; 16.8% of comparisons), eS25 (158; 9.6% of comparisons), eS19 (137; 8.3% of comparisons), and uS7 (120; 7.3% of comparisons) (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 5B). The greater number of collision predictions relative to individual RP sets supports the previous finding of widespread collisions in biology [9, 42], and suggests that variation in collision abundance is a common occurrence. Interestingly, the top predicted protein sets are at the head and surface of the small ribosomal subunit (Fig. 5B), suggesting that this is a hotspot for variations in ribosome structure. Supporting this idea, eS25 has been shown previously to exhibit substoichiometry in actively translating ribosomes [4], and eS19 is the most commonly mutated RP in diamond-Blackfan anemia, showing that haploinsufficiency is tolerated in most cells [43].

Co-predictions highlight potential relationships within the ribosome

In addition to identifying potential ribosome heterogeneities, ARF also allows us to explore the relationships between them. Such relationships could stem from several different sources, both technical and biological, and differentiating between these is essential for meaningful biological interpretation. As most of our sets are based on RPs’ proximity to rRNA, there is some overlap between RP sets that are close to each other on the ribosome. As a result, a change in the digestion of rRNA caused by the altered stoichiometry of one RP may also result in the prediction of another RP. This could present as an increase in co-prediction above what would be expected (Supplementary Fig. 6A). We therefore normalized co-prediction counts for positional overlap (see Materials and methods), retaining pairs with a normalized score >1 as genuine co-predictions (Fig. 6). Using this normalization, we probed the relationships between predictions. Unsurprisingly, we found the most co-predictions associated with ribosome collisions. Collisions are known to reshape the ribosome, causing extensive changes to ribosomal structure and producing degradation intermediates [44] that may be detectable using ARF. Indeed, ARF co-predicted changes to uS3, eS7, and uS10 alongside collisions, all of which are known to be ubiquitinated upon ribosome collisions [45–47], as well as uL24, which is UMFylated upon collisions [48]. Additional network analysis of co-predictions highlighted collisions/Rib.Col., eS25, and uS19 as well-connected sets (Supplementary Fig. 6B and C). Among these three sets, uS19 warrants further attention. Though it is not a highly predicted set (n = 50), uS19 predictions were associated with ribosome collision and eS25 predictions (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 6B). However, the reverse was not observed, indicating that this relationship is nonreciprocal, and suggesting that uS19 changes occur specifically in ribosome states characterized by collisions and eS25 compositional changes. This interpretation is supported by the co-prediction of uS19 and uS3, an RP associated with ribosome collisions and stalling [49], which is also co-predicted with uS19. Based on structural data, uS19 helps stabilize A- and P-site tRNAs (Fig. 5B) [50], and changes in uS19 in translating ribosomes could plausibly affect translation elongation dynamics. Altogether, collisions/Rib.Col., eS25, and uS19 form a core interconnected cluster in the ARF ribosome heterogeneity co-prediction network, whose biological interpretation warrants further investigation.

Discussion

The ARF approach provides a systematic test of changes in RPs and ribosome collisions using Ribo-seq data, extracting biologically meaningful information from rRNA reads that would otherwise be discarded.

To enable reliable cross-study application, we analyzed 166 human and mouse Ribo-seq studies to characterize the effects of experimental protocol variation on rRNA read distributions. When comparing studies with different protocols, we found that cell or tissue type, RNase used, and maximum fragment size were the dominant factors affecting rRNA read distributions, consistent with their established roles as major sources of variability in Ribo-seq library preparation. As each specific study uses the same protocol across experimental groups, the biases are consistent across samples and should not impact the predictive power of ARF. One caveat is that complete digestion of specific rRNA regions by certain protocol choices could yield false-negative ARF predictions.

Analysis of these datasets revealed that raw ARF prediction scores are susceptible to overestimation, likely driven by the protocol-dependent noise profiles that affect ES2 score behavior across studies. However, this analysis allowed us to improve the approach by adding a correction step to the ARF prediction score to reduce these false-positive predictions, as validated across three independent datasets. ssRPSEA-derived weighting was implemented as part of the updated ARF pipeline, with weighted scores reported alongside raw ES2 scores in ARF’s output.

On the back of these weighted scores, we assessed the datasets for the most commonly predicted sets. Changes in ribosome collisions were the most predicted alteration across the datasets, being identified in of comparisons. Considering their abundance (estimated to be 10% of ribosomes in homeostatic conditions, rising to 30% in stress conditions [9, 51]) and their role as sensors of stress [7, 52], this is consistent with both the known abundance of collisions under stress conditions and the prevalence of stress-related experimental designs across the datasets.

Also highly predicted were eS25 (9.6% of comparisons), eS19 (8.3% of comparisons), and uS7 (7.3% of comparisons). All three RPs have been suggested to play a role in cap-independent translation [53–55]. As stress conditions commonly shift the balance from cap-dependent to cap-independent translation, the frequent use of stressors across these datasets may explain their elevated prediction rates by ARF.

Having run ARF on these datasets also allowed us to probe the relationships between our predictions. This analysis suggests that co-predicted changes within the same comparison are infrequent, indicating that coordinated changes affecting multiple sets simultaneously are not a dominant feature of the biological contexts represented in these datasets (Supplementary Fig. 5D). However, the analysis did reveal some interesting correlations with potential biological relevance. Prediction of changes in ribosome collisions overlapped with the most RP predictions. This may be due to ribosomes lacking RPs being more prone to collisions, or due to ARF detecting RP modification or degradation downstream of the collisions [44].

However, network analysis highlighted three sets (Rib. Col., eS25, and uS19) that are convergence points for predicted ribosomal structural changes (Supplementary Fig. 5E). As described above, ribosome collisions are abundant in the cell, and are known to change in response to stress; eS25 is substoichiometric, and appears to regulate ribosomal output in key cellular contexts [3, 4]; and uS19 is mutated in 8%–15% of relapsed chronic lymphocytic leukemia [56, 57]. We propose that these three sets may reflect a common ribosomal stress state, whereby changes in eS25 stoichiometry or uS19 integrity at the decoding center alter translation elongation dynamics and increase the propensity for ribosome collisions, a hypothesis that warrants direct experimental testing.

Finally, beyond the detection of RP heterogeneity, these data may also carry additional information. We show that modifications may be detectable, although to confirm this will require extensive optimization of ARF. Additionally, the positions not assigned to RP-sets may also carry information. Many of these positions map to the rRNA expansion segments (ESs), eukaryote-specific insertions located predominantly at the ribosome surface. Consistent with their surface exposure, ES-associated positions show lower read counts than assigned positions, reflecting their greater accessibility to nuclease digestion. ESs mediate inter-ribosomal contacts and interactions with translation factors [58, 59], and changes in ES-associated read patterns could in principle reflect alterations in higher-order ribosome organization. Defining ES-specific position sets represents a natural extension of the ARF framework that we leave for future work.

Conclusion

We demonstrate the potential of ARF (dripARF and dricARF) to identify changes in ribosome biology across multiple datasets through systematic within-study analysis, leveraging pre-existing Ribo-seq data. Across these datasets, ribosome collisions and compositional changes at the head of the small ribosomal subunit, particularly involving eS25, eS19, and uS7, emerged as the most frequently predicted alterations, with collisions, eS25, and uS19 forming a core co-prediction module suggestive of a shared ribosomal state. More broadly, we demonstrate that rRNA reads routinely discarded in Ribo-seq analysis carry biologically meaningful information about ribosome composition and collision state, and the vast body of publicly available Ribo-seq data therefore represents a largely untapped resource whose utility will only grow as protocols standardize and higher-resolution ribosomal structures are determined.
