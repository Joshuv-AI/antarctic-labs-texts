In situ NIR-IIb imaging of endogenous H2S signaling for high-resolution abiotic stress visualization in plants
Authors: Jianxing Feng, Shengchun Sun, Hong Hu, Ning Shi, Yibin Ying, Yixian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In vivo analysis of plant stress responses remains a major challenge in precision agriculture, limiting dynamic optimization of crop growth under variable environmental conditions. Fluorescence imaging enables nondestructive tracking of stress biomarkers, but its accuracy is compromised by the low abundance of endogenous signaling molecules, tissue autofluorescence interference, and limited signal penetration depth. Here, we develop a ratiometric near-infrared IIb fluorescent probe for sensitive monitoring of endogenous hydrogen sulfide (H2S). This nanoprobe integrates lanthanide nanoparticles with H2S-responsive molecular units, enabling enhanced tissue penetration and high-resolution imaging through an absorption competition-induced emission mechanism. Under abiotic stress conditions, the probe visualizes stress-induced fluctuations of H2S in living plants. This work establishes an H2S-centered strategy for plant stress visualization and provides a foundation for developing early diagnosis platforms.

Introduction

Modern agriculture is essential for human societies, providing essential resources, including food, fiber, and industrial raw materials1,2. Nonetheless, rapid global population growth, which is expected to reach around 9.8 billion by mid-century, together with climate change and natural resource depletion, poses severe challenges to global agricultural systems3,4. Abiotic stresses driven by these global-scale changes, including heat, drought, soil salinization, and heavy metal toxicity, disrupt cellular homeostasis and physiological processes. These stresses result in substantial yield losses, directly threatening food security and agricultural sustainability5,6. Consequently, developing approaches for early detection of abiotic stress before visible phenotypic changes emerge is critical for enabling timely interventions and advancing precision agricultural management.

Recent advances in plant stress physiology have revealed that early signaling molecules, including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and nitric oxide (NO), are dynamically regulated during the initial stages of stress responses, preceding visible morphological changes6,7. This early signaling process provides an important window for early intervention. H2S is an important endogenous reactive sulfur species (RSS) in plants, which is primarily biosynthesized through L-cysteine desulfhydrase (L-CDes)-mediated desulfuration during abiotic stress8,9. In addition, H2S exhibits synergistic crosstalk with reactive oxygen species (ROS), NO, abscisic acid (ABA), and enzymatic antioxidant systems, thereby contributing to plant stress adaptation9,10. In contrast to the widely used stress marker ROS, which suffers from cross-reactivity and a transient lifetime, H2S exhibits characteristic nucleophilic properties and chemical stability8,11. These properties, together with their close association with dynamic stress responses, enable reliable monitoring of plant stress progression.

In situ monitoring technologies based on microelectrodes, fluorescence, Raman spectroscopy, and colorimetry have been developed to monitor physicochemical signals in plants2,12–15. When integrated with flexible sensing systems and portable instruments, these approaches enable on-site stress detection and support timely stress management16–18. However, among these approaches, microelectrodes can induce localized oxidative stress in plant tissues and suffer from electrode passivation caused by biofouling or metabolite deposition during prolonged use2,19. Conventional spectroscopic approaches also suffer from low signal-to-background ratios (SBR) for stress response detection, mainly due to the heterogeneity of plant tissues and the dynamic nature of their microenvironments20–22.

To overcome these limitations and improve spatiotemporal resolution, second near-infrared (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) fluorescence bioimaging has emerged as a promising approach for monitoring plant stress responses23,24. This technique relies on the reduced photon scattering and absorption of biological tissues at longer wavelengths. Specifically, within the NIR-IIb window (1500–1700 nm), reduced tissue scattering and minimized interference from phytochrome autofluorescence enable substantially improved SBR and image contrast compared with visible and NIR-I imaging25. As a result, lanthanide-doped down-shifting nanoparticles (DSNPs), with abundant 4f electron energy levels, represent promising candidates for NIR-II fluorescent probes. Their NIR emission can be precisely tuned by regulating energy transfer among Ln3+ ions. Although 4fn intraconfigurational transitions are Laporte-forbidden, nanoscale doping-induced crystal field distortions can partially relax this restriction26,27. Consequently, DSNPs exhibit narrowband emission, large Stokes shifts, prolonged fluorescence lifetimes, and exceptional photostability, enabling in situ monitoring of signaling molecules within complex plant tissues28,29.

In this work, we develop a non-destructive plant stress sensing platform based on NIR-IIb bioimaging for in situ monitoring of H2S signaling during abiotic stress responses. By covalently conjugating the H2S-responsive unit IR-820-(COOH)2 to dual-band absorbing DSNPs through a PEI interlayer, we construct a ratiometric absorption competition-induced emission (ACIE) system (Fig. 1a, b). This design enables H2S-triggered NIR-IIb emission from DSNPs@PEI-820, facilitating in situ imaging of endogenous H2S with high spatiotemporal resolution. Furthermore, we demonstrate the capability of our platform to monitor endogenous H2S dynamics in living plants subjected to mechanical wounding, Cd2+ exposure, and heat shock, highlighting its cross-species applicability for abiotic stress sensing and plant physiology studies (Fig. 1c).Fig. 1Schematic design of ratiometric luminescent nanoprobes for in situ visualization of abiotic stress-induced H2S signaling in living plants.a Schematic illustration of the chemical structure of IR-820-(COOH)2 before and after the response to the plant stress signaling molecule hydrogen sulfide (H2S). b Schematic illustration of the absorption competition-induced emission (ACIE)-driven ratiometric nanoprobe for near-infrared IIb (NIR-IIb) fluorescence imaging. Results

Assembly of H2S-activated NIR-IIb nanoprobes for in situ plant imaging

In our design, H2S-specific NIR-IIb fluorescent nanoprobes were constructed by integrating IR-820-(COOH)2 with DSNPs30. To minimize interference from plant autofluorescence, DSNPs exhibiting ratiometric emission at 1550 nm were fabricated through a layer-by-layer coating strategy based on the co-precipitation of rare earth metal acetates31. As the H2S-responsive subunit, IR-820-(COOH)2 was covalently linked to the PEI layer modified on the DSNP surface to construct an activatable fluorescent sensor (Fig. 2a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that the NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce core and NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce@NaGdF4:Yb,Nd core–shell nanoparticles were nanosized crystals with diameters of 10.98 ± 0.16 nm and 13.09 ± 0.08 nm, respectively (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1–3). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of the DSNPs revealed average lattice spacings of 5.13 Å and 3.01 Å, corresponding to the (1 0 0) and (1 1 0) facets of NaGdF4 (JCPDS No. 27-0699). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) results confirmed the typical single-phase hexagonal crystalline structure of the DSNPs (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5a). The core–shell architecture of NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce@NaGdF4:Yb,Nd nanoparticles was verified by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. Supplementary Fig. 7 illustrates the energy transfer pathway of NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce@NaGdF4:Yb,Nd DSNPs. In this architecture, Nd3+ and Yb3+ primarily serve as sensitizers, whereas Er3+ acts as the activator responsible for NIR-IIb emission32. The Yb3+–Yb3+ bridge facilitates cross-layer energy transfer, enabling photon migration from the sensitizers to the Er3+ activator. Meanwhile, Ce3+ doping promotes non-radiative phonon-assisted cross-relaxation from the Er3+ 4I11/2 level, suppressing upconversion emission and facilitating population of the 4I13/2 state responsible for 1550 nm down-shifting luminescence (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Note 2)33. Figure 2d shows the NIR emission characteristics of the DSNPs. Under 808 and 980 nm excitation, the dominant Er3+ emission peak (4I13/2 → 4I15/2) was observed at 1550 nm. Furthermore, the outer shell layer enhances fluorescence efficiency by reducing surface quenching of the luminescent core in aqueous media33. Therefore, compared with the core-only nanoparticles, the engineered core–shell DSNPs exhibited 50.30-fold and 1.36-fold increases in Er3+ emission intensity under 808 and 980 nm excitation, respectively (Fig. 2e). These results demonstrate that the core–shell architecture and doping strategy effectively enhance DSNP emission intensity and enable tunable high-efficiency NIR-II emission.

As shown in Fig. 2f and Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10, the particle size of DSNPs increased to 16.94 ± 0.26 nm after surface modification with PEI and IR-820-(COOH)2, accompanied by the observation of an amorphous PEI layer on the nanoparticle surface. Compared with DSNPs, the Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of DSNPs@PEI exhibited changes in O–H/N–H stretching and N–H bending vibration intensities, indicating coordination between PEI and DSNPs (Supplementary Fig. 11a and Supplementary Note 3)30. The covalent conjugation of IR-820-(COOH)2 onto the nanoparticle surface was further verified by FT-IR and XPS analyses (Supplementary Figs. 11b, 12 and Supplementary Note 4). The appearance of characteristic amide I/II bands and corresponding changes in N–H stretching vibrations in the FT-IR spectra, together with the emergence of amide-nitrogen signals in the XPS profiles, supported the formation of stable amide linkages30,32,34. Furthermore, Supplementary Fig. 13a, b show that DSNPs@PEI and DSNPs exhibit comparable emission properties, suggesting that the modified PEI primarily functions as a non-photoreactive intermediate covalent linking layer. Zeta potential measurements showed that modification with positively charged PEI increased the surface potential of DSNPs from 36.43 to 45.66 mV. Following conjugation with IR-820-(COOH)2 containing abundant negatively charged carboxyl groups, the zeta potential decreased to −16.31 mV (Supplementary Fig. 13c)30.

To optimize H2S detection sensitivity and minimize background interference, we adjusted the molar ratio of DSNPs to IR-820-(COOH)2 to regulate the ACIE-mediated photon distribution. Increasing the IR-820-(COOH)2 loading from 0 to 756.9 mg mol−1 enhanced absorption at 808 nm and reduced 1550 nm emission under 808 nm excitation, whereas the emission under 980 nm excitation remained constant (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). DSNPs@PEI-820 exhibited a fluorescence “turn-on” response to S2− (25–200 μM) at all loading levels (Fig. 2g). Low loadings (22.9/168.9 mg mol−1) resulted in high background signals, whereas high loadings (421.8/756.9 mg mol−1) exhibited limited response changes. The optimized loading of 297.3 mg mol−1 achieved a balance between high SBR and effective H2S detection.

Mechanistic study on the specific response of nanoprobes to H2S

H2S exhibits nucleophilicity owing to the lone pair of electrons on the sulfur atom, enabling addition reactions at electrophilic sites35. This reaction mechanism can be exploited by cyanine dyes containing suitable electrophilic centers. Accordingly, we selected IR-820-(COOH)2 as the H2S recognition unit in our probe design. IR-820-(COOH)2 is an indocyanine green (ICG) analog derivative comprising two benzo[e]indolium units bridged by a chlorinated cyclohexenyl linker through a heptamethine chain, with carboxyl groups functionalized at the indolene N-alkyl terminals. As shown in Fig. 3a, upon exposure to S2−, the absorption of IR-820-(COOH)2 at 820 nm decreased markedly, while a new absorption band emerged at 380 nm, resulting in a pronounced blueshift of the main absorption peak. Furthermore, Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17 show that the absorbance at 820 nm decreases over the reaction time. The change in peak absorbance intensity correlates negatively (k < 0) with S2− addition, indicating distinct dependencies on reaction time and target concentration. Additionally, to verify whether IR-820-(COOH)2 conjugated on DSNPs@PEI-820 retained selective reactivity toward H2S, we used hypotaurine (HT) as an H2S scavenger36,37. To further clarify the mechanism underlying nucleophilic addition between IR-820-(COOH)2 and H2S, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out. Electrostatic potential (ESP) mapping of IR-820-(COOH)2 revealed charge polarization, with positive potential localized along the π-conjugated heptamethine bridge and negative potential localized at the carboxylate groups (Fig. 3b). Frontier orbital distribution analysis revealed LUMO localization around the benzoindolium N+ center and adjacent C−2 atom (Fig. 3c). The electron-deficient nature of the benzoindolium N+ center increases the electrophilicity of the adjacent C−2 atom, suggesting it as the preferred site for H2S-mediated nucleophilic addition in IR-820-(COOH)238. This proposed site was supported by HRMS and FT-IR analyses, which supported the formation of the thiolated adduct and disruption of the cyanine conjugated system (Supplementary Fig. 19 and Supplementary Note 5)39–42. DFT calculations also revealed that H2S addition at the C=N+ site induced bond elongation and dihedral twisting, thereby disrupting π-conjugated coplanarity (Fig. 3d)43,44. The consequent increase in the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)–LUMO gap explains the observed attenuation in absorption43. To further examine these mechanistic findings, we compared IR-820-(COOH)2 with a homologous series of cyanine dyes (Cy3, Cy5, and Cy7.5) sharing the identical C=N+ moiety. Compared to Cy3, Cy5, and Cy7.5, IR-820-(COOH)2 showed a more positive electrostatic potential at the terminal C=N+ sites, rendering it more susceptible to nucleophilic attack (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 20a and Supplementary Note 6)45. Furthermore, the narrower HOMO–LUMO gap of IR-820-(COOH)2 may lower the kinetic barrier for nucleophilic addition, collectively contributing to enhanced H2S reactivity of this cyanine dye (Fig. 3c–f, Supplementary Figs. 20b and 21).

To assess anti-interference capability, we evaluated the H2S-responsive behavior of IR-820-(COOH)2, the recognition unit of the nanoprobe, in the presence of potential interferents at concentrations comparable to or exceeding their typical physiological levels in planta. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22, IR-820-(COOH)2 displayed high selectivity toward H2S, with negligible spectral responses observed in the presence of a broad panel of potential interferents, including representative cations, anions, signaling molecules, phytohormones, ROS, and endogenous biothiols. Due to its low molecular weight and the high electron density of the sulfur atom, H2S exhibits lower steric hindrance and higher nucleophilicity during interaction with IR-820-(COOH)211,35. Moreover, the pKa of H2S is lower than that of glutathione (GSH) and cysteine (Cys), both of which also exhibit nucleophilic activity46. This enables H2S to dissociate more readily as a nucleophile under physiological microenvironments. Consequently, IR-820-(COOH)2 exhibits preferential reactivity toward H2S over other biothiols. Similarly, under interference from a simulated plant tissue matrix, DSNPs@PEI-820 maintained high selectivity toward H2S, supporting its application for specific monitoring of endogenous H2S in plants (Fig. 3g).

NIR-IIb ratiometric H2S imaging enabled by absorption-competition induced emission

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 23a, the absorption peaks of IR-820-(COOH)2 and DSNPs overlap at 808 nm, whereas only DSNPs exhibit apparent absorption at 980 nm. Under 808 nm excitation, competitive absorption by IR-820-(COOH)2 modulates the Er3+ emission intensity at 1550 nm, which serves as the response signal. In contrast, the stable Er3+ emission intensity at 1550 nm under 980 nm excitation is used as the reference signal. Absorption spectroscopy of DSNPs@PEI-820 before and after H2S exposure confirmed the proposed mechanism. Upon exposure to H2S, the characteristic competing absorption of IR-820-(COOH)2 at 808 nm was eliminated, thereby revealing the intrinsic Nd3+ absorption at this wavelength, while the Yb3+ absorption at 980 nm remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 23b). This strategy enables monitoring of H2S dynamics via ratiometric fluorescence imaging in the NIR-IIb window47. Emission spectroscopy revealed a positive linear correlation between the 1550 nm emission intensity of DSNPs@PEI-820 and S2− concentrations under 808 nm laser excitation, indicating the expected “turn-on” characteristic (Supplementary Fig. 24). In contrast, the emission peak intensity at 1550 nm under 980 nm excitation remained constant. Time-dependent monitoring revealed that the ratiometric fluorescence of DSNPs@PEI-820 gradually increased upon exposure to H2S and eventually reached a plateau (Supplementary Fig. 25).

To further validate the feasibility of ratiometric fluorescence imaging, we imaged DSNPs@PEI-820 after co-incubation with varying concentrations of H2S using an indium gallium arsenide (InGaAs) imaging system. As shown in Fig. 3h, under 808 nm excitation, the NIR-IIb fluorescence intensity of the reaction system gradually increased with H2S concentration, whereas the signal under 980 nm excitation remained constant. Consequently, the calculated ratiometric signal increased accordingly. Statistical analysis of the image signal intensity showed a linear correlation between the ratiometric fluorescence intensity of DSNPs@PEI-820 and increasing S2− concentrations, with a calculated limit of detection (LOD) of 0.28 μM (Fig. 3i). To evaluate operational stability, repeated measurements under a constant H2S concentration were conducted over 21 days. Both the individual emission channels and the resulting I808/I980 ratio showed minimal fluctuations (<5%), indicating the reliable reproducibility of the DSNPs@PEI-820 sensing system (Supplementary Fig. 26).

NIR-IIb window-emissive nanoprobes for enhanced leaf tissue imaging

Distinct from light–tissue interactions in animal models, optical imaging of plant leaves remains challenging due to their highly heterogeneous microarchitecture and strong tissue self-absorption (Supplementary Fig. 27). Unlike scattering caused by subcellular organelles, lipid membranes, and aqueous matrices in animal tissues, the abundant intercellular air spaces in plant mesophyll generate pronounced refractive index heterogeneity. This heterogeneity induces strong multiple scattering at multiphase interfaces, resulting in pronounced signal attenuation and spatial resolution loss in the visible and NIR-I regions48–50. Furthermore, endogenous pigments such as chlorophyll and carotenoids exhibit strong UV–visible absorption and intrinsic autofluorescence, and these effects are more pronounced in plants than in animal tissues, potentially overlapping with conventional probe emission. This overlap leads to signal attenuation and increased background interference (Supplementary Figs. 28, 29a and Supplementary Note 7)20,25,48. Compared with conventional UV–visible and NIR-I fluorescent probes, the NIR-IIb optical window provides an advantageous window for high-resolution plant imaging. This spectral range markedly mitigates light scattering within heterogeneous tissues and effectively circumvents the strong absorption bands of endogenous pigments (Supplementary Figs. 29b and 30)20,48. In addition, operation at 1550 nm within the NIR-IIb window exploits the intrinsic water absorption profile (1450 nm) to preferentially attenuate multiply scattered photons. These photons travel longer optical paths than ballistic photons, making them more susceptible to water-mediated attenuation51. This water-mediated suppression effectively enhances the SBR in bioimaging (Supplementary Fig. 31).

To investigate the tissue penetration capability of the NIR-IIb window, a homogenized lettuce leaf tissue model with varying depths was employed to compare DSNPs@PEI-820 with visible (Rhodamine B, Cy3, Cy5) and NIR-I (Cy7.5, IR-820-(COOH)2) probes (Fig. 4a). Quantitative assessment via Gaussian fitting revealed that visible and NIR-I probes exhibited rapid and moderate SBR degradation, respectively, with increasing tissue thickness. In contrast, the NIR-IIb channel maintained a high SBR (47.75) even under substantial attenuation (Fig. 4b)47,52,53. FWHM analysis revealed the improved spatial resolution of the NIR-IIb probe, which consistently yielded narrower fluorescence profiles across all tested depths. To further extend these findings to intact plant systems, we assessed the in situ imaging performance of NIR-IIb probes in living leaves with different microstructural features and thicknesses (Supplementary Fig. 32, 33 and Supplementary Note 8). In intact leaves, visible and NIR-I signals exhibited pronounced scattering-induced distortion. In contrast, the 1550 nm NIR-IIb emission maintained high spatial fidelity and achieved a higher SBR than visible and NIR-I probes, as well as conventional 1000–1350 nm NIR-II nanoprobes54. This improved in situ imaging performance can be primarily attributed to reduced photon scattering at longer wavelengths, combined with intrinsic water absorption in the NIR-IIb window, which suppresses background signals and enhances image contrast. Spectral profiling of major autofluorescence sources showed that strong foliar background under UV-visible excitation substantially interfered with conventional probe signals. Conversely, this interference was greatly reduced under 808 nm or 980 nm NIR excitation, preserving the NIR-IIb signal emission window (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 34, and Supplementary Data 1). Nanoprobe localization and in situ visualization of exogenous H2S in leaf tissues

Plant leaves serve as primary sites for early stress responses and provide a planar geometry suitable for macroscopic optical imaging. To non-destructively monitor in situ signaling within these organs, we employed needle-free injection of NIR-IIb activatable nanoprobes. Leaves subjected to mild-pressure injection exhibited morphological recovery within 1 h, confirming the low invasiveness of the method (Supplementary Fig. 35). This needle-free infiltration induced only transient physiological signal fluctuations that spontaneously recovered within a short period. The signal from DSNPs@PEI-820 remained stable throughout the process, minimizing potential operational artifacts (Supplementary Figs. 36, 37 and Supplementary Note 9). Furthermore, microscopic evaluations indicated that stomata on the lower epidermis serve as major entry sites, as their relatively larger apertures in lettuce, spinach, pak choi, and tobacco facilitate the penetration of DSNPs@PEI-820 (Supplementary Fig. 38). Following entry, open stomata allowed the pressure-driven delivery of nanoparticles into the intercellular spaces of leaf tissues (Fig. 4d). Correspondingly, bright fluorescence was observed throughout the infiltrated interstitial spaces, indicating that the nanoprobes accessed the apoplastic compartment (Fig. 4e)23,55. Once localized, vascular bundles acted as selective diffusion barriers owing to their lignified cell walls, thus leading to vascular vein-confined infiltration patterns (Supplementary Fig. 39a)56. Continuous monitoring over 60 h confirmed that the injected nanoprobes remained predominantly localized, without detectable systemic redistribution through metabolic or vascular pathways (Supplementary Fig. 39b, c). Further investigation showed that, although nanoprobe delivery to root tissues via solution absorption was feasible, their upward translocation to aerial organs was limited by the Casparian strip, which acts as a filtration barrier in the root endodermis (Supplementary Fig. 40)57. Therefore, in situ leaf injection was selected over root absorption to enable rapid deployment and accurate local dosage control.

To evaluate in situ activation of DSNPs@PEI-820 for H2S detection, nanoprobes and exogenous H2S were introduced into lettuce leaves, and the response was monitored using a customized imaging system equipped with dual-wavelength excitation and an InGaAs camera (Supplementary Fig. 41). Under 808/980 nm excitation, the ratiometric signal (I808/I980) gradually increased in the H2S-treated group but remained constant in controls, eventually reaching a plateau indicative of reaction saturation (Fig. 4f, g). Compared with conventional multi-wavelength emission ratiometric nanoprobes, the single-wavelength emission design minimizes wavelength-dependent differential attenuation (Supplementary Fig. 42a). In planta calibration curves in lettuce and tobacco leaves showed that DSNPs@PEI-820 maintained a comparable linear response across a range of exogenously applied H2S concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 42b–d). Furthermore, the signal readout of DSNPs@PEI-820 remained stable against variations in excitation light intensity (Supplementary Fig. 43). Even under relatively high excitation power, the nanoprobe maintained a stable ratiometric signal. Furthermore, ROS at physiologically relevant levels in plants did not induce nonspecific responses from DSNPs@PEI-820, reducing potential interference from this major biological background component (Supplementary Fig. 44). Together, these results highlight the role of the nanostructured design in enabling reliable and high-resolution H2S imaging in complex plant tissue environments.

In situ imaging of Cd2+-induced H2S dynamics in plant tissues

Fluorescent probe-based NIR-II imaging enables non-destructive and in situ monitoring of plant stress with high spatial resolution, owing to deep tissue penetration and low autofluorescence12,23,25,58. NIR-II imaging enables monitoring of H2S-associated redox dynamics during abiotic stress, in which H2S functions as an endogenous signaling molecule involved in mitigating ROS accumulation caused by stress-induced metabolic disturbances (Supplementary Fig. 45)13,23,55. Thus, we used NIR-IIb imaging with DSNPs@PEI-820 to monitor abiotic stress responses by dynamically tracking endogenous H2S fluctuations in stressed plants.

Cadmium ions (Cd2+) are major environmental contaminants that enter plant tissues through various pathways, inducing oxidative stress and triggering H2S-associated signaling responses37,59. Using hydroponically grown lettuce under Cd2+ stress as a model system, we performed in situ imaging of endogenous H2S with DSNPs@PEI-820. The results showed that DSNPs@PEI-820 delivered to plant tissues via either leaf infiltration or root uptake enabled visualization of Cd2+-induced H2S accumulation, with the ratiometric fluorescence signal progressively increasing over time and reaching a plateau (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 46). Because H2S functions as a gasotransmitter capable of membrane diffusion, it exhibits both intracellular and extracellular distributions under Cd2+ stress (Supplementary Fig. 47)60,61. To evaluate this spatial distribution, semi-quantitative analysis of H2S signal intensity was performed in intracellular and adjacent extracellular regions under varying Cd2+ stress intensities (Supplementary Fig. 48). Across the observed range of endogenous H2S changes in tissues, extracellular H2S fluctuations exhibited a synchronized correlation with intracellular H2S dynamics. Biochemical analyses were performed to elucidate the relationships among Cd2+ stress, endogenous H2S dynamics, and redox homeostasis by characterizing changes in the plant antioxidant defense system. H2S, primarily produced from L-Cys through L-Cys desulfhydrase (L-CDes)-mediated desulfhydration, serves as an important signaling molecule involved in regulating plant responses to oxidative stress (Fig. 5c)35,60,62. Under Cd2+ stress, elevated H2S levels in leaf tissues were accompanied by increased H2O2 accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 49)62,63. Consistently, Cd2+ treatment enhanced L-CDes activity, indicating that stress-induced H2S production is enzymatically regulated in response to oxidative stress (Fig. 5d, e). Meanwhile, increased H2O2 levels promoted membrane lipid peroxidation, resulting in elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) content as a marker of oxidative damage62. To evaluate antioxidant defense responses associated with H2S accumulation, we further analyzed enzymatic antioxidant systems9,10,36. Following Cd2+ stress, the activities of major antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD), increased by 1.22- to 2.17-fold compared with controls, indicating enhanced enzymatic ROS scavenging capacity (Fig. 5d, e)10,37,62. In addition, the levels of GSH and ascorbic acid (AsA), together with the activity of ascorbate peroxidase (APX), which are involved in ROS removal through the GSH-AsA cycle, were significantly increased under oxidative stress36,37,63. Together, these results demonstrate the coordinated activation of H2S-associated antioxidant defenses, contributing to the regulation of redox homeostasis in stressed plant tissues37.

To evaluate the specificity of DSNPs@PEI-820 toward endogenous H2S under Cd2+ stress, chemical interventions were employed to validate the signal reliability. At the tissue level, both the H2S scavenger HT and the biosynthesis inhibitor hydroxylamine (HA) suppressed Cd2+-induced fluorescence enhancement (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 50a, b). Mechanistically, HT treatment diminished the detected signals by directly scavenging H2S37,64. In contrast, HA treatment caused a concentration-dependent decrease in intracellular L-CDes activity, thereby reducing endogenous H2S accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 50c–e). Consequently, the fluorescence signals of DSNPs@PEI-820 in the apoplastic space decreased linearly with the reduction in L-CDes activity. To genetically validate these findings, we further assessed the in planta specificity of DSNPs@PEI-820 using Arabidopsis thaliana lcd/des1 double mutants lacking both L-CDes and D-cysteine desulfhydrase (DES1) (Supplementary Fig. 51). Under Cd2+ stress, wild-type plants exhibited pronounced NIR-IIb turn-on signals, whereas neither unstressed wild-type plants nor lcd/des1 mutants displayed appreciable fluorescence enhancement. However, injection of exogenous H2S into unstressed lcd/des1 mutant leaves also triggered an increase in DSNPs@PEI-820 signals. This genetic evidence indicates that the observed fluorescence enhancement is closely associated with stress-induced enzymatic H2S accumulation, supporting DSNPs@PEI-820 as a specific reporter for monitoring H2S dynamics during plant stress responses. To further assess the robustness of DSNPs@PEI-820 under dynamic redox conditions, we evaluated nanoprobe stability after H2S activation during subsequent ROS exposure. Both in vitro assays and in planta validation using the lcd/des1 mutant showed that the H2S-activated nanoprobe remained unaffected by subsequent exposure to ROS (H2O2 or 1O2) (Supplementary Fig. 52). Furthermore, the nanoprobe kinetics were consistent with the gradual accumulation trend of stress-induced H2S, enabling monitoring of early physiological dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 53 and Supplementary Note 10). To support prolonged monitoring, DSNPs@PEI-820 showed long-term stability, maintaining consistent sensitivity without detectable nonspecific activation over 15 days in both simulated plant matrices and in vivo pre-infiltration models (Supplementary Figs. 54 and 55a–c). Following activation, the stress-induced fluorescence signal remained stable in planta for an additional 15 days (Supplementary Fig. 55d, e).

In vivo monitoring of abiotic stress through H2S signaling dynamics in plant oxidative responses

The role of H2S in regulating antioxidant defense is conserved across diverse plant species, enabling dynamic modulation of redox homeostasis under various abiotic stresses9,10,36,58,62. In this study, we selected hydroponically cultivated lettuce and soil-grown spinach, pak choi, and tobacco as model systems to explore the response characteristics of DSNPs@PEI-820 to endogenous H2S under various abiotic stressors (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 56). In response to mechanical wounding and heat stress, the nanoprobe injection sites exhibited time-dependent increases in ratiometric fluorescence (Fig. 6b, c). Extended in planta monitoring across multiple stress conditions revealed characteristic kinetic profiles consisting of an initial rising phase followed by a sustained plateau (Supplementary Fig. 57a)45. Subsequent extraction of multiparametric kinetic indicators revealed distinct kinetic fingerprints associated with different abiotic stresses in lettuce leaves (Supplementary Fig. 57b and Supplementary Table 1). These variations may arise from differences in stress mechanisms, progression patterns, and L-CDes-associated antioxidant responses, suggesting that these fingerprints could provide additional information for distinguishing plant stress states. Figure 6d and Supplementary Figs. 58–60 show the time-dependent fluorescence enhancement at the probe injection sites in spinach, pak choi, and tobacco under various abiotic stresses. The ratiometric fluorescence intensity after stress treatment was significantly higher than that in corresponding controls. Physiobiochemical analyses across multiple plant species highlight the potential of DSNPs@PEI-820 as a broadly applicable tool for abiotic stress monitoring. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 61 and 62 and Supplementary Note 11, mechanical wounding and heat shock induced oxidative stress in leaf tissues, similar to that observed under Cd2+ exposure, resulting in elevated ROS levels. This oxidative cascade caused membrane damage and compromised selective permeability, which may contribute to programmed cell death (Supplementary Fig. 63 and Supplementary Note 12). These cellular alterations were accompanied by phenotypic changes, including variations in chlorophyll and nitrogen contents (Supplementary Fig. 64). To maintain cellular homeostasis, proline, a crucial osmolyte, accumulated significantly during stress responses. Concurrently, plants exhibited increased antioxidant enzyme activities and elevated levels of endogenous antioxidants, contributing to enhanced total antioxidant capacity (TAOC) and mitigation of oxidative damage (Supplementary Figs. 65, 66 and Supplementary Note 13). Simultaneously, the level of the endogenous signaling molecule H2S increased significantly, potentially contributing to stress adaptation by modulating oxidative stress regulatory networks (Supplementary Fig. 67 and Supplementary Note 14). The conserved role of H2S-associated oxidative defense across plant species supports the development of cross-species analytical tools. Leveraging this conserved signaling feature, DSNPs@PEI-820 provides a potential strategy for monitoring crop stress responses. Its nucleophilic specificity enables dynamic visualization of endogenous H2S changes across different plant species and stress conditions.

DSNPs@PEI-820 also showed good biocompatibility in plant tissues. As shown in Fig. 6e, f, Evans blue and DAPI/PI staining revealed negligible effects on membrane integrity and cell viability, with no apparent cell death induced by nanoprobe treatment14. In addition, needle-free injection did not cause significant changes in chlorophyll and nitrogen contents compared with the control group (Fig. 6g). These results indicate that DSNPs@PEI-820 maintains plant tissue integrity at imaging doses, supporting its application for long-term physiological monitoring in living plants.

Discussion

In situ visualization of stress-induced oxidative responses in plants through leaf optical imaging provides direct insights into plant stress responses. Compared with conventional monitoring of leaf physicochemical parameters, such as temperature, humidity, and chlorophyll content, molecular imaging approaches provide enhanced sensitivity for early stress assessment13,17. However, conventional UV-visible imaging of plants is limited by chloroplast-derived autofluorescence and the complex three-dimensional architecture of leaf tissues20,65. Strong photon absorption and scattering at these wavelengths substantially compromise imaging resolution and sensing reliability in plant tissues. Here, we establish an NIR-IIb ratiometric imaging platform based on ACIE-driven DSNPs@PEI-820 nanoprobes for in planta stress sensing. The NIR-IIb emission window minimizes interference from plant autofluorescence while improving signal penetration and imaging resolution. The nanoprobe design provides selective H2S recognition over other biothiols with a low LOD. Needle-free infiltration enables stomatal delivery of nanoprobes, allowing visualization of H2S dynamics under diverse stresses across plant species. Supported by long-term stability and functional durability, pre-infiltrated sensors may enable in situ tracking of early stress-associated molecular events under acute environmental challenges or chronic heavy metal exposure13,58. This capability enables early indication of stress responses before visible phenotypic symptoms emerge. Thus, this strategy provides an opportunity to move from post-phenotypic assessment toward earlier molecular-level monitoring, supporting more timely and precise agricultural management.

Nonetheless, the reliance on InGaAs-based detectors currently limits the broader application of NIR-II imaging. Although effective, these systems are substantially more expensive and require stricter cooling conditions than silicon-based cameras25. These limitations hinder further miniaturization and field deployment. Recent advances in InGaAs detector technology are facilitating device miniaturization. Compact thermoelectric cooling (TEC) modules have reduced the size and cost of InGaAs-based detection systems66,67. Meanwhile, high-brightness NIR-II nanoprobes can compensate for increased background noise in moderately cooled detectors by providing high SBR. This feature reduces the requirement for extreme cooling performance of detectors68,69. Therefore, combining compact TEC-cooled InGaAs cameras with high-brightness NIR-II nanoprobes represents a feasible strategy for developing miniaturized and portable imaging platforms. Such systems may facilitate future field deployment for early monitoring of plant stress responses.

Moreover, although key stress defense networks are conserved across plant species, substantial phenotypic variability among individual plants can affect the accuracy and reproducibility of stress monitoring. Therefore, accurate optical signal calibration is essential for reliable stress assessment17. Beyond signal calibration, current nanoprobes generally provide limited physiological information, restricting the ability to distinguish stress states and underlying mechanisms. Future research should therefore focus on developing multifunctional nanoprobes integrated with signal deconvolution strategies13,58. By simultaneously monitoring multiple redox-associated signals, such approaches may enable comprehensive analysis of stress-related networks and better understand the spatiotemporal regulation of plant stress responses.

Beyond nanoprobe development, integrating optimized machine learning or deep learning frameworks for analyzing abiotic stress-associated imaging data represents a promising direction. Such approaches may identify imaging patterns associated with different oxidative stress states and antioxidant responses across plant species and stress conditions58. Classification of stress conditions based on nanoprobe response dynamics may provide new opportunities for understanding stress-response mechanisms and developing early diagnostic strategies23. Furthermore, integrating these analytical approaches with Internet of Things (IoT)-based platforms may facilitate their translation toward field applications. Supported by embedded microcomputing platforms, such as Raspberry Pi-based systems, these platforms could enable on-site acquisition, processing, and transmission of crop stress signals. Such integration may improve the efficiency and timeliness of physiological data analysis13,17,18. Together, these advances could support earlier stress assessment and more informed agricultural management.

Methods

Materials and characterization

Detailed information on materials, reagents, and characterization procedures is provided in Supplementary Method 1.

Fabrication of NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce nanocrystals

The aqueous solution of rare earth metal precursors (0.4 mM, Gd: Yb: Er: Ce = 0.73: 0.2: 0.02: 0.05) was mixed with 1-octadecene (ODE) (6 mL) and oleic acid (OA) (4 mL) and stirred at room temperature to ensure thorough homogenization. Then, the mixture was heated to 130–150 °C to completely remove water. After cooling to room temperature, a methanol solution of ammonium fluoride (NH4F) (0.4 M, 3.3 mL) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (0.5 M, 2 mL) was rapidly added to the mixture, which was continuously stirred at 45–50 °C to facilitate nucleation. Subsequently, the nucleated solution was gradually heated to a gentle boil to remove methanol. N2 was introduced under vacuum to ensure the complete removal of methanol residues. The system was then rapidly heated to 300 °C for 1.5 h. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation (7100 × g, 10 min) and washed with ethanol three times, yielding the NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce nanocrystal core31,33.

Fabrication of NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce@NaGdF4:Yb,Nd core–shell nanostructures

Aqueous solutions of rare earth metal precursors (0.4 mmol, Gd: Yb: Nd = 0.7: 0.1: 0.2) were mixed with ODE (6 mL) and OA (4 mL). The mixture was stirred at room temperature to ensure thorough homogenization and then heated to remove water. After cooling to room temperature, NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce nanocrystal cores, as well as a methanol solution of NH4F (0.4 M, 3.3 mL) and NaOH (0.5 M, 2 mL), were rapidly added to the mixture. The resulting solution was continuously stirred at 45–50 °C to facilitate nucleation. Subsequently, the mixture was heated thoroughly to remove methanol, and N2 was introduced under vacuum to ensure the complete removal of methanol residues. The system was then rapidly heated to 300 °C for 1.5 h. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation (7100 × g, 10 min) and washed with ethanol three times, yielding NaGdF4:Yb,Er,Ce@NaGdF4:Yb,Nd core–shell structured nanocrystals.

Fabrication of DSNPs@PEI-820

Detailed procedures for the synthesis of nanocomposites and surface functionalization are described in Supplementary Method 2.

Nanoprobe specificity evaluation

The specificity of DSNPs@PEI-820 toward H2S was evaluated against potential interfering substances in plant leaf matrices. Detailed procedures for the anti-interference experiments are provided in Supplementary Method 3.

Plant cultivation and physiological measurements

In this study, seeds of spinach (Spinacia oleracea), pak choi (Brassica rapa subsp. Chinensis), tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana), and Arabidopsis thaliana were sterilized and sown in seedling trays measuring 65 × 65 mm, which were filled with peat substrate. Once the seedlings grew two fully expanded leaves, they were transplanted into pots containing nutrient-rich soil. For the lettuce (Lactuca sativa L. var. ramosa Hort.), a hydroponic system containing modified Hoagland nutrient solution was used. All plants were cultivated in an artificial climate chamber with the following parameters: light intensity of 180 μmol m2 s−1, a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, a temperature regime of 22 °C during the day and 18 °C at night, and a relative humidity of 65%. The plants were regularly irrigated with deionized water and nutrient solution, adjusted according to their growth conditions and substrate moisture levels. Additionally, the plants were routinely monitored for signs of disease.

Plant physiological and biochemical parameters, including H2O2, H2S, proline, AsA, MDA, TAOC, REC, chlorophyll and nitrogen contents, as well as enzyme activities, were determined according to established protocols. Detailed procedures for these measurements are provided in Supplementary Method 4.

In vivo NIR-II imaging studies in plants

A total of 0.1 mmol of nanoprobes was homogeneously dispersed in MES buffer solution. Subsequently, 10 µL of the nanoprobes was infiltrated into the leaf using a 1 mL needleless syringe. During the infiltration process, gentle pressure was applied to the leaf to facilitate penetration while minimizing mechanical damage. The leaf surface was then thoroughly rinsed with water to remove any residual probes. Following nanoprobe infiltration, a 1-h stabilization period was implemented before imaging or stress application to eliminate baseline interference caused by the injection process.

For macroscopic imaging, live plant leaves infiltrated with nanoprobes were positioned for NIR-IIb imaging using a system equipped with a 1400 nm long-pass (LP) filter (Thorlabs, FEL 1400) and a 2D InGaAs detector (640 × 512 pixels). NIR-IIb fluorescence emission from intratissue nanoprobes was recorded under sequential excitation with 808 nm and 980 nm lasers using LightField imaging software. For microscopic imaging, nanoprobe infiltration sites on plant leaves were mounted on microscopy slides. The spatial distribution of probe fluorescence in tissue sections was visualized using a customized NIR fluorescence microscope equipped with a 1400 nm LP filter under excitation from 808 nm and 980 nm lasers. Simultaneously, NIR-IIb signals within the microstructure were analyzed using LightField imaging software.

Assessment of the penetration depth of fluorescence in plant tissues

Lettuce leaves were thoroughly crushed using a plant grinder and ultrasonic cell disruptor to prepare a leaf tissue homogenate. Glass capillaries containing IR-820-(COOH)2 or DSNPs were placed in Petri dishes with varying volumes of the simulated tissue homogenate. Fluorescence images of the glass capillaries were acquired using an InGaAs imaging system, employing 808 nm and 980 nm lasers as excitation sources. Fluorescence parameters for different biowindows were calculated based on data obtained from the same region of interest (ROI) at various penetration depths.

In situ sensing of stress-induced H2S production in living plants

For the mechanical wound treatment, incubated leaves from live plants were positioned in front of the InGaAs imaging system, which was equipped with a 1400 nm LP filter. Mechanical wounds were created on the leaf surface using a sterilized rigid metal array needle (approximately 280 μm in diameter), with the puncture site located approximately 1.0 cm from the center of the probe infiltration area and the depth controlled at 30 μm. To induce heat stress, a metal rod was preheated in a water bath maintained at 60 °C until it reached thermal equilibrium. The preheated metal rod was then brought into contact with the upper surface of the plant leaf for 30 s to apply a heat shock. For Cd2+ stress treatment, plants were first transferred to Hoagland nutrient solution and pre-adapted for 24 h, with an initial 12 h period to minimize background interference from transplantation. Subsequently, CdCl2 solution was added to the Hoagland nutrient solution to achieve a final concentration of 2.5 mM, followed by 12 h of stress exposure before imaging. Detailed protocols for nanoprobe delivery, in planta imaging, tissue distribution analysis, and biological validation of H2S-responsive imaging are described in Supplementary Method 5.

Histochemical analysis of plant tissues

Detailed procedures for histochemical and fluorescence staining of plant leaf tissues are provided in Supplementary Method 6.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

DFT calculations used to investigate the molecular recognition mechanism were performed according to the procedures described in Supplementary Method 7.

Statistics and reproducibility

In this study, all data were obtained from a minimum of five independent replicate experiments and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). All experiments involving plant samples were performed with a minimum of five biological replicates. For microscopy and imaging data, representative images from at least five independent biological replicates are shown, with similar results observed across all replicates. Data analysis was conducted using Minitab 18.0 software. A one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, was used to analyze differences between groups. The significance levels were indicated as follows: n.s., not significant, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.
