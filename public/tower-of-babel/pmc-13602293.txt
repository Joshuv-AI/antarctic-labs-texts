Greater Motor and Nonmotor Burden at Diagnosis Is Associated With Amyloid Copathology in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Han Kyu Na, Yeeun Sun, Chan Wook Park, Jung Hyun Lee, Yun Young Choi, Quynh Phuong Vo, Seok Jong Chung, Han Soo Yoo, Chul Hyoung Lyoo, Young H. Sohn, Jin Yong Hong, Phil Hyu Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConcurrent Alzheimer's disease pathology is increasingly recognized as a poor prognostic factor in Parkinson's disease (PD), yet reliable clinical indicators for early identification of AD copathology remain poorly established.ObjectiveTo investigate baseline motor and nonmotor symptom profiles associated with amyloid‐β (Aβ) copathology in newly diagnosed PD.MethodsAmong patients with PD who underwent Aβ imaging, we retrospectively identified 152 patients who completed the Cross‐Cultural Smell Identification Test (CC‐SIT), autonomic function tests, neuropsychological assessment, and Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q) at drug‐naive state. Predictors of Aβ positivity were identified using stepwise multivariable logistic regression and validated with Random Forest classifiers employing Boruta feature selection.ResultsCompared with Aβ‐negative counterparts (n = 93), Aβ‐positive (Aβ + PD, n = 59) patients demonstrated greater olfactory dysfunction (CC‐SIT, P = 0.002), dysautonomia (Composite Autonomic Severity Scale [CASS], P < 0.001), and mood disturbance (NPI‐Q‐mood, P < 0.001) and higher prevalence of probable rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (P = 0.009) and neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (P = 0.006). Aβ + PD patients showed greater motor disability (Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III [UPDRS‐III], P < 0.001) despite comparable striatal dopamine transporter uptake. Logistic regression identified lower CC‐SIT scores (odds ratio [OR] = 0.821, 95% confidence interval [CI95%]: 0.703–0.959), higher CASS scores (OR = 1.380, CI95%: 1.144–1.666), higher NPI‐Q‐mood scores (OR = 1.057, CI95%: 1.005–1.111), higher UPDRS‐III (OR = 1.080, CI95%: 1.030–1.132), and APOE ε4 carrier status (OR = 3.643, CI95%: 1.479–8.969) as independent predictors of Aβ positivity, which were also confirmed as important variables by Boruta feature selection.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that greater motor and nonmotor symptom burden at diagnosis, characterized by olfactory/autonomic dysfunction, mood disturbance, and motor deficits disproportionate to dopaminergic denervation, was associated with Aβ positivity in PD. © 2026 The Author(s). Parkinson's disease (PD) is pathologically characterized by aggregates of intraneuronal α‐synuclein in dopaminergic neurons of the midbrain. 1 α‐Synuclein has a unique feature of cell‐to‐cell transmission, leading to aggregation in multiple organs across brain and peripheral systems. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Consequently, along with classic parkinsonian motor symptoms, patients with PD present with a wide variety of nonmotor symptoms (NMSs), including dysautonomia, hyposmia, rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD), cognitive impairments, or neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPSs). 2 , 3 , 4 The phenotype of PD exhibits considerable heterogeneity in both motor and nonmotor manifestations, with this clinical diversity supported by functional and structural imaging markers, pathological variability in α‐synuclein distribution, and genetic heterogeneity. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 However, the underlying mechanism for PD heterogeneity remains poorly understood.

Notably, autopsy studies frequently show pathologies coexisting with α‐synucleinopathy in PD. 6 , 7 , 8 Among these, Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology has been identified as one of the most frequent copathologies, observed in approximately 30% of PD cases at autopsy, with its coexistence increasing up to 50% in patients with PD dementia (PDD). 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 AD copathology has been suggested to adversely influence clinical trajectory in PD, with affected patients demonstrating faster cognitive deterioration, earlier dementia conversion, and increased mortality risk compared with those without. 7 , 8 Moreover, we recently demonstrated that higher amyloid‐β (Aβ) burden in PD had poorer motor prognosis in terms of dyskinesia or higher dopaminergic medication dose requirements. 10 These findings suggest that AD copathology may be associated with a more malignant PD subtype with respect to both motor and cognitive trajectories. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 However, existing data are largely restricted to advanced‐stage patients, and currently, there are no clinical markers that are capable of reliably predicting the presence or subsequent development of AD copathology in early‐stage PD.

Emerging evidence suggests NMS profiles could provide critical insight into the phenotypic variance of PD, 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 capturing differences in the timing, severity, and composition of symptoms not reflected in motor assessments alone. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Moreover, Aβ proteins have been reported to facilitate abnormal α‐synuclein aggregation through cross‐seeding effects, 19 , 20 , 21 potentially altering synuclein spreading patterns 22 , 23 , 24 and thereby contributing to distinct NMS constellations in Aβ‐positive PD (Aβ + PD). Accordingly, characterizing Aβ‐associated motor and NMS profiles may facilitate early identification of patients with PD at elevated risk for AD copathology.

In this study, we investigated potential clinical profiles associated with Aβ positivity in patients with PD who completed motor and NMS assessments at diagnosis, before initiation of antiparkinsonian therapy. We hypothesized that Aβ + PD patients would exhibit disproportionately severe NMS and motor deficits beyond those attributable to nigrostriatal degeneration alone, compared with Aβ‐negative PD (Aβ − PD).

Subjects and Methods

Study Participants

Because this study aimed to investigate the baseline clinical profiles associated with Aβ copathology, we retrospectively identified patients with PD who had completed motor/nonmotor assessments in their drug‐naive state and had undergone Aβ imaging with 18F‐Florbetaben (18F‐FBB) positron emission tomography (PET). Notably, Aβ imaging was not performed systematically at diagnosis. It was obtained either through enrollment in a cohort study or, on a clinical basis, in patients with a relatively rapid or atypical cognitive trajectory that raised concern for coexistent AD pathology.

Specifically, patients with PD underwent Aβ imaging between July 2016 and February 2025; those who met the following criteria were included: (1) clinical diagnosis of PD according to the United Kingdom PD Society Brain Bank diagnostic criteria; (2) reduced dopamine transporter (DAT) uptake in the posterior putamen on 18F‐N‐(3‐fluoropropyl)‐2β‐carbon ethoxy‐3β‐(4‐iodophenyl) nortropane (18F‐FP‐CIT) PET; (3) completion of NMS assessments including Cross‐Cultural Smell Identification Test (CC‐SIT), a comprehensive battery of autonomic function tests, neuropsychological assessments, the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q), and the RBD Screening Questionnaire (RBDSQ) at diagnosis before initiation of antiparkinsonian medications; and (4) available apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotyping. We excluded patients with (1) other neurologic disorders, (2) unexplained structural brain lesions on magnetic resonance imaging, (3) features suggestive of atypical parkinsonism, and (4) conditions affecting olfactory (eg, sinusitis) or autonomic function (eg, heart failure or arrhythmia). 17

A total of 152 patients met these criteria and were included in the final analysis. Of these, 57 (37.5%) had undergone Aβ imaging prompted by clinical suspicion for a relatively rapid cognitive trajectory raising concern for potential AD copathology, whereas the remaining 95 (62.5%) had been imaged as part of the cohort study.

Motor subtypes (tremor‐dominant, postural instability/gait difficulty [PIGD], or indeterminate) were determined based on tremor‐to‐PIGD score ratio derived from the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS). 25 The severity of motor symptoms was assessed using the UPDRS Part III (UPDRS‐III) before antiparkinsonian therapy initiation.

The institutional review board of Yonsei University Severance Hospital approved this study (4‐2024‐1391), and informed consent was waived because of its retrospective nature.

Assessment of NMSs

Olfactory function was assessed using the CC‐SIT, a standardized 12‐item odor identification test. 26 Based on the total CC‐SIT scores, participants were classified as anosmia (0–4), hyposmia (5–8), and normosmia (9–12).

Autonomic dysfunction was evaluated using a comprehensive battery of autonomic function tests, 27 from which the Composite Autonomic Severity Scale (CASS) total scores (0–10) and their subscores (sudomotor, 0–3; cardiovagal, 0–3; adrenergic, 0–4) were derived. 27 Orthostatic hypotension (OH) and neurogenic OH were defined based on blood pressure (BP) and heart rate responses during the head‐tilt test (Supporting Information Method S1). 28

The presence of RBD was assessed using the RBDSQ, with RBDSQ score ≥ 6 defined as probable RBD (pRBD). 29

Cognitive status and performance were assessed using the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery (Supporting Information Method S2). 30 The NPS burden was assessed using the Korean version of the NPI‐Q. 31 The 12 items of the NPI‐Q were classified into three major subdomains: mood, hyperactivity, and psychosis (Supporting Information Method S3). 32

Neuroimaging Analysis

Protocols for acquisition, processing, and quantitative analyses of PET images are described in Supporting Information Method S4. Standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) of 18F‐FP‐CIT and 18F‐FBB PET images was estimated using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK) and in‐house software, implemented in MATLAB R2021a (MathWorks, Natick, MA). 33 , 34 , 35 Aβ positivity was defined by visual assessment of delayed‐phase 18F‐FBB PET scans rather than a prespecified quantitative threshold, given the lack of a universally accepted cutoff in the PD population. The asymmetry index (AI) and anteroposterior (AP) gradient for striatal DAT availability were calculated as described previously. 33 White matter hyperintensities (WMHs), including deep WMH (DWMH) and periventricular WMH (PWMH), were rated according to the modified Fazekas scale (Supporting Information Method S5). 36

Statistical Analyses

Demographic/clinical characteristics by Aβ status were compared using the t test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. In comparing imaging factors (subregional DAT availability along with AI or AP gradient), motor symptom burden (UPDRS‐III), and quantifiable NMS features (CC‐SIT score, CASS score, 3‐minute BP change on head‐up tilt test, cognitive performance, and NPS burden) by Aβ status, analysis of covariance was performed using age, sex, education years, APOE ε4 carrier status, DWMH/PWMH grade, and cognitive status as covariates.

To investigate whether Aβ status modifies the association between striatal dopaminergic denervation and severity of motor symptoms, we applied a residual‐based motor reserve approach. 37 , 38 , 39 First, separate multivariable linear regression models were fitted within each Aβ group, with UPDRS‐III as the dependent variable and striatal subregional DAT uptake as the independent variable, adjusted for age, sex, education years, APOE ε4 carrier, DWMH/PWMH burden, and Clinical Dementia Rating sum‐of‐boxes (CDR‐SB). Third, the residual distributions between Aβ groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test to determine whether Aβ copathology exerts an additive detrimental effect on motor function independent of nigrostriatal dopaminergic denervation.

To identify clinical features associated with Aβ positivity, we performed analyses in two steps. First, baseline clinical and DAT imaging variables were individually evaluated using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for potential confounders, with Aβ positivity as the dependent variable (Supporting Information Method S6). Second, prediction models were constructed using two complementary approaches: stepwise multivariable logistic regression (model 1) and Random Forest (RF) classification (Supporting Information Method S7). For the RF analysis, a full model incorporating all candidate variables was first constructed (model 2), followed by a refined model retaining only variables confirmed by Boruta‐based feature selection (model 3).

Cox proportional hazards models (adjusting for age, sex, disease duration, APOE ε4 carrier, and baseline cognitive status) were constructed to investigate whether Aβ status exerts an independent effect on the risk of PDD conversion. In this analysis, only nondemented participants with a follow‐up duration of >24 months were included (Supporting Information Method S8). 40 , 41 , 42 Analyses were performed using SPSS (v29.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY) or R software (v4.4.2; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). The P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical Characteristics of Study Participants

Detailed clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. A total of 152 patients with PD were enrolled and stratified by Aβ status into Aβ − PD (n = 93 [61.2%]) and Aβ + PD (n = 59 [38.8%]) groups. The Aβ + PD group was older at symptom onset (P = 0.019) and diagnosis (P = 0.036), with a higher proportion of APOE ε4 carriers (42.4% vs. 23.7%, P = 0.015). The interval from diagnosis to 18F‐FBB PET was 2.09 ± 2.23 years (median: 1.32, interquartile range [IQR] 0.06–3.88), without significant difference across Aβ groups (mean ± standard deviation, 2.13 ± 2.32 vs. 2.04 ± 2.11 years; median: 1.36 [IQR 0.02–3.99] vs. 1.31 [IQR 0.16–3.54]; P = 0.805). No significant between‐group differences were observed in sex distribution, educational attainment, disease duration (the interval from symptom onset to diagnosis), vascular risk factors, or baseline cognitive performance.

NMS Profiles According to Aβ Status

Detailed NMS profiles according to Aβ status are presented in Table 2. At diagnosis, Aβ + PD demonstrated with lower CC‐SIT scores (4.15 ± 2.44 vs. 6.01 ± 2.90; P < 0.001) and higher CASS total scores (4.81 ± 2.49 vs. 3.18 ± 2.18; P < 0.001; Fig. S1), with greater dysfunction in the cardiovagal (1.37 ± 1.11 vs. 0.90 ± 0.96; P = 0.007) and adrenergic domains (2.22 ± 1.49 vs. 1.43 ± 1.34; P = 0.009). In addition, the Aβ + PD group had higher proportions of patients with pRBD (67.8% vs. 46.2%; P = 0.009) and neurogenic OH (47.5% vs. 25.8%; P = 0.006).

Differential Dopamine–Motor Relationship According to Aβ Status

Although no significant difference across Aβ status was observed in terms of DAT imaging factors (subregional DAT availability, AI, and AP gradient), WMH burden, or proportion of motor phenotypes, the Aβ + PD group showed significantly higher UPDRS‐III scores compared with the Aβ − PD group (29.59 ± 10.03 vs. 22.18 ± 8.72; P < 0.001; Table 1).

In regression analyses, putaminal 18F‐FP‐CIT SUVR was significantly associated with UPDRS‐III scores in Aβ − PD (β = −0.461; P < 0.001), whereas the association in Aβ + PD failed to reach statistical significance (β = −0.137, P = 0.365; Fig. 1B). Using the regression model derived from Aβ − PD as the reference for DAT–motor relationship uninfluenced by Aβ copathology, residuals (UPDRS‐IIIObserved − UPDRS‐IIIPredictedAβ−PD) were calculated for each participant (Fig. 1A). The distribution of residuals from the predicted values (UPDRS‐IIIPredictedAβ−PD based on the putaminal DAT availability) was significantly shifted toward positive values in Aβ + PD compared with Aβ − PD (P < 0.001; Fig. 1C), indicating greater‐than‐expected motor severity at a given level of striatal dopamine depletion. Similar findings were observed when using DAT uptake in other striatal subregions (Fig. S2).

Clinical Profiles Associated with Aβ Positivity

Multivariable logistic regression analyses adjusted for age at diagnosis, sex, and disease duration (symptom onset to diagnosis) showed that higher UPDRS‐III, NPI‐Q‐mood, and CASS scores, lower CC‐SIT score, APOE ε4 carrier, and presence of pRBD were individually associated with Aβ positivity (Table S1). These associations were consistently observed in sensitivity analyses using covariates defined at the time of Aβ imaging and in models additionally adjusted for APOE ε4 carrier status (Tables S2 and S3).

Prediction Models for Aβ Positivity

Multivariable logistic regression using stepwise variable selection (model 1) identified five independent predictors of Aβ positivity: lower CC‐SIT score (odds ratio [OR] = 0.821, 95% confidence interval [CI95%]: 0.703–0.959), higher CASS total score (OR = 1.380 [1.144–1.666]), greater mood disturbance (OR = 1.057 [1.005–1.111]), presence of APOE ε4 allele (OR = 3.643 [1.479–8.969]), and higher UPDRS‐III score (OR = 1.080 [1.030–1.132]) (Table 3). Model 1 demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.813 (CI95%: 0.733–0.875).

PDD Conversion During Follow‐up According to Aβ Status

Participants were followed up in outpatient clinics at 3‐ to 6‐month intervals (median: 3.95 [IQR 2.66–7.06] years). For Cox regression analyses, 127 participants (83.6%) without dementia at baseline and who had >2 years of follow‐up (median: 4.89 [IQR 2.98–7.59] years) were included (50/59 Aβ + PD [84.7%] and 77/93 Aβ − PD [82.8%]). The Aβ + PD group had a significantly increased hazard of PDD conversion (hazard ratio [HR]: 3.505 [CI95%: 2.115–5.810]; P < 0.001) compared with the Aβ − PD group (Fig. S3).

Discussion

This study investigated whether patients with PD exhibit differential baseline clinical profiles according to Aβ status. First, Aβ + PD patients demonstrated a substantially greater NMS burden compared with their Aβ − PD counterparts, characterized by pronounced olfactory impairment, dysautonomia, mood disturbance, and more prevalent pRBD. Second, Aβ + PD patients exhibited more severe motor dysfunctions that could not be fully accounted for by striatal dopaminergic depletion, suggesting that Aβ copathology may exert an additive detrimental effect on motor function beyond nigrostriatal degeneration. Third, logistic regression analysis (model 1, AUROC = 0.813) identified lower CC‐SIT score, higher CASS score, greater NPI‐Q‐mood score, motor symptom severity, and APOE ε4 allele carrier as independent predictors associated with Aβ positivity, and their relevance was corroborated by RF classification with Boruta feature selection (model 3, AUROC = 0.807).

With accumulating evidence that NMSs in PD tend to reflect the distribution of α‐synuclein pathology, 13 there has been growing interest in leveraging these NMS constellations to delineate clinical heterogeneity in PD. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 This framework gains further significance in light of emerging evidence that AD copathology may alter the spatiotemporal propagation pattern of synucleinopathy, thereby affecting phenotypic variance of PD. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 22 , 23 , 24 We therefore investigated whether NMS profiles at diagnosis distinguish Aβ + PD patients using multiple prediction models.

Herein, olfactory dysfunction was consistently identified as a robust predictor of Aβ positivity. These findings may be explained by the shared vulnerability of the olfactory system and its limbic/paralimbic projections to both AD and PD pathologies. 16 , 43 , 44 , 45 According to connectome‐based models of protein propagation, olfactory structures such as anterior olfactory nucleus, amygdala, and entorhinal cortex are known to serve as key hubs that interconnect networks preferentially vulnerable to AD (entorhinal‐hippocampal‐cortical) and PD (amygdala‐dorsal motor vagal nucleus). 44 Within these regions, convergent Aβ/tau and α‐synuclein pathologies may not only increase local pathological burden but also facilitate α‐synuclein propagation along AD‐vulnerable networks. 43 , 44 , 45 Supporting this model, autopsy studies have reported that PD brains with AD copathology tend to demonstrate increased Lewy body pathology within the entorhinal‐hippocampal‐cortical circuits and basal forebrain. 43 , 44 , 45 Accordingly, pronounced olfactory dysfunction in Aβ + PD may reflect greater regional vulnerability and more widespread network‐level neuropathological changes involving olfactory, limbic, and cholinergic structures elicited by AD copathology.

Dysautonomia was also significantly more pronounced in Aβ + PD. Although dysautonomia in PD has been traditionally attributed to impairment in the peripheral nervous system, increasing evidence suggests that compromised higher‐order autonomic regulation involving the central autonomic network (CAN) may also play a role. 46 , 47 , 48 Because structures comprising the CAN (eg, insular cortex, amygdala, and anterior cingulate cortex) are vulnerable to both synuclein and Aβ pathology, 47 their concurrent involvement may account for the more profound dysautonomia in Aβ + PD. Simultaneously, repeated orthostatic stress or chronic hypoperfusion status may further accelerate AD copathology through impaired Aβ clearance and facilitate tau hyperphosphorylation, thus forming a vicious cycle. 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Furthermore, chronic exposure to orthostasis tends to promote neurovascular and inflammatory stress, with diminished vagal tone contributing to proinflammatory conditions, thereby fostering a pathophysiological environment predisposing to the development of AD. 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54

Although the detrimental impact of AD copathology on cognition and NPSs in PD is well established in advanced disease stages, 6 whether these symptoms are overt at the early stage remains unclear. In this study, baseline cognitive performance did not differ by Aβ status. Several explanations may account for this finding. First, ascertainment bias within the Lewy body disease spectrum may have attenuated baseline cognitive differences. Patients with concomitant AD and Lewy body pathologies who present with early dementia are more likely to be diagnosed with DLB in a memory clinic than with PD in a movement disorder–based cohort such as ours. Second, given that a substantial proportion of participants underwent Aβ imaging owing to concern about relatively rapid cognitive decline (57/152, 37.5%), the absence of baseline cognitive differences may partly be attributable to selection bias in our cohort rather than a feature generalizable to the broader PD population. Third, the findings may reflect the temporal dissociation between Aβ accumulation and its cognitive consequences. 55 Indeed, Aβ positivity was associated with an increased risk of conversion to PDD (Fig. S3), consistent with several prior reports that similarly found no baseline cognitive association with AD biomarkers yet demonstrated their predictive value for subsequent cognitive decline and dementia conversion. 56 , 57 , 58 These findings suggest that the cognitive effects of Aβ copathology may not necessarily manifest as discernible differences at the time of diagnosis. Meanwhile, greater mood disturbance at diagnosis was consistently associated with Aβ positivity in our cohort, suggesting that neuropsychiatric manifestations may serve as an early clinical marker suggestive of underlying Aβ copathology, one that may precede measurable cognitive deficits. Supporting this, a recent report found that Aβ copathology was associated with more frequent depression and anxiety episodes even in the prodromal stage of synucleinopathy (ie, polysomnography‐confirmed RBD). 59 Similar observations have been documented in preclinical AD populations, where anxiety and apathy have been proposed as early behavioral correlates of underlying AD pathology that precede overt cognitive impairment. 60

Beyond NMS profiles, Aβ + PD patients were likely to exhibit motor impairments that substantially exceed those expected on the basis of their degree of presynaptic dopaminergic denervation. Although nigrostriatal degeneration undoubtedly serves as the primary neural substrate of parkinsonian symptoms, considerable variability in the severity of motor deficits among patients with comparable DAT availability suggests the presence of compensatory or exacerbating factors that modulate the relationship between dopaminergic degeneration and motor symptom expression (ie, the “motor reserve” concept). 10 , 37 , 38 , 39 Within this framework, the influence of copathology is increasingly recognized as one of the key determinants of motor reserve capacity. 10 , 22 , 61 Notably, recent evidence indicates that Aβ/tau pathology in extra‐nigrostriatal pathways may contribute to motor dysfunction independent of nigrostriatal degeneration. 22 , 62 We hypothesized that patients with PD with concomitant Aβ pathology would exhibit motor deficits exceeding those predicted by their degree of dopaminergic denervation. Expectedly, Aβ + PD exhibited higher residuals (ie, low motor reserve; Fig. 1), which may explain why higher UPDRS‐III scores were consistently identified as important predictors of Aβ positivity. Similarly, previous cerebrospinal fluid studies in the Parkinson Progression Markers Initiative cohort found that AD biomarkers tended to be associated with greater motor severity 63 , 64 , 65 and less favorable motor phenotypes (PIGD 64 or diffuse‐malignant subtype 65 ), along with greater NMS burden—traits that largely overlap with Aβ + PD in our cohort, although the association with PIGD phenotype was not replicated. Collectively, Aβ copathology appears to augment motor disability through extra‐nigrostriatal mechanisms, 62 and a disproportionate motor deficit for a given DAT loss could serve as a pragmatic clinical clue to increased risk of Aβ copathology.

However, our findings should be interpreted with caution. Although each of the individual clinical features demonstrated significant associations with Aβ positivity, it would be overly reductive to interpret these features in isolation as direct indicators of Aβ copathology. Given that these features are prevalent across the PD population regardless of Aβ status, prediction relying on a single isolated manifestation may have limited specificity. Rather, specific constellations of clinical features may offer improved specificity and provide clinically meaningful clues for biologically distinct PD endophenotypes. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Previous studies have suggested that PD patients presenting with both dysautonomia and hyposmia at diagnosis exhibited greater motor disability, more apathetic symptoms, and accelerated cognitive decline. 16 , 17 , 18 Likewise, the co‐occurrence of hyposmia and RBD has been reported to be associated with an unfavorable motor and cognitive prognosis. 14 , 15 , 16 Collectively, these findings suggest that specific clusters of clinical profiles may offer greater prognostic and biological significance compared with any single symptom in isolation. Although our study was not designed to delineate a specific PD subtype, the Aβ‐associated clinical profiles identified herein may, at least in part, provide preliminary evidence for future studies identifying PD endophenotypes susceptible to Aβ copathology. However, given the cross‐sectional study design and inconsistent interval between DAT and Aβ imaging, it should be noted that causality cannot be inferred. Our current data cannot determine whether these disproportionately severe clinical symptoms are indeed driven by Aβ copathology, or whether these Aβ‐associated symptoms may instead represent a certain PD subtype predisposed to subsequent Aβ accumulation.

Our study has several limitations. First, Aβ imaging was not performed systematically at diagnosis. In particular, 57 of 152 patients (37.5%) underwent Aβ imaging owing to clinical concern about relatively rapid cognitive decline. This overrepresentation of patients with a more aggressive clinical trajectory may have inflated the prevalence of Aβ + PD in our cohort (38.8%) and exaggerated the differences in clinical symptom burden between Aβ groups. Although the relatively short interval from diagnosis to Aβ imaging (median: 1.32 [IQR 0.06–3.88] years) and sensitivity analyses adjusting for disease duration at Aβ imaging yielded consistent results (Table S2), this limitation may nonetheless have influenced our findings. Furthermore, the inconsistent timing of Aβ imaging precludes us from determining whether the identified clinical profiles emerge as downstream consequences of Aβ copathology or represent specific PD phenotypes vulnerable to subsequent Aβ accumulation. Second, our study was conducted in a single cohort with a modest sample size and without external validation. Further validation in independent cohorts is warranted to determine whether the Aβ‐associated clinical profiles identified herein retain their significance in the general PD population. Third, although this study focused on clinical predictors of AD copathology, only the Aβ status was used, without consideration of tau.

Altogether, greater olfactory dysfunction, dysautonomia, mood disturbances, and disproportionately severe motor deficits that cannot be fully accounted for dopamine depletion at diagnosis may represent a distinct clinical profile susceptible to Aβ copathology, offering important clues for early risk stratification.
