Bayesian Calibration of the Crossterms Eigenvolume HRG Model: Integrating Lattice QCD and Experimental Data
Nachiketa Sarkar

Abstract
We perform a Bayesian calibration of the Crossterms Eigenvolume Hadron Resonance Gas (Cross EV-HRG) model, which incorporates flavor-dependent repulsive interactions in a thermodynamically consistent framework. For the first time, the model is simultaneously constrained using lattice QCD (LQCD) thermodynamic observables and centrality-resolved hadron yield data from Pb–Pb collisions at s NN = 2.76 \sqrt{s_{\mathrm{NN}}}=2.76 TeV, measured by the ALICE Collaboration. Model parameters, including species-dependent eigenvolume radii and chemical freeze-out conditions, are inferred using Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling. We find that LQCD data alone do not tightly constrain the parameters, particularly in the strange hadron sector, suggesting possible incompleteness in the hadron resonance spectrum. To account realistically for experimental uncertainties, we construct a phenomenological covariance matrix incorporating correlated systematic errors, which significantly affect both the marginal distributions and interdependencies of the inferred parameters. In the joint calibration, we extract a consistent set of hadron radii that simultaneously describe both LQCD and experimental data, demonstrating the viability of a unified thermal description of the hadronic medium. Residual uncertainties in the strange sector highlight the need for future studies incorporating QCD-predicted states and higher-order fluctuation observables.



I
Introduction

The investigation of hadronic matter forms a fundamental pillar of high-energy nuclear physics, providing crucial insights into the thermodynamic behavior of strongly interacting systems under extreme conditions. At high enough temperature and density, hadronic matter is expected to transition into the quark–gluon plasma (QGP), a deconfined phase that is believed to have prevailed in the microseconds following the Big Bang
Shuryak:1980tp
. In order to probe this transition and the overall structure of the QCD phase diagram, relativistic heavy-ion collision experiments are conducted at various center-of-mass energies. Major experimental programs at the LHC (CERN), RHIC (BNL), and SIS18/SIS100 (GSI)
Aamodt:2008zz
;
Adcox:2004mh
;
Sturm:2010yit
, along with upcoming facilities such as NICA (JINR)
Kekelidze:2016hhw
and FAIR (GSI)

;
Cleymans:1999st
;
Braun-Munzinger:2014lba
;
Alba:2017mqu

Despite its simplicity, it has proven remarkably successful in reproducing many thermodynamic observables from first-principles LQCD simulations

;

;
Borsanyi:2010cj
;
Fodor:2018wul
;
Borsanyi:2011sw

;

;

;
Bellwied:2018tkc

Numerous studies have demonstrated that introducing short-range repulsive interactions into the HRG framework improves agreement with LQCD results and enhances fits to hadron yield data

;

;
Vovchenko:2016ebv

These findings highlight the importance of accurately determining hadronic interaction volumes to achieve a reliable description of heavy-ion collision data within thermal models.

Beyond the physical assumptions embedded in the HRG framework, the statistical calibration strategy and the nature of the experimental input also play a critical role in determining the model parameters. Choices such as whether to fit absolute yields or particle ratios—and, in the case of ratios, how they are constructed—as well as the treatment of experimental uncertainties and correlations, can significantly affect the inferred freeze-out conditions. The systematic impact of several of these methodological choices has been thoroughly investigated in studies such as Ref.

II
Model Description: Cross-term Eigenvolume HRG Model

The HRG model has been extended by incorporating excluded-volume (EV) corrections, which account for the finite spatial extent of hadrons in a thermodynamically consistent manner
Yen:1997rv
;
Rischke:1991ke
. In this framework, each hadron species is assigned an eigenvolume, effectively modeling the short-range repulsive interactions that become significant in dense hadronic matter. As mentioned earlier, this extension plays a crucial role in enabling a more reliable extraction of chemical freeze-out parameters and significantly improves agreement with LQCD results
Andronic:2017pug
;
Andronic:2012ut
;
Vovchenko:2014pka
;
Vovchenko:2015cbk
. However, parametrizing eigenvolume interactions remains a nontrivial but essential component of EV-HRG modeling. A common approximation assumes a uniform hard-core radius corresponding to same eigenvolume for all hadron species
Yen:1997rv
;
Braun-Munzinger:1999hun
;
Vovchenko:2014pka
;
Sarkar:2017bqy
;
Sarkar:2017ijd
, which facilitates analytical treatment but leads to a near cancellation of excluded-volume effects when particle yield ratios are used for freeze-out parameter extraction. More sophisticated schemes assign species-dependent eigenvolumes, either differentiating between mesons and baryons
Bugaev:2013sfa
;
Begun:2012rf
;
Bugaev:2013sfa
, or even by assigning eigenvolumes according to the hadron’s quark content
Alba:2017bbr
;
Motornenko:2020yme
;
Alba:2016hwx
. Mass-dependent eigenvolume schemes have also been explored as a continuous alternative to discrete species assignments
Albright:2014gva
;
Alba:2016fku
. In particular, Ref.
Alba:2016hwx
introduced a flavor-sensitive parametrization in which the eigenvolume of light (non-strange) hadrons scales proportionally with their mass, while that of strange hadrons decreases inversely with mass. This hybrid scheme was shown to significantly enhance the fit quality across a range of collision energies, while also yielding freeze-out parameters consistent with LQCD constraints. Efforts to extract effective hadron radii—carried out by independently confronting the EV-HRG model with LQCD fluctuation observables
Karthein:2021cmb
—have demonstrated that the inclusion of additional high-mass resonances beyond those listed by the Particle Data Group (PDG) can be essential for reproducing observed features in the respective datasets.

While the conventional EV-HRG model captures repulsive interactions by assigning eigenvolumes to individual hadron species, it does not incorporate cross-terms in the virial expansion that account for interactions between different species. A more detailed treatment is realized in the Cross EV-HRG model—explicitly incorporates inter-species interactions, thereby allowing independent control over interactions among mesons, baryons, and their antiparticles. This, in principle, enables one to indirectly account for particle–antiparticle annihilation effects
Satarov:2016peb

Although this formulation is numerically challenging due to the complexity of solving coupled nonlinear equations, it remains consistent with the second-order virial expansion, thereby providing a more physically grounded and thermodynamically consistent description of short-range repulsive interactions
Gorenstein:1999ce
;
Vovchenko:2016ebv
;
Vovchenko:2017zpj
;
Satarov:2016peb
. A detailed study of the sensitivity of thermal fits to the choice of eigenvolume parametrization within the Cross EV- HRG framework was presented in Ref.
Vovchenko:2016ebv
. Notably, this work explored a bag-like eigenvolume parametrization, in which the effective bag constant was calibrated to reproduce the proton’s ground-state radius. Together, these findings underscore the critical sensitivity of thermal model predictions to the parametrization of repulsive interactions, necessitating a precise and systematic treatment of eigenvolume parameters in HRG frameworks.

In this study, we employ the Cross EV-HRG model without imposing any predefined parametrization of hadron radii. Instead, hadron radii are treated as independent free parameters, grouped according to their baryon number and strangeness content. This flavor-based grouping is motivated by observed differences in interaction cross-sections and mass hierarchies among hadrons, as evidenced or suggested by studies of lattice QCD results and experimental measurements
Alba:2016hwx
;
Karthein:2021cmb
. The complete list of free parameters used in the present work is summarized in Table
1
. These parameters are directly constrained via Bayesian inference by confronting model predictions with both LQCD observables and experimental data. This fully data-driven approach enables a systematic and unified determination of species-dependent short-range repulsive interactions, as well as the extraction of chemical freeze-out parameters consistent with both theoretical and experimental constraints.

Gorenstein:1999ce
;
Vovchenko:2016ebv
:

P_{i}(T,\mu_{i})=P^{\text{id}}_{i}\left(T,\mu_{i}-\sum_{j\in H}\tilde{b}_{ij}P_{j}\right),

(1)

Here,

denotes the temperature, and
\mu_{i}
is the chemical potential of hadron species

. The set

includes all hadrons, and

\tilde{b}_{ij}=\frac{2b_{ii}b_{ij}}{b_{ii}+b_{jj}}.

(2)

The parameter
b_{ij}
represents the geometric eigenvolume characterizing interactions between species
i
and
j
. It is defined as:

{b}_{ij}=\frac{2\pi}{3}(r_{i}+r_{j})^{3},

(3)

with,
r_{i}
denoting the hard-core radius of hadron species
i
.
P^{\text{id}}_{i}\left(T,\mu^{\star}_{i}\right)
is the ideal gas pressure evaluated at an effective chemical potential,
\mu^{\star}_{i}
, which incorporates the repulsive interaction effects and is defined as:

\mu_{i}^{*}=\mu_{i}-\sum_{j}b_{ij}P_{j},

(4)

where,
P_{j}
represents the partial pressure of species

. The full system of coupled nonlinear equations is solved iteratively using Broyden’s method.

The number density
n_{i}
for each species can be computed from the following relation
Vovchenko:2016ebv
:

Tn_{i}+P_{i}\sum_{j\in H}\tilde{b}_{ij}n_{j}=P_{i}.

(5)

Once the pressure is determined self-consistently, other thermodynamic quantities—such as entropy density

, energy density

, the trace anomaly
(\varepsilon-3P)
, specific heat
C_{v}

ParticleDataGroup:2020ssz
compilation, but exclude light nuclei as well as charm and bottom hadrons, whose contributions to the bulk thermodynamics at the relevant temperatures are negligible.

III
Analysis Methodology: Bayesian Approach

In this study we adopt a Bayesian analysis framework to calibrate the parameters of the Cross EV-HRG model. Calibration is performed by simultaneously fitting the model to experimental hadron yield data and LQCD thermodynamic observables. The Bayesian approach provides a systematic methodology for incorporating prior information, quantifying uncertainties, and identifying correlations among model parameters. This enables a statistically rigorous and data-driven characterization of the species-dependent repulsive interactions encoded in the model. The complete methodology—including model implementation, data preprocessing, likelihood construction, and MCMC sampling procedures, is detailed below.

III.1
Gaussian Process (GP) emulator

The Cross EV-HRG model is computationally more demanding than other HRG formulations, as it requires solving a large system of coupled nonlinear equations—one for each hadron species included in the analysis. This complexity is further amplified when simultaneously fitting multiple observables or datasets, such as LQCD thermodynamic quantities and centrality-dependent experimental hadron yields. As a result, the high computational cost renders direct sampling (e.g., MCMC using the full Cross EV-HRG model at every proposal) impractical for Bayesian inference. To overcome this challenge, we construct a Gaussian Process (GP) emulator, using the
scikit-learn
library
Rasmussen:2006gpr
;
Pedregosa:2011sklearn
. The emulator serves as a surrogate model, efficiently approximating the Cross EV-HRG output across the parameter space. This surrogate significantly accelerates the Bayesian calibration process while preserving the essential features of the original model.

We train separate emulators for the lattice and yield problems by generating two distinct training datasets — one calibrated to lattice thermodynamic observables and one to hadron yields — because the two tasks have different output structures and input dimensionalities. Sampling is performed using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS)
McKay1979
, which ensures well-distributed coverage across the parameter ranges. The specific parameter ranges used for sampling are summarized in Table

For the lattice data, we generate 300 training points in an 8-dimensional parameter space, denoted as
X^{\text{lqcd}}_{m\times n}
, using LHS. For the yield data, we generate another 300 training points in a 9-dimensional parameter space,
X^{\text{yield}}_{m\times n}
, which includes temperature as the additional dimension. This sample size was found to be more than sufficient to achieve the desired emulator accuracy while keeping computational cost manageable.

We explicitly evaluate the Cross EV-HRG model for each set of training points to generate the corresponding model outputs. For the lattice data, we consider six thermodynamic observables over a temperature range from 110 to 160 MeV in 5 MeV intervals, resulting in 66 observables per parameter point. The resulting output matrix is denoted
Y^{\text{lqcd}}_{m\times p}
, with dimensions
300\times 66
, where
p

is the number of scalar observables per sample. For the yield data, we include eleven particle yields with resonance decay contributions, producing an output matrix of size
300\times 11
, denoted as
Y^{\text{yield}}_{m\times p}
. In principle, these output matrices could be used directly to train the emulator; however, because emulators are inherently limited to scalar-valued outputs, we instead reduce their dimensionality using
Principal Component Analysis
(PCA) and train GPs on the leading principal components. This approach enables rapid interpolation of Cross EV-HRG model predictions without repeatedly solving the full set of model equations.

PCA transforms the correlated observables into an mutually orthogonal orthogonal set of principal components (PCs). The transformation is given by:

Z=YU,

(6)

where
U
is the matrix of eigenvectors of the covariance matrix
Y^{T}Y
. Here,

Y
denotes standardized (zero mean, unit variance per observable) output matrix.

To compute
U
, we apply
Singular Value Decomposition
(SVD) to the standardized model output matrix:

Y=V\Sigma U^{T}.

(7)

The covariance matrix can be expressed as:

Y^{T}Y=U\Sigma^{2}U^{T},

(8)

where
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values, which are the square roots of the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix.

These principal components are uncorrelated and ranked by their associated eigenvalues, with the first few components capturing most of the variance in the data. Consequently, PCA also serves as a dimensionality reduction technique, allowing us to retain only the leading
"q" PCs while safely neglecting those corresponding to negligible eigenvalues:

Z_{q}=YU_{q}.

(9)

In the present analysis, we retain 20 PCs for the lattice data and 5 PCs for the yield data, which capture 99.5% of the total variance of the respective data. These reduced components are then treated as new observables to be trained by the emulator, significantly improving computational efficiency without compromising accuracy.

We use following covariance function (kernel) for the emulator:

\sigma(x,x^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}_{\text{GP}}\exp\left(-\sum_{k}\frac{(x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime})^{2}}{2l_{k}^{2}}\right)+\sigma^{2}_{n}\delta_{xx^{\prime}},

(10)

where
\sigma^{2}_{\text{GP}}
denotes the signal variance,
l_{k}
are the characteristic length scales associated with each input parameter, and
\sigma^{2}_{n}
is the noise variance. These hyperparameters are learned during the training phase by maximizing the marginal likelihood of the emulator outputs. Although the Cross EV-HRG model itself is deterministic and noise-free, we include a small noise term
\sigma^{2}_{n}
to enhance numerical stability.

After training the emulator, the next critical step is to validate its performance. To this end, we construct a test dataset consisting of 25 randomly selected points within the relevant parameter spaces—eight-dimensional for the lattice observables and nine-dimensional for the hadron yield calculations. At each of these test points, we evaluate the full Cross EV-HRG model and independently compute the same observables using the trained emulator. The strong agreement observed between these results, as shown in Fig.
1
, demonstrates the accuracy and reliability of the emulator in capturing the response of the full Cross EV-HRG model throughout the parameter space. Although emulators inherently provide predictive uncertainties, we find that both the emulator uncertainty and the deviation from the true model predictions remain below 3% for all observables and test points. Given that this level of uncertainty is significantly smaller than the experimental and LQCD uncertainties, the emulator is sufficiently accurate for use in the Bayesian calibration procedure.

III.2
Bayesian Calibration

After verifying the predictive accuracy of the emulator, we proceed with the Bayesian calibration of the Cross EV-HRG model parameters through comparison the model predictions to experimental hadron yield data and LQCD observables. The goal of this calibration is to sample the posterior distributions of the model parameters by combining prior knowledge with the likelihood of the observed data, thereby enabling statistically consistent inference of parameter values, along with their uncertainties and correlations.

Bayes theorem is given by:

P(\theta|\mathcal{D})\propto P(\mathcal{D}|\theta)P(\theta),

(11)

where:

P(\theta|\mathcal{D})
, is the posterior distribution of the parameters given the data
\mathcal{D}
,

P(\mathcal{D}|\theta)
, is the likelihood, representing the probability of observing the data for a given set of parameters and

P(\theta)
, is the prior distribution, encoding any prior knowledge about the parameters.

For the prior distributions
P(\theta)
, we adopt a noninformative flat prior on the model parameters, constant within the design ranges specified in Table
1
and zero outside, i.e.,

P(\theta)\propto\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\min(\theta_{i})\leq\theta_{i}\leq\max(\theta_{i})\ \forall i,,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This choice reflects the absence of reliable prior information beyond physical limits and avoids unjustified informative assumptions. For the calibration with LQCD data, we use the first eight model parameters with uniform prior ranges as specified in Table
1
. For the yield calibration, we introduce two additional free parameters: the temperature and the fireball radius for each centrality class. The prior range for the temperature, common to all centrality classes, is provided in Table
1
, while fireball radii are assigned centrality-specific ranges to speed up MCMC convergence. In earlier studies using the EV-HRG model, it was observed that the extracted chemical freeze-out temperature exhibited large uncertainties and a double-minimum
\chi^{2}
profile
Vovchenko:2015cbk
;
Vovchenko:2016ebv
, with one of the minima corresponding to an unphysical high-
T
solution. As argued in Ref.
Vovchenko:2016ebv
, such high-
T
values should not be interpreted as physical freeze-out points, but rather as artifacts arising from the model’s sensitivity to the treatment of eigenvolume interactions, as at such high temperatures, hadronic degrees of freedom are not expected to survive. Therefore, in the present work, we restrict the temperature prior to lie around the pseudo-critical region.

With the prior ranges defined, we proceed to compute the posterior distribution using Bayes’ theorem, where the likelihood function is given by:

P(\mathcal{D}|\theta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}[y_{m}(\theta)-y_{e}]^{T}\Sigma^{-1}[y_{m}(\theta)-y_{e}]\right).

(12)

where
y_{m}(\theta)
are the model predictions, which depend on the parameters
\theta
, and
y_{e}
are the experimental/LQCD data points corresponding to the same observables.
The covariance matrix,
\Sigma
, encapsulates both experimental/LQCD measurements and emulator interpolation errors. We have calculated the covariance matrix,
\Sigma
as prescribed in Ref.
Bernhard:2018hnz
. We also adopt the additional calibration uncertainty term corresponding to the model systematic,

\sigma^{y}_{m}
, as prescribed in Ref.
Bernhard:2018hnz
, which they referred as an “uncertainty of uncertainty.”

The joint likelihood function for simultaneous calibration of multiple data sets—such as in our case, combining experimental and LQCD data—can be written as the product of the individual likelihoods
Bernhard:2018hnz
:

P(\mathcal{D}|\theta)=\prod_{s}P(\mathcal{D}_{s}|\theta_{s}).

(13)

It should be noted that in this joint calibration of LQCD observables and hadron yields, the two likelihoods have inherently different scales and sources of uncertainty. Therefore, we omit the additional calibration uncertainty term
\sigma^{y}_{m}
, as a single unified value would not be appropriate across both datasets.

We sample the posterior distribution using the affine-invariant ensemble MCMC sampler
ForemanMackey2013
, implemented via the
emcee
package. The chains were initialized with
\mathcal{O}(10^{4})
burn-in samples, discarded after equilibration, followed by
\mathcal{O}(10^{5})
production samples used to extract the posteriors. To accelerate convergence, the burn-in was carried out in two phases. The degree of convergence was assessed using the Gelman–Rubin statistic
\hat{R}

Gelman:1992zz
, ensuring
\hat{R}\approx 1
for all parameters. Trace plots were also examined to confirm proper mixing and stability. The full analysis code is available in Ref.
HepBayes:2023
.

\chi^{2}=(\mathbf{y}^{\text{exp}}-\mathbf{y}^{\text{model}})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}^{\text{exp}}-\mathbf{y}^{\text{model}}),

IV.2.2
Yield Data Fitting Without Correlations

We now turn to the calibration using experimental yield data only. We constrain the model parameters and extract the chemical freeze-out temperature by fitting to centrality-dependent midrapidity hadron yields from ALICE Collaboration in
\mathrm{Pb}+\mathrm{Pb}
collisions at
\sqrt{s_{NN}}=2.76~\mathrm{TeV}
. The fit includes,
\pi^{\pm}
,
K^{\pm}
,
p
,
\bar{p}

ALICE:2013mez
,
\Lambda

ALICE:2013cdo
,
\phi

ALICE:2014jbq
,
\Xi^{\pm}
, and
\Omega+\bar{\Omega}

ALICE:2013xmt
;
Becattini:2014hla
across multiple centrality classes.

We incorporate uncertainties via a diagonal covariance matrix summing statistical and systematic errors in quadrature, assuming no correlations between hadron species or centralities. This setup provides a useful baseline for evaluating the impact of correlated systematic effects, which will be explicitly addressed in the following section.

The results of the yield-only calibration are presented in Figs.
5
and
6
, with corresponding numerical values summarized in Table
3
. The inset in Fig.
5
confirms MCMC convergence and excellent fit quality via stable and low
\chi^{2}/\mathrm{ndf}
values.

Fig.
5
shows that hadronic yield data substantially sharpen parameter constraints, producing well-defined posterior peaks and narrower credible intervals, especially for non-strange mesons and baryons compared to the LQCD-only fit. This improvement arises from the strong sensitivity of hadron yields to excluded-volume effects and chemical freeze-out conditions
Andronic:2017pug
;
Vovchenko:2019pjl
, as further supported by the Sobol sensitivity indices shown in Fig.
2
. A comparison of the centrality-dependent experimental hadron yields with model predictions is shown in Fig.
6
. Posterior predictions agree well with the data across all centralities, consistent with the low
\chi^{2}/\mathrm{ndf}\sim 1.12\pm 0.82
from the fit. Small deviations appear in peripheral collisions for multi-strange hadrons, likely due to larger experimental uncertainties and lower multiplicities.

The extracted freeze-out temperatures are well constrained, with mean values lying within a narrow and nearly centrality-independent range,
T\sim 150\text{--}157~\mathrm{MeV}
, consistent with previous thermal fits
Becattini:2014hla
;
Alba:2016hwx
. This centrality independence behavior of the chemical freeze-out temperature
Biswas:2020dsc
is often interpreted as the thermalized nature of the system across all centrality classes
Kumar:2023acr
;
Gupta:2014ova
. Unlike the LQCD-only calibration, which favored a nearly point-like meson radius, the present fit yields a significantly larger and well-constrained value for the non-strange and single-strange meson radius,
r_{M}\sim 0.31~\mathrm{fm}
. This increase in eigenvolume parameter helps suppress the light hadron yields—particularly pions and kaons—resulting in improved agreement with the experimental data.

The baryon and antibaryon radii remain nearly degenerate (
r_{B}\approx r_{\bar{B}}
), reflecting matter–antimatter symmetry at vanishing baryochemical potential
\mu_{B}\approx 0
. The absence of statistically significant differences suggests that hadronic annihilation effects, are either negligible at chemical freeze-out or effectively absorbed into the eigenvolume parametrization through the extracted radii.

A clear flavor-dependent structure in the repulsive interactions is observed, consistent with an approximate inverse mass hierarchy in the extracted eigenvolumes of strange baryon sector
Alba:2016hwx
. However, the broader posterior distributions for the double- and triple-strange baryon radii (
r_{BS2}
,
r_{BS3}
) indicate limited statistical precision, primarily due to their low multiplicities and larger experimental uncertainties—particularly in peripheral collisions. These features may also hint at the presence of sequential freeze-out
Chatterjee:2013yga
;
Bugaev:2013sfa
;
Chatterjee:2014ysa
residual effects from incomplete treatment of the PDG spectrum and resonance feeddown contribution
Andronic:2017pug
;
Vovchenko:2020dmv
.

The off-diagonal contours of the corner plot Fig.
5
, reveal underlying degeneracies within the excluded-volume HRG framework. Eigenvolume parameters associated with lighter hadrons exhibit strong mutual correlations, whereas only moderate correlations are observed between the light and multi-strange sectors. A pronounced anti-correlation is observed between each centrality-dependent chemical freeze-out temperature
T_{i}
and the corresponding freeze-out volume parameter
R_{i}
. Similarly, significant anti-correlations emerge between hadron radii and freeze-out temperatures. These trends reflect compensations between temperature and repulsive strength that produce similar thermodynamic behavior, highlighting intrinsic parameter degeneracies in thermal models.

Overall, these results reveal notable differences between fit outcomes from LQCD and hadron yield data, highlighting their complementary roles in constraining the flavor-dependent eigenvolume structure of the HRG model (see Fig.
7
, top panel). The analysis also uncovers strong correlations and compensatory relationships between model parameters—such as eigenvolumes—and chemical freeze-out conditions, reflecting the typical degeneracies inherent in thermal descriptions.

IV.2.3
Yield Data Fitting With Correlation

In this section, we explore how correlations among the experimental measurements influence the calibration of thermal model parameters. A proper treatment of uncertainties in the fitting procedure is essential for the accurate extraction of physical parameters and for assessing the reliability of the inferred posterior distributions. Ignoring these correlations treats data points as independent, which can lead to incorrect uncertainty estimates or biased best-fit values, thus compromising the physical interpretation.

Experimental uncertainties generally consist of statistical and systematic components combined in quadrature,
\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{stat}}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{sys}}
. While statistical uncertainties are generally uncorrelated between measurements, systematic uncertainties often display significant correlations across different hadron species and centrality bins. These correlations arise due to shared detector effects, common reconstruction algorithms, or analysis procedures. However, constructing an exact correlation matrix is a highly nontrivial task, as it requires detailed information about every aspect of the experimental setup. Since full correlation data are typically unavailable from experimental collaborations, we adopt a phenomenological approach to estimate the correlation structure. This method, summarized in Appendix
A
, is based on reasonable assumptions about key systematic sources—such as tracking efficiency, particle identification, and detector acceptance—that are expected to induce correlations across yields and centralities. However, the purpose here is not to present the exact correlation matrix; instead, we aim to understand the potential influence of correlated systematic uncertainties on the thermal model calibration. Despite its approximate nature, this approach offers a more realistic representation of uncertainties than assuming diagonal errors alone. We incorporate this phenomenological covariance matrix, which accounts for both intraspecies and centrality correlations in the systematic uncertainties, into our Bayesian calibration framework to assess its impact on the extracted model parameters. Of course, the statistical uncertainties are also included in the usual manner.

Including correlated systematic uncertainties significantly broadens the marginal posterior distributions of model parameters, as illustrated in Fig.
9(a)
and summarized in Table
4
, reflecting a more conservative and realistic estimate of parameter uncertainties. Notably, the posterior mean of the chemical freeze-out temperature shifts slightly upward, while the extracted system radius decreases. V
Summary and Conclusions

In this study, we presented a comprehensive Bayesian calibration of the Cross EV- HRG model by simultaneously incorporating constraints from LQCD thermodynamic observables and centrality-resolved hadron yield data measured by the ALICE Collaboration in Pb–Pb collisions at
\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{NN}}}=2.76
TeV. The Cross EV-HRG model accounts for flavor-dependent short-range repulsive interactions in a thermodynamically consistent manner. Rather than adopting fixed or parametrized eigenvolume inputs, we extracted the eigenvolume radii for different hadron species directly through Bayesian inference. A phenomenological covariance matrix was constructed to incorporate correlated systematic uncertainties in the experimental yield data. We explored model sensitivity and dataset-specific influences by performing Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) calibrations across four scenarios: using LQCD data only, yield data with diagonal errors, yield data with full correlated errors, and a joint fit to both datasets.

Our findings indicate that the LQCD data alone were insufficient to tightly constrain the eigenvolume parameters, particularly for mesons and strange hadrons, whose posteriors distributions remained broad. The small fitted radii for pions and kaons, along with the large uncertainties in the strange sector, hint at an incomplete hadron spectrum. Additionally, thermodynamic observables show limited sensitivity to species-specific eigenvolumes. In contrast, calibrations based on yield data produced sharply peaked posteriors and tighter constraints on both interaction and freeze-out parameters, except in the strange sector. The extracted freeze-out temperatures exhibit minimal centrality dependence, suggesting thermalization across all centrality classes. Baryon–antibaryon symmetry in terms of eigenvolume parameters is preserved in all fits, as expected near vanishing baryochemical potential. The emergence of strong correlations between model parameters highlights the complex interplay between repulsive interactions and thermal conditions. Inclusion of correlated systematic uncertainties leads to substantial changes in the marginal posteriors and modifies the interdependencies among parameters, emphasizing the importance of proper uncertainty modeling in thermal fits.

The joint calibration, incorporating both LQCD and hadron yield data, demonstrates that a unified thermal description of the hadronic medium is feasible using a common set of eigenvolume parameters. While some eigenvolume parameters—especially those associated with strange mesons—remain weakly constrained. Notably, flavor-dependent eigenvolume interactions are observed in all calibrations, although the evidence is not conclusive in the LQCD-only case due to large uncertainties. However, when including yield data, the flavor dependence becomes clearly evident. Specifically, the baryonic strange sector follows an inverse mass hierarchy, while the mesonic sector exhibits a mass-dependent hierarchy.

In conclusion, this work establishes a statistically robust framework for thermal model calibration using Bayesian techniques. The weak constraints on several eigenvolume radii in the strange sector for all calibration highlight the need for additional input. Future improvements could include incorporating QCD-predicted hadron states beyond the PDG listings or utilizing higher-order conserved charge fluctuations from lattice QCD, which are more sensitive to flavor-specific sectors. Such extensions may help resolve the current degeneracies and are promising directions for future investigations. The approach is readily extensible to studies at finite baryochemical potential, where it may help explore the
\mu_{B}
-dependence of repulsive interactions or identify signatures of the QCD critical point and first-order phase transitions via fluctuation observables.
