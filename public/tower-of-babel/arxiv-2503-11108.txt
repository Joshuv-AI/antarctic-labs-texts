Time and Memory Trade-off of KV-Cache Compression in Tensor Transformer Decoding
Yifang Chen, Xiaoyu Li, Yingyu Liang, Zhenmei Shi, Zhao Song, Yu Tian

Abstract
The key-value (KV) cache in the tensor version of transformers presents a significant bottleneck during inference. While previous work analyzes the fundamental space complexity barriers in standard attention mechanisms [Haris and Onak, 2025], our work generalizes the space complexity barriers result to tensor attention version. Our theoretical contributions rely on a reduction from communication complexity and deduce the memory lower bound for tensor-structured attention mechanisms when d = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) d=\Omega(\log n) . Furthermore, we introduce two types of tensor attention cache and present a trade-off between time and memory for two scenarios. Overall, our work provides a theoretical foundation for us to understand the time-memory tradeoff of KV-Cache compression in tensor attention decoding and offers more perspectives in developing more memory-efficient tensor attention Transformer architectures.

3.4
Attention Mechanism with KV Cache

We restate a definition from previous work
[
12
]
.

Definition 3.6

(Tensor Attention, a variation of
[
12
]
)
.

Let
d,i\in\mathbb{R}
where
d
is the embedding dimension and
i
is the current sequence length. Let
\odot:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the Hadamard product and
\sigma_{i^{2}}
is the Softmax function from Definition
3.2
. Given a query vector
q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and four matrices
K_{1,i},K_{2,i},V_{1,i},V_{2,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{i\times d}

\displaystyle K_{1,i}=\begin{bmatrix}k_{1,1}^{\top}\\
k_{1,2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
k_{1,i}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},~K_{2,i}=\begin{bmatrix}k_{2,1}^{\top}\\
k_{2,2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
k_{2,i}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},~V_{1,i}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1,1}^{\top}\\
v_{1,2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
v_{1,i}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},~V_{2,i}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{2,1}^{\top}\\
v_{2,2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
v_{2,i}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

are four
i\times d
matrices containing the historical key and value vectors.

Then we are able to define the tensor attention function as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Attn}

\displaystyle(q_{i},K_{1,i},K_{2,i},V_{1,i},V_{2,i}):=\underbrace{(V_{1,i}\oslash V_{2,i})^{\top}}_{d\times i^{2}}\cdot\sigma_{i^{2}}(\underbrace{(K_{1,i}\oslash K_{2,i})}_{i^{2}\times d}\cdot\underbrace{q_{i}}_{d\times 1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and
\oslash:\mathbb{R}^{i\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{i\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{i^{2}\times d}
denotes the column-wise Kronecker product from Definition
3.1
, which for any row indices
a,b\in[i]
with corresponding flattened index
\ell=(a-1)i+b\in[i^{2}]
, gives:

\displaystyle(K_{1,i}\oslash K_{2,i})_{\ell}

\displaystyle=k_{1,a}\odot k_{2,b}

\displaystyle(V_{1,i}\oslash V_{2,i})_{\ell}

\displaystyle=v_{1,a}\odot v_{2,b}

Then we are able to present the definition of key-value cache in tensor attention.

Definition 3.7
.

For a list of indices
1,\cdots,i
, we say matrices
K_{1,i},K_{2,i},V_{1,i}
and
V_{2,i}
(be defined as Definition
3.6
) are collectively called the key-value (KV) cache. Specifically,
K_{1,i},K_{2,i}
store key representations and
V_{1,i},V_{2,i}
store value representations, facilitating efficient tensorized attention computations.

Next, we define the attention mechanism that consolidates the four KV cache matrices into two, leveraging the column-wise Kronecker product.

Definition 3.8

(Tensor Attention with KV Cache, Two Cache Matrices Case)
.

Let
d,i\in\mathbb{R}
where
d
is the embedding dimension and
i
is the current sequence length. Given a query vector
q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and two matrices
\widetilde{K}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{i^{2}\times d}
, we define the tensor attention function as:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Attn}(q_{i},\widetilde{K}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{i}):=\widetilde{V}_{i}^{\top}\cdot\sigma_{i^{2}}(\widetilde{K}_{i}\cdot q_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and the two cache matrices
\widetilde{K}_{i}
and
\widetilde{V}_{i}
are derived from the column-wise Kronecker product (see Definition
3.1
) of the original key and value matrices:

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{i}

\displaystyle:=K_{1,i}\oslash K_{2,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{i^{2}\times d}

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{i}

\displaystyle:=V_{1,i}\oslash V_{2,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{i^{2}\times d}

where
K_{1,i},K_{2,i},V_{1,i},V_{2,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{i\times d}
are the four original cache matrices from Definition
3.7
, and
\sigma_{i^{2}}
is the Softmax function from Definition
3.2
.

This definition converts the original four KV cache matrices into two matrices,
\widetilde{K}_{i}
and
\widetilde{V}_{i}
, each of size
i^{2}\times d
, by precomputing the column-wise Kronecker products. This approach reflects the trade-off between preprocessing and space complexity, as discussed in Remark
3.9
.

Remark 3.9

(Memory-Speed Trade-off Between Two and Four Cache Matrices)
.

The two formulations of tensor attention KV Caches present a trade-off between memory efficiency and computational speed during inference:

•

Four Cache Matrices Case:
This approach stores
K_{1,i},K_{2,i},V_{1,i},V_{2,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{i\times d}
, requiring
O(id)
memory. However, during inference, it must compute the column-wise Kronecker products
K_{1,i}\oslash K_{2,i}
and
V_{1,i}\oslash V_{2,i}
on-the-fly, resulting in
O(i^{2}d)
computational complexity for each attention operation.

•

Two Cache Matrices Case:
This approach directly stores the precomputed Kronecker products
\widetilde{K}_{i}=K_{1,i}\oslash K_{2,i}
and
\widetilde{V}_{i}=V_{1,i}\oslash V_{2,i}
, requiring
O(i^{2}d)
memory. While this uses significantly more memory (quadratic in
i
rather than linear), it enables faster inference as the expensive Kronecker product computation is eliminated.

Figure 1:
Comparison of memory and computational requirements between four-cache and two-cache matrix formulations in tensor attention. The four-cache approach
(K_{1,i},K_{2,i},V_{1,i},V_{2,i})
uses linear memory
O(id)
but incurs
O(i^{2}d)
computational cost for Kronecker products during inference. The two-cache approach
(\widetilde{K}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{i})
pre-computes these products, requiring
O(i^{2}d)
memory but enabling faster inference.

This illustrates a fundamental space-time trade-off in attention mechanisms: the four cache matrices formulation is memory-efficient but computationally expensive during inference, while the two cache matrices formulation uses more memory but achieves faster inference times (see Figure
1
). Our lower bound proof in Theorem
4.2
demonstrates that the memory requirement of
\Omega(n^{2}d)
for the two cache matrices case is unavoidable, highlighting the inherent cost of optimizing for inference speed in this setting.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have addressed the critical memory challenges posed by the key-value (KV) cache in the tensor version of Transformers, expanding the foundational space complexity barriers from standard attention to tensor attention. Through a reduction from communication complexity, we derived information-theoretic bounds that reveal how memory consumption scales under higher-order token correlations. Our results demonstrate that when
d=\Omega(\log n)
, there exists a running time and memory trade-off between two cache scenarios. The four-cache-matrix case requires a space lower bound of
\Omega(nd)
and the two-cache-matrix case requires
\Omega(n^{2}d)
, while the two-cache-matrix case computation is faster by
\Omega(n^{2}d)
compared to the four-cache-matrix case. By underscoring these theoretical and algorithmic insights, we aim to guide the development of more memory-efficient tensor attention Transformer architectures.

B.2
Memory Lower Bound for Two Cache Matrices Case

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
B.2
.

Theorem B.2

(Memory Lower Bound for Two Cache Matrices case, Formal Version of Theorem
4.2
)
.

If the following conditions hold:

•

Let
C_{u}>1
be a universal constant.

•

Let
d\geq C_{u}\log n
.

•

Let the output of any algorithm produces
o_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
o_{n}=\mathsf{Attn}(q_{n},\widetilde{K}_{n},\widetilde{V}_{n})
, where
\widetilde{K}_{n}=K_{1,n}\oslash K_{2,n}
and
\widetilde{V}_{n}=V_{1,n}\oslash V_{2,n}
.

Then we have:

•

Any algorithm producing
o_{n}
with probability at least
\frac{9}{10}
must use at least
\Omega(n^{2}d)
bits of memory.

Proof.

We construct a reduction from the
Index
communication problem. In this context, Alice holds a bit string
x\in\{0,1\}^{n^{2}\times d}
(which can be viewed as an
n^{2}\times d
matrix), and Bob holds an index pair
(i,j)\in[n^{2}]\times[d]
. Alice sends a single message to Bob, whose goal is to output
x_{i,j}
with probability at least
2/3
. The communication complexity of this problem is
\Omega(n^{2}d)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a streaming algorithm for calculating the tensor attention function using
S
bits of memory. We will show that
S=\Omega(n^{2}d)
by constructing a protocol for the
Index
problem using
\mathcal{A}
.

Alice:

Alice inserts the following
n^{2}
triples
\{(q_{t},\widetilde{k}_{t},\widetilde{v}_{t})\}_{t=1}^{n^{2}}
into the streaming algorithm
\mathcal{A}
:

•

q_{1},\ldots,q_{n^{2}}
are all the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

•

\widetilde{k}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{k}_{n^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are calculated before the protocol starts using the JL lemma to produce vectors such that with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}
it holds for all
i\neq j
that:

\displaystyle|\widetilde{k}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{k}_{j}|\leq\epsilon

and for all
i\in[n^{2}]
that:

\displaystyle|\widetilde{k}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{k}_{i}-1|\leq\epsilon

•

\widetilde{v}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{v}_{n^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
contain the rows of
x\in\{0,1\}^{n^{2}\times d}
. In other words:

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{n^{2}}:=x

After inserting these
n^{2}
triples into
\mathcal{A}
, Alice sends its memory state (
S
bits) to Bob.

Bob:
With the index
(i,j)\in[n^{2}]\times[d]
, Bob enters a single triple
(q,\widetilde{k}_{n^{2}+1},\widetilde{v}_{n^{2}+1})
into
\mathcal{A}
:

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle:=C\cdot\widetilde{k}_{i}

\displaystyle\widetilde{k}_{n^{2}+1}

\displaystyle:={\bf 0}_{d}

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}_{n^{2}+1}

\displaystyle:={\bf 0}_{d}

where
C
is a positive value to be determined later.

Now, Bob can recover the value of
x_{i,j}
from the output
o_{n^{2}+1}
that
\mathcal{A}
produces:

\displaystyle o_{n^{2}+1}=\mathsf{Attn}(q,\widetilde{K}_{n^{2}+1},\widetilde{V}_{n^{2}+1})=\widetilde{V}_{n^{2}+1}^{\top}\cdot\sigma_{n^{2}+1}(\widetilde{K}_{n^{2}+1}\cdot q)

Starting with
s:=\widetilde{K}_{n^{2}+1}\cdot q\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}+1}
, we know with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}
that:

\displaystyle s_{\ell}\leq C\epsilon\mathrm{~for~}\ell\neq i\mathrm{~and~}s_{i}\geq C(1-\epsilon)

Let
\xi:=\sigma_{n^{2}+1}(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}+1}
. The softmax vector
\xi
“spikes” at index
i
, which allows Bob to extract
x_{i,j}
via the value matrix. The
j
-th entry is as follows with
\widetilde{v}_{n^{2}+1}={\bf 0}_{d}
..

\displaystyle(\widetilde{V}_{n^{2}+1}^{\top}\cdot\xi)_{j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}+1}\xi_{\ell}\cdot(\widetilde{v}_{\ell})_{j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}\xi_{\ell}\cdot x_{\ell,j}

Then, we have two cases:
x_{i,j}=0
and
x_{i,j}=1
.

Case One

{x_{i,j}=0}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}\xi_{\ell}\cdot x_{\ell,j}=

\displaystyle~0\cdot\xi_{i}+\sum_{\ell\in[n^{2}]\setminus\{i\}}\xi_{\ell}\cdot x_{\ell,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\ell\in[n^{2}]\setminus\{i\}}\xi_{\ell}\cdot x_{\ell,j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\sum_{\ell\in[n^{2}]\setminus\{i\}}\xi_{\ell}

where the first step follows from substitution, the second step follows from basic algebra, and the third step follows from
x_{\ell,j}\leq 1
.

Then, we can further show that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in[n^{2}]\setminus\{i\}}\xi_{\ell}=

\displaystyle~1-\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~1-\frac{e^{s_{i}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}e^{s_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}e^{s_{\ell}}-e^{s_{i}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}e^{s_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{\sum_{\ell\in[n^{2}]\setminus\{i\}}e^{s_{\ell}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}e^{s_{\ell}}}

where the first step follows from basic algebra, the second step follows from the definition of
\xi_{i}
, the third step follows from basic algebra, and the fourth step follows from basic summation rules.

Given that
s_{\ell}\leq C\epsilon
for
\ell\neq i
and
s_{i}\geq C(1-\epsilon)
, we can bound this as:

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{\ell\in[n^{2}]\setminus\{i\}}e^{s_{\ell}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}e^{s_{\ell}}}\leq\frac{n^{2}\cdot e^{C\epsilon}}{e^{C(1-\epsilon)}+n^{2}\cdot e^{C\epsilon}}:=\delta

Case Two

{x_{i,j}=1}
:
By substituting
x_{i,j}=1
, we can have

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}\xi_{\ell}\cdot x_{\ell,j}=

\displaystyle~\xi_{i}+\sum_{\ell\in[n^{2}]\setminus\{i\}}\xi_{\ell}\cdot x_{\ell,j}

Since
\xi_{i}=\frac{e^{s_{i}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}e^{s_{\ell}}}
, we can establish a lower bound:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}\xi_{\ell}\cdot x_{\ell,j}\geq

\displaystyle~\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{e^{s_{i}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}}e^{s_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{e^{C(1-\epsilon)}}{e^{C(1-\epsilon)}+n^{2}\cdot e^{C\epsilon}}:=\Delta

where the first step follows from basic inequality, the second step follows from the definition of
\xi_{i}
, the third step follows from
s_{i}\geq C(1-\epsilon)
.

For Bob to distinguish two cases, we need to compare the upper bound of case one with the lower bound of case two.

Since both
\delta
and
\Delta
have the same denominator, if the final goal is to show
\delta<\Delta
, it is sufficient to show

\displaystyle n^{2}\cdot e^{C\epsilon}<e^{C(1-\epsilon)}

The above equation is equivalent to

\displaystyle n^{2}<e^{C(1-2\epsilon)}

which is further equivalent to

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle>\frac{2\ln n}{1-2\epsilon}

(7)

When
n\geq 3
and
\epsilon\in(0,0.1)
, we choose
C=4\ln n
, which we automatically satisfy Eq. (
7
), allowing Bob to determine the
j
-th component of the attention output correctly.

Then we conclude the overall bits communicated is equal to the memory of
\mathcal{A}
, which is
\Omega(n^{2}d)
bits.

Thus, we complete the proof.
∎

This theorem demonstrates that precomputing the tensor structure in two cache matrices incurs a memory cost of
\Omega(n^{2}d)
, a quadratic increase over the four-matrix case, highlighting the trade-off between preprocessing and space complexity in attention-based computations.

B.7
Definition of Covering Number

To analyze clusterability in low-dimensional spaces, we introduce the covering number, a geometric concept that quantifies the complexity of covering a set with balls of a given radius. This definition, adapted from
[
143
]
, supports our subsequent bounds.

Definition B.7

(Covering Number,
[
143
]
)
.

Define the covering number
N(\Theta,\|\cdot\|,\delta)
of a set
\Theta
with respect to a norm
\|\cdot\|
and a radius
\delta
as the minimum number of
\|\cdot\|
-balls of radius
\delta
needed to cover
\Theta
.

The covering number provides a tool to estimate the number of clusters required for a dataset, linking geometric properties to algorithmic efficiency in low-dimensional settings.
