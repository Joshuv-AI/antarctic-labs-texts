Compressible N-phase fluid mixture models
M.F.P. ten Eikelder, E.H. van Brummelen, D. Schillinger

Abstract
Fluid mixture models are essential for describing a wide range of physical phenomena, including wave dynamics and spinodal decomposition. However, there is a lack of consensus in the modeling of compressible mixtures, with limited connections between different classes of models. On the one hand, existing compressible two-phase flow models accurately describe wave dynamics, but do not incorporate phase separation mechanisms. On the other hand, phase-field technology in fluid dynamics consists of models incorporating spinodal decomposition, however, a general phase-field theory for compressible mixtures remains largely undeveloped. In this paper, we take an initial step toward bridging the gap between compressible two-phase flow models and phase-field models by developing a theory for compressible, isothermal N-phase mixtures. Our theory establishes a system of reduced complexity by formulating N mass balance laws alongside a single momentum balance law, thereby naturally extending the Navier-Stokes Korteweg model to N-phases and providing the Navier-Stokes Cahn-Hilliard/Allen-Cahn model for compressible mixtures. Key aspects of the framework include its grounding in continuum mixture theory and its preservation of thermodynamic consistency despite its reduced complexity.



Key words
. N-phase flows, compressible flow, phase-field models, mixture theory, thermodynamic consistency.

Introduction

1.1
Background

The modeling of compressible multiphase fluid mixtures is fundamental to a wide range of scientific and engineering applications, including geophysical flows, aerospace engineering, and industrial processes. A key challenge in describing such systems lies in formulating a mathematical framework that consistently captures the interactions between phases
‡
‡

‡

We utilize “phase” to denote different fluid constituents (e.g. air and water).
while accounting for compressibility effects, diffusion, and interfacial dynamics. A widely used class of PDE-based models for multiphase flows consists of sharp-interface models, which either explicitly track the phase boundaries and prescribe conditions at the interface
[
1
,
2
]
, or are approximated by smooth interface (level-set) approximations
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. These models have been successfully applied to problems where the interface between phases remains well-defined. Complex mixture problems, however, where phases are dispersed throughout the domain and interpenetrate one another, or emerge from phase separation, do not belong to the class of sharp-interface problems. As a consequence, these problems demand an alternative modeling approach. Below we discuss two classes of models that describe these complex mixtures:
compressible two-phase models
and
phase-field models
.

Compressible two-phase flow models
play a fundamental role in describing mixture flows, where compressibility and interfacial dynamics are essential. Among these models, the Baer-Nunziato model is often regarded as one of the most comprehensive frameworks for describing two-phase flows
[
6
]
. This diffuse-interface, non-equilibrium model consists of separate mass, momentum, and energy balance equations for each phase, along with an additional equation governing the topology of the two-fluid interface. Over time, significant extensions have been proposed, including generalizations to
N
-component mixtures (see e.g.
[
7
]
), broadening the applicability to more complex multiphase systems.

Despite their generality, Baer-Nunziato-type models introduce considerable computational complexity due to their large number of equations and wave families. To address this, reduced models with fewer equations have been developed
[
8
,
9
]
. These reduced formulations are often derived through relaxation procedures that assume velocity, pressure, or temperature equilibrium between phases
[
10
]
. However, such simplifications come at a cost: reduced models may fail to strictly conserve energy, suffer from an ill-posed mixture speed of sound
[
11
]
, or lose asymptotic consistency with the full nonlinear system
[
12
]
. These limitations motivate the exploration of alternative modeling approaches for compressible mixtures, particularly those that incorporate interfacial dynamics and phase transition effects in a thermodynamically consistent manner.

Over the past decades,
phase-field modeling
has become a valuable tool for moving boundary problems in computational fluid mechanics
[
13
,
14
]
. It addresses two key challenges: geometrical representation and physical modeling. The phase-field variable naturally captures topological changes, providing advantages over traditional interface-tracking methods for complex flows. Additionally, phase-field models offer a thermodynamically consistent framework for capillary effects, phase transitions, and material composition. They also provide an effective description of wetting, making them well-suited for complex wetting phenomena
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

The state-of-the art phase-field models for fluids may roughly be classified into two categories. The first category describes describes single-fluid flow with phase change
[
18
,
19
]
through the Navier-Stokes Korteweg (NSK) model. The two phases correspond to the liquid and gaseous states of the same component. In the NSK model, the density field itself acts as phase-field variable. Areas with low density identify as vapor and high-density regions as liquid. Liquid-vapor transitions are often incorporated in the NSK model by means of the Van der Waals capillarity model, named after the 1910 Nobel Laureate in physics
[
20
]
.

The second category consists of models that describes viscous (incompressible) mixture flow through the Navier-Stokes Cahn-Hilliard (Allen-Cahn) (NSCHAC) model. The origin of NSCHAC models may be traced back to 1977 when Hohenberg and Halperin proposed a NSCHAC model via the coupling between the Navier-Stokes equations, describing viscous fluid flow, and the Cahn-Hilliard equation, describing spinoidal decomposition
[
21
]
. The most important limitation of this model is that the density of the fluids are assumed equal. This precludes the applicability of the model to a large number of practical problems such as ink-droplet dynamics. Since the end of the last century, a number of efforts of extending the model to the case of non-matching densities have been made, e.g.
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. Each of these works aims to describe the same physics, yet the proposed models are different. The occurrence of a many NSCH(AC) models shows that consensus in the realm of single-phase multi-component flows has been missing. Note that this is in contrast to single-fluid flow with phase change, where the NSK model is the widely adopted model. In two recent articles we have proposed a unified mixture-theory framework of NSCHAC models as a resolution to this open problem (see
[
28
,
29
]
for the theoretical framework and
[
30
]
for supporting benchmark computations). The framework indicates a single NSCHAC model that is invariant to the choice of fundamental variables.

1.2
Objective and main results

Although significant progress has been made in modeling multiphase flows, a general phase-field theory for compressible mixtures remains largely undeveloped, despite some important contributions (see e.g.
[
31
,
32
]
and the references therein). While compressible two-phase flow models exist and provide a well-established framework for describing compressible mixtures, they differ fundamentally from phase-field models in both structure and formulation. Phase-field models, on the other hand, have been extensively used for capturing interfacial dynamics in incompressible fluid mixtures but have not been systematically extended to fully compressible regimes. As a result, the connection between compressible two-phase flow models and phase-field approaches is largely unresolved. Although both modeling strategies aim to describe similar physical processes, their underlying formulations remain distinct. This disconnect has hindered the development of a unified theoretical framework that accommodates both compressibility effects and the diffuse-interface nature of phase-field models.

In this paper, we take an initial step toward expanding the theoretical foundation of compressible mixture modeling by developing a thermodynamically consistent theory for compressible, isothermal
N
-phase mixtures. As part of this development, we draw connections to both phase-field models and compressible two-phase flow models, outlining key points of overlap and distinction. Our approach introduces a set of
N
mass balance equations coupled with a single momentum balance law.
In particular, we derive the following multi-phase-field model for constituents
\alpha=1,...,N
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\sum_{{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\nabla\mu_{\beta}-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\mathbf{D}+\lambda({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})-{\rm div}\left(\sum_{{\beta}}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\mu_{\beta}\right)+\sum_{{\beta}}m_{\alpha{\beta}}\mu_{\beta}

\displaystyle=~0,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}-\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}+{\rm div}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=~0,

(1.1c)

where
\mathbf{v}
is the fluid velocity,
\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}
are the partial densities,

is the mixture density,
\mathbf{g}
is the force vector,

\mathbf{D}
is the symmetric velocity gradient. Furthermore,

A distinguishing feature of the model, in contrast to compressible two-phase flow models, is its preservation of thermodynamic consistency despite its reduced complexity (i.e. the combination of
N
mass balance laws with just a single momentum balance law). Furthermore, the model connects to phase-field models and distinguishes itself from compressible two-phase flow models as follows. For specific closure models, it extends the Navier-Stokes-Korteweg (NSK) framework to multiple fluids, where the last two terms in (
1.1b

Additionally, under particular closure choices, these terms take the form of Cahn-Hilliard and Allen-Cahn-type contributions, establishing a direct link to NSCH(AC) models. Key distinctions from existing compressible two-phase flow models lie in (1) the absence of an explicit evolution equation for the volume fraction and (2) the formulation of the equation of state, as given in (

2
Continuum mixture theory

This section outlines the continuum theory for mixtures of compressible, isothermal constituents, serving as a foundational framework. While the theory is well known, its inclusion here is essential, particularly as it differs from the starting point for the design of compressible two-phase flow models. The outlined mixture theory description is closely aligned with
[
33
,
29
]
, underscoring its suitability as a first-principles basis for the development for a wide range of models.

Continuum mixture theory is based on three general principles outlined in the groundbreaking work of Truesdell and Toupin
[
34
,
35
]
:

1.

All properties of the mixture must be mathematical consequences of properties of the constituents.

2.

So as to describe the motion of a constituent, we may in imagination isolate it from the rest of the mixture, provided we allow properly for the actions of the other constituents upon it.

3.

The motion of the mixture is governed by the same equations as is a single body.

The first principle explains that the mixture is formed by its individual constituents. The second principle describes that the components of the physical model are interconnected through interaction terms. Lastly, the third principle states that the movement of the mixture cannot be distinguished from that of a single fluid.

In
Section

2.1
, we provide the groundwork for the continuum theory of mixtures, covering essential kinematics. Following this,
Section

2.2
presents the evolution laws for individual constituents and mixtures.

2.1

Instead of working with the full set of balance laws (

\displaystyle=~0,

(3.1c)

and where

(

Remark 3.1

(Decomposition mass transfer)
.

Rather than modeling
\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}
and
\zeta_{\alpha}

and

is introduced solely for interpretative purposes. For example, assuming identical constituent velocities,

We postulate the energy-dissipation law:

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathscr{E}=\mathscr{W}-\mathscr{D},

Remark 3.2

(Energy-Dissipation Statement)

In the energy-dissipation law (

In this article we postulate the free energy to belong to the constitutive class:

\displaystyle\Psi=\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N},\left\{\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N}\right),

where we note that both
\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N}

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}.

(3.5)

Remark 3.3

(Relation to compressible two-phase flow models)
.

The modeling choices presented in this section distinguish our approach from conventional compressible two-phase flow models in several important ways. First, our formulation is based on the reduced set of balance laws (
3.1
). In contrast, compressible two-phase flow models typically include additional evolution equations for the volume fractions, which do not naturally arise from the continuum mixture theory described in
Section

2
. Consequently, while the state variables in compressible two-phase flow models consist of specific densities
\{\rho_{\alpha}\}
and volume fractions
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}
(subject to the saturation constraint (
2.5b
)), our approach employs partial densities
\{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\}
instead. Finally, the constitutive class (
3.4
) depends on all partial densities, thereby enabling the modeling of interaction (Van der Waals) forces—an aspect that is absent in standard compressible two-phase flow models.

Remark 3.4

(Constitutive class free energy)
.

In many conventional phase-field models, including those of the Cahn-Hilliard type, the free energy is expressed in terms of order parameters such as volume fractions or mass fractions (often referred to as concentrations). In contrast, the constitutive class (
3.4
) is formulated in terms of partial densities. We explore the relationship between these approaches in the binary case in
Section

6
and provide the corresponding derivation in
Appendix

C
.

3.2
Modeling restriction

We proceed with the evaluation of the evolution of the energy (

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\hat{\Psi}~{\rm d}v=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\partial_{t}\hat{\Psi}~{\rm d}v+\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{R}(t)}\hat{\Psi}\mathbf{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}~{\rm d}a.

(3.6)

We apply the divergence theorem and expand the derivatives:

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\hat{\Psi}~{\rm d}v=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}

\displaystyle~\hat{\Psi}~{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\sum_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{\alpha}+\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\cdot\left(\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right)\dot{}~{\rm d}v.

(3.7)

Substituting the following identity for the material derivative:

\displaystyle(\nabla\omega)\dot{}=\nabla(\dot{\omega})-(\nabla\omega)^{T}\nabla\mathbf{v},

(3.8)

with
\omega=\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}
into (
3.7
) yields:

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\hat{\Psi}~{\rm d}v=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}

\displaystyle~\hat{\Psi}~{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\sum_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{\alpha}+\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\cdot\nabla(\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle~-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\cdot(\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha})^{T}\nabla\mathbf{v}~{\rm d}v.

(3.9)

By subsequently integrating by parts, we arrive at:

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\hat{\Psi}~{\rm d}v=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\hat{\Psi}~{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{\alpha}-\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}:\nabla\mathbf{v}~{\rm d}v

\displaystyle~+\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{R}(t)}\sum_{\alpha}\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}~{\rm d}a.

(3.10)

Substituting the constituent mass balance laws (
2.14
) and applying integration by parts leads to:

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\hat{\Psi}~{\rm d}v=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\hat{\Psi}~{\rm div}\mathbf{v}-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle~~~-\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}:\nabla\mathbf{v}+\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\zeta_{\alpha}~{\rm d}v

\displaystyle~+\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{R}(t)}\left(\sum_{\alpha}\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}-\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}~{\rm d}a.

(3.11)

Subsequently, the evolution equations for the kinetic and gravitational energies follow straightforward from (
3.1a
) and (
3.1b
) (see
[
28
]
for details):

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\mathscr{K}~{\rm d}v=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}-\nabla\mathbf{v}:\mathbf{T}+\rho\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{g}~{\rm d}v+\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{R}(t)}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}~{\rm d}a,

(3.12a)

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\mathscr{G}~{\rm d}v=

\displaystyle~-\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\rho\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{g}~{\rm d}v.

(3.12b)

Summing (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) yields:

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathscr{E}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{R}(t)}\left(\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{T}-\sum_{\alpha}\left(\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}-\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\right)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}~{\rm d}a

\displaystyle~-\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\left(\mathbf{T}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}+\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}\right):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+\sum_{\alpha}\left(-\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\zeta_{\alpha}\right)~{\rm d}v,

(3.13)

where we identify the rate of work and the dissipation as:

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{R}(t)}\left(\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{T}-\sum_{\alpha}\left(\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}-\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\right)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}~{\rm d}a,

(3.14a)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}\left(\mathbf{T}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}+\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}\right):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+\sum_{\alpha}\left(-\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\zeta_{\alpha}\right)~{\rm d}v.

(3.14b)

Since the control volume
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(t)
is arbitrary, the energy-dissipation law is satisfied provided that the following local inequality holds:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{T}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}+\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}\right):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha}\zeta_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

(3.15)

3.3
Selection of constitutive models

By means of the Colemann-Noll procedure, the inequality (
3.15
) permits to restrict to mixture stress tensors
\mathbf{T}
, constituent diffusive fluxes
\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}
and constituent mass fluxes
\zeta_{\alpha}
that belong to the constitutive classes:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{T}}\left(\nabla\mathbf{v},\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\right\},\left\{\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\right\},\left\{\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right\},\left\{\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right\}\right),

(3.16a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha}\left(\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\right\},\left\{\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\right\},\left\{\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right\},\left\{\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right\}\right),

(3.16b)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}\left(\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\right\},\left\{\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right\}\right).

(3.16c)

We seek for constitutive models (
3.16
) that render each of the three terms in (
3.15
) non-negative:

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathbf{T}}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}+\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}\right):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.17a)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.17b)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

(3.17c)

Constitutive choices
. We make the following constitutive choices:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{T}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}-\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}+\nu\left(2\mathbf{D}+\lambda({\rm div}\,\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right),

(3.18a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta},

(3.18b)

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\beta}m_{\alpha{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{\beta},

(3.18c)

where
\nu\geq 0
is the dynamic viscosity,
\lambda\geq-2/d
(with
d
denoting the number of dimensions),
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}
is a symmetric positive definite tensor, with the same dependencies as (
3.16b
), satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=0,\quad\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}|_{Y_{\gamma}=1}=0\quad\text{for all }{\gamma}=1,\dots,N,

(3.19)

and
m_{\alpha{\beta}}
is a symmetric positive definite scalar mobility, with the same dependencies as (
3.16c
), satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}m_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sum_{\beta}m_{\alpha{\beta}}=0,\quad m_{\alpha{\beta}}|_{Y_{\gamma}=1}=0\quad\text{for all }{\gamma}=1,\dots,N.

(3.20)

Moreover, compatibility with the angular momentum condition (
3.1c
) requires

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}=\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\otimes\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}.

(3.21)

Lemma 3.5

(Compatibility of constitutive choices)
.

The choices (
3.18a
)–(
3.18c
) are compatible with the restrictions (
3.17a
)–(
3.17c
), as well as with the balance of relative gross motion (
2.11
) and the balance of mass supply (
2.18a
).

Proof.

For the stress tensor, a direct calculation yields

\displaystyle\Biggl(\hat{\mathbf{T}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}+\Bigl(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\Bigr)\mathbf{I}\Biggr):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=2\nu\Biggl(\mathbf{D}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\,\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\Biggr):\Biggl(\mathbf{D}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\,\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\Biggr)+\nu\Biggl(\lambda+\frac{2}{d}\Biggr)({\rm div}\,\mathbf{v})^{2}\geq 0,

which confirms compatibility with (
3.17a
). For the diffusive flux, the condition
\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=0
guarantees compatibility with the balance (
2.11
), while the positive semi-definiteness of
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}
ensures the thermodynamical restriction (
3.17b
). Similarly, for the mass transfer term, the properties
\sum_{\alpha}m_{\alpha{\beta}}=0
and the positive definiteness of
m_{\alpha{\beta}}
ensure compatibility with the mass supply balance (
2.18a
) and the restriction (
3.17c
).
∎

Remark 3.6

(Mass transfer in compressible two-phase flow models)
.

We consider the special case:

\displaystyle m_{\alpha{\beta}}=\begin{cases}-\hat{m}_{\alpha{\beta}},&\text{if }\alpha\neq{\beta},\\
\sum_{{\gamma}\neq\alpha}\hat{m}_{\alpha{\gamma}},&\text{if }\alpha={\beta}.\end{cases}

(3.22)

Under this assumption, we obtain:

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\beta}\neq\alpha}m_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}-m_{\alpha\alpha}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\beta}\neq\alpha}\hat{m}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}-\sum_{{\gamma}\neq\alpha}\hat{m}_{\alpha{\gamma}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\beta}}\hat{m}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla(\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mu}_{\beta}).

(3.23)

This formulation is commonly used for binary flows in compressible two-phase models; see, e.g.
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
.

This concludes the fundamental exploration of constitutive models compatible with the imposed energy-dissipative modeling restriction. Substitution provides the following model:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\nabla p+{\rm div}\left(\sum_{\beta}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}\right)

\displaystyle-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\mathbf{D}+\lambda({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(3.24a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})-\sum_{{\beta}}{\rm div}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right)+\sum_{{\beta}}m_{\alpha{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=~0,

(3.24b)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}+{\rm div}\left(\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=~0,

(3.24c)

where the pressure
p
takes the form:

\displaystyle p=\sum_{\beta}(\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta})-\hat{\Psi}.

(3.25)

Remark 3.7

(Relation to Cahn-Hilliard/Allen-Cahn/Korteweg models)
.

The framework connects to Cahn-Hilliard/Allen-Cahn/Korteweg-type models by selecting

\hat{\Psi}=\hat{\Psi}_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{{\beta}{\gamma}}\lambda_{{\beta}{\gamma}}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\gamma}
, where
\hat{\Psi}_{0}=\hat{\Psi}_{0}(\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\right\})
and where
\lambda_{{\beta}{\gamma}}
are model parameters.

Remark 3.8

(Alternative pressure variable)
.

A common alternative approach is to decompose the stress tensor into its volumetric (isotropic) and deviatoric (traceless) components, which allows one to define the volumetric pressure
p^{\rm vol}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{T}}=

\displaystyle~-p^{\rm vol}\mathbf{I}+\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{\rm dev},

(3.26a)

\displaystyle p^{\rm vol}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta}\left(\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}-\frac{1}{d}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}\right)-\hat{\Psi}-\nu\left(\lambda+\frac{2}{d}\right){\rm div}\mathbf{v},

(3.26b)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{T}}^{\rm dev}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta}\left(\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}-\frac{1}{d}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}\mathbf{I}\right)+2\nu\left(\mathbf{D}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right).

(3.26c)

We explicitly state the compatibility with the energy dissipation condition.

Theorem 3.9

(Compatibility energy dissipation)
.

The model (
3.24
) is compatible with the energy dissipation condition (
3.2
).

Proof.

This follows from
Lemma

3.5
, in particular we have:

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}2\nu\left(\mathbf{D}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right):\left(\mathbf{D}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right)+\nu\left(\lambda+\frac{2}{d}\right)\left({\rm div}\mathbf{v}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle~\quad\quad+\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}M_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}+\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}m_{\alpha{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}~{\rm d}v\geq 0.

(3.27)

Properties

. Next, we discuss the decomposition of the free energy in

4.1
Compact formulations

Simplification of the model (
3.24
) can be achieved by employing the following identity.

Lemma 4.1

(Identity Korteweg stresses)
.

We have the following identity for the pressure and Korteweg stresses:

\displaystyle\nabla p+{\rm div}\left(\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\right)=\sum_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}.

(4.1)

Proof.

See
Lemma

A.1
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The identity in
Lemma

4.1
represents the multi-component extension of the well-known identity for the Korteweg stress.

Applying
Lemma

4.1
we find the more compact form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\sum_{{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\mathbf{D}+\lambda({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})-\sum_{{\beta}}{\rm div}\left(M_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right)+\sum_{{\beta}}m_{\alpha{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=~0.

(4.2b)

Furthermore, we note that the model may alternatively be written in a form that more closely links to existing phase-field models:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\rho\sum_{{\beta}}Y_{\beta}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\mathbf{D}+\lambda({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.3a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+{\rm div}(\rho\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.3b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho Y_{\alpha})+{\rm div}(\rho Y_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})-\sum_{{\beta}}{\rm div}\left(M_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right)+\sum_{{\beta}}m_{\alpha{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.3c)

for
\alpha=1,...,N-1
. We note that the combination of the mixture mass balance (
4.3b
) and the
N-1
constituent balance laws (
4.3c
) are equivalent to the
N
balance laws (
4.2b
) or
N
balance laws (
4.3c
).

We observe that, in the single-fluid regime, the model simplifies to the compressible Navier–Stokes equations.

Proposition 4.3

(Reduction to compressible Navier-Stokes)
.

If the chemical potentials
\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}
are well-defined for
\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}=0
(
\alpha=1,...,N
), the multi-constituent system (
4.3
) reduces to the compressible Navier-Stokes equations in the single-constituent regime (
Y_{\beta}=1
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\nabla p-{\rm div}\left(\nu\left(2\mathbf{D}+\lambda{\rm div}\mathbf{v}\right)\right)-\rho\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.4a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+{\rm div}(\rho\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.4b)

with
\rho_{\beta}=\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}=\rho
and
p=p(\rho)
.

4.2
Decomposition of the free energy

We proceed with decomposing the free energy into its components; in agreement with the first metaphysical principle of continuum mixture theory we have:

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N},\left\{\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N}\right)=\sum_{\beta}\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}\left(\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N},\left\{\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1,...,N}\right),

(4.5)

where
\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}
is the volume-measure constituent free energy. The split of the free energy, eq. (
4.5
), highlights that the constituent free energies may depend on quantities of other constituents (
\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}
and
\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha=1,...,N
) allowing for the incorporation of e.g. attraction/repelling forces.

Next, we associate
constituent chemical potentials
and
constituent partial pressures
with each
\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}
in the sense:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{{\beta}\alpha}:=

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}},

(4.6a)

\displaystyle p_{\beta}=

\displaystyle~\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{{\beta}\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right)-\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}.

(4.6b)

The constituent partial pressure
p_{\alpha}
are an extension of the classical partial pressure that includes gradient contributions essential to modeling surface tension effects. These constituent quantities satisfy the properties:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{{\beta}\alpha},

(4.7a)

\displaystyle p=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta}p_{\beta}.

(4.7b)

The latter may be referred to as
Dalton’s law
.
As such, the split (
4.5
) reveals that the system may be written as:

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta}\left(\partial_{t}(\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\nabla p_{\beta}+{\rm div}\left(\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\right)-\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\mathbf{g}\right)

\displaystyle-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\mathbf{D}+\lambda({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.8a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})-\sum_{{\beta}}{\rm div}\left(M_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right)+\sum_{{\beta}}m_{\alpha{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=~0.

(4.8b)

Remark 4.4

(Absence of interaction forces)
.

When interaction forces are absent, i.e.
\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}=\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}(\tilde{\rho}_{\beta},\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta})
we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{{\beta}\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha{\beta}},

(4.9a)

\displaystyle p_{{\beta}}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}-\hat{\Psi}_{\beta},

(4.9b)

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}=\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}.

(4.10)

4.3
Equilibrium conditions

We characterize the equilibrium conditions of the model (
4.8
) by:

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}=0.

(4.11)

The identity (
3.27
) provides:

\displaystyle 2\nu^{\rm eq}\left(\mathbf{D}^{\rm eq}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v}^{\rm eq})\mathbf{I}\right):\left(\mathbf{D}^{\rm eq}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v}^{\rm eq})\mathbf{I}\right)=

\displaystyle~0,

(4.12a)

\displaystyle\nu^{\rm eq}\left(\lambda+\frac{2}{d}\right)\left({\rm div}\mathbf{v}^{\rm eq}\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle~0,

(4.12b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}^{\rm eq}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\cdot\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}^{\rm eq}=

\displaystyle~0,

(4.12c)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}m_{\alpha{\beta}}^{\rm eq}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}^{\rm eq}=

\displaystyle~0,

(4.12d)

where the superscripts ‘
{\rm eq}
’ denote the equilibrium of the quantity.
Identities (
4.12a
) and (
4.12b
) dictate that
\mathbf{v}^{\rm eq}
are rigid motions. Since
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}
is symmetric positive definite, we decompose it as
(\mathbf{M}_{ij})_{\alpha{\beta}}=(\mathbf{B}_{ij})_{\alpha}(\mathbf{B}_{ij})_{{\beta}}
(with
i,j
the indices of the tensor), for some
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
with the same dependencies as
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}
. Hence, (
4.12c
) implies
\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}=0
. In a similar fashion we deduce
\sum_{\alpha}b_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}=0
where
m_{\alpha{\beta}}=b_{\alpha}b_{{\beta}}
. These conditions imply
\hat{\mathbf{H}}^{\rm eq}_{\alpha}=0,\hat{\zeta}^{\rm eq}_{\alpha}=0
for
\alpha=1,...,N
. Finally, restricting to zero velocities,
\mathbf{v}^{\rm eq}=\mathbf{0}
, the mass balance laws imply constant in time partial densities,
\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}(\mathbf{x},t)=\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}(\mathbf{x})
, while the momentum balance dictates:
\sum_{\alpha}Y_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}=\mathbf{g}
. Summarizing, we have the equilibrium conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\rm eq}=

\displaystyle~\mathbf{0},

(4.13a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}=

\displaystyle~{\rm const}~(\text{in time}),

(4.13b)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}b_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}=

\displaystyle~0,

(4.13c)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}=

\displaystyle~0,

(4.13d)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}Y_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}=

\displaystyle~\mathbf{g}.

(4.13e)

Remark 4.5

(No equilibrium conditions pressure)
.

If the free energy
\Psi
does not depend on
\left\{\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\right\}
, the condition (
4.13e
) converts into
\nabla p=\rho\mathbf{g}
(see
Lemma

4.1
). Thus there is no equilibrium condition on the partial pressures
p_{\alpha},\alpha=1,...,N
. This is in contrast to some compressible two-phase models in which pressure equilibrium is assumed, see e.g.
[
8
,
9
,
40
]
(and
Section

7.1
).

5
Analysis of the first-order system

In this section, we examine the first-order system associated with (
3.24
). We first introduce the system in

5.1
First-order system

We study the first-order system associated with (
3.24
) in absence of mass transfer (
\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}=0
) and body forces (
\mathbf{b}=0
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\nabla p

\displaystyle=~0,

(5.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=~0.

(5.1b)

The system satisfies the energy law (
3.2
) with
\mathscr{D}=0
. This may be written in the local form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\Psi+\bar{\mathscr{K}}\right)+{\rm div}\left(\left(\Psi+\bar{\mathscr{K}}\right)\mathbf{v}\right)=0.

(5.2)

Remark 5.1

(Derivation energy law)
.

The energy law (
5.2
) follows directly from (
5.1
) by multiplying with the appropriate weights. In this regard, we notice the following identities:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}\left(\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})\right)=

\displaystyle~\partial_{t}\Psi+{\rm div}(\Psi\mathbf{v})+p{\rm div}\mathbf{v},

(5.3a)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{v}|^{2}\left(\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})\right)\right)=

\displaystyle~-\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{v}|^{2}\left(\partial_{t}\rho+{\rm div}(\rho\mathbf{v})\right),

(5.3b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\cdot\left(\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v})+\nabla p\right)=

\displaystyle~\partial_{t}\bar{\mathscr{K}}+{\rm div}(\bar{\mathscr{K}}\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla p

\displaystyle~+\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{v}|^{2}\left(\partial_{t}\rho+{\rm div}(\rho\mathbf{v})\right).

(5.3c)

Remark 5.2

(Energy-law for non-smooth solutions)
.

In case of shocks or other regularities the stability condition takes the form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\Psi+\bar{\mathscr{K}}\right)+{\rm div}\left(\left(\Psi+\bar{\mathscr{K}}\right)\mathbf{v}\right)\leq 0,

(5.4)

which may be established using vanishing-viscosity solutions.

In order to analyze the wave speeds of the model, we deduce the evolution equations of the partial pressures from (
4.2b
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{\alpha}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla p_{\alpha}+\sum_{{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}{\rm div}\mathbf{v}=0,

(5.5a)

where the speed of sound quantities
a_{\alpha{\beta}}
account for the interaction between constituents:

\displaystyle a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}:=\dfrac{\partial p_{\alpha}}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}.

(5.6)

The evolution of the classical mixture pressure results from the superposition of the constituent pressure equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla p+\rho a^{2}{\rm div}\mathbf{v}=0,

(5.7)

where the mixture speed of sound
a
satisfies:

\displaystyle\rho a^{2}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{{\beta}}^{2},

(5.8)

and the constituent speed of sound quantities are given by:

\displaystyle a_{\beta}^{2}=

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}=\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}.

(5.9)

Remark 5.3

(Speed of sound compressible two-phase models)
.

The explicit dependence of the free energy on each constituent (see eq. (
3.4
)) is reflected in the definitions of the sound speeds
a_{\beta}
and
a_{\alpha{\beta}}
. This approach contrasts with the sound speed definitions typically employed in compressible two-phase flow models (e.g.,
[
6
]
). In the absence of interaction forces (cf.
Remark

4.4
), the relation reduces to
a_{\beta}^{2}=\mathrm{d}p_{\beta}/\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}
, where the standard definition in those models is
a_{\beta}^{2}=\mathrm{d}p_{\beta}/\mathrm{d}\rho_{\beta}
.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}+\rho^{-1}\nabla p=

\displaystyle~0,

(5.10a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{\alpha}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla p_{\alpha}+\sum_{{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}{\rm div}\mathbf{v}=

\displaystyle~0,

where the momentum equation is a direct consequence from (

Remark 5.4

(Van der Waals equation of state)
.

The van der Waals equation of state does not, in general, guarantee that
a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}>0
, and consequently, the resulting system is not hyperbolic.

Proposition 5.5

(Structure of the waves)
.

The characteristic fields associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j},j=2,...,N
are linearly degenerate (i.e.
\mathbf{r}_{j}\cdot\partial\lambda_{j}/\partial\mathbf{W}=0
), and those associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j},j=1,N+1
are genuinely non-linear (i.e.
\mathbf{r}_{j}\cdot\partial\lambda_{j}/\partial\mathbf{W}\neq 0
).

This means that fields associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j},j=2,...,N
correspond to contact discontinuities, whereas fields associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j},j=1,N+1
correspond to and shock or rarefaction waves, respectively.

Proof.

A direct computation shows that the fields associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j},j=2,...,N
are linearly degenerate (i.e.
\mathbf{r}_{j}\cdot\partial\lambda_{j}/\partial\mathbf{W}=0
).

Next we focus on the field associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j},j=1,N+1
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{1}\cdot\partial\lambda_{1}/\partial\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{r}_{N+1}\cdot\partial\lambda_{N+1}/\partial\mathbf{W}=a+\sum_{\alpha{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial a}{\partial p_{\alpha}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}.

(5.17)

Next we note that for an arbitrary variable
\omega
we have:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial a}{\partial\omega}=\frac{1}{2a\rho}\left(\dfrac{\partial(\rho a^{2})}{\partial\omega}-a^{2}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\omega}\right).

(5.18)

Applying this identity for
\omega=p_{\alpha}
and substituting (
5.8
) yields:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial a}{\partial p_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2a\rho}\left(\sum_{{\gamma}{\tau}}\dfrac{\partial(\tilde{\rho}_{\tau}a_{{\gamma}{\tau}}^{2})}{\partial p_{\alpha}}-a^{2}\sum_{\tau}\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\tau}}{\partial p_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2a\rho}\left(\sum_{{\tau}{\gamma}}\tilde{\rho}_{\tau}\dfrac{\partial a_{{\gamma}{\tau}}^{2}}{\partial p_{\alpha}}+\sum_{{\tau}{\gamma}}a_{{\gamma}{\tau}}^{2}(a^{2})^{-1}_{{\tau}\alpha}-a^{2}\sum_{\tau}(a^{2})^{-1}_{{\tau}\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2a\rho}\left(\sum_{{\tau}}\tilde{\rho}_{\tau}\dfrac{\partial a_{{\tau}}^{2}}{\partial p_{\alpha}}+1-a^{2}\sum_{{\tau}}(a^{2})^{-1}_{{\tau}\alpha}\right).

(5.19)

Inserting into (
5.17
) gives:

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{1}\cdot\partial\lambda_{1}/\partial\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{r}_{N+1}\cdot\partial\lambda_{N+1}/\partial\mathbf{W}=

\displaystyle~a+\sum_{\alpha{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial a}{\partial p_{\alpha}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~a+\frac{1}{2a\rho}\sum_{\alpha{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}\left(\sum_{{\tau}}\tilde{\rho}_{\tau}\dfrac{\partial a_{{\tau}}^{2}}{\partial p_{\alpha}}+1-a^{2}\sum_{{\tau}}(a^{2})^{-1}_{{\tau}\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~a+\frac{1}{2a\rho}\left(\sum_{\alpha{\beta}{\tau}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}\tilde{\rho}_{\tau}\dfrac{\partial a_{{\tau}}^{2}}{\partial p_{\alpha}}+\sum_{\alpha{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}-a^{2}\sum_{{\tau}}\tilde{\rho}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~a+\frac{1}{2a\rho}\sum_{\alpha{\beta}{\tau}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}\tilde{\rho}_{\tau}\dfrac{\partial a_{{\tau}}^{2}}{\partial p_{\alpha}},

(5.20)

which is strictly positive by the aforementioned assumption
a_{\alpha{\beta}},a>0
in combination with noting that the phase speed of sound increases with the pressure
\partial a_{{\tau}}/\partial p_{\alpha}>0
(see also
[
9
]
for a similar assumption).

5.3
Riemann invariants and jump conditions

Next, we derive the Riemann invariants of the first-order system (
5.1
).

Proposition 5.6
.

The Riemann invariants associated with the various waves are:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}:

\displaystyle\quad\left\{\left\{Y_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}},v+\int_{p}\frac{1}{\rho a}{\rm d}p\right\}

(5.21a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{N}:

\displaystyle\quad\left\{v,p\right\}

(5.21b)

\displaystyle\lambda_{N+1}:

\displaystyle\quad\left\{\left\{Y_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}},v-\int_{p}\frac{1}{\rho a}{\rm d}p\right\},

(5.21c)

for some fixed
{\beta}\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
.

Proof.

To find the Riemann invariants, we seek for
\omega_{i}
such that
\partial_{\mathbf{W}}\omega_{i}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}=0
. Focusing on the Riemann invariants associated with
\lambda_{1}
, we have:

\displaystyle 0=\dfrac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{W}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{1}=a\dfrac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial v}-\sum_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\beta}^{2}\dfrac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial p_{\beta}}.

(5.22)

A direct computation reveals that
Y_{\alpha}=\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}/\rho
are Riemann invariants:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial Y_{\alpha}}{\partial\mathbf{W}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{1}=

\displaystyle~-\sum_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\beta}^{2}\dfrac{\partial Y_{\alpha}}{\partial p_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~-\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\beta}^{2}\dfrac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}{\partial p_{\beta}}+\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}{\rho^{2}}\sum_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}a_{\beta}^{2}\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~-\frac{1}{\rho}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}+\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}}{\rho^{2}}\sum_{\beta}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~0.

(5.23)

Next, we verify that
v+\int_{p}\frac{1}{\rho a}{\rm d}p
is the final Riemann invariant:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial v+\int_{p}\frac{1}{\rho a}{\rm d}p}{\partial\mathbf{W}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{1}=

\displaystyle~a\dfrac{\partial v+\int_{p}\frac{1}{\rho a}{\rm d}p}{\partial v}-\sum_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}^{2}\dfrac{\partial v+\int_{p}\frac{1}{\rho a}{\rm d}p}{\partial p_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~a-\sum_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}^{2}\dfrac{\partial\int_{p}\frac{1}{\rho a}{\rm d}p}{\partial p_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~a-\sum_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}^{2}\frac{1}{\rho a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~0.

(5.24)

In a similar fashion we find that the Riemann invariants associated with
\lambda_{N+1}
are
Y_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=1,...,N
and
v-\int_{p}\frac{1}{\rho a}{\rm d}p
.
Finally, we compute the Riemann invariants associated with
\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{N}
:

\displaystyle 0=\dfrac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{W}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}=-\dfrac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial p_{1}}+\dfrac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial p_{j}},\quad\text{for }j=2,...,N.

(5.25)

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial p_{1}}=\dots=\dfrac{\partial\omega_{i}}{\partial p_{N}}.

(5.26)

It is now easy to verify that
\omega_{i}=v,p
solve system (
5.26
).
∎

Finally, we note that the conservative nature of the system permits to uniquely define shock-waves due to the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions:

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}(v-\sigma)\right)=

\displaystyle~0,\quad\alpha=1,...,N

(5.27a)

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\rho v(v-\sigma)+p\right)=

\displaystyle~0,

(5.27b)

with
\Delta\phi=\phi_{R}-\phi_{L}
the jump between the left and right states of the shock-wave, and
\sigma
the speed of the shock-wave.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we have developed a thermodynamical framework for compressible, isothermal
N
-phase mixtures. The framework is formulated within the principles of continuum mixture theory, which serves as a fundamental basis for modeling multi-physics systems. We have established some connections with existing phase-field and compressible two-phase flow models, in an effort to initiate to the gap between these fields of research. Specifically, the proposed formulation extends the Navier-Stokes-Korteweg (NSK) model to multiple fluids and adapts the Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard/Allen-Cahn (NSCH/AC) models to general
N
-phase compressible mixtures. A key distinction from existing compressible two-phase flow models is that reducing the complexity of those models (i.e., using fewer equations) often leads to the loss or degradation of essential thermodynamic properties. In contrast, the framework proposed in this work maintains thermodynamic consistency regardless of its level of complexity.
Several open challenges remain for future research. We highlight some key directions. First, the framework offers a foundation for studying spinodal decomposition in compressible mixtures, with potential applications in phase-change problems such as evaporation and condensation. Second, by incorporating free energies with explicit interface width parameters, one can investigate sharp-interface limits, both formally and rigorously. Other important extensions include the incorporation of chemical reactions and non-isothermal effects. Furthermore, an essential avenue for future work, beyond the scope of this paper, is the development of efficient numerical schemes that accurately capture interfacial dynamics while preserving key thermodynamic properties.

\displaystyle~\dfrac{1-Y}{2},

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\rho}{2},

\displaystyle\quad\dfrac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{2}}{\partial Y}=

\displaystyle~-\dfrac{\rho}{2},

\displaystyle~1,

\displaystyle\quad\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{2}}=

\displaystyle~-\dfrac{2\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{(\tilde{\rho}_{1}+\tilde{\rho}_{2})^{2}}.

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\partial\nabla\rho}{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{2}}=0,

\displaystyle-4\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}{(\tilde{\rho}_{1}+\tilde{\rho}_{2})^{3}}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{1}+2\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{1}-\tilde{\rho}_{2}}{(\tilde{\rho}_{1}+\tilde{\rho}_{2})^{3}}\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{2},

Lemma B.1

(Relation Korteweg stresses)
.

The Korteweg stresses are related via:

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{1}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{1}}+\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{2}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{2}}=\nabla\rho\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\rho}+\nabla Y\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla Y}.

(B.4)

Proof.

The chain rule and the variable transformations (
B.2
) provide:

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{1}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1+Y}{2}\nabla\rho+\frac{\rho}{2}\nabla Y,

(B.5a)

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{2}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1-Y}{2}\nabla\rho-\frac{\rho}{2}\nabla Y.

(B.5b)

Next, the chain rule and the variable transformations (
B.3
) provide:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{1}}

\displaystyle=~\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\rho}+\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla Y}\dfrac{1-Y}{\rho},

(B.6a)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{2}}

\displaystyle=~\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\rho}-\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla Y}\dfrac{1+Y}{\rho}.

(B.6b)

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{1}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{1}}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1+Y}{2}\nabla\rho\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\rho}+\frac{1-Y}{2}\nabla Y\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla Y}

\displaystyle~+\frac{1-Y^{2}}{2\rho}\nabla\rho\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla Y}+\frac{\rho}{2}\nabla Y\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\rho},

(B.7a)

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{2}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\tilde{\rho}_{2}}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1-Y}{2}\nabla\rho\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\rho}+\frac{1+Y}{2}\nabla Y\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla Y}

\displaystyle~-\frac{1-Y^{2}}{2\rho}\nabla\rho\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla Y}-\frac{\rho}{2}\nabla Y\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\rho}.

(B.7b)

∎

Lemma B.2

(Relations chemical potentials)
.

The chemical potentials are related via:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{1}\tilde{\rho}_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{2}\tilde{\rho}_{2}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mu}_{\rho}\rho,

The eigenvectors are scaled so that
\mathbf{r}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{l}_{j}=1
for all
j=1,...,N+1
. Both right and left eigenvectors are linearly independent and span
\mathbb{R}^{N+d}
. This implies that the system is hyperbolicity if the eigenvalues are real-valued, which holds when
a_{\alpha{\beta}}^{2}={\rm d}p_{\alpha}/{\rm d}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}>0
. Similar to the one-dimensional case, fields associated with
\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{N+{2}}
correspond to contact discontinuities, and those associated with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{N+{3}}
correspond to shock or rarefaction waves, respectively.
