Development and validation of the Korea University Social Isolation Scale
Authors: Jaehee Jung, Jueun Hwang, Juhee Choi, Ye-Seul Kim, Jisoo Park, Yong-Chun Bahk, Kee-Hong Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial isolation is a complex phenomenon encompassing behavioral, cognitive, emotional, and functional dimensions that represents a major public health issue. Beyond reduced social contact, it is associated with subjective distress and difficulties in academic and occupational engagement. To address this complexity, we developed and validated the Korea University Social Isolation Scale (KUSIS), a multidimensional tool designed to identify individuals at high levels of social isolation and capture the diverse experiences of isolation.MethodsFollowing item generation and a pilot study (n = 100), the main validation study was conducted with 206 community-dwelling young adults in Korea. The psychometric properties of the KUSIS were evaluated using exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, as well as internal reliability and validity. Furthermore, split-sample cross-validation and measurement invariance across gender were conducted. Convergent validity was examined through correlations with the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support and the Loneliness and Social Isolation Scale. Known-groups validity was assessed by comparing scores of socially isolated (n = 62) and non-socially isolated (n = 144) groups, classified using semi-structured interviews. To confirm classification accuracy, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was also performed.ResultsFactor analyses supported a three-factor structure with the final nine items: Social Avoidance, Sense of Social Dysfunction, and Sense of Social Disconnection. This structure was generally supported by split-sample cross-validation and was invariant across gender. The KUSIS showed strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ω, and composite reliability all indicating good reliability. Convergent validity was supported by significant correlations with criterion measures, and known-groups validity was supported by significant differences between socially isolated and non-socially isolated participants. The KUSIS showed strong classification accuracy (area under the curve = 0.937), with a cutoff of 8.5 providing optimal sensitivity (0.887) and specificity (0.826).ConclusionsThe KUSIS is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing multiple dimensions of social isolation among Korean young adults. Its concise structure and empirically derived cutoff scores may help in the identification of socially isolated individuals and offer preliminary guidance for considering different support needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-27886-0.Keywords: Social isolation, Scale development, Psychometric validation, Factor analysis

Introduction

Social isolation is a major public health issue worldwide [1], associated with significant risks to both mental and physical health, including increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [2–7]. Socially isolated individuals experience various difficulties in their daily lives. Social isolation can stem from diverse factors such as biological vulnerability, socioeconomic adversity, and psychological or behavioral patterns [8–10]. This complexity requires a comprehensive understanding of how isolation manifests in individuals’ lives and how to identify those affected by it.

Traditionally, social isolation has been operationalized primarily in terms of the size and frequency of social networks [11, 12]. For example, network-based tools such as the Berkman-Syme Social Network Index and the Lubben Social Network Scale assess structural characteristics and the size of social networks that provide emotional and instrumental support [13–16]. Although useful for quantifying structural connections and widely used to indicate social isolation [17, 18], these network-based tools capture little of the subjective distress associated with isolation or its broader consequences for daily life.

Many socially isolated people report low perceived social support, reflecting the awareness that there are few people upon whom they can rely for emotional or practical support, which often leads to loneliness. Although loneliness can occur with or without much social contact [19], it can co-occur with social isolation. When both are present, the psychosocial burden may be especially pronounced, with the greatest deficits in perceived support and increased likelihood of psychological distress [20]. This combination is also associated with increased all-cause mortality [21]. Reflecting these empirical patterns, recent studies advocate incorporating subjective and qualitative aspects of relationships into assessing social isolation [22–24].

Social isolation is also associated with reduced engagement in social roles, particularly in academic and occupational domains. Socially isolated individuals often struggle to sustain participation in education and work. School dropouts and unemployment correlate with higher isolation levels [25, 26] and frequently coincide with reduced labor-market participation or NEET status, defined as “not being in education, employment, or training” [27, 28]. Such patterns are associated with broader social and economic implications at the population level [29, 30]. Moreover, given that participation in academic and occupational roles represents a core domain of mental health [31], and that role-related outcomes are recommended as important indicators in the assessment of mental disorders [32], measures of social isolation should also capture aspects of role engagement.

Previous findings suggest that a comprehensive understanding of social isolation requires examining social behavior, perceived support, co-occurring loneliness, and social role engagement. However, to our knowledge, no existing scale comprehensively captures social isolation. This study therefore developed and validated the Korea University Social Isolation Scale (KUSIS) to address this gap. The KUSIS integrates behavioral, cognitive-emotional, and functional domains. Instead of measuring the size of social networks, it focuses on subjective experiences of social isolation and its impact on role engagement. By providing empirically derived cutoff scores for each subscale, the KUSIS can help identify Korean young adults with high levels of social isolation and may provide preliminary guidance for considering different support needs.

Methods

As shown in Fig. 1, the development process consisted of three sequential phases. Phase 1 comprised literature review, individual interviews, and expert evaluation to generate and refine the initial item pool. Phase 2 comprised a pilot study to examine preliminary validity and reduce items. Phase 3 comprised the main validation study, in which the scale’s factor structure, reliability, validity, model stability, and classification accuracy were evaluated.

Three-phase development and validation process of the KUSIS

Phase 1: item generation and refinement

To develop the initial items, we reviewed existing instruments measuring the behavioral, cognitive-emotional, and functional aspects of social isolation, and used them to identify candidate items for refinement. For the behavioral domain, we focused on observable patterns of social withdrawal and reduced interpersonal engagement by evaluating measures of social network characteristics [14, 23, 33–36]. In the cognitive–emotional domain, we examined measures of perceived social support [37, 38]; loneliness [39–44]; and related constructs such as social anxiety [45], a preference for solitude [46–49], and life satisfaction [46]. For the functional domain, we reviewed measures of impairment in academic, occupational, and broader social roles to capture the functional consequences of social isolation [50–54].

After generating items, we set the questionnaire format, verb tense, measurement period, and instructions. The scale did not include reverse-coded items. To minimize potential cognitive burden and response errors among socially isolated adults, all items were phrased in the same direction to enhance measurement clarity. The instructions specified that participants should exclude interactions with current cohabitants. This criterion prevents underestimating social isolation among individuals living with family or partners but lacking external social contact.

To further refine items, trained clinical psychology doctoral students conducted one-hour individual interviews with seven participants (five men and two women, aged 23–45 years) who had experienced isolation for an average of seven years. All four doctoral students had completed one year of structured clinical training prior to their involvement in the study, and they conducted interviews under the ongoing supervision of two licensed clinical psychologists. The participants were recruited through two community centers and included four currently isolated individuals, two in recovery, and one fully recovered. The interviews explored lived experiences, focusing on relationships, emotional distress, coping mechanisms, and daily functioning. The interviews revealed a recurring theme of worthlessness due to perceived inability to fulfill social roles. During periods of social isolation, participants reported that they often had doubts about their ability to reintegrate into society, felt a lack of belonging or believed their social functioning had declined. Items reflecting this theme were added accordingly. Participants also reviewed initial items to assess face validity and readability.

Finally, five external experts reviewed the preliminary item pool. These included two licensed clinical psychologists, one psychiatrist, one psychometrician, and a counseling team leader from a community center supporting socially isolated young adults. These experts provided qualitative assessments of validity, readability, and clinical utility. They also quantitatively rated items using a content validity index (CVI) on a 0–2 scale. Items with a CVI below 0.8 were prioritized for revision. Consequently, 37 preliminary items were retained for the pilot survey.

Phase 2: pilot study

To examine preliminary convergent validity, we conducted a pilot survey with 100 Korean adults (52% men; age = 22–49 years, M = 35.69, SD = 7.66). Participants were recruited through a research panel company. All participants had been unemployed for at least six months at the time of the survey. None had any kind of employment, including part-time work. The preliminary scale comprised 37 items, which participants rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 3 = “very much”).

We examined correlations between the preliminary scale and the Loneliness and Social Isolation Scale (LSIS). The LSIS assesses loneliness, social support, and social networks [41]. Higher scores indicate greater loneliness, lower social support, and a smaller social network. Among the LSIS subscales, loneliness had the strongest correlation with the preliminary scale (r = 0.63, p < .001). Social support followed (r = 0.52, p < .001), then social networks (r = 0.43, p < .001). These correlations, particularly with loneliness and social support, indicate acceptable levels of convergent validity with the target construct [55].

Furthermore, we analyzed the distribution of preliminary item scores, which were right-skewed and showed low overall agreement on isolation-related content. Although the LSIS lacks a specified cutoff [41], one study classified individuals as socially isolated when the sum of the social support and social network subscale scores exceeded 4 points [56]. Using this criterion, the sample demonstrated an elevated level of social isolation (M = 6.39, SD = 2.29). The discrepancy between the low preliminary item means and the elevated LSIS scores suggested that the initial items underestimated isolation in this group. Accordingly, we revised items with weak domain correlations or redundancy. A second content validity review using a 0–2 rating scale yielded a refined pool of 18 items.

Phase 3: main validation study

Participants

The participants were 206 Korean young adults. All provided informed consent and took part voluntarily. They were recruited via social media (e.g., KakaoTalk, Instagram), online communities, and community support centers for socially isolated young adults. Exclusion criteria were: (1) neurological impairment or psychotic symptoms preventing participation; (2) functional impairment from temporary physical injury or illness, where expected to resolve naturally; (3) inability to participate in online research via computer or smartphone; (4) insufficient proficiency in Korean. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University (KUIRB-2024-0252-06).

Procedure

The KUSIS initially consisted of a refined 18-item version. Participants first completed an online survey that included the KUSIS and other psychological measures. They then took part in individual 40-minute Zoom interviews for criterion classification. With the participants’ consent, most interviews were recorded, transcribed, and reviewed to ensure data quality. Initial case classification was performed according to predefined operational criteria. When a case required further review, the authors discussed the case with reference to the interview transcripts and reached a consensus-based final decision. Consequently, no cases remained ambiguous in the final dataset.

Instruments

The semi-structured classification interview was developed for this study to identify participants with high levels of social isolation who were likely to require professional support. Four clinical psychology doctoral students, each with over a year of training in assessment and psychotherapy, conducted the interviews. Prior to developing the protocol, they received training from an external licensed clinical psychologist on the structure and administration of clinical interviews, including the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders–Clinician Version [57]. Based on this training, the interview protocol was drafted and subsequently reviewed and refined by two licensed clinical psychologists within the research team. All interviews and classifications were conducted under their supervision. Interviewers were blind to all questionnaire data, including KUSIS scores.

The interview covered four domains: (1) frequency of leaving home in the past six months; (2) whether the participant had no individuals with regular voluntary contact maintained for at least three months; (3) whether the participant perceived that there was no one who could provide emotional or financial support for at least three months; and (4) whether clinically significant distress or functional impairment in daily, academic, occupational, or self-care activities attributable to domains (1)–(3) was present. The criteria for domain (4) were considered met if the participant exhibited clear functional impairment, or marked subjective distress rated 5 or higher on a 0–10 scale, even in the absence of observable impairment. The interview questions were informed by theoretical models of hikikomori [9, 36], a national survey of socially isolated youth in Korea [58], and conceptualizations of social isolation among young adults [59]. The socially isolated group was defined as those meeting the criteria for domains (2), (3), and (4), including the specifically withdrawn subgroup that met the criteria for all four domains (1–4).

The refined 18-item KUSIS is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 3 = very much) and assesses respondents’ experiences of social isolation over the past month, excluding interactions with current cohabitants.

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) consists of 12 items [37, 38], each rated on a 5-point Likert scale. It assesses perceived social support across three subscales: family, friends, and significant others. The internal consistency reliability of the Korean version is acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) [38].

The LSIS consists of six items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale [41]. Internal consistency reliability for the subscales was reported as follows: loneliness (α = 0.81), social support (α = 0.77), social networks (α = 0.59) [41].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for the main validation study were conducted using R statistical software [60]. The dataset contained no missing values, and no items required reverse coding. Preliminary checks for normality, linearity, and homogeneity were performed.

Given that the KUSIS is a newly developed instrument with no previously established factor structure, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to identify its latent structure without imposing a predefined model, using the psych package [61]. Maximum likelihood estimation was selected as a well-established method for model-based statistical evaluation, and oblique rotation was used because the dimensions of social isolation were expected to be correlated, consistent with the subsequent confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Sampling adequacy was verified using Bartlett’s test of sphericity and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure, with KMO values ≥ 0.60 considered acceptable for factor analysis [62]. The number of factors to retain was determined based on results from multiple criteria: inspection of the scree plot, parallel analysis, and theoretical interpretability.

The resulting factor structure was then evaluated using CFA with the lavaan package [63]. Model fit was evaluated using χ²/df, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Acceptable model fit was defined as χ²/df < 2, CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.10, and SRMR ≤ 0.07 [64]. Post-hoc model modifications were considered only when the modification indices exceeded 10, a commonly used threshold intended to ensure substantial improvements in model fit [65]. Furthermore, such modifications were implemented only when theoretically justified, such as when item pairs reflected closely related aspects of the same underlying domain.

To further examine model stability, we conducted a supplementary split-sample cross-validation by randomly dividing the total sample into two equal subsamples (n = 103 each), performing EFA on the first subsample and CFA on the second. Additionally, measurement invariance across gender was examined using multi-group CFA to evaluate configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance. Invariance was determined based on changes in model fit between nested models, with ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 indicating invariance [66].

The KUSIS’s reliability and validity were also assessed. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω. McDonald’s ω provides a factor model-based estimate of reliability that does not assume equal item loadings [67]. Values ≥ 0.90 were interpreted as excellent, 0.80–0.89 as good, and 0.70–0.79 as acceptable [55]. Composite reliability (CR) for assessing construct reliability and average variance extracted (AVE) for assessing convergent validity were computed to evaluate the measurement model, with CR ≥ 0.70 and AVE ≥ 0.50 considered acceptable [68, 69]. Discriminant validity followed the Fornell-Larcker criterion, confirming that the square root of the AVE for each construct exceeded its correlation with all other latent variables [69].

Convergent validity with external constructs was examined using Pearson correlations with the MSPSS and LSIS. To further examine whether the KUSIS provided information beyond a closely related construct, we conducted a supplementary nested logistic regression analysis using interview-based isolation status as the dependent variable. LSIS loneliness was entered in the first model, and the KUSIS total score was added in the second model.

Known-group validity was examined using independent samples t-tests to compare KUSIS total scores between the socially isolated and non-socially isolated groups, classified via semi-structured interviews. Owing to potential violations of the homogeneity of variance assumption, Welch-adjusted Hedges’ g was calculated as the effect size [70, 71].

Inter-rater reliability for the interview-based classification was evaluated using Fleiss’ kappa. Specifically, 40 cases were randomly selected from the 188 participants who had consented to interview recording, using stratified sampling to preserve the ratio of socially isolated to non-socially isolated cases in the recorded sample. These cases were independently rated by the four interviewers based on the interview transcripts, while the raters remained blind to the final consensus-based classifications. Interpretation was informed by both the conventional criterion of excellent agreement at ≥ 0.75 [72] and a more recent, stricter guideline classifying 0.80–0.90 as strong agreement [73].

Finally, classification accuracy was assessed using the pROC package [74]. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated, and cutoff scores were determined using Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1), with values closer to 1 indicating higher diagnostic efficiency [75]. AUC ≥ 0.80 was considered useful [76, 77].

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics of the 206 participants (37.4% men; age range = 19–39 years, M = 29.92, SD = 5.98). Based on semi-structured classification interviews, participants were classified into either the socially isolated group (n = 62) or the non-socially isolated (n = 144) group. Compared to the non-socially isolated group, the socially isolated group displayed a lower prevalence of graduate-level education (8.1% vs. 27.8%) and a higher proportion of individuals who were unemployed and not seeking employment (16.1% vs. 0.7%). Full-time on-site employment was also less frequent among the socially isolated group (8.1% vs. 32.6%). Only 1.6% of the isolated group perceived their economic status as “good” or higher, compared to 18.8% in the non-socially isolated group (Table 1). These patterns indicate distinct socioeconomic disadvantages in the socially isolated group.

Exploratory factor analysis

An EFA was initially conducted on the 18 items. A visual inspection of the scree plot based on the elbow criterion suggested a single-factor structure, whereas parallel analysis and theoretical considerations indicated a multidimensional solution. Given the conceptualization of social isolation as a multidimensional construct, solutions with 2 to 4 factors were examined. Among these alternatives, the three-factor solution showed a clearer factor structure and was the most interpretable and theoretically coherent. Therefore, the three-factor model was retained to guide item refinement. Next, items with excessive cross-loadings, high inter-item correlations, or conceptual redundancy were removed. A final EFA on the nine retained items then supported a three-factor solution (Table 2). Descriptive statistics for the final items among the 206 participants are presented in Table 3. To further examine model stability, an EFA on a split sample (n = 103) was conducted, in which the three-factor solution was generally replicated, although Item 13 showed cross-loading across two factors.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

CFA was conducted to evaluate the factor structure. Modification indices indicated that model fit would improve by allowing covariance between the residuals of Items 2 and 4; this was the only item pair exceeding the predefined criterion. In response, a modified model including this residual covariance was estimated and compared with the base model (Table 4). The modified model had lower Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values, and a chi-square difference test indicated a significant improvement in model fit . (Δχ² = 9.78, Δdf = 1, p < .01). The modified model also exhibited superior goodness-of-fit indices, with all indices falling within the acceptable range. Therefore, the modified model was retained as the final model (Fig. 2).

A CFA conducted in the second subsample (n = 103) showed acceptable fit based on CFI, TLI, and SRMR (CFI = 0.935, TLI = 0.902, SRMR = 0.056), although RMSEA was elevated (0.125). Additionally, measurement invariance across gender was generally supported up to the strict level according to the predefined ΔCFI criterion, suggesting that the scale operated equivalently for men and women.

Evaluation of reliability and validity

The full KUSIS demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.91, 95% CI [0.89, 0.93]; McDonald’s ω = 0.94, 95% CI [0.92, 0.94]). Subscale reliability coefficients also indicated good internal consistency. For Social Avoidance, reliability was α = 0.88 (95% CI [0.84, 0.90]) and ω = 0.88 (95% CI [0.84, 0.91]); for Sense of Social Dysfunction, α = 0.84 (95% CI [0.79, 0.87]) and ω = 0.86 (95% CI [0.81, 0.89]); and for Sense of Social Disconnection, α = 0.84 (95% CI [0.79, 0.87]) and ω = 0.85 (95% CI [0.80, 0.88]). Overall, these coefficients indicated good to excellent internal consistency.

At the construct level, the CR values for the three factors ranged from 0.80 to 0.89, exceeding the acceptable threshold. Convergent validity was supported, with AVE values ranging from 0.62 to 0.74, indicating that the items within each factor shared substantial common variance. Discriminant validity was established because the square roots of the AVEs exceeded the corresponding inter-factor correlations.

To further examine convergent validity with external constructs, correlations were computed between the KUSIS total score and subscales and the MSPSS and LSIS subscales (Table 5). The KUSIS total score was negatively correlated with the MSPSS (r = − 0.780, p < .001) and positively correlated with the LSIS subscales: social support (r = 0.711, p < .001), social networks (r = 0.553, p < .001), and loneliness (r = 0.760, p < .001). At the subfactor level, the MSPSS exhibited the strongest correlations with Sense of Social Disconnection (F3; r = − 0.808, p < .001), followed by Social Avoidance (F1; r = − 0.613, p < .001) and Sense of Social Dysfunction (F2; r = − 0.601, p < .001). Similarly, the LSIS subscales were most strongly associated with F3 (social support: r = 0.767, p < .001; social networks: r = 0.569, p < .001; loneliness: r = 0.787, p < .001), followed by F1 and F2. The LSIS social network subscale exhibited a weaker positive correlation with F2 (r = 0.386, p < .001).

Inter-rater reliability

In a stratified random subset of 40 recorded cases, inter-rater reliability for the interview-based classification was strong, with a Fleiss’ kappa of 0.896 (z = 13.90, p < .001) across four raters.

Classification accuracy

ROC analysis was conducted to confirm whether the KUSIS effectively classified individuals into socially isolated and non-socially isolated groups. For the total score, the cutoff score was 8.5 (range: 0–27) with a sensitivity of 0.887, specificity of 0.826, positive predictive value of 0.688, negative predictive value of 0.944, and positive likelihood ratio of 5.11 (Table 7). The AUC was 0.937 (95% CI [0.904, 0.970]; Table 8).

Discussion

This study developed and validated the KUSIS, a brief instrument designed to assess the behavioral, functional, and emotional–cognitive experiences of social isolation. The scale was developed to assess multiple dimensions of social isolation in Korean young adults and may provide an initial basis for identifying individuals with elevated levels of social isolation. The scale also demonstrated good internal consistency, strong convergent and known-groups validity, and high classification accuracy in the current sample.

Through theoretical frameworks and data analysis, three factors were identified: Social Avoidance, Sense of Social Dysfunction, and Sense of Social Disconnection. The Social Avoidance factor reflects behavior driven by social discomfort and represents a core behavioral characteristic of isolation. This factor also overlaps with central symptoms of social anxiety disorder. Given the strong association between social isolation and social anxiety disorder [78, 79], many individuals are likely to present both patterns simultaneously. However, individuals with social anxiety disorders who are not socially isolated are less likely to score high on the second and third factors.

The Sense of Social Dysfunction factor reflects cognitions related to diminished academic or occupational functioning and the accompanying negative self-evaluation. Item 2 (“I feel useless in society”) and Item 4 (“I am not engaged in academic or economic activities”) both capture difficulties with social-role functioning, which may explain the residual covariance added in the CFA. However, the items are not redundant. Item 2 reflects a subjective sense of uselessness. By contrast, Item 4 reflects disengagement from academic or economic roles. Item 17 (“My life is a failure”) also loaded on this factor. It reflects a broader negative self-evaluation, which may result from prolonged disengagement from social roles. This item was derived from interviews with socially isolated individuals during the preceding research phase. Prior findings consistently show that reduced engagement in social roles, such as employment or education, is strongly associated with lower self-esteem [80, 81]. Difficulties sustaining productive roles may contribute to negative self-evaluative perceptions.

Finally, the Sense of Social Disconnection factor reflects cognitive appraisals of relational insufficiency and the emotional distress derived from such experiences. This factor aligns with existing accounts of internal social isolation, which encompass satisfaction with social relationships and loneliness [24]. Considering that loneliness typically arises from a cognitive discrepancy between actual and desired levels of social relationships [82, 83], it is plausible that cognitive and emotional elements converge into a single factor. Although this factor is important for understanding how individuals perceive and experience isolation, it is not equivalent to social isolation itself. Accordingly, a low F3 score does not imply the absence of social isolation. Consistent with this distinction, the KUSIS showed incremental validity beyond LSIS loneliness, suggesting that it captures broader aspects of isolation-related experiences than loneliness alone.

To facilitate real-world application, this study also established cutoff scores for both the total KUSIS scale and each factor. Specifically, the cutoff score for the total KUSIS was 8.5. Given that the scale yields integer scores, a raw score of 9 or higher may help identify individuals in this age group with elevated social isolation. This cutoff is not a diagnostic threshold and does not imply that social isolation is a mental disorder. It is a research-based threshold to help identify people who may need further assessment or support. Factor-specific cutoffs (3.5 for Social Avoidance, 2.5 for Sense of Social Dysfunction, 3.5 for Sense of Social Disconnection) may provide a preliminary framework for considering different support needs.

When several factors exceed their cutoff scores, individuals are likely to experience more severe isolation-related difficulties. The Social Avoidance subscale reflects restricted social engagement, a core feature of social isolation. Its score should be appropriately weighted in interpretation. If Social Avoidance scores are low, but other scores are elevated, difficulties may arise from sources other than isolation. However, a low Social Avoidance score does not necessarily mean that the individual does not need psychosocial support. They may still be experiencing substantial distress.

Different combinations of elevated factors may reflect distinct profiles with potentially different support needs. This approach is consistent with that in prior research suggesting that social isolation is heterogeneous and that the interventions required may vary depending on its characteristics [10]. One possible profile may be characterized by high Social Avoidance and Sense of Social Dysfunction scores alongside a low Sense of Social Disconnection score. This pattern may reflect withdrawal and marked reductions in engagement in everyday roles, despite relatively limited perceived relational deficits. In such cases, interventions that focus first on restoring daily routines and goal-directed activity, rather than immediately expanding interpersonal networks, may be worth considering. Behavioral activation may be particularly relevant in this context. As a structured, reinforcement-based intervention, it employs monitoring and planned activation to reduce avoidance behaviors and increase engagement in activities aligned with an individual’s values [84]. Its effectiveness has been demonstrated across diverse populations [85–90], and it is well-suited to increasing behaviors that support outings and daily functioning.

By contrast, when Social Avoidance and Sense of Social Disconnection scores are high, but Sense of Social Dysfunction is low, individuals may feel interpersonal discomfort and loneliness. However, they still maintain role engagement. In such cases, bridging the gap between relational needs and avoidance tendencies may be important. Cognitive-behavioral therapy for social anxiety may help individuals manage interpersonal and internal difficulties through exposure, cognitive restructuring, and relaxation training [91]. This approach may be particularly relevant for those who desire connection but avoid interactions owing to social anxiety or related psychological vulnerabilities. Consistent with this interpretation, cognitive-behavioral interventions have been shown to reduce loneliness [92, 93]. Moreover, longitudinal evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between social anxiety and loneliness, such that reductions in one are associated with lower levels of the other over time [94].

Taken together, these findings suggest that the multidimensional structure of the KUSIS may help inform more targeted intervention strategies. However, these interpretations should be regarded as tentative, because the present study did not empirically validate specific intervention approaches for different factor combinations.

Beyond these practical implications, this study has several strengths. First, the KUSIS is grounded in a clear, multidimensional framework, providing a solid conceptual foundation for assessing social isolation. This enables simultaneous assessment of multiple aspects of isolation, unlike many unidimensional tools. Second, the item development process integrated both the practical expertise of community-based practitioners and in-depth interviews reflecting the lived experiences of socially isolated individuals, thereby enhancing the scale’s ecological validity and real-world relevance. Third, the study incorporated both structural validation and classification-based validation procedures. Notably, the scale also demonstrated strong classification accuracy, with an AUC of 0.937, indicating high discriminative ability within the sample studied. Finally, the empirically derived cutoff scores may serve as an initial reference point for identifying young adults with elevated social isolation.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, the scale omitted reverse-coded items. This choice reduced cognitive load for socially isolated adults and simplified scoring in applied contexts. However, it also limited reliability checks and may have hindered the detection of response biases. Second, EFA and CFA were performed on the same full sample (n = 206), which may increase the risk of overfitting and limit structural validity. Although supplementary split-sample cross-validation was subsequently conducted, the relatively small subsample size (n = 103 per group) may have reduced the stability of parameter estimates. Therefore, future studies should confirm the factor structure using a fully independent sample. Third, inter-rater reliability for the interview-based classification was evaluated in a stratified random subset of recorded cases. Although the raters were blind to the final consensus-based classifications, the resulting estimate should be interpreted as reflecting agreement within the re-rated recorded subset rather than as a full-sample estimate from the original classification process. Fourth, Items 2 and 13 showed relatively low communalities (0.40 and 0.41), indicating more unique variance. Although values below 0.40 are considered potentially problematic [95], neither item fell below this threshold. Both were retained to preserve content validity, given their theoretical importance in capturing core aspects of social isolation. Finally, the study’s cross-sectional design and focus on a specific age group (19–39 years) restrict generalizability. This also prevents drawing conclusions about temporal stability or predictive validity. Future research should examine the KUSIS across a broader range of age groups and populations. Longitudinal studies are also needed to assess temporal stability, responsiveness to intervention-related changes, and utility for tracking trajectories of social isolation across life stages.

Conclusion

We developed and validated the KUSIS, a concise 9-item instrument capturing multiple dimensions of social isolation in Korean young adults. The KUSIS showed strong psychometric properties and classification accuracy, indicating its potential utility for assessing elevated social isolation in this group. Further work is needed to establish cross-cultural validity and to examine the instrument’s stability and predictive validity in broader community samples using longitudinal designs.
