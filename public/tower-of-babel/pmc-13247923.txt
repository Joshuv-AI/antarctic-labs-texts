Natural history and clinical characteristics of pulmonary subsolid nodules in pediatric patients: a cohort study
Authors: Hua Ren, Ya Zou, Rong Yao, Luna Wang, Yunshu Zhao, Shiyu Liu, Gege Jiang, Jing Shen, Hong Yu, Jianlin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of subsolid nodules (SSNs) in children and adolescents remains controversial. The aim of this study was clarifying the natural history of SSNs in the pediatric patient population and generate insights into the effective follow-up of these nodules.MethodsPatients aged 18 years or younger admitted to a tertiary medical center from July 2010 to June 2025 with persistent SSNs on computed tomography (CT) scans but no extrapulmonary malignancy were retrospectively enrolled and assessed. Retrospective recruitment was performed in September 2025. The data analyzed included the incidence of interval growth; clinical, radiologic, and pathological features; and follow-up outcomes of the SSNs.ResultsA total of 105 patients (52 females) comprising 141 SSNs [including 137 pure ground-glass nodules (PGGNs) and four part-solid nodules (PSNs)] were analyzed and evaluated. The median follow-up time was 23 months [interquartile range (IQR), 13–36 months]. Of the 141 SSNs, 5 (4%) (3 PGGNs and 2 PSNs) exhibited interval growth. One PSN demonstrated an increase in solid components, was surgically resected, and was pathologically confirmed to be minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA). One PGGN and one PSN exhibited an increase in nodule size, and the PSN was confirmed to be invasive adenocarcinoma (IVA). Two PGGNs grew a small solid component, and one of them was MIA. The interval from initial CT examination to growth in these five SSNs was 12, 17, 20, 37, and 98 months. Ten SSNs (eight PGGNs and two PSNs) in nine stable individuals underwent pulmonary resection, including one IVA, seven MIAs, and two adenocarcinomas in situ. One PGGN underwent microwave ablation.ConclusionsPersistent SSNs in pediatric patients should not be overlooked. Approximately 4% of persistent SSNs in children and adolescents showed subsequent growth in our study. The findings suggest that longer interval surveillance may be valuable in pediatric patients, particularly for reducing unnecessary radiation exposure, and management should be performed on an individualized basis.Keywords: Natural history, follow-up, subsolid nodules (SSNs), pediatrics, lung cancer

Introduction

With the increasing application of computed tomography (CT) in pediatric patients, the detection rate of pulmonary nodules has risen dramatically (1-3). Studies have demonstrated that incidental pulmonary nodules are frequently detected in children without malignant disease. Renne et al. reported a prevalence of 33% in pediatric patients without malignant diseases (1). Similarly, Samim et al. found pulmonary nodules in 38% of children undergoing trauma CT (2). After a study reported that the majority of persistent subsolid nodules (SSNs) are signs of early-stage lung adenocarcinoma in children and adolescents, these nodules have garnered heightened attention in clinical practice (4). According to the presence or absence of solid components, SSNs can be categorized as pure ground-glass nodules (PGGNs) or part-solid nodules (PSNs).

In the 2015 World Health Organization’s pathological classification standard of lung adenocarcinoma (5), lung adenocarcinoma can be divided into preinvasive lesions and invasive lesions. The former includes atypical adenomatous hyperplasia and adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), while the latter includes minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) and invasive adenocarcinoma (IVA). In the 2021 World Health Organization lung tumor classification, precursor glandular lesions, containing atypical adenomatous hyperplasia and AIS, are not a subtype of lung adenocarcinoma (6). Due to the variability in disease progression and patient prognosis, different clinical treatment principles (operation methods or follow-up intervals) can be adopted for patients with SSNs. In this context, principles for SSN management have been laid out in various clinical guidelines (7-11).

However, there are no guidelines for optimal management of SSNs in the pediatric population. The 2017 Fleischner Society guideline refers to incidentally encountered pulmonary nodules detected at CT in adult patients who are older than 35 years old without immunocompromised disease and known primary cancers (9). These guidelines were informed by evidence indicating that children and adults younger than 35 years have a low incidence of lung cancer (9). Incidental nodules encountered in young patients are more likely to be the result of an infectious process rather than malignancy (9). The guidelines of the American College of Chest Physicians and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network emphasize lung cancer screening for older age high-risk groups years (7,11). Meanwhile, consensus guidelines for clinical practice in Asia do not specify an age range (8).

In clinical practice, radiologists often incidentally encounter pulmonary nodules, especially SSNs in children and adolescents. However, the appropriate clinical management and follow-up in such scenarios remains unclear, and this issue has attracted considerable research attention (12-18). Pediatric-specific guidelines were developed in 2015 and 2022, and these emphasized the importance of individualized care (13,17,18). Compared to adult-based guidelines for lung cancer, which include standardized follow-up plans based on nodule size, nodule type, and risk factors (9), pediatric-specific recommendations comprise a stratified, conceptual framework guided by clinical symptoms, underlying disease, and nodule morphology (17,18). This distinction highlights the inapplicability of adult-centric recommendations to children and underscores the urgent need to define pediatric-specific management strategies. Several previous studies assessed pulmonary nodules in children and adolescents. However, these were limited to case series and systematic reviews and were not focused on the natural history of SSNs over long-term follow-up (12,14-16).

Due to the limited amount of data on the natural history of pediatric SSNs, clinicians have insufficient evidence for evaluating progressive or malignant lesions and face difficulty in appropriately balancing the risks of radiation exposure and effective treatment. Thus, characterizing the long-term growth patterns of pediatric SSNs is critical to establishing an evidence base for devising personalized, low-risk management strategies. Therefore, the purpose of this retrospective study was to clarify the natural history of SSNs in children and adolescents over a 15-year period. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0231/rc).

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Shanghai Chest Hospital (approval No. IS25116). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

This study was performed at a tertiary specialized hospital, Shanghai Chest Hospital. On September 2025, a single radiologist (H.R., with 9 years of experience in chest imaging) searched the radiology information system and the electronic medical records of Shanghai Chest Hospital using various descriptive keywords (e.g., “ground-glass opacity”, “GGO”, “ground-glass nodule”, “GGN”, “nonsolid nodule”, “subsolid nodule”, “pure ground-glass nodule”, “PGGN”, “mixed ground-glass nodule”, “MGGN”, “part-solid nodule”, and “PSN”). The search period spanned from July 2010 to June 2025 and included participants aged 18 years or younger with SSNs on initial CT examinations. The inclusion criteria were (I) PGGNs or PSNs, (II) SSNs with a long axial diameter of 3 cm or less on CT axial images, and (III) two or more available CT examinations with thin-section (≤1.5 mm) images. The exclusion criteria were (I) solid nodules, (II) cases with thick-section preoperative CT images, (III) lesions greater than 3 cm in size, (IV) motion artifacts, (V) diffuse ground-glass opacities (GGOs), (VI) discontinuation of follow-up, (VII) transient SSNs, (VIII) a follow-up time less than 12 months, and (IX) known extrapulmonary malignancy. The search retrieved records of 711 lesions from 615 participants. Persistent SSNs were defined as lesions remaining visible on CT obtained 3 months after baseline detection. Patients with multiple SSNs who underwent surgical resection of dominant lesions prior to our study’s enrollment and whose residual SSNs were under follow-up were considered to have a previous lung cancer history. Patients with malignant tumors originating outside the lung were considered to have a known extrapulmonary malignancy. A further search of the electronic medical records or telephone follow-up was used to collect the following clinical data: age, sex, smoking status, second-hand smoke exposure, family history, cancer history, multiplicity, current status, radiologic characteristics, and pathological data. Data from surgically treated patients with complete clinical and pathological data were collected.

CT examination

CT scans were performed with six CT scanners, including the ICT 256, Brilliance 64, Ingenuity (Phillips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands), Discovery CT750 HD, Revolution CT (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA), and uCT 510 (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) systems. All thorax CT scans were performed over the entire thorax, at full inspiration, and in the supine body position with a tube voltage of 80–120 kVp, automated exposure control, a pitch of 1.0, and a section thickness of 1–1.25 mm. Contiguous axial transverse images were reconstructed with standard reconstruction kernels, stored in a 512×512 or 1,024×1,024 data matrix, and visualized in lung windows [width 1,500 Hounsfield units (HU); level −500 HU] and mediastinal windows (width 400 HU; level 40 HU). Contrast agent was administered in six patients due to mediastinal lymph node enlargement or mediastinal masses. After persistent SSN was confirmed, the interval between follow-up CTs was determined according to the referring physician’s or patient’s availability.

Radiologic evaluation

In accordance with the recommendation of the Fleischner Society (10), two radiologists (H.R. and R.Y., with 10 years of experience in chest imaging) measured the initial average diameter (the mean of the long- and short-axis diameters of nodules measured on axial images) of the SSNs, and the initial long-axis diameter of the solid component was measured on lung windows. The observers were blinded to the surgery and pathological results. In cases of discrepant interpretations, a consensus was achieved via discussion. When the observers could not reach a consensus, final judgement was determined by another experienced radiologist (H.Y., with 30 years of experience in chest radiology). Radiologic characteristics, including initial nodule type (PGGN or PSN), lobe location, distribution (GGN located within the central one-third region was regarded as central distribution), initial average diameter of SSNs, initial long-axis diameter of the solid component of PSNs, and reversed halo sign (RHS; defined as a well-defined annular ground-glass nodule on CT axial images, with the central density of lesions lower than that of the peripheral ring, but higher than that of the normal pulmonary parenchyma). All noncontrast CT images were evaluated on lung windows (width 1,500 HU; level, −500 HU) and mediastinal windows (width 400 HU; level 40 HU) via a picture archiving and communication system.

Based on multiple follow-up CT scans, SSN growth was defined as (I) a ≥2 mm increase in the mean diameter of the entire nodule or the maximum diameter of solid component of PSN, (II) new appearance of a solid component, and (III) centripetal growth (a slow and gradual centripetal increase in attenuation with stable dimension from RHS to halo sign) (19-24). Other conditions were regarded as stable or no growth.

Surgical, pathologic, and survival data

We reviewed and recorded the clinical, surgical (surgery date and surgical procedure), and pathologic (pathological diagnosis, pathological stage, and tumor genetics) data if lesions were surgically resected. Pathological diagnosis was reviewed by board-certified thoracic pathologists according to the fifth edition of the 2021 World Health Organization lung tumor classification (6). Pathological stage was evaluated according to the ninth edition of the lung cancer tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging (25). The survival data from all patients with follow-up records were retrieved as of September 2025.

We performed surgical treatment when the growth of SSNs satisfied the above-mentioned conditions or according to the degree of progression of SSNs. Surgical treatment options were discussed among the multidisciplinary team, patient, and family. There were difficulties in deciding upon surgery due to ambiguous criteria, clinician uncertainty, or patient and family anxiety.

Statistical analysis

The incidence and interval growth of SSNs in children and adolescents ≤18 years was analyzed. We examined their clinical characteristics, pathologic data, radiologic features, survival outcome, and the results of interval growth. The threshold for the mean diameter of SSNs was 10 mm in accordance with previous research that reported 10 mm to be a risk factor for the histological invasiveness degree and growth (26). Quantitative data with a normal distribution are expressed as the mean and standard deviation. Quantitative data with a continuous nonnormal distribution are expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR) or 25% and 75% quartiles. Qualitative data are expressed as a rate (in percentage). Interobserver agreement was assessed via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous measurements. Agreements were interpreted as follows: <0.20, poor; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, good; and 0.81–1.00, excellent. Data analysis was performed with SPSS software version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Software version 6 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Characteristics of the study patients

Among the initially retrieved 711 lesions from 615 study patients, 570 lesions from 510 patients were excluded due to following reasons: manifesting as SSNs in thick-section CT images but appearing as solid in thin-section images (two nodules in two patients), thick-section preoperative CT images (15 nodules in 15 patients), lesions greater than 3 cm (5 nodules in 5 patients), motion artifacts (12 nodules in 8 patients), diffuse GGOs (5 nodules in 5 patients), discontinuation of follow-up (416 nodules in 386 patients), transient SSNs (13 nodules in 9 patients), follow-up time less than 12 months (100 nodules in 78 patients), and known extrapulmonary malignancy (2 nodules in 2 patients). Thus, a total of 141 incidentally detected persistent SSNs in 105 patients (52 females and 53 males; median age 17 years; IQR, 15–18 years) who were followed up for at least 12 months were enrolled in the study population. The number of cases increased significantly since 2019. The decrease in cases in 2024 might have been due to the short follow-up time. The detailed characteristics of 105 patients who fulfilled these criteria are shown in Table 1. The initial indications for CT were as follows: respiratory tract infection (n=45, 43%), physical examination (n=17, 16%), chest pain and tightness (n=9, 8%), incidental detection during a follow-up for another disease (n=14, 14%), trauma (n=6, 6%), chest wall deformity (n=4, 4%), foreign body (n=1, 1%), and unknown reasons (n=9, 8%). Of the patients, 92 (87%) were never smokers, and 7 (7%) had a smoking history. Of the 105 patients, 25 (24%) were exposed to second-hand smoke, and 99 (94%) were not from areas affected by the tuberculosis epidemic. No patients had a history of extrapulmonary malignancy, and 4 had GGN-featured lung adenocarcinomas. Another 23 of the 105 (22%) patients had multiple nodules (82 patients had 1 nodule, 15 had 2 nodules, 5 had 3 nodules, 2 had 4 nodules, and 1 had 6 nodules), and 82 patients had solitary nodules. Of the 105 participants, 92 (88%) continued to be followed up, and 13 (12%) underwent surgery. The median follow-up time was 23 months (IQR, 13–36 months). The median number of chest CT scans was 3 times (IQR, 2–4).

CT characteristics of SSNs

Among the 141 SSNs, 137 were PGGNs and 4 were PSNs (Table 2). The ICCs for average diameter and solid component measurements were 0.960 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.945–0.971] and 0.989 (95% CI: 0.836–0.999), respectively. The median diameter of SSNs and PGGNs was 5 mm (IQR, 5–7 mm). For PSNs, the median diameter was 10 mm (IQR, 9–13 mm), and the median diameter of the solid component was 5 mm (IQR, 4–10 mm). Of the 141 SSNs, 5 (4%) were larger than or equal to 10 mm. One PSN had a solid component larger than 6 mm. Moreover, 20 of 141 SSNs (14%) had a central distribution, 121 SSNs (86%) had a peripheral distribution, and 14 (10%) manifested as RHS GGNs.

Follow-up data, growth incidence, and growth tendency

Among the 141 SSNs, 10 (7%) were followed up for more than 5 years, 37 (26%) were for 3–5 years, 23 (16%) for 2–3 years, and 71 (50%) for 1–2 years.

Of the 141 SSNs, 5 (4%) showed interval growth (Tables 2,3). Three of the five (60%) progressive SSNs showed true growth. One PSN exhibited an increase in solid component, was surgically resected, and was pathologically confirmed to be MIA with ALK gene rearrangement (nodule #1 in Table 4). One PGGN and one PSN exhibited an increase in nodule size, and the PSN (Figure 3) was confirmed to be IVA (nodule #3 in Table 4). Two PGGNs appeared with a new small solid component, one of which was confirmed to be MIA (nodule #2 in Table 4). The intervals from initial CT examination to growth in the 5 SSNs was 12, 17, 20, 37, and 98 months, respectively (Table 3). No centripetal growth was observed in the RHS GGNs (Figure 3).

Surgical and histopathologic findings

In the stable group, 10% of patients (10/100) underwent surgical treatment, a lower proportion than that in the growth group (3/5, 60%) (Table 2). Moreover, 9 patients with 10 stable SSNs (nodules #4–13 in Table 4; 8 PGGNs and 2 PSNs) underwent pulmonary resection via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, and 1 (with a PGGN) underwent microwave ablation (nodule #14 in Table 4) after discussion due to parent or patient preference. One patient underwent lobectomy, and eight patients underwent wedge resection or segmentectomy. There were no lymph node metastases or pleural invasion. Among the 10 pathologically confirmed SSNs, there was 1 papillary pattern-predominant IVA, 7 MIAs, and 2 AISs (Figure 4). Mutation testing was performed in seven patients. Two (one with IVA and one with AIS) had epidermal growth factor receptor mutations. Three MIAs had HER2 mutations, one MIA had rearrangement during transfection (RET), and 1 MIA had ROS proto-oncogene 1 (ROS1) fusion. The median nodule size on CT images was 8 mm (IQR, 6–8 mm), the median diameter of pathological tumors was 7 mm (IQR, 5–8 mm), and the median postoperative follow-up duration was 37 months (IQR, 10–43 months). All patients survived without recurrence.

Discussion

The management of SSNs in children and adolescents remains controversial due to a lack of relevant evidence. This study attempted to retrospectively investigate the natural history of the persistent SSNs in children and adolescents. A small proportion (5/141, 4%) of persistent SSNs had subsequent growth. In addition, the surgically resected incidental persistent SSNs were found to be precursor glandular lesions or adenocarcinoma according to pathological testing.

A critical question emerging from these findings is why so many SSNs develop in young patients, some of whom are ultimately diagnosed with lung cancer. Smoking is the predominant risk factor for lung cancer among the adult population. However, only 7% of patients had a history of smoking in our pediatric cohort, while there were no smokers in Chen et al.’s study (4). Chronic exposure to different environmental factors, notably air pollution and second-hand smoke, has been identified as a potential factor for an increased risk of lung cancer (27). Among the patients in our study, 24% were exposed to second-hand smoke, which is in line with other research (28,29), in which over 50% of patients with lung cancer were never-smokers, and air pollution was found to affect lung adenocarcinoma incidence and patient survival outcomes (28). In our study, 20% of patients had a family history of lung cancer, suggesting that young individuals with a family history of cancer may be at heightened risk of lung cancer. As reported previously, young patients with lung cancer typically have high therapeutic actionability (30). In our cohort, about 62% (8/13) of patients underwent surgical resection and had at least one targetable genomic alteration. These genetic alterations contribute substantially to malignant transformation and lung carcinogenesis in pediatric populations. Future prospective studies are need to better characterize their associated risks.

The greater frequency of CT being administered in children and adolescents has led a higher rate of incidental SSN discovery in this population. Although the implementation of lung cancer screening tailored to nonsmoking individuals, particularly never-smoking women, may confer significant prognostic benefits in Asian countries (31), it is clearly not feasible for pediatric cohorts given the relatively low incidence in this age group. The expansion of lung cancer screening poses a theoretical risk of overdiagnosis and overmanagement (32), but there is currently a lack of relevant high-level evidence for pediatric populations. In the near future, individualized clinical risk prediction models may enhance the identification of high-risk nonsmoking individuals, particularly in Asian populations (31).

Although the guidelines of the Fleischner Society, National Comprehensive Cancer Network, and American College of Chest Physicians do not specify management of SSNs in children and adolescents (7,9,11), previous studies have examined follow-up strategies for SSNs (12,16). The Society for Pediatric Radiology Thoracic Imaging Committee recommends that management of pulmonary nodules in children should involve individualized clinical judgment and follow-up (17,18). Indeed, while there are detailed follow-up strategies in adult guidelines, specific follow-up intervals are difficult to determine for pediatric patients with SSNs due to a lack of data. Balancing the nodule growth risk and radiation exposure is critical for the management of SSNs. According to the principle of minimizing radiation exposure, high-frequency surveillance to confirm persistence, including CT scans at 3–6 months or 6–12 months, may be inappropriate for the pediatric population. Given the rate of interval growth in our study (4%), a longer interval in surveillance may be more reasonable in pediatric patients as compared to adults. Our results align with those reported by Breen et al.—who found that follow-up CT may not be necessary in the pediatric patient population with small pulmonary nodules and without prior history of malignancy (12)—and those by Wu et al.—who proposed that children with SSNs should receive follow-up CT scans every 1–2 years for 5 years (16). Our preliminary research findings may contribute to the development of appropriate follow-up management plans for pediatric patients with SSNs. However, further studies are needed to better characterize the natural growth pattern of pulmonary nodules in children.

Several studies with varying sample sizes and inclusion criteria have reported different frequencies of growth in SSNs [i.e., 2% (33), 3.9% (34), and 26.4% (35)]. Compared with these earlier studies, we found a relatively lower growth rate (4%). This discrepancy may be attributed to the differences in age ranges of the study samples. In contrast to other work in this area, the focus of our study was not on adults but rather on children and adolescents. Moreover, the criteria for the growth of SSNs differed between our study and previous ones. Specifically, we included centripetal growth, but this pattern was not common in our study. In addition, Tang et al. proposed three different growth classifications: true growth (2-mm growth threshold), substantial growth (5-mm growth threshold), and stage shift (19); they reported corresponding-rates for SSNs of 46.9% (60/128), 31.3% (40/128), and 7.8% (10/128), respectively (19). A 2-mm threshold is widely accepted as the gold standard for SSN growth assessment, and the rate of true growth was relatively higher compared to that of the other categories. However, it is worth noting that for small PGGNs, applying the 2-mm growth threshold may lead to overmanagement and overdiagnosis (32), notably in children and adolescents. Substantial growth may improve the feasibility of SSN management. However, substantial growth and stage shift are rare in the pediatric patients with SSNs. This evidence suggests that the inconsistency in growth criteria should be addressed in future research.

Deciding upon surgical resection for pediatric patients with persistent SSNs is challenging and should be scientifically determined after thoughtful discussion among multidisciplinary teams, patient, and parents and according to the degree of progression of SSNs and potential benefits and harms. In our study, surgically resected nodules tended to be precursor glandular lesions or adenocarcinoma. This observation is at odds with a previous study by McCarville et al. (36), in which nodules with sharply defined margins were consistently metastases (36), and all pediatric patients in their study had a history of malignant tumors. In contrast, no patient had a history of malignancy in our study. We thus recommend that partially persistent ground-glass nodules should be carefully considered as an early stage of lung adenocarcinoma. Meanwhile, Chen et al. suggested that persistent SSNs may indicate early-stage GGN-featured lung adenocarcinoma in pediatric patients (4), although in our study, a portion of the SSNs regressed or disappeared within the follow-up period (Figure 5). To avoid overdiagnosis (32,37), personalized, patient-centered medicine is recommended for the pediatric patients with persistent SSNs, in accordance with the principles of beneficence, cost-effectiveness, and evidence-based practice (18,38). Therefore, surgical treatment should only be decided upon after careful consideration, and the slow growth rate of SSNs in pediatric patients should be accounted for. A portion of stable nodules may undergo surgery after clinical discussion, which may introduce management selection bias and potentially contribute to overdiagnosis and overtreatment (32,37). The appropriate window for surgical management remains controversial. Early intervention can achieve a good prognosis, especially for patients with AIS or MIA. However, progression in SSN size or the emergence of solid components during surveillance may necessitate more extensive surgical resection or indicate a more adverse prognosis. This highlights the importance of personalized management, and it is hoped our findings can provide a degree of initial guidance and draw attention to the management of children and adolescents with SSNs.

Our study involved several limitations that should be addressed. First, we employed a retrospective design and keyword-based case identification, which might have introduced selection bias. Specifically, cases with more conspicuous SSNs might have been more likely to be included, while the number of smaller indolent SSNs might have been underestimated. This could potentially overestimate the rate of growth or clinical intervention in our cohort. To mitigate this bias, we applied consistent inclusion and exclusion criteria to all identified cases and restricted our analysis to nodules with complete longitudinal follow-up data to minimize missing information. Future large-scale, multicenter studies with expanded sample sizes are warranted to construct and validate a multivariate predictive model for pediatric SSN growth. Second, radiologic measurement variability might have influenced the quantitative assessment of GGN growth. Minor technical variability (e.g., CT slice thickness and reconstruction kernels across CT follow-up) and inherent human subjectivity in manual measurement of SSNs could have potentially led to subtle misclassification of growth status. To minimize this, all measurements were performed on standardized and consistent axial lung window thin-section images, with two independent chest radiologists achieving excellent interobserver agreement. Third, volume doubling time was not applied, and this method may more accurately reflect the growth of pulmonary nodules as compared to diameter (39). In this regard, mass and volume were not measured due to the small sample size. Thus, the statistical power is limited. Finally, we could not evaluate the growth and outcomes of SSNs based on a deep learning strategy and integrated multiomics signatures (40,41), as this method is a time-consuming and complex process. Overall, the aim of our study was to generate preliminary clinical data to guide further research into the management of SSNs in children and adolescents and to pave the way for further validation studies in this field.

Conclusions

Although only 4% of persistent SSNs in children and adolescents showed subsequent growth in our study, these lesions should not be overlooked. Our findings suggest that longer interval surveillance may be reasonable in pediatric patients, particularly to reduce unnecessary radiation exposure, and individualized management should be prioritized.
