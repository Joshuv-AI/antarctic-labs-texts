Novel Combination Dexmedetomidine and Propofol Anesthetic Regimen for DISE Improves Procedural Efficiency Compared to Single‐Agent Regimens
Authors: Ryan Patrick, Ann Birmingham, Paul Allen, Sveta Karelsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study examines a combination anesthetic regimen of propofol and dexmedetomidine during drug induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) and its procedure time, VOTE findings, and perioperative hemodynamic profiles when compared to procedures conducted with either anesthetic agent alone.MethodsRetrospective chart analysis of 407 patients undergoing DISE at a single institution with anesthetic regimens of dexmedetomidine alone, propofol alone, or a combination of both. Demographic information, DISE findings via VOTE scoring, and perioperative vital signs and timing intervals were compared.ResultsWith respect to operative time, the combination regimen cohort demonstrated an average operative time of 22.1 min, 14% shorter than the dexmedetomidine only (25.8 min; p = 0.0001) and 42% shorter than the propofol only (38.4 min; p = 0.0001) cohorts. Recent intra‐system analysis demonstrates a current average DISE time of 14 min with the combination regimen. With respect to perioperative vital sign findings, the combination regimen demonstrated a higher intra‐ and post‐operative minimum heart rate (61.7, 60.9 bpm) compared to the dexmedetomidine regimen (56.9, 56.9 bpm; p = 0.0003, p = 0.0014) and a lower intraoperative O2 nadir (86.0%) than the dexmedetomidine regimen (90.0%, p = 0.0001). With respect to DISE findings, the propofol cohort demonstrated fewer patients with grade 2 tongue base obstruction and a greater portion with compete concentric collapse of the velum as compared to the combination cohort.ConclusionThis novel combination anesthetic regimen of dexmedetomidine and propofol during DISE leads to increased procedural efficiency with preservation of vital sign stability.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common condition with an estimated global prevalence of nearly 1 billion people [1]. OSA poses a significant health burden and has been demonstrated to cause daytime sleepiness and morning headaches as well as contribute to chronic cardiovascular, neurovascular, and neurocognitive conditions. Positive airway pressure (PAP) remains the gold standard therapy for OSA but has poor tolerance; long term adherence to PAP therapy is estimated between 25% and 50% [2, 3]. Given the pervasive intolerance to PAP, alternative therapeutic approaches have evolved including surgical interventions such as neurostimulator implantation, uvulopalatopharyngoplasty, expansion sphincter pharyngoplasty, and transoral robotic surgery [4].

To evaluate candidacy for sleep surgery and optimize targeted intervention, drug induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) has been developed to examine site‐specific patterns of obstruction of the upper airway during a drug‐induced state that mimics natural sleep [5]. An ideal DISE anesthetic protocol would simulate natural sleep, demonstrate upper airway obstruction patterns like those of a patient's OSA pathophysiology, maintain hemodynamic safety during the procedure, allow for safe induction and emergence, and be efficient in order to optimize access to care and resource utilization. Given the prominent disease burden and relatively few equipped and trained sleep surgeons, recent investigations have examined interventions to improve procedural efficiency [6]. More efficient anesthetic protocols during DISE would allow for expanded access for patients with OSA to undergo evaluation for sleep surgery candidacy earlier in their disease processes, mitigating the risks of long‐term, un‐ or under‐treated OSA.

DISE was first described in 1991, and the real‐time assessment of upper airway collapsibility patterns has since been standardized through the advent of the VOTE classification system as first described in 2011 [7, 8]. Despite this standardization of classifying endoscopic findings, anesthetic regimens during DISE remain varied. Anesthetic agent choice can influence DISE outcomes and, therefore, surgical decision making [9]. Though early DISE protocols used benzodiazepines for sedation, most descriptions of DISE in the last 2 decades have demonstrated the use of propofol, and the rationale for its use has been well established [10, 11, 12]. Propofol is an intravenous hypnotic agent used for induction and maintenance of sedation in general anesthesia; its mechanism of action involves potentiation of gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), an inhibitory neurotransmitter [13]. Adverse events associated with propofol include blunting of airway reflexes, hypotension, hypoventilation, and apnea [13]. Dexmedetomidine has been used in more recent years as a sedation agent for adult as well as pediatric DISE and was used and studied for single‐agent sedation for adult DISE at our center from 2016 to 2023 [14]. Dexmedetomidine is an agent with sedative, anxiolytic, sympatholytic, and analgesic‐sparing effects; it is an alpha‐2‐adrenoreceptor agonist. Adverse events associated with dexmedetomidine include bradycardia and hypotension, though the medication demonstrates minimal respiratory depression as compared with propofol [15]. With varying pharmacologic profiles, each agent has specific procedural implications. Propofol demonstrates an oxygen saturation nadir similar to that of patients' polysomnograms, while dexmedetomidine has been shown to most reliably simulate NREM sleep, with minimal cross‐sectional airway obstruction [9, 16, 17, 18]. While propofol has been correlated with tongue base obstruction, dexmedetomidine has led to an increased number of sedation failures as compared to propofol [18, 19]. Other studies have found that DISE findings are generally in agreement between propofol and dexmedetomidine sedation [19]. A 2017 systematic review found that dexmedetomidine was suggested to be the better agent for DISE by most investigators based on its pharmacologic and respiratory safety profile [20]. Both propofol and diazepam have been suggested to have higher levels of base of tongue obstruction, lower oxygen saturation, and lower bispectral index (BIS) scores as compared to dexmedetomidine [9].

The establishment of a DISE anesthetic regimen that maximizes efficiency and best simulates a patient's pathophysiologic anatomic obstruction has significant implications for sleep surgery decision making. A recent meta‐analysis suggests that a combined regimen of dexmedetomidine with a hypnotic agent may represent an optimized DISE regimen [21]. With this in mind, and in the interest of minimizing the dose of any one agent given and, therefore, its side effect profile associated with higher doses, the authors have devised a combined dexmedetomidine and propofol sedation protocol for adult DISE. We examine this DISE anesthetic regimen primarily as compared to a dexmedetomidine‐only regimen and also examine a cohort of DISE with propofol only. The combined regimen of dexmedetomidine and propofol is hypothesized to reduce procedure length, preserve cardiorespiratory stability, and demonstrate similar DISE findings to more established sedation regimens.

Materials and Methods

Retrospective, single‐institution chart review of 407 patients undergoing DISE between 2013 and 2023 was performed at the University of Rochester after receiving IRB exemption. Inclusion criteria were patients over age 18 with OSA and PAP intolerance who presented to the senior author for PAP‐alternative treatment evaluation, had BMI under 35, had no contraindications to sedation or DISE, and agreed to undergo the procedure for the purposes of clinical care during the specified period. Patients were excluded if they received any anesthetic agent during DISE other than that being studied, including receiving one of the studied regimens outside of the specific time period for each cohort, or if they underwent any concurrent procedures. Two sequential, yearlong cohorts undergoing DISE receiving either dexmedetomidine alone (123 patients, from April 1, 2021 to April 1, 2022) or dexmedetomidine and propofol (180 patients, from April 5, 2022 to April 5, 2023) were ultimately identified for analysis [Figure 1]. Chronologically sequential cohorts were chosen to reduce variability in surgeon and institutional experience that may occur over longer periods of time. There was no overlap in regimens and there was no interim transition regimen used. An additional cohort of patients was identified who received propofol alone during DISE (36 patients, from October 25, 2013 to April 8, 2016). The anesthetic protocols were employed as follows: For the dexmedetomidine alone regimen: initial infusion via 1 mcg/kg bolus over 10 min, then maintained at 1 mcg/kg/h with additional increments of 0.2 mcg/kg administered as needed for adequate sedation, which was confirmed by observed sleep, snoring, and nonresponse to verbal or tactile stimuli [14]. For the combination dexmedetomidine and propofol regimen: the 1 mcg/kg dexmedetomidine bolus was administered as previously described, followed by 20 mg propofol boluses at the discretion of the anesthesiologist and surgeon while continuously monitoring levels of sedation, also as above. For the propofol alone regimen: initial infusion rate of 75 mcg/kg/min that was then gradually increased until desired sedation level was achieved. Glycopyrrolate was used in all groups and dosed at the discretion of the anesthesiologist. No supplemental oxygen or airway manipulation was used intraoperatively while DISE assessment was ongoing. Procedural and sedation consistency were accomplished with a single attending surgeon (senior author) performing all procedures, a written protocol for each sedation regimen shared with the anesthesiology team annually and at the time of the protocol change, and in‐person attending surgeon to attending anesthesiologist communication at the beginning of each day verifying each sedation protocol. Our facility has an all‐faculty anesthesiologist group, all of whom are familiar with DISE. Supplemental oxygen and other interventions were used during recovery as needed. DISE scoring was performed by the senior author when sedation level was appropriate and stable obstruction pattern for a minimum of two breaths was verified. The score was recorded in the electronic medical record at the time of the procedure.

Flow chart outlining patient cohort selection. Exclusion criteria included concurrent procedures and use of anesthetic agents other than those of the studied agents in the studied time periods.

Data collected from patient electronic medical records included demographic information (age, body mass index (BMI)), apnea hypopnea index (AHI) on sleep study, VOTE scoring, and perioperative timing. The measured perioperative timing interval consisted of the time the anesthetic was administered (“anesthesia start”) to the time the patient had achieved a safe level of awareness and stability following the procedure to allow for their transfer to the next phase of care, thus freeing up the operating room (“out of room”). These timepoints were manually abstracted from each EMR, where they had been recorded by the operating room staff in a standardized fashion. These timepoints were selected because they represent the modifiable time with respect to procedural efficiency, including both sedation time and the DISE procedure itself. Perioperative vital signs, including maximum and minimum heart rate, oxygen saturation, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) for both intra‐operative and immediate post‐operative (PACU) phases of care, were recorded. Patient specific, individual maximum and minimum recorded vital sign values were chosen instead of averages as mean values would be skewed—more values are often recorded in the setting of vital sign abnormality, thus biasing recorded averages toward brief episodes of clinical significance. MAP of < 65 mmHg was considered clinically significant hypotension, and heart rate of 60–100 bpm was considered normal. The intergroup comparisons for perioperative vital signs, operative timing, and demographics were performed via paired t‐test utilizing a statistically significant p value of 0.025 after Bonferroni correction. The DISE VOTE scoring results were compared via chi‐squared analysis. No data were missing from this data set.

Results

The studied cohorts consisted of 180 patients in the combination regimen protocol, 123 patients in the dexmedetomidine only protocol, and 36 patients in the propofol only protocol. The propofol only cohort composed a patient population with a significantly younger makeup (average age 42.6 years) than the combination regimen (58.1 years); p < 0.0001. The propofol only cohort also had a statistically significantly lower BMI than the combination regimen, 27.1 vs. 29.1, respectively; p = 0.0021. There were no statistically significant differences in AHI between cohorts. No sedation failures were observed in any cohort.

Heart Rate

The combination regimen demonstrated a significantly higher minimum intraoperative heart rate (61.7 bpm) as compared to the dexmedetomidine alone regimen (56.9 bpm); p = 0.0003. There were no other significant inter‐group differences in minimum intraoperative heart rate. With respect to postoperative minimum recorded heart rate, the combination cohort also demonstrated a statistically significant higher value (60.9 bpm) as compared to the dexmedetomidine cohort (56.9 bpm); p = 0.0014. For maximum heart rates, the propofol only cohort demonstrated a statistically significantly higher maximum intraoperative heart rate (108.2 bpm) as compared to the combination regimen (77.5 bpm); p = 0.0001. Statistically significant differences exist when comparing the postoperative maximum recorded heart rates among the three cohorts, but none appear clinically significant as all fall between 71 and 88 bpm.

Blood Pressure

There were no statistically significant differences in intraoperative minimum or maximum MAP between groups. Post‐operatively, a statistically significant difference occurred between minimum MAP of the propofol only cohort (82.6) and the combination regimen (71.6); p = 0.0029. There were no other significant inter‐group differences. All intra‐ and post‐operative MAP values fell within the normotension range.

Oxygen Saturation

The combination regimen demonstrated a statistically significantly lower intra‐operative oxygen saturation nadir (86.0%) than the dexmedetomidine regimen (90.0%); p = 0.0001. There were no other significant differences in intraoperative minimum oxygen saturations between groups. Statistically significant differences were also found when comparing the postoperative minimum recorded oxygen saturation level, but none appear to be clinically significant as all minimum oxygen saturations ranged between 92.7% and 95%. There were no statistically significant inter‐group differences in intra‐ or postoperative maximum recorded oxygen saturations.

VOTE scoring results between cohorts demonstrated some statistically significant differences in findings involving the grade of tongue obstruction and configuration of velum obstruction. With respect to the tongue, the propofol only cohort demonstrated a significantly lower proportion of patients (21%) with grade 2 tongue base obstruction as compared to both the dexmedetomidine only (50%) and combination (54%) cohorts; p = 0.0028. With respect to the velum, the propofol only cohort demonstrated a significantly greater portion of patients with complete concentric obstruction (34%) compared to both the dexmedetomidine only (15%) and combination (12%) cohorts; p = 0.0045. There were no other statistically significant differences in VOTE findings between the three cohorts, including no significant differences in scores between the combination and dexmedetomidine sedation regimens.

Discussion

We describe a novel, combination‐agent adult DISE sedation protocol consisting of dexmedetomidine and propofol. While both propofol and dexmedetomidine are well‐studied independent agents for DISE sedation, to our knowledge this represents the first time this combination regimen is described. It decreases the amount of any one agent given and compares favorably in terms of efficiency to single‐drug sedation while preserving cardiovascular and respiratory stability.

The new combination sedation regimen led to a 14.3% reduction in time from start of sedation to leaving the procedure room compared to the previously described dexmedetomidine regimen, and a 42.4% reduction in this time compared to the propofol regimen. Our DISE practice initially utilized propofol sedation and subsequently transitioned to dexmedetomidine after review of literature indicated that it may be a preferred agent for DISE and because the amount of time needed to achieve propofol sedation was often prolonged and inconsistent. After utilizing the dexmedetomidine sedation protocol, we observed its associated and often delayed vital sign abnormalities, prolonged stays in PACU which are unfortunately not recorded by our EMR, and resulting consternation of perioperative staff. We therefore designed the combination regimen to minimize the use of any one sedation mechanism and maximize efficiency. The adoption and optimization of this combination anesthetic regimen in the years since the currently reported cohort has led to an average procedure time of under 15 min (Figure 3), allowing for several DISE procedures to be performed per hour. This efficiency gain is most practically significant as compared to propofol sedation alone. It may represent an important improvement for patient access to sleep surgery, and for cost management in the operating room or procedure suite.

Maintenance of respiratory and hemodynamic stability is a necessary feature of DISE sedation protocols. The novel combination drug regimen had a statistically and clinically significantly higher minimum intraoperative and postoperative heart rate than dexmedetomidine alone, with minimum HR values over the minimum normal value of 60 bpm for the former and under 60 bpm for the latter. The post‐operative bradycardia seen in our dexmedetomidine‐only cohort was not seen in the combination sedation cohort. Intra‐ and post‐operative MAPs remained above 70 in all cohorts, corresponding to clinical normotension, although the presence of dexmedetomidine sedation decreased post‐operative MAP as may be expected.

Oxygen desaturation during sleep or sedation is a feature of OSA and should be represented during DISE without causing excess desaturation or respiratory suppression as may be seen with propofol sedation [22]. The minimum recorded intraoperative oxygen saturation was lowest in the combination regimen (86%) in our cohort, significantly lower than that of the dexmedetomidine alone regimen at 90%. This value is within the range of what may be expected in sedated OSA patients breathing spontaneously without supplemental oxygen and may not be clinically significant despite statistical significance. However, the finding should be kept in mind if using this combination protocol. Post‐operative differences in minimum oxygen saturation also did not appear clinically significant between groups despite statistical significance, as all were between 92% and 95%, consistent with a cohort of OSA patients emerging from sedation.

With regard to DISE findings, there were no significant differences at any airway level between dexmedetomidine and combination regimens. Interestingly, the propofol cohort showed reduced tongue base obstruction burden as compared to the other anesthetic regimen cohorts, contrary to current literature demonstrating associations between propofol use and increased tongue base obstruction [6, 12, 22]. Additionally, the propofol cohort demonstrated increased rates of complete concentric collapse as compared to the combination or dexmedetomidine alone regimens. These findings should be carefully considered, as the propofol cohort in this study was the smallest of the three and was composed of a statistically significantly younger population with a lower BMI as compared to the combination regimen. Additionally, the propofol DISE procedures were conducted with the least amount of DISE experience on the part of both surgical and anesthesia teams.

This study has some important limitations. It is limited by its retrospective nature, the subjective nature of DISE, and the fidelity of the data accessible from the EMR. All VOTE scoring was performed by one author, which does not allow for inter‐rater reliability assessment. Depth of sedation was not routinely measured with a BIS monitor, instead relying on procedural consistency and large patient numbers. In addition, the historic propofol‐only sedation cohort is small, significantly different from the other cohorts with respect to demographic makeup, and was coincident with the performing surgeon's and anesthesiologists' novelty to DISE. This introduces the possibility of experience‐related confounding factors with regard to the propofol‐only protocol in particular. Therefore, this study should be interpreted mainly as a comparison of the dexmedetomidine‐only and combination sedation protocols. Moreover, the study took place in a very specific environment favorable to the adoption of consistent DISE practices, and favorable efficiency findings of the results may not be generalizable to all settings.

Conclusions

The utilization of a novel, combination anesthetic regimen of dexmedetomidine and propofol during adult DISE leads to significantly faster procedure times and preserved perioperative patient stability compared to more traditionally described protocols. This finding has positive implications for resource utilization and patient access to surgical OSA treatment. Future studies designed to compare this protocol to the more commonly used propofol DISE sedation protocol, in particular, would likely be necessary for widespread adoption and standardization.
