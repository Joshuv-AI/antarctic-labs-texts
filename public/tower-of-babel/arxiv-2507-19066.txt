Sensing magnonic quantum superpositions using a bosonic mode as the probe
Bashab Dey, Sonu Verma, Mathias Weiler, Akashdeep Kamra

Abstract
Sensing quantum superpositions of a magnonic mode has been accomplished using a superconducting qubit by realizing an effective dispersive interaction between the two systems. Here, we theoretically demonstrate that a seemingly classical bosonic mode can be utilized as a probe for sensing quantum superpositions of a magnon mode, while outperforming a qubit in various regards as the sensor. Considering another magnon mode in an antiferromagnet as the probe mode, we delineate the required dispersive coupling emerging directly from antiferromagnetic exchange interaction. When a phonon is used as the probe mode, we derive the effective dispersive coupling emerging from the lowest-order nonlinear magnon-phonon interactions. Our two considered examples provide the general design principles for identifying and utilizing a bosonic probe mode for sensing quantum superpositions in a physical platform of interest.

In this Letter, we theoretically demonstrate a protocol for resolving quantum superpositions of a magnon mode using a simple harmonic mode as the probe. This is achieved via a dispersive cross-Kerr coupling
\hbar\chi\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha\beta^{\dagger}\beta
between the magnon and probe bosonic (annihilation operator
\beta
) modes which makes the spectroscopically measured probe frequency depend on the magnon-number state (Fig.
1
). Considering an antiferromagnetic magnon as the probe mode, we show that the required dispersive coupling is
direct
and results from the exchange interaction in the antiferromagnet. To address a broader range of platforms such as a photon or a phonon as the probe mode, we derive the required
effective
dispersive coupling from the lowest order three-particle interactions between the two modes. We further find that the unbounded energy spectrum of the probe bosonic mode enables it to outperform the qubit as superposition sensor under non-ideal conditions, such as higher temperatures and stronger coherent drives employed for quasi-classical probe spectroscopy.

Direct dispersive coupling between two magnon modes.—

The exchange interaction has recently been shown to cause a direct dispersive coupling between a spin qubit and the magnon mode to be sensed
Römling et al. 2023
;
Römling and Kamra 2024
. Thus, we may anticipate a similar exchange-induced coupling between the two uniform magnon modes in an antiferromagnet (AFM), with one of them serving as the probe. With this motivation, we first examine an easy-axis anisotropy AFM with
\hat{e}_{z}
as the easy axis. In the presence of an external magnetic field
{\bf H_{0}}=H_{0}\hat{e}_{z}
, the Hamiltonian of the AFM is given by
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{E}}+\mathcal{H}_{\text{A}}+\mathcal{H}_{\text{Z}}
with the Heisenberg exchange contribution,
\mathcal{H}_{\text{E}}=\frac{J}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{i}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}{\bf S}_{\text{A}}({\bf r}_{i})\cdot{\bf S}_{\text{B}}({\bf r}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\delta}),
the easy-axis anisotropy
\mathcal{H}_{\text{A}}=-\frac{K}{\hbar^{2}}\left(\sum_{i}[S_{\text{A}}^{z}({\bf r}_{i})]^{2}+\sum_{j}[S_{\text{B}}^{z}({\bf r}_{j})]^{2}\right),

and the Zeeman term
\mathcal{H}_{\text{Z}}=-\gamma\mu_{0}H_{0}\left(\sum_{i}S_{\text{A}}^{z}({\bf r}_{i})+\sum_{j}S_{\text{B}}^{z}({\bf r}_{j})\right).
Here, the constant
J>0
is the strength of antiferromagnetic exchange interaction,
K>0
parameterizes easy-axis anisotropy,
\gamma
the gyromagnetic ratio. Indices
i
and
j
, representing sites on sublattices A and B respectively, span the entire set of
N
magnetic unit cells. The summation
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
in the exchange interaction accounts for all
q
nearest neighbor sites. The ground state of this system is an antiferromagnetically ordered state with the Néel vector oriented along the
z
-axis. Employing Holstein-Primakoff transformations
Holstein and Primakoff 1940
;
Hamer et al. 1992
;
Kamra et al. 2019
, the Hamiltonian for uniform magnon (
{\bf k}=0
) modes is obtained as

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\hbar\omega_{\alpha}\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha+\hbar\omega_{\beta}\beta^{\dagger}\beta+\hbar\chi\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha\beta^{\dagger}\beta.

(1)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are the annihilation operators of the eigenmodes (See SM
SM
for details). The bare magnon frequencies are
\omega_{\alpha(\beta)}=\omega_{0}+(-)\mu_{0}\gamma H_{0}
, where
\hbar\omega_{0}=2S\sqrt{K(Jq+K)}
,
S
is the spin length and
\chi=-Jq/\hbar N
the dispersive coupling strength. Retaining terms up to the fourth order in the magnon operators is needed to obtain the desired interaction. Since the dispersive coupling commutes with the uncoupled magnon modes, the energy eigenstates of (
1
) are
|n_{\alpha}\rangle|n_{\beta}\rangle
with energies

E_{n_{\alpha},n_{\beta}}=\hbar\omega_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}+\hbar(\omega_{\beta}+\chi n_{\alpha})n_{\beta},

(2)

where
n_{\alpha}
and
n_{\beta}
are the magnon numbers of the two modes.
Due to the dispersive interaction, the frequency of one mode depends on the magnon number of the other. Considering
\beta
as the probe bosonic mode, the dispersive coupling manifests itself as the multivalued resonant frequency of the probe oscillator,
\omega_{\text{res}}^{n_{\alpha}}=\omega_{\beta}+\chi n_{\alpha}

when the magnon mode
\alpha
exists in a superposition of the magnon number states
|n_{\alpha}\rangle
[Fig.
1
.(b)].

Resolving magnonic quantum superpositions.—
The probe mode frequency can be determined in various ways. For concreteness and theoretical convenience, we consider the probe mode to be driven by a simple harmonic drive and the probe response is recorded as a function of the drive frequency. When the magnon mode is in a superposition state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{n_{\alpha}}c_{n_{\alpha}}|n_{\alpha}\rangle
, the response of the probe mode should peak at a drive frequency of
\omega=\omega^{n_{\alpha}}_{\text{res}}
with
n_{\alpha}=0,1,2\dots
, the peak heights being proportional to the occupation probabilities
P_{n_{\alpha}}\equiv|c_{n_{\alpha}}|^{2}
of the corresponding magnon number states. This is analogous to qubit spectroscopy used for the dispersive readout of photon-, magnon- and phonon-number statistics
Schuster et al. 2007
;
Lachance-Quirion et al. 2017
;
Arrangoiz-Arriola et al. 2019
;
Römling et al. 2023
.

We demonstrate the sensing of magnonic superpositions by numerically simulating the corresponding spectroscopy
Römling et al. 2023
;
Römling and Kamra 2024
. The setup consists of the magnon mode dispersively interacting with the probe bosonic mode, which in turn is coupled to a thermal bath at temperature
T
[Fig.
2
.(a)]. A thermal bath is chosen to check the efficiency of the probe at finite temperatures. A coherent drive
\mathcal{V}(t)=d\cos\omega t(\beta+\beta^{\dagger})
of strength
d
is applied on the probe. The time evolution of the system is studied by numerically solving the standard Lindblad master equation
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Lindblad 1976

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\mathcal{H}(t),\rho(t)]+\sum_{n=1,2}C_{n}\rho(t)C_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho(t),C_{n}^{\dagger}C_{n}\right\}

(3)

for a bosonic thermal bath. Here,
\rho(t)
and
\mathcal{H}(t)\equiv\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{V}(t)
are the density matrix and Hamiltonian of the magnon-probe system, respectively. The collapse operators
C_{1}=\sqrt{\kappa(n^{\beta}_{\text{th}}+1)}~\beta
and
C_{2}=\sqrt{\kappa~n^{\beta}_{\text{th}}}~\beta^{\dagger}
characterize energy emission to, and absorption from, the bath, respectively. The decay rate of the probe mode is
\kappa
, while its equilibrium thermal occupation is
n^{\beta}_{\text{th}}=[e^{\hbar\omega_{\beta}/k_{B}T}-1]^{-1}
.
When a steady state is reached, the probe excitation
\langle\beta^{\dagger}\beta\rangle\equiv\text{Tr}[\rho\beta^{\dagger}\beta]
peaks at the resonant frequencies
\omega=\left\{\omega_{\text{res}}^{n_{\alpha}}\right\}
[Fig.
2
(a)]. The separation between the peaks is
|\chi|
. At
T=0
, the excitation at
\omega=\omega_{\text{res}}^{n_{\alpha}}
can be written as
\langle\beta^{\dagger}\beta\rangle_{n_{\alpha}}=kP_{n_{\alpha}}
, where
k
is a constant of proportionality that depends on
d
and
\kappa
.

Resolvability of the superpositions at finite temperatures.—

In contrast with the two-level qubit, a bosonic mode at finite temperature occupies several number states. Thus, one may expect finite temperature to be more detrimental to the bosonic mode as a sensor. We examine this question now and find instead that the bosonic mode driven with a coherent drive is more robust than the qubit for sensing the desired quantum superpositions.

In Fig.
3
, we show the spectroscopy simulation data at different temperatures for weak and strong driving. Away from resonance, the probe mode has the mean thermal excitation
n_{\text{th}}^{\beta}
, which determines the baselines in spectroscopy plots. At a resonance, the drive additionally excites the probe mode creating a Glauber state
Glauber 1963
which represents the classical mode of oscillation. So, the magnitude of this extra excitation should be independent of temperature and proportional to the driving power. This is indeed reflected in the spectroscopy as peak heights, measured from the baseline, do not change with temperature [Fig.
3
(a)] and still measure the desired quantum superposition. The consequent net occupation of the probe
\langle\beta^{\dagger}\beta\rangle_{n_{\alpha}}
is expressed as
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Drummond and Walls 1980
:

\langle\beta^{\dagger}\beta\rangle_{n_{\alpha}}=kP_{n_{\alpha}}+n_{\text{th}}^{\beta},

(4)

where
kP_{n_{\alpha}}
is the peak height from the baseline. At high temperatures and weak driving, the peak heights are much smaller than
n_{\text{th}}^{\beta}
which can limit their detectability. This issue is circumvented by driving the probe stronger so that the peaks are high enough [Fig.
3
(b)] without compromising their direct relation to the quantum superpositions being sensed.

Figure 4:
Schematic depiction of the quantum transitions in the probe mode. (a) At
T=0
, a photon is resonantly absorbed at
\omega=\omega_{\text{res}}
causing a transition
|n_{\alpha}\rangle|0\rangle\to|n_{\alpha}\rangle|1\rangle
. (b) At
T\neq 0
, the probe mode has a thermal distribution of the boson number states. Stimulated absorptions (up-arrow) and emissions (down-arrow) occur between adjacent levels. The probe still remains a simple harmonic oscillator as the energy levels remain equally spaced i.e.
\Delta=\hbar\omega_{\text{res}}^{n_{\alpha}}
despite the magnonic superpositions. This makes the bosonic probe a robust superposition sensor even at finite temperatures.

The resonant peaks and their immunity to temperature can be understood intuitively in terms of the quantum transitions that occur between the energy levels
|n_{\alpha}\rangle|n_{\beta}\rangle
of the system. The energy difference between any two levels is

\displaystyle E_{n_{\alpha}^{\prime},n_{\beta}^{\prime}}-E_{n_{\alpha},n_{\beta}}

(5)

\displaystyle=\hbar\big[\omega_{\alpha}(n_{\alpha}^{\prime}-n_{\alpha})+\omega_{\beta}(n_{\beta}^{\prime}-n_{\beta})+\chi(n_{\alpha}^{\prime}n_{\beta}^{\prime}-n_{\alpha}n_{\beta})\big]

The drive
\sim f(\omega t)(\beta+\beta^{\dagger})
only couples levels for which
n_{\alpha}^{\prime}=n_{\alpha}
and
n_{\beta}^{\prime}=n_{\beta}\pm 1
. The corresponding energy difference is

E_{n_{\alpha},n_{\beta}\pm 1}-E_{n_{\alpha},n_{\beta}}=\pm\hbar(\omega_{\beta}+\chi n_{\alpha})=\pm\hbar\omega_{\text{res}}^{n_{\alpha}},

(6)

which is independent of the probe mode occupation
n_{\beta}
. Hence, within linear response, the drive causes resonant transitions
|n_{\alpha}\rangle|n_{\beta}\rangle\to|n_{\alpha}\rangle|n_{\beta}\pm 1\rangle
when
\omega=\omega_{\text{res}}^{n_{\alpha}}
. A schematic of the quantum transitions at a resonance is shown in Fig.
4
for zero and finite temperatures of the probe. At
T=0
, the probe mode is in its ground state. A resonant transition
|n_{\alpha}\rangle|0\rangle\to|n_{\alpha}\rangle|1\rangle
occurs when
\omega=\omega_{\text{res}}^{n_{\alpha}}
[Fig.
4
(a)] and measures the probability of magnon mode being in
n_{\alpha}
state thereby sensing the quantum superposition in the magnon mode. At
T\neq 0
, the probe comes to thermal equilibrium with the bath. Both stimulated absorptions
|n_{\alpha}\rangle|n_{\beta}\rangle\to|n_{\alpha}\rangle|n_{\beta}+1\rangle
and emissions
|n_{\alpha}\rangle|n_{\beta}\rangle\to|n_{\alpha}\rangle|n_{\beta}-1\rangle
occur upon probe driving, as shown by up- and down-arrows, respectively, in Fig.
4
(b). Due to equal level-spacings, the resonant frequencies of stimulated absorption and emission are identical i.e.
\omega_{\text{res}}^{n_{\alpha}}
. Since the resonant frequency is independent of the boson number
n_{\beta}
, it is not modified by the thermal distribution of the probe number states and the spectroscopy protocol for sensing the quantum superpositions in the
\alpha
mode remains valid.

Due to its two-level nature, the qubit is constrained with respect to its maximum excitation. The latter needs to remain small for the spectroscopy to reveal the desired quantum superpositions
Römling et al. 2023
. This constraint gets lifted when using a bosonic mode as the probe since it has an infinitely large Hilbert space thereby enabling effective superposition sensing at higher temperatures. A detailed simulation and comparison presented in the SM
SM
corroborates this superiority of the bosonic mode over the qubit as a sensor.

Effective dispersive interaction between a magnon and phonon mode.—
Unlike the case of an AFM, there may not be a direct dispersive interaction between the magnon mode to be sensed and a generic probe bosonic mode. However, an effective dispersive interaction could be engineered in a wide range of systems that do not host direct dispersive coupling. For example, the linear Rabi coupling between a qubit and bosonic mode results in an effective dispersive coupling in the detuned limit
Savage et al. 1990
;
Peixoto de Faria and Nemes 1999
;
Blais et al. 2004
;
Zueco et al. 2009
. This effective interaction has indeed been utilized for dispersive readout of a superconducting qubit state
Wallraff et al. 2004
;
Liu et al. 2005
;
Lupaşcu et al. 2006
and QND measurement of magnon-
Lachance-Quirion et al. 2017
and photon-number statistics
Schuster et al. 2007
.

Magnomechanical interactions in a ferromagnet also result in magnon-phonon couplings
Abrahams and Kittel 1952
that are linear
\sim\alpha^{\dagger}\beta
+ H.c.
Potts et al. 2021
or quadratic
\propto\alpha^{\dagger 2}\beta,\alpha^{\dagger 2}\beta,\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha\beta
+ H.c.
Rückriegel et al. 2014
;
Gonzalez-Ballestero et al. 2020a
;
Bittencourt et al. 2023
;
Künstle et al. 2025
in the magnon operators, where
\alpha
and
\beta
are the annihilation operators of the magnon and phonon modes respectively. Among the quadratic ones, only the parametric coupling
\sim\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha\beta
+ H.c. is considered relevant in the far-detuned limit
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Gonzalez-Ballestero et al. 2020b
. In our analysis aimed at achieving the desired dispersive coupling between the two bosonic modes, we find that the linear
\sim\alpha^{\dagger}\beta
+ H.c. and parametric couplings
\sim\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha\beta
+ H.c. do not generate any dispersive interaction in the far-detuned limit (see SM
SM
). The parametric-conversion terms
\sim\alpha^{\dagger 2}\beta
+ H.c. as well as the counter-rotating terms
\sim\alpha^{2}\beta
+ H.c. indeed give rise to the desired effective dispersive interaction. To establish design principles, we consider the most general three-particle interaction:

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\hbar\chi_{1}(\alpha^{\dagger}\beta^{2}+\alpha\beta^{\dagger 2})+\hbar\chi_{2}(\alpha\beta^{2}+\alpha^{\dagger}\beta^{\dagger 2})

(7)

\displaystyle+\hbar\chi_{3}(\alpha^{2}\beta^{\dagger}+\alpha^{\dagger 2}\beta)+\hbar\chi_{4}(\alpha^{2}\beta+\alpha^{\dagger 2}\beta^{\dagger}).

In the far-detuned limit (
\omega_{\beta}\gg\omega_{\alpha}
), a Schrieffer-Wolff transformation
Schrieffer and Wolff 1966
;
Landi 2024
on the magnon-phonon Hamiltonian
H_{\text{mp}}=\hbar\omega_{\alpha}\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha+\hbar\omega_{\beta}\beta^{\dagger}\beta+V
gives an effective Hamiltonian

\left[H_{\text{mp}}\right]_{\text{eff}}=\hbar\omega_{\alpha}\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha+\hbar\omega_{\beta}\beta^{\dagger}\beta+\hbar\chi_{\text{eff}}\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha\beta^{\dagger}\beta,

(8)

where

\chi_{\text{eff}}=-\frac{4\chi_{1}^{2}}{2\omega_{\beta}-\omega_{\alpha}}-\frac{4\chi_{2}^{2}}{2\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\alpha}}+\frac{4\chi_{3}^{2}}{\omega_{\beta}-2\omega_{\alpha}}-\frac{4\chi_{4}^{2}}{\omega_{\beta}+2\omega_{\alpha}}

(9)

is the effective dispersive strength (see SM
SM
for details). While we mention a phonon mode as a probe here, our analysis is generic and applies to any bosonic mode that bears a nonlinear coupling with the magnon mode to be sensed.

Physical realization.—
Considering the AFM with exchange-mediated direct dispersive coupling as the platform, hematite in its easy-plane anisotropy phase hosts a high-frequency magnon mode in sub-THz regime and a low-frequency one in few GHz range
Morrish 1995
;
Boventer et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Al-Hamdo et al. 2025
. Hence, one may utilize the latter for sensing the quantum superpositions in the former at moderately high temperatures. A wide temperature range is expected to appear as nearly zero to the high-frequency mode. Since we show that a finite temperature is not a major problem for the probe mode, the desired sensing of quantum superpositions is feasible. Since the linewidths of antiferromagnetic resonances are of the order of MHz, the spectroscopic peaks should be resolvable for nanometer-sized AFMs where the direct dispersive strength
\chi=Jq/\hbar N
can be in the low GHz regime
Zysler et al. 2004
;
Vamvakidis et al. 2013
;
Yang et al. 2015
;
Foner 1963
. For larger AFMs,
\chi
may be enhanced by making thin films
Lui et al. 1986
;
Cortie et al. 2012
;
Yuan et al. 2016
;
Appel et al. 2019
which would reduce
N
along the thickness of the film. Using a phononic mode as the probe for ferromagnetic magnons offers more control over the effective dispersive coupling. Here, the detuning can be optimally chosen for maximizing
\chi_{\text{eff}}
.

Apart from the dispersive term, Kerr-like nonlinearities
(\sim\alpha^{\dagger^{2}}\alpha^{2},\beta^{\dagger 2}\beta^{2}
), frequency-pulling
(\sim\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha,\beta^{\dagger}\beta)
and mixed terms
(\sim\alpha^{\dagger^{2}}\beta^{\dagger}\beta,
etc.) also appear in the full Hamiltonians of Eqs. (
1
) and (
8
). The Kerr nonlinearity renders the driven probe nonclassical due to which the resolution of the superpositions is affected at finite temperature (see SM
SM
for details). A suitable choice of parameters can reduce this nonlinearity. The frequency-pulling term creates only a global shift in the frequencies of the two modes, which does not affect the simulation results.

To conclude, we have demonstrated how the quantum superpositions of the number states of a magnon eigenmode can be sensed using a bosonic probe by coupling it to the magnon mode dispersively. The spectroscopy works well even at high temperatures and stronger driving of the probe mode, which offers a significant advantage over the use of a qubit for this sensing. We discussed two concrete examples of the probe mode - a magnon mode of an AFM and a phonon mode of a ferromagnet, but any bosonic mode can be used. Our analysis lays the groundwork for identifying and utilizing the optimal bosonic probe modes in a given system of interest for quantum manipulation and sensing of superpositions.
