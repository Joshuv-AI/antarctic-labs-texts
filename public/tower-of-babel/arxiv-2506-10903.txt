Beyond Gold Standards: Epistemic Ensemble of LLM Judges for Formal Mathematical Reasoning
Lan Zhang, Marco Valentino, André Freitas

Abstract
Autoformalization plays a crucial role in formal mathematical reasoning by enabling the automatic translation of natural language statements into formal languages. While recent advances using large language models (LLMs) have shown promising results, methods for automatically evaluating autoformalization remain underexplored. As one moves to more complex domains (e.g., advanced mathematics), human evaluation requires significant time and domain expertise, especially as the complexity of the underlying statements and background knowledge increases. LLM-as-a-judge presents a promising approach for automating such evaluation. However, existing methods typically employ coarse-grained and generic evaluation criteria, which limit their effectiveness for advanced formal mathematical reasoning, where quality hinges on nuanced, multi-granular dimensions. In this work, we take a step toward addressing this gap by introducing a systematic, automatic method to evaluate autoformalization tasks. The proposed method is based on an epistemically and formally grounded ensemble (EFG) of LLM judges, defined on criteria encompassing logical preservation (LP), mathematical consistency (MC), formal validity (FV), and formal quality (FQ), resulting in a transparent assessment that accounts for different contributing factors. We validate the proposed framework to serve as a proxy for autoformalization assessment within the domain of formal mathematics. Overall, our experiments demonstrate that the EFG ensemble of LLM judges is a suitable emerging proxy for evaluation, more strongly correlating with human assessments than a coarse-grained model, especially when assessing formal qualities. These findings suggest that LLM-as-judges, especially when guided by a well-defined set of atomic properties, could offer a scalable, interpretable, and reliable support for evaluating formal mathematical reasoning. 1 1 1 Code and data will be made available upon publication.

In summary, the contributions of this work are as follows:

1.

We introduce an epistemically and formally grounded (EFG) taxonomy for formal mathematical reasoning, encompassing key dimensions such as logical preservation, mathematical consistency, formal validity, and formal quality. These dimensions are operationalized through a set of interpretable atomic properties as estimators.

2.

We develop a fine-grained and interpretable framework for automatically evaluating autoformalization based on the introduced taxonomy, which aggregates the outputs of LLM judges specialized on the defined atomic properties into a linear ensemble.

3.

We empirically validate the use of EFG ensembles of LLM-as-judges for autoformalization evaluation. Our results show a strong correlation with human judgments, no evidence of cross-model family bias, and robustness to generation randomness, indicating that EFG ensembles can serve as effective and reliable proxies for evaluating formalization quality.

4.

We investigate the impact of coarse-grained versus fine-grained evaluation aspects in the framework. Our findings reveal that smaller LLMs grounded on EFG criteria can outperform larger LLMs relying on coarse-grained and direct judgment.

2.2
A Taxonomy for Formalization Evaluation

We start by introducing a taxonomy which elicits the constituting parts entailed by a formalization evaluation framing a set of well-defined computable properties (Figure
1
):

Alignment Faithfulness and Formalization Correctness.

A correct candidate formalization
\phi
of a natural language statement
s
should satisfy two key properties:

1.

Alignment Faithfulness:
As a representation of
s
in the formal domain
\mathcal{F}
, the formalization
\phi
must accurately capture the intended semantics of
s
from the natural language domain
\mathcal{S}
.

2.

Formalization Correctness:
As a construct within the formal domain
\mathcal{F}
, the formalization
\phi
must be valid, natural and well-formed within the conventions of the target formalism.

Logical Preservation and Mathematical Consistency.

To ensure that a formalization
\phi
faithfully represents a source statement
s
, it is essential that
\phi
maintains the logical structure and mathematical intent inherent in
s
. We introduce two conceptual properties to characterize this alignment:

1.

Logic Preservation
captures the degree to which the logical content and inferential structure of
s
is preserved within
\phi
.

2.

Mathematical Consistency
reflects how coherently and accurately the mathematical objects and operations present in
s
are represented in
\phi
.

Formal Validity and Quality.
To clarify the intrinsic properties, which can be assessed independently of any source statement
s
, of a formalization
\phi
, we present the following conceptual properties:

1.

Formal Validity
refers to whether
\phi
is syntactically valid within the target formal system. A formally valid expression conforms to all grammatical and structural rules of the language.

2.

Formal Quality
captures the structural clarity and conciseness
\phi
(e.g. non-redundant statements).

Operable Atomic Properties (OAPs).
The properties previously articulated are then materialized into a set of Operable Atomic Properties (OAPs), which are functions designed to be both interpretable and computable. We define a total of 12 such properties, each capturing a distinct and measurable aspect of a candidate formalization. The detailed description of each OAP is provided in Appendix
B
.

3.1
A Linear Ensemble Model for Autoformalization Evaluation

Based on the above analysis, we propose a linear evaluation model to derive a single normalized score that synthesizes all core properties of a candidate formalization. The score is defined as:

S_{\text{OA}}(s,\phi)=w_{\text{LP}}*S_{\text{LP}}(s,\phi)+w_{\text{MC}}*S_{\text{MC}}(s,\phi)+w_{\text{FV}}*S_{\text{FV}}(\phi)+w_{\text{FQ}}*S_{\text{FQ}}(\phi).

(1)

where
S_{\text{OA}},S_{\text{LP}},S_{\text{MC}},S_{\text{FV}},S_{\text{FQ}}\in[0,1]
, and the weights
w_{\text{LP}},w_{\text{MC}},w_{\text{FV}},w_{\text{FQ}}>0
satisfy the constraint
w_{\text{LP}}+w_{\text{MC}}+w_{\text{FV}}+w_{\text{FQ}}=1
. To estimate the optimal component weights, we construct a feature matrix
X
using binary justifications of the core properties, and normalize the 5-point human assessment ratings to the range
[0,1]
, forming the target vector
\boldsymbol{y}
. The optimal weights
\boldsymbol{w}
are then obtained by solving the following constrained quadratic programming problem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{w}}\|X\boldsymbol{w}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad w_{i}\geq 0,\quad\sum w_{i}=1

(2)

Solving this yields the weights
w_{\text{LP}}=0.25,w_{\text{MC}}=0.19,w_{\text{FV}}=0.32,w_{\text{FQ}}=0.24
, with root mean square error normalized by mean
\text{NRMSE}=0.284
and correlation coefficient
\text{Coef}=0.785
.

4.1.1
Results & Discussion

Across different settings, using GPT-4.1 as direct judges generally results in better performance for assessing alignment faithfulness, as indicated by higher accuracy, stronger agreement with human preferences (Cohen’s kappa), more positive correlation, and lower mean squared error for logical preservation and mathematical consistency. In contrast, using GPT-4.1-Mini as OAP judges yields the best performance for evaluating formal quality.
For logical preservation and mathematical consistency, GPT-4.1 achieves the highest precision, suggesting it is more cautious and less likely to incorrectly classify a misaligned candidate formalization as aligned with the original natural language statement. However, the highest recall values for these two aspects are achieved by methods using GPT-4.1-Mini, indicating it is more sensitive to correctly identifying aligned formalizations, despite its smaller size. When comparing the performance of the GPT-4 series on Isabelle/HOL versus Lean4, alignment aspects of Lean4 formalizations are generally more difficult to judge, while formal quality is easier to assess. This is likely due to fundamental differences in the design of the two formal languages.

We conduct a qualitative study on cases where GPT-4.1 misjudges logical preservation and mathematical consistency in formalizations. Specific examples are provided in Appendix
E
. GPT-4.1 often provides reasonable and correct explanations of the formalizations, and its judgments generally align with those explanations. The primary reason for incorrect judgments is the overlap between the logical and mathematical aspects of formalizations, which makes it difficult for GPT-4.1 to clearly distinguish the subtle differences between them through prompt-based interaction.

In assessing formalizations overall, we compute scores using the proposed evaluation model, which incorporates both LLM judgments and formal validity from theorem provers. The correlation coefficients and NRMSE values between these scores and human evaluation scores are reported in Table
4
. Using GPT-4.1 to judge overall assessment, combined with syntactic correctness from the theorem prover, shows a surprisingly strong correlation with human evaluations. However, it still underperforms compared to GPT-4.1-Mini with OAP-WA, particularly on Lean4.
Among the judging settings, using OAP judgments from GPT-4.1-Mini yields the highest positive correlation and the lowest NRMSE with human assessments on both Isabelle/HOL and Lean4.
Across both formal languages, employing GPT-4.1-Mini as the direct judges outperforms GPT-4.1 under the same settings. These results suggest that a more powerful LLM is not necessarily a better judge for evaluating formalizations.

Judging with OAPs enables the generation of non-binary scores for core properties in the proposed evaluation model by weighting relevant OAP judgments.
One possible approach is to assign equal weights to all relevant OAPs for each core property. This setting yields the best performance across both formal languages, achieving positive correlations of 0.662 and 0.479 with human assessments for Isabelle/HOL and Lean4, respectively.
This approach likely performs better because binary values for core properties obscure the individual signal from each OAP, thereby reducing their effectiveness.

5
Related Work

5.1
Autoformalization

Autoformalization bridges the gap between natural and formal languages, enabling natural language inputs to be translated into formal representations. This task has seen promising developments across various domains.

In natural language processing, autoformalization has been applied to validate explanations in natural language inference
[
10
,
26
]
. In formal mathematics, it has emerged as a key component in automated theorem proving pipelines
[
1
,
27
]
. Transformer-based models have been employed for autoformalization in Coq
[
28
]
, while large language models (LLMs) have shown efficacy in Isabelle
[
11
]
.

More recently, retrieval-augmented generation has further enhanced performance in Isabelle
[
12
]
and Lean
[
29
,
30
]
.
Lu et al. 2024a
introduced a process-driven framework for autoformalization in Lean4, and
Li et al. 2024
proposed a scoring framework that selects optimal outputs via complementary self-consistency techniques.

In parallel,
Jiang et al. 2024
and
Liu et al. 2025b
developed data generation pipelines for constructing large-scale parallel corpora of theorem statements. On the evaluation front,
Poiroux et al. 2025
proposed scalable type-check filtering with self-consistency, while
Lu et al. 2024b
trained a model to assess semantic alignment between informal and formal statements. Despite these advances, standardized and comprehensive evaluation metrics for autoformalization remain under-explored. Our work seeks to address this critical gap.

5.2
LLM-as-a-Judge

LLM-as-a-Judge
[
16
]
was proposed for evaluating multi-turn questions and dialogues and has become an increasingly popular approach for assessing NLP tasks using LLMs.

The effectiveness of LLM judges can be further enhanced by designing LLM programs consisting of different modules
[
37
]
, using synthetic training data without human annotation for iterative self-improvement
[
38
]
, exploring improved Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
[
39
]
, or applying reinforcement learning
Whitehouse et al. 2025
.

Bavaresco et al. 2024
conducted an extensive empirical study on replacing human judges with LLMs across 20 NLP evaluation tasks and emphasized the need to validate LLMs carefully against human judgments before using them as evaluators.

Gu et al. 2025
surveyed various methods for developing LLM-as-a-Judge systems and discussed the reliability of such systems.

Wang et al. 2025
examined the presence of judging biases in large reasoning models.

In the domain of mathematical evaluation,
Zhang et al. 2025c
explored the use of LLM judges for scoring natural language proofs across four dimensions. However, limited research has been conducted on employing LLM-as-a-Judge in the field of formal mathematics. Our work takes a step in this direction.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we address the under-explored challenge of evaluating autoformalization by introducing a fine-grained taxonomy that captures multiple aspects of a candidate formalization and propose a linear evaluation model to synthesize core properties into a single overall assessment score.

Through human evaluation and empirical analysis, we demonstrate that this automatic evaluation model provides meaningful indication on formalization performance. Furthermore, we show the feasibility of EFG ensemble of LLM-as-a-Judge, both directly and via OAPs. Our experiments reveal that LLM-based judgments correlate more closely with human assessments than traditional reference-based metrics.

Lightweight LLMs like GPT-4.1-Mini, when guided by OAPs, can serve as effective and cost-efficient judges. However, we also find that LLMs are not yet reliable substitutes for theorem provers in validating formal validity.

Taken together, our findings contribute new insights and tools for automatic and fine-grained evaluation of autoformalization systems, paving the way for more systematic benchmarking and future improvements in this emerging area. Further directions could focus on fine-tuning smaller open-sourced LLMs specialized as judges for autoformalization evaluation and using LLM explanations of judgments to improve autoformalization performance.

Limitations

As in different studies involving human annotations, some of our results may be partially affected by subjective biases. However, we attempt to limit subjectivity by leveraging annotators with background knowledge in both AI and formal languages, capable of articulating the rationale behind the annotation’s decisions. Additionally, the scale of human-annotated samples is relatively small due to the complexity involved in the annotation. While expanding the annotation would likely yield more robust and generalizable results, we do not expect it to alter the main findings. Moreover, the set of operable atomic properties and LLMs used in our approach is not exhaustive. Although alternative configurations might yield slightly different results, we believe they would not substantially impact the core conclusions of the study.

We explore the potential of using LLM-judges as proxies for theorem provers in three settings: (i) GPT-4.1 judging directly; (ii) GPT-4.1-Mini judging directly; and (iii) GPT-4.1-Mini judging OAPs of formal validity, where formal validity is considered negative if any individual OAP judgment is negative. We employ Cohen’s kappa to measure the agreement between these settings and theorem provers on GPT-4.1 zero-shot autoformalization results from the miniF2F validation set, with kappa values reported in Figure
5
. Overall,
LLM-judges are not reliable substitutes for theorem provers, as Cohen’s kappa does not exceed 0.30 in any setting.
The use of LLM-judge approaches as proxies is particularly limited on Lean4, where agreement with theorem provers is even lower. Among the settings, GPT-4.1 direct judging appears more likely to align with theorem prover judgments.
