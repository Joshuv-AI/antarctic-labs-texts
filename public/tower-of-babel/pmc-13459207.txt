DNA methylation signatures of inflammation in youth living with perinatally-acquired HIV
Authors: Stephanie Shiau, Sean S. Brummel, Junjie Hu, Jasmine Douglas, Francesca Zumpano, Michael Corley, Jennifer Jao, Murli Purswani, Kunjal Patel, Carmen J. Marsit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpigenetic modifications may illuminate mechanisms by which HIV and antiretroviral therapy (ART) exposure during critical developmental periods affect biological pathways and chronic comorbidity risk. We examined genome-wide DNA methylation (DNAm) in youth with perinatally-acquired HIV (YPHIV) compared to perinatally HIV-exposed uninfected youth (YPHEU) at two timepoints. In YPHIV, we assessed associations of HIV RNA viral load, CD4 count, and inflammation with DNAm alterations.MethodsGenome-wide DNAm was measured in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 32 YPHIV and 7 YPHEU at two timepoints separated by at least 3 years via the PHACS Adolescent Master Protocol. Differentially methylated CpG sites were evaluated genome-wide (FDR p < 0.05 and ≥ 5% methylation difference) and across 15 pre-selected targeted genes at each timepoint. Regional and pathway enrichment analyses were conducted. Among YPHIV, genome-wide associations of HIV viral load, CD4 count, and inflammation markers with DNAm were assessed.ResultsNo genome-wide differentially methylated CpG sites were identified between YPHIV and YPHEU at either timepoint. For targeted genes, CpG sites on PSMB9 were differentially methylated at both timepoints. Among YPHIV, neither viral load nor CD4 count was associated with DNAm. In contrast, C-reactive protein was associated with 17 and 18 CpG sites at timepoints 1 and 2, respectively, with 7 overlapping genes, including two involved in blood pressure regulation and kidney function. Interleukin-6 was associated with 3 and 2 CpG sites at timepoints 1 and 2, respectively, with 2 overlapping genes involved in fatty acid metabolism and neuronal signaling.ConclusionMarkers of inflammation were associated with DNA methylation signatures in YPHIV during childhood/adolescence. These epigenetic signatures may provide insight into the inflammatory pathways that contribute to chronic comorbidity risk in YPHIV and suggest inflammation as a potential target for early intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02148-3.

Introduction

Although antiretroviral therapy (ART) has greatly improved survival for people living with HIV, health risks persist [1]. HIV-associated comorbidities and complications, such as cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, liver and kidney disease, and cognitive decline are observed at earlier ages and higher rates in adults with HIV [2, 3]. Youth living with perinatally-acquired HIV (YPHIV) also show early indicators of these comorbidities, including metabolic complications such as dyslipidemia and low bone mass [4]. These observations have led to speculation that HIV and its treatment accelerates aging processes, leading to premature aging. Although exact mechanisms involved are not known, recognized pathways involve chronic immune activation and inflammation leading to cellular senescence and increased vulnerability for HIV-associated comorbidities [5, 6].

Epigenetic modifications can change gene expression without directly altering the DNA base pair sequences in genomic code [7]. These modifications may provide mechanisms through which HIV and ART exposure during critical developmental periods affect biological pathways and disease risk. One of the most commonly studied epigenetic changes is DNA methylation (DNAm), the addition of a methyl group to a cytosine followed by a guanine (cytosine-phosphate-guanine; CpG), which can affect gene transcription and regulate biological processes [8]. Epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) can test for DNAm changes across CpG sites and identify affected biological pathways. Using this genome-wide approach to characterize patterns of DNAm in the host genome may identify novel biological pathways affected by HIV and its treatment.

Several recent studies have studied the effect of HIV on DNAm in the host genome. A study of a monozygotic twin pair (one with HIV, one without HIV) reported 4679 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) associated with HIV, including hypermethylation at IGFBP6 and SATB2 [9, 10]. A study of 137 adults with HIV and 44 without primarily identified hypomethylation in the region encoding the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region, which plays an important role in the adaptive immune system [11]. An EWAS of DNAm in the Veterans Aging Cohort Study (VACS) identified 20 significantly differentially methylated (DM) CpG sites associated with HIV in 216 adults with HIV and 117 adults without HIV, including hypomethylation at a CpG site located in the promoter region of NLRC5, which encodes a transcription factor that regulates the HLA region [12].

We previously reported evidence that changes in the epigenome, specifically in DNAm profiles, are detectable in youth 4–9 years with HIV on suppressive ART [13]. Widespread DNAm differences associated with HIV and ART were detected including 1309 significant DM CpG sites on genes involved in B-cell maturation and regulation of the adaptive immune system. Overall, in contrast to adults we observed more hypermethylation than hypomethylation. Of note, CpG sites on NLRC5 were hypomethylated as observed in adults. Specific genes with DM CpG sites included genes in the HLA region (RFP, HCG22, PSORS1C1, SLC44A4, AGER, HLA-DQB2, TAP2, PSMB9) and outside the HLA region (NLRC5, XDH, SPERT, EBF4, FOXP1, FNDC3B, DLL1).

Here, we examined whether perinatally-acquired HIV is associated with genome-wide alterations in the DNA methylome by comparing YPHIV and youth who are perinatally HIV-exposed uninfected (YPHEU) at two timepoints. The 15 targeted genes identified from prior work were also assessed for DM CpG sites. Among YPHIV, we examined associations between HIV RNA viral load (VL), CD4 count, and markers of inflammation with genome-wide alterations in the DNA methylome.

Methods

Study sample

Thirty-two YPHIV and 7 YPHEU with peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples collected at two timepoints ≥ 3 years apart were selected from the US-based Pediatric HIV/AIDS Cohort Study (PHACS) Adolescent Master Protocol, a longitudinal, prospective cohort study of YPHIV and YPHEU enrolled from March 2007 to November 2009 at 15 sites in the US including Puerto Rico. The Institutional Review Boards (IRB) at all participating sites and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health approved the study. Written informed consent was provided by parents or legal guardians for child and caregiver participation. Youth provided assent per local IRB guidelines. All YPHIV had VL indicators within 1 year of birth, and within 1 year of each sample timepoint. Participants were evenly distributed by sex and prioritized for the largest time difference between the first and last sample. Those with Hepatitis B or C co-infection or who reported smoking were excluded from the sample.

Measurements

Demographic and clinical information including history of ART, VL, and CD4 count were collected. Time-averaged area under the curve (AUC) CD4 count and VL were calculated using the trapezoidal rule. Covariates included sex, age at each timepoint of blood draw, race/ethnicity, and cell type proportions. Additional covariates for YPHIV included: ART regimen (current at each time of blood draw) and age at ART initiation.

Systematic inflammatory markers were measured in serum at each timepoint, included: C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, interleukin-6 (IL-6), soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (sVCAM-1), adiponectin, E-selectin (sE-selectin), monocyte chemoattractant protein (sMCP-1), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (sICAM-1), and P-selectin (sP-selectin). Inflammatory marker’s levels were quantified using immunoassays and nephelometry with low inter- and intra-assay variability as previously described [14–16].

DNA methylation assessment

Frozen PBMC samples were sent to Emory University for DNA extraction using Omega M6399 extraction kit (Omega BIO-TEK, Norcross, GA) on a KingFisher Flex instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The DNA was quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Tecan Infinite M200 Pro plate reader (Tecan Systems Inc., San Jose, CA). DNA quality was assessed by agarose gel. Bisulfite modification was performed using the Zymo EZ-96 DNAm kit (Zymo Inc, Irvine, CA). DNAm was assessed using the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip, covering over 850,000 CpG sites (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA).

All pre-processing and analyses were performed using R (version 2024.09.1 + 394). Standard pre-processing pipeline procedures were performed using the minfi Bioconductor package (version 3.4.4) [17]. Quality control metrics and filtering procedures were performed on the data. 3070 CpG probes were excluded due to poor detection p values (p value > 0.01). No samples had poor detection p values in more than 5% of CpG probes. Overall, a total of 78 samples from 39 young adults were included in the analysis. Overall, 862,789 CpG sites were retained for further analysis.

The data were normalized and background-corrected [18, 19]. DNAm data were exported from minfi as β-values (proportion of methylated alleles at each CpG site) and standardized across probe types with beta mixture quantile (BMIQ) normalization [18]. Normalized β-values were adjusted for potential batch effects using the combat function in the R package, sva [20, 21]. Cell type proportions (CD4 T-cells, CD8 T-cells, granulocytes, monocytes, B-cells, natural killer cells) were estimated by the Houseman method [18]. The EPIC methylation annotation was based on the IlluminaHumanMethylationEPICanno.ilm10b4.hg19 package [13].

Statistical analyses

Epigenome-wide association study (EWAS)

The epigenome-wide association of HIV infection with DNAm at each CpG site was evaluated using unadjusted and adjusted linear regression models with the CpGassoc package (version 2.6) and limma package (version 3.48.3) [20, 21]. Difference in average methylation was compared between YPHIV and YPHEU groups at both timepoints using unadjusted models. Among YPHIV, we conducted genome-wide analyses of VL, CD4 count, and inflammation markers with DNAm using both unadjusted and adjusted models. Adjusted models included sex, race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic Black or not), and age at the sample timepoint, ART regimen and age at ART initiation as covariates. Significant DM CpG sites were identified if false discovery rate (FDR) at 5% using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure and |Δβ|> 0.05, where Δβ is the mean difference between the average DNAm of the groups or mean change in DNAm for each 1 unit increase in inflammatory markers for YPHIV. Control of the family-wise error rate at 5% (Bonferroni corrected p value < 5.928 e−8) was also presented. For transparency, we presented the top 50 CpG sites ranked by adjusted p value, regardless of statistical significance.

Regional analysis for DMRs associated with HIV was conducted using the DMRcate package (v2.6.0) [22, 23]. DMRcate applies Gaussian kernel smoothing (bandwidth = 1000, scaling factor = 2) to squared, moderated t-statistics calculated through limma for individual CpG sites. P values are calculated from the modeled, smoothed statistic and use a Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction (FDR = 0.05) to combine significant sites into regions. A Stouffer transformation of limma p values was used to determine statistical significance of the regions. A region was considered significantly DM if the Stouffer p value was < 0.05, |Δβ|> 0.05, and the region contained at least 2 CpG sites.

Targeted gene analysis

Fifteen targeted genes identified from prior work were assessed for DM CpG sites [13]. Unadjusted and adjusted linear regression models were applied, adjusting for sex, race/ethnicity, and cell type proportion. Genes of interest included: RFP, HCG22, PSORS1C1, SLC44A4, AGER, HLA-DQB2, TAP2, PSMB9, NLRC5, XDH, SPERT, EBF4, FOXP1, FNDC3B, DLL1. Significant DM was determined by BH FDR adjusted p value < 0.05 and |Δβ|> 0.05. Comparisons were made between YPHIV and YPHEU groups at both timepoints.

Pathway enrichment

Biological pathway enrichment analyses were conducted for significant CpG sites and regions using missMethyl (version 1.26.1) package [24]. GOmeth tested CpG sites against Gene ontology (GO) categories, where the p values were obtained using Wallenius’ noncentral hypergeometric distribution [25]. Significant pathways were considered enriched for DM by a BH FDR adjusted p value < 0.05.

Longitudinal analysis

To evaluate differences in methylation rate changes between groups, longitudinal analysis of the association of HIV infection with average DNAm was evaluated over time (timepoints 1 and 2). An unadjusted linear mixed effects model was applied at each CpG site, with participant ID as a random effect and HIV group (YPHIV or YPHEU) as a fixed effect, assuming an exchangeable correlation structure within participants. Restricted maximum likelihood (ReML) estimation was used to estimate the average differential methylation between groups. The nlme package in R was used to fit the mixed effects models. The BH procedure was used to control for multiple hypothesis testing with FDR < 0.05. CpG sites with |Δβ|> 0.05 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly DM [26].

Post hoc power analysis was conducted using the pwrEWAS R package (0.99.64) [27]. Power was calculated for a range of effect sizes, 0.1–0.5, assuming 1500 DM CpG sites, a sample size of 39 (32 YPHIV and 7 YPHEU), 862,789 tested CpG sites, and an FDR threshold of 0.05.

Discussion

In this study, we examined if perinatally-acquired HIV was associated with DNAm by comparing YPHIV and YPHEU, and analyzed associations of VL, CD4 count, and inflammatory markers with genome-wide alterations in the DNA methylome among YPHIV. Unlike previous studies predominately conducted in adult populations, this study focused on a US-based younger sample to provide insight into early epigenetic changes. Although our EWAS did not identify any CpG methylation sites that were strongly associated with HIV infection status, VL levels, or CD4 counts, the targeted analysis revealed associations between HIV infection and DM CpG sites on PSMB9. Moreover, we detected associations between markers of inflammation, particularly CRP and IL-6, and epigenetic signatures among genes involved in blood pressure regulation, kidney function, fatty acid metabolism, and neuronal signaling in YPHIV at both timepoints. These findings highlight inflammation-linked epigenetic alterations that might contribute to chronic cardiometabolic comorbidities in YPHIV.

Our analysis did not detect any significant CpG sites associated with HIV infection status in young adults. This result contrasts with previous findings from predominantly adult studies, which identified DM CpG sites associated with HIV infection [28, 29], including hypomethylation in the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region and in the promoter region of NLRC5 [11, 12]. The lack of findings is likely attributed to the smaller sample size in our study. Additionally, our study focused on a younger population, hence early-life epigenetic changes might manifest differently compared to adults.

The targeted analysis revealed hypomethylation in PSMB9 among YPHIV compared to YPHEU. PSMB9 is critical for CD8 + T-cell activation and immune responses modulation and is upregulated by interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), a pro-inflammatory cytokine [30, 31]. This upregulation enhances antigen processing through MHC class I, creating a feedback loop of increased CD8 + T-cell activation and IFN-γ production, further promoting PSMB9 expression and enhancing the immune response [31]. The observed hypomethylation of PSMB9 in YPHIV in our study may reflect chronic immune activation due to HIV infection.

In this study of YPHIV on ART, DNAm was not found to be linked to VL levels or CD4 counts, in contrast to prior reports. One study of 184 ART-naïve adults in Europe observed a weak association between DNAm changes after ART initiation and changes in CD4 counts [28]. Another study of 261 adults (79% on ART) found that methylation of two CpG sites in the NLRC5 gene was negatively associated with VL in those with HIV [12]. The inconsistency might be driven by our small sample size and relatively young sample, or differences in variability of VL levels due to ART.

Even with effective ART, individuals with HIV often experience elevated levels of chronic inflammation. Our study found significant hypomethylation at CpG sites associated with inflammatory markers, CRP and IL-6, at both timepoints in YPHIV, indicating that these represent stable epigenetic signatures of chronic immune activation, rather than transient inflammatory responses. Among the genes showing consistent associations, the FA2H gene is involved in fatty acid metabolism, encoding a protein that catalyzes the synthesis of 2-hydroxysphingolipids, a subset of sphingolipids with 2-hydroxy fatty acids [32]. ADCY2 is involved in neuronal signaling and encodes an enzyme typically expressed in the human brain; it was one of the most relevant genes in a network associated with HIV status and fatigue [33]. Interestingly, it has also been shown to regulate IL-6 expression in bronchial smooth muscle cells [34]. PRKAR1B encodes the regulatory subunit of cyclic AMP-dependent protein kinase A and has been shown to be downregulated in an HIV transcriptional profiling study [35]. The ACE gene provides instructions for making the angiotensin-converting enzyme, which helps to regulate blood pressure and may be involved in lung function and immune response [36]. SHROOM3 plays a role in maintaining normal kidney function and has been linked to chronic kidney disease [37]. Both CRP and IL-6 are known to be associated with cognition [38, 39] and metabolic disorders such as diabetes and insulin resistance [40, 41] that YPHIV are at higher risk for [4, 42], and these findings point to epigenetic changes as a potential mechanism.

While the CRP-associated DMRs did not meet our effect size threshold, several identified regions have been previously linked to inflammation. PHACTR1 shows differential methylation in coronary artery disease and associates with endothelial inflammation [43]. TBC1D7 has been linked to immune responses and inflammatory pathways in benzene-exposure studies [44]. GUSBP5 has been identified as a marker of cumulative epigenomic damage in H. pylori infection, suggesting that methylation changes in this region may reflect chronic inflammation [45].

In addition, prior studies have shown links between epigenetic changes at IL-6 and CRP genes and adverse health outcomes. For example, hypomethylation of IL-6 has been shown to increase the risk of essential hypertension and coronary heart diseases [46, 47]. DNAm on CRP is linked to neurodegenerative diseases, cardiometabolic and respiratory diseases [48, 49]. Furthermore, IL-6 is associated with accelerated epigenetic aging in individuals with HIV [50], and increased likelihood of developing age-related and HIV-related comorbidities, such as renal disease [51]. Our findings provide evidence that inflammation may affect epigenetic pathways that drive chronic comorbidities and HIV-related diseases.

This study has several strengths and limitations. The longitudinal study design allows the evaluation of DNAm changes at two distinct timepoints, providing a dynamic understanding of epigenetic modifications among YPHIV. Additionally, focusing on a pediatric/adolescent population minimizes confounding from age-related DNAm changes, which provide a clearer association of the epigenetic mechanisms. However, the study’s small sample size, particularly the limited number of YPHEU, reduced the statistical power to detect genome-wide significant DM CpG sites between groups. Power calculations indicate 34–73% power to detect effect sizes ranging from 0.1 to 0.5, assuming 1500 target DM CpG sites, demonstrating that the study is likely underpowered to detect smaller differences. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted.

In conclusion, our study revealed that inflammatory markers IL-6 and CRP are significantly associated with epigenetic signatures in YPHIV at two timepoints. These findings suggest that chronic inflammation potentially contributes to HIV-associated chronic comorbidities among YPHIV.
