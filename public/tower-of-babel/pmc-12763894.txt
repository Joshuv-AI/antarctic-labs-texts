SGMS2+ macrophages enhance NR4A3hi NK cell infiltration to improve prognosis and PD-1 treatment efficacy in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Fansheng Meng, Fulin Nian, Haokang Feng, Zhixue Chen, Qiuyu Jiang, Feng Zhang, Lin Xu, Yueying Chen, Hao Zhang, Tao Ye, Biwei Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is characterized by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) that critically propels malignant evolution. Sphingomyelin synthase 2 (SGMS2), the pivotal sphingolipid metabolizing enzyme, catalyzes the conversion of ceramide to sphingomyelin. Emerging evidence highlights sphingolipid metabolism as a key regulator of tumor immunity, particularly in shaping macrophage phenotypes within the TME. However, the macrophage-specific contributions of SGMS2 to HCC progression remain unexplored. This study elucidates the critical role of SGMS2 in shaping TME and influencing patient prognosis.MethodsBulk RNA-seq analysis was performed using TCGA-LIHC datasets (n = 371). Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics were used to identify SGMS2-expressing cell types and evaluate spatial immune interactions. Clinical validation was performed on a cohort of 188 HCC patients (2017–2022) using multiplex immunofluorescence to quantify SGMS2+ macrophages and NR4A3hi NK cells. THP-1 monocytes were transfected with lentiviral SGMS2 overexpression vectors and differentiated into macrophages via PMA induction. Co-culture assays with liver tumor cells were performed in transwell plates for 48 h, followed by tumor cell apoptosis assessment using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and flow cytometry.ResultsHigh SGMS2 expression in HCC tissues was associated with improved patient prognosis. SGMS2 was mainly expressed in macrophages within the HCC immune microenvironment, promoting their polarization toward the M1-like phenotype. High infiltration of SGMS2+ macrophages significantly prolonged overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), and early RFS. Notably, patients with high infiltration of SGMS2+ macrophages accompanied by low infiltration of SGMS2- macrophages had the most favorable prognosis. Mechanistically, SGMS2+ macrophages secreted CXCL2, recruiting CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high cytotoxic NK cells, enhancing tumor cell apoptosis and improving prognosis. In patients receiving PD-1 therapy, those with high infiltration of SGMS2+ macrophages and CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells demonstrated superior treatment responsiveness and prolonged survival.ConclusionsSGMS2 could polarize macrophages toward the M1 phenotype. SGMS2+ macrophages secrete CXCL2 to recruit CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells, thereby enhancing tumor apoptosis. High infiltration of SGMS2+ macrophages and CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells correlates with superior PD-1 therapy responsiveness and extended survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07040-x.Keywords: Macrophage, Sphingomyelin synthase 2, Natural killer cell, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Tumor microenvironment, Immune checkpoint inhibitors

Introduction

Primary liver cancer is one of the most common malignancies globally with a poor prognosis. It ranks sixth in global cancer incidence and third in mortality [1], with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounting for approximately 80% of cases [2]. Although surgical resection remains the most effective first-line treatment for HCC, postoperative recurrence rate remains alarmingly high, ranging from 50 to 70% [3]. Notably, 70% of recurrent cases occur within the first two years, severely threatening patient survival and quality of life [4]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) combined with targeted therapies has been incorporated into the 2024 liver cancer management guidelines as a first line treatment regimen [5]. However, the overall response rate of such therapies remains limited, at approximately 20% [6]. Consequently, exploring novel immunotherapeutic strategies for HCC is an urgent priority.

Macrophages, as critical components of the HCC immune microenvironment, play a pivotal role in modulating tumor biology [7]. Within the HCC immune microenvironment, macrophages interact with immune cells, including NK cells and T cells, to influence anti-tumor immune responses and shape clinical outcomes [8]. Given their central role, macrophage-targeted immunotherapies have garnered significant attention [9–12]. A promising strategy involves reprogramming macrophage phenotypes within the immune microenvironment, shifting tumor-promoting, immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages toward pro-inflammatory, tumor-suppressive M1-like phenotypes [9, 13]. However, the efficacy of current therapies remains suboptimal. For instance, monotherapy or combination regimens targeting macrophages have demonstrated objective response rate of only 0–10% in patients [14, 15]. A key limitation lies in the oversimplified M1/M2 dichotomy, which fails to account for the functional heterogeneity of macrophage subsets [16]. Identifying and characterizing distinct molecular subtypes of macrophages and their functions may uncover novel therapeutic targets for macrophage-centric immunotherapy. Sphingomyelin Synthase 2 (SGMS2, also known as SMS2), is a key enzyme in sphingomyelin synthesis, promoting sphingolipid metabolism and plasma membrane homeostasis. Previous studies have suggested that SGMS2 regulated macrophage inflammatory responses by modulating the synthesis of long-chain sphingomyelins [17]. SGMS2-deficient macrophages exhibit markedly attenuated inflammatory responses upon immunogenic stimulation, reducing the risk of immune-related inflammatory diseases in mice, such as atherosclerosis, endotoxin-induced lung injury, and colitis-associated colorectal cancer [18–20].

NK cells, known for their cytotoxic effects, are effector cells in anti-tumor immunity [21]. Early recurrent HCC tumors often exhibit a marked deficiency in NK cell infiltration [22]. However, NK cells display functional heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment. Beyond the two classical subsets—the CD56brightCD16—population, which primarily secrete cytokines, and CD56dimCD16+ subset, which induce apoptosis in target cells via cytolytic granules—NK cells can be further divided into molecular subtypes based on distinct surface markers or transcriptional signatures [23]. This diversity has made NK cell-targeted immunotherapy a cutting-edge area in cancer treatment, aiming to modulate endogenous NK cell activity or deliver exogenous NK cells for adoptive therapy [24].

In our preliminary studies, SGMS2 was identified as a key molecule mediating NK cell recruitment. However, the mechanisms by which SGMS2 regulates NK cell recruitment and its impact on patient prognosis remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the role of SGMS2 in macrophage and its influence on the immune microenvironment, with a particular focus on its interactions with NK cell subsets. By elucidating these molecular mechanisms, this study seeks to identify novel therapeutic targets to reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment, overcome the limitations of current immunotherapies, and improve clinical outcomes for HCC patients.

Methods

Patients and tissue microarrays

A total of 200 HCC tumor tissue samples were collected from patients who underwent hepatectomy at Minhang Hospital, Shanghai, between January 2008 and December 2013. After excluding ten patients due to incomplete follow-up data and two paired cases with insufficient tumor content (< 5%), 188 patients were included in the final analysis. Tissue microarrays (TMAs) were constructed by extracting 1.5-mm diameter cores from representative tumor regions of each sample. The clinicopathological characteristics of all patients are detailed in Table 1. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Minhang Hospital.

Datasets acquisition and process

RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinicopathological information for the TCGA-LIHC cohort (n = 371 tumor patients, n = 50 normal patients) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). We performed Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis to evaluate the association between SGMS2 expression levels (cutoff value: 444 for 36 months and 562 for 60 months) and disease-specific survival (DSS) in HCC patients using previously published HCC sequencing data and clinical profiles [25]. The analysis was conducted using an online tool accessible at https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=service&cancer=liver_rnaseq. Additionally, the GSE76427 dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, and Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve analysis was performed to investigate the association between SGMS2 expression levels and early recurrence-free survival (early RFS) in patients. Spatial transcriptomics sequencing data were obtained from http://lifeome.net/supp/livercancer-st/data.htm[26] and analyzed using Seurat in R. Subsequently, SCTtransform normalization was performed. For the identification of different subsets of NK cells using spatial transcriptomics data, we downloaded gene sets of various NK cell types from previous studies [23] and quantified them using ssGSEA enrichment analysis.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining

Slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated by ethanol. Antigen retrieval (AR) was performed in Tris–EDTA buffer (PH9.0) at boiling temperature, followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Non-specific antigens were blocked with goat serum solution. Slides were incubated with primary antibodies overnight (Detailed in Additional file 2: Table S1), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature. Opal tyramide signal amplification (TSA) fluorochromes (Opal 7-Color Manual IHC Kit, Perkin Elmer, NEL811001KT) were applied for 20 min at 37 °C. DAPI was applied for visualizing nuclei, and sealed with glycerin. Slides were scanned using the Akoya Vectra Polaris™ Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System to generate qptiff result files. The qptiff files were opened in QuPath 0.5.0, where cell segmentation was performed based on DAPI staining using the software’s “positive cell detection” function. This process enabled the acquisition of fluorescence intensity values for each marker in every cell. Following cell segmentation, cell types were defined based on the fluorescence intensity of specific markers (CD68/SGMS2 for macrophages, CD56/CD16/NR4A3 for NK cells). Cell density was calculated to quantify immune cell infiltration within each tumor tissue sample on TMA.

CIBERSORT analysis

Matrix of gene expression values was obtained from the TCGA database. Using the LM22 file as the reference for 22 immune cell gene sets, CIBERSORT analysis was performed in R using the CIBERSORTx package to obtain enrichment scores for 22 immune cells in each tumor sample, including M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages.

Stable transfection cell lines construction

THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, NY, USA) and 1% ampicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, NY, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. For stable overexpress clone, cells were established via lentiviral transduction. Briefly, full length SGMS2 cDNA were cloned into pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Greenpuro lentivector. The recombinant plasmid and virus packaging plasmids were co-transfected into HEK-293 T cells by lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, CA, USA). The virus-containing supernatants were collected and filtered after 24 h. Finally, cells were cultured with the virus-containing supernatants for 48 h and screened using 2 μg/mL puromycin for 1 week and then verified by western blotting analysis.

Co-culture of THP-1-derived macrophages and HCC Cells

Before the experiment, THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages by treatment with 100 ng/mL PMA for 12 h, and the cell supernatant was collected for subsequent detection. Macrophages and hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Hun7, HepG2 and MHCC97-H) were seeded into a 6-well plate at a 1:1 ratio (5 × 105 cells each). After co-culture for 36 h, adherent cells were digested with trypsin. Following the termination of digestion, cells were centrifuged and resuspended for flow cytometry detection.

Western blotting

Following experimental treatments, cells cultured in 10-cm dishes were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF protease inhibitor (Beyotime, ST506). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to pellet cellular debris. Protein concentrations were denatured in loading buffer (Epizyme, LT103) at 95 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of whole-protein extract were resolved on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Germany), which were probed with primary antibodies against SGMS2 (abs112079, Absin), iNOS (22226-1-AP, Proteintech), CD80 (66406-1-Ig, Proteintech) and GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech). Proteins were detected by goat anti-rabbit (SA00001-2, Proteintech) and anti-mouse (SA00001-1, Proteintech) horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies and then revealed using High-sig ECL Western Blotting Luminol/Enhancer Solution(180-5001, Tanon).

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Cells were washed with PBS and digested with trypsin. Harvested cells were resuspended for counting, and a portion were centrifuged to remove the supernatant. Annexin V solution was added and incubated in the dark. PI solution was added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated away from light. A negative control tube without Annexin V or PI was prepared. Flow cytometry was used to detect apoptotic cells.

ELISA assays

Supernatants were collected after THP-1 differentiated into macrophages. CXCL2, CCL3, CCL20, TNF-a and IL-6 concentrations were determined using ELISA kits (EK1264, EK161, EK1211, EK182 and EK1153, MultiSciences, China). Optical density values were measured and further analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 20 and GraphPad Prism. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Differences between multiple groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA, and pairwise comparisons were conducted using the least significant difference test. Student’s t-test was performed to analyze the differences between the two groups with normal distributions, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for groups with non-normal distributions. Pearson’s correlation assessed relationships between variables. Survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank test and presented using the KM method. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Expression of SGMS2 and its associations with clinicopathologic features in HCC

To explore the expression patterns of SGMS2, data from TCGA database were analyzed. SGMS2 expression was significantly lower in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues in HCC (Fig. 1a). KM analysis revealed that HCC patients with higher SGMS2 expression exhibited significantly longer 3-year and 5-year DFS (Fig. 1b). Similarly, analysis of the GSE76427 dataset, which included SGMS2 expression and recurrence times, revealed that patients with high SGMS2 expression had significantly prolonged early recurrence-free survival (RFS) (Fig. 1c).

To further elucidate the association between SGMS2 and HCC patient outcomes, immunofluorescence (IF) and image were performed to quantify SGMS2+ cell density in 188 HCC tumor tissues. The mean age was 55.47 (± 12.02) years, with 165 (87.8%) male patients, most of whom had a history of hepatitis. Over half (107 patients) had a history of liver cirrhosis, and 103 (54.8%) presented with elevated AFP (Table S1). The densities of SGMS2+ cells ranged from 0 to 4425.40/mm2 (median: 72.77; mean: 305.4). Based on the median density of SGMS2+ cells, patients were divided into high-density and low-density groups (Fig. 1d). KM analysis demonstrated that patients with high SGMS2+ cell density had significantly prolonged overall survival (OS) (P < 0.001), RFS (P < 0.001), and early RFS (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1e). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further indicated that SGMS2+ cell infiltration was a strong predictor of HCC prognosis (5-year OS: AUC = 0.804; 5-year RFS: AUC = 0.762; early RFS: AUC = 0.704) (Fig. 1f). When AUC > 0.7, it suggests that the marker has good predictive efficiency. These findings demonstrate SGMS2’s effective predictive capacity for 5-year mortality risk, 5-year relapse incidence, and 2-year early recurrence probability, establishing SGMS2+ macrophages as prognostic biomarkers strongly correlated with clinical outcomes. Comparative analysis of clinicopathological features using chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test revealed that patients in the high SGMS2+ cell density group were less likely to present with advanced BCLC stage B-C, vascular invasion, microvascular invasion, incomplete tumor capsule, or elevated AFP levels (Table 1). These findings suggest that SGMS2+ cell density is closely associated with favorable clinicopathological features and improved prognosis in HCC patients.

SGMS2 is primarily expressed in macrophages within the immune microenvironment and correlates with improved prognosis and favorable clinicopathological features in HCC

To determine the immune cell subsets expressing SGMS2 in the HCC immune microenvironment, we analyzed a public scRNA-seq database of HCC [16]. SGMS2 was predominantly expressed in macrophage subsets (Fig. 2a). This finding was validated through mIF staining of HCC tissue specimens, which confirmed that SGMS2+ cells were primarily macrophages, with minimal expression in other immune cell types such as T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, and NK cells (Fig. 2b). Further correlation analysis using the TCGA database demonstrated that SGMS2 expression was significantly positively correlated with the expression of CD68 and CD86, but not with CD206 (Fig. S1a). Consistently, CIBERSORT analysis of TCGA-LIHC data showed a significant positive correlation between SGMS2 expression and M1-like macrophages, but no significant correlation with M0 or M2-like macrophages (Fig. 2c). Additionally, mIF staining further confirmed that SGMS2+ macrophages exhibited high CD86 expression and low CD206 expression, suggesting an M1-like phenotype (Fig. 2d). Western blotting (WB) analysis showed that SGMS2 overexpression significantly increased the expression levels of iNOS and CD80 in macrophages (Fig. S1b). Detection of TNF-α and IL-6 secretion levels in cell supernatants also revealed that SGMS2 overexpression macrophages secreted higher levels of these two cytokines (Fig. S1c). And previous studies [16] suggest that SGMS2 is predominantly expressed in c1-THBS1-c5-VCAN lineage macrophages, rather than in the c2-C1QA lineage macrophages that showed significant association with poor patient prognosis (Fig. S1d). These results indicate that SGMS2 overexpression endows macrophages with a stronger pro-inflammatory phenotype, consistent with the M1 polarization of macrophages.

Next, we investigated the relationship between SGMS2+ macrophage infiltration and patient prognosis. Using QuPath for cell segmentation, SGMS2+ macrophages were identified based on co-expression of SGMS2 and CD68 (Fig. S1e). Patients were stratified into high and low infiltration groups based on the median density of SGMS2+ macrophages (Fig. 3a). KM analysis revealed that patients with high SGMS2+ macrophage infiltration had significantly prolonged OS (P < 0.001), RFS (P < 0.001), and early RFS (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3b). ROC analysis further confirmed that SGMS2+ macrophages infiltration was a strong predictor of HCC prognosis (OS: AUC = 0.781; RFS: AUC = 0.792; early RFS: AUC = 0.733) (Fig. S1f). In contrast, analysis of total macrophages and SGMS2- macrophages showed that high infiltration of SGMS2- macrophages was associated with significantly shorter OS (P < 0.001), while their infiltration had no significant impact on other prognostic outcomes (Fig. S2). To further refine the analysis, patients were classified into four groups based on the combined infiltration patterns of SGMS2+ and SGMS2- macrophages: SGMS2+ high/SGMS2- low, SGMS2+ high/SGMS2- high, SGMS2+ low/SGMS2- low, SGMS2+ low/SGMS2- high (Fig. 3a). Patients with SGMS2+ high/SGMS2- low infiltration exhibited the most favorable OS, RFS, and early RFS (Fig. 3c). Finally, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated that SGMS2+ macrophages were independent influencing factors for both OS and RFS of patients (Tables 2 and 3). These findings highlight the critical role of SGMS2+ macrophages in influencing HCC patient outcomes.

SGMS2+ macrophages promote tumor cell killing by recruiting CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK Cells

To explore how SGMS2+ macrophages influence HCC patient prognosis, we examined their role in the tumor immune microenvironment. While Macrophages could directly kill tumor cells through cytotoxic effects, their primary function is to modulate the immune microenvironment. To determine whether SGMS2+ macrophages primarily exert direct cytotoxic effects or immune regulation, we constructed an SGMS2-overexpressing THP-1 cell line and differentiated it into macrophages in vitro. WB analysis confirmed the high expression of SGMS2 compared to controls (Fig. 4a). Co-cultured experiments with tumor cells followed by flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis levels (Fig. 4b, S3a) showed that SGMS2 overexpression did not directly promote tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. 4c, S3b). These findings suggest that SGMS2+ macrophages primarily regulate the immune microenvironment rather than directly killing tumor cells.

Our previous studies identified SGMS2 as a key molecule involved in recruiting NK cells within the HCC immune microenvironment [27]. To further explore, we analyzed the spatial relationship between SGMS2 expression and NK cell subsets using four spatial transcriptomics datasets [26]. Spots expressing or lacking SGMS2 were selected (Fig. 4d), and ssGSEA was performed using signature gene sets of NK cell subsets based on a published single-cell RNA-seq study [23]. The analysis revealed that only the CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cell subset was significantly enriched in SGMS2+ spots across all four samples (Fig. 4e). To validate these findings, mIF staining for SGMS2, CD68, CD56, CD16, and NR4A3 was performed on HCC tissue specimens. The results confirmed CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells were enriched around SGMS2+ macrophages (Fig. 4f). Analysis of 188 TMA samples further showed that patients with high SGMS2+ macrophage infiltration had significantly higher levels of CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cell infiltration compared to those with low SGMS2+ macrophage infiltration (Fig. 4g). Correlation analysis also demonstrated a significant positive relationship between SGMS2+ macrophage density and CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cell density (Fig. 4h). The CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells are known to be a highly cytotoxic NK cell subset. Consistent with this, mIF staining results revealed increased tumor cell apoptosis in tissues with high infiltration of CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells subset (Fig. S3c). Patients with high infiltration of this NK cell subset exhibited significantly prolonged OS (P < 0.0001), RFS (P = 0.0007), and early RFS (P < 0.0001) (Fig. S3d).

Finally, we assessed the combined impact of SGMS2+ macrophages and CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells on patient prognosis. Patients with high infiltration of both cell types had the most favorable prognosis (Fig. 4i). These findings suggest that SGMS2+ macrophages promote tumor cell killing by recruiting highly cytotoxic CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells, thereby improving HCC patient outcomes.

SGMS2+ macrophages recruit CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells via CXCL2 secretion

Cell migration and recruitment are often mediated by chemokines. To investigate the mechanisms underlying the recruitment of CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells by SGMS2+ macrophages, we analyzed the differential expression of chemokines between SGMS2+ and SGMS2- spots in spatial transcriptomics datasets. Although no chemokine was consistently differentially expressed across all four samples, CXCL2, CCL3, and CCL20 showed significant differences in three of the samples (Fig. 5a). Next, we assessed the association between these chemokines and HCC patient prognosis using the TCGA database. Among the three, only CXCL2 was significantly correlated with improved patient outcomes (Fig. 5b). To validate this, we measured chemokines levels in the supernatants of control and SGMS2-overexpressing macrophages. The results showed that CXCL2 secretion was significantly increased in SGMS2-overexpressing macrophages, while no significant changes were observed for CCL3 or CCL20 (Fig. 5c). The mIF staining further confirmed that SGMS2+ macrophages exhibited high CXCL2 expression (Fig. 5d).

We performed double-labeling staining of NK cell markers CD56 and CXCR2 in tissue specimens, and it was found that there was indeed co-localization between the two markers (Fig. S3e). To investigate the impact of SGMS2+ macrophages and CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells on the response to anti-PD1 therapy in HCC patients, we analyzed a cohort of 48 HCC patients treated with anti-PD1 therapy. Based on MRI imaging for evaluation of treatment efficacy, 31 patients were classified as having progressive disease (PD), while 17 were classified as having a partial response (PR). The mIF staining for SGMS2, CD68, NR4A3, and CD16 was performed on HCC tissue sections to assess the densities of SGMS2+ macrophages and CD16highNR4A3high NK cells. Patients were stratified into high and low infiltration groups based on the median density of each cell type (Fig. 6a). Consistent with previous findings, patients with high SGMS2+ macrophage infiltration exhibited a higher proportion of CD16highNR4A3high NK cell infiltration (Fig. 6b). Analysis of treatment response revealed that the proportion of patients with high SGMS2+ macrophage infiltration (Fig. 6c) and high CD1highNR4A3high NK cell infiltration (Fig. 6d) was significantly higher in the PR group compared to the PD group.

Furthermore, we found that patients with high SGMS2+ macrophage infiltration (Fig. 6e) or high CD16highNR4A3high NK cell infiltration (Fig. 6f) had significantly prolonged OS compared to those with low infiltration. Discussion

HCC, as a malignant tumor with high incidence and mortality rates worldwide, urgently requires effective prognostic biomarkers and emerging therapeutic targets to alleviate its disease burden [28]. The immune microenvironment plays a pivotal role in the development of efficient diagnostic models and therapeutic strategies. Our study revealed that high expression of SGMS2 in HCC tissues was linked to better patient prognosis. SGMS2 was predominantly expressed in macrophages within the HCC immune microenvironment and could drive their polarization towards the M1-like phenotype. Importantly, patients with a high infiltration level of SGMS2-positive macrophages along with a low infiltration of other macrophage subtypes had the most favorable prognosis. Mechanistically, SGMS2-positive macrophages secreted CXCL2 to recruit cytotoxic CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells, which enhanced tumor cell apoptosis and thus improved prognosis. Moreover, among patients undergoing PD-1 therapy, those with high infiltration of both SGMS2-positive macrophages and CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells exhibited enhanced treatment responsiveness and longer survival.

SGMS2 has been shown to play an important role in regulating immune responses and chronic inflammatory diseases, such as atherosclerosis, obesity, and fatty liver [29, 30], and has also been found to be closely associated with tumor diseases. Although studies on SGMS2 in HCC are limited, existing research has demonstrated that its overexpression can promote apoptosis in HepG2 cells and enhance cisplatin sensitivity [31]. The expression of SGMS2 has been associated with poor prognosis in various tumors. However, the results of this study show that in HCC, SGMS2 expression is associated with improved patient prognosis. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that the same molecule can have different effects on diseases depending on the cell type in which it is expressed. Previous studies on SGMS2 in tumors have primarily focused on its expression in tumor cells and its direct impact on tumor cell behavior. However, through single-cell transcriptomic sequencing and mIF, this study revealed that SGMS2 is predominantly expressed in macrophages, where it promotes an M1-like pro-inflammatory phenotype. In previous study, SGMS2 was shown to directly promote macrophage activation and a pro-inflammatory state through the formation of very long-chain sphingomyelin [17]. Moreover, the unique infiltration pattern of high SGMS2+ macrophages and low SGMS2- macrophages was strongly associated with improved prognosis in HCC patients, providing a novel perspective on the role of SGMS2 in tumor immunity. Previous single-cell sequencing studies [16] demonstrated predominant SGMS2 expression in c1-THBS1-c5-VCAN lineage macrophages. Notably, c2-C1QA TAM-like macrophages showed significant association with poor patient prognosis, while c1-THBS1 macrophages exhibited no prognostic correlation in these studies. These findings indicate that SGMS2 is not primarily expressed in macrophages with detrimental prognostic effects, providing indirect evidence supporting our observation of improved prognosis in cases enriched with SGMS2+ macrophages.

Furthermore, NK cells, as key immune effector cells in the HCC immune microenvironment, represent a promising yet underdeveloped therapeutic direction [32]. In our study, we found that SGMS2-positive macrophages could recruit CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells, which has been identified as a key effector in tumor immunity [23]. Previous studies have suggested that there is a complex interaction network between macrophages and NK cells [33], with different macrophage subpopulations exerting distinct effects on NK cell [34]. TREM2 + macrophages were shown to inhibit the aggregation and cytolytic activity of NK cells in tumor tissues. Conversely, in colorectal cancer, the STING signaling pathway in macrophages can enhance the anti-tumor function of NK cells and inhibit liver metastasis of colorectal cancer [35]. Based on our team’s previous research on sequencing data from a large cohort, it has been found that SGMS2 is an important molecule for recruiting NK cells in the HCC immune microenvironment.

Our research further identified that the CXCL2-CXCR2 signaling axis may play a critical role in mediating the recruitment of CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells by SGMS2+ macrophages. Although studies directly exploring the impact of CXCL2-CXCR2 on NK cells are limited, existing evidence supports its relevance. For instance, in a study on polyethylene glycol-functionalized black phosphorus (BP-PEG) nanosheets in mouse breast cancer, macrophages engulfing BP-PEG were found to secrete CXCL2, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor activity of NK cells in tumors [36]. Additionally, CXCR2, the receptor for CXCL2, is predominantly expressed in CD56dimCD16high NK cells and is less expressed in CD56brightCD16- NK cells [37]. Genetically modified NK cells with high CXCR2 expression exhibit stronger tumor-specific migration and the cytotoxic activity [38]. Conversely, the deletion of CXCR2 reduces NK cell migration and infiltration, leading to extremely low NK cell density in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and resulting in tumor immune escape [39]. In this study, we found that SGMS2+ macrophages express CXCL2, while its major receptor CXCR2 is highly expressed in CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells, and patients with high expression of CXCL2 had better prognosis. This indicates that the CXCL2-CXCR2 signaling axis plays a critical bridging role in the interaction between SGMS2+ macrophages and NK cells.

The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors has revolutionized the treatment landscape of HCC. However, the occurrence and progression of tumors involve an extremely complex regulatory network within the immune microenvironment, and the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors alone remains limited, with an overall response rate of only about 20%. Therefore, it is of vital importance to explore new key molecules for immune regulation. Chimeric antigen receptor macrophages have been shown to remodel the tumor microenvironment and increase NK cells infiltration. When combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, CAR-M significantly enhances the sensitivity of HER2 + solid tumors to treatment [40]. In liver cancer, macrophages with different phenotypes exhibit varying effects on immunotherapy responsiveness. For instance, short-term fasting can remodel tumor-associated macrophages into an anti-tumor phenotype, thereby improving the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy [41]. Our study further analyzed the connection between SGMS2+ macrophages, CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells, and responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy. Despite the small cohort size of 48 patients, the results demonstrated a significant improvement in treatment response and survival outcomes in patients with high infiltration of these two cell types. These findings underscore the potential of SGMS2+ macrophages and CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells as promising therapeutic targets to optimize immune checkpoint inhibitor-based therapies.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, the patient cohort was derived from a single medical center and included a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should expand the sample cohort to validate the results and enhance their clinical applicability. Second, the exploration of underlying mechanisms was not sufficiently in-depth. Technical challenges, such as the nuclear localization of NR4A3, hindered the precise sorting and isolation of CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells for in vitro co-culture experiments. Additionally, the CXCL2-CXCR2 signaling pathway requires further investigation to clarify its core bridging role. Finally, the in vivo interaction mechanisms between SGMS2+ macrophages and CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells remain a critical direction for future research.

In conclusion, this study systematically explored the key role of SGMS2+ macrophages in shaping the immune microenvironment of HCC. By secreting CXCL2, SGMS2+ macrophages recruit highly cytotoxic CD56dimCD16highNR4A3high NK cells, promoting tumor cell apoptosis, improving OS, RFS, and early RFS, and enhancing the sensitivity of anti-PD-1 therapy. These findings highlight the importance of SGMS2+ macrophages in shaping a unique tumor immune microenvironment, offering a promising direction and theoretical basis for the development of effective therapeutic targets.
