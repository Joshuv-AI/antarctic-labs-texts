MedRule-KG: A Knowledge-Graph–Steered Scaffold for Mathematical Reasoning with a Lightweight Verifier
Crystal Su

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) often produce fluent reasoning steps while violating simple mathematical or logical constraints. We introduce MedRule-KG , a compact typed knowledge graph coupled with a symbolic verifier, designed to enforce mathematically interpretable rules in reasoning tasks. MedRule-KG encodes entities, relations, and three domain-inspired rules, while the verifier checks predictions and applies minimal corrections to guarantee consistency. On a 90-example FDA-derived benchmark, grounding in MedRule-KG improves exact match (EM) from 0.767 to 0.900, and adding the verifier yields 1.000 EM while eliminating rule violations entirely. We demonstrate how MedRule-KG provides a general scaffold for safe mathematical reasoning, discuss ablations, and release code and data to encourage reproducibility.

2
Method

2.1
MedRule-KG Schema

MedRule-KG is a deliberately compact knowledge graph designed to act as a mathematical scaffold rather than a large biomedical database.
It contains two entity types,
Drug
and
Enzyme
, which are sufficient to express the interaction rules we study.
Relations are limited to
inhibits
and
metabolized_by
, representing causal biochemical interactions.
Each drug also carries a boolean attribute
prolongs_qt
, which abstracts whether it increases the risk of QT prolongation.
An input instance therefore corresponds to a small induced subgraph with two drug nodes, one or more enzyme nodes, and the labeled relations between them.
This design choice makes the graph easy to inspect, minimizes irrelevant information, and ensures that rule checking can be done deterministically in constant time.

2.2
Constraints

We encode three interpretable rules (C1–C3) that simulate mathematically precise constraints.
Rule C1 states that if drug
A
inhibits enzyme
E
and drug
B
is metabolized by the same
E
, then co-administration is unsafe (
y=1
).
Rule C2 states that if both drugs prolong QT, then co-administration is unsafe.
Finally, Rule C3 enforces that if neither C1 nor C2 applies, then predicting
y=1
is a violation, since it would indicate a false positive.
These rules form a closed set of constraints where every valid label is either 0 or 1 and can be computed deterministically from the graph.
We treat these rules as the “ground truth” mathematics of the domain, against which model predictions can be evaluated.

2.3
Prompting Strategy

We evaluate three prompting conditions.
In the
CoT (no KG)
condition, the model only receives natural language descriptions of the drugs and is asked to reason in free-form text using chain-of-thought prompting.
In the
CoT + MedRule-KG
condition, we serialize the graph facts into the prompt as structured text.
For example, the model may see:

Drug A inhibits CYP3A4.
Drug B is metabolized by CYP3A4.
Drug A prolongs QT: No.
Drug B prolongs QT: Yes.
Rules: C1, C2, C3.
Question: Is the combination of Drug A and Drug B unsafe?

This grounding makes the rules explicit and reduces the need for the model to infer structure from unstructured prose.
Finally, in the
MedRule-KG + Verifier
condition, the same setup is used but model predictions are post-processed by a rule checker.

2.4
Verifier Algorithm

The verifier operates as a symbolic layer applied after the LLM’s prediction.
It first parses the predicted binary outcome
\hat{y}\in\{0,1\}
from the model output.
It then evaluates the three rules against the given MedRule-KG facts.
If
\hat{y}
already satisfies all rules, the prediction is accepted unchanged.
If
\hat{y}
violates any rule, the verifier applies a deterministic correction: it sets the prediction to 1 if either C1 or C2 holds, and 0 otherwise.
This guarantees that the final output is always rule-consistent.
Since rule evaluation involves only set membership checks and boolean logic, the verifier runs in
O(1)
time per example and is trivially interpretable.

2.5
Complexity and Interpretability

Both MedRule-KG and the verifier are small enough to be human-interpretable.
Inspecting a single input graph immediately reveals whether C1 or C2 applies, and the correction logic is fully transparent.
This stands in contrast to opaque neural verification methods, and allows users to audit each prediction.
The trade-off is that the coverage of MedRule-KG is intentionally narrow: it enforces only the constraints encoded in C1–C3.
Nevertheless, this narrowness allows us to test the hypothesis that even small, explicit structures can dramatically improve the reliability of LLM reasoning.

3
Experiments

3.1
Dataset Construction

To evaluate MedRule-KG, we built a dataset of 90 examples derived from FDA-published tables of substrates and inhibitors of cytochrome P450 enzymes, along with publicly available QT-prolongation annotations.

Each example consists of a drug pair and their associated enzyme interactions and QT attributes.
If one drug inhibits an enzyme that metabolizes the other, C1 applies. If both drugs prolong QT, C2 applies. The gold label is assigned by these rules.
This dataset replaces earlier synthetic generation and provides realistic, clinically motivated cases.

By design, the dataset balances positive and negative cases, with approximately half labeled unsafe (
y=1
).
This ensures that models cannot succeed by trivial baselines such as always predicting 0.

3.2
Experimental Setup

We evaluate three systems:
(1)
CoT (no KG)
— baseline chain-of-thought prompting with only natural language descriptions.
(2)
CoT + MedRule-KG
— the same prompting with serialized MedRule-KG facts and rules provided in the context.
(3)
MedRule-KG + Verifier (Ours)
— identical to (2), but predictions are passed through the symbolic verifier.

All models are prompted with a few-shot template including 3 illustrative examples followed by the test case.
The template explicitly encourages step-by-step reasoning before giving a binary prediction (“Answer: 0 or 1”).
Predicted outputs are parsed automatically by a regex-based script that extracts the final integer.

3.3
Evaluation Metrics

We report two metrics:

Exact Match (EM)
measures the proportion of predictions that exactly equal the gold label.

Average Rule Violations
counts, per example, how many of the constraints C1–C3 are broken by the predicted label.
This captures structural reliability beyond accuracy.

3.5
Analysis

Most errors in the CoT baseline stem from ignoring enzyme relations (C1), leading to false negatives when one drug inhibits an enzyme metabolizing the other.
Grounding with MedRule-KG reduces these errors substantially, but residual false positives remain when the model over-predicts risk (violating C3).
The verifier eliminates both classes of errors by enforcing the rules deterministically.
The confusion matrices in Appendix
confirm this progression: CoT leaves 21 false positives, MedRule-KG reduces this to 12, and the verifier removes all.

4
Conclusion

We introduced MedRule-KG, a knowledge-graph scaffold plus verifier for enforcing math-like reasoning in LLMs.
On a 90-example FDA-derived benchmark, our pipeline achieved 100% rule consistency, highlighting how small structured scaffolds can complement LLMs.
Future work should expand the schema and evaluate real-world clinical applications and broader scientific domains.
Additional notes on limitations, interpretability, and generalization are provided in Appendix A.

Appendix A
Additional Notes

Our experiments show that explicit structure and post-hoc verification are complementary: EM improves from 0.767 (CoT) to 0.900 with MedRule-KG, and to 1.000 with the verifier, indicating that most baseline errors stem from missing inductive bias rather than incapacity. Grounding surfaces key relations and rules, while the verifier guarantees compliance with C1–C3 at negligible cost.

The FDA-derived dataset anchors evaluation in real drug–enzyme cases but remains narrow, covering only selected enzymes and a binary QT attribute. This suffices for C1–C3 but does not span the full range of clinical or mathematical reasoning tasks. Similarly, our verifier enforces only the specified rules, leaving generalization to richer domains as future work.

The compact design is both strength and limitation: it allows transparent inspection of each correction, but sacrifices breadth. Extending MedRule-KG to larger graphs or multi-rule systems will require balancing interpretability with coverage. Beyond biomedicine, the scaffold could support domains such as algebraic reasoning or software verification, where explicit rules are critical.
