What Language Models Know But Don’t Say: Non-Generative Prior Extraction for Generalization
Sara Rezaeimanesh, Mohammad M. Ghassemi

Abstract
In domains like medicine and finance, large-scale labeled data is costly and often unavailable, leading to models trained on small datasets that struggle to generalize to real-world populations. Large language models contain extensive knowledge from years of research across these domains. We propose LoID (Logit-Informed Distributions), a deterministic method for extracting informative prior distributions for Bayesian logistic regression by directly accessing their token-level predictions. Rather than relying on generated text, we probe the model’s confidence in opposing semantic directions (positive vs. negative impact) through carefully constructed sentences. By measuring how consistently the LLM favors one direction across diverse phrasings, we extract the strength and reliability of the model’s belief about each feature’s influence. We evaluate LoID on ten real-world tabular datasets under synthetic out-of-distribution (OOD) settings characterized by covariate shift, where the training data represents only a subset of the population. We compare our approach against (1) standard uninformative priors, (2) AutoElicit, a recent method that prompts LLMs to generate expert-style priors via text completions, (3) LLMProcesses, a method that uses LLMs to generate numerical predictions through in-context learning by formatting tabular data as natural language prompts and (4) an oracle-style upper bound derived from fitting logistic regression on the full dataset. We assess performance using Area Under the Curve (AUC). Across datasets, LoID significantly improves performance over logistic regression trained on OOD data, recovering up to 59% of the performance gap relative to the oracle model. LoID outperforms AutoElicit and LLMProcessesc on 8 out of 10 datasets, while providing a reproducible and computationally efficient mechanism for integrating LLM knowledge into Bayesian inference.

Introduction

Machine learning models used in critical domains like medicine and finance often lack access to large, well-balanced, and comprehensively labeled datasets. Clinical datasets may cover only a single hospital or demographic, while credit-risk tables can be skewed toward historical lending patterns. For example,
25
benchmarked several deep learning models on clinical prediction tasks—including mortality and length of stay—using the MIMIC-III intensive care electronic health record dataset from a single hospital site. As a consequence, models trained on small, non-representative samples frequently fail to generalize when confronted with real-world populations that differ from their training distribution
5
;
20
;
2
. Bayesian methods offer a principled way to encode prior knowledge and mitigate such brittleness, but manually eliciting high-quality priors at scale remains a longstanding challenge
24
.

LLMs, trained on vast corpora of scientific literature and domain-specific data
6
, offer a promising new source of priors for downstream modeling. Recent efforts have explored LLMs both as zero-shot and few-shot predictors for tabular classification
11
;
28
, and as in-context learners capable of producing joint probabilistic predictions
30
. Other work has investigated the integration of LLMs with classical statistical methods: LLM-FE
1
uses LLMs to guide feature engineering, LLM-Lasso
34
for feature selection, and LLMCorr
35
for post-hoc model correction. AutoElicit
9
introduced a technique to query LLMs for Gaussian priors, but its reliance on generative sampling introduces run-to-run variability.

Parallel to this, out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization has gained increasing attention, as real-world deployment often involves data that differs from the development environment. Meta-learning approaches like TabPFN
13
and Drift-Resilient TabPFN
12
address OOD robustness through synthetic task generation and causal modeling. Others have proposed logit-based calibration strategies such as UE-NL
14
, or LLM-guided data augmentation methods like Summary Boosting
22
and LLMOverTab
16
to cover underrepresented regions of the feature space. However, despite this momentum, few works have systematically explored how to extract priors from the internal structure of LLMs (e.g. hidden states, logits, etc.) for tabular tasks under distribution shift.

We address this gap with
LoID (Logit-Informed Distributions)
, a deterministic framework that elicits informative priors for Bayesian logistic regression directly from an LLM’s token-level logits. Instead of relying on generated completions or examples, LoID probes the LLM with semantically paired sentences highlighting positive and negative feature-target relationships. The logit difference between the relational tokens quantifies directional belief, and the variance across paraphrased prompts yields an uncertainty estimate—together forming Normal priors over model coefficients. These priors integrate into Bayesian workflows, without any fine-tuning or in-context learning.

We evaluate LoID on ten publicly available tabular datasets that are widely used benchmarks and have appeared in numerous prior studies, ensuring comparability with existing methods. We compare LoID against uninformative Bayesian inference and LLMProcesses. To provide an upper bound, we additionally train and evaluate a logistic regression model on the
entire
dataset, offering insight into performance when full population data is available. Across extensive out-of-distribution splits, LoID outperforms existing approaches in predictive accuracy (AUC). By deterministically extracting domain knowledge encoded in large language models, LoID enables robust and reproducible inference.

In summary, our contributions include:

•

Logit-based deterministic prior elicitation.
LoID is the first method to extract Bayesian priors directly from the token-level logits of large language models, eliminating the variability associated with text generation and prompt sampling.

•

Baseline comparison across ten OOD datasets.
We evaluate LoID on diverse OOD tabular datasets from healthcare, finance, and social science. Our experiments demonstrate that LoID often outperforms existing approaches across most settings.

2
Related Work

2.1
LLMs for Tabular Data Modeling

The integration of LLMs into tabular data modeling has garnered attention, particularly in addressing challenges such as limited data availability, complex feature interactions, and the necessity for domain-specific feature engineering.

Several studies have explored the use of LLMs as standalone predictors for tabular data.
11
introduce TabLLM, a framework that applies large language models to zero-shot and few-shot classification of tabular data by converting rows into natural-language strings, enabling effective classification even with minimal labeled examples.
28
propose LLMProcesses, a framework that enables LLMs to produce probabilistic regression outputs by conditioning on both numerical data and natural language descriptions of prior knowledge.
30
present JOLT, a method that leverages the in-context learning capabilities of LLMs to define joint probabilistic predictions over tabular data.

Beyond standalone applications, recent research has focused on leveraging LLMs to enhance classical statistical models. For instance,
1
introduced LLM-FE, a framework that combines evolutionary search with the reasoning capabilities of LLMs to iteratively generate and refine feature transformations for tabular data. Similarly, LLM-Lasso
34
is a domain-informed approach to feature selection and regularization by leveraging large language models to guide the selection process.
9
proposed AutoElicit, a method that interacts with LLMs to extract priors for Gaussian-based probabilistic models; however, this approach primarily focuses on Gaussian priors and lacks flexibility in handling other priors that may be more suitable for different data characteristics. In a different vein,
35
presented LLMCorr, a training-free framework where LLMs act as post-hoc correctors to propose corrections for the predictions of arbitrary machine learning models.
21
investigated the reasoning capabilities of LLMs over tabular data and proposed combining symbolic and textual reasoning to improve performance on table-based question answering tasks.

2.2
Out-of-Distribution Generalization

OOD generalization is a fundamental challenge in real-world applications of tabular machine learning. Models trained on limited or biased datasets often fail to generalize when deployed on test data that differ from the training distribution. A variety of approaches have been proposed to address this issue through data augmentation, distributionally robust optimization, causal modeling, Bayesian inference, and most recently, the use of LLMs.

One line of work focuses on meta-learning for small-data generalization.
13
introduced TabPFN, a foundation model trained on millions of synthetic tabular tasks to approximate Bayesian posterior predictive distributions in one forward pass. The model shows strong generalization across novel tasks and distributions. Building on this,
12
proposed Drift-Resilient TabPFN, which introduces temporally shifting causal mechanisms during training to improve robustness under distribution shift.

Several recent studies have explored logit-based and uncertainty-aware methods for detecting or adapting to OOD inputs.
14
proposed Uncertainty Estimation with Normalized Logits (UE-NL), a calibration technique that regularizes softmax confidence and improves both in-distribution accuracy and OOD detection.

In the LLM context, OOD robustness has been approached in several creative ways. Summary Boosting
(
22
)
uses GPT-generated feature summaries as weak learners to improve robustness in small or under-specified datasets. LLMOverTab
(
16
)
performs data augmentation by prompting LLMs to generate realistic examples, improving classifier performance on underrepresented regions of the feature space. These strategies demonstrate that LLMs can inject external knowledge or expand the training distribution to mitigate generalization failure.

Our work, LoID, complements and extends this growing body of research by proposing the first deterministic logit-based method to elicit Bayesian priors from LLMs for structured tabular tasks. Our method consistently improves OOD generalization across ten tabular datasets, offering a lightweight and almost deterministic integration of LLM knowledge into classical statistical pipelines.

3
Method

We propose LoID (Logit-Informed Distribution), a deterministic framework for extracting priors for tabular dataset features from LLMs using token-level logits, rather than relying on generated samples. Our goal is to derive informative priors for Bayesian logistic regression that generalize robustly in low-data settings.

3.1
Problem Setting

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote a training dataset, where each
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector of
d
features and
y_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}
is the corresponding label. In Bayesian logistic regression, we model the likelihood as

p(y_{i}=t\mid x_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sigma(x_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}),

where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the sigmoid function and
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the regression coefficients. Bayesian inference is performed by specifying a prior distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and computing the posterior

p(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}).

In data-scarce regimes, informative priors can substantially improve generalization. LoID constructs such priors by leveraging domain knowledge encoded in large language models, without access to the training data.

3.2
Querying LLM Beliefs via Logit Differences

For each feature
x_{j}
, we construct paired prompts that express opposing semantic claims about its effect on the target variable. Each prompt is carefully designed such that all relevant context appears before a sentiment-bearing token (e.g., “positive” or “negative”), ensuring that the token’s probability considers the entire context for the task, including the name of the feature and the prediction task (see examples in
A.2.1
). For example, given a prompt prefix like:

‘‘The impact of
f_{j}
on
t
is ’’

we use the probabilities from the LLM’s softmax over its vocabulary to extract the probability of the next token being “positive” or “negative”. We define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=P(\texttt{``positive''}\mid\text{prefix})

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=P(\texttt{``negative''}\mid\text{prefix})

where
f_{j}
is a natural language description of feature
x_{j}
(e.g., “age of the patient”) and
t
describes the prediction task (e.g., “disease risk”).

We define the
logit preference score
for a single prompt
p_{j}
as:

\text{logit}(p_{j})=\log\left(\frac{p_{j}}{1-p_{j}}\right)

Where

p_{j}=\frac{P_{j}^{+}}{P_{j}^{+}+P_{j}^{-}}

The logit preference score quantifies the extent to which the model prefers the token “positive” over “negative” in a given context. While this difference may also reflect other patterns learned during training, our hypothesis is that by constructing sentences that do not grammatically or syntactically favor either token, we reduce the influence of such confounding factors. As a result, the observed preference is driven primarily by the semantic relationship between the feature and the target concept.
Finally, applying the logit transformation maps probabilities from the bounded interval (0, 1) to the unbounded real line, making the resulting scores directly appliable as coefficients in logistic regression models.

The
mean prefrence score

\mu_{j}
for feature
x_{j}
is given by averaging this score across a set of paraphrased prompts
{T}
:

\mu_{j}=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{t\in T}\text{logit}_{t}(p_{j})

This value reflects the LLM’s normalized preference for a positive versus negative association with the feature across diverse prompt formulations. Figure
1
demonstrates how LoID extracts informative priors from model logits.

Figure 1:
LoID Pipeline: To extract informative priors, LoID uses multiple sentence templates for each feature and computes the probabilities of the tokens "positive" and "negative" as the next token. From these probabilities, we calculate the logit preference score, which quantifies the model’s preference for one token over the other. Using the logit preference scores from all sentences, we compute their mean and standard deviation. We then apply a transformation to the standard deviation to reduce overconfidence, allowing the data to have more influence on the resulting distribution.

3.3
Constructing Priors from Logits

We model each coefficient
\beta_{j}
with a Gaussian prior:
\beta_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j},\sigma_{j}^{2})
.
The
mean

\mu_{j}
is the average logit preference score across paraphrases, which reflects the direction and strength of influence the LLM assigns to feature
x_{j}
. To capture uncertainty in the LLM’s belief, we define the standard deviation
\sigma_{j}
as a scaled function of variability in the individual logit preference scores:

\sigma_{j}^{2}=\alpha+\gamma\cdot\text{std}(logit_{1}(p_{j}),\ldots,logit_{|T|}(p_{j}))

Here,
logit_{t}(p_{j})
denotes the logit preference score for prompt
t
and feature
j
.
\alpha>0
and
\gamma>0
are fixed scaling hyperparameters that can be tuned to optimize LoID’s performance on a certain task. We perform a comprehensive hyperparameter tuning in
A.2.4
to find the hypermarameters that achieve the highest cumulative AUC. This design assigns higher variance to features for which the LLM expresses inconsistent preferences across paraphrases, thereby reflecting greater uncertainty about the feature’s influence in the model.

3.4
Bayesian Inference with LoID Priors

With the LoID priors in place, posterior inference is performed using the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), a variant of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC). HMC augments the parameter space with auxiliary momentum variables and explores the posterior by simulating Hamiltonian dynamics, enabling efficient, gradient-informed proposals that avoid the random-walk behavior of traditional MCMC methods.

Specifically, HMC introduces a momentum variable
\mathbf{r}
for the regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and defines the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{r})=-\log p(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{r}^{\top}\mathbf{r}.

Proposals are generated by numerically simulating the resulting Hamiltonian dynamics using a leapfrog integrator, which leverages gradients of the log-posterior to propose distant yet highly probable states.

NUTS eliminates the need to manually tune the trajectory length by adaptively extending the simulation until a U-turn condition is detected, ensuring efficient exploration while maintaining detailed balance. This results in faster mixing and improved effective sample sizes, particularly in high-dimensional parameter spaces.

We run multiple NUTS chains with warm-up steps for step-size and mass-matrix adaptation, and discard burn-in samples before collecting posterior draws. All reported results are computed using posterior expectations estimated from these samples.

3.5
Summary

LoID transforms the implicit domain knowledge encoded in large language models into explicit, feature-level priors suitable for probabilistic modeling. Unlike prior approaches that rely on generated samples, in-context learning, or fine-tuning, LoID achieves this deterministically by directly probing token-level logits. This design enables reproducibility and principled integration into Bayesian models.

3.6
Evaluation

To assess the quality of the resulting posteriors, we compute the AUC score on the
entire
dataset using models trained solely on the OOD training split. We then compare this AUC to that of a logistic regression trained on the
entire
dataset. This comparison quantifies how much of the predictive performance gap, caused by training on an OOD distribution, is recovered by our method.

4.2
LLM-Based Prior Extraction

We use the
Gemma-2-27B
,
Gemma-3-27B
,
llama-3-8B
, and
GPT-2
large language models to extract logit-based priors as described in Section
3.2
. We use these models in
zero-shot
mode without any fine-tuning or in-context examples. In our experiments, the queried lexical items are represented as single tokens across models, enabling direct logit-based comparisons.

Each dataset is processed independently. For each feature, we issue 10 prompts to extract token-level log-probabilities for the
positive
and
negative
completions. The number of prompts was selected via hyperparameter tuning (Section
A.2.4
). The resulting feature-wise prior means and variances are then used to define informative Gaussian priors in a Bayesian logistic regression model. Posterior inference is performed using PyMC with a Laplace approximation.

We compare LoID to the following baselines:

•

OOD-LR
: Logistic regression model trained on OOD train set.

•

AutoElicit
: LLM-generated expert-style priors via sampled completions.

•

LLMProc (LLMProcesses)
: In-context learning approach that generates numerical predictions by formatting tabular data as natural language prompts. For fair comparison, we use the OOD training examples as few shot examples for the LLM.

•

Cap
: An oracle-style upper bound achieved by training a logistic regression model on the entire dataset, simulating access to the full population.

We evaluate each method using AUC score obtained from training on the ood train set and testing on the entire dataset.

5.1
AUC Comparison

LoID outperforms logistic regression trained on the OOD training set for all datasets except Liver Disease. Notably, on the Diabetes and Pima Diabetes datasets, LoID almost completely closes the performance gap induced by OOD training, indicating that it can successfully infer the underlying data distribution. On average, LoID closes 59% of the performance gap introduced by the OOD training setting.

LLMProcesses often underperforms even compared to uninformative priors. Since this approach does not explicitly produce priors, it is difficult to determine the exact cause of its poor performance. One possible explanation is its complete reliance on LLMs without leveraging traditional machine learning models, which are often more robust at pattern extraction in structured data.

LoID also achieves superior performance on 8 out of 10 datasets, achieving an average improvement of 99.7% in gap closed when it outperforms AutoElicit. To further understand these performance differences, we analyze the elicited prior distributions produced by both methods across selected winning and losing datasets. Section
A.5.1
presents this comparison.

Finding 1: Non-Monotonic Relationship Between Prior Uncertainty and Performance

•

Optimal range (
\sigma\approx 0.4
–
0.5
):
Achieves best performance on 7 datasets: Liver, Income, Bank, Creditg, Diabetes, Pima, Occupancy

•

Too weak (

Underperforms AutoElicit: Blood, Heart

This shows that moderately weak priors (
\sigma\approx 0.4
–

) provide sufficient regularization while allowing the Bayesian posterior to correct systematic biases in the elicited means through the data likelihood. Excessively weak priors (
\sigma\gtrsim 0.8
) fail to leverage LLM knowledge effectively, approaching uninformed priors.

LoID excels when Prior uncertainty is moderate, datasets have sufficient samples to correct sign biases, and AutoElicit’s tight priors impose incorrect strong beliefs.

Finding 3: Superior Mean Estimation Despite Directional Bias

While LoID exhibits systematic positive bias in sign coverage (0–6% negative priors vs. AutoElicit’s 20–50%), the absolute quality of the mean estimates appears superior. Evidence from performance gaps suggests:

1.

Magnitude accuracy over sign accuracy:
On datasets where LoID wins, the improvement over AutoElicit indicates that the LLM-elicited means—despite sign bias—capture feature importance better than AutoElicit’s conversational approach.

2.

Comparison to uninformed priors:
LoID achieves 145% better gap closed vs. uninformed priors (see full comparison in
A.6.1
), demonstrating that the mean estimates contain genuine signal beyond just the regularization effect of uncertainty.

3.

Interaction with uncertainty:
The combination of (a) approximately correct magnitudes in means and (b) moderate uncertainty allows the Bayesian posterior to correct sign errors while preserving magnitude information. In contrast, AutoElicit’s tight uncertainties amplify errors in both magnitude and direction.

5.2
Computational Efficiency.

LoID requires a fixed number of LLM queries per feature and performs prior elicitation once per dataset, resulting in a lower computational cost than AutoElicit and LLMProcesses.
Across all datasets, LoID achieves a consistent
1.4–1.5
\times
speedup
over AutoElicit during prior elicitation. LLMProcesses on the other hand, scales linearly with test set size and incurs significantly longer runtimes, requiring
15–20 hours
for datasets with 100–400 test instances, compared to

for prior-based methods.

6
Discussion

The success of our LoID method challenges the conventional assumption that more accurate prior means necessarily lead to better performance. AutoElicit provides strong, confident priors (
\sigma\approx 0.05
–
0.12
) with
\sim
40–50% correct negative signs. However, the incorrect confident priors harm inference by overpowering the data likelihood.

On the other hand, LoID provides weaker and uncertain priors (
\sigma\approx 0.4
–
0.5
). This allows the weaker means to be overridden by data, providing soft regularization: the priors gently guide inference toward reasonable parameter spaces without rigidly enforcing incorrect beliefs. The result is that even when LoID’s directional beliefs are wrong, the posterior can recover because the prior is not insisting on its incorrectness. However, the benefits of this approach diminish when priors become too weak (
\sigma>0.8
).

7
Limitations and Future Work

While our results demonstrate the effectiveness of LoID, several limitations should be noted, which also point toward directions for future work.

Semantic granularity.

Our current approach probes binary directional relationships (positive vs. negative). Extending this framework to multi-token or scalar outputs could capture more nuanced associations, such as non-monotonic effects.

Hyperparameter selection:

Although we use fixed hyperparameters (
\alpha=0.2
,
\gamma=2.0
) across all test datasets, optimal values vary by task. Developing automated calibration methods or meta-learning approaches for selecting these parameters would improve out-of-the-box applicability.

LLM access requirements:

LoID requires access to token-level logits, limiting compatibility with closed-source APIs.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce LoID, a deterministic method for extracting priors from LLMs. We show that priors elicited with LoID substantially mitigate the performance degradation caused by training on small, out-of-distribution datasets, recovering over 59% of the lost performance.

Our comparative analysis with AutoElicit demonstrates that the effectiveness of the LoID approach stems from honest uncertainty quantification, and correct mean magnitude estimation for logistic regression coefficients. By maintaining moderate prior uncertainty (
\sigma\approx 0.4
–
0.5
), LoID provides soft regularization while allowing the Bayesian posterior to correct systematic biases through the data likelihood.

As a result, LoID outperforms AutoElicit on 80% of benchmark datasets, achieving an average improvement of 99.7% in gap closed when it wins. Against LLMProcesses, LoID achieves superior performance on all 10 datasets, with particularly dramatic gains on datasets where LLMProcesses’s end-to-end LLM approach fails to leverage structured data patterns.

A.2.3
Variance Estimation Formula

The prior standard deviation
\sigma_{j}
for feature
j
quantifies the uncertainty in the elicited prior mean. We explored two methods for computing
\sigma_{j}
from LLM token probabilities, each capturing different aspects of elicitation uncertainty.

Method 1: Variance of Logit Differences

This method uses the variance across multiple sentence formulations as a measure of prior uncertainty:

\sigma_{j}^{2}=\alpha+\gamma\cdot\text{std}(logit_{1}(p_{j}),\ldots,logit_{|T|}(p_{j}))

where

\text{logit}(p_{j})=\log\left(\frac{p_{j}}{1-p_{j}}\right)

and

p_{j}=\frac{P_{j}^{+}}{P_{j}^{+}+P_{j}^{-}}

Rationale:
Higher variance across sentence formulations indicates inconsistent LLM responses, suggesting greater epistemic uncertainty. The hyperparameters
\alpha
and
\gamma
control the base uncertainty level and sensitivity to variance, respectively.

Method 2: Entropy-Based Uncertainty

This method uses Shannon entropy to measure the uncertainty in the LLM’s token probability distribution:

\sigma_{j}=\beta\cdot\frac{1}{N_{\text{sent}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{sent}}}H(p_{\text{pos}}^{(i)},p_{\text{neg}}^{(i)})

Rationale:
Higher entropy (closer to
\log 2\approx 0.693
) indicates the LLM is uncertain between “positive” and “negative” tokens, reflecting ambiguity about the feature’s effect direction. Lower entropy indicates confident predictions. The scaling factor
\beta
maps entropy to prior standard deviation.

A.4.2
OOD Splits

We construct out-of-distribution (OOD) train splits to simulate a training set that does not represent the entire population. For each dataset, we consider numerical features with at least two unique values and apply five shift strategies to induce distributional gaps between training and test data:

1.

extreme_10
, training on the bottom 10%

2.

extreme_5_95
, training on the bottom 5%

3.

moderate_20_80
, training on the bottom 20%;

4.

tail_0_50
, training on the 0–25% range;

5.

tail_50_100
, training on the 50–75% range.

For each split, we designate a different feature as the shift variable (cycling through available features), compute the corresponding quantile thresholds, and construct train–test masks while enforcing a minimum of 50 samples per split. Models are trained on the OOD training set and evaluated on the
entire
dataset to quantify performance degradation under distribution shift. Splits with insufficient class diversity (fewer than two classes in either the training or test set) are discarded. This procedure simulates realistic deployment settings in which models must generalize to regions of the feature space not observed during training.

A.5
LoID vs Existing Baselines

A.5.1
Prior Performance

Since LoID and AutoElicit employ similar prior elicitation strategies and exhibit more comparable performance than other baselines, Table

focuses on analyzing where and why each method performs better. The underlying intuition behind these differences is discussed in detail in Section
5.1
.

A.5.2
Computational Efficiency

We compare the computational complexity and runtime of LoID with AutoElicit and LLMProcesses.
For a dataset with
d
features, the LoID method queries the LLM using

N_{\text{sent}}
sentence formulations per feature and extracts the probabilities
of two tokens (
positive
and
negative
). Its time complexity is:

T_{\text{logit}}=\mathcal{O}(d\times N_{\text{sent}}\times T_{\text{LLM}}),

where
T_{\text{LLM}}
denotes the inference time of a single LLM query.
In our experiments,
N_{\text{sent}}=10
, resulting in approximately 20 queries per feature.

AutoElicit employs an interactive multi-round elicitation process with iterative refinement,
leading to a higher complexity:

T_{\text{AE}}=\mathcal{O}(d\times N_{\text{rounds}}\times N_{\text{queries}}\times T_{\text{LLM}}),

where
N_{\text{rounds}}\in[3,5]
and
N_{\text{queries}}\in[5,10]
.
This results in more than 28 LLM queries per feature on average.

Empirically, this yields a consistent
1.4–1.5
\times
speedup
in prior elicitation
for LoID across all evaluated datasets.

LLMProcesses follows a fundamentally different paradigm: it generates predictions for each test
instance by sampling from the LLM
N_{\text{samples}}
times using in-context examples.
For a test set of size
n_{\text{test}}
with
n_{\text{train}}
training examples in context,
its complexity is:

T_{\text{LLMP}}=\mathcal{O}(n_{\text{test}}\times N_{\text{samples}}\times T_{\text{LLM}}(n_{\text{train}}\cdot d)),

where
T_{\text{LLM}}(n_{\text{train}}\cdot d)
reflects the increased inference time
caused by long prompts containing
n_{\text{train}}
in-context examples.
Unlike LoID and AutoElicit, which perform prior elicitation once per dataset,
LLMProcesses scales linearly with the test set size and incurs substantially longer runtimes
(
15–20 hours
for datasets with 100–400 test instances, compared to

minutes
for prior elicitation methods).

A.6
Prior Impact on Model Performance

A.6.1
LoID Priors vs.
\mathcal{N}
(0, 0.45) vs.
\mathcal{N}
(0, 1)

To isolate the contribution of the prior means inferred by LoID, we evaluate a baseline using normal priors with zero mean and standard deviation
0.45
(the Goldilock setting).
This comparison quantifies how much LoID’s learned prior means contribute to downstream AUC.
As shown in Table
11
, incorporating LoID-derived means yields a substantial performance gain, increasing the gap closed by approximately 154% on average. This difference is most prominent in datasets where the uninformed priors catastrophically fail: Occupancy (+1156%), Diabetes (+105%), and Pima (+98%). In these cases, the
\mathcal{N}(0,0.45)
prior provides insufficient directional guidance, causing the Bayesian posterior to converge toward coefficients near zero and perform worse than the OOD baseline. Conversely, LoID’s informative means—despite systematic positive bias—capture feature importance magnitudes that guide the model toward the correct solution space. Even on datasets where LoID underperforms (Heart: -32%, Liver: -10%), the performance gap remains small compared to the massive gains elsewhere. This demonstrates that
prior mean quality matters substantially more than perfect directional correctness
when paired with appropriately calibrated uncertainty (
\sigma\approx 0.4
–
0.5
).

Finally, we include an additional
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
baseline to illustrate the improvement achieved over a more weakly informative prior that is commonly used with Bayesian inference.

A.6.2
\mathcal{N}
(0, 1) vs.
\mathcal{U}
(-1, 1)

To understand the impact of prior distribution choice, we compare two configurations: (1) uninformative Normal priors
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and (2) uninformative Uniform priors
\mathcal{U}(-1,1)
.

Table
12
shows the catastrophic failure of Uniform priors (average AUC 0.42, with Occupancy at 0.02) and reveals that while Uniform priors ostensibly provide “maximum uninformativeness” by assigning equal probability across a range, they fundamentally fail as regularizers for regression coefficients because:

1.

Lack of soft regularization:
Uniform distributions do not encode the reasonable prior belief that smaller coefficient magnitudes are more plausible than larger ones. This absence of gradient-based regularization toward zero can lead to poor MCMC sampling behavior and overfitting.

2.

Incompatibility with MCMC:
The flat density of Uniform priors provides no gradient information to guide sampling algorithms, resulting in inefficient exploration and convergence issues, particularly visible in the Occupancy dataset collapse.

3.

Prior-likelihood mismatch:
For logistic regression, coefficients naturally follow a scale where values near zero are common and extreme values are rare. Uniform priors violate this structure, treating
\beta=0.1
and
\beta=4.9
as equally plausible a prior.
