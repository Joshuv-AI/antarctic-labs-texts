From surveillance to intelligence: a scoping review of machine learning for antimicrobial resistance surveillance intelligence across One Health
Authors: Syed Arman Rabbani, Mohamed El-Tanani, Ismail I. Matalka, Shrestha Sharma, Manita Saini, Rakesh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a leading global health threat requiring coordinated surveillance across human, animal, environmental, and genomic systems. Machine learning is increasingly applied to AMR data, yet its contribution to actionable surveillance intelligence, rather than prediction alone, remains poorly defined.ObjectiveTo map how machine-learning approaches generate AMR surveillance intelligence, to characterise their validation and implementation maturity, and to propose a framework distinguishing technical prediction from actionable surveillance intelligence.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review following JBI methodology and PRISMA-ScR reporting. PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched from January 2015 to May 2026 for studies applying machine learning or related methods to AMR surveillance intelligence. Two reviewers independently screened and charted records. Of 1,985 records, 66 met eligibility and formed the working evidence base; 41 studies (40 core empirical and one supporting preprint) were appraised against TRIPOD+AI- and PROBAST-aligned reporting, validation, and implementation-readiness domains.ResultsMachine learning was applied across five clusters: clinical and electronic-health-record risk prediction and decision support; genomic and whole-genome-sequencing prediction; MALDI-TOF-based rapid resistance prediction; wastewater and metagenomic surveillance; and environmental, animal, food-chain, and One Health early warning. Prediction and risk stratification predominated, but validation maturity was limited: most studies were retrospective or internally validated, with few using external, cross-country, temporal, prospective, or drift-focused evaluation. On appraisal, discrimination was reported in 31 of 41 studies (76%) and explainability in 26 (63%); by contrast, external or temporal validation was present in only 15 (37%), calibration in 5 (12%), prospective evaluation in 1 (2%), and operational deployment with measured clinical or public-health impact in a single study (2%).ConclusionMachine learning can support AMR surveillance intelligence across clinical, genomic, diagnostic, environmental, and One Health settings, but the evidence demonstrates technical feasibility far more convincingly than operational readiness. Realising this transition will require external and prospective validation, calibration and drift monitoring, transparent and equitable reporting, workflow integration, and explicit linkage of model outputs to clinical and public-health action. We propose a One Health AMR Surveillance Intelligence Framework to organise this shift from data generation toward actionable, adaptive surveillance intelligence.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is among the most pressing global public-health threats. Bacterial AMR was directly responsible for an estimated 1.27 million deaths globally in 2019 and contributed to 4.95 million deaths in the same year (1, 2). Global Burden of Disease modelling attributes 1.14 million deaths to bacterial AMR in 2021, with 4.71 million associated deaths (3). Without effective intervention, AMR is projected to be directly responsible for 1.91 million deaths and associated with 8.22 million each year by 2050 (3).

AMR is not confined to hospitals or human medicine. It is a One Health problem (Figure 1) because resistant organisms, resistance genes, antimicrobial use, and environmental contamination can involve human, animal, food-production, plant, and ecosystem interfaces (1, 4). The Quadripartite One Health Joint Plan of Action frames health risks at the human-animal–plant-environment interface as requiring coordinated One Health action (4). For AMR, this means that surveillance systems must increasingly account for clinical infections, antimicrobial use, laboratory susceptibility data, animal and food-chain reservoirs, wastewater, environmental signals, genomic data, and population-level risk patterns, rather than relying on isolated laboratory reporting (1, 4, 5).

Antimicrobial resistance as a One Health problem: Transmission across sectors and drivers of spread. Resistant bacteria and resistance genes circulate among human health (clinical antibiotic use), animal health (livestock antibiotic use), the food chain (production, processing, distribution and consumption), and the environment (wastewater, soil and water) through direct contact, foodborne transmission, environmental contamination, and resistance-gene transfer. Major drivers of AMR spread include antibiotic use in humans and animals, horizontal gene transfer between bacteria, wastewater release and environmental contamination, and global connectivity (travel, trade, and movement of people and goods).

The global policy architecture has continued to move toward this integrated view. The 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List reprioritised 24 resistant pathogens across 15 families to direct research, development, and surveillance (6). In 2026, the World Health Assembly adopted a Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance for 2026 to 2036 that reaffirms coordinated One Health surveillance and response (7). At its 2024 high-level meeting, the United Nations General Assembly adopted a political declaration on antimicrobial resistance that set a target of reducing AMR-associated human deaths by 10% by 2030 (8).

Surveillance data alone do not automatically become actionable intelligence. Descriptive AMR surveillance can identify resistance patterns and monitor trends, but clinical and public-health decision-making increasingly requires earlier detection, prediction of future risk, recognition of high-risk patients or settings, identification of emerging resistance signals, and translation of those signals into timely interventions. The methodological question is therefore not only whether AMR can be predicted, but whether heterogeneous AMR-related data can be transformed into interpretable, validated, and decision-relevant intelligence for stewardship, infection prevention, laboratory diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and One Health response.

Machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) have emerged as tools for this task, learning from high-dimensional clinical, microbiological, genomic, metagenomic, laboratory, and environmental data to support prediction, classification, forecasting, anomaly detection, and decision support (9–11). Prior reviews, however, have largely framed the field as a prediction problem. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis found that machine-learning approaches showed promise for AMR prediction but were constrained by retrospective development, non-standard data processing, and scarce real-world validation (9). A 2025 review of critical and high-priority pathogens reached similar conclusions, noting that gradient boosting, random forest, and XGBoost were among the better-performing model families (10). A translational review identified genomic prediction, mechanistic or drug-response discovery, and electronic-health-record-based stewardship as the principal application areas (11).

These reviews establish that machine learning can predict resistance, but prediction is only one component of surveillance intelligence. A model becomes useful for surveillance only when its outputs are timely, interpretable, validated in the target setting, and connected to a clinical, laboratory, public-health, environmental, or One Health decision pathway. Such a system can indicate where resistance is emerging, who or what is at risk, which sectors are involved, and whether interventions modify future risk.

A rapidly expanding literature now applies these methods across the full breadth of AMR-relevant data. Clinical, electronic-health-record, and laboratory information systems support patient-level prediction, risk stratification, and early susceptibility forecasting (12–24). Whole-genome and metagenomic sequencing support genotype-to-phenotype prediction and resistance-gene mapping (25–30). MALDI-TOF (matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight) mass spectrometry enables rapid resistance inference (31–36). Wastewater, environmental, animal, and food-chain monitoring support population-level tracking, forecasting, and early warning (37–45).

Despite this breadth, the literature remains uneven in the dimensions that determine real-world usefulness, including external validation, temporal stability, prospective evaluation, calibration, and explicit linkage of model outputs to decisions. It has rarely been synthesised through a surveillance-intelligence lens that separates technical prediction from actionable, deployable intelligence.

This scoping review addresses that gap by mapping how machine learning and data-analytic approaches are being used to generate AMR surveillance intelligence across human, animal, environmental, food-chain, wastewater, genomic, diagnostic, and One Health contexts. Specifically, the review examines the data sources used, machine-learning methods applied, surveillance-intelligence functions supported, validation and implementation maturity, explainability, One Health integration, and remaining evidence gaps. The review also proposes a One Health AMR Surveillance Intelligence Framework to distinguish technical AMR prediction from actionable surveillance intelligence and to organise the transition from data generation toward risk stratification, forecasting, early warning, decision support, and learning feedback.

Research gap and novel contribution

Prior reviews have advanced the field but have largely treated machine learning for AMR as a prediction task. Existing syntheses have summarised model families and reported performance for resistance prediction, yet they have given limited attention to whether model outputs are validated in the target setting, calibrated and monitored over time, interpretable, and connected to a defined clinical, laboratory, environmental, or One Health action (9–11). Three gaps follow from this. First, the literature has rarely been read through a surveillance-intelligence lens that separates technical prediction from validated, deployable intelligence. Second, evidence from clinical, genomic, diagnostic, environmental, wastewater, animal, and food-chain streams has usually been reviewed in isolation rather than mapped together across the One Health system. Third, the dimensions that determine operational usefulness, namely external and temporal validation, calibration, prospective evaluation, and explicit linkage to decisions, have not been charted systematically across these streams.

This scoping review addresses these gaps in three ways. It maps machine-learning evidence across the full One Health AMR data ecosystem within a single synthesis. It appraises each study against validation and implementation-readiness domains aligned with TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST rather than by performance alone. It then organises the findings into a One Health AMR Surveillance Intelligence Framework that distinguishes AMR data generation from actionable surveillance intelligence and identifies what is required to move from retrospective prediction toward validated and deployable use. The contribution is integrative and evaluative rather than a claim of primacy, and it is intended to reframe how the evidence is read and to guide the next stage of development.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a scoping review of machine learning-enabled AMR surveillance intelligence. A scoping-review design was selected because the evidence spans heterogeneous data streams, AMR outcomes, host sectors, modelling tasks, validation designs, and implementation stages. The review was informed by JBI scoping-review methodology and PRISMA-ScR reporting guidance. It was designed to map the evidence, identify conceptual and implementation gaps, and develop a synthesis framework rather than to estimate pooled model performance. The review protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; registration https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF. IO/7WEM3). This article is a scoping review, and the conceptual framework presented in this work is a synthesis product of that review.

Review question

The primary review question was: How are machine learning and data-analytic approaches being used to generate surveillance intelligence for AMR tracking, risk stratification, forecasting, early warning, and decision support across human, animal, environmental, and One Health systems? For this review, AMR surveillance intelligence was defined as the transformation of AMR-related data into timely, interpretable, and actionable information that can support clinical, laboratory, public-health, environmental, or One Health decision-making.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were structured using a population-concept-context framework.

The population or problem of interest was antimicrobial resistance in human, animal, environmental, food-chain, wastewater, genomic, metagenomic, laboratory, or One Health contexts. Studies were eligible if they addressed AMR, antimicrobial susceptibility or resistance prediction, multidrug-resistant organisms, resistance genes, resistome profiles, or pathogen-specific resistance outcomes.

The concept of interest was the use of machine learning, artificial intelligence, statistical learning, predictive modelling, forecasting, risk stratification, anomaly detection, genomic prediction, metagenomic analytics, MALDI-TOF spectral modelling, or related data-analytic approaches. Eligible studies had to include an analytic method that went beyond descriptive prevalence reporting.

The context of interest was surveillance intelligence. Eligible studies had to contribute to at least one surveillance-intelligence function. These functions were AMR tracking, patient-level risk prediction, antimicrobial susceptibility or resistance prediction, resistance prevalence forecasting, early warning, and hotspot mapping. They also included genomic, environmental or wastewater, and One Health surveillance, as well as laboratory decision support, antimicrobial stewardship decision support, and public-health response planning.

Studies were eligible if they were peer-reviewed original research articles, systematic reviews, scoping reviews, methodological papers, or framework papers directly relevant to AMR surveillance intelligence. The main empirical synthesis prioritised original modelling, surveillance, genomic, environmental, diagnostic, or One Health studies. Reviews and framework papers were retained as contextual evidence but were not counted as primary empirical evidence.

Studies were excluded if they focused only on antimicrobial drug discovery, molecular mechanisms of resistance without a surveillance or prediction component, general infectious-disease artificial intelligence without AMR relevance, descriptive AMR prevalence without analytic modelling, or non-AMR stewardship interventions. They were also excluded if they were opinion pieces without a methods or framework contribution, case reports, or conference abstracts without sufficient methodological detail, or if they could not be verified as eligible after full-text and source checking. The date range was January 2015 to May 2026. The language restriction was English-language publications.

Information sources and search strategy

Three electronic bibliographic databases were searched: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search combined controlled-vocabulary terms, where available, with free-text keywords across three concept blocks. The first block covered antimicrobial resistance, for example antimicrobial or antibiotic resistance, multidrug-resistant organisms, resistance genes, resistome, and antimicrobial susceptibility. The second covered machine-learning and data-analytic methods, for example machine learning, artificial intelligence, deep learning, predictive modelling, forecasting, anomaly detection, genomic or metagenomic prediction, and MALDI-TOF spectral modelling. The third covered surveillance-intelligence functions, for example surveillance, tracking, risk stratification, forecasting, early warning, decision support, and One Health. The three concept blocks were combined with Boolean operators. Within each block, controlled-vocabulary terms and free-text keywords were combined with OR, and the three concept blocks were combined with AND. Controlled vocabulary comprised MeSH headings in PubMed/MEDLINE and the equivalent index or topic fields in Scopus and Web of Science, with free-text terms searched in title, abstract, and keyword fields. Records from all databases were exported to reference-management software, where duplicates were removed automatically and then checked manually before screening. Searches were restricted to English-language records published from January 2015 onward, and all databases were last searched on 1 May 2026. Database searching was supplemented by backward and forward citation chasing. The full database-specific search strategies, including all search terms, field tags, and limits, are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Record management and study selection

Search results from each database were imported into reference-management software and de-duplicated, with manual checking of uncertain matches. Screening was conducted in two stages by two reviewers working independently, with disagreements resolved by discussion and consensus. First, titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria. Records were classified as include, exclude, maybe or uncertain, or background/contextual. Records were advanced to full-text screening if they clearly met the eligibility criteria or if relevance could not be confidently resolved from the title and abstract. At full-text screening, reports were excluded for mutually exclusive primary reasons, which are summarised with their counts in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Figure 2). The number of records identified, screened, assessed at full text, and excluded with reasons is reported in the Results and in Figure 2.

PRISMA-ScR flow diagram for review.

Data extraction

Data were charted independently by two reviewers into a structured review-specific data collection form. The extraction framework included bibliographic information, study design, country or region, AMR domain, pathogen or organism, data source, sample size or dataset size where reported, AMR outcome, machine-learning or analytic method, surveillance-intelligence function, validation design, performance metrics, explainability approach, implementation readiness, One Health relevance, limitations, evidence role, and source-verification status. The main surveillance-intelligence functions coded were AMR tracking, AMR or antimicrobial susceptibility prediction, patient-level risk stratification, resistance prevalence forecasting, early warning, hotspot mapping, laboratory or clinical decision support, One Health integration, and framework or surveillance-system development.

Quality, validation, and implementation appraisal

Because this was a scoping review across heterogeneous study types, studies were not excluded solely on the basis of formal risk-of-bias appraisal. Instead, each study was assessed using structured quality, validation, and implementation-readiness domains relevant to AMR surveillance intelligence. Domains included data source and data quality, AMR outcome definition, model development approach, validation design, performance reporting, calibration or threshold reporting where available, explainability, external validity and transportability, implementation readiness, One Health integration, and source-verification caveats.

Each study was additionally appraised against the reporting items of TRIPOD+AI (46) and the risk-of-bias and applicability concepts of PROBAST and its artificial-intelligence update PROBAST+AI (47, 48). Scoring used only reported information across validation design, external or temporal validation, calibration, decision-analytic utility, prospective evaluation, explainability, and operational deployment.

Evidence synthesis

A structured narrative synthesis was conducted. Studies were grouped by data source, AMR domain, machine-learning method, surveillance-intelligence function, validation maturity, implementation readiness, and One Health relevance. Quantitative pooling was not performed because the studies differed substantially by pathogen, AMR endpoint, data source, model architecture, validation design, and performance metric. Counts are therefore reported as synthesis-set counts and should not be interpreted as prevalence estimates for the entire AMR machine-learning literature. The review was reported in line with the PRISMA-ScR checklist (49). The completed PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided as Supplementary File S2.

Development of the conceptual framework

A conceptual framework was developed from the structured synthesis. The framework was designed to distinguish AMR data generation from actionable surveillance intelligence. Evidence from clinical, laboratory, genomic, environmental, wastewater, food-chain, animal-sector, and One Health studies was mapped onto six functional layers: One Health data streams, data harmonization and governance, machine-learning and analytics engine, surveillance-intelligence outputs, decision support and public-health action, and a learning feedback loop. The framework was not intended to imply that all included studies had operational deployment or prospective evaluation. Rather, it was used to organise the evidence and identify requirements for moving from retrospective AMR prediction toward validated, interpretable, and actionable surveillance intelligence.

Results

Study selection

The searches identified 1,985 records before deduplication: 943 from PubMed/MEDLINE, 318 from Scopus, 642 from Web of Science, and 82 from manual or supplementary screening. After removing 791 duplicates, 1,194 records were screened by title and abstract; 1,069 were excluded; 125 full-text reports were assessed; and 59 were excluded. The full-text exclusions were assigned to mutually exclusive primary reasons: no ML/AI or comparable analytic method (n = 14), not surveillance-intelligence or decision-support relevant (n = 12), not AMR-focused or no AMR/resistance outcome (n = 9), wrong publication type for empirical synthesis (n = 8), drug discovery/basic mechanism focus without surveillance relevance (n = 6), insufficient extractable data (n = 5), and duplicate/overlapping reports, inaccessible full texts, or insufficient source verification (n = 5). Sixty-six studies/reports were retained in the working evidence set. The data-extraction set comprised 42 sources, and the core empirical synthesis set comprised 40 studies. The remaining 24 were retained as contextual, methodological, or background sources that were not charted. Figure 2 shows the PRISMA-ScR flow.

Evidence set used for synthesis

The final working evidence set retained after full-text assessment comprised 66 studies or reports. The synthesis tables focus on the 42 sources that underwent full data extraction. Forty studies were classified as core empirical evidence, one was retained as supportive preprint evidence, and one was contextual review/framework evidence. The remaining 24 sources were retained as contextual, methodological, reporting-standard, or framework sources that informed interpretation but were not charted as study-level empirical evidence.

Study characteristics

The evidence base was dominated by original empirical or modelling studies. Only one of the 42 data-extraction sources was a systematic review, Ardila et al., which was retained for contextual interpretation rather than as primary empirical evidence (10). The core empirical studies covered hospital and intensive-care risk prediction, large-scale surveillance database modelling, genomic and metagenomic AMR prediction, wastewater and environmental surveillance, food-chain or animal-sector surveillance, One Health surveillance, and MALDI-TOF-based rapid resistance prediction. Characteristics of the included studies.

The clinical studies used electronic health records (EHRs), microbiology laboratory data, laboratory information systems (LIS), antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results, complete blood count (CBC) variables, or intensive-care unit (ICU) time-series data. Examples include ICU EHR-based early alert prediction, blood-culture AMR prediction using EHR and socioeconomic deprivation indices, and early forecasting of antimicrobial susceptibility from bacteriology workflow data (12–14). Other examples include multimodal EHR prediction, carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA) risk stratification, and ICU multidrug-resistant organism (MDRO) risk prediction (15, 18, 20). Additional examples include electronic medical record (EMR)-based resistance prediction, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) culture-positivity prediction, and interpretable ICU time-series prediction of multidrug resistance (22, 23, 50).

Data sources and AMR targets

The reviewed studies showed that AMR surveillance intelligence is being developed from multiple data streams rather than from a single dominant data type (Table 2). Clinical and hospital studies used EHRs, EMRs, ICU records, microbiology data, AST results, and laboratory workflow data. Genomic studies used whole-genome sequencing (WGS) or metagenomic sequencing data to predict AMR phenotypes, identify resistance markers, or map resistance-gene risk. MALDI-TOF studies repurposed routine diagnostic mass-spectrometry spectra for rapid AMR prediction or screening. Environmental and wastewater studies used sewage, wastewater, environmental indicators, metagenomic resistome profiles, quantitative PCR (qPCR), agricultural indicators, and geospatial variables.

Machine-learning and analytical approaches

Tree-based machine-learning methods were common across clinical, surveillance database, genomic, and environmental studies (Table 3). Random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, gradient boosting, and CatBoost were used in several clinical, surveillance, genomic, and MALDI-TOF examples (13, 17, 34). Deep-learning approaches were used in multimodal EHR prediction, MRSA time-series prediction, and interpretable ICU time-series modelling (15, 23, 50). Bayesian, probabilistic, and time-series approaches appeared in early susceptibility forecasting, broiler-chain early warning, and farm-level AMR time-series prediction (14, 44, 45).

Surveillance-intelligence functions

Across the 41 studies mapped in Figures 3, 4, prediction and risk stratification were the most frequently represented functions, with additional evidence for tracking, forecasting, early warning, hotspot mapping, decision support, and One Health integration. In Figure 3, EHR and clinical data mapped most strongly to risk stratification and decision support, and WGS and genomic data mapped primarily to prediction. MALDI-TOF and microbiology or LIS data mapped to prediction and decision support, while wastewater, metagenomic, environmental, and geospatial data contributed mainly to tracking, early warning, hotspot mapping, and One Health integration. Forecasting and early-warning evidence was present but heterogeneous. Forecasting studies included hospital AMR prevalence prediction, early susceptibility forecasting across bacteriology workflow stages, and time-series/geospatial resistance forecasting (14, 16, 42). Early-warning studies included environmental anomaly detection, broiler-chain early-warning modelling, and interpretable clinical time-series modelling for early multidrug-resistance prediction in ICU patients (43, 44, 50).

Validation and performance

Validation maturity varied substantially (Table 4). In the validation and implementation-readiness map (Figure 4), eight were descriptive surveillance, forecasting, or methods studies without a discriminative classifier and were not assigned a model-validation tier. Among the remainder, 13 were coded as internal validation or split, four as cross-validation, six as temporal validation, six as external-site validation, two as cross-country validation, and two as prospective validation. These counts are restricted to the synthesis mapping set and should not be interpreted as prevalence in the entire AMR machine-learning literature. Performance metrics were not pooled because the data sources, AMR outcomes, pathogens, validation designs, and class imbalance differed across studies.

Several studies reported strong discrimination or classification performance, but the performance metrics were not directly comparable because the data sources, outcomes, pathogens, validation designs, and class imbalance varied. Diaz et al. reported RF/XGBoost and multiclass random-forest performance for blood-culture AMR outcomes (13). Ureña et al. reported increasing area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) across bacteriology workflow stages (14). Yu et al. reported internal and external validation for CRPA risk stratification (18). Pan et al. reported WGS- and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS)-based rapid prediction for Enterobacter cloacae complex with area under the curve (AUC) values of 88.17–91.25% across five antibiotics and >95% accuracy in 104 clinical specimens (29).

Implementation readiness and limitations

Most studies remained at retrospective model-development or retrospective validation stages. In the implementation-readiness map, 26 studies were classified as retrospective model development, 13 as retrospective models with external or temporal validation, and two as prospective evaluation or operational deployment. The evidence therefore supports growing technical capability for AMR surveillance intelligence, but it does not yet show broad operational deployment into real-time surveillance systems, public-health dashboards, antimicrobial stewardship workflows, or One Health response platforms. Common limitations included single-centre design, retrospective analysis, internal validation only, limited external transportability, class imbalance, one retained supportive preprint record, lack of prospective clinical-impact evaluation, and limited operational deployment evidence.

Feng B et al. provide environmental AMR surveillance evidence in a national-scale study of rural domestic sewage across 39 sites in 22 Chinese provinces. The study profiled 702 antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) subtypes by metagenomic sequencing and applied machine learning to predict ARG health risk (39).

Synthesis of evidence gaps

The evidence base demonstrates broad technical activity across clinical, laboratory, genomic, environmental, wastewater, animal, food-chain, and One Health data streams (Table 5). The clearest evidence clusters were EHR-based risk prediction, genomic or WGS-based AMR prediction, MALDI-TOF rapid resistance prediction, and wastewater/metagenomic surveillance. The least mature areas were prospective deployment, real-time early-warning systems, operational dashboards, implementation-effect studies, model maintenance under temporal drift, and cross-sector One Health response platforms. Overall, the evidence supports movement from passive AMR monitoring toward surveillance intelligence, but most studies remain closer to retrospective analytics than routine operational response systems.

Reporting completeness and the feasibility-to-deployment gap

Appraisal of the 41 studies against TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST showed a consistent gradient (Figure 5 and Table 6). The synthesis set combines prediction-model studies with descriptive surveillance, forecasting, and methods studies, so markers specific to prediction models were scored as absent for studies without a discriminative classifier. The percentages are therefore reported across all 41 studies as reporting completeness within this heterogeneous set, not as risk-of-bias estimates restricted to prediction models. Markers that are intrinsic to prediction models, such as calibration and discrimination, are necessarily absent for non-prediction studies, and the percentages would be higher if restricted to the prediction-model subset.

Discrimination was reported in 31 studies (76%) and explainability in 26 (63%). By implementation stage, 26 studies (63%) remained at retrospective model development, 13 (32%) reached external or temporal validation, and two (5%) reached prospective evaluation or operational deployment.

Discussion

Principal findings

This scoping review shows that machine learning is being applied to AMR surveillance across several distinct data streams, including electronic health records, laboratory information systems, antimicrobial susceptibility testing databases, whole-genome sequencing, metagenomics, wastewater surveillance, MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, food-chain data, animal-sector data, and environmental indicators. The evidence does not support a single dominant model of AMR surveillance intelligence. Instead, it shows a technically diverse field in which prediction, risk stratification, forecasting, rapid resistance inference, environmental tracking, and One Health mapping are developing in parallel.

The strongest empirical clusters in the evidence set were clinical and EHR-based AMR prediction, genomic or WGS-based AMR prediction, MALDI-TOF-based rapid resistance prediction, wastewater or metagenomic surveillance, and One Health or food-chain early-warning applications. However, the field appears more mature for retrospective prediction than for operational surveillance intelligence. This distinction is central to the manuscript: a technically accurate model is not equivalent to a surveillance system unless it is validated, interpretable, updated over time, and connected to an action pathway.

A central implication is that AMR surveillance intelligence should not be evaluated by model discrimination alone. Several studies reported useful prediction, classification, or forecasting performance, but fewer demonstrated how model outputs changed prescribing, diagnostic triage, infection-prevention action, environmental monitoring, outbreak response, or One Health coordination. The next stage of the field should therefore shift from model development toward implementation science, data governance, calibration monitoring, decision-threshold evaluation, user-facing outputs, and measurable clinical or public-health impact. Within the synthesis set, two studies illustrate that this transition is achievable at different stages of readiness. The first was a retrospective electronic health record-based algorithm for uncomplicated urinary tract infection that would have reduced second-line antibiotic use by 67% and inappropriate therapy by 18% relative to clinicians (51). The second was a deployed urinary tract infection decision-support tool that lowered antibiotic mismatch rates in routine outpatient care (52), the single study demonstrating operational deployment with measured impact.

Clinical and EHR-based prediction

Clinical and EHR-based studies formed one of the clearest evidence clusters. These studies used intensive-care records, blood-culture data, laboratory information systems, routine clinical variables, complete blood count parameters, longitudinal EHR data, or multimodal EHR inputs to predict AMR, MDRO infection, bloodstream resistance, MRSA culture positivity, or patient-level resistance risk. Examples include EHR/socioeconomic prediction, early susceptibility forecasting, and MRSA time-series prediction (13, 14, 23). This cluster is directly relevant to antimicrobial stewardship because the outputs are potentially actionable at the point of care, including empiric antibiotic selection, early risk stratification, and prioritisation of patients for infection-control action.

Genomic and WGS-based prediction

Genomic and WGS-based studies show how AMR surveillance can move from phenotypic reporting toward molecularly informed prediction and population-level tracking. Examples include cross-country E. coli WGS validation, Gram-negative WGS prediction, Acinetobacter baumannii WGS prediction, Enterobacter cloacae complex WGS/mNGS prediction, and nontyphoidal Salmonella WGS-based food-chain/public-health modelling (25–29). Nsubuga et al. directly addressed generalizability by training AMR prediction models on England E. coli WGS data and validating against independent African datasets from Uganda, Nigeria, and Tanzania (25). Woh et al. extended genomic AMR prediction to nontyphoidal Salmonella from Hong Kong and Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) countries, linking WGS-based XGBoost and convolutional neural network (CNN) prediction to food safety and public-health surveillance (26). The WGS evidence supports genomic surveillance as a major component of AMR surveillance intelligence, but it also shows that genomic prediction depends on data representativeness, lineage structure, phenotype quality, geographic transferability, sequencing infrastructure, and model updating. Whole-genome-based prediction of minimum inhibitory concentrations was demonstrated early and at scale for foodborne Salmonella (53), and emerging protein-language-model approaches now predict resistance genes and mechanisms directly from sequence, including for genes with low similarity to known references (54).

MALDI-TOF and rapid diagnostic analytics

MALDI-TOF plus machine learning was a recurrent rapid-diagnostic theme in the evidence set. Weis et al. trained calibrated classifiers on the DRIAMS database, which combined more than 300,000 MALDI-TOF spectra with more than 750,000 AMR phenotypes. They reported AUROC values of 0.80 for S. aureus, 0.74 for E. coli, and 0.74 for K. pneumoniae in selected clinically important prediction tasks (31). The retrospective 63-case clinical review in Weis et al. suggested that classifier-informed recommendations would have changed treatment in nine cases and been beneficial in eight of them. The authors emphasised, however, that prospective multicenter clinical evaluation would be required before implementation (31). Wiesmann et al. further showed that MALDI-TOF-based AMR classifiers can perform well on local data but lose performance across unrelated location/time settings and over 18 months without retraining (36). These studies are important because MALDI-TOF spectra are already generated in many diagnostic workflows, creating the possibility of extracting AMR-relevant signals before conventional susceptibility results are complete. Surveillance use will still require laboratory-specific validation, platform calibration, drift monitoring, data-quality control, and prospective evaluation.

Forecasting and early warning

Forecasting and early-warning studies were present but heterogeneous in setting, data granularity, and validation maturity. Vihta et al. used hospital AMR prevalence forecasting at National Health Service (NHS) Trust level, Ureña et al. linked prediction to laboratory workflow timing, and Kim et al. used farm-level AMR data for time-series forecasting (14, 16, 45). Csorba et al. developed environmental anomaly detection and broiler-chain early-warning approaches (43, 44). These studies show proof-of-concept AMR early-warning or forecasting approaches at multiple levels: patient, hospital, farm, environmental, and food-chain systems. However, AMR forecasting should be discussed as an emerging surveillance-intelligence function rather than as a mature operational field.

Environmental, wastewater, and One Health intelligence

Environmental, wastewater, food-chain, and One Health studies broadened the surveillance-intelligence concept beyond clinical prediction. Hendriksen et al. used untreated sewage metagenomics from 79 sites in 60 countries. Feng et al. analysed cross-sector samples in China. Knight et al. compared wastewater surveillance methods in Wales. Woh et al. linked nontyphoidal Salmonella WGS-based AMR prediction to food-chain and public-health surveillance across Hong Kong and ASEAN countries (26, 37, 38, 41). A subsequent global analysis of sewage from 101 countries further mapped the worldwide resistome and its regional patterns (55). These studies are relevant to AMR surveillance because AMR emergence and dissemination involve settings beyond hospitals. However, environmental, food-chain, wastewater, and One Health studies often produce ecological or population-level intelligence rather than direct clinical prescribing recommendations.

Nevertheless, validation remained heterogeneous, and study-by-study interpretation is safer than cross-study performance comparison. Part of this difficulty reflects inconsistent reporting. Adoption of shared standards would make the evidence easier to appraise and compare. Prediction-model development and validation should follow TRIPOD+AI (46), and early-stage clinical evaluation of decision-support systems should follow DECIDE-AI (56).

However, interpretability alone does not prove decision usefulness. A newer class of methods is now entering this space. Foundation models and large language models are being evaluated for antibiotic decision support, but current evidence shows uneven performance. In a comparison of 14 large language models across 60 clinical cases with antibiograms, the best-performing model gave appropriate antibiotic recommendations in about 72 percent of cases, and accuracy fell as case complexity increased (57). Reviews of artificial intelligence for antibiotic prescribing reach a similar conclusion, noting potential value alongside unresolved problems of transparency, explainability, and accountability that currently preclude autonomous use (58). These tools reproduce the central tension of this review: analytic capability has advanced faster than validated, accountable integration into surveillance and stewardship. Studies that apply large language models in this setting should be reported against the dedicated TRIPOD-LLM guideline (59).

These studies show that ML-enabled AMR surveillance intelligence can be relevant beyond high-income hospital settings; for example, locally-tailored machine-learning models have guided empiric antibiotic prescribing in a children’s hospital in Cambodia (60). However, global applicability cannot be assumed from models developed in data-rich environments. Equity also operates at the level of the model. Approaches that draw on socioeconomic or demographic features, such as deprivation-informed prediction (13), can improve discrimination but may encode and amplify existing inequities if they are deployed without fairness assessment. Transparent reporting of subgroup performance and bias, as required by current prediction-model standards (46), is therefore part of equitable AMR surveillance rather than separate from it.

Across all domains, authors should distinguish technical prediction tasks from surveillance-intelligence functions. Read against TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST, the evidence base reports discrimination and explainability far more often than calibration, external validation, or prospective evaluation, which are the domains where prediction-model studies accrue risk of bias and lose applicability. The implication is not that the models are wrong, but that the literature cannot yet tell a surveillance programme which approaches are ready for use. Consistent reporting against TRIPOD+AI (46), early clinical evaluation following DECIDE-AI (56), and explicit linkage of model outputs to measured action would make model quality, calibration, and clinical usefulness directly comparable across studies and close the distance between feasibility and deployment.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. First, its scope spans the full One Health AMR data ecosystem, integrating clinical and electronic-health-record models, laboratory-workflow prediction, whole-genome and metagenomic prediction, MALDI-TOF diagnostic analytics, wastewater and environmental surveillance, and food-chain and animal-sector studies within a single synthesis rather than treating any one stream in isolation. Second, it applies an explicit surveillance-intelligence lens that separates technical prediction from validated, deployable intelligence, reframing a literature that is usually read only as a prediction problem. Third, every appraised study was assessed against TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST and mapped onto transparent validation- and implementation-readiness tiers, giving a reproducible, criterion-based account of maturity rather than a narrative impression. Fourth, the synthesis is translated into a usable conceptual framework that links data streams, governance, analytics, outputs, decision support, and feedback. The review also has limitations. First, counts for study characteristics and evidence maps are counts within the synthesis set, not prevalence estimates for the entire AMR machine-learning literature. Second, one retained record remains a supportive preprint rather than peer-reviewed core evidence; it was therefore interpreted cautiously and separated from peer-reviewed full-text evidence. Excluding the single supportive preprint from the appraisal left this gradient essentially unchanged. Third, the heterogeneity of data sources, AMR outcomes, pathogens, validation designs, and performance metrics prevented meta-analysis or algorithm ranking. Fourth, because operational deployment evidence was limited, the review identifies implementation gaps but cannot estimate the effect of ML-enabled AMR surveillance on prescribing, infection control, environmental intervention, or public-health policy. Fifth, eligibility was restricted to English-language publications indexed in three databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science) and supplemented by citation searching. Relevant studies published in other languages may therefore have been missed, and a language bias cannot be excluded.

Proposed One Health AMR surveillance intelligence framework

Based on the synthesis set, the reviewed studies mapped onto six functional layers of AMR surveillance intelligence (Figure 6) namely, One Health data streams, harmonization and governance, machine-learning analytics, intelligence outputs, decision support and action, and a learning feedback loop. This framework separates data generation from actionable surveillance intelligence. In this structure, AMR data become surveillance intelligence only when they are harmonised, analysed with validated and interpretable methods, translated into decision-relevant outputs, and linked to action pathways with feedback for model updating and surveillance improvement.

One Health AMR Surveillance Intelligence Framework. The framework is read from left to right across six layers: (1) One Health data streams; (2) harmonization and governance; (3) the machine-learning and analytics engine; (4) intelligence outputs; (5) decision support and action; and (6) a learning feedback loop, with the dashed arrow returning feedback (outcomes, resistance trends, workflow impact, and policy response) to update the data pipelines and models. Representative evidence anchors (Author et al., Year) are grouped by domain beneath the schematic; the figure is a conceptual synthesis and the anchors are supporting examples, not evidence that all framework stages were operationally deployed.

The first layer consists of One Health data streams. Clinical and hospital studies used electronic health records, laboratory information systems, antimicrobial susceptibility testing data, intensive-care records, blood-culture data, complete blood count variables, and microbiology workflow data for AMR prediction, early susceptibility forecasting, and risk stratification. Examples include ICU/EHR prediction, EHR/socioeconomic prediction, and LIS-based early susceptibility forecasting (12–14). Genomic and metagenomic studies used WGS or mNGS data for AMR prediction, resistance-gene mapping, cross-country validation, or rapid resistance inference (25–30). MALDI-TOF studies used routine diagnostic mass-spectrometry spectra for rapid AMR prediction or resistance screening (31, 32, 34, 36). Environmental and One Health studies used wastewater, sewage, environmental, food-chain, animal-sector, and farm-level data to support AMR tracking, early warning, or cross-sector surveillance (26, 37, 38, 41, 44, 45).

The second layer is harmonization and governance. Because human, animal, food-chain, environmental, and wastewater data are generated through different sampling systems and laboratory workflows, surveillance intelligence depends on standardised AMR phenotypes, interoperable metadata, data-quality control, and cross-sector linkage under appropriate privacy and bias safeguards (37, 38, 41). Privacy-preserving and federated approaches, in which models are trained across institutions and sectors by exchanging model parameters rather than patient-level or sector-level records, offer one route to cross-sector learning while protecting data confidentiality (61).

The third layer is the machine-learning and analytics engine. Across the reviewed evidence this layer applied tree-based, neural, deep-learning, MALDI-TOF spectral, anomaly-detection, Bayesian, and time-series methods. Representative examples include symbolic-regression ICU prediction, XGBoost and convolutional-network WGS-based Salmonella prediction, MALDI-TOF LightGBM/MLP resistance prediction, and Bayesian broiler-chain early warning (12, 26, 31, 44). Foundation models and large language models are an emerging addition to this layer; they are being tested for antibiotic decision support but are not yet validated for autonomous AMR decisions (57, 58).

The fourth layer is intelligence outputs. In the reviewed evidence, outputs included patient-level AMR risk scores, predicted susceptibility or resistance, MDRO or MRSA risk estimates, hospital or regional prevalence forecasts, rapid resistance signals from MALDI-TOF, WGS, or mNGS, wastewater or environmental resistome signals, ARG risk profiles, and animal or food-chain early-warning indicators. Some outputs are designed for bedside stewardship or infection-control decisions, while others are designed for laboratory interpretation, regional surveillance, wastewater monitoring, environmental tracking, food-chain surveillance, or One Health situational awareness. Representative examples include CRPA risk stratification, WGS-based Salmonella AMR prediction, wastewater surveillance, and food-animal AMR forecasting (18, 26, 41, 45).

The fifth layer links intelligence outputs to decision support and action. Clinical models may support empiric therapy selection, antimicrobial stewardship, isolation precautions, or prioritisation of high-risk patients (13, 18, 23). Genomic, MALDI-TOF, mNGS, and laboratory-based models may support earlier resistance inference and laboratory decision support (27, 29, 31, 32, 36). Environmental and wastewater models may support public-health alerts, hotspot detection, or targeted surveillance (39, 41). Animal-sector, food-chain, and integrated One Health models may support cross-sector response planning (26, 38, 44). The evidence supports technical feasibility across these domains, but it does not show that most models have been prospectively deployed into operational AMR response systems.

The sixth layer is the learning feedback loop. Surveillance intelligence should be updated using observed outcomes, resistance trends, workflow impact, model calibration, data drift, and policy or clinical response. This is necessary because AMR epidemiology, diagnostic workflows, prescribing patterns, pathogen distributions, and data-generating systems can change over time. The need for feedback and updating is supported by the broader pattern of retrospective model development and the limited number of studies with external, temporal, cross-site, or drift-focused validation. Representative validation-oriented examples include cross-country genomic validation, external EHR validation, temporal MALDI-TOF validation, and DRIAMS cross-site/temporal MALDI-TOF analyses (23, 25, 31, 36). Overall, the framework positions AMR surveillance intelligence as a learning system rather than a single prediction model.

Comparison with existing frameworks and novelty

The proposed framework is intended to complement, not replace, established guidance for trustworthy health AI. Reporting and quality standards such as TRIPOD+AI (46) and PROBAST specify how individual prediction models should be reported and appraised, and DECIDE-AI (56) structures the early clinical evaluation of decision-support systems. Broader governance guidance, including the WHO guidance on the ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health (62) and the FUTURE-AI consensus principles of fairness, universality, traceability, usability, robustness, and explainability (63), sets out ethical and operational conditions for deployable AI. These frameworks operate mainly at the level of a single model or a single clinical tool.

The novelty of the One Health AMR Surveillance Intelligence Framework lies in three features that these standards do not by themselves provide. First, it is specific to AMR and spans human, animal, food-chain, environmental, wastewater, and genomic data within one structure rather than addressing a single model in a single setting. Second, it positions reporting, governance, and evaluation standards as components within a larger surveillance system, linking data harmonization, analytics, intelligence outputs, decision pathways, and feedback rather than treating model appraisal as the endpoint. Third, it makes the distinction between technical prediction and actionable surveillance intelligence explicit and operational, so that existing standards such as TRIPOD+AI, DECIDE-AI, FUTURE-AI, and the WHO guidance can be applied at the appropriate layer. In this way the framework aligns with these standards and organises where each applies within an AMR One Health surveillance programme.

Validating the framework

The framework is a conceptual synthesis, and it is presented as a testable structure rather than a validated system. A staged validation strategy would allow it to be evaluated in practice. In a first stage, retrospective application would use the framework to classify existing AMR machine-learning studies and surveillance programmes by layer and by readiness tier, testing whether the layers and the prediction-to-intelligence distinction capture the evidence without gaps or overlap. In a second stage, prospective pilot implementation in one or more settings, for example a hospital antimicrobial stewardship service, a diagnostic laboratory using MALDI-TOF or sequencing, or a wastewater surveillance programme, would instrument each layer with predefined metrics. Candidate metrics include data completeness and harmonization at the input layer, discrimination and calibration at the analytics layer, timeliness and interpretability at the output layer, change in clinician or public-health action at the decision layer, and model drift and retraining frequency at the feedback layer. In a third stage, early clinical evaluation of any decision-support component would follow DECIDE-AI (56), individual prediction models would be reported against TRIPOD+AI (46), and overall trustworthiness would be assessed against the FUTURE-AI principles (63). A deployed stewardship tool that reduced antibiotic mismatch in routine outpatient care provides a concrete precedent for the decision and feedback layers and a template for such prospective evaluation (52). Success would be defined not by model discrimination alone but by measurable and sustained improvement in surveillance and stewardship action.

Implementation and economic considerations

Moving from a working model to an operational surveillance component carries practical and economic costs that shape feasibility. Development and training of deep-learning and genomic models can require substantial computation and specialised hardware, while routine inference for many clinical and laboratory tools, such as MALDI-TOF spectral classifiers or tree-based clinical models, is comparatively light and can run on standard hospital infrastructure. The larger and more recurrent costs usually arise after deployment. Models degrade as pathogens, prescribing, and data systems change, so periodic revalidation and retraining are needed to maintain performance, as shown by the decline in MALDI-TOF classifier accuracy over 18 months without retraining (36).

Sustained operation also depends on data engineering, laboratory and information-technology integration, interoperable metadata, quality control, clinical governance, and staff training, none of which are one-time expenses. A systematic review of economic evaluations of health-care AI found that rigorous cost and cost-effectiveness evidence remains limited, so the economic case for most tools is still unproven (64). These considerations bear directly on equity. Settings with constrained computation, sequencing capacity, connectivity, or laboratory infrastructure may be unable to sustain data-intensive tools, which risks concentrating benefit in well-resourced systems. Sustainable adoption therefore depends on matching model complexity to available infrastructure, favouring interpretable and low-maintenance approaches where appropriate, budgeting for monitoring and retraining from the outset, and reporting implementation and cost alongside accuracy. The single deployed tool in this evidence set shows that operational integration is achievable, but also that such evidence remains rare (52).

Future directions and research priorities

Several priorities would move the field from technical feasibility toward operational surveillance intelligence. Prospective, multicentre, and cross-country validation is the most pressing need, because most current evidence is retrospective and internally validated, and external and temporal performance often falls. Multimodal approaches that combine electronic health records, laboratory, genomic, and imaging or spectral data may improve prediction where single data streams are insufficient, provided they are validated and calibrated rather than only benchmarked (15). Foundation models and large language models are entering AMR decision support, but current performance is uneven, and their use should be evaluated against dedicated reporting guidance before any autonomous role (57–59). Explainable AI should move beyond feature-attribution displays toward explanations that demonstrably support correct clinical, laboratory, or public-health decisions and that expose subgroup performance and bias, consistent with prediction-model reporting standards (46) and trustworthy-AI principles (63). Federated and privacy-preserving learning offers a route to cross-institution and cross-sector models without sharing patient-level or sector-level data, which suits One Health surveillance (61).

Finally, these methods should be connected to established public-health architecture, including alignment and interoperability with WHO surveillance and policy systems such as the Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System and the Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance, so that model outputs feed coordinated One Health monitoring and response rather than remaining standalone tools (5, 7). Progress against these priorities, evaluated with prospective designs and standardised reporting, would let the field show not only that resistance can be predicted but that surveillance intelligence changes decisions and outcomes.

Conclusion

Machine learning is now being applied to AMR across the full One Health spectrum, from clinical and electronic-health-record systems, laboratory and susceptibility workflows, and whole-genome and metagenomic sequencing to MALDI-TOF spectra, wastewater and environmental monitoring, and animal and food-chain systems. Across these settings it supports prediction, risk stratification, forecasting, rapid resistance inference, and early warning.

The decisive message of this review is that the field demonstrates technical feasibility far more convincingly than operational readiness. Most models are still developed and validated retrospectively, whereas external and prospective validation, calibration and drift monitoring, and evidence of real-world impact remain uncommon. AMR data become surveillance intelligence not when a model is merely accurate, but when its outputs are validated in the target setting, calibrated and monitored over time, interpretable, explicitly linked to a clinical, laboratory, environmental, or One Health action, and re-evaluated as epidemiology and workflows change.

Closing this gap will require standards-based development and reporting, external and prospective validation across sites and over time, explicit coupling of model outputs to decisions, and cross-sector data governance, with prediction models reported against TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST and decision-support tools evaluated early against DECIDE-AI. The proposed One Health AMR Surveillance Intelligence Framework organises this shift from data generation toward actionable, adaptive intelligence. The task for the next phase of the field is no longer to show that resistance can be predicted, but to build surveillance systems that change what clinicians, laboratories, and public-health agencies actually do.
