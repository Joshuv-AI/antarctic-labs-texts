A theoretical one-dimensional model for variable-density Rayleigh–Taylor turbulence
Chian Yeh Goh, Guillaume Blanquart

Abstract
In an early theoretical work published in 1965, 3 proposed a turbulent diffusivity model for Rayleigh–Taylor (RT) mixing. We review its derivation and present alternative arguments leading to the same final similarity equation. The original work then introduced an approximation that led to a simplified ordinary differential equation (ODE), which was used primarily to derive the important scaling result, h ∼ ( ln ⁡ R ) ​ g ​ t 2 h\sim(\ln R)gt^{2} . Here, we extend the analysis by examining the solutions to both the full similarity ODE and the simplified ODE in detail. It is shown that the full similarity equation captures many now well-known features of non-Boussinesq RT flows, including asymmetric spike and bubble growth and a systematic shift of velocity statistics toward the light-fluid side. Comparisons of the theoretical model with numerical and experimental studies show reasonable agreement in both spatial profiles and growth trends of mixing layer heights. We further show that a global mass correction applied to the simplified solution closely approximates the full solution, highlighting that, to leading order, RT mixing is governed by the competing dynamics between diffusion of ln ⁡ ρ ¯ \ln\bar{\rho} and mass conservation.



I
Introduction

The Rayleigh–Taylor (RT) instability
(
8
;
12
)
arises in a variety of flow applications, including atmospheric science, astrophysics, and inertial confinement fusion
(
16
)
. Between two unstably stratified fluid reservoirs of densities
\rho_{H}>\rho_{L}
under gravity
g
, RT instabilities develop at the interface, and the flow transitions to turbulence. In an infinitely large domain, the flow eventually reaches a late-time state of self-similar growth
(
13
)
, during which the mixing layer height
h(t)
grows in time as

h=F(R)gt^{2},

(1)

where
R=\rho_{H}/\rho_{L}
is the density ratio,
F(R)
is an unspecified function of
R
, and
t
is measured relative to a virtual time origin. In the Boussinesq limit,
9
showed that the mixing layer height scales linearly with the Atwood number,
A=(R-1)/(R+1)
, and Eq. (
1
) can be written as

h\approx\alpha Agt^{2},

(2)

where
\alpha
is a dimensionless growth parameter.

Despite the breakdown of the Boussinesq assumption at finite Atwood number, most variable-density RT studies
(
4
;
2
;
14
;
15
)
continue to interpret results using
\alpha
values defined by Eq. (
2
). When Eq. (
2
) is applied separately to the heights of rising bubbles and falling spikes, the bubble growth parameter
\alpha_{b}
was found to be largely insensitive to the Atwood number, while the spike growth parameter
\alpha_{s}
was found to increase with the Atwood number. As a result, both their sum (
\alpha_{T}=\alpha_{b}+\alpha_{s}
) and their ratio
\alpha_{s}/\alpha_{b}
increase with the Atwood number. Such asymmetries are also reflected in the Favre-averaged velocity and turbulent kinetic energy, which exhibit a systematic shift in their spatial profiles toward the light-fluid side as
A
increases
(
7
;
6
)
.

Interestingly, a different density scaling for the mixing layer height was proposed much earlier by
3
. Using a turbulent diffusivity model, they derived a self-similar solution for the mean density that yields
h\propto(\ln R)gt^{2}
, which is consistent with Eq. (
2
) at small
A
. Although this work is commonly credited as an early proponent of
h\sim gt^{2}
scaling
(
1
;
14
;
16
;
10
)
, the associated
\ln R
dependence has, to our knowledge, not been adopted in modern RT studies until it was independently proposed by
6
. This may be due to the original work being published in Russian and thus less accessible to the broader research community, or due to the rather specific assumptions involved that are difficult to verify without detailed data from high-Atwood-number RT flows.

The objective of the present work is to revisit the turbulent diffusivity model proposed by
3
, examine its assumptions, and use it to address questions that have arisen in the RT literature since the model was first proposed. Throughout, we clearly distinguish between restatements of the reference work and new contributions introduced here. Section
II
introduces the turbulent diffusivity model, Sec.
III
details the derivation of the self-similar solution, Sec.
IV
contains further analysis of the model, and Sec.
V
summarizes the physical insights gained.

II
Mathematical description

In
3
, the mathematical framework was initially formulated for the case of a thin turbulent RT mixing layer superposed on an arbitrary, large-scale background flow. Its presentation involves lengthy derivations, contains a substantial number of typographical errors, and employs a relatively loose notation that does not distinguish between variables associated with the full three-dimensional (3D) equations, the one-dimensional (1D) Reynolds-averaged equations, and the analogous 1D diffusion problem.

Despite these shortcomings in its presentation, the resulting model has the potential to be highly useful due to its simplicity. Motivated by this, the present section aims to clarify and streamline the original formulation. We focus solely on the canonical RT setup with a quiescent background, thereby significantly reducing the number of variables. In addition, we present simpler, alternative derivations where possible and adopt a clear and consistent notation throughout. Section
II.1
presents the canonical one-dimensional (1D) problem governed by molecular diffusion, while Sec.
II.2
describes the analogous Reynolds-averaged formulation of the RT problem with turbulent diffusion. Finally, in Sec.
II.3
, we derive a turbulent diffusivity model that recovers the final form proposed by
3
, albeit via slightly different arguments.

II.1
Molecular diffusion in one dimension

In one dimension, the temporal variable-density (VD) diffusion problem in
(t,y)
coordinates is entirely determined by the continuity, scalar transport, and state equations. They are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\rho v}{\partial y}=0,

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho Y}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\rho vY}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\rho D\frac{\partial Y}{\partial y}\right),

(4)

\displaystyle\rho(Y)=\frac{\rho_{H}\rho_{L}}{\rho_{H}-Y\Delta\rho},

(5)

where
D
is the molecular diffusivity,
Y
is the mass fraction, and
\Delta\rho=\rho_{H}-\rho_{L}
. These equations can be combined into a single equation for density

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(D\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial y}\right),

(6)

which admits an analytical solution
\rho=\rho_{L}+(\Delta\rho/2)[1+\erf(y/\sqrt{4Dt})]
, with the velocity fully determined as
v=-D(\partial\rho/\partial y)/\rho
.

II.2
Turbulent diffusion in three dimensions

3
formulated the turbulent RT problem as a diffusive perturbation to a general background flow, before later specializing to the planar configuration with a quiescent background. The resulting 3D equations were subsequently reduced to 1D without any explicit connection between the 1D variables and the underlying 3D flow. Here, we start directly from the canonical RT problem and reduce the 3D equations to 1D using the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) framework.

The Reynolds-averaged continuity, scalar, and state equations for turbulent RT flow (where
\boldsymbol{g}=-g\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y}
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}\tilde{v}}{\partial y}=0,

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}\widetilde{Y}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}\tilde{v}\tilde{Y}}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left\langle\rho D\frac{\partial Y}{\partial y}\right\rangle-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\rho D_{t}\frac{\partial\tilde{Y}}{\partial y}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(\tilde{Y})=\frac{\rho_{H}\rho_{L}}{\rho_{H}-\tilde{Y}\Delta\rho},

(9)

where
\bar{\phi}
denotes the Reynolds average of
\phi
,
\tilde{\phi}=\overline{\rho\phi}/\bar{\rho}
denotes the Favre average, and the turbulent scalar flux is expressed in terms of a turbulent diffusivity

D_{t}=-\frac{\widetilde{vY}-\tilde{v}\tilde{Y}}{\partial\tilde{Y}/\partial y}.

(10)

Combining Eqs. (
7
)–(
10
), and assuming a sufficiently high Péclet number (
{\rm Pe}\sim h\dot{h}/D
) such that
D_{t}\gg D
, the equations can be reduced to

\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(D_{t}\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial y}\right).

(11)

This is the same mean density equation as (2.21) from
3
, and analogous to the 1D diffusion problem described by Eq. (
6
).

II.3
Turbulent diffusivity model

The turbulent diffusivity model proposed by
3
has a relatively complex derivation, which involves (1) assuming a Prandtl mixing length model, (2) applying Kolmogorov scaling for viscous dissipation, and (3) matching the local entropy contributions from scalar mixing and viscous dissipation, along with additional hydrodynamic and thermodynamic approximations. In the present derivation, we retain the same Prandtl mixing length model, but arrive at the identical turbulent diffusivity model through a more direct line of reasoning. The assumptions are

(i)

The turbulent diffusivity is modeled using Prandtl mixing length theory as

D^{*}_{t}(t,y)\sim v_{t}(t,y)h_{t}(t),

(12)

where
h_{t}
and
v_{t}
are the characteristic length and velocity scales of the turbulence.

(ii)

While
h_{t}
is left undefined, the characteristic turbulent velocity
v_{t}
is related to
h_{t}
by assuming a constant scaling of the global potential energy loss with the kinetic energy of the flow,

\Delta\rho gh_{t}^{2}=gh_{t}^{2}\int\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial y}{\rm d}y\sim\int\bar{\rho}v_{t}^{2}{\rm d}y.

(13)

(iii)

It is then assumed that this relationship holds true locally (i.e., at each
y
), yielding

v_{t}\sim\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\rho}}\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial y}gh_{t}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(14)

Combining Eqs. (
12
), (
14
), and including a constant of proportionality
K
, the model is

D_{t}^{*}=K\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\rho}}\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial y}\right)^{1/2}g^{1/2}h_{t}^{2}.

(15)

Unlike molecular diffusivity, the turbulent diffusivity
D_{t}
is zero in the unmixed reservoirs, and the mean density varies over a compact support
y\in[-h_{s},h_{b}]
, with boundary conditions

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(-h_{s})=\rho_{L},\qquad\bar{\rho}(h_{b})=\rho_{H},

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial y}(-h_{s})=0,\qquad\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial y}(h_{b})=0,

(17)

where
h_{s}
and
h_{b}
are not known
a priori
and need to be solved for. In this theoretical model, Eqs. (
16
)–(
17
) are the definitions of the spike and bubble heights, respectively. The total mixing layer height is defined as
h_{T}=h_{b}+h_{s}
.

III
Self-similar solution

Using the turbulent diffusivity model proposed in Eq. (
15
), the mean density equation (
11
) admits a self-similar solution. The corresponding similarity equation derived by
3
is reviewed in Sec.
III.1
. Our primary contribution to Sec.
III
is in Sec.
III.2
, where we develop a general mapping between similarity and physical-space variables. Finally, the analytical solution obtained by
3
for small density ratios is presented in Sec.
III.3
.

III.1
Similarity equation

This section follows the analysis of
3
closely, with some notational changes and added clarification. The turbulent diffusivity model (
15
) is used with Eq. (
11
) to yield

\frac{1}{Kg^{1/2}h_{t}^{2}}\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[\frac{1}{\bar{\rho}^{1/2}}\left(\frac{\partial\bar{\rho}}{\partial y}\right)^{3/2}\right].

(18)

We apply the substitution

\varphi=\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho_{H}},\qquad\xi=\frac{y}{h_{0}},\qquad{\rm d}\tau=\frac{Kg^{1/2}h_{t}^{2}}{h_{0}^{5/2}}{\rm d}t,

(19)

where
h_{0}
is a constant reference length introduced for non-dimensionalization. Equation (
18
) can be written in terms of the nondimensional variables as

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\tau}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left[\frac{1}{\varphi^{1/2}}\left(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\xi}\right)^{3/2}\right].

(20)

We seek a self-similar solution of the form
\varphi=\varphi(\eta)
with the similarity coordinate

\eta=\frac{\xi}{a\tau^{s}},

(21)

where
s
and
a
are parameters to be determined. Substituting Eq. (
21
) into Eq. (
20
), we get

-\frac{s\eta}{\tau}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\eta}=\frac{1}{(a\tau^{s})^{5/2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\left[\left(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\eta}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\varphi^{1/2}}\right].

(22)

For a self-similar solution to exist,
s=2/5
is necessary to eliminate
\tau
. For notational simplicity of the resulting differential equation,
a=5^{2/5}
is chosen to yield

-2\eta\varphi^{\prime}=\left[\varphi^{\prime 3/2}\frac{1}{\varphi^{1/2}}\right]^{\prime},

(23)

with the similarity coordinate

\eta=\frac{\xi}{(5\tau)^{2/5}},

(24)

and boundary conditions

\displaystyle\varphi(-\lambda_{s})=1/R,\qquad\varphi(\lambda_{b})=1,

(25)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(-\lambda_{s})=0,\qquad\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda_{b})=0,

(26)

where
\lambda_{i}
is the dimensionless mixing layer height in
\eta
coordinates and
i
represents bubble, spike, or total height (
\lambda_{T}=\lambda_{b}+\lambda_{s}
).

Equation (
23
) can be further reduced to a first order ordinary differential equation (ODE) by the substitution
x^{2}=\varphi^{\prime}/\varphi
, resulting in

-2\eta=3\frac{{\rm d}x}{{\rm d}\eta}+x^{3}.

(27)

The Neumann boundary conditions in Eq. (
26
) become Dirichlet boundary conditions on
x
:

x(-\lambda_{s})=0,\qquad x(\lambda_{b})=0,

(28)

and the Dirichlet boundary conditions (
25
) on
\varphi
become an integral relation on
x^{2}
:

\int^{\lambda_{b}}_{-\lambda_{s}}x^{2}{\rm d}\eta=\ln\left(\frac{\rho_{H}}{\rho_{L}}\right)=\ln R.

(29)

If
\lambda_{s}
or
\lambda_{b}
were known, Eq. (
27
) would be fully determined by specification of either boundary condition in Eq. (
28
). However, because
\lambda_{s}
and
\lambda_{b}
are also unknown, solving Eq. (
27
) requires both boundary conditions from Eq. (
28
) and the additional constraint Eq. (
29
). Without solving the equations directly, it can be inferred from the form of Eqs. (
27
)–(
29
) that
x(\eta;R)
and all dimensionless heights
\lambda_{i}(R)
are functions of
\ln R
.

III.2
Scaling of physical quantities

Following the derivation of Eqs. (
27
)–(
29
),
3
proceeded to introduce an approximation that leads to an analytical solution and transformed only that specific result back into physical space. Before reviewing that analysis in Sec.
III.3
, we contribute a new discussion by considering transformations of the general similarity solution
(\eta,x,\varphi)
into physical-space variables (
t,y,\bar{\rho},D_{t}
), allowing for cases where
x
may be determined analytically or numerically. These general results are then used directly in Sec.
III.3
and subsequent sections. To facilitate comparisons between similarity variables and physical RT data, equivalent normalizations in both coordinate systems are also established.

The physical coordinate
y
is related to the similarity variable
\eta
by Eqs. (
24
) and (
19
). Hence, any physical height
h_{i}(t,R)
can be related to its dimensionless form
\lambda_{i}(R)
by applying these transformations to yield

h_{i}(t)=\lambda_{i}(5\tau)^{2/5}h_{0}.

(30)

This is clearly valid for the bubble, spike, and total heights, which are explicitly expressed in terms of spatial coordinates. For a self-similar solution, all heights vary proportionally to each other, including the yet-to-be-defined turbulence height
h_{t}
(or
\lambda_{t}
). We apply Eq. (
30
) to
h_{t}
, substitute it into the equation for
{\rm d\tau}
(
19
), and integrate the resulting expression, leading to

(5\tau)^{1/5}=K\lambda_{t}^{2}\left(\frac{g}{h_{0}}\right)^{1/2}t.

(31)

Combining Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
), any physical height of the mixing layer can be written as

h_{i}=K^{2}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{t}^{4}gt^{2}.

(32)

This recovers the well-known
h\sim gt^{2}
scaling, with density-ratio effects captured by
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{t}^{4}
. Equation (
32
) involves two distinct dimensionless heights: the subscript
i
denotes the specific height being predicted, while the subscript
t
refers to the turbulence height on which the turbulent diffusivity model (and underlying dynamics) is based. In general, these two heights may take on different definitions and are notated distinctly to reflect this subtlety. In self-similar RT flows, a natural choice for the
normalized
spatial coordinate is

\frac{y}{h_{i}}=\frac{\eta}{\lambda_{i}}.

(33)

The physical density is easily determined from Eq. (
19
) as
\bar{\rho}(t,y)=\rho_{H}\varphi(\eta)
. Although
\varphi
is dimensionless, its range varies with the density ratio. To account for density-ratio effects in the normalization, we consider instead the mole fraction
X
, defined as

X=\frac{\bar{\rho}-\rho_{L}}{\rho_{H}-\rho_{L}}=\frac{R\varphi-1}{R-1},

(34)

with values ranging from 0 in the unmixed light fluid to 1 in the unmixed heavy fluid.

The final quantity of interest is the turbulent diffusivity (
15
), which can be written in similarity variables as

D_{t}^{*}=K\left(\frac{1}{\varphi}\frac{{\rm d}\varphi}{{\rm d}\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial\xi}\frac{{\rm d}\xi}{{\rm d}y}\right)^{1/2}g^{1/2}h_{t}^{2}=\frac{Kg^{1/2}h_{t}^{2}}{(5\tau)^{1/5}h_{0}^{1/2}}x.

(35)

Combining Eqs. (
31
), (
32
), and (
35
), the similarity variable
x
can be interpreted as a dimensionless turbulent diffusivity of the form

x(\eta)=\frac{D^{*}_{t}}{K^{4}\lambda_{t}^{8}g^{2}t^{3}}=\frac{2\lambda_{i}^{2}}{h_{i}\dot{h}_{i}}D^{*}_{t}.

(36)

Equation (
36
) shows that
x(\eta)
, though dimensionless, has a density-ratio dependence through the factor
\lambda_{i}^{2}
. By explicitly separating the similarity variables from the physical variables, the normalized turbulent diffusivity may therefore be written as

\frac{D^{*}_{t}}{h_{i}\dot{h}_{i}}=\frac{x}{2\lambda_{i}^{2}}.

(37)

III.3
Analytical solution for small density ratios

While Eqs. (
27
)–(
29
) can be solved numerically,
3
considered an approximation that leads to an analytical solution. For small density ratios,
x^{3}=(\varphi^{\prime}/\varphi)^{3/2}
is bounded by
(\Delta\rho/\rho_{L})^{3/2}=(R-1)^{3/2}\ll 1
. Neglecting the
x^{3}
term, Eq. (
27
) can be simplified to

\frac{{\rm d}\hat{x}}{{\rm d}\eta}=-\frac{2}{3}\eta,

(38)

where
\hat{\cdot}
denotes a solution variable to this simplified ODE. Based on the form of Eq. (
38
),
\hat{x}
must be an even function with boundaries defined by
\hat{\lambda}_{s}=\hat{\lambda}_{b}=\hat{\lambda}_{T}/2
. The solution to Eq. (
38
) is easily shown to be

\hat{x}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{T}^{2}}{4}-\eta^{2}\right),

(39)

and Eq. (
29
) simplifies to

\int^{\hat{\lambda}_{T}/2}_{-\hat{\lambda}_{T}/2}\hat{x}^{2}{\rm d}\eta=\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{T}^{5}}{270}=\ln R,\qquad{\rm or}\qquad\hat{\lambda}_{T}=\left(270\ln R\right)^{1/5}.

(40)

The density field satisfies
\hat{x}^{2}=(\ln\hat{\varphi})^{\prime}
and can be integrated analytically as

\hat{\varphi}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{T}^{5}}{2160}\left[4-15\left(\frac{\eta}{\hat{\lambda}_{T}}\right)+40\left(\frac{\eta}{\hat{\lambda}_{T}}\right)^{3}-48\left(\frac{\eta}{\hat{\lambda}_{T}}\right)^{5}\right]\right\}.

(41)

Applying Eq. (
32
) to the total height, assuming
\lambda_{t}\propto\hat{\lambda}_{T}
, and substituting Eq. (
40
), we get

\hat{h}_{T}\propto K^{2}\hat{\lambda}_{T}^{5}gt^{2}=270K^{2}(\ln R)gt^{2},

(42)

which reveals an explicit density-ratio dependence in terms of
\ln R
instead of the Atwood number, a result consistent with recent work by
6
. By comparing the integral magnitudes of the neglected term,
|\int(x^{3}/3){\rm d}\eta|
, with the RHS of Eq. (
38
),
|\int(2\eta/3){\rm d}\eta|
,
3
proposed a range of validity for Eq. (
42
) to be
R\lesssim 4
.

\ln R

IV.1.2
Simplified ODE

For the simplified ODE (
38
),
3
reported the analytical
\ln R
growth for
\hat{h}_{T}
in Eq. (
42
) without examining the spatial profiles in detail. This section discusses important physical insights that can be gained by analyzing these profiles directly.

The normalized diffusivity
\hat{x}/2\hat{\lambda}_{T}^{2}
and mole fraction
\hat{X}
are shown in Figs.
4
(a,b), respectively. It is straightforward to show that the analytical form of the normalized diffusivity collapses exactly with Atwood number, as demonstrated in Fig.
4

\hat{x}
is an even function,
\hat{X}(\eta)
exhibits significant asymmetry within the nominally symmetric interval

\eta=0
, suggesting that mass conservation is violated when the

\hat{x}^{2}=\hat{\varphi}^{\prime}/\hat{\varphi}=(\ln\varphi)^{\prime}
, the even symmetry of
\hat{x}

in Fig.

\frac{\ln(R\varphi)}{\ln R}=\frac{\ln\bar{\rho}-\ln\rho_{L}}{\ln\rho_{H}-\ln\rho_{L}}

\ln\bar{\rho}

\bar{\rho}

11

IV.2
Mixing layer heights

In this section, we consider the scaling of mixing layer heights, first in terms of definitions that encompass the full extent of the mixing layer, followed by the heights of spikes and bubbles.

IV.2.1
Total height

A key result from
3
states that the total mixing layer height scales with
\ln R
. Because this scaling was derived for the solution to the simplified ODE,
\hat{h}_{T}
, the authors restricted the validity of this result to
R\leq 4
(i.e.
A\leq 0.6
). Additionally, it was also assumed in the derivation of Eq. (
42
) that the turbulence height
h_{t}
scales with
\hat{h}_{T}
. In this section, we examine the sensitivity of
\ln R
scaling to these two assumptions, first by employing the numerical solution to the full ODE, and second by considering different height definitions for both the predicted height and the turbulence height.

The general scaling relationship for any height is Eq. (
32
), with density-ratio effects captured via
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{t}^{4}
. This factor represents the scaling of the predicted height
h_{i}
assuming that the turbulence height governing the underlying dynamics of the mixing layer is
h_{t}
. Using the full ODE solution, the density-ratio scaling factor is computed by considering two different height definitions,
\lambda_{1}=\int 4X(1-X){\rm d}\eta
and
\lambda_{T}=\lambda_{s}+\lambda_{b}
, as possible candidates for both the predicted height
\lambda_{i}
and turbulence height
\lambda_{t}
. Each version of
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{t}^{4}
is first divided by
\ln R
, then normalized by its
A\rightarrow 0
value, and shown in Fig.
6
as a function of Atwood number.

For a fixed choice of turbulent height
\lambda_{t}
, the scaling of
h_{T}\sim\lambda_{T}\lambda_{t}^{4}
increases more rapidly with Atwood number than
h_{1}\sim\lambda_{1}\lambda_{t}^{4}
, indicating that localized growth at the edges (as opposed to a spatially averaged growth) becomes increasingly significant at high
A
. For a given
h_{i}
, the sensitivity to the choice of
\lambda_{t}
is amplified by its quartic dependence, where
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{1}^{4}
scaling is sub-logarithmic in
R
, while
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{T}^{4}
scaling is super-logarithmic. All cases shown in Fig.
6
remain within 20% of
\ln R
scaling up to
A\approx 0.84
(or
R\approx 11
), but diverge significantly as
A\rightarrow 1
. The physically appropriate choice of
h_{t}
remains an open question. Given the approximations in the theoretical model, the divergent behavior of
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{1}^{4}
and
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{T}^{4}
scalings, and the lack of converged high-Atwood-number data for validation, we propose that
\ln R
scaling may be used as a “median” estimate across all Atwood numbers, at least until further developments become available. As shown in
6
,
\ln R
scaling lies well within the observed scatter from available numerical and experimental data, supporting its applicability over a wide range of Atwood numbers.

IV.2.2
Spikes and bubbles

It is common in the VD RT literature to track the growth of spikes and bubbles separately. As discussed in the introduction, it has been generally observed that bubble heights scale linearly with Atwood number while spike heights do not; this translates to near-constant values of
\alpha_{b}(A)

Growth parameters

2

32

\alpha_{i}=\frac{K^{2}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{t}^{4}}{A}=\frac{K^{2}\lambda_{i}(270\ln R)^{4/5}}{A},

\lambda_{t}=\hat{\lambda}_{T}

For ease of comparison, all
\alpha_{i}

V
Summary and discussion

Despite its relevance and simplicity, the proposed turbulent diffusivity model from
3
, along with the associated
\ln R
scaling, has seen limited adoption in the RT literature. In the present work, we have streamlined the original derivation, validated the model, extended the analysis, and contextualized the results within more recent developments in RT turbulence.

The full self-similar ODE captures many qualitative features of non-Boussinesq RT mixing layers, namely

1.

The total mixing layer height scales approximately with
\ln R
, rather than
A
.

2.

Spike heights appear to deviate more strongly from
Agt^{2}
scaling than bubble heights.

3.

The ratio of spike to bubble heights increases with Atwood number.

4.

Velocity profiles (represented by
x
) are shifted toward the light-fluid side.

These behaviors are inherently captured by the model of
3
; however, with the exception of observation (1), they were not discussed in the original paper. The present work brings these results to light and highlights that many now-known features of non-Boussinesq RT flows are already contained within a simple 1D theoretical framework that has long been available but whose relevance has perhaps been overlooked.

Although the full ODE (
27
) is straightforward to solve numerically, we propose a mass correction to the simplified ODE solution and demonstrate that it reproduces the same qualitative characteristics. In this formulation, the normalized turbulent diffusivity is represented exactly by a downward parabola following
\ln R
scaling, with a spatial shift imposed by mass conservation. This further simplification offers two main advantages. First, the simple representation of the turbulent diffusivity as a spatially-shifted parabola may facilitate analytical developments of Reynolds-averaged turbulence models (e.g.,
11
); a full set of closed-form expressions for this representation is provided in Sec.
IV.1.2
. Second, the model highlights the competing dynamics between diffusion of
\ln\bar{\rho}
and mass conservation, offering a mechanistic explanation for the observed asymmetry in non-Boussinesq RT mixing layers.
