p53 regulates mitochondrial function and alpha-synuclein aggregation in Parkinson's disease
Authors: Kaiying Hou, Jialin Chen, Yuxin Xie, Tingting Liu, Jiaying Li, Chong Meng, Dongxue Niu, Meiyan Xian, Jingwen Li, Chaoyang Zhu, Hong Li, Jianshe Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease, in which mitochondrial dysfunction and abnormal aggregation of alpha-synuclein (α-syn) play key roles in the pathology of PD. As a classic tumor suppressor, p53 has also been found to be involved in the pathological process of PD in recent years. However, the specific mechanism by which p53 regulates mitochondrial function and abnormal aggregation of α-syn is still unclear. Here, we observed that the expression of α-syn and p53 was increased and mitochondria were impaired in the MPTP-induced PD mouse model, leading us to speculate on whether p53 affects mitochondrial impairment and abnormal α-syn aggregation in PD pathology. Next, cellular experiments revealed that the p53 inhibition by pifithrin-α regulates mitochondrial function through mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics, then ameliorates oxidative stress and apoptosis in PD. Meanwhile, the in vitro study showed that the p53 protein interacted with α-syn to accelerate the process of α-syn liquid-liquid phase separation and amyloid fibril formation, promoting the development of PD pathology. In summary, p53 modulates mitochondrial function through mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics, stimulating the pathogenic aggregation of α-syn protein and neurodegeneration in Parkinson’s disease.

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common progressive neurodegenerative disease (1, 2). The pathological hallmarks of PD are the absence of dopaminergic (DA) neurons in the substantia nigra compacta (SNc) and the misfolding of alpha-synuclein (α-syn) in the neuronal cell bodies and synapses, which accumulates and forms amyloid fibrils enriched in β-folded aggregates called Lewy bodies (LB) and Lewy neurites (LN) (2, 3). Numerous studies conducted in the last several decades have shown the pathways by which many pathogenic processes, such as neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, abnormal protein aggregation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, impact the development of PD (4, 5, 6, 7). Even with our tremendous advancements, we currently have little insight into the precise mechanisms by which these factors work, and no proven preventive or therapeutic measures are beneficial for Parkinson's disease (PD). The study of the pathogenesis of PD, which has profound implications for diagnosis and treatment, remains a formidable challenge that needs to be continually explored.

Mitochondria are important organelles with multiple functions, including cellular energy production, signal transmission, Ca2+ homeostasis, and metabolism (8). Mitochondria are highly dynamic, primarily achieved through mitochondrial quality control systems. These include mitochondrial biogenesis, mitophagy, and mitochondrial dynamics, which work synergistically together to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, and dysfunctional mitochondrial quality control is associated with neuronal dysfunction and PD (9, 10, 11). The process by which damaged and aged mitochondria are delivered to lysosomes for degradation through the autophagy pathway for mitochondrial quality and quantity regulation is known as mitophagy. Effective mitophagy eliminates damaged mitochondria generated during excessive mitochondrial fission and is an important mechanism for attenuating or reversing the pathogenesis of PD (12, 13, 14). Mitochondrial dynamics refers to the dynamic balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion. When the cellular environment is altered, especially under pathological conditions, it can trigger changes in the function or activity of mitochondrial fission-fusion-related proteins, leading to impaired mitochondrial morphology, quantity, quality, and function (15, 16, 17).

α-syn is a soluble small-molecule protein consisting of 140 amino acids encoded by the PD-related gene SNCA. Under pathological conditions, α-syn misfolds in neurons and forms insoluble fibrils, exerting a toxic effect on neurons and ultimately leading to the development of PD (18, 19). Increasing evidence suggests that overexpression and aberrant aggregation of α-syn play an important role in the neurodegenerative process of PD, which can be regarded as a characteristic biomarker of PD (20, 21). Biomolecules (e.g., nucleic acids, proteins, etc.) can form condensates in cells through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) and participate in the execution of cellular functions (22, 23, 24, 25). Recently, it was found that LLPS is involved in and regulates the abnormal aggregation process of α-syn. α-syn initially forms highly mobile liquid condensates through LLPS, and over time, the condensates undergo a liquid-solid transition to form amyloid hydrogels containing oligomeric and fibrillar substances, which eventually transform into solid-like amyloid fibril aggregates. Several PD-related conditions, such as familial PD gene mutations, post-translational modifications, metal ions, and drugs, have also been found to affect the α-syn LLPS process and its subsequent maturation (26, 27, 28, 29).

The p53 protein is a major tumor suppressor in humans and has recently been found to play an important role in neurodegenerative diseases such as PD, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (30, 31). p53 may be closely associated with pathogenic events such as cellular stress and apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, protein homeostasis, regulation of cellular metabolism, and inflammatory responses (32, 33, 34). Pathogenic p53 integrates multiple cellular stresses, activates these downstream events, and induces DA neuronal death, which plays a crucial role in the disease progression of PD and appears to be a potential target for its treatment.

Although multiple studies have shown that p53, mitochondria, and α-syn are key components of PD pathology, there is still a lack of understanding of how p53 affects mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics processes in PD and whether p53 is involved in the aberrant aggregation process of α-syn. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of inhibiting p53 expression on mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics related to mitochondrial function in PD, and focused on the role of p53 protein on the formation of amyloid fibers by abnormal aggregation of α-syn, to further clarify the role of p53 in the pathology of PD.

Results

Construction of MPTP-induced PD mouse model

MPTP-induced PD mouse model was constructed for behavioral examination and sampling (Fig. 1A). Compared with the Saline group, the mice in the MPTP group had reduced movement distance and times of entering the central area in the open field test (Fig. 1B). In the elevated plus maze test, mice in the MPTP group spent less time in the open arm and had fewer entries into the open arm (Fig. 1C). This suggests that there are some symptoms of anxiety in the MPTP group of mice. In both the swimming and hanging tail experiments, the mice in the MPTP group showed an increase in resting time compared to the Saline group, suggesting that some depressive symptoms existed in the MPTP group mice (Fig. 1, D and E). The step distance of mice in the MPTP group was reduced compared with the Saline group, and the sticking time of mice in the MPTP group in the rotarod was significantly lower than that in the Saline group, suggesting that the motor ability of mice in the MPTP group was impaired (Fig. 1, F and G). There was no significant change in the body weight of mice in the MPTP group compared to the Saline group (Fig. 1H). Meanwhile, the intensity of TH (DA neuronal marker) staining in the substantia nigra (SN) and striatum (CPU) of mice in the MPTP group was significantly lower, with the percentage of TH strongly positive, positive, and weakly positive neurons significantly decreased compared with that in the saline group (Fig. 1, I and J). Subsequently, we examined the expression of α-syn and p53 in the cells, which revealed an increase in α-syn expression in the MPP + group and a decrease in p53 expression in the PFT-α group, suggesting that PFT-α suppressed the expression of p53 and MPP + -induced PD-like pathological changes in the cells (Fig. 3, C and D). Inhibition of p53 expression ameliorates impaired mitophagy in PD

Mitophagy is an important pathway for cells to remove damaged mitochondria, next, we detected the change in mitophagy-associated proteins (Fig. 4, A and B). Compared with the DMSO group, the MPP+ group showed decreased expression of Pink1 and Parkin, increased expression of p62, and an attenuated LC3-Ⅰ to LC3-Ⅱ transition, suggesting that mitophagy was impaired in the pathology of PD. When p53 expression was inhibited by PFT-α, the expressions of Pink1 and Parkin were increased, the expression of p62 was decreased, and the LC3-Ⅰ to LC3-Ⅱ transition was increased in the P + M group compared with the MPP + group, indicating that inhibition of p53 expression increased the level of mitophagy in PD. Regarding the role of p62, Pink1, Parkin proteins, and LC3I-LC3II conversion in mitophagy: In the PINK1-Parkin pathway, Pink1 accumulates on damaged mitochondria and recruits Parkin to ubiquitinate outer membrane proteins (35). p62 acts as an autophagy adaptor that binds ubiquitinated mitochondria and promotes their clustering and association with LC3-II–positive autophagosomes (36). LC3-I is lipidated to form LC3-II, which is incorporated into autophagosomal membranes and is a key marker for autophagosome formation and cargo degradation (37). In our study, MPP + treatment decreased Pink1 and Parkin, increased p62, and reduced LC3-II/LC3-I ratio. These changes are consistent with impaired mitophagy in PD cells. In addition, we further verified the effect of inhibition of p53 expression on mitophagy in PD pathology by immunofluorescence (Fig. 4, C–F). We observed increased Pink1 and Parkin expression in the P + M group compared with the MPP + group. Inhibition of p53 expression alleviates disturbed mitochondrial dynamics in PD

Mitochondrial dynamics is an important component of mitochondrial quality control which plays a key role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. To further clarify the effect of inhibiting p53 expression on mitochondrial dynamics in PD, we detected the expression changes of mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins (Fig. 5, A and B). Compared with the DMSO group, the MPP+ group had elevated Drp1 and Fis1 protein expression, indicating increased mitochondrial fission, and decreased Opa1 and Mfn2 protein expression, suggesting decreased mitochondrial fusion. Meanwhile, after inhibiting p53 expression, the protein levels of Drp1 and Fis1 in the P + M group were significantly lower than those in the MPP + group, Opa1 was higher, and Mfn2 showed an increasing trend, which suggests that inhibition of p53 expression to some extent reduces the excessive mitochondrial fission and increases mitochondrial fusion in the pathology of PD. Next, we further performed immunofluorescence on the cells, which again revealed a trend of decreased expression of Drp1, lower expression of Fis1, and higher expression of Opa1 and Mfn2 in the P + M group compared to the MPP + group, which further validated this result (Fig. 5, C–F). Since oxidative stress-triggered apoptosis is the main form of DA neuronal cell death in PD pathology, in this study, the apoptotic status was detected by Tunel staining (Fig. 7, B and C). Compared with the DMSO group, apoptosis was increased in the MPP+ group, whereas apoptosis was decreased in the P + M group compared with the MPP + group. p53 accelerates α-syn LLPS process and amyloid fibril formation

To further investigate the effect of p53 on the pathology of PD, pET28a-α-syn-mCherry and pET28a-p53-EGFP recombinant plasmids were constructed in this study, and expressed and purified to obtain α-syn-mCherry and p53-EGFP recombinant proteins (Fig. S1). We observed the LLPS process of p53 and α-syn and found that each of these proteins could undergo LLPS to form liquid condensates in the presence of 10% PEG-8000. The size of the condensates gradually increased with the increase in protein concentration, and at a concentration of 30 μM, the p53 protein had already appeared as an irregularly shaped solid condensate. (Fig. 8, A and B). Next, we further observed the LLPS of 50 μM α-syn in the presence of different concentrations of p53 (Fig. 8C). We have observed that as p53 increased, α-syn condensates gradually changed from droplets to irregular condensates and eventually to amyloid fibril precipitation. We have performed quantitative analysis on the fluorescence images of 50 μM α-syn at different p53 concentrations based on condensate number, average radius, and circularity (Fig. 8, G–I). Condensate number per field increased from 12 ± 2 to 38 ± 4, average radius from 1.2 ± 0.1 μm to 3.5 ± 0.3 μm, and circularity (0=irregular, 1=perfect sphere) decreased from 0.85 ± 0.03 to 0.32 ± 0.04. These results clearly demonstrate p53-induced transition of α-syn condensates from liquid droplets to solid-like fibrils. In addition, we examined the phase behavior in the system by turbidity tests (Fig. 8D). As the concentration of p53 in the system gradually increases, the turbidity value rises, indicating a phase transition in the system. We also examined the effect of different concentrations of p53 on the formation of 50 μM α-syn amyloid fibrils by the Thioflavin T (ThT) assay in the incubation at 37 °C (Fig. 8E). When only α-syn was present in the system, the formation of amyloid fibrils increased with the increase of incubation time; when p53 was added to the system, the formation of α-syn amyloid fibrils increased significantly and was positively correlated with the incubation time and p53 concentration. The aggregation lag time, defined as the time required for the normalized ThT fluorescence to reach 10% of its maximum(Fig. 8F). The lag time with increasing p53 concentration, from 12.3 ± 0.8 h for α-syn alone (0 μM p53) to 3.5 ± 0.2 h in the presence of 30 μM p53. Discussion

p53 is a tumor suppressor protein encoded by the oncogene tp53, and previous studies have focused on its role in cancer (38, 39, 40), but recently p53 has also been found to be involved in the pathological process of some neurodegenerative diseases (30, 41, 42, 43). However, the specific mechanisms of p53 in PD pathology remain unclear. Our study revealed abnormally increased p53 and impaired mitochondrial function in PD mice, suggesting that p53 may play an important role in PD pathology by regulating mitochondrial function.

Mitochondria are highly active double-membrane organelles with extensive functions in eukaryotic cells. Mitochondria maintain homeostasis through their quality control systems, such as mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics, they cooperate to ensure the normal function of mitochondria (10, 11, 44). Previous studies have shown impaired mitophagy and disordered mitochondrial dynamics in PD pathology (45, 46, 47), and our results are consistent with this. In MPP + -induced MN9D cells, the expression of Pink1 and Parkin was reduced, p62 expression was elevated, LC3 II/Ⅰ was reduced, autophagy was inhibited; the expression of Drp1 and Fis1 was elevated, Opa1 and Mfn2 expression was reduced, the level of fission was enhanced, and the level of fusion was weakened. In addition, the roles of p62, PINK1, Parkin and the conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II in mitophagy that we elaborated in Section 2.4, inhibition of p53 expression ameliorates impaired mitophagy in PD, are fully consistent with the experimental results of our present study. To further elucidate the effects of p53 on mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics, we pretreated MN9D cells with the p53 inhibitor PFT-α, which revealed that inhibition of p53 expression significantly ameliorated impaired mitophagy and disordered mitochondrial dynamics in the MPP + -induced PD cells.

Next, this study investigated the effect of p53 on mitochondrial function in PD pathology showing that in the MPP+-induced PD cells, mitochondrial membrane potential was reduced, suggesting that the normal function of mitochondria was impaired, which is in line with the findings of Imai et al. (48, 49). Meanwhile, MN9D cells with inhibited p53 expression showed elevated mitochondrial membrane potential upon MPP+ stimulation, suggesting that inhibition of p53 expression attenuated the reduction of mitochondrial membrane potential in the PD cells. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that mitochondrial dysfunction can lead to ROS production and stress-induced apoptosis (45), which was confirmed by this study, and it was further found that inhibition of p53 expression antagonized the MPP+-induced rise in MDA and ROS and the decrease in SOD and catalase in MN9D cell model, reducing apoptosis. Combined with the above results, we hypothesized that inhibition of p53 expression induces restoration of mitochondrial function and plays a neuroprotective role in PD.

α-syn, as a major component of LB and LN, is a pathological marker protein for PD, and its abnormal aggregation and amyloid fibril formation are closely related to PD pathology (18, 50, 51). Previous studies have shown that p53 interacts with α-syn under physiological conditions and the upregulation of p53 expression leads to a strong increase in α-syn promoter activity and mRNA levels, suggesting an association between α-syn and p53 (52, 53). Our findings indicate that intracellular inhibition of p53 expression effectively reduces α-syn protein levels, and recombinant p53 protein accelerates the LLPS process of α-syn and promotes amyloid fibril formation in vitro. These results suggest that p53 accelerates the pathologic progression of PD by contributing to the aggregation of α-syn, whereas inhibition of p53 expression effectively ameliorates the pathologic progression of Parkinson's disease.

In summary, we found that inhibition of p53 expression promotes mitophagy and maintains mitochondrial dynamic homeostasis, thereby attenuating mitochondrial damage in PD. Meanwhile, p53 is directly involved in the process of abnormal α-syn aggregation, which promotes α-syn LLPS progression and amyloid fibril formation and accelerates the development of PD pathology. Overall, our findings provide new insights into the mechanisms of PD pathology and suggest that p53 is a potential target for PD therapy and may be important for other neurodegenerative diseases associated with protein aggregation.

However, there are still some limitations in this study, such as we still do not know whether p53 affects mitochondrial function by promoting abnormal α-syn aggregation; the specific mechanism by which p53 affects mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics; and the molecular basis for the interaction of p53 with α-syn. In addition, more studies are needed to explore other pathological manifestations of PD, such as inflammatory response and metabolic abnormalities, and explore other potential mechanisms of p53.

Experimental procedures

Animals, cells and plasmids

The experimental animals were 2-month-old male C57BL/6J mice (body mass 20 ± 2 g) provided by Zhengzhou Huaxing Laboratory Animals. The animals were housed in an SPF environment with constant temperature and humidity, free access to water and food, and alternating black and dark light for 12 h. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Henan University (HUSOM2022-131). MN9D cells were purchased from Changsha Kewen Biotechnology Limited Company, and cultured in DMEM high-sugar medium containing 10% FBS. The pET28a-mCherry and pET28a-EGFP plasmids used were purchased from the Beijing Genomics institution.

Construction of MPTP-induced PD mouse model and behavioral assays

The experimental animals were randomly divided into two groups. The MPTP group was intraperitoneally injected with MPTP (AbMol) at a dose of 25 mg/kg once a day for 14 days, and the Saline group was injected with the corresponding volume of saline. After the injection, the state of the mice was observed, and on the 21st day, behavioral assays such as open field, elevated plus maze, tail suspension, swimming, stepping distance, and rotarod were performed. All animal experiments were supervised by the Ethical Review Committee of Henan University (HUSOM2022-131).

Cell model construction

Pifithrin-α (PFT-α, AbMol) was used to inhibit p53 expression, and the PD cell model was constructed with MPP+ (AbMol). MN9D cells were cultured for 12 h to logarithmic phase, the p53 inhibitor group (PFT-α) and p53 inhibitor + PD model group (PFT-α+MPP+, P + M) were pretreated by adding PFT-α with a final concentration of 20 μM, and then after 24 h, the PD model group (MPP+) and P + M group were added with MPP+ at a final concentration of 0.5 mM, the control group was added with the corresponding volume of DMSO, and the model was successfully constructed after 24 h.

Western blot

Mice brain tissue and MN9D cell samples were lysed in RIPA lysate (Beyotime), subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, and wet-transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). α-syn, p53, Pink1, Parkin, Drp1, Fis1, Mfn2, Bax, Bcl-2, Caspase3 (Affinity), LC3 (Proteintech), SQSTM1/p62 (Servicebio), Opa1 (BD Biosciences) antibodies were used for immunoblotting. The relative expression levels of proteins in the extracts were quantified by chemiluminescence (Affinity). Images were acquired by chemiluminescence imager (Sage Creation), and the intensity of immunoreactive bands was determined by ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Mice brain tissue sections and MN9D cells were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with immunohistochemistry using DAB (Servicebio) or double immunofluorescence staining using a fluorescent secondary antibody (Affinity), and then observed by imaging through the microscope (Olympus).

Transmission electron microscopy

A small part of the tissue block was cut from the SN and CPU of mice and fixed in an electron microscope fixative solution, dehydrated, embedded, and trimmed, then sectioned with a frozen ultrathin microtome (JEOL) and observed by transmission electron microscope (JEOL).

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay and Tunel staining

JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit (Servicebio) and one-step Tunel apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime) were used for staining and observation under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

Malondialdehyde, superoxide dismutase, catalase, and reactive oxygen species assay

Malondialdehyde detection kit (Beyotime), total superoxide dismutase activity detection kit (Beyotime), catalase detection kit (Beyotime), and reactive oxygen species detection kit (Servicebio) were used for treatment. A microplate reader (Biotek) and a fluorescence microscope (Olympus) were used for detection.

Plasmid construction and protein expression purification

PCR amplification of cDNA sequence of human SNCA and tp53, SNCA primers for F: 5′-ACGCGTCGACATGGATGTATTCATGAAAGGACTTTCAAAGGC-3′, R: 5′-CCCAAGCTTGGCTTCAGGTTCGTAGTCTTGATACC-3′; tp53 primers for F: 5′-CCGGAATTCATGGAGGAGCCGCAGT-3′, R: 5′-CCCAAGCTTCTCAGTCTGAGTCAGGC-3′. The target gene and empty vector were digested and ligated to construct the recombinant plasmid. Sequencing and double enzyme digestion verified the successful construction of the plasmid. Protein expression was induced by IPTG. His-tag protein purification kit (inclusion body protein) (CWBIO) and His-tag protein purification kit (soluble protein) (CWBIO) were used for protein purification. Protein dialysis was performed in dialysate of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 0.5 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, and 5%(v/v) glycerol, and protein concentration was performed by PEG-8000 standing at 4 °C.

Liquid–liquid phase separation, turbidity test, and Thioflavin T detection

The α-syn and p53 were diluted to the corresponding concentrations with 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) and 50 mM NaCl phase separation buffer, and 10% PEG-8000 was added as a molecular aggregating agent. The mixture was homogenized, and then the LLPS was observed under the microscope (Olympus) and detected by an enzyme-labeled instrument (Biotek) at 405 nm for the turbidity test. For ThT detection, α-syn and p53 were diluted to the corresponding concentrations in PBS and added to 96-well plates, and then ThT (Solarbio) solution with a final concentration of 40 μM was added. After mixing, they were incubated at 37 °C and then assayed at 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h and 48 h with an enzyme-labeled instrument at an excitation wavelength of 440 nm and an emission wavelength of 485 nm. (Biotek).

Normalization and lag time analysis of ThT aggregation Kinetics

To improve comparability and minimize systematic bias, the ThT fluorescence data were normalized to the maximum fluorescence intensity within each experimental group (peak value set to 1). The aggregation lag time, defined as the time required for the normalized ThT fluorescence to reach 10% of its maximum.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used to analyze the data, and the statistical results were expressed as mean ± standard error (mean ± SEM). One-way ANOVA was used to determine the differences between different groups, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
