Anionic solvation reconstruction stabilizes interfacial chemistry for high-temperature and high-voltage Li metal batteries
Authors: Zixiong Shi, Simil Thomas, Georgian Melinte, Christian G. Canlas, Dong Guo, Yongjiu Lei, Jehad K. El-Demellawi, Nouruddin Mansour, Osman M. Bakr, Omar F. Mohammed, Husam N. Alshareef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of high-voltage Li metal batteries is crucial to meeting increasing demand for high specific energy. However, their high-temperature operation remains a huge challenge due to reduced electrolyte oxidation stability and aggravated interfacial side reactions. Herein, a multimodal 19F nuclear magnetic resonance technique is developed to reveal temperature-mediated evolution of electrolyte anion solvation chemistry, thus identifying its vital roles in stabilizing high-voltage positive electrodes. A universal solvent screening strategy is proposed to customize an anion-anchored compact solvation structure electrolyte with large-size and anion-compressed solvation structure. This strategy simultaneously elevates anti-oxidation ability, stabilizes electrode–electrolyte interphase, and maintains structural integrity of the positive electrodes. Consequently, a 317 Wh kg–1 Li metal pouch cell based on the total cell mass achieves high thermal safety and cycling stability at 55 °C. Our work elucidates the reaction mechanisms of solvation structure and interfacial chemistry in high-temperature and high-voltage Li metal batteries, which offers insightful guidance for designing wide-temperature battery electrolytes.

Introduction

Lithium||LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (Li||NCM811) batteries are recognized as a promising next-generation energy storage system due to high specific energy and long cycling lifespan1–4. Sustaining stable high-temperature (HT) operation is crucial to achieving practical application in various specialized scenarios like aerospace, petroleum exploration, and mining, whereas it is still a tremendous challenge due to reduced oxidation stability of electrolytes and deteriorated cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI)5–8. In this respect, although common ether-based electrolytes are compatible with Li metal negative electrodes, they always fail to support high-voltage (HV) NCM811 positive electrodes since their highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy level is relatively high9,10. Even worse, the increase in temperature will narrow the energy level of electrolytes, accompanied by a lowered potential of positive electrode. Thus, the electrolyte’s HOMO electrons are prone to shift toward positive electrodes and trigger severe electrolyte decomposition at high charging voltages. Besides, in conventional electrolytes with rich free solvents and high activity, the formation of detrimental byproducts along with the dissolution of transition metal ions will be accelerated at high temperatures, which further exacerbates the interfacial stability and cycling performance of HV Li metal batteries.

Designing better ether-based electrolytes that enhance anti-oxidation ability and stabilize the CEI layer is key to constructing reliable HT Li||NCM811 batteries11,12. Prevailing strategies mainly focus on increasing electrolyte concentration and reducing free active solvents (e.g., localized high concentration electrolyte, LHCE), thus alleviating the solvent decomposition and side reactions at high charging voltages13,14. In the meantime, it facilitates the formation of compact ion pairs (CIPs) and aggregates (AGGs), which in turn contribute to anion-derived CEI rich in inorganic components. However, high anion concentration inevitably incurs high electrolyte cost and potential safety hazards. In addition, exothermic reactions between the electrolyte and electrode materials will be aggravated at high temperatures; hence common ether solvents with a low boiling point like 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) are intrinsically unstable.

Besides, the evolution of electrolyte solvation structure and its correlation with CEI chemistry at high temperatures is still not clear15,16. It is traditionally accepted that the decomposition of highly active ether solvents is the origin of the inferior interfacial stability for HV positive electrodes. Therefore, previous studies focus on reducing free solvents and promoting cation–anion coordination in the inner-layer solvation sheath of electrolytes17. Considering that anions are adsorbed on the positive electrode surface due to the Coulombic force to form an inner Helmholtz layer (IHL) upon the charging process and subsequently oxidized to form CEI, regulating anion solvation chemistry might be of vital importance to stabilize HV positive electrodes, yet it has received insufficient attention to date18. Besides, how the evolution of electrolyte solvation structure at high temperatures affects the interfacial chemistry and structural properties of HV positive electrodes is questionable. In this respect, characterization tools that can precisely investigate the coordination environment and intermolecular interactions of anions are lacking19. Furthermore, universal solvent screening strategies and electrolyte design principles for HT and HV Li metal batteries have not been established.

Inspired by the above discussions, an anion dual-regulation strategy is proposed via the concurrent introduction of compressing solvent (CS) and anchoring solvent (AS), wherein ethylene glycol dibutyl ether (EGDBE) and 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) were respectively employed as a proof-of-concept to reconstruct anionic solvation structure. The as-derived anion-anchored compact solvation structure electrolyte (A-CSSE) is featured by large-size and anion-compressed solvation clusters, unlike the conventional LHCE with small and loose ion pairs. A multimodal 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technique was developed to study the temperature-mediated evolution of anion solvation chemistry. It is revealed that effective anion immobilization and large solvation clusters are maintained at high temperatures. Thus, A-CSSE facilitates the formation of thin, homogeneous, and inorganic-rich CEI while maintaining the structural integrity of NCM811 positive electrodes. Consequently, Li||NCM811 batteries harvest steady cycling performance within a wide temperature range of 25–100 °C. More encouragingly, an as-fabricated pouch cells delivers a specific energy of 317 Wh kg–1 based on the total mass of the complete pouch cell and sustains 50 stable cycles at 55 °C, which also showcases high thermal safety and accomplishes the nail penetration test. Our study uncovers the crucial importance of anion solvation structure in stabilizing HT and HV Li metal batteries, which is anticipated to guide wide-temperature electrolyte design for next-generation battery technologies.

Results

Electrolyte design and characterization for HT Li||NCM811 batteries

From the perspective of electron energy level (Fig. 1a), an ideal electrolyte is one whose highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels can cover the Fermi levels of both positive and negative electrodes, thus avoiding electrolyte decomposition20,21. However, at high temperatures, the energy levels of electrolytes become narrower due to the apparent elevation of HOMO level, thus triggering the jumping of HOMO electrons toward the positive electrode and causing electrolyte decomposition. In this respect, it is crucial to enhance the oxidation stability of electrolytes for operating in HT working environment. Taking lithiumbis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) dissolved into DME into consideration, energy levels of various solvation species are studied by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. As shown in Fig. 1b, on one hand, the HOMO level of TFSI– is higher than that of DME and TFSI–-DME shows the most positive HOMO level, suggesting that anion-solvent complex is the most unstable species for HV positive electrodes. On the other hand, the HOMO levels of each solvation species are lowered upon coordinating with Li ions. However, the dynamic evolution of solvation structure can barely be reflected in DFT calculation results, which are further supported by the electrochemical test of oxidation stability. To elevate the oxidation stability of ether-based electrolytes, one promising method is to elevate the oxidation stability of the anion-solvent complex (Fig. 1c). To this end, AS that has a strong affinity with anion and a low HOMO energy level is desired. Besides, reducing free anions and promoting Li+-anion coordination is another effective solution, which can be achieved by introducing weakly solvating CS. Furthermore, a series of solvent candidates are taken into consideration (Supplementary Table 1), which are generally divided into linear ethers: ethylene glycol diethyl ether (EGDEE); EGDBE; diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G2); tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (G4) and cyclic ethers: 1,3-dioxolane (DOL); 1,3-dioxane (1,3-DX) as CS. In the meantime, benzenes: toluene (TOL); fluobenzene (FB) and fluorinated ethers: bis(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) ether (BTFE); TTE are expected to serve as AS. Based on the above solvent screening principles, their energy levels and binding energy with anions (BEA) were calculated (Fig. 1d). Obviously, F-containing groups aid in enhancing the interaction with anions while lowering the HOMO energy level, yet they exhibit reduced molecular polarity and thus show low solubility for Li salts. In this case, TTE with a high boiling point was selected to serve as AS. In parallel, electrostatic potential (ESP) was calculated (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Data 1). Specifically, the lowest negative electrostatic potential (ESPmin) represents the nucleophilicity of the solvent. A decreased solvating effect will be reflected by a more positive ESPmin22. A large value of the highest positive electrostatic potential (ESPmax) is a representative feature of non-solvating antisolvents with low polarity. It is observed that, as for EGDEE and EGDBE, the extension of the terminal alkyl chain maintains high nucleophilicity with Li ions while guaranteeing high polarity. Besides, they show weakly solvating effect to enable anion-rich inner-layer solvation sheath owing to high C/O atom ratio and large steric hinderance23. It is worth noting that cyclic ethers show larger ESPmax values than linear ethers, thus struggling to dissolve Li salts. Especially, EGDBE shows a very negative ESPmin along with the lowest ESPmax amongst various solvents, making it serve as a suitable CS candidate.

Note that ɛ stands for the dielectric constant, μ represents the dipole moment of the solvent, r is the distance between the ions and the center of the solvent, and θ is the angle of the solvent relative to the line linking the ion and the center of the solvent molecule. In this respect, TTE as an anti-solvent possesses a relatively high dipole moment of 2.36 D, thus enhancing the anion-dipole interaction25. Besides, EGDBE, which has a large molecular size, is conducive to weakening Li+-EGDBE interaction and compressing anions to enter Li-ion inner-layer solvation structure.

Specifically, loose solvation structure electrolyte (LSSE) was prepared via dissolving 0.9 M LiTFSI and 0.1 M lithium difluoro(oxalate)borate (LiDFOB) into DME while A-CSSE was obtained via replacing DME with EGDBE/TTE mixture with a volume ratio of 1: 1. A handful of LiDFOB additive was used to alleviate the corrosion of current collector at high charging voltages since LSSE without the addition of LiDFOB can scarcely achieve normal charging operation at high temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 2)26. Besides, the volume ratio of EGDBE and TTE is fixed to 1: 1, thus achieving a balance between sufficient salt dissolution and favorable ion transport (Supplementary Fig. 3). To study Li-ion solvation structure and ion-dipole interactions in electrolytes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out, in which the trace LiDFOB additive was not taken into consideration unless otherwise stated. As displayed in the radial distribution functions (RDF) curves (Fig. 1f, g), LSSE exhibits a typical characteristic of solvent-dominated solvation structure, in which a multitude of DME molecules dominate the inner-layer solvation sheath, indicating a strong coordination effect of DME. In stark contrast, A-CSSE promotes the participation of anions into the Li-ion solvation structure with a similar coordination number of anions and EGDBE molecules, suggesting that EGDBE exhibits a weakly solvating effect to enable anion-rich solvation configuration. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, RDF curves of both A-CSSE and LSSE with the addition of trace LiDFOB were collected. In comparison with TFSI–, DFOB anion is always closer to the central Li ions in the solvating inner sheath of electrolytes, which is anticipated to elevate the interfacial stability of HV positive electrodes with the formation of B-containing species. It is intriguing that at high temperatures, more anions can coordinate with Li ions, whose coordination number is even higher than that of EGDBE (Supplementary Fig. 5). This suggests that the anion-compressed solvation clusters can be maintained. Note that Li+···EGDBE distance is close to Li+···DME, again confirming the high nucleophilicity of EGDBE to dissolve Li salts. In the meantime, the weakly solvating effect of EGDBE mainly originates from its appropriate molecular configuration with a high C/O atomic ratio. Unlike the coordination scenario of DME, more anions have to coordinate with Li ions in A-CSSE to achieve charge balance since two EGDBE molecules with a long carbon chain can barely be accommodated into Li-ion inner-layer solvation sheath.

As shown in Fig. 1h, there are two typical peaks for solvation clusters with a scattering vector (Q) value of 1.08 and 1.56 Å−1 in LSSE, which correspond to a characteristic interatomic correlation distance (r) value of 7.15 Å and 4.95 Å, respectively. However, the peaks exhibit an obvious shift toward lower Q values for A-CSSE, and a peak around 0.5 Å can be observed, suggestive of the formation of much larger solvation clusters27. This can be attributed to the apparent increase of TFSI– in the inner-layer solvation sheath since anions have a large size. Besides, the distance between the central Li+ and surrounding EGDBE molecules is very close, thus forming a compressed solvation structure with a large size. As shown in Raman spectra (Supplementary Fig. 6), the peak around 740 cm–1 is ascribed to TFSI–. In this respect, A-CSSE possesses a much higher proportion of coordinated anions as compared to LSSE, again confirming the anion-rich solvation structure that facilitates Li-ion desolvation process. It is therefore concluded that under the concurrent regulation of AS and CS, more anions are constrained to form large solvation clusters, wherein the coordination between Li ions and anions is enhanced. In the meantime, TFSI–-TTE complex has a strong interaction to enable the effective immobilization of anions, which also displays a lower HOMO energy level than free anions and TFSI–-DME (Supplementary Fig. 7). The high oxidation stability of A-CSSE is confirmed by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) test at 55 °C (Supplementary Fig. 8). Obviously, it shows a higher potential of electrolyte decomposition at 5.6 V than that (5.0 V) of LSSE, which is envisaged to restrict oxidative parasitic reactions at high charging voltages. Taken together, A-CSSE elevates the electrolyte anti-oxidation ability via regulating anionic solvation structure, which would sustain interfacial stability for HT and HV positive electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 9).

2D NMR homonuclear or heteronuclear correlation spectroscopy can visualize the chemical interactions between anions and other solvation species in electrolytes. 1D 19F NMR spectra of A-CSSE is shown in Fig. 2b. Peaks around –70 ppm are assigned to LiPF6 reference. TFSI– shows only one peak at ca. –81 ppm, which is beneficial for performing 2D NMR tests and subsequent integration analysis. There are four peaks that are ascribed to the F atoms of TTE on different groups. Therefore, it is feasible to build a correlation between anions and AS, CS, or Li ions through the combination of 19F with other NMR nuclei (i.e., 1H and 7Li), thereby further elucidating the anion solvation chemistry.

Note that a customized NMR tube with a coaxial internal insert tube was employed to eliminate the possible influence of deuterated solutions on the solvation structure of electrolyte samples, wherein 10 mM LiPF6 was dissolved in DMSO-d6 with a high boiling point as the standard reference for 1H, 7Li, and 19F nuclei28. 7Li resonance exhibits an evident deshielding effect after the addition of trace LiDFOB, while 19F NMR chemical shift is almost unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 10). This manifests that DFOB– participates in the solvating inner sheath of Li ions and has a stronger electron-withdrawing capability than TFSI–, which is consistent with the forgoing RDF results. Such an enhanced Li+-DFOB– coordination is anticipated to play a pivotal role in promoting the formation of inorganic-rich components in CEI and thus elevating the interfacial stability of HV positive electrodes. With respect to the 1D 19F NMR spectra (Fig. 2c), the peaks of TFSI– exhibit a similar shift trend for four electrolytes with the increase of temperature. Specifically, the chemical shifts of CSSE and LSSE are similar, yet they exhibit an obvious upfield shift upon the addition of TTE. This again confirms the anchoring effect of TTE; hence, such strong interaction enables the electron transfer from TTE to anions29. In the meantime, when replacing DME with EGDBE, the corresponding electrolytes exhibit a downfield shift in 7Li NMR spectra, indicating less electron transfer from ethereal oxygen atoms to Li ions. In this respect, EGDBE exhibits a long alkyl chain and a weakly solvating effect; hence, fewer EGDBE molecules can be accommodated in the Li-ion inner solvation sheath as compared to DME. Besides, the resonance of all peaks shifts to a more positive ppm with increased temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicative of lower electron cloud density around TFSI– due to lowered viscosity and accelerated ion dynamics. As for two electrolytes without the addition of AS, the temperature-mediated change of chemical shift for CSSE is apparently larger than that of LSSE, manifesting the enhanced Li+-TFSI– coordination under the regulation of EGDBE since Li+ is a strong electron acceptor.

As shown in Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 12, 19F diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) NMR tests were conducted to achieve DA. It is intriguing that A-CSSE and CSSE exhibit a similar DA value, suggesting that EGDBE is an effective CS to extrude the solvation structure and restrict the diffusion of anions. Although the DA of A-LSSE is a little higher than that of A-CSSE and CSSE at 25 ± 0.5 °C, it exhibits a higher slope and a more obvious increase with the elevation of temperatures (Supplementary Table 2). This indicates that, for one thing, EGDBE shows a weakly solvating effect, thus extruding anions into the inner-layer solvation sheath and restricting their diffusion. For another, TTE is prone to immobilize free TFSI– in the outer-layer solvation shell instead of compressing Li+-TFSI– coordination to form a compact solvation structure. In stark contrast, LSSE exhibits both a much larger DA and slope than that of other electrolyte systems, especially at high temperatures, suggestive of its loose solvation configuration with abundant free anions, which will deteriorate the quality of the CEI layer and interfacial stability. Moreover, EGDBE-based electrolytes display an apparently smaller 19F T1 value in comparison with DME-based electrolytes, especially at 70 °C (Supplementary Figs. 13, 14), indicative of a slower tumbling rate of 19F nucleus. This further confirms that EGDBE serving as an effective CS can maintain compact solvation structure and enhance Li+-TFSI– coordination in Li-ion inner-layer solvation sheath, thus affording A-CSSE with high thermal stability and reduced anion reactivity30. In this case, the motion of TFSI– can be suppressed into large solvation clusters instead of isolated and small CIPs or AGGs. Besides, LSSE exhibits a much higher 19F T1 as compared to that of the other three electrolytes at each temperature, suggesting that most of anions are in a free state without effective immobilization and constraint. Such a loose anion solvation structure might cause severe electrolyte decomposition and interfacial side reactions for HT and HV positive electrodes. In the temperature-viable fitting (Fig. 2e), A-CSSE and CSSE show both a smaller τc and lower Ea than that of DME-based electrolytes, which manifests that EGDBE not only restricts anion movement via squeezing Li+-TFSI– coordination to form large solvation clusters but also enables a low thermodynamic barrier for the local motion of anions, thus reducing anion activity for the formation of high-quality CEI layer and enhancing the thermal stability of HV positive electrodes. To enhance the reliability of the NMR analysis on electrolyte anion solvation structure from our multimodal 19F NMR test technique, four control electrolytes via replacing EGDBE with other isomeric long-chain ether solvents were prepared and tested (Supplementary Figs. 15–18 and Supplementary Note 1). Electrolytes were then subjected to comprehensive analysis on their chemical interaction, solvation structure, and diffusion dynamics based on the as-obtained 19F NMR results (Supplementary Notes 2, 3), which align well with the effective immobilization and compression of anionic solvation configuration in A-CSSE.

2D NMR correlation spectroscopy visualizes the intermolecular interaction between various electrolyte components as well as its evolution with the change of temperatures, wherein the interaction strength can be quantitatively compared by the integration analysis of corresponding cross peaks (Supplementary Figs. 19, 20)31. 19F–19F correlation spectroscopy (COSY) was employed to study the interaction between anion and AS (Fig. 2f), which can be reflected by four cross peaks. By integrating the peak intensities (I), it is found that the I(TFSI––TTE)/I(TFSI––TFSI–) is increased from 25 °C to 70 °C, indicative of the enhanced anion-AS interaction at high temperatures. 19F–1H heteronuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (HOESY) was employed to investigate the interaction between TFSI– fluorine and EGDBE/TTE protons. As displayed in Fig. 2g, I(TFSI––TTE) is always lower than I(TFSI––EGDBE), especially at high temperatures, which manifests that the strong affinity between TTE and TFSI– mainly originates from the weak Van der Waals force of F–F intermolecular interaction32,33. In other words, electron-withdrawing F-containing groups of TTE are prone to interact with TFSI– to form a stable anion-AS complex. 19F–7Li HOESY NMR spectra probes chemical interaction between Li+ and the fluorine atoms of TFSI–/TTE (Fig. 2h). It is well accepted that the motion of ions and molecules is accelerated at high temperatures. However, low I(Li+–TTE) at 70 °C suggests that Li+–TTE interaction remains very weak, which is consistent with foregoing RDF results. In conclusion, our multimodal 19F NMR technique precisely confirms the solvation structure of A-CSSE. For one thing, EGDBE with extend alkyl chain enables a compact solvation structure with large solvation clusters, where more anions are compressed into inner-layer solvation sheath and coordinated with Li ions. For another, TTE enables a strong affinity with TFSI– via F–F intermolecular interaction to immobilize free anions, thus elevating electrolyte oxidation stability at a relatively low concentration. Hence, thermally stable A-CSSE is anticipated to simultaneously enhance anti-oxidation ability and sustain interfacial stability for HT and HV Li metal batteries.

Study of CEI layer on NCM811 positive electrode

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images were collected to disclose the structural features of CEI layers formed on the cycled NMC811 positive electrodes at different temperatures (Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Fig. 21)34. Note that positive electrodes that are extracted from the batteries after 100 cycles at X °C in A-CSSE are denoted as A-CSSE-X. Both LSSE-55 and LSSE-70 exhibit uneven CEI morphologies with a thickness over 20 nm, which is caused by severe decomposition of DME solvents. This suggests that the loose solvation structure rich in free solvents and anions will give rise to inferior oxidation stability of electrolytes. On the contrary, thin and uniform CEI layers can be visualized in A-CSSE-55, indicative of suppressed electrolyte decomposition and interfacial side reactions. It is therefore concluded that a compact solvation structure with large solvation clusters in A-CSSE enables high compatibility with HV positive electrodes, thus promoting the formation of a high-quality CEI layer with low interfacial resistance. Consequently, A-CSSE-70 still ensures the high homogeneity of CEI layer owing to the low interfacial reactivity of solvents and restricted anion movement in compact solvation structure. Furthermore, the interfacial resistance of NCM811 was probed by in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests and distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 24), which reveals the interfacial chemistry after the long-term cycling process at high temperatures35. Consequently, four relaxation processes were resolved and characterized by the logarithm of time constants (log(τ)), pertaining to the contact resistance (Rcon), CEI resistance (RCEI), charge transfer resistance (Rct), and diffusion resistance (Rd). With respect to RCEI and Rct, A-CSSE exhibits more reversible relaxation processes and much lower peak intensity in comparison with LSSE at both 55 °C and 70 °C (Fig. 3e–h and Supplementary Fig. 25), again confirming high interfacial stability and kinetically favorable Li-ion migration on NCM811 positive electrodes. Besides, the Rcon of A-CSSE is evidently lower than that of LSSE, suggesting that LSSE suffers from severe decomposition and consumption of solvents after long-term operation in HT working environments. In conclusion, benefiting from the large size and anion-compressed solvation structure, A-CSSE possesses good thermal durability and affords a high-quality CEI layer to enhance the interfacial stability of HT and HV positive electrodes.

It is well established that anion-derived CEI is crucial to preventing electrolyte decomposition and transition metal (TM) dissolution. In this respect, the spatial distribution of various chemical composites was studied by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)36. A higher ratio of F along with a lower C proportion is observed in A-CSSE-55 than that in LSSE-55 at etching times of 2 and 5 min (Supplementary Figs. 26, 27 and Supplementary Table 3), suggesting that enhanced Li+-TFSI– coordination facilitates the preferential decomposition of anions to form a LiF-rich inner layer in the CEI. As shown in the high-resolution XPS F 1 s spectra (Fig. 3i, j), a higher content of LiF is formed in A-CSSE-55 as compared to that of LSSE-55 after the sputtering process, again confirming the promoted decomposition of anions to generate an inorganic-rich CEI layer. Note that the C–F peak exhibits a slight shift upon the sputtering treatment due to the possible surface reconstruction of the CEI layer37. In the meantime, B–F peak can only be detected for A-CSSE at an etching time of 5 min, which is attributed to the preferential adsorption of LiDFOB additive on the positive electrode accompanied by the formation of BOF2− product. However, it can barely be visualized for LSSE since the decomposition of free DME solvents is accelerated at high temperatures. The strong anti-oxidation ability and high mechanical strength of LiF are conducive to protecting NCM811 from irreversible structural deterioration. Besides, C=O and C–O signals of LSSE-55 are apparently higher than those in A-CSSE-55 (Supplementary Fig. 28), which results from unstable organic components that are detrimental to the reversibility of NCM811 positive electrodes. A higher C–F peak intensity can be observed in LSSE-55 prior to sputtering, suggesting that the as-formed low-quality CEI layer struggles to alleviate interfacial side reactions. Overall, thin, inorganic-rich and uniform CEI layers can be achieved with the aid of synchronous management of AS and CS, thus sustaining the interfacial stability for HT and HV Li metal batteries. The time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectroscopy (ToF-SIMS) was further used to confirm the components and distribution of CEI. As shown in Fig. 3k, l and Supplementary Fig. 29, the LiF2– signal intensity representing inorganic species is higher and more uniform in A-CSSE-55 as compared to that in LSSE-55, especially for the inner layer of CEI, while CO3– and C2HO– signals of organic components exhibit an opposite trend. Besides, the signal of CoO– is much more apparent in LSSE-55 through the entire CEI layer, indicative of severe TM dissolution. Note that A-CSSE promotes the formation of desired BO2– species, especially in the upper layer of CEI, which is attributed to the preferential adsorption and decomposition of trace LiDFOB additive on the surface of positive electrodes. However, the content of BO2– is reduced in LSSE due to high interfacial reactivity and severe solvent decomposition. With respect to XPS F 1s and C 1s spectra along with ToF-SIMS 3D spatial distribution of A-CSSE-70, LiF is still dominant in the inner layer of CEI (Supplementary Fig. 30). Supplementary Table 4 summarizes the thickness of CEI layers formed in various LHCEs at high temperatures, which is apparently larger than that generated in A-CSSE. Taken together, it is concluded that our anionic solvation reconstruction strategy is beneficial to achieving thin and inorganic-rich CEI layers in HT working environments, which is attributed to the large solvation cluster and effective anion immobilization in electrolyte solvation structure (Supplementary Fig. 31).

Study of localized NCM811 structure

Structural integrity of NCM811 positive electrodes after long-term cycling at high temperatures was further probed by morphological characterizations. Focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) images show that A-CSSE-55 can maintain the structural stability of NCM811 without the formation of cracks and holes (Fig. 4a, e and Supplementary Figs. 32, 33). In stark contrast, some intergranular cracks can be visualized inside NCM811 particles for LSSE-55, which are detrimental to battery performance. As shown in Fig. 4b, high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) unveils that interfacial phase transition is alleviated in A-CSSE-55, thus rendering a thin and uniform rock-salt layer with a thickness of ~1.2 nm38,39. In the meanwhile, the layered structure is well kept, thus suppressing stress accumulation during long cyclic operation (Fig. 4c). Even at a harsher working temperature of 70 °C, such intact structure is still preserved with a slightly thicker rock-salt layer ( ~2 nm) on the surface of NCM811 positive electrodes (Fig. 4d), which is mainly attributed to the high thermal stability of A-CSSE and robust CEI layer. Electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) was conducted from the outermost surface to the bulk of NCM811 particles (Fig. 4i–l and Supplementary Fig. 34), which can reflect the properties of lattice oxygen as well as the changes in chemical states of TM ions40. As shown in O–K edge spectra, the appearance of a pre-peak (peak i) around 532 eV suggests the formation of lattice oxygen defects, which can be attributed to the rock-salt layer on the surface of NCM811. In this respect, this peak can be observed from the 7th EELS spectrum in A-CSSE-70, which is apparently earlier than that of LSSE-70. This indicates that a much thinner and inorganic-rich CEI layer is generated in A-CSSE-70, thus reducing lattice oxygen loss and enhancing interfacial stability. Besides, the energy loss difference of A-CSSE between the pre-edge and the main-edge peak is larger than that of LSSE-70 in the rock-salt layer region, confirming the suppressed Ni4+ reduction to lower valence states at the positive electrode surface. With respect to the Ni L-edge spectra, the peaks of L3 and L2 can be visualized at higher energy loss for LSSE-70, accompanied by a more obvious shift to lower energy loss from the surface to the bulk, which manifests the severe reduction of TM ions and formation of mixed phases. In the meanwhile, as for LSSE-70, Ni L3/L2 intensity ratio is significantly decreased in both the surface and bulk structure, indicative of an increased proportion of low-valence Ni species. No doubt the as-derived Li+ deficiency will aggravate the structural integrity and cycling performance of NCM811 positive electrodes. Collectively, the EELS results strongly confirm that the compact solvation structure with anion-compressed large-size clusters of A-CSSE plays a key role in stabilizing interfacial chemistry and enhancing structural stability of NCM811 positive electrodes in HT working environments.

Mechanisms of A-CSSE on stabilizing NCM811 positive electrode

H-transfer reaction energy (ΔE) was calculated to study the oxidation tolerance of solvents41. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 35, ΔE values follow a sequence of DME < EGDBE < TTE, manifesting that DME is energetically favorable for oxidative parasitic reactions on HV positive electrodes. This aligns well with the foregoing calculation results of energy levels, again confirming that both EGDBE and TTE are conducive to enhancing thermal stability of electrolytes and alleviating oxidation decomposition at high charging voltages. Furthermore, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulation was employed to reveal the dynamic migration of electrolytes on positive electrodes. Under a positive potential that represents charging states, it is visualized that Li+ moves away from the positive electrode while TFSI– tends to accumulate onto the positive electrode due to the Coulombic force with the increase of times (Fig. 5a, b, Supplementary Fig. 36 and Supplementary Data 2). More interestingly, TTE achieves a co-migration effect along with TFSI– while the distribution of EGDBE almost keeps the same. This corroborates that TTE as an AS possesses strong chemical interaction with anions, which enhances the anti-oxidation ability of electrolytes and facilitates the formation of LiF-rich CEI. With respect to LSSE, a multitude of free DME solvents stay closer to the positive electrode as compared to TTE and EGDBE in A-CSSE driven by a positive potential (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Data 3), which would accumulate into the IHL and deteriorate the positive electrode stability. Besides, EGDBE is prone to move away from positive electrodes with TFSI– under a negative potential (Supplementary Fig. 37), thus alleviating the aggregation of free active solvents. In stark contrast, DME is still close to the positive electrode due to the small solvation cluster without effective anion immobilization, thus causing severe solvent decomposition. In situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) test was carried out at 55 °C to investigate the structural evolution of NCM811 positive electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 38). As displayed in Fig. 5e, f, upon the charging process, the (003) peak shifts toward lower angles and reaches the minimum 2θ value. This is attributed to the extended lattice distance by electrostatic repulsion. Subsequently, the shrinkage of the lattice c-axis by the H2-H3 phase transition leads to a fast shift of the (003) peak to higher angles until the end of the charging process, which is crucial to the structural integrity of HV positive electrodes. It can be observed that with respect to LSSE, the angular shift of the (003) peak for the H2-H3 phase transition process is apparently larger than that of A-CSSE, suggesting that LSSE suffers from severe phase transition in the positive electrode bulk due to the absence of a high-quality CEI layer. Similar trends of large volume change can also be visualized for the (104) peak in LSSE, which might trigger the formation of cracks and distortions in NCM811 positive electrodes, thus giving rise to irreversible capacity loss. Encouragingly, A-CSSE enables smaller structural change as well as more reversible phase transition during the complete charge/discharge process at 55 °C, which is anticipated to sustain smooth cycling performance of HT Li | |NCM811 batteries.

Quantification of oxidative parasitic reaction

Quantification of oxidative parasitic reactions at the positive electrode is crucial to digging deep into the underlying interfacial mechanism. The TM dissolution from the positive electrode into the electrolyte could deteriorate the structural stability and electrochemical performance of Li||NCM811 batteries, which was probed by quantitative inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 39, the TM contents in A-CSSE are apparently lower than those in LSSE after 100 cycles at 55 °C, while the Ni content is reduced by nearly 4 times. This result suggests that the dissolution of TM ions is well suppressed by A-CSSE owing to the as-formed high-quality CEI layer. In situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) test was conducted to detect the release of lattice oxygen, which plays a key role in the degradation of NCM811 positive electrode structure42. Impressively, A-CSSE achieves complete inhibition of oxygen formation while postponing the CO2 emission to 4.42 V (Fig. 6a). On the contrary, LSSE undergoes severe CO2 and O2 release starting at 4.36 V with high gas concentration (Fig. 6b), thus leading to rapid structural collapse during the cycling process. Thermal oxidation stability of the electrolytes was evaluated in floating current test of Li | |NMC811 batteries by holding certain charging voltages for 10 hours at 55 °C and 70 °C43. As displayed in Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 40, LSSE shows an evidently higher leakage current density than A-CSSE at all voltages while it is sharply increased from an earlier voltage stage and continuously augmented, indicative of severe electrolyte decomposition on the positive electrode. A quantitative NMR (qNMR) test was further performed to study the consumption of anions and solvents in the cycled electrolytes, which is of vital importance to the cyclic lifetime of HT Li||NCM811 batteries. Note that 2,4-dichlorobenzotrifluoride (DCBF) as the external reference exhibits only one 19F NMR peak at –61.2 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 41). It is close to that of TFSI– yet without the interference, thus enabling feasible phase adjustment and peak integration for NMR data analysis. Note that the I(TFSI–)/I(DCBF) values of pristine A-CSSE and LSSE are almost the same (Fig. 6d), indicative of high reliability of our qNMR method. Based on the integration results of 19F and 1H NMR spectra after different cycles (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Figs. 42, 43), the consumption rate of TFSI– and DME/EGDBE was calculated (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 44). A-CSSE shows a higher retention rate of both anions and solvents than that of LSSE, especially after 100 cycles, which suggests that A-CSSE has a high thermal oxidation stability owing to its compact and anion-compressed solvation structure, thus aiding in extending the cycling lifespan of HT Li||NCM811 batteries. The underlying mechanism of A-CSSE on stabilizing HT and HV positive electrodes is displayed in Fig. 6g, h. At the charging stage, TFSI– will move to the positive electrode surface accompanied by TTE due to the strong anchoring effect. Such an anion-AS complex harnesses high oxidation stability to alleviate interfacial side reactions, which contributes to a thin and even CEI layer as well as a crack-free positive electrode structure with less phase transition. In parallel, EGDBE enables a compact solvation structure with compressed Li+-anion coordination and a large solvation cluster, thus inhibiting both electrolyte consumption and gas formation. In contrast, LSSE with a loose anion solvation structure undergoes severe DME decomposition, rapid anion consumption, and aggravated gas formation at high temperatures, which also suffers from a low-quality CEI layer and severe phase transition. Thus, NCM811 positive electrodes would undergo fast structural degradation and capacity decay.

Electrochemical performance of HT Li | |NCM811 batteries

Except for a stable CEI layer, a highly reversible Li negative electrode is also vital to the cycling performance of Li||NCM811 batteries44,45. In this respect, precipitated Li metal at 55 °C exhibits distinct morphologies in A-CSSE and LSSE (Supplementary Fig. 45). Uniform and dense Li can be observed in A-CSSE, which is mainly attributed to the anion-rich solvation sheath. On the contrary, deposited Li in LSSE is loose and porous with a multitude of Li dendrites, which will damage the reversibility of Li plating/stripping and curtail the cyclic lifetime of Li metal batteries. Moreover, cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) discloses the uniform and thin solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formed in A-CSSE, while that formed in LSSE is uneven and thick (Supplementary Fig. 46), suggesting that A-CSSE with enhanced Li+-anion coordination improves the quality of the SEI layer and stabilizes the Li metal negative electrode. XPS was further used to uncover the composition and distribution of SEI formed in different electrolytes while XPS spectra were recorded prior to and after 2-min sputtering treatment (Supplementary Fig. 47). For one thing, as displayed in high-resolution XPS O 1 s and F 1 s spectra, more inorganic components (i.e., Li2O and LiF) can be observed in A-CSSE-55. For another, the proportion of organic species (i.e., C–O and O–C=O) content in LSSE-55 is much higher than that in A-CSSE-55, further corroborating that A-CSSE is conducive to generating inorganic-rich SEI and thus improving the reversibility of the Li metal negative electrode. Besides, A-CSSE exhibits a lower Li-ion desolvation barrier than that of LSSE due to the weakened ion-dipole interaction (Supplementary Figs. 48, 49), thus enabling the formation of an anion-rich inner-layer solvation sheath. Consequently, higher average Coulombic efficiency in Li-Cu half cells as well as longer cycling lifetime in Li||Li symmetrical cells can be achieved at 55 °C (Supplementary Figs. 50, 51). In conclusion, A-CSSE with compressed Li+-anion coordination and large-size solvation clusters enhances the reversibility of the Li metal negative electrode at high temperatures.

Furthermore, Li | |NCM811 batteries were assembled and tested with a cut-off voltage of 4.3 V to verify the effectiveness of our anionic solvation reconstruction strategy. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 52, both CSSE and LSSE without the regulation of TTE additive exhibit an evidently lower capacity along with a lower capacity retention rate as compared to that of TTE-containing electrolytes at 0.2 C and 55 °C. In parallel, although A-LSSE possesses a comparable initial capacity with A-CSSE, it suffers from inferior cycling stability due to loose solvation structure and high interfacial reactivity. It is therefore concluded that synchronous AS and CS regulation is conducive to enhancing interfacial stability and sustaining smooth operation of HT Li||NCM811 batteries. As expected, A-CSSE also enables much higher cycling stability in comparison with LSSE at 1 C (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 53), whose capacity retention reaches 96.5% over 100 cycles (Supplementary Table 5). In terms of the differential capacity-voltage (dQ/dV) analysis, the peak assigned to the phase transitions from H2 to H3 under a high voltage regime exhibits a high overlap for A-CSSE with different cycles while the peak shift of LSSE is quite large (Fig. 7b, c). This result is consistent with the foregoing in situ XRD detection, which is indicative of the alleviated electrode polarization and good structural integrity of NCM811 positive electrodes with the management of A-CSSE during long cycling process46. As demonstrated in rate performance (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Fig. 54), for one thing, A-CSSE affords a higher capacity than that of LSSE especially at high current densities. For another, the capacity can be readily recovered when the current density is switched back to 0.2 C. As for galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) test at 55 °C (Supplementary Fig. 55), A-CSSE enables a lower diffusion overpotential of 41.8 mV than that (44.7 mV) of LSSE, implying more favorable Li-ion diffusion in a well-maintained layered NCM811 structure. Even though the positive electrode loading is increased to 10 mg cm–2, stable cycling performance is achieved by A-CSSE over 80 cycles while complete failure is observed for LSSE after 50 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 56). It is worth nothing that routine LHCE (r-LHCE) based on DME solvent can maintain the normal operation of Li||NCM811 batteries for only 15 cycles with fast capacity decay at 70 °C (Supplementary Fig. 57), suggesting that electrolytes with highly active solvent and small solvation clusters are unable to sustain interfacial stability at high temperatures. In the meanwhile, only 43.8% of the initial capacity can be retained at the 120th cycle by LSSE (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 58), which is mainly attributed to the exacerbated CEI layer and degraded structure of NCM811 positive electrodes at high temperatures. In stark contrast, A-CSSE bestows Li||NCM811 batteries with a high capacity retention rate of 76.3% at 0.2 C over 120 cycles owing to its high interfacial and structural stability. Such a high cyclic stability at 70 °C is further reflected at 1 C (Supplementary Fig. 59), which delivers a capacity retention of 80 % over 100 cycles. When elevating the cut-off voltages to 4.4 V, the cyclic stability of A-CSSE is still higher than that of LSSE (Supplementary Fig. 60). In this respect, A-CSSE maintains the smooth cycling of Li | |NCM811 batteries with a cut-off voltage of 4.5 V while normal operation can barely be obtained by LSSE, again confirming high compatibility of A-CSSE with HT and HV positive electrodes. Impressively, stable cycling performance can be obtained by A-CSSE at both 25 °C and 80 °C (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Figs. 61, 62), demonstrative of good wide-temperature adaptability of A-CSSE for achieving practical application. HV calendar aging performance plays a pivotal role in achieving practical application. In this respect, fully charged Li||NCM811 batteries were rested for 60 hours at 55 °C, which were then fully discharged to evaluate the reversible capacity. Encouragingly, A-CSSE maintains a steady reversible voltage of 4.3 V over long storage over 500 h, accompanied by a 78% capacity retention (Fig. 7g). On the contrary, LSSE suffers from a rapid voltage decay to below 4 V after only 300 hours along with low capacity retention, manifesting inferior calendar aging performance under HT and HV working conditions. More impressively, when respectively replacing EGDBE and TTE with another CS (diethylene glycol dibutyl ether, denoted as DEGDBE) and AS (1H,1H,5H-octafluoropentyl 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether, denoted as OTE) with higher boiling points above 140 °C, Li||NCM811 batteries equipped with upgraded A-CSSE (DEGDBE/OTE) can support smooth operation at 100 °C (Fig. 7h and Supplementary Fig. 64), suggestive of good universality and versatility of our electrolyte design strategy. In conclusion, A-CSSE with the synergistic regulation of CS and AS compares favorably with LSSE and r-LHCE regarding various aspects (Supplementary Fig. 65). To further showcase the potential of A-CSSE in achieving practical HT and HV Li metal batteries, a 1.2 Ah-level Li||NCM811 pouch cell was fabricated and tested in harsh working conditions of lean electrolyte usage and limited Li source (Supplementary Table 6), which readily delivers a specific energy of 317 Wh kg–1 based on the total mass of the complete pouch cell and retains 90% capacity after 50 cycles at 55 °C (Fig. 7i and Supplementary Figs. 66, 67). More encouragingly, A-CSSE still enables the smooth operation of pouch cells at elevated temperatures of 80 °C (Supplementary Fig. 68), again confirming its strong thermal anti-oxidation ability and stable interphasial chemistry. Accelerating rate calorimetry (ARC) was employed to evaluate the thermal safety of Li||NCM811 pouch cells, which can detect the heat generation in fully charged cells. Note that the onset temperature of exothermic reactions (TOER) and thermal runaway temperature (TTR) were obtained at a heating rate of 0.1 °C min–1 and 10 °C min–1. Encouragingly, A-CSSE enables a higher TOER of 199 °C as compared to that (171 °C) of LSSE (Fig. 7j). Moreover, it could maintain a thermally stable state of the pouch cell over 10 h around 150 °C without battery failure. With respect to the TTR, A-CSSE increases this value from 228 °C to 296 °C as compared to LSSE (Fig. 7k), which is mainly attributed to the high thermal stability of EDGBE and TTE as well as a stable anion-compressed solvation structure. Furthermore, a nail penetration test was conducted to study combustion behaviors under mechanical abuse. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 69, a fully charged pouch cell with A-CSSE exhibits good structural integrity after the test. On the contrary, continuous combustion can be visualized for LSSE upon the test. In this respect, a multitude of flammable gases are ignited by tremendous Joule heat from the volume expansion and internal short circuit of pouch cells. In the meantime, the cell’s surface temperature containing LSSE is suddenly increased to 600 °C at 40 s while A-CSSE maintains a smooth temperature within the 2-min test process (Supplementary Fig. 70). The above results again corroborate the high thermal stability of A-CSSE in pouch cell configuration, which is anticipated to avoid safety hazards and achieve practical application in HT working environments.

Discussion

In summary, we elucidated the origin of low oxidation stability for ether-based electrolytes and designed a thermally stable A-CSSE via an anionic solvation reconstruction strategy. Temperature-dependent evolution of anion solvation chemistry was unveiled by a multimodal 19F NMR technique, which was further correlated to the interfacial and structural properties of HT and HV NCM811 positive electrodes. It is revealed that under the synchronous regulation of AS and CS, both the oxidation stability of the anion-solvent complex and Li+-anion coordination are enhanced, which accordingly affords thin, homogeneous, and inorganic-rich CEI while alleviating electrolyte consumption and TM dissolution. Consequently, Li||NCM811 batteries with A-CSSE obtain stable cycling performance within 25–100 °C while the an as-fabricated pouch cell delivers a specific energy of 317 Wh kg–1 based on the total mass of the complete pouch cell and achieves high thermal safety and stable cycling performance. The proposed solvent screening strategy and multimodal 19F NMR technique in our study would play a crucial role in designing better electrolytes for wide-temperature Li metal batteries.

Methods

Materials of electrolyte

All solvents and salts used in this work were purchased from Suzhou DoDochem Co., Ltd. All solvents were dried with 4 Å molecular sieves for at least 24 hours before electrolyte preparation. DME (DoDoChem, 99.9% purity, 20 ppm H2O max), EGDBE (DoDoChem, 99.8% purity, 20 ppm H2O max), DEGDBE (DoDoChem, 99.9% purity, 20 ppm H2O max), TTE (DoDoChem, 99.8% purity, 20 ppm H2O max), OTE (DoDoChem, 99.9% purity, 20 ppm H2O max), LiTFSI (DoDoChem, 99.9 % purity, 100 ppm H2O max), and LiDFOB (DoDoChem, 99.9 % purity, 100 ppm H2O max).

Preparation of NCM811 electrode

For the slurry preparation, single-crystal NCM811 powder MA-EN-CA-0052 (90 wt%, Canrud, 99.9%), Ketjenblack ECP600JD (5 wt%, DoDoChem, 99.9%) and poly (vinylidene fluoride) 5310 (5 wt%, DoDoChem, 99.9%, molecular weight: ~1 million) were mixed and ball-milled with the appropriate addition of N-Methylpyrrolidone (DoDoChem, 99.9%) as the solvent. The as-obtained homogeneous slurry was then cast onto a piece of clean Al foil (16 µm) via doctor blade. Finally, it was dried at 80 °C for 12 hours under vacuum environment to remove the water and oxygen residue. The as-prepared electrode was cut into small disks with a diameter of 12 mm prior to the battery assembly. Note that the routine loading in a typical electrode is ca. 3 mg cm−2 and that in a high-loading positive electrode is ca. 10 mg cm−2.

GITT measurement

GITT curves were collected at 55 °C after 100 cycles at 55 °C by applying a pulse current of 0.3 C for 10 min with a time interval of 90 min within a voltage range of 2.7–4.3 V.

In-situ EIS measurement

Li||NCM811 batteries were pre-cycled at 55 °C or 70 °C at 1 C for 100 cycles. In situ EIS test was conducted with a current density of 100 mA at multiple voltage stages (from 3.7 V charged to 4.3 V and then discharged to 3.7 V with an interval of 0.1 V per test voltage) during a complete charge and discharge process under the same temperatures.

LSV measurement

The oxidation stability of the electrolytes was investigated by LSV test at 55 °C. A clean Al foil was used as the working electrode, and Li metal was used as both the reference and counter electrodes. The scan rate was set to 0.2 mV s–1.

PITT measurement

PITT test was operated by galvanostatically charging Li||NCM811 batteries to 4.2 V with a current density of 0.1 C, then the voltage was held for 10 hours and gradually increased with a 0.1 V interval per step for 10 hours until the voltage reached 5 V to study the leakage current.

Characterizations

Raman spectra were collected by the Oxford WITec Alpha300 R spectrometer. XRD patterns were collected by the Bruker D8 Phaser. 19F-7Li solution-state NMR HOESY experiments were obtained on a 900 MHz Bruker AVANCE III wide-bore NMR spectrometer equipped with a 3.2 mm H/F-X CPMAS probe. 19F-1H HOESY and 19F-19F COSY NMR experiments were obtained on a 600 MHz Bruker AVANCE III wide-bore NMR spectrometer equipped with a BBFO probe. XPS spectra were acquired using a Kratos Axis Ultra DLD spectrometer, which is equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.6 eV) and operates at 75 W. ToF-SIMS analysis was performed using a PHI nanoTOF II system. The Cryo-TEM images of SEI and HRTEM images of CEI were collected using a Titan 300 TEM. For STEM-HAADF imaging of NCM811 structure, FIB lamellas were prepared for each sample using a Helios G4 (ThermoFisher™) dual-beam microscope. SEM images of deposited Li were obtained on a FEI Magellan 400 XHR. Cycled Li||NCM811 cells were disassembled in an argon-filled glovebox and the electrodes were rinsed with DME. Thus, NCM811 powders were scraped from the Al foil and subjected to TEM imaging. WAXS measurement was performed on a Xenocs Xeuss 3.0 high-resolution X-ray scattering system using a Genix3D CuKα radiation source at wavelength λ = 1.542 Å (50 kV, 60 mA). ICP-MS test was conducted on NexION350X. In situ DEMS measurement was operated on Linglu Instruments, Shanghai, QAS 100Li.

The final molar mass of the remaining anions and solvents after the cycling process can be obtained by multiplying the original qNMR results by 3.

Electrochemical tests

CR2032 coin-type cells were used to test the electrochemical performance of Li||NCM811 batteries, which were assembled with NCM811 electrodes, Li metal foil without special treatment (DoDoChem; a piece; thickness: 300 μm; diameter: 16 mm), and Celgard 2500 (DoDoChem; a piece; thickness: 25 μm; diameter: 19 mm; porosity: 55%; average pore size: 65 nm) serving as the positive electrode, negative electrode, and separator, respectively. 50 µL of electrolyte was added to each coin cell with a micropipette. 3.6 g of electrolyte was injected into each pouch cell with a pipette, which was vacuum-sealed at 150 °C under –90 kPa. A constant external pressure of 0.35 MPa was applied during cycling. LSV, EIS, and current–time profiles were recorded on the Bio-logic VMP3 electrochemical workstation. The frequency in the EIS measurement was kept within the region of 300,000 − 0.01 Hz with an amplitude of 10 mV (6 points per decade). Before carrying out the EIS measurement, the cells were rested for 6 hours. GCD, GITT, and rate/cycling curves were achieved on the NEWARE Battery Test System (CT-4008T-5V50mA-164, Shenzhen, China) within a certain electrochemical voltage range. All the electrochemical tests at 25, 55, 70, 80, and 100 °C were conducted in temperature-controlled chambers (Neware MGW-150-B-220V).

Computational details

All DFT calculations were performed to determine the ESP of molecules, binding energy, and energy levels of the HOMO and LUMO. All calculations were carried out using the ωB97X-D/6-31 + G** level of theory with the Gaussian 16 (Revision C.02) software package47.

Classical MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2023.1 to investigate the coordination behavior of Li⁺ ions48. Force-field parameters were generated via Sobtop49, and Restrained Electrostatic Potential (RESP) charges were derived from DFT-optimized geometries using Multiwfn50. Initial configurations were prepared with Packmol51 based on experimental densities, followed by energy minimization for 10,000 steps using the steepest descent algorithm. Equilibration was performed in the NVT ensemble at 298 and 328 K for 1 ns, followed by the NPT ensemble at 298 and 328 K and 1 atm for 1 ns. Final production simulations were conducted in the NVT ensemble at 298 and 328 K for 20 ns.

AIMD simulations were performed for a slab geometry with an applied electric field (0.01 V/nm) consisting of solvent molecules, ion pairs, and a single LiNiO2 electrode slab (used as a simplified model for Ni-rich NCM811). The orthorhombic simulation box measured 5.1 × 4.9 × 30 nm, with the (001) surface of LiNiO2 exposed to the electrolyte. The Z-axis was defined as the surface normal, and periodic boundary conditions were applied only in the XY plane to eliminate spurious interactions along Z, and electrode atoms were kept fixed throughout the simulations. Initial configurations were generated with Packmol and minimized using 10,000 steps of the steepest descent algorithm, followed by equilibration in the NVT ensemble at 298 K for 1 ns and production simulations for 20 ns under the same conditions.

DFT calculations for modeling the H-transfer reaction were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)52. The PBE + U method, following the approach of Dudarev53, was used to account for the on-site Coulomb interactions among localized Ni 3 d electrons. Long-range dispersion interactions were included using the Grimme D3 semi-empirical correction with Becke–Johnson damping54,55. The U value (U = 2.5 eV) for Ni 3 d electrons was adopted from previous studies56. All atomic positions in the bulk and slab geometries were relaxed until the residual Hellmann–Feynman forces on each atom were below 0.02 eV Å⁻¹. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 400 eV and a Γ-centered 3 × 3 × 1 k-point mesh were used for Brillouin zone sampling. A 5 × 5 supercell (with a size of 14.3 Å × 14.3 Å) was employed for the calculations, with a sufficient vacuum (~20 Å) to separate the repeating slab.
