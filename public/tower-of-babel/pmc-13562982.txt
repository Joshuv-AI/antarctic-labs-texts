Longitudinal Assessment of Cognitive Development in 23 Patients With Mucopolysaccharidosis (MPS) Type II: Results of up to 14 Years of Follow‐Up
Authors: J. (Julia) Holdorp, M. E. (Michelle) Kruijshaar, A. A. M. (Audrey) Vollebregt, A. B. (Andre) Rietman, M. R. K. (Marianne) Dijkstra, C. (Carina) Klees, M. (Margreet) Wagenmakers, A. H. P. (Annie) Nguyen, E. (Esmee) Oussoren, A. T. (Ans) van der Ploeg, J. M. P. (Hannerieke) van den Hout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study investigated long‐term cognitive development and genotype–phenotype relationships in patients with Mucopolysaccharidosis Type II (MPS II). A nationwide prospective cohort study was conducted in the Netherlands with cognitive follow‐up since 2007. Patients were classified as neuronopathic or non‐neuronopathic based on iduronate‐2‐sulphatase (IDS) genotype; novel variants based on age and intelligence quotient (IQ). IQ and Mental Age (MA) were analysed individually and, using linear mixed‐effect models, at group level to compare trajectories by genotype and phenotype. Twenty‐three male patients (22 children, 1 adult) underwent 136 cognitive assessments, beginning at a median age of 2.9 years with a median follow‐up of 6.3 (range 0–13.5) years. IQ and MA trajectories significantly differed between neuronopathic and non‐neuronopathic patients (p < 0.001). Non‐neuronopathic patients (n = 5) showed normal or mildly impaired cognition. Neuronopathic patients (n = 18) initially developed normally, then stagnated, plateaued, and declined; IQ fell below 70 at a median age of 4 years. Patients with deletions (n = 3) experienced the earliest, most severe impairment (p < 0.001), while those with other IDS variants showed more variable trajectories. Neurocognitive patterns diverge early in MPS II, highlighting the importance of understanding genotype‐specific developmental trajectories. This insight is essential for guiding timely brain‐targeted interventions, ideally initiated before neurocognitive decline begins.

Introduction

Mucopolysaccharidosis Type II (MPS II; Hunter syndrome; OMIM 309900) is a rare X‐linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the iduronate‐2‐sulphatase (IDS) gene. Deficiency of the resulting enzyme leads to accumulation of the glycosaminoglycans heparan sulphate and dermatan sulphate in connective tissues and organs [1, 2]. Clinical manifestations include coarse facial features, hepatosplenomegaly, skeletal, respiratory, and auditory dysfunction, and dysfunction of the peripheral and central nervous system. The clinical spectrum is broad, but is generally divided into a non‐neuronopathic and neuronopathic form, depending on neurological involvement. Non‐neuronopathic patients exhibit normal cognitive development, whereas neuronopathic patients experience progressive neurocognitive decline, behavioural problems, and sleeping disturbances, and rarely survive into adulthood without treatment. Neurodevelopment in neuronopathic patients typically follows four phases: normal development, stagnation with slower skill acquisition, plateau with cessation of mental age advancement, and eventual decline with skill regression and decreasing mental age [2, 3, 4, 5].

Over 720 pathogenetic IDS variants have been identified, including deletions, missense, nonsense, and frameshift variants [6]. Deletions and frameshift variants are typically associated with the neuronopathic phenotype, while missense variants may occur in either form [7, 8, 9, 10]. Although genotype correlates to phenotype, reliable genotype–phenotype prediction in MPS II is often not possible because many disease‐causing variants are private mutations with uncertain phenotypic consequences. This limited predictive ability has been a major challenge for early clinical decision‐making, including newborn screening. Therefore, the course of cognitive decline requires further systematic study.

Intravenous enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with recombinant human IDS has been available since 2007. ERT improves somatic symptoms, including pulmonary function, organomegaly, joint mobility, walking distance, and quality of life [3, 11]. However, the enzyme does not cross the blood–brain barrier and therefore cannot prevent neurocognitive decline [12]. Therapies targeting the central nervous system (CNS), such as gene therapy and immunoglobulin G‐IDS fusion proteins, are in development [13]. As an example, tividenofusp alfa was recently approved following a Phase 1/2 clinical trial [14, 15]. Evaluating such therapies requires detailed understanding of cognitive trajectories in untreated brains and variation by IDS genotype. Data remain limited—especially in young children—and few studies have investigated genotype–phenotype variation [4, 16, 17].

Following the introduction of ERT, a nationwide prospective follow‐up study of Dutch MPS II patients was initiated. We previously reported cognitive outcomes for 15 patients up to 2016 [9]. This expanded study includes more and younger patients and longer follow‐up. Our aim was to characterise the onset and course of cognitive decline in MPS II at both individual and group levels, in relation to IDS genotype. This knowledge is essential to understand the relationship between IDS variants and neurocognitive development, and to inform the design and interpretation of trials involving emerging brain‐targeted therapies.

Methods

Participants and Data Collection

The Center for Lysosomal and Metabolic Diseases at Erasmus Medical Center, Rotterdam, is the Dutch reference centre for MPS II. All patients are enrolled in a standardised, institutional research board‐approved follow‐up programme including cognitive assessments. Written informed consent was obtained from patients and/or their legal representatives. For this analysis, patients were included if they had at least one cognitive assessment through this programme since 2007 and had not received brain‐targeted treatments. Earlier or external assessments were included when available. Diagnosis of MPS II was confirmed via mutation analysis, reduced IDS activity in leukocytes or fibroblasts, and elevated urinary glycosaminoglycans. All patients except Patient 6 received weekly ERT with recombinant human iduronate‐2‐sulphatase (Elaprase, Takeda Pharmaceutical Company Limited, Japan, 0.5 mg/kg). Clinical data on sleep and behavioural problems at diagnosis, and hearing status at diagnosis and during follow‐up were collected from patient charts.

Phenotype classification was based on reported IDS variant effects (Table 2). For novel variants, patients aged ≥ 4 were classified as neuronopathic if their latest cognitive function score was below 70. Patients aged < 4 were classified as neuronopathic, as cognitive decline may not yet be apparent.

Cognitive assessments were conducted by neuropsychologists experienced in testing patients with cognitive delay (MD, CK). Tests included the Griffiths Mental Development Scale, Bayley Scales of Infant Development (Bayley‐II‐NL, Bayley‐III‐NL), Wechsler scales (WPPSI‐III, WISC‐III, WISC‐V, WAIS‐IV), Snijders‐Oomen Nonverbal Intelligence Test‐Revised (SON‐R), and Kaufman ABC (K‐ABC). Until 2017, the Griffiths scale was preferred. Thereafter, following international consensus, the Bayley and Wechsler scales became the tests of choice [19, 20]. Test selection was based on developmental age.

Data Analysis

Cognitive functioning was assessed using standardised intelligence tests, performed according to published manuals [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. These tests yielded either a full‐scale intelligence quotient (IQ) or a mental age (MA), or both, depending on the instrument and developmental level. Cognitive testing is limited by the floor effect: scores below the test minimum cannot differentiate between patients with varying abilities. When only MA was available (Griffiths, Bayley) and for floor‐level results, a developmental quotient (DQ) was calculated (DQ = MA/chronological age × 100), following the approach by Shapiro et al. in MPS I [27]. This allows us to better capture the lower end of development—where multiple MPS II patients are known to be. Although IQ and DQ are not fully equivalent, both provide standardised measures of global cognitive functioning and were analysed jointly as cognitive functioning scores to describe overall developmental trajectories. In healthy individuals, IQ is stable with a mean of 100 (range: 85–115) and MA aligns with chronological age (CA). Cognitive impairment was defined as IQ or DQ below 70 (> 2 SD below population mean) [28].

Cognitive functioning scores and MA trajectories in different phenotype and genotype groups were analysed using linear mixed‐effects models to account for repeated measurements (R v4.3.2, nlme package). For MA, both MA and CA were log‐transformed to approximate normality. Fixed effects included nonlinear age, using orthogonal quadratic polynomials, group, and their interaction. Cognitive function scores required no transformation. Fixed effects included a nonlinear effect of age using natural cubic splines with 2 degrees of freedom and boundary knots at 2.3 and 15.5 years, group, and their interaction. Both models included random intercepts and slopes. Fixed‐effects structures (i.e., the appropriate function of age, group, and interaction) were selected via F‐tests and likelihood ratio tests with Kenward‐Roger degrees of freedom. Model assumptions were validated using residual plots. Group differences in trajectories were assessed with likelihood ratio tests. Based on clinical interpretation and visual inspection of patient trajectories, comparisons were made between the phenotype groups and, within the neuronopathic group, between deletions and other IDS variants.

Results

Study Population

Figure 1 illustrates patient inclusion. Thirty male MPS II patients participated in our follow‐up programme, comprising 23 children and seven adults. Of these, 23 patients (22 children, one adult) met inclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion were absence of a cognitive test (n = 6) and stem cell transplantation (n = 1). Five patients were classified as non‐neuronopathic and 18 as neuronopathic. Patient characteristics are summarised in Table 1A,B. Median age at diagnosis was 3 years (range: 0–6) for all children, 3 years in the neuronopathic group (range: 0–6) and 3.5 years in the non‐neuronopathic group (range: 3–5). The adult was diagnosed at 44 years. Follow‐up duration varied considerably (median 6.3 years, range: 0–13.6). Six patients died during follow‐up: one non‐neuronopathic adult (cancer) and five neuronopathic children (MPS II complications).

Hearing loss was identified in 18 patients, of whom 16 were fitted with hearing aids. In Patient 1, hearing loss was mild and did not require intervention, whereas Patient 12 could not tolerate hearing aids due to behavioural difficulties. Hearing loss did not affect cognitive testing, except in two patients. In Patient 4, hearing difficulties influenced the first assessment, although IQ was within normal range. In Patient 12, uncorrected hearing loss was not considered sufficient to explain the consistently low IQ (< 10), which was also consistent with parent‐reported abilities. Two additional patients were suspected of mild hearing loss, but reliable assessment was not possible due to behavioural problems. Parents of Patient 17 reported adequate hearing in daily life. In Patient 13, the low IQ score (< 35) aligned with parent‐reported abilities, making a substantial impact of potential hearing loss on cognitive performance unlikely. Behavioural problems were reported in eight patients, including hyperactivity, aggression, impulsivity, and distractibility. Three patients had sleep disturbances, involving falling asleep or maintaining sleep; two of these also had behavioural problems. Details on hearing status during follow‐up, and behavioural and sleep problems at diagnosis are provided in the Supporting Information.

Full‐Scale Intelligence Quotient

Figure 2A shows cognitive functioning scores by chronological age (median: 6 tests per patient, range: 1–12). Three of four non‐neuronopathic children and the adult had stable IQs in the normal range (92–117). The fifth non‐neuronopathic patient with missense variant c.1122C>T (Patient 18) had IQ scores of 65 and 71 at age 5.5 and 6.7 years, respectively. Most neuronopathic patients showed a decline of cognitive functioning scores. Four patients (19–22) did not show neurocognitive impairment at their last assessment (IQ range 72–96), all at a young age (3.4–3.8 years). All other patients had cognitive functioning scores below 70 at their last assessment. The median age at which cognitive impairment (cognitive functioning score < 70) was first identified was 4.0 years (range 2.8–11.3). After scores initially dropped below 70, all but Patient 23 showed further decline, with most approaching the floor of the applied instruments.

Median age at which initial cognitive impairment was observed varied across groups with different IDS variants. Patients with IDS deletions (n = 3) showed earliest and fastest decline, with cognitive impairment first observed at a median age of 3.3 years (range: 2.8–3.8). The patient with an IDS‐FMR1 deletion had the lowest cognitive functioning scores. Four of five patients with frameshift variants showed impairment, with median onset at 4.4 years (range: 2.8–4.6). Among patients with nonsense variants, only Patient 11 showed cognitive impairment, with onset at 3.2 years. The other two remained within the normal range but were still young. Of seven neuronopathic patients with missense variants, six showed impairment: two early (3.5 and 3.7 years; Patients 2 and 10), two late (8.8 and 11.3 years; Patients 14 and 23), and two first assessed around age 10, when cognitive functioning scores were already below 40 (Patients 12 and 13).

Linear mixed‐effects models showed that IQ trajectories differed significantly between neuronopathic and non‐neuropathic children (p < 0.001, Figure 2B) at group level. Patients with deletions declined more rapidly compared with neuronopathic patients with other variants (p < 0.001, Figure 2C). Mental Age

Figure 3A shows MA trajectories from 128 tests (median: 5 tests per patient, range: 1–12). Non‐neuronopathic patients followed age‐appropriate trajectories, except Patient 18, who had a lower MA than expected. Neuronopathic patients deviated from the normative trajectory, following the expected sequence: initial normal development, stagnation, plateau, and decline, though the timing and duration varied. Patients with deletions (n = 3) showed early, brief stagnation and did not exceed an MA of 2.0 years. Patients with frameshift and nonsense variants showed more variability in age of onset and duration of stagnation, with maximum MAs of 1.5–5.0 and 2.3–3.9 years, respectively. The largest variation was observed in patients with neuronopathic missense variants, in whom maximum observed MA ranged from 0.6 (at age 10.8; Patient 12) to 7.2 years (at age 10.9; Patient 23).

Linear mixed‐effects models showed significantly different mean MA trajectories between neuronopathic and non‐neuronopathic groups (p < 0.001, Figure 3B). Neuronopathic children reached a mean maximum MA of 2.5 years (95% CI [2.1–3.0 years]) at a CA of 4.5 years, followed by decline, while non‐neuronopathic patients followed the trajectory of healthy peers. Within the neuronopathic group, patients with deletions declined faster than those with other variants (p < 0.001, Figure 3C). Mean maximal MA in patients with deletions was 1.7 years (95% CI [1.3–2.2 years]) at a CA of 2.9 years. For with deletions, the CA at which MA deviated 1 and 2 years from the normative line occurred at 2.7 (95% CI [2.0–3.1 years]) and 3.7 years (95% CI [3.2–4.1 years]) respectively, compared with 3.6 (95% CI [1.1–4.4 years]) and 4.8 years (95% CI [4.2–5.7 years]) in patients with other neuronopathic variants. Discussion

This study provides new insights into the neurocognitive development of patients with MPS II. By combining long‐term, standardised prospective follow‐up with detailed genotype–phenotype data in a nationwide cohort, we identified distinct cognitive trajectories associated with specific IDS variant groups. This extends previous reports, which were limited by smaller cohorts and shorter follow‐up periods [4, 16, 17], and characterises, for the first time, the onset and progression of cognitive decline in relation to IDS genotype. These findings refine current understanding of neurocognitive trajectories in MPS II and provide a valuable reference for evaluating and optimising the timing of emerging brain‐targeted therapies.

Cognitive functioning scores and MA trajectories differed significantly between neuronopathic and non‐neuronopathic children (p < 0.001). Most non‐neuronopathic patients showed normal IQ values and age‐appropriate cognitive development. One patient with the missense variant c.1122C>T had a stable but subnormal IQ, which could not be explained by comorbidities, as he had no craniosynostosis or white matter abnormalities. Classification as non‐neuronopathic was considered appropriate as his latest IQ remained above 70, although lower than that of the other non‐neuronopathic patients. Given the reported phenotypic variability associated with this variant, this patient may represent the milder end of a spectrum extending into intermediate disease [29]. Similar variability among non‐neuronopathic patients with missense mutations has previously been described, including differences in school placement ranging from mainstream to special education [5]. In addition, white matter abnormalities and attention deficits despite preserved global intelligence have been reported in non‐neuronopathic MPS II patients [30]. Together, these findings support the concept of CNS involvement in so‐called non‐neuronopathic disease, and suggest that non‐neuronopathic patients with stable but subnormal cognition may also benefit from therapies capable of reaching the CNS.

Most patients were neuronopathic and, as a group, showed cognitive decline. The trajectory of those with deletions differed significantly from other neuronopathic variants (p < 0.001); their decline was more rapid and homogeneous, and no individual achieved a mental age above 2.0 years. The most severe course occurred in the patient whose deletion also encompassed the FMR1 gene, supporting earlier reports and highlighting the importance of considering deletion size and involvement of cognition‐related genes such as FMR1 [31]. In contrast, patients with missense, nonsense, and frameshift variants demonstrated a slower decline and more heterogeneous trajectory, reflected in broader ranges of maximum MA and IQ. All patients with nonsense variants in our cohort attained higher MA values than those reported by Seo et al., in which the maximum MA was 2.0 years. One patient with extended follow‐up exhibited a prolonged period of stagnation, consistent with Seo's observations [16]. Two additional patients with nonsense variants were still young at their most recent assessment and continued to show developmental progress, suggesting the possibility of attaining higher MA at subsequent evaluations. Within the frameshift subgroup, the maximum observed MA was 5 years in a patient who experienced a protracted phase of stagnation, whereas others declined earlier or remained too young for definitive trajectory assessment. The largest variation was observed among neuronopathic patients with missense mutations, where the maximum MA reached was 7.2 years. These findings highlight that the onset and rate of neurocognitive decline vary across genotypes. Recognising this variability is essential when evaluating the effects of emerging therapies against the natural history of disease.

Genotype–phenotype correlations in our cohort were consistent with previous reports, although some genetic variants were novel. Two siblings carrying the variant c.889_900delins17 presented early with a neuronopathic phenotype characterised by abnormal behaviour, sleep problems, low IQ, and sagittal craniosynostosis. In addition, patients with novel variants c.1505G>T and c.1187_1227dup, who initially showed normal or near‐normal cognition, developed behavioural problems, sleep disturbance, and cognitive decline during follow‐up, in line with their a priori classification in the neuronopathic group.

Although the conventional dichotomous classification captured the major cognitive trajectories observed in our cohort, some patients demonstrated greater variability than anticipated. One patient classified as non‐neuronopathic exhibited persistently subnormal but stable cognitive functioning, whereas several neuronopathic patients showed slower decline or prolonged plateaus before deterioration became apparent. These observations highlight the biological heterogeneity of neurocognitive involvement across MPS II, particularly among patients carrying specific missense variants. They further suggest that clinically relevant CNS involvement may extend beyond the classical neuronopathic phenotype, indicating that a subset of patients traditionally classified as non‐neuronopathic may also benefit from brain‐targeted therapies. While acknowledging this biological variation within the conventional binary classification, we support its continued use, as it provides a clinically interpretable framework for characterising cognitive trajectories and establishing well‐defined reference groups for future studies of CNS‐targeted therapies. At the same time, genotype provides important additional prognostic information by refining expectations within these clinically relevant categories.

In this cohort, global cognitive functioning was assessed using both IQ and DQ, reflecting the diversity of available instruments and the developmental range of the patients. DQ is mathematically derived from MA and chronological age and may appear disproportionately low at older ages when developmental gains plateau while chronological age continues to increase. Consequently, combining IQ and DQ introduces heterogeneity and may underestimate cognitive level in older, severely affected patients. However, excluding DQ‐based assessments would have removed a substantial proportion of data from younger and more severely affected children, thereby truncating the early part of the developmental trajectory. We therefore chose to analyse IQ and DQ jointly as measures of global cognitive functioning, while interpreting age‐related declines in DQ with caution. This approach allowed us to capture the full spectrum of disease severity and to provide a more comprehensive natural history benchmark for future brain‐targeted interventions.

Muenzer et al. reported on the Hunter Outcome Study (HOS), an observational registry in 650 patients [32]. Their work provides important insights into the clinical spectrum of MPS II and offers extensive information on genetic variants at a large scale. However, cognitive status in the HOS was based on clinical impression at the last assessment and recorded dichotomously (cognitive impairment “yes/no”), as formal neuropsychological testing is not part of routine care in most centres. As a result, the HOS data do not capture the full variation in cognitive functioning or the longitudinal trajectories of cognitive development. In their analysis of IDS variants, Muenzer et al. also highlighted substantial phenotypic variability within individual mutation classes and emphasised that genotype alone does not reliably predict the presence or severity of cognitive impairment. Our data therefore complement HOS by characterising cognitive trajectories from a younger age window that is particularly relevant for guiding timely intervention. It should be noted that cognitive assessments in infancy and early toddlerhood capture different constructs and have more limited predictive validity for later intellectual functioning than assessments in middle childhood and beyond; early “age‐appropriate” performance must therefore be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, the same research group reported short‐term cognitive outcomes in patients with MPS II using the Differential Ability Scales (DAS) to assess cognitive function and the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Skills (VABS) to assess adaptive functioning [17]. While the VABS potentially provides valuable information, the DAS is not included in the international consensus recommendations for neuropsychological endpoints in MPS [20]. Nevertheless, adaptive functioning may represent an important complementary domain, as IQ or DQ alone may not fully capture the spectrum of neurocognitive involvement in MPS II, particularly regarding the impact of disease on everyday functioning [33].

Because cognitive divergence in neuronopathic MPS II emerges early, treatment timing is critical. Therapies that reach the CNS are unlikely to reverse established damage, as shown in other lysosomal storage disorders [34]. Therefore, they should ideally be initiated before or at the earliest signs of developmental deviation. While regular cognitive follow‐up can help guide this, treating as early as possible—potentially enabled by newborn screening—is preferable [35]. However, CNS involvement in patients with novel missense mutations cannot yet be predicted, highlighting the need for better biochemical or clinical markers to guide early treatment decisions.

An important consideration for future clinical studies is patient selection. Patients with IDS deletions represent the most homogeneous group, but their rapid decline requires treatment before the early onset of cognitive deterioration, making intervention challenging. By contrast, patients with missense variants decline more slowly and offer a broader therapeutic window, although greater heterogeneity complicates evaluation of treatment effects. These differences underscore the need for careful patient selection and stratification in future trials.

Limitations

Despite adherence to a strict follow‐up protocol, cognitive assessments varied due to age‐dependent test selection, evolving test preferences, and occasional external evaluations, resulting in the use of multiple instruments over time. However, within patients, score patterns did not show major shifts coinciding with test changes, and declines were observed in those with a neuronopathic course, suggesting that true disease progression had a greater impact than instrument variability. The non‐neuronopathic subgroup was small (n = 5) with variable data completeness, so findings for this group may not fully represent the broader non‐neuronopathic MPS II population. Although the underlying neurocognitive phenotype of MPS II shows biological heterogeneity within the conventional dichotomy, we classified patients as neuronopathic or non‐neuronopathic and combined IQ with DQ (which is not equivalent to IQ and may appear disproportionately low at older ages); together, these choices may oversimplify intermediate phenotypes and introduce some distortion into observed trajectories, but were considered necessary to construct clinically interpretable reference groups.

Recommendations for Future Research

Building on existing collaborative efforts such as MetabERN, future research should prioritise international collaboration to establish a standardised long‐term follow‐up from early childhood, enabling the creation of harmonised international cohorts and enlargement of the sample size. This would allow even more precise characterisation of developmental trajectories, especially in patients with missense variants. In parallel, the use of internationally endorsed and up‐to‐date neurocognitive assessment tools will be essential to ensure reliable and comparable outcome measurements across studies and clinical trials [20].

Conclusions

This study provides new data on the neurocognitive development of MPS II patients, particularly at young ages, and is the first to demonstrate distinct trajectories of cognitive decline across IDS variant groups. It provides reference data for the cognitive development of neuronopathic patients whose CNS remained untreated. In the subgroup analysis, patients with IDS deletions showed the most rapid and homogeneous cognitive decline, whereas neuronopathic patients with missense, nonsense, and frameshift variants showed more heterogeneous trajectories, varying in both onset and speed of decline. These findings underscore the importance of early and consistent cognitive assessments to capture developmental changes, which is essential for evaluating the efficacy of emerging treatments.
