How Noise Benefits AI-generated Image Detection
Jiazhen Yan, Ziqiang Li, Fan Wang, Kai Zeng, Zhangjie Fu, School of Computer Science, Nanjing University of Information Science, Technology

Abstract
The rapid advancement of generative models has made real and synthetic images increasingly indistinguishable. Although extensive efforts have been devoted to detecting AI-generated images, out-of-distribution generalization remains a persistent challenge. We trace this weakness to spurious shortcuts exploited during training and we also observe that small feature-space perturbations can mitigate shortcut dominance. To address this problem in a more controllable manner, we propose the Positive-Incentive Noise for CLIP (PiN-CLIP), which jointly trains a noise generator and a detection network under a variational positive-incentive principle. Specifically, we construct positive-incentive noise in the feature space via cross-attention fusion of visual and categorical semantic features. During optimization, the noise is injected into the feature space to fine-tune the visual encoder, suppressing shortcut-sensitive directions while amplifying stable forensic cues, thereby enabling the extraction of more robust and generalized artifact representations. Comparative experiments are conducted on an open-world dataset comprising synthetic images generated by 42 distinct generative models. Our method achieves new state-of-the-art performance, with notable improvements of 5.4% in average accuracy over existing approaches.

3
Methodology

Generalization to out-of-distribution samples remains a fundamental challenge in AI-generated image detection. We identify spurious shortcuts as the primary cause of overfitting in existing detectors. To address this issue, we introduce Positive-incentive Noise to explore the constructive role of noise in AI-generated image detection from a novel perspective, termed Positive-incentive Noise for CLIP (PiN-CLIP).

3.1
Problem Formulation

Given an image
x\in\mathcal{X}
and its binary label
y\in\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
indicating whether it is
real
or
fake
,
the goal of AIGI detection is to learn a classifier
h_{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
that estimates the posterior probability

q_{\phi}(y|x)=\operatorname{softmax}(\hat{h}_{\phi}(x)).

(1)

From an information-theoretic perspective, the proposed
\pi
-noise mechanism aims to maximize the mutual information
I(\mathcal{T};\mathcal{E})
between the injected noise
\mathcal{E}
and the target task
\mathcal{T}
:

I(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{E})=H(\mathcal{T})-H(\mathcal{T}|\mathcal{E}),

(2)

thereby guiding the detector toward more confident and reliable predictions by reducing predictive uncertainty.

\pi
-noise regarding AIGI detection.

To quantify the intrinsic difficulty of the detection task on an arbitrary dataset, we first measure its
task entropy
, which characterizes the uncertainty of predicting the authenticity label.
Following prior work
80
;
20
;
32
, task entropy can be expressed as:

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{T})=H(y|x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim{\mathcal{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p(y|x)}[-\log p(y|x)]

(3)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[-\log p(y|x)].

Analogously, the conditional task entropy given the injected noise
\mathcal{E}
is defined as:

H(\mathcal{T}|\mathcal{E})=\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y,\varepsilon)}[-\log p(y|x,\varepsilon)].

(4)

The mutual information between the task and the injected noise can thus be rewritten as

I(\mathcal{T};\mathcal{E})=H(\mathcal{T})-H(\mathcal{T}|\mathcal{E}).

(5)

Since
H(\mathcal{T})
is constant during optimization, maximizing
I(\mathcal{T};\mathcal{E})
is equivalent to minimizing the conditional task entropy
H(\mathcal{T}|\mathcal{E})
.
This encourages the detector to learn noise-invariant yet task-discriminative representations, thereby improving its robustness and generalization to unseen generative models and domains.

3.2
Variational Objective

The variational objective of our framework is derived from the non-negativity property of the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, expressed as:

\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\geq 0\;\Leftrightarrow\;\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[\log p(x)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[\log q(x)].

(6)

Based on this property, we apply the principle of variational inference to obtain a tractable upper bound of the conditional task entropy
H(\mathcal{T}|\mathcal{E})
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{VPN}}=\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y,\varepsilon)}[-\log q(y|x,\varepsilon)]\geq H(\mathcal{T}|\mathcal{E}),

(7)

where
q(y|x,\varepsilon)
is a variational approximation of the intractable posterior
p(y|x,\varepsilon)
.
Intuitively, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VPN}}
reduces the conditional uncertainty of task predictions, thereby guiding the detector toward more confident and reliable decisions.
To avoid direct integration over the continuous data distribution, we approximate the expectation in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VPN}}
using Monte Carlo sampling:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{VPN}}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{p(\varepsilon|x_{i},y_{i})}\left[-\log q(y_{i}|x_{i},\varepsilon)\right].

(8)

We assume the conditional noise distribution follows a Gaussian form,

p(\varepsilon|x_{i},y_{i})=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}),\Sigma_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i})),

(9)

where the mean
\mu_{\theta}
and covariance
\Sigma_{\theta}
are outputs of a learnable function
f_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i})
parameterized by
\theta
.
Since the integral over the continuous variable
\varepsilon
remains intractable, we further employ the
reparameterization trick

26
to enable gradient backpropagation through stochastic nodes.
Specifically, we express the sampling process as:

\varepsilon=G_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i},\epsilon)=\Sigma_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i})\cdot\epsilon+\mu_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}),

(10)

where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
is a standard Gaussian random variable, and
G_{\theta}
denotes a differentiable function that generates task-oriented noise.

Under the Monte Carlo approximation, the final training objective can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{VPN}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}\left[-\log q\big(y_{i}|x_{i},G_{\theta}(\epsilon,x_{i},y_{i})\big)\right]

(11)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{n\cdot m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big[-\log q\big(y_{i}|x_{i},G_{\theta}(\epsilon_{ij},x_{i},y_{i})\big)\Big],

where
m
denotes the number of noise samples per input.
Accordingly, the final training object contains two components:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{\text{VPN}},

(12)

where
\gamma
is a trade-off coefficient and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}
represents the original cross entropy loss of the base model
h_{\phi}
.

3.3
Joint Training with
\pi
-noise

Leveraging the powerful generalization capability of CLIP models in AI-generated image detection tasks, we select the pre-trained CLIP model as the base model. The framework has been illustrated in Figure
2
.

Input and Feature Extraction.

For each image
x
, we attach a minimal text prompt
z
that is aligned with its label, such as "A real photo." for real images, and "A fake photo." for fake images. CLIP provides an image encoder
E_{\text{img}}
and a text encoder
E_{\text{text}}
, the feature extraction processes are defined:

f\;=\;E_{\text{img}}(x;\varphi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;\;\;\;t\;=\;E_{\text{text}}(z;\phi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(13)

where
\varphi
denotes the trainable parameters of the image encoder, and
\phi
is frozen.

Architecture of Noise Generator.

As illustrated in Eq.
10
, generating conditional noise typically relies on explicit label information. Instead of using conventional one-hot labels, we condition the perturbation on label-aligned textual embeddings
t
extracted from CLIP’s text encoder, enabling the noise generator to adapt its perturbations more precisely to the underlying forensic categories. Given the CLIP image feature
f\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the corresponding text feature
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we instantiate a lightweight cross-attention module
G_{\theta}(f,t)
to generate per-dimension Gaussian parameters
(\mu,\text{var})
in the feature space. Specifically, we define the weight matrices
W_{q},W_{k},W_{v},W_{\mu},W_{\text{var}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times H}
, where
H=d/r
is a bottleneck hidden size (
r{\geq}1
). The process can be formulated:

\displaystyle q=W_{q}f,\quad k=W_{k}t,\quad v=W_{v}t,

\displaystyle a=\text{SoftMax}(qk^{\top})v\in\mathbb{R}^{H},

(14)

\displaystyle\mu=W_{\mu}a,\qquad\text{var}=\psi\!\left(W_{\text{var}}a\right),

where
\psi(\cdot)
enforces element-wise nonnegativity. Reparameterized sampling and feature perturbation are as follows:

\varepsilon=\mu+\text{var}\odot\xi,\quad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),\qquad\tilde{f}=f+\varepsilon.

(15)

In this way, we combine the artifact-conditional Gaussian perturbations
\varepsilon
with artifact representation
f
to maximize
I(\mathcal{T};\mathcal{E})
illustrated in (
5
).

.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}=\ell\big(h(f_{j}),\;y_{j}\big)

Update

,

Loss Definitions.

By Eqs. (
1
) and (
11
), optimization is expressed as cross-entropy over the clean feature
f
and its perturbed counterpart
\tilde{f}
. Thus, we use a clean head
h(\cdot)
on
f
and a noisy head
h_{\text{noise}}(\cdot)
on
\tilde{f}=f+\varepsilon
.

The training objective can be formulated as follows:

\mathcal{L}=\underbrace{\ell\big(h(f),y\big)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}}+\lambda\ \underbrace{\ell\big(h_{\text{noise}}(f+\mu+\mathrm{var}\odot\xi),y\big)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{VPN}}},

(16)

where
\ell
means the BCELoss.

The Parameters Update.

We first define the feature-space gradients of the two branches:

\displaystyle g_{\text{clean}}:=\frac{\partial\,\ell\big(h(f),y\big)}{\partial f},

(17)

\displaystyle g_{\text{noisy}}:=\frac{\partial\,\ell\big(h_{\text{noise}}(\tilde{f}),y\big)}{\partial\tilde{f}},\quad\tilde{f}=f+\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon
depends on
f
via the noise generator
G_{\theta}(f,t)
, the chain rule yields the total gradient w.r.t.
f
:

g_{\text{final}}\;=\;g_{\text{clean}}\;+\;\lambda\,\!\left[\underbrace{g_{\text{noisy}}}_{\partial\ell/\partial\tilde{f}}\cdot\underbrace{\Big(\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial f}\Big)}_{I\;+\;\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial f}}\right],

(18)

where

\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial f}\;=\;\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial f}\;+\;\operatorname{Diag}(\xi)\,\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{var}}{\partial f}.

(19)

By the chain rule, the update of the CLIP’s image encoder (LoRA) parameters
\varphi
can be expressed as:

\varphi\;\leftarrow\;\varphi\;-\;\eta\,\Big(\frac{\partial E_{\mathrm{img}}(x;\varphi)}{\partial\varphi}\Big)^{\!\top}\,g_{\mathrm{final}},

(20)

where
\eta
denotes the learning rate.

In addition, regarding the injected conditional noise, its mean steers feature updates toward higher confidence for the correct class, while its variance imposes a curvature-weighted penalty that discourages shortcut-prone directions. Specifically, the expected noisy loss satisfies:

\partial_{\mu}\,\ell\big(h_{\text{noise}}(f+\mu+\mathrm{var}\odot\xi),y\big)=\nabla_{f}\ell(\cdot),

(21)

so the learned
\mu
aligns with the local negative input-gradient and increases the correct-class logit. What’s more, a second-order expansion yields:

\ell\big(h_{\text{noise}}(f+\varepsilon),y\big)\approx\ell\big(h_{\text{noise}}(f),y\big)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{Tr}\;\big(H_{f}\,\text{Diag}(\text{var}^{2})\big),

(22)

where
H_{f}=\nabla_{f}^{2}\ell(\cdot)
, effectively penalizing high-curvature directions. The dual constraints from
\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VPN}}
jointly shape the feature extractor: the clean term preserves performance on unperturbed features, while the noisy term provides a task-anchored, artifact-conditional signal that suppresses shortcut-sensitive directions and amplifies stable forensic cues, yielding consistent and trustworthy predictions. with the clean head

4.3
Ablation Studies

To further verify the effectiveness of our method, we conduct additional analyzes on the role of noise, as summarized in Table
4
. Without any noise, the average accuracies on the two datasets are 90.6% and 71.8%, respectively. Introducing our Positive-incentive Noise improves the results by 4.8% and 10.3%, confirming that task-beneficial perturbations effectively enhance CLIP’s detection capability. For comparison, we also evaluate two alternative noise types: random Gaussian noise and Gaussian noise aligned with the visual feature mean. Both yield moderate improvements (sometimes surpassing existing baselines) supporting our hypothesis that small feature-space perturbations can disproportionately disrupt brittle shortcut evidence. However, compared with our PiN-CLIP, these variants show noticeable performance degradation, further validating that our designed perturbation suppresses shortcut-sensitive directions while amplifying stable forensic cues under beneficial stochastic transformations, thereby achieving consistent and reliable predictions.

Qualitative Analysis

To further assess the generalization ability of our method, we visualize the feature distribution before binary classification, as shown in Fig
3
. 5
Conclusion

In this paper, we systematically analyze the limitations of current AI-generated image detectors and identify spurious shortcuts as the root cause of overfitting. We observe that introducing small random perturbations into the feature space can effectively mitigate this problem and significantly improve generalization. Building on this insight, we propose a novel framework, Positive-incentive Noise for CLIP-based detection(PiN-CLIP), which transforms random noise perturbations into controllable, task-oriented signals. Specifically, we employ a cross-modal attention mechanism to integrate visual features and label embeddings, generating task-adaptive, forgery-conditional Gaussian perturbations. By jointly optimizing the noise generator and the visual encoder, PiN-CLIP suppresses shortcut-sensitive directions under beneficial stochastic transformations while amplifying stable forensic cues, leading to more generalized artifact representations and opening a new direction for noise-driven learning in multimedia forensics.
