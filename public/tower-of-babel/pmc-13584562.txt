Sucralose Induces Neurobehavioral Alterations and Exacerbates Alzheimer’s Disease-Like Phenotypes in Zebrafish Models
Authors: Xiang Li, Zhuang Miao, Jiying Bi, Jie Zhang, Ning Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial sweeteners are widely consumed, yet their potential neurotoxic effects remain poorly understood. Using sucralose (SUC) as a model compound, this study develops an approach to rapidly screen the neurotoxicity of artificial sweeteners and explore their potential associations with neurodegenerative-like processes, especially Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-like phenotypes. SUC is minimally absorbed and metabolized by mammals, leading to its widespread presence in the environment. Here, we integrate high-throughput behavioral phenotyping in larval zebrafish with targeted analyses in adult and AD transgenic models, employing a “behavioral phenoblast” strategy adapted from prior large-scale sleep-wake drug screens. SUC effects were evaluated across a broad concentration range (5 μg/L to 100 mg/L), covering low-dose to high-dose exposure conditions. Hierarchical and K-means clustering revealed that SUC’s behavioral signatures closely resembled those of compounds associated with neurodegenerative risks. To validate these findings, adult zebrafish were exposed to SUC within the Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) range (1–100 mg/L) for 3 months, resulting in dose-dependent increases in anxiety-like behaviors and impairments in learning and memory. Notably, treatment of transgenic zebrafish expressing human misfolded Aβ protein with SUC exacerbated Aβ-related pathological phenotypes in a dose-dependent manner. These findings highlight the need for further investigation into the long-term neurological impacts of artificial sweeteners and provide a methodological framework for evaluating the potential health risks of artificial sweeteners.

Introduction

Artificial sweeteners are widely used as low-calorie sugar substitutes, with sucralose (SUC) holding a dominant 62% market share in the $1.5 billion artificial sweetener industry. , SUC, a chlorinated derivative of sucrose, is 320–1,000 times sweeter than sugar and has been approved for use in over 80 countries. , Produced by the chlorination of sucrose, SUC exhibits extremely low metabolism in mammals (65–99% is excreted unchanged) and is removed at rates below 20% during wastewater treatment. − Due to its metabolic stability, SUC has become widely detectable across various exposure contexts. Artificial sweeteners, including SUC, have been reported at low concentrations across multiple settings, reflecting their widespread presence. Despite the extensive use and generally recognized safety of SUC, emerging research suggests potential risks associated with SUC consumption, necessitating further investigation into its long-term effects on public health. , Recent studies have reported that SUC affects metabolism, drug bioavailability, gut microbiota, and persists as an environmental contaminant, − highlighting the need to better understand its biological effects under sustained exposure conditions. Of particular concern are the potential neurotoxic effects of SUC, which have been understudied despite the critical importance of neurological health in an aging global population.

A bulk of studies using animal models and clinical trials has systematically evaluated the safety of SUC under both acute and chronic exposure. Data from over 110 studies in animals and humans, reviewed by the FDA, indicate that SUC consumption causes no detectable toxic effects, including carcinogenic, reproductive, and neurological effects. The FDA approval process has confirmed that consuming SUC in typical amounts as a sweetener is safe. The FDA recommends a safe consumption level of SUC of 900 mg/kg BW/day, or about 60 g for a 70 kg (150 lb) person, over a lifetime without adverse effects. Additionally, the “highest no-effect level” (HNEL) for SUC has been established at 1,500 mg/kg BW/day. The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) has set the acceptable daily intake (ADI) of SUC at 0–15 mg/kg BW. As a result, over 80 countries have approved SUC as a food additive for a wide range of applications.

Despite the widespread recognition of SUC’s safety, its biological effects remain to be fully characterized: (1) SUC is not biologically inert in rodents and may have unknown effects on human metabolism. It can alter metabolic parameters and affect body weight. (2) SUC may impact detoxification mechanisms and drug metabolism, as it significantly increases the protein expression of P-GP and CYP enzymes in the small intestine, even within the safe intake levels approved by the FDA and EU. This could reduce the bioavailability of therapeutic drugs. , The effects of SUC and/or its metabolites on bioaccumulation, particularly in the presence of other drugs, have not been fully explored. (3) SUC disrupts the balance of bacterial colonies in the digestive tract, significantly inhibiting beneficial anaerobic bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) while having less affect on harmful bacteria (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae). (4) SUC is considered an emerging contaminant, with studies showing that it is not degraded during water treatment and can be found in groundwater and coastal waters. − (5) SUC has potential ecotoxic effects; recent studies have shown that it induces cholinesterase activity and lipid peroxidation in the aquatic organism Daphnia magna, suggesting that exposure to SUC can lead to neurological disorders and oxidative stress, with potentially significant effects on animal behavior and physiology. Given the long-term and large-scale use of SUC, it is crucial to continue evaluating its potential risks to public health.

Recently, zebrafish have become increasingly popular in hazard identification and translational toxicology research on chemical compounds because of their ability to bridge the gap between in vitro and mammalian studies and to combine the genetic and physiological advantages of mammalian models with the high-throughput capabilities and genetic manipulability of invertebrate models. − The primary challenge in using zebrafish to accurately predict human toxicity lies in the lack of ideal technologies that can link the phenotypic data induced by chemicals to potential biological targets and mechanisms of action (MOAs) in humans.

To address the above-mentioned issues, the sleep-wake behavioral phenotype-driven high-throughput approach was applied to systematically assess the neurotoxic potential of food additives, using SUC as a case study (Figure A).

The innovation in this approach lies in the application of zebrafish neurobehavioral phenomics (ZNPs) and neurobehavioral pharmacodynamic fingerprints (NPFs). − , These methodologies allow us to link rich phenotypic data induced by chemicals to potential biological targets and mechanisms of action (MOAs), addressing a limitation in zebrafish-based toxicology studies. To explore the potential targets and mechanisms of action of SUC, the pharmacodynamic fingerprints of 547 compounds, representing 463 unique structures with known targets and mechanisms of action, were selected for clustering and correlation analysis with SUC.

The study design encompasses a broad concentration range of SUC, spanning low-dose exposure levels (5–100 μg/L) to higher concentrations within the acceptable daily intake (ADI) range (1–100 mg/L), enabling dose-dependent evaluation of neurobehavioral effects (Figure 1A). A standardized toxicological protocol based on fish embryo tests was employed to obtain the NPFs of SUC. By comparing these fingerprints with those of known neurotoxic compounds, potential neurotoxicological effects of SUC were predicted. To validate the high-throughput findings, chronic exposure studies in adult zebrafish were conducted, employing a battery of standardized neurobehavioral tests (Figure B). Furthermore, transgenic zebrafish models expressing human misfolded Aβ protein were utilized to investigate SUC’s potential effects on AD-like pathological phenotypes (Figure B and Figure C), addressing a critical gap in the understanding of artificial sweeteners’ long-term neurological effects. Finally, the behavioral findings were corroborated with transcriptomic and neurotransmitter analyses, providing mechanistic insights into SUC’s neurotoxic potential. This study employed a multitiered methodology to thoroughly assess the neurological impacts of SUC, thereby establishing a framework for evaluating the safety of food additives. It addressed the pressing need for more comprehensive safety evaluations of widely used food additives, especially considering potential long-term neurological effects. By leveraging advanced zebrafish models and multiparameter phenotyping techniques, an efficient and translatable pipeline for identifying potential neurotoxic effects of chemical compounds is provided. , The potential health risks of SUC are not only understood through this research but also demonstrate the power of integrating high-throughput screening with targeted mechanistic studies in toxicology research. Significant implications for public health policies and safety guidelines regarding artificial sweeteners may arise from these findings, underscoring the importance of continued vigilance in artificial sweetener safety assessments.

Results

SUC Elicits Minimal Embryonic Teratogenicity That Can Recover Spontaneously during Embryonic Development

To systematically and comprehensively evaluate the potential developmental toxicity of SUC, fertilized zebrafish embryos were continuously exposed to varying concentrations of SUC (wild type, 5 μg/L, 10 μg/L, 50 μg/L, 100 μg/L, 1 mg/L, 10 mg/L, 100 mg/L). Wild-type (WT) fish were set as the control group with 0 μg/L SUC exposure for all experiments. All WT groups mentioned below correspond to the 0 μg/L SUC group. The time points of SUC treatment, along with the time points and parameters for morphological and behavioral assessments, are illustrated in the flowchart provided in the Supporting Information (Supporting Information schematic diagram 1). This flowchart clearly outlines the experimental timeline for exposing embryos to various concentrations of SUC from 0 to 96 h postfertilization (hpf) and records the observed morphological changes (such as delayed epiboly, developmental retardation, hypopigmentation, and pericardial edema) and behavioral changes (such as alterations in hatching rate).

Continuous observation of the embryos from 0 to 96 hpf revealed several developmental anomalies. At 2 hpf, a delay in embryonic epiboly was noted (see Supplementary Figure 1). By 24 hpf, embryonic development was significantly retarded (see Supplementary Figure 2), and by 48 hpf, the hatching rate of embryos (see Supplementary Figure 3) showed a significant decrease in response to increasing concentrations, starting at 10 μg/L. At 72 hpf and 96 hpf, SUC treatment did not significantly enhance hypopigmentation; however, there was a trend toward increased hypopigmentation (see Supplementary Figure 4).

At 72 and 96 hpf, pericardial edema became apparent (see Supporting Information Figure 5 and Supporting Information Figure 6). While these teratogenic indicators were occasionally abnormal, they did not exhibit a clear dose–response relationship.

On the basis of the above phenotypic data, it is concluded that the general time window of toxic effects for embryonic development is 24–72 hpf under continuous SUC exposure, after which the observed developmental abnormalities diminished over time. From 24 to 96 hpf, the specific toxicity of SUC may be related to the inhibition of early embryonic developmental processes and the inhibition of pigmentation. This was accompanied by a weak effect, causing pericardial edema and spinal shortening.

(B) Long-term treatment with SUC was administered to adult zebrafish. After the behavioral experiments, brain tissues were extracted for transcriptomics and HPLC-MS analysis to verify the effects of SUC on the brain tissue’s genome and neurotransmitters. (C) To verify if SUC accelerates the progression of AD-like manifestations, SUC was administered to AD transgenic zebrafish. The expression of human misfolded Aβ protein in the brain was captured using a stereomicroscope and confocal microscopy. Subsequently, qPCR analysis was conducted.

Classification and Prediction of the Potential Neurotoxic Effects of SUC Based on a Pharmacodynamic Fingerprint Derived from the Sleep-Wake Behavior of Zebrafish Using the Phenoblast Method

As shown in Figure A and B, normalized waking activity (Figure A) and rest graphs (Figure B) are plotted for behavior-altering effects for the WT group (dark blue) and SUC-treated groups (5 μg/L light blue, 10 μg/L dark red, 50 μg/L pink, 100 μg/L green, 1 mg/L yellow, 10 mg/L black, 100 mg/L gray). The sleep-wake curves for each group were averaged from data collected from eight fish and then normalized (See Supplementary Figure 7). The overall results indicate that, following treatment with all concentrations of SUC, only the amplitude of the normalized sleep and rhythm curves changed (as shown by changes in the vertical axis), while the phase of the rhythm remained unchanged (as shown by the horizontal axis). Specifically, SUC at concentrations of 5 μg/L, 10 μg/L, 50 μg/L, and 100 μg/L decreased waking activity during the first daytime period and increased waking activity during the second daytime period. For the sleep curves, SUC at these concentrations reduced sleep activity during both day and night (See Supplementary Figures 8–22).

After normalizing and averaging the sleep and waking activity curves according to the WT group, the pharmacodynamic fingerprint profile of SUC was obtained (Figure C). Five behavioral parameters were defined, creating 10 feature vectors across two daytime and two nighttime periods. For each concentration, these feature vectors formed a pharmacodynamic fingerprint profile, as shown in the legend. For each feature vector (represented by a grid in the heatmap), red indicates a value higher than the WT, while green indicates a value lower than the WT. The total range of fluctuations is within ±3 standard deviations (SD). The pharmacodynamic fingerprint profile visualizes that, for all concentrations of SUC treatment, there is significant upregulation of total activity and waking activity across both daytime and nighttime periods (See Supplementary Figures 17–22) and significant downregulation of total sleep, sleep bouts, and sleep-bout length (See Supplementary Figures 8–16). Notably, the effects observed at 100 μg/L and 100 mg/L are significantly different from those at the other concentrations, suggesting that the effects at these two concentrations might differ from those at the other concentrations. The correlation plot of the 10 feature vectors (Figure C), based on the parameters from Figure B shows a significant difference in correlation at the 1 mg/L concentration, indicating that this concentration serves as a boundary distinguishing different pharmacological effects between concentrations above and below it.

Experiments were conducted using the method established in 2010 and published in Science. In that study, 5,648 compounds were screened, including 3,968 unique structures and 1,680 duplicates, recording over 60,000 behavioral profiles in total. According to stringent statistical criteria, 547 compounds (representing 463 unique structures) significantly altered behavior relative to that of controls. The sleep-wake fingerprint profiles of zebrafish treated with different concentrations of SUC were compared to the library of 547 compounds to identify the compound or series of compounds that most closely resembled the phenotypic effects of SUC.

The identified drug groups demonstrate significant impacts on cognitive function and neurological processes. Within the glucocorticoid category, Betamethasone and Dexamethasone have shown notable effects on cognitive function and neuroinflammatory processes. These compounds have particularly concerning impacts on hippocampal function and have been demonstrated to increase Aβ production in animal models. Additionally, they have been associated with enhanced tau pathology and accelerated cognitive decline in experimental studies. Similarly, Clobetasol Propionate and Flumethasone exhibit significant effects through altered glucocorticoid receptor expression, , while also impacting memory consolidation processes and neuroinflammatory responses.

In the category of neurotransmitter-system modulators, nicotinic compounds, specifically nicotine ditartrate and (−)-nicotine, have been shown to affect neural function primarily through receptor desensitization mechanisms and by impacting cholinergic function. Mecamylamine hydrochloride, another significant compound in this category, has been particularly notable for its documented effects on memory impairment, with studies indicating increased vulnerability in age-related contexts.

These results indicate that SUC’s neurotoxicity profile shares similarities with compounds associated with neurodegenerative-related phenotypes, particularly those linked to potential AD-like phenotypic alterations. Based on these findings and existing literature reports, we hypothesize that SUC may influence Aβ-related neurotoxicity and contribute to AD-like phenotypic alterations in zebrafish models.

Long-Term SUC Treatment Induces Transcriptomic Changes in Adult Zebrafish Brain Tissue

After a 3-month exposure to SUC, transcriptomic analysis of adult zebrafish brains compared to the WT group revealed that SUC significantly upregulated the expression of over 100 genes (Figure A, Figure C, and Figure E). Enrichment analysis (Figure B, Figure D, and Figure F) using the GO database identified several biological processes (BP) that were markedly upregulated in the treatment group, including protein folding; chaperone-mediated protein folding; “De novo” protein folding; “De novo” posttranslational protein folding; protein refolding; response to heat. These biological processes are closely related to protein folding and refolding. In AD-like phenotypes, the accumulation of misfolded proteins (such as β-amyloid and tau proteins) is a key pathological feature. Consistent with the enrichment analysis, several representative differentially expressed genes (DEGs) involved in protein folding and cellular stress responses were significantly altered, including heat shock protein family members (hsp70.1, hsp70.2, hsp70.3, hsp70l, and hsp90aa1.2), cochaperones (dnajb1b and hsph1), a mitochondrial protease (lonp1), and a stress-responsive transcription factor (fosab). These gene-level changes further support the activation of chaperone-mediated protein quality control and cellular stress-response pathways following chronic sucralose exposure, providing molecular evidence linking sucralose exposure to proteotoxic stressa key feature of AD-like neurotoxicity.

In terms of cellular components (CCs), significantly upregulated components included the neuronal cell body, cell body, mitochondrial matrix, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, synaptic vesicle membrane, intermediate filament cytoskeleton, cytoplasmic microtubule, presynaptic cytoskeleton, presynaptic active zone cytoplasmic component, and myelin sheath. These cellular components play crucial roles in neuron function and structure and may be affected by AD-like phenotypes.

Regarding molecular functions (MFs), the significantly upregulated functions included protein binding involved in protein folding; heat shock protein binding; ATPase activity; chaperone binding; unfolded protein binding; calmodulin-dependent protein kinase activity; and Hsp70 protein binding. These molecular functions are related to protein folding, cellular stress responses, and signal transduction, and potentially play important roles in the pathological processes of AD.

In summary, the transcriptomic data suggest that SUC treatment induced neurodegeneration-associated phenotypes in the zebrafish brain, affecting multiple aspects such as protein folding, cellular stress responses, and neuronal structure and function, all of which are closely related to the pathogenesis and pathological changes of AD. The accumulation of misfolded proteins, dysfunction of organelles, synaptic abnormalities, and changes in neuronal structure are significant features of AD-related alterations.

SUC Treatment Alters Neurotransmitter Levels in Adult Zebrafish Brain

Long-term treatment with SUC interfered with 7 neurotransmitters in adult zebrafish brains, including serotonin (5-HT), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), acetylcholine (ACh), norepinephrine (NE), dopamine (DA), glutamine (Gln), and cortisol. After standardization, the radar chart demonstrated that long-term SUC treatment exhibited typical hormesis (i.e., toxic excitatory effects): low concentration (1 mg/L) treatment increased the levels of all neurotransmitters (Figure H), while neurotransmitter levels decreased with increasing concentration (Figure I and Figure J). Specifically, the levels of AD-related neurotransmitters, including acetylcholine (ACh), glutamine (Gln), norepinephrine (NE), serotonin (5-HT), and dopamine (DA), decreased at high concentrations of SUC. Therefore, from the perspective of neurotransmitter levels, long-term SUC treatment increased the risk of AD-like phenotypic alterations in adult zebrafish.

Discussion

The environmental persistence and emerging toxicological profiles of artificial sweeteners have been recently reviewed. The current study presents evidence regarding the potential neurotoxic effects of SUC and its possible role in exacerbating AD-related pathological phenotypes in zebrafish models. Utilizing a comprehensive approach that combines high-throughput behavioral screening with targeted mechanistic studies reveals several key findings that warrant careful consideration.

Novel Screening Approach Identifies Potential Neurotoxicity

The innovative application of sleep-wake behavioral phenotyping in zebrafish larvae successfully identified potential neurotoxic effects of SUC. The behavioral fingerprinting approach revealed that SUC’s effects closely resemble those of compounds associated with neurodegenerative risk. This similarity is especially concerning, given these compounds’ documented associations with an increased risk of neurodegenerative conditions. , While previous safety assessments have primarily focused on acute toxicity and carcinogenicity, the findings suggest that the current safety evaluation paradigm may need to be expanded to include long-term neurological effects.

Chronic Exposure Effects Mirror AD-Like Symptoms

The behavioral alterations observed in adult zebrafish following chronic SUC exposure are particularly noteworthy. In the present study, adult zebrafish were subjected to 3 months of continuous exposure, which is a recognized design to simulate the long-term accumulation and progressive neurotoxicity of exogenous substances in humans. Given their short lifespan and rapid progression of AD-related pathology, zebrafish are therefore highly suitable for evaluating chronic neurotoxicity and neurodegenerative lesions. The high SUC concentrations (1–100 mg/L) used here exceed typical environmental levels but are commonly applied in zebrafish toxicology to ensure observable, reproducible phenotypes and clarify underlying mechanisms under controlled laboratory conditions. The increased anxiety-like behaviors, reduced exploratory activities, and impaired learning and memory capabilities closely parallel early symptoms of neurodegenerative disorders, including AD. , These behavioral changes were observed at concentrations within the currently acceptable daily intake (ADI) range, raising important questions about long-term safety at approved consumption levels. These behavioral findings are supported by recent primary studies in aquatic models. In zebrafish, acesulfame at sub-NOEC levels alters locomotor activity and anxiety; saccharin induces anxiety-like behaviors and memory deficits; sucralose-acesulfame mixtures evoke anxiety, oxidative stress, and AChE inhibition; and sucralose-stevia coexposure causes anxiogenic and cognitive deficits. In Daphnia, sucralose and acesulfame reduce heart rate and swimming activity. In the marine fish Sebastes schlegelii, acesulfame induces anxiety-like behavior via dopamine signaling. Collectively, these studies corroborate that chronic artificial sweetener exposure produces neurobehavioral alterations.

Molecular Evidence Consistent with Neurodegeneration-Associated Pathology

The transcriptomic analysis of brain tissue provides molecular evidence for SUC’s potential effects on pathways associated with neurodegeneration. The significant upregulation of genes involved in protein folding and stress response pathways aligns with known neurodegenerative pathology. This is particularly relevant given that protein misfolding and aggregation, especially of β-amyloid and tau proteins, are typical features of AD-related pathology. , The altered expression patterns of cellular components crucial for neuronal function and synaptic transmission further suggest potential neurodegenerative effects of SUC.

Neurotransmitter Alterations Support Behavioral Findings

The observed changes in neurotransmitter levels provide a mechanistic link between molecular alterations and behavioral outcomes. Long-term SUC exposure demonstrated characteristic biphasic neurotoxic effects, manifesting as elevated neurotransmitter expression at low doses and neurotransmitter depletion at high doses. These neurochemical disturbances were accompanied by impairments in learning and memory behaviors and a concentration-dependent increase in Aβ accumulation in transgenic zebrafish. Consistent with these neurochemical disruptions, transcriptomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of protein-folding and chaperone-related pathways, suggesting disruption of neuronal protein homeostasis. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that sucralose can induce cholinesterase-related biochemical responses in aquatic organisms, potentially affecting cholinergic neurotransmission. In the present study, we observed the suppression of monoaminergic neurotransmitters, indicating potential AD-associated neurotoxicity of SUC through metabolic modulation.

Direct Evidence from Transgenic Models

Perhaps the most compelling evidence comes from the transgenic zebrafish studies expressing the human misfolded Aβ protein. The dose-dependent increase in Aβ protein expression and accumulation, even at concentrations within the ADI range, provides direct evidence that SUC may accelerate AD-related pathology in zebrafish models. This finding is particularly concerning given the widespread use of SUC in the food supply.

Limitations and Future Directions

While the study provides strong evidence for SUC’s potential neurotoxic effects, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while zebrafish models offer numerous advantages for neurotoxicology research, evolutionary differences exist between zebrafish and humans. The two species differ in sweet taste receptor expression and metabolic clearance of artificial sweeteners, so direct extrapolation to human effects requires careful consideration. Second, the concentrations used in the adult exposure experiments exceed typical environmental levels; therefore, extrapolation to real-world human exposure should be made cautiously in future studies. In addition, species differences prevent zebrafish models from fully replicating the slow, multiyear progression of AD-like phenotypes in humans. Furthermore, the lack of direct brain-tissue sucralose quantification (e.g., LC-MS/MS) represents a limitation and underscores the need for future pharmacokinetic studies in teleost models. Finally, only female zebrafish were used to reduce experimental variability, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Notably, this design does not affect our core findings regarding the neurotoxicity of SUC in zebrafish. As a preliminary exploration, this study can be further explored by combining mammalian models and clinical evaluations to promote the translation of research findings into human applications.

Materials and Methods

General Experimental Pipeline

All embryos and adult zebrafish used in the experiment were visually screened to exclude malformed embryos and abnormal adult fish that could affect the experimental results. According to the European Chemicals Agency, the Fish Embryo Acute Toxicity (FET) test with zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryos, as described in OECD Test Guideline (TG) 236, has been designed as an alternative for acute fish toxicity testing, such as the OECD Acute Fish Toxicity Test (TG 203). Therefore, in the current research, FET was introduced to primarily determine the potential teratogenic effects of SUC.

The stock solution of SUC was prepared by dissolving SUC into ddH2O with a concentration of 1g/L. To offer comprehensive information on the potential neurotoxic effects of SUC, a wide range of concentrations (WT, 5 μg/L, 10 μg/L, 50 μg/L, 100 μg/L, 1 mg/L, 10 mg/L, 100 mg/L) was set. The final concentrations in this experiment were obtained by gradient dilution (WT, 5 μg/L, 10 μg/L, 50 μg/L, 100 μg/L, 1 mg/L, 10 mg/L, 100 mg/L).

To systematically investigate the potential neuroactive effects induced by SUC, acute neurotoxicity tests on embryonic zebrafish and chronic neurotoxicity tests on adult zebrafish were performed (as demonstrated in Scheme S1).

For the acute toxicological tests, the embryos were exposed to SUC from fertilization. At 2 hpf, embryonic epiboly was assessed. At 24 hpf, developmental retardation was evaluated. At 48 hpf, three embryonic developmental endpoints (hatching rate, hypopigmentation, and pericardial edema) were assessed to determine the potential developmental toxicity of SUC. From 4 days postfertilization (dpf), a 60-h locomotion test consisting of two light-dark cycles was conducted to obtain sleep/wake-based locomotion fingerprints. Importantly, all locomotion fingerprints at various SUC concentrations were correlated with those of activators or inhibitors of potential targets and pathways of action, suggesting potential neural targets and pathways for SUC.

To detect chronic neurotoxicological effects of SUC, 48 6-month-old adult zebrafish (all female to exclude sex-related differences in the experiment) were continuously exposed to SUC at concentrations of 1, 10, and 100 mg/L for 3 months. Subsequently, a cohort of neurobehavioral tests was conducted to comprehensively investigate phenotypic alterations. Following these tests, changes in transcriptomic levels and neurotransmitter content in the brain were examined. Furthermore, the potential neurotoxic effects were corroborated by comparing the target and pathway information predicted by pharmacodynamic fingerprinting with the results of transcriptomic sequencing. To investigate the potential neurotoxic effects of SUC and its impact on AD-related phenotypes, validation experiments were conducted using the zebrafish AD line Tg­(tol2-appb), which expresses human misfolded Aβ protein (green fluorescent marker).

Zebrafish Husbandry

Wild-type adult zebrafish (AB strain) were purchased from the China Zebrafish Resource Center. The fish were maintained in zebrafish recirculating water culture systems (Beijing ESEN Biotech. Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The aquaria temperature was maintained at 28.5 °C with a 14-h light/10-h dark cycle. Behavioral Phenotypic Screening Experiment Based on Sleep-Wake Cycles

A standard 96-well plate was used for sleep-wake locomotion tracking, with one individual placed into each well containing 650 μL of standard system water. The 96-well plates (8 × 12) were divided into eight groups based on SUC concentration (WT, 5 μg/L, 10 μg/L, 50 μg/L, 100 μg/L, 1 mg/L, 10 mg/L, 100 mg/L), with 12 replicate wells per concentration group.

The sleep-wake behavior assay was conducted according to previous reports. ,− Rest and wake locomotion activity was monitored and recorded for 60 h using a customized, automated video-tracking system (Viewpoint Life Sciences, France). The Zebrabox chamber was illuminated with white light from 9 AM to 11 PM and continuously with infrared light. The equipment parameters were set as follows: detection threshold, 40; burst, 25; and freeze, 4. The movement of each larva was captured at 30 Hz and recorded using the quantization mode with 1min time bins.

Detailed methods were demonstrated in previous articles. Specifically, the total experiment time was set to 60 h, with alternating day and night parameters: light-on time (9:00 AM), light-off time (11:00 PM), light intensity at 100%, and data recorded every 60 s. The observation program was started by clicking the START button. After 60 h, the STOP button was clicked to output the recorded data. The Zebrabox automatically recorded the number of movements per minute for each fish over a continuous 60-h period. Five behavioral parameters were set to demonstrate the rest/wake behavior of larvae and generate neurobehavioral pharmacodynamic fingerprints. These parameters are sleep_total, sleep_bouts, sleep_boutlength, activity_total, and waking_activity. ,, Sleep in larval zebrafish was defined as movement lasting less than 0.1 s in a minute, and a rest bout was defined as a continuous sequence of rest minutes lasting 2 min or longer. The five parameters were specifically defined as follows:1)Sleep_total (min): the sum of rest time during day and night.2)Sleep_bouts: the number of rest bouts during day and night.3)Sleep_bout_length (min): The average length of a series of rest bouts.4)Activity_total (min): The average time of movement during the day and night period.5)Waking activity (min): The average time spent exercising during waking periods (excluding all time defined as rest).

Four fish per test group were used, with three independent experiments conducted for each treatment group, totaling 12 fish. Each parameter value for each period was the average of three experiments. As the experiment lasted for 2 days and 2 nights, multiple parameters were extracted. These parameters were normalized to form a feature vector by using the blank treatment group data as the base. Hierarchical clustering and K-means clustering were performed using Cluster 3.0, and visualized with Java TreeView. To explore the potential targets and mechanisms of action of SUC, the pharmacodynamic fingerprints of 547 compounds representing 463 unique structures with known targets and mechanisms of action were selected for clustering and correlation analysis with SUC. All behavioral data were normalized into neurobehavioral pharmacodynamic fingerprints, which were used to link the chemical structure of SUC to its possible targets and mechanisms of action.

RNA-Seq Analysis of Brain Samples

Brain tissue from adult zebrafish subjected to chronic SUC exposure (3 months) was harvested immediately after behavioral testing for RNA-seq analysis. For each concentration group, brain tissue from three zebrafish was used for transcriptomic assays.

Raw Data and Quality Control

A paired-end 150 bp sequencing strategy was employed to sequence the transcriptome using the Illumina sequencing platform. After RNA sample quality verification, eukaryotic mRNA was enriched using magnetic beads with Oligo­(dT) (for prokaryotes, mRNA was enriched by removing rRNA with a kit). The mRNA was then fragmented by addition of a fragmentation buffer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using six-base random hexamers with mRNA as the template, followed by the addition of a buffer, dNTPs, DNA polymerase I, and RNase H. Second-strand cDNA was subsequently synthesized. The purified double-stranded cDNA was end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with sequencing adapters by using AMPure XP beads. Fragment size selection was then performed with AMPure XP beads. Finally, PCR amplification was carried out, and the PCR products were purified with AMPure XP beads to obtain the final library. After library construction, initial quantification was performed using a Qubit 2.0 instrument, and the library was diluted, followed by detection of the insert size using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Once the insert fragment met expectations, the effective concentration of the library was accurately quantified using qPCR to ensure library quality.

The latest zebrafish genome (version GRCz11) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/grc/zebrafish) was used as the reference genome, and clean data were mapped onto the genome using HISAT software.

Screening for Differentially Expressed Genes

The DESeq2 software was used to identify differentially expressed genes for each sample, with expression normalized across samples. The criteria for differential gene screening were |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and baseMean ≥ 100. BaseMean represents the average expression value across samples, and genes with baseMean ≥ 100 were selected to ensure sufficient expression levels. A |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 indicates differential upregulation of ≥2-fold or downregulation of ≤ 0.5-fold.

GO and KEGG Enrichment Analysis

Differentially expressed genes were divided into upregulated and downregulated sets, and GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed separately. The clusterProfiler software package was used for the analyses. Screening was performed with an FDR of <0.05, and entries meeting this criterion were considered significantly enriched.

Measurement of Neurotransmitter Content in the Brain

One zebrafish was randomly selected from each experimental group for neurotransmitter analysis. The head was removed and weighed, and then homogenized in ultrapure water to achieve a concentration of 1 mg/μL. The homogenate was further diluted to 0.5 mg/mL with ultrapure water. Protein precipitation was performed by adding three volumes of acetonitrile to the diluted homogenate. The mixture was thoroughly vortexed and centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 4 min. A 50 μL aliquot of the supernatant was collected for analysis. Samples from each group were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS). The peak spectra of dopamine, acetylcholine, glutamine, norepinephrine, γ-aminobutyric acid, and cortisol in zebrafish brain samples were identified by comparison with standard controls. Neurotransmitter concentrations were determined using peak area-concentration standard curves derived from the standard controls. Each neurotransmitter was quantified using the external standard method.

Transgenic Zebrafish Model for Assessing SUC-Induced Aβ-Related Pathological Phenotypes

Zebrafish Exposure and Treatment

Tg­(tol2-appb:APPsw-P2A-EGFP-PA-intron) transgenic zebrafish were crossed with AB wild-type zebrafish for breeding. Four dpf embryos were used for all experiments. Randomly selected 4 dpf zebrafish were placed in 6-well plates, with 10–30 fish per well, depending on the experiment. Each well contained 4 mL of solution. SUC concentrations were set at 1 mg/L, 10 mg/L, and 100 mg/L. Each concentration was tested in triplicate, and the plates were incubated at 28 °C for 48 h.

Confocal Imaging

To inhibit pigment formation, 0.003% PTU was added to the zebrafish at 24 hpf. After treatment, zebrafish were fixed in 1.5% low-melting-point agarose and imaged using a 40× water-immersion objective on a confocal microscope. The excitation wavelength was set to 488 nm, and all images were captured by using identical parameters. No separate wild-type nontransgenic control group was established to rule out tissue autofluorescence, as the fluorescence signal originates from the APPsw-EGFP transgene, not from endogenous tissue autofluorescence, and wild-type zebrafish lack this transgene and do not produce a specific GFP signal. In a previous study, the brain region of wild-type zebrafish showed virtually no detectable signal at the 488 nm excitation wavelength. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Gene Expression Analysis

Following SUC exposure, samples were collected with three replicates per group, resulting in six tubes in total, each containing 30 zebrafish. RNA was extracted by using Trizol reagent. Data analysis and graphing were performed by using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides substantial evidence that chronic SUC exposure, across a range of low to high doses, including levels relevant to typical intake, induces significant neurotoxic and behavioral effects, potentially contributing to AD-like phenotypes in zebrafish models. These findings suggest that SUC may be associated with AD-like phenotypes, underscoring potential risks to human neurological health, given its widespread environmental persistence and dietary use. While further studies are required to fully elucidate the implications for human health, these results suggest that current safety guidelines for SUC consumption may need to be reconsidered in light of potential neurodegenerative risks.
