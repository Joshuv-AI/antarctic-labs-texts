Herbal concoction usage before labor, mode of preparation, and association with child and maternal outcomes in urban Ghana: a concurrent mixed-method study
Authors: Seth Christopher Yaw Appiah, Abigail Nkromah Asieduwaa, Martin Klu, Dorcas Sekyi, Andrew Nketsia Arthur, Richard Ofori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study explored herbal concoction use before labor, mode of preparation and association with child and maternal outcomes in urban Ghana.MethodsThe study employed a concurrent mixed-method design involving 376 mothers at Tafo Government Hospital and Manhyia District Hospital in Kumasi Metropolis, Ghana. Data was collected using a structured questionnaire. Women who had given birth and were within first to sixth weeks after delivery and consented to interview were engaged for quantitative interviews. Women who indicated ever using herbal concoction were selected for qualitative interviews. Thematic analysis informed the qualitative analysis while the quantitative data was analyzed using a binary logistic regression model with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsKnowledge of herbal concoctions was high among 358 (95.6%). Prevalence of herbal usage was reported among 67(17.9%). The majority of the women who used herbal concoctions took it throughout their pregnancy 32 (47.7%), used it to induce labor 23 (34.3%) and produced desired results for 64 (95.5%) users. Women of age 26 and above [aOR = 2.150, 95% CI = 1.342–3.446, p = 0.001] were 2.15 times more likely to use herbal concoctions during pregnancy compared to those of lesser years 15–25. Women with other occupations [aOR = 2.677, 95% CI = 1.402–5.110, p = 0.003] were more than 2 ½ times more likely to use herbal concoctions compared to those in the security and banking sectors. Women who underwent episiotomy had significantly higher odds of taking herbal medicines during pregnancy [aOR = 5.576, 95% CI = 2.360-13.178, p = 0.001] than those who did not undergo episiotomy. Mothers with complication experience after birth had reduced odds of consuming herbal concoctions [aOR = 0.286, 95% CI = 0.094–0.870, p = 0.027] prior to labour. Thematic analysis revealed women used concoctions to facilitate labor, for therapeutic value, while socio-cultural influence and secrecy shielded herbal concoction use prior to labour from healthcare providers.ConclusionImplementing evidence-based educational programs on the possible dangers of using herbal concoctions and correct use where evidence supports efficacy of use remains an effective way of decreasing the abuse and incorrect usage of herbal medication by pregnant women in Ghana.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05474-4.Keywords: Herbal concoction, Labor, Traditional medicine, Maternal outcomes, Ghana

Introduction

Utilization of herbal medicine is a crux for maternal healthcare delivery, particularly in developing regions. Herbal medicine is characterized as products containing active substances from plants used for their intended therapeutic effect [1]. This can be used alone or in combination with treating, diagnosing, and preventing illnesses and preserving health. Herbal medications include herbs, herbal materials, herbal preparations, and final herbal products that include active substances derived from plants or other plant components [2]. In many settings, pregnant women and other vulnerable groups remain heavily dependent on these medicinal plants for health and safety [3, 4]. The utilization of herbal medicine is a global phenomenon, with prevalence rates in the general population rising significantly across high-income regions such as North America and Europe [5–7]. About 40% of all medical treatment provided in Asian nations like China is provided through traditional medicine [8]. However, in Africa, this usage is more than a cultural preference. It is a central feature of the maternal healthcare system [9]. Up to 80% of people in the African region have been noted turn to traditional medicine to aid them with their medical needs [10]. The utilization of herbal medicine among pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa indicates significant regional variations, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 12% to 93% [11]. This wide variety is largely owed to the urban-rural differences in the availability of conventional maternal health services, lower education, usage before the index pregnancy, age, self-medication practices, economic position, and pregnant trimester across the continent [12–14]. They are found in homes and serve as a source of primary healthcare, most especially for developing nations like Ghana [15]. While the role of herbal medicine in pregnancy, delivery, and postpartum care is firmly established in socio-cultural tradition and has been widely recognized since the precolonial era [16], there remains a paucity of empirical data regarding contemporary usage patterns prior to labour and their associated clinical outcomes. Most existing evidence is focused on rural populations, leaving a significant gap in understanding how traditional practices intersect with obstetric care in urban settings. Earlier studies reporting on the matter advance that the prevalence of herbal medicine usage among pregnant women in Ghana was 43.5% before pregnancy and 52.7% during pregnancy [17]. Evidence shows pregnant women utilize herbal medicine, most typically ginger, neem leaves, fenugreek, garlic, damakesse, peppermint, bitter leaf extracts and guava leaf tea [18–20]. Herbs are often used during delivery, with most herbs including raspberry, peppermint, and ginger, used as teas or infusions during pregnancy [21]. They are considered safe and the recommended consumption level is limited to two cups per day. In other studies, ginger and peppermint have been reported to be most commonly used during the 1st and 2nd trimesters, such that the common sources of information on herbal medicines are from relatives, self-sourced, sought from the internet or based on the advice of an apothecary, and all to induce labor [22]. What remains yet unknown is how during pregnancy, herbal concoctions are prepared and how the usage influences maternal and neonatal outcomes. Interest in herbal treatments has risen without data on safety, effectiveness, or rates of usage during pregnancy [23]. Pregnancy causes physical and physiological changes in the female body, resulting in a variety of pregnancy-related issues such as nausea, vomiting, constipation, and heartburn. Because women get worried about the foetus’ safety, pregnant women often choose natural medicinal plants (MPs) instead of prescribed medications to cope with the changes [24]. The popularity of herbal medicine among pregnant women is mostly due to the prevalent notion that herbs are natural and free of any side effects that are normally connected with conventional medications [14]. In Ghana, more than a third of herbal medicines used during pregnancy contained harmful substances or non-standardised [25]. Although evidence of postnatal complications like severe bleeding, vaginal, discharge, surgical infection, fainting, coma, high fever, pelvic pain, urinary incontinence, and bowel incontinence has been confirmed [16], the impact of herbal medicine on pregnancy outcomes is not well-studied beyond the perception level to actual or exact, leaving many aspects unclear. Further research is needed to understand its potential benefits, risks, and safety for both mother and baby.

Earlier work [26] cautions that inducing labor is a common obstetric practice and that cervical ripening should be assessed before any medicine is selected and introduced. In nearly all earlier studies on herbal medicine use among pregnant women in Africa [9, 27–29] and [16, 17, 30] Ghana, the focus has largely been on the types, prevalence, and factors associated with its usage. There is very little known and documented about the association between the usage of herbal medicine and maternal and neonatal outcomes of pregnant women in Ghana. A holistic understanding will require investigation to empirically determine how the usage of herbal medicine impacts maternal and neonatal outcomes beyond what is perceived. This is crucial for developing targeted interventions grounded in evidence to regulate the use of herbal medicine by pregnant women in Ghana. In the context of this study, we define herbal concoction as a non-standardized extract produced from raw plant materials for use. This study examines herbal concoction use before labor, mode of preparation, and association with child and maternal outcomes in urban Ghana. Specifically, we aim to determine the prevalence and socio-demographic and cultural predictors of herbal concoction intake, the modes of preparation, and motivations for use.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employs a concurrent mixed-method design. This involved the collection of both qualitative and quantitative data simultaneously [31] from two hospitals: Tafo Government Hospital and Manhyia District Hospital in the Kumasi Metropolis of Ghana. Both urban hospitals cater to a diverse population, enabling a comprehensive understanding of herbal concoction usage among different urban demographics. The quantitative arm was a cross-sectional survey employed to determine prevalence and associations. The qualitative arm involved a phenomenological design to explore the experiences of pregnant mothers who utilized herbal concoctions. The complementary nature of the design makes the study findings more robust and aids in a holistic perusal of this subject matter.

Sampling and sample size estimation

The study population consisted of women of reproductive age who visited the two designated study areas. Eligibility criteria include: postpartum women between their first and sixth week after delivery, and women who received maternal care at selected study sites. For the qualitative arm, women must have specifically indicated the use of herbal concoctions during their pregnancy and provided additional consent for an interview. Women who were critically ill or experiencing severe post-delivery complications were excluded from the study.

Guided by the Cochran (1977) formula for larger populations,

A total of 384 postpartum women were sampled for the quantitative arm of the study. Where n is the required sample size, Z is the critical value of the normal distribution at a 95% confidence level (1.96), and p is the prevalence of herbal concoction use. Since the prevalence is unknown, 50% (0.5) was used to ensure a maximum sample size, and d denotes the margin of error, expressed as a proportion of 0.05. After adjusting for incomplete data, a final sample of 375 postpartum women was used for the analysis. Postpartum mothers were selected from the labor ward and/or postnatal facilities, including weighing units. A simple random sampling technique was adopted to select the respondents. To ensure representative data from both study areas, proportional allocation was used based on attendance frequency for the health facilities. Postpartum women who were identified to have used herbal medicine and consented to interviews were engaged in the qualitative aspect of the study. This arm utilized a purposive sampling technique where women who met the criterion for herbal concoction usage were invited for in-depth interviews. The selection process ended when data saturation was reached.

Data collection procedure

An interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to collect data on herbal medicine usage among postpartum women in the study setting. The questionnaire was developed after a careful review of the literature [16, 29, 30]. The questionnaire was pretested in one of the study’s sites to ensure that issues with question clarity, construction, and response options were addressed before data collection began. Data collection was completed within five months. A structured interview guide was used to obtain qualitative data from participants. The questionnaire and interview guide were constructed in the English Language and administered in the local Asante Twi, the widely spoken Ghanaian language within the community and its neighboring towns to ensure participants accurately express their deeply held nuances and beliefs. Translation was done by a professional belonging to the research team fluent in English as well as Twi and conversant of the research context. Each interview lasted for about 45 min. Expert review processes were conducted to refine the interview guide content. The constructed interview guide was consistent across interviews, having flexibility for probing and follow-up questions based on participants’ responses. Measurement

Outcome variable was herbal concoction use measured as a binary variable with a “Yes” and “No” response to the question “Did you use herbal concoction during pregnancy?” The independent variables were informed by an extensive review of literature [16, 29, 30], which included age, occupation, educational level, knowledge, beliefs, neonatal and maternal complications. Maternal outcome variables were measured as categorical variables, including mode of delivery and performance of episiotomy. Neonatal (child) outcomes were measured by the presence of complications after birth reported by the mother, and were verified against health records. Variables were primarily treated as categorical. Continuous data, such as age, were categorized into clinically and sociologically relevant groups to enhance the interpretability.

Data analysis

The study’s close-ended questionnaire was pre-coded. Data was entered, cleaned, and checked using a computer-assisted data analysis software, SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., 2011, NY), after which data was presented in frequencies, descriptive statistics from which inferences have been drawn on the population of study. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Frequency tables were used to summarize categorical and continuous variables. Quantitative data were analyzed using binary logistic regression to determine the independent predictors of herbal concoction use. This model was fitted due to the dichotomous nature of our outcome variable. Bivariate analysis was done, and a multivariate regression analysis was consequently employed to control for potential confounders and identify the most significant predictors of herbal concoction use. The qualitative data, which was audio recorded, was transcribed into English language texts, and the analyzable texts were exported to a computer. The analyzed texts were coded before entering them into NVivo 8 analysis software. Coding these texts required identifying the frequencies of the answers or relating the question to the open-ended questions of other variables. For transcribed texts and audio files, we adopted Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic content analysis to determine the presence of peculiar concepts, themes, and/or words [32]. This analytic tool helped to measure and analyze key concepts in the data. With this analysis, we were able to examine patterns in a replicable and systematic manner and also assess the meanings and relationships of themes and concepts in the results. Saturation was considered when no new or varying themes or concepts emerged from the concluding interviews, implying the issue under investigation is extensively captured. The identified themes were used for the qualitative write-up.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was sought from respective bodies before the commencement of data collection for this study. Ethical clearance was given by the Humanities and Social Science Research Ethics Committee of KNUST (HuSSREC/AP/S/VOL.2), administration of hospitals involved and informed consent was sought from participating mothers. Participants were educated on the purpose of the study and were assured of confidentiality. Mothers involved in this study were informed about the voluntary nature of the study and their liberty to leave the study without any consequences. All respondents who participated in this study signed a written informed consent. For non-literate participants, the consent form was explained in Asante Twi, and verbal consent was obtained. Respondents’ personally identifiable information was not collected to ensure anonymity. We engaged in reflexivity practices to mitigate researcher-imposed bias. We kept a reflexive journal in the research process to record and critically assess any preconceived notions, opinions, or biases that potentially could affect the collection of data, analysis, and interpretation of findings. Additionally, we excessively relied on verbatim quotations echoing participants’ voices throughout the qualitative findings.

Binary and multivariable logistic regression on predictors of herbal concoction usage before labor

The binary logistic regression shows the independent predictors of herbal concoction usage before labor among postpartum women in the study setting. Women who were 26years and above [cOR = 1.499, 95% CI = 1.073–2.094, p = 0.018] were 1 ½ times more likely to use herbal concoction as compared to those found between the group of 15–25 years. Women who were engaged in other occupations [cOR = 1.615, 95% CI = 1.126–2.316, p = 0.009] had significantly higher odds of using herbal concoctions as compared to those involved in security and banking at the crude level. Persons with other levels of education [cOR = 0.732, 95% CI = 0.571–0.938, p = 0.014] were 27% less likely to consume herbal concoction during pregnancy compared to those who had only attained primary education. Women who faced complications after birth/delivery were less likely to utilize herbal concoctions compared to their counterparts [cOR = 0.389, 95% CI = 0.160–0.942, p = 0.036]. Women who had vaginal delivery were almost four times more likely to use herbal medicines during pregnancy as compared to those who had cesarean delivery [cOR = 4.354, 95% CI, p = 0.001]. Mothers who underwent episiotomy were nearly four times more likely to use herbal concoctions during pregnancy as compared to those who did not have episiotomy [cOR = 3.790, 95% CI, p = 0.001].

After controlling for other variables, significant variables were analyzed using the multilevel logistics regression. Herbal concoction use during pregnancy is statistically significantly influenced by the age of women most especially those of 26years and above [aOR = 2.150, CI = 95%, P = 0.001] who had an increased the odds of using herbal concoctions during pregnancy by 2.15 folds as compared to younger women aged 15–25 years. The results show that occupation particularly, women who engaged themselves in other occupation [aOR = 2.677, 95% CI = 1.402–5.110, p = 0.003] were more than and half times likely to use herbal concoctions as compared to those who were in security and banking, during pregnancy periods.

Complications after birth significantly predicted the use of herbal concoctions during pregnancy, the results found a statistical significance [aOR = 0.286, 95% CI = 0.094–0.870, p = 0.027], the odds of those who face complications after birth to take herbal concoctions during pregnancy has significantly decreased when compared to women who experienced complications during delivery. The results show that women who underwent episiotomy during delivery were threefold likely to utilize herbal preparations during pregnancy but after adjusting the odds increased to a fifth fold time [aOR = 5.576, 95% CI = 2.360-13.178, p = 0.001] as compared to those who do not undergo episiotomy; here the odds of those who have undergone episiotomy. The educational status of the women did not mediate the use of herbal concoction before labor [aOR = 0.872, 95% CI = 0.604–1.259, p = 0.465]. Findings are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

This is an analytical cross-sectional mixed-method study on an often neglected and misdiagnosed problem, the use of herbal concoctions by pregnant women before labor. The prevalence of herbal concoctions use during pregnancy in the present study was 17.9%. While this prevalence remains high, it is lower than studies conducted in Northern Uganda at 20% [28], Tanzania at a prevalence of 23% [33], and Ethiopia at 47% [34]. The use of herbal concoction before labor was dominant during the first and third trimester of pregnancy, where the first trimester is the stage at which pregnant women use herbal concoction for the prevention and treatment of early pregnancy ailments and issues. In the third trimester, our findings show that the herbal concoction is used by women largely to prepare the uterus for labor and delivery. In this study, herbal concoction usage was influenced by background-defining factors including age and occupation, reflecting consistent patterns with earlier studies [34, 35] although our present findings rather differ from those in Malaysia [36, 37] and Ethiopia [14], where age, occupation status, and education were not significant predictors of herbal concoction usage among pregnant women. While our established prevalence may appear low, it is important to note that our setting differs from previous studies conducted largely in rural communities [16, 38] and further characterized by high levels of underdevelopment and inadequate inadequate health care facilities in those rural communities. The present study rather was conducted in two urban areas and present a unique finding in terms of the use of herbal concoction as a very common practice among women in urban spaces. The nature of the herbal concoction preparation was known by some of the women, while other women had no idea of its nature. Among those who knew the nature of the concoction stated they had been prepared from given roots and stems of plants, while others were made from leaves. This finding affirms an earlier discovery that women were at the low end of the herbs knowledge in Malaysia in respect to the composition or what constitutes the prepared concoction for use [37].

In terms of the mode of preparation, the majority of the women had the herbs boiled into a liquid state, which were being administered to them, and others were asked to boil them on their own. Only one participant admitted to the fact that the concoction was ground and administered to the woman seeking health, and she was asked to add other plant parts to it before it could be taken. The women studied largely took the medication orally, while a few used it by smearing and through vaginal insertion. Insertion practice of herbal concoctions appears common, as has been noted by a previous study in South Africa [39] where ingestion had been reported as the primary mode of administering herbal medicine/concoction among pregnant women. A high number of postpartum mothers took the herbal medication in the early stages of pregnancy as compared to its intake in the later stages of their pregnancy. Although, this is not consistent with previous findings from Northern Uganda [28] and Malaysia [40], where most women used herbal concoctions during their second and third trimester. It demonstrates the trust many of the urban Ghanaians women had in the use of herbal concoctions. It also demonstrates the ease of use of herbal concoctions for sections of urban Ghanaian women. Our belief in why women in our setting employ herbal concoctions in the early stages stems from the cultural notion among many Ghanaians that the first trimester is the most crucial period in the pregnancy towards securing the viability of the fetus.

The use of herbal concoctions was high among women aged 26 and above compared to those below this age range. This shows that relatively old women in their thirties dominated the intake of herbal medicines during pregnancy as opposed to much younger women and very old women. This finding confirms an earlier work [41] which found that herbal concoction usage was common among individuals aged 35–44 and 45–54 in Jamaica. Very young women under 26 years with possible first time preganancy experience may not be privy to the varrying types of herbs used during pregnancy. While the usage of herbal concoction may complement western medication, it is plausible to explain that as people age and near their forties, they are more likely to explore all alternatives that may help them get a child, a societal value based on expectation. Young women may not use herbal medication because they may not have so many alternatives to explore and may not be worried so much about the lower odds of not being able to make a child even if their pregnancy is not successful, since age is considered to be on their side.

Complications after birth were a major predictor of the use of herbal concoctions by postpartum women. Findings from the multivariable regression model found a negative association between herbal use and general postpartum complications. Our quantitative results showed that the incidence of herbal concoction use was significantly lower among women who experienced postpartum complications compared to their counterparts. This finding is in contrast to a study conducted in Uganda where it was reported mothers with complications including postpartum hemorrhage were influenced to utilize herbal remedies [42]. This finding requires careful interpretation. In our sample, only one woman who utilized herbal concoctions reported postpartum complication. This may be attributed to the relatively healthy profile of the urban mothers surveyed or the small number of reported general complications in this group. The qualitative narratives indicated a nuanced view of psychological motivations behind herbal use. Some participants expressed that the fear of experiencing a difficult birth or potential complications influenced them to opt in for herbal remedies. Conditions affecting the bonding of the mother and baby and adverse health effects like insomnia and loss of energy in mothers. Our study has confirmed the extant literature [43–45] where women who used herbal medicine were reported to experience prolonged labor and caesarean sections, stillbirth, excessive uterine contraction, birth asphyxia, postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), rupture of the uterus, puerperal pyrexia, and maternal mortality.

The results indicated that the approach or how babies were born was also affected by the use of herbal concoction. Women who underwent episiotomy had vaginal delivery, were seen as the high consumers of herbal concoction usage during pregnancy as compared to those who underwent cesarean section. The performance of episiotomy has both positive and negative implications for both mothers and offspring. Episiotomy has been known to be useful by health care professionals when the baby’s positioning isn’t right, when the baby is too big, and when the mother’s pelvis is not proportionate to the baby’s head, requiring the mother to control the pushing of the baby. When it comes to its negative impact or implications on the mother, episiotomy is known to bring about hemorrhage, bladder injury, urethral injury, wound separation (episiotomy dehiscence), pain, chronic pain, and infection in the long run and sexual dysfunction. Herbal concoction intake affects the health of mothers and increases the cost of health care delivery when these problems become long-term effect. In respect to why women who underwent episiotomy had an increased likelihood of taking herbal medication prior to labor, it may be possibly linked to some chemicals in the herbs impair the ability of the women to push, thereby calling for episiotomy to save both baby and mother. This study finding, however, does not support the earlier study in Norway where women who took herbal drugs were less likely to undergo episiotomy compared to women who did not use herbal medicine [46]. Difference in study population, socio-cultural milieu and demographic variation may explain the differential outcome.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of our study lies in leveraging a mixed-method approach by integrating quantitative and qualitative data to provide a comprehensive depth and nuanced understanding of the phenomenon under study. Additionally, we sampled from multiple health facilities to mitigate biases. Even so, readers should be careful when interpreting the study findings. To begin with, while our study offers significant insights to herbal concoctions, we sampled women who received postnatal care at urban health facilities indicating the findings may not be generalizable to other geographical contexts. As highlighted in our qualitative theme of secrecy, many women conceal their use of herbal concoctions from healthcare providers to avoid judgment, implying some participants may have underreported the potency or usage of herbal concoctions simultaneously with conventional medicine. Lastly, the cross-sectional nature of the study provides a snapshot of associations. This design does not allow us to establish causality inferences. Nonetheless of the aforementioned limitations, our study offers evidence worthy of informing policies and strategies to address standardized and appropriate use of herbal remedies during pregnancy.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the ongoing awareness but relatively low prevalence of herbal concoction use among pregnant women in urban Ghana, with usage frequently motivated by the desire to induce labor or treat minor symptoms. Despite the perceived benefits, using herbal mixtures has significant health concerns, emphasizing the importance of focused, evidence-based public health initiatives. Educational programs aimed to dispel myths and educate pregnant women on the risks of unsupervised herbal use could be a key step towards encouraging better maternal health practices. These findings underscore a critical need for policy actors to improve antenatal education focusing on the specific risks of labor-inducing herbal concoctions rather than general discouragement. Collaboration between healthcare providers and traditional medicine users can improve culturally sensitive maternal care and reduce dependency on unregulated herbal treatments. Behavioral interventions should move beyond clinical discouragement toward a collaborative integration model involving religious and traditional heads as ambassadors to influence maternal behaviors and safe obstetric practices.
