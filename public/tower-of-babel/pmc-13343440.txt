Controlled analog Ising machine: An accelerated and accuracy-enhanced intelligent solver for edge computing
Authors: Langyu Li, Yu Pan, Wei Wang, Jinhu Lü, Qing Gao
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The proliferation of edge artificial intelligence, intelligent manufacturing, vehicular networks, and smart grids has created an urgent demand for hardware-efficient solvers capable of addressing large-scale combinatorial optimization problems in edge computing systems, amid increasing requirements for computational speed and accuracy to maintain low-latency and real-time features. Based on mature CMOS technology, analog Ising machines (AIMs), which exploit physical dynamical processes inspired by quantum computing to approximate solutions of quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problems, have emerged as a promising computing paradigm beyond the traditional von Neumann architecture. Despite their potential efficiency advantages, existing AIMs suffer from fundamental limitations arising from their uncontrollable autonomous Lyapunov dynamics, with an inherent trade-off between the reachability of ground states and computing speed.

In this letter, we illustrate a controlled AIM (CAIM) framework that fundamentally extends AIMs from autonomous physical systems to feedback-controlled dynamical optimizers. By treating the binarization strength as a time-dependent control variable and integrating the theory of control Lyapunov function (CLF) with asynchronous momentum-based optimization, CAIMs implement an intelligent control algorithm to accelerate computation processes and enhance solution accuracy and robustness in real time. Experiments on benchmark problems show that CAIM consistently outperforms conventional AIMs, making CAIM a scalable optimization architecture that aligns naturally with the requirements of real-time edge intelligence.

CAIM breaks the theoretical accuracy limitation of traditional AIM

Most combinatorial optimization problems, including some in task scheduling, path planning, and resource allocation, can generally be abstracted as a universal form of QUBO problems or equivalently as Ising problems1 as follows:(Equation 1)argminsH(s)=∑i,jJijsisj+∑ihisi,si∈{−1,+1},with s∈{−1,+1}n being the n-node binary variable, Jij being the coupling, and hi being the bias. The computational complexity of these problems grows exponentially with the system size n, making it impractical to rely solely on conventional von Neumann architectures for real-time large-scale optimization at the network edge.

In recent years, Ising machines have emerged as a specialized computing paradigm for solving QUBO problems, demonstrating the potential to overcome the limitations of traditional architectures. Among them, CMOS-compatible AIMs, such as oscillator-based Ising machines (OIMs), bistable resistively coupled Ising machines (BRIMs), and ring oscillator-based AIMs (ROSCs), are regarded as particularly promising candidates for edge deployment due to their fast solving speed, polynomial computational complexity, and highly parallel dynamical evolution.2,3,4,5 These systems are typically constructed using oscillatory circuits in CMOS technology, where devices are interconnected to form oscillator networks. By employing binarization mechanisms such as injection locking, AIMs realize a Lyapunov process in which the QUBO cost function serves as the system energy E(ϕ(t)) and decays autonomously over time, i.e., E˙(ϕ(t))≤0, thereby enabling its end states ϕ(tend) as approximate solutions to QUBO problems.2,6 ϕ(t) is the state vector describing the current phase of each oscillator. However, most existing AIMs operate as autonomous dynamical systems and are fundamentally constrained by their intrinsic dynamics, which is uncontrollable. On the one hand, strong binarization constraints help ensure solution correctness, the approximation metric of the theoretical optimal solution but significantly reduce system reachability—the probability of reaching ground states—causing the dynamics to become trapped in local minima. On the other hand, weakening the constraints improves exploration capability at the expense of the equivalence between the obtained solution and the original Ising formulation. In this letter, we present an adaptive injection-enhanced CAIM architecture that overcomes the aforementioned performance limitations at the level of system dynamics by introducing lightweight control loops into conventional AIMs. Unlike prior approaches that rely on fixed parameters or stochastic perturbations, we adopt a unified perspective rooted in control theory and non-convex optimization. Within this framework, AIMs are modeled as continuous optimization systems with regularization constraints, i.e.,(Equation 2)E(ϕ(t))=K(ϕ(t))+μR(ϕ(t)),with K(ϕ(t)) denoting the energy of oscillator networks, R(ϕ(t)) denoting the binarization energy, and μ acting as the strength of binarization. The Ising Hamiltonian H(s) can be approximated by K(ϕ(t)) under the condition E˙(ϕ(t))≤0.2 The final spin configuration that encodes the solution can be mapped from the state vector ϕ(tend) depending on the specific hardware implementation of AIMs. This formulation enables a systematic analysis of the fundamental role played by binarization strength in balancing solution equivalence and dynamical reachability.

Since the binarization strength is linearly related to the injection strength, CAIM extends the fixed injection strength used in conventional AIMs to a time-dependent, state-feedback control variable μ(t). By sampling the states of individual oscillators, a closed-loop controllable architecture can be established. Independent sampling of each oscillator provides access to the phase states of the oscillator network, and based on the analysis of these phase states, control algorithms can be designed to modulate the injection signals. Through injection modulation, transitions between arbitrary network states can be realized. This control architecture is generic and can accommodate both digital and analog controllers while allowing control algorithms to be tailored to specific application scenarios.

To enhance both the solution speed and correctness of AIMs, an intelligent control algorithm called the asynchronous adaptive control strategy that combines CLFs with momentum-based optimization is proposed by7(Equation 3)μ(t)≡μ(k)=−β1∇R(ϕ(k−1))·(ϕ(k−1)−ϕ(k−2))︸asynchronousmomentumterm+η∇E(ϕ(k−1))·∇R(ϕ(k−1))︸CLFterm,forkτ≤t<(k+1)τ.

By simultaneously partitioning the time axis of sampling and control into equal-length intervals τ, the strategy naturally accommodates interaction delays between the controller and the oscillator network, such as those arising from the computation time of digital controllers. The CLF component provides a steepest-descent direction for E(ϕ(t)) and guarantees that the system dynamics of CAIM continue to approximately satisfy the Lyapunov condition, ensuring a globally decreasing energy trend and accelerating energy dissipation.7 However, CLF-based control alone tends to drive the phase states toward local minima, and excessive delays may undermine Lyapunov stability. In contrast, asynchronous momentum methods facilitate guided transitions among local minima, improving solution correctness while enhancing Lyapunov stability by converting instability induced by asynchrony into bounded linear steady-state errors.8,9 The proposed strategy introduces effective second-order dynamical behavior, enabling the system to maintain strong exploration capability during early evolution and to avoid premature trapping in local minima. By adaptively regulating the strength of binarization constraints, the accuracy of the obtained solutions is significantly improved. This adaptive energy-landscape-shaping mechanism fundamentally breaks the performance limitations imposed by fixed regularization parameters in conventional AIMs.

Physical implementation and performance improvements of CAIM

At the hardware level, an inductor-capacitor (LC) oscillator network is employed as the analog computing core, and an field-programmable gate array (FPGA) is introduced as the digital control unit, as shown in Figure 1C, forming a tightly coupled hybrid analog-digital closed-loop system. The FPGA samples oscillator states at discrete time intervals, extracts phase information using fast sliding-window Fourier transforms, and dynamically adjusts the injection-locking strength of each oscillator according to the asynchronous momentum-based control law. Notably, the control loop does not require explicit recomputation of the coupling matrix, and its computational complexity scales quadratically with the number of nodes, making it well suited for edge deployment. Our prior theoretical analysis explicitly characterizes the impact of delay on stability and convergence accuracy, providing practical design guidelines for hardware implementation.

Experimental results demonstrate that CAIM exhibits significant performance improvements on multiple standard benchmark problems, including Max-Cut and the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick spin-glass model. In a fully connected 200-node prototype, as shown in Figure 1D, CAIM achieves approximately a 10% acceleration in convergence while maintaining stable operation, along with an average improvement of nearly one order of magnitude in solution success probability compared to conventional autonomous AIMs. These gains translate into an order-of-magnitude improvement in the efficiency metric known as time to solution (TTS).10 More importantly, the observed performance improvements do not rely on specific problem structures or fine-tuned parameters but remain robust across different initial conditions, noise levels, and asynchronous update periods, validating the generality of the proposed control framework.

Outlook

From the perspective of edge computing, CAIM introduces a new system-level collaborative paradigm: high-dimensional energy relaxation and parallel exploration are handled by the analog oscillator network, while global energy landscape modulation and convergence guidance are performed by a lightweight digital control unit. This division of labor not only reduces dependence on high-precision digital computation but also mitigates the limited controllability inherent in purely analog systems, thereby laying a foundation for programmable and interpretable optimization accelerators at the network edge. Looking forward, this architecture is expected to integrate deeply with edge AI model compression, online scheduling, and adaptive decision-making tasks and to serve as a fundamental computational primitive for next-generation edge intelligence.

In summary, by introducing controlled injection and adaptive feedback mechanisms, this work extends the capability boundaries of AIMs from both theoretical and hardware perspectives and demonstrates an efficient and scalable path for combinatorial optimization in edge computing scenarios. We believe that this deep integration of control theory, non-convex optimization, and emerging computing hardware will provide new insights into the development of specialized computing architectures.
