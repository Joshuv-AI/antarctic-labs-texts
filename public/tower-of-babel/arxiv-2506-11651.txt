A short proof of a central limit theorem for the order of the giant component and k k -core
Michael Anastos 1, Joshua Erde 2 and Mihyun Kang 2 and Vincent Pfenninger 2

Abstract
. In this note we outline a new and simple approach to proving central limit theorems for various ‘global’ graph parameters which have robust ‘local’ approximations, using the Efron–Stein inequality, which relies on a combinatorial analysis of the stability of these approximations under resampling an edge. As an application, we give short proofs of a central limit theorem for the order of the giant component and of the k k -core for sparse random graphs.



1.
Introduction

In discrete probability, and in particular the theory of random graphs, there has been much interest in establishing
central limit theorems
for various parameters of interest. More precisely, given a sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables defined on the probability spaces
G(n,p)
, we are interested in when

X_{n}\coloneqq\frac{Z_{n}-\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{n}\right]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\left[Z_{n}\right]}}\quad\overset{d}{\to}\quad\mathcal{N}(0,1),

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, that is, when
X_{n}
converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution. Here, by
G(n,p)
we denote the binomial random graph, that is, the random graph on
n
vertices where each edge appears independently with probability
p
.

Early work on this topic established such results for various types of
subgraph counts
in random graphs, and many different techniques have been developed to deal with these parameters — the method of moments
[
17
,
18
,
25
]
, Stein’s method
[
4
]
,
U
-statistics and related methods
[
15
,
21
]
, and martingale methods
[
3
,
9
]
. Moreover, these methods can also be used to show
multivariate central limit theorems
for finite collections of subgraph counts, establishing
joint normality
of the rescaled random variables. In particular, this implies that, for appropriate ranges of
p
and excluding some pathological cases, that any finite linear combination of these parameters also satisfies a central limit theorem, which in turn implies central limit theorems for a broad class of ‘local’ graph parameters — those which can be determined by looking at bounded-order subgraphs of
G(n,p)
.

More recently, central limit theorems have been proven for more ‘global’ graph parameters. An early example of this comes from the work of Pittel and Wormald on the order of the giant component in a supercritical sparse random graph
[
23
,
24
]
which used enumerative/analytic methods to establish a central limit theorem. More modern proofs
[
5
,
6
]
involve the analysis of a graph exploration process using martingale methods. Central limit theorems have also been shown for the order of the
k
-core
[
8
,
16
]
and very recently the rank and the matching number
[
13
,
14
,
19
]
, both of which analyse certain peeling processes on
G(n,p)
, using martingale methods or the differential equations method, respectively.

Recent work of the authors
[
2
]
establishes a central limit theorem for another ‘global’ graph parameter, the
circumference
, which is the the length of the longest cycle. Rather than analysing the evolution of some random process, the proof instead employs a
perturbative
argument using more elementary, combinatorial methods based on the Efron–Stein inequality, using the joint normality of certain subgraph counts as a ‘black-box’. However, the application in
[
2
]
is still quite complicated and technical, due in part to certain ‘non-monotonicty’ issues that arise within the structural analysis in the particular case of the circumference.

The purpose of this note is to demonstrate the simplicity of the method deveolped by the authors in
[
2
]
by giving some short proofs of known results of central limit theorems — for the order of the giant component and the
k
-core in supercritical sparse random graphs.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
c>1
, let
p=\frac{c}{n}
and let
L
be the largest component in
G(n,p)
. Then
|L|
satisfies a central limit theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k\geq 3
, and let
\hat{c}_{k}>k
be the unique real number such that

(1)

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{Poisson}\left(\hat{c}_{k}\right)\leq k-1\right]=(e\hat{c}_{k})^{-1}.

Let
c>\hat{c}_{k}
, let
p=\frac{c}{n}
and let
K
be the
k
-core of
G(n,p)
. Then
|V(K)|
satisfies a central limit theorem.

It is straightforward to show that
\hat{c}_{k}
is well defined and that
\hat{c}_{k}=k+\sqrt{2k\log k}+O(\sqrt{k})
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
For comparison, the threshold value of
c
for the existence of a (non-empty)
k
-core is shown in
[
22
]
to be asymptotically
k+\sqrt{k\log k}+O(\log k)
, and so, while
Theorem

1.2
is not quite sharp, it holds very close to the threshold. We also note that with a bit more care, it is possible to extract quantitative bounds on the variances in
Theorems

1.2
and
1.1
, as in
[
2
]
.

The proofs for both
Theorems

1.2
and
1.1
follow the same rough idea: We take a sequence of ‘local’ approximations to the order of the giant component /
k
-core by taking, for each vertex
v\in V(G)
, an approximation of the indicator of the event that
v
lies inside the giant component /
k
-core, based on the ‘local’ structure of
G\sim G(n,p)
around
v
, taking into account larger and larger neighbourhoods as our sequence develops.

A relatively standard application of Stein’s method will show that each of these local approximations satisfies a central limit theorem and so, modulo some small technical details, it is sufficient to bound in a sufficiently strong manner the errors of these approximations, which we do using the Efron–Stein inequality to bound the variance of these errors.

Roughly, the Efron–Stein inequality allows us to bound the variance of graph parameter by bounding the expected effect of resampling a single edge. A simple combinatorial analysis will show that, in both cases, the effect of resampling an edge
e=uv
on the error of the approximation is in some way ‘localised’ within the components
C_{u}
and
C_{v}
containing
u
and
v
of the
remainder
/
mantle
, the graph
R
remaining after we delete the giant component or
k
-core, respectively. However, simple first moment calculations demonstrate that, for sufficiently large
c
, the component structure of
R
resembles that of a subcritical random graph, and so the expected effect of resampling an edge is ‘small’.

In the case of the giant component, this holds for any
c>1
, whilst for the
k
-core we require that
c(k)
is sufficiently large (see (
1
)). In fact, heuristically this should hold already for any
c
above the threshold for the existence of a (non-empty)
k
-core, although there does not seem to be a simple proof of this fact, and it is unclear if it follows directly from known results in the literature. However, we note that such a result, stating that the mantle has a subcritical distribution for a certain range of
p
, is all that would be required to extend the proof of Theorem
1.2
to this range of
p
.
We note also that, whilst the statement and proof of Theorem
1.2
extends without change to the case
k=2
, in fact similar calculations as that for the giant component show that the threshold for the appearance of a linear
2
-core coincides with that for the giant component, and that the ‘mantle’ of the
2
-core will have a subcritical distribution for any
c>1
. It is then possible to show an analogue of Theorem
1.2
for
k=2
holds for any
c>1
using similar methods, although we omit the details.

Finally, we note that, in many cases, it can be shown that multiple parameters of interest simultaneously satisfy a
multidimensional central limit theorem
. For example, Pittel and Wormald
[
23
,
24
]
showed that the order of the giant component and the order of the
2
-core, when appropriately rescaled, are jointly Gaussian. It would be interesting to know if our methods could be extended to the multidimensional case, reducing then the derivation of a multidimensional central limit theorem to the combinatorial analysis of the simultaneous effect of resampling an edge on some collection of parameters.

2.
Preliminaries

In this paper, we consider a
component
to be a maximal
set of vertices
that induces a connected subgraph. For a graph
G
and a vertex set
A\subseteq V(G)
, we define
G[A]
to be the
induced subgraph of
G
with respect to
A
, that is, the graph
G[A]
with
V(G[A])=A
and
E(G[A])=\{xy\in E(G)\colon x,y\in A\}
. Moreover, we let
G-A\coloneqq G[V(G)\setminus A]
.

The
k
-core
of a graph
G
is defined to be the maximal subgraph
K\subseteq G
of minimum degree at least
k
, which is easily seen to be unique. A useful alternative characterisation of the
k
-core is given by the following
peeling process
. Starting with
G
, we recursively delete some vertex of degree less than
k
. Again it is easy to see that the resulting graph
K
is the
k
-core of
G
. We note that, for any vertex
w
which lies in a component
C_{w}
of
G-V(K)
, there is some
k
-degenerate ordering
of
C_{w}
, that is, an ordering of the vertices of
C_{w}
such that each vertex is adjacent to fewer than
k
vertices which appear before it or in
K
.

2.1.
Probabilistic inequalities

We use the following lemma, proved in
[
2
]
, which bounds the variance of ‘local’ graph parameters using the Efron–Stein inequality
[
11
]
by considering the effect of resampling an edge.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G\sim G(n,p)
be a random graph, let
(\psi_{G}(v)\colon v\in V(G))
be a collection of (isomorphism-invariant) random variables and let
Z\coloneqq\sum_{v\in V(G)}\psi_{G}(v)
. Let
f\in\binom{V(G)}{2}
be arbitrary and let
G^{+}=G+f
and
G^{-}=G-f
. Suppose that
M>0
is such that
|\psi_{G}(v)|\leq M
for each
v\in V(G)
and let

D\coloneqq\{v\in V(G)\colon\psi_{G^{+}}(v)\neq\psi_{G^{-}}(v)\}.

Then

\mathbb{V}\left[Z\right]\leq 2M^{2}p(1-p)n^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[|D|^{2}\right].

We will also use a probabilistic form of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, see for example
[
20
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Probabilistic Cauchy–Schwarz inequality)
.

For jointly distributed random variables
Y
and
Z
with
\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{2}\right],|\mathbb{E}\left[YZ\right]|<\infty
,

|\mathrm{cov}(Y,Z)|\leq(\mathbb{V}\left[Y\right]\mathbb{V}\left[Z\right])^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In particular,

(2)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{V}\left[Y\right]-\mathbb{V}\left[Z\right]\right|\leq\mathbb{V}\left[Y-Z\right]+2\left(\mathbb{V}\left[Y-Z\right]\mathbb{V}\left[Z\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In the proof we will consider sequences of approximations which are themselves defined on the sequence
G(n,p)
of random graphs. For this reason, when talking about limits, it will be useful to have a version of Slutsky’s Theorem
[
26
]
for ‘double limits’. It can easily be checked that the proof carries over into this setting.

Given a doubly-indexed sequence
(a_{\ell,n})_{\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers and
a\in\mathbb{R}
we write

(3)

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,n\to\infty\\
n\gg\ell\end{subarray}}a_{\ell,n}=a,

if there exists a function
N\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\ell_{0}
such that for all
(\ell,n)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
with
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
and
n\geq N(\ell)
we have
|a_{\ell,n}-a|<\varepsilon
. Given a doubly-indexed sequence
(X_{\ell,n})_{\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real random variables and a real random variable
X
, we write
X_{\ell,n}\overset{d}{\to}X
as
\ell,n\to\infty
if, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
at which
x\mapsto\mathbb{P}\left[X\leq x\right]
is continuous, we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,n\to\infty\\
n\gg\ell\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left[X_{\ell,n}\leq x\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[X\leq x\right].

Similarly, we write
X_{\ell,n}\overset{p}{\to}X
as
\ell,n\to\infty
if for all
\delta>0
,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,n\to\infty\\
n\gg\ell\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left[|X_{\ell,n}-X|\geq\delta\right]=0.

Theorem 2.3

(Slutsky’s Theorem
[
26
]
)
.

Let
(X_{\ell,n})_{\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}},(Y_{\ell,n})_{\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}},
and
(Z_{\ell,n})_{\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}}
be doubly-indexed sequences of real random variables. Let
Y
be a real random variable and let
x,z\in\mathbb{R}
. If
X_{\ell,n}\overset{p}{\to}x
,
Y_{\ell,n}\overset{d}{\to}Y
and
Z_{\ell,n}\overset{p}{\to}z
as
\ell,n\to\infty
, then

X_{\ell,n}+Y_{\ell,n}\cdot Z_{\ell,n}\quad\overset{d}{\to}\quad x+zY,\qquad\text{ as $\ell,n\to\infty$}.

2.2.
A central limit theorem for weighted neighbourhood sums

We will also need the following general result, which follows from work in
[
2
]
, which asserts a central limit theorem for a range of graph parameters which have a representation as a weighted neighbourhood count.

More precisely, let
\mathcal{H}
be the set of all rooted graphs
(H,r)
with radius
at most
\ell
and at most
n
vertices and let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of rooted trees
(T,r)\in\mathcal{H}
with
2\leq v(T)\leq t(c,\ell)
, for some appropriately large function
t
of
c
and
\ell
. A
weighted
\ell
-neighbourhood count
is a random variable of the form

X=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\sum_{(H,r)\in\mathcal{H}}\alpha_{(H,r)}\mathds{1}_{\{(G[B_{G}(v,\ell)],v)\cong(H,r)\}}

where
\alpha_{(H,r)}\in[0,1]
for each
(H,r)\in\mathcal{H}
and

B_{G}(v,\ell)
is the set of vertices at distance at most
\ell
from
v
.
Moreover, we define the
truncation
of
X
to be

Y=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\sum_{(T,r)\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{(T,r)}\mathds{1}_{\{(G[B_{G}(v,\ell)],v)\cong(T,r)\}}.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
c>1
and let
p=\frac{c}{n}
. If
X
is a weighted
\ell
-neighbourhood count and
Y
is the truncation of
X
, then the following hold.

(i)

\mathbb{V}\left[X-Y\right]\leq f_{1}(n,\ell)\cdot n
, where
\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,n\to\infty\\
n\gg\ell\end{subarray}}f_{1}(n,\ell)=0
.

(ii)

If
\mathbb{V}\left[Y\right]=\omega\left(n^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)
, then
Y
satisfies a central limit theorem.

For a proof see
[
2
, Lemmas 5.2 and 7.6]
.

2.3.
Typical properties of the remainder and mantle

We will require the following properties of a supercritical sparse random graph which follow from standard arguments, but for completeness we include a short proof.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let

Let

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}\right]=1-o\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)
.

Furthermore, let

be the component of
R^{-}
containing

and

\mathbb{E}\left[|W|^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{|W|\geq\ell\}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\right]\leq f_{2}(n,\ell),

where
\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,n\to\infty\\
n\gg\ell\end{subarray}}f_{2}(n,\ell)=0
.

Proof.

The first part of the lemma is a straightforward modification of a standard argument that appears in any textbook on random graphs (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
).

Furthermore, by the first part we can assume in the second part that all components of

have order at most

and then calculate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|W|^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{|W|\geq\ell\}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=\ell}^{\log^{4}n}j^{2}\binom{n-2}{j-2}j^{j-2}p^{j-2}(1-p)^{j(n-j)}+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=\ell}^{\log^{4}n}j^{2}\left(ce^{1-c+o(1)}\right)^{j-2}+o(1):=f_{2}(n,\ell)

and the conclusion follows since

.
∎

2.4.
Variance of the order of the giant and
k
-core

For our arguments, it will be important that the variance of the order of the giant component /
k
-core is linear in
n
, which can be shown by elementary considerations. We include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
k\geq 3
, let
\hat{c}_{k}
be as defined in (
1
), let
c>\hat{c}_{k}
and
p=\frac{c}{n}
, let
L
be the largest component of
G(n,p)
, and let
K
be the
k
-core of
G(n,p)
.

(V1)

If
c>1
, then
\mathbb{V}\left[|L|\right]=\Omega(n)
.

(V2)

If
c>\hat{c}_{k}
,
then
\mathbb{V}\left[|V(K)|\right]=\Omega(n)
.

Proof.

We just prove the second statement, the first can be proved with an analogous argument.

We utilise a sprinkling argument, taking
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
such that
(1-p_{1})(1-p_{2})=\frac{c}{n}
, where
p_{1}=\frac{c^{\prime}}{n}
, for some
c^{\prime}
such that
c>c^{\prime}>\hat{c}_{k}
thus
p_{2}=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
. Let
G_{1}\sim G(n,p_{1})
and
G_{2}\sim G(n,p_{2})
be independent random graphs and let
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
so that
G\sim G(n,p)
. By
Lemma

2.6
