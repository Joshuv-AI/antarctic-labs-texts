Persian version of the international consultation on incontinence questionnaire-pediatric lower urinary tract symptoms caregiver module: translation, validation and cultural adaptation
Authors: Mahdi Norouzi, Elham Aghaei, Farshid Alizadeh, Jalil Hosseini, Mahtab Zargham, Farshad Gholipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Pediatric Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (ICIQ-CLUTS), a well-established tool for assessing LUTS in children, is not available in Persian. This study aims to translate and validate this tool for Persian-speaking caregivers.MethodsThe ICIQ-CLUTS was translated and validated following ICIQ recommendations. The final version was self-administered by 67 caregivers of children aged 5 to 9 years. The study was conducted between June and November 2024. A concise clinical diagnosis of LUTS was used as a diagnostic reference. Internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) and test-retest reliability (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC) were computed to assess the reliability. Construct validity was measured through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) by principal component analysis (PCA) using varimax rotation. Discriminative validity was measured using ROC analysis and diagnostic accuracy metrics.ResultsOf the total 67 participants, 32 were cases. Internal consistency was good (α = 0.768), and test-retest reliability was excellent (ICC = 0.983). Standard error of measurement and minimum detectable change were 0.53 and 1.46, respectively. EFA found three factors—urgency, incontinence and bladder control, and voiding and bowel dysfunction—accounting for 72.1% of the variance. The questionnaire demonstrated excellent discriminative capability (AUC = 0.917, 95% CI: 0.848–0.986). Additionally, 75% sensitivity, 100% specificity, and 88.1% accuracy were obtained for a cut-off point of 15.5.ConclusionICIQ-CLUTS-Pe is psychometrically sound and culturally adapted to discriminate LUTS among Persian-speaking caregivers. The tool is validated to effectively quantify symptom severity and monitor treatment response in both clinical and research settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07386-1.Keywords: Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms, Questionnaire validation, Urination disorders, Pediatric

Introduction

Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) affect over 15% of children__ prominently females younger than 10 [1, 2]. According to the International Children’s Continence Society (ICCS), the LUTS encompasses storage, voiding, and late voiding phase symptoms, mostly categorized in four manifestations: frequency, urgency, daytime incontinence, and nocturnal enuresis [3]. An increasing prevalence of LUTS has been outlined in the literature [4]. Additionally, LUTS is closely associated with anatomical and behavioral comorbidities such as constipation, anxiety, and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Moreover, it stands as a risk factor for future psychological issues in adolescence [5]. The absence of definitive consensus on diagnosing and severity of pediatric LUTS, coupled subsequent difficulties in pediatric LUTS research underscores the development of a ubiquitous and standardized assessment. As stated by ICCS, the term LUTS, in essence, is more descriptive rather than quantitative; however, to explore this divergent yet significant challenge in children, more concrete insight is required.

A simple means of quantifying subjective LUT symptoms into scores is questionnaires. The scoring system allows for appraising the extent and controlling the therapeutic endpoints. A categorization for pediatric LUTS questionnaires is the functional and psychological ones [3]. For instance, the dysfunctional voiding symptom score (DVSS) or short screening instrument for psychological problems in enuresis (SSIPPE) represent examples of functional and psychological questionnaires, respectively [6, 7]. However, a more convenient and holistic tool is more practical in clinical settings. The International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Pediatric Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (ICIQ-CLUTS) is a self-administered 12-item questionnaire introduced by Gennaro et al. [8]. The original module was released in English, Italian, and German, and validated in the respective cohorts [8]. Later on, the Turkish, Spanish, and Chinese translations, validation, and contextually engaged ICIQ-CLUTS version were also released [9–11]. The child module of the ICIQ-CLUTS has already been validated by the author [12]. To date, no Persian caregiver-report version of ICIQ-CLUTS has been available nor psychometrically evaluated in Iran. Hence, due to the overwhelming burden, costly diagnostic approaches, and practical applications in clinical and research settings, this study aims to present the Persian translation, cross-cultural adaptation, and psychometric properties of caregiver-designed ICIQ-CLUTS for children aged 5 to 9 years (ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe).

Materials and methods

Translation, validation, and cross-cultural adaptation of the caregiver version of ICIQ-CLUTS were carried out according to ICIQ guidelines [13]. Measurement properties were assessed in accordance with COSMIN criteria, including reliability, structural validity, and measurement error [13, 14]. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the local Institutional Review Board (Approval No. IR.ARI.MUI.REC.1402.080). Informed consent was obtained from the participants. Also, consent from the original ICIQ-CLUTS authors was granted through email. As the main investigators suggested, with later replications in other translations, in this study only the caregiver version of the questionnaire was adopted for children aged less than 10, mainly due to limited literacy and cultural barriers [8, 11].

Participants

Participants were caregivers of the children who visited the pediatric urology clinic, a tertiary academic center at Alzahra Hospital, Isfahan, Iran. Sixty-seven parents of children aged 5 to 9 were recruited between June and November 2024 using convenience sampling (Fig. 1). Parents were asked to complete the questionnaire before the visit.

The flow diagram of the translation and backtranslation process of ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe

Adhering to the original study, we carried out a two-step inclusion criteria. The children were included if: (a) aged between 5 and 9 years old and (b) diagnosed as having/ not having LUTS. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) uncontrolled insulin-dependent diabetes, (b) have undergone urological procedures, (c) neurological disorders, and (d) uro-anatomical abnormalities. The parents of the included children were included in case of: (a) informed consent, (b) able to understand and complete the questionnaire, and (c) standing as the living and legal guardian of the children. The diagnosis of LUTS depended on a clinical evaluation performed by a urologist that was accompanied by the findings from laboratory tests. Therefore, the children who had LUTS were considered to be the cases, whereas the ones that did not have LUTS belonged to the control group.

Original module

We implemented the Caregiver (parents) version of ICIQ-CLUTS. The questionnaire is a 12-item self-administered survey for caregivers of children aged between 5 and 9 years. Items 1 and 2 address the age and sex, and item 3 addresses the presence of UTI in the past four weeks. The following 9 items mainly consider the LUTS symptoms. The responses are in a 4-choice Likert manner, with scores ranging between 1 and 4. Subsequently, the overall score falls between 9 and 36.

Forward translation

The translation and backtranslation process was conducted according to the ICIQ guideline (Fig. 1). At first, two independent bilingual translators (native Persian speakers) were invited to translate the original English questionnaire into Persian. Translators were unfamiliar with medical literature and were asked to translate conceptually rather than literally, in a way comprehensible to children at the age of 14. They were asked to preserve the essence and integrity of the original questionnaire while resonating authentically with the cultural context of the study. A team consisting of translators and main investigators who were medical staff with years of experience in urology and incontinence were gathered to revise the two independent versions; subsequently, the expert panel synthesized a reconciled version. The researchers and translators were also asked to consider the cultural context and colloquial language of Iranian parents. For instance, there were discrepancies in translating the terms “four weeks” __ as it may be more convenient as “month” in Iranian culture__ “Squatting”, and “Bowel movement”. For example, in the “four weeks” instance, as the week has been replicated in later response choices, the team used the “four weeks” literal translation, as this may enhance the recall process. Through the translation process, the consensus took precedence over each individual’s opinion.

Back translation

Likewise, adhering to ICIQ protocol, a translator was asked to return the Persian version back to English. The back-translated version was submitted to the original ICIQ developers for further assessments. Following this, the pre-test study encompassing 10 parents was conducted. During the cognitive interview, the participants were asked to ‘think aloud’ while reading the questionnaire, and their additional comments were also taken into account. Finally, the last version of ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe (Supplementary file) was prepared based on the consensus opinion and comments from consecutive cognitive interviews to ensure it is well received by the audiences.

Psychometric analysis

Psychometric properties, i.e., reliability and validity, were assessed according to COSMIN and ICIQ guidelines. In terms of reliability, the test-retest and internal consistency analyses were conducted. The questionnaire was readministered 7 to 10 days later to ensure the chronological stability, and no treatment was initiated between the two administrations. Also, the standard error of measurement (SEm) and minimum detectable change (MDC) were calculated.

Construct validity was assessed through principal component analysis (PCA). Considering this, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to determine the main components of the questionnaire. Regarding the discriminant validity, the medical diagnosis of LUTS (a combination of expert diagnosis and relevant lab tests) was used as a reference to ensure the appropriate diagnostic performance of the translated tool. Additionally, the ceiling and floor effects were evaluated.

Statistics

SPSS version 27.0 was used for analysis. Descriptive analysis was used to report the demographic and baseline characteristics. For continuous variables, the mean and the standard deviations (mean ± Sd) is reported; while, for qualitative variables, the frequency (%) is reported. The significance threshold was set as P < 0.05, except for the Barlett test of sphericity (P < 0.001).

The sample size determination for this study has been done based on the test-retest reliability criteria of the ICIQ-CLUTS questionnaire. On the basis of the intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.85 as stated by De Gennaro et al. [15], along with the lowest acceptable intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.60, with an 𝛼 of 0.05 and 80% power, the minimum number of required sample size has been found to be 29. Our sample size is 67, which is more than this minimum figure.

The internal consistency was assessed by Cronbach’s α coefficient, and reliability over time by two-way mixed effect intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC was interpreted as: 0.5 > poor reliability, 0.5–0.75 moderate reliability, 0.75–0.9 good, and exceeding 0.9 indicating excellent reliability. Likewise, a Cronbach’s α coefficient of more than 0.7 was considered appropriate. The SEm was calculated using the Sd x √(1 − R) formula, where R (reliability) was ICC and Sd was the test’s standard deviation. Similarly, MDC was calculated using SEM × √2 × 1.96. Ceiling or floor effect is assessed using the data distribution at the two extremities. In case of more the 15% data aggregation in lower or upper limits, it would be attributed to the floor or ceiling effect, respectively.

Exploratory factor analysis was performed using principal component analysis with Varimax rotation. The number of retained factors was initially evaluated using the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1), inspection of the scree plot, and subsequently verified using Horn’s parallel analysis (1,000 randomly generated datasets, 95th percentile criterion). The final factor solution was selected considering statistical results together with the interpretability of the factor structure and consistency with the original questionnaire.

Additionally, for the sake of sampling adequacy, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) analysis was considered, with a value above 0.7 indicating adequate sampling. Also, the Barlett test of sphericity significance threshold was set as P < 0.001. By meeting these thresholds, the items are deemed appropriate for factor analysis. Also, to define the best predictor items and the variance explained by each, multiple stepwise regression was conducted. Based on the extracted standardized beta coefficient and zero-order correlation coefficient the Pratt’s importance value was calculated. Since all items were included in the regression model, the sum of Pratt’s value (= R2) was 1.

A receiver operative curve (ROC) was carried out for determining the diagnostic performance (namely, sensitivity, specificity, and likelihood ratios) and cut-off point of the questionnaire in the Iranian context. The Youden index (J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1) was used in determining the cut-off point. As aforementioned, the concise clinical diagnosis was used as a reference in evaluating the ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe ability in discriminating symptomatic vs. asymptomatic patients.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 67 parents filled out the questionnaire (Table 1). No missing items were reported. Thirty-two children indicated LUTS, who were assigned to the case group, while the other 35 were assigned to the control group. The mean age of the participants was 6.2 ± 1.4. Almost 60% of participants were girls. There was no significant difference regarding age and gender in cases and controls (p values > 0.05). 81% of the children with LUTS had UTI. Participants’ scores ranged from 10 to 28 on ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe, with a mean score of 14.72 ± 4.04.

Reliability

The Cronbach’s α was 0.768, indicating good internal consistency of the questionnaire. The ICC was 0.983, which represents excellent temporal reliability. Additionally, the SEm and MDC were calculated to be 0.53 and 1.46, respectively.

Factor analysis

The results of the EFA analysis using PCA coupled with varimax rotation are presented in Fig. 2 and the supplementary file. Three factors were retained based on the Kaiser criterion and inspection of the scree plot. Horn’s parallel analysis produced a slightly more conservative estimate; however, considering the theoretical structure of the original ICIQ-CLUTS, the simple and clinically interpretable rotated solution, and consistency with previous validation studies, the three-factor solution was retained. The three components explained more than 72% of the variance. Given the correlation matrix, the KMO and Bartlett’s sphericity test indicated appropriate sample adequacy and factor analysis. The KMO for the translated questionnaire was 0.719 with a significant Bartlett’s sphericity test (P < 0.001). The scree plot is shown in Fig 2. Diagnostic accuracy

Using the medical diagnosis of LUTS as reference, the ROC curve, precision-recall curve, and diagnostic performance metrics of the questionnaire were calculated. The diagnostic curves are depicted in Figs. 3 and 4. The area under the curve (AUC) of 0.917 (CI 95%: 0.848–0.986) indicates the excellent discriminative performance of the ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe. The score 15.5 indicated 75% sensitivity and 100% specificity, with a Youden index of 0.75. Based on these metrics, the LR + was ∞ and the LR- was 0.25, with an overall accuracy of 88.1%.

Ceiling and floor effects

Regarding that neither the minimum nor the maximum [10–28] of the test result reaches the maximum and minimum score of the questionnaire [9–36], it is conceivable that the ceiling or floor effect was not present.

Discussion

The ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe represents a convenient, validated, and accurate tool in diagnosing LUTS in Iranian children. A few relevant items, along with a user-friendly structure, indicate the accessibility of the questionnaire, as no items are missing in our study. The ICIQ-CLUTS is among the few available tools for pediatric LUTS that consider bowel dysfunction. The bowel and bladder dysfunction (BBD) is a well-known and theoretically backed pathology in children [16].

Higher missing items, coupled with the lower reliability of the child’s self-reported version of ICIQ-CLUTS for younger children, led us to adopt only the caregiver version. Additionally, the caregiver version for 5–9 years aligns more with the Persian cultural context and tacit beliefs. Also, the Turkish version, as a closer contextual and cultural setting, advocated the poor reliability of the 5–10 version for children and the 10–13 version for parents [11]. Also, in some translations, the investigators had to explain the items for the child, which interferes with the aims and notion of the questionnaire [10]. Hence, through our study, to mitigate the interviewer bias, the investigators refrained from assisting the participants during questionnaire completion.

The α = 0.768 and ICC = 0.983 indicated satisfied internal consistency and excellent chronic stability. Likewise, the original module and later translations indicated high reliability. Although in the original module, the age-stratified α for children and parents of children under 9 did not surpass the 0.7 threshold. Additionally, the parent vs. children version indicated a relatively lower ICC of 0.836. Subsequently, we adopt only the parent version, which outperformed the original one in terms of reliability. The differences may stem from variations in the cohort of the original study (from three distinguished nations). The Chinese and Spanish caregiver versions for < 9, indicated an α of 0.692 and 0.81, with both outperforming the equivalent children’s version. The SEm and MDC of 0.53 and 1.46 were identified by our study, showcasing the excellent capacity of the ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe to demonstrate that a change of 2 scores is meaningful in clinical settings. The results also align with Blanco-Diaz et al. [9] study on Spanish speaking participants. Although Horn’s parallel analysis yielded a more conservative estimate of the number of factors, the retained three-factor model demonstrated excellent interpretability, aligned with the conceptual domains of the original ICIQ-CLUTS, and explained more than 72% of the total variance. Given the modest sample size and the cross-cultural validation context, the three-factor solution was considered the most appropriate representation of the Persian version. The component one (Q6-Q7-Q11) mostly addresses the urgency, component two (Q4-Q5-Q8-Q10) incontinence and bladder control, and component three (Q9-Q12) voiding and bowel dysfunction. This closely aligns with the findings of Tan et al. [10]. The best predictor questions in our survey were Q7 (urgency), Q4 (bed wetting during nights), Q11(wetting the clothes while rushing for urination), and Q5 (wetting the clothes), respectively. Likewise, Gennaro et al. [8] found Q5, Q4, Q7, and Q10 to be the best predictors of outcome in the parent version.

The overall performance of our questionnaire was satisfying while aligning with previous validations. The LR + and LR- of ∞ 0.25 render the perfect rule-in capability, while moderately good rule-out performance of the test. However, a stronger sample size is required to enhance the diagnostic metrics. A cutoff point of 15.5 was deemed ideal in our study, with sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 75%, 100%, and 88.1%, respectively. However, a varied cutoff score has been determined in similar studies. The score of 13 was determined by the original study; however, the score is not age-oriented [8]. Likewise, the Chinese version indicated the cutoff point of 13.5 for the parent version [10]. More aligned with our cutoff, the Spanish version survey outlined a score of 15 as the cutoff. We acknowledged these discrepancies as contextual differences. A reason might be an overprotective parent or slight deviations in the normal behavior of the children. Furthermore, an AUC of 0.963, 0.922, and 0.862 is reported by Spanish, Chinese, and original studies, respectively, aligning with our reported 0.917. The higher sample size in Spanish version may explain the higher performance.

In this research, the focus is on the validation of the Persian version of the ICIQ-CLUTS caregiver module in younger children. It is nevertheless worth mentioning that similar validation work has been done for the self-report module in the older pediatric population. The rationale behind presenting them separately is the substantial difference in the target population, their developmental appropriateness as respondents and distinct psychometric methods. Both studies prove that the measures generate reliable information. Specifically, the caregiver module uses a structured three-factor model to generate diagnostics accuracy (AUC = 0.917; sensitivity 75%; specificity 100%). The tool is designed to be used for the younger patient sample since they are not able to fill out the questionnaire independently. The self-report module showed excellent reliability (α = 0.927) and temporal stability. Therefore, both validated tools give an age-specific set of measures to assess pediatric LUTS.

Several limitations affected our findings. A greater sample size is required to calibrate the diagnostic performance, especially the cutoff point and its associated metrics. Single-center, tertiary clinic population may not represent the community or rural settings. Of note, in our study, only the caregiver version of the ICIQ-CLUTS was translated and validated in 5–9 children. Future studies must consider other age groups. Another future direction is to assess the diagnostic performance of ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe among patients with diverse urological conditions. The prevalence of LUTS symptoms in our study, similar to others, doesn’t match the real-world settings, which aligns with the purpose of using this tool in urology clinics for diagnostic purposes. However, in terms of screening, new studies using real-world settings and populations are required. Last but not least, reaching a mutual consensus in translation and back translation was not completely applicable and may prompt loss of nuances during questionnaire translation.

Conclusion

ICIQ-CLUTS-Pe is a validated, convenient, and accurate tool for diagnosing LUTS in children, making it suitable for clinical and research purposes. The questionnaire can now be used to investigate the severity, therapeutic endpoints, and prevalence of LUTS in Persian-speaking children.
