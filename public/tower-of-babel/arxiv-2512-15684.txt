High-Dimensional Partial Least Squares: Spectral Analysis and Fundamental Limitations
Victor Léger victor.leger@grenoble-inp.fr, Florent Chatelain florent.chatelain@grenoble-inp.fr

Abstract
Partial Least Squares (PLS) is a widely used method for data integration, designed to extract latent components shared across paired high-dimensional datasets. Despite decades of practical success, a precise theoretical understanding of its behavior in high-dimensional regimes remains limited. In this paper, we study a data integration model in which two high-dimensional data matrices share a low-rank common latent structure while also containing individual-specific components. We analyze the singular vectors of the associated cross-covariance matrix using tools from random matrix theory and derive asymptotic characterizations of the alignment between estimated and true latent directions. These results provide a quantitative explanation of the reconstruction performance of the PLS variant based on Singular Value Decomposition (PLS-SVD) and identify regimes where the method exhibits counter-intuitive or limiting behavior. Building on this analysis, we compare PLS-SVD with principal component analysis applied separately to each dataset and show its asymptotic superiority in detecting the common latent subspace. Overall, our results offer a comprehensive theoretical understanding of high-dimensional PLS-SVD, clarifying both its advantages and fundamental limitations.

Introduction

Partial Least Squares (PLS) is a widely-used dimension reduction method originally developed by Hermann Wold
(
32
)
in the 1960s to address econometric problems. Since then, it has been applied across many scientific domains, including chemometrics and spectrometric modeling
(
12
)
, as well as bioinformatics and genomics
(
5
)
. PLS is particularly effective in high-dimensional settings with limited sample sizes, a typical scenario in genomics, where datasets may contain around 20,000 gene expression measurements for only a few hundred individuals. This strenght has led to its broad adoption in genomic research for diverse tasks, such as analyzing gene correlations in yeast cells
(
11
)
, imputing missing data
(
6
)
, classifying cancer subtypes
(
27
)
, and predicting therapeutic outcomes
(
16
)
.
Although numerous PLS algorithm variants exist
(
31
)
, they all rely on the same underlying principle: constructing latent components that maximizes the covariance with the reponse variables. Let
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
denote the matrix of the predictors and
{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
the matrix of the responses, where
p
and
q
are the respective numbers of variables and
n
is the common sample size. The PLS approach seeks to identify the projection directions that maximise the covariance between
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
.

More precisely, letting
r
denote the dimension of the latent space shared by
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
, the goal is to extract
r
pairs of directions
({\mathbf{u}}_{k},{\mathbf{v}}_{k})
that maximize the sample covariance between the projected variables
{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
. At each iteration, the matrices
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
(or their cross-covariance) are updated before computing the next pair of directions. For the first component, all PLS variants reduce to the same optimization problem:

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{v}}_{1}}\penalty\

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\right)^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\equiv\argmax_{{\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{v}}_{1}}\penalty\ {\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{XY}{\mathbf{v}}_{1}

s.t.

\displaystyle\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\rVert=1\text{ and }\lVert{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\rVert=1,

where
\mathbf{S}_{XY}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}
is the normalized sample cross-covariance matrix.
This problem admits a unique solution for
({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{v}}_{1})
, which corresponds to the leading left and right singular vectors of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
, as guaranteed by the Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem.

After extracting the first component, the subsequent steps depend on the specific PLS variant. Some algorithms perform separate deflation of
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
, removing the part explained by the previously obtained components; others deflate the cross-product
{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}
directly. In addition, each variant imposes orthogonality constraints to ensure identifiability of the successive directions. These constraints differ across PLS formulations and lead to distinct properties (typically geared toward prediction or modeling).
At iteration
k>1
, the next pair
({\mathbf{u}}_{k},{\mathbf{v}}_{k})
is obtained by solving

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\mathbf{u}}_{k},{\mathbf{v}}_{k}}

\displaystyle\penalty\ {\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{XY}^{(k)}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}\penalty\ \lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\rVert

\displaystyle=1\text{ and }\lVert{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\rVert=1,\ \text{and orthogonality constraints},

where
\mathbf{S}_{XY}^{(k)}
denotes the updated version of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
at step
k
.

Because numerous algorithmic variants exist, our goal is not to survey all implementations but rather to highlight the behavior and the fundamental challenges faced by PLS in high-dimensional settings. For this purpose, we focus on the PLS-SVD variant
(
31
)
, which imposes that each new direction satisfies

{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\perp\operatorname{span}\{{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}\},\qquad{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\perp\operatorname{span}\{{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\}.

Under these constraints, the Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem ensures that all components can be obtained simultaneously through the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
, thereby avoiding any iterative deflation step. Our analysis therefore focuses on the spectral properties of the square symmetric matrices

\displaystyle{\mathbf{K}}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{pq}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}=\mathbf{S}_{XY}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{XY}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q},

(1)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbf{K}}}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{pq}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{S}_{XY}\mathbf{S}_{XY}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},

(2)

whose nonzero eigenvalues coincide with the nonzero squared singular values of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
, and whose eigenvectors correspond respectively to the right and left singular vectors of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
.

In this work, we further consider the following asymptotic regime.

Assumptions A1

(High-dimensional asymptotic regime)
.

We assume that
p,q,n\to\infty
with the ratios of the sample size to the dimensions converging to finite positive constants:

\displaystyle\frac{n}{p}\to\beta_{p}>0,\qquad\frac{n}{q}\to\beta_{q}>0,\qquad\beta\equiv\max(\beta_{p},\beta_{q}),

(3)

where
\beta
can be interpreted as the limit of the ratio
\frac{n}{d}
with
d=\min(p,q)
.

This high-dimensional framework is particularly well suited for random matrix theory, which provides sharp characterizations of the limiting spectral distribution of the involved large matrices, the behavior of their largest eigenvalues, and the alignment of the corresponding eigenvectors.

1.1
Related Works

The statistical properties of PLS have been studied from various perspectives over the past decades. Early theoretical work focused on establishing the algorithmic foundations and interpretations of PLS
(
14
;
13
)
. Later theoretical developments analyzed the structure of PLS estimators and derived conditions for their consistency and asymptotic behavior in classical settings where the number of samples grows while dimensions remain fixed
(
24
;
15
)
.
However, these results do not directly apply to modern high-dimensional scenarios, and subsequent work has highlighted the limitations of PLS consistency when the number of variables is large relative to the sample size
(
7
)
.

Random Matrix Theory and Spiked Models.

The application of random matrix theory (RMT) to understand high-dimensional statistical methods has gained considerable attention.
Foundational results include the Marčenko–Pastur law
(
22
)
, which characterizes the limiting spectral distribution of an isotropic sample covariance matrices, and the Baik–Ben Arous–Péché (BBP) phase transition
(
2
)
for spiked covariance models, which specify the threshold above which signals yield isolated eigenvalues.
These tools have been successfully applied to principal component analysis
(
26
;
21
;
4
)
, as well as to computationally efficient implementations
(
9
)
,
providing precise characterizations of eigenvalue behavior and eigenvector consistency in high-dimensional regimes.
Multimodal data have also been studied through the asymptotic analysis of low-rank tensor decompositions; see, for example,
(
19
)
.
Our work extends these techniques to the PLS setting, where the presence of two coupled data matrices creates additional complexity.

PLS in High Dimensions.

Despite its widespread use across numerous scientific domains, PLS remains comparatively less understood from a theoretical standpoint in high-dimensional settings. Recent efforts have begun addressing this gap.
7
proposed sparse PLS methods to regularize the solutions, while
8
analyzed the asymptotic prediction behavior of PLS regression across various high-dimensional regimes, establishing convergence rates. These contributions, however, primarily concern regression problems and stop short of providing a full characterization of the spectral properties of PLS matrices.

29
provide, in a very recent work on cross-covariance matrices in noise-only (i.e., spike-free) settings, the necessary foundation for understanding the bulk distribution of the singular values in these regimes.
Concurrent and complementary to our work,
23
independently analyzed correlated spiked cross-covariance models motivated by multi-modal learning, providing a characterization of BBP-type phase transitions for PLS using free probability techniques. While their model focuses on partially aligned signals across two channels with explicit correlation parameters, our framework (
4
) emphasizes the decomposition into joint (
{\mathbf{T}}
), individual (
{\mathbf{M}}
,
{\mathbf{N}}
), and noise components, directly connecting to integrative data analysis frameworks like JIVE
(
20
)
.
Notably, the two approaches employ different RMT methodologies:
23
leverage free probability theory and subordination relations
(
3
)
, while our analysis relies on deterministic equivalents and resolvent methods
(
10
)
. These complementary perspectives provide different insights into the high-dimensional behavior of PLS and cross-covariance matrices.

Deflation Schemes and Algorithm Variants.

While our analysis focuses on the PLS-SVD formulation, several alternative deflation schemes have been proposed in the literature
(
31
)
.
These variants differ in their deflation strategy and orthogonality constraints. Importantly, across all these variants, the first step is identical to PLS-SVD, which serves as the essential initial step for subsequent analysis.

1.2
Summary of Contributions

This paper provides a comprehensive theoretical analysis of Partial Least Squares (PLS) in high-dimensional settings through the lens of random matrix theory. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

Deterministic equivalents and limiting spectral distribution.
We establish deterministic equivalents for the resolvent matrices associated with the PLS cross-covariance kernels (Theorem
1
), which serve as the keystone for all subsequent results. Building on these equivalents, we characterize the limiting distribution of the squared singular values of the normalized cross-covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}_{XY}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}{\mathbf{X}}^{T}{\mathbf{Y}}
(Proposition
2
). We also show a confinement result showing that, in noise-only models, the empirical singular values remain bounded within the support of this limiting distribution. Together, these foundational results provide the technical framework for analyzing signal detection and recovery in PLS.

•

Phase transitions and spike detection.
We derive explicit phase transition thresholds that determine when signal components yield isolated singular values detectable above the noise (Propositions
3
and
5
). For individual-specific components (
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
), as well as for the common shared structure (encoded in
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
), we identify the critical signal-to-noise ratio
\tau
defined by equation (
14
), above which spikes emerge from the bulk distribution. We provide closed-form expressions for the asymptotic locations of these spikes as functions of the underlying signal singular values.

•

Eigenvector alignment and fundamental limitations.
We precisely quantify the alignment between PLS singular vectors and the true signal directions (Propositions
4
and
6
). Our analysis reveals two fundamental limitations of PLS in high-dimensional regimes:

–

Spurious individual components:
When individual-specific structures
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
are present, PLS can spuriously align with these uninformative directions rather than with the shared signal. Proposition
4
shows that the corresponding singular vectors do not align with any deterministic signal direction beyond their generating component, representing noise-driven artifacts.

–

Systematic skewing of common components:
Even for the shared signal encoded in
{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}
, PLS singular vectors do not recover singular vectors of the true signal. Instead, they align with skewed versions of these directions (Proposition
6
). This distortion, induced by noise, persists asymptotically and vanishes only in the limit of infinite signal strength or in special cases (Remarks
6
and Section
3.5
).

•

Comparison with PCA.
We establish that, under the same asymptotic regime, PLS exhibits strictly greater statistical power than separate PCA applied independently to each data matrix for detecting shared latent directions (Proposition
10
). Specifically, we prove that whenever separate PCA detects all
r
common spikes, PLS necessarily detects them as well, with strictly larger spectral separation. This theoretical advantage confirms PLS as a superior method for integrative analysis.

•

Model framework.
Our analysis is conducted within a general signal-plus-noise model that decomposes the data matrices
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
into joint, individual, and noise components (
4
). This framework directly connects to integrative data analysis methods such as JIVE
(
20
)
and provides a natural setting for understanding multi-modal learning. Although we focus on the PLS-SVD variant for simplicity and clarity, all PLS variants share the same initial step, and our approach can be extended to other deflation schemes.

Taken together, these results offer the first explicit and comprehensive theoretical characterization of PLS in high-dimensional regimes within a generic integrative signal-plus-noise framework, accounting for joint, individual, and noise components. This reveals both the advantages of PLS for signal detection and its fundamental limitations for signal recovery. Our findings point to promising directions for developing enhanced methods that filter out spurious components while preserving the shared latent structure.

Outline of the paper.

Section
2
introduces the notations, the proposed data integration model, and the underlying assumptions in the high-dimensional regime.
In Section
3
, we conduct a random matrix analysis of the singular vectors of the cross-covariance matrix associated with the proposed model. These results are then exploited to provide a quantitative analysis of the reconstruction performance of the signal components obtained with the PLS-SVD approach, and to shed light on some of its counter-intuitive and limiting behaviors.
Building on these findings, Section
4
is devoted to a comparison with principal component analysis applied separately to each data matrix. We demonstrate the asymptotic superiority of PLS-SVD for detecting the directions of the latent common subspace.
Finally, Section
5
concludes the paper and discusses the results. Most proofs are deferred to the appendix

2.2
Signal-Plus-Noise Model

We assume that
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
can be represented as a signal-plus-noise model in the following manner:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbf{X}}&={\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}}+{\mathbf{E}}\ \in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},\\
{\mathbf{Y}}&={\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{N}}+{\mathbf{F}}\ \in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q},\end{split}

(4)

where, for each data matrix (
{\mathbf{X}}
or
{\mathbf{Y}}
), the last term corresponds to noise while the first two terms are deterministic matrices representing the signal components: the first term captures the joint structure shared between
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
, the second term describes the individual structure specific to the given data matrix. More precisely:

•

{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
is a common score matrix shared by
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
, with rank
r
.

•

{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and
{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
are the corresponding loading matrices.

•

{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
{\mathbf{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
are low-rank matrices capturing the individual structure of
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
, respectively.

•

{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
{\mathbf{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
are noise matrices with respective entries
E_{ij}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
F_{ij}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Remark 1

(On the noise model)
.

Assuming Gaussian noise is convenient for deriving our main results, as they rely on Gaussian-based calculations. However, asymptotic properties of classical spiked models are known to be universal with respect to the i.i.d. noise distribution, provided mild assumptions such as the existence of a finite fourth moment. Similar universality is expected to hold in our setting as well, although establishing a rigorous proof lies beyond the scope of this work.
Moreover, for the purposes of our analysis, we may assume without loss of generality that the noise is standardized. Any variance factor can indeed be absorbed into a rescaling of the signal matrices, so that the effective signal-to-noise ratio is entirely captured by the magnitude of the signal components. Working with standardized noise thus simplifies the exposition while preserving the generality of the model.

Several variants of this information-plus-noise model have already been introduced, notably for canonical correlation analysis within a Bayesian framework
(
17
)
, as well as for applications in genomics
(
20
)
, metabolomics
(
18
)
, and neuroscience
(
34
)
. These approaches are all built upon the JIVE (Joint and Individual Variation Explained) framework
(
20
)
, which employs the same decomposition: a low-rank component capturing the joint structure shared by
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
, low-rank components specific to each data type, and a residual noise term.

Such models share similarities with
(
30
)
, which explicitly account for structured components in the residuals. However, PLS models are most often encountered in simpler forms:
either by implicitly absorbing the specific components

{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
into a generic residual term that also includes noise
(
24
;
15
;
7
)
,
or through generic formulations such as the one in
(
33
)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbf{X}}&={\mathbf{U}}\tilde{{\mathbf{P}}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{E}}\ \in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\\
{\mathbf{Y}}&={\mathbf{V}}\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{F}}\ \in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q},\end{split}

(5)

where
{\mathbf{U}}
and
{\mathbf{V}}
are respectively
n\times l
and
n\times m
matrices of the latent vectors, and
\tilde{{\mathbf{P}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times l}
and
\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}
are loading matrices.
In fact, the two formulations are equivalent provided that certain orthogonality conditions hold. Specifically, starting from the latter model, one can extract an orthonormalized common component
{\mathbf{T}}
from
{\mathbf{U}}
and
{\mathbf{V}}
such that
\col{({\mathbf{T}})}=\col{({\mathbf{U}})}\cap\col{({\mathbf{V}})}
, where
\col{({\mathbf{A}})}
denotes the column space of the matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
. Consequently,
{\mathbf{U}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{T}}&\bar{{\mathbf{M}}}\end{pmatrix}
and
{\mathbf{V}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{T}}&\bar{{\mathbf{N}}}\end{pmatrix}
, with the column spaces of
{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
,
\bar{{\mathbf{M}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(l-r)}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{N}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m-r)}
being mutually orthogonal. By similarly partitioning
\tilde{{\mathbf{P}}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{P}}&\bar{{\mathbf{P}}}\end{pmatrix}
and
\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{R}}&\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}\end{pmatrix}
, one recovers the first model with
{\mathbf{M}}=\bar{{\mathbf{M}}}{\bar{{\mathbf{P}}}}^{\top}
and
{\mathbf{N}}=\bar{{\mathbf{N}}}{\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}}^{\top}
. This construction highlights the orthogonality constraints that define the identifiability conditions of our model.

Conditions C1
.

The following orthogonality constraints hold for the model (
4
):

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}^{\top}{\mathbf{T}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{I}}_{r},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}{\mathbf{T}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}_{p\times r},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{N}}^{\top}{\mathbf{T}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}_{q\times r},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}}_{p\times q},

The latter conditions can be imposed without loss of generality on our model (
4
).
Moreover, to ensure that the problem is non-trivial, we also impose the following assumptions.

Assumptions A2
.

In the asymptotic regime described in Assumption
A1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{p}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{q}{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{p}{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{q}{\mathbf{N}}^{\top}{\mathbf{N}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{P}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{N}}{\mathbf{R}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),

\displaystyle r_{M}\equiv\Rank{{\mathbf{M}}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad

\displaystyle r_{N}\equiv\Rank{{\mathbf{N}}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),

\left\lVert\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)

The third line of assumptions is less intuitive. Although
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
are

and

r_{M}=l-r
and

, where

and

are the respective ranks of the matrices

and
{\mathbf{V}}
defined in model (

), and

is the rank of the common component. Again, assuming that the columns of

are i.i.d. with

, the matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}{\bar{{\mathbf{P}}}}^{\top}{\mathbf{P}}
has entries with zero mean and unit variance. Since
\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{{\mathbf{M}}}\times r}
is low-dimensional, we deduce that

\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{P}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\bar{{\mathbf{M}}}{\bar{{\mathbf{P}}}}^{\top}{\mathbf{P}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\bar{{\mathbf{T}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}\right\rVert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}\mathcal{O}(1),

where
\bar{{\mathbf{T}}}=\bar{{\mathbf{M}}}^{\top}\bar{{\mathbf{M}}}

has bounded operator norm (otherwise the common part
{\mathbf{P}}
becomes asymptotically negligible and the problem becomes trivial).
The corresponding conditions on
{\mathbf{R}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
follow symmetrically.

Finally, the last condition in Assumption
A2
is satisfied when the ranks of the individual components
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
remain fixed and finite. This standard low-rank assumption is essential for identifying potential spikes based on the noise-only model. The shared signal components already possess a finite-rank structure by construction in (

), as the factors
{\mathbf{T}}
,
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
have a fixed number
r

3
Analysis of PLS-SVD in the High-Dimensional Regime

This Section is dedicated to the analysis of the singular decomposition of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}
. More specifically, we will characterize the limiting singular distribution of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
(Proposition
2
), as well as the spiked singular values due to the signal components of our model (Propositions
3
and
5
). Finally, we characterize the alignments of the associated singular vectors (Propositions
4
and
6
). This analysis is performed by the means of the kernel matrices
{\mathbf{K}}
and
\tilde{{\mathbf{K}}}
, defined respectively in (
1
) and (
2
). As these objects are defined symmetrically, we often focus on the kernel
{\mathbf{K}}=\frac{1}{pq}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}
, and deduce the results for
\tilde{{\mathbf{K}}}
. All the results of this section are obtained under Assumptions
A1
and
A2

3.1
Deterministic equivalents

To analyze the spectral decomposition of the kernel
{\mathbf{K}}
, we introduce a tool called the resolvent matrix, which is
{\mathbf{Q}}(z)=(\frac{1}{pq}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}-z{\mathbf{I}}_{q})^{-1}
. Let’s write the eigenvalue decomposition
{\mathbf{K}}={\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}
, with
{\mathbf{U}}=[{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{q}]\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
the eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{K}}
and
{\mathbf{\Lambda}}={\mathcal{D}}\left(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{q}\right)
the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{K}}
. Then we have this interesting property:

{\mathbf{Q}}(z)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{q}\frac{{\mathbf{u}}_{i}{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\top}}{\lambda_{i}-z},

(6)

which means that the eigendecomposition of
{\mathbf{K}}
can be deduced from the analysis of
{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
, and more specifically the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{K}}
can be read as the singular points of
{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
. In order to make the statistical analysis feasible, we will replace the resolvent
{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
by an object
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}(z)
, for which scalar observations will be asymptotically identical to the scalar observations of the resolvent itself. Such an object is called a deterministic equivalent, defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Deterministic Equivalent)
.

Let
{\mathbf{Z}}
be a random matrix and
\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}
be a deterministic matrix, both in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.
We write
{\mathbf{Z}}\leftrightarrow\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}
if, for any deterministic matrix
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and vectors
{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of bounded norms (spectral and Euclidean norms respectively)

\frac{1}{m}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}\left({\mathbf{Z}}-\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}\right)\xrightarrow[m\to+\infty]{\text{a.s.}}0,\quad{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\left({\mathbf{Z}}-\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}\right){\mathbf{b}}\xrightarrow[m\to+\infty]{\text{a.s.}}0.

The matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}
is called a
deterministic equivalent
of
{\mathbf{Z}}
. For more details on this notion, see
10
.

We are interested in the limiting spectral distribution
\mu
of the squared singular values of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
. We will characterize this distribution
\mu
through a polynomial equation over its Stieltjes transform, which is defined in Definition
2
. To ensure that the solution of this equation is indeed a valid Stieltjes transform, it needs to belong to a specific ensemble, defined in Definition
3
.

Definition 2

(Stieltjes Transform)
.

For a real probability measure
\mu
with support
\Supp(\mu)
, the Stieltjes transform
m_{\mu}(z)
is defined, for al
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\Supp(\mu)
, as:

m_{\mu}(z)\equiv\int\frac{1}{t-z}\mu(dt)

Definition 3
.

For
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{C}
, we define the ensemble of ”valid” Stieljes transform pairs as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\{(z,m)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathbb{C},\textrm{ s.t. }(\Im(z)\Im(m)>0\textrm{ if }\Im(z)\neq 0)

\displaystyle\textrm{ or }(m>0\textrm{ if }z\in\mathbb{R}\textrm{ and }z<\inf\mathcal{A}^{C}\cap\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\textrm{ or }(m<0\textrm{ if }z\in\mathbb{R}\textrm{ and }z>\sup\mathcal{A}^{C}\cap\mathbb{R})\}

Given
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
from (
4
),
the resolvent matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}(z)
of
{\mathbf{K}}
defined in (
1
), and the coresolvent matrix
\tilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}(z)
of
\tilde{{\mathbf{K}}}
defined in (
2
), are given by

{\mathbf{Q}}(z)=\left(\frac{1}{pq}\,{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}-z{\mathbf{I}}_{q}\right)^{-1},\qquad\tilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}(z)=\left(\frac{1}{pq}\,{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}-z{\mathbf{I}}_{p}\right)^{-1}.

Theorem
1
then provides deterministic equivalents for these key quantities. A main advantage of the deterministic equivalent approach is that, starting from the resolvent and using concentration of traces, one can derive the limiting linear spectral statistics of the kernel matrices, as well as the limiting spectral distribution. Moreover, the isolated eigenvalues are given by the singular points of the deterministic equivalent. Last, the concentration of bilinear forms makes it possible to analyze the alignment of the associated eigenvectors. This latter property will be of primary interest in our spiked model analysis.

Theorem 1

(Deterministic equivalent)
.

Some deterministic equivalents
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}
of
{\mathbf{Q}}
, and
\bar{\tilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}}
of
\tilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}

where

, and

is the unique solution in

(7)

The matrices
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}_{X}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}_{Y}
are expressed as:

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}_{X}(z)

(8)

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}_{Y}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{m_{Y}(z)}{\mathbf{I}}_{q}+\frac{1}{1+m_{Y}(z)}\frac{1}{q}\left({\mathbf{N}}^{\top}{\mathbf{N}}+{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\right)+\right.

\displaystyle zm^{2}_{X}(z)

\displaystyle zm^{2}_{Y}(z)

The proof of Theorem

Remark 2

(On the Marčenko–Pastur Stieltjes transform)
.

The Stieltjes transforms

m_{X}(z)
and
m_{Y}(z)
involved in Theorem
1
can be defined alternatively using the Stieltjes transform of the well-known Marčenko–Pastur distribution. Indeed,
m_{X}(z)=\beta_{p}^{-1}m_{\mathrm{MP};\beta_{p}^{-1}}\left(z\beta_{p}^{-1}\right)
and
m_{Y}(z)=\beta_{q}^{-1}m_{\mathrm{MP};\beta_{q}^{-1}}\left(z\beta_{q}^{-1}\right)
, where
m_{\mathrm{MP};c}(\tilde{z})
is the Stieltjes transform of the Marčenko–Pastur distribution with parameter
c
.

The expressions obtained in Theorem
1
highlight the complementary nature of the two deterministic equivalents. We can already observe that
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}(z)
, which contains information about the right singular vectors of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
, depends, among the individual terms, only on
{\mathbf{N}}
, whereas
\bar{\tilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}}(z)
associated with the left singular vectors, depends only on the individual terms
{\mathbf{M}}
.

3.2
Limiting singular distribution

Proposition 2

(Limiting Singular Distribution of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
as in (
4
). We define
\mu
the limiting distribution of the squared singular values of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
. Then the density
f
of
\mu
is given on
\Supp(\mu)\backslash\{0\}
as
f(x)=\frac{\beta}{\pi}\Im(\bar{m}(x))
, where
\bar{m}(z)
is defined as the complex solution with positive imaginary part of

-\bar{m}^{3}(x)\beta_{p}\beta_{q}x^{2}+\bar{m}^{2}(x)\left(\beta_{p}+\beta_{q}-2\beta_{p}\beta_{q}\right)x+\bar{m}(x)\left[x-(1-\beta_{q})(1-\beta_{q})\right]+1=0.

(12)

The support of this density is given by
\Supp(\mu)\backslash\{0\}=[x_{-},x_{+}]
, where
x_{-}
and
x_{+}
are the two real nonnegative roots of the third-order polynomial
\Delta(x)
, with the expression given in equation (
44
) of Appendix
C
.
If
\beta<1
, there is an additional mass in zero
\mu(\{0\})=1-\beta
.

Moreover, when
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
are noise-only matrices (
i.e.,
in the specific case where
{\mathbf{P}}
,
{\mathbf{R}}
,
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
are null matrices), we have the confinement of the spectrum. More precisely:

\max\lambda(\mathbf{S}_{XY})\xrightarrow[n,p,q\to+\infty]{\text{a.s.}}x_{+},

where
x_{+}
is the right edge of
\Supp(\mu)
.

For completeness, the proof of the limiting distribution
\mu
is provided in Appendix
B
, even though this result was very recently established in the same form
29
for noise-only matrices (up to a difference in the normalization of the cross-covariance matrix
{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}
which is
1/n
in their setting rather than in
1/\sqrt{pq}
in ours).
Note that the presence of spikes does not alter the limiting distribution, since their number is fixed under the low-rank assumptions and their contribution becomes negligible in the limit.
Note also that the proof strategies differ: our derivation follows directly from the deterministic equivalent established in Theorem
1
, whereas
29
rely on free-probability techniques and
S
-transforms.
More importantly for our purposes, we also establish a confinement result for the spectrum, which will play a key role in the analysis of the spiked model (see remark below).

Remark 3

(On the squared singular values)
.

For simplicity, we derived the limiting distribution of the
squared
singular values of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
.
Note that the nonzero squared singular values of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
coincide with the nonzero eigenvalues of the kernel matrices (
1
) and (
2
).
Thus, their limiting spectral distributions can be inferred directly from those of the kernels, up to a rescaling that accounts for the differing numbers of zero eigenvalues.
Finally, the limiting density of the
nonzero singular values
themselves follows the standard change of variables
f_{\sigma}(\sigma)=2\sigma f(\sigma^{2})
.

Remark 4

(On the confinement of the spectrum)
.

Beyond establishing the limiting singular value distribution, we also provide a “no singular values outside the bulk” result: for noise-only matrices, the empirical singular values are asymptotically confined to the compact support of the limiting distribution, and in particular none escape beyond the upper edge. This confinement property will be crucial for identifying isolated singular values as spike-induced outliers, which in turn carry information about the underlying signal components in the data matrices.

3.3
Singular vectors due to the specific components

The deterministic equivalents given by Theorem
1
indicate that the deterministic left (resp. right) singular vectors of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
only depend on
{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{R}}
and
{\mathbf{M}}
(resp.
{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{R}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
). In fact, we can go further by showing that the left (resp. right) singular space of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
generated by
{\mathbf{M}}
(resp.
{\mathbf{N}}
) is asymptotically orthogonal to the left (resp. right) singular space generated by the the common components
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
.

Lemma 1

(Asymptotical orthogonality of the eigenspaces)
.

Let’s define
{\mathbf{K}}_{M}\equiv\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}
(resp.
{\mathbf{K}}_{N}\equiv\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{N}}^{\top}{\mathbf{N}}
) the kernel associated with
{\mathbf{M}}
(resp.
{\mathbf{N}}
). Then we have:

•

\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{M}}\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}_{X}(z){\mathbf{M}}^{\top}=\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{M}}\left(\frac{1}{m_{Y}(z)}{\mathbf{I}}_{p}+\frac{1}{1+m_{Y}(z)}{\mathbf{K}}_{M}\right){\mathbf{M}}^{\top}+o(1)

•

\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{N}}\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}_{Y}(z){\mathbf{N}}^{\top}=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{N}}\left(\frac{1}{m_{Y}(z)}{\mathbf{I}}_{q}+\frac{1}{1+m_{Y}(z)}{\mathbf{K}}_{N}\right){\mathbf{N}}^{\top}+o(1)

Therefore, the singular values due to
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
can be deduced separately from the singular values due to
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
. This result, which is a consequence of the assumptions
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{P}}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)
and

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{N}}{\mathbf{R}}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)
from Assumption
A2
, will be illustrated later in Figure
5
. Accordingly, we will first describe the isolated singular values associated with the individual components
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
(Proposition
3
) as well as the alignment of the associated singular vectors (Proposition
4
). Then, we will do the same for the isolated singular values and singular vectors associated with the joint components
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
(Propositions
5
and
6
). For each of these singular values, there exists a threshold above which signals yield isolated singular values. Below this threshold, the singular values live in the “bulk” distribution described in Proposition
2
, and are indistinguishable from noise. We prove in Appendix
E
that this threshold can be characterized as the largest positive root, denoted as
\tau>0
, of the following polynomial equation:

\displaystyle\lambda^{3}-\lambda(\beta_{p}\beta_{q}+\beta_{p}+\beta_{q})-2\beta_{p}\beta_{p}=0,

(13)

which can be expressed in closed-form as

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{p}\beta_{q}+\beta_{p}+\beta_{q}}{3}}\cos{\left(\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{acos}{\left(\beta_{p}\beta_{q}\left(\frac{\beta_{p}\beta_{q}+\beta_{p}+\beta_{q}}{3}\right)^{-3/2}\right)}\right)}.

(14)

{\mathbf{Y}}

Unlike for the specific components, Proposition

shows that the eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{K}}
and
\tilde{{\mathbf{K}}}
do not align directly with those of
{\mathbf{K}}_{T}
and
\tilde{{\mathbf{K}}}_{T}
. Instead, an unexpected and counter-intuitive result appears, revealing another fundamental limitation of PLS algorithms in the high-dimensional regime. Indeed, referring once again to the noiseless setting where
\mathbf{S}_{XY}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}
, one could expect the singular vectors of
\mathbf{S}_{XY}
to align with those of
{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}
. Instead, they align with
skewed
version of these singular vectors. Moreover, the directions of these deterministic limits are not necessarily mutually orthogonal (even though the singular vectors themselves are)! This skewing is once again induced by noise, and this effect vanishes only in the limit where the signal becomes infinitely strong. The two remarks below provide a heuristic interpretation of the alignment directions of the spikes in the limiting cases.

Remark 5

(When the common signal becomes dominant)
.

In the specific case where both the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}
go to infinity, then
{\mathbf{K}}_{T}\simeq{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{1/2}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{1/2}
, thus
\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}
becomes the
k
th right singular vector of
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{1/2}
. In this regime, the limiting deterministic direction
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
becomes proportional to the following unnormalized vector:

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{-1}\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}\propto{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k},

where the last proportionality relation follows from the fact that
\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}
is an eigenvector of
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{1/2}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{1/2}
and thus also of this inverse.
Since
\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}
tends to the right singular vector of
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{1/2}
, the vector
\bar{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}
becomes its corresponding left singular vector. Because the left singular vectors of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{1/2}
coincide with those of
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}
, we indeed recover in this case an alignment with the
k
th left singular vector of
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}
(which is equivalently the
k
th right singular vector of
{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}
). Symmetrically, we also obtain that
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
tends to align with the
k
th left singular vector of
{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}
.

Remark 6

(When one of the component becomes dominant)
.

If one of the components becomes dominant (say
{\mathbf{K}}_{R}\gg{\mathbf{K}}_{P}\simeq{\mathbf{0}}
), then it drives the behavior of the kernel
{\mathbf{K}}_{T}\simeq{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
. A reasoning similar to that of the previous remark shows that the expression of
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
becomes proportional to

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{-1}\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}\propto{\mathbf{R}}\tilde{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}.

This limiting direction is therefore aligned with the
k
th left singular vector of
{\mathbf{R}}
. Note also that this special case reduces to the same setting as Proposition
4
for the specific components (since when
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}\simeq{\mathbf{0}}
there is no longer any common component), and we recover a similar result, with the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{K}}
being mapped to those of
{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
.

3.5
The “diagonal” (commuting) case for the kernel matrices
{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}

To ease the interpretation of the results presented in Section

{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}
admit a common diagonalization basis. This case is typically met if the rank of the common component is

{\mathbf{K}}_{P}
and

and

the eigenvalues of the matrix
{\mathbf{K}}_{T}
defined in Prop.
5

\displaystyle\lambda_{T,k}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{P,k}+\lambda_{R,k}+\lambda_{P,k}\lambda_{R,k},

(19)

where
(\lambda_{P,k})_{k}
and
(\lambda_{R,k})_{k}
are the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
, ordered so that the
(\lambda_{T,k})_{k}
are in non-increasing order. Thus, we can restate Propositions
5
and
6
in the following simpler version.

Proposition 7

(Isolated eigenvalues associated with
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
in the “diagonal” case)
.

For diagonal kernel matrices

and

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{T,k}\xrightarrow[n,p,q\to+\infty]{\text{a.s.}}\xi_{T,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\lambda_{T,k}+1)(\lambda_{T,k}+\beta_{p})(\lambda_{T,k}+\beta_{q})}{\lambda_{T,k}^{2}}.

Proposition 8

(Alignment of the top eigenvectors associated with
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
in the “diagonal” case)
.

Let

4
PLS versus PCA

We compare the ability of PLS and separate principal component analysis (PCA), applied independently to
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
, to detect shared latent directions in the high-dimensional regime.
To simplify the interpretation of PCA, we restrict attention to the case where only common components are present in the integrative model (
4
),
i.e.,
where
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
are null matrices.
Then the shared latent components associated with
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
may be recovered by performing PCA separately on each data matrix.
We first recall classical results describing the behavior of PCA in our setting.

Proposition 8

(PCA performance in the high-dimensional regime
(
4
)
)
.

Under the high-dimensional regime of Assumption
A1
, PCA applied separately to
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
detects all
r
spikes if and only if the smallest spikes satisfy

\lambda_{P,r}>\sqrt{\beta_{p}}.\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{R,k}>\sqrt{\beta_{q}},

where the
\lambda_{P,k}
and
\lambda_{R,k}
for
k\in[r]
are the eigenvalues of the size
r
kernel matrices
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
defined in Proposition
5
.
In this case, the PCA loading vectors, given by the
r
leading eigenvectors of

\mathbf{S}_{XX}=\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathbf{S}_{YY}=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}
, denoted
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}(\mathbf{S}_{XX})
and
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}(\mathbf{S}_{YY})
respectively, align with the dominant left singular vectors of
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}
, denoted
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}({\mathbf{P}})
and
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}({\mathbf{R}})
respectively:

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{k}(\mathbf{S}_{XX}),{\mathbf{u}}_{k}({\mathbf{P}})\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n,p,q\to+\infty]{\text{a.s.}}\zeta^{\mathrm{PCA}}_{P,k}=1-\frac{\lambda_{P,k}+\beta_{p}}{\lambda_{P,k}(\lambda_{P,k}+1)},

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{k}(\mathbf{S}_{YY}),{\mathbf{u}}_{k}({\mathbf{R}})\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n,p,q\to+\infty]{\text{a.s.}}\zeta^{\mathrm{PCA}}_{R,k}=1-\frac{\lambda_{R,k}+\beta_{q}}{\lambda_{R,k}(\lambda_{R,k}+1)}.

To compare these results with the statistical performance of PLS, we now consider the eigenvalues
\lambda_{T,k}
of the
r\times r

{\mathbf{K}}_{T}
introduced in Proposition
5

The matrices
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
involved in the construction of

are symmetric positive definite and full rank.
Using standard spectral inequalities, we obtain the following lower bound.

Lemma 9

(Lower bound on the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{K}}_{T}
)
.

For all
k\in[r]
,

\lambda_{T,k}\geq\lambda_{P,r}+\lambda_{R,r}+\lambda_{P,r}\,\lambda_{R,r}.

Proof.

By Weyl’s inequality for symmetric matrices,

\lambda_{k}({\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{B}})\geq\lambda_{r}({\mathbf{A}})+\lambda_{r}({\mathbf{B}}),\qquad\text{for all }k\in[r].

Applying this inequality yields

\lambda_{T,k}\geq\lambda_{P,r}+\lambda_{r}\!\left(({\mathbf{I}}_{r}+{\mathbf{K}}_{P})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}({\mathbf{I}}_{r}+{\mathbf{K}}_{P})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

The eigenvalues of the latter matrix coincide with those of

{\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbf{I}}_{r}+{\mathbf{K}}_{P}){\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}={\mathbf{K}}_{R}+{\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore,

\lambda_{T,k}\geq\lambda_{P,r}+\lambda_{R,r}+\lambda_{r}({\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Since
{\mathbf{K}}_{P}
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{R}
are positive definite,

\lambda_{r}({\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{K}}_{P}{\mathbf{K}}_{R}^{\frac{1}{2}})=\lambda_{r}({\mathbf{K}}_{P}{\mathbf{K}}_{R})\geq\lambda_{P,r}\,\lambda_{R,r},

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now show that, under the same asymptotic regime, PLS exhibits strictly greater statistical power than separate PCA for detecting and recovering shared spikes.

Proposition 10

(PLS dominates separate PCA for shared spike detection)
.

Assume that, under the high-dimensional regime of Assumption
A1
, PCA applied separately to
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Y}}
detects all
r
spikes,
i.e.,

\lambda_{R,r}>\sqrt{\beta_{q}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{P,r}>\sqrt{\beta_{p}}.

Then, for all
k\in[r]

\lambda_{T,k}>\sqrt{\beta_{p}}+\sqrt{\beta_{q}}+\sqrt{\beta_{p}\beta_{q}},\quad\text{and in particular}\quad\lambda_{T,k}>\tau,

where
\tau
denotes the PLS spectral phase-transition threshold defined in (
14

Consequently, all common spikes detectable by separate PCA are also detectable by PLS, and this implication is generally strict.

Proof.

Lemma
9
implies

\lambda_{T,k}>m=\sqrt{\beta_{p}}+\sqrt{\beta_{q}}+\sqrt{\beta_{p}\beta_{q}}.

Evaluating the threshold polynomial (
13

\lambda=m
reduces to

2\beta_{p}^{3/2}\sqrt{\beta_{q}}+2\sqrt{\beta_{p}}\beta_{q}^{3/2}+2\beta_{p}^{3/2}\beta_{q}+2\beta_{p}\beta_{q}^{3/2}+2\beta_{p}\sqrt{\beta_{q}}+2\sqrt{\beta_{p}}\beta_{q}+4\beta_{p}\beta_{q}

Moreover, the derivative of the polynomial is increasing and, for all
\lambda\geq m

\lambda=m

Therefore,

for all

This establishes the claimed superiority of PLS for spike detection in the considered regime.

Concluding remarks

This work provides a comprehensive theoretical analysis of Partial Least Squares (PLS) in high-dimensional settings using random matrix theory. We established deterministic equivalents for the key resolvent matrices and characterized the limiting spectral distribution of the cross-covariance singular values. Our main contributions include the precise characterization of phase transitions determining when signal components yield isolated singular values, and the quantification of eigenvector alignment with the underlying signal structure. Importantly, we explicitly demonstrate that PLS exhibits greater statistical power than individual PCA applied to each data matrix separately for detecting the shared latent directions in the high-dimensional regime, confirming its theoretical advantage for integrative analysis.

A striking finding of our analysis is the identification of fundamental limitations in the high-dimensional regime. Even in the absence of individual-specific components (
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
), noise induces a skewing effect that prevents PLS singular vectors from aligning with the true signal directions, except in some special cases or in the limit of infinite signal strength. More problematically, when individual components are present, PLS can spuriously align with these uninformative structures rather than with the shared signal, potentially degrading performance in prediction and interpretation tasks.

These theoretical insights open several promising research directions. First, our precise asymptotic characterization of spurious spikes suggests the development of filtering procedures to remove these artifacts while preserving the shared latent structure, in the spirit of the work of
30
. Such methods could reduce model complexity and improve predictive performance by focusing on the common components that PLS is designed to extract.
Second, while we focused on the PLS-SVD formulation, our framework naturally extends to other common deflation schemes described in
(
31
)
.
Even though the deflated components depend on the data matrices in a non-trivial way, all PLS variants share the same first step, and subsequent iterations involve only low-rank updates. As a result, similar deterministic equivalent techniques should allow to characterize their high-dimensional behavior. This would provide a theoretical understanding of the broader PLS family of algorithms, including regression tasks.

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{n^{2}p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{2}\left(\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16}{n^{2}p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{16}{n^{2}p^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{4}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{8}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{4}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}{\left(\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{16}{n^{2}p^{2}}\right)^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{8}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{4}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{8}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{4}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\frac{16}{n^{2}p^{2}}\right)^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{8}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{8}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{4}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]}_{\overset{a.s.}{=}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)}\left(\mathrm{Var}{\left(\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right),

which proves that:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}{\left(\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(25)

We now need to bound the following term
\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}=\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathrm{Var}{\left({\mathbf{a}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{b}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}\right)}
. Using the previous work on
\mathrm{Var}{\left({\mathbf{a}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{b}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}
, we have:

\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathrm{Var}{\left({\mathbf{a}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{b}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}\right)}\leq\left(\frac{4}{n^{2}p^{2}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Var}{\left(\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}

From the law of total variance:

\mathrm{Var}{\left(\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}{\left(\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}\right]+\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}

As the variance of an expectation is null, the first term vanishes. Let’s now bound the second term, using the triangular inequality and Jensen’s inequality.

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]^{2}\right]\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right]\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{8}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{8}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{4}\right]\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{8}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{8}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{4}\right]\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{6}\right)

Finally:

\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right])b\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

(26)

Ultimately, combining equations (
23
), (
24
), (
25
) and (
26
) we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}\right\rvert^{4}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

We can now apply Lemma
2
, which proves the almost sure convergence to
0
of
{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]){\mathbf{b}}
.

A.2.2
Convergence of
\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right])

To apply Lemma
2
, we will prove that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right])\right\rvert^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
. By applying the law of total variance:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right])\right\rvert^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Var}{\left(\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}{\left(\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}\right]+\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}

(27)

By applying Nash–Poincaré inequality (Lemma
4
), we prove that

\mathrm{Var}{\left(\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{\partial\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{E}}_{i,j}}\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

By applying Lemma
5
, it comes that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{E}}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{q}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{q}{\mathbf{A}}_{k,l}\frac{\partial{\mathbf{Q}}_{l,k}}{\partial{\mathbf{E}}_{i,j}}=-\frac{1}{np}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{q}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{q}{\mathbf{A}}_{k,l}\left[({\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top})_{l,i}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{j,k}+({\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})_{l,j}({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{i,k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{np}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}+{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{\partial\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{E}}_{i,j}}\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}p^{2}q^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})_{i,j}\right\rvert^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})_{i,j}\right\rvert^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n^{2}p^{2}q^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{n^{2}p^{2}q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{A}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert^{2}\right],

Finally, as
\left\lVert{\mathbf{A}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq q\left\lVert{\mathbf{A}}\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n)
,
\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert\overset{a.s.}{=}\mathcal{O}(1)
,
\left\lVert{\mathbf{X}}\right\rVert\overset{a.s.}{=}\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
and
\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert\overset{a.s.}{=}\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
, we can prove that:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}{\left(\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right)}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

(28)

Let’s now tackle the second term
\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}
. By applying Nash–Poincaré inequality (Lemma
4
), we prove that

\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]}{\partial{\mathbf{F}}_{i,j}}\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

By applying Lemma
6
, it comes that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]}{\partial{\mathbf{F}}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{q}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{q}{\mathbf{A}}_{k,l}\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]_{l,k}}{\partial{\mathbf{F}}_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{np}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{q}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{q}{\mathbf{A}}_{k,l}\left[(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right])_{l,i}({\mathbf{Y}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right])_{j,k}+(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top})_{l,j}(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right])_{i,k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{np}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\right)_{i,j}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{\partial\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]}{\partial{\mathbf{F}}_{i,j}}\right\rvert^{2}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}p^{2}q^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top})_{i,j}\right\rvert^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top})_{i,j}\right\rvert^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n^{2}p^{2}q^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{n^{2}p^{2}q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{A}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right\rVert^{4}\right]\leq\frac{4}{n^{2}p^{2}q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{A}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{4}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right],

which finally proves that:

\mathrm{Var}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{Q}}|{\mathbf{Y}}\right]\right)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)

(29)

Ultimately, combining (
27
), (
28
) and (
29
), we can conclude that:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right])\right\rvert^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

By applying Lemma
2
, it proves the almost sure convergence to
0
of
\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{Q}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right])
.

A.3
Computation of
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\mid{\mathbf{Y}}\right]

We recall that
{\mathbf{Q}}=\left(\frac{1}{pq}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}-z{\mathbf{I}}_{q}\right)^{-1}
. Since
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{Q}}\mid{\mathbf{Y}}]\right]
, we begin by considering
{\mathbf{Y}}
to be fixed.
From (
1
) and (
4
), we can write

{\mathbf{K}}=\frac{1}{pq}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}=\frac{1}{p}({\mathbf{Z}}+{\mathbf{W}})({\mathbf{Z}}+{\mathbf{W}})^{\top}

with:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{E}},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}}),

Since the entries of
{\mathbf{E}}
are standard i.i.d Gaussian variables with columns denoted as
{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
, for
i\in[p]
, the columns of
{\mathbf{Z}}
, denoted as
{\mathbf{z}}_{i}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
, are i.i.d centered vectors with covariance

{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\mathrm{Cov}({\mathbf{z}}_{i}\mid{\mathbf{Y}})=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{b}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\right]{\mathbf{Y}}=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}.

This leads us to first study the resolvent of a sample covariance matrix for a signal-plus-noise model with correlated noise
{\mathbf{Z}}
and signal matrix
{\mathbf{W}}
.
In the subsequent computations of section
A.3
, we assume that
{\mathbf{Y}}
is fixed, and therefore the columns of
{\mathbf{Z}}
have covariance
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
.

Then, starting from
{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}{\mathbf{Q}}={\mathbf{I}}_{q}
, we obtain:

\frac{1}{p}\left({\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right)={\mathbf{I}}_{q}+z{\mathbf{Q}}.

(30)

A.3.1
Term
\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}_{ia}({\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}})_{aj}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ib}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{Z}}_{ba}}\right]_{aj}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ib}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{e}}_{a}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{aj}-\frac{1}{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{\top}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{aj}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{e}}_{a}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{aj}+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{\top}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{aj}+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{e}}_{a}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{aj}\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ib}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{bj}\right]-\frac{1}{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}]_{ab}[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}]_{aj}\right]\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}}]_{aa}{\mathbf{Q}}_{bj}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}]_{ab}[{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}]_{aj}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}}]_{aa}{\mathbf{Q}}_{bj}\right]\bigg)

where the second line comes from Stein’s lemma
3
for correlated Gaussian vectors. We thus obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]-\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\Tr{{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}}}{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{p^{2}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]-\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\Tr{{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}}}}{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]-\frac{1}{p^{2}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right].

The last three terms in the previous equation are
o(1)
in norm. In particular, the one with the trace is
o(1)
because
{\mathbf{W}}
has a fixed low rank. As
{\mathbf{W}}
is low-rank, we also have, from (
30
):

\frac{1}{p}\Tr\left({\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right)\overset{a.s.}{=}\Tr\left({\mathbf{I}}_{q}+z{\mathbf{Q}}\right)+o(1)=q+z\Tr{\mathbf{Q}}+o(1).

As we have shown in Appendix
A.2
,
\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\mathbf{Q}}
converges almost surely to
\frac{1}{q}\Tr\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]-\left(\frac{q}{p}zm(z)+\frac{q}{p}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right],

(31)

where
m(z)
is defined as the Stieltjes transform of the limiting spectral distribution of
\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}
, satisfying
m(z)=\frac{1}{q}\Tr\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}(z)
.

A.3.2
Term
\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}

Using similar calculations, it comes that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ij}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}_{ia}({\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}})_{aj}\right]=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ib}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{Z}}_{ba}}\right]_{aj}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ib}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{\top}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{e}}_{a}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{\top}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{e}}_{a}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{aj}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ib}\mathbb{E}\left[({\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}})_{ab}({\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}})_{aj}+({\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}})_{aa}{\mathbf{Q}}_{bj}+({\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}})_{ab}{\mathbf{Q}}_{aj}+({\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}})_{aa}{\mathbf{Q}}_{bj}\right]

Thus we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[Z{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr{({\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top})}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr{({\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top})}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right])

\displaystyle=o(1).

A.3.3
Term
\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ij}=\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}\right]_{ji}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{a=1}^{q}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{ja}{\mathbf{Z}}_{ak}\right]{\mathbf{W}}_{ik}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{a=1}^{q}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ab}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{Z}}_{bk}}\right]_{ja}{\mathbf{W}}_{ik}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{q}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ab}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{b}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ja}{\mathbf{W}}_{ik}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\sum_{a=1}^{q}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{b=1}^{p}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ab}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{jb}({\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}})_{ka}+({\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Z}})_{jk}{\mathbf{Q}}_{ba}+{\mathbf{Q}}_{jb}({\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}})_{ka}+({\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{W}})_{jk}{\mathbf{Q}}_{ba}\right]{\mathbf{W}}_{ik}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\Tr({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}})+{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\Tr({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}})\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\Tr{({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}})}+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\Tr{({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}})}).

The two first terms of the right hand side are negligeable, thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\left(1+\Tr{({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}})}\right)\right)=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\Tr{({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}})}.

Taking
\frac{1}{p}\Tr{(\cdot)}
from (
31
), it comes that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\Tr{({\mathbf{\Sigma}}{\mathbf{Q}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{q}{p}zm(z)+\frac{q}{p}}{1-\frac{q}{p}zm(z)-\frac{q}{p}}+o(1),

\displaystyle=-1-\frac{1}{z\tilde{m}(z)}+o(1),

where
\tilde{m}(z)
is defined as the Stieltjes transform of the limiting spectral distribution of
\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Z}}
, satisfying
\tilde{m}(z)=\frac{1}{p}\Tr\bar{\tilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}}(z)
. By using standard properties of the resolvent, we can prove that
z\tilde{m}(z)=\frac{p}{q}zm(z)-\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)
, hence the last equality.

Finally,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=-\left[1+z\tilde{m}(z)\right]\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+o(1).

A.3.4
Term
\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}

Since
{\mathbf{W}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}})

it comes that
\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}

where
{\mathbf{D}}=\frac{1}{p}\left({\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}}\right)\left({\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{\top}
is a bounded low rank matrix.

A.3.5
Synthesis

Putting all the terms together, we obtain from (
30
) that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{I}}_{q}+z\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{p}\left({\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{p}{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\frac{1}{p}(1-(1+z\tilde{m}(z)))\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{q}{p}zm(z)-\frac{q}{p}\right){\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]-z\tilde{m}(z)\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right],

\displaystyle=-z\tilde{m}(z)\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]-z\tilde{m}(z)\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right],

\displaystyle=-z\tilde{m}(z)\left(\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}\right){\mathbf{Y}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right],

which yields (assuming now that
{\mathbf{Y}}
is a random vector)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{z\tilde{m}(z)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}\right){\mathbf{Y}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{m}(z)}{\mathbf{I}}_{q}\right)^{-1}\right].

(32)

A.4
Computation of the expectation w.r.t
{\mathbf{Y}}

We define the following
q\times q
resolvent matrix

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}_{\mathbf{Y}}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}\right){\mathbf{Y}}-z{\mathbf{I}}_{q}\right)^{-1},

where we recall that
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{H}}+{\mathbf{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
,
{\mathbf{H}}={\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
and
{\mathbf{D}}=\frac{1}{p}\left({\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}}\right)\left({\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are deterministic low rank matrices, and
{\mathbf{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
is the noise matrix with i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries. This a ’compound’ spike model with both a covariance spike and an information-plus-noise spike.

The objective is to compute its mean:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{\mathbf{Y}}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}\right){\mathbf{Y}}-z{\mathbf{I}}_{q}\right)^{-1}\right],

(33)

Then we can directly derive from
(
32
) our deterministic equivalent for the original problem as

\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}(z)=\frac{-1}{z\tilde{m}(z)}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left(\frac{-1}{\tilde{m}(z)}\right)\right]
.
In the following we will denote the Stieltjes transform of the limiting eigenvalue distribution of the matrix
\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}\right){\mathbf{Y}}
as
m_{Y}(z)
. In fact we will check latter in p.
11
that
m_{Y}(z)=\frac{1}{\beta_{q}}m_{\mathrm{MP}}\left(\frac{z}{\beta_{q}}\right)
, where
m_{\mathrm{MP}}(\tilde{z})
is the Stieltjes transform of the Marčenko–Pastur distribution with parameter
\frac{1}{\beta_{q}}
. Moreover the
{\mathbf{Y}}
index for the resolvent in (
33
) will be dropped when there is no risk of confusion.

As usual, we start with the following development:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}+\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}+\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}={\mathbf{I}}_{q}+z{\mathbf{Q}}.

(34)

A.4.1
1st term
\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}

According to Stein’s lemma lem:stein,
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}_{ki}({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{kj}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{F}}_{ki}}\right]_{kj}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}_{ki}({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{kj}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{F}}_{ki}}\right]_{kj}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{kj}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{kj}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}\right]-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{kj}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}\right]-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{ki}\left(({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right)_{kj}+\left({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)_{kk}{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}\right]-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\Tr\left({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}\right]\right)

\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=\frac{n}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\Tr\left({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\right)

We have
\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}={\mathbf{I}}_{q}+z{\mathbf{Q}}
, thus
\frac{1}{q^{2}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Q}}({\mathbf{I}}_{q}+z{\mathbf{Q}})
and

\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert\leq\frac{1}{q}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert+\frac{|z|}{q}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)=o(1)
.
This shows that the second term on the right hand side of the above equation vanishes.
In addition,

\frac{1}{q^{2}}\Tr{\left({\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)}=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\Tr{\left({\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right)}=\frac{1}{q}\Tr{\left({\mathbf{I}}_{q}+z{\mathbf{Q}}\right)}=1+zm_{Y}(z)
. Then

\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\left(\frac{n}{q}-(1+zm_{Y}(z))\right)+o(1)

A.4.2
2nd term
\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}_{ki}({\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{kj}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{F}}_{ki}}\right]_{kj}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{kj}-\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{kj}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{D}}_{ki}{\mathbf{Q}}_{kj}\right]-\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[({\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{ki}(({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{kj}+({\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}))_{kk}{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ij}-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ij}+\Tr({\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}))\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}\right]\right)

\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]+\Tr({\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}))\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\right)

We see that
\lVert{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)
thus
\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=o(1)
. Besides, we have the following inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\rVert^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right\rVert\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)

with
\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
\left\lVert{\mathbf{D}}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\frac{1}{q}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

For the other term we have, with Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left\lvert\Tr\left({\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)\right\rvert=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left\lvert\Tr\left(({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left\lVert({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert_{F}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{F}\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\left\lVert({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{l}{q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\right\rVert=\frac{l}{q^{2}}\left\lVert{\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\mathbf{D}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\mathbf{Q}}\right\rVert\left\lVert{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{l}{q}\right)=o(1).

All the terms vanished so:

\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}^{\top}{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=o(1)

A.4.3
3rd term
\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{ik}{\mathbf{Q}}_{kj}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial{\mathbf{Q}}}{\partial{\mathbf{F}}_{ik}}\right]_{kj}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}+{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{e}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{kj}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{kk}(({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}})_{ij}+({\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}))_{ki}{\mathbf{Q}}_{kj}\right],

and finally

\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\Tr({\mathbf{Q}})+\mathbb{E}\left[({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}^{2}\right]\right).

We know that that
\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left\lVert({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}^{2}\right\rVert=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{q\sqrt{q}}\right)=o(1)
, thus

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{F}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=-\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]\Tr{({\mathbf{Q}})}=-({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]m_{Y}(z).

Then it comes that

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=-({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]m_{Y}(z)+{\mathbf{H}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right],

which gives that

({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+m_{Y}(z)({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}))\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]={\mathbf{H}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right],

and finally

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]=\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+m_{Y}(z)({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right].

Returning to the original term, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}}){\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Y}}{\mathbf{Q}}\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+m_{Y}(z)({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+m_{Y}(z)({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)^{-1}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{H}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}\right]

A.4.4
Conclusion

Putting all three terms together, we have:

\displaystyle z\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}\right]+{\mathbf{I}}_{q}=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}\right]\left(\frac{n}{q}-(1+zm_{Y}(z))\right)+\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+m_{Y}(z)({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)^{-1}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}H\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left(\left(-z+\frac{n}{q}-(1+zm_{Y}(z))\right){\mathbf{I}}_{q}+\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+m_{Y}(z)({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)^{-1}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{H}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}\right]={\mathbf{I}}_{q}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}\right]=\left(\left(-z+\frac{n}{q}-(1+zm_{Y}(z))\right){\mathbf{I}}_{q}+\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+m_{Y}(z)({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)^{-1}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{H}}\right)^{-1}

Taking
\frac{1}{q}\Tr{(\cdot)}
on both sides of the first line in the previous equations, and discarding the rightmost term, which is vanishing, yields that
m_{Y}(z)
satisfies:

zm^{2}_{Y}(z)-\left(\frac{n}{q}-1-z\right)m_{Y}(z)+1=0,

(35)

which is equivalent to say that
m_{Y}(z)=\frac{1}{\beta_{q}}m_{\mathrm{MP}}(\frac{z}{\beta_{q}})
, where
m_{\mathrm{MP}}(\tilde{z})
satisfies:

\displaystyle\tilde{z}\frac{1}{\beta_{q}}m^{2}_{MP}(\tilde{z})-(1-\frac{1}{\beta_{q}}-\tilde{z})m_{\mathrm{MP}}(\tilde{z})+1=0,

which is the Marčenko–Pastur equation with parameter
\frac{1}{\beta_{q}}
. This means that
m_{Y}(z)
can be interpreted as the Stieltjes transform of the limiting spectral distribution of
\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}
. From equation (
35
),
\frac{n}{q}-1-z-zm_{Y}(z)=\frac{1}{m_{Y}(z)}
, and it comes that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{m_{Y}(z)}{\mathbf{I}}_{q}+\frac{1}{q}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+m_{Y}(z)({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})\right)^{-1}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\mathbf{D}})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{H}}\right)^{-1}

(36)

We recall that
{\mathbf{H}}={\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{N}}
and
{\mathbf{D}}=\frac{1}{p}({\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}})({\mathbf{T}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{M}})^{\top}
. Using (two times) Woodbury identity yields now:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Q}}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{m_{Y}(z)}{\mathbf{I}}_{q}+\frac{1}{1+m_{Y}(z)}\frac{1}{q}\left({\mathbf{N}}^{\top}{\mathbf{N}}+{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\right)+\right.

by using (

). As

, with

\displaystyle{\mathbf{I}}_{r}+\frac{h(z)}{1+h(z)}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{RP}{\mathbf{V}}_{\star}^{\top}\left({\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}^{-1}+\left[(1+h(z)){\mathbf{I}}_{r}+h(z){\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}\right]^{-1}\right){\mathbf{V}}_{\star}

\displaystyle\left({\mathbf{I}}_{r}+\frac{h(z)}{1+h(z)}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{RP}{\mathbf{V}}_{\star}^{\top}\left({\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}^{-1}+\left[(1+h(z)){\mathbf{I}}_{r}+h(z){\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}\right]^{-1}\right){\mathbf{V}}_{\star}\right)^{-1}

In the limit

where

{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{k}(z)={\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{RP}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{V}}_{\star}^{\top}\left[(1+h(z)){\mathbf{I}}_{r}+h(z){\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}\right]\left({\mathbf{I}}_{r}+{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{U}}_{T}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}_{T}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{r}+{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{V}}_{\star}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{RP}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We recall our initial problem:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k},{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{z\to\xi_{k}}\frac{z-\xi_{k}}{z\tilde{m}(z)}\frac{h(z)}{1+h(z)(\lambda_{k}+1)}{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{k}(z){\mathbf{x}}_{k}.

As in Appendix

\displaystyle\langle\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k},{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{z\to\xi_{k}}\frac{z-\xi_{k}}{z\tilde{m}(z)}\frac{h(z)}{1+h(z)(\lambda_{k}+1)}{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{k}(z){\mathbf{x}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{h(\xi_{k})}{\xi_{k}\tilde{m}(\xi_{k})}{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{k}(\xi_{k}){\mathbf{x}}_{k}\lim_{z\to\xi_{k}}\frac{z-\xi_{k}}{1+h(z)(\lambda_{k}+1)}=\frac{h(\xi_{k})}{\xi_{k}\tilde{m}(\xi_{k})}{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{k}(\xi_{k}){\mathbf{x}}_{k}\frac{1}{h^{\prime}(\xi_{k})(\lambda_{k}+1)},

the last equality being obtained through L’Hôpital’s rule. From the previous computations, we already know that

\displaystyle h(\xi_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{\lambda_{k}+1},

(60)

\displaystyle\tilde{m}(\xi_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{-\lambda_{k}}{(\lambda_{k}+1)(\lambda_{k}+\beta_{q})},

(61)

\displaystyle\xi_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\lambda_{k}+1)(\lambda_{k}+\beta_{p})(\lambda_{k}+\beta_{q})}{\lambda_{k}^{2}},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{k}(\xi_{k}){\mathbf{x}}_{k}=\frac{1}{\|\bar{{\mathbf{v}}}_{k}\|^{2}}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}_{T}^{\top}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{r}+{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{V}}_{\star}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{RP}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{RP}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{V}}_{\star}^{\top}\left[(1+h(\xi_{k})){\mathbf{I}}_{r}+h(\xi_{k}){\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}\right]{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{r}+{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{P}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{U}}_{T}{\mathbf{e}}_{k}
