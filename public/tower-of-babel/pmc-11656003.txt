Analysis of transcriptomic data reveals the landscape of cholesterol metabolism in prostate cancer and impact of related signature on survival
Authors: Jian-Xuan Sun, Ye An, Meng-Yao Xu, Si-Yang Ma, Chen-Qian Liu, Jin-Zhou Xu, Qi-Dong Xia, Shao-Gang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCholesterol metabolism is essential for the development and progression of prostate cancer (PCa). Our previous study provided a new insight of cholesterol metabolism in the systematic management of PCa. However, the comprehensive role of cholesterol metabolism in PCa remains unclear.MethodsUsing the cholesterol metabolism related genes (CMRGs) downloaded from the MSigDB database, and gene expression data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), we constructed a cholesterol risk index by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) model, and correlated the risk index with prognosis, tumor mutation burden (TMB), tumor microenvironment (TME) infiltration and response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. RT-qPCR, western blot, immunohistochemistry, cell proliferation assays by CCK-8 and EdU assays, and cell apoptosis assays by flow cytometry analysis were also performed.ResultsWe found PCa was tightly correlated with the cholesterol metabolism pathways. The cholesterol risk index was an excellent and independent predictor of prognosis for PCa. A nomogram involving the risk index and other clinical factors (age, T stage) was established to explore the clinical value of risk index. We found high-risk index group was associated with worse prognosis, higher TMB, lower infiltration level of CD8+ T cells and a worse response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. RT-qPCR, western blot and immunohistochemical staining validated the expression level of important CMRGs in PCa. In vitro experiments revealed downregulation of cholesterol metabolism could inhibit the proliferation of PCa cells and promoted their apoptosis.ConclusionsWe demonstrated the comprehensive role of cholesterol metabolism in PCa. Using the risk index, we could predict the prognosis, TME infiltration and response to chemotherapy/immunotherapy of PCa. Better understanding and evaluating the cholesterol metabolism could aid in precision medicine and promoting prognosis of PCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01658-x.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignancies. With approximately 1.4 million new cases and more than 300 thousand cancer specific deaths in 2020, PCa counts for the second highest incidence and the fifth cause of cancer related death in men worldwide [1]. Although advanced PCa indicates poor prognosis, patients with low-risk PCa usually have high 5-year survival rates [2]. Patients could receive active surveillance, radiotherapy (RT), radical prostatectomy (RP) and androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) for treatment depending on different stages of PCa [3]. Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is a serine protease, which is almost exclusively secreted by prostatic epithelial cells [4]. Consequently, elevated levels of PSA can indicate the progression of PCa to a certain extent. To date, PSA measurement remains the most sensitive approach for monitoring PCa recurrence [5]. The definition of biochemical recurrence (BCR) is the rerunning of PSA after radical treatment, and 27–53% of patients who accepted either external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) or RP experience BCR [6]. Despite BCR does not necessarily lead to disease progression or metastasis, BCR is still one of the best predictive factors for clinical progression or recurrence of PCa.

Our previous study has demonstrated that statins (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors) usage had positive impact on patients with PCa and could reduce the risk of BCR, especially for those who accepted RT [7]. Besides, we also reported that statins played a unique role in reducing cancer-specific mortality and all-cause mortality in patients with PCa [8]. It’s known that PCa is a highly androgen-dependent disease. Androgen receptor (AR) expression has been found in almost all PCa with different stage or grade, including primary PCa and metastatic PCa. Even in the majority of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), whose growth and survival were independent of AR signaling, the AR expression was still preserved [9]. Cholesterol is a precursor for the synthesis of androgen, and it’s reported that the presence of PCa had tight connection with the accumulation of cholesterol in prostatic tissue [10]. Sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs), including SREBP 1a, SREBP 1b and SREBP 2, could be transported into the nucleus and activate the expression of lipid metabolism related genes such as HMG CoA synthase and reductase. Especially, SREBP 2 isoform is important to cholesterol metabolism and promotes the intracellular accumulation of cholesterol. Studies have found SREBPs were significantly upregulated in an AR dependent way in PCa, especially after castration resistance [11]. It was also reported that the inhibition of the activity of SREBPs by fatostatin could suppress the proliferation and growth of AR positive PCa cells both in vitro and in vivo [11]. Statins are capable of suppressing cholesterol synthesis, therefore, reduce the serum cholesterol level, and are usually used to prevent the cardiovascular disease. We believe the prostatic cholesterol metabolism played a non-negligible role in the neogenesis and progression of PCa, and would bring a novel insight for the comprehensive management of PCa.

However, previous studies reported that the serum cholesterol level was not related to the risk of incident PCa [12] or low serum cholesterol level may associate with an increased risk of PCa [13]. These studies indicated that the relationship between cholesterol metabolism and PCa is more complex than previously assumed, necessitating further investigation to unravel its intricacies. It is noteworthy that Stopsack et al. discovered an association between the expression levels of squalene monooxygenase (SQLE), a key enzyme in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, and the risk of developing lethal PCa [14]. A recent study also proposed SQLE as a therapeutic target for advanced PCa [15]. Therefore, it’s essential and urgent to comprehensively analyze the association between cholesterol metabolism and PCa, the latent regulatory mechanisms behind them, and establish a system to help screen out appropriate patients with better prognosis after treatment.

In this study, using 352 cholesterol metabolism-related genes (CMRGs) and 27 cholesterol metabolism pathways from the MigDSB database, we screened out 19 CMRGs which might play a significant role in PCa. Then, we developed a cholesterol risk index by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method, and successfully stratified patients into low- and high-risk index subgroups. We evaluated the association between tumor mutation burden (TMB) and cholesterol risk index, and correlated them with patients’ prognosis. The association between tumor microenvironment (TME) and cholesterol risk index was also investigated. Furthermore, a nomogram involving the cholesterol risk index and other clinical characteristics was constructed for predicting the prognosis of patients with PCa. We also correlated the cholesterol risk index with the response to chemotherapy or immunotherapy to aid in precision medicine. Finally, we verified the expression levels of essential CMRGs in PCa cells and explored the effect of inhibiting cholesterol synthesis by atorvastatin on the proliferation and apoptosis of PCa cells.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human prostate carcinoma epithelial cells LNCaP clone FGC (CL-0143) were kindly provided by Pricella Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. (Wuhan, China). 22Rv1 (CX0041) cell line was purchased from Boster Biological Engineering (Wuhan, China). Normal prostate epithelial cell line RWPE1 (SNL-569) were obtained from SUNNCELL Biotech (Wuhan, China). Before all experiments, they were authenticated by short tandem repeat DNA profiling. RWPE1 was cultured in Prostate Epithelial Cell Medium (Catalog #4411, PEpiCM, ScienCell) added with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Cat. #0503, P/S solution, ScienCell) and Prostate Epithelial Cell Growth Supplement (Cat. #4452, PEpiCGS, ScienCell). LNCaP and 22Rv1 cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 including 1% penicillin-streptomycin (R8758, Sigma-Aldrich) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (AB-FBS-1050, ABW, Shanghai, China). All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the AFTSpin Tissue/Cell Fast RNA Extraction Kit (RK30120, ABclonal, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and the 260/280 nm ratio with a UV spectrophotometer (NanoDrop One/Onec, Thermo Fisher Scientific). 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using ReverTra Ace® qPCR RT Master Mix kit (FSQ-201, TOYOBO, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with the SYBR Green Master Mix (1708880, Bio-Rad, USA) on a QuantStudioTM 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. A melting curve analysis was conducted post-amplification to confirm the specificity of the PCR products. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH and β-actin as the endogenous control [16]. The PCR primers were shown in Table 1.

Western blot

Protein extracts were obtained from cellular lysates using RIPA buffer enriched with PMSF and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the lysates were sonicated with a sonication device (BiLon, China) at 4 °C, applying an 8% amplitude for a total duration of 3 min with 10-second intervals to prevent overheating. Protein concentrations were measured employing the BCA assay (Beyotime, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal quantities of protein (30 µg) were loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel (Epizyme, China) and subjected to electrophoresis at 100 V for 90 min. The proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA) at 230 mA for 90 min using a wet transfer apparatus. The membranes were then blocked with 5% BSA (Yeasen, China) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature before being incubated with primary antibodies targeting ABCA2 (1:1000, ABclonal, China), β-tubulin (1:5000, ABclonal, China) and ANXA2 (1:1000, Baijia, China) overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with TBST for 10 min each, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000, ABclonal, China) or anti-mouse IgG (1:10000, ABclonal, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Following another three washes with TBST for 10 min each, the immunoreactive bands were detected using an ECL detection system (ChemiDoc™ MP, Bio-Rad, USA).

Cell proliferation assay

The CCK-8 assay (RM02823, ABclonal, China) was used to assess the viability of 22Rv1 and LNCaP cells. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 8 × 103 cells/well and added 100 µL of complete medium. The cells were treated with different concentrations of atorvastatin (0, 5, 10, 20, 40 µM) for 24 and 48 h at 37 °C. Next, 10 µL of the CCK-8 solution was added to cultures and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine cell proliferation assay

The BeyoClick™ EdU-555 kit (C0075S, Beyotime, China) was used to monitor prostate cancer cell growth after atorvastatin treatment. For the EdU experiment, PCa cells (3 × 104 cells/well for LNCaP and 8 × 104 cells/well for 22Rv1) were plated in 24-well plates and were treated by different concentrations of atorvastatin (0, 20, 40 µM) for 48 h at 37 °C. Then cells were treated for 2 h with EdU solution (10 µM). After fixation with 4% formaldehyde (Servicebio), 0.5% Triton X-100 was applied to infiltrate the cells. A Click-iT reaction mixture was utilized to stain the cells, and counterstaining was performed using DAPI. Five random fields were photographed via a fluorescence microscope (ZOE Fluorescent Cell Imager, Bio-Rad, USA).

Cell apoptosis assays

For cell apoptosis assays, PCa cells (LNCaP and 22Rv1, 1 × 105 cells/well) were plated in 6-well plates and were treated by different concentrations of atorvastatin (MedChemExpress, USA) (0, 20, 40 µM) for 48 h at 37 °C. Then cell apoptosis assays were performed using Annexin V-PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (E-CK-A211, Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction, and followed by flow cytometry analysis (Beckman, USA).

Data retrieval and preprocessing

27 cholesterol metabolism pathways containing 352 CMRGs were downloaded from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) and uploaded as supplementary materials (Table S1). The RNA bulk-seq and corresponding clinical data were retrieved and downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). External verification cohorts (GSE46602 and GSE70770) were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Then we used the Combat algorithm with the default index in package “sva” to merge these three cohorts and eliminate the batch effects [17]. We also performed the principal components analysis (PCA) to confirm the efficacy of mergence.

Identification of differentially expressed CMRGs, development and verification of the cholesterol risk index

We screened out the differentially expressed CMRGs between normal prostatic tissues and PCa tissues by R package “limma” with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| >0.585 (FC > 1.5) [18]. The differentially expressed CMRGs were plotted in a Volcano diagram. Then, we separately merged the expression matrix with their corresponding prognosis information for the TCGA cohort and two GEO cohorts. As the sample size in TCGA is much larger, we chose TCGA as the training set and the other two GEO cohorts as external validation sets. Following this, we performed the univariate Cox regression in TCGA cohorts to find those differentially expressed CMRGs with prognostic values, and finally, 19 genes were screened out. The LASSO model was constructed to further screen out the appropriate CMRGs without over-fitting in the TCGA cohort based on the 19 genes. Finally, the minimum lambda equal to 0.0134 (log(λ)=−4.3159) was selected as the most appropriate lambda, 13 genes were left, and a cholesterol risk index was established by integrating these 13 genes. Each sample in TCGA, GSE46602 and GSE70770 could acquire a cholesterol risk index based on this formula:

In this formula, coef(i) represents the coefficient of the ith CMRGs in cholesterol risk index, and expr(i) represents the normalized expression value of the ith CMRGs. Then, we set the median value of the cholesterol risk index from the TCGA cohort as the threshold. Patient who had a risk index higher than the threshold was regarded as high-risk, and vice versa. Therefore, every sample in the TCGA cohort, GSE46602 and GSE70770 verification cohorts could be stratified to low- or high-risk index groups according to their index.

Subsequently, Kaplan–Meier method survival curve and log-rank survival analysis were performed to compare the survival advantage between low-risk index group and high-risk index group in TCGA, GSE46602 and GSE70770 cohorts. To further validate the predictive capability of risk index, we plotted the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Since TCGA, GSE46602 and GSE70770 cohorts are independent from each other, both heterogeneity analysis and meta-analysis were performed to integrate these cohorts and obtained a pooled hazard ratio (pHR). In addition, we associated the risk index with clinical characteristics (N stage, T stage and immune subtype). To further dig out the clinical implication of the risk index, a nomogram involving cholesterol risk index, age and T stage was constructed. Finally, the calibration curves in TCGA cohort and the two verification cohorts were used for checking the predictive efficacy of our nomogram.

The correlation between TMB, TME immune infiltration and cholesterol risk index

To further dig out the latent mechanisms that contribute to the differential characteristics between different risk groups, we investigated the mutation distribution in these two groups. The mutation profile of the TCGA cohort was downloaded from GDC_Data_Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), and the TMB of each sample was calculated. Then differences in mutation distribution between low- and high-risk groups were tested and visualized as waterfall plots. We evaluated the correlation between risk index and TMB, and united them to stratify patients and examine the predictive capability.

A comprehensive analysis of TME immune infiltration was conducted using the CIBERSORT-ABS, XCELL, CIBERSORT, EPIC, QUANTISEQ, TIMER and MCPCOUNTER algorithms from the TIMER 2.0 database (http://timer.cistrome.org/) [19]. We also performed differential immune infiltration analyses and correlation analyses between different risk groups. Moreover, we also evaluated the relative expression levels of immune checkpoint genes and their correlation with risk index.

GSVA, GSEA, sensitivity to chemotherapy or immunotherapy, and the histological validation

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) in low- and high-risk index groups was performed by the “GSVA” package in R software [20]. The cell senescence-related gene sets, KEGG_signaling pathway, the programmed cell death-related gene sets, and the HALLMARK pathways were separately applied to GSVA. Similarly, we conducted the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the “clusterProfiler” package in R software [21], and the GO_signaling pathways and cholesterol metabolism-related pathways were the enrichment gene sets. All the data sets were retrieved and downloaded from MSigDb.

We predict the therapeutic response to chemotherapy by the ProPhetic algorithm [22], and the drug sensitivity (visualized as IC50) was analyzed and compared between low- and high-risk groups. Not only the commonly used chemotherapy drug, docetaxel, was investigated, but the drugs that have not yet been used in PCa treatment were also involved. The response to immunotherapy was investigated by the TCIA method [23]. Finally, considering the role of post-translational modification in the development and progression of cancers, we conducted further histological validation of several important CMRGs in protein levels between normal prostatic and tumor tissues. The histological sections were searched and downloaded from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) [24].

Statistical analysis

All the data collecting, processing, analyzing and figure plotting were performed in R software vision 4.1.1. A P value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistical significance.

Results

Enrichment of cholesterol metabolism pathways and identification of 19 vital CMRGs in PCa

The whole analysis process of this study was visualized as a flowchart and shown in Fig. 1. Initially, we corrected for batch effects in the TCGA, GSE46602, and GSE70770 datasets and integrated them into a single meta-cohort. Prior to processing, the three cohorts were distinctly separable by PCA (Fig. 2A), while they merged well after processing (Fig. 2B). The fundamental characteristics of the patients with PCa in the cohort were exhibited in Table 2. We first investigated the differential enrichment pathways in the 27 cholesterol metabolism pathways between normal and tumor tissues. We found regulation of cholesterol esterification (Fig. 2C), regulation of cholesterol metabolic process (Fig. 2D), blood HDL cholesterol cis-QTL (Fig. 2E), blood HDL cholesterol trans-QTL (Fig. 2F) and statin inhibition of cholesterol production (Fig. 2G) pathways were enriched in tumor tissue. The original plots for these five pathways are displayed in Figure S1A. The enrichment of the statin inhibition of cholesterol production pathway in tumor tissue suggests a therapeutic potential of statins in PCa, consistent with our previous findings [7]. Subsequently, we identified differentially expressed CMRGs between normal and tumor tissues (Fig. 2H), with the top 50 most highly expressed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) shown in Fig. 2I. Following this, we screened 19 DEGs with significant survival advantages using univariate Cox regression (Fig. 2J). Among them, eight genes (EGF, STARD4, ANXA2, APOF, ATF3, LGALS3, CYP27A1 and PPARGC1A) showed significantly reduced risk of BCR, while the remaining eleven genes (GRAMD1A, INHBA, ABCA2, APPOE, TREM2, YJEFN, MSI1, SCRIB, TP53INP1, NFYB AND PDIA2) showed increased risk of BCR. Although the mutation spectrum revealed a low frequency of somatic mutations in these 19 genes (8 out of 484 samples, 1.65%) within the TCGA cohort (Fig. 2K), we intriguingly discovered a significant correlation among these genes, indicating co-mutation events (Fig. 2L).

Establishment and internal verification of cholesterol risk index in TCGA cohort

Using the 19 DEGs with prognostic value, we performed the LASSO regression analysis in the TCGA cohort, and finally screened out 13 genes (Fig. 3A and B). Table 3 showed the information and specific coefficient of these 13 prognostic genes. We then examined the correlations among them, revealing a complex correlation network (Fig. 3D, Figure S1B). Based on the expression levels of these 13 genes, we calculated a cholesterol risk index for each patient, subsequently stratifying them into low- and high-risk groups using the median risk index from the TCGA cohort as the threshold (Fig. 3C, Figure S1C and D). We employed PCA to assess the ability to distinguish between low- and high-risk patients using the original 352 CMRGs (Fig. 3E) and these 13 genes (Fig. 3F), discovering that the 13 genes provided better patient stratification than the original set. Furthermore, we plotted the survival status of patients in the TCGA cohort, noting a significant enrichment of BCR in the high-risk index group (yellow dots), indicating poorer prognosis for patients with a high-risk index (Fig. 3G). Survival analysis confirmed that patients with a high-risk index had inferior biochemical recurrence-free survival (BCRFS) compared to those with a low-risk index (Fig. 3H). Additionally, the ROC curve for the cholesterol risk index in the TCGA cohort demonstrated accuracies of 0.734, 0.760, and 0.748 in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year BCRFS, respectively (Fig. 3I). Compared to other clinicopathological factors (T stage, N stage, and age), the cholesterol risk index exhibited a larger area under the curve (AUC) (Fig. 3J), signifying superior predictive power.

External verification and the correlation of clinicopathological characteristics with cholesterol risk index

To further verify the efficacy of cholesterol risk index, similarly, PCA, survival status analyses and survival analyses were performed in GSE46602 and GSE70770 cohorts. The results of PCA were exhibited in Figure S1E and F. Consistent with our expectations, we observed a significant overrepresentation of patients with BCR in the high-risk index group (Figure S1G and H), and a markedly better prognosis for patients with a low-risk index compared to those in the high-risk group (Fig. 4A and B). Considering TCGA, GSE46602 and GSE70770 are three distinct datasets, a meta-analysis was conducted to further validate the effectiveness of risk index. We found patients from high-risk index group had significantly higher probability of BCR (pHR = 3.09, 95% CI = 2.35–4.06, I2 = 8%, common effect model) (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these results suggest that the cholesterol risk index is a robust prognostic predictor in PCa.

The correlation between tumor mutation burden and cholesterol risk index

Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was described as a good predictor of the therapeutic effect of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in some solid tumors [25]. However, when applied in PCa, TMB lacks a consistent result. Luo et al. reported a better prognosis or patients in high TMB group, while Liao et al. reported a wore prognosis [26, 27]. Therefore, we first evaluated the prognostic value of TMB in PCa, and found patients from low TMB group had better BCRFS than those in high TMB group (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, we depicted the mutation spectra for patients in the low-risk index group (Fig. 5B) and high-risk index group (Fig. 5C). Generally, there were no obvious differential distribution between these two groups, but, notably, we found significant differences in TP53 and FOXA1. Patients in the low-risk index group exhibited a significantly lower TMB (Fig. 5D), and the subsequent linear regression indicated that the value of risk index was significantly positively correlated with TMB (Fig. 5E). To further investigate the clinical prognostic value of the cholesterol risk index, we integrated the risk index with TMB as a novel predictive factor. Survival analyses revealed that patients with low TMB and low risk index had the best BCRFS, and patients with low-risk index had better prognosis than those with high index irrespective of high or low TMB, which indicated risk index was a prognostic predictor independent of TMB (Fig. 5F). In 2018, a study did a comprehensive immune infiltration analysis involving 33 cancers based on the TCGA data sets, and successfully grouped all tumors into six immune subtypes [28]. Among them, subtype C3 was defined as the inflammatory subtype which have high infiltration levels of lymphocytes and macrophages, and the best prognosis than other subtypes. We investigated the correlation between risk index and immune subtype, and surprisingly found immune subtype C3 was correlated with the lowest risk index (Fig. 5G). These results indicated a considerable prognostic value of the combination of TMB and risk index for PCa.

Cholesterol risk index correlated with immune infiltration characteristics of TME

The state of immune cell infiltration in TME determines the fate of cancers to some extent, and we wondered whether patients from different cholesterol risk groups were corresponding to different characteristics of immune cell infiltration. Therefore, we performed TME immune cell infiltration analysis by CIBERSORT-ABS, CIBERSORT, TIMER, QUANTISEQ, XCELL, EPIC and MCPCOUNTER. We discovered that T cell regulatory (Treg), M2 macrophage and cancer associated fibroblast were highly enriched in high-risk index group, while CD8+ T cell and plasma B cell exhibited high enrichment in low-risk group (Fig. 6A). The following correlation analysis between infiltration level of immune cells and risk index reconfirmed these results (Fig. 6B). Then, we used linear regression analysis to examine whether the risk index could predict the infiltration level of immune cells. We discovered that the infiltration level of M2 macrophage and Treg cell were significantly positively associated with the value of risk index (Fig. 6C - G), while CD8+ T cell was significantly negatively correlated with risk index (Fig. 6H and I). Tumor-associated macrophages are generally divided into two subtypes, namely classical activated M1 macrophage and alternatively activated M2 macrophage [29]. M1 macrophages contribute to anti-tumor effect and lead to tumor suppression, while M2 macrophages promote proliferation and progression of tumor cells [30, 31]. For usually leading to immune evasion, tumor angiogenesis, progression and metastasis, Treg is an essential kind of immunosuppressive cell [32]. It’s known that CD8+ T cell and plasma B cell are key cells for anti-tumor immunity. Therefore, it is not difficult to understand that patients from low-risk group acquired more survival advantages than that from high-risk index group.

To find out the differential enrichments of cell death mode, hallmark pathways and cholesterol metabolism pathways between low- and high-risk index groups, we performed GSVA and GSEA using cholesterol metabolism related pathways, HALLMARK gene sets, cell senescence related gene sets, Gene Ontology (GO) signaling pathway, programmed cell death related gene sets and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) signaling pathway. Although PCa is considered as “cold tumor” which has a poor response rate to immunotherapy, for patients with bone-predominant metastasis, pembrolizumab showed promising therapeutic efficacy irrespective of PD-L1 expression level (KEYNOTE-199) [34]. Meanwhile, pembrolizumab has been approved for all cancers with high TMB by the FDA. Therefore, we wondered if the risk index was capable of predicting the response to immunotherapy to aid in precision medicine. The patients were divided into four subgroups according to the usage of anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 treatments, namely, CTLA-4 negative PD-1 negative (Fig. 8B), CTLA-4 positive PD-1 negative (Fig. 8C), CTLA-4 negative PD-1 positive (Fig. 8D), CTLA-4 positive PD-1 positive (Fig. 8E). We found low-risk index group had significant higher IPS score than high-risk index group in three subgroups, which indicated a better response to anti-CTLA-4 or anti-PD-1 monotherapy in patients from low-risk index group. These results revealed that risk index could help screen patients who are appropriate for docetaxel or immunotherapy in PCa and instruct clinical medication.

The impact of deregulation in cholesterol metabolism in PCa in vitro and the validation of the expression of three vital cholesterol metabolism associated genes

RWPE1 cells were normal human prostate epithelia cells, and LNCaP cells and 22Rv1 cells were both PCa cell lines. LNCaP cells were cloned from the left supraclavicular lymph node of a patient with metastatic prostate cancer and were AR-dependent while 22Rv1 was a human prostate carcinoma epithelial cell line derived from a xenograft that was serially propagated in mice and was AR-independent. Therefore, we chose these three cell lines for further validation since they could represent normal prostate tissues and PCa of different stages. To further validate the expression level of these three genes, we performed qPCR in 22Rv1 cells, LNCaP cells and RWPE1 cells (Fig. 8F-G). Using different reference genes (β-actin and GAPDH), we validated ABCA2 and YJEFN3 were highly expressed in tumor tissues, while ANXA2 had high expression level in normal tissues. Then we collected the images of immunohistochemical staining of ABCA2, ANXA2 and YEFN3 in normal and prostate cancer tissues from the Human Protein Atlas (Fig. 8H-J). Consistent with the results of qPCR, ABCA2 and YJEFN3 showed high expression level in LNCaP, while ANXA2 was highly expressed in RWPE1. The result of western blot was also consistent with the previous conclusions (Fig. 8K).

To further validate the vital roles of cholesterol metabolism in PCa, we used atorvastatin, a commonly used drug in cardiovascular disease, which could inhibit the cholesterol synthesis by suppressing the activity of the rate-limiting enzyme, 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase, to interfere the cholesterol metabolism of different PCa cell lines. We found after atorvastatin treatment, especially atorvastatin of high concentration for 48 h (20 and 40µM), the proliferation of both AR-dependent LNCaP cells and AR-independent 22Rv1 cells were significant inhibited (Fig. 9A-D). Besides, the proportion of apoptotic cells significantly increased after treated by atorvastatin of high concentration (20 and 40µM) (Fig. 9E-F). All these results revealed the key roles of cholesterol metabolism in PCa of different stages in vitro.

Discussion

Cholesterol metabolism dysregulation is one of the vital characteristics of PCa. Although extensive analyses between risk of PCa and blood cholesterol have been conducted, the results are still controversial [12, 13, 35]. It is now considered that the level of cholesterol in serum is not as important as in prostatic tissues in the development, progression and metastasis of tumor. Importantly, compared to normal prostatic tissue, PCa cells have higher cholesterol concentration in cytoplasm, membrane and nucleus [36, 37]. Sterol regulatory element–binding proteins (SREBPs) are transcription factors that play a role in the regulation of lipid synthesis and other biological signaling pathways, in which SREBP2 involves in cholesterol synthesis and is essential for maintaining the intracellular cholesterol balance [38, 39]. It is reported that SREBP2 activity was increased in prostate cancer cell and correlated with worse clinical outcomes [40, 41]. These discoveries highlighted the role of cholesterol metabolism in PCa. However, the mechanisms under the metabolism networks remain unclear and there still lacks comprehensive analysis of cholesterol metabolism in PCa.

Our previous studies elucidated that the usage of statins was correlated with decreased risk of BCR and associated with better survival in patients with PCa. Statins are reported to prevent PCa cells growth through cholesterol mediated and non-cholesterol mediated pathways [42]. Considering the significance of cholesterol metabolism in PCa, we performed comprehensive analysis of cholesterol metabolism to discover the potent mechanisms behind the development of PCa and established a gene signature to aid in precision medicine.

We first evaluated the differential enrichments of cholesterol related pathways between tumor and normal tissues. It is intriguing to find high statin inhibition of cholesterol production pathway enriches in tumor tissue, which is consistent with our previous study that statins play a unique role in promoting the prognosis of patients and proposes statins as a potential therapeutic agent for PCa. Moreover, we found regulation of cholesterol esterification and blood HDL-cholesterol were highly enriched in tumor tissue. Previous study reported an abnormal accumulation of cholesteryl ester in advanced or metastatic PCa, and the depletion of cholesteryl ester impaired the proliferation and invasion capability of tumor in mouse xenograft models [43]. This result reiterates the significance of cholesterol metabolic process in PCa. Then we found differential expressed CMRGs between normal and tumor tissues, and finally screened out 19 vital DEGs by univariate Cox regression. We didn’t find significant somatic mutation of these genes among patients, but interestingly, we found co-mutation exists among these 19 genes which indicated a dysregulation of interconnected metabolic pathways in tumor tissue.

Subsequently, we performed the LASSO regression in the TCGA cohort, and finally obtained 13 genes from the 19 genes with prognostic value. The 13 genes are INHBA, ABCA2, EGF, TREM2, YJEFN3, STARD4, ANXA2, MSI1, SCRIB, APOF, ATF3, TP53INP1 and PDIA2. EGF regulates the cell functions via binding to human epidermal growth factor receptor (Her) family (including EGFR, Her-2 and Her-3), and the role of EGF axis in PCa has been frequently assessed. The expression level of EGFR is low in normal prostatic tissue [44], while it is significant higher in PCa [45]. The research of Mandel et al. suggested a correlation between high EGFR expression level and poor prognosis of PCa [46]. Additionally, more and more evidence suggested the cross-talks between AR signaling pathway and EGFR signaling pathway rendered PCa cells independent of androgen [47]. Our study highlights the importance of EGF in PCa again. The actual effect of YJEFN3 in cancer remains unclear, but interestingly, a study screened out YJEFN3 as a potential target for mRNA vaccine for PCa [48]. ANXA2 is a calcium dependent phospholipid binding protein which is expressed on the surface of virous types of cancer cells, endothelial cells and mononuclear cells and [49]. ANXA2 is reported to promote cancer cell proliferation, progression or metastasis in various malignancies including nasopharyngeal carcinoma, gastric cancer and esophageal cancer [50–52]. However, ANXA2 plays a more complicated role in PCa. Studies reported that prostate cancer was associated with down-regulated expression level of ANXA2 [53], and down-regulated ANXA2 corelated with recurrence, metastasis and poor prognosis [54]. Nevertheless, studies from Lnokuchi et al. and Tan et al. revealed that ANXA2 was associated with malignant phenotype, higher grade and worse pathological stage [55, 56]. In fact, these studies either focused on single PCa cell line or small-sample cohort, and more in vivo and clinical research are needed. Anyway, our result provided a new insight into the role of ANXA2 in PCa. The rest genes (including TREM2, STARD4, MSI1, APOF and PDIA2) either didn’t be reported to be correlated with the pathology of PCa or were only used for bioinformatic analysis. We believe the 13 genes are important targets for the management of PCa in the future.

To further quantify the cholesterol metabolism model in individuals, a scoring system named cholesterol risk index was established in the basis of the expression levels of the 13 CMRGs. Using the risk index, we stratified patients into two different groups, namely, low- and high-risk index groups. We found patients from low-risk group were significantly associated with better prognosis than those from high-risk group no matter in the TCGA or verification cohorts, and the meta-analysis including these three cohorts confirmed our result. Additionally, the risk groups showed stronger capability in predicting the prognosis than other clinicopathological information (T stage, N stage and age). Interestingly, we found low-risk index group was correlated with high expression levels of EGF, STARD4, ANXA2, APOF and ATF3. As we discussed above, the specific role of these genes in PCa still remain unclear, therefore, the expression characteristics of these genes might provide new insight for PCa management. A nomogram involving risk groups, age and T stage was established to predict the BCRFS and showed excellent prediction accuracy.

Following this, we verified the prognostic value of TMB in PCa. Consistent with the research from Luo et al. [26], we found patients from low TMB group was associated with better BCRFS. The mutation spectrums of two risk index groups revealed a distribution difference in TP53 and FOXA1. The tumor-suppressor gene TP53 is an essential gene in normal cells growth and tumor prevention [57], and the mutation of TP53 frequently indicates a status of more rapid progression, resistant to anti-tumor treatment and poor prognosis in cancers [58]. Notably, mutated TP53 could promote SREBP2 activity by enhancing SREBP2 stability and upregulating the mevalonate pathway [37]. Our work revealed a higher mutation rate of TP53 in samples from high-risk group, which was in accordance with the above results that high-risk group was related to poor prognosis than low-risk group. As a pioneer factor, FOXA1 is a driver of development and progression of PCa [59]. It was reported that FOXA1 could reprogram the binding sites of androgen receptor (AR) and drive the oncogenic process along with HOXB13 [60]. In addition, FOXA1 was reported to be the third most frequently mutated genes in PCa [61], and especially, up to 41% mutation rate of FOXA1 in localized PCa was found in the Chinese Prostate Cancer Genome and Epigenome Atlas (CPGEA) cohort which was significantly higher than western cohort [62]. The coding mutation of FOXA1 was thought to be capable of promoting epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and progression of PCa [59], which was in accordance with our result that high-risk group showed higher mutation rate. We also noticed a positive association between TMB and the value of risk index.

Tumors are capable of recruiting the ostensibly normal cells to create TME (including cancer cells, immune cells and stroma cells) which contributes to acquiring the hallmarks of cancer [63, 64]. The heterogeneity of TME contributes to different endpoints of tumors, and TME infiltrating CD8+ T cells play a crucial role in anti-tumor immune responses. A recent study revealed that high enrichment of cholesterol in tumor tissues and cholesterol content in CD8+ T cell was positively correlated with upregulated expression levels of LAG-3, TIM-3, 2B4 and PD-1 in T cell, and therefore, led to the exhaustion of CD8+ T cell [65]. Therefore, we wondered whether the risk index could predict the characteristics of TME immune cells infiltration. We proposed that a high-risk index indicated a phenotype which was infiltrated with higher immune suppression cells (Treg and M2 macrophage) and lower CD8+ T cell. In addition, we found patients from high-risk index group were correlated with higher expression level of immune checkpoint genes, and the higher of risk index, the higher expression of most genes. These results are in accordance with the TCIA that patients from low-risk index group are related to better response to anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy. Generally, patients from high-risk index group were associated with higher TMB, higher infiltration levels of immune suppression cells, higher expression levels of immune checkpoint genes and poor response to immunotherapy.

HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in mevalonate pathway, can be competitively inhibited by statins. Mevalonate-5-phosphate, an intermediate in the mevalonate pathway, could stabilize conformational or misfolded mutant p53 [37]. Statins could reduce the generation of mutant p53 by inhibiting the mevalonate pathway, and at the same time, induce mutant p53 degeneration by interacting with DNAJA1 [66]. Our previous studies proposed a positive impact of statin use on the prognosis of PCa [7]. Since our work revealed that the statin inhibition of cholesterol production pathway showed high enrichment in high-risk index group (Fig. 7E) and patients from high-risk index group had higher mutation rate of TP53 than low-risk index group, we proposed that statin use played a unique part in promoting prognosis of PCa, especially for those in high cholesterol risk index group. Besides, using atorvastatin, we also demonstrated downregulation of cholesterol could significantly inhibit cell proliferation and promote apoptosis both in AR-dependent and AR-independent PCa cell lines. Natalia et al. have reported de novo fatty acid synthesis is essential for cell cycle completion and come up with a new conception “lipogenic checkpoint” [67]. Lipogenic enzymes were possible targets for cancer treatment and the development of inhibitors for this pathway together with those for cell cycle-related kinases raises the possibility of combined therapy. Similar to fatty acid, as an important component of cell membrane, cholesterol was also important for cell division. Therefore, we hypothesized that there also existed a “cholesterol checkpoint” in the cell cycle, thus, this work may aid in precision medicine to help screen patients with PCa who have better fit for treatment targeted cholesterol metabolism.

However, a few limitations exist in this study. First, the CMRGs are acquired from the MSigDB; since many novel CMRGs have been identified recently, the genes included in our study may be not comprehensive enough and may lead to bias. Second, transcriptomic data used in this study only include the level of mRNA and lack the expression level of proteins. Considering the important role of post-translational modification in cancer, this may lead to some inaccuracies. Third, the clinical samples included in this study is limited and further clinical verification is needed. Finally, it’s not clear how exactly the cholesterol metabolism affects the PCa, and more researches are needed to uncover the detailed role of cholesterol metabolism in PCa.

In conclusion, this work elucidated the comprehensive role of cholesterol metabolism in PCa, and correlated the cholesterol risk index with TMB, TME infiltration, prognosis and response to chemotherapy or immunotherapy. We also established a nomogram model for prognosis prediction of patients with PCa. Importantly, we highlighted the role of statins in anti-prostate cancer effect, and help screen out patients with PCa who have better fit for statins therapy. Better understanding and evaluating the cholesterol metabolism could aid in precision medicine and promoting prognosis of PCa.
