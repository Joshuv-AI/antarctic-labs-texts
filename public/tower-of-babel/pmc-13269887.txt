Elucidating metal (Zr, Hf, Th, U)-hydride covalency using 1H NMR chemical shifts and density functional calculations
Authors: M Gayanethra Kaumini, Xiaojuan Yu, Steven P Kelley, Jochen Autschbach, Justin R Walensky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal-ligand covalency is not fully understood for the actinides and studies comparing group IV transition metals with isostructural Th and U complexes are limited. Herein, we report the synthesis and characterization of [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)M(H)], M = Hf, Th, and U, which can be compared to the previously reported Zr analog, to explore the differences between transition metal and actinide bonding situations. The 1H NMR chemical shifts of each complex, including the paramagnetic U(IV) compound, showed reasonable agreement with the computed shifts and benchmark the covalency of the metal-hydride bonds. Subject terms: Chemical bonding, Chemical bonding

Introduction

A major challenge confronting the f-element community, particularly regarding the 5f elements, is understanding the role of the valence f orbitals in structure, bonding, and reactivity. In recent years, research groups have used Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, mainly of ligand nuclei, primarily in tandem with Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations with relativistic Hamiltonians, to probe covalency of actinide-ligand bonds1,2. As a complement to X-ray absorption spectroscopy techniques3,4, ligand NMR is a very efficient way of examining actinide-ligand bonding. For instance, NMR data for the nuclides 13C5–12, 15N9,13,14, 29Si15, 31P16, 77Se17–19,and 125Te17 have been previously reported. Far less explored is 1H because the paucity of actinide hydride complexes, particularly ones that have transition metal analogs.

Our group, along with others, has been interested in examining metal-ligand bonding between transition metals and actinides, in particular Group IV metals20–24. The strategy employed is investigating isostructural complexes with tetravalent Group IV metals vs. Th, and U. While differences in orbital overlap between metal and ligand orbitals in d versus f element compounds exist, the reality is far more complicated since 5f metals can exhibit covalent bonding.

Recently, our group has reported terminal hydride complexes of uranium, specifically, [(C5Me5)2(ArO)U(H)], Ar = 2,4,6-Me3C6H2 (Mes)25 and 2,6-tBu2-4-MeC6H2 (BHT)26. As we were continuing our variation of the aryloxide ligand, we moved to the 2,6-iPr2C6H3 (Dipp) analog and noticed that the Zr analog, [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)Zr(H)], Zr-H, was reported by Stephan and co-workers27. While the Zr analog of the mesityl substituent is also known, our rationale was that the Dipp substituted aryloxide would be more promising because bis(Dipp) complexes are not known, so ligand distribution would not be a potential issue. We have also examined this [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)M(X)] framework with Ce(IV)28.

A feature when comparing metal hydrides is the downfield shift in the 1H NMR spectrum that is seen moving from Zr, Hf, and Th. For example, terminal Zr-H compounds have 1H NMR resonances on the order of 5 ppm, while those of Hf and Th hydrides are much larger, around 10 ppm and 20 ppm, respectively. It has been shown that the relativistic electron spin-orbit (SO) interaction plays an important in the NMR shift values and is at least in part responsible for the hydride downfield trend29–31. However, to our knowledge, there are no experimental studies that correlate this behavior between d0 and d0f0 metals. For example, [(C5Me5)2M(H)2], M = Ti, Zr, Hf, are known, but the analogous thorium complex is dimeric in the solid-state and the solution speciation has not been determined. Herein, we investigate four hydride compounds, [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)M(H)], M = Zr, Hf, Th, and U, and examine how the electronic structure of these metals changes the NMR shift and metal-hydride covalency.

Results and discussion

Syntheses and characterization

Since we have had success making the terminal uranium hydrides with the BHT, O-2,6-tBu2-4-MeC6H2, and Mes, O-2,4,6-Me3C6H2, substituents, we targeted the diisopropyl analog since its steric properties are between those of BHT and Mes. Using a similar approach, the U(III) compound, [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)U], was reacted with half an equivalent of Zn(CH2CH3)2 yielding the U(IV) ethyl complex, [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)U(CH2CH3)], U-Et, Scheme 1. As in the previous examples, this reaction is quantitative (94% crystalline yield). The formation of the ethyl was evident from the 1H NMR spectrum with the CH2 group observed at −181.9 ppm and the CH3 at −6.72 ppm which are similar to the −182.8 ppm and −9.81 ppm for the methylene and methyl, respectively, in [(C5Me5)2(2,4,6-Me3C6H2O)U(CH2CH3)]25.

Synthesis of U-Et and U-H.

Hydrogenolysis of U-Et at room temperature with 1 atm of H2 formed the hydride complex, [(C5Me5)2(2,4,6-Me3C6H2O)U(H)], U-H, Scheme 1. in 68% crystalline yield. The terminal hydride has a characteristic chemical shift at +283.8 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum, which can be compared to the BHT and Mes derivatives, which show hydride shifts at 352.2 ppm26 and 272.7 ppm25, respectively. The hydride resonance in 2 fits nicely within the range of the BHT and Mes, which flank its donating properties. Like other metallocene complexes, scrambling occurs in which D/H exchange is observed with the hydrogens in the Cp* ligand when U-Et is treated with D232–34, but a resonance at 276.9 ppm is observed in the 2H NMR spectrum which we assign to the U-D. The FTIR vibrational spectrum of U-H shows an absorption at 1431 cm−1 which is also in between 1413 cm−1 and 1472 cm−1 observed for the U-H stretch in the BHT and Mes complexes, respectively.

To synthesize the thorium hydride, an alternative approach was needed since Th(III) is not as readily accessible as U(III). Therefore, [(C5Me5)2Th(CH3)(I)] was treated with KO-2,6-iPr2C6H3 to yield [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)Th(CH3)], Th-Me, Scheme 2. The resonance for the methyl group in the 1H and 13C NMR spectra is 0.41 ppm and 59.7 ppm, respectively, consistent with a mono-methyl thorium(IV) complex35,36. Hydrogenation of 3 at 110 °C for 48 h yields the desired product, [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)Th(H)], Th-H, Scheme 2. The hydride resonance was located at 18.6 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum, which is in the expected region compared to other terminal thorium hydrides. For example, [(C5H3tBu2)2Th(H){(P-2,4,6-iPr3C6H2)SiH2Ph)]37, [(C5H2tBu3)2Th(H)(N-(p-tolyl)SiHPh2)]38, [(C5H3tBu2)3Th(H)]39, and [{(SiMe3)2N}3Th(H)]40 have Th-H resonances at 17.40, 17.84, 12.95, and 23.56 ppm, respectively. Further evidence of the Th-H stretching frequency in the FTIR vibrational spectrum at 1433 cm−1.

Synthesis of Th-CH3 and Th-H.

While several published preparations of [(C5Me5)2Hf(H)2] are reported41,42, we found that formation of the bis(benzyl) complex, [(C5Me5)2Hf(CH2Ph)2], Hf-CH2Ph, through salt metathesis of [(C5Me5)2Hf(Cl)2] with two equivalents of KCH2Ph, worked best in our hands43. The dihydride was attempted to be synthesized from reaction of [(C5Me5)2Hf(Cl)2] with two equivalents with tBuLi in toluene. This procedure was followed in analogy to the preparation of zirconocene dihydrides44, but, with Hf, the metalation of toluene was faster than the salt metathesis of the chlorides. Both are high yielding reactions (>90%) to form Hf-CH2Ph, Scheme 3. The CH2 of the benzyl group is assigned at 1.41 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum while the Cp* resonance is at 1.73 ppm. This can be compared to the 1.40 ppm and 1.87 ppm observed for the CH2 and Cp*, respectively, in [(C5Me5)2Th(CH2Ph)2]32.

Synthesis of hafnium complexes done in this work.

After hydrogenation of Hf-CH2Ph and isolation of [C5Me5)2Hf(H)2], Hf-H2, in high yield (88%), protonolysis with HO-2,6-iPr2C6H3, formed [(C5Me5)2(2,6-iPr2C6H3O)Hf(H)], Hf-H, in good yield (75%), Scheme 3. The hydride resonance in Hf-H is located at 10.51 ppm which can be compared to other terminal hydrides of hafnium compounds such as [(C5Me5)2Hf(H)(NHCH3)] which shows a resonance at 9.1 ppm45, 9.93 ppm in [(1,2,4-Me3C5H2)2Hf]2(H)2(μ-{NH-Si(H)(Cy)-NH}]46, or 11.39 ppm in [(C5Me5)Hf(H)(NMe2BH3)2]47. Again, scrambling occurs with Hf as well, but a small resonance at 9.26 ppm is observed in the 2H NMR spectrum, which we attributed to Hf-D. A stretching frequency of 1431 cm−1, identical to U-H and Th-H is also observed for the Hf-H.

Solid-state characterization

The solid-state structure of each complex was established using single crystal X-ray crystallographic analysis. Complex U-Et is a rare example of an f element ethyl compound. The U-C(CH2) distance is 2.441(15) Å, nearly identical to the 2.42(1) Å in [(C5Me5)2(2,4,6-Me3C6H2O)U(CH2CH3)]25. The Th-Me complex crystallizes with two molecules in the unit cell. Each displays the typical pseudo-tetrahedral geometry about the thorium(IV) metal center with Th-O1 distances of 2.20(2) and 2.17(2) Å with Th-C(Me) distances of 2.48(4) Å and 2.47(4) Å. While the quality of the data for the Th-Me complex was weak, the unit cell parameters match the analogous uranium compound48.

The Hf-C(benzyl) distances in Hf-CH2Ph, Fig. 1, of 2.303(2) and 2.312(2) Å are identical to the 2.301(8) Å in [(C5Me5)(C5Me4CH2)Hf(CH2Ph)43.The Hf-C27-C(ipso) and Hf-C34-C(ipso) bond angles are 133.3(2)° and 130.2(2)°, respectively, with a C27-Hf-C34 angle of 90.03(8)°.

The three hydrides, Hf-H, Fig. 1, Th-H, and U-H were also structurally characterized. However, the crystals are incredibly air-, moisture, and thermally sensitive and quickly decomposed, even in Paratone oil. In Hf-H, the molecule is disordered in such a way that it approximated D3d symmetry. The Hf atom is on either side of the plane formed by the centroids of the two Cp* rings and the coordinating O atom, with the Hf-H bond pointing normal to this plane. The disorder is close to 50/50 (55.5 to 45.5+/−0.8%). Difference map peaks in realistic positions for coordinated hydride atoms for both disordered Hf atoms could be located and were refined with the Hf-H distances restrained to be equal. The Hf-H distances are not reliable, but the pyramidalization of the Hf atom in either direction relative to the Cp*-Cp*-O plane is a highly reliable observation which is best explained by the presence of a fourth ligand which, given the absence of any other major peaks, could only be hydride.

In both Th-H and U-H, they contain an 80% (Th-H) and 90% (U-H) occupancy of the hydride mixed with 20% (Me, Th-CH3) or 10% (Et, U-Et). The crystals crystallize in the same unit cell so combined with the spectroscopic characterization, we are confident in our assignments as terminal hydrides.

Computational analysis

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed for the metal (Zr, Hf, Th, U)-hydride complexes, Zr-H, Hf-H, Th-H, and U-H, using the B3LYP hybrid functional49–51, a relativistic Hamiltonian (ZORA)52, and matching all-electron Slater-type basis sets, as implemented in the ADF program included in the 2023 version of the Amsterdam Modeling Suite53. Complete computational details are provided in the Supporting Information (SI). B3LYP is widely employed for molecular structure optimizations and bonding analyses; accordingly, it was used for these purposes in the present study54,55. Selected optimized bond distances of complexes Hf-H and Th-H were compared with the experimental crystal structure data. As seen in Table S3, the optimized M − O bond lengths align closely with the experimental data, with deviations of only 0.01 Å for both the U − O and Th−O distances, thereby confirming the reliability of the chosen computational approach. It is worth noting that the U-hydride complex calculations were conducted for a spin-triplet ground state owing to the 5f2 configuration. The other complexes have closed-shell singlet ground states. According to the theoretical calculations, the bands around 1433 and 1431 cm−1 in the IR spectra of the Th-H and U-H complex, respectively, were assigned to the corresponding metal-hydride stretch. These results provide further support for the formation of the corresponding hydride complexes, even in the absence of X-ray crystallographic evidence.

The covalent M − H bonding interactions in the metal (Zr, Hf, Th, U)-hydride complexes were assessed via natural localized molecular orbital (NLMO) and Wiberg bond order (WBO) analyses as generated by the NBO program56. The NLMOs representing the covalent metal-hydride σ bonds are depicted in Fig. 2, and the atomic hybrid contributions, along with the WBOs representing the important bonding interactions, are summarized in Tables S4 and S5. For the Zr-hydride complex, the bonding NLMO reveals a σ(Zr–H) bond with 30% total Zr density weight, composed of 16% 5s and 83% 4d contributions at the metal. The density weight at the hydride ligand is 67%; the remainder of the orbital’s density is associated with other atoms, likely as a result from the NLMO orthogonality. For the Hf complex, the bonding NLMO displays similar but slightly weaker covalency and a stronger s-d hybridization at the metal. For the two actinide complexes, in keeping with typical trends for Th vs. U, the U-hydride has more equal metal-ligand density sharing than the Th complex and a larger contribution from 5f at the metal. The aforementioned trends of the degree of metal-ligand density sharing in the bond NLMO are reflected in the WBOs: Zr-H = 0.75, Hf-H = 0.72, Th-H = 0.63, U-H = 0.75. Overall, the bonding characteristics in these complexes are indicative of dative covalent interactions, where the bonding orbitals are polarized toward the hydride ligand.

The 1H chemical shifts for the (Zr, Hf, Th, U)-hydride complexes were calculated with and without SO effects, as described in the Supporting Information (SI). For these complexes, the 1H chemical shifts show relatively weakly dependent on the choice of functional. Herein, we focus on the results obtained with the hybrid functional designated here as PBE0(40) [a PBE-based hybrid with 40% exact exchange]57. This functional has previously been shown to accurately reproduce NMR chemical shifts in organometallic complexes12,57,58. The calculated shielding constants and corresponding chemical shifts are collected in Table S6.

The calculated 1H chemical shift for the Zr-hydride complex is 5.9 ppm, in reasonable agreement with the experimental value of 4.4 ppm. This includes a 2.3 ppm downfield shift from SO effects. For comparison, the hydride 1H NMR shift of the Hf-hydride complex was calculated to be 9.2 ppm at the SO-PBE0(40) level, which is also not far from the experimental value of 10.5 ppm. This shift includes a more pronounced SO-induced deshielding of 6.2 ppm. The increased magnitude of SO deshielding in the Hf-hydride complex as compared to the Zr analogue is consistent with the similar bond covalency, as indicated by NLMO and WBO analyses, combined with the higher nuclear charge of Hf which causes a larger relativistic SO contribution to the hydride shift. Notably, SO contributions to the 1H NMR shifts in these transition metal hydrides are similar in scale to those reported for the [(C5Me5)2MH2] series (M=Zr, Hf)29.

The calculated 1H shift for the Th-hydride complex is 16.8 ppm (expt.: 18.5 ppm), which includes a more substantial 10.3 ppm downfield shift from SO coupling. The SO effect remains relatively small, however, because the bond covalency (transmitting the SO effect from the metal to the proton) for the Th complex is the lowest in the set, and the 5f contributions to the bond are not large. For this closed-shell Th-H complex, inclusion of solvent model leads to negligible changes in the calculated NMR shift of the hydride ligand (see Table S8 in the SI). Most notably, the 1H shift for the U-hydride complex was calculated to be 240 ppm, which is in acceptable agreement with the experimental value of 283 ppm given (i) the substantial difficulties associated with calculating NMR shifts for paramagnetic complexes in general59 and (ii) the likelihood that the zero-point vibrations and thermal motion of the hydride ligand (which was not modeled) affects the resulting NMR data. The H vs. D chemical shift differences for a given metal can be used as evidence that zero-point and thermal motion of the hydride ligand is not negligible, but the differences do not seem so large that the zero-point and thermal motion would be the only reason for the deviations between the calculated and experimental NMR shift for U-H. Note that, in the case of the open-shell U–H complex (triplet), a modest increase in the deviation from experiment is observed, with the discrepancy increasing from ~15% for gas-phase calculations to approximately 21% when solvent effects are described via a continuum model (see Table S9 in the SI). Nevertheless, the overall agreement remains qualitatively reasonable. Due to the presence of the two unpaired electrons, the NMR shifts for the U(IV) complex were computed using the ‘pNMRShift’ program60,61, based on DFT data for the electron g-factors of the complex and the proton hyperfine coupling that were generated with the ADF code. The calculated shift includes a large (102 ppm) deshielding from the combined effects of the electron paramagnetism and the SO interaction. This SO effect must be attributed to the substantial bond contributions from the uranium 5f (and 6d) orbitals. Furthermore, as presented in Table S5, the shielding contributions from the electron paramagnetism are dominated by the pseudo-contact (PC) mechanism, not by the Fermi contact interaction (or rather, its relativistic analogue). This further underscores the critical importance of including relativistic effects—particularly, SO coupling—when interpreting the NMR data of actinide hydride complexes. The large calculated hydride NMR shift for the U complex is seen to be consistent with the paramagnetism of the complex as well as with the substantial U—H bond covalency assigned based on the NLMO analysis.

In summary, a series of metallocene aryloxide complexes of Zr, Hf, Th, and U with terminal hydrides has been investigated through a combined experimental and computational study to correlate metal-hydride covalent character. Good correlation was observed between the experimental and computed NMR resonances, even for the paramagnetic U(IV) complex. While the metal-hydride bonds in all four compounds were found to be polarized toward the ligand, the covalent character as measured by the varying density weight of the σ(metal–H) bond NLMO at the metal, along with the WBOs, was found to be Th < Hf < U ≈ Zr. When evaluating the hydride NMR shifts in terms of bond covalency, it is important to keep in mind that the metals with higher atomic number (and therefore higher nuclear charge) have a stronger SO interaction compared to the lighter elements. The very large hydride NMR shift for the U complex is seen to be a combination of strong SO coupling, strong bond covalency, and the additional paramagnetic effects on the ligand shift, such that it would seem difficult to interpret the experimental value without the help of quantum chemical calculations.

Methods

General considerations

All syntheses were carried out under an N2 atmosphere using glovebox and Schlenk techniques unless otherwise stated. All non-deuterated solvents used were dried by passing through a solvent purification system (MBraun, USA), and stored over sieves, and potassium for aromatic and aliphatic solvents, or calcium hydride for ethereal solvents. [(C5Me5)2U(O-2,6-iPr2-C6H3)(THF)]62, [(C5Me5)2Th(I)CH3]63, and [(C5Me5)2HfCl2]41,64 were prepared according to literature methods. The potassium salt, KO-2,6-iPr2C6H3, was synthesized from treatment of the phenol with KN(SiMe3)2 and passage through a plug of potassium graphite immediately before use. Benzene-d6 (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) was degassed by three freeze−pump−thaw cycles and stored over molecular sieves. Hexamethyldisiloxane was distilled from potassium-ketyl. All 1H spectra were taken on 600 MHz, 500 MHz, or 300 MHz Bruker spectrometers. All NMR chemical shifts are reported in ppm. 1H NMR chemical shifts were referenced internally to the residual solvent peaks. IR was taken on a Nicolet Summit Pro FTIR spectrometer, using a KBr pellet. Elemental analysis was performed at the University of Missouri, Columbia, on a Carlo Erba 1108 elemental analyzer, outfitted with an A/D converter for analysis using Eager Xperience software.

Synthesis of [(C5Me5)2Hf(CH2Ph)2], Hf-CH2Ph (Method A)

On a Schlenk line, a 100 mL bomb flask was charged with [(C5Me5)2HfCl2] (200 mg, 0.443 mmol) and cooled to −78 °C. Synthesis of [(C5Me5)2Hf(H)2], 2

[(C5Me5)2Hf(CH2Ph)2], Hf-H2, (100 mg, 0.151 mmol) was dissolved in benzene in a J. Young tube to this solution dihydrogen was added (760 torr). The solution was heated to 70 °C and stirred 48 h to yield a clear solution of [(C5Me5)2Hf(H)2]. Volatiles were removed under reduced pressure and the solid was redissolved and filtered in a minimal amount of pentane (~3 mL).
