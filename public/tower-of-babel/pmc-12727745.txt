Tetrahydrocurcumin for Major Depressive Disorder with Therapeutic Potential and Mechanistic Insights from Clinical and Preclinical Studies
Authors: Ying Guo, Jianping Xie, Haiyun Luo, Yun Yuan, Fang Long, Jingyuan Meng, Hui Tan, Yuanping Li, Bibo Cai, Meiling Chen, Liyan Yang, Jingmei Zhong, Yanqing Zhu, Heng Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Major depressive disorder (MDD) remains a leading cause of disability worldwide, and while selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are standard treatments, they have limited efficacy and adverse effects. Tetrahydrocurcumin (THC), a bioactive metabolite of curcumin, shows anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties that may augment antidepressant therapy. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and mechanisms of THC in treating MDD. A randomized, open-label, parallel-group pilot trial enrolled 19 patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) who received either escitalopram (ESC, 10 mg/day) or ESC plus tetrahydrocurcumin (ESC + THC, 10 mg ESC + 200 mg THC per day) for 29 days. Participants were randomized 1:1, with blinded raters assessing depressive severity using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) at baseline and Day 29. Sixteen participants completed the primary endpoint assessment. Serum underwent data-independent acquisition (DIA-PASEF) proteomics, molecular docking, and ELISA. In parallel, chronic restraint stress (CRS) mice received THC (80 mg/kg) and were evaluated by behavior, immunofluorescence, and serum biomarkers. THC augmentation improved gastrointestinal symptoms in patients (p = 0.025), though total HAMD scores showed no significant group differences. Proteomic analysis identified 32 differentially expressed serum proteins, with THC modulating neurodegenerative pathways. In CRS mice, THC administration reversed anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors (open field, tail suspension, and forced swim tests), normalized prefrontal cortex (HSP90, KRT6A, P4HB, C1QA, APOM, CDH13) and hippocampal (HSP90, P4HB, CDH13) protein expression in mice, and restored serum levels of P4HB, C1QA, and CDH13 in both humans and mice, while additionally modulating LTF, TNF-α, IL-1β, cAMP, and DA in mice serum. These findings demonstrate that THC exerts multimodal antidepressant effects through coordinated anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective mechanisms. THC demonstrates antidepressant potential through anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective mechanisms, supporting its use as a safe augmentation strategy in MDD treatment. Further trials are warranted to validate its clinical efficacy and molecular targets.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-025-05627-5.

Introduction

Depressive disorders affect approximately 3.4% of the global population, with Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) rising 16.4% from 2010–2019 [1]. In 2019, depression ranked among the top causes of Years Lived with Disability (YLD) worldwide [2]. Major depressive disorder (MDD) presents with psychological symptoms such as anhedonia, fatigue, and sleep disturbances[3] as well as physiological manifestations including agitation and gastrointestinal dysfunction [4] [5]. While selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are first-line treatments, they often cause adverse effects (e.g., sexual dysfunction, weight gain, emotional blunting) [6]. Escitalopram (ESC), though better tolerated than fluoxetine, may still induce hyponatremia [7], serotonin syndrome [8], or neuroendocrine disruptions [9]. To address these limitations, augmentation strategies, combining SSRIs with adjunctive therapies, are increasingly used to enhance efficacy while reducing SSRI dosage and side effects.

Tetrahydrocurcumin (THC), the primary metabolite of turmeric-derived curcumin, overcomes curcumin's low bioavailability limitations while exhibiting enhanced absorption and stability [10, 11]. This natural compound demonstrates diverse pharmacological properties, including antioxidant [12], anti-inflammatory [13], and neuroprotective effects [14]. The compound's therapeutic potential for depression is particularly noteworthy given the established link between immune dysregulation and depression pathophysiology. Elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)−1, and IL-6 in patients with depression may disrupt emotional regulation by crossing the blood–brain barrier [15], while depressive behaviors can further exacerbate inflammation [16]. Our previous research demonstrated THC's ability to downregulate inflammatory factors in the chronic restraint stress (CRS) depression model [17]. Importantly, THC shows an excellent safety profile: acute toxicity studies revealed no adverse effects at doses up to 10,000 mg/kg [13], while sub-chronic administration (400 mg/kg) caused no significant toxicological findings [18]. These properties position THC as a promising candidate for depression intervention, though clinical translation requires further investigation. Bridging the translational gap in THC treatment for MDD requires a bidirectional research approach. While preclinical studies reveal THC's molecular mechanisms, MDD's complex etiology-involving genetic, environmental, and psychosocial factors-demands clinical validation. A unidirectional paradigm proves inadequate; instead, integrating clinical observations (to refine animal models) with preclinical investigations (to elucidate pathways) creates synergistic validation. This reciprocal strategy enhances the translational relevance of THC's antidepressant potential.

We hypothesized that THC's anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties could safely enhance SSRI efficacy in depression treatment. To validate this, we designed a pioneering study with three key innovations: the first clinical trial of THC as an augmentation therapy in patients with depression; a bidirectional clinical-preclinical approach combining human data with CRS mouse model studies; and proteomic profiling to elucidate THC's molecular targets. This integrated strategy aims to establish THC's therapeutic potential while identifying novel targets for antidepressant development.

Methods and Materials

Study Design and Participants

This randomized, open-label, parallel-group pilot trial was conducted at the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province between May and June 2023. A total of 19 patients with moderate-to-severe major depressive disorder (HAMD-17 ≥ 17) were enrolled following psychiatric confirmation by two board-certified psychiatrists. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either escitalopram (ESC, n = 9; 10 mg/day, Sichuan Kelun Pharmaceutical, China) or ESC + tetrahydrocurcumin (ESC + THC, n = 10; 10 mg ESC + 200 mg THC, Swanson, USA) for 29 days. HAMD testing was assessed at days 0 and 29. Three dropouts occurred: two (experimental) due to travel and one (control) refused final blood sampling, resulting in 16 participants who completed the full study protocol and were included in the final analysis. Detailed inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria in Supplementary File S1.

Proteomics Research

Protein Extraction and Digestion

Whole blood of patients collected at days 29 was centrifuged (1,500 g, 10 min) for serum isolation. Proteins were extracted using ice-cold lysis buffer (30 min), followed by centrifugation (12,000 g, 30 min, 4 °C). Protein concentration was determined via BCA assay before SDS-PAGE separation. Aliquots (100 μg protein) were reduced (10 mM TCEP, 37 °C, 60 min), alkylated (40 mM IAA, RT, 40 min, dark), and precipitated (10,000 g, 20 min). Pellets were resuspended in 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer, digested with trypsin (37 °C, overnight), then lyophilized. Peptides were desalted (HLB columns), vacuum-dried, and quantified (Thermo Fisher peptide assay).

DIA Mass Spectrometry Detection

Peptides were reconstituted in MS loading buffer with iRT standards and analyzed using a timsTOF Pro2 system (Bruker, Germany) coupled to an EASY-nLC (Thermo, USA). Separation was performed on a C18 column (75 μm × 25 cm; Ionopticks, USA) with a 60-min gradient: 3–28% B (0–45 min), 28–44% B (45–50 min), 44–90% B (50–55 min), and 90% B (55–60 min) (A: 2% ACN/0.1% FA; B: 80% ACN/0.1% FA; flow rate: 250 nL/min). DIA-PASEF acquisition used positive ion mode (1.5 kV) with m/z 100–1700 scan range and 0.6–1.6 Vs·cm⁻2 ion mobility. Each TIMS cycle comprised 1 MS and 10 PASEF MS/MS scans across 64 DIA windows (25 isolations).

Protein Identification and Bioinformatics Analysis

Raw DIA data were processed in Spectronaut (v14) using the UniProt human database (https://www.uniprot.org/taxonomy/9606) with iRT calibration. Quantification criteria: ≥ 6 peptides/protein, ≥ 3 fragment ions/peptide (excluding shared/modified peptides). Differential expression (p < 0.05, fold-change > 1.2) was determined via t-test in R. Functional annotation utilized GO (http://geneontology.org/) and KEGG pathway (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) analyses, with protein–protein interactions mapped via STRING v11.5. The data were analyzed through the free online platform of majorbio cloud platform (cloud.majorbio.com).

Molecular Docking

The 3D structure of THC (SDF format) was obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), while receptor protein structures (PDB format) were downloaded from RCSB PDB (http://www.rcsb.org). Using AutoDock, we prepared the receptor by removing water molecules and adding hydrogens, converted THC to PDB format using OpenBabel (with added hydrogens), and performed docking with AutoGrid to generate 3D scoring grids covering active sites, followed by binding energy and hydrogen bond calculation with AutoDock. Results were visualized in PyMOL.

Behavioral Experiment in Mice

Thirty male ICR mice (6–8 weeks old, 20–22 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Kunming Medical University, China. The mice were housed in groups of five per cage under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 18–22 °C, with ad libitum access to food and water. After one week of acclimatization, they were randomly divided into three groups: control (CON), CRS, and CRS + THC (80 mg/kg). THC was procured from Yien Chemical Technology, Shanghai, China. All groups except CON underwent 21 days of CRS, which involved daily 4-h confinement in ventilated 50 mL tubes. Intragastric administration was performed in all groups for 21 consecutive days prior to CRS induction [17].

Behavioral tests: The open field test (OFT) was conducted in a 50 × 50 × 28 cm arena, with mouse activity recorded for 6 min. For the tail suspension test (TST), mice were suspended 2 cm from the tail tip, and immobility was quantified over 6 min. In the forced swim test (FST), mice were placed in a water-filled cylinder (30 cm height × 12 cm diameter; 20 cm depth, 25 ± 1 °C), and immobility duration was measured during the 6-min trial. All behavioral data were analyzed using the Smart Video Tracking Software V3.0 (Panlab, Spain).

Immunofluorescence

After deep anesthesia and perfusion, mouse brains were extracted, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned coronally using a microtome. Forebrain sections were blocked with 10% goat serum (1 h), incubated (4 °C, overnight) with primary antibodies, followed by Cy3-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (1 h). Following DAPI counterstaining (Solarbio, China), samples were imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Average optical density of heat shock protein 90 (HSP90), keratin 6 A (KRT6A), protein disulfide-isomerase (P4HB), apolipoprotein M (APOM), cadherin-13 (CDH13), and complement component 1q subcomponent A (C1QA) was quantified in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus (Antibody details in Supplementary Table 2).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay Analysis

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay Analysis (ELISA) kits were used to measure the levels of P4HB, CDH13, C1QA, Lactotransferrin (LTF), TNF-α, IL-1β, cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), dopamine (DA), 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), and mannan-binding lectin serine protease 2 (MASP2) in serum of patients or mice. All ELISA kits were used according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were analyzed using independent samples t-tests (continuous variables) and chi-square tests (categorical variables). HAMD-17 symptom improvement was assessed by change scores (Δ = Post–Pre), with between-group differences evaluated using Mann–Whitney U tests (p < 0.05) due to non-normal distribution. For human and mouse studies (behavioral, immunofluorescence, and serological data), normality testing was first performed. Multiple group comparisons used one-way ANOVA with LSD post hoc tests, while two-group comparisons employed either t-tests (normal distribution) or Mann–Whitney U tests (non-normal), with significance set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted in SPSS 19.0.

Results

Effects of THC on HAMD-17

The study found no significant differences in baseline characteristics (age, sex, HAMD-17 total scores) or post-treatment total scores between ESC and ESC + THC groups (all p > 0.05, Table 1). However, itemized analysis revealed that THC augmentation specifically improved gastrointestinal symptoms (U = 16.00, p = 0.025) compared to ESC monotherapy, while showing marginally significant lesser improvement in psychomotor agitation (p = 0.053). No between-group differences were observed in core depressive symptoms (low mood, guilt, suicidal ideation) or anxiety measures (Table 2). These data suggest THC may selectively ameliorate certain somatic symptoms when combined with SSRIs, without significantly affecting other depression domains. Proteomic analysis of THC on Patients with Depression

In the PCA plot (Fig. 1B), samples clustered by treatment group, demonstrating group-specific proteomic signatures. Pearson correlation heatmap analysis demonstrated high intra-group reproducibility among biological replicates (Fig. 1C). Comparative analysis identified 32 significantly altered proteins (22 upregulated, 10 downregulated) in ESC + THC versus ESC groups (Fig. 1D). These proteins were assigned by GO enrichment to several key functional terms, such as nucleotide binding, nucleoside phosphate binding, and programmed cell death, among others (Fig. 1E). KEGG enrichment highlighted involvement in neurodegeneration pathways (Parkinson's, Alzheimer's), HIF-1 signaling, and glycolysis (Fig. 1F). STRING network analysis (12 nodes, 3 edges) revealed protein–protein interactions among differentially expressed proteins in LTF, HSP90AA1, KRT6A, P4HB, MASP2, APOM, and CDH13 (Fig. 1G). Molecular Docking Analysis between THC and the Core Target Proteins

To further elucidate the molecular interactions between THC and key target proteins identified through proteomic analysis, molecular docking simulations were performed. As illustrated in Fig. 2, THC fits well into the binding pockets of the core targets, forming three hydrogen bonds with APOM, two hydrogen bonds with LTF, P4HB, and C1QA, and one hydrogen bond with HSP90AA1, MASP, KRT6A, and CDH13. THC exhibited varying binding affinities to these proteins, with binding energies of −5.79 kcal/mol (HSP90AA1), −5.24 kcal/mol (LTF), −3.61 kcal/mol (P4HB), and −3.06 kcal/mol (MASP), −5.29 kcal/mol (APOM), −1.26 kcal/mol (KRT6A), −4.80 kcal/mol (CDH13), and −5.70 kcal/mol (C1QA). The binding energies were converted to predicted dissociation constants (Kd) using the relationship ΔG = RT ln(Kd): HSP90AA1: Kd ≈ 40 µM (ΔG = −5.79 kcal/mol); C1QA: Kd ≈ 47 µM (ΔG = −5.70 kcal/mol); APOM: Kd ≈ 195 µM (ΔG = −5.29 kcal/mol); LTF: Kd ≈ 286 µM (ΔG = −5.24 kcal/mol). Anxiolytic- and Antidepressant-like Effects of THC on CRS Mice

To evaluate the anxiolytic- and antidepressant-like effects of THC, mice were subjected to the CRS model (Fig. 3A). In the first and second weeks, CRS group showed weight loss compared to CON group (Fig. 3B). The locomotor activity trajectory in the OFT was displayed (Fig. 3C). No intergroup differences were observed in total distance of OFT (Fig. 3D), indicating that THC did not affect the motor function of mice. CRS-induced mice showed reduced center distance (p < 0.05), center time (p < 0.05), zone transitions (p < 0.01), and rearing number (p < 0.01) in OFT, along with increased immobility time of TST (p < 0.05) and FST (p < 0.05) versus CON group. THC treatment effectively reversed these CRS-induced behavioral deficits, significantly improving center distance (p < 0.05), center time (p < 0.05), zone transitions (p < 0.05), rearing number (p < 0.05), and reducing immobility time of TST (p < 0.01) and FST (p < 0.05) compared to CRS group (Fig. 3E-J). Discussion

In this study, the CRS model was used to induce chronic stress through repeated restraint, a well-validated approach for modeling depression-like behavior and HPA axis dysregulation. This paradigm offers high standardization, reproducibility, and low mortality, and reliably produces anxiety-/depressive-like phenotypes along with HPA axis and inflammatory changes. The behavioral outcomes (OFT, TST, FST) and cytokine shifts (TNF-α, IL-1β) are well-established and closely match the endpoints examined in our work. CRS was selected because it effectively mimics core chronic stress-induced symptoms-inflammatory response and behavioral deficits, thereby allowing assessment of drug efficacy without confounding influences from other disorder-specific symptoms. Additionally, the model accommodates repeated oral gavage necessary for THC administration. Notably, the observation that paroxetine ameliorates behavioral deficits in the stress re-stress model aligns with our finding that antidepressant treatment significantly improves CRS-induced behavioral abnormalities [19]. This consistency supports the validity of both models in revealing mechanisms underlying antidepressant drug actions.

Our findings demonstrate that THC exerts anti‑inflammatory effects in a CRS‑induced depression model, as indicated by reduced serum levels of IL‑1β and TNF‑α. This anti‑inflammatory activity may contribute to the alleviation of depression‑related behaviors, particularly by ameliorating inflammation‑associated gastrointestinal symptoms [5]. At the neurochemical level, THC restored stress‑induced DA depletion and 5‑HT levels, indicating a synergistic modulatory influence on monoaminergic systems. The preservation of 5‑HT in CRS mice further suggests that early stress may predominantly impair dopaminergic pathways. In a myocardial infarction‑induced depression model, Ginkgolide B lowered IL‑1β via the STAT3 pathway and raised 5‑HT, supporting the concept that anti‑inflammatory interventions can normalize neurotransmitter imbalances [20]. Other compounds, including 6‑Shogaol, hibiscetin, and malvidin, also alleviate depression‑ and motor‑related deficits by lowering pro‑inflammatory cytokines and modulating DA, 5‑HT, and GABA [21–23]. The cAMP signaling pathway appears central to integrating inflammatory and neurotransmitter responses. Corticosterone‑induced depression‑like behaviors are associated with hippocampal apoptosis and suppressed cAMP expression [24], consistent with reports of impaired cAMP signaling in chronic stress [25]. Weakened cAMP/PKA signaling attenuates CREB phosphorylation, compromising the transcription of neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and leading to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss-key processes in depression pathogenesis. 5‑HT₆ receptor activation alleviates neuropathic pain‑related antidepressant via the AC‑cAMP‑PKA‑MEK‑CREB‑BDNF cascade [26], and oxytocin protects dopaminergic function against corticosterone through PKA/CREB signaling [27]. Notably, the anti‑inflammatory and neurotransmitter‑modulating effects of THC may converge on the cAMP/PKA/CREB/BDNF pathway. Collectively, these mechanisms form a positive feedback cycle: reducing inflammation helps restore neurotransmitter balance, which in turn further dampens neuroinflammatory responses.

Our research results show that THC reverses the elevated HSP90 in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus of depressive-like mice. Emerging evidence demonstrates that HSP90 plays a central role in the integrated immune-neuroplasticity-stress pathway of major depressive disorder. HSP90 cooperates with its co-chaperone FKBP51 to regulate glucocorticoid receptor function, jointly maintaining HPA axis stress response homeostasis [28]; dysregulation of this process leads to glucocorticoid receptor resistance, HPA axis dysfunction, and neuroinflammation [29]. Preclinical studies confirm that targeting HSP90 effectively ameliorates depressive-like behaviors: quercetin inhibits HSP90 to suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation and levels of cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α), alleviating hippocampal neuroinflammation [30]; while rosmarinic acid downregulates HSP90 expression while activating the BDNF/TrkB/PI3K signaling pathway to promote neurogenesis [31]. Furthermore, HSP90 inhibition has been found to possess neuroprotective potential by preserving blood–brain barrier integrity [32]. Uncaria rhynchophylla, which exhibits neuroprotective effects both in vivo and in vitro, can increase the levels of dopamine and its metabolites by inhibiting the expression of HSP90 [33]. These findings collectively establish that the anti-inflammatory and neurotransmitter-modulating effects of THC may be linked to HSP90-mediated immune regulation of the stress response and its control of neuroplasticity.

Our findings demonstrate that THC administration significantly reduced P4HB levels in the serum of patients with MDD compared to those receiving escitalopram monotherapy. This effect was paralleled by a downregulation of P4HB both systemically (in serum) and in key brain regions (the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus) in the CRS mouse model of depression. As an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-resident chaperone and oxidoreductase, P4HB plays an essential role in protein folding and disulfide bond formation. Its dysfunction under nitrosative stress leads to impaired protein folding, ER stress, and neuronal apoptosis [34]. In the context of MDD, P4HB dysregulation may amplify ER stress and promote apoptotic signaling, thereby contributing to the disruption of neuro-immune and neuroplasticity pathways. Notably, ovariectomized rats exhibiting depression-like behaviors show elevated P4HB expression, further linking this protein to stress-related pathophysiology [35]. Moreover, the antidepressant paroxetine has been reported to modulate P4HB expression in neural cells [36], suggesting a role for P4HB in the mechanism of antidepressant action. By decreasing ROS production and inactivating the NF-κB pathway, P4HB knockout inhibits the inflammatory responses of macrophages, including migration, M1 polarization, and secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 [37]. DA can bind to P4HB, and the protective effect of DA against oxidative neurodegeneration is attributed to its inhibition of P4HB [38]. Our results imply that THC’s antidepressant effects may involve the inhibition of P4HB, thereby attenuating neuroinflammatory responses and promoting DA.

We report that THC administration upregulates CDH13 levels in both patients with MDD and mouse models of depression. CDH13, a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored member of the cadherin superfamily, has been identified as a risk gene for depression and several other neuropsychiatric conditions, including bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, ADHD, and autism spectrum disorders [39, 40]. Functionally, CDH13 is implicated in axonal growth, synapse formation, and neuronal plasticity-processes frequently impaired in depression [41]. Human genetic studies further associate CDH13 variants with personality traits such as low adaptability and altered neural responses during cognitive tasks, particularly in ADHD, underscoring its role in stress adaptation and executive function [42]. In Cdh13-deficient mice, disrupted excitatory-inhibitory balance in hippocampal circuits, deficits in learning and memory, and maladaptive behavioral responses to early-life stress have been observed, supporting CDH13’s function as a neuroprotective factor and a mediator of synaptic plasticity [43]. Specific knockout of CDH13 in 5-HT neurons increases neuronal density in the dorsal raphe and prefrontal cortex innervation, leading to delayed learning and impulsive-like behavior in mice [44]. CDH13 gene polymorphisms have higher C-reactive protein level and are significantly associated with immune-mediated and inflammatory diseases[45]. Importantly, CDH13 expression is regulated by chronic stress and participates in modulating neural and behavioral responses to environmental challenges. By upregulating CDH13 expression, THC may promote the restoration of synaptic integrity and adaptive plasticity, thereby counteracting the detrimental effects of chronic stress and systemic inflammation on neural circuit function.

Our study found that THC reduces serum C1QA levels in patients with depression, and this phenomenon was consistently observed both peripherally (in serum) and centrally (in the prefrontal cortex) in mouse models of depression. The complement system, as a key component of innate immunity, participates in neuroimmune regulation through multiple activation pathways. Among these, C1QA, the initiating protein of the classical pathway, plays a critical role in neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders. Chronic stress can activate the complement system, promote neuroinflammation, and exacerbate depression-like behaviors [46]. Clinical studies have also shown that serum C1Q levels are significantly elevated in patients with depression and are positively correlated with age [47]. In aged rat models exhibiting anxiety-like behaviors, C1QA gene expression is similarly upregulated [48]. Furthermore, elevated C1QA levels have been observed in patients with long COVID experiencing gastrointestinal symptoms and cognitive impairment [49]. Notably, in our study, while THC ameliorated depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors, it not only effectively alleviated gastrointestinal symptoms but also significantly reduced C1QA expression. In an Alzheimer's disease mouse model, upregulation of the immune system is evidenced by increased expression of proinflammatory genes, including elevated levels of C1QA and the cytokines TNF-α and IL-18. The rise in C1QA triggers excessive microglial activation, which in turn drives aberrant synaptic pruning, ultimately leading to synapse loss and disrupted synaptic transmission [50]. This suggests that by reducing C1QA levels, THC may attenuate microglia-mediated excessive synaptic pruning, restore synaptic integrity and adaptive plasticity, and thereby counteract the detrimental effects of stress and systemic inflammation on neural circuit function.

Our study demonstrates that THC administration upregulates serum LTF level, while downregulating prefrontal KRT6A and upregulating prefrontal APOM expression in a mouse model of depression. LTF, a multifunctional immunomodulatory protein, presents inhibition for Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, decreased the secretion of inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) [51], suggesting its broad role in maintaining immune homeostasis. Similarly, keratin proteins including KRT6A have emerged as potential biomarkers in MDD [52]. KRT6A is overexpressed in AD patients and can be further stimulated by proinflammatory cytokines, suggesting its involvement in inflammatory skin pathology. Mechanistically, KRT6A activates STAT3 and enhances proinflammatory cytokine expression in keratinocytes by reducing JAK1 ubiquitination [53]. Furthermore, it also functions as a negative regulator of IL-17a expression, thereby blocking IL-17 signaling [54]. Collectively, these findings position KRT6A as a multi-faceted regulator of skin inflammation. Its downregulation following THC treatment may thus indicate a broader role in immune modulation. Concurrently, THC-induced APOM upregulation may activate dual protective mechanisms. APOM, by binding sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), not only triggers anti-inflammatory signaling via S1P receptors but also suppresses inflammation by inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation through the PI3K/AKT pathway [55, 56]. This anti-inflammatory role is further corroborated by evidence showing that APOM-S1P inhibits ox-LDL-induced inflammation in HUVECs via the S1PR2-mediated PI3K/Akt pathway[57] and that APOM⁻/⁻ mice exhibit significant increases in pro-inflammatory proteins like iNOS, NF-κB, and IL-1β [58]. Collectively, by rebalancing LTF, KRT6A, and APOM, THC may mitigate stress-induced inflammation, ultimately alleviating depressive symptomatology.

Our integrated approach demonstrates strong concordance between computational predictions and experimental validation. Molecular docking identified several protein targets of THC, including HSP90AA1 and C1QA, whose significant binding affinities were corroborated by their altered expression in proteomic, immunofluorescence, and/or serum analyses. Other targets such as KRT6A, P4HB, APOM, and CDH13 were also consistently dysregulated across multiple assays. This docking-guided framework provided mechanistic hypotheses for THC’s pleiotropic effects and prioritized key targets for translational development. Notably, proteins consistently altered in both human serum and mouse brain (P4HB, C1QA, CDH13) form a High-Confidence Translational Core, suggesting their central role in THC’s therapeutic mechanism beyond species- or compartment-specific noise. Discordant biomarkers offer additional insights: LTF (serum-specific) may reflect a peripherally-driven immune response, whereas targets like APOM and HSP90AA1 (brain-specific) likely indicate central nervous system-specific mechanisms not fully captured in peripheral circulation. Collectively, this cross-species signature provides a systems-level roadmap of THC’s action and informs future biomarker-driven studies in depression.

In addition, THC lacks the α,β-unsaturated diene structure and consequently exhibits improved solubility, physicochemical stability, and bioavailability compared with curcumin [13, 59]. Preclinical studies have shown that chronic oral administration of THC results in higher hepatic and serum concentrations of THC and its glucuronide or sulfate conjugates relative to curcumin, indicating superior gastrointestinal absorption [60]. According to the toxicological evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority, the recommended maximum daily intake for adults (excluding pregnant and lactating women) is 300 mg when used as a dietary supplement, with no observed adverse nutritional or genotoxic effects [61]. This dosage provides a reference for the safety range in future clinical studies. To enhance the oral absorption of curcumin and its derivatives, a variety of formulation strategies have been developed [62]. Current delivery platforms, such as micelles, liposomes, phospholipid complexes, micro- and nano emulsions, solid lipid nanoparticles, nanostructured lipid carriers, biopolymer nanoparticles, and microgels, have been shown to markedly improve bioavailability [63]. Based on previous population and longitudinal proteomic studies, body-fluid protein profiles have been shown to be systematically associated with depressive phenotypes and treatment outcomes [64]. Future research may employ individualized proteomic assessments to monitor dynamic changes in differential proteins during THC treatment, thereby elucidating its potential regulatory mechanisms and biomarker value in antidepressant response.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. We planned a 1:1 allocation of 20 participants; however, 19 were randomized and 3 discontinued, which lowered power for the primary endpoint and may increase the chance of a type II error. Psychiatrists performing the ratings were blinded, yet the open-label design with active antidepressant therapy in both groups may have introduced expectancy and reporting bias. The lack of a between-group difference in HAMD-17 total should therefore be viewed cautiously. The combination of gastrointestinal improvement and concordant proteomic and inflammatory signals indicates that THC may preferentially affect somatic and inflammatory domains during the first four weeks. Longer follow-up is needed to evaluate other HAMD subdomains. Finally, this was a single-center pilot with a 29-day treatment course, which limits generalizability and may miss delayed mood responses. Larger, blinded, multicenter trials with extended follow-up are needed.

Conclusion

This study provides the first clinical and mechanistic evidence supporting THC as a novel augmentation strategy for depression. In clinical trials, THC improved gastrointestinal symptoms in patients with depression, while proteomic profiling revealed its unique capacity to modulate 32 serum proteins. Preclinically, THC reversed CRS-induced behavioral deficits in mice, ameliorating anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and restoring prefrontal-hippocampal protein. Crucially, THC exerts multimodal antidepressant effects through coordinated anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective mechanisms. These findings establish a mechanistic basis for THC's potential as an effective augmentation strategy.
