Cultural Adaptation and Evaluation of Selected Measurement Properties of Slovenian Version of Consumer Emergency Care Satisfaction Scale (CECSS‐SLO)
Authors: Urška Fekonja, Matej Strnad, Zvonka Fekonja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe aim of this study was to translate and culturally adapt the Consumer Emergency Satisfaction Scale (CECSS) into Slovenian to evaluate selected measurement properties of the CECSS‐SLO.BackgroundPatient satisfaction with emergency department (ED) care represents an important quality indicator in healthcare provision. Instruments measuring satisfaction among older ED patients are scarce, and their cross‐cultural adaptation and psychometric validation remain limited. CECSS assesses satisfaction with nursing care in the ED and has shown promising validity in international settings.MethodsA methodological cross‐sectional study was conducted in Slovenian EDs. The instrument was translated and culturally adapted using forward‐backwards translation, and expert assessment of content validity, face validity, internal consistency reliability and cultural suitability, using principal component analysis. Reliability was estimated using Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega. Ethical approval was obtained.ResultsContent validity was assessed by 10 nursing experts with a PhD degree and demonstrated excellent item‐ and scale‐level validity. Face validity and cultural adaptation were evaluated separately by 10 experienced emergency care nurses, who assessed the clarity, comprehensibility and contextual suitability of the translated items. The patient‐based psychometric evaluation was conducted among 288 patients from Slovenian EDs. PCA identified a three‐component solution whose item composition differed from the previously reported structure. Several items cross‐loaded between components, indicating ambiguity in their component membership. The three components were identified as caring, information and dissatisfaction. Internal consistency reliability was high, with alpha and omega coefficients surpassing acceptable thresholds.ConclusionThe CECSS‐SLO demonstrated satisfactory content validity and internal consistency for assessing patient satisfaction with nursing care in Slovenian EDs. Further studies should evaluate additional measurement properties including test–retest reliability, measurement error, cross‐cultural validity/measurement invariance and responsiveness. CECSS enables systematic evaluation of patient experience, identification of areas for improvement and implementation of targeted quality initiatives. Background

Patient satisfaction is a unique and subjective phenomenon that results from a comparison between the patients' needs, personal experiences during medical treatment, their subjective criteria or expected or desired outcomes, and the medical care received (Aljabouri et al. 2023; Hoonakker et al. 2022; Karasu and Öztürk 2025; López Hernández et al. 2025; Sonis et al. 2018). Other researchers have also reported that patient satisfaction can be indirectly influenced by their past experiences and the opinions of those around them, for example, relatives, friends and acquaintances (Xiong et al. 2018). In recent years, patients have become central to the comprehensive evaluation of health care quality, with patients' perceptions of the care received being a key indicator (Hoonakker et al. 2023; López Hernández et al. 2025). Patient satisfaction, which is increasingly recognised in modern healthcare systems as one of the key indicators of the quality of healthcare and the effectiveness of healthcare policies, is based on the assessment of the opinions, experiences and satisfaction of patients and their relatives during visits and treatment at healthcare institutions (Abidova et al. 2020; Amiri et al. 2020; Amporfro et al. 2021; Berkowitz 2016; Jaklič et al. 2018; Karaca and Durna 2019; Merkouris et al. 2013; Stefanini et al. 2021; Xesfingi and Vozikis 2016; Wright et al. 2013). In modern healthcare systems, patients are active users of healthcare services. In this context, patient satisfaction is becoming a key indicator of quality, which is continuously monitored using various standardised satisfaction questionnaires (Senić and Marinković 2012; Zupanc Terglav et al. 2019; Xesfingi and Vozikis 2016). Patient satisfaction is closely linked to important outcomes, including better treatment adherence, reduced use of healthcare services, fewer medical malpractice lawsuits and improved prognosis (Xesfingi and Vozikis 2016).

A higher level of patient satisfaction with the healthcare received is associated with faster physical and psychological recovery. At the same time, more satisfied patients are more willing to cooperate with the treatment process and adhere to medical instructions (Aljabouri et al. 2023; Özlü and Uzun 2015; Xesfingi and Vozikis 2016). Patient satisfaction in the emergency department (ED) must be understood within the specific clinical and organisational context of emergency care. Patients commonly present with acute symptoms, pain, anxiety and uncertainty, whereas care is delivered in an environment characterised by varying levels of patient acuity, time pressure, ED crowding and waiting times.

Patient satisfaction reflects the interplay between perceived clinical quality, interpersonal interactions, communication, timeliness and the organisation of care. Across studies, patients evaluate care more positively when healthcare professionals are perceived as clinically competent, experienced and capable of delivering safe and professional care (Aaronson et al. 2018; Blanco‐Abril et al. 2010; Messina et al. 2014). However, technical competence alone does not ensure a positive patient experience. Satisfaction is also associated with whether patients feel respected, heard and treated as individuals rather than merely as clinical cases (Buchanan et al. 2015; Rehman and Ali 2016; López Hernández et al. 2025).

Empathy, kindness, attentiveness, staff availability and responsiveness to patients' physical and emotional needs are associated with trust and a sense of security. Conversely, impersonal interactions, limited contact with staff, and the perception that staff are rushed or unavailable contribute to dissatisfaction (Buchanan et al. 2015; Ríos‐Risquez and García‐Izquierdo 2016; Haug et al. 2022; López Hernández et al. 2025). Communication is particularly important throughout the ED encounter. Clear, understandable, and timely information about procedures, test results, treatment progress, waiting times and discharge contributes to a more positive experience (Taylor and Benger 2004; Abass et al. 2021; De Steenwinkel et al. 2022), whereas incomplete information, medical jargon and inconsistent communication increase dissatisfaction (Hermann et al. 2019; Haug et al. 2022).

The organisation and timeliness of care further contribute to patients' evaluations of their ED experience. Shorter actual and perceived waiting times, effective triage and coordinated patient flow are associated with greater satisfaction, whereas delays, crowding and uncertainty about waiting times have the opposite effect (Jaklič et al. 2018; Fontova‐Almató et al. 2019; Bhojak et al. 2023). Effective pain management is related to satisfaction (Welch 2010; Fontova‐Almató et al. 2019). The physical and psychosocial environment also shapes the patient's experience. Privacy, cleanliness, comfort, calm surroundings, involvement in decision‐making, and a sense of physical and psychological safety contribute to a more favourable overall evaluation of emergency care (Tanniru and Khuntia 2017; Stefanini et al. 2021).

Systematic assessment of patient satisfaction is particularly important in the ED because it provides information about aspects of care that may not be adequately captured by conventional clinical indicators, including interpersonal care, communication, responsiveness, perceived safety and preparation for discharge. These aspects are especially relevant in an environment in which patients may experience pain, anxiety, limited continuity of care and limited time for communication with healthcare professionals. Assessing satisfaction can therefore help identify modifiable aspects of emergency nursing care, guide quality‐improvement initiatives and support the evaluation of patient‐centred care.

One of the most widely used instruments for measuring patient satisfaction with nursing care in the ED is the Consumer Emergency Care Satisfaction Scale (CECSS) (Hoonakker et al. 2023). The CECSS was developed in the late 20th century by Barbara A. Davis and originally comprised 25 items that captured key aspects of patients' experiences in the emergency care setting such as psychological safety, the technical competence of healthcare personnel, communication of information and patient education at discharge (Davis et al. 1997). It is based on a conceptual analysis of the literature on patient satisfaction with emergency medical care as well as on theoretical perspectives related to caring and satisfaction. The CECSS, as a validated empirical measurement instrument, is intended to assess patient satisfaction and personal experiences in the ED as a specific clinical environment (Chan and Chau 2005; Davis and Duffy 1999; Messina et al. 2014; Saiboon et al. 2008). The main purpose of the CECSS questionnaire is to identify patient needs and improve the quality of nursing care in the ED (Hoonakker et al. 2023).

The CECSS has previously been administered to patients in a Slovenian ED. Davis and Bush (2003) included a Slovenian cohort of 60 patients in a cross‐national comparison of patient satisfaction in Slovenia, Australia and the United States. However, the translation used in that study was not validated through back‐translation, and the study was not designed as a systematic cultural adaptation or validation of a Slovenian‐language version. It focused on comparisons of the caring and teaching subscales and did not evaluate content validity or the dimensional structure of the translated instrument. Although the CECSS had previously been administered to Slovenian patients, no version developed through a documented process of translation, cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation was available. The present study addresses this gap by developing the CECSS‐SLO to enable consistent assessment of caring, communication and information among patients discharged home from Slovenian emergency departments, whereas recognising that linguistic, cultural and organisational differences require evaluation of the instrument's measurement properties before its use in clinical practice and research. Psychometric evaluation of the CECSS‐SLO is therefore necessary to establish that the Slovenian version measures the intended dimensions of satisfaction reliably before it is implemented in clinical practice or research.

Aim

The aim of this study was to translate and culturally adapt the Consumer Emergency Care Satisfaction Scale (CECSS) into Slovenian to evaluate selected measurement properties of the CECSS‐SLO, specifically content validity and internal consistency.

Methods

Instrument

The Consumer Emergency Care Satisfaction Scale (CECSS)

The CECSS consists of 19 items scored on a five‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) (Chan and Chau 2005; Cuñado et al. 2002; Davis 2009; Davis and Duffy 1999; Ekwall and Davis 2010; Hoonakker et al. 2022, 2023; Johansson et al. 2011; López Hernández et al. 2025; Messina et al. 2014; Saiboon et al. 2008). The CECSS is not designed for every patient seen in the ED; it applies only to those who are discharged to go home. It excludes patients who are admitted to the hospital. Some items on the CECSS concern discharge instructions, and hospitalised patients do not receive these instructions while still in the ED (Hoonakker et al. 2022).

Because patient satisfaction questionnaires often exhibit a ceiling effect, we applied an extended version of the CECSS that includes negatively worded items, thereby broadening the score distribution and enabling a more sensitive assessment of patient satisfaction. For the analysis of patient satisfaction, a modified form of the CECSS questionnaire was used. Based on the most recent validation study by Hoonakker et al. (2023), we adopted a three‐dimensional model comprising the following sub‐scales: (I) caring (12 items), (II) information (3 items) and (III) dissatisfaction (4 items). For scoring purposes, we followed the established CECSS scoring methodology described by Hoonakker et al. (2023). First, we calculated the total CECSS score, which reflects the patient's overall experience with emergency nursing care (19–95 points), followed by separate scoring for caring (12–60 points), information (3–15 points) and dissatisfaction (4–20 points). The scoring includes four negatively worded items, which are incorporated into the CECSS to minimise response bias. CECSS‐SLO retained four negatively worded items (Item 5, Item 9, Item 14 and Item 17), which were reverse‐scored (1 = 5, 2 = 4, 3 = 3, 4 = 2 and 5 = 1) before the calculation of the overall CECSS‐SLO score, consistent with the scoring approach used by Kristensson and Ekwall (2008) and described by Hoonakker et al. (2022, 2023). Consequently, higher overall scores indicated a more positive patient experience. Consequently, higher scores on both the overall scale and the reverse‐scored dissatisfaction subscale indicated greater patient satisfaction.

The earlier Slovenian application reported by Davis and Bush (2003) used the original CECSS scoring structure, comprising the 12‐item caring and three‐item teaching subscales, whereas the four negatively worded items were included to minimise response bias but were not scored. In contrast, the present study used the extended 19‐item approach, in which the four negatively worded items were included in scoring after reverse coding, and examined the resulting three‐component empirical structure in a Slovenian ED population.

The overall CECSS‐SLO score was calculated as a composite indicator of patient satisfaction across the assessed aspects of emergency nursing care, consistent with the reporting of full‐scale scores by Hoonakker et al. (2023). It is interpreted alongside sub‐scales and item‐level findings, without assuming one‐dimensionality of the instrument. Consequently, lower total CECSS scores indicate greater overall patient dissatisfaction with emergency nursing care, whereas higher total scores indicate a more positive patient experience.

The Process of Translating the Questionnaire

CECSS, developed and adapted by Peter Hoonakker, was used (Hoonakker et al. 2023) for the purposes of this article. Prior to adaptation, formal written permission to use, translate, and validate the CECSS questionnaire in our study was obtained from the author Peter Hoonakker.

CECSS was translated from English into Slovenian using a forward–backward translation procedure following the guidelines of Polit and Beck (2021).

Two independent native Slovenian speakers (one clinically active nurse researcher and one bilingual expert) translated the questionnaire into Slovenian. The translator was a bilingual language specialist with formal postgraduate education in the English language and professional certification in translation. Both translators were instructed to use straightforward language and avoid metaphors, informal wording, passive constructions and hypothetical phrasing. The translations were compared and synthesised by an expert review committee, resulting in a preliminary Slovenian version. To achieve semantic equivalence, a third independent translator performed the back‐translation into English. The meaning of each item after translation was the same as in the original. Discrepancies between the original and back‐translated versions were discussed and resolved, resulting in a pre‐final Slovenian version. This version was subsequently evaluated for content validity, followed by expert assessment of face validity and cultural adaptation. No difficulties arose throughout the consecutive translation process. The study was conducted by researchers in an urban area in Slovenia. The items in the translated version of CECSS‐SLO were easy to understand, and the questionnaire could be completed in less than 10 min, which is comparable to other versions of the CECSS questionnaires.

The Process of Testing the Validity Based on the Expert's Agreement

Content Validity

Ten experts with a PhD degree in nursing participated in the review and evaluation of the questionnaire (Bannigan and Watson 2009; Polit and Beck 2006). The content validity assessment was performed in accordance with the methodological guidelines for calculating the Content Validity Index (CVI) by Yusoff (2019). The experts evaluated each item and the overall questionnaire using a four‐point scale where 1 indicated ‘not relevant’, 2 ‘somewhat relevant’, 3 ‘quite relevant’ and 4 ‘highly relevant’ (Polit and Beck 2021). Item Content Validity Index (I‐CVI), Scale Validity Index (S‐CVI), Average Scale Validity Index (S‐CVI/Ave) and Scale‐level Content Validity Index based on Universal Agreement (S‐CVI/UA) were calculated. For the I‐CVI calculation, only ratings 3 and 4 were considered relevant. The scores were rated good when I‐CVI was ≥ 0.78, S‐CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 and S‐CVI/UA ≥ 0.80 (Polit and Beck 2021; Shi et al. 2012). When interpreting the results, modified Cohen's kappa κ* was calculated, using the following formula κ*=ICVI−Pc1−Pc and following the guidelines of Cicchetti and Sparrow (1981). The following 𝜅* values were interpreted as moderate (0.40–0.59); good (0.60–0.74) and excellent (> 0.75) (Fleiss et al. 2003; Cicchetti and Sparrow 1981; Polit et al. 2007).

The I‐CVI, S‐CVI, S‐CVI/Ave, S‐CVI/UA and 𝜅* values were calculated using Microsoft Excel.

Expert‐Assessed Face Validity and Cultural Adaptation

Face validity and cultural adaptation of the instrument were evaluated using a convenience sample of 10 experienced nurses working in emergency care settings. Emergency care nurses were selected because of their familiarity with the terminology, communication practices, care processes and organisational context of Slovenian EDs. They assessed whether the translated items appeared clear, comprehensible, linguistically appropriate, and suitable for use in Slovenian EDs. They were also invited to propose alternative wording where an item was considered ambiguous or insufficiently adapted to the Slovenian emergency care context. The research team reviewed the proposed modifications and reached consensus on the final wording. This procedure provided an expert‐based assessment of face validity and cultural suitability. It did not include a structured patient‐based face‐validity assessment or cognitive interviewing.

The Process of Psychometric Testing Based on the Survey Data

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study was conducted.

Setting and Participants

The study was conducted in 10 Slovenian EDs. Patients were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, understood Slovenian, had received ED care for at least 2 h, had received nursing care during the current ED visit, were clinically stable, and were expected to be discharged home. Patients were not approached if they had an impaired level of consciousness, a diagnosed or clinically apparent cognitive or psychological condition that could interfere with questionnaire completion, a life‐threatening condition, severe pain, nausea or excessive fatigue, or if they were unable to complete the questionnaire in Slovenian. Those patients were excluded because participation during emergency treatment was neither clinically nor ethically appropriate. Patients admitted to hospital, transferred to another facility or leaving the ED before completion of care were not eligible because the CECSS includes items concerning nursing care and information provided during a completed ED encounter.

Data Collection Procedure

Data collection was conducted from October 2025 to January 2026. The required sample size was estimated using Cochran's formula, assuming a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, which yielded a target sample of 221 participants. Sample‐size planning also followed the commonly used recommendation of recruiting at least 10 participants per questionnaire item for psychometric evaluation (Polit and Beck 2021), a criterion also applied in a previous psychometric evaluation of the 19‐item CECSS (Wennick et al. 2021). This corresponded to a minimum of 190 participants. The final sample of 288 participants exceeded both sample‐size criteria.

Prior to data collection, approval was obtained from the management of each participating hospital, the relevant ethics committees and the nurse managers of the participating emergency departments (EDs). Questionnaire distribution was conducted in accordance with arrangements agreed upon with the management of each ED. The researchers personally delivered the questionnaires to four EDs. In one ED, the questionnaires were made available electronically through the 1KA platform at the request of the head nurse. In all other participating EDs, they were sent by post in accordance with prior agreements. The head nurses of the participating EDs were responsible for distributing the questionnaires to patients. Eligible patients were invited to participate after the treating physician had informed them that they would be discharged home. They received the questionnaire while waiting for their discharge documentation including the discharge letter, and completed it independently in the ED waiting area. Completed questionnaires were placed in sealed envelopes and deposited in a designated collection box at each participating ED, thereby preventing clinical staff from accessing or reviewing individual responses. No names, contact details, medical‐record numbers or other personal identifiers were collected. Data collection concluded at the end of the predefined study period. For logistical reasons, completed paper questionnaires were collected in person from one ED. In another ED, responses were collected electronically, and the online survey was closed after 2 months. All remaining EDs returned the completed questionnaires to the principal researcher by post. The principal researcher neither participated in identifying participants nor had access to any information that could link individual questionnaires to specific respondents. Following collection, each questionnaire was coded and assigned a unique numerical identifier.

A total of 445 eligible patients were offered a questionnaire, of whom 288 returned a completed questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 64.7%. The remaining 157 questionnaires were not returned. Because participation was anonymous, it was not possible to distinguish patients who explicitly declined from those who accepted but did not return the questionnaire. No individual reminders or follow‐up contacts were made.

Internal Consistency

To assess the internal consistency of the measurement instrument, we used Cronbach's α coefficient and McDonald's ω. We adhered to the following criteria for Cronbach's α coefficient: α ≤ 0.50 = is considered unacceptable; 0.50 ≤ α ≤ 0.60 indicates poor reliability; 0.60 ≤ α ≤ 0.70 = reflects acceptable reliability; 0.70 ≤ α ≤ 0.90 represents good reliability; and α ≥ 0.90 corresponds to excellent reliability (Tavakol and Dennick 2011).

When interpreting the McDonald's ω values, we applied the recommended cut‐offs: ω < 0.60 indicates insufficient reliability; 0.60 < ω < 0.69 is considered marginal reliability; 0.70 < ω < 0.79 reflects acceptable reliability; 0.80 < ω < 0.89 represents good reliability; and ω ≥ 0.90 corresponds to excellent reliability (Hayes and Coutts 2020).

Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega were calculated using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0. Furthermore, McDonald's omega was computed in IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 using the supplementary OMEGA macro described by Hayes and Coutts (2020).

Principal Component Analysis

PCA is a multivariate data‐reduction technique that transforms correlated observed variables into a smaller number of uncorrelated linear components that successively maximise explained variance while retaining as much information as possible (Jolliffe and Cadima 2016), whereas orthogonal varimax rotation increases the contrast between high and low loadings, reduces cross‐loadings and facilitates the interpretation of distinct components (Tabachnick and Fidell 2019).

PCA with orthogonal varimax rotation was selected as an exploratory data‐reduction method to examine how the 19 observed CECSS‐SLO items grouped empirically and whether the resulting item groupings were broadly consistent with the dimensions reported in previous CECSS studies. The analysis aimed to summarise the observed items into a smaller number of meaningful, distinct and practically interpretable components that could be used to derive internally consistent component scores. Orthogonal varimax rotation was applied to obtain a simpler and more interpretable component structure by increasing the contrast between high and low cross loadings and facilitating the assignment of items to individual components. Similar applications of PCA with varimax rotation have been reported in patient‐satisfaction research, where the method has been used to reduce questionnaire items to fewer interpretable dimensions and derive composite scores for analyses (Kleefstra et al. 2012; Sparkes et al. 2019; Karasu and Öztürk 2025). In the present study, the resulting orthogonal solution was interpreted as an empirical data‐reduction structure rather than as evidence that the corresponding dimensions of patient satisfaction were conceptually independent or as an estimate of an underlying latent‐variable model.

To assess the suitability of the data for PCA, we calculated the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) index to assess sampling adequacy for PCA. In doing so, we followed the guidelines of the authors Tabachnick and Fidell (2019), whereby KMO values ≥ 0.6 indicate acceptable suitability of the data for PCA, and KMO values > 0.8 indicate very good suitability. Simultaneously with the KMO, we verified the adequacy of the correlation matrix using Bartlett's test of sphericity. Bartlett's test of sphericity showed us whether the variables are interrelated and suitable for PCA. In the final step of the PCA analysis, we produced a graphical representation for deciding on the number of components of the measurement instrument (Cattell's Scree Plot) (Tabachnick and Fidell 2019). We considered eigenvalues > 1.0 and included cross loadings with absolute values ≥ 0.4 (Tabachnick and Fidell 2019). Variables with communalities below 0.40 were excluded to ensure meaningful contributions to the total variance.

Data Analysis

We analysed the data using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 28.0). We also used descriptive statistics to characterise the study sample, including gender and age, marital status, ED admission process, duration of overall treatment and the recommendation of the visited ED to family and/or friends. We calculated means (M) and standard deviations (SD) to describe normally distributed data.

Missing data were examined at the item level before statistical analysis. Item‐level missingness was low, ranging from 0 to 5 responses per item (0%–1.74%). Across the 19 CECSS‐SLO items, 43 of 5472 possible item responses were missing (0.79%). No imputation of missing values was performed. Listwise deletion applied according to the requirements of the respective statistical analysis. The exact number of participants included in each psychometric analysis is reported in Section 3.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval has been obtained from the Ethics Committee of the University of Maribor, Faculty of Health Sciences (Approval no. 038/2025/3075‐3/901) on 30 June 2025, and the National Medical Ethics Committee of the Republic of Slovenia (Approval no. 0120‐355/2025‐2711‐3) on 22 July 2025.

Participants were informed in writing about the study and their rights including confidentiality, anonymity and the right to withdraw at any stage of the research. Completion of the questionnaire indicated implied and voluntary consent. The authors follow the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association 2001) and the Oviedo Convention (Council of Europe 2001). Anonymity was ensured by not collecting any directly identifiable personal data. The questionnaires did not include participants' names, initials, dates of birth, addresses, medical record numbers or other identifiers that would allow individual participants to be recognised. Completed questionnaires were handled without personal identifiers, and the data were entered, analysed and reported only in aggregated form. Access to the completed questionnaires and electronic dataset was restricted to the research team.

Reporting Framework and Measurement Properties

The CECSS is a patient‐reported measure of satisfaction with nursing care in the ED and can be considered a context‐specific patient‐reported experience measure (PREM). It reflects patients' views of the care they received, particularly in relation to caring, communication and information. The COSMIN (COnsensus‐based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments) methodology has previously been applied to PREMs (Mihaljevic et al. 2022). Therefore, the relevant COSMIN recommendations were followed when evaluating and reporting the measurement properties of the CECSS‐SLO.

Following the COSMIN taxonomy, the study assessed content validity, expert‐assessed face validity and cultural adaptation, internal consistency and the empirical component structure using PCA. Structural validity was not fully evaluated because EFA and CFA were not performed. Test–retest reliability, measurement error, cross‐cultural validity/measurement invariance, hypothesis testing for construct validity, criterion validity and responsiveness were not assessed because the design involved a single anonymous administration without repeated measurements, comparison groups, external measures or an accepted criterion.

The cross‐sectional component of the study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist.

Results

Validation Based on Experts' Agreement

The expert panel consisted of 10 nursing experts with a PhD degree. The panel reviewed the translated CECSS‐SLO and assessed the relevance of each item and of the overall questionnaire for use in the Slovenian emergency care context. Their evaluations were used to calculate the item‐level and scale‐level content validity indices.

Content Validity

Content validity showed strong consensus among experts and is presented in Table 1. All items meet or exceed the minimum I‐CVI criteria of 0.78, confirming satisfactory relevance for the Slovenian context. The modified kappa index ranged from 0.899 to 1.00, indicating excellent agreement. The S‐CVI/Ave was 0.989, and the S‐CVI/UA was 89.47%. These indices demonstrated high expert agreement regarding overall content validity of the translated CECSS items for the Slovenian emergency care context and supported proceeding to subsequent psychometric testing. These findings support the suitability of the translated instrument for subsequent psychometric testing.

Expert‐Assessed Face Validity and Cultural Adaptation

Based on expert feedback, minor clarifications and cultural adaptations were recommended, whereas all items remained in the CECSS‐SLO instrument.

The experts considered all 19 items understandable, linguistically appropriate and applicable to the Slovenian ED context. Nevertheless, expert assessment resulted in minor linguistic clarifications and contextual adaptations to three negatively worded items. In Item 5 (‘The health care workers should have been more attentive than they were’), the direct formulation that the nurse should have been more attentive was rephrased using a more natural and less accusatory formulation in Slovenian (‘At times, I felt that I needed more attention from nursing staff’). In Item 9 (‘The nurse seemed too busy at the nurses' station to spend time talking with me’), the reference to the ‘nurses' station’ was omitted because staff availability in Slovenian EDs is not necessarily associated with a specific physical location; the item therefore focused on perceived staff busyness and lack of time for communication (‘Nurses were too busy and could not spare the time to speak with me’). In Item 14, the idiomatic expression ‘treated me as a number instead of a person’ was rendered as ‘being treated impersonally’, which was considered to retain the central concept and more natural in Slovenian. These adaptations were intended to retain the evaluative direction and core meaning of the original items while improving linguistic naturalness and contextual applicability. No items were added or deleted. Additional editorial refinements were limited to grammatical clarity and naturalness of expression and were not intended to change the meaning of the items. The general terms such as references to an individual nurse or to health care workers were systematically translated as ‘Zaposleni v zdravstveni negi’ (English: nursing staff), thereby aligning the wording with the instrument's specific focus on nursing care.

Following the expert assessment of face validity, the CECSS‐SLO was considered ready for a subsequent psychometric evaluation.

Psychometric Testing Based on A Cross‐Sectional Study

Participants

The researchers distributed 445 questionnaires, of which 288 were returned which represented 64.7% response rate. Valid responses across individual CECSS‐SLO items ranged from n = 283 to n = 288. Item‐level missingness within the 19‐item CECSS‐SLO was minimal. The number of missing responses ranged from 0 to 5 per item (0%–1.74%), with 43 missing responses among 5472 possible item responses overall (0.79%). Communalities were analysed to assess the extent to which the underlying component structure explained each item's variance. Communalities for the CECSS‐SLO ranged from 0.433 to 0.760, indicating that the extracted components explained 43.3%–76.0% of each item's variance. Five items (1, 6, 11, 12 and 15) showed loadings above 0.40 on both components, information and caring, indicating overlap in their item composition. The small differences between primary and secondary loadings for Items 11 (0.608 vs. 0.565) and 1 (0.543 vs. 0.454) suggest dimensional ambiguity and warrant caution in assigning these items exclusively to either component (Table 4).

Component 1: Information

Component 1 was provisionally labelled ‘Information’ to emphasise its informational content, particularly guidance on problems to watch for (Item 6), understandable explanations (Item 10), opportunities to ask questions (Item 16) and ensuring that questions are answered (Item 19), which may support patients' understanding of care and preparation for discharge. However, explanations before procedures (Item 8), understanding patients' feelings (Item 15) and taking time to meet their needs (Item 18) indicate that the component also encompasses broader informational and interpersonal aspects of nursing care. Seven items loaded primarily on Component 1, labelled ‘Information’, with primary component loadings ranging from 0.629 to 0.787. Two items also showed secondary loadings on Component 2. Item 15 (‘The nurse seemed to understand how I felt’) loaded primarily on information (0.706) and secondarily on caring (0.484), whereas Item 6 (‘The nurse told me what problems to watch for’) loaded 0.668 on information and 0.403 on caring. These cross‐loadings are consistent with the close relationship between the provision of information and interpersonal caring. Information provided by nurses may also be perceived by patients as an expression of attentiveness, understanding and concern for their individual need.

The first component explained 45.296% of the variance.

Component 2: Caring

Component 2 was provisionally labelled ‘Caring’ because its eight items describe professional and interpersonal aspects of nursing care alongside preparation for care at home: skilful performance of nursing duties (Item 1), knowledge of the patient's illness or problem (Item 2), recognition of treatment needs, information about what to expect at home (Item 4 and 7), understanding when listening to patients' problems (Item 11), concern for pain, fear and anxiety (Item 12), and gentleness during painful procedures (Item 13).

Eight items loaded primarily on Component 2, labelled ‘Caring’, with primary component loadings ranging from 0.543 to 0.776. Three items also loaded on Component 1. Item 12 (‘The nurse seemed genuinely concerned about my pain, fear, and anxiety’) loaded 0.668 on Component 2 and 0.465 on Component 1. Item 11 (‘The nurse was understanding when listening to my problem’) loaded 0.608 on Component 2 and 0.565 on Component 1, whereas Item 1 (‘The nurse performed his/her duties with skill’) loaded 0.543 on Component 2 and 0.454 on Component 1. These findings are compatible with the clinical reality that caring, attentive communication and the provision of understandable information are closely connected aspects of patients' experiences of nursing care. The particularly similar loadings of Item 11 on the two components suggest that the item may reflect both interpersonal caring and information‐related communication.

The second component explained 12.394% of the variance.

Component 3: Dissatisfaction

Component 3 was provisionally labelled ‘Dissatisfaction’ because its four items describe unfavourable interpersonal experiences of nursing care: insufficient attentiveness (Item 5), limited availability for conversation (Item 9), impersonal treatment (Item 14) and perceived unfriendliness (Item 17).

Four items loaded on Component 3, provisionally labelled ‘Dissatisfaction’, with component loadings ranging from 0.781 to 0.824. No cross‐loadings above the predefined threshold of 0.40 were observed for these items. All four items were negatively worded and reverse scored. Their common loading on a separate component is consistent with their shared focus on unfavourable experiences of nursing care. As noted above, however, the dissatisfaction component comprised all negatively worded items in the instrument; their grouping may reflect substantive dissatisfaction, a wording‐related method effect, or both. The present PCA cannot distinguish between these explanations. Therefore, this component should be interpreted cautiously and should not be regarded as unequivocal evidence of a distinct substantive dimension (DiStefano and Motl 2006).

The third component explained 5.319% of the variance.

Discussion

This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt and evaluate the measurement properties of the CECSS‐SLO for assessing patient satisfaction with nursing care in Slovenian EDs. Particular emphasis was placed on examining expert‐assessed content and face validity, internal consistency and the empirical component structure to determine whether the Slovenian version meets methodological standards for both clinical and research applications.

This study provides evidence supporting expert‐assessed content validity and internal consistency of the CECSS‐SLO and describes its empirical component structure. These findings represent an initial evaluation of selected measurement properties. In our study, the CECSS‐SLO showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.902; ω = 0.893).

Comparable reliability estimates have been reported in previous studies. Cronbach's alpha of the full CECSS scale ranged from α = 0.81 (Cuñado et al. 2002) to α = 0.97 (Chan and Chau 2005), with additional studies reporting α = 0.87 in paediatric EDs (Wennick et al. 2021) and α = 0.93 for adapted versions of the CECSS (Elder et al. 2004). The caring subscale showed reliability coefficients ranging from α = 0.77 (Chan and Chau 2005) to α = 0.92 (Kristensson and Ekwall 2008; Walsh and Walsh 1999), α = 0.85 (Davis and Duffy 1999), α = 0.84–0.89 (Davis and Bush 2003), α = 0.93 (Ekwall et al. 2008) and α = 0.88 (Wennick et al. 2021). Whereas the Teaching subscale ranged between α = 0.85 (Wennick et al. 2021) and α = 0.90–0.95 (Davis and Bush 2003), α = 0.86 (Chan and Chau 2005), α = 0.88 (Kristensson and Ekwall 2008), α = 0.88 (Davis and Duffy 1999) and α = 0.89 (Walsh and Walsh 1999), with Buckley (2009) reporting α = 0.93.

In comparison, the CECSS‐SLO demonstrated good to excellent internal consistency across all subscales (α = 0.907, 0.897 and 0.810 for information, caring and dissatisfaction), suggesting strong psychometric performance and cross‐cultural stability. Strong content validity (S‐CVI/Ave = 0.989; S‐CVI/UA = 89.47%) indicated strong expert agreement regarding item relevance, providing additional support for selected measurement properties of the Slovenian version.

The PCA revealed a three‐component solution, namely caring, information and dissatisfaction. The inclusion of four negatively worded items, the CECSS can better identify patients who are dissatisfied with certain aspects of care. These items also reduce the ceiling effect and make the questionnaire more sensitive to differences in satisfaction (Hoonakker et al. 2022, 2023). Using the previous scoring method, 23.4% of patients achieved the maximum possible score, indicating a substantial ceiling effect. After including the four negatively worded items, the proportion of patients with the maximum score decreased to 4.8%, suggesting improved instrument sensitivity and reduced ceiling effects. Similar findings were reported by Hoonakker et al. (2023), who demonstrated that the original CECSS scoring method resulted in a substantial ceiling effect, with 42% of older ED patients achieving the maximum possible score. After incorporating the four negatively worded items into the scoring procedure, the percentage of patients with a maximum score decreased to 0%, indicating improved discriminatory capacity of the scale. Overall, both studies show that the negatively worded items help reduce score clustering at the top and make it easier to distinguish different levels of patient satisfaction in the ED. Although the PCA identified three components, their item composition differed from the original described structure, with 8 items in caring, 7 items in information and 4 items in dissatisfaction. Several items cross‐loaded between caring and information, with the near‐equivalent loadings of Item 11 (0.608 and 0.565, respectively) indicating ambiguity in its component membership. The component labels therefore represent provisional interpretations of the predominant item content, and the findings should not be regarded as a replication of the previously reported structure.

From a clinical perspective, the availability of the CECSS‐SLO addresses an important gap in the systematic assessment of patient satisfaction with nursing care in Slovenian EDs. Although the CECSS had previously been administered to a Slovenian patient cohort (Davis and Bush 2003), no systematically translated, culturally adapted and psychometrically evaluated Slovenian version had been available for use in ED. The CECSS‐SLO provides healthcare professionals and ED managers with a standardised instrument for assessing patients' perceptions of key aspects of emergency nursing care, including caring, information and dissatisfaction. Its use in clinical practice may facilitate the identification of areas in which patients report lower levels of satisfaction and provide patient‐reported information to support quality‐improvement initiatives. Repeated use of the instrument may also enable EDs to monitor patterns in patient satisfaction over time and evaluate differences in patient experience across clinical settings. At the organisational level, aggregated CECSS‐SLO results may complement other quality indicators by incorporating the patient perspective into the evaluation of emergency nursing care. However, given that responsiveness and test–retest reliability have not yet been established, its use for evaluating changes over time or the effects of specific quality‐improvement interventions should be interpreted with caution and requires further psychometric evaluation.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is the systematic translation, cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation of the CECSS‐SLO using a multistep approach that included forward–backward translation, expert assessment, face and content validity evaluation and psychometric testing in a patient sample. The inclusion of both Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega provided complementary estimates of internal consistency, whereas the assessment of content validity using item‐ and scale‐level indices strengthened the evaluation of the relevance of the translated items. The sample size was also adequate for the exploratory assessment of the component structure of the 19‐item instrument.

Several methodological limitations should nevertheless be considered.

First, in accordance with the COSMIN framework, the present study evaluated content validity and internal consistency and explored the empirical component structure using PCA. However, several other measurement properties were not examined. Test–retest reliability and measurement error could not be assessed because repeated measurements were not included in the cross‐sectional design. Responsiveness was not evaluated because the study did not involve longitudinal follow‐up or an intervention. Criterion validity was not examined because no appropriate gold‐standard measure was available, whereas cross‐cultural validity/measurement invariance was not formally tested. Hypothesis testing for construct validity against related measures was also beyond the scope of the present study (Mokkink et al. 2010; Prinsen et al. 2018). Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence supporting selected measurement properties of the CECSS‐SLO rather than as a comprehensive confirmation of its psychometric performance.

Second, the dimensional structure of the CECSS‐SLO was explored using principal component analysis (PCA). Although PCA provided useful information on the empirical grouping of items, it is primarily a data‐reduction technique and does not provide the same evidence for latent structural validity as common factor analysis or confirmatory factor analysis (Fabrigar et al. 1999; Costello and Osborne 2005). Future studies should therefore examine the proposed three‐component structure using factor‐analytic methods, particularly confirmatory factor analysis in an independent sample.

Third, 288 of the 445 distributed questionnaires were returned, corresponding to a response rate of 64.7%. Although this provided an adequate sample for the present psychometric analyses, information about non‐respondents was not available. Eligibility was limited to clinically stable, Slovenian‐speaking adults discharged home. Consequently, the findings may not represent patients with greater clinical acuity, language barriers, or those admitted or transferred, whose experiences and care needs may differ. Consequently, potential non‐response bias cannot be excluded, as patients who chose to participate may have differed systematically in their experiences or satisfaction from those who did not participate (Groves and Peytcheva 2008). In addition, the CECSS is intended for patients discharged home and therefore does not capture the experiences of patients who were admitted to hospital, transferred or left against medical advice. Consequently, the sample may not be fully representative of the broader population of ED patients discharged home, and the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution regarding generalisability. Future studies using random or consecutive sampling strategies would strengthen the representativeness of the study sample.

A key limitation of the CECSS instrument, as highlighted by Hoonakker et al. (2022), is its limited applicability across the full spectrum of ED patients. Specifically, the instrument is suitable only for patients discharged home and does not capture the experiences of those who are admitted, transferred or leave against medical advice. This restricts the generalisability of the findings and limits the instrument's usefulness for evaluating overall quality of ED care. An additional consideration concerns the four negatively worded items included in the instrument. Although such items may help reduce ceiling effects and improve differentiation among highly satisfied respondents, negatively phrased items can increase cognitive demands and may introduce response inconsistency or method effects (van Sonderen et al. 2013; Suárez‐Álvarez et al. 2018). This may be particularly relevant in an ED population experiencing pain, fatigue, stress or emotional distress. Their performance should therefore be examined further in future psychometric studies. An additional limitation concerns the interpretation of the four‐item dissatisfaction component, which was formed entirely by these negatively worded items. Its emergence as a separate component may therefore reflect substantive dissatisfaction, a wording‐related method effect, or both. Consequently, the dissatisfaction component should not be interpreted as unequivocal evidence of a distinct substantive dimension, and scores derived from it should be used and interpreted with corresponding caution. The present exploratory PCA cannot distinguish substantive variance from method variance associated with negative wording. Future studies in independent samples should compare theoretically justified substantive CFA models with appropriately identified models that explicitly incorporate a wording‐related method factor.

A further methodological limitation is that patients from the target population were not directly involved in a structured face‐validity assessment or cognitive interviewing during the pretesting stage. Although experienced emergency care nurses assessed the apparent clarity, comprehensibility, linguistic appropriateness and contextual suitability of the translated items, their professional perspective cannot fully represent how patients understand and interpret the questionnaire.

Finally, data collection was conducted during the winter months, when ED attendance and crowding may differ from other periods of the year. Seasonal variation in patient volume, waiting times, and workload may have influenced patients' experiences and satisfaction ratings. Future studies conducted across different seasons, healthcare institutions and patient populations are needed to examine the generalisability and stability of the CECSS‐SLO findings.

Implications for Nursing Practice

Satisfaction remains a complex construct to assess and continues to present methodological challenges for researchers. Nevertheless, when it serves as an indicator of nursing‐care quality, the individual who received the care can reasonably be considered the most relevant source of information.

In Slovenian ED, the CECSS‐SLO can be used to obtain structured feedback from clinically stable, Slovenian‐speaking adults who are discharged home after completing their emergency care. Aggregated item‐level findings can help nurses and nursing managers recognise aspects of the patient experience that are not routinely captured by clinical or organisational indicators, including whether patients felt heard and treated attentively, whether communication was understandable, and whether they received sufficient information to manage their care after discharge.

The findings can be translated into focused improvements within individual EDs and, where applicable, associated observation units. Lower ratings on caring‐related items may guide clinical supervision, reflective team discussions and education addressing empathy, responsiveness and communication during periods of crowding and time pressure. Less favourable evaluations of information may prompt a review of verbal and written instructions, the consistent explanation of warning signs, and confirmation that patients understand how to continue their care at home.

Conclusion

This study introduces a culturally adapted version of the CECSS with an empirically derived three‐component structure that reflects patient satisfaction in the Slovenian ED. Evaluating patient satisfaction has become increasingly relevant in healthcare, although reliable measurement remains challenging. The availability of the CECSS‐SLO provides Slovenian EDs with a standardised patient‐reported instrument that may support the systematic assessment of satisfaction with emergency nursing care and help identify areas for quality improvement. Further research should evaluate additional measurement properties, particularly test–retest reliability and responsiveness, before the instrument is used to assess changes over time or the effects of specific interventions.
