Ubiquity and Dominance of Comammox Over AOB and AOA and their Links To ARGs in Antarctic Lake Sediments
Authors: Mengjie Guo, Zeming Zhou, Yanling Zheng, Dongsheng Fu, Lijun Hou, Renbin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplete ammonia oxidizers (comammox), oxidizing ammonia to nitrate directly, have been found to exist widely in multiple environments, but their distribution patterns are still under-explored in Antarctic environments. For the first time, the sediments were collected from West Antarctic lakes to investigate distribution patterns and community structure for comammox, ammonia oxidizing archaea (AOA) and bacteria (AOB), and nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (NOB), as well as the associations between ammonia oxidizers and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). Comammox clade B and AOB were dominant ammonia oxidizers, with the abundances of (1.62 ± 0.10) × 102 – (5.21 ± 0.74) × 106 and (0.17 ± 0.05) × 105 – (4.79 ± 0.65) × 105 copies g− 1 sediment, respectively. Comammox clade B, instead of clade A, occurred in all sediments, exhibiting higher abundances than AOB and AOA in most of the sediments. The abundances for comammox clade B demonstrated significant positive correlation (p < 0.01) with NH4+-N levels, but negative correlation (p < 0.05) with C: N ratios. The coexistence of ammonia oxidizers in lake sediments was jointly structured by niche differentiation and environmental variables, and pH, modulated by penguin guano input, was found to be the most crucial factor in shaping their community structure. Co-occurrence network analyses revealed strong synergistic interactions between comammox and AOB, AOA, NOB, which played a critical role in nitrification processes. Our results further confirmed that comammox could act as important hosts for ARGs, hence stimulated their transmission and proliferation in the sediments. This study presented novel insights into the distribution patterns for ammonia oxidizers, their niche differentiation and the associations with ARGs in natural lake sediments of West Antarctica.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-025-02664-4.Keywords: Comammox, Ammonia oxidizer, ARGs, Antarctica, Lake sediment, Nitrification

Introduction

Nitrification, a microbial process converting ammonia into nitrate, is an important link in the nitrogen cycle, serving as a vital oxidative bridge between nitrogen fixation and denitrification processes in environments [1]. For over one century, nitrification has been regarded as a two-step process involved in the oxidation of ammonia into nitrite by ammonia-oxidizing microorganisms, and subsequent oxidation of nitrite into nitrate by nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (NOB) [2]. In nitrification, ammonia oxidation is mediated by ammonia oxidizing archaea (AOA) and bacteria (AOB) as the first rate-limiting step [3]. Recently, complete ammonia oxidizers (comammox) have been identified, which overturned long-held conception of nitrification [4, 5]. Comammox has been documented in various environments, including soil [6], river and lake water and sediment [7–9], tidal flats [10], and estuaries [11]. However, there is a notable lack of reports on comammox in extreme Antarctic environments.

Comammox oxidizes ammonia directly to nitrate via nitrite because their genomes contain a complete set of genes encoding enzymes for both ammonia and nitrite oxidation [4, 5]. Comammox exhibit high growth yields following a low specific growth rate in oligotrophic and dynamical habitats due to strong ammonia affinity [12, 13]. The known comammox species do not form a monophyletic clade within lineage II of the Nitrospira in phylogenetic trees according to 16 S rRNA genes or nxrB (the gene encoding the β-subunit of nitrite oxidoreductase). Instead, they are interspersed with the NOB species in the phylogenetic trees [4, 5, 14]. In complex microbial communities, the amoA gene is an ideal phylogenetic biomarker for identifying and quantifying comammox bacteria, and phylogenetic analysis of amoA gene has shown that comammox bacteria are the sister group for AOB [4, 14, 15]. To date, four comammox Nitrospira species, including Candidatus Nitrospira nitrosa, kreftii, nitrificans, and inopinata, have been reported [4, 5, 13, 16]. Generally, comammox Nitrospira was divided into clades A and B, and clade A could be further subdivided into clades A.1, A.2 and A.3 [2, 17]. These clades exhibited niche partitioning in different habitats due to effects of environmental variables [18]. Nevertheless, the distribution patterns and community structure for comammox remain to be fully explored in Antarctic environments.

In Antarctica, the biogeochemical cycles are exclusively driven by microbes in its terrestrial habitats due to extreme environmental conditions and scarce resources [19, 20]. Nitrogen is the main limiting factor in the Antarctic ecosystems, therefore research into microorganisms involved in nitrogen cycle is crucial [21, 22]. Recent studies have examined nitrogen transformation processes in Antarctic soils, including nitrogen fixation [23], nitrification [24], denitrification [25], and dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA) [23–28]. The heterogeneous spatial distribution for AOB and AOA communities in terrestrial soils was driven by sea animals [24], indicating that their activities had important effects on soil nitrification process in Antarctica. For terrestrial soils or lake sediments in East Antarctica, recent research has emphasized the significant role played by comammox Nitrospira in nitrification, and its remarkable adaptation strategies to the Antarctic environments [29]. Comammox Nitrospira with its unique physiological and ecological characteristics is able to efficiently participate in nitrification process under extreme Antarctic conditions, making it a key player in maintaining the nitrogen balance.

On the other hand, ammonia oxidizers have the potential to be important hosts for emerging contaminants, such as antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) [30], which can be disseminated across different environments and microorganisms via horizontal gene transfer (HGT), which was mediated by mobile genetic elements (MGEs), such as plasmids, integrons, and insertion sequences [31, 32]. ARGs can also be disseminated in fertilized soils during nitrification via vertical gene transfer (VGT) [33]. In wastewater treatment systems and activated sludge, the abundances and metabolic activities for ammonia oxidizers have been confirmed to be associated with the dynamics of ARGs [32, 34–36]. ARGs may also regulate the functional gene expression, including amoA in comammox and AOB [30, 31, 37]. High enrichment for ARGs occurred in Antarctic lake sediments affected by penguin guano input [38]. Despite the well-documented links between ammonia oxidizers and ARGs in the environment, the contribution of ammonia oxidizers, especially the newly discovered comammox, to the dissemination of ARGs remains to be elucidated in these extremely vulnerable Antarctic ecosystems. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate their relationship to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the ecological risks posed by the dissemination of ARGs in Antarctica.

Numerous shallow lakes are a prominent feature of the ice-free Antarctic areas, receiving runoff from a variety of sources, such as local snowmelt, surrounding streams, thawing permafrost and precipitation [39]. Additionally, large amounts of penguin guano is excreted into tundra ecosystems during nesting and breeding, and ultimately transported into lakes via ice-snow meltwater [40, 41]. Penguin guano deposition strongly affects sediment biogeochemical properties, and forms ornithogenic lake sediments (OLS), which are particularly rich in phosphorus and nitrogen [38]. However, the ecological niche for comammox and interactions with other ammonia oxidizers remain largely unexplored in these lake sediments. Therefore, it is essential to conduct in-depth research into distribution patterns and characteristics of ammonia oxidizers in Antarctic lake sediments, which is also indispensable for achieving a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of nitrogen cycle.

Sediment samples were collected from seven lakes of West Antarctica to investigate distribution patterns and community structure for ammonia oxidizers, including comammox, AOB and AOA, and their affecting factors. Specifically, main objectives of this study were: (1) to investigate the distribution patterns and community structure for comammox and other ammonia oxidizers in Antarctic lake sediments; (2) to explore the ecological niche preferences for ammonia oxidizers, and their relationship with ARGs; (3) to discuss environmental factors influencing distribution patterns and community structure for ammonia oxidizers. This study could enhance our understanding to distribution patterns, community structure and niche preferences for ammonia oxidizers in extremely cold Antarctic lakes.

Materials and Methods

Study Areas and Sample Collection

Our study areas were situated on the Fildes Peninsula (62°11′S, 58°58′W) and Ardley Island (62°13′S, 58°56′W) of West Antarctica (Fig. 1). Fildes Peninsula is the largest ice-free area nearby (about 29 km2), and it is connected to Ardley Island (1.2 km2) via a sand bar, which is exposed during low tide [42]. This peninsula has a cold oceanic climate with average annual temperature of − 2.2 °C, and summer temperature above 0 °C [43–45]. Both Fildes Peninsula and Ardley Island have gentle topography, with a broad central plain as their main landscape [46]. The study areas contain many lakes, and are among Antarctic regions with the strongest human activity [39], and the majority of these lakes formed in glacial basins [47]. However, almost all lakes on Ardley Island were affected by penguin activity, resulting in the formation of ornithogenic lake sediments (OLS) rich in P and N [40, 41]. Most lakes on Fildes Peninsula have remained unaffected by penguin activity [48], and their sediments are referred to as non-ornithogenic lake sediments (NOLS).

During the 35th Antarctic Research Expedition of China in 2018, sediment samples were collected from seven lakes, including Long Lake (LL) and Yanou Lake (YO) on Fildes Peninsula, and G Lake (GL), Y2 Lake (Y2), Y3 Lake (Y3), Y4 Lake (Y4) and Yueya Lake (YY) on Ardley Island. Three sampling sites were established at different locations within the shallow littoral zone of each lake. At each site, the 5–10 cm of surface sediments were sampled using a sterilized shovel, and immediately stored in a sterile sampling bag. All samples were divided into three parts: the first part was stored at 4 °C and used for the analyses of physicochemical properties; the second and third parts were immediately stored at -80 °C and used for the analyses of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), and for the analyses of gene abundances and community structures of ammonia oxidizers, respectively. All analyses were completed within one month after sampling.

Analyses To Sediment Geochemical Properties

The NH4+-N and NO3--N concentrations were analyzed through a continuous-flow analyzer (Skalar, the Netherlands) after the fresh sediment samples were extracted with 1 M KCl [49]. In addition, the sediment samples were freeze-dried, and sieved through 200 mesh (75 μm) for subsequent geochemical analyses. Total carbon (TC), nitrogen (TN) and sulfur (TS) levels were measured using a Vario MACRO CNS analyzer (Elementar, Germany) [25, 50]. Total organic matter (TOM) levels were quantified by measuring mass difference between the samples before and after incineration in muffle furnace under 550 °C for 2 h [51]. The sediment samples were digested with the mixture of HNO3-HCl-HF-HClO4 at 190 °C, and total phosphorus (TP) and trace elements, including Cu, Zn, Sr, Ag, Cd, Ba and Pb, were determined by ICP‒OES (PerkinElmer 2100DV; Waltham, MA, USA) [52]. The accuracy of these elements was verified using the Standard Reference Material (GBW07406). The lithogenic element compositions (SiO2, TiO2, Al2O3, Fe2O3, MgO, MnO and CaO) were determined using X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (ZSX Primus II XRF). The accuracy was estimated to be better than ± 2% based on the results of replicate and reference materials.

DNA Extraction and Quantitative real-time PCR

Total genomic DNA was extracted from 0.15 g of lake sediments according to the protocol for Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek). The extracted DNA was eluted in 80 µL of elution buffer, yielding the final volume of 70 µL for collection. DNA integrity was verified by electrophoresis via 1.5% (wt/vol) agarose gel. DNA samples in the sediments (n = 3) were obtained for each lake, and analyzed individually to check the quality and quantity using a Micro Spectrophotometer (Merinton Instrument, Inc.). For all samples, the DNA concentrations ranged from 20.6 to 96.9 ng µL− 1, and the A260/A280 ratios ranged from 1.37 to 1.67 (Table S2).

The gene abundances of ammonia oxidizers were determined in all samples using a LightCycler480 II Fluorescence qPCR Instrument (Switzerland). The primers CA377f and C576r were used for comammox clade A amoA gene [53], CB377f and C576r for comammox clade B amoA gene [53], arch-amoAF and arch-amoAR for AOA amoA gene [54], and amoA-1 F and amoA-2R for AOB amoA gene [55]. SYBR Green I-based real-time PCR assays were performed in a volume of 10 µL, with 5 µL of SybrGreen qPCR Master Mix, 0.2 µL of each primer (10 µM), 1 µL of 10-fold diluted DNA template and 3.6 µL of double-distilled H2O (ddH2O). PCR cycle conditions were 3 min at 95 °C, 45 cycles with 15 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 60 °C based on instrument guidelines. All samples were measured in triplicate, and the qPCR amplification specificity was determined using melting curve analysis. Standard curves were produced using 10-fold dilutions of the known-copies plasmid solution with the targeting genes. The consistency for qPCR assay was confirmed according to strong inverse linear relationship (R2 ≥ 0.99 for all genes) between the threshold cycle (CT) and the log value of gene copy number for the primer sets. The amplification efficiencies of AOA, AOB, comammox clade A and clade B amoA were 87.5%, 96.8%, 79.4% and 97.9%, respectively. Under these optimized conditions, the qPCR assays exhibited high precision with the coefficient of variation (CV%) of < 3.8% for all technical replicates (Table S3), indicating no evident PCR inhibition. In all experiments, negative controls were used to check and exclude potential contamination or carryover. The specific primers are provided in Supplementary Information Table S1.

High-throughput Sequencing and Phylogenetic Analysis

Comammox, AOB and AOA amoA and Nitrospira nxrB genes were amplified by PCR with corresponding primers listed in Table S1. The PCR products were then sequenced on Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform using a NextSeq 1000/2000 P1 Reagents (600 Cycles) kit, with the service provided by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The raw sequences obtained were analysed using QIIME2 [56]. The paired-end data for raw sequence were demultiplexed, and quality filtered using Cutadapt through q2-cutadapt [57]. The sequences were then trimmed, denoised, jointed and summarized into features [58]. The feature table was subjected to de novo clustering with no reference based on the representative sequences using the q2-vsearch plugin, and the sequences with 97% similarity were classified as operational taxonomic units (OTUs). The obtained OTU sequences were considered valid for phylogenetic analysis after the quality control, and were confirmed as the target genes by BLASTN comparison against the NCBI nucleotide database (high identity and low E-values). Taxonomic classification of all functional gene OTUs (comammox, AOB and AOA amoA and Nitrospira nxrB) was performed using BLAST similarity searches against reference sequences obtained from the NCBI GenBank database. Phylogenetic tree was built from the nucleotide alignment using neighbor-joining method with MEGA software (version 5.03). The 1000 bootstrap replications were conducted to verify the stability of phylogenetic tree topology [59].

High-throughput Quantitative PCR Analysis for ARGs in the Sediments

In order to elucidate the relationship of ammonia oxidizers with ARGs, the data about the abundance and composition of ARGs and MGEs used in this study were retrieved from Zhou et al. (2024) [38], where the abundance and composition of ARGs in Antarctic lake sediment samples were detected by high-throughput qPCR. Total 295 primer sets were used to detect all major classes of ARGs (283 primer sets), integron-integrase genes (4 primer sets), transposase genes (8 primer sets).

Statistical Analyses

Physicochemical properties, microbial abundances and diversity indices were analyzed using Origin 2021b. The correlations of geochemical properties with microbial abundances were examined by Pearson correlation, and their correlation figures and confidence intervals were made using Origin 2021b. Pearson correlation was also made to show the relationship between the abundances for functional genes and ARGs. The richness estimator Chao1, Shannon, Inverse Simpson, and Pielou indices were calculated by the R language (version 4.4.0). Specifically, the Chao1, Shannon, Inverse Simpson indices were computed using the vegan package (version 2.6-6). Pielou’s evenness index (J) was calculated as J = H/ln(S), where H and S are the Shannon-Wiener index and the number of observed OTUs, respectively. Differences in geochemical properties, microbial abundances, and diversity indices among groups (e.g., between OLS and NOLS, or across different lakes) were assessed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Following a significant ANOVA result, Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons to identify specific group differences. Both the statistical analysis and the corresponding plots with significance letters were generated using the Paired Comparison Plot tool in Origin 2021b. Based on the combined data from all 21 sediment samples, co-occurrence networks of ammonia oxidizers, Nitrospira bacteria and ammonia oxidizers-ARG subtypes were constructed via Pearson correlation analysis using the R language (version 4.4.0). To ensure the reliability of the network connections, stringent filtering criteria were applied, and only correlations with an absolute Pearson correlation coefficient r > 0.6 (p < 0.05) were retained for network inference. The resulting correlation matrix was then used to construct the network using the igraph package in the R environment, and the network was visualized using Gephi 0.10.1. Redundancy (RDA) and canonical correlation analyses (CCA) were made using CANOCO Version 5 for Windows to show correlations among the abundances of ammonia oxidizers, amoA OTUs and physicochemical properties in the sediments.

Results

Geochemical Properties for the Lake Sediments

The concentrations for total carbon (TC), total nitrogen (TN) and total phosphorus (TP) were significantly higher (p < 0.05) in ornithogenic lake sediments (OLS) on Ardley Island than in non-ornithogenic sediments (NOLS) on Fildes Peninsula (Supplementary Information Table S4). The mean contents of total sulphur (TS, 0.21 ± 0.14%) and total organic matter (TOM, 13.99 ± 5.00%), and C: N ratios (8.31 ± 0.99) were higher in the OLS than in the NOLS, although their differences were almost not significant. All lake sediments exhibited weakly acidic conditions with significantly lower pH values in the OLS (5.40–6.00) than in the NOLS (5.88–6.90). The NH4+-N content ranged from 0.20 to 7.90 mg kg− 1, with the lowest and highest levels in the Y2 (0.33 ± 0.06 mg kg− 1) and Y3 (6.67 ± 1.64 mg kg− 1) lake sediments, respectively. The mean NH₄⁺-N content was higher in the OLS (2.99 ± 2.97 mg kg− 1) than in the NOLS (1.94 ± 0.63 mg kg− 1). The NO₃⁻-N contents ranged from 0.10 to 1.60 mg kg− 1 in the sediments, which was much lower than the NH4+-N contents. The levels for Cu, Zn, Sr, Ag and Cd in the sediments of Lakes Y2 and Y3 were significantly higher (p < 0.05) than those in other lakes (Table S5). These metal elements also exhibited significantly higher levels in OLS than in NOLS (p < 0.05). The metal elements Ba, Pb and the lithogenic element compositions SiO2, Fe2O3 and MnO showed no significant variation in the OLS and NOLS (Table S6). However, the TiO2 and CaO contents were significantly higher in OLS than in NOLS, whereas the Al2O3 and MgO contents were significantly lower. Overall our results indicated that penguin guano deposition greatly altered geochemical properties (significantly enhanced TC, TN, TP and NH₄⁺-N levels, but decreased pH values) in the sediments.

Occurrence and Abundances for Comammox and Other Ammonia Oxidizers in Sediments

Comammox clade B amoA genes, instead of clade A amoA genes, were detected in all sediment samples, with the abundance range from 1.65 × 104 to 4.79 × 105 copies g− 1 sediment (Fig. 2). The highest abundances for comammox clade B amoA genes were found in Lake YY sediments (mean: (3.65 ± 1.10) × 105 copies g− 1 sediment), significantly higher (p < 0.05) than those in the sediments of Lakes YO, G, Y2 and Y4. The lowest gene abundances occurred in the Lake Y2 sediments (mean: (3.77 ± 3.02) × 104 copies g− 1 sediment), significantly lower (p < 0.05) than those in the sediments of Lakes YY and Y3. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference (p > 0.05) in comammox clade B amoA gene abundances between OLS and NOLS. This indicated that comammox clade B was widely present in Antarctic lake sediments. AOB amoA genes were detected in almost all lake sediments except Lake Y2. However, the gene abundances varied greatly, with the range of 1.62 × 102 to 5.21 × 106 copies g− 1 sediment. The AOA amoA gene was only detected in the sediments of Lakes YO (n = 3), G (n = 1) and Y3 (n = 1), with much lower abundances (< 8.92 × 102 copies g− 1 sediment) than the comammox and AOB amoA genes. Overall, the mean proportion of comammox clade B amoA genes relative to total amoA gene abundances of ammonia oxidizers amounted to as high as 57.01% in all lake sediments. Comammox clade B dominated ammonia oxidizers (> 50%), particularly in the sediments of Lakes LL, YO, Y2 and YY, indicating a competitive dominance over AOB and AOA in the sediments.

Biodiversity and Community Structure for Ammonia Oxidizers in Sediments

For comammox communities, Chao1 indexes were significantly higher in NOLS than in OLS (p < 0.05, Fig. 3). Significantly enhanced Inverse Simpson, Shannon and Pielou indices had been observed in the sediments of Lakes YO and G. However, no significant differences in these diversity indices were observed between other lake sediments (p > 0.05). There were also no statistically significant differences in Chao1, Shannon, and Inverse Simpson indices of AOA communities between NOLS and OLS (p > 0.05, Table S6). The Pielou evenness indexes for AOA communities in OLS were significantly higher than those in NOLS (p < 0.05). For AOB communities, the diversity indices exhibited no significant differences between OLS and NOLS (p > 0.05), although almost all diversity indices for AOB communities were slightly higher in OLS than in NOLS. In addition, average Chao1, Shannon and Inverse Simpson indices for comammox and AOB were generally higher than those for AOA, but almost no significant differences in these diversity indices (p > 0.05) were found among comammox, AOB and AOA communities in all sediment samples.

A total of 747,866 valid comammox amoA gene sequences were obtained, and total 80 valid comammox amoA OTUs were used for phylogenetic analysis, with a 97% similarity to allow comparison and classification (Fig. 4a). Almost all OTUs belonging to comammox clade B, rather than clade A, were detected, indicating that clade B had an absolute advantage in comammox community. The OTU1 accounted for 71.19% of all obtained sequences, but its phylogenetic clade remains unique, with no documented species sharing significant sequence similarity. The sequence counts of OTU1 showed no significant difference between OLS and NOLS, but the counts were significantly higher in Lake LL than in Lakes YO, Y4 and YY (Fig. S1). A total of 11 OTUs were related to metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) Nitrospira sp. CG24A, accounting for 7.56% of all valid gene sequences, and 7 OTUs (2.73%) and 6 OTUs (1.39%) were related to MAGs Nitrospira sp. RSF6 and Nitrospira sp. RCB, respectively. Furthermore, 3 OTUs (0.32%) clustered with Nitrospira sp. sequences isolating from wetland soils (MT409087), mangrove mudflat sediments and Spartina alterniflora rhizosphere (MT133500). 12 OTUs (1.64%) were found to be closely related to sequences derived from the overlying water of high-elevation rivers (MN011851), a habitat characterized with oligotrophic conditions and low levels of anthropogenic disturbance. 7 OTUs (13.17%) formed a clade with Nitrospira sp. sequences from New Zealand soil (ON469867), while 6 OTUs (0.47%) clustered with Nitrospira sp. sequences from pasture (OQ604611) and forest (MF347289) soils, indicating their adaptation to terrestrial ecosystems with varying nutrient conditions.

In addition, AOA and AOB amoA genes were detected in the sediments, with 164,052 and 888,203 valid sequences, respectively. A total of 32 OTUs for AOB amoA gene (Fig. 4c), and 24 OTUs for AOA amoA gene (Fig. 4d) were obtained for phylogenetic analysis, with 97% sequence similarity. The AOB OTUs were all members of the β-Proteobacteria Nitrosomonadaceae family, with 23 OTUs belonging to Nitrosompira sp., accounting for 86.35% of the AOB community, and 9 OTUs Nitrosomonas sp. (13.65%). Among the AOA amoA genes, 16 OTUs were identified as Nitrososphaera sp., accounting for 98.75% of the AOA community, and 8 OTUs were identified as Candidatus Nitrosocosmomicus sp. (1.25%). All of these OTUs belonged to the order Nitrososphaerales (phylum Nitrososphaerota), and the group I.1b cluster.

A total of 283 valid nxrB OTUs were detected through high-throughput sequencing of 426,724 functional Nitrospira nxrB genes, with a defined nucleotide difference of < 3%. A total of 66 OTUs were selected for Nitrospira phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 4b). These OTUs contained 425,749 nxrB gene sequences, accounting for 99.77% of all sequences, and each OTU accounted for ≥ 0.01%. The 52 OTUs belonged to Nitrospira lineage II, accounting for 58.48% of total Nitrospira sequences, while the rest were Candidatus Nitrospira defluvii (36.16%), Ca. N. bockiana (5.31%), and Ca. N. marina (0.04%). Comammox Nitrospira all belonged to Nitrospira lineage II and comammox clade B, accounting for 14.74% of the total Nitrospira, with the range of 0.07% – 43.21% (Fig. S2). They did not appear to outcompete the NOB of the Nitrospira community in Antarctic lake sediments.

Co-occurrence Network of Ammonia Oxidizers and Nitrospira in Sediments

In the co-occurrence network among AOA, AOB and comammox clade B, a total of 131 nodes were identified, forming 1,320 interaction links, 99.70% of which were positive (Fig. 5a). Almost all correlations were positive among AOA, AOB and comammox clade B, with only a negligible number of negative correlations. The majority of comammox clade B OTUs exhibited strong positive correlation with each other. The AOA OTU7 (0.36% of all AOA) and the AOB OTU6 (1.96% of all AOB) belonged to group I.1b Nitrososphaera cluster and Nitrosompira sp., respectively, which were closely and positively correlated with a large number of comammox clade B OTUs. The AOA OTU1, 2, 6, 14 and 17 were also closely and positively correlated with comammox clade B OTUs. The correlations among comammox OTUs were stronger than those between comammox OTUs and either AOA or AOB OTUs.

Within the network of the Nitrospira community (comammox clade B and NOB), a total of 66 nodes produced 552 interaction connections, with 90.58% being positive and 9.42% negative (Fig. 5b). Co-occurrence network analysis of Nitrospira revealed a significant positive association between comammox clade B and NOB. Almost all comammox nxrB OTUs were positively correlated with lineage II NOB, except for Nitrospira nxrB OTU4, which belongs to comammox clade B and accounts for 5.99% of analyzed Nitrospira. This OTU showed a negative correlation with N. bockiana, which belongs to NOB. Our results indicated that the co-existent and complex relationship existed among the comammox clade B, AOA and AOB, with potential cooperation during the nitrification processes in the shallow Antarctic lake sediments. Meanwhile, comammox clade B generally exhibited positive correlations with most of NOB within the Nitrospira community.

Correlations and co-occurrence of Ammonia Oxidizers with ARGs

Pearson correlation analyses revealed that the abundances for comammox clade B amoA genes exhibited positive correlations with several ARG classes (aminoglycoside, sulfonamide, β-lactamase, tetracycline and macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin B (MLSB)) and transposases (Fig. 6a). Specifically, the abundances for comammox showed significant positive correlations with sulfonamide (r = 0.881, p < 0.01) and tetracycline (r = 0.782, p < 0.05). In contrast, the abundances of AOB and AOA amoA genes showed weaker correlations with ARG classes. Notably, AOA amoA gene abundance displayed consistent negative correlations with almost all ARGs and MGEs, and especially significant negative correlations were obtained for MLSB (r = -0.814, p < 0.05).

In the co-occurrence network for ammonia oxidizers, ARGs and MGEs, a total of 244 nodes were identified, and 11,022 interaction links were formed, with 99.98% of the links being positive (Fig. 6b), which encompassed more nodes and interaction links, and featured a higher proportion of positive correlations, compared with co-occurrence network among ammonia oxidizers (Fig. 5a), suggesting that ARGs and MGEs enhanced the network complexity. Meanwhile, strong positive correlation of ammonia oxidizers with ARGs and MGEs (p < 0.05) implied that ARGs had an important impact on community structure and functions for ammonia oxidizers, and tended to facilitate their synergistic interactions. Specifically, the 25 OTUs of comammox clade B and only 7 OTUs of AOB were closely associated with the subtypes of major ARG classes, including multidrug, chloramphenicol, aminoglycoside, vancomycin, sulfonamide, β-lactamase, tetracycline, and MLSB, as well as the two integron subtypes (intI3 and intI-1LC), indicating that comammox clade B might have stronger effects on the distribution and dissemination of ARGs compared to AOB, and played more critical roles in ecological association of ammonia oxidizers with ARGs in lake sediments.

Discussion

Ubiquity of Comammox in Antarctic Lake Sediments

In the lake sediments of West Antarctica, comammox and AOB were abundant types of ammonia oxidizers, with comammox being more widely present (Fig. 2). However, the abundances for comammox amoA genes were relatively lower, compared with those in lake sediments of East Antarctica, the wetlands in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, and some urban lakes, but slightly higher than that in the sediments of the Qinghai Tibet Plateau rivers (Table S9). In this study, comammox amoA gene was detected in all sediments, whereas AOA amoA gene was detected only in Lakes YO, G and Y3 with much lower abundances than comammox and AOB. Comammox bacteria tend to thrive in microaerobic and oligotrophic settings [60, 61]. In riparian ecosystems, higher comammox abundances and activities occurred in fringe soils/sediments of riparian zone, compared to other sites, and their abundances were higher than those of AOA and AOB [60]. Generally, the metabolic cycles of AOB (CBB cycle) and AOA (Thaumarchaeal HP/HB cycle) were oxygen-tolerant, whereas the metabolic cycle of comammox bacteria (reductive rTCA cycle) has mainly been discovered in environments with low oxygen levels due to the oxygen sensitivity of their key enzymes ( [62–64]. Comammox might possess higher affinity for oxygen, making them more adaptable to low oxygen concentrations [12]. Furthermore, the sediments were weakly acidic in West Antarctic lakes (Table S4), and comammox Nitrospira has been noted to predominate under acidic conditions [65–67]. Multiple acid-tolerance-related genes, including TC.SSS, HypC/D, KdpA/D, and PdaD, detected in the recovered comammox Nitrospira genomes, are thought to enhance their ability to survive under weakly acidic conditions [68]. Therefore, our results suggested that the sediments in the shallow lakes of West Antarctica, which are confirmed to be weakly acidic and likely have relatively low dissolved oxygen levels, might create a favorable environment conducive to the ubiquitous growth and widespread presence of comammox.

In all sediments of Antarctic lakes, comammox clade B, rather than clade A, was detected. Similar results had also been obtained in the lake sediments of East Antarctica (Table S9), with comammox clade B as main driver for nitrification [29]. Comammox clade B is dominant over clade A in some habitats, accounting for 96% of total comammox in forest soils and over 40% in plateau and paddy soils, and river sediments [2]. For comammox clade A, Ca. N. nitrosa and nitrificans were enriched at 23 °C, whereas Ca. N. kreftii and inopinata were enriched at 25 °C and 46 °C, respectively [4, 5]. However, comammox clade B exhibits stronger freeze-thaw tolerance, leading to its greater abundance in low-temperature environments compared to clade A [69]. In maritime Antarctica, the mean summertime temperature (about 4 °C) is relatively low, which might be not suitable for the survival of comammox clade A. Additionally, pH and/or ammonium availability might be niche defining parameters between comammox clades A and B [67, 70–72]. Comammox clade B preferred substrates with low and fluctuating NH4+ levels [60, 73], whereas clade A thrived in high-ammonium environments [74, 75]. Lower pH increased ammonium availability, thus was favourable for the clade B growth in particular due to its high ammonium affinity [76, 77]. For example, comammox clade B was detected only in agricultural soils of New Zealand because of lower pH values than other soils [70, 78]. Therefore, slightly acidic environments were favourable for wide existence of comammox clade B in Antarctic lake sediments.

On the other hand, genomic investigations have shown that comammox clade B genomes could encode MEP-type ammonia transporters with the feature similarly identified in AOA [76, 79], which had high ammonium affinity but low uptake capacity [80]. Comammox clade B had an extra non-operon amoC gene, contributing to its acclimation to low nitrosative stress and ammonia limitation [76, 77, 81]. Consequently, comammox clade B was more adaptable to sedimentary environments in shallow Antarctic lakes, such as low temperature, low NH4+-N levels and acidic environments, compared to comammox clade A.

Coexistence of Comammox with AOA, AOB and NOB in Sediments

In the sediments of West Antarctic lakes, the synergistic effects among comammox, AOA and AOB have been found in the nitrification based upon co-occurrence network analyses, and furthermore stronger positive correlations occurred between comammox clade B and AOA than between comammox clade B and AOB (Fig. 5), which might be related to their niche separation. Comammox can derive energy from ammonia and nitrite oxidation [12, 82]. Generally, comammox and canonical Nitrospira directly competed with other nitrifiers (AOA, AOB and NOB), leading to different selective niches [83]. In our study, comammox clade B, AOB and AOA might exhibit distinctive ecological niche preferences, and even greater niche differences might occur between comammox and AOA, which were further modulated by environmental variables in the sediments. The abundances for AOA amoA genes were strongly constrained by low pH, whereas AOB amoA gene abundance was primarily influenced by TC and TOM (Fig. 7). The C: N ratios in different types of soils were an important variable affecting comammox distribution patterns [84–86], and the abundances for comammox clade B amoA genes significantly increased when soil C: N ratios decreased [87, 88], which was in accordance with our results. In addition, the abundances for comammox clade B were limited by NH4+-N (Fig. 7). The TC, NH₄⁺-N, TOM content and C: N ratios in sediments were significantly influenced by penguin guano input. The niche separation of comammox Nitrospira and canonical ammonia oxidizers also occurred in the soils subjected to long-term nitrogen deposition [87]. Overall, our results indicated that niche separation and environmental variables, affected by penguin guano input, jointly structured the coexistence for ammonia oxidizers in Antarctic lake sediments (Fig. 9).

The ecophysiological versatility also supported the cooperative associations between comammox and NOB within the Nitrospira community [76, 89, 90]. Comammox and NOB could coexist and mainly had a cooperative relationship in the sediments (Fig. 5). Generally, comammox and NOB could stably coexist in natural and engineered ecosystems via genetic niche differentiation and ecophysiological versatility, thereby enabling cross-feeding interactions [7, 13, 76, 89, 90]. In contrast to previous studies where Nitrospira community interaction links were dominated by competitive relationships (89.9%) in riparian ecosystems [60] and cooperative relationships (66%) in coastal wetlands [91], our results revealed notably higher proportion of cooperative interactions (90.58%) within the Nitrospira community, highlighting the unique ecological dynamics of Nitrospira in Antarctic lake sediments, where cooperative associations might play a pivotal role in maintaining nitrification processes under extremely environmental conditions.

Interactions between Ammonia Oxidizers and ARGs in Antarctic Lake Sediments

There existed complex ecological associations between ammonia oxidizers and ARGs in the sediment of West Antarctica lakes. Overall, our results suggested that ammonia oxidizers had a synergistic symbiotic relationship with ARGs (Fig. 6). In particular, there was a closer synergistic association between comammox clade B and ARGs, and comammox clade B might be a key participant related to the proliferation of ARGs, which collectively emphasized the complexity of microbial functional relationships under Antarctic conditions. Comammox clade B was widely present in Antarctic lakes and coexisted synergistically with other nitrifying microorganisms, which might make it a hub for the transmission and proliferation of ARGs. In this study, significant positive correlations of comammox clade B with sulfonamide (p < 0.01) and tetracycline (p < 0.05), and close positive correlation of the OTUs for comammox clade B and AOB with various ARG subtypes and integrons (intI3 and intI-1LC) (Fig. 6), indicated that comammox clade B and AOB might be potential hosts of ARGs, among which comammox clade B was more dominant. It has been reported that OP8, TM7, Firmicutes, Nitrospirae and Cyanobacteria were potential dominant hosts for most of ARGs in the sediments of Antarctic lakes [38]. Comammox Nitrospira belonged to phylum Nitrospirae, thus our results further confirmed that comammox clade B was potential dominant host for ARGs. It was also found that Nitrospira had a significant correlation with ARGs (qnrS) in sludge treatment [92], which provided cross-environmental evidence for the association between comammox clade B and ARGs.

In addition, penguin guano input not only indirectly modulated the distribution and community structure of ammonia oxidizers but also enhanced the abundance and horizontal transfer potential of ARGs [38], which potentially reinforced the linkage between ammonia oxidizers and ARGs in the lake sediments. The synergistic interaction between AOB and other nitrifiers might also increase the horizontal transfer of ARGs, thus the transmission of ARGs [93–95]. The selective pressure of antibiotics, which is a major driver for the enrichment and propagation of ARGs, had been shown to stimulate the formation of entrenched microbial communities, such as comammox and AOB, with complex community-specific interactions [92, 96]. Our study indicated that comammox clade B and AOB played crucial roles in the ARGs transmission and proliferation in Antarctic lake sediments, and the widespread niche distribution of comammox and its synergistic interactions with other nitrifying microorganisms might have enhanced the potential to carry ARGs.

Distribution Patterns for Comammox and Other Nitrifiers Were Driven by NH4+-N Levels and pH in Sediments

The NH4+-N level was a key variable affecting the distribution patterns for comammox, AOB and AOA in lake sediments. As the primary substrate for nitrification, NH4+-N directly regulated the nitrifier growth [5, 86]. Comammox has the highest affinity for the substrate among almost all ammonia oxidizers [13, 97]. Kinetic analyses indicated that comammox had stronger affinity for NH4+-N (Ks ≈ 30–60 nM) [13, 16], compared to AOB (Ks ≈ 0.14–1.9 mM) [98], and AOA (Ks ≈ 0.13 µM) [99]. In our study, NH4+-N levels (0.33–6.67 mg kg− 1) were close to those in the sediments of East Antarctic lakes (0.65–5.96 mg kg− 1) [29]. The lowest NH4+-N levels occurred in the sediments of Lake Y2, even lower than the range that AOB could tolerate, and thus no AOB amoA gene was detected. Additionally, the abundances for comammox amoA genes exhibited significant positive correlation with NH₄⁺-N contents (p < 0.01), which was in accordance with previous studies [87, 100, 101], indicating that NH₄⁺-N levels might play a critical role in shaping distribution pattern for comammox. Several studies have shown significant negative correlation between comammox abundances and ammonium levels, and high levels for available ammonium could inhibit comammox clade A [8, 13, 16, 102]. Comammox clade B became increasingly abundant under no or low ammonium supplementation, but decreased significantly with 50 mg kg− 1 ammonium supplied [103], indicating that high NH₄⁺-N levels might inhibit the comammox clade B growth, but low levels might promote its growth. Consequently, low NH₄⁺-N levels had an important positive effect on comammox clade B in Antarctic lake sediments.

In addition, pH in sediments might be a driving factor for the abundances and community composition for comammox in different ecological niches [60, 102, 104, 105]. In this study, pH had no significant effects on the abundances for comammox clade B amoA gene. However, significant positive correlations between pH and the indices of Chao1, Shannon and Inverse Simpson for comammox community (p < 0.01) and high contribution of pH to the distribution of dominant comammox clade B OTUs (Fig. 7) indicated that the variability in the diversity and community structure for comammox was strongly associated with pH, which was modulated by penguin guano input into Antarctic sediments. On the other hand, pH also had an important impact on the occurrence of AOA and community structure for AOA and AOB (Fig. 8). The abundances for AOA amoA genes in West Antarctic lake sediments was much lower than those in East Antarctic lakes with divergent differences in community structure [29]. The pH of the overlying water on East Antarctic lake sediments ranged from 6.32 to 7.66, whereas the lake sediments, especially the OLS, are more acidic in our study areas due to penguin guano input (Table S4). Generally, soil AOA abundances showed significant positive correlations with pH in the range of 4.2–8.3, and even below the detection limit in acidic soils, but pH had no effect on AOB abundances [106]. Similar results also occurred in sludge-treated soils [107], upland red soils [108], and agricultural soils [109]. Therefore, lower pH might greatly decrease the AOA abundance in West Antarctic lake sediments, even making it undetectable, which might lead to much lower AOA abundances despite better adaptation to oligotrophic conditions than AOB [99].

Recently, several studies have shown that pH is one of main factors influencing community structures for both AOB and AOA, with their compositions significantly varying across different acid-base substrates [110–113]. In acidic soils with pH < 6.5, AOA are limited to specific clades, such as Ca. Nitrosotaleales (group I.1a-associated) and Nitrososphaerales (group I.1b), especially the clade closely related to the genus Nitrososphaera, whereas AOA in neutral (pH: 6.5–7.5) or alkaline (pH >7.5) soils showed greater diversity, and sparser phylogenetic affiliation [114]. Most of AOA detected in East Antarctic lake sediments belonged to terrestrial-adapted group I.1b cluster, more OTUs belonged to Candidatus Nitrosocosmomicus sp. than Nitrososphaera sp., and only a small amount of oceanic group I.1a was detected [29, 114]. The AOA detected in West Antarctic lake sediments all belonged to terrestrial-adapted group I.1b cluster, and more OTUs of Nitrososphaera sp. were detected than Candidatus Nitrosocosmomicus sp. Overall, pH emerged as the most crucial factor shaping community structures for AOA and AOB in Antarctic lake sediments.

Conclusions

For the first time, comammox clade B, rather than clade A, was found to exist widely in the shallow lake sediments of West Antarctica, indicating that comammox clade B more adapted to cold lake environments, compared to AOB and AOA. The pH was the most crucial factor shaping community structures for ammonia oxidizers in the sediments, and comammox clade B was better adapted to acidic conditions. The low NH₄⁺-N contents had significant positive effects (p < 0.01) on comammox clade B, and the abundances for comammox clade B amoA genes were significantly negatively correlated with C: N ratios (p < 0.05). The abundances for AOB amoA genes exhibited positive correlation with TC (p < 0.05) and TOM (p < 0.01). Comammox clade B, AOB and AOA had different ecological niche preferences, and their co-occurrence suggested that potential synergistic interactions might contribute to the nitrification process in the lake sediments. Meanwhile, the observed synergistic effect between comammox clade B and NOB within the Nitrospira community might contribute to the maintenance of nitrification processes under extreme environments. Notably, comammox clade B might have been potential hosts for ARGs in the sediments, indicated its important position in microbial-resistance gene interaction. Overall this study broadened our understanding of comammox abundance and community structure in Antarctic lake sediments, highlighting the critical synergistic interactions among comammox and other nitrifiers, as well as the associations between ammonia oxidizers and ARGs, alongside their unique responses to environmental variables, influenced by penguin guano input. This work provided a framework for understanding the distribution and interactions of key ammonia oxidizers in vulnerable Antarctic ecosystems, and offered new perspectives on ARGs interactions with ammonia oxidizers in extreme environments.
