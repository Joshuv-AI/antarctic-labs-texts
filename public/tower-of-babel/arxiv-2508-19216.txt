Momentum–mass normalized dark–bright solitons to one dimensional Gross–Pitaevskii systems
Salvador López-Martínez

Abstract
We rigorously establish the existence of dark-bright solitons as traveling wave solutions to a one dimensional defocusing Gross–Pitaevskii system, a widely used model for describing mixtures of Bose–Einstein condensates and nonlinear optical systems. These solitons are shown to exhibit symmetry and radial monotonicity in modulus, and to propagate at subsonic speed. Our method relies on minimizing an energy functional subject to two constraints: the mass of the bright component and a modified momentum of the dark component. The compactness of minimizing sequences is obtained via a concentration-compactness argument, which requires some novel estimates based on symmetric decreasing rearrangements.



Keywords:

Defocusing Schrödinger system, Gross–Pitaevskii system, dark-bright solitons, non standard conditions at infinity, concentration-compactness, symmetric decreasing rearrangements.

1
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to analyze the existence of solitonic solutions to the following system:

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}\Psi=\partial_{xx}\Psi+(1-|\Psi|^{2}-\alpha|\Phi|^{2})\Psi,&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
i\partial_{t}\Phi=\partial_{xx}\Phi-(\alpha|\Psi|^{2}+\beta|\Phi|^{2})\Phi,&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\Psi
and
\Phi
are complex-valued functions, and
\alpha,\beta
are real parameters. This system consists of two coupled nonlinear Schrödinger equations in one spatial dimension and is commonly referred to as the Gross-Pitaevskii system. This is a classical model for describing the dynamics of quasi-one dimensional Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs) consisting of a mixture of two different species
[
20
,
3
]
, as well as for the multi-mode propagation of pulses in nonlinear optical fibers
[
23
,
22
]
. The sign of
\alpha
determines the nature of the mutual interactions between species: for
\alpha>0
, the interaction is repulsive (defocusing), while for
\alpha<0
, it is attractive (focusing). Similarly,
\beta>0
corresponds to self-defocusing interactions in the second component, and
\beta<0
to self-focusing ones. The case
\beta=0
is special, as the second equation becomes linear in
\Phi
. In this regime, the system is sometimes referred to as the Gross–Clark system, which models the motion of an uncharged impurity (represented by the wave function
\Phi
) within a Bose-Einstein condensate
[
18
]
.

In both BECs and optics, coherent structures know as
solitons
have been experimentally observed. These are localized waves that maintain their shape while traveling at a constant velocity. Based on their qualitative properties, solitons are formally classified into different types. For instance, localized density bumps that vanish at infinity are called
bright solitons
, while localized density dips with nonzero background are referred to as
dark solitons
.

Solitonic pairs consisting of combinations of dark and bright components (and other structures such as antidark solitons or domain walls) have also been observed in mixtures of BECs and nonlinear optical systems, though their specific configurations depend on the nature of the internal and mutual interactions
[
21
]
. For instance, it is well known that bright solitons do not exist in the scalar
(\alpha=0)
defocusing
(\beta>0)
case. However, if both internal and mutual interactions are repulsive, i.e.
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
, then the existence of dark-bright soliton pairs is physically justified by a symbiotic relationship between the components, that is, the dark component effectively
supports
the bright one.

In this work, we focus on the mathematical analysis of dark-bright soliton pairs, i.e. solutions to (
1.1
) that satisfy the conditions at infinity:

|\Psi(x,\cdot)|\to 1,\quad|\Phi(x,\cdot)|\to 0,\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.

(1.2)

Under these conditions, we can define the
energy
functional, which is formally conserved along the flow:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{x}\Psi|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{x}\Phi|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-|\Psi|^{2})^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}|\Phi|^{4}+\frac{\alpha}{2}|\Phi|^{2}|\Psi|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(1.3)

where time dependence is implicit. The energy functional will play a fundamental role in our analysis. In fact, we will work in the functional framework that keeps the energy finite. This topic will be developed in next section.

The motion of the solitons at constant velocity while maintaining shape is achieved mathematically by solutions to (
1.1
) of the form:

\Psi(x,t)=u(x-ct),\quad\Phi(x,t)=e^{i((\lambda+c^{2}/4)t-cx/2)}v(x-ct),

for some
c,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, where
u
and
v
are complex-valued functions which represent
traveling waves
with velocity
c
. The equations for
u
and
v
are:

\begin{cases}icu^{\prime}+u^{\prime\prime}+(1-|u|^{2}-\alpha|v|^{2})u=0,&x\in\mathbb{R},\\
-v^{\prime\prime}=(\lambda-\alpha|u|^{2}-\beta|v|^{2})v,&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(1.4)

We keep imposing the asymptotic conditions:

|u(x)|\to 1,\quad|v(x)|\to 0,\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.

(1.5)

The system (
1.4
) with (
1.5
) is invariant under translations in
x
and the phase transformations
(u,v)\mapsto(e^{ik}u,e^{i\ell}v)
for any
k,\ell\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, by complex conjugation, we may assume without loss of generality that
c\geq 0
. Also, since the second equation in (
1.4
)
has only real coefficients, we will restrict our attention to solutions whose second component
v
is real-valued.

We now examine the particular case
\alpha=0
, in which the equations are decoupled. First of all, the second equation becomes

-v^{\prime\prime}=(\lambda-\beta v^{2})v,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.6)

The theory of bright solitons in the focusing case
\beta<0
is a classical subject and the references are extensive, we only refer to
[
9
]
. In contrast, the defocusing equation for
\beta\geq 0
admits a unique solution in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, which is
v=0
.

On the other hand, the first equation

icu^{\prime}+u^{\prime\prime}+(1-|u|^{2})u=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.7)

corresponds to the traveling wave profile equation for the defocusing Gross-Pitaevskii equation. It is well-known that, for every subsonic speed, i.e. for every
c\in[0,\sqrt{2})
, there exists a unique (up to translations and phase shift) finite-energy dark soliton solution to (
1.7
), which is explicit, and its stability properties have been extensively studied
[
25
,
13
,
5
,
6
,
19
]
. We provide more details on this subject in next section. For a general overview, see the survey
[
4
]
.

Of course, the pair
(u,0)
, where
u
is a nontrivial solution to (
1.7
), trivially satisfies (
1.4
) even for
\alpha\not=0
. We refer to such solutions as
semi-trivial
, while
trivial solutions
correspond to
(e^{ik},0)
for some constant
k\in\mathbb{R}
.

Significant efforts, coming mainly from the physics community, have been devoted to obtaining dark-bright solitons as solutions to the system (
1.1
) in the coupled case
\alpha\not=0
. However, much of the existing work relies on numerical methods or formal expansions
[
23
,
20
]
. Despite its obvious relevance for theoretical and experimental physics, a rigorous derivation of fully nontrivial dark-bright solitons as solutions to the Gross–Pitaevskii system is missing in the literature. To the best of our knowledge, only partial results are available. For instance, the case
\beta=0
has been dealt with in
[
30
,
2
]
. On the other hand, it is well known that the system (
1.4
) with the choice
\alpha=\beta=1
, referred to as Manakov system
[
28
]
, is integrable, and explicit fully nontrivial solutions exist
[
8
,
32
]
. However, the physically relevant range of parameters extends beyond the integrable case. A broader, though still limited, range for
\alpha
and
\beta
is considered in the recent work
[
29
]
, where semi-explicit dark-bright solitons are constructed via a Lagrangian variational method.

The main contribution of this work is an analytical proof of the existence of dark-bright soliton solutions to (
1.1
) in the whole defocusing regime, i.e. for any value of the parameters
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
. Although the solutions we obtain are not explicit, we show that they are symmetric and radially nonincreasing in modulus. Moreover, we will verify that they propagate at subsonic speed. Our approach relies on minimizing an energy functional under two constraints, one for the momentum of the dark component, and another one for the mass of the bright component, in the spirit of
[
2
]
. Arguing this way, the constants
c
and
\lambda
arise as Lagrange multipliers, one corresponding to each constraint.

The proof of the compactness of minimizing sequences is based on the well-known Concentration-Compactness principle of Lions
[
26
,
27
]
. This approach requires excluding the potential issues of
vanishing
and
dichotomy
. Ruling out dichotomy for functionals of more than one variable is not a trivial task, and the literature in this matter is limited. To address this challenge, the use of symmetric decreasing rearrangements (see Section
3
) has been proven effective. Indeed, a refined Pólya–Szegő inequality due to
[
14
]
was the key for establishing compactness for system of Klein–Gordon equations. This technique has also been successfully employed in various other contexts, always for solutions to systems whose both components are real-valued and vanish at infinity. These include nonlinear Schrödinger–Korteweg–de Vries systems
[
1
]
, focusing nonlinear Schrödinger systems
[
31
]
, and higher-dimensional problems
[
33
,
16
,
17
]
.

In the case of dark-bright solitons, the use of symmetric decreasing rearrangements presents a particular challenge, as the dark component does not vanish at infinity. To address this, nontrivial manipulations of the energy and the momentum of the dark component in terms of its lifting are required. The detailed formulation and the statements of the main results are provided in Section
2
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, Section
3
is devoted to the statements of well-known preliminary results on symmetric decreasing rearrangements. After that, Section
4
includes the proof of Theorem
2.3
. Next, Section
5
contains the proof of the compactness of minimizing sequences. Finally, we prove Theorem
2.4
in Section
6
.

2
Functional setting and main results

Let us define the energy space

\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})=\{u\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}):\,u^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}),\,1-|u|^{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C})\},

and the nonvanishing energy space

\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})=\{u\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}):\,|u|>0\}.

The fundamental properties of these spaces are derived in
[
15
]
. For instance, we stress that
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, and also that
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
contains oscillating functions at infinity, which in particular implies that the energy space is not a vector space, not even an affine space. On the other hand, every function in the nonvanishing energy space admits a lifting. More precisely, for every
u\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists
\theta\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\theta^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
u=\rho e^{i\theta}
in
\mathbb{R}
, where
\rho=|u|
. We will use this property and this notation repeatedly throughout the paper.

For
u=\rho e^{i\theta}\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})
, let us introduce the functional

p(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(|1-\rho|)\theta^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\text{where }\quad G(s)=s(2-s),\,s\geq 0.

(2.1)

Notice that
G
is increasing in
[0,1]
. Observe also that
G(|1-\rho|)
recasts as

G(|1-\rho|)=1-\rho^{2}+4\chi_{\{\rho>1\}}(\rho-1),

(2.2)

where
\chi
denotes the indicator function. Therefore, if
\rho\leq 1
in
\mathbb{R}
, it follows that
p(u)=Q(u)
, where
Q
is the standard renormalized momentum
[
4
]

Q(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho^{2})\theta^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle iu^{\prime},u\rangle\frac{1-|u|^{2}}{|u|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.3)

The sort of redefined momentum (
2.1
) is suitable for applying symmetric decreasing rearrangements, as can be verified in Section
4
. In any case, we will show that eventually the solutions we obtain have modulus smaller than one.

Let us define the sets

\mathcal{X}=\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}):\,|u|<2\},\quad\mathcal{X}_{q,m}=\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}:\,\,p(u)=q,\,\|v\|^{2}_{2}=m\},

for
q\geq 0
,
m\geq 0
. Notice that we consider only real-valued functions for the second component of the pairs in
\mathcal{X}
. We also stress that, if
u\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})
and
|u|<2
, then
|1-|u||\in(0,1)
, which is precisely the interval where
G
in (
2.1
) is increasing. This idea will be behind some proofs of Section
4
.

We will look for critical points in
\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
of the renormalized energy functional

E(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}|u^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(v^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-|u|^{2})^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}v^{4}-\frac{\alpha}{2}(1-|u|^{2})v^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(2.4)

which is finite for every
u\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
,
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, for
u=\rho e^{i\theta}\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})
, the energy recasts as

E(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-\rho^{2})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(v^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}v^{4}-\frac{\alpha}{2}(1-\rho^{2})v^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Notice that, if
(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
, then
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u|^{2}v^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=m-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-|u|^{2})v^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
.
Thus, the proper energy (
1.3
) and the renormalized energy (
2.4
) differ only in a constant. From now on, we will frequently just call energy to (
2.4
).

Observe that

E(u,v)\geq-\frac{\alpha}{2}m,\quad\text{for every }(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}.

Therefore, the energy is bounded from below in
\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
and, as a consequence, one may define the miminizing surface

E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)=\inf\{E(u,v):\,(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}\}.

Let us first analyze the semitrivial cases
E_{\textup{min}}(0,m)
and
E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)
. On the one hand, it is simple to see that

E_{\textup{min}}(0,m)=0,\quad\text{for every }m\geq 0,

and the infimum is never attained if
m>0
. On the other hand, for
m=0
we have the following well-known result:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

For every
q\in(0,\pi/2)
there exists
\mathbf{u}_{q}\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})
, with
0<|\mathbf{u}_{q}|<1
,
p(\mathbf{u}_{q})=q
, such that

E(\mathbf{u}_{q},0)=E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)<\sqrt{2}q.

(2.5)

Moreover, there exists
c=c(q)\in(0,\sqrt{2})
such that
E(\mathbf{u}_{q},0)=(2-c^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}/3
, and
\mathbf{u}_{q}
is the unique solution, up to invariances, to (
1.7
). These solutions are explicit and are given by

\mathbf{u}_{q}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2-c^{2}}{c}}\tanh\left(\frac{\sqrt{2-c^{2}}}{2}x\right)-\frac{c}{\sqrt{2}}i.

(2.6)

Besides, there exists
\mathbf{u}_{\pi/2}\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R})
such that

E(\mathbf{u}_{\pi/2},0)=\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3}=\min\left\{E(u):\,u\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{R}),\,\inf_{\mathbb{R}}|u(x)|=0\right\},

(2.7)

which is the unique solution, up to invariances, to (
1.7
) with
c=0
, and whose explicit expression is (
2.6
) with
c=0
.

Remark 2.2
.

We note that the classical minimization problem establishes the renormalized momentum
Q
, given by (
2.3
), as a constraint. Specifically,

E(\mathbf{u}_{q},0)=\inf\{E(u,0):\,u\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R}),\,Q(u)=q\}:=F_{\textup{min}}(q).

However, we will show in Lemma
4.2
that the infimum in
E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)
may equivalently be taken over functions
u\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})
such that
|u|\leq 1
. Therefore, in light of (
2.2
), and taking into account that
0<|\mathbf{u}_{q}|<1
in
\mathbb{R}
, it follows

E(\mathbf{u}_{q},0)=F_{\textup{min}}(q)\leq\inf\{E(u,0):\,0<|u|\leq 1,\,Q(u)=q\}=E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)\leq E(\mathbf{u}_{q},0).

Therefore, the identity
E(\mathbf{u}_{q},0)=E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)
in the statement of Theorem
2.1
remains entirely valid.

The properties of the scalar dark soliton stated in the previous result will be used to obtain fundamental regularity and monotonicity properties of the minimizing surface
E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)
. These include the key subadditivity property required for ruling out dichotomy. The properties are gathered in the following result:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
.

(i)

For every
m\geq 0
, the function
q\mapsto E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)
is nondecreasing in
[0,\infty)
. In particular,
E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)\geq 0
for all
q\in[0,\pi/2)
,
m\geq 0
.

(ii)

For every
q\geq 0
, the function
m\mapsto E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)+\alpha m/2
is nondecreasing in
[0,\infty)
.

(iii)

For
0\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}<\pi/2
and
0\leq m_{1}\leq m_{2}
, one has

E_{\textup{min}}(q_{2},m_{2})\leq E_{\textup{min}}(q_{1},m_{1})+\sqrt{2}(q_{2}-q_{1}).

(2.8)

In particular, for every
q\in[0,\pi/2)
, the function
m\mapsto E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)
is nonincreasing, and

E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)<E_{\textup{min}}(q,0),\quad\text{for all }q\in(0,\pi/2),\,m>0.

(2.9)

(iv)

The function
(q,m)\mapsto E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)
is Lipschitz in
[0,\pi/2)\times[0,\infty)
.

(v)

For
q_{1}\in[0,\pi/2)
,
q_{2}\in[0,\pi/2)
,
m_{1}\geq 0
, and
m_{2}\geq 0
, it follows

E_{\textup{min}}(q_{1}+q_{2},m_{1}+m_{2})\leq E_{\textup{min}}(q_{1},m_{1})+E_{\textup{min}}(q_{2},m_{2}),

(2.10)

and the equality in (
2.10
) holds if, and only if,
(q_{1}+q_{2})(q_{1}+m_{1})(q_{2}+m_{2})=0
.

The properties of
E_{\textup{min}}
ultimately lead to the compactness of the minimizing sequences and, in turn, to the existence of a finite-energy solution to (
1.4
)-(
1.5
). For instance, we highlight that (
2.9
) plays a central role in guaranteeing the nonvanishing of minimizing sequences. This inequality can be formally interpreted as expressing an
energetic advantage
of nontrivial bright components over those with vanishing mass.

The main result of the paper is the following:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
\beta\geq 0
,
q\in(0,\pi/2)
, and
m>0
. Assume one of the following two conditions:

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}<\beta,\quad E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)<\Big(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\Big)\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3},

(H1)

\displaystyle E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)+\frac{\alpha m}{2}<\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3}.

(H2)

Then, there exists
(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
such that
E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)=E(u,v)
. Moreover,
0<|u|<1
in
\mathbb{R}
,
v>0
in
\mathbb{R}
, and
1-|u|
and
v
are even and radially nonincreasing. Furthermore, there exist
c,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

0<c<\sqrt{2},\quad\frac{c^{2}}{2}\alpha<\lambda<2\alpha+\sqrt{32}\frac{q}{m},

(2.11)

and
(u,v)
is a solution to (
1.4
).

Notice that, as expected from the scalar case, we obtain a subsonic propagation speed, namely
c\in(0,\sqrt{2})
.

We also remark that, in the context of mixtures of BECs, the condition
\alpha^{2}<\beta
corresponds to the so-called
miscibility regime

[
20
,
3
]
. In physical terms, this conditions says that the repulsive forces between the two species in the mixture of condensates are weaker than the repulsive interatomic interactions. In this regime, i.e. under condition (
H1
), our main result yields the existence of a dark-bright soliton provided that
E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)
is small enough. As shown by (
2.9
), a sufficient condition for (
H1
) is

\alpha^{2}<\beta,\quad E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)\leq\Big(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta}\Big)\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3},

which means that
q
is small, while
m
can be as large as desired.

In the
immiscibility regime

\alpha^{2}>\beta
, and in the transition regime
\alpha^{2}=\beta
(within which the Manakov case falls), our result also follows under condition (
H2
). Notice that, by virtue of Theorem
2.1
, this condition is trivially satisfied for
m=0
. Moreover, as shown by item
(i)
in
Theorem
2.3
,
E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)\geq 0
for all
q\in(0,\pi/2)
and
m>0
, which implies that (
H2
) means a smallness requirement for the mass. It follows also from (
2.9
) in Theorem
2.3
that a sufficient condition for (
H2
) is

E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)+\frac{\alpha m}{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3},

where, recall,
E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)
is explicit, see Theorem
2.1
. In any case, it remains unclear whether conditions (
H1
) and (
H2
) are essential or simply technical.

We conclude this section with a brief discussion on the stability of dark-bright solitons. To the best of the author’s knowledge, a rigorous analytical proof of their orbital stability remains an open problem, even in the Gross–Clark
(\beta=0)
and the Manakov
(\alpha=\beta=1)
models. A standard strategy for establishing the orbital stability of constrained minimizers is the approach developed in
[
10
]
. However, this method is not directly applicable here, as the quantity
p(u)
, which is fixed in our variational framework, is not conserved along the flow. Nevertheless, the variational structure underlying our existence result suggests, at least on a heuristic level, that orbital stability should hold, and we hope our approach lays the groundwork for a future rigorous proof.

3
Basics of symmetric decreasing rearrangements

In order to be self-contained, let us state some basic definitions and properties of symmetric decreasing rearrangements. More details can be found in
[
7
,
24
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we say that it
vanishes at infinity
if
|\{|f|>t\}|<\infty
for all
t>0
. If
f
vanishes at infinity, its
symmetric decreasing rearrangement
is defined as

f^{\star}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi_{\{|f|>t\}^{\star}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\{|f|>t\}^{\star}
is the open interval centered at zero whose length coincides with
|\{|f|>t\}|
, and
\chi
denotes the indicator function.

One deduces straightaway from the definition that
f^{\star}
is a nonnegative, even and lower semicontinuous function which is also nonincreasing in
(0,\infty)
. Furthermore, one of the fundamental properties of the symmetric decreasing rearrangements is that, for every
t>0
, the following identity holds:

\{f^{\star}>t\}=\{|f|>t\}^{\star},\quad\text{and therefore,}\quad|\{f^{\star}>t\}|=|\{|f|>t\}|.

As a consequence, one has the following relation.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function that vanishes at infinity, and let
G:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that
G=G_{1}-G_{2}
, where
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are both nondecreasing and, for at least one
j\in\{1,2\}
, one has that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{j}(|f(x)|)\,\mathrm{d}x
is finite. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(|f(x)|)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(f^{\star}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.1)

In particular,
\|f\|_{p}=\|f^{\star}\|_{p}
for every
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

The identity (
3.1
) can alternatively be proved by applying the following elementary property.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function that vanishes at infinity and
G:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is nondecreasing with
G(0)=0
, then
G\circ|f|
is measurable, it vanishes at infinity, and
(G\circ|f|)^{\star}=G\circ f^{\star}
.

The following result is commonly known as the Hardy-Littlewood inequality.

Theorem 3.4
.

If
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
are measurable functions that vanish at infinity, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}fg\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\star}g^{\star}\,\mathrm{d}x.

We also recall the well-known Pólya-Szegő inequality.

Theorem 3.5
.

If
f\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R})
for some
p\in[1,\infty)
, then
f^{\star}\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R})
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|(f^{\star})^{\prime}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f^{\prime}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x.

We finally state the version in
[
1
]
of the key result due to
[
14
]
, which essentially says that Pólya-Szegő inequality is strict for functions that decompose in the sum of disjoint bubbles.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be even, nonnegative and radially nonincreasing. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
f(\cdot+x_{1})
and
g(\cdot+x_{2})
have disjoint supports, and let
h=f(\cdot+x_{1})+g(\cdot+x_{2})
. Then,

\|(h^{\star})^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\|f^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}+\|g^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}-\frac{3}{4}\min\{\|f^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2},\|g^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}\}.

Properties of the minimizing surface

The following key lemma assures that functions conveniently symmetrized have smaller energy.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
\beta\geq 0
,
q>0
,
m\geq 0
, and
(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
, with
u=\rho e^{i\theta}
. Then, there exists
\gamma\in(0,1]
such that, fixing any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and defining

\tilde{u}=\tilde{\rho}e^{i\tilde{\theta}},\quad\text{where}\quad 1-\tilde{\rho}=(1-\rho)^{\star},\quad\tilde{\theta}=\gamma\int_{x_{0}}^{x}(\theta^{\prime})^{\star}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

it follows that
(\tilde{u},v^{\star})\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
and
E(\tilde{u},v^{\star})\leq E(u,v)
.

Proof.

We will start by proving that
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})
and
|\tilde{u}|<2
in
\mathbb{R}
. To do so, let us first take
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|1-\rho(x_{0})|=\|1-\rho\|_{\infty}
. From Proposition
3.2
, it follows

1-\tilde{\rho}\leq\|1-\tilde{\rho}\|_{\infty}=\|(1-\rho)^{\star}\|_{\infty}=\|1-\rho\|_{\infty}=|1-\rho(x_{0})|.

Due to the fact that
0<\rho<2
in
\mathbb{R}
, it is easy to see that
|1-\rho(x_{0})|<1
and, in turn,
\tilde{\rho}>0
in
\mathbb{R}
. Besides, since
(1-\rho)^{\star}\geq 0
, it follows that
\tilde{\rho}\leq 1<2
in
\mathbb{R}
.

Next, by definition of
G
in (

), we get
1-\tilde{\rho}^{2}=G(1-\tilde{\rho})=G((1-\rho)^{\star}).
Hence, using that
G
is increasing in the interval
[0,1]
, and thus so is
G^{2}
, Proposition
3.2
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\tilde{\rho}^{2})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G((1-\rho)^{\star})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(|1-\rho|)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(4(1-\rho)^{2}-4|1-\rho|^{3}+(1-\rho)^{4}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(4(1-\rho)^{2}-4(1-\rho)^{3}+(1-\rho)^{4}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho^{2})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

In particular,
(1-\tilde{\rho}^{2})^{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In addition, Theorem
3.5
implies

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\tilde{\rho}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(((1-\rho)^{\star})^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

That is,
\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
too, it follows that
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{NE}(\mathbb{R})
.

On the other hand, by Proposition
3.3
and Theorem
3.4
, we deduce

\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(1-\tilde{\rho})(\theta^{\prime})^{\star}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(|1-\rho|)^{\star}(\theta^{\prime})^{\star}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(|1-\rho|)|\theta^{\prime}|\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 2p(u)=2q>0.

Therefore, the real number

\gamma=2q\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(|1-\rho|)^{\star}(\theta^{\prime})^{\star}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{-1},

is well-defined,
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
p(\tilde{u})=q
.
Furthermore, Proposition
3.2
assures that
v^{\star}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|v^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}=m
. Hence,
(\tilde{u},v^{\star})\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
.

Let us now estimate the remaining terms in
E(\tilde{u},v^{\star})
. Thus, from Proposition
3.2
, it follows

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\tilde{\theta}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\gamma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}((\theta^{\prime})^{\star})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\gamma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

In addition, by Proposition
3.3
and Theorem
3.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\tilde{\rho}^{2})(\tilde{\theta}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(|1-\rho|)^{\star}((\theta^{\prime})^{2})^{\star}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\gamma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(|1-\rho|)(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|1-\rho|(2-|1-\rho|)(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\gamma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(2(1-\rho))-(1-\rho)^{2})(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\gamma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho^{2})(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Therefore, recalling that
\gamma\in(0,1]
, we derive

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\rho}^{2}(\tilde{\theta}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\tilde{\theta}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\tilde{\rho}^{2})(\tilde{\theta}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\gamma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho^{2}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho^{2}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Arguing as above, we also derive

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\tilde{\rho}^{2})(v^{\star})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho^{2})v^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}((v^{\star})^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(v^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}(v^{\star})^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x.

We conclude that
E(\tilde{u},v^{\star})\leq E(u,v)
.
∎

Let us introduce the set

\mathcal{X}^{0}_{q,m}=\left\{(\rho e^{i\theta},v)\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}\left|\begin{array}[]{l}1-\rho,\theta^{\prime},v\text{ are even, nonnegative, radially nonincreasing}\\
\text{functions in }\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\text{ with }p(\rho e^{i\theta})=q,\,\|v\|^{2}_{2}=m\end{array}\right.\right\}.

Next lemma shows that, even if

is contained in

, the infima of the energy in both sets coincide. Thus, one is allowed to take minimizing sequences of compactly supported, symmetric functions. We stress that one cannot expect the infimum in
\mathcal{X}_{q,m}^{0}
to be attained since solutions in
\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
cannot have compact support (see the proof of Theorem

).

Lemma 4.2
.

For every
\alpha>0
,
\beta\geq 0
,
q>0
,
m\geq 0
, the following identity holds:

E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)=\inf\{E(u,v):\,(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}^{0}_{q,m}\}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
q>0
and
m\geq 0
, and let
(u,v)\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}
be such that

E(u,v)<E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)+\varepsilon.

By Lemma
4.1
,
u
can be taken such that
|u|\leq 1
. Since
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, there exist
1-\rho_{1},\zeta_{1},v_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\rho_{1}>0
and, if
\theta_{1}
is any primitive of
\zeta_{1}
and if
u_{1}=\rho_{1}e^{i\theta_{1}}
, then

E(u_{1},v_{1})<E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)+o_{\varepsilon}(1),\quad p(u_{1})=q+o_{\varepsilon}(1),\quad\|v_{1}\|^{2}_{2}=m+o_{\varepsilon}(1),

where
o_{\varepsilon}(1)
denotes any constant such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}o_{\varepsilon}(1)=0
. Next, using the notation of Lemma
4.1
, we have
|\tilde{u}_{1}|>0
and

E(\tilde{u}_{1},v_{1}^{\star})<E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)+o_{\varepsilon}(1),\quad p(\tilde{u}_{1})=q+o_{\varepsilon}(1),\quad\|v_{1}^{\star}\|^{2}_{2}=m+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

Observe that
1-|\tilde{u}_{1}|
,
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}_{1}
and
v_{1}^{\star}
are even, nonnegative, radially nonincreasing, and have compact support. However, they need not be smooth. Let us thus define

1-\rho_{2}=\phi_{\varepsilon}*(1-|\tilde{u}_{1}|),\quad\zeta_{2}=\phi_{\varepsilon}*\tilde{\theta}_{1}^{\prime},\quad u_{2}=\rho_{2}e^{i\theta_{2}},\quad v_{2}=\phi_{\varepsilon}*v_{1}^{\star},

where
\theta_{2}
is a primitive of
\zeta_{2}
,
*
denotes the usual convolution, and
\phi_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
is an even and radially decreasing mollifier that we choose in such a way that

E(u_{2},v_{2})<E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)+o_{\varepsilon}(1),\quad p(u_{2}):=q_{\varepsilon}=q+o_{\varepsilon}(1),\quad\|v_{2}\|^{2}_{2}:=m_{\varepsilon}=m+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

Finally, let us take

\rho_{3}(x)=\rho_{2}(x),\quad\zeta_{3}(x)=\frac{q}{q_{\varepsilon}}\zeta_{2}(x),\quad u_{3}=\rho_{3}e^{i\theta_{3}},\quad v_{3}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{m}{m_{\varepsilon}}}v_{2}(x),

where
\theta_{3}
is a primitive of
\zeta_{3}
. It is immediate to check that
(u_{3},v_{3})\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}^{0}
and

\displaystyle E(u_{3},v_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho_{2}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{2}^{2})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{q^{2}}{2q_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{2}^{2}(\theta_{2}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{m}{2m_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(v_{2}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta m^{2}}{4m_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{2}^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\alpha m}{2m_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{2}^{2})v_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=E(u_{2},v_{2})+o_{\varepsilon}(1).

Therefore,
E(u_{3},v_{3})<E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)+o_{\varepsilon}(1)
. This proves the result.
∎

In what follows, we investigate the properties of the minimizing surface. The result below establishes that it is bounded from above by
E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)
, being such a bound strict provided that the mass is small.

Lemma 4.3

Let

(4.2)

where

(4.3)

Proof.

with

\|v\|_{2}^{2}=m

\displaystyle E_{\textup{min}}(q,m)

\displaystyle\leq E(\mathbf{u}_{q},v_{s})=E_{\textup{min}}(q,0)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}(v_{s}^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}v_{s}^{4}-\frac{\alpha}{2}(1-|\mathbf{u}_{q}|^{2})v_{s}^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\alpha>0

(4.6)

2\|\rho^{\prime}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|1-\rho_{n}^{2}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\rho_{n}\theta_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|v_{n}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}+\beta\|v_{n}\|_{4}^{4}=4E(u_{n},v_{n})-2\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{n}^{2}v_{n}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\alpha\|v_{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

From (

Next result establishes a suitable positive bound from below for minimizing sequences.
This is a key step for proving a subadditivity property for

. The second part of the lemma will also be used to rule out vanishing in the concentration-compactness argument, see Remark

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
\beta\geq 0
,
q\in(0,\pi/2)
, and
m\geq 0
, and let

be a minimizing sequence, i.e.

(4.7)

Let us consider the lifting

. Then, there exists

, such that
\|\rho_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}>\delta
for all large enough
n
. In addition, if

, then
\|v_{n}\|_{4}>\delta
for all large enough

Proof.

We start by showing that
\|\rho_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq\delta
. Arguing by contradiction, assume that, up to a subsequence,
\|\rho_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}\to 0
as

. From Young inequality, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{4}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{n}^{\prime})^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})\theta_{n}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since
(u_{n},v_{n})\in\mathcal{X}_{q,m}^{0}
, it follows that
\rho_{n}\leq 1
in
\mathbb{R}
, which implies that

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})\theta_{n}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x=p(u_{n})=q.

Therefore,

\displaystyle E(u_{n},v_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho_{n}^{2}(\theta_{n}^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})v_{n}^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{n}^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})(\alpha v_{n}^{2}+(\theta_{n}^{\prime})^{2})\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{2}q-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})(\alpha v_{n}^{2}+(\theta_{n}^{\prime})^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x.

On the other hand, by Hölder inequality, and the fact that

is bounded, we deduce

\displaystyle(1-\rho_{n}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\int_{-\infty}^{x}(1-\rho_{n}(y))\rho_{n}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=-2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{(1-\rho_{n}(y)^{2})}{1+\rho_{n}(y)}\rho_{n}^{\prime}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq 2\|1-\rho_{n}^{2}\|_{2}\|\rho_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq 2(4E(u_{n},v_{n})+2\alpha m)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\rho_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}\to 0,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

In particular,
\|1-\rho_{n}\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
n\to\infty

n\to\infty
. Furthermore, since

, it follows that
\rho_{n}\geq 1/2
in

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\rho_{n}^{2})

\displaystyle(\alpha v_{n}^{2}+(\theta_{n}^{\prime})^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\|1-\rho_{n}^{2}\|_{\infty}\Big(m\alpha+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\theta_{n}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\|1-\rho_{n}^{2}\|_{\infty}\Big(m\alpha+4\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{n}^{2}(\theta_{n}^{\prime})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\|1-\rho_{n}^{2}\|_{\infty}(m\alpha+4(2E(u_{n},v_{n})+\alpha m))\to 0,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Passing to the limit in (
4
), we derive

, which contradicts Lemma

Let us now assume that

as

. Consequently, by Hölder inequality and (

On the other hand, it is immediate to check that

Passing to the limit, we conclude

∎
