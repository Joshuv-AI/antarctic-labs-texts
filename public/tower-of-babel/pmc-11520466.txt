Association of MRI-based knee osteoarthritis structural phenotypes with short-term structural progression and subsequent total knee replacement
Authors: Yukang Liu, Zikai Xing, Baoer Wu, Ning Chen, Tianxing Wu, Zhuojian Cai, Donghong Guo, Gaochenzi Tao, Zikun Xie, Chengkai Wu, Peihua Cao, Xiaoshuai Wang, Jia Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe failure of disease-modifying osteoarthritis drugs (DMOADs) trials lies mainly in the heterogeneity of the disease, which calls for a more precise population with specific progression and outcomes. This study aimed to determine whether and which MRI-based structural phenotype of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is associated with short-term structural progression and subsequent total knee replacement (TKR).MethodsA longitudinal study was conducted among participants with baseline Kellgren-Lawrence grade (KLG) ≥ 2 from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI). The structural phenotypes at baseline were defined as subchondral bone, meniscus/cartilage and inflammatory phenotypes according to the MRI Osteoarthritis Knee Score (MOAKS). The primary outcome was the progression of structural abnormalities within 24 months and multivariable logistic regressions were applied to evaluate the associations. The secondary outcome was the incidence of TKR during 108 months. Cox regressions and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used for the analysis.ResultsA total of 733 participants with KOA were finally included in our study, with 493 (67.3%) having the three main structural phenotypes. For the primary outcome, the subchondral bone phenotype (OR [95% CI]:1.71 [1.02, 2.83], 1.52 [1.06, 2.18], 1.65 [1.11, 2.42], respectively) and the inflammatory phenotype (OR [95% CI]: 1.69 [1.05, 2.74], 1.82 [1.31, 2.52], 2.15 [1.48, 3.14], respectively) were both associated with the short-term progression of joint space narrowing, osteophytes and sclerosis in 24 months, whereas the meniscus/cartilage phenotype was only associated with the progression of osteophytes and sclerosis. For the secondary outcome, the subchondral bone phenotype (HR [95% CI]: 1.71 [1.06–2.78]) and inflammatory phenotype (HR [95%CI]: 2.00 [1.02–2.67]) were associated with shorter time to subsequent TKR, but not the meniscus/cartilage phenotype. Besides, the cumulative effect when the structural phenotype overlapped was confirmed in both outcomes.ConclusionsThe subchondral bone phenotype and inflammatory phenotype were associated with the progression of joint space narrowing, osteophytes and sclerosis in 24 months, along with subsequent TKR in 108 months. Besides, additive effects of overlapped phenotypes were further determined. These phenotypes could serve as valuable screening tools for future clinical trials and provide guidance for risk evaluation.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a prevalent joint disorder causing chronic pain, functional disability and health burdens [1, 2]. Current treatments have been limited to physical activity, analgesics and, ultimately, total knee replacement (TKR) [3]. However, no identified disease-modifying osteoarthritis drugs (DMOADs), the pharmacological agents specifically designed to delay or reverse joint structural progression, have been approved by regulatory agencies [4]. The main obstacles could be attributed to the heterogeneity of KOA and the involvement of various mechanisms, which may lead to different therapeutic targets [5]. In addition, owing to the duration limitation, the DMOADs trials call for participants at risk of rapid KOA progression to improve trial efficiency and reduce cost.

To illustrate this heterogeneity, KOA phenotypes have been proposed, defined as subtypes of KOA characterized by distinct underlying mechanisms with structural and functional consequences [6, 7]. Recently, the structural phenotype, a new concept of KOA phenotype, was pointed out for the potential to identify heterogeneity and promote more targeted populations under the DMOADs context [8]. With the assistance of imaging techniques, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) exhibits more heightened sensitivity in detecting structural degeneration compared to conventional radiography, enabling a more precise assessment of tissue alterations. [9, 10]. The Rapid OA MRI Eligibility Score (ROAMES) was then introduced and applied as a simplified approach for detecting structural phenotypes based on MRI, which allowed phenotypic stratification and helped to screen targeted populations with specific phenotypes [8, 11]. Thus, it could be especially important to make it clear whether multiple structural phenotypes differ in structural progression and subsequent TKR, which implies clues for distinct structural targets for specific DMOAD trials.

Three main MRI-based structural phenotypes have been put forward considering specific structure-driven damages, which are subchondral bone, meniscus/cartilage and inflammation [8]. These phenotypes might vary in the disease progression of KOA. Several studies have discussed this issue but induced inconsistent results. Roemer et al. reported that only the subchondral bone phenotype was associated with radiographic progression within 48 months, rather than the other two phenotypes [12]. Besides, it was found that the progression rate of the meniscus/cartilage phenotype differed in Kellgren-Lawrence grade (KLG) 2 and 3 knees [13]. Furthermore, Lee et al. found that all three phenotypes were associated with radiographic KOA incidence [14]. However, given the limited sample size and outcomes, their associations with the progression of different structures in a shorter term and the end outcome, TKR, need to be further verified. Besides, the additive effect of overlapped phenotypes also remains unclear and requires further investigation. Addressing these questions is crucial for screening efforts in the inclusion of future DMOAD trials.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between MRI-based structural phenotypes and structural progression in 24 months, as well as subsequent TKR in the long run. In addition, we aimed to further explore the additive effect of overlapped phenotypes on disease progression.

Methods

Data source and study design

Data were collected from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI), a longitudinal, multicentered, prospective cohort study. From February 2004 to May 2006, 4,796 participants [9,592 knees] aged 45–79 were enrolled at four centers. Ethics approval and informed consent were provided by the OAI. Further details are provided at https://nda.nih.gov/oai/about-oai. Participants with knee osteoarthritis, defined as KLG ≥ 2, were included at baseline visits in this study. If a participant had osteoarthritis on either knee, we randomly chose one for inclusion. Before phenotypic definition and grouping, knees with missing baseline data or a history of knee replacement were excluded. Then, the remaining knees were labeled as corresponding structural phenotypes based on MRI semi-quantitative scores for further analysis. Structural progression was evaluated at the 24 month follow-up visit, and the incident of TKR within 108 months was collected. Exposure

At baseline, the structural phenotypes of the included participants were defined through MRI. MRI was performed on 3 T systems (Siemens Trio) and featured structures were assessed through the MRI Osteoarthritis Knee Score (MOAKS) system [15, 16]. Using the definitions of previous studies [11, 12, 14], the subchondral bone phenotype was defined as knees with bone marrow lesions (BMLs) in at least one knee compartment (Fig. 2A). The meniscus/cartilage phenotype was defined as knees with any type of meniscal substance loss/maceration and ipsilateral compartmental cartilage damage (Fig. 2B). The inflammatory phenotype was defined as knees with any effusion-synovitis or Hoffa-synovitis (Fig. 2C-D). Outcomes

The primary outcome was the progression of three joint abnormalities in the first 24 months, including joint space narrowing (JSN), osteophytes, and sclerosis. These individual structures were assessed by the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) grade and progression was defined as at least one or more OARSI grades increasing from baseline to the follow-up visit [17]. The secondary outcome was subsequent TKR with a confirmed medical record. The incident timepoint of TKR within the first 108 months was collected for further analysis.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of the knees were compared between groups with and without MRI-structural phenotypes via Student’s t tests, Chi-square tests or Kruskal–Wallis tests when appropriate. Logistic regression models were used to investigate the associations between structural phenotypes and short-term radiographic structural progression. Potential confounding variables, including sex, BMI, age, race, knee injury, knee surgery, knee alignment, baseline symptoms and radiographic KOA were adjusted by multivariable regression models. Cox regression models were employed to analyze the associations between structural phenotypes and the risk of subsequent TKR, with the same adjustments applied. Besides, survival analysis of the knees was also performed using Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank tests. P values (at a prespecified significance level of P < 0.05) and odds ratio (OR)/hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were obtained from the models. All the analyses were conducted in R, version 4.2.3.

Results

Secondary outcome: subsequent TKR

During the 108-month follow-up period, a total of 76 confirmed cases of TKR were identified, with 61 cases in the group with structural phenotypes and 15 cases in the group without structural phenotypes. The regression analysis revealed that the subchondral bone phenotype (adjusted HR [95% CI]: 1.71 [1.06–2.78]) and inflammatory phenotype (adjusted HR [95% CI]: 2.00 [1.02–2.67]) were associated with subsequent TKR (Table 3). The odds also increased when structural phenotypes overlapped (P for trend < 0.05). Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that the subchondral bone phenotype, inflammatory phenotype and overlapped phenotypes were associated with a shorter time to TKR (Fig. 3A-D). Discussion

This study demonstrated the associations between MRI-based structural phenotypes and short-term structural progression, as well as subsequent TKR. The subchondral bone and inflammatory phenotypes were found to be associated with the short-term progression of JSN, osteophytes and sclerosis in 24 months, as well as subsequent TKR. The meniscus/cartilage phenotype was not associated with subsequent TKR, though it was also associated with the short-term progression of osteophytes and sclerosis. Overlapped phenotypes could increase these risks compared to a single phenotype. These findings imply early identification of susceptible subjects at high risk of disease progression and suggest the potential application of MRI-based structural phenotypes during the recruitment of clinical trials.

The associations between structural phenotypes and the progression of specific joint abnormalities have not been fully investigated before. Previous studies paid attention to radiographic progression over a longer time scale, reporting an association between the subchondral bone phenotype and a decrease in joint space width (JSW) in 48 months [12]. Given the limitations of trial duration, the efficacy of DMOADs may be most effectively assessed in patients exhibiting structural phenotypes with rapid KOA progression. In this study, in order to identify key structural phenotypes that may be predictive of rapid disease progression, we studied in a shorter time scale (24 months). Besides, we focused on different structural abnormalities, including JSN, osteophytes and sclerosis. Our findings not only support but also extend the existing knowledge, suggesting that the presence of subchondral bone phenotype and inflammatory phenotype could imply earlier joint structural changes, including JSN, osteophytes and sclerosis. However, the meniscus/cartilage phenotype was found not to be associated with the progression of JSN in 24 months, which was consistent with the results during a follow-up period of 48 months [12]. Similar results were found in previous studies, which reported that MRI-detected cartilage loss and meniscus subluxation was not associated with the progression of JSN within 2 years [18, 19]. Based on our results, the presence of cartilage or meniscus defects does not appear to be a significant predictor for substantial JSN. This needs to be validated with longer follow-up time in future studies. Anyways, our findings suggest that bone marrow lesions (BMLs) and inflammation may possibly independently contribute to and play more important roles in short-term structural changes compared to traditional mechanical factors, such as cartilage loss or meniscus tears. The two structural phenotypes are more likely to be characterized by rapid progression and have the potential to be widely applied in the DMOADs trials.

TKR is often the final choice and treatment for end-stage KOA, but there was still a considerable proportion of patients who did not have a good prognosis after the surgery [20, 21]. Consequently, it is important to identify patients with KOA at high risk of subsequent TKR for intervention as early as possible. As the association between the structural phenotype and TKR is not clear, we explore this relationship for the first time. In our study, the subchondral bone phenotype and inflammatory phenotype were found to be associated with subsequent TKR. This finding confirms the important role of BMLs and synovitis in the progression to knee replacement, which has also been supported by previous studies [22–24]. Rather than considering them as separate structures, we proposed this association under the concept of structural phenotypes. This association could be explained by the severe pain caused by BMLs and inflammation, which is a crucial factor in deciding to undergo surgery [25–27]. However, no significant associations with TKR were found in the meniscus/cartilage phenotype. The relationship between meniscus morphology, cartilage loss and knee replacement is controversial, and the results of relevant studies are inconsistent [28, 29]. Recent studies found that they are weakly associated with knee pain in clinical symptoms, which may affect treatment choices and surgery decisions [30, 31]. Overall, the association with TKR was likely to be influenced by multiple factors and required further investigation. Our findings suggest that the subchondral bone phenotype and inflammatory phenotype could be applied as independent and promising screening tools for identifying patients at high risk for TKR, thus enhancing the understanding of therapeutic targets in clinical trials. Moreover, these structural phenotypes need to be identified earlier to initiate intervention before irreversible damage occurs.

The overlap of structural phenotypes in established KOA was found to be common and multiple structural phenotypes may work together to contribute to KOA progression. The inflammatory phenotype was found to be the most common partner in combinations of various overlapped phenotypes. Synovitis was previously discovered to precede structural changes of osteoarthritis and mediate the association of other structures, such as the BMLs and meniscus, with KOA progression [32–34]. This may suggest that inflammation could interact with other structures and eventually promote the formation of overlapped phenotypes [35, 36]. Even though our study cannot determine the chronological order of overlapped phenotypes, it suggests that inflammatory phenotypes may play a large role and could become important targets for intervention. Hence, the inflammatory phenotype may require significant attention and may hold the key to unlocking the secrets of how overlapped structural phenotypes form and progress. It may provide more detailed screening criteria and research directions for future clinical trials.

To date, most completed phase II/III clinical trials of DMOADs have failed to find both symptomatic and structural improvements, although they were usually grounded in proof-of-principle studies [37]. A recent study about the protective effect of zoledronic acid on TKR also failed to identify significant findings [38]. The study included patients with symptomatic KOA and without severe JSN, however, more and more evidence showed that radiography was far from enough to detect structural heterogeneity [10]. Our findings indicated structural phenotype stratification could be the possible answer for more targeted clinical trials and could promote the success of these trials. According to our findings, the results could be different if we select specific participants with TKR-associated subchondral bone phenotype, since zoledronic acid tends to exert a therapeutic effect on subchondral bone [39, 40]. Based on a large cohort and the technique of MRI, our study recommended phenotypic populations with a risk of rapid progression or TKR for the efficiency and precision of the DMOADs trials. As a result, drugs that were previously deemed ineffective may require re-evaluation and re-investigation in populations with specific structural phenotypes.

Our study also has some limitations. Firstly, except for the three structural phenotypes we discussed, atrophic and hypertrophic phenotypes have also been proposed [8]. However, due to the rare cases of the other two phenotypes, we focused on the three main MRI-based structural phenotypes. Secondly, specific combinations among structural phenotypes were not further studied for the cumulative effect of overlapped phenotypes. As we mentioned before, four overlapped phenotypes were found in our study, but a limited sample size could lead to an underestimation of their true role. Future studies with larger databases could further investigate the effect of different overlapped phenotypes based on the evidence reported in our study. Thirdly, the progression trajectories of knee osteoarthritis may differ depending on the KLG [13]. Therefore, we adjusted for potential confounders including the baseline KLG, and future studies stratified by KLG are still needed to enhance the credibility of the findings. Fourthly, our study was conducted among participants from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI), which may not fully represent the diverse KOA population worldwide and needs to be validated in other cohorts to prove the generalizability. Additionally, information bias could have occurred because of the reliance on self-reported data for some variables, such as knee injury history and surgical history.

In summary, we found that both subchondral bone and inflammatory phenotypes were associated with the short-term progression of JSN, osteophytes and sclerosis in 24 months, as well as subsequent TKR. The meniscus/cartilage phenotype was not associated with subsequent TKR, although it was also associated with short-term progression of osteophytes and sclerosis. Moreover, the additive effects of overlapped phenotypes were further determined. Our findings tend to highlight more attention to the subchondral bone and inflammatory phenotypes compared with the meniscus/cartilage phenotype, especially in retarding TKR. This could serve as a valuable screening tool for future clinical trials and provide guidance for risk evaluation.
