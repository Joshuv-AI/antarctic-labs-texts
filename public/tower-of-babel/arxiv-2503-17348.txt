Universality for catalytic equations and fully parked trees
Alice Contat, &, Nicolas Curien

Abstract
We show that critical parking trees conditioned to be fully parked converge in the scaling limits towards the Brownian growth-fragmentation tree, a self-similar Markov tree different from Aldous’ Brownian tree recently introduced and studied in [ 7 ] . As a by-product of our study, we prove that positive non-linear polynomial equations involving a catalytic variable display a universal polynomial exponent 5 / 2 5/2 at their singularity, confirming a conjecture by Chapuy, Schaeffer and Drmota & Hainzl. Compared to previous analytical works on the subject, our approach is probabilistic and exploits an underlying random walk hidden in the random tree model.

Introduction

Universality for polynomial equation with a catalytic variable.

Starting with the pioneer works of Tutte on the enumeration of planar maps
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
, the idea of introducing a “catalytic” variable to solve an equation involving generating functions has been an extremely fruitful idea. Formally those equations are of the form

P\big(x,y,F(x,y),F_{1}(x),F_{2}(x),...,F_{\mathrm{K}}(x)\big)=0,

where
P
is a polynomial,
F(x,y)
is the generating function of interest, and the series
F_{i}(x)
, which are part of the unknowns, are typically related to the coefficient of
y^{i}
in
F(x,y)
. The variable
y
is called the
catalytic
variable since its standard fate is to be eliminated to get
[y^{0}]F(x,y)
. Building on the quadratic and kernel methods, Bousquet-Mélou & Jehanne
[
10
]
developed a systematic approach to solve those equations. This technique is proved to always work in the case where

\displaystyle F(x,y)=xQ\big(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\Delta^{(2)}F,...,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F\big),

(1)

where
Q
is polynomial and
\Delta^{(i)}F=\Delta^{(i)}F(x,y)
are the
discrete differences
of
F(x,y)
with respect to
y
i.e.

\displaystyle\Delta^{(i)}F=\Delta^{(i)}F(x,y):=\dfrac{F(x,y)-\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}y^{j}[y^{j}]F(x,y)}{y^{i}},\quad\mbox{ for }i\geqslant 1.

(2)

In this case,
[y^{0}]F(x,y)
is an algebraic generating function of
x
amenable to singularity analysis
[
37
]
via Puiseux’s expansion. In particular, when the dominant singularity is unique, the coefficients of
F(x,y)
satisfy

\displaystyle[x^{n}][y^{0}]F(x,y)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}C\cdot x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{-n}\cdot n^{-\alpha},\quad\mbox{ for some }x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}>0,\quad C>0\mbox{ \ \ and }\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}\backslash\{1,2,3,...\}.

(3)

This method has been applied with tremendous success for many combinatorial problems such as enumeration of many (many) classes of planar maps
[
9
]
, Tamari intervals
[
16
]
, description trees
[
22
]
or fighting fishes
[
34
]
just to name a few. In all these cases, the exponent
\alpha
popping-up in (
3
) is always either
\frac{3}{2}
, characteristic of “tree” enumeration, or
\frac{5}{2}
, characteristic of “planar map” enumeration. This surprising fact has been rigorously proved for equation (
1
) with positive coefficients by Drmota, Noy & Yu
[
33
]

in the case
{\mathrm{K}=1}
(see also
[
52
,
35
]
) and extended to the case
\mathrm{K}=2
, modulo an extra assumption in a technical tour de force by Drmota & Hainzl
[
31
]
. One of the outcome of our work is to extend this result for arbitrary discrete differences
\mathrm{K}\geqslant 1
:

Theorem 1
.

Let
F
be the solution to Equation (
1
) with a polynomial
Q
having positive coefficients. We suppose that
Q
obeys our standing assumptions on aperiodicity and branching conditions, see (*) below. Then there exists
x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}\in(0,\infty)
and
C>0
so that

[x^{n}][y^{0}]F(x,y)\sim C\cdot(x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}})^{-n}\cdot n^{-5/2},\quad\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

The conditions of branching and aperiodicity are described below in Section
2
. The branching assumption implies that the equation is non-linear, whereas the aperiodicity condition is roughly equivalent to the fact that the degree of the catalytic variable may increase and decrease and is not contained in a strict sub-lattice. Both conditions will be enforced in the rest of the paper. Contrary to the analytical approach taken in
[
33
,
31
]
our techniques to prove the above theorem are
probabilistic
and heavily rely on
random walks
and random trees as well as their scaling limits, the stable Lévy processes and self-similar Markov trees introduced in
[
7
]
. In particular, the variable
y
becomes an
explicative variable
instead of a discredited
catalytic one
.

Let us caricature the overall idea: A positive equation of the form (
1
) is naturally associated with an integer-type Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree, the (degree of the) catalytic variable
y
representing the (non-negative) label while the (degree of the) variable
x
accounts for the size of such trees. Such labeled trees have been encountered many times in the literature and especially recently in connection with the parking process on random trees. The fact that (
1
) involves only discrete differences implies that the evolution of labels along long branches of those integer-type Bienaymé–Galton–Watson are intimately connected to discrete random walks with i.i.d. increments. In particular, the scaling limits of such trees which is a self-similar Markov tree in the sense of
[
7
]
, must be intimately connected to stable Lévy process of exponent
\alpha-1
. Among all (distributions of) stable self-similar Markov trees this singles out the cases
\alpha\in\{3/2,5/2\}
. The case
\alpha=3/2
corresponding to the “subcritical” case where the underlying random tree is the Brownian Continuum Random Tree (CRT), while
\alpha=5/2
corresponds to the critical case
x=x_{c}
where the underlying random tree is the Brownian Growth-Fragmentation tree that shows up inside the Brownian sphere,
[
5
,
6
,
27
,
48
]
. Let us now develop our approach in more details:

Parking on trees and their scaling limits.

The first step is to interpret the function
F(x,y)
solving (
1
) for polynomial

with positive coefficient as the generating series of a model of

. The study of parking on trees has witnessed many developments in recent years in probability
[
1

18
,
20
,
21

]
and in combinatorics
[

]
after the pioneer work of Lackner & Panholzer
[
44
,
51
]
. In our case, the parking model we consider is as follows: Consider a finite rooted plane tree
\mathrm{t}
with two labeling functions

\ell^{\mathrm{car}}:\mathrm{Vertices}(\mathrm{t})\to\{0,1,2,...\}\quad\mbox{ and }\ell^{\mathrm{spot}}:\mathrm{Edges}(\mathrm{t})\to\{0,1,2,...\}.

The label
\ell^{\mathrm{car}}
is interpreted as
car arrivals on the vertices
of
\mathrm{t}
, whereas
\ell^{\mathrm{spot}}
are
parking spots capacities of the edges
. We then imagine that the cars arrive on their arrival vertex and go down the tree to park on the edges (according to their parking capacities) as soon as possible, see Figure

\mathbbm{t}=(\mathrm{t},\ell^{\mathrm{car}},\ell^{\mathrm{spot}})

is

if there are no available spots on the edges after the parking process. We shall only consider such situations below and for each

the

of cars at

\mathbf{t}=(\mathrm{t},\phi)\equiv(\mathrm{t},(\phi_{\mathbbm{t}}(u))_{u\in\mathrm{t}}),

the obtained
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
-labeled tree, see Figure

We shall denote by

\phi_{\mathbbm{t}}(\varnothing)=p

n
vertices in total. We shall now put a measure on those objects using weights. A

is collection of non-negative numbers

w_{c,k,(s_{1},...,s_{k})}\quad\mbox{ for }c,k,s_{1},...,s_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0},

and we write
w_{c,0,\varnothing}
when

.
This can be used to produce a sigma-finite measure
\mathrm{w}
on the set of all finite fully parked trees by setting

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}(\mathbbm{t})=\mathrm{w}(\mathrm{t},\ell^{\mathrm{car}},\ell^{\mathrm{spot}})=\prod_{u\in\mathrm{t}}w_{\ell^{\mathrm{car}}(u),k_{u}(\mathrm{t}),(\ell^{\mathrm{spot}}(e_{i}(u,\mathrm{t})))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k_{u}(\mathrm{t})}},

(4)

where
k_{u}(\mathrm{t})
is the number of children of
u
in
\mathrm{t}
and

and

, we form the

of the measure

\displaystyle W_{p}^{x}=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sum_{\mathbbm{t}\in\mathrm{FPT}_{p}^{n}}x^{n}\cdot\mathrm{w}(\mathbbm{t}).

(5)

A priori, this defines a family of formal power series
\in\mathbb{R}[\hskip-1.00006pt[x]\hskip-1.00006pt]

Remark 2

An underlying random walk.

As we said above, the key to our approach is to use a
random walk
hidden in the parking model on trees. Such random walks have already been identified in the enumeration of planar maps, see
[
26
]
, the inspiring works of Timothy Budd
[
12
,
13
]
or the monograph
[
23
, Section 5.1]
. Our work can be seen as a vast generalization and unification of those results. To describe this random walk, consider the random decorated tree
\mathbf{t}=(\mathrm{t},\phi)
under
\mathbb{P}^{x}_{p}
as
p\to\infty
. We prove in Section
3
that, among the children of the root vertex labeled
p
, typically only one of them has a label of order
p
, whereas the other ones are of order
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}_{p}^{x}}(1)
as
p\to\infty
. More precisely, we show in Proposition
9
that the difference between
p
and the maximal label of the children of the root converges in distribution under
\mathbb{P}_{p}^{x}
as
p\to\infty
towards a probability measure
\nu^{x}
which, under our standing hypotheses, is supported by
\{...,-3,-2,-1,0,1,...,\mathrm{K}\}
and satisfies, thanks to (
7
) that

\nu^{x}(-k)\sim\mathrm{Cst}^{x}\cdot k^{-\beta^{x}}\mbox{ as }k\to\infty,\quad\mbox{ for some }\mathrm{Cst}^{x}>0,

where
\beta^{x}
is the unknown exponent appearing in the asymptotic of
W_{p}^{x}
. Using an intrinsic property of the model, which can be thought of as an invariance under translation, we are able to describe the evolution of the labels along the locally largest branch of our trees as a
h
-transformation of a
\nu^{x}
-random walk where the function
h
is merely
W_{p}
itself. This is done in Proposition
10
and is named in these pages the “Key formula” because of its uttermost importance. For example, using the Key formula we are able to prove (without any calculations) that as soon as
\nu^{x}
has a finite first moment,
it must be centered
, so that random walks with i.i.d. increments of law
\nu^{x}
converge in the scaling limit towards the spectrally negative
(\beta^{x}-1)
-stable Lévy process. Passing then the Key formula to the scaling limit, we obtain an equation (see Eq. (
25
) below) involving the exponent
\beta^{x}
which enables us to pin-down its value:

\beta^{x}\in\left\{\frac{3}{2},\frac{5}{2}\right\}.

(8)

In fact, the case
\beta^{x}=\frac{3}{2}
corresponds to the subcritical case
x<x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}
whereas
\beta^{x}=\frac{5}{2}
corresponds to the critical case
x=x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}
, see Proposition
26
. We insist on the fact that although the inspiration is taken from the theory of self-similar Markov trees (see below), the derivation of (
8
) is self-contained and does not require prior knowledge on continuous random trees, but still uses fine properties of stable Lévy processes, in particular, their Lamperti representation.

Corollary 3

(Universality of asymptotics for partition functions)
.

Under our standing assumptions
(*)
, the functions
x\mapsto[y^{p}]F(x,y)
for
p\geqslant 0
have a common radius of convergence
x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}\in(0,\infty)
. For
x\in(0,x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}]
, if
y_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{x}
is the radius of convergence of
y\mapsto F(x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}},y)
then
y_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{x}\in(0,\infty)
and
F(x,y^{x}_{\text{\tiny{cr}}})<\infty
. Furthermore, for each
x\in(0,x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}]
there exists some constants
C^{x}>0
such that

\mbox{for }x<x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}},\quad W_{p}^{x}:=[y^{p}]F(x,y)\sim\mathrm{C}^{x}\cdot(y_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{x})^{-p}\cdot p^{-3/2},\quad\mbox{ as }p\to\infty,

\mbox{for }x=x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}},\quad W_{p}^{x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}}:=[y^{p}]F(x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}},y)\sim\mathrm{C}^{x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}}\cdot(y_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}})^{-p}\cdot p^{-5/2},\quad\mbox{ as }p\to\infty.

Self-Similar Markov trees.

At this point, the reader may think that the exponent
\beta^{x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}}=5/2
is the same as the exponent
\alpha
we are looking for in Theorem
1
, but this is merely a coincidence! The derivation of the exponent
\alpha
needs first to understand the typical
volume
of a tree under
\mathbb{P}_{p}^{x}
, or in analytic terms to understand
\partial_{x}W_{p}^{x}
. We in fact prove a full scaling limit result for the labeled trees using the powerful invariance principle recently established in
[
7
]
.
Indeed, the integer-type Galton–Watson trees discussed above exactly fall in the setting of the recent monograph
[
7
]
, and it is natural to conjecture that they converge in the scaling limit i.e. under
\mathbb{P}^{x}_{p}
and after rescaling of the distances by some power of
p
as
p\to\infty
to a natural self-similar Markov tree. A
self-similar Markov tree
is a random real rooted tree
(\mathcal{T},\rho)
given with a decoration
g:\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
which starts from
1
at the root
\rho
and is positive on its skeleton. Such trees come equipped with a natural mass measure
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
which is usually supported by the leaves of
\mathcal{T}
. Those random decorated trees enjoy both a Markov and a self-similar property and are believed to be all the possible scaling limits of multi-type Galton–Watson tree with type in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
. A special family of such random trees
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma}=(\mathcal{T}_{\gamma},g_{\gamma},\mu_{\mathcal{T}_{\gamma}})
was exhibited in
[
7
, Section 5.5]
in relation with spectrally negative stable processes of index
\gamma\in(0,2)
. In particular,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{1/2}
is nothing but a decorated version of the famous Brownian CRT of Aldous, and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{3/2}
is the Brownian growth-fragmentation tree which already appeared inside the Brownian sphere/disk and was conjectured to be the scaling limits of the parking trees appearing in our previous works
[
21
,
1
]
. Those two trees are in fact distinguished members of the family
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma}
since they are the only ones for which the evolution along “typical” branches is exactly given by a version of the
\gamma
-stable Lévy process. This striking property was first observed in a different context by Miller & Sheffield
[
49
]
, later made more explicit by Bertoin, Budd, Curien, Kortchemski in
[
5
, Section 5]
and finally explained in the context of self-similar Markov trees in
[
7
, Section 5.5]
to which we refer for details. Our Theorem
15
where
\beta^{x}
is proved to belong to
\{3/2,5/2\}
is directly inspired by those works.

Building on the fine properties of
W^{x}_{p}
established in Corollary
3
, we are able to apply the results of
[
7
, Chapter VI]
and show that indeed, the above multi-type Galton–Watson tree
\mathbf{t}
under the laws
\mathbb{P}_{p}^{x}
admit
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{1/2}
or
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{3/2}
as scaling limits. This can be seen as a universal
invariance principle for random fully-parked trees
where all distributions have bounded support. We write
\mu_{\mathbf{t}}
for the counting measure on the vertices of
\mathbf{t}
.

Theorem 4

(Universal self-similar limits for the fully parked trees)
.

Suppose
(*)
then we have

—

When
x<x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}
there exists some constant
s^{x},v^{x}>0
such that

\left(s^{x}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{t}}{p^{1/2}},\frac{\phi}{p},v^{x}\cdot\frac{\mu_{\mathbf{t}}}{p}\right)\mbox{ under }\mathbb{P}_{p}^{x}\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{(d)}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{1/2},

The proposition is a reformulation of the well-known

2.2
Positive system and standing assumptions

In all this work we shall suppose that
Q
and the local weight function are tighten as in the previous proposition. Equation (

W_{p}^{x}=xQ_{p}(x,W_{0}^{x},W_{1}^{x},...),\quad\mbox{ for }p\geqslant 0,

where

is a series with positive coefficients. Such systems of equations are very-well understood when there are finitely many of them, see
[
3
,
46
,
31
]
or the beautiful thesis of Hainzl
[
39
]
for more details. The salvation in our case will ultimately comes from a “translation invariance” property inherited from the parking model. In this section we prove that all partitions functions

have the same behavior at their singularity, yielding our first rough estimates.

Dependency graph.

We introduce the
dependency graph

\mathcal{G}_{Q}
of the system of functional equations on the
(W_{i}^{x})
induced by Equation (
1
), which is the oriented graph on the integers
\{0,1,2,...\}
constructed by putting an oriented edge going from
i
to
j
if in
W_{i}^{x}=[y^{i}]\left(xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)\right)
one can find a non-trivial term involving
W_{j}^{x}
. See Figure
2
for an illustration. In other words, the graph
\mathcal{G}_{Q}
contains an oriented edge from
i
to
j
if

\frac{\partial}{\partial W_{j}^{x}}[y^{i}]\left(xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)\right)\neq 0.

In particular this implies that
W_{i}^{x}\geqslant x^{d_{i\to j}}C_{i\to j}W_{j}^{x}
for some positive constant
C_{i\to j}
and some degree
d_{i\to j}\geqslant 1
.
Notice then an important property of our parking model (and thus of the system of functional equation), which can be thought of
translation invariance
and will eventually yield to the connection with random walks:

(trans.)

if there is a factor involving
W^{x}_{j}
in
[y^{i}]xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)
for
i,j\geqslant 0
, then we can find the exact same factor by replacing
W^{x}_{j}
with
W_{j+1}^{x}
in
[y^{i+1}]xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)
.

This is plain since in a fully parked tree, adding one to the flux of a descendant of a vertex automatically add
1
to the flux of this vertex. In particular, if the edge

in present in
\mathcal{G}_{Q}
, then all edges

are also present for

. As an example, if there is an oriented edge from

to some

and

q
to

where this second quantity can be made as large as we wish by taking

With this graphical construction at hands, one can now properly state our standing assumptions the local weight function denoted by (*) in the introduction:

Standing assumptions (*)

1.

(Exchangeability)
We assume that the local weight function is exchangeable in

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{c,k,(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})}:=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}w_{c,k,(s_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,s_{\sigma(k)})},

(Branching)
We assume that there exist

In particular the polynomial

4.

(Aperiodicity for the flux)

(Aperiodicity for the vertices)

Example 1

Example 2

(Planar maps)

A rooted planar map is a connected planar graph (loops and multiple edges are allowed) embedded into the sphere, and with one distinguished oriented edge called the root edge. The face that is left to the root edge is often called the exterior face and its length (number of edges adjacent to the face) is called the perimeter of the map. We denote by

By defining

\displaystyle N(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+x(y+1)^{2}N(x,y)^{2}+x(y+1)\frac{(y+1)N(x,y)-N(x,0)}{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+x(y+1)^{2}N(x,y)^{2}+x(y+1)\left(N(x,y)+\Delta^{(1)}N(x,y)\right),

We obtain an Equation of the form (

2.3
First properties of the generating function

using analytic combinatorics

We now gather a few basic properties of the partition functions

. Roughly speaking, we use standard analytic combinatorics arguments to prove that those functions behaves as
C\cdot\rho^{n}\cdot n^{-\beta}
for
\beta\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\backslash\{1,2,3,...\}
. The next sections will actually identify

as being

or

using a connection with an underlying random walk.

Recall the definition of

in the aperiodicity Assumption (

We start by proving the modest result below using our standing assumptions on the local weight function:

Lemma 6

(Introducing

)
.

We have the following:

—

The partition functions
(W_{p}^{x}:p\geqslant 0)
, as functions of

, are positive functions, and have a common finite radius of convergence
x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}\in(0,\infty)
. Furthermore,
W_{p}^{x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}}<\infty

.

—

For all
x\in(0,x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}]
, the function

has a finite and positive radius of convergence

and

(even for

).

Proof.

We start by considering the function
x\mapsto W_{p}^{x}
for
p\geqslant p_{0}
where we recall that the system is strongly connected for
p\geqslant p_{0}
. It implies that we can find a direct path
i_{0}:=p_{0}+1,i_{1},\dots,i_{k_{1}}:=p_{0}
from

to

, such that for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant k_{1}
, there exists

and

such that
W_{i_{j-1}}\geqslant c_{j}x^{\ell_{j}}W_{i_{j}}
. Writing
C_{1}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k_{1}}c_{j}
as well as

we deduce that
C_{1}x^{L_{1}}W^{x}_{p_{0}+1}\leqslant W^{x}_{p_{0}}
and performing the same reasoning with a path linking

to

yields the further existence of

such that

C_{1}x^{L_{1}}W^{x}_{p_{0}+1}\leqslant W^{x}_{p_{0}}\leqslant C_{2}x^{L_{2}}W^{x}_{p_{0}+1}.

In fact, our system is not only strongly connected, but it is also “translation invariant”. Indeed, if there is a factor involving
W^{x}_{p}
in
[y^{r}]xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)
for

, then the same factor involving

appears in
[y^{r+1}]xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)
.
In particular, for the same

as above, we have for all

and all

C_{1}x^{L_{1}}W^{x}_{p+1}\leqslant W^{x}_{p}\leqslant C_{2}x^{L_{2}}W^{x}_{p+1}.

(12)

This shows that the functions
(x\mapsto W^{x}_{p}:p\geqslant p_{0})
have the same (possibly infinite) radius of convergence

and the same behaviour at

. Moreover, for
0\leqslant p<p_{0}
, since they are no edge linking

to a smaller value in

, the function
W_{p}^{x}=[y^{p}]F(x,y)=[y^{p}]xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)
can be written as a finite combination of the
(W^{x}_{q}:q\geqslant p)
with positive coefficients. Furthermore, the only term involving
W_{p}^{x}
which might appear in
[y^{p}]xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)
are linear in
W_{p}^{x}
(with no other
(W_{q}:q\geqslant p)
). Indeed, if there is a term divisible by
W_{p}^{x}W_{q}^{x}
in
[y^{p}]xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)
, then the same term with replacing

by

appears in
[y^{p+1}]xQ(y,F,\Delta^{(1)}F,\ldots,\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F)
which implies that there is an edge in the dependency graph

from

to

. This means that there exists

such that

is a finite combination of the
(W^{x}_{q}:q>p)
with positive coefficients. Thus, the radius of convergence of
W_{p}^{x}
is the minimum between the radius of convergence of the
(W^{x}_{q}:q>p)

. But recall that our system is translation invariant, meaning that if

, and the whole family as the same radius of convergence.

Let us now prove that
x_{c}>0
. The first observation is that since the car arrivals, the number of children of a vertex and the number of spots on an edge are bounded by

\displaystyle W_{p}^{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}x^{n}\sum_{\mathbbm{t}\in\mathrm{FPT}_{n}^{p}}\mathrm{w}(\mathbbm{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}x^{n}\sum_{\mathbbm{t}\in\mathrm{FPT}_{n}^{p}}M^{n}\cdot\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{w}(\mathbbm{t})>0}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}x^{n}(4(\mathrm{K}+1)^{\mathrm{K}+2}M)^{n}.

In particular, the right-hand side is finite when
x<1/(4M(\mathrm{K}+1)^{\mathrm{K}+2})
, which proves that
x_{c}\geqslant 1/(4M(\mathrm{K}+1)^{\mathrm{K}+2})>0
.

To prove that
x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}
is finite, we use the branching assumption: We know that there exists
k\geqslant 2
and
(c,k,(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}))
such that
w_{c,k,(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})}>0.
Let
p\geqslant\max(c-s_{1},p_{0})
. Then by (
11
) we have

\displaystyle W_{p}^{x}\geqslant xw_{(c,k,(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}))}\cdot W_{p+s_{1}-c}^{x}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{k}W_{s_{i}}^{x}

Moreover, since
p+s_{1}-c
and
s_{2}
are connected via an oriented path to
p
in
\mathcal{G}_{Q}
, we can find an integer
n
and a constant
C
such that for all
x>0
we have

\displaystyle W_{p}^{x}\geqslant Cx^{n}(W_{p}^{x})^{2}.

In particular, for positive
x
and when
W_{p}^{x}
is finite we have

\displaystyle W_{p}^{x}\leqslant\frac{1}{C}x^{-n}.

Moreover, the function
x\mapsto W_{p}^{x}
is a non-zero series with positive coefficients. Thus there exists
C^{\prime}>0
and
m\geqslant 1
such that

W_{p}^{x}\geqslant C^{\prime}x^{m}.

This implies that
F(x,0)
can be finite only for the
x
such that
C^{\prime}x^{m}\leqslant Cx^{-n/(k-1)}.

In particular, the series
x\mapsto W_{p}^{x}
must have a finite radius of convergence
x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}<\infty
and also that
\lim_{x\to x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{-}}W_{p}^{x}<+\infty
so that
W_{p}^{x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}}<+\infty.

This proves the first item of the Lemma.

Let us now move to the second item and use similar ideas. Let us fix
x\in(0,x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}]
.
Again, on the one hand, we have
W_{p}^{x}>0
for all
p>0
, thus
F(x,y)\geqslant y^{p}W_{p}^{x}
. On the other hand, using again our branching assumption with the same notation as above, we have for all
p
large enough,

W_{p+c}^{x}\geqslant xw_{c,k,(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})}\cdot\sum_{q=0}^{p}\left(W_{q+s_{1}}^{x}W^{x}_{p-q+s_{2}}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=3}^{k}W_{s_{i}}^{x},

Summing over all possibilities for
p\geqslant 0
and recalling

we deduce that

y^{\mathrm{K}}\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F(x,y)+\sum_{i=0}^{\mathrm{K}-1}y^{i}W_{i}^{x}=F(x,y)\geqslant\mathrm{Cst}^{x}y^{d}\left(\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F(x,y)\right)^{2}.

Note that
\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F(x,y)\geqslant W_{\mathrm{K}}^{x}
. Thus, if
\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F(x,y)<\infty
, dividing the previous inequality by
y^{\mathrm{K}}\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F(x,y)
, we obtain

1+\frac{1}{W_{\mathrm{K}}^{x}}\sum_{i=0}^{\mathrm{K}-1}y^{i-\mathrm{K}}W_{i}^{x}\geqslant\mathrm{Cst}^{x}y^{d-\mathrm{K}}\Delta^{(\mathrm{K})}F(x,y).

Here the left-hand side tends to

as

tends to infinity, whereas the right-hand side is larger than any polynomial in

. We conclude as for the series in

that

must have a finite radius of convergence
y_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{x}
and must be finite at
y_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{x}
.
∎

Our next step (which is also folklore) is to use algebraicity and standard analytic combinatorics techniques to get a finer asymptotic on

Recall that by Proposition

Proof.

This proposition is a straightforward probabilistic reformulation of Tutte’s decomposition at the root proved in Proposition

.
∎

A word on canonical spaces.

In this work it will be convenient to deal with random variables of the same type (e.g. random trees, random discrete processes, random càdlàg processes…) but having slightly different laws. Instead of working on an abstract probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P},\mathcal{F})
and defining different random variables
X_{1},X_{2}...:\Omega\to E
having the different laws, we shall rather work on the canonical space
\Omega=E
(e.g.
E=\{\mbox{all finite plane labeled trees}\}
) endowed with its obvious Borel sigma-field and endow this canonical space with different probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2},...
under which the canonical random variable
\mathrm{Id}:\Omega\to\Omega
has the law of
X_{1},X_{2},...
. This will make many of our arguments more transparent to the cost of paying attention to the probability symbol first!

In the sequel
0<x\leqslant x_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}
is fixed and is dropped from notation for simplicity.

[0,t]

Proposition 10

(Key formula)

For any positive functional

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[f\Big(S|_{[0,t]},\eta|_{[0,t]}\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{S|_{[0,t]}\geqslant\mathrm{K}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\left[f\Big((S|_{[0,t]},\eta|_{[0,t]}\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{S|_{[0,t]}\geqslant\mathrm{K}}\cdot\frac{\widetilde{W}_{S_{t}}}{\widetilde{W}_{S_{0}}}\right].

Proof.

By the Markov property of both processes and the form of the above display, it is sufficient to prove the equality for
t=1
.
In that direction, fix
p\geqslant\mathrm{K}
,
q\geqslant\mathrm{K}-p
and fix
p+q>p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\geqslant 0
(so that the event
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is satisfied) and let us compute
\mathbb{P}_{p}(S_{1}=p+q\mbox{ and }Y^{1}_{j}=p_{j},\ j\geqslant 1)
using Proposition
8

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\mathbb{P}_{p}(S_{1}=p+q\mbox{ and }Y^{1}_{j}=p_{j},\ j\geqslant 1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k+1)\pi_{p}(p+q,p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k+1)\sum_{c\geqslant 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s_{0},\dots,s_{k})\\
{\forall i\geqslant 1,s_{i}\leqslant p_{i}\ \ p+q\geqslant s_{0}}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(p_{i}-s_{i})+c=s_{0}-q\end{subarray}}w_{c,k+1,(s_{0},\ldots,s_{k})}\cdot x\cdot\frac{W_{p+q}\cdot W_{p_{1}}\ldots W_{p_{k}}}{W_{p}}.

Notice that since
p+q\geqslant\mathrm{K}
the inequality

is always satisfied so that setting

\displaystyle\frac{W_{p+q}y_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{p+q}}{W_{p}y_{\text{\tiny{cr}}}^{p}}\cdot\hat{\nu}\big(q;*,(*),(p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{k})\big)=\frac{\widetilde{W}_{p+q}}{\widetilde{W}_{p}}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{p}^{\text{\tiny{RW}}}(S_{1}=p+q\mbox{ and }Y^{1}_{j}=p_{j},\ j\geqslant 1),

as desired. ∎

Before diving into the properties of the laws
\hat{\nu},\nu
let us explain why we named the above proposition the “Key formula”. It obviously links the locally largest exploration

under

to a much simpler process with i.i.d. increments under

\widetilde{W}_{p}
appear twice in the right-hand side of the key formula:

—

first implicitly in the law
\nu

of

which comes from the asymptotic of

in (

),

—

and second, it appears in the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{W}_{S_{t}}}{\widetilde{W}_{S_{0}}}
.

This double apparition will ultimately give an equation on

which will pin down its value in

. For this we need to pass the key formula to the scaling limit and perform a calculation using stable Lévy process. But in order to understand the scaling limits of
\nu

(as done in Proposition

Lemma 11

(No ties in the locally largest exploration.)
.

For all
p\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{p}\left(\exists\mbox{ tie at step $1$}\right)\lesssim p^{-\beta}.

In particular, for any
\delta,A>0
, then we have

\mathbb{P}_{p}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{\delta p}\wedge Ap^{\beta-1}}\big)\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}1.

Proof.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}(\exists\mbox{ tie at step $1$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}(\exists j\geqslant 1\mbox{ s.t. }Y_{j}^{1}=S_{1})

\displaystyle\underset{\mathrm{exch.}}{\lesssim}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}=p_{2}\geqslant p_{3},...,p_{k}\\
\sum_{i=3}^{k}p_{i}\in[p-2p_{1}-\mathrm{K},p-2p_{1}+\mathrm{K}^{2}]\end{subarray}}\pi_{p}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},...,p_{k})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}=p_{2}\geqslant p_{3},...,p_{k}\\
\sum_{i=3}^{k}p_{i}\in[p-2p_{1}-\mathrm{K},p-2p_{1}+\mathrm{K}^{2}]\end{subarray}}\frac{\widetilde{W}_{p_{1}}\cdots\widetilde{W}_{p_{k}}}{\widetilde{W}_{p}}

where the inequalities on
p_{i}
’s use the parking constraints (
15
). Recall from (
14
) that we must have
(p-\mathrm{K})/\mathrm{K}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant(p+\mathrm{K})/2
, and from the proof of Proposition

the fact that if

is large, the most probable scenario is that only one of the

\lesssim p^{\beta}\sum_{p_{1}=p_{2}\geqslant(p-\mathrm{K})/\mathrm{K}}p_{1}^{-\beta}p_{2}^{-\beta}(p-2p_{1})^{-\beta}\ \lesssim\ p^{-\beta}\sum_{i=0}^{p}i^{-\beta}\lesssim p^{-\beta}.

The second point follows from a union bound on all times

.
∎

Proposition 12

(Dichotomy (I), with drifts)

In the notation of Proposition

either
\beta\in(2,\infty)\backslash\{3,4,...\}

Proof.

Recall the notation
\tau_{p}

The easy claim is that by the law of large numbers, under

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\left(\Big(\tau_{\geqslant 3p/2}\Big)\wedge\Big(\tau_{p/2}\Big)\geqslant\frac{p}{|\mathbb{E}[\nu]|}\right)\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}0.

(22)

Furthermore, we claim that the event

(corresponds to a locally largest exploration with no ties up to time

\mathbb{P}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\left(\exists i\leqslant\theta_{p},1\leqslant j\leqslant K:Y_{i}^{j}\geqslant\frac{p}{3\mathrm{K}}\right)\lesssim p^{-\beta+2}\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\theta_{p}}\right)\ \geqslant\ \mathbb{P}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\left(\forall i\leqslant\theta_{p},1\leqslant j\leqslant\mathrm{K}\mbox{ we have }Y_{i}^{j}\leqslant\frac{p}{3\mathrm{K}}\right)\ \xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}1.

\displaystyle 1\xleftarrow[p\to\infty]{\mathrm{Lem.\ }\ref{lem:notiesll}}\quad\mathbb{P}_{p}(\mathcal{L}_{\theta_{p}})\underset{\mathrm{Prop.\ }\ref{prop:llrw}}{=}\mathbb{E}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{(S,\eta)\in\mathcal{L}_{\theta_{p}}}\frac{\widetilde{W}_{\theta_{p}}}{\widetilde{W}_{p}}\right]\quad\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{\eqref{eq:lundesdeux}}\begin{cases}\geqslant 2^{\beta}&\text{ if $\mathbb{E}[\nu]<0$}\\

4.1
Background on stable Lévy processes

Recall from Proposition

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(\gamma-1)}{\Gamma(2-\gamma)}\ \frac{\mathrm{d}r}{|r|^{\gamma+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{r<0},

The cases

Lemma 14

(Scaling limit of the decoration-reproduction processes)

Consider the rescaled processes
S^{(p)}_{t}=p^{-1}S_{[p^{\gamma}t]}
and the point measure
\eta^{(p)}=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\delta_{i/p^{\gamma},Y^{i}_{j}/p}

\left(S^{(p)},\eta^{(p)}\right)\mbox{ \ under }\mathbb{P}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\quad\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{(d)}\quad\Big((\mathfrak{c}\cdot\xi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\geqslant 0\\

where the convergence holds for the product topology with the Skorokhod topology for the first coordinate and the vague convergence of measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}

Proof.

The convergence of the first coordinate is granted by the above discussion. We thus need to show that the atoms of the measure are prescribed by the jumps of the process.
Recalling (
15

4.2
Identifying

\theta_{p}=\big(\tau_{\delta p}\big)\wedge Bp^{\gamma}.

Recall that by Lemma

\displaystyle 1=\lim_{p\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\left[\frac{\widetilde{W}_{S_{\theta_{p}}}}{\widetilde{W}_{p}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}_{\theta_{p}}}\right]\underset{\mathrm{Lem.\ }\ref{lem:scalinglimit}}{=}\mathbf{E}_{1}\left[\frac{(\xi_{\vartheta})^{-\beta}}{1}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{|\Delta\xi_{s}|<\frac{\xi_{s-}}{2}:\forall s\leqslant\vartheta\right\}\right],

\displaystyle\forall\delta\in(0,1),\qquad 1=\mathbf{E}_{1}\left[(\xi_{\tau_{\delta}})^{-\beta}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{|\Delta\xi_{s}|<\frac{\xi_{s-}}{2}:\forall s\leqslant\tau_{\delta}\right\}\right].

Theorem 15

(Lamperti’s magic)

Let
\beta\in(1,\infty)\backslash\{2,3,...\}

\gamma=(\beta-1)\wedge 2

\xi

\gamma

\mathbf{P}_{1}

\delta\in(0,1)

\tau_{\delta}=\inf\{s\geqslant 0:\xi_{s}\leqslant\delta\}

25

\beta=3/2
or
\beta=5/2

Before starting the proof let us explain how we will compute the RHS of (
25

25
) involves a local constraint which is scale invariant and only depends upon the process
\xi
killed at time
\tau_{0}
when it reaches negative values (since then the indicator function cannot be satisfied anymore). In particular, by the seminal work of Lamperti

\Big(x\cdot X_{t/x^{\gamma}}\Big)_{0\leqslant t\leqslant\tau_{0}\cdot x^{\gamma}}\mbox{ under }\mathbf{P}_{1}\quad\overset{(d)}{=}\big(X_{t}\big)_{0\leqslant t\leqslant\tau_{0}}\mbox{ under }\mathbf{P}_{x}.

This process can thus be seen, after time change, as the exponential of another Lévy process called
\zeta

\displaystyle\xi_{t}\mathbbm{1}_{t\leqslant\tau_{0}}=X_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{\zeta_{\kappa(t)}},\quad\mbox{ where}\quad\int_{0}^{\kappa(t)}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\zeta_{s}}\mathrm{d}s=t,

Recalling that under

5.1
Comparison of volumes

This section contains a lemma enabling us transfer back to
\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}
and
\widetilde{W}^{\bullet}_{p}
the knowledge we will acquire on
\mathrm{Vol}^{\circ}
and
\widetilde{W}^{\circ}_{p}
via random walk estimates. The take-away message being that
\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}
and
\mathrm{Vol}^{\circ}
are roughly proportional when large.

Lemma 18

(
\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}\preceq\mathrm{Vol}^{\circ}
)
.

There exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for
n\geqslant 1
large enough we have

\forall p\geqslant 0,\qquad\mathbb{P}_{p}(\mathrm{Vol}^{\circ}\geqslant n)\geqslant\mathbb{P}_{p}(\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}\geqslant\delta n)-\mathrm{e}^{-\delta n}.

We deduce from this lemma that the moments of
\mathrm{Vol}^{\circ}
and
\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}
are comparable up to constant, in particular they are simultaneously finite or infinite.

Proof.

We imagine that under
\mathbb{P}_{p}
we explore the underlying labeled tree
\mathbf{t}
vertex-by-vertex, say in the depth first order. Since the tree is a multitype Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree, this can be encoded in a Markov process
(\mathbb{X}_{k}:k\geqslant 0)
with canonical filtration
\mathcal{F}_{k}
where the current state is given by the finite list
\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{(\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant}0)}
of the labels of the vertices yet to be explored. The starting state is then
\mathbb{X}_{0}=\{p\}
under
\mathbb{P}_{p}
and when exploring a vertex, we remove the first element of the list
\mathbb{X}_{k}
and replace it with the list of the labels of its children (possibly empty) to get
\mathbb{X}_{k+1}
. The process stops at the first time the list
\mathbb{X}_{k}
becomes empty and since we are exploring one vertex at a time, this happens precisely at time
k=\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}
. We then claim that under our standing assumptions
(*)
, there exists
c>0
and an integer
N
such that for any time
k\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{discovering a vertex of label }\mathrm{K}\\
\mbox{ in the time intervalle }[k,k+N)\end{array}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k}\ \&\ \mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}>k\right)\geqslant c.

This is easy to see if the vertex to be explored is of large enough label, since then by Proposition
9
, there is a probability bounded away from
0
that the the left-most child of this vertex has label precisely
\mathrm{K}
(that is we take
N=1
). Otherwise, if the label of the vertex to explore is small, we claim that by aperiodicity consideration, there is a probability bounded away from
0
that when exploring in depth-first search we reach a vertex with large enough label within
N
steps so that the preceding reasoning applies.

To deduce the lemma and overcome the problematic correlation between
\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}
and the discovery of vertices of label
\mathrm{K}
, we decompose the exploration into time intervals
[kN,(k+1)N)
of length
N
, even after the absorption time, and for each
k\geqslant 0
, if
kN\leqslant\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}
we denote by
\epsilon_{k}=1
if we discover a vertex of label
\mathrm{K}
in this interval, and
0
otherwise. When
\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}<kN
, we complete this sequence by independent Bernoulli variables with parameter
c
. Using (
5.1
), it is easy to see that the sequence
(\epsilon_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
constructed this way always dominates a sequence of independent Bernoulli variables with parameter
c
. In particular, by large deviations estimates we have

\mathbb{P}_{p}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\leqslant\frac{c}{2}n\right)\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-\delta n},

for some
\delta>0
(independently of
p
). We can thus write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}(\mathrm{Vol}^{\circ}\geqslant\frac{c}{2}n)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}(\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}\geqslant nN)-\mathbb{P}_{p}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\epsilon_{i}\leqslant\frac{c}{2}n\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}(\mathrm{Vol}^{\bullet}\geqslant nN)-\mathrm{e}^{-\delta n}

as announced.
∎

5.4
Pointed exploration

If

In the case
\beta=5/2

large enough and all

\mathbb{P}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}_{p}(\tau_{p/2}\geqslant Ap^{3/2})\leqslant\mathrm{c}A^{-\mathrm{c}}.

Proof.

Recalling that

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}_{p}(\tau_{p/2}\geqslant Ap^{3/2})\leqslant\mathbb{P}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}_{p}(\tau_{\geqslant\sqrt{A}p}<\tau_{p/2})+\mathbb{P}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}_{p}(\tau_{p/2}\geqslant Ap^{3/2}\mbox{ and }\tau_{\geqslant\sqrt{A}p}>\tau_{p/2})

For the second term we argue that if

, then by Lemma
14
we have the existence of some
c>0
such that

\inf_{p/2\leqslant q\leqslant\sqrt{A}p}\mathbb{P}_{q}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\Big(\tau_{0}\leqslant\big(\sqrt{A}p\big)^{3/2}\Big)\geqslant\inf_{p/2\leqslant q\leqslant\sqrt{A}p}\mathbb{P}_{q}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}\Big(\tau_{0}\leqslant q^{3/2}\Big)\geqslant c.

In words, if the walk stays below
\sqrt{A}p
, in each time interval of length

it has a positive probability to drop below

. By successive applications of Markov property at time

for

we deduce that the second term in the above display is bounded above by
c^{A/(\sqrt{A})^{3/2}}=c^{A^{{1/4}}}
.

The first term is treated similarly. Again by the scaling limit result Lemma

we have the existence of some constant
c>0
such that

\liminf_{p\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{p}^{{\text{\tiny{RW}}}}(\tau_{\geqslant 2p}<\tau_{0})>c>0.

Again in words, once the walk has reached level
p
, it has some chance to drop below

before reaching the level

. Applying again the Markov property at the successive hitting times
\tau_{\geqslant 2p}<\tau_{\geqslant 4p}<\tau_{\geqslant 8p}...
(notice that those stopping times are distinct by (

Lemma 24

In both cases

\displaystyle p\cdot\mathbb{E}_{p}^{\circ}\left[(\mathrm{Vol}^{\circ})^{k}\right]

7
Comments

We end this work with some comments and possible generalizations.

The core of our approach is clearly the link with the
\nu
-random walk, in particular via the Key formula. In the case when
\mathrm{K}=1
, the
\nu
-random walk is skip-free ascending. Fluctuation theory for skip-free walks is much simpler than for general walks and this may again explain the particular role played by
\mathrm{K}=1
in the theory. Similarly, in absence of branching, that is for linear equation (
1
) then the step distribution
\nu
has a finite support making the analysis much less puzzling, see
[
45
,
46
]
. The link with random walk persists in more challenging cases: one could try to remove the bounded degree assumptions and allow analytic functions in (
1
) or even allow heavy tail and classify all possible universality classes. We also hope that the link with random walk may yield new information both in the discrete and in the continuous setting, for example shedding light on the beautiful result of Chapuy
[
16
]
obtained via analytic methods.
