Comment on “Prospective randomized clinical trial on comprehensive cardiac rehabilitation combined with individualized cognitive training in older adults with heart failure and mild cognitive impairment”
Authors: Ratchamarri Useni, Vincent Akshay Kumar, Rahul Kumar Thakur
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the trial protocol by Ishihara et al. [1], examining comprehensive cardiac rehabilitation combined with individualized cognitive training in older adults with heart failure and mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

The primary outcome measure overlaps almost entirely with the cognitive domains the intervention directly trains

The BrainHQ program trains attention, processing speed, memory, mental flexibility, and visuospatial ability, whereas the MoCA evaluates attention, executive function, memory, visuospatial ability, abstract reasoning, calculation, and orientation. Because the trained and tested domains are nearly identical, an improvement in the intervention group's score at discharge could reflect task-specific practice transfer to the assessment itself rather than a broader preservation of cognitive function with bearing on daily functioning [2]. This concern is compounded by administering the same instrument at baseline and again only two to three weeks later, a window short enough that item-level familiarity plausibly contributes to any observed gain, independent of true cognitive change [3]. No secondary or co-primary measure in the protocol assesses a cognitive or functional domain distinct from what BrainHQ trains; therefore, there is no way within this design to separate a genuine clinical benefit from a near-transfer training effect confined to the test itself [4]. A performance-based or informant-reported measure of everyday cognitive function, one that does not share item content or domain structure with the training tasks, would allow the trial to distinguish practice-driven score inflation from meaningful cognitive preservation.

The primary outcome window is not a fixed interval, yet exposure to both study arms scales directly with its length

The primary endpoint was the change in score from baseline to discharge, but the hospital length of stay was not fixed, standardized, or listed as a covariate in the analysis of covariance model, which adjusted only for baseline score, age, and left ventricular ejection fraction. Cognitive training is delivered for a minimum of two weeks but continues for as long as hospitalization lasts; therefore, a patient with a longer admission receives more total training sessions and more days of comprehensive cardiac rehabilitation exposure before the outcome is measured, while a patient discharged early receives less of both. If the length of stay differs systematically between groups for reasons related or unrelated to the intervention, the comparison at discharge conflates a true treatment effect with an exposure-duration effect that is not modeled. Reporting length of stay by group and including it as a covariate, or instead assessing the primary outcome at a fixed calendar time point common to all participants, would remove this ambiguity from the between-group comparisons.

This protocol addresses a genuinely underserved population, as prior acute heart failure rehabilitation trials have targeted physical function rather than cognition, and the prespecified intention-to-treat and per-protocol reporting structure is appropriately transparent. The clinical implication is direct: before a change in the Montreal Cognitive Assessment score is interpreted as prevention of in-hospital cognitive decline, the trial needs a cognitive or functional outcome independent of the trained domains and an accounting of how different lengths of stay contribute to the measured effect.
