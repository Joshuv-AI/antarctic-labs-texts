Existential thinking and religious/spiritual struggles in patients with major depressive disorder receiving cognitive-behavioral therapy
Authors: Szabolcs Kéri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExistential and spiritual issues may influence the symptoms and outcome of major depressive disorder (MDD). This study examined the interplay between existential thinking (fundamentals of meaning, purpose, and mortality), religious/spiritual (R/S) struggles (distress related to loss or questioning of faith, conversion to a new faith, or questioning of spiritual values), and mood/anxiety symptoms in individuals with MDD undergoing a 16-week course of cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT). Participants were MDD patients with and without R/S struggles, as well as healthy controls. Before and after CBT, we assessed depressive and anxiety symptoms, existential thinking, and R/S struggles by using standard scales and interviews. Results revealed that MDD patients with R/S struggles exhibited significantly higher levels of existential thinking than non-clinical control participants and MDD patients without R/S struggles both before and after CBT. While existential thinking did not change during CBT, depressive and anxiety symptoms decreased in all MDD patients. Critically, patients with DSM-5 R/S problems showed substantial reductions in R/S struggle scores during CBT. However, these values remained elevated compared to non-clinical controls and MDD patients without R/S struggles, even when the therapy was completed. Correlation analyses indicated positive associations between existential thinking, R/S struggles, and depression in MDD. These findings highlight that existential and spiritual dimensions are integral to a subtype of MDD and may require a tailored clinical approach in addition to conventional symptom-focused treatments.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Depression

Introduction

Mood disorders are a leading cause of disability worldwide1. Major depressive disorder (MDD), characterized by pervasive negative affectivity, a significant loss of interest and pleasure, and marked functional impairments, affects millions of individuals, with 12-month and lifetime prevalence rates recorded at 10.4% and 20.6%, respectively2. The etiology of MDD is multifaceted, involving genetic, neurochemical, environmental, and social factors3–5. Additionally, there has been a growing interest in exploring the role of existential and spiritual dimensions in the emergence of depressive symptoms6–9. These dimensions are frequently overlooked in clinical practice, even though they are necessary for a comprehensive understanding of depression and its impact on well-being and functional outcomes.

Existential thinking, which focuses on fundamental questions about meaning, purpose, and mortality in life, plays a vital role in mental health10,11. For many individuals, these existential inquiries are closely intertwined with religious and spiritual (R/S) quests, providing comfort and guidance12. However, for others, existential thoughts can become a source of internal conflict, manifesting as R/S struggles13–17. Existential concerns, such as death, isolation, and meaninglessness, are positively correlated with depression, with existential loneliness being the strongest predictor of low mood18,19. While religiosity does not moderate the relationship between existential thinking and mental health outcomes, purpose in life mediates the relationship between religiosity and depression20. In older adults, existential variables (e.g., personal meaning and choice responsibility) contribute uniquely to depression beyond traditional measures such as social resources and physical health21. Interestingly, existential anxiety mediates the relationship between the experience of meaning and depression severity, suggesting that it may be an essential underlying mechanism in depressive disorders22. However, we distinguish R/S struggles (experiences of tension, conflict, or strain centered on the sacred, e.g., divine anger and abandonment, doubt, moral conflicts, ultimate meaning, and interpersonal tensions) from existential thinking, which is a trait-like orientation that reflects on meaning, purpose, mortality, and responsibility. R/S struggles are typically distress-laden and may fluctuate with life events and treatment, whereas ET is not inherently pathological and may serve adaptive or maladaptive functions depending on its link to reflective meaning-making versus ruminative brooding10,11,14,23.

R/S struggles can profoundly shape an individual’s psychological landscape24,25. R/S problems are often associated with questioning religious traditions and dogmas, as well as sociocultural and relational problems related to converting to a new faith or deconversion (loss of faith in a religion). However, the interplay between existential concerns, R/S struggles, and depressive symptoms remains an underexplored issue23. Successfully resolving R/S struggles can foster growth, resilience, and a deeper spiritual understanding26,27. When unresolved or persistent, R/S struggles are often associated with adverse psychological outcomes, including anxiety, distress, and depressive symptoms28,29. In individuals with MDD, R/S struggles may exacerbate depressive symptoms, such as feelings of worthlessness, guilt, and hopelessness30. The mechanisms underpinning this relationship may involve a bidirectional loop, where depressive symptoms fuel existential and spiritual doubts, and these doubts, in turn, intensify depressive symptoms31. Individuals who perceive their suffering as incompatible with their religious or spiritual worldview may experience a deep sense of dissonance, which can amplify depressive symptoms. Similarly, those who interpret their depression as a punishment from a higher power may live through self-blame, shame, and distress14,32. Conversely, individuals with existential resilience, characterized by the ability to find meaning and coherence even in adversity, may cope with R/S issues with emotional stability, potentially buffering against depressive symptoms26,27.

The intersection of existential thinking and R/S struggles in the context of MDD has not been explored sufficiently. Existential thinking often emerges in response to adverse life events and crises, challenging individuals to confront questions about the purpose of their suffering, their place in the world, and their capacity for hope11. Existential reflection can provide a pathway to healing, fostering insight, new values, attributions, and attitudes33. However, it can also lead to rumination and despair, particularly when coupled with unresolved R/S struggles14. Understanding the conditions under which existential thinking serves as a protective factor versus a risk factor for depression is essential for developing tailored therapeutic interventions.

The clinical implications are significant. Traditional psychotherapeutic approaches, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and interpersonal therapy, often address cognitive distortions and relational dynamics but may overlook the existential and spiritual dimensions34. Emerging treatment modalities, such as existential therapy, spiritually integrated psychotherapy, and mindfulness-based interventions, emphasize the importance of engaging with existential and spiritual concerns13,35. However, incorporating these approaches into standard care requires an understanding of the nuanced relationship between existential thinking, R/S struggles, and depressive symptoms. It is also unclear how standard CBT for MDD may affect existential thinking and R/S struggles.

The present study aims to bridge this gap by examining the interplay between existential thinking, R/S struggles, and depressive/anxiety symptoms in individuals with MDD. We postulated the following hypotheses: (1) MDD patients with R/S struggles score higher on scales measuring existential thinking, depression, and anxiety than MDD patients without R/S struggles; (2) there is a positive correlation between existential thinking, R/S struggles, and the intensity of depressive symptoms; (3) there is a reduction of clinical symptoms, but not existential thinking and R/S struggles during a standard psychological intervention (CBT).

Methods

General design

The study focused on how R/S struggles and existential thinking change during psychological therapy for MDD in relation to symptom changes. We assessed R/S struggles and existential thinking using standard scales before and after a psychological intervention (16 sessions of CBT, one individual session/week) and measured clinical symptoms before and after CBT. A secondary objective was to evaluate existential thinking in MDD patients with or without R/S struggles as a categorical distinction.

Participants

We enrolled the patients with MDD (54 individuals without R/S problems and 32 with R/S problems) from 21 outpatient psychiatric centers and primary care services (National Psychiatric Center, Budapest; University of Szeged, Szeged, Hungary). We used the following inclusion criteria: fulfilling the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) criteria of MDD; age: 18–65 years; no evidence for lifetime psychiatric comorbidities (e.g., bipolar disorder, psychotic disorders, neurodevelopmental disorders, and substance misuse); no psychotropic medications before the study. The exclusion criteria included neurological disorders, head injury, and general medical conditions affecting the central nervous system. As described later, R/S struggles were measured dimensionally using the Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale (RSS-14) and categorically using the DSM-5 V62.89 “Religious or Spiritual Problem” category14,36.

We also enrolled 50 control participants without a history of mental disorders or central nervous system diseases. We used social media advertisements to recruit control volunteers from the general community with demographic features similar to those of patients with MDD. All participants were Caucasian (Hungarian) and declared themselves cisgender (he/him and she/her). We considered age, education, sex, religious affiliation, and socioeconomic status in our samples. The socioeconomic status was measured with the Kuppuswamy Scale (classes based on education, occupation, and income: < 5: lower class, 5–10: lower middle class, 11–15: middle class, 16–25: upper middle class, 26–29: upper class) (Table 1). The non-clinical comparison group was included to obtain baseline normative and reference values for existential thinking (Scale for Existential Thinking, SET) and R/S struggles (Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale, RSS), as well as to measure the stability of these scale scores over the study interval when no intervention was provided37,38.

Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Clinical evaluation of diagnosis and symptoms

All participants, including the patients with MDD and the control volunteers, received the structured clinical interview for DSM-539. Symptoms associated with mood disorders and anxiety were assessed with the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) and the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A)40–44. The DSM-5 and the HAM-D/HAM-A interviews were administered by trained clinical psychologists blind to the study’s aim.

The HAM-A consists of 14 items, each rated from 0 (not present) to 4 (severe) (total score: 0–56). The scale evaluates psychic anxiety (mental and psychological distress) and somatic anxiety (physical symptoms). Scoring ranges: 0 to 17—mild anxiety, 18 to 24—moderate anxiety, and 25 to 30—severe anxiety40.

The HAM-D consists of 17 items (e.g., mood, guilt, suicide ideation, insomnia, anxiety, and weight loss). Each item is scored on a scale of 0 to 2 or 0 to 4 (maximum score: 52). Scoring ranges: 0 to 7—normal or no depression; 8 to 13—mild depression; 14 to 18—moderate depression; 19 to 22—severe depression; > 23—very severe depression43.

R/S problems

R/S problems were assessed both categorically (presence vs. absence of the problem) and dimensionally (severity of the problem). We used the structured clinical interview for DSM-539 and the Cultural Formulation Interview36. Thirty-two patients with MDD meet the DSM-5 definition of “Problems related to other psychosocial, personal, and environmental circumstances, Religious or Spiritual Problem” [code: V62.89]36. The definition of the DSM-5 R/S problem is the following: “This category can be used when the focus of clinical attention is a religious or spiritual problem. Examples include distressing experiences that involve loss or questioning of faith, problems associated with conversion to a new faith, or questioning of spiritual values that may not necessarily be related to an organized church or religious institution.”36.

We administered the Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale (RSS) to measure the severity of R/S problems45,46. The RSS focuses on six domains of struggles: divine (feelings of anger or disappointment toward a higher power), demonic (experiences of feeling attacked or oppressed by evil forces), interpersonal (conflicts or tensions with others regarding religious or spiritual matters), moral (internal conflicts about right and wrong or ethical dilemmas), doubt (uncertainty or skepticism about one’s religious or spiritual beliefs), and ultimate meaning (existential concerns about the purpose and meaning of life). The 14 items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“a great deal”) (score range: 14–70). The Hungarian version of the full scale exhibited high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.86). However, we failed to find evidence for the six-factor structure in three independent samples by confirmatory factor analysis. Therefore, in the present study, we used the total scores.

Existential thinking

The Scale for Existential Thinking (SET) is an 11-item self-report measure designed to assess an individual’s existential concerns, such as the meaning of life and personal beliefs (sample items: “I often think about the meaning of life.”; “I contemplate my beliefs and values regularly.”)38. Each item is rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1: strongly disagree; 6: strongly agree). This yields a total score ranging from 11 to 66, with higher scores indicating a greater tendency toward existential thinking. The SET has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.82) and reliability.

Treatment intervention

Similar to our previous studies47–49, the patients with MDD participated in 16 individual sessions of CBT (one session/week, duration 1.5 h). The CBT sessions were administered by board-certified clinical psychologists or psychiatrists with extensive training and experience (> 10 years) and regular supervision (one supervision session/week). We measured the clinicians’ adherence to the treatment protocol by videotaping and independent rating. The treating clinicians were not aware of the R/S-status of the patients.

Data analysis

We used JASP50 and Spotifre® Data Science Workbench 14.2.0 (Tibco) for statistical analysis. Following standard descriptive statistics and data quality checks (normality of distribution and homogeneity of variance), scale scores were entered into repeated measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs). Covariance analyses (ANCOVAs) were also conducted, including age, sex, education, and socioeconomic status as covariates. We used post-hoc comparisons between the groups and time points (Holm-corrected p-values). Effect size values (Cohen’s d for between-group comparisons and η2 for ANOVAs) were also determined. Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficients (rs) were calculated between the scale scores. The level of statistical significance was set at α < 0.05.

Sample size calculations indicated that for repeated measures and between–within interactions, a minimum of 30 participants in each group is required to achieve 80% power for detecting an interaction with an effect size of f = 0.25.

As a secondary analysis, each statistically significant effect was re-tested using the Bayesian method (Bayesian t-tests and correlations). We determined the Vovk-Sellke Maximum p-Ratio (lower bound of the Bayes Factor), which is the maximum possible odds in favor of H₁ over H₀ equals 1/(-e p log(p )) for p ≤ 0.37. We also calculated the Bayes Factors (BF10) to characterize the level of evidence (alternate hypothesis: H1; null hypothesis: H0). The evidence ranges were as follows: BF10 = 1: no evidence for H1; 1 < BF10 < 3: weak evidence for H1; 3 < BF10 < 10: moderate evidence for H1; BF10 > 10: strong evidence for H150.

Results

Existential thinking and R/S struggles

This section of the data analysis presents the differences between community controls and MDD patients with and without R/S struggles in existential thinking (SET cores) and R/S struggles (RSS scores) at baseline and follow-up assessments (before and after CBT in patients, no intervention in controls).

The repeated-measures ANOVA conducted on the SET scores revealed a significant main effect of group (F(2,133) = 10.59, p < 0.001). The effect of treatment intervention and the interaction between the group and intervention were insignificant (p = 0.8 and p = 0.2, respectively).

A post-hoc comparison of the significant main effect of the group indicated no significant differences between the community controls and MDD patients without R/S problems in SET scores (p = 0.5, d = 0.14). Meanwhile, MDD patients with R/S problems achieved higher SET scores relative to the controls (p < 0.001, d = 0.83, BF10 = 61.0) and relative to MDD patients without R/S problems (p < 0.001, d = 0.98, BF10 = 197.7) (Table 2).

Clinical symptoms

This section of the data analysis presents the differences between MDD patients with and without R/S struggles (group main effect) in depressive symptoms (HAM-D) and anxiety (HAM-A) before and after the treatment intervention (CBT).

In the case of HAM-D scores, the group had no significant main effect (p = 0.6), but the treatment intervention had a significant effect (F(1,84) = 144.64, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.31). The two-way interaction between the group and intervention did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.5). The key finding from the post-hoc analysis was that the HAM-D scores markedly decreased in both MDD patients without R/S struggles (p < 0.001, d = 1.3, BF10 = 1.3 × 1010) and MDD patients with R/S struggles (p < 0.001, d = 1.5, BF10 = 1.7 × 106) (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study explored how existential thinking, R/S struggles, and depressive/anxiety symptoms interact in patients with MDD undergoing psychological therapy (CBT). The results indicated that MDD patients with R/S struggles display significantly higher levels of existential thinking than non-clinical control individuals and MDD patients without R/S struggles, and their heightened existential cognition persists even after CBT. As expected, R/S struggles were substantially elevated at baseline in the subgroup of patients with DSM-5 spiritual and religious problems, but these struggles significantly decreased following CBT. Interestingly, we did not find evidence for more severe depression or anxiety in MDD patients with R/S struggles compared to MDD patients without R/S struggles.

Our findings are consistent with the view that existential thinking primarily reflects a stable orientation toward ultimate life questions, which can be adaptive when coupled with reflective meaning-making and purpose, yet maladaptive when linked to brooding rumination. By contrast, R/S struggles index conflictual content about the sacred that is more state-sensitive and responsive to intervention. This helps explain why RSS decreased in MDD patients with R/S struggles, whereas existential thinking remained stable.

Depressive and anxiety symptoms improved significantly in all MDD patients, regardless of their R/S status, indicating similar treatment responses in patients with and without R/S problems when standard psychological therapy is used. The fact that we found no correlations between changes in depressive symptoms and changes in R/S struggles during the psychological intervention indicates the partial independence of clinical symptoms and spiritual experiences. Nevertheless, it cannot be excluded that depression itself may increase religious and spiritual thoughts, which might confound the interpretation of the results.

The robust reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms alongside a selective decline in R/S struggles but stable existential thinking are consistent with meta-analytic evidence that meaning-related resources and spiritual/existential distress are partly dissociable. Across 99 samples, a greater sense of purpose in life showed moderate inverse associations with both depression and anxiety (r = − 0.49 and r = − 0.36, respectively), implying that bolstering purpose may be a particularly relevant target when existential concerns are prominent51. Complementing this, a recent meta-analysis distinguished between the presence of meaning (consistently linked to lower psychological distress) and the search for meaning (often linked to higher distress), underscoring why simply thinking more about meaning need not be therapeutic unless it culminates in sustained presence and purpose52. In our data, existential thinking exhibited a more trait-like orientation, whereas R/S struggles functioned as a more state-sensitive index of conflict, decreasing during therapy. Shifting an existential orientation may require interventions that explicitly cultivate a sense of meaning and purpose.

These findings challenge traditional views of MDD by demonstrating that existential and spiritual dimensions are not merely tangential or epiphenomena but are integral to the symptom presentation and treatment response of some patients. In much of standard clinical practice, depressive symptomatology is generally conceptualized through cognitive-behavioral, psychodynamic, or biomedical models, with relatively little attention given to existential and spiritual questions53.

Our results refine the understanding of spiritual and existential dimensions in MDD. Earlier work suggests that existential distress (e.g., fears of meaninglessness, isolation, and mortality) can amplify depressive symptoms19,22. Likewise, unresolved R/S struggles have been associated with increased psychological distress14,24. The present data confirm that a subset of individuals with MDD experience R/S issues and that these issues hold clinical relevance. The persistent nature of existential thinking aligns with research showing that these thoughts are often deeply ingrained and may require psychological interventions explicitly focused on meaning, purpose, and spiritual integration16,54. Traditional CBT effectively targets maladaptive cognitions and behavioral patterns55, yet it generally does not directly address cognition that underpins existential thinking. Other modalities, such as existential therapy, meaning-centered therapy, or spiritually integrated psychotherapy, might be more effective in helping patients transform their existential worldviews56–58. As negative affectivity is diminished and coping abilities are strengthened, individuals may engage productively with moral and spiritual questions59,60.

For religiously affiliated patients, persistent R/S struggles (e.g., punitive God representations, unresolved doubt/moral injury) may benefit from spiritually integrated psychotherapy and targeted work on negative religious coping, ideally coordinated with trained chaplaincy or faith leaders as appropriate. For non-religious patients or those drawing meaning from secular sources, existential or meaning-centered approaches can cultivate the presence of meaning, values, and self-transcendence without requiring religious commitments. Working models in both streams exist and can be blended with CBT to address existential/spiritual dimensions alongside symptom change61–64. Moreover, Meaning-Centered Psychotherapy has been shown to improve spiritual well-being and reduce distress in randomized trials, outperforming supportive controls and producing durable gains over follow-up periods65,66. A meta-analysis of existential therapies further indicates large effects on meaning and small to moderate effects on psychopathology, especially for structured, meaning-focused protocols67. For patients whose depressive episodes are intertwined with sacred tensions, R/S integrated versions of bona fide therapies are viable: a comprehensive meta-analysis shows that R/S tailored psychotherapy yields greater improvements in psychological and spiritual functioning than non-tailored approaches68. A second meta-analysis, focusing on faith-adapted CBT, suggests potential advantages over standard CBT69. Finally, value-based therapies such as Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, which explicitly translate meaning and purpose into committed action, are as effective as standard treatments for depression, making them strong candidates when existential issues are central70. Taken together, these literatures suggest that augmenting standard CBT with meaning-centered or R/S-integrated modules may be a pragmatic approach to addressing the enduring existential dimensions highlighted by our study.

The strength of this study lies in its multifaceted assessment of clinical, existential, and spiritual experiences and beliefs, which enables an in-depth examination of how these dimensions intersect in MDD. By comparing MDD patients with and without R/S struggles and including healthy controls, the study clarifies that heightened existential thinking is not merely a function of having MDD but is linked explicitly to spiritual conflict. A pre-post-treatment design further highlights which factors can be changed during a standard psychological intervention. It is essential to emphasize that the present study is insufficient to prove that the detected changes during psychological therapy are a specific effect of CBT, as we did not include a control arm (e.g., a separate group of patients receiving a different type of psychological therapy).

Nonetheless, certain limitations must be noted. The study used CBT, a therapy not explicitly designed to address spiritual or existential content, which narrows the scope of the study. Thus, whether existential thinking or R/S struggles would respond differently to specialized interventions remains to be elucidated. Reliance on self-report measures may also introduce subjective biases, and factor structure issues in the R/S struggles measure prevented a more detailed examination of specific types of spiritual conflict. Given the absence of a randomized control arm, these within-patient changes cannot be attributed causally to CBT.

Moreover, the cultural and demographic homogeneity of the sample limits generalizability. Existential and spiritual dimensions of mental health can vary widely across cultures and religious traditions, and future research should examine more diverse populations. Further investigations could also incorporate qualitative methods, spiritual/existential scales, and longitudinal designs with control arms (e.g., different psychological treatments) to better understand how changes in existential or spiritual dimensions unfold over time and differ with various therapeutic approaches. Finally, we did not measure perceived religious support (e.g., support from clergy, congregation, and family) or positive religious coping.

In summary, this study provides insights into the interplay of existential and spiritual factors with MDD. While CBT may effectively reduce depressive and anxiety symptoms and can alleviate R/S struggles, existential thinking remains unchanged, pointing to the need for alternative treatments that directly address these concerns. Incorporating spiritual and existential dimensions into mental health care holds promise for a more comprehensive understanding of depressive disorders. It may yield enduring relief for those grappling with profound existential questions accompanying human suffering.
