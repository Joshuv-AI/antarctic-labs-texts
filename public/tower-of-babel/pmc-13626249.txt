Anterior tumour location as a risk factor for local recurrence after laparoscopic surgery for stage I ultra-low rectal cancers: exploratory analysis of the Ultimate trial
Authors: Atsushi Kohyama, Shinobu Ohnuma, Yuichiro Tsukada, Michiaki Unno, Jun Watanabe, Yosuke Fukunaga, Yasumitsu Hirano, Kazuhiro Sakamoto, Hiroki Hamamoto, Masanori Yoshimitsu, Hisanaga Horie, Nobuhisa Matsuhashi, Yoshiaki Kuriu, Shuntaro Nagai, Madoka Hamada, Shinichi Yoshioka, Tamuro Hayama, Koki Otsuka, Yusuke Inoue, Kazuki Ueda, Yuji Toiyama, Satoshi Maruyama, Shigeki Yamaguchi, Keitaro Tanaka, Motoko Suzuki, Toshihiro Misumi, Takeshi Naitoh, Masahiko Watanabe, Masaaki Ito, Ultimate Trial Group in the Japan Society of Laparoscopic Colorectal Surgery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnterior tumour location is an established risk factor for local recurrence (LR) in advanced rectal cancer, but its significance in stage I ultra-low rectal cancer remains unclear.MethodsThis exploratory supplemental analysis used data from Ultimate trial, a multicentre prospective phase II study across 47 Japanese institutions (2014–2017). Patients with cT1–2 N0 M0 ultra-low rectal cancer (≤ 5 cm from the anal verge) underwent laparoscopic resection. Tumour location was categorized as anterior (10–2 o’clock) or non-anterior (2–10 o’clock). The primary endpoint was LR; secondary endpoints included recurrence-free survival, overall survival, and stoma-free survival.ResultsOf 300 enrolled patients, 297 were analysed (median follow-up 37.2 months); 93 tumours (31.3%) were anterior. Baseline characteristics, operative outcomes, and pathological findings were similar between the two groups. The 3-year cumulative LR rate was 11.9% for anterior tumours and 3.0% for non-anterior tumours (hazard ratio (HR) 4.46; 95% confidence interval (c.i.) 1.67 to 11.89; P = 0.001). Multivariable analysis showed an independent association between anterior location and LR (HR 4.68; 95% c.i. 1.72 to 12.74); subgroup analysis revealed no difference in clinical (c) T1 patients, but a significantly higher LR rate for anterior tumours in cT2 patients (25.3% versus 4.7%; P < 0.001). Three-year recurrence-free survival (83.9% for anterior tumours versus 88.9% for non-anterior tumours; P = 0.222), overall survival (> 95%), and stoma-free survival were comparable between the two groups.ConclusionAnterior tumour location in the ultra-low rectum was associated with an increased risk of LR, particularly in cT2 patients, highlighting the need for a more refined treatment strategy and vigilant monitoring.Keywords: total mesorectal excision, intersphincteric resection, low anterior resection, abdominoperineal resection, resection margin

Introduction

Laparoscopic surgery is a widely accepted minimally invasive approach for rectal cancer. Randomized clinical trials1–3, including the COLOR II trial4, have demonstrated that laparoscopic rectal surgery achieves equivalent safety, resection margins, and oncological outcomes to open surgery. The Ultimate trial5 recently reported favourable long-term outcomes, high anus-preservation rates, and satisfactory postoperative anal function following laparoscopic resection for clinical (c) T1/T2 ultra-low rectal cancers located within 5 cm of the anal verge (AV). For such early-stage tumours, preoperative chemoradiotherapy is generally not indicated, and total mesorectal excision remains the standard curative procedure.

However, despite technical advances and improved visualization, local recurrence (LR) after curative resection remains a major clinical concern. Reported LR rates after laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery range from 5% to 12% in randomized trials6–8, and remain at approximately 4% even among patients with pathological (p) T1/T2 disease who undergo local excision (LE) followed by completion total mesorectal excision9. Accordingly, neither neoadjuvant radiotherapy nor chemoradiotherapy is used in the management of early-stage rectal cancer.

Tumour location within the rectum is an important determinant of recurrence. Lower rectal cancers exhibit higher LR rates than upper or middle rectal tumours10–12, and emerging evidence suggests that the circumferential orientation within the rectum further influences oncological outcomes. Anteriorly situated tumours are associated with higher LR and poorer survival10,13, possibly due to the thin anterior mesorectal tissue and the close proximity of the prostate, urethra, or vagina, which increases the likelihood of a positive circumferential resection margin (CRM) or incomplete mesorectal excision. Most existing data are from patients with advanced disease treated with multimodality therapy10–13, but and the relevance of these findings to early-stage rectal cancer is unclear.

The present study investigated whether anterior tumour location represents an independent risk factor for LR in patients with cT1/T2 stage I ultra-low rectal cancer undergoing laparoscopic total mesorectal excision.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was an exploratory supplemental analysis of the Ultimate trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of laparoscopic surgery for ultra-low rectal cancer. Patients were recruited from 47 specialized institutes in Japan between January 2014 and March 2017, with follow-up continuing until 20215. This study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000011750).

Eligible patients were adults (≥ 20 years) with histologically confirmed rectal cancer located within 5 cm of the AV or within 3 cm of the dentate line (DL), clinically staged as T1–2 N0 M0. Clinical staging was determined by colonoscopy, chest–abdominal–pelvic computed tomography with slice thickness < 10 mm, trans-anal ultrasonography, or magnetic resonance imaging. Patients who were not candidates for LE or who required additional surgery with lymphadenectomy after LE were included in the study. The exclusion criteria included having another malignancy within the past 5 years, conversion to open surgery before resection, intraoperative detection of distant metastasis, inadequate follow-up, and receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.

Tumour location, determined by rigid rectoscopy, endoscopy, or digital rectal examination, was classified before surgery according to the area of deepest infiltration: tumours between the 10 and 2 o’clock positions were defined as anterior, whereas those between the 2 and 10 o’clock positions were defined as non-anterior. Tumour distance from the AV and DL was determined by the same method both before and during surgery.

Registration

All eligible patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were registered before surgery by calling the registration office at the National Cancer Center Hospital East (Chiba, Japan). An eligibility report form was sent to the data centre at the clinical trial coordinating office at the National Cancer Center Hospital East.

Quality control, procedures, and follow-up

Only experienced surgeons who had performed more than 30 laparoscopic and 30 open operations for rectal carcinoma were accredited by the study chair to participate in this study. A centralized photographic review of the surgical procedure was performed for all patients. This included one image of the pelvic floor after total mesorectal excision and, in patients undergoing intersphincteric resection (ISR), an additional image documenting the level of the mucosal incision during the transanal phase5. Laparoscopic procedures included laparoscopic low anterior resection (LAR), ISR, Hartmann surgery, and abdominoperineal resection (APR). All surgical procedures were performed as described previously14,15. Laparoscopic dissection was performed down to approximately the lower two-thirds level of the prostate or vagina. When further dissection was required during ISR or APR, it was performed via a perineal approach under direct visualization. Briefly, laparoscopic resection of the rectum with adequate lymphadenectomy was performed. The extent of the lymphadenectomy, the ligation site, and division of the inferior mesenteric vessels were determined by the surgeon. Rectal mobilization, mesorectal excision, rectal transection, specimen removal, and reconstruction were performed using a pneumoperitoneal or extracorporeal approach via a < 8-cm incision. Bowel anastomoses were performed intracorporeally via a small incision using the double-stapling technique or transanal hand-sewn sutures5. To assess potential institutional differences, hospitals with more than 11 registered patients were classified as high-volume centres, and those with ten or fewer patients were classified as low-volume centres; treatment outcomes were compared between the two groups.

For sphincter-preserving operations, the decision to perform a protective ileostomy was based on the surgeon’s technical evaluation of anastomosis quality. Faecal diversion refers to a temporary diverting stoma created after sphincter-preserving surgery. Stoma creation at initial surgery refers to any stoma constructed during the index operation, including both temporary diverting stomas and permanent stomas associated with APR or Hartmann’s surgery. Conversion was defined as requiring an abdominal incision exceeding 8 cm to manage intraoperative complications or tumour extension. Surgical methods and pathology results were recorded according to the Japanese Classification of Colon and Rectal Carcinoma (seventh edition)16 and the TNM classification (sixth edition)17. A macroscopically adequate dissection margin was defined as the absence of gross tumour exposure along the dissection plane, as assessed intraoperatively by the operating surgeon. A pathological resection margin was classified as R0 when no tumour exposure was identified pathologically and as R1 when tumour exposure was identified along any resection margin.

For postoperative surveillance, blood tests, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), were performed every 3 months; chest–abdominal–pelvic computed tomography scans were obtained every 6 months; and a colonoscopy was conducted at 1, 3, and 5 years after surgery. LR was defined as tumour regrowth within the pelvis.

Outcomes of interest

The primary aim of this study was to determine whether anterior tumour location is an independent risk factor for LR after laparoscopic surgery in patients with stage I ultra-low rectal cancer. Secondary endpoints were 3-year recurrence-free survival (RFS), overall survival (OS), and stoma-free rates between anterior and non-anterior tumours and in subgroup analyses according to cT category.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 16.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables (age, body mass index, tumour size, distance from the AV/DL, CEA, CA19-9, operation time, blood loss, and length of hospital stay) are expressed as the median with range (minimum–maximum) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Follow-up duration is reported as the median with interquartile range (i.q.r.). Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test.

LR, RFS, and OS were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method, and the significance of differences between groups was assessed using log-rank tests. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was first performed to identify factors associated with LR. Variables with P < 0.10 and those considered clinically relevant were entered into the multivariable Cox model, while also taking into account the limited number of events to maintain an appropriate events-per-variable ratio. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated by testing time-dependent covariates (interaction terms between each variable and survival time) and by visually assessing Kaplan–Meier survival curves for evidence of non-proportionality. Hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence interval (c.i.) were calculated. Continuous variables were analysed per 1-unit increase.

Results

Study population

In all, 300 patients were enrolled across 47 Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum-affiliated institutions. Of these patients, three were excluded, one because of conversion to open surgery before the operation, one with intraoperative liver metastasis, and one with no available follow-up data (Fig. S1), leaving 297 patients for analysis. The median follow-up duration was 37.2 (i.q.r., 36.2–39.5) months.

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. In all, 93 tumours (31.3%) were classified as anterior and 204 (68.7%) were classified as non-anterior. The anterior and non-anterior groups did not differ significantly in terms of age (P = 0.210), sex distribution (P = 1.000), cT category (P = 1.000), tumour size (P = 0.344), tumour distance from the AV and DL, morphology, serum tumour markers, LE before surgery, or histological type.

Surgical and pathological outcomes

Surgical and pathological outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The distribution of surgical procedures (LAR, ISR, APR, Hartmann) was comparable between the anterior and non-anterior groups. Surgical outcomes, including the rate of macroscopically adequate dissection margin (P = 1.000), distal margin (P = 0.633), anastomotic leakage (P = 0.654), and stoma creation at initial surgery (P = 0.503), were also similar between the two groups. Faecal diversion was performed in 84.9% and 84.3% of patients in the anterior and non-anterior groups, respectively (P = 1.000). Oncological outcomes

The 3-year cumulative LR rate for the entire cohort was 5.9% (95% c.i. 3.7 to 9.3). Stratified by tumour location, LR was significantly higher for anterior than non-anterior tumours (HR 4.46; 95% c.i. 1.64 to 12.11; P = 0.001; Fig. 1). There was no significant difference between anterior and non-anterior tumours in the 3-year RFS (HR 1.50; 95% c.i. 0.75 to 3.01; P = 0.222) and OS (HR 0.57; 95% c.i., 0.19 to 1.74; P = 0.377; Fig. 1). No significant differences in LR were detected between high-volume and low-volume centres, either in the overall cohort (Fig. S2) or among patients with anterior lesions (Fig. S2).

Risk factors for local recurrence

To identify risk factors for LR, Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed that included patient-related variables and surgical factors, such as operative approach and distal resection margin (Table 3). In the univariable analysis, cT category (P = 0.005), morphology (P = 0.002), and tumour location (anterior versus non-anterior; P = 0.003) emerged as significant predictors of LR. In the multivariable analysis, only tumour location remained an independent predictor (HR 4.68; 95% c.i., 1.72 to 12.74; P = 0.003). The interaction term between anterior tumour location and time was statistically significant, suggesting a violation of the proportional hazard assumption for tumour location. To further identify high-risk subsets among anterior lesions, an additional subgroup analyses were performed by cT category (Fig. 2). Among patients with cT1 tumours, the 3-year LR rates were low and comparable between anterior and non-anterior tumours (HR 2.12; 95% c.i. 0.26 to 17.27; P = 0.443; Fig. 2a). In contrast, among patients with cT2 tumours, anterior location was associated with a significantly higher risk of LR (HR 5.89; 95% c.i. 1.87 to 18.51; P < 0.001; Fig. 2b). The distribution of pT categories among cT2 anterior and non-anterior tumours did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.838; Table S1).

Discussion

This prospective multicentre study revealed that anterior tumour location is associated with an increased risk of LR, even in early-stage (Union for International Cancer Control stage I) lower rectal cancer. Among 297 patients undergoing curative resection at 47 specialized centres in Japan, LR rates were significantly higher for anterior lesions than for non-anterior lesions, particularly in patients with cT2 disease.

Previous studies emphasized the prognostic relevance of tumour location in advanced rectal cancer10,13. Franz et al.10 identified ventral tumours ≥ 4 cm from the AV as independent predictors of LR in stage II/III disease, whereas Nagtegaal et al.13 found that an anterior location was associated with poorer local control and survival. However, these studies focused on advanced disease. The present study extend this evidence by demonstrating that anterior tumour location is also associated with increased LR risk in stage I ultra-low rectal cancers. Given that LR is strongly influenced by the CRM, this association warrants further investigation. Previous studies demonstrated that a positive or close CRM significantly elevates the LR risk18,19, and that anterior tumours are more frequently associated with CRM involvement20,21. In the present cohort, anterior tumours exhibited particularly high LR rates in subgroups with deeper invasion (for example, T2), suggesting that achieving an adequate CRM may be especially challenging in these patients. However, detailed CRM distances were not prospectively or systematically recorded in the original study protocol and complete quantitative CRM data were unavailable. Therefore, although anterior tumour location may plausibly relate to CRM adequacy, the present findings cannot establish a causal relationship, and the interpretation should remain cautious. Where available, qualitative CRM status (involved versus not involved) is reported.

From a technical perspective, anterior dissection is inherently more challenging due to both the pelvic anatomy and structural features of the mesorectum22. Although posterior dissection follows a relatively clear retrorectal plane, the anterior aspect offers a limited surgical margin. The mesorectum is thick posteriorly and laterally, but thinner anteriorly23, making secure CRM more difficult. Moreover, the anterior rectum lies close to critical structures, including the prostate, seminal vesicles, urethra, and vagina, thereby restricting the extent of dissection. Tsukada et al.24 further reported that longitudinal smooth muscle attachment is longer in the anterolateral portion of the anal canal and shorter posteriorly, with thicker smooth muscle layers posteriorly continuous with the levator ani. These anatomical features contribute to the vulnerability of the anterior dissection plane. Sex-related pelvic anatomy also influences surgical complexity. In men, the narrow pelvis and adjacency of the rectum to the prostate and seminal vesicles render anterior dissection particularly difficult. In women, although the rectum is separated from the vagina by a thinner septum, dissection remains constrained. Nagtegaal et al.13 reported that anterior tumours are more technically demanding than posterior tumours, particularly in men. However, in the present study, LR rates did not differ significantly between sexes, suggesting that anatomical complexity contributes to, but does not fully explain, the higher recurrence risk. Lower rectal cancer has a higher risk of LR than upper or middle rectal cancer. Because all tumours in the present cohort were located in the ultra-low rectum around the anal verge, the anatomical constraints inherent to this distal region applied uniformly across the study population. This region is associated with higher rates of CRM involvement and greater technical difficulty. A meta-analysis reported that the LR rates of rectal cancer after LE followed by complete total mesorectal excision were 4.1% (range 1.7% to 9.4%) for high-risk pT1 tumours and 4% (range 1% to 13%) for pT2 tumours9. The LR rates observed in the present study were slightly higher than those reported in this meta-analysis, likely because the cohort consisted exclusively of ultra-low rectal cancers around the anal verge. Distal tumours are known to have higher rates of CRM involvement than more proximal tumours20,21. Consistent with this, the positive margin rate in low T2 tumours (11.4%) has been reported to be more than twice that observed in higher T2 tumours12, and higher LR rates after APR than after LAR have also been described10. For stage I lower anterior rectal cancer, strategies to improve local control warrant further investigation. Robotic surgery, as demonstrated in the REAL trial, has been associated with favourable LR rates compared with laparoscopy25 and may offer technical advantages in anatomically constrained anterior dissections. Similarly, neoadjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy reduces LR in advanced disease26–28, although its potential role in selected high-risk early-stage anterior tumours remains uncertain. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether these approaches could improve local control in this subgroup.

This study has several limitations. First, although data collection was prospective, the original protocol was not designed to compare anterior and non-anterior tumours, resulting in a limited sample size and wide confidence intervals (HR 4.68; 95% c.i. 1.72 to 12.74). Although the proportional hazards assumption was not fully satisfied, the Cox model was retained because the violation was modest and the HR can still be interpreted as an average effect over time, which is considered reasonable in clinical studies with limited events. Second, the median follow-up of 3 years is relatively short for detecting LR. However, because a substantial proportion of recurrences after curative resection for colorectal cancer occur within the first few years after surgery29, the available follow-up still captures the early recurrence pattern in this cohort. Third, some variables were not uniformly documented across participating institutions. CRM distance was not consistently recorded, limiting mechanistic interpretation of the findings. In addition, magnetic resonance imaging was not performed in all patients, mainly because many tumours were early stage tumours. Although magnetic resonance imaging is valuable for assessing depth and localization30, tumours near the anal verge can generally be localized reliably using rigid rectoscopy, digital rectal examination, and endoscopy. However, because this was a multicentre study, formal assessment of interobserver reproducibility was not performed, and some degree of misclassification cannot be excluded. Fourth, surgeon experience may have influenced the outcomes. The Endoscopic Surgical Skill Qualification System (ESSQS), established by the Japan Society for Endoscopic Surgery in 200431 ensures high technical standards in laparoscopic surgery32,33, and ESSQS-certified surgeons participated in almost all procedures included in this study. In addition, LR rates did not differ significantly between the high-volume and low-volume centres, indicating that surgeon experience and institutional volume were unlikely to have materially influenced the main results. Despite these limitations, the multicentre design and substantial sample size of the present study strengthen the validity of the finding that anterior ultra-low rectal cancers are associated with an increased risk of LR following laparoscopic surgery.

In conclusion, anterior tumour location in ultra-low rectal cancer is associated with an increased risk of LR. Therefore, even early-stage anterior wall lesions may require careful therapeutic planning and close postoperative surveillance.
