Technical and clinical outcomes of endovascular treatment for splenic artery aneurysms
Authors: Xiong Zhang, Mo Wang, Ming Li, Hao He, Chenzi Yang, Chang Shu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the outcomes of endovascular treatment for splenic artery aneurysms (SAAs).MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of patients with SAAs who underwent endovascular treatment between January 2019 and June 2024. Patient demographics, aneurysm characteristics, treatment strategies, and perioperative and long-term outcomes were analyzed.ResultsA total of 19 patients with SAAs, with a median age of 53 years (IQR 47–57 years), were treated endovascularly. Of these, 15 patients were treated with embolization alone, and 4 with covered stent placement. Technical success, defined as successful deployment of devices and complete exclusion of the aneurysm on final angiography, was achieved in 18 out of 19 patients (94.74%). The mean contrast medium volume used was 88.5 ± 9.5 mL, and the median operative time was 84 min (IQR 65–105 min). Post-embolization syndrome (PES) occurred in 15.79% (3/19) of patients. One patient developed splenic artery dissection, and 47.37% (9/19) had splenic infarction. During a median clinical follow-up of 35.5 months (IQR 11.25–45.5 months), there was no mortality, no hepatic or intestinal ischemia, or need for reintervention in either the perioperative period or during follow-up. The median duration of CT imaging follow-up was 28 months (IQR 10.5–35.5 months). The median aneurysm diameter was 25 mm (IQR 19.5–30.6 mm) before treatment and decreased to 19.9 mm (IQR 16.9–24.8 mm) on the last follow-up CT scan (P = 0.130). Among the nine patients with splenic infarction, follow-up imaging revealed complete resolution of the infarcted area in four cases and a marked reduction in infarct size in the remaining five. One patient with a minor endoleak and a stable aneurysm was managed conservatively. The vast majority of aneurysms (94.74%, 18/19) were completely excluded and had achieved complete thrombosis. Univariate regression analyses found no significant association between spleen diameter and complete aneurysm thrombosis (P = 0.999) or the degree of aneurysm sac shrinkage (P = 0.297).ConclusionBased on our single-center experience, endovascular techniques appear to be feasible, safe, and effective for treating SAAs, with appropriate intervention tailored to anatomical location and aneurysm characteristics.Keywords: Splenic artery, Splenic artery aneurysm, Endovascular repair, Stent graft, Coil embolization

Manuscript

Splenic artery aneurysms (SAAs) are a rare clinical condition with a risk of rupture and fatal bleeding, with 2–10% of cases experiencing rupture accompanied by hypotension or shock [1]. SAAs account for 60% of all visceral artery aneurysms and are also the third most common abdominal aneurysms after aortic and iliac artery aneurysms [2–4]. Surgical treatment is recommended for pseudoaneurysms or aneurysms >2 cm in diameter and SAAs of any diameter in women of childbearing age. Conservative treatment and open repair have been the treatment options of choice for many years [5]. Since 2000, endovascular repair of SAAs has been increasingly used [6]. Endovascular treatment methods encompass coil embolization, stent graft implantation, or a combination of both. Their main benefits include reducing the length of hospital stay and facilitating faster recovery. However, complications—embolization failure (incomplete occlusion or persistent sac perfusion) and end-organ embolization, which may cause ischemia—remain significant concerns requiring close monitoring. This study retrospectively analyzed splenic aneurysm procedures performed by different interventional methods in our center, and discusses its safety and effectiveness.

Methods

The Ethics Committee of the 2nd Xiangya Hospital, Central South University reviewed and approved the study that involved human participants. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent to participate in this study and for the publication of any potentially identifiable images or data included in the article.

We retrospectively analyzed all consecutive patients who underwent endovascular treatment for SAAs at our institution from January 2019 to June 2024. Demographic data, anatomical features, comorbidities, clinical signs and symptoms, treatment modalities, and follow-up outcomes were comprehensively assessed. Splenomegaly was defined as a splenic craniocaudal length > 12 cm. Portal hypertension was defined as a hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) ≥ 5 mmHg.

Endovascular techniques

Indications for endovascular treatment encompassed ruptured or symptomatic SAAs, SAAs in women of childbearing age, and asymptomatic SAAs greater than 2 cm in diameter [7]. Patients were positioned supine, and following local anesthesia, splenic artery angiography was performed to identify the size and location of SAAs, whether there was contrast agent spillage, branch vessel blood supply, and the adjacent relationship with peripheral vessels. Arterial access was obtained via the brachial or femoral artery. In our center, the femoral artery is the preferred access route for endovascular procedures. However, the brachial approach was selected in cases where the femoral route was technically challenging—such as severe iliofemoral tortuosity or stenosis, or an acutely angulated celiac trunk origin from the abdominal aorta that impeded guidewire and catheter navigation.

Two interventional therapy methods were employed for this group of patients. The first method involved coil or vascular plug embolization. This technique encompassed embolization of both the aneurysm sac and the inflow/outflow arteries. Typically, the outflow vessels were embolized first when necessary. Specifically, the catheter tip was positioned within the outflow vessels following superselective catheterization. Coils of appropriate diameter or controllable release coils were then utilized for vascular embolization. Upon completion, the catheter was retracted into the aneurysm sac, and a 6–8 mm coil was selected based on the size of the aneurysm. Subsequently, the catheter was withdrawn to the inflow vessel for further embolization. For sac packing, 6–8 mm coils are preferred to achieve dense occlusion. Inflow and outflow vessels are embolized with coils oversized by 10–20% of the native vessel diameter to prevent migration (Fig. 1). In cases with short landing zones or acute angulation, detachable coils (e.g., Interlock™, Boston Scientific) are used for precise deployment. Regarding vessels arising from the aneurysmal sac, our strategy is to attempt selective catheterization and embolization of these branch vessels first. If this proves unfeasible, direct coiling of the aneurysmal sac is then performed.

The second method was stent-graft implantation, which was generally applicable to SAAs with a sufficient landing zone (> 0.5 cm) at both ends. With catheter guidance, the guidewire was superselected into the outflow vessel. A Viabahn™ covered stent graft (W. L Gore & Associates) with a diameter of 6–8 mm was introduced along the guidewire system to isolate the aneurysm cavity (Fig. 2). For patients who underwent covered stent placement, dual antiplatelet therapy was routinely administered for 3 months, followed by lifelong single antiplatelet therapy postoperatively, in the absence of bleeding risk.

Technical success was defined as successful deployment of devices and complete exclusion of the aneurysm without endoleak on final angiography. Postoperatively, all patients were monitored for body temperature and underwent laboratory evaluations, including white blood cell and platelet counts, as well as liver and renal function tests. Post-embolization syndrome (PES) was defined as a constellation of symptoms including fever (≥ 38 °C), abdominal pain, delayed gastrointestinal transit, pleural effusion, and, in some cases, pancreatitis following splenic infarction.

Follow-up

For all patients, the puncture site and signs of abdominal complications were closely monitored. Additionally, computed tomography angiography (CTA) was scheduled at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, followed by annual evaluations thereafter.

Statistical analysis

All data were collected and retrospectively analyzed. Clinical data analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 21, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Normality testing was conducted for continuous variables. Variables with normal distributions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while those with non-normal distributions were presented as median values and interquartile ranges (IQR). Group comparisons were conducted using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Univariate binary logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between spleen size and aneurysm thrombosis during follow-up. Univariate linear regression was used to assess the association between spleen size and the degree of aneurysm shrinkage. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From January 2019 to June 2024, a total of 38 patients with visceral artery aneurysms were treated at our center, comprising 19 SAAs, 6 superior mesenteric artery aneurysms, 6 celiac artery aneurysms, 5 renal artery aneurysms, and 2 hepatic artery aneurysms. All 19 splenic artery aneurysm (SAA) patients (7 men and 12 women), with a median age of 53 years (IQR 47–57 years), underwent endovascular repair in our department. Among them, 5 female patients were of childbearing age. The demographics and clinical characteristics of the SAA cohort are presented in Table 1. Of these, 15 (78.95%) patients were asymptomatic, and 4 (21.05%) patients were symptomatic and presented with abdominal pain. Comorbidities included hypertension (n = 4, 21.05%), diabetes mellitus (n = 1, 5.26%), hyperlipidemia (n = 3, 15.79%), splenomegaly (n = 5, 26.32%), and portal hypertension (n = 2, 10.53%). Among the two patients with portal hypertension, one also had viral hepatitis, cirrhosis, gastrointestinal bleeding, hypersplenism, and tri-lineage reduction, while the other presented with multiple SAAs, cirrhosis, splenomegaly, and Budd-Chiari syndrome.

Aneurysm characteristics and periprocedural outcomes

In our study, 21.05% of patients with SAAs (2 males, 2 females) were symptomatic, with all experiencing abdominal pain. As a result, these patients underwent targeted interventions for their SAAs: one patient had a stent placed, while three underwent embolization. Postoperatively, abdominal pain was relieved in all four patients, and follow-up CT scans confirmed complete thrombosis of the aneurysm sac.

A patient with multiple SAAs complicated by Budd - Chiari syndrome underwent embolization treatment involving the inflow artery of the maximum splenic artery aneurysm (SAA), as well as balloon dilation of the inferior vena cava, and after embolization, contrast agent imaging can still be seen in the aneurysm sac. Therefore, the technical success was achieved in 18 patients (94.74%). The mean contrast medium volume used was 88.5 ± 9.5 mL, and the median operative time was 84 min (IQR 65–105 min). No procedural mortality was observed, with a mean hospital stay of 8.6 ± 3.1 days. There were no instances of vascular injury, allergic reactions, or aneurysm rupture during procedure. 15.79% (3/19) patients developed PES after the procedure, which was primarily manifested as fever (2 cases) and mild abdominal pain (1 case). All three cases occurred in the embolization group. Laboratory tests did not reveal any significant abnormalities, and all symptoms were alleviated within 72 h after the procedure.

Postoperative CTA confirmed splenic infarction in 47.37% of patients (9/19), all occurring in the embolization group and managed successfully with observation alone. A splenic artery dissection was observed in one patient. The patient was a 43-year-old woman with a SAA measuring 30 mm at its greatest diameter. She underwent embolization of the aneurysm sac and inflow vessels via the right femoral artery. A follow-up CTA scan revealed a localized dissection of the splenic artery with a maximum diameter of 0.9 cm, and she reported no abdominal discomfort or any other symptoms. Given the absence of symptoms and rupture risk, conservative management was initiated. This regimen consisted of antiplatelet therapy for three months to prevent thrombus formation, strict control of blood pressure and heart rate, and annual follow-up with CTA to monitor for any changes in the dissection. No patients in the stent group developed post-embolization syndrome (PES) or splenic infarction.

Procedural outcomes were compared between the brachial and femoral puncture approaches (Table 3). Technical success rates were 100% (13/13) in the brachial group and 83.3% (5/6) in the femoral group (P = 0.130). Notably, the femoral approach was associated with shorter operative times compared to the brachial approach (69.5 [60.3–85.5] vs. 99 [67.5-106.5] minutes, P = 0.459), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of X-ray exposure time or contrast medium volume. One case of splenic artery dissection occurred in the femoral access group, while no access-site complications were recorded in the brachial group.

The median follow-up time was 35.5 months (IQR 11.25–45.5) months. All patients completed the follow-up via telephone or outpatient visits. There was no mortality and hepatic or intestinal ischemia, or reintervention was observed in the perioperative or follow-up periods. Postoperative CTA scans were performed on all patients. The median CT follow-up time was 28 months (IQR 10.5–35.5 months). A reduction in aneurysm size was demonstrated in 94.74% (18/19) of patients. The sole exception was a case of multiple aneurysms, which showed no significant change in size. For all patients, the median aneurysm diameters before and after the procedure were 25.0 mm (IQR 19.5–30.6 mm) and 19.9 mm (IQR 16.9–24.8 mm), respectively (P = 0.130). All five patients with preoperative splenomegaly exhibited a reduction in splenic size, with maximum transverse diameters measuring 59.38 ± 11.45 mm preoperatively and 55.22 ± 12.87 mm postoperatively (P = 0.333), although the difference was not statistically significant. Among the nine patients with splenic infarction, follow-up imaging revealed complete resolution of the infarcted area in four cases and a significant reduction in infarct size in the remaining five. As shown in Table 4, univariate binary logistic regression demonstrated no association between spleen diameter and aneurysm thrombosis during follow-up (OR 0.599, 95% CI 0.000–3.587, P = 0.999). Univariate linear regression also showed no significant relationship between spleen diameter and the extent of aneurysm sac shrinkage (β = 1.797, 95% CI − 1.727–5.321, P = 0.297).

Discussion

Visceral artery aneurysms are a rare but potentially life-threatening condition. Prevalence rates as high as 10.4% have been reported in some autopsy-based studies [8].The prevalence of SAAs in women is about four times higher than in men [9, 10]. Although the pathogenesis is not fully understood, risk factors include trauma, hormonal and local hemodynamic events during pregnancy, portal hypertension (including Caroli syndrome), arterial degeneration (intimal fibrodysplasia), and atherosclerosis [11, 12]. The lesions are usually located in the intermediate segment or the distal segment of the splenic artery [13], which was also confirmed through our experience. In our series, the majority of aneurysms were located in the middle third (47.37%), followed by the distal third (36.84%), with only a minority in the proximal third (15.79%). Most SAAs are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, and spontaneous aneurysm rupture is reported as the first manifestation in 2–10% of patients [12], with an average mortality rate of 25% after rupture.

SAAs may result in splenomegaly and portal hypertension, likely due to the compression of the splenic vein by the aneurysm. In this study, five patients were found to have preoperative splenomegaly. Following treatment, the reduction in the diameter of the SAAs was observed, which was concomitant with a decrease in spleen size (maximum transverse diameter: 59.38 ± 11.45 vs. 55.22 ± 12.87 mm, P = 0.333). Although no statistically significant difference was observed, these findings suggest that treatment of the splenic artery may contribute to the alleviation of splenomegaly to some degree. Portal hypertension and SAAs are considered closely related. Portal hypertension resulting from cirrhosis is a significant risk factor for the development of SAAs. Between 7% and 17% of patients with SAAs have cirrhosis, while the detection rate of SAAs is approximately 2.97% in patients with cirrhosis [14]. Hyperdynamic circulation, observed in patients with portal hypertension, is considered one of the causes of SAAs. Additionally, compression of the splenic vein by the SAAs can lead to splenic congestion, enlargement, hypersplenism, and increased pressure in the spleen and gastric regions of the portal venous system.

Treatment of SAAs can generally be accomplished by either open surgical or endovascular approaches. Endovascular techniques including transcatheter embolization and stent graft placement were introduced as alternatives to conventional surgery [15–18]. While the majority of SAAs can be treated through endovascular therapy, the selection of an interventional treatment approach should be tailored based on the anatomic location of the aneurysm, the flow dynamics of the inflow and outflow vessels, and the specific clinical condition of the patient. For proximal and middle third SAAs, stent graft placement has a satisfactory therapeutic effect [19–21], which requires a sufficient landing zone >0.5 cm for both inflow and outflow arteries, achieving aneurysm exclusion while preserving vessel patency [22]. In cases where patients lack an adequate landing zone, or when there is a twist in the splenic artery or the celiac trunk, embolization or open surgery should be considered as viable alternatives. Aneurysm sac embolization, in particular, offers distinct advantages, especially for SAAs located in the distal third of the splenic artery or near the splenic hilum. Embolization of the outflow vessel is an option when thick outflow vessels are present and accessible, typically undertaken as the initial step.

However, endovascular repair is not without its drawbacks. The PES and ischemia of terminal organs caused by this procedure are of significant concern. According to previous studies, the incidence of PES is approximately 30% [23]. The symptoms of PES include fever, pain, ileus, platelet dysfunction, elevated white blood cell count, and serological evidence of pancreatitis. In our study, 15.79% (3/19) of patients developed PES after the procedure, which was primarily manifested as fever (2 cases) and mild abdominal pain (1 case). Laboratory tests did not reveal any significant abnormalities, and all symptoms were alleviated within 72 h after the procedure.

Splenic infarction, following embolization of SAAs, is a common complication, its major consequences may include splenic rupture or abscess formation. In the study by Amish Patel, 68 patients with SAAs who underwent coil embolization were included, with 9% developing complications of splenic infarction following the procedure [24]. No reintervention was required due to splenic infarction. In a 2024 study by Shenjie Wang [25], which retrospectively reviewed 63 patients with SAAs who underwent interventional procedures, 11.1% (n = 7) developed splenic infarction, all of which occurred in the embolization group. In our study, postoperative CT scans revealed splenic infarction in nine cases (47.37%)—a notably higher incidence than previously reported—all occurring in the coil embolization group. Among these nine patients, eight underwent embolization of the aneurysm feeding vessels, including five where only inflow vessels were embolized and three where both inflow + outflow vessels were embolized. This aggressive approach, while potentially improving the completeness and durability of aneurysm exclusion, inherently compromises collateral circulation and increases the risk of parenchymal ischemia, further underscoring the limitations of current embolization techniques.

Although most splenic infarctions do not require specific treatment, they may lead to complications such as PES, pneumonia, or atelectasis. Moreover, extensive splenic infarction carries the risk of developing splenic abscesses, which are relatively rare but may necessitate percutaneous drainage or even splenectomy in severe cases. Therefore, although the majority of splenic infarctions resolve spontaneously without intervention, vigilant clinical monitoring is still imperative due to the associated risk of complications.

When interpreting the findings of this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design inherently introduces the potential for selection bias and limits the generalizability of the results. Second, the small cohort size (n = 19), particularly the limited number of patients in the stent-graft subgroup (n = 4), constrains the statistical power and precludes meaningful comparative analysis between treatment strategies. Consequently, the favorable technical success and low complication rates observed here may reflect the specific expertise and patient selection at a high-volume tertiary center and may not be fully representative of outcomes in broader clinical practice. Future research should focus on establishing multi-institutional, prospective registries to enroll larger patient cohorts, which would enable more robust statistical analyses and improved risk stratification for procedure-related complications such as splenic infarction.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our single-center experience suggests that endovascular treatment of SAAs is feasible and appears to be safe and effective within the limits of this small case series. The selection of treatment approaches must be tailored to the characteristics and anatomical location of the SAAs.
