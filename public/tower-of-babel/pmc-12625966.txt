Brain MRI for the diagnosis of dementia in low- and middle-income countries: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol of diagnostic utility and imaging findings
Authors: Rita Nassanga, Noeline Nakasujja, Stephen E Jones, Aloysius G Mubuuke, Martha Sajatovic, Kamada Lwere, Michael Grace Kawooya, Moses Ocan, Mark Kaddumukasa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionThe utility of brain MRI in dementia diagnosis offers critical insights into structural brain changes, such as hippocampal and thalamic atrophy, which are hallmark features of Alzheimer’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease-related dementias . However, its use, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), is affected by limited accessibility. This protocol outlines a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the diagnostic utility, feasibility and challenges of integrating brain MRI for dementia diagnosis in LMICs.Methods and analysisThe review follows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols with a priori eligibility criteria and is registered in PROSPERO. Searches (from inception to September 2025) will be run in MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science and PsycINFO, with supplementary bibliography screening. Adults ≥50 years in LMIC settings undergoing brain MRI for dementia evaluation will be eligible. Data will be synthesised narratively and, where appropriate, via random-effects meta-analysis with planned subgroup analyses by MRI approach (qualitative vs quantitative), magnet strength, sequence availability and reference standard. Introduction

Brain MRI plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis of dementia, providing critical insights into structural brain changes such as hippocampal atrophy, thalamic and cortical volume loss and white matter lesions, features that support the diagnosis and subtyping of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Alzheimer’s disease-related dementias (ADRD).1,3

In high-income settings, MRI is routinely integrated into diagnostic workflows, clinical criteria and research protocols, and also serves as a valuable tool for dementia education and clinical training.1 4 In contrast, diagnosis in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains largely dependent on clinical assessment and use of cognitive tools such as the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE),5 due to limited access to advanced diagnostics as a result of barriers such as high cost, limited availability of MRI equipment, insufficient trained personnel and lack of standardised imaging protocols.6,9 Standard diagnostic approaches such as neuroimaging and biological markers from cerebrospinal fluid or blood are often costly, limited in availability and typically confined to major urban centres.10 As a result, the integration, feasibility and effectiveness of brain MRI for dementia diagnosis in LMICs remain understudied and underutilised, contributing to delays in diagnosis, underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis, and ultimately hindering appropriate clinical management, patient outcomes and research efforts in these settings.11

Dementia poses a significant global health challenge, with rising prevalence projected to double or even triple by 2050. This is further exacerbated by the fact that it will affect a substantial proportion of individuals in LMICs where even life expectancy is increasing.12 It has been reported that over two-thirds of people with dementia are expected to live in LMICs, where health systems are often under-resourced and underprepared for the growing burden of neurodegenerative diseases.13 Compounding this is the increased burden of vascular risk factors in African and other LMIC populations including hypertension, diabetes and smoking14 15 which contribute to mixed and atypical dementia presentations.

In LMICs, where more than half of the dementia cases occur, the increase in life expectancy is projected to escalate the prevalence of dementia due to the anticipated ageing of the population.16 17 Limited representation in neurodegenerative disease (NDD) research, combined with an increased burden of vascular risk factors such as smoking, diabetes and hypertension in Africans, further contributes to the challenges of understanding and addressing dementia in these settings.18,24 In a systematic review of the Epidemiology of Dementia among the elderly in Sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of dementia varied widely between 2.29% and 21.60% demonstrating geographical variability, hence necessitating country-based estimations.24,26 While these systematic reviews have examined the epidemiology of dementia in LMICs, they have largely neglected neuroimaging and specifically the diagnostic utility of brain MRI which is increasingly being advocated for during the diagnostic work-up of people with suspected dementia. Therefore, understanding how MRI is used, its diagnostic yield and the challenges of its integration into dementia care in LMICs is essential for informing clinical practice and policy. This systematic review and meta-analysis will therefore assess the diagnostic utility, feasibility and challenges of integrating brain MRI in the diagnosis of dementia in LMICs. The findings will potentially inform clinical guidelines, training priorities and health policy aimed at improving dementia care in resource-limited settings.

Rationale

There is limited consolidated evidence on how brain MRI is being used in the diagnostic evaluation of dementia in LMICs. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to fill that gap by synthesising published studies that report brain MRI findings in individuals with suspected or confirmed dementia in LMICs. Specifically, the review will describe the types and frequencies of structural abnormalities identified on MRI in dementia. It will also assess the proportion of individuals in whom dementia was diagnosed based on MRI and compare these findings with results from neuropsychological tools such as the MMSE or Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), where available. In addition, the review will summarise MRI protocols and explore how these imaging findings relate to the clinical and demographic characteristics of the study populations, including comorbidities and laboratory investigations such as Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis or electroencephalogram (EEG) findings. These insights will provide a comprehensive picture of the current diagnostic utility of brain MRI for dementia in LMIC settings and inform research and clinical priorities in resource-limited environments. The results could support more informed dementia care guidelines and strengthen advocacy for neuroimaging infrastructure in LMIC contexts. Ultimately, this review will contribute evidence to inform regional adaptations of global frameworks, such as the WHO’s Global Action Plan on Dementia.27

Outcomes and prioritisation

Main outcomes

The primary outcomes will be diagnostic accuracy metrics for MRI alone and for MRI combined with cognitive screening tools (eg, sensitivity, specificity, area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC), likelihood ratios) against the stated reference standards.

Additional outcomes

Secondary outcomes will include the prevalence and patterns of MRI abnormalities, subtype-specific features and MRI protocols and magnet strengths, as well as associations between MRI findings and clinical measures.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

This review will include studies of adults aged ≥50 years from LMICs who present with cognitive symptoms and undergo brain MRI as part of the initial diagnostic work-up for suspected dementia. This threshold focuses the review on predominantly late-onset dementia, reflects the age distributions reported in LMIC imaging studies and reduces heterogeneity associated with younger, atypical/early-onset presentations. Inclusion criteria are guided by the PECOST framework (online supplemental file 1).

Study designs

Eligible designs will be observational: cross-sectional, case-control and cohort (prospective or retrospective). To contextualise regional gaps, African case reports and small case series (<10 participants) will be included for qualitative mapping only (eg, patterns/feasibility) and excluded from all quantitative synthesis and diagnostic-accuracy estimates. Cohort studies will be eligible only if they analyse the baseline (index) MRI obtained at first clinical assessment and report its diagnostic contribution to the initial dementia diagnosis. Studies focused solely on longitudinal progression after diagnosis (eg, serial MRI to track atrophy rates without reporting baseline diagnostic contribution) will be excluded.

Outcomes of interest

Primary outcomes concern the diagnostic contribution/accuracy of MRI (alone or combined with cognitive screening tools) to the initial diagnosis. Secondary outcomes will include the prevalence and patterns of MRI abnormalities, subtype-specific features and MRI protocols and magnet strengths used.

Timeframe and setting

We will include hospital-based LMIC studies published from 1990 onward to reflect wider availability of MRI in LMICs and the likelihood of relevant diagnostic imaging research during this period.

Exclusions

We will exclude studies involving participants <50 years; studies that do not involve brain MRI as the exposure of interest; articles lacking MRI-related diagnostic outcomes; studies conducted exclusively in high-income countries; non-original research (reviews, editorials, commentaries) and longitudinal progression studies that do not analyse the baseline diagnostic role of MRI. Case reports and very small case series are excluded from quantitative synthesis; however, African case reports/small series (<10 participants) may be included for qualitative mapping only and appraised with JBI tools. Studies in non-English language will be excluded and acknowledged as a limitation.

Screening and selection

Search results will be imported into reference management software (eg, EndNote/Mendeley) and deduplicated. Two reviewers will independently screen titles/abstracts and then full texts against the criteria, with disagreements resolved by discussion or a third reviewer. The process will be reported using PRISMA 2020, with a flow diagram (online supplemental file 2: PRISMA-P checklist).

Database search strategy

A structured search strategy will be developed using Boolean logic, combining MeSH and free-text terms related to dementia, brain MRI, older adults, LMICs and observational study designs. The search will be guided by the PECOST framework and tailored to each database’s indexing system and syntax.

Systematic searches will be conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO and EMBASE, chosen for their comprehensive coverage of medical, psychological and global health literature. The search will be limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English, with no initial date restrictions. Filters for human studies, English language and LMIC settings (as defined by the World Bank classification) will be applied during the screening phase. See online supplemental file 3 for the complete PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science and PsycINFO strings.

Search terms will be derived from titles, abstracts and subject headings of relevant studies and refined through iterative testing to optimise sensitivity and specificity. Reference articles known to be relevant will be used to validate the strategy. Additional sources such as grey literature, conference proceedings and bibliographies of included studies will also be screened to identify further eligible publications.

The core search concept combines terms as follows:

((elderly OR aged OR “older adults”)

AND (“brain MRI” OR neuroimaging)

AND (dementia OR “cognitive impairment”)

AND (observational OR cohort OR “cross-sectional”))

AND (LMIC filter)

A detailed draft PubMed search strategy is provided in online supplemental file 1, with comparable strategies adapted for the other databases. Final search results and article yield will be reported in the full systematic review.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts retrieved from the database search will be independently screened by two reviewers (RN and LAA). Full texts of potentially eligible studies will then be assessed against the inclusion criteria. Disagreements at either stage will be resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer (MO) will be consulted when necessary. Only studies meeting all inclusion criteria will be included for quality assessment and data extraction.

Data items

Data will be extracted using a standardised and piloted data extraction form. The planned data extraction dates are 1 November 2025 to 30 December 2025. The variables to be collected will include bibliographic details (title, database source, author names, year of publication, DOI), study characteristics (country, region, study design, year of data collection, Research Ethics Committee (REC) number, sampling method, and overall sample size), and participant demographics (mean and median age, IQR, comorbidity profile, and population characteristics). Information on methods used for dementia diagnosis will be collected, including neuropsychological tests such as the MMSE or MoCA, the number of participants tested, number testing positive, mean cognitive scores and the estimated prevalence of cognitive impairment. Where applicable, data on biomarker integration such as cerebrospinal fluid, blood-based biomarkers, or EEG and the fraction testing positive will be recorded. Imaging details will include MRI magnet strength, imaging protocols used, descriptive MRI findings and the reported prevalence of dementia as detected by MRI. The type of dementia subtype identified (e.g, AD, vascular dementia) based on MRI findings will also be extracted. In addition, each study will be assessed for risk of bias, and any relevant comments or clarifications provided by study authors will be noted.

Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form will be developed and implemented in Microsoft Excel to ensure consistency and completeness in capturing key variables from the included studies. The form will be piloted and refined as needed before full use. Two independent reviewers (RN and LAA) will extract data from all eligible studies using this form. The extracted data will then be compared, and any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion and consensus.

Missing data

In cases where MRI findings or key outcome data are unclear or missing from the included studies, efforts will first be made to contact the corresponding authors for clarification. If no response is received within 2 weeks, the missing information will be treated as unavailable. Such studies will still be considered for inclusion in the narrative synthesis, but may be excluded from quantitative analysis depending on the extent and nature of the missing data.

To ensure accurate and transparent synthesis, the handling of missing data and conversions will follow a structured approach. For studies with incomplete summary statistics (eg, prevalence figures), data retrieval will be attempted via author contact. If this fails, imputation methods such as estimating means from medians or ranges will be considered, where methodologically appropriate. When data cannot be retrieved or reliably estimated, the study will be excluded from meta-analysis but included in descriptive synthesis if relevant.

For studies reporting outcomes using different scales or formats, data conversions will be applied to harmonise outcome measures. This may involve standardising results using means and SD, or applying effect size metrics such as standardised mean differences. Where available, individual participant data will be used to calculate effect sizes directly.

If MRI findings are poorly categorised or lack standardisation, their methodological quality will be assessed. Inclusion in the synthesis will depend on relevance, completeness of other data and risk of bias. All assumptions or imputations will be clearly documented and justified. The potential impact of missing data will be explored through sensitivity analyses and discussed as a limitation where applicable.

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

To assess potential publication bias, funnel plots will be generated for any meta-analysis that includes at least 10 studies. Visual asymmetry in the plots will be further examined using Egger’s regression test or Begg’s test, where appropriate. In addition, the trim-and-fill method will be applied to estimate the number of potentially missing studies and adjust the overall effect size accordingly.28

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to test the robustness of the meta-analysis findings. These will include (i) excluding studies at high risk of bias based on Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) assessment, (ii) comparing results using different statistical models (fixed-effect vs random-effects), (iii) assessing the impact of missing data by re-running analyses with and without imputed values and (iv) evaluating the influence of outlier studies. These approaches will help determine whether the pooled estimates remain stable under different assumptions and data conditions.

Assessment of heterogeneity and subgroup analyses

Heterogeneity across included studies will be assessed using both clinical and statistical approaches. Clinical heterogeneity will be examined by comparing key study characteristics such as participant demographics, MRI techniques (eg, visual vs quantitative analysis), diagnostic criteria for dementia and outcome measures. These factors are particularly relevant given the variation in healthcare infrastructure and neuroimaging access across LMICs.

If statistical synthesis is appropriate, statistical heterogeneity will be quantified using the I² statistic and χ² test. Interpretation of I² will follow Cochrane guidelines: 0–40% may indicate low heterogeneity, 30–60% moderate, 50–90% substantial and 75–100% considerable heterogeneity.29

To explore sources of heterogeneity across studies, subgroup analyses will be conducted based on several key variables. These include the type of dementia diagnosis, such as Alzheimer’s disease compared with vascular or frontotemporal dementia, to assess whether diagnostic patterns vary by clinical subtype. Differences in MRI methodology will also be evaluated, including the use of visual rating scales versus quantitative volumetric analysis, as well as magnet strength (≤1.5T vs >1.5T), to determine whether imaging protocols influence reported findings.

Further stratification will be done by study design, including cross-sectional versus cohort studies, and by geographic region or national income level, such as Sub-Saharan Africa compared with South Asia, to identify contextual influences on MRI use and diagnostic outcomes. Analyses will also examine publication year or data collection period (for instance, before vs after 2010) to assess trends over time.

Participant characteristics will be explored in subgroups defined by age group, comorbidity status and population risk profile. In addition, the type of neuropsychological testing used, such as the MMSE, MoCA or other cognitive screening tools will be assessed. Finally, studies integrating biomarkers (including cerebrospinal fluid analysis, PET-CT or blood biomarkers) will be compared with those using MRI alone to evaluate whether multimodal diagnostic approaches influence reported findings.

These subgroup analyses aim to identify methodological and contextual factors that may explain variation in imaging findings or diagnostic utility across studies.

Study risk of bias assessment

As our eligibility focuses on observational designs, ROBINS-I will be the primary tool30 ; RoB 2.0 will be applied only if randomised diagnostic studies are encountered.31 This assessment will cover seven key domains: bias due to confounding, selection of participants into the study, classification of interventions (or exposures), deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes and selection of the reported result. Two independent reviewers (RN and LAA) will perform the risk of bias assessments, and any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (MO). All judgements will be systematically documented in a summary table and will inform the interpretation of the review’s findings.

Planned data synthesis

To manage methodological variability in the narrative synthesis, we will group studies by (i) MRI approach (qualitative reads vs quantitative metrics such as volumetry or Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC), (ii) magnet strength (≤1.5T vs >1.5T), (iii) sequence availability (structural only vs structural plus advanced sequences) and (iv) diagnostic reference standard (clinical criteria alone vs biomarker-supported). We will present stratified narrative summaries and structured tables for each grouping and, where feasible, examine these factors as subgroups or moderators in the meta-analysis.

Where data are sufficiently homogeneous in terms of design and outcome reporting, a meta-analysis will be performed. For dichotomous outcomes (eg, prevalence of dementia diagnosed on MRI), pooled proportions or ORs will be calculated. For continuous outcomes (eg, neuropsychological test scores), standardised mean differences will be used.

If quantitative synthesis is not feasible, a narrative summary will be provided. All decisions regarding inclusion in synthesis will be transparently documented.

Data management and analysis

All search results will be exported into EndNote reference management software, where duplicates will be identified and removed using the built-in duplicate detection function. The included studies will then be organised for data extraction and synthesis.

The analysis will include both narrative and quantitative synthesis. For studies eligible for quantitative analysis, data will be exported into R programming version 4.3.2. Meta-analysis will be considered for studies with low to moderate heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses will be conducted where applicable, based on factors such as geographic region, type of cognitive assessment tool used (eg, MMSE, MoCA) and MRI protocol (eg, structural imaging, morphometric techniques). Final subgroup categories will be determined after reviewing the characteristics of the included studies.

Narrative synthesis will be used to summarise findings from studies not amenable to meta-analysis, highlighting key patterns in MRI features, diagnostic approaches and reported dementia subtypes across diverse settings.

Discussion

This systematic review will synthesise the available evidence on brain MRI features and diagnostic accuracy in the diagnosis of dementia within LMICs. Given the growing burden of dementia and limited neuroimaging capacity in many LMICs, understanding how MRI is currently used and what diagnostic findings are most prevalent or informative is essential for improving clinical practice and policy planning.

By identifying patterns in MRI findings, diagnostic tools used and regional variations in diagnostic accuracy, this review aims to inform the development of standardised imaging protocols and capacity-building efforts. In particular, the results will help guide local radiology training programmes, inform dementia care guidelines and highlight research gaps in imaging-based dementia diagnosis in under-resourced settings.

We will contextualise LMIC findings against established high-income countries literature and guidelines, highlighting convergences and divergences in diagnostic utility and discussing the implications of differences in scanner availability, acquisition protocols and workforce capacity. We anticipate some limitations, including potential heterogeneity in study designs, MRI protocols and diagnostic definitions, which may limit the feasibility of meta-analysis. Publication bias and limited access to full-text articles in certain regions may also pose challenges. Nonetheless, this protocol outlines a robust and transparent process to minimise bias and ensure comprehensive evidence synthesis.

The results of this review will contribute to building neuroimaging expertise for dementia diagnosis in LMICs and support advocacy for increased investment in diagnostic imaging infrastructure.
