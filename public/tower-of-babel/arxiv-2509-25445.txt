Designing Compact ILPs via Fast Witness Verification
Michał Włodarczyk

Abstract
The standard formalization of preprocessing in parameterized complexity is given by kernelization. In this work, we depart from this paradigm and study a different type of preprocessing for problems without polynomial kernels, still aiming at producing instances that are easily solvable in practice. Specifically, we ask for which parameterized problems an instance ( I , k ) (I,k) can be reduced in polynomial time to an integer linear program (ILP) with 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( k ) \mathsf{poly}(k) constraints. We show that this property coincides with the parameterized complexity class WK[1], previously studied in the context of Turing kernelization lower bounds. In turn, the class WK[1] enjoys an elegant characterization in terms of witness verification protocols: a yes-instance should admit a witness of size 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( k ) \mathsf{poly}(k) that can be verified in time 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( k ) \mathsf{poly}(k) . By combining known data structures with new ideas, we design such protocols for several problems, such as r r -Way Cut , Vertex Multiway Cut , Steiner Tree , and Minimum Common String Partition , thus showing that they can be modeled by compact ILPs. We also present explicit ILP and MILP formulations for Weighted Vertex Cover on graphs with small (unweighted) vertex cover number. We believe that these results will provide a background for a systematic study of ILP-oriented preprocessing procedures for parameterized problems.

Introduction

Kernelization
[
38
]
provides a rigorous theory for data preprocessing in the following sense: given an instance
I
with parameter
k
we would like to transform it in polynomial time into an equivalent instance
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})
so that
|I^{\prime}|+k^{\prime}\leq f(k)
for a slowly growing function
f
.
Historically, kernelization was meant as just another tool for establishing fixed-parameterized tractability
[
25
]
but later it grew into a mature theory on its own, especially after a hardness framework has been established
[
6
]
.
The main question in this paradigm is: which parameterized problems admit polynomial kernels, that is, when can the function
f
be estimated as polynomial?
From the practical point of view, once the instance size is reduced, it may become amenable to brute-force or heuristic methods.

In recent years, empirical observations have provided evidence that
analyzing preprocessing through the lens of kernelization might be too restrictive.
In the annual PACE challenge
[
29
,
43
,
54
,
55
]
, co-organized with the IPEC conference, competitors solve a collection of instances of a certain NP-hard problem related to parameterized complexity.
It turned out that the many successful submissions
[
3
,
4
,
9
,
23
,
24
]
do not follow the algorithms from FPT handbooks but instead reduce the problem to (mixed) integer linear programming (ILP) and then employ a specialized solver.
Even though the contestants are not allowed to use the state-of-the-art industrial solvers, already the open source solvers often outperform sophisticated FPT algorithms with provable worst-case guarantees.
However, it is usually challenging to model the problem in question as a concise ILP.

In our opinion, parameterized complexity should seek an explanation of this phenomenon and understand which problems can be handled with
ILP-driven preprocessing.
In this work, we make a first step in this direction and investigate one of the possible ways of formalizing such a notion of preprocessing.
Even though modeling NP-hard problems as succinct (mixed) ILPs has been a subject of operation research
since 1950s
[
14
,
22
,
37
,
42
,
66
,
67
,
72
]
,
no systematical study has addressed this question from the perspective of parameterized complexity so far.

What kind of preprocessing?

Following the design of kernelization theory, we consider polynomial-time procedures that output an equivalent instance (of a possibly different decision problem) that should be well-structured as long as the
initial parameter is not too large.
While there are many ways to measure the difficulty of an ILP
[
49
,
1
,
18
,
40
,
36
,
30
,
11
,
51
,
17
,
28
]
,
the most natural are the number of constraints and the number of variables.
In this work, we focus on the first one.

Specifically, we are interested in processing
an instance
(I,k)
in polynomial time to rewrite it as an ILP of the form
(Ax\leq b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})
, where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n},\,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and
m
is polynomially bounded in
k
.
In other words, we would like to reduce solving the problem in question to feasibility check of an ILP with only
\mathsf{poly}(k)
constraints.
This strategy matches the assumption guiding the kernelization paradigm: when the parameter
k
is small, then heuristic algorithms should work fast in practice when the search space has ‘‘dimension’’
1
1

1

Usually the “dimension” of an ILP refers to the number of variables but the known FPT algorithms
[
32
,
52
]
for solving ILP parameterized by the number of constraints
m
perform computations in a lattice of dimension
m
.

\mathsf{poly}(k)
.
Naturally, it would be desirable to obtain ILP formulations with
m=\mathcal{O}(k)
or
m=\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
but we believe that a new framework should first focus on establishing general bounds and more
fine-grained results will follow.
For comparison, the first work on polynomial kernelization for
Feedback Vertex Set
only guaranteed a kernel of size
\mathcal{O}(k^{11})

[
13
]
but it was soon improved to
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})

[
70
]
.

What kind of problems?

Before we delve further into technicalities, let us reflect on for which parameterized problems the question above makes sense.
First of all, we are interested in problems without (known) polynomial kernels.
Secondly, any problem that can be reduced as described above must (1) belong to NP and (2)
admit an FPT algorithm with running time of the form
2^{\mathsf{poly}(k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
52
]
.
Restricting to only such problems allows us to assume
\log n\leq\mathsf{poly}(k)
because otherwise the FPT running time above can be estimated as polynomial in
n
.
Later we will also see another property that must be satisfied: an instance
(I,k)
should admit an NP-witness of size
\mathsf{poly}(k,\log|I|)
.
Therefore, we should not hope to handle structural parameterizations like treewidth due to known barriers
[
27
]
.

Fast witness verification.

The question outlined above can be formalized
in the following way: we ask which parameterized problems admit a
polynomial parameter transformation
(PPT) to ILP feasibility checking parameterized by the number of constraints.
It is easy to see that
Set Cover
parameterized by the universe size admits such a reduction and we will present a reduction in the opposite direction as well.
In turn, the class of problems reducible by PPT to
Set Cover
is known as WK[1].
The WK-hierarchy has been introduced by Hermelin, Kratsch, Sołtys, Wahlström, and Wu
[
46
]
in the context of Turing kernelization.
What is interesting for us, the class WK[1] comprises those parameterized problems that admit an NP-witness of size
\mathsf{poly}(k)
whose correctness can be verified in time
\mathsf{poly}(k)
.
This elegant characterization has remained merely a curiosity because the main focus of
the work
[
46
]
was to prove problem hardness with respect to the WK-hierarchy
2

2

This characterization is implicit in
[
46
]
so we provide a proof for completeness

1.1
Our contribution

We show that the class WK[1] provides an exact characterization of parameterized problems that can be reduced to ILP with few constraints as long as the entries in the corresponding matrix cannot be too large.
The latter restriction is necessary because unbounded entries allow one to express
Unbounded Subset Sum
(which is NP-hard
[
63
]
) with just one equality constraint.
More formally, we consider the feasibility problem of the form
(Ax\leq b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})
, where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n},\,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
,
and define
\Delta(A)
to be the maximal absolute value of an entry in the matrix
A
.

Theorem 1
.

Integer Linear Programming Feasibility
is
WK[1]
-complete when:

•

parameterized by
m+\log(\Delta(A))
,

•

parameterized by
m
only, under the assumption that
\Delta(A)=1
and the vector
b
is given in unary.

Consequently, a parameterized problem admits a polynomial parameter transformation to
ILP Feasibility
(under the considered parameterizations) if and only if it belongs to the class WK[1].
Notably, it follows that allowing for polynomially large numbers in an ILP does not lead to any qualitative advantage.
A similar equivalence is known for
the variant with bounded variables
[
69
]
but therein the reduction involves exponentially many variables.
That variant is easily seen to be WK[1]-hard (
\Cref
obs:ilp:binary).

Hermelin et al.
[
46
]
already showed that WK[1] includes problems of local nature,
such as
k
-Path
,
Connected Vertex Cover
,
Min Ones
d
-SAT
, or
Steiner Tree
, all parameterized by the solution size.
Motivated by
\Cref
thm:ilp-wk1, we show that this class captures many more problems, also those of seemingly non-local nature.
To this end, we employ various data structures to design fast witness verification protocols. The definitions of the listed problems and the related results are discussed in the corresponding sections.

Theorem 2
.

The following parameterized problems belong to the class
WK[1]
:

•

r
-Way Cut
/ solution size,

•

Vertex Multiway Cut
/ solution size,

•

Minimum Common String Partition
/ partition size,

•

Long Path
/ feedback vertex set number,

•

Steiner Tree
/ number of terminals,

•

Optimal Discretization
/ solution size.

Theorems
1
and
2
imply that all the listed problems can be reduced to ILP with
\mathsf{poly}(k)
constraints.
In fact, we provide parameterized reductions to
Binary NDTM Halting
, which then reduces to
Set Cover
, which in turn can be readily modeled by a compact ILP.
We remark that the proofs of containment in WK[1] are not technically involved but they exploit dynamic data structures in a novel context.
In our opinion, their value lies in creating a new link between the fields of integer programming, proof protocols, and data structures.

The results above only provide implicit reductions to ILP where the number constraints is an unspecified polynomial in the parameter.
We complement those with an explicit reduction for
Weighted Vertex Cover / VC
, where the parameter is the vertex cover number of the input graph.
We attain only linearly many constraints, however with a caveat that we require all the variables to be binary.
In addition, we present a mixed integer linear program where the number of integral variables is also linear in the parameter.

Theorem 3
.

Weighted Vertex Cover / VC
admits a linear parameter transformation to

parameterized by
m+\log(\Delta(A))
.
Furthermore, the problem admits a linear parameter transformation to

Preliminaries

On default, we measure the running time of algorithms in the standard RAM model with logarithmic word length.
All the summoned algorithmic results are deterministic.
Throughout the paper, we use the variable
n
to denote the input size during the reductions or the number of variables when analyzing integer programs.
The number of constraints is denoted by
m
.

Integer linear programming.

For
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, we consider ILP in the
standard form without upper bounds
:

(Ax\leq b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})
.
Note that it is allowed to write inequalities “
\geq
” as well since one can multiply coefficients by -1.
The problem
Integer Linear Programming (ILP) Feasibility
asks, given
A,b
, whether there exists a vector
x
satisfying the system above.
One can also consider ILP in the
equality form
:
(Ax=b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})
where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
It is easy to transform a system of inequalities
(Ax\leq b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})
into the equality form by introducing
m
new non-negative variables and turning each inequality
\sum a_{i}x_{i}\leq b
into equality
\sum a_{i}x_{i}+z=b
where
z
is a fresh variable.
This transformation does not affect the number of constraints and results in matrix
A^{\prime}
obtained from
A
by inserting
m
columns with only 0 and 1 entries.
We make note of this observation in the following terms.
Recall that
\Delta(A)
is the maximal absolute value of an entry in the matrix
A
.

Observation 4
.

A system of inequalities
(Ax\leq b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})
,
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n},\,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, can be transformed in polynomial time into an equivalent system of equalities
(A^{\prime}y=b,\,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+m}_{\geq 0})
where
A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times(n+m)}
and
\Delta(A^{\prime})=\max(1,\Delta(A))
.

Another common variant of ILP is the
standard form with upper bounds
:

(Ax\leq b,\,x\leq u,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})
where
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
This form effectively expresses
n
additional inequalities which are not counted as constraints.
When
u
is the all-1s-vector, we call the corresponding decision problem
Binary ILP Feasibility
.
All variants admit FPT algorithms parameterized by the number of constraints
m
and
\Delta(A)
.
On the other hand, parameterization by
m
alone is unlikely to be FPT even when the input is given in unary
[
39
, Theorem 2]
.

Proposition 5

(
[
52
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Feasibility of an ILP in the standard form
without
upper bounds can be decided in time
\mathcal{O}(m\cdot\Delta(A))^{2m}\cdot\log(||b||_{\infty})
.

Proposition 6

(
[
32
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

Feasibility of an ILP in the standard form
with
upper bounds can be decided in time
(m\cdot\Delta(A))^{\mathcal{O}(m^{2})}\cdot n+s^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
where
s
is the input encoding size.

In
mixed integer linear programming
(MILP) we have two groups of variables:
n
integral ones and
\ell
fractional ones.
Let
x[a,b]
denote the truncation of the vector
x
to coordinates
[a,b]
.
In a variant with upper bounds we look for a vector
x
satisfying the system
(Ax\leq b,\,x\leq u,\,x[1,n]\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0},\,x[n+1,n+\ell]\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}_{\geq 0})
where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times(n+\ell)}
,
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+\ell}
, and
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
When the number
n
of integral variables is small, one can solve a MILP using an extension of Lenstra’s algorithm.
Note that in contrast to parameterization by
m
, now
\Delta(A)
can be unbounded.

Proposition 7

(
[
53
]
)
.

Feasibility of a MILP with
n
integral variables can be determined in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(n)}\cdot s^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, where
s
is the input encoding size.

Class WK[1].

A
polynomial parameter transformation
(PPT) is a reduction
between parameterized problems
P,Q

that runs in polynomial time and maps instance
(I,k)
of
P
to an equivalent instance
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})
of
Q
satisfying

k^{\prime}\leq\mathsf{poly}(k)
.
The WK-hierarchy has been introduced
as a hardness hierarchy for problems unlikely to admit a polynomial Turing kernel
[
46
]
.
For each
t\geq 1
the class WK[
t
] comprises problems that are PPT-reducible to
Weighted SAT
over a certain class of formulas, parameterized by the number of 1s in an assignment times
\log n
.
Since we are only interested in the class WK[1], it is more convenient to rely on its equivalent characterization as the PPT-closure of the following WK[1]-complete problem
[
46
]
.

Binary NDTM Halting

Parameter:

k

Input:
nondeterministic Turing machine
M
over a binary alphabet, integer
k

Question:
can
M
halt on the empty string in
k
steps?

Another canonical WK[1]-complete problem is
Set Cover
.

Set Cover

Parameter:

|U|

Input:
set
U

\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{U}
, integer
\ell

Question:
is there a subfamily

of size

such that the union of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is
U
?

WK[1]-completeness of
Binary NDTM Halting
gives rise to a particularly elegant characterization of WK[1] in terms of witness verification protocols.

Definition 8
.

A witness verification protocol for a parameterized problem
L
is a pair of algorithms
(\cal A,B)
with the following specification.

Given an instance
(I,k)
the algorithm
\cal A
runs in polynomial time and outputs a string
s(I,k)\in\{0,1\}^{*}
and integer
\ell(I,k)
.
The algorithm
\cal B
is given the concatenation of
s(I,k)
and a string
w\in\{0,1\}^{\ell(I,k)}
and outputs a yes/no answer.
We require that
(I,k)\in L
if and only if there exists
w
for which
\cal B
answers “yes”.

For a function
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
we say that the protocol has complexity
f
if (a)
\ell(I,k)\leq f(|I|,k)
and (b) the running time of
\cal B
on
s(I,k)+w
is bounded by
f(|I|,k)
.

The following characterization was stated implicitly in
[
46
, §1]
. We provide a proof in
\Cref

Theorem 9
.

A parameterized problem
L
belongs to the class WK[1] if and only if:

1.

(I,k)\in L
can be decided in time
2^{\mathsf{poly}(k)}\cdot|I|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, and

2.

L
admits a witness verification protocol of complexity
\mathsf{poly}(k,\log|I|)
.

WK[1]-completeness of ILP

We summon several facts about the structure of solutions to ILP in the equality form.
First we bound the support of a solution using the integral Carathéodory’s Theorem.

Proposition 10

(
[
31
, Corollary 5]
)
.

Consider an optimization ILP of the form
\min\{c^{T}x\colon Ax=b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}\}

where
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
If this program has a finite optimum, then it has an optimal solution
x^{*}
in which the number of non-zero entries is
\mathcal{O}(m(\log m+\log(\Delta))
where
\Delta
is the maximum absolute value of an entry in
A
and
c
.

Next, we bound the
\ell_{1}
-norm of a solution.

Proposition 11

(
[
52
, Lemma 3]
)
.

If the system
(Ax=b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})
is feasible, it has a solution

satisfying
||x^{*}||_{1}\leq\mathcal{O}(m\cdot\Delta(A))^{m}\cdot(||b||_{\infty}+1)
.

At the expense of solving an LP relaxation, we can assume that the entries in the vector

Proposition 12

(

)

and

(Ax=b,\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0})

We prove WK[1]-hardness by reducing from

This problem is known to be WK[1]-complete

]

For each set

For each

By

The running time from

). The algorithm

Vertex Multiway Cut
.
We will employ connectivity oracles to quickly verify that a solution candidate forms a cut.

Definition 14
.

A vertex (resp. edge) failure connectivity oracle for a graph
G
and integer
d
supports two types of operations.
An update operation is performed only once and it processes a set
D
of vertices (resp. edges) from
G
of size at most
d
.
In a query operation, for given vertices
u,v\in G
the oracle determines whether
u,v
are connected in
G-D
.

Proposition 15

(
[
62
]
)
.

There is a deterministic vertex (resp. edge) failure connectivity oracle for undirected graphs with polynomial preprocessing time, update time
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}\cdot\log^{4}n\cdot\log^{4}d)
, and worst-case query time
\mathcal{O}(d)
.

The statement in
[
62
]
concerns vertex failure connectivity only but this setting generalizes edge failure connectivity.
To see this, subdivide each edge in
G
and mimic removal of an edge by removing the corresponding vertex (see also
[
47
]
).

r
-Way Cut

Parameter:

k

Input:
undirected graph
G
, integers
r
and
k

Question:
is there a set
X\subseteq E(G)
such that
G-X
has at least
r
connected components and
|X|\leq k
?

The vertex-deletion variant of the problem is
W[1]-hard already under the parameterization by
k+r

[
64
]
.
Edge deletion is easier and the above stated variant is FPT
[
19
]
.
However, it does not admit a polynomial kernel unless NP
\subseteq
coNP/poly
[
20
]
.
We will show that
r
-Way Cut
belongs to WK[1] which, in the light of
\Cref
thm:ilp-wk1, makes the problem amenable to ILP-based preprocessing.

Lemma 16
.

r
-Way Cut
\in
WK[1].

Proof.

By
\Cref
thm:characterization we need to check that the problem is solvable in time
2^{\mathsf{poly}(k)}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
and that it admits a witness verification protocol with complexity
\mathsf{poly}(k,\log n)
.
The first condition is satisfied due to known
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time algorithm
[
19
]
.

Observe that removal of one edge can increase the number of connected component by at most one.
Let
\mathcal{C}(G)
denote the set of connected components of
G
and
\ell=|\mathcal{C}(G)|
.
If
\ell+k<r
then clearly there can be no solution, so let us assume
r-\ell\leq k
.
Suppose that
X\subseteq E(G)
is a solution.
For every
C\in\mathcal{C}(G)
that is being split into
r_{C}>1
parts, there exists vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{r_{C}}\in V(C)
that are pairwise disconnected in
G-X
.
The number of connected component of
G-X
equals
\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(G)}r_{C}
.
Since it suffices to split
r-\ell\leq k
components, we may assume that there at most
k
components
C\in\mathcal{C}(G)
for which
r_{C}>1
.
Therefore, when
X
is a solution, there must exist
\leq 2k
vertices that can be divided into
t\leq k
groups
V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{t}
so that (i) each group
V_{i}
is pairwise connected in
G
, (ii)
v\in V_{i}
and
u\in V_{j}
are not connected in
G
whenever
i\neq j
, (iii) distinct
u,v\in V_{i}
are not connected in
G-X
, and (iv)
\sum_{i=1}^{t}(|V_{i}|-1)=r-\ell
.
This constitutes a certificate that
X
is a solution.

We now design the witness verification protocol (
\cal A,B
).
The algorithm
\cal A
computes the connected components of
G
, assigns vertices in each component a unique label, and
initializes the data structure from
\Cref
prop:cut:oracle.
The algorithm
\cal B
inherits the state of the data structure together with labels marking the connected components and is provided the following witness: a set
X
of at most
k
edges and
t\leq k
vertex sets
V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{t}
with
\leq 2k
vertices in total.
First we check that all the given vertices are pairwise distinct, in time
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
.
Then we check the conditions (i)-(iv).
The conditions (i)-(ii) can be verified using labels in time
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
.
To handle condition (iii), we first perform the update operation on the edge failure connectivity oracle for set
X
in time
\mathcal{O}(k^{3}\cdot\log^{4}n)
.
Then we query the oracle for each pair
u,v\in V_{i}
for each
i\in[1,t]
to check that they are disconnected in
G-X
.
This takes total time
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
.
Finally, condition (iv) boils down to number additions, which are performed in time
\mathcal{O}(k)
.
This concludes the description of the protocol.
∎

We move on to another cut-based problem.

The existence of a polynomial kernel for

[

or where the input graph is planar

, and a quasi-polynomial kernel for the edge-deletion variant

Proposition 17

(
[

, Lemma 2.7]
)
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given an instance

of

Vertex Multiway Cut
, outputs an equivalent instance

Our witness verification protocol combines this procedure with the vertex failure connectivity oracle.

Lemma 18
.

Vertex Multiway Cut
\in

Proof.

can be solved in time
2^{k}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
21
]
and it remains to construct a witness verification protocol.
The algorithm
\cal A
executes the reduction from
\Cref

.
Then it initializes the vertex failure connectivity oracle from

For two strings

of equal length, their

Parameter:

k

Input:
strings

of equal length, integer

Question:
is there a common string partition of

of size at most
k
?

The study of this problem is motivated by applications in comparative
genomics
[
12
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, nothing is known about its kernelization status.
In order to design a witness verification protocol, we will employ the following data structure for maintaining a dynamic collection of strings.

Proposition 19

(
[
65
]
)
.

There is a deterministic data structure that supports the following operations in worst-case time
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}m\cdot\log^{*}m)

where
m
is the total number of operations.

1.

Create a singleton string with symbol

.

(2,3)
keep the host strings in the collection.

Lemma 20
.

Minimum Common String Partition
\in
WK[1]
.

Proof.

The problem can be solved in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2}\log k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
12
]
and so it suffices to give a witness verification protocol of complexity

.
The algorithm
\cal A

builds strings

using operations
(1,3)
of data structure from
\Cref

4.3
Long Path

A
feedback vertex set
(FVS) in a graph
G
is a set
X\subseteq V(G)
for which
G-X
is acyclic. The FVS number of
G
is the size of its smallest feedback vertex set.

Long Path / FVS

Parameter:

k=
FVS number of
G

Input:
undirected graph
G
, integer
\ell

Question:
is there a path on
\ell
vertices in
G
?

It is known that
Long Path
admits a polynomial kernel when parameterized by the vertex cover number
[
8
]
and in the same paper it was asked whether the same holds for the FVS number parameterization.
To the best of our knowledge, this question remains open since 2013.
An FPT algorithm for
Long Path / FVS
is implied by the following result because treewidth is never larger that the FVS number.

Proposition 21

(
[
5
, Theorem 3.20]
)
.

Long Path
can be solved in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{tw})}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
where
\mathsf{tw}
denotes the treewidth of the input graph.

We remark that this result has been stated under the assumption that a tree decomposition of width
\mathsf{tw}
is provided in the input but this assumption can be dropped due to known approximation algorithms for treewidth
[
5
, §5]
.

For a tree
T
and
u,v\in V(T)
let
P_{T}(u,v)
denote the unique
(u,v)
-path in
T
.
The length of a path
P
is defined as
|V(P)|
.

Lemma 22
.

The problem
Long Path / FVS
belongs to
WK[1]
.

Proof.

The FPT algorithm follows from
\Cref
prop:path:fpt since
\mathsf{tw}(G)\leq\mathsf{fvs}(G)
.
We construct a witness verification protocol (
\cal A,B
).
The algorithm
\cal A
first runs 2-approximation for
Feedback Vertex Set

[
2
]
so we can assume that a feedback vertex set
X
of size at most
2k
is known.
Next, it computes the connected components of
G-X
by assigning vertices in each component a unique label.
Then, on each tree
T
we compute the following information.
For each
v_{1},v_{2}\in V(T)
we store the length
of the path
P_{T}(v_{1},v_{2})
.
For each
v_{1},v_{2},u_{1},u_{2}\in V(T)
we store a bit indicating whether
V(P_{T}(v_{1},v_{2}))\cap V(P_{T}(u_{1},u_{2}))=\emptyset
.
The algorithm
\cal A
outputs all these structures, together with the adjacency matrix of
G
.

Observe that when
P
is a path in
G
, then it has at most
2k+1
maximal subpaths
P_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{t}
in
G-X
.
The witness is a description of a path
P
as a sequence of vertices from
X
and pairs of vertices from
G-X
that form the endpoints of subpaths
P_{i}
.
To verify the witness we need to check (i) that the total length of path is
\ell
, (ii) that the consecutive vertices in the description are adjacent in
G
, and (iii) that the given subpaths are pairwise disjoint.
To check condition (i) we read the length of each path
P_{i}
specified by endpoints
v_{1},v_{2}
.
This quantity can be retrieved from the precomputed table and
the total length is then computed using
\mathcal{O}(k)
addition.
Condition (ii) is tested with the adjacency matrix.
To check condition (iii), we consider all pairs
i,j\in[1,t]
and if the endpoints of
P_{i},P_{j}
belong to the same tree, we determine disjointedness of
P_{i},P_{j}
using the precomputed table.
Then we also compare the listed vertices from
X
to ensure that they are pairwise distinct.
The witness can be encoded with
\mathcal{O}(k\log n)
bits while the verification takes time
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
.
This yields a witness verification protocol of complexity
\mathsf{poly}(k,\log n)
.
∎

Steiner Tree / T

Parameter:

k=|T|

Input:
graph
G
, set
T\subseteq V(G)
, integer
\ell

Question:
is there a tree
X\subseteq E(G)
that spans
T
and
|X|\leq\ell
?

Hermelin et al.
[
46
]
showed that

is WK[1]-complete when parameterized by
\ell
.
Since
k\leq\ell
in any non-trivial instance, this implies that the problem is WK[1]-hard when parameterized by
k
and, furthermore, that it does not admit a polynomial kernel unless NP
\subseteq
coNP/poly.

Lemma 23
.

The problem
Steiner Tree / T
belongs to WK[1].

Proof.

Steiner Tree
can be solved in time
3^{k}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
26
]
and
we present a witness verification protocol.
The algorithm
\cal A
computes all the pairwise distances in
G
and outputs them together with the set
T
.
The witness supplied to algorithm
\cal B
is given by a set
Y\subseteq V(G)\setminus T
of at most
k-1
vertices and a tree
F
on the vertex set
T\cup Y
.
The verification protocol reads the distances in
G
between each pair
u,v\in T\cup Y
where
uv\in E(F)
and checks if their sum is at most
\ell
.

If the verification succeeds then clearly
G
contains a connected subgraph spanning

on

edges, so it also contains a tree with this property.
In the other direction, suppose that there exists a solution tree
X
.
This tree has at most
k
leaves so the number of vertices of degree greater than 2 is at most

.
The witness guesses such vertices that are not in
T
and the tree
F
obtained from

by contracting each vertex

of degree two to one of its neighbors.
The total number of edges lying on paths corresponding to
E(F)
is clearly

which bounds the sum of distances read by the algorithm
\cal B
.
This concludes the correctness proof of the protocol.
The witness can be encoded using
\mathcal{O}(k\log n)
bits while verification requires
\mathcal{O}(k)
additions of numbers from
[1,n]
.
∎

4.5
Optimal Discretization

Consider sets
W_{1},W_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{2}
.
A pair
(X,Y)
of sets
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
is called a separation of
(W_{1},W_{2})
if for every
(x_{1},y_{1})\in W_{1}
and
(x_{2},y_{2})\in W_{2}
there exists an element of
X
strictly between
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
or an
element of
Y
strictly between
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
.
A geometric interpretation of
(X,Y)
is a family of axis-parallel lines (vertical line
\ell_{x}
for each
x\in X
and horizontal line
\ell_{y}
for each
y\in Y
) so that for every component
C
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{z\in X\cup Y}\ell_{z}
the closure of
C
contains either only points from
W_{1}
or only points from
W_{2}
.

Optimal Discretization

Parameter:
k

Input:
sets
W_{1},W_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{2}
, integer
k

Question:
is there a separation
(X,Y)
of
(W_{1},W_{2})
with
|X|+|Y|\leq k
?

The geometric interpretation makes the problem attractive from the perspective of learning theory.
This motivated the study of parameterized algorithms for
Optimal Discretization
: it turns out to be FPT
[
59
]
but only when we consider axis-parallel separating lines
[
10
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, nothing is known about its kernelization status.

For
F\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
we define
h(W)=\{\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}\colon\,x_{1},x_{2}\in F\}
.
For
W\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{2}
we define sets
h_{X}(W),h_{Y}(W)\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
as respectively
h(W_{X}),g(W_{Y})
, where
W_{X},W_{Y}
are projections of
W
into
X/Y
-coordinates.
It is easy to modify any separation
(X,Y)
of
(W_{1},W_{2})
to only use coordinates that lie exactly in between two coordinates from the input, without affecting the set of pairs that are separated.

Observation 24
.

There exists a minimum-size separation
(X,Y)
of
(W_{1},W_{2})
satisfying
X\subseteq h_{X}(W_{1}\cup W_{2}),\,Y\subseteq h_{Y}(W_{1}\cup W_{2})
.

Lemma 25
.

Optimal Discretization
\in
WK[1].

Proof.

First we check that
Optimal Discretization
can be solved in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2}\log k)}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
59
]
.
The algorithm
\cal A
of the witness verification protocol considers all tuples
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
where
x_{1},x_{2}\in h_{X}(W_{1}\cup W_{2})\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
,
y_{1},y_{2}\in h_{Y}(W_{1}\cup W_{2})\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
,
x_{1}<x_{2}
, and
y_{1}<y_{2}
.
Their number is polynomial in
|W_{1}|+|W_{2}|
.
For a tuple
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
it checks whether
[x_{1},x_{2}]\times[y_{1},y_{2}]
contains at least one point from
W_{1}
and from
W_{2}
.
If it contains both, then such tuple is marked as bad.
The algorithm
\cal A
outputs the set of bad tuples, sorted lexicographically.

The witness supplied to the algorithm
\cal B
is a pair
(X,Y)
where
X\subseteq h_{X}(W_{1}\cup W_{2})
,
Y\subseteq h_{Y}(W_{1}\cup W_{2})
, and
|X|+|Y|\leq k
.
By
\Cref
lem:disc:h we can assume that there exists an optimal solution of this form.
Moreover, we require the sets
X,Y
to be listed in the increasing order of elements.
The verification mechanism considers all tuples
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
where
x_{1},x_{2}
are consecutive elements in
X\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
and likewise for
y_{1},y_{2}
.
There are
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
such tuples, each corresponding to a connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{z\in X\cup Y}\ell_{z}
.
Then
(X,Y)
forms a separation for
(W_{1},W_{2})
if and only if none of these tuples has been marked as bad.
One can check if
(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
is bad by performing binary search in the data structure provided by the algorithm
\cal A
.
In summary, the witness can be encoded with
\mathcal{O}(k\cdot\log n)
bits whereas verification takes time
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}\cdot\log n)
.
∎

5
Weighted Vertex Cover

The
vertex cover number
(VC number) of
G
is the size of the smallest vertex cover in
G
.

Weighted Vertex Cover / VC

Parameter:
VC number of
G

Input:
graph
G
, weight function
w\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
given in unary, integer
\ell

Question:
is there a vertex cover in
G
of total weight at most
\ell
?

The unweighted variant of
Vertex Cover
is probably the most heavily studied problem in kernelization
[
16
,
35
,
38
,
58
]
.
The weighted variant has a polynomial kernel when parameterized by the number of vertices in the sought solution
[
34
]
but it is
unlikely to admit a polynomial kernel when parameterized by the VC number, even when the weights are given in unary
[
48
]
.
We remark that if the weights were provided in binary, the theorem below would also hold but only when treating the logarithm of the maximal weight as an additional parameter.

See
3

Proof.

We can assume that a vertex cover
Y
of
G
of size at most
2k
is known, due to the classic 2-approximation algorithm.
The problem can be readily solved in time
2^{|Y|}\cdot|V(G)|
so by the standard argument we can assume that
\log n\leq k
, where
n
is the total input size.
Consider the following mixed integer linear program.

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G)}w(v)\cdot x_{v}

\displaystyle\forall_{u\in Y}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in N_{G}(u)}x_{v}+\text{deg}_{G}(u)\cdot x_{u}\geq\text{deg}_{G}(u)

\displaystyle\forall_{v\in V(G)}\quad

\displaystyle 0\leq x_{v}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall_{u\in Y}\quad

\displaystyle x_{u}\in\mathbb{Z}

The number of integral variables, as well as the number of constrains, is
|Y|\leq 2k
.
Recall that the bounds
0\leq x_{v}\leq 1
are not counted as constraints in our setup.

We claim that the optimum of this program equals
the minimum weight vertex cover in
G
.
First, suppose that
S\subseteq V(G)
is a vertex cover.
We claim that the binary characteristic vector
x
of
S

satisfies all the constraints.
Consider
u\in Y
.
If
u\in S
then clearly
\sum_{v\in N_{G}(u)}x_{v}+\text{deg}_{G}(u)\cdot x_{u}\geq\text{deg}_{G}(u)
.
If
u\not\in S
then
N_{G}(u)\subseteq S
and again the inequality holds.

In the other direction, consider a feasible solution
x
.
We claim that set
S=\{v\in V(G)\colon x_{v}=1\}
forms a vertex cover.
We need to show that every edge
uw\in E(G)
is covered by
S
.
Since
Y
is also a vertex cover, we can assume w.l.o.g. that
u\in Y
.
Suppose that
x_{u}<1
.
Because
x_{u}
is an integral variable, this implies
x_{u}=0
, and so it must hold that
\sum_{v\in N_{G}(u)}x_{v}\geq\text{deg}_{G}(u)
.
All the variables are upper bounded by 1, which enforces that
x_{v}=1
for every
v\in N_{G}(u)
, in particular for
v=w
.
Hence the presented MILP indeed models minimum weight vertex cover.

In order to obtain a feasibility problem we replace the objective function with a constraint
\sum_{v\in V(G)}w(v)\cdot x_{v}\leq\ell
.
This proves the second part of the theorem.
Observe that the entries in the obtained ILP matrix are vertex degrees and vertex weights, which are at most
2^{k}
because we assumed
\log n\leq k
.
This gives the bound on
\Delta(A)
and proves the first part of the theorem, by simply treating all the variables as integral.
∎

It is tempting to try to get rid of the upper bounds
x_{v}\leq 1
and attain an ILP in the form considered in
\Cref
thm:ilp-wk1.
If we allow for not
\mathcal{O}(k)
but
\mathsf{poly}(k)
constraints, this is equivalent to proving that
Weighted Vertex Cover / VC
is in WK[1].
A potential witness verification protocol could guess the intersection of a solution
S
with the vertex cover
Y
but it is unclear how to verify quickly that the
remaining part of the solution has small weight.

6
Characterization of WK[1]

To prove
\Cref
thm:characterization we will reduce problems with fast witness verification protocols to
Binary NDTM Halting
.
Since the running time is measured in the RAM model, we need to translate the protocol to short computation of a Turing machine.
However, the standard translation methods do not preserve sublinear running times because a regular Turing machine spends a lot of time moving the head along the tape.
To overcome this issue, we will consider Turing machines that can operate their heads more efficiently.

Definition 26

(Random-access Turing Machine (RTM)
[
44
]
)
.

An RTM is a Turing machine with three linear tapes over binary alphabet, called the main tape
M
,
the address tape
A
and the auxiliary tape
X
, such that the head of the main tape (the main
head) is always in the cell whose number is the contents of the address tape in binary. An instruction
for an RTM has the form

(p,\alpha_{M},\alpha_{A},\alpha_{X})\to(q,\beta_{M},\beta_{A},\beta_{X},\gamma_{A},\gamma_{X})

and means the following: If the control state is
p
and the symbols in the observed cells on
the three tapes are binary digits
\alpha_{M},\alpha_{A},\alpha_{X}
respectively, then print binary digits
\beta_{M},\beta_{A},\beta_{X}

in the respective cells, move the address head to the left (resp. to the right) if
\gamma_{A}=-1

(resp.
\gamma_{A}=+1
), move the auxiliary tape head with respect to
\gamma_{X}
, and go to control state
q
.

Proposition 27

(

]
)
.

Any computation in the RAM model with logarithmic word length on

Proof.

If
L\in

In the other direction, we will show that any problem satisfying both conditions admits a PPT to

Consider the verification algorithm
\cal B

\cal A

By

We will modify

Let

The input word

The machine finds the pair occurrence

with

and finishes in one of three states, reporting that either
b_{i}=0
,
b_{i}=1
, or that such a pair does not exit, and moving the head to the initial position.
Clearly, there exists such a machine
\cal T
that runs in time
\mathcal{O}(|y|^{c})

.

We now create an RTM
{\cal B}_{2}

{\cal B}_{1}
and

{\cal T}

The machine

After reading a symbol from

.
After this subroutine, the state of
\cal T

at that time.
If the machine
{\cal B}_{1}

We are ready to describe the reduction from

.
Given an instance

If yes, the running time

The reduction first runs the algorithm
\cal A

We showed that the existence of the algorithm

{\cal B}_{2}

The input to

7
Conclusion

We have presented a characterization of parameterized problems reducible to ILP with few constraints and classified several new problems into this category through witness verification protocols.
We find this connection surprising and inspiring for exploring new directions in parameterized complexity.
Can we further extend this classification?
In particular we ask whether the following problems belong to WK[1]:
Weighted Vertex Cover / VC
,
Connected FVS
,
Directed FVS
, and
Vertex Planarization
(the last three parameterized by the solution size).

Our classification concerns ILPs in the standard form without upper bounds.
Allowing for upper bounds on variables gives a greater expressive power when the number of constraints is a parameter (see
\Cref
obs:ilp:binary and also
[
69
]
).
It would be interesting to also characterize parameterized problems reducible to upper-bounded ILP with few constraints.
However, the location of such a feasibility problem in the parameterized complexity landscape remains unclear because we do not know whether it admits short NP-witnesses
[
73
]
.
Yet another direction is to describe problems that are reducible to ILP with few variables.

Containment in WK[1] only ensures that a problem can be modeled by ILP with
\mathsf{poly}(k)
constraints.
Even though the degree of this polynomial can be retraced from the chain of reductions, it is unlikely to be optimal.
As the natural next step towards bridging the gap between theory and practice, we would like to optimize the polynomial degree, by designing explicit ILP formulation with
\mathcal{O}(k^{c})
constraints, where
c
is as small as possible, ideally one.
A good starting point would be to consider
problems with simple proofs of containment in WK[1], such as

k
-Path
or
Connected Vertex Cover

[
46
]
.
