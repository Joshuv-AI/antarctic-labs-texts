Preparedness for the emerging risk of yellow fever in the Americas
Authors: Helena J. Chapman, Bienvenido A. Veras-Estévez, Elena G. Chicaiza-Mora, Concepción Zúniga Valeriano, Reyna M. Durón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTYellow fever is a viral zoonosis of variable geographic distribution in the Americas. With no specific treatment for this vector-borne disease, vaccination is the best prevention available. This flavivirus has been circulating in the Americas since the late 15th century, and over the past decades, the virus has reemerged and is now endemic in 13 countries in South and Central America. Ecosystem changes may have had a role in this reemergence. The International Health Regulations were approved by the World Health Assembly in 2005 as a guidance document to support national policies with important measures for disease control and prevention of new epidemics. This article presents an illustrative case of the efforts of three ministries of health in the Americas (Dominican Republic, Ecuador, and Honduras) to implement international guidelines and reduce yellow fever transmission in the region. Using the One Health framework, we propose selected recommendations for the prevention and control of yellow fever outbreaks.

Based on World Health Organization (WHO) reports, cases of yellow fever in humans in 2024 were recorded mainly in the Amazon region in the Americas. However, while the 2025 yellow fever outbreak mostly affected the Amazon basin, confirmed human cases of yellow fever were reported in an extended geographic area. The report of the wider spread of yellow fever cases highlights the continuing sylvatic spread and ecosystem interactions between humans and mosquitoes near forest borders (1). Additionally, environmental factors, including increased temperatures and land use changes (e.g., deforestation), may have helped expand the risk of yellow fever in the Americas region beyond the Amazon. By May 2025, 235 human cases had been reported in five countries: Bolivia, Plurinational State of (four cases, two deaths [50.0% case fatality rate]), Brazil (111 cases, 44 deaths [39.6%]), Colombia (74 cases, 31 deaths [41.9%]), Ecuador (eight cases, six deaths [75.0%]), and Peru (38 cases, 13 deaths [34.2%]) (1). Recent events, such as the death of three monkeys in Colombia in July 2025, have triggered an immediate response to contain the yellow fever outbreak in the Americas (2).

Understanding the ecological and environmental factors affecting habitat suitability for vectors, the impact of increased tourism and migratory patterns, and geographic gaps in vaccine access will offer insight on the urgent regional actions needed to operationalize the One Health approach (the human–animal–environment relationship) and limit the expansion of disease transmission risk to non-endemic countries in the Americas (2). This article presents an illustrative case of the efforts of the ministries of health of the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, and Honduras to implement the International Health Regulations (IHR) and reduce the risk of yellow fever transmission in the Americas. This comparative analysis highlights endemic transmission linked to the sylvatic transmission cycle in Ecuador (3), and provides concrete lessons that can be applied in the Dominican Republic and Honduras, where no circulating strains are currently present. We used techniques in document analysis to review publicly available ministry of health reports and website content.

CONTEXT OF DISEASE TRANSMISSION

Yellow fever is a viral zoonosis transmitted by mosquitoes of the genera Haemagogus, Sabethes, and Aedes, and is endemic in sub-Saharan Africa and tropical South America. In South America, transmission occurs through three main cycles: the sylvatic (jungle) cycle, which involves non-human primates and Haemagogus and Sabethes mosquitoes; the urban cycle, which involves A. aegypti and humans; and the intermediate cycle (specific to Africa), which involves both humans, primates, and Aedes mosquitoes (4). Most infections cause a febrile illness within the first 5 days of infection. However, an estimated 12% of cases develop the hemorrhagic stage (with a 30–60% case fatality rate) leading to jaundice, bleeding, seizures, and kidney and liver failure (4). Without the availability of specific treatments, prevention is focused on the single dose vaccine to provide lifetime immunity and control disease transmission, although a booster dose may be needed for immunocompromised individuals (e.g., with HIV) or children (younger than 2 years) (5).

The virus has been circulating in the Americas since the late 15th century and in 1881, Cuban physician and scientist Carlos Finlay identified that the mosquito vector was the mode of transmission (6). During the 17th to the 19th centuries, maritime trade and commerce influenced the geographic expansion and spread of A. aegypti (7). Endemic circulation of the virus was observed with sporadic outbreaks in the region linked to socioeconomic, ecological, and demographic factors, especially enzootic transmission cycles near to urban areas (6, 7). Since the 1970s, however, the virus has reemerged and is now endemic in 13 countries in South and Central America. This reemergence is due to increased temperatures that favor vector habitat suitability and deforestation that has increased human–mosquito proximity (8).

Approved by the World Health Assembly (WHA58.3) in 2005, the IHR provides guidance on selected infectious diseases of importance, including yellow fever (9). Recognizing the urgency for disease control, the Strategy to Eliminate the Yellow Fever Epidemics (EYE) was launched in 2017. This strategy brought together more than 50 partners to support at-risk populations across more than 40 countries in Africa and the Americas in their prevention and response efforts (8). Together with these global initiatives, the launch of the One health joint plan of action 2022–2026: working together for the health of humans, animals, plants and the environment offers tangible approaches to operationalize One Health, harmonized with multisectoral, multidisciplinary strategies for vector control to tackle endemic and emerging zoonotic and vector-borne diseases (10).

FACTORS SHAPING MOSQUITO ACTIVITY

Population mobility

Each country has open landscapes and waterbodies. Ecuador (with 18.2 million inhabitants) has Colombia to the north, Peru to the south-east, and access to the Pacific Ocean. Honduras (with 10.8 million inhabitants) has Guatemala to the west, El Salvador to the south-west, Nicaragua to the south-east, and access to the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. The Dominican Republic (with 11.4 million inhabitants) has Haiti to the west and access to the Atlantic Ocean and Caribbean Sea. The movement of people through domestic and international tourism and migratory patterns present complex factors, which include the presence of susceptible individuals in new geographic areas, altered biology or epidemiology of pathogens and vectors, and inadequate pre-travel health messaging.

Population movement is increasing and affecting mosquito activity. For example, the ministries of tourism of the three countries have confirmed that tourism strengthened economic development in 2024, with the Dominican Republic reaching 11 million tourists (a 9% increase from 2023), Ecuador reaching 1.1 million tourists, and Honduras reaching 1.9 million tourists (a 12% increase from 2023). Furthermore, increased human movement in the Americas has been linked to significant malaria outbreaks (Anopheles darlingi) in the Darién Gap (connecting Panama and Colombia), with 9000 crossings in 2018 to more than 500 000 crossing in 2023 (11). Additionally, as a result of rural to urban migration in search of job and educational opportunities, the population living in urban areas is projected to increase globally from 55% in 2018 to 68% by 2050.

Ecosystem changes

The delicate balance of natural ecosystems is susceptible to temperature and precipitation changes and extreme weather events, which can increase the risk of human–animal or human–mosquito proximity, pathogen or vector adaptation to new habitats, and new pathogen strains (12). Warmer temperatures can accelerate vector breeding cycles and expand habitats to new geographic areas, whereas dry conditions can quickly ignite vegetation into forest fires, leading to habitat loss and poor air quality. Prolonged drought and limited waterbodies can promote mosquito feeding and survival with drought-resistant Aedes spp. eggs, while flooding can leave stagnant water that provides habitats for vector survival and disease transmission.

Ecosystem alterations can occur through natural and anthropogenic factors, including deforestation from farming, logging, and mining that can trigger habitat and biodiversity loss. This transition has led to projections that 10% to 47% of the Amazon forests may experience significant ecological disturbances by 2050 (13). Reduced ecosystem resilience coupled with the increased frequency of extreme weather events worldwide, offer a “perfect storm” for the human and animal cycles of yellow fever transmission.

ENDEMIC TRANSMISSION OF YELLOW FEVER IN ECUADOR

Over the past decade, the Ecuador Ministry of Health has reported yellow fever cases in 2012 (Napo province), 2017 (Sucumbíos province), and 2024 (imported case from Colombia), and effectively halted the outbreaks. As the rainy season occurs between December and May, the Ministry of Health, Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), and the National Institute of Public Health Research established 44 health district brigades to conduct entomological and educational campaigns in more than 900 homes in the Zamora and Yacuambi health districts (14). After inspecting homes for mosquito breeding sites, they educated residents about yellow fever symptoms and how to identify and eliminate A. aegypti breeding sites, and fumigated inside and outside of homes.

To support institutional collaborations between different bodies, the National Emergency Operations Committee implemented a plan in May 2025, with a comprehensive approach that included the health, finance, education, and tourism ministries, the Police, the Armed Forces, and local governments (15). This plan aimed to strengthen epidemiological surveillance, vaccination campaigns, public communication, and logistical and territorial coordination (15). The Ecuador Ministry of Health supported the widespread vaccination campaign conducted in six Amazonian provinces from April to June 2025, and administered more than 157 874 doses to community residents (aged 1–60 years). Only 11 laboratory-confirmed cases were reported in three provinces (Morona Santiago, Zamora Chinchipe, and Sucumbíos), with eight deaths (72.7% case fatality rate) and three survivors with documented vaccination (16).

As of mid-July 2025, the Ministry of Health had administered 481 760 doses and visited almost 4000 homes to support vector control activities in six Amazonian provinces – Morona Napo, Orellana, Pastaza, Santiago, Sucumbíos, and Zamora Chinchipe (17). The Ministry also acquired 200 000 additional vaccine doses to guarantee sufficient supplies to reinforce prevention measures in high-risk areas. Mass vaccination in these susceptible areas, together with the timely availability of vaccines to respond to larger outbreaks, thus consolidated a solid, flexible, and targeted prevention strategy.

SUSTAINED NATIONAL EFFORTS IN DOMINICAN REPUBLIC, ECUADOR, AND HONDURAS

Over the past 2 decades, the ministries of health of the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, and Honduras have supported robust prevention and control measures to ensure epidemiological surveillance, vaccination campaigns, and travel advisories in the Americas. The countries represent three geographic examples of South and Central America and the Caribbean, and in all areas, ecological and environmental disturbances driven by natural and anthropogenic phenomena are intensifying human–mosquito contact in urban and peri-urban settings. With the adoption of the third edition of the IHR in 2005, subsequently implemented in 2007, Member States affirmed political commitment to robust epidemiological surveillance systems that monitor disease trends as well as support timely reporting nationally and internationally (18). Supporting epidemiological surveillance

The three ministries of health rely on national laboratories, under the departments of epidemiology, with access to diagnostic tests for suspected cases of yellow fever. The Ecuador Ministry of Health, due to the endemic transmission, has further established internal protocols and institutional coordination with support from PAHO. They have utilized an integrated approach with active sentinel (primates as sentinels) and passive surveillance as needed to monitor disease outbreaks in at-risk regions and maintain routine reporting measures. The health ministries of the Dominican Republic and Honduras, aware of endemic outbreaks in the geographic region, have continued to prioritize national policies that reinforce surveillance systems designed to prevent disease transmission and spread.

Increasing public awareness through vaccination campaigns

The Ecuador Ministry of Health has supported vaccination campaigns, with relevant health messaging to correct misinformation and disinformation and improve health literacy. Through multisectoral coordination aligned with the One Health approach and the IHR and in collaboration with national institutions and international partners such as PAHO/WHO, these actions strengthen risk communication and support coordinated prevention and response efforts. The health ministries of the Dominican Republic and Honduras do not actively engage in public awareness campaigns related to yellow fever transmission, as efforts focus on international travelers (not at-risk communities).

Communicating travel advisories about yellow fever

In all three countries, people traveling from these countries to other countries with endemic yellow fever (namely, sub-Saharan Africa and the Amazon) via airports, seaports, and land border crossings must show evidence of yellow fever vaccination at least 10 days before travel. Each country offers yellow fever vaccines at no cost at authorized ministry of health locations and widely shares information on disease symptoms, outbreak alerts, and vaccines via ministry of health websites and social media platforms. These sustained national efforts help reduce economic and geographic disparities in vaccine access.

USING A ONE HEALTH APPROACH

Although these three ministries of health lack a comprehensive institutional framework focusing on One Health, health leaders have incorporated the One Health approach within selected programs and policies aligned with national priorities. They have spearheaded key activities to catalyze modernization of existing frameworks and strengthen health system preparedness and resilience in the face of emerging health risks. First, together with national and international agencies, they have supported Field Epidemiology Training Programs, with an emphasis on One Health, as a resource to train “disease detectives” for the national workforce. Second, they have expanded and reinforced their national epidemiological surveillance systems, including sentinel surveillance sites, for appropriate data collection and analysis of circulating pathogens (e.g., severe acute respiratory infections). Third, they have collaborated with different ministries (e.g., Agriculture, Livestock, and Environment and Natural Resources) and other stakeholders to develop training courses on national epidemiological surveillance related to human, animal, and environmental health risks (e.g., dengue, malaria, rabies, and severe acute respiratory infections). These efforts will require monitoring and evaluation to ensure their effective and operational implementation across sectors. Similarly, the health systems in Brazil, Chile, and Colombia have undertaken strategic steps to develop academic programs, scientific events and symposia, regional networks, and intersectoral initiatives aligned with the One Health approach (29).

Using a One Health approach, together with health and entomological surveillance records, geospatial data and community participation projects (e.g., GLOBE Mosquito Habitat Mapper, Mosquito Alert, and iNaturalist), can offer spatial and temporal information to identify mosquito habitats and emerging disease hotspots, which can improve integrated vector management (30). Researchers can use artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms to integrate Earth science data (e.g., land surface temperature, precipitation, and vegetation) and develop risk maps and early warning systems to inform decision-making (30). Incorporating the 4 Cs (communication, coordination, collaboration, and capacity-building) in field applications can guide and strengthen vector surveillance and monitoring (10). Conclusion

The complex dynamics underlying yellow fever transmission highlight the need for health systems to effectively adopt and implement the One Health approach in public policies and community-based programs, and systematically integrate human, animal, and environmental health components. The reemergence of yellow fever in the Americas, with reported cases in at least 13 countries in South and Central America, provides public health authorities with an opportunity to learn from Ecuador’s rapid, targeted, and coordinated response to contain outbreaks. This insight can be collectively discussed and subsequently adapted to other geographically vulnerable settings, such as the Caribbean.

To ensure the sustainability of vector control and surveillance achievements in the Americas, it will be key to expand systematic vaccination coverage, strengthen surveillance in remote areas, guarantee investment in integrated vector management, and adapt prevention strategies to the context of climate change and vector expansion. A comprehensive and sustained approach aligned with the IHR is essential to mitigate the spread and transmission of arboviruses such as yellow fever and other emerging and reemerging diseases. The ongoing review and assessment of the IHR guidelines can help identify strengths, gaps, and opportunities for improvement, in alignment with evolving epidemiological, environmental, and climatic conditions affecting the Americas region. Such an approach represents a promising direction for future research to strengthen health system governance, emergency preparedness, and resilience.
