A GAN-physical simulation coupled framework for joint optimization of building energy prediction and spatial configuration
Authors: Liu JinYan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBuilding energy optimization faces challenges in simultaneously addressing spatial design and energy performance prediction. This paper proposes a novel framework integrating generative adversarial networks (GANs) with physics-based simulation engines to achieve joint optimization of building energy consumption prediction and spatial configuration. The framework establishes bidirectional coupling between conditional GAN architectures and EnergyPlus thermodynamic calculations, embedding physical constraints directly into the generative process through a physics-informed discriminator. Experimental validation across office, residential, and educational building typologies demonstrates superior performance, achieving 6.8% mean absolute percentage error in energy prediction and 23.7% average energy consumption reduction compared to code-compliant baselines. The model generates well-distributed Pareto fronts containing 42–56 non-dominated solutions, outperforming conventional sequential optimization approaches by 8–12 percentage points while maintaining computational efficiency. Introduction

The building sector accounts for approximately 40% of global energy consumption, presenting both a critical challenge and an opportunity in the transition toward carbon neutrality1. As urbanization accelerates and architectural complexity increases, the need for accurate energy consumption prediction has become more pressing than ever. Traditional approaches to building energy modeling often struggle with the inherent nonlinearity and high-dimensional characteristics of energy systems, particularly when attempting to simultaneously optimize spatial configurations2. This dual challenge—predicting energy performance while optimizing spatial arrangements—has emerged as a focal point in sustainable building design, yet existing methodologies remain fragmented and often fail to capture the intricate interdependencies between architectural form and energy behavior.

Recent advances in machine learning, particularly deep learning architectures, have shown remarkable potential in energy forecasting tasks3. However, conventional neural networks typically require extensive labeled datasets and exhibit limited generalization when confronting scenarios that deviate from training distributions. Meanwhile, physical simulation tools, despite their theoretical rigor, demand substantial computational resources and expert calibration, making them less practical for iterative design processes4. The research community has increasingly recognized that neither purely data-driven nor purely physics-based approaches can adequately address the multifaceted nature of building energy optimization. This realization has spurred interest in hybrid methodologies that leverage the strengths of both paradigms, though substantial gaps remain in their integration5.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have demonstrated exceptional capabilities in learning complex distributions and generating realistic synthetic samples across various domains6. Their adversarial training mechanism enables the capture of intricate patterns without explicit feature engineering, which aligns well with the complex energy-geometry relationships in buildings. Nevertheless, most existing applications of GANs in the building domain focus narrowly on either energy prediction or spatial generation, rarely attempting to unify these two objectives within a coupled framework7. Furthermore, the interpretability concerns inherent to deep learning models remain inadequately addressed, particularly when stakeholders require transparent decision-making processes in architectural design8.

The coupling of GANs with physical simulation engines represents a promising yet underexplored direction. Physics-informed machine learning has gained traction in fluid dynamics and structural analysis, but its application to building energy systems coupled with spatial optimization remains nascent9. Existing studies often treat energy prediction and space configuration as sequential rather than concurrent processes, missing opportunities for synergistic optimization. Additionally, the computational burden of iteratively invoking physics-based simulators during GAN training poses practical barriers that current research has not sufficiently resolved10.

This paper proposes a novel joint optimization framework that integrates GANs with physical simulation for simultaneous building energy consumption prediction and spatial configuration optimization. The primary innovations include: first, a physics-constrained GAN architecture that embeds thermodynamic principles into the generative process, ensuring that predicted energy patterns remain physically plausible; second, a bidirectional coupling mechanism that enables real-time interaction between data-driven generation and physics-based validation; and third, a multi-objective optimization strategy that balances energy efficiency, spatial functionality, and architectural aesthetics within a unified computational pipeline. By addressing the limitations of isolated approaches and establishing a coherent integration between machine learning and physical modeling, this research advances the frontier of intelligent building design and provides a scalable solution for sustainable architecture in the face of escalating environmental challenges.

Generative adversarial network theory

In the context of building energy prediction, GANs present compelling advantages over conventional forecasting approaches. Building energy physical simulation methods

Physical simulation of building energy consumption rests upon fundamental thermodynamic principles that govern heat transfer and energy conversion within architectural envelopes16. At its core, the approach treats buildings as complex thermodynamic systems where energy flows obey conservation laws and transport phenomena. Contemporary simulation engines such as EnergyPlus and TRNSYS implement sophisticated solution algorithms that address the nonlinear, coupled nature of building physics equations19. EnergyPlus employs a heat balance method with simultaneous solution of surface and air temperatures, while TRNSYS adopts a modular component-based architecture facilitating custom system configurations. Despite their widespread adoption, these tools face inherent constraints. Computational expense escalates rapidly with model complexity—detailed multi-zone buildings with hourly timesteps over annual periods can require minutes to hours per simulation run. Calibration demands expertise and measured data that are often unavailable during early design stages. Perhaps most critically, these deterministic engines struggle with uncertainty quantification and cannot efficiently explore the vast design space needed for optimization tasks20. The deterministic nature also limits their ability to capture stochastic occupant behavior and equipment failures that significantly impact actual energy performance. These limitations motivate the hybrid approach developed in this research, where physics-based rigor complements rather than replaces data-driven learning.

Building space configuration optimization theory

Spatial configuration optimization in architectural design presents a combinatorial challenge where designers must determine room assignments, dimensions, and adjacencies that satisfy functional requirements while optimizing performance metrics21. The mathematical formulation typically casts this as a discrete–continuous hybrid problem, where discrete variables encode topological relationships and room types, while continuous variables represent geometric parameters such as dimensions, window-to-wall ratios, and material properties. This dual nature complicates the optimization landscape considerably, as the solution space exhibits both discrete discontinuities and continuous gradients.

Multi-objective optimization frameworks prove essential for architectural applications, given the inherent tension among competing design goals—energy efficiency, construction cost, spatial quality, daylighting performance, and occupant comfort rarely align perfectly22. Pareto optimality concepts guide the search for non-dominated solutions, where improvement in one objective necessitates degradation in another. Constraint handling poses particular difficulties in architectural contexts. Hard constraints include minimum room dimensions mandated by building codes, accessibility requirements, structural load limits, and budgetary ceilings. Soft constraints encompass preferences such as adjacency relationships between functionally related spaces or orientation preferences for specific rooms. Penalty function methods, feasibility preservation operators, and repair mechanisms constitute common strategies for managing constraint violations, though each approach carries computational trade-offs.

Intelligent optimization algorithms have gained traction as traditional gradient-based methods falter on the non-convex, multi-modal landscapes characteristic of spatial configuration problems24. Genetic algorithms encode spatial layouts as chromosomes and apply crossover and mutation operators inspired by biological evolution. Particle swarm optimization treats candidate solutions as particles moving through the design space, guided by personal best positions and global optima. Simulated annealing and ant colony optimization offer alternative metaphors, each exhibiting distinct exploration–exploitation characteristics. Recent applications demonstrate promise—genetic algorithms have optimized office layouts for daylighting and views, while multi-objective evolutionary algorithms have generated residential floor plans balancing energy performance and spatial efficiency25. Yet current implementations typically decouple energy prediction from layout generation, evaluating each candidate through separate simulation calls. This sequential approach proves computationally prohibitive when exploring thousands of design alternatives. The gap between optimization algorithms and energy assessment motivates the integrated framework proposed here, wherein generative models and physical simulations operate in concert rather than isolation.

GAN-physical simulation coupled joint optimization model construction

Overall framework design

The proposed model architecture establishes a bidirectional coupling between generative adversarial networks and physics-based energy simulation, creating a unified computational environment where spatial generation and energy assessment occur simultaneously rather than sequentially26. This integration fundamentally differs from conventional approaches that treat layout design and energy evaluation as separate stages, instead embedding thermodynamic constraints directly into the generative process. The framework comprises five interconnected modules: a conditional generator network that synthesizes spatial configurations, a physics-informed discriminator that evaluates both geometric plausibility and energy performance, a physical simulation engine that computes detailed thermal behaviors, a feedback adaptation mechanism that refines generation based on simulation outcomes, and a multi-objective optimization controller that balances competing design criteria.

Rather than passing these configurations to an external evaluator, the discriminator network simultaneously assesses geometric validity and preliminary energy characteristics through learned patterns from historical simulation data27. Configurations that pass discriminator screening then enter the physical simulation engine for rigorous thermodynamic validation.

Energy consumption prediction GAN model design

The generator network adopts a conditional architecture wherein design intentions and contextual parameters serve as explicit inputs alongside random noise vectors, enabling controlled generation of spatial configurations paired with their anticipated energy profiles30. The input layer accepts a concatenated vector comprising three components: a 128-dimensional latent code sampled from Gaussian distribution, a 64-dimensional conditional vector encoding building type and climate characteristics, and a 32-dimensional constraint vector specifying regulatory requirements such as maximum energy use intensity targets. This composite input passes through five fully connected layers with progressively expanding dimensionality—256, 512, 1024, 2048, and finally 4096 neurons—each followed by batch normalization and LeakyReLU activation to facilitate gradient flow during backpropagation. The terminal layer employs sigmoid activation to produce normalized outputs representing voxelized spatial layouts, where each voxel encodes room function, envelope properties, and geometric attributes.

Feature extraction within the generator proceeds hierarchically. Early layers learn abstract spatial patterns—circulation networks, zoning strategies, envelope-to-volume ratios—while deeper layers resolve finer details such as window placements, thermal mass distribution, and shading device configurations. Residual connections bridge non-adjacent layers, allowing low-level geometric features to bypass intermediate transformations and directly inform final outputs31. This skip-connection strategy proves particularly valuable when preserving fundamental architectural principles—symmetry, modularity, structural grids—that might otherwise become obscured through successive nonlinear transformations.

As shown in Fig. 3, the discriminator architecture mirrors the generator in complexity but operates in reverse, progressively compressing high-dimensional spatial representations into scalar judgments about authenticity and energy performance. The network begins with a 3D convolutional layer accepting 64 × 64 × 64 voxel grids as input, where each voxel contains multi-channel attributes encoding geometry, materials, and thermal properties. Four successive convolutional blocks—each comprising 3 × 3 × 3 kernels, stride-2 downsampling, batch normalization, and LeakyReLU activation—reduce spatial resolution while expanding channel depth from 32 to 64, 128, and finally 256 feature maps. A global average pooling layer collapses spatial dimensions, followed by two fully connected layers terminating in dual output heads: one producing a probability score for geometric authenticity, the other predicting normalized annual energy use intensity32.

Physical constraint embedding operates through three complementary mechanisms. First, hard constraints such as structural feasibility and code compliance enter through post-processing filters that reject generated configurations violating fundamental requirements before they reach the discriminator. Second, soft constraints related to thermal physics appear as auxiliary loss terms—penalizing unrealistic thermal bridging, excessive glazing ratios inconsistent with climate, or zone geometries that would produce severe temperature stratification. Third, the discriminator’s dual-head architecture explicitly conditions adversarial learning on energy prediction accuracy, forcing the generator to internalize thermodynamic relationships rather than merely mimicking geometric patterns33.

Training stability remains a persistent challenge in GAN applications, particularly when coupling with computationally expensive simulation callbacks. Several strategies mitigate instability. Spectral normalization applied to discriminator weights constrains the Lipschitz constant, preventing gradient explosions. A two-timescale update rule—updating the discriminator twice per generator update—maintains equilibrium between adversarial components. Progressive growing initiates training at coarse spatial resolution (16 × 16 × 16 voxels) and gradually increases fidelity, allowing the model to master fundamental spatial organization before refining details. Gradient penalty terms discourage discriminator outputs from saturating at distribution extremes. Experience replay buffers store previously generated samples, preventing the discriminator from overfitting to the generator’s current state. Together, these mechanisms enable stable convergence across 50,000 training iterations, though occasional manual intervention remains necessary when mode collapse symptoms emerge despite these safeguards.

Experimental results and analysis

Experimental data and environment configuration

Experimental validation employs three distinct building typologies to assess model generalizability across functional programs and scales: a medium-rise office building (12 floors, 8500 m2), a residential apartment complex (6 floors, 4200 m2), and a mixed-use educational facility (3 floors, 3800 m2)38. These cases were selected deliberately to span diverse occupancy patterns, HVAC system configurations, and thermal zoning strategies. The office building features a central core layout with perimeter zones, the residential complex adopts double-loaded corridor arrangements, while the educational facility presents irregular geometry accommodating specialized laboratories and lecture halls. Each case underwent detailed energy audits during 2023–2024, providing measured consumption data against which model predictions could be benchmarked.

Dataset construction drew upon multiple sources to ensure representativeness. Primary geometric and energy performance data originated from the Building Performance Database maintained by the U.S. Department of Energy, containing anonymized specifications for over 80,000 commercial and residential structures39. This repository supplied building envelope characteristics, system efficiencies, and annual energy use intensities segmented by end-use categories. Supplementary spatial layout data came from architectural drawing archives at three design firms, providing 1847 floor plans spanning office, residential, educational, and retail typologies. These plans underwent vectorization and semantic segmentation to extract room boundaries, circulation paths, window placements, and functional zone classifications.

Data preprocessing addressed several quality issues endemic to heterogeneous building datasets. Missing envelope properties were imputed through vintage-specific lookup tables cross-referenced with ASHRAE Standard 90.1 requirements for corresponding construction years18. Outlier detection identified and removed 73 buildings exhibiting physically implausible characteristics—window-to-wall ratios exceeding 0.95, reported energy intensities below 20 kWh/m2/year in cold climates, or zone counts inconsistent with floor areas. Normalization scaled continuous variables to zero mean and unit variance, while categorical attributes underwent one-hot encoding.

The spatial layout vectorization process followed a systematic pipeline. Architectural drawings in DWG and PDF formats were first converted to standardized vector representations using AutoCAD’s ObjectARX API. A custom semantic segmentation algorithm, trained on 500 manually annotated floor plans, identified room boundaries, circulation paths, window locations, and door positions with 94.3% accuracy40. Each identified element received functional labels—office, corridor, restroom, mechanical room—based on spatial characteristics and annotation text recognition.

Voxelization transformed 2D floor plans into 3D building models suitable for GAN processing. Our ray-casting algorithm operated as follows: (1) extrude floor plans vertically according to floor-to-floor heights extracted from building metadata; (2) cast rays from a regular 64 × 64 × 64 grid through the extruded geometry; (3) assign material properties to each voxel based on intersected building elements. Interior voxels received thermal mass properties corresponding to furniture and equipment loads, while envelope voxels inherited construction assembly properties from the building database. This voxelization achieved geometric fidelity within 2.3% volume error compared to original BIM models where available.

The case study begins with conditional inputs specifying: building type (office), gross floor area (8500 m2), climate zone (4A—mixed humid), target EUI (< 85 kWh/m2/year), and budget constraint (< $180/m2 construction premium). The generator synthesizes a voxelized configuration in 0.22 s, which the translation module converts to an EnergyPlus IDF containing 18 thermal zones, 847 surfaces, and detailed HVAC specifications. Annual simulation requires 23 s, returning hourly energy consumption, zone temperatures, and comfort metrics.

The optimization process explored 2847 unique configurations over 290 generations, ultimately identifying 42 Pareto-optimal solutions. The recommended design achieves 78.3 kWh/m2/year—an 8% margin below the target EUI—while maintaining spatial quality scores above 7.5 on all metrics. Key spatial decisions include: relocating the elevator core from the geometric center to the north facade (reducing solar-heated core area), increasing south-facing glazing from 45 to 55% WWR with automated exterior blinds, and introducing a double-height atrium providing stack-effect ventilation during shoulder seasons. These modifications represent coordinated design moves that conventional optimization rarely discovers through independent parameter adjustment.

Baseline model selection established competitive benchmarks against which the proposed approach could be evaluated. Four alternatives represented current state-of-practice: support vector regression (SVR) trained on geometric and operational features, random forest ensemble methods, standard feedforward neural networks without adversarial training, and conventional multi-objective genetic algorithms coupled with EnergyPlus but lacking learned generative components42. Each baseline underwent hyperparameter tuning via grid search to ensure fair comparison.

The evaluation framework depicted in Fig. 6 encompasses multiple dimensions: prediction accuracy measured through mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) and coefficient of determination (R2), computational efficiency quantified by wall-clock time and simulation calls required, optimization quality assessed via hypervolume indicator and spacing metrics on Pareto fronts, and practical viability evaluated through constraint satisfaction rates and design feasibility percentages. This multi-faceted assessment prevents overemphasis on any single performance aspect while illuminating trade-offs inherent to different methodological approaches.

The proposed GAN-physics coupled model achieved mean absolute percentage error of 6.8% when predicting total site energy use intensity, demonstrably outperforming alternative approaches43. This represents meaningful improvement over conventional regression methods, which struggled particularly with buildings exhibiting atypical spatial configurations or occupancy patterns divergent from training distribution norms. The physics-informed discriminator appears instrumental in this enhanced performance, as it constrains predictions to thermodynamically plausible regions even when encountering novel geometric arrangements.

The results presented in Table 7 reveal compelling patterns across multiple evaluation dimensions. The standard deep neural network without adversarial training achieves moderate performance, yet fails to match the proposed framework’s ability to generalize beyond training samples44. Vanilla GAN architectures lacking physics integration exhibit intermediate performance, suggesting that adversarial learning alone provides benefits, though these compound substantially when coupled with thermodynamic constraints.

Spatial configuration optimization performance analysis

Joint optimization of spatial configuration and energy performance through the coupled GAN-physics framework yielded substantial improvements over sequential or isolated optimization strategies48. Applying the model to redesign baseline building layouts achieved average energy consumption reductions of 23.7% for office buildings, 19.4% for residential complexes, and 26.3% for educational facilities when compared against code-compliant reference designs. These gains stem from the model’s ability to simultaneously consider thermal zoning strategies, envelope optimization, and daylighting integration rather than addressing these aspects independently. For instance, the optimized office layout concentrated high-occupancy zones toward the building core while positioning perimeter zones for passive solar gains—a configuration that conventional zone-by-zone optimization rarely discovers since it requires coordinated adjustments across multiple spatial elements.

The results summarized in Table 9 reveal several notable patterns distinguishing joint optimization from alternative approaches. Sequential methods that first optimize spatial layouts then assess energy performance achieve modest gains but require dramatically longer computation times—142 h versus 55 h for the proposed approach—while discovering fewer non-dominated solutions along the Pareto frontier49. Energy-only optimization predictably delivers strong consumption reductions but produces spatially compromised designs scoring poorly on functional metrics like circulation efficiency and daylighting uniformity. Conversely, spatial-only methods yield architecturally refined layouts that unfortunately exhibit mediocre energy performance. The proposed framework navigates this tension effectively, achieving near-optimal performance on both objectives simultaneously.

Discussion

The experimental findings illuminate several underlying mechanisms that account for the coupled model’s superior performance. The bidirectional information flow between generative networks and physics-based simulation creates a feedback loop wherein the generator progressively internalizes thermodynamic principles while the discriminator refines its evaluation heuristics based on rigorous energy calculations. This synergy proves particularly valuable when confronting design scenarios absent from training data—the physics engine prevents the generator from proposing thermodynamically implausible configurations, while the learned patterns accelerate exploration beyond what exhaustive simulation-based search could accomplish within reasonable timeframes. Essentially, the coupling allows data-driven learning to inherit the generalizability of first-principles physics while physics-based methods gain the computational efficiency of learned approximations.

Yet this integration delivers differential benefits depending on problem characteristics. The approach excels when dealing with complex, multi-zone buildings where spatial-thermal interdependencies become significant—educational facilities with diverse functional programs, for instance, benefit enormously as the model discovers non-obvious thermal zoning strategies. Conversely, simple single-zone structures or highly standardized layouts like repetitive residential units show more modest improvements, since these cases lack the geometric complexity where joint optimization truly shines. Climate extremity also matters; buildings in temperate zones with modest heating and cooling demands exhibit smaller optimization potential compared to structures in harsh climates where envelope and spatial decisions profoundly impact energy performance.

The joint optimization’s gains over sequential approaches trace to its elimination of suboptimality inherent in staged decision-making. When spatial layouts lock in before energy assessment, certain high-performance configurations become unreachable—a phenomenon economists term "path dependence." By simultaneously navigating geometric and energetic objectives, the model accesses solution regions that sequential methods structurally exclude. This accounts for the 8–12 percentage point improvement in energy reduction beyond what independent optimization achieves.

Scalability considerations suggest the framework can extend beyond the building typologies examined here. The modular architecture permits substitution of alternative simulation engines for specialized applications—computational fluid dynamics for natural ventilation optimization or moisture transport models for envelope hygiene assessment. Practical deployment would benefit from incorporating additional stakeholder preferences through interactive constraint specification interfaces, transforming the model from a black-box optimizer into a collaborative design exploration tool that augments rather than replaces human expertise.

Conclusion

This research developed a novel framework coupling generative adversarial networks with physics-based simulation for joint optimization of building energy consumption prediction and spatial configuration. The proposed model integrates conditional GAN architectures with EnergyPlus thermodynamic calculations through bidirectional data exchange mechanisms, enabling simultaneous consideration of geometric design and energy performance rather than treating these as sequential decisions.

Experimental validation across office, residential, and educational building typologies demonstrated substantial improvements over conventional approaches. Energy prediction accuracy achieved 6.8% mean absolute percentage error, outperforming pure data-driven methods by 3–5 percentage points while maintaining computational efficiency orders of magnitude superior to exhaustive physics-only simulation. Joint spatial-energy optimization yielded average consumption reductions of 23.7% compared to code-compliant baselines, exceeding sequential optimization strategies by 8–12 percentage points. The model generated well-distributed Pareto fronts containing 42–56 non-dominated solutions, empowering stakeholders to select designs matching project-specific priorities.

The primary innovations encompass: first, a physics-informed discriminator architecture embedding thermodynamic constraints directly into adversarial training; second, an adaptive coupling protocol minimizing computational overhead while preserving simulation fidelity; third, a multi-objective formulation balancing energy minimization with spatial quality metrics through dynamic weighting schemes. These contributions advance both machine learning methodology and sustainable building design practice, demonstrating how hybrid approaches can transcend limitations inherent to purely empirical or purely physics-based methods.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The framework currently addresses single-building optimization rather than district-scale energy systems where inter-building interactions become significant. Occupant behavior modeling relies on standardized schedules rather than stochastic representations capturing individual variability. Computational demands, though substantially reduced versus exhaustive simulation, remain non-trivial for resource-constrained practitioners.

Future research should extend the framework toward urban-scale applications incorporating microclimate effects and energy distribution networks. Integrating uncertainty quantification would enhance decision-making under incomplete information. Incorporating life-cycle assessment beyond operational energy—embodied carbon, material circularity, adaptive reuse potential—would provide more holistic sustainability evaluation. Developing interactive interfaces enabling real-time design exploration could transform the model from an offline optimization tool into an active collaborative platform augmenting architectural creativity throughout iterative design processes.
