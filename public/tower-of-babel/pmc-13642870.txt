A narrative review of school-based preventive interventions on mental disorders and addictions
Authors: Remedios Aguilar-Moya, Raquel Ibáñez-Martínez, David Melero-Fuentes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, there has been considerable concern about the deterioration of mental health in children and adolescents, and there is a coincidence between mental disorders and addictive disorders. In view of this situation, it is important to review preventive interventions from the educational field, since it is considered an area of socialization par excellence of the subjects that can avoid or reduce exposure to adversities at an early age. For this purpose, a narrative review was carried out. The databases PubMed, PsycINFO and ERIC were searched. A total of 2096 records were retrieved from which 16 studies were selected. Most of the interventions reviewed adopted a universal approach. Most of the articles reviewed refer to interventions around 13–15 years of age and, in general terms, no specific actions are specified by groups according to their needs or risks. The intervention is usually applied by teachers in collaboration with other health experts. The focus is mainly on depression, anxiety, alcohol, cannabis and tobacco, but mental health is also promoted with reinforcement of emotional competence and training in social skills. Also, there is a scarce presence of programs on non-substance addictions and long-term follow-up studies. Based on what has been studied in this research, preventive initiatives are at a point of unquestionable reflection that requires redirecting efforts based on solid scientific evidence that determines approaches adjusted to the realities of the target populations and that helps to make decisions on when to carry out universal or specific interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44192-026-00532-2.

Introduction

In recent years there has been considerable concern in child and youth health due to the increase in cases of mental health disorders [107, 112, 115, 116, 119] and the repercussions that this may entail considering the significant changes at this stage at the biological, psychological, familial and social levels [50, 70, 91].

While this concern has been observed with the increasing prevalence in recent decades of adolescent depression and anxiety [9, 69] and with the emergence of social determinants [55], for example Gage and Patalay [31] cohort study observed that weight perception was more strongly associated with depressive symptoms in 2015 than in comparison to 1986 and 2005.

Mental health goes beyond the absence of illness and is considered a fundamental human right recognised at the international level by the United Nations in the Declaration of Human Rights in 1948.

According WHO [113] is "state of mental well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn well and work well, and contribute to their community".

The concern associated with the absence of mental health, therefore, is not only health-related, but also has political and social implications in addressing it [102] whose purpose is to ensure adequate personal, community and socio-economic development [61].

Globally, data show a worsening mental health problems [9, 31, 55, 69]. According to WHO [115], about 14.3% of the population aged 10–19 years suffer from some form of mental disorder, accounting for 15% of adolescent morbidity and mortality. The main causes of illness are associated with depression, anxiety and behavioural disorders. About 4.4% of adolescents aged 10–14 and 5.5% of adolescents aged 15–19 suffer from an anxiety disorder. As for depression, 1.4% of adolescents aged 10–14 and 3.2% of adolescents aged 15–19 suffer from depression [115].

Notably, studies suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic increased anxiety among adolescents and has exacerbated the situation [14, 52, 64]. It is estimated that in the first year of the pandemic, one in five young people experienced symptoms of anxiety [82].

Other factors associated with poorer mental health include contextual or cultural variables, such as the migration, racism, family discord, economic hardship, maltreatment, bullying, loneliness, gender and sex-based discrimination [55], as well as vital and neurobiological determinants [27, 32, 113]

Likewise, behavioural disorders are no less worrying and can interfere significantly with academic performance, among other things [41, 90], given that they are more frequent among young people than among adults. Some 3.1% of subjects between 10 and 14 years of age have a behavioural disorder, and the figure stands at 2.4% in the population between 15 and 19 years of age [115]. And they develop at a vulnerable stage with physical, emotional and social changes associated with risks in the onset of mental disorders [59, 94] .

In this scenario, addictions do not go unnoticed because they can be coping mechanisms for mental health issues and are closely interrelated [12, 16, 21, 95, 103].

Substance addictions exhibit usage rates to be considered, especially because the age of onset is early, and the youth population group is highly vulnerable [26, 71, 73]. The annual rate of cannabis use among 15–16 year olds is 5.34% [103], alcohol use among 15–19 year olds is 22% [116] and tobacco use among 15–24 year olds is 14.2% [112].

With regard to non-substance addictions, there has been a growing study of empirical evidence worldwide in recent years [85]. In general terms, one in four people suffer from disorders related to non-substance addictions [93], the most widely accepted in clinical and research work being pathological gambling, video game addiction or shopping addiction [93].

Significantly, and due to its link with the growing use of the Internet from an early age, the notable increase in the problematic use of video games in recent years should be highlighted, with 34% of adolescents playing digital video games on a daily basis. Of these, at least 22% play at least four hours a day and 12% are at risk of playing in a problematic way [115].

Previous studies suggest that problematic video game playing behaviour is on the rise, with implications for mental health in young people, such as lower life satisfaction or the presence of psychological distress [106].

Faced with this situation, which is evidence of a mental health crisis, it is essential to implement effective interventions from an early age that strengthen a change in mental health for the prevention of mental disorders and reduce the need for mental health care [28, 117].

To this end, school becomes an ideal environment for prevention, detection and intervention where actions aimed at guaranteeing health promotion can be implemented with proven effectiveness [17, 55, 57]. Thus, several studies show the effectiveness of prevention programmes for mental health problems at school, promoting greater knowledge about mental disorders and even reducing the stigma associated with them [19, 30, 60, 109, 111].

This significance in the alliance between education and health has given rise to remarkable research on their positive association and mutual influence [83, 86, 121], both axes being recognised as fundamental rights at the international level by the United Nations in the Declaration of Human Rights in 1948 and as part of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, specifically in its goal 3 referred to ‘Health and Wellbeing’ and goal 4 ‘Quality Education’ [104].

However, despite the fact that this association has led to a proliferation of school-based actions aimed at promoting mental health and preventing associated problems [25, 36, 111], there is a recognised lack of research on the study of school-based prevention from a comprehensive and interrelated approach to mental disorders and addictions [44], and consequently, school-based prevention programmes address mental disorder and addiction problems separately [44, 78, 111]. In this direction, there are reviews of school-based mental health [110, 122] or addiction programmes [10, 46], but to our knowledge, there has been no review of the scientific literature on school-based programmes with joint intervention of mental disorders and addictions.

Therefore, school being an area of socialisation and health education par excellence that can prevent or reduce exposure to adversities from an early age, it is urgent to observe simultaneous actions in mental health and addictions from the educational environment in the early stages. In this context, the following research question arises: What evidence exists on actions on mental disorders and addictions in the infant-juvenile population where the educational context is represented?

Thus, and given that no previous studies have been found that compile evidence along these lines, the purpose of this study is to synthesise and characterise the research carried out on preventive interventions at the school level related to mental disorders and addictions in the child and youth population.

Information sources and search strategy

The search was conducted on 13 February 2025 in the ERIC and PyscINFO databases through the EBSCOhost (https://search.ebscohost.com) and PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) platforms, with no limits on the date of publication.

The search equation was constructed with the addiction and mental health descriptors/terms from the thesaurus of each database (ERIC Thesaurus, Thesaurus of Psychological Index Terms and MeSH-Medical Subject Headings). In addition, educational terms were included in the non-educational databases (PsycINFO and PubMed.

In order to retrieve articles published in scientific journals, the ‘journal article’ filter was used in ERIC and PsycINFO.

To filter by samples that included humans, children and adolescents, the terms indexed in the thesaurus were used in PubMed. Since this option was not available in ERIC and PsycINFO, after discarding duplicates, we manually reviewed the samples in PubMed and PsycINFO.

Following the search strategy described above, the search terms used in the equations (Appendix 1) can be grouped into terms related to education, mental health, and addiction. The total number of bibliographic records retrieved was 2096 (PubMed 669, PyscINFO 282, ERIC 1145). Subsequently, 778 duplicate records were eliminated.

Eligibility criteria and selection process

Records were reviewed by RAM and RIM in order to assess compliance with the study criteria: (a) original articles published in peer-reviewed scientific journals; (b) research on addiction and mental health with intervention from elementary and secondary education stages; and (c) with regard to design, protocols (research proposal) or empirical study with evaluation of the intervention.

The exclusion criteria were: (a) other documentary typologies (systematic reviews, literature reviews, theoretical descriptions, reports, theses, notes, commentaries, letters to the editor etc.); and (b) survey or interview reports in the field of education without intervention application.

Two rounds of reviews were conducted [42], firstly, the titles and abstracts of 1322 records were examined. 1291 bibliographic records were excluded because they did not meet the eligibility criteria. Selected records (n = 31) were examined in full text in the second round of review. Finally, the bibliographic references and citations of the articles selected in the second round (n = 12) were consulted in order to determine the possible existence of relevant articles not retrieved through the search equations. Through this third review, four additional articles were selected. Thus, the total of articles included in this study were (n = 16).

Data collection and synthesis

Two authors (DMF and RAM) independently extracted data from the articles included in this study. The information was synthesised into the following categories: (a) Aim; (b) Sample; (c) Mental disorder and Addiction; (d) Intervention provider; (e) Intervention procedures (including the level of prevention: universal, selective or indicated); (f) Intervention components; and (g) Outcomes assessed. The information was pooled, and discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached.

The analysis was conducted using textual summaries and explanations of the findings by category, which are synthesized using a thematic approach that relates and evaluates the evidence presented in the studies [58, 66].

Another link between articles is found in the studies by Newton et al. [75] and Halladay et al. [44] that compare and evaluate the CLIMATE Schools and Preventure programmes together.

The results are described below in relation to the characteristics of the sample (countries of interventions and age groups), characteristics of programs (mental disorder and addictions, intervention provider, intervention procedures, intervention components) and outcomes assessed.

Sample characteristics

Discarding the three protocols [8, 76, 97], a good part (nine articles; 56.25%) [44, 74, 75, 77, 80, 96, 98, 99] (Kenney et al. 2016) studies interventions aimed at students aged 13–15 years (depending on the country, this is considered to be the end of primary education or middle, intermediate or secondary education), although there are initiatives at earlier stages of primary education (Elementary), from the age of 6 years [7, 53] (Kenney et al. 2016) and in more advanced stages of secondary education between 16 and 19 years [2], as well as another longitudinal study, applied in primary education, which focuses on the impact of the programme when the subjects are between 19 and 21 years of age [81]. No studies have been found that work with samples of children under 6 years of age.

The studies occur in six countries, with Australia leading the way with eight studies (50%) in New South Wales, Queensland and Western Australia [8, 44, 74–76, 96–98] and the United States with five studies (31.25%) in Minnesota [7], Baltimore [53], Virginia (Kenney et al. 2016) and Oregon [80]. They are followed by Canada (Quebec) Therriault et al. [99], Italy (Pescara) Amorosi et al. [2] and Finland (Oulu) Onnela et al. [77].

Note that the samples under study are considered as differentiated or vulnerable populations in the studies conducted in the United States (8 articles; 50%) (Students from semi-rural schools from Caucasian families with low-low-middle socioeconomic status [7], African-American students from low-income families [53, 81], African-American pupils with low school achievement (Kenney et al. 2016),disadvantaged groups [80] with no detailed information provided in the remaining studies (11 articles, 68.75%).

Characteristics of the programs

The interventions observed take a universal approach, although the Newton et al. [75] and Halladay et al. [44] studies evaluate the targeted Preventure programme. Studies with indicated intervention were not found.

In the studies by Bernat et al. [7] and Kenney et al. (2016) there are actions from the educational system (teachers) and the family, while the rest of the articles (14 articles, 87.5%) focus on interventions with students (without involving the family). In 12 of these 14 articles, the figure of the teacher in collaboration with other experts is implicit [8, 44, 53, 74–77, 81, 96–99]. On the other hand, the remaining two articles involve mental health professionals from School-Based Health Centres and behavioural health consultants from the educational system [80] and professionals from the Department of Mental Health of Pescara, Italy [2].

In this context, the profiles involved in each school usually include teachers (13 articles; 81.25%) [7, 8, 44, 53, 74–76, 81, 96–99] (Kenney et al. 2016), but there is also support from other public health professionals, such as nurses, community workers, consultants, health centre staff or unspecified (7 articles, 43.75%) [2, 7, 53, 77, 80, 81, 99].

The mental disorders studied are most commonly associated with anxiety and depression. Anxiety is associated with 12 articles (75%) [8, 44, 53, 74–77, 96–99] (Kenney et al. 2016) and with depression 10 articles (62.5%) [2, 8, 44, 53, 75, 77, 80, 96–98]. To a lesser extent, other disorders such as oppositional defiant disorder [7], eating disorders [77], personality disorder [53] or moral disengagement [74].

With regard to addictions, work aimed at substance use prevention predominates, with alcohol use leading (14 articles; 87.5%), except Amorosi et al. [2] and Kenney et al. (2016), followed by cannabis (8 articles, 50%) [8, 74–76, 80, 96–98]. To a lesser extent some studies consider tobacco and other drugs (see Mental disorder and Addiction column, Table 2) or one programme also includes non-prescribed medications [80].

As far as non-substance addictions are concerned, two studies stand out where intervention is provided with regard to internet abuse [2, 99]. Similarly, the work by Amorosi et al. [2] also deals with pathological gambling and compulsive shopping.

The intervention components of the programmes can be grouped into four main blocks: (a) addiction prevention (11 articles; 68.75%) [2, 8, 44, 74–76, 80, 96–98] (Kenney et al. 2016), (b) anxiety, depression and stress reduction (5 articles; 31.25%) [2, 8, 96–99] (Kenney et al. 2016), (c) mental health promotion with reinforcement on emotional competence (10 articles; 62.5%) [7, 8, 44, 53, 77, 80, 96–99] and (d) social skills training (5 articles; 31.25%) [7, 44, 53, 75, 81] (Kenney et al. 2016).

In general, the programmes carried out include several training sessions with cross-sectional post-intervention evaluation. Only the following seven papers also consider more longitudinal evaluations: Bernat et al. [7] evaluate over 6 years (3 years of programme, 2 years of reinforcement and one year of follow-up) Kellam et al. [53] and Poduska et al. [81] evaluate two cohorts of adolescents aged 19–21 who had received universal preventive intervention between first–second grade (aged 6–8), Newton et al. [75] and Halladay et al. [44] evaluate the 3-year effects of alcohol and mental health prevention programmes in a school population (13–16 years),and the CLIMATE schools-combined programme is evaluated at 30 months [97, 98] and 72 months [8, 96].

Outcomes assessed

In the column ‘outcomes assessed’ (see Table 2) shows how the research highlights the success of educational programs, suggesting that, in general, school-based preventive interventions offer promising results in reducing mental disorders and addictions in the child and youth population. However, it should be noted that the studies analyzed in this paper are heterogeneous in terms of design, population, outcome measures, and follow-up duration. Furthermore, it is also important to note that some studies report unexpected results: in the study by Poduska et al. [81] no impact of the programme is observed in women,in the research by Kenney et al. (2016) a greater need for involvement of the educational community as a whole is observed; in the study by Onnela et al. [77] in relation to mental health and addictions literacy, there is no change in stigma reduction,and in the work of Teesson et al. [96] no significant differences with the control group and the long term, considering that their results suggest that a universal prevention programme in adolescence is not sufficient to have lasting effects on mental health and substance use disorders in the long term.

Discussion

The results are discussed below in relation to the characteristics of the sample (countries of interventions and age groups), characteristics of programs (mental disorder and addictions, intervention provider, intervention procedures, intervention components) and outcomes assessed.

Countries of the samples

Regarding the countries reporting the most studies, Australia and the United States lead the way, with studies confirming that children and adolescents with mental health problems were not receiving adequate care from a professional [13, 34, 40]. This may have led to an increased interest in the situation and a rethinking of the efforts invested in the school context as one of the most common settings for accessing mental health-related services [34, 40, 45, 45, 87].

As noted, the volume of studies found in Australia is the highest. In particular, Professors Teesson and Newton are notable for their leadership in this area of work, not only for the development and promotion of the CLIMATE Schools programme (now ourfuturesinstitute.org.au), but also for their association with Professor Conrod in the implementation and evaluation of CLIMATE Schools together with the PreVenture programme (preventureprogram.com), mainly since the 2010s. Subsequently, high rates of mental health and addiction issues with a notable influence of the COVID pandemic in Australia prompted further preventive action [98].

Another programme with strong evidence, in this case from the United States, with several studies after 14 years of its application is the Good Behavior Game [53, 81]. The United States, for its part, is a country of note in the incorporation into the curriculum of actions aimed at the prevention of mental health problems [89]. Likewise, at present, there seems to be an awareness of the social, health and economic effects derived from mental health that may be on the rise, given that data from the Annual Survey on Mental Health of the American Psychiatric Association (APA) [1], show a high social perception of the increase in mental health problems in children and young people in the last ten years, which leads to greater social concern that demands a response from the competent institutions in the field.

All of this data also reinforces the bias of studies toward samples from high-income Western countries, with a total of 10 articles focusing on one of these three programs: CLIMATE Schools, PreVenture, and the Good Behavior Game (the latter of which uses vulnerable or low-income populations in the U.S.), so, as already noted regarding contextual variables, generalizability—for example, to other non-English-speaking countries, low- and middle-income countries, or those with different educational and cultural systems—is limited. Therefore, as in other areas of psychoeducation, there is a need for cross-cultural studies that incorporate socioeconomic variables [47, 63] to address mental health and addiction issues worldwide.

Age groups

Many of the articles reviewed allude to interventions at around 13–15 years of age, coinciding with the appearance of mental health and addiction problems where early intervention and treatment is crucial to reduce the severity and minimise the associated risks [15, 45], making school an outstanding place for this purpose, as school attendance is prolonged, which allows the implementation of preventive actions in a large number of subjects [57, 94].

However, it is important to take into account the developmental differences between children and adolescents in terms of manifestations of mental health and substance use disorders, as well as the application of prevention models (universal, selective, and targeted). Thus, it is observed how protective factors adapt to psychological development processes. In the case of children, socio-emotional development and support from family and school are observed [7, 24, 53, 81]. Through specific interventions such as parent–child communication skills and Family support networks [24].

In programs for adolescents, however, the family is no longer considered a protective factor; instead, health professionals and teachers are the primary contributors [44, 75, 96, 98, 99]. Programs with selective interventions also emerge, tailored to the heterogeneity of adolescents [44, 75], implementing specific interventions adapted to their development, such as: personality style or problematic coping behaviors [44, 75], stress and anxiety management [99], or reduction of stigma toward mental illness [77].

Mental disorder and addictions

Analysing the most studied mental disorders and addictions, the results obtained show that they are associated with depression and anxiety and the consumption of alcohol, cannabis and tobacco.

On the one hand, anxiety and depression may be being studied in school-based preventive interventions, given that they are the most prevalent disorders in the child and youth population [115], and those that have been most researched in the school setting [11, 18, 38], which would justify the predominance observed in our study of the presence of these disorders over others found, such as compulsive shopping. Similarly, it is logical that the most common addictions worldwide, such as alcohol, tobacco and cannabis use (UNOCD 2024) [116], are the object of study in prevention carried out at school, as their use begins at an early age and the school becomes the focus of attention for prevention.

Although there are two studies on non-substance addictions [2, 99], the absence of strongly contrasted working models for non-substance addictions, such as those found with CLIMATE School, PreVenture and Good Behavior Game in substance addictions, is relevant. Above all, this is due to the increase in problems derived in young people and adolescents due to addiction to video games, gambling, pornography or social networks [5, 6, 88, 101].

Intervention provider

With regard to the person responsible for the implementation of preventive actions at school, the research carried out maintains that it is the teachers in collaboration with other experts in the field of health. In this context, it has been found that the participation in the school of professionals specialised in depression and who are external to the school, is more beneficial than when the intervention is carried out by teachers [111].

This external collaboration with teachers has the potential to improve mental health support for students, however, it still generates some uncertainty in practice about the effectiveness of interventions [22, 23]. This is shown by several reviews whose results show little sustained impact on pupil mental health that deserves to be studied as a priority, evaluating the figures responsible for school prevention [23, 111]. Similarly, in the specific area of addictions, it seems to be unclear what kind of school prevention officers are the most suitable [108].

Accordingly, although partnerships between schools and external health professionals can be positive, it is primarily the education system that should be predisposed to the implementation of preventive interventions on mental health and addiction problems, which points in the direction of the review by March et al. [62]. According to this study, there are some variables to take into account given that they can hinder the success of preventive interventions such as the lack of interest and involvement of the management team, the lack of stability of teachers in schools or the lack of relevant training to work on these issues in the classroom.

Intervention procedures

For the most part, the interventions seen adopt a universal approach and do not specify specific actions by groups according to their needs or risks, which could lead to unintended effects or not appeal to the target study population, except in the studies by Newton et al [75] and Halladay et al. [44] where the selective programme Preventure is evaluated.

Studies such as those by Andrews et al. [3] argue that one-size-fits-all models should not be applied to all students and, where required, the implementation of specific interventions may have greater effectiveness and sustained effect as, with young people who have some form of mental disorder, the application of universal preventive strategies is not effective and may even be inappropriate. This is demonstrated in the review by Foulkes and Stringaris [29] who highlight the evidence for evaluating mental health programmes given that certain aspects may increase clinical symptoms in some students, i.e. generate the so-called iatrogenic effect. In this line of work, Werner-Seidler et al. [111] reveal that universal, therapeutically based interventions are less effective than targeted approaches and generate null effects. Similarly, studies such as Tinner et al. [100] warn that some universal interventions in the field of addictions may be achieving little benefit even in the short term and this deserves to be reconsidered because of the impact it may have on the subjects, leading to ineffective prevention.

From this perspective, research suggests that, despite the availability of evidence on what works and what does not work in prevention, the transfer of applied knowledge is a pending task, programmes are diverse, lack evaluations that could provide feedback for future actions [65, 120] and do not take into account sustainability, making implementation a stable task at the centre [62]. Therefore, research in this field and continuous evaluation are urgently needed to analyse the effectiveness of the programmes and to optimise their design and implementation.

Intervention components

Finally, an analysis of the intervention components of the programmes found gives rise to a classification in four main blocks: (a) addiction prevention; (b) reduction of anxiety, depression and stress; (c) promotion of mental health with a reinforcement of emotional competence and (d) training in social skills.

These aspects are focused on work on risk and protective factors that also appear in reviews focused on the field of addictions such as those of Nawi et al. [72]. A similar situation is found in the field of mental disorders where interventions, especially on anxiety and depressive disorders, were marked by a component aimed at improving coping strategies, emotion management skills and improving social interaction [123].

It should be emphasised that not many studies have been found that advocate work on literacy, but more work on literacy is expected in line with the growing trend towards prevention of mental disorders and promotion of mental health in a meaningful way [45]. This interest is also reflected in future work in the school setting on teachers' mental health literacy in order to recognise and support pupils [20].

On the other hand, there is a clear gap in intervention components that focus on concepts of comorbidity, such as the self-medication hypothesis [54], although, to name a few, intervention components with this link are presented, such as risks of addiction and depression and healthy habits [2], drug use prevention and mental health [98]

Outcomes assessed

On the other hand, this research highlights the scarce presence of long-term follow-up studies of combined prevention programmes and those studied focus on the problems in isolation. The study by Teesson et al. [96] deserves special attention for evaluating one of the pioneering programmes worldwide (CLIMATE schools) that addresses the prevention of anxiety, depression and substance use and which proved the effectiveness of the programme and significant effects in adolescence. This study suggests that universal implementation of a prevention programme for mental health and substance use disorders is most effective when implemented together and has significant preventive effects during adolescence but warns that this approach is not sufficient to have lasting effects in early adulthood. In this regard, they observed no significant differences in the predicted probability of alcohol use between the experimental and control groups at 72-month follow-up, so caution should still be exercised in programme evaluations.

Similarly, although the studies reviewed highlight the success of these programs in improving the mental health of the child and youth population [48, 122, 123], many of the studies in this research do not show significant differences with respect to contextual variables when the scientific evidence confirms the need to be considered [35]. This aspect should be taken into account because of the influence it has on programme design and applications. Studies such as those by Howard et al. [49] or Ladegard et al. [56] underline this importance by highlighting how contextual factors should influence preventive interventions and, on the other hand, are not considered as much as they should be and are little studied, ignoring the impact they can have on the final results.

Limitations

The results obtained are based on a narrative review that utilized three scientific literature databases; therefore, studies hosted on other platforms and works written in other languages may have been excluded, although PubMed, PsycINFO, and ERIC are the primary subject-specific databases in health, psychology, and education with high-quality standards in the scientific literature they cover [67]. Therefore, future studies employing different review approaches should consider including works written in other languages, as well as other types and sources of information [68, 105].

It should also be noted that the selection criteria covered school-based prevention programs; therefore, the absence of potential targeted prevention studies masked within therapeutic studies that recruited samples of students with clinical symptoms is understandable.

On the other hand, the nature of this narrative review does not include formal quality assessment, analyses of evidence or effects. However, the thematic synthesis itself has identified areas of interest for future meta-analytic studies. For example, (i) the analysis of the level of evidence from studies based on cluster randomized controlled trials—of which there are eight in this study’s sample—where schools are randomly assigned to different experimental conditions [7, 44, 53, 74, 75, 81, 96, 98], or (ii) the analysis of effects, such as those observed by gender in the studies by Kellam et al. [53] and Poduska et al. [81].

However, the heterogeneity among studies on key issues such as sample characteristics and outcome indicators makes this task difficult to achieve. Furthermore, in addition to this, the studies are skewed toward samples from high-income Western countries (Australia, USA, Canada, Finland, Italy), so generalizability to other low- and middle-income countries, as well as in terms of cross-cultural applicability, is limited.

Conclusions

Based on the results obtained, there are few preventive interventions aimed at children and young people that focus on the study of mental health and addictions together in the school context, despite the importance recognised in recent years at a global level of supporting mental health and wellbeing in school settings [37]. In this regard, initiatives with global impact such as Global Accelerated Action for the Health of Adolescents (AA-HA!): guidance to support country implementation [114] or ‘Helping Adolescents Thrive’ [117], which call for improving the quality of school environments to promote and protect the mental health of schoolchildren, stand out.

At a global level, scientific evidence confirms that schools are at the forefront of prevention [51] and are an ideal component not only for prevention but also for the detection and care of children and young people for various mental disorders and addictions [28, 118], which enables the acquisition of tools, knowledge and socioemotional and critical thinking skills that guarantee better health quality indices [112].

However, the data collected in this study show a scarcity of scientific literature regarding school-based preventive interventions that combine mental disorders and addictions following the trend of previous studies where most school-based programmes addressed both issues separately and evaluated outcomes independently [44, 78, 111] despite the co-occurrence of addictions and mental health problems in school-aged adolescents [43, 44]. In addition to this scarcity, there is also considerable diversity in the objectives, samples, outcome measures, and follow-up methods across the programs analyzed, a factor that makes it difficult to identify a consistent model for a successful school-based program addressing this issue (mental health and addiction).

In this respect, a recent study [84] points out that prevention programmes are aimed at the emergence of new mental disorders or the avoidance of major risks in the present ones [39] or at work on addictions, especially to substances or technologies [33].

Based on what has been studied in this research, preventive initiatives are at a point of unquestionable reflection that requires redirecting efforts based on solid scientific evidence that determines approaches adjusted to the realities of the target populations and that helps to make decisions about when to carry out interventions, whether universal or specific. Prevention can undoubtedly reduce the rates and costs associated with mental health and addictions, but it must be based on scientific evidence that already highlights the use of technology to improve access to programmes, the adoption of holistic and interdisciplinary approaches and development from an early age [4, 96].

Therefore, this study suggests implications for program design focused on (a) early intervention; (b) training children and adolescents in social-emotional skills; (c) training teachers in mental health and addiction literacy and the identification of risk signals; (d) hybrid universal and selective programs, adapted to specific sociocultural and socioeconomic contexts, with a special emphasis on differential vulnerabilities and risk factors; and (e) integrating teachers and mental health and addiction professionals into intervention roles.

In this regard, it is indisputable that policymakers should promote comprehensive prevention models that involve teachers and mental health and addiction professionals, prioritizing strategies that address contexts differing in sociodemographic variables, with the aim of ensuring the success of programs in any type of setting and in response to the needs of the school.
