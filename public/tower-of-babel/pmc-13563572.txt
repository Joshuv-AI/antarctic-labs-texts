HFpEF-ABA Score for the Diagnosis and Prognosis of HFpEF in Outpatients With Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Rafael Gonzalez-Manzanares, Manuel Anguita, Javier Muñiz, Vivencio Barrios, José Antonio Gimeno-Orna, Antonio Pérez, Luis Rodríguez-Padial, María Anguita-Gámez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is highly prevalent among adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D) but often remains undiagnosed. The HFpEF-ABA score (based on age, body-mass index and atrial fibrillation) has demonstrated excellent diagnosis performance in patients with unexplained dyspnea but lacks validation for opportunistic screening of HFpEF.ObjectivesWe aimed to evaluate the diagnostic and prognostic utility of the HFpEF-ABA score in outpatients with T2D.MethodsWe analyzed 1,125 outpatients with T2D from the prospective, multicenter DIABET-IC cohort. HFpEF diagnosis was based on the European Society of Cardiology guidelines criteria and confirmed by a cardiologist. We evaluated the diagnostic performance of HFpEF-ABA score and its association with 3-year all-cause death, HF hospitalization, and their composite.ResultsHFpEF was confirmed in 190 participants (16.9%). The HFpEF-ABA score yielded an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.71-0.79). Over 3 years, 61 deaths and 23 HF hospitalizations occurred. Per 10-percentage-point increase in HFpEF probability, the risk increased for all-cause death (HR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.17-1.54), HF hospitalization (HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 0.94-1.43), and the composite endpoint (HR: 1.28; 95% CI: 1.14-1.44). Scores ≥75% identified patients at higher risk of death (HR: 2.40; 95% CI: 1.45-3.97), HF hospitalization (HR: 2.51; 95% CI: 1.10-5.72), and the composite (HR: 2.41; 95% CI: 1.55-3.73).ConclusionsIn ambulatory adults with T2D, the HFpEF-ABA score showed moderate diagnostic discrimination for HFpEF and was associated with adverse clinical outcomes at follow-up.Key words: cardiometabolic syndrome, heart failure, HFpEF, type 2 diabetes

Heart failure (HF) with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) now accounts for half of all HF cases, a growing trend driven by population aging and increasingly prevalent metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2D).1 Approximately 45% of patients with HFpEF have coexisting T2D, and up to 12% of T2D outpatients might have underdiagnosed HFpEF.2

Until recently, guideline-directed medical therapy was limited to HF with reduced ejection fraction. However, several therapies have now demonstrated clinical benefit in patients with HFpEF. These include sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, the nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone, and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists for obesity-related HFpEF.3, 4, 5, 6

The convergence of the growing burden of HFpEF with the availability of novel cardiometabolic therapies creates a timely opportunity to implement strategies for the early recognition of this condition. However, diagnosing HFpEF remains challenging and often requires a comprehensive, multiparametric evaluation involving imaging, exercise testing, or even invasive hemodynamic assessment. Moreover, patients with HFpEF usually have multiple comorbidities and are commonly assessed by various specialists across different levels of care.1 As a result, diagnosis may be delayed until overt symptoms are present or hospitalization for HF occurs.

The HFpEF-ABA score is a simple tool that estimates the probability of HFpEF in patients with unexplained dyspnea using 3 readily available clinical variables: age, body mass index (BMI), and a history of atrial fibrillation (AF).7 However, it lacks external validation for opportunistic HFpEF screening in high-risk populations. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the diagnostic and prognostic utility of the HFpEF-ABA score in a prospective cohort of outpatients with T2D attending cardiology and endocrinology clinics for reasons unrelated to HF. We hypothesized that the HFpEF-ABA score, despite being developed in symptomatic patients with unexplained dyspnea and validated against invasive hemodynamics, would retain diagnostic and prognostic utility when applied opportunistically to an unselected, largely asymptomatic outpatient population with T2D. To our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of the score in this screening context.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

We analyzed data from the DIABET-IC study, a prospective, multicenter, observational cohort of outpatients with T2D from 30 centers in Spain.8,9 Between January 2018 and December 2019, patients with T2D attending cardiology and endocrinology outpatient clinics for reasons unrelated to HF, were systematically screened for HF. Eligible participants were adults aged ≥18 years with a known diagnosis of T2D for at least 1 year. Key exclusion criteria included end-stage chronic kidney disease and an expected life expectancy of less than 3 years due to noncardiovascular causes. For the present study, we included the subset of patients with no-HF and HFpEF at baseline. The DIABET-IC study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Toledo (Toledo, Spain). The study was conducted in accordance with institutional regulations and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Variables, data sources, and measurements

Data collection was standardized across all participating centers using electronic case report forms. Data were gathered by the lead-site cardiologist and endocrinologist at each center through periodic structured interviews and reviews of electronic medical records. Participants were scheduled for at least 1 annual follow-up visit during the 3-year study period. Three-year prediction is clinically relevant, since the incidence of HF in T2D is enough high,2 and the incidence of death and HF hospitalizations in patients with HF are also high. Laboratory measurements, including N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide levels, were conducted in accredited local laboratories. Echocardiographic studies were performed at each center in accordance with international guidelines.10,11

HFpEF definition and outcomes

HFpEF diagnosis was based on the 2016 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) HF guidelines and in all cases was confirmed by a cardiologist.12 According to these criteria, a diagnosis of HFpEF required the presence of the following: a left ventricular ejection fraction ≥50%, elevated natriuretic peptide levels (N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide >125 pg/mL), and evidence of structural (eg, left atrial enlargement or left ventricular hypertrophy) or functional abnormalities consistent with diastolic dysfunction.

Clinical outcomes were assessed at 3-year follow-up through scheduled visits and systematic review of electronic health records. The prognostic performance of the HFpEF-ABA score was evaluated for the following endpoints: 1) death from any cause; 2) HF hospitalization; and 3) the composite of HF hospitalization or death from any cause.13 All-cause mortality, rather than cardiovascular mortality, was selected as the mortality endpoint because cause-of-death attribution is unreliable in observational cohorts without central adjudication, to avoid the competing-risks bias inherent in cause-specific mortality, and for consistency with the original HFpEF-ABA outcome analyses.

HFpEF-ABA score

The HFpEF-ABA score was calculated using baseline values of age, BMI, and history of AF. HFpEF predicted probabilities were derived from the log-odds obtained from the previously published logistic regression equation.7

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Incidence rates for clinical outcomes were reported as cases per 100 person-years.

Logistic regression was used to assess the diagnostic performance of the HFpEF-ABA score, with HFpEF diagnosis as the dependent variable and the score as the independent predictor. Discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed visually using a calibration plot of the observed and predicted probabilities and with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between the HFpEF-ABA score and the risk of clinical outcomes. The proportional hazards assumptions were verified using Schoenfeld residuals. The score was evaluated both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable using a prespecified cutoff (HFpEF probability <75% vs ≥75%). The choice of 75% cutoff was based on the original paper in which this score was first described.7 A competing risk analysis using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model was also conducted for HF hospitalizations, and subdistribution HRs were shown. Time-to-event outcomes were plotted using Kaplan-Meier survival estimates.7 All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Stata 18 was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Study population

A total of 1,523 outpatients with T2D were enrolled in the DIABET-IC cohort. At baseline, 1,150 were classified as either having no HF (n = 954) or a diagnosis of HFpEF (n = 196). The study flowchart and reasons for exclusion are detailed in Figure 1. Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 66.9 ± 10.1 and 422 (36.7%) were women. The mean BMI was 30.5 ± 5.4 kg/m2 and AF was present in 204 participants (17.7%). The prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors other than T2D was high, coronary artery disease was present in 413 (35.9%) participants, and chronic kidney disease in 227 (19.7%).

HFpEF diagnosis and the HFpEF-ABA score

Of the 1,150 participants eligible for analysis, 1,125 (97.8%) had complete data for all HFpEF-ABA variables and comprised the diagnostic validation cohort. HFpEF was confirmed in 190 patients (16.9%), whereas 935 (83.1%) had no evidence of HF. When the HFpEF-ABA score was entered as a continuous predictor in a logistic-regression model, it showed good discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.746 (95% CI: 0.706-0.785) (Figure 2). In the graphical assessment of calibration, predicted and observed probabilities agreed well across most of the risk spectrum, with a modest underestimation that was evident only in the highest decile, indicating minor miscalibration in the upper tail (Hosmer-Lemeshow test P = 0.019) (Figure 3). The discrimination of the integrated score (AUC: 0.746) exceeded that of any single component (age 0.564, BMI 0.536, AF 0.603) (Supplemental Table 2), indicating that the score’s performance is not attributable to a single dominant variable. At the prespecified 75% threshold, the score showed a sensitivity of 65.3% and a specificity of 72.2% (positive predictive value 32.3%, negative predictive value 91.1%) for guideline-based HFpEF (Supplemental Table 1). Diagnostic discrimination was numerically higher in women (AUC: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.73-0.83) than in men (AUC: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.64-0.76), although the difference was not statistically significant (DeLong P = 0.064) (Supplemental Table 3, Figure 4). There was no evidence of miscalibration by sex.

Clinical outcomes

Three-year follow-up was completed in 893 participants, yielding 2,257 person-years of observation. During this period, 61 patients died (2.70 events per 100 person-years; 95% CI: 2.10-3.47), 23 were hospitalized for HF (1.02 events per 100 person-years; 95% CI: 0.68-1.53), and 81 experienced the composite endpoint of death or HF hospitalization (3.63 events per 100 person-years; 95% CI: 2.92-4.51). Kaplan-Meier survival estimates stratified by the 75% threshold are shown in Figure 5.

The HFpEF-ABA score was significantly associated with each outcome when modeled both continuously and categorically using a 75% cutoff point (Table 2). For every 10-percentage-point increase in predicted HFpEF probability, the HR for the composite endpoint was 1.28 (95% CI: 1.14-1.44). In addition, patients with a HFpEF-ABA score ≥75% had a 2- to 3-fold higher risk of death or HF hospitalization compared with those with a HFpEF-ABA <75% (HR: 2.41; 95% CI: 1.55-3.73). Discussion

In this prospective, multicenter cohort of outpatients with T2D, the HFpEF-ABA score, a simple clinical model based solely on age, BMI, and AF, showed good discrimination for diagnosing HFpEF, and predicted all-cause mortality and HF hospitalization over 3 years (Central Illustration). These findings extend the score’s utility beyond its original purpose and highlight its screening value in high-risk T2D outpatients who are routinely managed by endocrinologists and cardiologists for conditions other than HF.

HFpEF is increasingly recognized as a systemic cardiometabolic syndrome closely linked to T2D, obesity, and chronic kidney disease.14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Yet its diagnosis remains challenging, and dyspnea and exercise intolerance are frequently under-recognized or attributed to obesity, pulmonary disease, or deconditioning.21, 22, 23 Furthermore, natriuretic peptide concentrations are often spuriously low in obese individuals.24, 25, 26, 27, 28 Consistent with this, in the present cohort the disagreement between the score and the guideline diagnosis was bidirectional, and because nearly half of the cohort was obese, a proportion of the apparent false positives may represent true HFpEF in whom natriuretic peptides were suppressed and the guideline threshold was not met; conversely, some apparent false negatives reflect HFpEF driven by mechanisms other than age, body-mass index, and AF. A high HFpEF-ABA score in this setting should therefore prompt further evaluation rather than be interpreted as a confirmed or excluded diagnosis. Even when echocardiography is available, confirming HFpEF often typically requires integrating clinical variables with diastolic Doppler indices, strain imaging, and structural parameters using complex algorithms not routinely applied outside dedicated HF units or imaging laboratories.29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 As a result, noncardiology clinicians who regularly manage patients with T2D and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome (endocrinologists, nephrologists, and primary-care physicians) may miss the window to initiate evidence-based treatments such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors or finerenone.5,6,35,36 In this context, the availability of easy-to-use, rapid, and efficient diagnostic tools is essential to improve the early recognition of HFpEF in routine clinical practice.37

The HFpEF-ABA score was originally developed as a pragmatic screening model for patients presenting with unexplained dyspnea. The score showed good discrimination for HFpEF confirmed by exercise hemodynamic testing in both derivation (AUC: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.80-0.88) and validation cohorts (AUC: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.78-0.85). Furthermore, it also performed well in a cohort where HF hospitalization was used as the diagnostic criteria (AUC: 0.93; 95% CI: 0.91-0.95).7 Subsequent analyses also linked higher HFpEF-ABA scores to worse outcomes in patients with precapillary pulmonary hypertension.38

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to externally validate the HFpEF-ABA score in ambulatory patients with T2D not specifically referred for dyspnea evaluation. Importantly, the DIABET-IC cohort includes a Mediterranean population, which was not represented in the original international validation cohort comprising centers in the United States, the Netherlands, Denmark, and Australia.7 Despite these demographic and clinical differences, discrimination remained good (AUC: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.71-0.79). We hypothesize that this slight reduction in performance may reflect the opportunistic nature of screening in a population not referred for dyspnea, as well as the use of noninvasive diagnostic criteria for HFpEF based on the ESC guidelines.12 Nevertheless, the consistency of performance across diverse clinical settings underscores its potential as a broadly applicable tool for early identification of HFpEF in high-risk populations.

By contrast, a recent analysis reported poor concordance between the 2021 ESC criteria and more complex scores such as HFA-PEFF (Heart Failure Association–Pre-test assessment, Echocardiography/natriuretic peptides, Functional testing, Final etiology) and H2FPEF (Heavy, Hypertensive, Atrial Fibrillation, Pulmonary hypertension, Elder, Filling pressures) in patients with unexplained dyspnea.39 Both of those algorithms require additional echocardiographic variables (eg, estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure or Doppler-derived filling pressures) that are seldom available in busy outpatient clinics or to noncardiology specialists.33,40 We were unable to compute the H2FPEF or HFA-PEFF scores in this cohort because tissue-Doppler E/e’ was not uniformly available, a mandatory input for both algorithms. This limitation itself underscores the practical advantage of the HFpEF-ABA score, which relies solely on 3 routinely available variables and remains computable when the echocardiographic indices required by more complex scores are missing. The main advantage of the HFpEF-ABA score is its parsimony. All 3 variables are routinely available within seconds, enabling point-of-care calculation by any clinician. Augmenting the score with cardiometabolic biomarkers available in this cohort (glycosylated hemoglobin and urinary albumin excretion) or with comorbidities such as obstructive sleep apnea might further improve discrimination, but at the expense of the parsimony that is the score’s principal practical advantage; this trade-off warrants dedicated study. For health-system administrators and payers, the score offers a tool to identify patients with T2D most likely to derive incremental benefit, and cost-effectiveness, from cardioprotective agents.

Beyond diagnosis, every 10-point increase in the predicted probability of HFpEF translated into a 28% higher risk of the composite endpoint, and a ≥75% probability identified individuals with a 2- to 3-fold increase in mortality or HF hospitalization. This supports the HFpEF-ABA score as a pragmatic HF events risk tool that allows clinicians to prioritize higher-risk patients for intensified surveillance, timely referral, or early initiation of therapies with proven benefit in HFpEF.

In summary, optimizing HFpEF care will require a coordinated, patient-centered approach involving multispecialty clinicians. In this context, the HFpEF-ABA score offers a simple tool to aid in the early detection and risk stratification of HFpEF among patients with T2D. Its use may help prioritize high-risk individuals for specialized evaluation, timely initiation of emerging cardiometabolic therapies, and more efficient allocation of health care resources at both the system and policy levels.

Study Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First and most important, this study lacked an invasive gold standard for HFpEF. Diagnosis relied on the noninvasive 2016 ESC criteria, which require an elevated natriuretic peptide concentration, rather than the invasive exercise hemodynamics used in the original HFpEF-ABA derivation cohort. Because natriuretic peptides are frequently suppressed in obesity, and nearly half of our cohort was obese, HFpEF was probably underascertained, and the true prevalence is likely higher than the observed 16.9%. The HFpEF-ABA score was therefore evaluated against an imperfect, partly misclassified reference standard, which may bias its measured discrimination downward and accounts for part of the observed disagreement (Supplemental Table 1). Second, both in the original HFpEF-ABA cohorts and in the present study, patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction were excluded; thus, any screening strategy should still incorporate a basic echocardiogram to rule out left ventricular systolic dysfunction. Third, the advanced HFpEF scores (H2FPEF and HFA-PEFF) could not be computed because tissue-Doppler E/e’ was not uniformly available. Fourth, all participants were enrolled in Spain; further external validation across other geographic regions and ethnic groups is desirable.

Conclusions

In outpatients with T2D, the HFpEF-ABA score showed good diagnostic discrimination and was associated with the risk of death and HF hospitalization. Its simplicity supports routine use as a first-line screening and triage tool in routine care for patients with T2D.

COMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE: In ambulatory adults with T2D, the HFpEF-ABA score, calculated from age, BMI, and AF alone, identifies patients more likely to have undiagnosed HFpEF and at higher 3-year risk of death or HF hospitalization, and can be applied at the point of care by any clinician.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Prospective studies should test whether HFpEF-ABA-guided screening, followed by confirmatory evaluation and early initiation of therapies with proven HFpEF benefit, improves outcomes, and whether augmenting the score with cardiometabolic biomarkers improves discrimination without sacrificing its parsimony.
