Booster dose reshapes SARS-CoV-2 RBD-specific B cell immunity in people living with HIV
Authors: Jacopo Polvere, Simone Lucchesi, Giorgio Montesi, Fabio Fiorino, Gabiria Pastore, Massimiliano Fabbiani, Mario Tumbarello, Francesca Montagnani, Donata Medaglini, Annalisa Ciabattini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong-term persistence of spike-specific memory B cells (MBC) upon mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccination remains poorly explored in people living with HIV (PLWH). We previously observed that the primary two-dose immunization elicited a B cell response quantitatively similar but phenotypically different compared to healthy participants (HC), with higher prevalence of CD27-IgD- double-negative cells and lower proportion of CD27+ Ig-switched MBC.MethodsThe PatoVac_COV longitudinal prospective single-centre study included 74 PLWH vaccinated with mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccines. Building upon our previous findings, blood samples were collected before and after the third and fourth doses, and two years after the first dose. PBMC were analysed for the RBD-specific MBC response by multidimensional flow cytometry, while spike-specific IgG were tested in plasma samples by ELISA and by surrogate virus neutralization assay. A supervised decision tree model was used to identify clinical and demographic predictors of RBD-specific MBC persistence.ResultsThe third vaccine dose robustly recalled RBD-specific MBC, driving their differentiation into Ig-switched resting MBC, a subset undetectable before boosting and dominant also in HC. The fourth vaccine dose minimally impacted on the MBC response. IgG+ resting MBC, recognizing both wild type and Omicron BA.2 strains, were still present two years after vaccination. Clinical and demographic factors associated to the persistence of IgG+ resting RBD-specific MBC were time since initiation of current ART, BMI, CD8+ cells count, granulocytes count and blood creatinine levels.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the impact of boosting, particularly the third dose, in enhancing and shaping the RBD-specific MBC in PLWH. Clinical and demographic variables associated with long-term MBC persistence may serve as potential biomarkers for predicting durable vaccine responses in PLWH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07414-1.Keywords: Memory B cells, SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, mRNA vaccine, HIV, DN, Omicron-adapted vaccines

Background

People living with HIV (PLWH) exhibit a diminished induction and persistence of immune responses conferred by various vaccine formulations [1–4] requiring tailored vaccination policies [5]. Despite the effectiveness of antiretroviral therapy (ART) in suppressing HIV replication, PLWH often experience persistent immune dysregulation and generalized immune exhaustion [6].

In the context of COVID-19 pandemic, studies indicated that PLWH with low CD4 T-cell counts and uncontrolled viraemia were at increased risk of severe SARS-CoV-2 infections [7–11] and showed reduced SARS-CoV-2 vaccine-induced immunogenicity [12]. However, among PLWH with undetectable viremia and high CD4 T cell count [13–17], mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccination is able to elicit both antibody and cell-mediated responses. Anyway, only a few studies have characterized the B cell response to SARS-CoV-2 in PLWH [18–24]. In a previous study, we observed that subjects on stable ART who completed the primary two-dose mRNA vaccine cycle developed spike-specific memory B cells (MBC) with a magnitude similar to healthy controls (HC). However, notable phenotypic differences emerged, including a lower frequency of Ig-switched (CD27+IgD-) and a higher frequency of double-negative (DN, CD27-IgD-) spike-specific B cells [18]. Other studies have similarly underlined a reduced induction of anti-SARS-CoV-2 MBC and altered phenotypes, although these responses appeared to be effectively reactivated following booster doses [19, 20]. Despite these insight, current data are limited by the low number of patients and short-term follow up, leaving key aspects of the immunological memory in PLWH unresolved. The emergence of highly transmissible SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern, particularly those of the Omicron lineage which are still in widespread circulation, has raised questions on the potential immune evasion [25]. While Omicron variant exposure, either through natural infection or variant-adapted vaccination, has been shown to enhance the recognition of circulating Omicron subvariants [26], the induction and persistence of variant-specific MBC response in PLWH are understudied [21, 27]. In addition, the possible role of clinical and demographic variables in the persistence of SARS-CoV-2 specific MBC in PLWH is not clear. Given these limitations, it is crucial to characterize the long-term MBC response in PLWH and its potential determinants.

Here, we performed a longitudinal observational cohort study in PLWH to address the persistence of SARS-CoV-2-specific MBC and the modulatory effect of vaccine boosters on the spike receptor binding domain (RBD)-specific B cell phenotype. The longitudinal evolution of RBD-specific B cell response was analysed from the completion of the primary two-doses immunization cycle up to two years, identifying, through a supervised decision tree model, the clinical and demographic factors associated to higher values of RBD-specific MBC responses.

Methods

Study population

A longitudinal observational cohort study, including 74 PLWH under stable ART and 71 HC, was conducted at the University Hospital of Siena (Siena, Italy), between March 2021 and July 2023. PLWH were enrolled in the context of the PatoVac_COV clinical study, while HC were enrolled in the IMMUNO_COV study. HC were identified as HIV-negative by self-report and specifically selected to ensure a comparable age profile with PLWH cohort. All participants were recruited at the Infectious and Tropical Diseases Unit, where they provided written informed consent before participation to the study. Inclusion criteria for the PatoVac_COV study were: (i) age ≥ 18 years; (ii) compliance with the COVID-19 vaccination campaign; (iii) vaccine schedule including only mRNA vaccines; (iv) confirmed diagnosis of HIV infection. Exclusion criteria were: (i) pregnancy; (ii) withdrawal of consent; (iii) administration of high-dose steroids or other immunosuppressive drugs; (iv) comorbidities associated with relevant immunosuppression (e.g. active cancer, organ transplantation) other than HIV infection itself; (v) clinical problems for additional blood sampling beyond the amount required for routine care. For the IMMUNO_COV study, inclusion criteria were: (i) age ≥ 18 years; (ii) compliance with the COVID-19 vaccination campaign; (iii) vaccine schedule including only mRNA vaccines. Exclusion criteria were: (i) pregnancy; (ii) withdrawal of consent; (iii) being affected by any immunocompromising condition (congenital, acquired, or drug-related).

According with national vaccination guidelines, participants received the original strain mRNA vaccines for both the primary 2 doses-cycle and the third dose, the original strain (wild type; WT) or the bivalent vaccines (Original/Omicron BA.1 or Original/Omicron BA.4–5) as fourth dose, and the bivalent or the monovalent Omicron XBB.1.5 vaccines as fifth dose. Information on vaccine formulation and timing was self-reported at the blood-collection visits.

SARS-CoV-2 infections were identified through passive surveillance. Participants self-reported any SARS-CoV-2 infection to clinicians during blood collection visits, providing the date of their positive test. The diagnosis was based on antigenic or molecular testing performed on nasopharyngeal swabs, which were either self-administered or collected by healthcare professionals. Participants reporting SARS-CoV-2 infection since December 2021, were assumed to be infected by Omicron, the prevalent variant in Italy after that date [28].

Ethics

Written informed consent was obtained from the participants according to the Declaration of Helsinki/International Conference on Harmonization Guideline for Good Clinical Practice. The study was performed in compliance with all relevant ethical regulations and the protocols were approved by local Ethical Committee for Clinical experimentation (Comitato Etico Regione Toscana - Area Vasta Sud Est, CEAVSE; protocol code n.19479 v1.0 approved on 3rd March 2021 for PatoVac_COV, and n.18869 v1.0 approved on the 21st December 2020 for IMMUNO_COV).

PBMC isolation and plasma collection

Blood samples were collected in heparin-coated blood tubes (Vacutainer; BD, NJ, USA) at the following time-points: pre III (1 day-1 month before the third dose, that corresponds to 6–8 months after second dose), post III (1–2 months after third dose), pre IV (1 day-1 month before the fourth dose, that corresponds to 6–9 months after third dose), post IV (1–4 months after fourth dose) and two years following the first vaccine dose). PBMCs were isolated by density-gradient sedimentation, using Ficoll-Paque (Lymphoprep; STEMCELL Technologies, BC, Canada), and cryopreserved in cell recovery medium [10% DMSO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and 90% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (Sigma Aldrich, MO, USA)] by an initial overnight freezing step at -80 °C, followed by long-term storage in liquid nitrogen. Plasma samples were stored at -80 °C.

Multiparametric flow cytometry

Circulating MBC, collected from Pre III up to Post IV time points, were analysed with a multiparametric flow cytometry panel including fluorescent antibodies and SARS-CoV-2 bait proteins (panel A). Two millions PBMCs were incubated with BD human FC block (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA) and stained with spike S1 + S2 ECD-His recombinant biotinylated-protein (Sino Biological, China) conjugated with streptavidin (SA)-R-Phycoerythrin (PE; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and WT receptor binding domain (RBD) recombinant biotinylated-protein (BioLegend, CA, USA) conjugated with SA-Allophycocyanin (APC; Thermo Fisher Scientific), together with the following fluorescent antibodies: CD3-BV650 (clone OKT3), CD21-FITC (clone B-LY4), CD19-BUV395 (clone SJ25C1), IgM-BV605 (clone G20-127), IgD-BV711 (clone IA6-2), CD27-BV786 (clone O323), CD11c-BB700 (clone 3.9), CD20-APCH7 (clone 2H7), CD38-BUV737 (clone HB7, all from BD Biosciences). Following antibody staining, cells were washed once with PBS and labeled with BD Horizon™ Fixable Viability Stain 780 (L/D FVS-780; BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer instructions. For samples collected at two years we used a different antibody and proteins panel (panel B). PBMCs were incubated with WT RBD biotinylated-protein (BioLegend) conjugated with SA-BV421 (BD Biosciences) and BA.2 RBD recombinant biotinylated-protein (ACROBiosystems, DE, USA) conjugated with SA-BUV737 (BD Biosciences). Then, cells were stained with the following antibodies: CD3-BV650 (clone OKT3), CD21-FITC (clone B-LY4), CD19-BV605 (clone SJ25C1), IgM-BV11 (clone G20-127), IgD-PECF-594 (clone IA6-2), CD27-BV786 (clone O323), CD11c-BB700 (clone 3.9), CD20-APCH7 (clone 2H7), CD38-BUV395 (clone HB7), IgG-PE-Cy7 (clone G18-145, all from BD Bioscences), IgA-VioGreen (clone IS11-8E10; Miltenyi Biotec). All antibodies were titrated for optimal dilution. Following surface staining, cells were washed once with PBS and labeled with BD Horizon™ Fixable Viability Stain 700 (L/D FVS-700; BD Bioscences) according to the manufacturer instructions. Cells were fixed in BD fixation solution (BD Biosciences) and acquired with SO LSRFortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Manual data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10 (TreeStar, OR, USA), and computational analysis was performed into R environment.

FlowSOM clustering

Utilizing FlowJo v10 (TreeStar) the MBC populations were gated as live, singlet, CD3− CD19+ cells, excluding the naïve B cells (CD27− IgD+) from the analysis. FCS files were imported into R environment, compensated, and transformed as previously described [29, 30]. Clustering analysis was performed following the FlowSOM function pipeline (FlowSOM package v2.4.0) [31]. Marker expression was normalized as z-score (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) and grid size was set to 10 × 10. Similar nodes were merged into metaclusters, setting the parameter nClus = 15 for longitudinal analysis and nClus = 20 for long term analysis at two years. Metaclusters with similar phenotypes were manually merged, yielding a total of 14 metaclusters for longitudinal analysis and 16 metaclusters for long term analysis at two years. The Euclidean distance was used in both the FlowSOM clustering and metaclustering. Thresholds to bisect positive and negative cells for each marker expression were automatically set with the flowDensity package v1.34.0 [32]. FlowJo gates were imported in R environment using GetFlowJoLabels function to calculate the frequencies of antigen-specific cell within each cluster.

Supervised decision tree modeling and variable importance assessment

To investigate the clinical predictors associated with elevated RBD+ IgG+ resting MBC responses two years after first vaccine dose, a supervised classification approach based on decision tree modelling was implemented using R (version 4.5.0) and the packages caret v7.0-1 and rpart v4.1.24 [33]. Clinical parameters used were reported in Supplementary Table 1. Missing values in numeric variables were imputed using the median of available observations, while missing values in categorical variables were imputed using the mode. To construct the binary classification target, the variable representing RBD-specific IgG+ resting MBC response was dichotomized using its median value, that coincided with the lower quartile of the response distribution among HC. A decision tree classifier was trained using the rpart algorithm, with the Gini index as the splitting criterion. Model complexity was controlled using a minimum bucket size of 4 observations and a pruning complexity parameter (cp.) set to 0.05. The model was fitted on the full dataset to prioritize interpretability and facilitate identification of key discriminative features. Classification performance was evaluated by computing accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly classified cases. The trained tree was visualized using the fancyRpartPlot function from the rattle package v2.6.26 [34] and Biorender (Biorender.com), displaying hierarchical splits, node purity, and class distributions. Variable importance scores were extracted via the varImp function and plotted to highlight the most influential predictors contributing to the classification task. Only features with non-zero importance were retained; variables were ranked in ascending order of relevance and only the ones above the elbow point were finally plotted.

ELISA

Circulating spike-specific IgG produced upon mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccination were tested on Maxisorp microtiter plates (Nunc, Denmark) coated with recombinant SARS-CoV-2 spike S1 + S2 ECD (either from WT or Omicron BA.2 subvariant) (1 µg/ml protein; Sino Biological), as previously reported [35]. Briefly, plates were blocked (PBS and 5% skimmed milk powder, 0.05% Tween 20) and then added with heat-inactivated plasma samples titrated in twofold dilutions in duplicate. Anti-human horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated IgG was added in diluent buffer for 1 h at RT, then plates were developed with 3,3’,5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Thermo Fisher Scientific) substrate for 10 min at RT, followed by addition of 1 M stop solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured on a Multiskan FC Microplate Photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

ACE2/RBD inhibition assay

The antibody ability to inhibit the binding between human ACE2 and viral spike RBD was assessed with a SARS-CoV-2 surrogate virus neutralization test (sVNT) kit (cPass™; Genscript, Netherlands), according to the manufacturer protocol. Diluted plasma samples (1:10), positive and negative controls were incubated 1:1 with HRP-RBD solution (either from WT or Omicron BA.2 subvariant) for 30 min at 37 °C. A volume of 100 µl of each mixture was added to wells of a ACE2 pre-coated 96 wells plate and incubated for 15 min, RT. After 3 washing steps, 100 µl of substrate were added and the plate was incubated for 15 min, RT. Afterwards, the colorimetric reaction was stopped adding 50 µl of stop solution. Absorbance at 450 nm was immediately measured on a Multiskan FC Microplate Photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Results are reported as follows: percentage inhibition = (1 - sample OD value/negative control OD value) * 100. Inhibition values ≥ 30% are regarded as positive results, while values < 30% as negative results, according to manufacturer indications.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (number, proportion, median, interquartile range [IQR]) were used to describe the baseline characteristics of individuals. Categorical variables were compared between groups using the Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U test and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Statistically significant differences in ELISA titres, ACE2/RBD inhibition and B cell subsets frequencies were assessed with Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post-test for multiple comparisons. Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the significant differences in percentages of ACE2/RBD inhibition positive subjects. The statistical analysis of FlowSOM results were performed with Mann-Whitney U test. Differences were considered significant at P ≤ 0.05. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA).

Results

Participants

A total of 74 PLWH and 71 HC individuals were enrolled in this observational cohort study. Participants were vaccinated with mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccines up to five times and blood samples were collected before and after each vaccine dose to analyse the phenotypic modulation of RBD-specific MBC, and the long-term persistence of both WT and BA.2 RBDspecific MBC and antibodies (Fig. 1).

Study design. People living with HIV (PLWH, 74 subjects) and healthy controls (HC, 71 subjects) with undergoing SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination (either BNT162b2 Pfizer-BioNTech or mRNA-1273 Moderna) were enrolled for the observational cohort study. Blood samples were collected at pre III (1 day-1 month before the third dose, that corresponds to 6–8 months after the second dose), post III (1–2 months after third dose), pre IV (1 day-1 month before the fourth dose, that corresponds to 6–9 months after third dose), post IV (1–4 months after fourth dose) and at 2 years (two years from first dose). RBD-specific memory B cell (MBC), reactive against wild type (WT) or Omicron BA.2 substrain, were assessed at the different time points indicated in figure. Antibody response, specific for WT and Omicron BA.2 substrain, was assessed in plasma samples collected at two years

Clinical and demographic data of PLWH and HC subjects were collected before the first vaccine dose and reported in Supplementary Table 1. PLWH had a median age of 51.6 years (IQR 45.4–59.0), about 78% (58 subjects) were male and about 22% (16 subjects) were female. About 76% (56 subjects) had CD4+ T cells count ≥ 500 cell/mmc, while about 7% (5 subjects) had CD4+ T cells count ≤ 350 cells/mmc. About 44% (32 subjects) showed optimal immunological recovery (OIR), defined as CD4+ T cells count ≥ 500 cell/mmc, CD4+ T cells % ≥ 30% and CD4/CD8 ratio ≥ 1 [36, 37]. 5% (4 subjects) reported SARS-CoV-2 infection before December 2021, while 23% (18 subjects) reported infections after Omicron strains wide spreading in Italy. Flowchart reporting the number of volunteers who donated blood samples at each time point is reported in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The HC individuals had a median age of 50 years (IQR 41.5–60.5), matching the median age and age distribution of PLWH (P > 0.05 for both tests; Supplementary Table 1). About 65% (46 subjects) HC were females and 35% (25 subjects) were males, a significantly different sex-ratio compared to PLWH (P < 0.001). All HC were Caucasian (while 15.1% of PLWH had non-Caucasian ethnicity, P < 0.001). 6% of HC (4 subjects) reported SARS-CoV-2 infection before December 2021, while 42% (30 subjects) were infected afterwards. All infections reported by PLWH and HC were asymptomatic or mild, none requiring hospitalization.

Modulation of RBD-specific memory B cells following SARS-CoV-2 mRNA booster vaccinations in PLWH

We recently characterized the RBD-specific MBC response to the primary vaccination cycle in PLWH [18]. Five months after the second vaccine dose, we showed that PLWH had a frequency of spike-specific MBC similar to HC but with significant phenotypic differences, such as a higher frequency of spike-specific double negative (DN) and a lower frequency of IgG+ resting MBC in PLWH compared to HC18. Here, we analysed the phenotypic modification of the RBD-specific MBC after the third and the fourth vaccine doses. WT RBD+ cells were identified within live singlet CD19+ MBC using a combination of fluorescently labelled WT spike protein (S) and WT RBD markers, after exclusion of naive IgD+ CD27− cells (Fig. 2a).

The booster dose increased the frequency of WT RBD-specific MBC (RBD+) in PLWH from 0.32 to 0.67% of CD19+ cells (median 0.320 [IQR 0.052–0.543] pre III; 0.670 [0.160–1.25] post III; Fig. 2b), while the fourth vaccine dose did not boost these frequencies (0.320 [0.150–0.610] pre IV dose; 0.235 [0.175–0.355] post IV dose; Fig. 2b). Moreover, after the fourth dose the frequencies of RBD+ MBC in PLWH were significantly lower compared to controls (0.235 [0.175–0.355] vs. 0.510 [0.275–0.875]; P ≤ 0.05; Fig. 2b).

To longitudinally profile the phenotypes of WT RBD+ MBC, we performed a computational analysis of multidimensional flow cytometry data employing the FlowSOM clustering algorithm. By using the combination of IgD, IgM, CD27, CD21, CD11c, CD38, and CD20 surface markers expression, 15 B cell clusters were identified among CD19+ B cells. After merging clusters with similar markers expression, we obtained 14 clusters (Fig. 2c). These were further grouped into the subsets of Ig-switched (Ig-Sw, CD27− IgD− CD20+), double negative (DN, CD27− IgD−), Ig-unswitched (Unsw, CD27+ IgD+ CD20+) and plasmablasts (PB, CD27+ IgD− CD20− CD38+).

The frequencies of WT RBD+ MBC were assessed within each cluster, before and after the third and fourth vaccine doses (Fig. 2d). Most of the WT RBD+ MBC fell into the Ig-switched clusters 1, 8, and 2, and into the DN cluster 4 in both PLWH and HC. Resting (CD27+ CD21+) WT RBD+ MBC within cluster 1 were significantly lower in PLWH compared to HC at both pre third dose (0 [0-0.035] vs. 0.145 [0.079–0.256], respectively; P ≤ 0.001; Fig. 2e) and early post third dose (0 [0-0.104] vs. 0.165 [0.078–0.380]; P ≤ 0.05), while they increased and became comparable to HC at longer time points after the third dose (0.130 [0.040–0.460] at pre IV; 0.060 [0.060–0.250] at post IV). The WT RBD+ MBC into cluster 2 (the most abundant resting cluster) also increased overtime in PLWH, starting from very low frequency at pre-III dose (0 [0-0.137] vs. 0.195 [0.075–0.324]; P > 0.05 vs. HC), and reaching levels like HC at post-III and IV doses. Activated cells (CD27+ CD21−, cluster 8) showed an increase after the third dose, highlighting the booster stimulation effect, and then declined, although still higher in PLWH compared to HC (P ≤ 0.05) at pre IV. The DN1 cells (CD21+, cluster 4) did not undergo appreciable variations in PLWH, and no significant differences were observed when compared to HC at all time points.

Overall, the third dose increased the percentage of WT RBD+ MBC in PLWH and reduced the phenotypical differences between PLWH and HC previously observed upon the 2-dose vaccination cycle [18], promoting the increase of Ig-switched resting WT RBD+ MBC.

Profiling the long-term RBD-specific memory B cells in PLWH

The persistence of SARS-CoV-2-specific MBC was evaluated in blood samples collected two years after the first vaccination. MBC clones specific for the RBD from the WT strain, the Omicron BA.2 subvariant or reactive against both, were detected by flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 3a). Among PLWH, the WT+ BA.2+ population was the most expanded (median 0.110 [IQR 0.075–0.252] %), followed by WT+ cells (0.120 [0.073–0.212] %) and significantly rarer BA.2+ (0.009 [0.002–0.028] %; P ≤ 0.001 vs. WT+ BA.2+ and vs. WT+; Fig. 3b). Frequencies of RBD-specific clones were compared between cohorts, showing significantly lower BA.2+ MBC in PLWH compared to HC (0.020 [0.007–0.048] %; P ≤ 0.05 vs. HC; Fig. 3c). To assess the impact of Omicron-adapted vaccine formulation, we also stratified individuals according to the vaccine received for the fourth and fifth doses (Fig. 3c). Participants were assigned to the WT mRNA group if they had received only original (ancestral/WT) mRNA vaccines. Participants were assigned to the Omicron-adapted group if they had received ≥ 1 dose of an Omicron-adapted mRNA vaccine (Original/Omicron BA.1 bivalent, Original/Omicron BA.4/5 bivalent, or XBB.1.5 monovalent). As no significant differences were observed within-cohort subgroups or across-cohort subgroups (P ≥ 0.05), the samples were collectively analysed in the subsequent phenotypic characterization of MBC. Using the combination of IgD, CD27, CD21, CD11c, CD38, CD20, IgM, IgA, and IgG surface marker expression, FlowSOM algorithm identified 20 cell clusters within CD19+ MBC. After manually merging similar clusters, 16 distinct clusters were obtained (Fig. 4a). The distribution of MBC subpopulations reactive against WT, WT BA.2, or BA.2 RBD was assessed in both PLWH and HC samples (Fig. 4b). The cluster with the highest prevalence of WT+, WT+ BA.2+ or BA.2+ MBC was cluster 5 (IgG+ resting), followed by clusters 15 (IgA+ resting), 16 (IgM+ resting), 4 (activated), 9 (DN1) and 14 (Unswitched; Fig. 4b). To note, the phenotypic profile observed among the MBC recognizing only the Omicron BA.2 RBD was more heterogenous compared to the one among recognizing WT RBD. The relative abundance of clusters within each antigen-specific B cell populations was visualised with the box and whiskers plots, that highlighted the dominance of IgG+ resting cluster 5 among WT+, WT+ BA.2+ and BA.2+ MBC (Fig. 4c). To note, the phenotypic profile observed among the MBC recognizing the Omicron BA.2 RBD was more heterogenous compared to the one among MBC recognizing the WT RBD (Fig. 4c).

The frequency of different clusters among WT, WT BA.2 or BA.2 RBD+ MBC in PLWH compared to HC was reported in Fig. 4c. Among WT RBD+ MBC, no significant differences were observed. Impact of clinical variables on spike-specific memory B cells response

To investigate how demographic and clinical parameters contribute to the long-term antigen-specific MBC responses in PLWH, a classification model was implemented. IgG+ resting MBC (cluster 5 of Fig. 4), reactive for WT, WT BA.2 or BA.2 RBD were merged and used for the model (Fig. 5a). Different clinical parameters were used to build a tree-based classifier for identifying PLWH with frequency of IgG+ resting MBC above or below the µ median value, that coincided with the lower quartile of the response distribution among HC, achieving an accuracy of 0.862 (Fig. 5a).

The importance score of all variables used to build the model (listed in Supplementary Table 1), was computed using the Gini-index. Only variables whose scores laid above the elbow point, corresponding to the inflection where the contribution of additional variables begins to plateau, were shown (Fig. 5b). Among the top-ranked features were granulocyte count, years from initial and current ART, BMI and CD8+ cell count, which mostly contributed to the model classification accuracy. Other variables such as CD4/CD8 ratio, CD4 + cell count and creatinine also exhibited relatively high importance scores, suggesting a potential role in distinguishing individuals with high or low frequencies of IgG+ resting RBD-specific MBC.

As a result of model optimization and pruning, some variables with non-zero importance scores did not appear in the final tree due to overlapping predictive information or redundancy with more informative features (Fig. 5c). The most discriminative variable was the time from initiation of the current ART regimen, since all individuals on their current regimen for less than 1.1 years, corresponding to 9% of the cohort, had IgG+ resting RBD-specific MBC below the median value (100% ≤ µ, 0% ≥ µ). Among the remaining 91% of individuals, the next most relevant variable was BMI, with individuals having a BMI ≥ 30 clustering in the group with IgG+ resting RBD-specific MBC below the median value (22% of the total, with 77% ≤ µ). The individuals with BMI < 30 were further stratified based on CD8+ cell counts, with a threshold of 1417 cells/mmc. Those above this threshold, representing 7% of the cohort, were entirely in the low MBC response group (100% ≤ µ). Conversely, individuals with CD8+ cells < 1417/mmc (62% of total) showed a more mixed response and were further split based on granulocytes levels. The majority of those with granulocyte count > 4.5 × 10^3 cells/mmc, representing 41% of the cohort, had MBC responses above the median (88% ≥ µ). Contrarily, those with granulocyte count < 4.5 × 10^3 cells/mmc (21% of the total cohort) were further stratified based on creatinine levels. Subjects showing creatinine levels below the threshold value of 0.86, representing 9% of patients, had all IgG+ resting RBD-specific MBC below the median value, while most individuals having creatinine levels > 0.86 mg/dl, had MBC above the median value.

Taking together, the variables associated to higher values of RBD-specific IgG+ resting MBC responses selected by the model were time since current ART initiation, BMI, CD8+ cell count, creatinine, and granulocyte levels at the baseline. In particular, individuals in current ART regimen from more than 1.1 years, with BMI in a range below 30, CD8+ cells/mmc lower than 1417, granulocyte higher than 4.5 × 10^3 cells/mmc and creatinine levels higher than 0.86 mg/dl were predominantly high responders, while those that changed ART regimen less than 1.1 years before or with BMI ≥ 30 or high CD8+ cells were most likely to have lower MBC responses.

To address also the functionality of antibodies, we tested plasma samples with a surrogate virus neutralization assay based on the inhibition of the binding between human ACE2 and SARS-CoV-2 RBD (Fig. 6c, d). No subject of the study showed values of ACE2/WT RBD binding inhibition under the positivity threshold and both groups had mean inhibition values higher than 90% (Fig. 6c). Also, when the assay was performed against the BA.2 RBD the mean inhibition values were high (> 80% in HC and > 70% in PLWH), and most subjects retained the ability to block viral binding to ACE2 (97% of PLWH and 96% HC; Fig. 6d).

As for long-term MBC characterization, donors were categorized according to vaccine formulation received (either WT mRNA or Omicron-adapted) but we did not register any significant difference in circulating IgG production nor in ACE2/RBD binding inhibition, both within-cohort subgroups or across-cohort subgroups (Fig. 6a-d).

Discussion

In our previous work, we demonstrated that primary mRNA vaccination against SARS-CoV-2 induced a spike-specific B cell response in PLWH that was quantitatively comparable, yet phenotypically distinct, from that of healthy controls [18]. In this study, we longitudinally assessed the phenotype modulation of RBD-specific MBC elicited by booster immunizations, within the same cohort of vaccine recipients.

We showed that the third dose had a significant impact on the immune response of PLWH, strongly increasing the amount of SARS-CoV-2-specific MBC. In contrast, the fourth dose had a minimal effect, and the frequency of antigen-specific MBC detected in PLWH afterward was lower compared to HC, possibly reflecting an accelerated disappearing of antigen-specific MBC in PLWH [4]. Importantly, booster doses reshaped the WT RBD+ MBC phenotype. Indeed, we previously demonstrated that WT RBD-specific MBC elicited by the first vaccination cycle showed a higher frequency of CD27− IgD− DN and a lower frequency of IgG+ resting MBC, compared to healthy controls [18]. DN B cells have been characterized as antigen-experienced cells that exhibit somatic hypermutation and frequent isotype switching [38], along with the expression of surface inhibitory markers (as CD32b, CD72, CD22, and PD-1) [39. The expansion of the DN subset has been linked to immune exhaustion in chronically infected patients, including PLWH, and in autoimmune conditions [38, 40]. Here, we observed that third dose gradually modulated the WT RBD+ MBC phenotypes by a skewing to the Ig-switched resting memory subset, typically associated with long-term memory, and dominant in healthy individuals [39]. After the fourth dose, the predominance of Ig-switched resting MBC in both PLWH and HC was more remarkable, confirming the modulatory role of the booster doses. Other studies have observed the induction of spike-specific MBC resting memory in PLWH after the mRNA-based primary vaccination cycle [23]. However, that study enrolled a special cohort of vertically infected PLWH, with younger age (median age 29.6 years) and long-time from the initiation of ART, both factors potentially supporting a different immune response. Touizer and colleagues found abnormalities in the global MBC compartment after the third vaccine administration, finding increased atypical cells (CD27− CD21−) and reduced resting MBC, but not within the SARS-CoV-2-specific response [19]. Accordingly, they hypothesized that impaired responses against SARS-CoV-2 infection may stem from the global memory pool rather than antigen-specific B cells [19]. Although it has been demonstrated that the global MBC compartment is impaired in PLWH and may indirectly influence the immune memory to other antigens [6, 41], our longitudinal analysis allowed us to appreciate the phenotypic shift in Ig-switched resting WT RBD+ MBC after booster doses.

Given the significant impact of the Omicron variant on the general population, we aimed to investigate its role in PLWH, especially considering that some individuals in our cohort had received Omicron-adapted booster vaccines. The analysis focused on MBC specific to the WT RBD and those recognizing the Omicron BA.2 subvariant. Most B cell clones in PLWH were reactive to the WT RBD (WT RBD+) or to both WT and BA.2 (WT BA.2 RBD+), while only a small fraction showed exclusive specificity for BA.2 (BA.2 RBD+). This trend was observed both among the vaccinees receiving the Omicron-adapted vaccines and the others. This could be due to the low number of individuals who received Omicron-adapted vaccine formulations included in our cohort of PLWH, as well as to the presence of unreported breakthrough infections, likely due to the frequently asymptomatic nature of Omicron infections that can impact on the SARS-CoV-2-specific MBC in vaccinated PLWH [24]. Similar results have been observed among healthy individuals, who developed a MBC response reactive also against Omicron antigen, even if no exposure to Omicron antigens was declared, as already reported [21, 42–44]. Interestingly, when comparing the total amount of the three MBC antigenic-specificity between PLWH and HC, the already rare BA.2 RBD+ MBC appear to be even rarer in PLWH. The phenotypic analysis showed that WT RBD+ and WT BA.2 RBD+ MBC were mainly IgG+ resting cells, while BA.2 RBD+ MBC presented appreciable frequency of activated cells. Since activated MBC are generated early after antigenic encounter [39], this supports the idea of a more recent generation of new BA.2-specific clones, while WT+ BA.2+ MBC were likely induced by prior vaccinations. PLWH and HC presented an overall comparable MBC phenotype profile of WT and WT BA.2 RBD+ clones, while within BA.2 RBD+ MBC clones, PLWH presented significant lower frequencies of IgG+, IgA+, IgM+ and unswitched MBC compared to HC. Altogheter, the differences between PLWH and HC are consistent with a reduced induction of de novo MBC clones against the Omicron RBD and a slower transition to resting MBC in PLWH. This pattern may reflect fewer Omicron breakthrough infections in PLWH, as self-reported by participants. However, we cannot exclude a contribution from impaired lymph-node/germinal centre function in PLWH [41].

Considering that clinical parameters can impact on the induction of the antigen-specific MBC responses, a tree-based classification model was developed to assess which of them could mostly affect the B cell response. Individuals in the current ART regimen from more than 1.1 years, with BMI lower than 30, CD8+ cells/mmc lower than 1417, granulocyte higher than 4.5 × 10^3 cells/mmc and creatinine levels higher than 0.86 mg/dl generated and maintained a high frequency of RBD-specific IgG+ resting MBC. Specifically, the same antiretroviral regimen in the past 12 months and the BMI lower than 30 were the top-ranked features. The choice of maintaining the same ART regimen reflects clinical stability of the patient and effective long-term viral suppression, depicting a more controlled immunological environment [41]. Extreme values of BMI, specifically obesity, are not rare in PLWH and associated to a reduced responsivity to vaccines possibly due to the immunomodulatory effect of fat tissues [45]. In PLWH, a hallmark of immune dysregulation is the decline in CD4+ T cells and, particularly when CD4-mediated immune surveillance is impaired [46], a compensatory expansion of CD8+ T cells. Here, the tree classifier identified the CD8+ over the normal range (typically 150–1000/mmc) as negatively associated to RBD-specific resting MBC generation. Notably, we already shown that CD4/CD8 ratio cells as well as BMI are involved in modelling SARS-CoV-2 vaccine-induced antibody response in PLWH [47]. The model also identified a specific threshold of both the granulocytes count and creatinine level associated with higher RBD+ resting MBC response (4.5*10^3/mmc and >0.86 mg/dl, respectively). Granulocytes, mainly composed of neutrophils, represent a general indicator of immune reconstitution and functioning [48], while the low creatinine in blood can be a sign of cachexia and sarcopenia, common conditions in PLWH that result in reduced immune system functionality [49]. Taking together, the variables associated to higher values of RBD-specific IgG+ resting MBC responses selected by the model were time since current ART initiation, BMI, CD8+ cell count, creatinine, and granulocyte levels at the baseline. Therefore, the model was able to identify a combination of clinical parameters, including both classical and novel ones, associated with higher values of MBC response that could be helpful in predicting or monitoring the antigen-specific memory response to vaccination in PLWH.

We also addressed the persistence of humoral response to mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccination and the neutralizing antibody activity, usually associated with protection [50]. Multiple studies have reported the successful induction of anti-spike IgG by the first cycle of immunization in most PLWH [13, 16, 18, 51–55], that was further enhanced by the administration of booster doses [17, 20, 56–60]. Two years after the first vaccine dose, WT RBD-specific antibody titres were significantly higher than those observed prior to the third dose, and reported in our previous study [18]. At two years, PLWH showed also an Omicron BA.2-reactive humoral response. Both WT and BA.2 spike-specific IgG titres were lower compared to HC, reflecting a possible reduced long-term persistence of vaccine immunity [4]. When stratifying vaccinees into those who received an Omicron-adapted booster and those boosted with the original strain vaccine, no significant differences were observed, consistent with findings from the MBC response. Interestingly, at two years the ability of inhibiting the ACE2/RBD binding persisted in PLWH, suggesting circulating neutralizing antibodies last for long period.

This work presents some limitations. The selected HC cohort had comparable median age and comparable age distribution but did not match PLWH sex ratio and ethnicity. Patients’ adherence to sampling schedule was not optimal at all time points, preventing per-participant kinetic analyses across the full study window. The reduced sample size and statistical power at some time points may led to underestimate biological differences. To increase the sample size and so the analysis power at two years, we grouped together people with different number of vaccine doses (from 2 to 5 in PLWH and from 2 to 4 in HC cohorts) and heterogeneous intervals since their last dose. While this approach may obscure dose- or time-dependent heterogeneity, it reflects the real-world situation at that time point. This study was not designed to systematically screen donors for SARS-CoV-2 infections, that were self-reported to clinicians at the time of blood withdrawal. Accordingly, the SARS-CoV-2 infection prevalence could be underestimated thus preventing robust infection-related analyses. All people enrolled received mRNA-based vaccines (either BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273) and our findings may not be applied to people receiving SARS-CoV-2 vaccines based on other technologies. Our PLWH cohort was entirely composed of patients under ART and about 76% had high CD4+ T cell/mmc count so may be not representative for all PLWH. A larger longitudinal study involving PLWH with low CD4+ T cell count, detectable viremia or with discontinued ART may be required to study the induction, phenotype and persistence of the RBD-specific MBC in this cohort of immunocompromised people.

Conclusions

In conclusion, these data indicate that, unlike other immunocompromised conditions [61], the third vaccine dose in PLWH significantly modulates the composition of RBD-specific B cell subsets, leading to an expansion of resting memory B cells and a concomitant decline in the population of DN B cells, initially induced by the primary vaccination cycle. Moreover, clinical and demographic factors associated to higher values of RBD-specific IgG+ resting MBC identified by the model, could be used as clinical biomarkers for predicting and monitoring the antigen-specific memory response to vaccination in PLWH. Our study further supports the critical importance of a comprehensive evaluation of the immune response to vaccination beyond humoral analyses to include the characterization of antigen-specific B cell responses. These findings provide novel insights into vaccine-induced immunity in PLWH receiving ART and underscore the need to consider tailored vaccination strategies for individuals with chronic health conditions.
