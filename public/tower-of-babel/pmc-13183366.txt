Mindfulness and Avoidant Coping: Examining the Behavioral Correlates of Suicidal Ideation Among LGB+ College Students
Authors: Matt B. Siroty, Amber L. Pope, Adrian J. Bravo, Stimulant Norms and Perceptions (SNAP) Team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, pansexual, and asexual (LGB+) individuals experience heightened psychopathology compared to heterosexual counterparts, including suicidal ideation (SI). Previous research has indicated risks of avoidant coping and protective effects of mindfulness on these disparities. We examined how these factors contribute to differences in SI among college students (n = 927, 124 LGB+). Avoidant coping did not differ by sexual orientation. Exploratory analyses revealed higher acting with awareness and non-judging (mindfulness facets) attenuated higher observed SI risk among LGB+ individuals. We outline possible mechanisms behind SI disparities among LGB+ individuals and encourage mindfulness as a counseling mechanism to target minority stressors.

Individuals who identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer, and asexual (LGB+)1 consistently report poorer mental health outcomes compared to their heterosexual counterparts, including depression and suicidal ideation (SI; or thoughts of committing suicide) (Janković et al., 2020; Mueller et al., 2015). Moreover, gay and bisexual individuals are three to six times more likely to attempt suicide compared to heterosexual individuals (Hottes et al., 2016). These disparities among LGB+ individuals can be explained by minority stress theory, which posits that mental health disparities by marginalized identity status stem from perpetual experiences with minority stressors, or chronic exposures to psychological strain, including prejudice, stigma, and discrimination (Meyer, 2003). According to minority stress theory and applied to the present study, mental health disparities are attributed to disproportionate adverse experiences (i.e., exposure to minority stressors) related to one’s sexual orientation, and the corresponding impact of these stressors is thought to contribute to poor mental health outcomes (Meyer, 2007). As such, it is important to identify factors that may lower LGB+ clients’ likelihood of adverse mental health outcomes due to minority stress.

Predominant counseling approaches for LGB+ individuals, including cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), dialectical behavioral therapy (DBT), and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), equip individuals to navigate minority stressors via the development of adaptive coping strategies (i.e., resources to actively and effectively address stressors; Beard et al., 2017; Iacono, 2019). Researchers have adapted these approaches to specifically address LGB+-specific minority stressors (Moitra et al., 2023; Russon et al., 2022). Additionally, mindfulness-based training has shown promise in improving mental health outcomes associated with LGB+-specific minority stressors (Iacono, 2024; Sun et al., 2021). Taken together, a daptive coping responses and mindfulness may both serve to protect against SI for LGB+ individuals.

Adaptive coping strategies as a protective factor

Minority stressors prompt a coping response, which encompasses both cognitive and behavioral approaches to navigating potentially problematic events resulting from marginalization. The utilization of coping strategies can be considered a product of an individual’s unique experiences and social roles (Pearlin & Schooler, 1978; Stanisławski, 2019). Previous literature has divided common coping strategies into three categories (Litman, 2006): self-sufficient coping is defined by activities motivated by the individual to directly target distress, socially-supported coping is defined by seeking connections with others when experiencing distress, and avoidant coping is defined by the alteration of thoughts or behavior to deflect distress.

Coping mechanisms aligned with self-sufficient and socially-supported coping are considered adaptive coping responses (Algorani & Gupta, 2023). Compared to avoidant coping, adaptive coping strategies are not as strongly linked to mental health outcomes among LGB+ populations (Hall, 2018). There are mixed findings surrounding the impacts of self-sufficient coping on suicidality (Ambrus et al., 2020; Yi et al., 2021). Though low social support is generally considered a risk factor for SI and other adverse mental health outcomes, particularly among LGB+ individuals (McDonald, 2018), socially-supported coping has not been found to relate to SI (Ambrus et al., 2020; Yi et al., 2021).

Trying to avoid or escape the source of stress (i.e., avoidant coping) is considered a natural response to minority stress among marginalized individuals (Meyer, 2003). Choosing to not directly engage with negative emotions or stressful events may be adaptive, especially in the short-term, by providing temporary relief. However, over-utilizing avoidant strategies can lead to higher stress (Mann et al., 2022), as people seek to deflect or distract from sources of distress rather than addressing stress directly. Given the ongoing nature of stressors specific to LGB+ individuals, such as family rejection, internalized homophobia, or experiences of discrimination, ongoing avoidance may create greater challenges than resolution. Further, experiences specifically related to identity among LGB+ individuals predict change in affect (Mohr & Sarno, 2016). In support of this assertion, research has drawn a relationship between avoidant coping and suicidality (Ambrus et al., 2020; Yi et al., 2021). Avoidant coping and SI were correlated in a sample of LGB French young adults (Charbonnier et al., 2018) and a meta-analysis among LGB+ youth identified avoidant coping as a risk factor for depressive symptoms (Hall, 2018).

The utilization of different coping strategies are known to contribute to the impact of minority stressors on mental health outcomes (Meyer, 2003), yet limited research exists directly measuring differences in use of adaptive coping (i.e., self-sufficient and socially supported coping) between LGB+ and heterosexual individuals. Avoidant coping, however, has been found to be utilized more among LGB+ high school students and adult gay men compared to heterosexual counterparts (Lock & Steiner, 1999; Sandfort et al., 2009). These distinctions in coping responses may stem from minority stressors, which make adaptive coping strategies less accessible for LGB+ individuals and may inhibit individuals’ motivations for such coping (e.g., need to focus on safety and basic resources rather than enhancing internal resources). For instance, the heightened risk for loss of family or social support (Hall, 2018) may preclude socially-supported coping strategies, whereas increased risk for poverty, housing instability, or employment instability (Romero et al., 2020) may hinder the development of self-sufficient coping in order to focus on taking care of basic needs. Taken together, the increased use of avoidant coping strategies and barriers to developing adaptive coping among LGB+ individuals may increase the likelihood of experiencing SI.

Mindfulness as a protective factor

Another protective factor that may alleviate SI disparities across sexual orientation is mindfulness. Dispositional mindfulness is typically defined as an inherent awareness in the present moment without judgment (Bishop et al., 2004) and is considered a protective factor for a mental health outcomes, including SI (Tucker et al., 2014). Mindfulness allows individuals to navigate negative thoughts and experiences with a neutral perspective, and is especially relevant for those with marginalized identities who may experience stressors at higher rates due to societal oppression and stigma (Meyer, 2007). Dispositional mindfulness is often conceptualized to be represented by the following five facets (Baer et al., 2006): observing (sensory awareness), describing (labeling of experiences), acting with awareness (choices aligning with the present moment), non-judging of inner experience (avoiding the “inner critic”), non-reactivity to inner experience (detachment from thoughts and emotions).

The mechanisms behind the protective effects of mindfulness can be explained by Monitor and Acceptance Theory (MAT; Lindsay & Creswell, 2017), which proposes mindfulness promotes attention monitoring (i.e., ongoing awareness), and thus improves cognitive functioning. When combined with increased acceptance (i.e., a non-judgmental perspective and engaging actively), individuals with higher dispositional mindfulness can modify their relationship with their cognitions and demonstrate improvements in mental health. In line with Baer et al. (2006), these processes can be conceptualized by two particular facets– non-judging and acting with awareness. Compared to other facets (observing, describing, non-reactivity), non-judging and acting with awareness require intentional response to and engagement with the present and Monitor and Acceptance Theory emphasizes the importance of these when considering the impact of mindfulness on mental health outcomes (Lindsay & Creswell, 2017). Non-judging and acting with awareness are associated with mental health improvements, including lower suicidality (Bravo et al., 2018a; Tucker et al., 2014). However, there is limited research comparing the impact of mindfulness on SI between LGB+ and heterosexual individuals.

In the few studies examining this link, LGB+ individuals report lower levels of acting with awareness and non-judging (Wedell et al., 2022). Further, dispositional mindfulness was negatively correlated with avoidant coping in a college student sample (Palmer & Rodger, 2009). This finding is consistent with the idea that avoidant coping involves evading present (presumably negative) experiences, whereas mindfulness increases engagement with the present moment. Given LGB+ individuals’ reports of lower rates of mindfulness and higher rates of avoidant coping than heterosexual individuals, understanding this relationship may help to explain the observed disparities in SI among LGB+ individuals. Specifically, higher dispositional mindfulness (particularly acting with awareness and non-judging of inner experience) may serve as a protective factor for SI by weakening the relationship between LGB+ identity and avoidant coping.

Present study

The present study sought to expand the knowledge base on contributors, namely coping responses and dispositional mindfulness, to SI disparities among LGB+ individuals. The goal of the study was to identify how coping strategies and mindfulness may interact and serve as potential protective factors against SI for LGB+ individuals. This study addressed the following research questions: (a) Are differences in the utilization of coping strategies used among LGB+ college students (compared to their heterosexual counterparts) associated with disparities in SI? and (b) Do differences in dispositional mindfulness attenuate these disparities? We hypothesized that higher avoidant coping would contribute to higher SI in LGB+ college students compared to their heterosexual counterparts, and that higher dispositional mindfulness (particularly acting with awareness and non-judging of inner experience) would weaken the relationship between identity and avoidant coping, thus reducing the risk for SI (see Figure 1). We did not expect to observe any statistically significant effects for self-sufficient coping, socially supported coping, or the other mindfulness facets (observing, describing, non-reactivity) on SI disparities.

Method

The present study is a secondary analysis of previously collected data focused on examining non-prescription stimulant use among college students (Looby et al., 2021). All procedures were approved via a single-site IRB at University of Wyoming.

Participants and procedure

Participants were undergraduates in Psychology Department Participant Pools recruited from seven U.S. universities across six states. A total of 4,764 students took the online survey between Fall 2019 and Spring 2020 for research participation credit. A total of 927 participants who completed measures/questions regarding sexual orientation, SI, coping strategies, and dispositional mindfulness were included in our analyses (see Table 1).

Measures

Demographics

Participants responded to demographic questions on sexual orientation, gender, age, race/ethnicity, class standing, and state of residence. For the sexual orientation item, participants who responded “gay or lesbian,” “bisexual,” “pansexual,” or “asexual” were operationalized as LGB+. Those participants who answered “questioning/don’t know” were excluded from analyses, due to high potential variability in identity among these individuals.

Coping mechanisms

Coping was assessed via the COPE inventory (Carver et al., 1989), which asked about the frequency of coping activities on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = I usually don’t do this at all, 4 = I usually do this a lot). The COPE inventory assesses 15 distinct coping strategies; however, following Litman (2006), we coded the items into: self-sufficient coping (planning, active coping, positive reinterpretation and growth, suppression of competing activities, acceptance, restraint, humor, turning to religion; α = .93), avoidant coping (denial, behavioral disengagement, substance use, mental disengagement; α = .87), and socially-supported coping (emotional social support, instrumental social support, focus on and venting of emotions; α = .91).

Suicidal ideation

SI was measured using the DSM Self-Rated Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptoms Measure – Adult (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Specifically, the 1-item assessing SI asked participants: “In the past TWO (2) weeks, how much (or how often) have you been bothered by the following problems?: Thoughts of actually hurting yourself?” on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = None, not at all, 4 = Severe, nearly every day). The measure has been demonstrated as both a reliable and valid measure of SI in college students (Bravo et al., 2018b).

Dispositional mindfulness

Mindfulness was assessed using the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ; Baer et al., 2006). The FFMQ assesses five distinct mindfulness facets: observing (α = .85), describing (α = .81), acting with awareness (α = .89), non-judging (α = .91), and non-reactivity (α = .82) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never or very rarely true, 5 = Very often or always true). The FFMQ has been demonstrated to have high validity following the examination of its psychometric properties (Baer et al., 2008).

Data analysis

SPSS Version 27.0 was used to conduct analyses. We first calculated descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations of study constructs among LGB+ and heterosexual individuals. Next, we conducted independent t-tests among all constructs (SI, coping strategies, mindfulness facets) across sexual orientation groups. Mediation models were performed using SPSS Macro, PROCESS v4.0, Model 4 (Hayes, 2017) to examine whether coping strategies indirectly influenced the relationship between sexual orientation and SI. We also performed moderated mediation models (see Figure 1) with Model 7 of PROCESS to determine whether dispositional mindfulness moderated the relationship between sexual orientation and coping strategies, which in turn would impact disparities in SI. All continuous variables that defined products of the interaction term were mean centered and all interaction terms were probed regardless of statistical significance. Moderation effects were probed by analyzing the conditional effect of the focal predictor (sexual orientation) on the mediator (coping strategies) at differing values of the moderator (mindfulness) corresponding to 1 SD below the mean, the mean, and 1 SD above the mean. The index of moderated mediation for each model was used to determine the statistical significance of the moderated-mediated effect. For all models, statistical significance was determined using 95% bootstrap (10,000 samples) confidence intervals that did not contain zero within the bounds of the confidence intervals.

Results

Results of bivariate correlations and independent t-test across sexual orientation groups are reported in Table 2. LGB+ individuals reported higher levels of SI (Cohen’s d = 0.37), socially supported coping (Cohen’s d = 0.27), and observing (Cohen’s d = 0.39) compared to heterosexual participants. LGB+ individuals also reported lower levels of acting with awareness (Cohen’s d = 0.38) and non-judging (Cohen’s d = 0.30). There were no statistically significant differences among other study variables (including avoidant coping). Within correlational analyses, both samples exhibited positive relationships between SI and avoidant coping, and negative relationships between SI and both acting with awareness and non-judging.

Results of mediation models (Research Question A)

Within the avoidant coping model, LGB+ identity was not significantly associated with avoidant coping (b = 1.51, 95% CI [−0.02, 3.06]) but was uniquely associated with greater SI (b = 0.26, 95% CI [0.11, 0.42]). Avoidant coping was positively associated with SI (b = 0.03, 95% CI [0.03 0.04]). Though the indirect effect was significant (b = 0.05, 95% CI [0.001, 0.11]), caution should be taken given the non-significant a-path in the model (i.e., no differences in avoidant coping by sexual orientation). For adaptive types of coping, LGB+ identity was not associated with self-sufficient coping (b = −1.51, 95% CI [−4.71, 1.69]) but was uniquely associated with greater SI (b = 0.31, 95% CI [0.15, 0.48]). However, LGB+ identity was significantly associated with socially supported coping (higher for LGB+ individuals; b = 2.30, 95% CI [0.70, 3.91]) and uniquely associated with greater SI (b = 0.31, 95% CI [0.15, 0.47]). Both self-sufficient coping and socially supported coping were not significantly associated with SI (b = 0.00, 95% CI [−0.003, 0.004] and b = 0.001, 95% CI [−0.005, 0.01], respectively) and the indirect effects were not significant (b = −0.001, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.01]; b = 0.004, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.02]).

Exploratory analyses: impacts of mindfulness on SI disparities by sexual orientation

Given non-significant indirect effects in our path models (including non-significant a-path in the avoidant coping model), we did not move forward with planned moderated-mediation analyses (Research Question B). However, in further exploring the potential impact of dispositional mindfulness to explain SI disparities for LGB+ individuals, we conducted exploratory moderation models using Model 1 of PROCESS. Specifically, each mindfulness facet was uniquely (controlling for effects of other mindfulness facets) examined as a moderator of the relationship between LGB+ identity and SI. Within these moderation models (see Table 3), we found significant moderation effects for non-judging of inner experience and acting with awareness. Notably, the disparity of SI among LGB+ individuals (compared to heterosexual participants) was weakened among higher levels of non-judging of inner experience and acting with awareness.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationships between coping strategies, dispositional mindfulness, and SI among LGB+ college students (compared to their heterosexual peers). LGB+ college students exhibited higher levels of SI, observing, and socially supported coping, as well as lower levels of acting with awareness and non-judging. Counter to our hypothesis, LGB+ students did not report higher levels of avoidant coping, and the relationship between sexual orientation and SI was not significantly mediated by coping strategies. Due to the aforementioned null indirect relationships, we were unable to test the impacts of variations in mindfulness on the relationship between sexual orientation and coping. However, exploratory moderation analyses revealed that higher levels of non-judging and acting with awareness attenuated SI risk among LGB+ individuals (observing, describing, and non-reactivity did not significantly impact this relationship).

We did not observe differences in avoidant coping by sexual orientation, which was contradictory to findings in prior studies (Lock & Steiner, 1999; Sandfort et al., 2009). Though our research supported prior findings drawings links between avoidant coping and SI (Ambrus et al., 2020; Charbonnier et al., 2018; Yi et al., 2021), perhaps this framework applies to individuals regardless of sexual orientation. There were no differences in self-sufficient coping by sexual orientation (in line with hypotheses), but LGB+ individuals exhibited higher levels of socially supported coping. As our sample consisted of undergraduate students, they may have been able to seek support in LGB+ spaces and communities on their respective campuses.

Our findings regarding acting with awareness and non-judging of inner experience being lower among LGB+ individuals were in line with our hypotheses and replicate prior research (Wedell et al., 2022). Given the protective effects of these facets on SI (Bravo et al., 2018a; Tucker et al., 2014), it is plausible to expect lower levels among LGB+ individuals. Perhaps the internalization of minority stressors prevents LGB+ individuals from non-judgmental cognitions and the ability to respond in line with one’s values. We also found higher levels of observing among our LGB+ sample. This is not surprising given prior findings suggesting its link with maladaptive psychological symptoms which are higher among LGB+ populations, attributed in nonmeditating samples to the process of noticing unpleasant situations without the tools to navigate them (Baer et al., 2008).

Due to the lack of significant indirect effects in our path models, we did not test the impacts of dispositional mindfulness on the relationship between sexual orientation and coping strategies. Prior research found that mindfulness moderated the mediation framework of LGB+ identity, affective lability, and SI (Wedell et al., 2022); perhaps the protective effects associated with mindfulness target emotion regulation processes rather than those related to coping. Our exploratory analyses revealed protective effects of non-judging and acting with awareness, such that the disparity in SI between LGB+ and heterosexual students weakened among individuals reporting higher levels of both acting with awareness and non-judging. These findings support Monitor and Acceptance Theory (Lindsay & Creswell, 2017), such that LGB+ individuals with higher non-judging may feel more comfortable navigating their sexual orientation (e.g., reduced self-stigma), and individuals with higher acting with awareness may experience increased emotional regulation and processing of potentially harmful identity-specific experiences. Future research can examine the impact of these mechanisms on mental health symptomatology.

Implications for clinical practice

The current study supports an ever-present need for establishing strategies that may reduce SI among LGB+ individuals. Developing models for addressing LGB+-specific stressors and providing tools to mitigate their impacts on mental health have become prioritized using interventions (Pachankis et al., 2023b). Given our observed unique protective effects of mindfulness—specifically in the areas of non-judging and acting with awareness—on SI among LGB+ individuals, providers can target these specific skills when working with LGB+ clients. Non-judging is considered a cognitive skill, wherein individuals objectively observe their inner experiences without criticizing them (Alkholy, 2021). Non-judging can be cultivated through DBT mindfulness skills (Linehan & Wilks, 2015), ACT cognitive diffusion techniques (Harris, 2006), or self-compassion exercises (Toplu-Demirtaş et al., 2018). Acting with awareness, the ability to attend to the present moment (Raphiphatthana et al., 2016), also can be developed through DBT mindfulness skills (Linehan & Wilks, 2015), as well as ACT or CBT exercises (e.g., grounding, breathwork, sensory awareness) that promote present-moment contact (Harris, 2006).

Multiple evidenced-based therapeutic approaches (e.g., CBT, DBT, ACT) focus mindfulness for clients in the context of navigating and responding to minority stressors. For instance, development of a solely mindfulness-based intervention for LGB+ young adults has demonstrated promise in improving mental health outcomes (Iacono, 2024). ACT for LGB+ populations fosters identity acceptance (Stitt, 2014), which is associated with reduced internalized homophobia (Yadavaia & Hayes, 2012). Additionally, while traditional DBT may not improve symptoms as strongly for LGB+ individuals (Oshin et al., 2024), adaptations that target minority stressors have demonstrated improvements among LGB+ veterans (Cohen et al., 2021). Similarly, clients with higher levels of internalized homophobia demonstrated decreased psychological distress after completing an online affirmative CBT intervention in which “increasing mindful awareness of LGBTQ-related stress reaction” was one of the treatment objectives (Pachankis et al., 2023b, p. 13). However, participants did not demonstrate improvements in SI (Pachankis et al., 2023a). Although more research is needed to explore the utility of mindfulness interventions in reducing SI in LGB+ individuals, the overall efficacy of adapted interventions with mindfulness as a core component further emphasizes the protective potential in promoting these cognitions among LGB+ populations.

Mindfulness-based skills may reduce minority stress and internalized homophobia through cultivating psychological flexibility (Fowler et al., 2022), such that individuals are able to separate external factors that may denigrate their sexual identity and become more centered in their identity development (Meyer, 2007). Internalized homophobia has been demonstrated to mediate the relationship between discrimination experiences and poorer mental health outcomes (Walch et al., 2016). However, mindfulness may disrupt the process in which LGB+ individuals attribute negative sociocultural experiences toward their own identities (Meyer, 2003). Thus, mindfulness may promote comfort and stabilization of individuals’ LGB+ identities, which would allow individuals to deemphasize their relationships with minority stressors, which otherwise serve as a risk for poor mental health, including increased SI.

In terms of coping, mindfulness techniques have been shown to reduce the overuse of avoidant coping (Alkholy, 2021; Harris, 2006). Further, familial and social support serve as a buffer to minority stress, thus reducing LGB+ individuals’ risk for adverse mental health outcomes (Greene & Britton, 2015; Hall, 2018). Social support also may help cultivate mindfulness facets for LGB+ people, such as a less judgmental and more compassionate stance toward oneself (Greene & Britton, 2015; Toplu-Demirtaş et al., 2018). Hence, socially-supported coping—when combined with mindfulness skills—may offer a particularly effective approach for mitigating the psychological impact of minority stress and promoting the well-being of LGB+ clients.

Limitations

Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, we are unable to test causality or temporal order. Additionally, our sample consisted of undergraduate students, primarily female and underclassmen. Further, the secondary analysis nature of our study led to a small LGB+ sample, a majority of which identified as bisexual, and our samples come from liberal-leaning undergraduate environments where LGB+ acceptance is presumably higher. Furthermore, our current framework was limited to individual level, rather than systemic factors (e.g., discriminatory policies and legislation). Perhaps there are differing coping mechanisms that LGB+ individuals utilize when facing structures that uphold heteronormativity and homophobia across the United States. Additionally, our single-item measure of SI may not have captured SI fully and did not allow us to examine extensive differences in severity.

Future directions

Future research can adapt our models to a longitudinal/experimental design to test the moderating effects of mindfulness facets on SI disparities among LGB+ individuals over time. More specifically, adapting our models to an intervention framework (e.g., specific strategies to engage the different facets of mindfulness) would allow us to examine how developing adaptive navigation strategies to minority stressors contribute to changes in SI over time. For instance, SI has never been studied as an outcome across mindfulness-centered interventions for LGB+ individuals (Sun et al., 2021). Directly examining SI as an outcome variable (especially in conjunction with other mental health outcomes) would also provide additional context for LGB+ individuals who may be struggling most. We also recommend that future research focuses on the recruitment of LGB+ individuals to encapsulate more diversity of identity. The stratification of historically underrepresented demographics within LGB+ communities (i.e., gender diverse individuals, BIPOC individuals) could allow for more nuanced research on health disparities associated with SI. These models could be expanded to assess the impacts of intersectional marginalized identities on SI to further understand the impacts of intersectional marginalized identity statuses.

Future research can adapt to samples outside of LGB+ undergraduate students, which may have contributed to some unexpected results (i.e., greater socially supported coping, no difference in avoidant coping). Perhaps studying older LGB+ individuals will uncover differences in coping uses by sexual orientation. We also recommend future research further explores potential interactions between mindfulness and coping in jointly reducing SI risk among LGB+ populations. Future research can also stratify based on gender identity to examine the role of gender identity in our research questions. Altogether, these findings demonstrate the importance of cultivating mindfulness skills as a means of preventing and reducing SI among LGB+ individuals. These preliminary linkages provide further support for the use of mindfulness-based interventions to target LGB+-specific minority stressors.
