CPX-351 vs. conventional chemotherapy cardiotoxicity in high-risk AML: a post hoc phase III trial analysis
Authors: Joshua D. Mitchell, Michael Pfeiffer, John Boehmer, John Gorcsan, Shunsuke Eguchi, Yoshiyuki Orihara, Nalina Dronamraju, Sonja Dhani, Stefan Faderl, Tara L. Lin, Geoffrey L. Uy, Jeffrey E. Lancet, Jorge E. Cortes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCPX-351, a dual-drug liposomal encapsulation of daunorubicin and cytarabine in a synergistic 1:5 molar ratio, has demonstrated significantly improved overall survival in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) compared with 7 + 3, but its impact on cardiac function remains unclear. In a post hoc analysis of the pivotal clinical trial, we sought to determine the relative cardiotoxicity of CPX-351 vs. 7 + 3 in high-risk AML.MethodsWe evaluated cardiotoxicity in 102 patients with AML (CPX-351, n = 57; 7 + 3, n = 45) who had normal baseline left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 53% and at least one post-baseline echocardiographic assessment. Cardiotoxicity was assessed through reported cardiac adverse events (AEs) and core lab assessment of echocardiograph changes in LVEF and/or left ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS).ResultsA clinically significant change in LVEF (absolute change from baseline > 10% and LVEF <53%) and GLS (relative change from baseline > 12% and GLS < 18%) was less common with CPX-351 vs. 7 + 3 at follow-up 1 and/or 2 (8.8% vs. 20.0% and 21.1% vs. 44.4%, respectively). No CPX‑351-treated patients evaluated at final follow-up (follow-up 2) had decreased LVEF < 53% at follow-up 2, compared to 17.8% of 7 + 3-treated patients. The frequency of reported cardiac AEs was similar with CPX-351 (40.4%) and 7 + 3 (42.2%); most frequent were tachycardia in CPX-351-treated patients (CPX-351, 21.1%; 7 + 3, 8.9%) and atrial fibrillation/flutter in 7 + 3-treated patients (CPX-351, 7.0%; 7 + 3, 11.1%). ConclusionIn addition to improving overall survival as demonstrated in the pivotal trial, CPX-351 may also be associated with less cardiotoxicity than 7 + 3 in high-risk AML patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-025-00421-7.

Background

For decades, cytarabine at 100–200 mg/m2 administered as a continuous infusion on days 1–7 alongside daily anthracycline (daunorubicin at 45–90 mg/m2 or idarubicin at 12 mg/m2) on days 1–3, referred to as the 7 + 3 regimen, has been the standard induction chemotherapy for patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [1–4]. With this regimen, complete remission has been reported in 35%–71% of patients with AML who had received no prior therapy [5, 6]. In patients with secondary AML, complete response rates with 7 + 3 have been lower at approximately 26%–52% [6]. Clinical outcomes with 7 + 3 have remained largely unchanged over the last decades with any improvement in survival rates potentially attributable to the introduction of hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) and better supportive care [3, 7–9].

Anthracyclines used in the 7 + 3 regimen are a primary cause of cardiotoxicity [10, 11]. The risk of cardiac dysfunction increases with higher cumulative doses of anthracyclines, increasing age, and pre-existing cardiovascular disease [12–15]. The incidence of symptomatic heart failure in patients with leukemia treated with anthracyclines is ~ 12% and 1-year cumulative incidence is as high as 35% in high-risk patients [14]. The risk of heart failure may remain elevated for years to decades following anthracycline exposure [13, 16–18]. Although the significant risk of concomitant cardiotoxicity has limited anthracycline use in patients with prior anthracycline exposure or cardiac comorbidities, regimens without anthracycline have not been as effective in improving survival outcomes as the standard anthracycline-based regimens [19, 20].

To limit the risk of cardiotoxicity, international guidelines recommend reducing the cumulative dose of anthracyclines or switching to liposomal formulations [15, 21, 22]. CPX-351 (United States/Canada: Vyxeos®; European Union/United Kingdom: Vyxeos® liposomal) is a dual-drug liposomal encapsulation of daunorubicin and cytarabine in a synergistic 1:5 molar ratio approved for newly diagnosed high-risk AML, including therapy-related AML (t-AML) and AML with myelodysplasia-related changes (AML-MRC) [23–26]. In the pivotal phase III clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01696084) in adults aged 60–75 years with newly diagnosed t-AML or AML-MRC, CPX-351 demonstrated significantly improved overall survival (OS) after a median follow-up of 20.7 months (median OS: 9.56 months vs. 5.95 months; one-sided p = 0.003), which was maintained at 5-year follow-up, and a comparable overall safety profile vs. conventional 7 + 3 chemotherapy [27, 28]. Achieving remission and proceeding to transplant were key predictors of 5-year survival [28].

In an animal study, CPX-351 offered prolonged, limited tissue distribution, which resulted in less toxicity compared with the higher unbound plasma and tissue concentration of non-liposomal cytarabine and daunorubicin [29]. Furthermore, an in vitro model assessing the relative cardiac safety profile of anthracyclines (including viability, function, mitochondrial function, and biomarkers related to cardiomyocyte damage) showed that CPX-351 was considerably less toxic to human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes than free daunorubicin plus cytarabine [30]. However, the risk of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity with CPX-351 in the clinical setting has not been fully assessed. Here, we performed a post hoc analysis of the pivotal phase III trial to assess the impact of CPX-351 vs. conventional 7 + 3 chemotherapy on cardiac impairment in patients with newly diagnosed t-AML or AML-MRC.

Methods

Study design and patient population

The randomized, open-label, multicenter, phase III trial was described previously [27]. Briefly, eligible patients were 60–75 years old with newly diagnosed t-AML or AML-MRC according to the World Health Organization 2008 criteria (≥ 20% blasts in peripheral blood or bone marrow) [27, 28, 31]. Patients with prior cumulative anthracycline exposure of > 368 mg/m2 daunorubicin (or equivalent) were excluded from the trial. Patients received up to two induction cycles and up to two consolidation cycles of CPX-351 or 7 + 3. The number of inductions and consolidations received were dependent on response. All randomized patients were required to have left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) assessed by echocardiographic or multiple gated acquisition scans and have a cardiac ejection fraction ≥ 50% at baseline.

All patients provided written informed consent prior to randomization. The ethics committees of the participating centers approved the study.

Assessment of cardiac impairment

Cardiac impairment was assessed by analyzing cardiac adverse events (AEs) and echocardiographic measures of cardiac function, including LVEF and left ventricular (LV) global longitudinal strain (GLS) by echocardiography scans at baseline (within 14 days prior to randomization), at follow-up 1 (30–45 days after the last induction or prior to consolidation or salvage therapy, whichever was later) and/or follow-up 2 (day 150 from randomization or 45 days from last treatment, whichever was later). Scans from diagnostic imaging were sent to a central cardiac vendor for archive and review. All echocardiographic measurements were blinded to clinical and outcome data. GLS measurements were obtained using endocardial tracking from apical 4 chamber and 2 chamber views using TomTec TTA2.42 (TOMTEC Imaging System GmbH, Munich, Germany) software.

Criteria for LV dysfunction can vary but generally are defined by LVEF < 50%–53% or GLS ≥ − 17% or ≥ − 18% (normal strain is a negative number by convention) [10, 17, 21, 32–36]. For simplicity and ease of understanding, we subsequently report GLS measurements as absolute rather than negative values. Here, we defined cardiac impairment as LVEF < 53% or GLS < 18%, based on previously published guidelines and an integration of prior studies evaluating the optimal strain cutoff for cardiotoxicity [17, 21, 33–35]. Clinically significant changes in LVEF and GLS were defined as > 10% absolute decrease from baseline with LVEF < 53% and > 12% relative decrease from baseline with GLS < 18%, respectively.

AEs, including cardiac AEs, were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) coding dictionary (version 16.0) and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 3.0. The maximum grade for each type of toxicity was recorded and reported for each patient.

Analysis

The post hoc analysis was designed to be descriptive, without hypothesis testing. Patients with normal baseline LVEF (assessed by central cardiac vendor echocardiography and defined as LVEF ≥ 53%) and at least one post-baseline echocardiographic measure (LVEF or GLS) were included. Patients with pre-existing left ventricular dysfunction (baseline LVEF < 53%) were excluded to ensure a homogenous population and to accurately assess treatment-related cardiotoxicity in individuals without prior cardiac impairment, as their cardiac safety profile may differ. Echocardiographic measures were assessed at follow-up 1 and 2: proportions of patients with cardiac impairment, median changes in LVEF and GLS from baseline, and proportions of patients with clinically significant changes in LVEF and GLS. As some patients may have only had an echocardiogram at either follow-up 1 or follow-up 2 (but not both), the same outcomes were assessed in a subset of patients who had echocardiographic assessments at both post-baseline follow-ups to provide a comprehensive evaluation through all planned time points. Cardiac AEs were also evaluated.

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the patient demographic and clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, AEs, LVEF and GLS measurements, and follow-up times. Proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CI), calculated using modified Clopper-Pearson method, were reported for the analysis of clinically significant change in LVEF or GLS only, as this was deemed to be the most important comparison clinically.

Results

Patient population and treatment received

In the phase III trial, 309 patients were enrolled and randomized 1:1 to either CPX-351 (n = 153) or 7 + 3 (n = 156) treatment arms. Overall, of 109 patients who received echocardiographic assessment at baseline and at least one follow-up, 102 patients had baseline LVEF ≥ 53% and were included in the post hoc analysis to assess the impact of treatment on cardiac function in patients with no cardiac impairment at baseline (CPX-351, n = 57; 7 + 3, n = 45; Fig. S1).

The median (min, max) time from baseline to follow-up 1 was 50 (15, 181) days for CPX-351-treated patients (n = 53) vs. 49 (18, 84) days for 7 + 3-treated patients (n = 43), and the median (min, max) time from baseline to follow-up 2 was 154 (41, 210) days for CPX-351-treated patients (n = 30) vs. 140 (52, 169) days for 7 + 3-treated patients (n = 23). Patients treated with CPX-351 received a median cumulative dose of daunorubicin (min, max) of 189.4 mg/m2 (130.9, 324.6) and patients treated with 7 + 3 received 296.5 mg/m2 (174.0, 436.1).

The most common reasons for discontinuation of study drugs were receiving HCT (CPX-351, n = 22 [38.6%]; 7 + 3, n = 11 [24.4%]) or persistent disease (CPX-351, n = 13 [22.8%]; 7 + 3, n = 21 [46.7%]). There was only one cardiac-related discontinuation in the population included in this post hoc analysis. One (1.8%) patient in the CPX-351 arm discontinued treatment, at the discretion of the site investigator, due to cardiomyopathy (LVEF remained normal but declined from 67.5% to 57.3% at follow-up 1 and was not evaluated at follow-up 2); the relationship to the study drug was noted as probable.

Examination of individual LVEF values of the patients treated with CPX-351 with available echocardiographic assessments at follow-up 1 only (n = 53) or follow-up 1 and 2 (n = 26) revealed that of the seven patients who had normal baseline LVEF that decreased below the normal threshold at follow-up 1, none were found to have persistent LVEF < 53% at follow-up 2 (three subsequently recovered to LVEF ≥ 53% and the remaining four had no available measurements at follow-up 2; Fig. 1B). Individual LVEF values of patients treated with 7 + 3 who had available echocardiographic assessments at follow-up 1 only (n = 43) or follow-up 1 and 2 (n = 20) showed that of eight patients who had normal baseline LVEF that decreased below the normal threshold at follow-up 1, two had persistent LVEF < 53% at follow-up 2 (three recovered to LVEF ≥ 53%, and three were not captured at follow-up 2; Fig. 1C).

At follow-up 1, 26.1% of patients without baseline cardiac impairment and who were treated with CPX-351 had GLS < 18%, compared with 39.4% of those treated with 7 + 3. By follow-up 2 there were 15.2% of CPX-351-treated patients with GLS < 18%, compared with 27.3% of patients treated with 7 + 3 (Fig. 1D; data for a subset of patients with GLS assessments at baseline, follow-up 1 and 2 [CPX-351, n = 26; 7 + 3, n = 20] is presented in Fig. S2B). The change in individual GLS values at follow-up for individual patients is presented in Fig. S3. The proportion of patients with GLS < 18% at all time points during the study was lower with CPX-351 (30.4%) than 7 + 3 (51.5%).

Changes in LVEF and GLS and proportion of patients with clinically significant changes

The median baseline LVEF was similar at 63.3% and 62.0% in patients treated with CPX-351 and 7 + 3, respectively. At follow-up 2, median (min, max) LVEF was 59.4% (53%, 75%) and 57.8% (41%, 76%) in patients treated with CPX-351 and 7 + 3, respectively. The median (min, max) change from baseline LVEF in CPX-351- vs. 7 + 3-treated patients at follow-up 1 was − 1.6 (− 23, 19) vs. − 3.4 (− 43, 10), and in follow-up 2 was − 3.0 (− 14, 6) vs. − 5.0 (− 18, 5; Fig. S4A). The median baseline GLS was 21.3% and 21.1% in patients treated with CPX-351 and 7 + 3, respectively. At follow-up 2, median (min, max) GLS was 19.8% (8%, 24%) and 19.0% (10%, 24%) in patients treated with CPX-351 and 7 + 3, respectively. The median (min, max) change from baseline GLS in CPX-351- vs. 7 + 3-treated patients at follow-up 1 was 0.7 (− 12, 11) vs. 1.4 (− 3, 16), and in follow-up 2 was 2.0 (− 10, 9) vs. 1.9 (− 9, 10; Fig. S4B). In the overall population with baseline LVEF ≥ 53% and ≥ 1 follow-up (CPX-351, n = 57; 7 + 3, n = 45), the proportions of patients with clinically significant changes in LVEF or GLS are presented in Fig. 2. While acknowledging that patient drop-out during the study affected the completeness of the data, none of the patients treated with CPX-351 and with follow-up 2 data showed a clinically significant change in LVEF from baseline to follow-up 2, while 13.3% (95% CI 4.9%–26.3%) of patients treated with 7 + 3 and with follow-up 2 data had a clinically significant change in LVEF. In a smaller population of patients who had measurements at baseline and both follow-up assessments (CPX-351, n = 26; 7 + 3, n = 20), generally similar trends were observed in both the median change and clinically significant change in LVEF and GLS at both follow-up assessments (Fig. S5 & S6).

Of the patients who received only one induction (CPX-351, n = 39; 7 + 3, n = 20), four patients in each treatment arm (CPX-351, 10.3%; 7 + 3, 20.0%) had a clinically significant change in LVEF at follow-up 1 and/or 2, whereas, of the patients who received two inductions (CPX-351, n = 18; 7 + 3, n = 25), one patient (5.6%) in the CPX-351 arm and five patients (20.0%) in the 7 + 3 arm had clinically significant change in LVEF at follow-up 1 and/or 2. Of the patients who received only one induction, seven patients (17.9%) in the CPX-351 arm and eight patients (40.0%) in the 7 + 3 arm had a clinically significant change in GLS at follow-up 1 and/or 2, whereas, of the patients who received two inductions, five (27.8%) patients in the CPX-351 arm and 12 (48.0%) patients in the 7 + 3 arm had clinically significant change in GLS at follow-up 1 and/or 2.

Assessment of the impact of CPX-351 vs. 7 + 3 on cardiac adverse events

The frequency of cardiac AEs (any grade) was similar for both treatments; 23 (40.4%) patients in the CPX-351 arm and 19 (42.2%) patients in the 7 + 3 arm experienced an event (Table 2). Of those, five patients (8.8%) in the CPX-351 arm and four (8.9%) patients in the 7 + 3 arm experienced a grade 3–4 cardiac AE. There were no deaths due to cardiac AEs in the CPX-351 arm, and one death due to cardiorespiratory arrest in the 7 + 3 arm. Regarding any-grade cardiac AEs, tachycardia was more common in CPX-351-treated patients (21.1% vs. 8.9%) and atrial fibrillation/flutter was more common in 7 + 3-treated patients (7.0% vs. 11.1%). Two patients (3.5%) in the CPX-351 arm experienced grade 3–4 atrial fibrillation events, but none were recorded in the 7 + 3 arm. Additionally, the only documented grade 3–4 congestive heart failure event also occurred in the CPX-351 arm.

Discussion

The results of post hoc analyses of echocardiographic data from the phase III trial suggest that treatment with CPX-351 results in less cardiotoxicity than 7 + 3 by measures of LV function in adults with newly diagnosed high-risk or secondary AML and supports the pre-clinical findings in cardiac myocytes [30].

A clinically significant change in LVEF was less common with CPX-351 vs. 7 + 3 at follow-up 1 and/or 2 (8.8% vs. 20.0%, respectively). Median LVEF decline was similarly lower in CPX-351 vs. 7 + 3 at both follow-up 1 (− 1.6 vs. − 3.4) and follow-up 2 (− 3.0 vs. − 5.0). The most important cardiac assessment is arguably the LVEF at end of study, and all LVEF values in patients treated with CPX‑351 and evaluated at follow-up 2 had normal LVEF (≥ 53%). Conversely, 17.8% showed persistent LVEF < 53% in the 7 + 3 group at follow-up 2. Acknowledging that there were drop-outs in both arms during the study that affected the completeness of the data, it does support a favorable cardiac safety profile of CPX-351 that can be further validated in future studies.

While LVEF is the accepted standard measurement for LV function, we also evaluated GLS as a potential earlier marker of cardiotoxicity. As with the LVEF analysis, there were fewer patients with a clinically significant decline in GLS in the CPX-351 arm than the 7 + 3 arm at follow-up 1 or 2 (21.1% vs. 44.4%). However, 15.2% of patients in the CPX-351 arm still had strain values below the normal threshold at the final assessment at follow-up 2. Previous studies have suggested reduced GLS can manifest earlier than LVEF changes and predict late onset (i.e., 3−6 months post-therapy) anthracycline-based cardiotoxicity [22, 37]. It is unclear whether the GLS abnormalities at follow-up 2 foreshadow future LVEF decline or symptomatic heart failure and whether long-term cardiac monitoring following cardiotoxic cancer treatment are warranted [17].

Rates of cardiac adverse events were similar in both treatment arms. Grade 3–4 LV dysfunction was not reported with CPX-351, whereas there were two occurrences in the 7 + 3 group. Importantly, more patients were recognized to have LV dysfunction on central cardiac vendor review of trial echocardiograms (21 patients) than were initially reported by the investigator during the trial (three patients). Underreporting of cardiac events in oncology trials has been previously recognized and the disparity in LV dysfunction identification between core lab review and investigator-reported events highlights the importance of centralized cardiac monitoring in future oncology trials [38].

The suggestion of more favorable cardiac outcomes with CPX-351 vs. 7 + 3 chemotherapy may be attributable to the lower cumulative dose of anthracyclines. Anthracycline dose intensity has been associated with improved outcomes in AML [9]. In this context, the difference in dose is particularly notable, given that CPX-351 showed improved efficacy at a lower dose than 7 + 3, as supported by the primary endpoint of OS in the pivotal phase III trial [28].

The liposomal formulation of CPX-351 could also lead to better cardiac outcomes. Liposomal delivery enables selective drug delivery and spares organs with tight capillary junctions, including the heart, from excessive exposure while maintaining antitumor effects [12, 29, 39]. This is thought to be due to the polyethylene glycol coating around liposomes, which protects the molecule from phagocytosis, resulting in a targeted, gradual release of the drug at tumor sites [39]. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating the anthracycline regimens liposomal doxorubicin, epirubicin, and free-drug doxorubicin in the metastatic or adjuvant setting in breast cancer reported lower rates of grade ≥ 3 cardiac events with liposomal doxorubicin than free-drug doxorubicin (odds ratio 0.60; 95% CI 0.34–1.07) [40]. Another meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating patients treated with an anthracycline for breast or ovarian cancer, sarcoma, lymphoma, or myeloma found that clinical cardiotoxicity risk was significantly lower with liposomal doxorubicin than free-drug doxorubicin (odds ratio 0.18; 95% CI 0.08–0.38) [41]. In combination with a preclinical study on the prolonged, targeted tissue distribution of CPX-351 compared with non-liposomal cytarabine and daunorubicin, our findings suggest that anthracyclines within liposomal formulations such as CPX-351 may be associated with less cardiotoxicity [29]. Therefore, our results may be attributable to the liposomal delivery (and 1:5 synergistic molar ratio) of CPX-351, but confirmation will require further study.

CPX-351 appears to be a valuable option for patients with AML offering significantly longer survival compared with conventional intensive chemotherapy, with a comparable safety profile through selective targeting of leukemia cells [27, 28, 42]. This analysis suggests that CPX-351 confers additional benefits, such as limiting cardiotoxicity, a common issue with current AML treatment options. The observed trend towards reduced cardiotoxicity with CPX-351 in this population may lessen the need for routine dexrazoxane administration. However, prospective studies are needed to confirm this and to define the optimal strategy for cardio-protection.

Study limitations

The limitations of this analysis include its post hoc design with descriptive analysis, the small patient subset with echocardiographic assessments at baseline and follow-ups, and limited follow-up time points. In terms of the post hoc nature of this analysis, the following limitations should be acknowledged. While the phase III trial was randomized and the baseline demographic/clinical characteristics were generally balanced in this post hoc analysis, the evaluation of patients with complete data who were more likely to have received consolidation therapy (due to the requirement of follow-up data) may have introduced selection bias. Furthermore, within this analysis, patients in the 7 + 3 arm received a second induction more frequently than those on the CPX-351 arm (56% vs. 32%), a difference not observed in the overall population of the phase III study [27]. This imbalance in subsequent therapy may impact the assessment of cardiotoxicity, as exposure to additional anthracyclines during the second induction could influence cardiac outcomes. However, in our subgroup analyses directly comparing patients in each arm with only one induction, or with two inductions, the results were similar to our primary comparison with reduced cardiac dysfunction in the CPX-351 arm relative to the 7 + 3 arm. Additionally, the absence of a standardized echo surveillance schedule in the trial represents a significant limitation. This variability, combined with the potential for delayed onset of anthracycline-related cardiotoxicity that may not have been captured within this study’s follow-up period, may have resulted in an underestimation of cardiotoxic events. Moreover, not all patients with dysfunction at follow-up 1 had another echocardiogram at follow-up 2, preventing determination of LVEF recovery in all patients. Finally, a degree of heterogeneity exists in defining cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity, i.e., LVEF < 53% or < 50%, and this analysis used the former cut-off to ensure all patients with cardiac impairment were captured [10, 17, 21, 32, 33]. We acknowledge that the application of a different LVEF threshold could yield different results. Further investigations are required to establish CPX-351 as a less cardiotoxic alternative treatment for patients with AML, specifically to include assessments over a longer period post-treatment in larger patient populations which would allow multivariable analysis of possible comorbidities.

Conclusions

Despite the absence of formal statistical comparisons in this post hoc analysis, the observed trends in cardiovascular outcomes suggest a potential advantage for CPX-351 regarding cardiovascular safety compared to 7 + 3. These trends indicate that CPX-351 may offer a more favorable cardiovascular profile. Therefore, the choice of treatment should be carefully considered on an individual patient basis, weighing the potential benefits and risks of each treatment.

Patients with AML, especially secondary AML, often have limited treatment options due to prior exposure to anthracycline regimens, which has resulted in a surge in utilization of less intensive chemotherapy options [11, 15, 21]. Combined with the improved OS and remission rates established in the phase III trial, CPX-351 may provide the dual benefit in this patient population of prolonging OS while reducing anthracycline-related cardiovascular risks. Additional studies are required to further elucidate the mechanisms behind this reduced cardiotoxicity and determine whether reductions in GLS precede further abnormalities in cardiac function over long-term follow-up.
