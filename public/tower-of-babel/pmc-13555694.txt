Mössbauer spectroscopy in drug discovery: revealing Fe- and Fe-S cluster dependent targets
Authors: Jiahua Chen, Trent Quist, Ronabelle Fang, Henry Thanh Nguyen, Maria-Eirini Pandelia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster-containing proteins are the most ubiquitous and functionally versatile class of Fe-containing metalloproteins across all domains of life, including viruses [1–7]. Fe-S clusters commonly occur as [2Fe-2S], [3Fe-4S], and [4Fe-4S] forms, although clusters with alternative nuclearities and coordination environments are also encountered [3,8–10]. Irrespective of the cluster type, these cofactors are typically coordinated in a tetrahedral geometry by inorganic sulfide and protein ligands (i.e., cysteine and histidine) [3,6,8,11]. While Fe-S clusters have historically been regarded as electron-transfer cofactors, their biological functions extend beyond redox chemistry. In this respect, they fulfill structural, catalytic, and regulatory roles in diverse biological processes, including DNA repair, genome replication, metabolic regulation, and host–pathogen interactions [1–3,7,12,13]. Consequently, disruption of Fe-S cluster biogenesis, trafficking, or maintenance is directly linked to a broad spectrum of human pathologies, including cancer, neurodegeneration, infectious disease, and ferroptosis-related disorders [3,5,8]. Fe-S clusters are also increasingly being identified as potentially druggable cofactors in viral proteins vital for replication, as exemplified by the SARS-CoV-2 polymerase and helicase, in which disruption of the clusters with cell-permeable small molecules abolished viral activity and suppressed viral replication [6,14–16]. These findings highlight the broad occurrence and therapeutic significance of Fe-S cluster-containing proteins and position Fe-S cluster-dependent pathways as promising targets for infectious disease drug discovery.

Despite their therapeutic potential, Fe-S cluster-containing proteins remain a comparatively unexplored frontier in drug discovery relative to classical enzyme- or receptor-based targets [8]. This gap arises, in part, because the same structurally plastic and dynamic chemical behavior that enables Fe-S clusters to perform a diverse array of biological functions also complicates their characterization. First, Fe-S clusters are highly sensitive to oxygen and oxidative stress and can undergo structural rearrangements or degradation under aerobic conditions [6,14,17,18]. As a result, their presence can be masked by incorporation of redox-inert Zn ions as both cofactors can occupy the same Cys- and His-rich binding sites [6,14]. In addition, Fe-S clusters are not static entities but dynamic cofactors, whose assembly, oxidation state, and coordination geometry can change in response to cellular conditions [8,19]. Current drug discovery approaches, however, remain predominantly sequence-, structure-, and protein-centric, often overlooking the metal-dependent chemistry that governs Fe-S cluster function [19]. Thus, therapeutically relevant Fe-S cluster states remain hidden, thereby limiting the identification of metal-dependent disease mechanisms.

The identification and characterization of Fe-S cluster cofactors is further obscured by limitations of conventional biochemical, spectroscopic, and structural approaches, a challenge that is often exacerbated when individual techniques are applied in isolation rather than integrated [11,19,20]. Sequence motif analysis can be ambiguous because Cys- and His-rich motifs may support either Zn binding or Fe-S cluster coordination [6,11,14]. Likewise, elemental analyses, such as Inductively Coupled Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES), ferrozine, and Ellman’s tests, detect total Fe or sulfide abundance but do not define cofactor identity, oxidation state, cluster type, or coordination environment [20]. UV–Vis spectroscopy can reveal absorbance features consistent with Fe-S cluster binding, but cannot unambiguously distinguish between different cluster types or resolve heterogeneous Fe species [11]. On the other hand, Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) spectroscopy detects only paramagnetic states, thus limiting access to many biologically relevant Fe-containing cofactors that are EPR-silent [11,19]. Structural methods such as X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM often capture stable or oxygen-degraded states but may fail to resolve the native or biologically relevant Fe-bound form. Consequently, Fe-S cluster-containing proteins may be misannotated, mistaken for Zn-binding proteins, or entirely overlooked, leading to inaccurate mechanistic models and target validation strategies.

In this respect, Mössbauer spectroscopy is uniquely poised to address these challenges as it directly probes the Fe nucleus and provides site-specific information on chemically distinct Fe species [11,19–21]. Unlike many conventional approaches, Mössbauer spectroscopy is noninvasive and performed at cryogenic temperatures, enabling preservation of cofactor integrity and resolution of kinetically transient cofactors allowing insights into coordination environment, nuclearity, oxidation and spin state [11,20–24]. These capabilities enable identification of Fe-containing cofactors even in proteins that are partially unfolded, intrinsically disordered, conformationally heterogeneous, oxygen-sensitive, or dynamically interconverting between multiple states [11]. Importantly, Mössbauer spectroscopy can be applied to both purified proteins and intact cells, enabling in situ analysis within a cellular context while minimizing artifacts introduced during purification [11,20,21]. Thus, Mössbauer spectroscopy provides more than confirmatory characterization; it can directly facilitate target discovery by revealing hidden metallocofactors and uncovering mechanistic and kinetic information. It should be noted that the prowess of Mössbauer spectroscopy is further amplified when integrated with elemental analyses and complementary spectroscopic techniques, such as UV/Vis and EPR spectroscopy, together providing a multidimensional framework for defining Fe-dependent biochemical states.

In this review, we take an in-depth look at workflows for metalloprotein characterization and target discovery centered on Mössbauer spectroscopy. We illustrate how Mössbauer spectroscopy can be employed for uncovering hidden Fe-S cluster cofactors by highlighting two recent examples of two viral proteins: the Hepatitis B virus X protein (HBx) and the Porcine Reproductive and Respiratory Syndrome Virus (PRRSV) Nsp1α protease. These two case studies demonstrate how Mössbauer spectroscopy can reveal previously unrecognized metallobiology and directly support the development of Fe-S-cluster-dependent pharmacology. As such, Mössbauer spectroscopy represents a uniquely powerful and underutilized platform for discovering metal-dependent therapeutic vulnerabilities and expanding the landscape of druggable targets.

Approaches for utilizing mössbauer spectroscopy to reveal Fe-S cluster drug targets

A general workflow for applying Mössbauer spectroscopy to metalloprotein discovery is outlined in Figure 1. Although this framework focuses on Fe-S cluster-containing proteins, it can be readily adapted to virtually any iron-containing sample [20,21]. Candidate proteins are typically expressed in the presence of isotopically enriched 57Fe, allowing selective incorporation of the Mössbauer-active isotope into the cofactor of interest [20,21,25]. Both heterologous expression systems and native cellular hosts can be employed, enabling analysis of purified proteins, intact cells, and more complex biological samples [20,21,26].

A recurring challenge in the characterization of Fe-S proteins is incomplete cofactor incorporation during overexpression. The endogenous Fe-S assembly machinery frequently becomes limiting when proteins are produced at high levels or when newly identified targets are poorly recognized by cellular maturation pathways [20]. Consequently, recombinant preparations often contain mixtures of apo- and holo-protein that complicate both mechanistic studies and spectroscopic analysis. To overcome these limitations, a variety of expression strategies have been developed to enhance Fe-S cluster incorporation, such as (a) co-expression of an Fe-S cluster assembly machinery from a dedicated helper plasmid or (b) expression in modified E. coli strains that allow for constitutive Fe-S biogenesis (Figure 1, green) [20].

In the first approach, the ISC overexpression plasmid pDB1282 is often the first resort to enhance iron-sulfur biogenesis through overexpression of the isc operon [27]. Alternatively, the SUF plasmid pGSO164 promotes cluster maturation under oxidative stress or iron-limiting conditions, which can be particularly beneficial for proteins with oxygen-sensitive and/or unstable clusters [28]. Depending on the application one can resort to more tailored options, such as the plasmid pRKISC (expression of radical-SAM dependent proteins), pISC (commercial version of pDB1282) or pSUF (often gives higher occupancy than pDB1282 for difficult proteins) [29]. In the second approach, the ΔIscR strain (lacking the transcriptional regulator IscR) enhances cofactor Fe-S cluster enrichment [30], while the SufFeScient strain restores expression of SufA and SufB, allowing for increased expression of the entire SUF operon and improving cluster occupancy of Fe-S proteins relying on this operon (Figure 1, green) [31]. These approaches substantially improve cofactor occupancy, increase 57Fe incorporation, and facilitate downstream biochemical and spectroscopic characterization.

Mössbauer spectroscopy can be applied even to challenging targets that are difficult to purify or structurally characterize. Insoluble proteins can often be analyzed directly in 57Fe-enriched cells or under denaturing conditions [11,21,32], while soluble proteins can be examined following purification and enrichment in the Fe-containing cofactor (Figure 1, orange) [20,33–35]. Because Mössbauer spectroscopy detects all iron species present in a sample, careful removal of adventitiously bound iron and optimization of cofactor occupancy are critical for obtaining interpretable spectra [20]. Fe-S cluster enrichment can be achieved by a process termed reconstitution using 57Fe as an iron source, with the specific strategy tailored for the protein or system under investigation. Such enrichment methods include (i) conventional chemical reconstitution (i.e., reducing agent, Fe, sulfide), ii) cysteine desulfurase-mediated (i.e., reducing agent, Fe, L-Cys, IscS) reconstitution, and iii) ISC- (i.e., reducing agent, Fe, L-Cys, IscS, IscU) or SUF -mediated (SufS, SufE, SufBCD, Fe, L-Cys) reconstitution [20,33–35]. Cofactor loading is then assessed by elemental analysis methods, such as colorimetric iron quantification using the ferrozine assay, sulfide assay (Ellman’s test), and ICP-AES to estimate total iron and sulfide content [20,36,37]. Desirable concentrations of isotopic enrichment range from 1–5 mM of 57Fe [20–22,38]. The purified samples are then flash-frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen prior to the measurements to preserve cofactor integrity (Figure 1, orange) [20].

Once suitable samples are obtained, Mössbauer spectroscopy provides a nondestructive means to define the identity and properties of iron cofactors with exceptional specificity (Figure 1, red) [11,21]. The technique readily distinguishes between different Fe-S cluster types, oxidation states, coordination environments, and cluster nuclearities, while also monitoring redox-dependent changes and cofactor interconversion [11,21,39]. In many cases, the characteristic Mössbauer parameters, the isomer shift (δ) and quadrupole splitting (ΔEQ), are sufficient to identify the chemical nature of the Fe-S center (Table 1) [11,21]. The energy values are in mm/s, and the two parameters give complementary information: the isomer shift reflects the oxidation and spin states, whereas the quadrupole splitting provides insight into the coordination environment and symmetry [22,38,39]. More advanced measurements performed under applied magnetic fields can further resolve complex electronic structures, mixed populations, and magnetic interactions, providing mechanistic insight into metallocofactor assembly and function [22,38,39].

The ability of Mössbauer spectroscopy to directly fingerprint iron cofactors has made it an important tool for uncovering metal-dependent biological vulnerabilities with therapeutic relevance. The technique has contributed to our understanding of iron metabolism in malaria, the development and characterization of pharmaceutical iron formulations, and the mechanistic investigation of bacterial Fe-S enzymes involved in essential metabolic pathways [40–47]. Mössbauer spectroscopy has also played a critical role in defining the metallocofactors of ribonucleotide reductase, a major target of anticancer therapeutics [45,48]. More recently, studies of viral proteins, including the SARS-CoV-2 helicase and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, the PRRSV Nsp1α protease, and the Hepatitis B virus X protein (HBx), have demonstrated how Mössbauer spectroscopy can reveal previously unrecognized Fe-S cofactors, redefine accepted biochemical models, and expose new opportunities for therapeutic intervention [15,16,49,50].

A redox-sensitive [4Fe-4S] cluster in the Hepatitis B virus X (HBx) oncoprotein

HBx is a transcriptional transactivator required for HBV replication and a key driver of HBV-associated liver disease, including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, the latter of which is the fifth leading cause of cancer worldwide [51–55]. Despite its significance as a therapeutic target, the molecular mechanism by which HBx contributes to HBV-associated liver disease has remained poorly understood. This knowledge gap largely stems from its historically poor solution behavior, lack of sequence homology to known proteins, and intrinsically disordered nature [50,56–58]. Together, these features have hindered precise biochemical and structural characterization of HBx and limited the reliability of computational modeling. Recently, HBx has emerged as a metalloprotein, challenging the prevailing view of this viral regulator as a solely Zn-binding factor [50,56,57,59]. Elemental analysis showed that HBx copurifies with Zn and Fe, suggesting that its metal-binding site may accommodate different cofactors [50,56,57,59]. In this context, Mössbauer spectroscopy has played a central role in redefining the metal-binding potential of HBx by directly resolving the identity, redox state, and assembly state of its Fe-containing cofactor, information that is otherwise obscured by conventional approaches [50].

Mössbauer spectroscopy enabled target discovery by revealing that the Fe associated with HBx is assembled into an Fe-S cluster cofactor rather than present as nonspecifically bound iron [6,11,50]. Mössbauer analysis showed that HBx coordinates a [2Fe-2S] cluster when isolated under aerobic conditions, whereas purification under O2-free conditions yields a predominantly [4Fe-4S] species (with a minor [2Fe-2S] component) (Figure 2(A)) [50]. This oxygen-dependent cofactor speciation indicates that the HBx metallocofactor is highly sensitive to biochemical and cellular conditions, helping to explain why its Fe-S cluster-binding capacity may have been overlooked or misassigned as Zn binding [6,50]. Subsequent chemoproteomic analyses further confirmed that Fe-S clusters and Zn compete for coordination at a conserved cysteine-rich metal-binding site, corroborating Fe-S cluster binding as an authentic cofactor state in HBx [59]. Collectively, these findings reposition HBx as an Fe-S cluster-binding viral target and highlight Fe-S cluster coordination as a potential therapeutic target.

Beyond establishing HBx as an Fe-S cluster-binding protein in purified proteins, Mössbauer spectroscopy further provided a cellular-level view of how this cofactor is assembled (Figure 2 (A, B)) [11,20,21,50,60]. Analysis of intact E. coli cells overexpressing HBx confirmed the presence of the Fe-S cluster in cellulo and suggested that cofactor speciation reflects both oxygen exposure during aerobic expression conditions and limitations in endogenous Fe-S cluster biosynthetic machinery during high-level viral protein expression (Figure 2(B)) [11,50]. Co-expression with exogenous Fe-S biogenesis machinery and anaerobic reconstitution yielded near-homogeneous [4Fe-4S] incorporation, indicating that endogenous Fe-S assembly is limiting for HBx maturation [20,50]. This strategy effectively bypassed cellular constraints on cofactor delivery and demonstrated that accumulation of the lower nuclearity [2Fe-2S] form can be largely suppressed under anaerobic conditions. These findings further support that endogenous Fe-S cluster biogenesis is unable to sustain efficient [4Fe-4S] cluster loading of this viral protein, leading to incomplete cluster maturation and enrichment in [2Fe-2S] instead. Mössbauer spectroscopy served both as a guide and a unique lens to demonstrate that Fe-S cluster coordination is conserved across HBx proteins from the five major HBV genotypes (A2, B, D, F1, C), identifying this cofactor as a broadly conserved feature of HBx function and supporting its potential therapeutic targetability [50].

In addition, Mössbauer spectroscopy can provide unique, detailed insight into the kinetics and behavior of the cofactor under different redox conditions and/or small-molecule interactions [20,21,60]. In HBx, this capability demonstrated that the [2Fe-2S] cluster can undergo a reductive coupling; upon treatment with a strong reducing agent (e.g. sodium dithionite), the [2Fe-2S]2+ cluster is reduced to a [2Fe-2S]1+ intermediate that subsequently dimerizes to generate a [4Fe-4S]2+ cluster in the absence of exogenous Fe and sulfide (Figure 2 (C)) [50]. This transformation can be monitored kinetically by freezing the samples at defined time points or reductant concentrations and tracking the corresponding spectral changes. This cofactor behavior of HBx parallels that of canonical Fe-S scaffold proteins, such as IscU and IscA, suggesting that HBx may exhibit scaffold-like Fe-S cluster chemistry during infection [35,61,62].

The Nsp1α leader protease of PRRSV is a [4Fe-4S] cluster-containing protein

PRRSV is a major global pathogen of the swine industry for which effective antiviral therapies remain unavailable [63–65]. The leader protease Nsp1α is a multifunctional virulence factor that is essential for viral replication, regulation of viral gene expression, and suppression of host innate immune responses, making it an attractive therapeutic target [66–70]. In addition to its protease activity, a conserved metal-binding domain is required for efficient viral transcription, replication, and inhibition of type I interferon (IFN) signaling, underscoring its central role in PRRSV pathogenesis [68,69]. Although this metal-binding site was originally assigned as a Zn-binding motif based on sequence analysis and crystallographic studies, it has recently been shown to coordinate an Fe-S cluster instead, with Mössbauer spectroscopy playing a central role in this reassignment [49].

Nsp1α can be heterologously expressed in E. coli cells and readily purified, but this approach yields protein that is devoid of iron and maximally contains one equivalent of Zn [49]. Nsp1α localizes to inclusion bodies, accumulating in the insoluble fraction, a commonly observed behavior when heterologously expressing viral or eukaryotic proteins. Inclusion bodies containing Nsp1α can, however, be solubilized in urea under an inert atmosphere, which yields a striking red color in the lysate, suggestive of the presence of an iron-containing cofactor (Figure 3). Nsp1α can be further purified by affinity purification under denaturing conditions, and the elution fractions flash frozen and subjected to Mössbauer spectroscopy [49]. The 80 K spectrum of this sample exhibits a quadrupole doublet with δ = 0.26 mm/s and ΔEQ = 0.49 mm/s, parameters characteristic of high-spin Fe(III) ions in [2Fe-2S]2+ clusters (Figure 3, Table 1), confirming coordination of an Fe-S cofactor.

Because [2Fe-2S] clusters are often breakdown-products of higher nuclearity [4Fe-4S] clusters [50,71,72], we performed a chemical reconstitution of the cofactor to address whether the [2Fe-2S] cluster is an oxidative byproduct or a true cofactor. Chemical reconstitution converted the [2Fe-2S] species observed in inclusion bodies into a [4Fe-4S] cluster, as first suggested by the color change of the solution to brown and further confirmed by the 80 K Mössbauer spectrum. The latter is now dominated by a quadrupole doublet with δ = 0.45 mm/s and ΔEQ = 0.95 mm/s, parameters characteristic of [4Fe-4S]2+ clusters [49]. This result confirms that the cofactor in Nsp1α is a [4Fe-4S] cluster, rather than a [2Fe-2S] cluster. A small signal corresponding to free Fe (II) is also present, arising from excess or nonspecifically bound iron, a common characteristic of chemically reconstituted Fe-S proteins (Figure 3). Mössbauer spectroscopy thus provided key information about the coordination of an Fe-S cluster in Nsp1α present in inclusion bodies, challenging previous assignments of the cofactor at the conserved metal binding site as Zn.

Heterologous expression of the full-length Nsp1 resulted in soluble Nsp1α, allowing us to also examine the cofactor speciation under native protein conditions [49]. Nsp1α copurifies with a small amount of Zn, which can be removed by chelation; subsequent chemical reconstitution of the Fe-S cluster yields the Fe-S cofactor-enriched protein. Initially, traditional chemical reconstitution was carried out by incubating the protein with DTT, Fe(II), and Na2S, followed by desalting and characterization by Mössbauer spectroscopy [49]. The resultant spectrum exhibited a higher degree of spectral complexity indicative of cofactor heterogeneity. The dominant spectral components are a quadrupole doublet characteristic of [4Fe-4S] clusters and a second signal that corresponds to adventitiously bound free Fe(II) accounting for approximately 30% of the total Fe in the sample. To improve Fe-S cluster reconstitution, a semi-enzymatic approach was employed using IscS and L-cysteine in place of Na2S, enabling more controlled sulfide delivery and reducing the amount of free Fe(II) present. This process essentially removed all free Fe(II) and resulted in the homogeneous enrichment of a [4Fe-4S] cluster, characterized by a quadrupole doublet best fit by two overlapping doublets with average parameters δ = 0.43 mm/s and ΔEQ = 0.86 mm/s (Figure 3). These values, despite slight differences owing to sample heterogeneity between denaturing and native conditions, consistently supported assignment of the Nsp1α cofactor as a [4Fe-4S] cluster, with the [2Fe-2S] form likely generated as an artifact of misassembly or degradation.

Conclusion

Mössbauer spectroscopy is uniquely suited for the identification and characterization of Fe-containing cofactors in biologically relevant systems. By defining cofactor identity, oxidation state, cluster nuclearity, coordination environment, and metal-site heterogeneity, the technique resolves ambiguities that often cannot be addressed by conventional biochemical, structural, or spectroscopic approaches alone [21–24,60]. Furthermore, its applicability to purified proteins, intact cells, and heterogeneous samples enables characterization of Fe-containing species under conditions that closely reflect their native biological context [11,20,21].

The examples of HBx and PRRSV Nsp1α illustrate how Mössbauer spectroscopy can reveal previously unrecognized Fe-S cluster cofactors, correct long-standing cofactor misassignments, and establish the authentic metal-dependent chemistry of disease-associated proteins. Beyond cofactor identification, the technique provides mechanistic insight into cofactor assembly, stability, and redox-dependent transformations, thereby linking metalation state to biological function. Together, these capabilities establish Mössbauer spectroscopy as a valuable component of metalloprotein characterization workflows and a powerful tool for the discovery and validation of Fe-dependent therapeutic targets.

Expert opinion

As drug discovery moves beyond sequence- and structure-centric paradigms toward a more complete understanding of metal-dependent biology, iron-containing cofactors are emerging as an increasingly important yet underexplored source of therapeutics. In our view, one of the major obstacles to exploiting these systems has been the inability to accurately define their native metalation state and dynamic metal-dependent chemistry. Mössbauer spectroscopy is uniquely positioned to address this challenge because it directly interrogates the Fe nucleus, enabling unambiguous identification of Fe-containing cofactors even in proteins that are intrinsically disordered, conformationally heterogeneous, oxygen-sensitive, kinetically unstable, or otherwise refractory to conventional structural approaches [20–24,60]. Unlike many structural and biophysical methods, the applicability of Mössbauer spectroscopy is not restricted to highly pure, homogeneous samples. Instead, it can be employed across a broader spectrum of systems, including cell lysates and intact cells. This flexibility enables direct interrogation of metalloproteins in human cells or in their native hosts, reducing the risk of misassigning cofactor composition or overlooking transient states that may be functionally important. Moreover, it allows examination of the fate of these cofactors in their native biological context after exposure to small molecules and inhibitors.

An additional strength of Mössbauer spectroscopy is its ability to capture and resolve distinct metalation, oxidation, and cluster-assembly states. Because samples can be rapidly frozen, transient intermediates and kinetically relevant states can be trapped and analyzed, providing a unique view of cofactor dynamics that is often inaccessible by other approaches. This capability is particularly valuable when examining responses to oxidative stress, host-derived immune mediators, or small-molecule therapeutics that alter cofactor integrity and function. Such information is increasingly important as growing evidence suggests that biological activity is frequently dictated not only by protein sequence and structure, but also by the chemical state of the associated metal cofactor.

The examples of HBx and Nsp1α discussed in this review illustrate this concept particularly well. Both were long regarded as Zn-binding proteins, yet Mössbauer spectroscopy revealed the presence of functionally important Fe-S cluster cofactors that had remained hidden despite extensive investigation. More broadly, these studies suggested that additional Fe-dependent proteins are likely to remain undiscovered across viral, bacterial, and eukaryotic systems. We believe that this may be especially relevant among proteins that contain cysteine-rich motifs, exhibit intrinsic disorder, display unusual redox sensitivity, or have resisted conventional biochemical characterization.

From a drug discovery perspective, we anticipate that the greatest impact of Mössbauer spectroscopy will extend beyond target identification. Many metalloproteins exist as dynamic ensembles that interconvert between distinct cluster-assembly states, yet current therapeutic strategies generally target the protein scaffold without considering the chemical state of the cofactor itself. Future therapeutic strategies should therefore take into consideration the specific metalation or oxidation states rather than solely the protein scaffold. The ability to directly monitor cofactor assembly, degradation, redox transformations, and responses to small molecules provides a powerful means of linking metal chemistry to biological function and pharmacological mechanism, while simultaneously identifying the chemically relevant states most suitable for therapeutic intervention. In this context, IspH, an essential [4Fe-4S] enzyme in the methylerythritol phosphate pathway, is a representative example of how Mössbauer spectroscopy can inform on structure-guided inhibitor design [73]. Mössbauer spectroscopy played a central role in defining the iron coordination environment and guiding inhibitor development by demonstrating that amino-, thiol-, and acetylene-based analogues coordinate the unique fourth Fe atom of the [4Fe-4S] cluster [74,75], thereby enabling optimization of inhibitor scaffolds [76].

Looking forward, integration of Mössbauer spectroscopy with modern structural biology, AI-driven protein prediction, chemical biology, and cellular studies will provide a more complete framework for metalloprotein discovery and validation. For example, the combination of nanoscale secondary ion mass spectrometry (NanoSIMS), cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), and Mössbauer spectroscopy could bridge cellular metal trafficking, protein structure, and metal speciation [77]. NanoSIMS can resolve the subcellular distribution of isotopically labeled 57Fe, cryo-EM can reveal the structural architecture of Fe-containing proteins, and Mössbauer spectroscopy can define the oxidation state, coordination environment, and electronic structure of the corresponding iron centers. Together, these complementary approaches would enable direct correlation of metal localization with structural and functional states, providing an unprecedented view of metalloprotein biology in its native cellular context.

Beyond Fe-S proteins, recent collaborative work from our laboratory illustrates how Mössbauer spectroscopy can be integrated with cellular approaches to investigate the mechanisms of ferroptosis [78]. In this study, a genetically encoded fluorescent reporter for redox-active iron enabled visualization of labile iron buffering in living cells, while Mössbauer spectroscopy demonstrated that specific polyamines directly coordinate ferrous iron, providing a molecular basis for defining altered iron homeostasis in ferroptosis-associated disease states. Moreover, Mössbauer spectroscopy has also been applied to characterize iron-containing pharmaceuticals by defining oxidation states, coordination environments, and formulation stability, including clinically used formulations such as ferumoxytol, ferric carboxymaltose, and iron isomaltoside [79,80].

More broadly, the application of Mössbauer spectroscopy in combinatorial strategies has the potential to transform metalloprotein drug discovery by connecting metal trafficking, metalloprotein assembly, and catalytic function with cellular phenotypes and therapeutic response. As the importance of Fe-S clusters and Fe cofactors continues to expand across infectious disease, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cancer, we expect Mössbauer spectroscopy to become an increasingly important orthogonal platform for identifying hidden metalloproteins, defining therapeutically relevant metal states, and accelerating the development of metal-directed therapeutic strategies. Rather than serving solely as a tool for metalloprotein characterization, we envision Mössbauer spectroscopy becoming an integral component of future drug discovery studies in which the metal cofactor is recognized as a dynamic therapeutic target.
