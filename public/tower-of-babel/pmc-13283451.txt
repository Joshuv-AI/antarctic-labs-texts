Development of Physical Activity from Childhood to Adolescence in Offspring at Familial High Risk of Schizophrenia or Bipolar Disorder and Population-Based Controls: The Danish High Risk and Resilience Study
Authors: Anne Søndergaard, Sinnika Birkehøj Rohd, Marta Schiavon, Doris Helena Feodora Bjarnadottir Streymá, Martin Wilms, Maja Gregersen, Anette Faurskov Bundgaard, Andreas Færgemand Laursen, Nicoline Hemager, Mette Falkenberg Krantz, Carsten Hjorthøj, Lotte Veddum, Aja Neergaard Greve, Anne Amalie Elgaard Thorup, Merete Nordentoft
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchizophrenia and bipolar disorder are associated with elevated morbidity and premature mortality, including cardiovascular disease. This excess risk may, among other factors, relate to reduced physical activity (PA) beginning in childhood. Studying children at familial high risk of schizophrenia (FHR-SZ) or bipolar disorder (FHR-BD) may help characterize early behavioural trajectories.Study MethodsWe conducted a sub study of PA within The Danish High Risk and Resilience Study VIA, a longitudinal cohort study. A total of 367 children underwent accelerometry at age 11 years, and 258 at age 15 years. Univariable analyses were performed to examine prospective associations between PA, body mass index (BMI), and motor functioning at age 11 and PA at age 15.Study ResultsAt age 15, adolescents at FHR-SZ and FHR-BD had fewer daily steps (Cohen’s d = 0.38 and 0.43) and less continuous walking (Cohen’s d = 0.37 and 0.38) than controls. From age 11 to 15, step counts and both sporadic and continuous walking declined across groups. Moderate-to-highs PA increased modestly in FHR-SZ and FHR-BD but not in controls, with no significant group interactions. Higher PA and better motor functioning at age 11 were associated with higher PA at age 15. At age 15, higher PA was associated with better functioning in adolescents and caregivers.ConclusionOffspring at FHR-SZ and FHR-BD exhibited reduced PA at ages 11 and 15. Early assessment of PA in high-risk populations may be informative. Continued follow-up is needed to clarify the role of PA in relation to cardiovascular risk and transition to severe mental disorders.

Introduction

Individuals with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder are at substantially higher risk for comorbid somatic illnesses compared with the general population, resulting in considerable overlap between a range of somatic illnesses and severe mental disorders.1 Physical activity (PA) constitutes a key determinant of general health and well-being, and regular exercise exerts a broad spectrum of beneficial effects on the body, including the regulation of cardiorespiratory and cardiovascular fitness, as well as the improvement of glycemic control and insulin sensitivity. Moreover, exercise contributes to the maintenance of both physical and mental health throughout the lifespan. Notably, it serves as a non-pharmacological strategy to mitigate the impact of physiological and pathological neurodegeneration on brain health.2 Higher levels of PA and lower body mass index (BMI) have been associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, including heart failure, in population-based studies.3 In contrast, sedentary behavior—more prevalent among individuals with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder4-6—is associated with greater severity of depressive symptoms, prolonged illness duration, higher antipsychotic use, reduced quality of life, and increased cardiometabolic risk.6,7

Specifically, an elevated risk of comorbidity for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases in patients with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder has been repeatedly demonstrated.8-12 These factors are related to the 10-15-year reduction in life expectancy observed in these populations.13-15

Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder are highly heritable disorders.16,17 The most robust risk factor for developing either disorder is having a first- or second-degree relative with either disorder, and throughout their lifespan individuals born to parents with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder face increased risk of developing concordant psychiatric disorders, as well as other psychiatric disorders.18,19 Already at a young age, offspring of parents with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder encounter an increased risk for psychopathology as well as impairments in cognitive-, social-, and motor-functions.20-23

Results from The Danish High Risk and Resilience Study, a longitudinal cohort study of children born to parents with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder and controls support the findings on the abovementioned domains, eg, in psychopathology,24,25 neurocognitive function, social functioning,26-28 and motor function29,30 at both ages seven and 11. Additionally, accelerometric assessment of PA levels conducted at age 11, revealed significantly lower levels of overall PA in children at familial high risk of schizophrenia (FHR-SZ) or bipolar disorder (FHR-BD), who also engaged in nearly two hours less of moderate-to-high intensity PA per week compared with population-based controls (controls) Finally, according to their primary caregivers, children in the familial high-risk groups were less likely to participate in leisure time PA, a tendency caregivers attributed to reduced level of daily functioning in both their child and themselves.31

This study aimed to examine differences in levels of accelerometric measurement of PA among 15-year-old adolescents at FHR-SZ or FHR-BD, compared with controls, and to investigate development in PA from age 11 to 15.

Methods

Participants

This study is part of the second follow-up study of The Danish High Risk and Resilience Study—VIA 7. At baseline, participants (n = 522) were assessed at age seven.32 Follow-up assessments were conducted at age 11 (first follow-up),33 and age 15, (second follow-up).34 At recruitment, children were identified by linking data from the Danish Psychiatric Central Research Register and the Danish Civil Registration System. The Danish Psychiatric Central Research Register contains information on psychiatric diagnoses based on contacts with Danish mental health services, including both inpatient and outpatient care. The Danish Civil Registration System includes information on age, sex, address, parent–child relationships, and family structure for all individuals residing in Denmark. Linkage of these registers enabled identification of children aged seven born to parents with schizophrenia spectrum psychosis (ICD-10: F20, F22, F25; ICD-8: 295, 297, 298.29, 298.39, 298.89, 298.99), parents with bipolar disorder (ICD-10: F30, F31; ICD-8: 296.19, 296.39), and parents without these disorders. According to the ICD-10 diagnostic hierarchy, children with one parent diagnosed with schizophrenia spectrum psychosis and one parent with bipolar disorder were classified in the schizophrenia-spectrum high-risk group.

In total, nine children had two parents with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, and three children had two parents with bipolar disorder. Children with at least one parent diagnosed with schizophrenia spectrum psychosis were matched to control children on age, sex, and municipality. Parents of control children could have other psychiatric diagnoses but not schizophrenia spectrum psychosis or bipolar disorder.

The Danish High Risk and Resilience Studies were conducted in the periods of January 2013-March 2016, March 2017-June 2020, and June 2021-May 2024. Assessors were blinded towards FHR status. Written consents were obtained from the participants’ custody holder(s). The Danish Data Protection Agency and The Danish National Committee on Health Research Ethics approved the study protocol (Protocol numbers P-2019-273 and H-20067908).

Sample Characteristics Measures

At age 11, blinded, trained research assistants assessed motor function using the Movement Assessment Battery for Children–Second Edition, comprising three subdomains: manual dexterity, aiming and catching, and balance.35 For this study we used the total score where higher scores reflect better motor functioning.

At age 15, blinded research assistants applied The Children’s Global Assessment Scale36 (CGAS) to assess level of global daily function during the previous month. The CGAS score ranges from 0 to 100 with lower scores indicating poorer functioning. We measured the participant’s height and weight three times using the median of each to calculate BMI.

The primary caregiver is defined as the parent or foster parent that knows the child best and spends most time with the child. At age 15, the primary caregiver of the adolescent provided information about leisure time PA. This information was dichotomized into either “one or more” or “none”.

Social and personal functioning of the primary caregiver during the previous month was assessed by research assistants applying the semi structured interview Personal and Social Performance Scale37 (PSP). PSP is a single score on a scale from 1 to 100 with lower scores denoting poorer functioning.

Physical Activity Measures

At age 11 and age 15, we aimed to assess PA levels over a one-week period. To achieve this, accelerometry data were collected using SENS motion, a wireless medical device designed for large-scale PA monitoring.38 The SENS motion system consists of a small, waterproof sensor (weighing 7 g, measuring 45 × 4.5 × 23 mm) equipped with a tri-axial accelerometer. The device samples acceleration at 12 Hz within a range of ±4G and wirelessly transfers data via a smartphone application to the SENS motion secure cloud for storage and analysis. The sensor was embedded in a hypoallergenic adhesive bandage and attached to the skin approximately 10 cm above the lateral epicondyle of the knee, positioned on the lateral aspect of the thigh. Participants and their caregivers were provided with extra adhesive patches to ensure that the sensor remained attached. They were instructed to wear the sensor for seven days if possible. The inclusion criterion required a minimum of 24 hours of recorded data. Raw data recorded by the sensor were transmitted when the device was returned to a research assistant. A predefined algorithm, which integrates sensor orientation and recorded acceleration, was used to classify different activity types. Orientation was defined as the angle of the average acceleration signal over a 5-second interval.

The SENS motion system differentiates between periods of sleep, inactivity, and varying levels of PA. In this study, activity was categorized into six measures: step count, sporadic walking, continuous walking, moderate-to-high intensity activity (eg, running and cycling), inactivity (sedentary time with minimal or no registered movement) and sleep. For the time-based measures, duration was calculated as the total time spent in each category divided by the total monitoring time, yielding the average number of hours per 24-hour period.

It is important to note that SENS motion detects “steps count” with a higher sensitivity than standard step-counting devices (eg, smartphone-based measurements), making direct comparisons to other step-counting methods inappropriate.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Version 22.0, with a two-sided significance level set at P < .05 for all analyses. Between-group differences in continuous variables, including age at inclusion, CGAS scores, BMI, and PSP scores were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Post hoc pairwise comparisons using Least Significant Difference (LSD) test were only conducted when the overall between-group difference was statistically significant (P < .05) (Table 1). An identical approach was applied for analysis of amount of PA (Figure 1). Between-group differences in dichotomous variables, including sex and reported leisure time PA were analyzed using Pearson’s chi square test (Table 1). Linear mixed models were applied to examine group differences at age 15 and development of PA from age 11 to 15 (Table 2), including group × time interaction effects. These models account for the dependency of repeated measurements within individuals and for the clustering of siblings within families and allow for participants with varying numbers of observations. To investigate association between amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA, step count, BMI, reported leisure time PA, and motor function at age 11 and amount of high/moderate intensity PA and step count at age 15, we conducted separate linear regression models with age 11-variables as predictors. FHR status was included in all models as a covariate to adjust for main effects of group differences (Table 3). Separate linear regression models were performed to investigate associations between PA and reported leisure time PA, BMI, CGAS score and PSP score at age 15. BMI, reported leisure time PA, CGAS score, and PSP were used as predictive variables. We included FHR status in all models as a covariate to adjust for main effect of group differences (Table 4). To examine whether associations, reported in Tables 3 and 4, differed by FHR status, interaction terms between FHR group and each predictor were tested.

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 367 children underwent accelerometry and measurement of BMI at age 11 years, and 258 at age 15 years. At age 15, this cohort included 258 adolescents with the following group distribution: Adolescents at FHR-SZ; n = 91, or FHR-B; n = 58, and controls; n = 109. The mean age of all groups was 15.9 years. FHR-SZ and FHR-BP adolescents had significantly lower CGAS scores than controls. We did not observe significant between-group differences in either primary caregiver reported leisure time PA engagement or BMI. Personal and Social Performance (PSP) scores of primary caregivers of adolescents in the FHR-SZ and FHR-BD groups were significantly lower than those of controls (Table 1).

The complete sample of the Danish High Risk and Resilience Study—VIA 15 comprised 417 participants. No significant differences in CGAS scores were observed between the 258 participants who completed accelerometry at age 15 and the 159 participants who did not, across the FHR-SZ, FHR-BD, and control groups.

Physical Activity at Age 15

Average assessment-time was approximately one week (24 hours a day) and did not significantly differ between the three groups (FHR-SZ mean 6.8 days, SD 1.3, FHR-BD mean 6.7 days SD 1.1, and controls mean 6.7 days, SD 1.1, P = .577).

Step count was significantly lower in adolescents at FHR-SZ (mean 11 586, SD 5463) when compared to controls (mean 13 676, SD 5463, P = .003, Cohen’s d = 0.38) and in adolescents at FHR-BD (mean 11 975, SD 4356, P = .033, Cohen’s d = 0.43).

The amount of sporadic walking was significantly lower in FHR-SZ (mean 1.13, SD 0.50) compared with controls (mean 1.35, SD 0.48; P = .001; Cohen’s d = 0.45), but not in FHR-BD (mean 1.23, SD 0.49; P = .109; Cohen’s d = 0.25). Similarly, the amount of continuous walking was significantly lower in the FHR-SZ group (mean 1.58, SD 0.74) compared with controls (mean 1.83, SD 0.61; P = .009; Cohen’s d = 0.37), and also lower in the FHR-BD group (mean 1.60, SD 0.61; P = .036; Cohen’s d = 0.38).

We did not find group-differences in amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA (FHR-SZ mean .31, SD.28, FHR-BD mean .31, SD .26, and controls mean .38, SD .27, between-group difference P = .098) (Figure 1), neither in inactivity (FHR-SZ mean 10.1, SD 1.6, FHR-BD mean 10.4, SD 1.9 and controls: mean 9.8, SD 1.7, between-group difference P = .073).

Development of Physical Activity from Age 11 to 15

Across all groups, analyses of accelerometry data from age 11 to 15 (n = 420; FHR-SZ = 153, FHR-BD = 96, controls = 171) showed a significant decline in step count and walking time (including both sporadic and continuous walking) over time.

Within the FHR-SZ and FHR-BD groups, the models estimated a slight but significant increase in the mean amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA compared with their levels at age 11 whereas no significant difference in amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA in controls was observed (Table 2). Interaction analyses did not show differences across the three groups.

Age 11 Physical Activity, BMI, Reported Leisure Time Physical Activity, and Motor Function Associated with Age 15 Physical Activity

Our results indicated that both higher amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA and step count and higher score of motor function at age 11 were significantly associated with higher amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA and higher step count at age 15, and reported leisure time PA at age 11 was associated with higher amount of step count at age 15. BMI at age 11 was not associated with either moderate-to-high intensity PA or step count at age 15 (Table 3). Our interaction analyses did not show significant differences across the three groups.

Age 15 Associations between Physical Activity and BMI, Reported Leisure Time Physical Activity and Level of Daily Functioning

At age 15, reported leisure time PA was associated with both higher levels of moderate-to-high intensity PA and step count, and both participants’ and their primary caregivers’ level of daily functioning were associated with higher amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA and step count. BMI was not associated with neither amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA nor step count. (Table 4). Interaction analyses did not show significant differences across the groups.

Discussion

Main Findings

In this longitudinal cohort study, we found that 15-year-old adolescents at FHR-SZ or FHR-BD were less physically active than controls. The overall amount of PA significantly declined from age 11 to age 15, meaning that beside the category of moderate-to-high intensity PA, the amount of PA equally decreased from age 11 to 15 across all three groups. The amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA in adolescents at FHR-SZ and FHR-BD slightly increased from age 11 to 15. We did not find a group interaction effect in any of our analyses. Our analyses revealed that both higher levels of PA, higher scores of motor function and reported physical leisure activity engagement at age 11 were associated with higher PA levels at age 15. At age 15, higher levels of PA were associated with physical leisure activity engagement and higher daily functioning levels of both participants and their primary caregivers. We did not find any associations between BMI and PA.

Interpretation

According to the WHO guidelines on PA and sedentary behavior, children and adolescents 5-17 years old should engage in at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-high intensity PA daily, as regular participation in PA is associated with improved physical fitness, cardiometabolic health, bone strength, cognitive function, and mental well-being. Additional, evidence suggests a dose–response relationship, where greater engagement in PA yields greater health benefits.39 In our cohort, amount of moderate-to-high intensity PA was equivalent to 18-21 minutes per day, so quite low compared with the recommendations. We aimed to assess our participants over a one-week period outside school holidays, but this was not always possible. However, only step count was associated with physical leisure activity engagement, while performance of moderate-to-high intensity PA possibly was not affected by disruptions in the participants’ PA routines—such as physical leisure activity engagement—during holidays. This finding may be interpreted as a reflection of the association between higher caregiver functioning and higher PA levels in the adolescent. Caregivers with higher functional capacity may be more likely to promote PA and even actively engage in it together with the adolescent, both during holiday periods and in everyday contexts. This interpretation aligns with existing research demonstrating that parental support and role modeling are positively associated with youth PA levels; a meta-analysis found that positive parental influence, including support and encouragement, is significantly correlated with higher PA levels in children and adolescents.40 We found that also adolescent’s own level of daily function was highly associated with levels of PA. Although this finding was expected it underscores that low function in general (eg, problems related to school, peers, diagnostic symptoms/diagnosis, or family relations) is likely to also include lower levels of PA.

While insufficient PA in all participants is a cause of concern, it should be acknowledged that offspring at FHR-SZ or FHR-BD may be particularly vulnerable. In addition to their predisposition for severe mental illness, individuals with FHR-SZ or FHR-BD also face a heightened risk of several somatic illnesses14 including cardiovascular and metabolic diseases,9,10,41 both of which are associated with sedentary behavior.5 Schizophrenia appears to involve systemic dysregulation with adverse effects on cardiac health, suggesting overlapping biological mechanisms between schizophrenia and cardiovascular disease.42 This raises the possibility that offspring at FHR-SZ have a congenital predisposition to cardiac illnesses, potentially exacerbating the risks associated with sedentary behavior in this group. Offspring at FHR-SZ are at increased risk for not only schizophrenia but all severe mental illnesses including depression. Evidence show a bidirectional relationships between depression and infection/inflammation,43 and recent research links certain somatic diseases, caused by dysregulated immune response, such as inflammatory bowel disease to PTDS or depression, suggesting a shared etiology.44,45 PA is known to positively impact the immune system,46 and a former study from the current cohort showed that higher concentrations of the inflammatory marker of neutrophilocytes were associated with lower levels of PA at age 11, but only in children at FHR-SZ compared with population-based controls,47 indicating that the immune system of these children might be more vulnerable to low activity levels. These findings are supported by a large-scale study identifying genetic factors influencing cytokine levels in newborns, revealing specific genomic regulatory elements affecting levels of inflammatory markers.48

Our findings of PA levels at age 11 predicting PA levels at age 15 align with previous longitudinal studies on PA in childhood and its association with PA in adulthood. Although correlations were low to moderate a longitudinal study conducted over a period of 21 years showed, that high levels of PA at ages 9 and 18 significantly predicted a higher level of adult PA.49 Another prospective study examined PA habits in children 9-15 years old, and at follow-up 20 years later. The results revealed a modest yet significant correlation between childhood and adulthood PA levels.50 Contrary to our hypotheses of PA and BMI as predictive markers, our hypothesis that motor function may predict amount of PA has, to our knowledge, not previously been tested. Motor impairments in childhood have been linked to altered neurodevelopment and are recognized as early markers of psychosis,51 particularly in schizophrenia.52 It may be hypothesized that poorer motor function contributes to reduced motivation for engaging in PA, which in turn potentially reinforces impaired development of motor function. As such, motor function may play a role not only in predicting later psychiatric outcomes but also in shaping PA behaviors.

Individuals born to parents with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder are at heightened risk for developing severe mental disorders which furthermore increases their risk for somatic illness. Both mental—and somatic health conditions are linked to, and often worsen by lack of PA. Therefore, repeatedly reduced PA in offspring at FHR-SZ or FHR-BD during both childhood and adolescence is alarming, and it may be that low levels of PA in early childhood age should be considered as a possible risk marker of later transition to psychosis or bipolar disorder.

To our knowledge this study is the first to examine development including predictors of PA in adolescents at FHR-SZ or FHR-BD. PA represents a modifiable behavior. Identifying early life determinants of PA may contribute to targeted prevention efforts and advance our understanding of health-related trajectories in populations at FHR-SZ and FHR-BD.

Strengths and Limitations

The prospective nature of the study is a strength enabling the examination of the development of PA at two time points before the typical age of onset of severe mental illness. It is a strength that accelerometry directly measures. Using accelerometry assessment at age 11 and age 15 over a period of almost one week in participants within a narrow age range is also a strength. Including both offspring at FHR-SZ and FHR-BD enables examination of shared and distinct risk markers. Additionally, blinding of assessors and a multifaceted test battery is a strength. However, the study is limited by a smaller group of participants at FHR-BD than groups at FHR-SZ and controls. Another limitation is the low retention rate from age 11 (n = 367) to age 15 (n = 258).

Conclusions

Adolescents with FHR-SZ or FHR-BD at age 15 are less physically active than controls. The three groups exhibited decreased levels of PA from age 11 to age 15. Higher PA levels, higher score of motor function, and engagement in physical leisure activity at age 11 predicted higher levels of PA at age 15 in participants at FHR-SZ or FHR-BD and controls. Amount of PA in all three groups is concerningly low, but adolescents at FHR-SZ and FHR-BD may be especially vulnerable to inactivity due to their familial predisposition, which underscores the need for particular attention to providing support and easy access to PA for these groups. Offspring at FHR-SZ or FHR-BD are at heightened risk for psychiatric disorders already in childhood, and examining pathways of physical conditions and habits associated with mental health/illness in offspring at FHR-SZ and FHR-BD could identify potential risk and resilience markers for later development of severe mental illness.
