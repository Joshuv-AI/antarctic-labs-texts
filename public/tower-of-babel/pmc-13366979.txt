A quality assessment and systematic review of clinical practice guidelines on central venous catheter care in China and internationally
Authors: Junting Chi, Wangao Guan, Fei Chen, Li Kang, Tianyan Yu, Yi Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough there is currently a large number of guidelines, expert consensus statements, and declarations on central venous catheters (CVCs), the methodological rigor and reporting quality of these documents remain unclear.MethodsThe study design followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases, as well as guideline databases and websites specifically dedicated to infusion therapy, were comprehensively searched for articles and information on CPGs for CVC published from database inception until 2024. Excel was used for the extraction of basic information and recommendations for CVC care. Four researchers assessed the methodological and reporting quality of the CPGs via the AGREE II instrument and the RIGHT checklist. The review protocol has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024513864).ResultsThirty-seven guidelines and consensus statements regarding CVC placement were reviewed. The average scores for the AGREE II domains were 50.14% for scope and purpose, 36.84% for participants, 37.86% for rigor, 45.87% for clarity, 28.34% for application, and 47.11% for editorial independence. In terms of the RIGHT evaluation items, item 13a had the highest reporting rate at 100.00%, followed by items 1a at 97.00%, 1b at 95.00%, 4/5/6 at 92.00%, 2 at 89.00%, 7a/15/19a at 84.00%, 20 at 81.00%, 1c at 76.00%, and 9b at 73.00%, with items 12 and 17 both at 68.00% and item 16 at 65.00%. The remaining items had reporting rates less than 60%. Subgroup analysis revealed that guidelines generally scored higher than consensus statements did in all AGREE II domains (rigor, clarity, application, and independence). A comparison of the guidelines and consensus statements in terms of region indicated that the average scores for both were lower in Asia than in other regions, particularly in the areas of scope and purpose, participants, and clarity. In terms of publication year, guidelines and consensus statements published after 2017 scored higher in AGREE II clarity, as well as in the basic information and evidence dimensions of the RIGHT tool, than those published before 2017.ConclusionsThe quality of the guidelines varied, although five guidelines stood out as being of particularly high quality. These guidelines hold significant potential for practical application and could be modified to align with local regulations and protocols.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13521-1.

Background

Central venous catheters (CVCs) are specialized catheters that are inserted into major veins, such as the internal jugular, subclavian, or femoral veins, with their tips resting in either the superior or inferior vena cava. These catheters are critical for providing vascular access for patients in emergencies and those requiring intensive care, as well as individuals undergoing surgery who need efficient blood flow management. CVCs have become indispensable in a variety of medical procedures, including intravenous therapy, fluid resuscitation, monitoring of the hemodynamic status, and the administration of parenteral nutrition. Moreover, CVCs play a crucial role in the administration of parenteral nutritional therapy to patients who are unable to consume food orally or who require specialized nutritional support [1–2].

There are distinct variations in the effectiveness of different CVCs nursing methods. For example, improper care of a CVCs can lead to complications such as blockage of the catheter and catheter-related bloodstream infections [3]. Catheter blockage, in particular, is a relatively common complication, with an incidence ranging from 25 to 38% [4]. Professional organizations and relevant bodies have successively issued clinical practice guidelines (CPG) and expert consensus statements on CVCs care [5]; nevertheless, a gap persists between clinical practice and CPG recommendations. Multicenter observational studies have shown that adherence rates to guidelines for CVCs insertion and maintenance are below 90%, and vary according to hospital and provider characteristics [6]. This indicates that if implementation fidelity is low, existing CPG have limited capacity to improve practice.

Professional and standardized CVCs insertion and management can ensure catheter safety, enhance patient safety services, and reduce catheter-related complications [7]. During the indwelling period of CVCs, healthcare professionals refer to the recommendations of CPG and consensus statements to guide their clinical practice; however, if inappropriate recommendations are followed, they may interfere with clinicians’ evidence based decision-making processes and result in wasteful use of medical resources [5]. Therefore, when formulating CPG and consensus, full consideration should be given to rigor, scientific validity, and feasibility, so as to ensure that they can truly be translated into clinically applicable practical tools.

This study adopts an evidence-based approach to analyze the methodology and reporting quality of CPGs for central venous catheters, and explores the clinical applicability of different nursing methods in central venous catheter care, providing a reference basis for subsequent clinical research.

Materials and methods

The study followed the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement developed by Hutton et al. in 2015 [8]. This global review has been preregistered at PROSPERO under the registration number CRD42024513864.

Data sources and search strategy

A thorough search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science, as well as clinical resources such as BMJ Best Practice, Up To Date, and Chinese databases such as CNKI, VIP, WanFang Data, and CBM. The search strategy used a combination of subject headings and free words to identify all relevant studies on the topic of CVC CPGs from the earliest available articles to February 9th, 2024. In addition, guidelines databases such as the National Guidelines Clearinghouse (NGC), National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN), Guidelines International Network (GIN), New Zealand Guidelines Group (NZGG), World Health Organization (WHO), and Medaitong were also searched to ensure a comprehensive review of relevant guidelines on the topic. The combination of an exhaustive search strategy across multiple databases and clinical resources was used to provide a comprehensive overview of the current literature and guidelines on CVC care.

Study extraction and data assessment

The criteria used for the guidelines included (1) meeting the definition of a guideline as set by the Institute of Medicine in 1990 [9]; (2) being developed by a reputable national committee or medical society and offering recommendations for the care of CVCs; (3) being the most up-to-date version available; (4) including recommendations specifically related to CVC care; and (5) being published in either Chinese or English.

Two researchers conducted a thorough review of the literature and carefully selected relevant studies on the basis of the predefined criteria. In cases where there was disagreement, a third investigator was consulted to reach a consensus. The screening process began with reviewing the titles of the articles, followed by a more detailed examination of the abstracts and full texts. If necessary, additional information was sought from the authors of the original study through correspondence. Data extraction was performed by two team members via a standard template, which captured key information such as title, authors, publication year, country of origin, level of evidence, and methodological considerations.

Assessment of CPG quality

Four experts who had undergone systematic training in the use of the Appraisal of Guidelines Research and Evaluation (second version) (AGREE II) instrument evaluated the quality of the guidelines. The AGREE II tool consists of 23 entries across six domains, namely, scope and purpose, participants, rigor, clarity, applicability, and independence [10]. The guidelines were categorized as “strongly recommended” if they received overall scores exceeding 60%, “recommended with modifications” for scores between 30% and 60%, and “not recommended” for those with scores below 30% [11]. The overall assessment determined whether the guideline would be suitable for practical use and was classified into three categories, namely, recommended (R), recommended with modifications (RM), and not recommended (NR) [11].

Two independent reviewers conducted thorough evaluations of the CPGs via the Reporting Items for Practice Guidelines in Healthcare (RIGHT) checklist. This comprehensive checklist comprises 22 items that cover various aspects of guideline development and reporting. These items include basic information, background information, evidence evaluation, recommendations, review and quality assurance processes, funding sources, disclosure of conflicts of interest, and management, as well as general information [12]. The reviewers assessed each item on the RIGHT checklist as “Yes”, “No”, or “Partial” on the basis of the information provided in the guidelines.

Data synthesis and analysis

Statistical analysis

For every CPG analysed, the AGREE II score was determined for each domain as a percentage of the total achievable score and then standardized. Descriptive statistics, such as the mean and standard deviation (SD), were used to present the results. The quality of reporting was assessed by the number of items from the RIGHT checklist included in each CPG, as well as the frequency of reporting for individual checklist items. The methodological scores and reporting of checklist items for all CPGs included in the study were organized and analysed via Excel 2021.

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to evaluate the consistency among the raters in each category [13]. The ICC values were interpreted as follows: a score between 0.01 and 0.20 indicated slight agreement, whereas fair agreement was represented by scores of 0.21–0.40, moderate agreement by scores of 0.41–0.60, substantial agreement by scores of 0.61–0.80, and scores of 0.81–1.00 indicated very good agreement [14]. Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Mapping the quality

Following the calculation of the quality scores of the CPGs via the AGREE II tool, as well as the counting of reported items via the RIGHT checklist, the scores for the overall methodological assessment and reported item counts of the CPGs included in the analysis were organized and ranked via Microsoft Excel 2021.

Subgroup analysis

For further in-depth analysis of the potential factors contributing to the variability in the results, subgroup analyses based on the geographical location of the institution (categorized as Asian or non-Asian) and the year of publication (up to 2017, as the RIGHT guideline was released in 2017) were conducted. Significant differences were represented by P values less than 0.05. Subgroup analyses were performed to identify any patterns or trends in the data that could help explain the observed heterogeneity. Specifically, it was hoped that the comparison of outcomes between Asian and non-Asian institutions and examination of the differences over time would provide insights that could offer greater clarity on the factors influencing the results.

Strength of recommendation and level of evidence

The gap between evidence and treatment was identified by the extraction of information on the strength of the recommendation and the level of evidence. This analysis was conducted to determine the extent to which the current treatment practices aligned with the existing evidence. The evaluation of the strength of the recommendation and the level of evidence enabled the identification of potential discrepancies and inadequacies.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study adheres to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province (No: KHLL2023-KY131).

Results

Basic information

A comprehensive review of 4345 records was conducted, ultimately identifying 37 CPGs that met the eligibility criteria [7, 15–50] (Fig. 1 Summary of evidence search and selection). Among these CPGs, 1 focused specifically on statements related to CVCs, 15 concentrated on achieving consensus regarding CVC practices, and the remaining 21 provided general clinical practice guidelines that also included recommendations for CVC care. Among the 37 selected CPGs, 11 originated from China, while the rest were from various countries, including the United States of America, Italy, the United Kingdom, Germany, the Netherlands, India, Chile, Japan, Turkey, Spain, Singapore, and Sweden (Appendix S1).

Methodological quality

On the basis of the assessment outcomes of the two evaluation instruments, 37 CPGs were categorized into three tiers (Fig. 2). Among all the clinical practice guidelines, expert consensus statements, and declarations included in this study, five guidelines specifically developed by authoritative institutions were rated as highly recommended, as their overall AGREE II scores exceeded 60% [16, 23–24, 41–42]. In addition, 23 guidelines, expert consensus statements, and declarations were classified as being of moderate quality, with methodological quality scores ranging from 32.54 to 58.82. The remaining nine clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) were not recommended, as their overall scores were all below 30% [15, 18–19, 28–31, 49–50] (Appendix S2).

Reporting quality

Reporting rates of the evaluation items varied widely. The highest rate was observed for item 13a (100.00%), followed by 1a (97.00%) and 1b (95.00%). Items 4, 5, and 6 each had a rate of 92.00%, while item 2 was reported at 89.00%. Items 7a, 15, and 19a shared a rate of 84.00%, with 20 slightly lower at 81.00%. Reporting rates then declined to 76.00% (1c) and 73.00% (9b). Items 12 and 17 were reported at 68.00%; 16 at 65.00%; all remaining items had rates < 60.00% (see Fig. 2, Appendix S3 for full details).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis revealed significant differences between guidelines and consensus documents across four AGREE II domains-rigor, clarity, applicability, and editorial independence-with guidelines scoring higher on average in all these areas.

By region, guidelines and consensus from Asia had lower mean AGREE II scores than those from other regions in the domains of scope and purpose, stakeholder involvement, and clarity, suggesting geographic disparities in guideline quality and implementation practices.

Regarding publication year, documents published after 2017 attained higher average scores than pre-2017 publications in the AGREE II claritydomain and in two RIGHT dimensions (basic informationand evidence), indicating a trend toward improved quality and transparency in more recent guidelines.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive review of 37 guidelines and consensus statements related to CVC procedures. In terms of the participants, the guidelines and consensus included scored relatively low in the participant domain. Nearly three quarters of the guidelines and consensus had incomplete aspects in this regard, while only four of them scored highly. The reasons for this are as follows: Firstly, the majority of the guideline and consensus developers lacked comprehensive expertise, lacking experts from disciplines such as medical statistics, evidence-based medicine, and epidemiology. Secondly, in the process of writing the participant information, most of the consensus and guidelines did not clearly define the specific roles and divisions of researchers. Moreover, insufficient attention was paid to the opinions of the target population. Only four guidelines elaborated on the views of patients in detail.

In terms of the degree of rigor, the average standardized rigor score of the guidelines and consensus included in this study was approximately 39.57%. The relatively low score might be due to the fact that only four of the reviewed guidelines underwent peer review before publication [24, 42, 51]. Only three guidelines provided specific information on guideline updates [5, 49, 51]. Therefore, inviting external peer experts to participate in peer review during the guideline development process can identify potential biases and errors and address them before publication, thereby enhancing the rigor of these guidelines and consensus documents, ensuring their credibility and reliability. Additionally, all guidelines should include detailed information on subsequent updates to increase the transparency of the guideline information and provide a guide for relevant personnel to read the guidelines.

The guidelines and consensus statements often fail to recognize potential barriers that may hinder their implementation. While these documents typically emphasize the positive aspects of implementation and highlight best available evidence, they tend to devote insufficient attention to the possible obstacles encountered during real-world application. This lack of consideration makes it difficult for healthcare professionals to effectively implement the guidelines, thereby potentially compromising patient outcomes. Another issue is that many guidelines and consensus statements give inadequate consideration to the costs and resource implications associated with implementation. For instance, central venous catheters made from different materials are priced inconsistently, which can significantly affect patients’ medical expenses. However, such financial considerations are frequently overlooked in guideline development, thereby limiting feasibility in actual clinical settings. In addition, most guideline and consensus statements place strong emphasis on theoretical frameworks while neglecting practical application. This disconnect between theory and practice may impede successful translation of recommendations into clinical care, as healthcare professionals may find it challenging to convert theoretical suggestions into actionable steps. In summary, although guidelines and consensus statements play a pivotal role in standardizing medical practice and improving patient outcomes, they must take into account barriers to implementation, cost considerations, and practical factors. By incorporating these elements into their development, healthcare institutions can better ensure that guideline recommendations are successfully adopted in routine clinical practice.

The results of this study show that among the six domains assessed by the AGREE II instrument, guidelines achieved higher mean scores than consensus statements in four domains: rigor, clarity, applicability, and editorial independence. The RIGHT scores for guidelines were also higher than those for consensus statements and declarations. A likely explanation for these findings is that most guidelines are developed using evidence based medicine methodologies, incorporate studies of relatively high methodological quality with lower risk of bias, and follow standardized processes, thereby demonstrating greater rigor. In contrast, consensus statements are generally formulated through expert consultation (e.g., Delphi method or panel meetings) across relevant fields. Although expert experience plays an important role in consensus development, the process tends to be less rigorous compared with guideline development. With regard to transparency and independence, guidelines clearly outperform consensus statements and declarations. Guidelines routinely disclose their funding sources, conflicts of interest, and the methods used to formulate recommendations, thereby providing users with a clear understanding of the basis for the guidance. Overall, guidelines offer healthcare professionals a reliable and standardized framework for clinical decision‑making based on the best available evidence. Therefore, when making clinical decisions, healthcare professionals should prioritize the use of guidelines over consensus statements to ensure the delivery of the highest quality of care to patients.

Based on the findings of this study, the following recommendations are proposed: (1) When developing guidelines, experts from diverse disciplines should be invited to participate, including professionals in clinical medicine, evidence‑based medicine, disease prevention, medical statistics, health policy, and medical imaging. Detailed information about participating experts (e.g., name, profession, affiliated institution, nationality, city of residence, and specific contributions) should be provided. (2) The preferences and perspectives of the target population must be fully considered during guideline development. Methods such as questionnaires and qualitative interviews can be employed to gain an in‑depth understanding of the needs and expectations of the target group regarding guideline application. (3) Prior to publication, guidelines should undergo external peer review by experts who were not involved in their development, and the identities of the reviewing experts should be disclosed to the public. (4) The practical application of central venous catheter (CVC) guidelines in clinical settings should be taken into account, and factors that may hinder or facilitate their real‑world implementation should be identified and analyzed.

The present study has two main limitations. First, although the intraclass correlation coefficient was relatively high among the four reviewers, the potential for subjectivity in the evaluation results cannot be excluded. Second, this study included only publicly available domestic and international guidelines and consensus statements related to central venous catheters (CVCs), which may have introduced publication bias.

Conclusions

Overall, the average scores of foreign guidelines and consensus statements across all six domains of the AGREE II instrument were higher than those of domestic guidelines and consensus statements. Their RIGHT scores were also higher than those of domestic counterparts. It is recommended that relevant professional organizations develop high‑quality guidelines in accordance with the item requirements of AGREE II and RIGHT, ensuring rigor, clarity, applicability, and independence, while also taking into account real world clinical conditions and practicability, so as to standardize clinical practice.
