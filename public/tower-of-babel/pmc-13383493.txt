Childhood trauma and impulsive behaviors: the multiple chain mediating effects of neuroticism, stress perception and depression
Authors: Huihui Yang, Zhaoxia Liu, Zhengmiao Miao, Wanrong Peng, Ming Cheng, Kaili Zheng, Suyao Liu, Qihong Chen, Jinyao Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood trauma and impulsive behaviors are frequently observed in clinical pathologies, such as borderline personality disorder and bipolar disorder. This study aimed to investigate the intricate interplay between childhood trauma and impulsive behaviors, the mediating roles of neuroticism, stress perception, and depressive symptoms, and sought to discern the distinct effects of emotional and physical trauma on impulsive behaviors.MethodsA cohort of 637 clinical patients completed scales assessing childhood trauma, impulsive behaviors, neuroticism, perceived stress, and depressive symptoms. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted to investigate the relationships among these variables. Additionally, emotional and physical trauma were treated as independent variables in the SEM, respectively.ResultsChildhood trauma exhibited both direct and indirect paths to impulsive behaviors. The total indirect effect was 0.210, arising from three distinct indirect statistical pathways: (1) childhood trauma → depressive symptoms → impulsive behaviors; (2) childhood trauma → neuroticism → depressive symptoms → impulsive behaviors; (3) childhood trauma → neuroticism → perceived stress → depressive symptoms → impulsive behaviors. Emotional trauma and physical trauma both showed direct and indirect paths to impulsive behaviors. Specifically, emotional trauma had a direct effect of 0.210(47.19% of total effects) and an indirect effect of 0.235(52.81% of total effects), while physical trauma had a direct effect of 0.288(65.16% of total effects) and an indirect effect of 0.154(34.84% of total effects).ConclusionsChildhood trauma had associations with impulsive behaviors that were statistically mediated by neuroticism, stress perception, and depressive symptoms. Specifically, emotional trauma had both direct and indirect effects on impulsive behaviors, whereas physical trauma mainly had a direct effect.Keywords: Childhood trauma, Neuroticism, Depression, Impulsive behaviors, Structural equation modeling

Background

Impulsivity is a prominent feature observed in various psychological disorders, including borderline personality disorder (BPD), bipolar disorder (BD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and so on [1]. It is associated with a range of impulsive behaviors, such as self-injury and suicidal attempts [2, 3], aggressive and antisocial behaviors [4, 5], interpersonal violence [6], risky behaviors such as risky sex and safety-threatening behaviors [7], binge eating [8], substance abuse [9], and criminal acts [10]. Childhood trauma has been recognized as a significant factor contributing to impulsive behaviors in adulthood [11]. Individuals who have experienced childhood trauma tend to display impulsive behaviors, including aggression and self-harm [12]. Childhood neglect and abuse have been closely linked to impulsive behaviors in adulthood [13], including self-directed violence (such as self-harm and suicide) and interpersonal violence (such as fighting or destroying others’ property) [14]. Childhood abuse was also considered a crucial risk factor for mental health, with adolescents who experienced childhood abuse showing more impulsive behaviors, including antisocial behaviors, substance use, and suicidal tendencies [15]. Prior research has documented associations between traumatic experiences and behavioral problems, including substance addiction and internet addiction [16]. For adolescents with psychological abuse, higher rates of drug and alcohol use and increased frequency of antisocial behaviors have been observed [17].

Severe traumatic events during childhood could significantly impact personality development. Neuroticism is a personality trait marked by emotional reactivity, specifically the propensity for quick emotional arousal and delayed emotional recovery following stimulation [18]. A longitudinal study found that individuals who suffered life-threatening traumatic events during childhood or adolescence reported higher levels of neuroticism in adulthood [19]. There existed a positive correlation between childhood trauma experience and neuroticism [20, 21]. Individuals with childhood trauma tended to have significantly higher neuroticism [22]. Moreover, neuroticism has been identified as a mediator between childhood trauma and depressive symptoms [20, 23], suggesting that childhood trauma contributed to the development of neuroticism, which then increased the likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms. In college students, neuroticism has been shown to positively predict depression and self-harm behavior [24]. High levels of neuroticism have been linked to emotional instability, which can contribute to increased depressive emotions and heightened suicidal attempts in individuals with low emotional stability [25].

High level stress situations could exacerbate risk-taking tendencies, leading to more impulsive behaviors [26]. Individuals with high neuroticism were more susceptible to stressful life events, which increased the likelihood of depressive symptoms contributing to impulsive behaviors [24, 27]. Perceived stress refers to the extent to which an individual perceives life situations as stressful (i.e., stressors), including potentially stress-inducing events [28], which may heighten suicidal ideation through its association with increased depressive severity [29]. Furthermore, severe depression was identified as a risk factor for non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) behavior [30]. Depressive symptoms refer to a cluster of affective, cognitive, and somatic disturbances, including persistent sadness, loss of interest, guilt, concentration difficulties, sleep and appetite changes, and fatigue [31]. Previous research has shown that early traumatic experiences predicted hopelessness in patients with depression [32]. Multiple childhood traumas were associated with psychological distress, such as stress and depression [33, 34]. Additionally, depressive symptoms mediated the relationship between setbacks in basic psychological needs and NSSI in adolescents [35]. Substance users with PTSD had difficulties controlling impulsive behaviors under the circumstance of experiencing emotional pains [36]. Individuals with a history of self-injury reported more severe childhood abuse, worse emotional regulation, and higher levels of depressive symptoms [37].

In summary, childhood trauma, neuroticism, perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and impulsive behaviors, such as self-injury, are intricately intertwined. Childhood trauma might impact impulsive behaviors through neuroticism, stress perception, and depression. Additionally, different types of childhood trauma may play distinct roles in the relationship among these variables. Previous studies have suggested that emotional abuse and neglect in childhood may promote the development of affective disorders, such as bipolar disorder [38], major depressive disorder [39], and borderline personality disorder [40]. Conversely, physical abuse and neglect may be more closely related to impulsive behaviors [41]. For instance, some studies have found that physical abuse and neglect were risk factors for suicidal ideation [42] and self mutilation [43]. Women with a history of physical abuse have shown higher rates of self mutilation and substance use [44]. However, the specific mechanisms by which emotional and physical trauma affect impulsive behaviors through multiple factors are still not fully understood. Moreover, prior research on the relationships between childhood trauma and related variables has largely lacked an integrated model that simultaneously incorporates personality (neuroticism), emotional (perceived stress, depressive symptoms), and behavioral (impulsive behaviors) variables. Recognizing the empirical interconnections among these constructs, the present study builds upon existing findings to investigate how these variables collectively show associations with impulsive behaviors. The study hypothesizes that childhood trauma has a direct statistical association with impulsive behaviors, as well as indirect statistical associations via neuroticism, stress perception, and depressive symptoms. Also, different types of childhood trauma (abuse vs. neglect) are associated with impulsive behaviors through distinct statistical pathways.

Method

Participants

Participants were recruited from two outpatient psychology clinics in China between August 2016 and October 2021. Trained psychiatrists confirmed diagnoses using the diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders, fifth Edition (DSM-5). Eligible individuals were aged 14 years or older, had at least one DSM-5 psychiatric diagnosis, provided written informed consent, and demonstrated adequate comprehension of the study measures. Exclusion criteria included serious physical illness, brain injury, or age below 14. The final sample consisted of 637 clinical patients (234 males and 403 females) with age ranging from 14 to 54 (21.27 ± 7.02), and their diagnoses were major depressive disorder (219, 34.38%), anxiety disorder (105, 16.48%), bipolar disorder (88, 13.81%), personality disorders (83, 13.03%), schizophrenia in remission (75, 11.77%), and other psychiatric disorders (67, 10.52%).

Instruments

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ)

The CTQ was used to measure early life traumatic experiences. It consists of 28 items scaled from 1 to 5. Only 25 of the items contribute to the calculation of the CTQ total score, ranging from 25 to 125, and the three validity (minimization/denial) items are not included in any score calculations [45]. The CTQ has five subscales that examine five types of childhood trauma: emotional abuse, physical abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, and sexual abuse. Following standard practice [45], we created two composite variables: (1) emotional trauma, defined as the sum of the emotional-abuse and emotional-neglect subscale scores (total score ranges from 10 to 50); (2) physical trauma, defined as the sum of the physical-abuse and physical-neglect subscale scores (total score ranges from 10 to 50). The reliability and validity of the Chinese version of the CTQ are satisfactory [46].

Impulsive behaviors subscale of borderline symptom list (BSL-23)

The impulsive behaviors subscale of BSL-23 was utilized to investigate impulsive behaviors, which contains 11 items on a scale of 0–3, with a total score ranging from 0 to 33, where a higher total score indicates more impulsive behaviors [47]. The 11 items measured several types of impulsive behaviors including self-injury, suicide attempts, overeating, risky behaviors (such as over speeding, and running on roofs), substance use, aggression and uncontrolled sexual behaviors. The Chinese version BSL-23 has favorable psychometric properties [48], with the Cronbach’s α of 0.75 for the impulsive behaviors subscale in this study.

Neuroticism subscale of NEO Personality Inventory Revised (NEO-PI-R-N)

The neuroticism subscale of the NEO-PI-R was used to evaluate individuals’ emotional sensitivity, consisting of 48 items rated on a 0–4 scale, with a total score in the range of 0 to 192. The neuroticism subscale comprises six dimensions: Anxiety, Angry Hostility, Depression, Self-awareness, Impulsivity and Vulnerability. The Chinese version of the neuroticism subscale indicates adequate reliability and validity [49], with the Cronbach’s α of 0.94 in this study.

Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CES-D)

The CES-D was employed to measure the severity of depressive symptoms, and the Chinese version of CES-D has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties [50]. The CES-D comprises 20 items on a scale ranging from 1 to 4, with a total score ranging from 20 to 80. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the CES-D was 0.85.

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)

The PSS evaluates the subjective sense of stress level experienced in the recent month, consisting of 10 items rated on a scale of 0–4, with a total score ranging from 0 to 40. The higher the total score, the stronger the stress sensation. The Chinese version of the PSS shows sufficient reliability and validity [28], with a Cronbach’s α of 0.87 in this study.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 and AMOS 26.0. Pearson’s correlation was performed to examine the associations among scores of CTQ, NEO-PI-R-N, PSS, CES-D and impulsive behaviors subscale of BSL-23. To examine multicollinearity, Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) and tolerance indices were calculated for all observed variables. The VIFs ranged from 1.14 to 3.32, and the tolerance indices ranged from 0.30 to 0.87, both well below the commonly accepted thresholds [51], confirming the absence of collinearity. Afterwards, structural equation model (SEM) was established with childhood trauma as independent variable, impulsive behaviors as outcome variable, and neuroticism, stress perception, depression as intermediate variables. All variables were treated as latent variables in the structural equation model. For childhood trauma and neuroticism, subscale scores were used as observed indicators. While for unidimensional scales measuring stress perception, depression, and impulsive behaviors, item parcels were used to reduce model complexity and improve the ratio of sample size to estimated parameters. Specifically, all items of each scale were randomly assigned to two equal halves, and the sum of the scores of all items within each half was treated as a parcel indicator. It was considered acceptable model fit with CFI, TLI, GFI greater than 0.90 and RMSEA less than 0.08 [52]. The initial model was fully theory-driven, including all hypothesized paths derived from the literature. According to the results of SEM, insignificant pathways (p > 0.05) with weak theoretical support were removed to obtain a more concise model. Subsequently, structural equation modeling analysis was conducted to investigate the relationships among emotional trauma/physical trauma, neuroticism, stress perception, depression and impulsive behaviors, respectively. The sexual abuse subscale was excluded from the SEM analyses due to the low prevalence of reported sexual abuse among participants, and the weak correlations between sexual abuse and the other variables.

The data in this study were assumed to follow an approximate normal distribution, as most observed indicators exhibited absolute skewness less than 2 and kurtosis less than 7 [53]. The maximum likelihood method was employed when path analysis was performed. The significance of parameter estimation was determined using deviation-corrected nonparametric percentile Bootstrap. In particular, the 95% confidence interval was calculated by repeated sampling 5000 times, and a confidence interval not including 0 denoted a significant effect for the pathway [54].

In the optimal model, childhood trauma showed direct effect on impulsive behaviors, with a standardized regression weight of 0.220. Moreover, childhood trauma also displayed indirect effects on impulsive behaviors through three pathways (see Table 2). In general, the direct effect accounted for 51.16% of the total effect, whereas the indirect effects accounted for 48.84% of total effect.

Discussion

The present study investigated the associations among childhood trauma, neuroticism, perceived stress, depression and impulsive behaviors. The correlations among all variables were found to be moderate to high in this study, indicating close interrelationships among these variables. This study also performed SEM to explore the relationships among all these variables, and our results indicated that childhood trauma not only had a direct effect on impulsive behaviors, but also had indirect effects on impulsive behaviors through neuroticism, perceived stress and depression. Furthermore, different patterns of associations were observed for emotional trauma versus physical trauma. Emotional trauma showed both direct and indirect statistical paths to impulsive behaviors, with significant indirect paths via neuroticism, stress perception, and depressive symptoms. In contrast, physical trauma was primarily associated with impulsive behaviors through a direct path, although smaller indirect paths were also present.

First, the study revealed a direct statistical association between childhood trauma and impulsive behaviors, suggesting that the two variables covary in a linear fashion. Previous research found that individuals who suffered childhood traumas demonstrated poorer inhibition control [55]. Inhibition control, an important component of executive function, refers to the cognitive process through which individuals can suppress impulses and other dominant responses [56]. Therefore, the failure of inhibition control might predict subsequent impulsive behaviors [56, 57].

In addition, this study found that childhood trauma indirectly linked to impulsive behaviors through depressive symptoms. Previous research has revealed that outpatients with childhood trauma reported more depressive symptoms [58], and the negative reinforcement model indicated that impulsive behaviors served as a coping mechanism to alleviate negative emotions [59, 60]. When overwhelmed by negative emotions and experiencing distress, individuals might have difficulties controlling impulsive behaviors [36]. Given that individuals who experienced childhood abuse often exhibited various changes in emotion processing, interruptions in emotion processing could be a potential mechanism through which childhood trauma showed model-based associations with impulsive behaviors [61].

Moreover, this study found an indirect link between childhood trauma and impulsive behaviors, mediated statistically by neuroticism and depressive symptoms. Previous research suggested that childhood trauma might be a determining factor for neuroticism [11]. One study found that individuals with high levels of neuroticism who experienced childhood abuse were prone to experiencing negative emotions, such as depression, anxiety, guilt, and fear [22]. However, neuroticism did not directly lead to self-harm behavior, but rather indirectly affected self-harm behavior through depression [22]. Studies have shown that neuroticism played a significant mediating role in the relationship between childhood abuse and depressive symptoms [62]. Individuals who experienced childhood trauma might exhibit emotional instability leading to depressive symptoms in adulthood [63], which then directly contributed to the occurrence of impulsive behaviors, such as self-harm [22]. It should be noted that although neuroticism includes the trait of impulsivity, which is conceptually related to impulsive behaviors, impulsive behaviors were assessed as specific actions. Hence, the two constructs are empirically and operationally distinct.

Furthermore, the study found that childhood trauma was also associated with impulsive behaviors through neuroticism, stress perception, and depressive symptoms. According to the stress sensitivity theory, childhood abuse has been associated with increased sensitivity of the neuroendocrine system to stress, as well as heightened sensitivity to proximal stressors. These patterns have been linked to a lower threshold for stress-induced depression and, consequently, higher levels of depressive symptoms [64–66]. To cope with negative emotions, individuals may then engage in impulsive behaviors, such as attacking others and self-harming behaviors [67]. However, given the conceptual overlap among neuroticism, stress perception, and depressive symptoms, the interpretation of these indirect paths warrants caution, and they should be considered as statistical associations that require further longitudinal validation.

This study found that emotional trauma associated with impulsive behaviors through direct pathway and indirect pathways of neuroticism, stress perception and depression, while physical trauma primarily linked to impulsive behaviors through direct way. Notably, depression was a key factor in the relationship between emotional trauma and impulsive behaviors across all indirect pathways, indicating that depressive symptom played a crucial role. Previous studies have found that childhood emotional trauma was a particular predictor of depressive symptoms [68, 69], and early emotional trauma has been associated with depression in adulthood, even after controlling for other forms of childhood abuse and psychological disorders [70], while depressed emotion has been linked to subsequent impulsive behaviors [36]. Dye reported that those with childhood emotional trauma had higher scores on depression, perceived stress, and neuroticism [71]. Lee found that early emotional trauma could predict suicide behaviors in adulthood through depressive symptoms [72]. Therefore, combining previous findings, our results suggested that emotional trauma not only directly linked to impulsive behaviors, but also associated with impulsive behaviors through neuroticism, perceived stress, and depression.

The direct effects of physical trauma on impulsive behaviors have been proved by previous research [73]. One study found that physical trauma was significantly related to self-injury [74]. Violent crimes and externalizing problems were relatively common among individuals with a history of physical abuse or neglect [75]. Childhood physical trauma has been directly linked to criminal behaviors, such as property crimes, violent crimes, and fraud [73]. According to the social learning theory [76, 77], children exposed to physical trauma may learn violent behavior patterns during the traumatic experiences and may replicate these patterns in their interactions with others outside the family. Therefore, if the parent-child relationship is characterized by physical abuse and hostility, these patterns are likely to persist in the child’s relationships with others beyond the family. As a result, the increased hostility and aggressive behaviors exhibited by individuals with physical trauma may be a result of social learning.

Several methodological limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference regarding the relationships among childhood trauma, neuroticism, stress perception, depressive symptoms, and impulsive behaviors. It is critical to acknowledge that alternative causal orders remain equally plausible, and the directionality of the observed associations cannot be determined. Future research using longitudinal or experimental designs is needed to test causal hypotheses. Second, reliance on self-report measures may introduce several biases, including recall bias (particularly for childhood trauma experiences), social desirability bias, and shared method variance. Although we assured participants of confidentiality and used well-validated scales, these measures still depend on participants’ subjective retrospective reports. Future studies could benefit from incorporating multiple informants or objective measures. Third, the participants in the study had several types of psychiatric disorders, a broad age range and both sexs. The observed associations might differ across diagnostic groups, age cohorts, or sexes. Future research should recruit larger, more homogeneous samples or explicitly test moderation by diagnosis, age, and sex to determine whether the proposed model holds across these factors. Fourth, the composite measure used in this study aggregates potentially distinct behavioral domains of impulsivity, which may have different relationships with the predictors examined. Thus, aggregating these behaviors into a single score may obscure domain-specific associations. Studies using domain-specific measures are needed to determine whether the observed patterns generalize across all impulsive behaviors or are driven by specific subtypes.

Conclusion

Childhood trauma not only had a direct effect on impulsive behaviors, but also showed associations through indirect pathways, with neuroticism, stress perception, and depressive symptoms identified as significant mediators. Emotional trauma showed both direct and indirect effects on impulsive behaviors, while physical trauma primarily displayed direct effect on impulsive behaviors. Future research with longitudinal designs is essential to determine the temporal relationships among these variables and to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms.
