Parental preferences for immunization advisory clinic settings for children with special health care needs and associated factors in Shanghai: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Tianxing Feng, Yaqing Xu, Jingjing Li, Yanling Ge, Zhonglin Wang, Danni Zhao, Zhuoying Huang, Mei Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChildren with special health care needs (CSHCN) face persistent immunization inequity despite high overall childhood vaccination coverage. Immunization advisory clinics (IACs) have expanded in China, yet parental preferences for different IAC settings remain poorly understood. This cross-sectional survey was conducted at the IAC of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University, Shanghai, between October and December 2023. Parents of CSHCN younger than 14 y with incomplete National Immunization Program schedules were invited. The primary outcome was parental preference for IAC setting (community clinic, general hospital, or children’s hospital); the secondary outcome was preferred vaccination-assessment provider under discordant recommendations. Multinomial and binary logistic regression models identified associated factors, adjusted for child, parent, and vaccine-attitude covariates. Sensitivity analyses were stratified by medication status. Reporting followed the STROBE statement. Of 102 questionnaires, 101 were valid (99.0% response rate). 51.0% of parents preferred community clinics, 25.7% preferred children’s hospitals, and 23.3% preferred general hospitals. Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV) score was not independently associated with setting preference. Parents of girls were more likely to prefer general hospitals (adjusted RRR, 7.02; 95% CI, 1.50–32.77), whereas lower household income was associated with reduced preference for general hospitals (adjusted RRR, 0.14; 95% CI, 0.03–0.75). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent findings. Parental preferences for IAC settings among CSHCN families are shaped by child and socioeconomic characteristics rather than by vaccine hesitancy alone. Hybrid hospital-community delivery models and standardized referral pathways may improve vaccination equity for medically complex children.KEYWORDS: Vaccination, children with special health care needs, vaccine hesitancy, immunization advisory clinic, questionnaires

Introduction

In China, routine childhood vaccination coverage among generally healthy children has reached levels exceeding 99%.1,2 In contrast, vaccination coverage among children with special health care needs (CSHCN), including those with chronic illnesses, preterm birth, immunodeficiency, or other medical complexities, remains strikingly low, with reported rates of approximately 6%.3,4 This disparity reflects the disproportionate challenges faced by CSHCN families in navigating immunization decisions and services, particularly when standard vaccination schedules require individualized risk assessment and cross-institutional coordination.

Parental vaccine hesitancy among CSHCN families in China is shaped by factors that align broadly with the internationally recognized 3C model (confidence, complacency, and convenience),5 yet with distinct contextual characteristics. Unlike Western settings, where hesitancy often centers on confidence in vaccine safety or scientific evidence, hesitancy among Chinese parents of CSHCN more commonly arises from system-level gaps in the delivery of vaccine advisory information, including inconsistent clinical guidance, limited provider capacity, and fragmented referral pathways between specialty care and community vaccination services. These gaps generate uncertainty regarding whether, when, and where vaccination should occur, rather than outright rejection of vaccines.

To address these challenges, several provinces and cities in China have established immunization advisory clinics (IACs), which provide individualized vaccination assessments for CSHCN.6,7 IACs typically offer services such as evaluation of vaccination contraindications, development of catch-up immunization plans, risk assessment and premanagement of potential adverse events following immunization, and postvaccination follow-up. Based on publicly available reports, IACs are most commonly located in tertiary hospitals, although some cities have integrated IAC services into community health centers. Models of service delivery vary across regions. For example, Qingdao has implemented an integrated model in which vaccination assessment and administration occur at the same site,8 whereas Shanghai adopts a separated model in which CSHCN are referred by community or specialist physicians to hospital-based IACs for assessment, followed by vaccination at community clinics according to specialist recommendations.9

Hospital-based IACs have been shown to improve vaccination uptake among CSHCN, with studies reporting that 87.3% to 96.0% of children completed catch-up immunization following assessment.10,11 Nevertheless, vaccine hesitancy among parents of CSHCN remains sensitive to health system factors, including the perceived credibility of assessment providers, referral mechanisms, and the institutional setting in which IAC services are delivered.12 Despite the rapid expansion of IACs, little is known about parental preferences for different IAC settings or the factors that shape these preferences. Furthermore, unlike countries such as the United States, where vaccination of CSHCN is typically coordinated within pediatric primary care or specialty clinics,13,14 China relies on a distinct separation between vaccination assessment and administration, underscoring the importance of understanding parental preferences for IAC settings.

This study aims to examine parental preferences for immunization advisory clinic settings among families of CSHCN and to identify child-, parent-, and system-level factors associated with these preferences. By characterizing how trust, perceived safety, and access considerations influence healthcare-seeking behavior, this study seeks to inform the design of IAC delivery models and referral pathways that better align immunization services with parental needs and improve vaccination equity for CSHCN. Although this study is conducted within the Chinese health system, similar challenges in coordinating vaccination services for children with complex medical conditions have been reported in other settings where responsibilities for specialty care and vaccination delivery are fragmented. Understanding how families navigate these systems is therefore relevant to broader efforts to improve vaccination equity globally. In contrast to countries where childhood immunization for medically complex children is delivered within an integrated medical home, such as the United States, China operates a hierarchical model in which vaccination assessment and administration are institutionally separated, with CSHCN typically assessed at hospital-based IACs and then vaccinated in community clinics. Clarifying parental preferences within this referral structure is therefore directly relevant to health policy, informing how IAC services should be sited, how referral pathways between specialty and community care should be standardized, and how scarce pediatric vaccination expertise should be distributed to improve immunization equity.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey study was conducted at the IAC of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University in Shanghai, a national children’s medical center and one of the earliest institutions in China to establish such clinics. The study population included parents of children younger than 14 y old with special health care needs, defined as those with chronic illnesses (e.g., congenital heart disease, neurological disorders), preterm birth, immunodeficiency, or other medical complexities requiring individualized vaccination assessment. Children who had completed the full National Immunization Program (NIP) schedule were excluded. Eligibility was assessed at clinic registration; because recruitment relied on voluntary participation through a posted QR code, the number of attendees who had completed the NIP schedule and were therefore ineligible was not systematically recorded.

Participants were recruited between October and December 2023 using consecutive convenience sampling. Based on prior literature indicating that 79.7% of visitors to IACs in Shanghai were CSHCN, a minimum sample size of 98 was estimated using a single-proportion estimation formula with a two-sided α of 0.05 and a margin of error of 0.1 p. A total of 102 questionnaires were collected, of which 101 were valid (99.0% response rate); the single invalid questionnaire was excluded because of substantially incomplete responses. Parents were invited to participate via QR codes posted in the clinic. After clicking to confirm electronic informed consent, participants completed a self-administered digital questionnaire, requiring approximately 10–15 min.

Survey instrument

The questionnaire consisted of four concise sections (supplementary 1). The first section collected demographic information on the child (age, gender, type of special health conditions, disease status, and medication use), and the parent (relationship to the child, education level, household income, and maternal age at childbirth). The second section assessed the clinical context of the visit, including referral source (community clinic, pediatric specialist, or self-referral), preferred vaccination administration site (children’s hospital, general hospital, or community clinic), and primary motivations for vaccination decisions (e.g., safety, convenience). The third section examined influences on vaccination decision-making using 5-point Likert scales, including school requirements, recommendations from health care professionals, and media exposure. The fourth section incorporated the validated Chinese version of the Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines scale15 to assess vaccine hesitancy, including concerns about adverse events and trust in clinicians. The instrument was adapted from previously developed and validated tools, and the vaccine-hesitancy module used the validated Chinese version of the PACV scale; no separate pilot study was conducted, although the research team reviewed all items for clarity and contextual appropriateness before deployment. In the present study population, the PACV score showed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.79).

Outcome variables/covariates

The primary outcome was parental preference for IAC setting (community clinic, pediatric outpatient clinic in a general hospital, or children’s hospital). Covariates were selected based on prior literature and clinical relevance and included child characteristics (age, gender, type of special health conditions, disease status, medication use), parent characteristics (relationship to child, education, household income, maternal age at childbirth), vaccination-related factors (referral source, history of omitted vaccination, preferred vaccination administration site), and Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV) score as a continuous measure. The secondary outcome was preference for vaccination assessment provider when recommendations differed between physicians at IACs and specialists managing the child’s underlying condition.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics overall and by preferred IAC settings. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages and compared across preference groups using χ tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Child age was missing for 8 of 101 participants (7.9%). To maintain the full sample size for analysis, mean imputation was applied. Given the small proportion of missing data, any potential deflation of variance or introduction of bias is expected to be negligible. Child age was categorized into three groups and compared as well.

Multinomial logistic regression models were used to examine factors associated with IAC setting preference, with community clinics specified as the reference category. Crude relative risk ratios (RRRs) and 95% CIs were estimated from univariable models. Adjusted RRRs and 95% CIs were estimated from multivariable models that included prespecified covariates, including child age and sex, parental education and household income, child health status, vaccination-related decision factors, and PACV score as a continuous variable. For the secondary outcome, responses indicating preference for specialist physicians were coded as 1, and preference for IAC physicians as 0. No participants reported preferring recommendations from community clinic clinicians under discordant scenarios; therefore, analyses were restricted to a binary outcome. Because the community-clinician option was never selected, it contained no events and could not be retained as a separate outcome category; the secondary analysis therefore compared preference for specialist physicians vs. IAC physicians only. Univariate logistic regression models were first fitted to estimate crude odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs for factors associated with preference for following the specialist physician’s recommendation. Multivariable logistic regression models were then used to estimate adjusted ORs and 95% CIs, controlling for prespecified covariates aligned with adjusted models.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by stratifying the analyses according to whether the child was on or off medication to assess the robustness of the associations across clinically relevant subgroups.

All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P < .05. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for cross-sectional observational studies.

Ethics approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Shanghai Ethics Committee for Clinical Research (Approval No. SECCR/2023–99-01, Approval Date: October 31, 2023). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before questionnaire completion.

Results

Participant characteristics

Among 102 collected questionnaires, 101 were valid. The study population included 59 boys (58.4%) and 42 girls (41.6%), with a median child age of 3.0 y (IQR, 0.5–5.0). Most parents were mothers (72.3%) and married (95.0%). Over half of mothers were aged ≤ 35 y at childbirth (82.2%), and 63.4% of parents had an undergraduate education or lower. CSHCN diagnoses included chronic diseases (50.5%), preterm birth (13.9%), allergy history (15.8%), suspected or confirmed adverse events following immunization (AEFIs) (12.9%), and other conditions (6.9%). Referral sources included community clinics (51.5%), followed by self-referral (34.7%) and pediatric specialists (13.9%) (Table 1).

Main findings

Parents exhibited distinct preferences for IAC settings: 51.0% of parents preferred community clinics, 25.7% preferred children’s hospitals, and 23.3% preferred general hospitals. In univariable multinomial logistic regression, parents of girls were more likely than parents of boys to prefer general hospitals over community clinics (crude RRR, 4.14; 95% CI, 1.06–16.12), whereas no other factor was significantly associated with setting preference (Table 2). In multivariable multinomial logistic regression with community clinics as the reference category (Table 3), PACV score was not significantly associated with clinic preference. Each 1-point increase in PACV score was associated with a lower relative likelihood of preferring general hospitals over community clinics (RRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.86–1.12) and a higher relative likelihood of preferring children’s hospitals over community clinics (RRR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.97–1.28). Although these associations did not reach statistical significance, the point estimates suggest a tendency for more vaccine-hesitant parents to favor children’s hospitals rather than general hospitals relative to community clinics.

Child sex and household income were significantly associated with preferred IAC settings. Parents of girls were more likely to prefer general hospitals over community clinics (RRR, 7.02; 95% CI, 1.50–32.77), whereas the association for children’s hospitals was not statistically significant (RRR, 1.75; 95% CI, 0.69–4.41). Lower household income (≤10,000 RMB/month) was associated with a reduced likelihood of preferring general hospitals over community clinics (RRR, 0.14; 95% CI, 0.03–0.75), while the association for children’s hospitals was not statistically significant (RRR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.19–1.43). These associations were based on small numbers of events in some categories and were accompanied by wide confidence intervals; they should therefore be interpreted with caution as exploratory findings. We observed that, among parents of preterm infants, only 35.7% (5/14) preferred community clinics, with the remainder divided between general hospitals (28.6%, 4/14) and children’s hospitals (35.7%, 5/14) (Table 1). We note that prior to conducting the multinomial logistic regression, multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), all of which were below 1.3, indicating no problematic collinearity. To comprehensively evaluate model performance, multiple goodness-of-fit metrics were calculated for Model 3. The multinomial model 3 demonstrated a classification accuracy of 64% and suggested a meaningful effect size, with a Cox and Snell R2 of 0.31 and a Nagelkerke R2 of 0.35, both indicating adequate proportion of variance explained. Yet, the overall likelihood ratio test did not achieve statistical significance due to limited sample size (χ2 = 36.96, df = 28, P = .12). This lack of significance likely reflects a constraint in statistical power relative to the limited sample size, which is addressed further in the Discussion section.

Secondary analyses

When presented with discordant recommendations, 59.0% of parents preferred physicians at IAC, and 37.0% preferred specialist physicians. In adjusted analyses, younger child age was marginally associated with preference for specialist physicians (aOR, 0.87; 95% CI 0.73–1.01; P = .07). Parents of girls were significantly less likely than parents of boys to prefer the specialist physician’s recommendation (aOR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.13–0.84; P = .02). PACV score was not significantly associated with provider preference after adjustment (aOR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.95–1.25; P = .21) (Table 4).

Sensitivity analyses stratified by medication status yielded directions of association consistent with the primary findings, though statistical power was limited within certain strata (Supplementary Table 2). For instance, estimates within the subgroup of children receiving medication were unstable due to data sparsity. Because several of these stratified and adjusted estimates resulted in highly imprecise confidence intervals, they should be regarded as strictly exploratory and interpreted with caution.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of parents of CSHCN in Shanghai, we observed distinct preferences for IAC settings, shaped by child characteristics, socioeconomic factors of parents, and vaccine-related attitudes. Although community clinics were the most commonly preferred setting, substantial proportions of parents favored hospital-based IACs, underscoring heterogeneity in trust and perceived safety across families. These findings may also have relevance beyond China, particularly in health systems where vaccination services for medically complex children are distributed across multiple levels of care, requiring coordination between specialists and primary care providers.

Higher PACV scores demonstrated a non-significant trend toward community clinic preference, suggesting that vaccine hesitancy among CSHCN parents may not uniformly translate into preference for higher-level institutions. This pattern contrasts with findings from Western health systems, where specialized centers are often preferred for medically complex children,16,17 and may reflect China’s distinct separation between vaccination assessment and administration, as well as perceptions of accessibility and familiarity in community clinics.

Parents of girls and those from higher-income households were more likely to prefer hospital-based IACs, potentially reflecting heightened safety concerns and expectations of specialized expertise. In China, community vaccination clinics are frequently staffed by general practitioners with limited pediatric subspecialty training,18 which may amplify parental concerns regarding rare adverse events and erode trust in community-based care for CSHCN.19 The association between child sex and setting preference was unexpected and, given the small sample and wide confidence intervals, should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory; it warrants replication in larger studies.

Notably, preterm infants constituted a distinct subgroup warranting separate consideration. Although China’s National Immunization Program explicitly states that clinically stable preterm infants should be vaccinated according to chronological age following the same schedule as full-term infants, only 35.7% of parents of preterm infants in our sample preferred community clinics – substantially lower than the 51.0% observed overall – while the remaining parents were evenly split between general hospitals (28.6%) and children’s hospitals (35.7%). In the fully adjusted model, parents of preterm infants showed a notably elevated, albeit statistically non-significant, relative likelihood of preferring general hospitals over community clinics compared with parents of children with chronic diseases (RRR, 7.84; 95% CI, 0.73–84.60). This pattern suggests that the availability of clear national vaccination guidelines alone may be insufficient to reassure parents of preterm infants, who may perceive their children as medically vulnerable and thus seek clinical environments with greater perceived specialist oversight and emergency preparedness. These findings align with the broader observation that parental trust in community-based immunization services for CSHCN is modulated not only by vaccination policy clarity but also by the perceived complexity and fragility of the child’s health status.20 From a service delivery perspective, this underscores the need for targeted communication strategies and enhanced provider training at community clinics to address the specific concerns of preterm infant families, potentially supplemented by specialist-supported reassurance pathways that bridge hospital-based neonatal follow-up and routine community vaccination services.

These findings have direct implications for health system design and service delivery. First, pediatric hospital–led networks could extend specialist expertise into community settings through outreach models, teleconsultation, or rotating IAC services, thereby improving local capacity while maintaining clinical credibility. Second, establishing standardized referral pathways between subspecialists and community vaccination providers may help translate the trust built in specialty care into improved vaccination uptake.21,22 More broadly, these results highlight the importance of aligning workforce distribution, service integration, and trust-building mechanisms when designing vaccination programs for medically complex pediatric populations.23 Because this was a single-center, cross-sectional study conducted within Shanghai’s hierarchical, separated immunization model, these implications should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger, multi-center studies before informing policy.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The study was conducted at a single tertiary children’s hospital using consecutive convenience sampling, so the small sample may not represent the broader CSHCN population and is subject to selection bias toward families seeking specialist care. Although a strong classification accuracy and substantial pseudo-R2 values were observed, this study may have been underpowered to detect overall model significance in the multinomial logistic regression analysis. Meanwhile, many subgroup and adjusted estimates were affected by sparse data, yielding wide confidence intervals that render them exploratory, and self-reported responses may introduce social-desirability and recall bias. Residual confounding cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment, and the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Finally, because the study reflects Shanghai’s hierarchical, separated immunization model, the findings may not generalize to settings with different vaccination-delivery structures.

Conclusions

Children with special health care needs face persistent barriers to timely and appropriate vaccination, rooted in fragmented care delivery and heterogeneous parental trust. These findings indicate the importance of aligning immunization advisory clinic settings and referral pathways with parental preferences and system-level realities, revealing critical gaps in China’s current immunization advisory model. Hybrid delivery models integrating pediatric expertise into community settings and strengthening specialist-to-community referrals may improve vaccination equity and efficiency for CSHCN. Given the exploratory, single-center nature of this study, these conclusions should be confirmed in larger, multi-center investigations before broad implementation.
