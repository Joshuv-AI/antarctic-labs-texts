Label-free Raman spectroscopic imaging enables high-content molecular phenotyping of respiratory diseases from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid
Authors: Ying Zhou, Minhao Fan, Xiaoli Ge, Xiaoyu Zhao, Cunyin Yuan, Xinhui Gong, Yong Ji, Yubao Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional BALF analysis is limited by time-consuming labeling, poor multiplexing, and lack of spatial resolution—constraints that Raman imaging can overcome through label-free, multiplexed molecular mapping. However, its clinical application to BALF remains unexplored. We aimed to address this gap by establishing a Raman imaging platform for simultaneous biomarker detection across respiratory diseases.MethodsFresh BALF samples were collected from patients diagnosed with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (n = 6), lobar pneumonia (n = 6), asthma (n = 6), and asthmatic bronchitis (n = 6), along with healthy controls (n = 6), following standard protocols. An automated Raman imaging method was then developed to identify biomarkers in BALF using confocal Raman microscopy.ResultsOur method enables the simultaneous Raman spectroscopy imaging of nine biomarkers in BALF samples across different respiratory diseases, including glycogen, hyaluronic acid, four specific proteins, and three lipid types. An entire saturated lipid droplet in the BALF of·asthmatic bronchitis was observed and reconstructed. Based on the reconstructed Raman images of biomarkers, we achieved rapid and stain-free differentiation of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia, lobar pneumonia, asthma, and asthmatic bronchitis. Our method can complete Raman data acquisition and Raman imaging reconstruction within 10 minutes.ConclusionsRaman images highlighted the unique distribution characteristics of various biomarkers, serving as a novel and rapid method for BALF testing and respiratory diseases diagnosis.

Introduction

Respiratory diseases, including major conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [1], asthma [2, 3], pneumonia [4], tuberculosis [5], and lung cancer [6], pose a substantial global health challenge and impose a significant burden on public health systems [7–9]. These illnesses contribute considerably to worldwide mortality and are associated with especially high rates of morbidity and mortality in developing countries, where factors such as poor air quality, high rates of tobacco use, and limited healthcare resources further exacerbate their impact [10, 11]. The collection and analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) provide a unique and effective approach for diagnosing and monitoring these respiratory conditions [12–15]. By examining cellular components, proteins, pathogens, and other constituents within the lavage fluid, BALF analysis supports not only early diagnosis but also precise pathogen identification, monitoring of inflammatory and immune responses, and evaluation of therapeutic efficacy, thereby playing an indispensable role in both clinical practice and research [16, 17].

However, differentiating these biological components in clinical practice remains challenging, particularly in cases with atypical presentations or mixed etiologies, such as viral-bacterial co-infections. Many respiratory diseases share overlapping clinical symptoms, complicating diagnosis based solely on BALF analysis. Moreover, the complex composition of BALF—including diverse immune cells, proteins, pathogens, and inflammatory markers—makes interpretation challenging.

Conventional BALF assays—including fluorescence staining, PCR, and immunoassays—remain widely used but are inherently label-dependent, time-consuming, and limited in multiplexing capacity [18, 19], often failing to provide the comprehensive molecular picture needed for rapid differential diagnosis. Collectively, the overlap in clinical symptoms, the occurrence of atypical presentations and co-infections, and the inherent constraints of conventional diagnostic approaches make it challenging for clinicians to accurately distinguish among respiratory diseases using standard BALF analysis [20–23]. Although a wide range of staining reagents is available for labeling specific proteins and nucleic acids, the simultaneous visualization of other biomolecular classes—such as carbohydrates, lipids, and metabolites—remains technically challenging [24]. Labeling-based methods are also constrained by the “color barrier,” which limits the number of targets that can be analyzed simultaneously within a single sample. For instance, conventional confocal microscopy typically allows visualization of a maximum of four targets per slide, whereas Raman imaging can resolve nine or more targets concurrently. In situ quantification of biomolecules is further hampered by reliance on indirect, relative measurements such as mean fluorescence intensity [25]. These limitations collectively impede the speed and precision of BALF-based diagnostics, highlighting the need for advanced, high-content, and high-resolution imaging tools that enable comprehensive, multifactorial characterization.

Confocal Raman spectroscopy imaging (RSI) offers several distinctive advantages that address the limitations of conventional BALF diagnostics. As a label-free technique, RSI probes intrinsic molecular vibrations, eliminating the need for antibodies, fluorescent dyes, or nucleic acid probes [26–29]. It enables simultaneous detection of multiple biomolecular classes—including proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids—in a single acquisition, overcoming the “color barrier” of fluorescence-based methods. Moreover, RSI preserves spatial information, allowing visualization of biomarker distributions across cells and extracellular structures, which is critical for interpreting the pathophysiological context of BALF samples. The technique is non-destructive and requires minimal sample preparation, making it potentially suitable for point-of-care applications.

However, Raman imaging also faces certain challenges that have limited its clinical translation. The inherently weak Raman scattering cross-section requires relatively long acquisition times or higher laser powers to achieve adequate signal-to-noise ratios, though this can be mitigated by optimized instrumentation and automated workflows. Spectral overlap among structurally similar biomolecules necessitates computational unmixing algorithms and well-curated reference libraries. Additionally, the high cost of instrumentation and the need for specialized expertise in spectral interpretation have hindered widespread clinical adoption. These challenges motivated our development of an automated Raman imaging workflow with integrated machine learning classification, aimed at reducing technical barriers and accelerating clinical translation.

In this study, we introduce a novel platform for rapid Raman imaging and classification of respiratory diseases using confocal RSI. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically apply automated Raman imaging to clinical BALF samples for the simultaneous differentiation of five respiratory disease categories (including healthy controls), achieving label-free detection and spatial mapping of nine biomarkers within a clinically compatible timeframe (≤10 minutes).

Methods

Study population and BALF processing

BALF samples were collected following standard clinical protocols. Briefly, sterile normal saline was instilled into the alveolar cavity through a flexible bronchoscope and subsequently aspirated via negative pressure suction. The collected fluid was centrifuged to separate the cellular pellet from the supernatant. Smears for Raman spectroscopic analysis were prepared from the cellular fraction.

This study included 30 BALF samples from patients diagnosed with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (n = 6; 4 female, 2 male; mean age 8.75 ± 2.68 years), lobar pneumonia (n = 6; 3 female, 3 male; mean age 7.33 ± 3.54 years), asthma (n = 6; 2 female, 4 male; mean age 6.53 ± 1.03 years), or Asthmatic bronchitis (n = 6; 4 female, 2 male; mean age 9.42 ± 2.81 years), alongside Healthy controls (n = 6; 2 female, 4 male; mean age 5.34 ± 1.83 years). All asthma patients were in acute exacerbation at the time of BALF collection, with a mean disease duration of 10.0 ± 2.6 months and a mean treatment duration of 9.6 ± 2.6 months; all were receiving regular inhaled corticosteroids (ICS; budesonide or fluticasone propionate) as maintenance therapy, with dosages adjusted according to age and disease severity per guidelines, and short-acting β₂-agonists were used on demand. None of the asthma patients had received systemic corticosteroids prior to BALF sampling. All asthmatic bronchitis patients were in the acute phase at the time of BALF collection, with a mean symptom duration of 5.7 ± 1.4 days, and none had received regular controller therapy prior to sampling. Healthy controls were defined as children with no history of respiratory symptoms, normal chest imaging, and negative results for routine respiratory pathogen screening, from whom BALF was obtained during clinically indicated procedures (e.g., elective non-pulmonary surgery) under institutional ethical approval. All diagnoses were confirmed according to established Chinese clinical criteria [29–31]. Given the proof-of-concept nature of this study, a formal a priori power analysis was not performed. However, post-hoc power analysis based on the observed effect sizes for the key between-group comparisons (e.g., the 2.4-fold difference in the 772 cm−1 peak between Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia and healthy controls) revealed large effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 1.2), with statistical power exceeding 80% at α = 0.05 for these comparisons. With n = 6 per group, the study was adequately powered only to detect large effect sizes and underpowered for moderate or small effects; the significant differences reported herein thus reflect large biological effects, while smaller but potentially relevant differences may not have been detected. These findings provide a basis for effect size estimation in future larger-scale validation studies.

Raman spectral acquisition and instrumentation

Raman measurements were performed using a confocal Raman microscopy system (BioSV, SuperVision Medicine Co., Ltd.). The system was equipped with an infinity-corrected objective lens featuring a high numerical aperture (NA) for excitation focusing, a 532 nm diode-pumped solid-state laser as the monochromatic excitation source, and a confocal pinhole module to improve spatial resolution by rejecting out-of-focus light. Raman signals were collected using a holographic imaging spectrometer coupled to a CCD camera. A high-throughput holographic transmission grating enabled rapid spectral dispersion across a broad wavenumber range. Sample scanning was performed via a piezo-actuated nano-positioning stage for precise raster motion. All spectra were acquired with a laser power of 32 mW, an integration time of 0.3 seconds per point, and an xy spatial resolution of 1–3 μm, covering a wavenumber range of 0–3600 cm−1.

Spectral processing and biomarker quantification

Raw Raman spectra were truncated to the spectral range of 400–3100 cm−1, encompassing both the fingerprint and C–H stretching regions for quantitative deconvolution in MATLAB. An automated preprocessing and analysis pipeline, implemented via a single command developed by SuperVision Medicine Co., Ltd., facilitated reproducible analysis without requiring specialized Raman expertise. Preprocessing steps included fluorescence background removal using an iterative polynomial fitting algorithm, cosmic ray spike removal, and intensity normalization to the total spectral area. After preprocessing, spectral unmixing was conducted using a non-negative least squares (NNLS) algorithm. The NNLS regression coefficients, representing the relative contribution of each reference spectrum to the mixed Raman signal at each voxel, were used to generate semi-quantitative spatial distribution maps of each biomolecular constituent. These coefficients were then normalized to the total spectral intensity at each voxel to account for intensity variations, providing relative abundance maps that reflect the proportional distribution of each biomarker across the imaged region. All reference spectra used in the NNLS unmixing were experimentally acquired from pure standard compounds (collagen, elastin, fibronectin, glycogen, hyaluronan, laminin, and three lipid standards) under the same instrument settings used for BALF sample measurements. These standards were selected from our comprehensive in-house spectral reference library containing over 470 pure substances spanning diverse biomolecular categories.

Data dimensionality reduction for disease classification

Due to the high dimensionality (each spectrum containing 1,024 wavenumber points) and inherent redundancy of the spectral data, we implemented a two-step dimensionality reduction approach. For each BALF sample, Raman spectra were acquired from multiple regions of interest, yielding approximately 5,000 individual single-point spectra per sample, for a total of 150,000 spectra across all 30 samples (30 samples × 5,000 = 150,000). The 1,024 spatial points described in the Results refer to the number of imaging pixels per reconstructed map, not the total number of spectra acquired per sample. First, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to compress the spectral data into a reduced set of principal components. Specifically, 30 or 60 principal components were retained as the input for subsequent analysis. The choice of 60 principal components was based on the cumulative explained variance threshold (>95%), while 30 was selected to evaluate the effect of more aggressive dimensionality reduction. Subsequently, t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) was applied to the PCA-reduced spectral data from all five BALF groups, generating a two-dimensional representation (t-SNE1 and t-SNE2) for each sample.

Spectral unmixing was then performed using a reference library containing unit-scale Raman spectra of the target biomarkers. A residual matrix was incorporated to account for components not represented in the library. This procedure generated a regression coefficient matrix for each biomarker, reflecting its absolute concentration at every voxel. Based on the spatial imaging dimensions, a two-dimensional map was reconstructed to visualize the distribution of each biomarker. The entire process—from spectral acquisition to image reconstruction—was completed within 10 minutes per BALF sample.

As demonstrated in Fig. 1C, this label-free method supports simultaneous imaging of multiple biomarkers, substantially reducing both the analytical time and the required BALF volume. Notably, a saturated lipid droplet was clearly visualized in a single-channel Raman image from a patient with asthmatic bronchitis (Fig. 1C). Such lipid droplets are generally absent in BALF from healthy individuals. The ability to rapidly and non-invasively detect lipid droplets via Raman spectroscopic imaging offers a valuable diagnostic advantage, supporting earlier and more precise clinical decision-making.

Disease-associated spectral features and biomarker identification

Distinct Raman peaks were identified across disease groups (Fig. 2A). In Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia samples, peaks at 772 cm−1 (phosphate groups) and 1018 cm−1 (aromatic amino acids) showed intensity increases of 2.4-fold (p < 0.001) and 1.8-fold (p = 0.002) compared to healthy controls, respectively. Raman peaks at specific wavenumbers correspond to characteristic vibrational modes of chemical bonds, serving as indicators of molecular structure and composition. In this study, we focused on nine key Raman peaks. The first notable peak, at 772 cm−1, is attributed to symmetric stretching of phosphate (PO₄) or sulfate (SO₄) groups. Phosphate groups likely originate from cellular debris or immune cell secretions, given their role in nucleotide structures, while sulfate groups may arise from sulfated proteins and peptides involved in immune activity or tissue repair, or from inorganic sulfate ions produced during inflammation or metabolism. This peak was absent only in Group E (healthy controls), suggesting its potential utility as a discriminant biomarker for respiratory disease.

The second peak, at 1018 cm−1, is associated with vibrational modes of aromatic amino acids such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan [32]. These compounds may be released from immune cells, lung tissue, or inflammatory secretions. Additionally, lipid-derived metabolites formed under oxidative stress or inflammation—such as leukotrienes—or inhaled pollutants like polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons may contribute to this signal. The 1018 cm−1 peak was present in all disease groups (A–D) but absent in healthy controls, supporting its diagnostic relevance.

The fourth peak, at 1277 cm−1, corresponds to C–N stretching and N–H bending vibrations, characteristic of the amide III band in proteins. This band is sensitive to protein secondary structure (e.g., α-helices, β-sheets), and its presence in groups B, C, and D suggests the accumulation of specific proteins—such as immune-related proteins, enzymes, or cellular debris—in these samples.

The eighth peak, at 1670 cm−1, reflects C = O stretching vibrations typical of carbonyl groups in aldehydes, ketones, or esters. Such groups may result from lipid peroxidation—a process driven by reactive oxygen species (ROS) that degrades polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs)—or from inhaled environmental pollutants such as tobacco smoke or industrial emissions. This peak was detected in all disease groups but not in healthy controls, indicating its association with pathological or environmental stress.

The third, fifth, sixth, seventh, and ninth Raman peaks—at 1115 cm−1, 1322 cm−1, 1460 cm−1, 1607 cm−1, and 2864 cm−1—were observed across all five BALF groups, reflecting common biological constituents. The 1115 cm−1 peak arises from C–O stretching in ethers and esters, commonly found in lipids and carbohydrates. The 1322 cm−1 peak may result from CH₂/CH₃ twisting and bending vibrations in lipids such as phospholipids or fatty acids. The 1460 cm−1 peak is assigned to CH₂ and CH₃ bending modes, abundant in phospholipids from lung surfactant or cellular membranes. The 1607 cm−1 peak suggests the presence of proteins with aromatic side chains, including phenylalanine, tyrosine, or tryptophan. Lastly, the 2864 cm−1 peak is attributed to symmetric CH₂ stretching in long-chain hydrocarbons, serving as a strong indicator of lipid content.

Spatial mapping of disease-specific biomarker distributions

Conventional diagnosis relying solely on Raman spectral features is often non-intuitive and prone to interpretive variability. To facilitate more direct and rapid disease identification, we developed a high-resolution Raman imaging approach for visualizing key biomarkers in BALF. Using this method, we spatially mapped nine biomarkers across five diagnostic categories: collagen, elastin, fibronectin, glycogen, hyaluronan, laminin, monounsaturated lipids, saturated lipids, and unsaturated lipids.

Reference Raman spectra for each biomarker were obtained from pure standards. BALF samples from Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia patients exhibited elevated levels of saturated lipids and collagen, consistent with the prominent lipid-related peak at 2864 cm−1. The increase in saturated lipids may reflect host cell membrane damage resulting from Mycoplasma infection. As a cell-wall-less pathogen, Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia engages in intimate membrane interactions with host cells, potentially causing direct injury to lung cell membranes and the release of membrane lipids into BALF—a cellular response to infection that aligns with clinical observations of enhanced mucus secretion in these samples.

Glycogen content was markedly higher in lobar pneumonia BALF samples compared to other disease types. This may be attributed to the nature of lobar pneumonia as a bacterial infection, typically caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, wherein bacterial proliferation and heightened metabolic activity of host immune cells increase glycogen demand or accumulation. These characteristics support the use of glycogen as a discriminatory biomarker for lobar pneumonia and are consistent with the characteristic Raman peak of glycogen at ~480 cm−1 (C1–O–C4 stretching and ring breathing), along with a relatively weaker lipid signal at 2864 cm−1.

Notably, all four respiratory disease groups showed elevated collagen levels compared to healthy controls, which may reflect a common tissue remodeling and fibrotic response to infection and inflammation, though histopathological validation would be required to confirm this interpretation. However, the concentrations of laminin, elastin, and fibronectin varied distinctly among disease types. Lobar pneumonia samples displayed the highest laminin content, likely due to rapid tissue and basement membrane repair following extensive alveolar damage. Asthma samples were characterized by elevated elastin, which may represent an adaptive response to chronic airway constriction and dilation, helping restore airway elasticity and function. In asthmatic bronchitis, higher fibronectin levels were observed, suggesting active involvement in airway remodeling—a process involving bronchial wall thickening, smooth muscle proliferation, and epithelial repair.

These biomarker distribution patterns, visually summarized in the Raman images, provide an intuitive basis for distinguishing clinically overlapping respiratory diseases, thereby aiding in rapid diagnostic decision-making.

In control samples from healthy individuals, all nine biomarkers were present at low levels. Among them, hyaluronan, monounsaturated lipids, and unsaturated lipids showed consistently low expression across all disease groups, suggesting limited utility as primary diagnostic biomarkers in BALF.

Machine learning-based classification of respiratory diseases

The interpretation of complex Raman spectra or large datasets poses significant clinical challenges due to time constraints and subjective variability. To address these limitations, we developed an automated machine learning pipeline integrating Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)-enhanced ensemble stacking for high-accuracy respiratory disease classification. This approach projects the high-dimensional average Raman spectra of BALF samples into a two-dimensional space, facilitating intuitive visualization. A key step involves quantifying similarity between high-dimensional data points using standardized Euclidean distance, which ensures balanced weighting of all spectral features and underlies the dimensionality reduction and visualization process. This method preserves the local structure of the data in the low-dimensional embedding, maintaining proximity among similar samples while increasing separation between dissimilar ones.

The plot reveals clear distinctions among the Raman datasets corresponding to each respiratory condition. Notably, some overlap is observed between the Mycoplasma pneumoniae and healthy control clusters, suggesting shared spectral characteristics in certain high-dimensional features. This may be attributable to the elevated and heterogeneously distributed saturated lipid content in M. pneumoniae samples, which can introduce substantial intra-class spectral variation during data acquisition. As shown in Fig. 4B, setting the principal components to 60 resulted in clear segregation of all clusters; however, this also revealed two to three subclusters within each group. These substructures may reflect intrinsic diversity among samples of the same category, potentially arising from the multi-peak nature of Raman spectra, which—when compressed into a reduced space—can highlight subtle spectral subdivisions. This phenomenon aligns with the varied biomarker compositions observed across individual BALF samples. The low-dimensional representations generated by t-SNE further facilitate downstream pattern recognition and machine learning tasks, enabling rapid and robust classification of BALF types using distance-based algorithms such as k-nearest neighbors.

Discussion

This research introduces and rigorously validates an automated Raman imaging system for label-free, high-throughput analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). This platform was designed to address the fundamental limitations of conventional BALF diagnostics identified in our introduction—namely, their dependence on labeling, inability to capture spatial distributions, and limited multiplexing capacity. The platform enables simultaneous detection and spatial localization of nine critical biomarkers, which, when integrated with a machine learning classifier, exhibit strong diagnostic performance in distinguishing multiple major respiratory pathologies. By merging molecular spectroscopic resolution with clinical demands for rapid, accurate pathology assessment, this work establishes a novel diagnostic framework for respiratory diseases.

This study presents several distinct novelties. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic application of automated Raman imaging to clinical BALF samples for simultaneous differentiation of four respiratory disease categories and healthy controls, moving beyond single-disease or cell-line studies. Second, we achieved unprecedented multiplexing by simultaneously imaging nine structurally diverse biomarkers—including extracellular matrix proteins (collagen, elastin, fibronectin, laminin), a carbohydrate (glycogen), a glycosaminoglycan (hyaluronan), and three lipid classes—in a single, label-free acquisition, overcoming the conventional “color barrier” that limits fluorescence-based methods to four targets per slide. Third, we established an end-to-end automated workflow integrating spectral acquisition, image reconstruction, and machine learning-assisted classification, eliminating the need for specialized spectral interpretation and completing the entire analytical process within 10 minutes per sample. These innovations collectively position our platform as a practical, high-content diagnostic tool with direct clinical translational potential. While the individual techniques employed-Raman spectroscopy, spectral unmixing, and machine learning-are established in their respective domains, the novelty of our platform lies in their integrated, end-to-end application to a specific clinical problem that has not been previously addressed: the rapid, label-free, multiplexed molecular phenotyping of clinical BALF samples for multi-disease respiratory diagnostics. The integration itself, tailored to this clinical context and validated on patient-derived samples, constitutes the core novelty of this work.

The most striking advantage of our Raman imaging approach is its ability to reveal biomolecular information and spatial distributions that are inaccessible to conventional diagnostic methods. The visualization and reconstruction of a complete saturated lipid droplet in the BALF from a patient with asthmatic bronchitis (Fig. 1C) is not merely a distinct imaging feature but may point to specific pathophysiological processes, such as severe inflammation-induced cellular membrane damage or aberrant lipid metabolism, which have been implicated in other obstructive lung diseases [33, 34]. This finding underscores the power of Raman imaging for discovering novel, non-invasive biomarkers. The analysis of average Raman spectra (Fig. 2) further identified a set of disease-associated spectral fingerprints. For instance, the absence of the 772 cm−1 and 1018 cm−1 peaks exclusively in the healthy control group suggests their origin in disease-related immune cell secretions, tissue damage products, or environmental exposures, consistent with known inflammatory processes that alter the BALF proteome and metabolome [35–37].

The consistent presence of the 1670 cm−1 peak across all disease groups likely indicates Amide I (C = O stretching) of proteins, C = C stretching vibrations of lipids, or carbonyl stretches from aldehydes/ketones associated with oxidative stress [38], a common hallmark of inflammatory lung conditions well-documented in conditions like asthma and COPD [39–41]. We acknowledge that this peak, together with the 772 cm−1 band assigned to phosphate/sulfate groups, reflects general inflammatory and immune cell activity rather than being uniquely specific to any single respiratory disease. Accordingly, the diagnostic value of our platform does not hinge on any individual “specific” peak; instead, it relies on the composite molecular fingerprint—the combined spatial distribution and relative intensities of multiple biomarkers—that collectively distinguishes each disease state. Nevertheless, we recognize that patients with non-respiratory inflammatory conditions (e.g., systemic inflammatory disorders or hematologic malignancies) may exhibit secondary alterations in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid composition, which could partially overlap with the spectral features observed herein. Further studies incorporating such patient cohorts are warranted to fully evaluate the disease specificity of these spectral signatures.

More importantly, the spatially resolved Raman images (Fig. 3) unveiled disease-specific biomolecular signatures that align closely with known pathobiology. The elevated levels of saturated lipids and collagen in Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia samples are consistent with direct host cell membrane damage by the cell-wall-less pathogen and the ensuing early fibrotic response, patterns observed in other studies of host-pathogen interactions in the lung [42–45]. The significantly higher glycogen content in lobar pneumonia could stem from bacterial glycogen storage (e.g., in Streptococcus pneumoniae) or from heightened energy demands of host immune cells in acute bacterial infection, a metabolic shift noted in activated immune cells during pneumonia [46, 47]. Furthermore, the distinct patterns of extracellular matrix components provide a mechanistic basis for differentiation: the high laminin in lobar pneumonia reflects rapid tissue and basement membrane repair after extensive alveolar damage [48, 49]; the elevated elastin in asthma is a likely adaptation to chronic airway constriction and dilation, part of the airway remodeling process [50, 51]; and the prominent fibronectin in asthmatic bronchitis is indicative of active airway remodeling and epithelial repair, a key feature in chronic inflammatory airway diseases [52, 53]. These findings not only validate the biological relevance of our method but also begin to construct a “molecular portrait” system for differential diagnosis based on BALF biochemical composition. We acknowledge that the age range of our study population (mean age 5.3–9.4 years across groups) and the sex distribution were not fully matched between groups. Several of the extracellular matrix biomarkers we examined—particularly collagen, elastin, laminin, and fibronectin—are known to vary with developmental stage in the pediatric lung [54]. Therefore, while the observed disease-specific biomarker patterns are consistent with known pathobiology, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that some of the differences reflect age-related variation in lung development and remodeling rather than disease-specific effects. Future studies with age- and sex-matched cohorts, or incorporating covariate adjustment, will be essential to confirm the disease specificity of these findings.

When contrasted with current clinical standards, the advantages of our platform become clear. Conventional BALF analyses, such as cell counts, pathogen culture, or ELISA for specific proteins, are often time-consuming, label-dependent, and low-plex, with limitations in sensitivity and specificity for certain pathogens or conditions [15, 18]. Our Raman imaging platform achieves “single-shot, multiplexed analysis”, quantitatively assessing proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates along with their spatial distributions from a single, unprocessed sample in approximately 10 minutes. This label-free, non-destructive nature not only avoids potential artifacts associated with staining or antibody cross-reactivity [55, 56] and preserves the sample for further analysis but also provides a high-content data foundation ideal for future AI-powered diagnostic algorithms, a growing trend in biomedical analytics.

It is important to clarify the intended clinical role of our RSI platform in relation to existing diagnostic standards. We do not propose RSI as a replacement for pathogen-specific assays such as PCR, culture, or serology, which remain essential for definitive pathogen identification and antimicrobial stewardship. Instead, we envision RSI as a rapid triage and adjunct tool that provides complementary molecular phenotyping information—particularly the spatial distribution of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates—within a clinically compatible timeframe (≤10 minutes). This information could: (i) help prioritize or refine the selection of downstream confirmatory tests in cases where the clinical presentation is ambiguous; (ii) provide early diagnostic guidance while awaiting pathogen-specific results (which typically require hours to days); and (iii) serve as a non-invasive monitoring tool for treatment response or disease progression, leveraging its label-free and non-destructive nature.

We acknowledge that the observed overlap between Mycoplasma pneumoniae and healthy control clusters in the t-SNE visualization underscores the current limitations in classification accuracy. In the present proof-of-concept stage, RSI outputs should not be used as standalone diagnostic evidence for clinical decision-making. The consequences of misclassification—particularly the potential for inappropriate antimicrobial use or delayed targeted therapy—would be substantial if the platform were deployed prematurely. Future studies with larger, well-characterized cohorts and prospective validation are essential before RSI could be considered for independent clinical application. At this stage, we therefore advocate for its use as an adjunctive tool to enrich the diagnostic information available to clinicians, rather than as a replacement for established methods.

To enhance clinical applicability, we employed unsupervised machine learning algorithms (PCA and t-SNE) to visualize high-dimensional spectral datasets comprising 150,000 individual spectra (Fig. 4). The distinct clustering of disease phenotypes in the reduced-dimensional space provides preliminary yet compelling evidence supporting the feasibility of Raman spectroscopy-based respiratory disease classification, building on prior successes in spectroscopic-machine learning integration [57, 58]. Notably, the partial overlap between Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia and healthy control clusters—likely attributable to heterogeneous lipid droplet distribution—truthfully reflects the complexity of real-world disease presentations. This observation underscores an opportunity for future refinement using supervised learning models (e.g., Support Vector Machines, Random Forests), which have demonstrated efficacy in other complex biomedical classification tasks [59, 60]. We envision that with expanded training datasets, an end-to-end automated diagnostic system could be built to translate raw BALF Raman images directly into diagnostic suggestions, significantly aiding clinical decision-making.

It is also important to clarify the rationale for our exclusive focus on the cellular fraction of BALF. While the supernatant contains soluble proteins, lipids, metabolites, and inflammatory mediators that are increasingly recognized as promising biomarkers for respiratory diseases [61], our study was specifically designed to exploit the unique advantage of Raman spectroscopic imaging—namely, the ability to simultaneously visualize the spatial distribution of multiple biomolecular classes within intact cells and extracellular structures. This spatially resolved information, which underpins the disease-specific phenotypic signatures we observed (e.g., saturated lipid droplets in asthmatic bronchitis, collagen and laminin deposition patterns in pneumonias), cannot be captured from supernatant alone. Moreover, the cellular pellet represents the standard diagnostic substrate in routine BALF cytology, rendering our approach clinically translatable within existing workflow frameworks. We therefore maintain that the cellular fraction constitutes the most appropriate and informative starting point for this proof-of-concept imaging study.

Beyond its diagnostic performance, our Raman imaging platform offers several practical advantages for clinical adoption. The entire workflow—from sample preparation to image reconstruction—can be completed within 10 minutes, with spectral acquisition and analysis requiring ≤5 minutes per sample, substantially faster than conventional staining or culture-based methods. Its label-free nature eliminates costly reagents, fixation, or lysis steps, reducing both material costs and technical variability. Simultaneous detection and spatial mapping of nine distinct biomarkers in a single acquisition provides high-content molecular information without consuming additional BALF sample. Notably, the non-destructive analysis preserves sample integrity, allowing subsequent examination by cytology, immunofluorescence, or molecular assays when needed. These features collectively position our approach as a practical, cost-effective, and complementary tool for point-of-care BALF diagnostics.

We acknowledge several limitations. First, the small sample size (n = 6 per group) limits statistical power and generalizability, though this is acceptable for a proof-of-concept study [58]. Second, our analysis was confined to the cellular pellet; supernatant was not examined. Third, medication records were retrospective and may lack treatment nuances. Fourth, the interpretation of collagen elevation as fibrosis is speculative without histopathological validation. Fifth, groups were not fully matched by age or sex, which may confound ECM biomarker interpretation. Sixth, rare subtypes and severity stages were not included. Finally, the specificity of inflammatory-related Raman peaks (772 and 1670 cm−1) requires validation in non-respiratory inflammatory conditions. Multi-center prospective studies are needed for clinical translation [59].

Future work should focus on: (1) large-scale, multi-center studies to validate the diagnostic model; (2) dynamic disease monitoring (e.g., treatment response, exacerbation prediction) leveraging the non-destructive nature of Raman analysis; and (3) expanding the diagnostic repertoire to include other respiratory conditions and integrating with other omics data. Additionally, correlation of Raman signatures (e.g., 772 cm−1 or 2864 cm−1 peaks) with clinical severity scores and inflammatory markers in larger prospective cohorts could extend the platform’s utility to disease staging and prognostic stratification.

In conclusion, we have developed an automated Raman imaging platform for label-free, high-content analysis of BALF, enabling rapid and multiplexed differentiation of major respiratory diseases. This proof-of-concept study establishes a foundation for next-generation precision pulmonology diagnostics, with the potential for further refinement through large-scale validation and integration into clinical workflows.
