SHERLOCK: Towards Dynamic Knowledge Adaptation in LLM-enhanced E-commerce Risk Management Conference: ; ;
Nan Lu, Yurong Hu, Jiaquan Fang, Yan Liu, Rui Dong, Yiming Wang, Rui Lin, Shaoyi Xu

Abstract
. The growth of the e-commerce industry has intensified the adversarial dynamics between shadow economy actors and risk management teams. Companies often conduct risk investigations into suspicious cases to identify emerging fraud patterns, thereby enhancing both preemptive risk prevention and post-hoc governance. However, the sheer volume of case analyses imposes a substantial workload on risk management analysts, as each case requires the integration of long-term expert experience and meticulous scrutiny across multiple risk dimensions. Additionally, individual disparities among analysts hinder the establishment of uniform and high-standard workflows. To address these challenges, we propose the SHERLOCK framework, which leverages the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) to assist analysts in risk investigations. Our approach consists of three primary components: (1) extracting risk management knowledge from multi-modal data and constructing a domain knowledge base (KB), (2) building an intelligent platform guided by the data flywheel paradigm that integrates daily operations, expert annotations, and model evaluations, with iteratively fine-tuning for preference alignment, and (3) introducing a Reflect & Refine (R&R) module that collaborates with the domain KB to establish a rapid response mechanism for evolving risk patterns. Experiments conducted on the real-world transaction dataset from JD.com demonstrate that our method significantly improves the precision of both factual alignment and risk localization within the LLM analysis results. Deployment of the SHERLOCK-based LLM system on JD.com has substantially enhanced the efficiency of case investigation workflows for risk managers.



1.
Introduction

The rapid growth of e-commerce has intensified the adversarial arms race between shadow economy operators and risk management systems, leading to an exponential surge in case investigation workloads. As fraudulent tactics evolve in sophistication, companies are compelled to conduct exhaustive risk investigations across hundreds of thousands of suspicious cases annually to identify emerging fraud patterns.
This investigative burden now dominates approximately 60% of daily risk management operations. Its importance is evident in three key aspects: First, customer service teams rely on rapid, accurate case assessments to address millions of daily complaints and sustain user trust. Second, fraud-detection models undergo rigorous validation through real-world case testing to ensure operational reliability. Third, risk inspection workflows demand labor-intensive manual reviews of system-flagged cases.

Existing methods
(
Caron et al. 2013
;
Song et al. 2019
;
Ali et al. 2022
;
Wang and Gao 2023
;
IBRAHIM et al. 2025
)
generate risk assessments directly but obscure reasoning logic within intricate features or parameters. This opacity necessitates labor-intensive manual analysis to retrace decisions during case evaluations. To address this inefficiency, we propose to augment risk management systems with the Large Language Model (LLM) as an automated case investigation tool by leveraging its capacity to provide human-readable reasoning chains. Hopefully, this approach can bridge the gap between detection outcomes and interpretable explanations, enabling risk managers to validate results efficiently.

Despite the potential of LLMs to automate case investigation, their direct application faces domain-specific barriers. Due to the sensitive nature of risk management data, general-purpose LLMs
(
AI 2024
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
DeepSeek-AI 2025
)
lack domain-specific contextual understanding, which proves inadequate for addressing intricate risk investigation problems. Our empirical validation on real-world cases reveals that the precision of LLM-generated analytical conclusions is notably low, see some examples provided in the block
“Judgments from Zero-shot LLM”
. The limited usability stems from the following obstacles.
Firstly, risk management cases typically contain a substantial amount of terms that may introduce ambiguities and interfere with LLMs’ risk investigation.
Secondly, there exist numerous specialized business scenarios characterized by unique risk analysis logic. The absence of such contextual knowledge may lead to erroneous judgments by LLMs.
Thirdly, the complexity of risk scenarios often necessitates reliance on longitudinal expert knowledge. Much of this expertise is difficult to codify as explicit knowledge points, instead requiring contextual interpretation through case-specific analysis.
Additionally, given the rapidly evolving nature of e-commerce and the adversarial dynamics inherent in risk management scenarios, the knowledge required by LLMs is subject to continuous change. Consequently, LLMs deployed in risk management applications must demonstrate sustainable adaptation capabilities to remain effective.

SHERLOCK is designed to provide comprehensive risk investigation by integrating dynamic domain knowledge and expert cognition into LLMs, enabling sustainable knowledge evolution. The SHERLOCK framework comprises three core components:
(1) Domain KB: We leverage LLMs and human experts to collaboratively extract high-quality risk management knowledge from multi-modal materials, such as documents, meeting recordings, and code repositories. We build a domain knowledge base (KB) encompassing terminology (e.g., clarifying that “Treasure Island” refers to JD.com’s auction service rather than a brand name), specialized business logic (e.g., recognizing that IP clustering in food delivery is a normal pattern), and expert cognition of risk patterns (e.g., distinguishing between scalper activity and legitimate multi-region deliveries like corporate gifting).
(2) Data Flywheel: We construct a unified platform integrating data labeling, model evaluation, and operational workflows. The system reduces annotation costs through a dual approach: (a) prioritizing reliable risk pattern selection over de novo expert annotation, and (b) Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
generation with a “suspect-then-rule-out” framework. We create a self-reinforcing cycle which simultaneously enhances daily operational efficiency and sample collection efficacy.
(3) R&R Module: We develop a Reflect & Refine (R&R) module that leverages knowledge from our domain KB to alleviate LLM hallucinations. This module primarily serves two functions: first, as a post-hoc inspection to eliminate false positives in the model, thereby enhancing assessment accuracy; second, as a hotfix module that dynamically injects corrective rules and new business logic into the domain KB to resolve emerging risk patterns in real-time.

2.
Methods

In this section, we introduce our SHERLOCK framework, which consists of three key modules. We have a risk management KB containing three types of knowledge (see Figure
1
): domain terminology, business logic and risk patterns. We also build a data flywheel integrated with sample collection, model evaluation, and routine case inspection. Additionally, we develop an R&R module which ensures assessment accuracy and enables dynamic updates. Given an input case from multiple sources, we first retrieve relevant domain terminology from the KB for prompt augmentation. Then the augmented input is passed to LLM adapted through the data flywheel cycles, followed by our R&R module to generate the refined risk investigation conclusions.

2.1.
Insights

To generate high-quality fine-tuning data for LLMs at minimal cost and maximize data utility for LLM-based risk management systems, we propose the following insights:

Leveraging Existing Assets:
While dedicated, LLM-ready risk control datasets are unavailable, we possess extensive domain-specific assets, including codebases, risk prevention policies, operational documentation, and meeting records. We will distill knowledge from these multi-modal, lower-density sources to construct a comprehensive domain Knowledge Base (KB). This distilled knowledge will support context augmentation via prompts and subsequently inform our reflection module.

Strategic Expert Annotation:
Embedding expert knowledge into LLMs for specialized domains necessitates expert input. However, large-scale manual annotation for risk management tasks, particularly CoT reasoning, is prohibitively costly. To mitigate this, we propose a semi-automated pipeline combining targeted expert annotations with LLM generation to synthesize high-quality CoT data. Crucially, we will establish a sustainable operational loop integrating business processes, expert annotation, and model evaluation to create a self-reinforcing data flywheel.

Incorporating Reflection:
Hallucination remains a significant challenge for LLMs in specialized domains like risk control, even after extensive training. To enhance robustness and mitigate this issue, we introduce a downstream Reflect & Refine (R&R) module. This module critically evaluates the analytical output of the LLM, optimizing conclusions and correcting errors. Designed for agility, the R&R module supports dynamic updates, enabling continuous improvement based on new insights and identified error patterns.

Domain Knowledge Base

Given the the diversity of specialized terminology, the complexity of business logic, and the variability of risk patterns in e-commerce risk management, one of the most effective approaches involves improving the understanding of LLMs through the integration of a knowledge base (KB)

Xu et al. 2020

. To build a risk management KB, we conducted a comprehensive exploration of multi-modal data sources, including various business documentation, meeting records, code repositories, and real-time strategies. The construction pipeline of our domain KB is depicted in Figure

The risk management terminology KB was constructed through systematic extraction and validation of domain-specific concepts from multi-modal business documentation, including standard operating procedures, training manuals, case investigation reports, and transcribed meeting recordings. This integrated pipeline combining automated scoring and human verification ensured both scalability in processing extensive documentation and precision in final knowledge representation.

2.2.2.
Business Logic

The e-commerce business logic is featured by its complexity and continuous evolution. Only through a profound understanding of these operational logic can we distinguish cases that appear risky but are justifiable within specific business contexts, which is essential for enhancing the precision of model analysis. To establish correlations between business logic and risk patterns, we employed LLMs to extract characteristics of business logic from operational documents based on predefined logic sets and further inferred potentially misjudged risk patterns. Finally, experts conducted a review of the knowledge generated, manually revising and supplementing the characteristics of the business logic and associated risk patterns.

2.2.3.
Risk Pattern

The risk management domain encompasses a multitude of rule-based models and real-time strategies, within which valuable expert knowledge is embedded, particularly regarding threshold determinations for specific risk indicators and analyses of interdependencies among different risk features. Therefore, we extracted structured risk pattern knowledge from the codebase of these rules and strategies. For each model or strategy, we employed few-shot prompting to guide LLM in extracting atomic risk patterns (serving as retrieval keys), along with the corresponding threshold determination logic and inter-feature relationships associated with each pattern. Given the substantial overlap of risk patterns across multiple models, we subsequently task the LLM with consolidating similar risk patterns and their associated knowledge. Finally, the developers responsible for these models or strategies respectively conduct manual verification of the risk pattern knowledge synthesized by the LLM.

2.3.
Data Flywheel

To address the high costs associated with data annotation, which stems from the inherent complexity of risk investigation tasks and their heavy reliance on expert knowledge, we developed a specialized annotation platform (illustrated in Figure
3
) that integrates routine case investigation workflows with prioritized case annotation processes. This system achieves dual optimization of operational efficiency and training sample quality.

Figure 3.
Illustration of Data Flywheel.

2.3.1.
Dynamic Routing

In the daily operations of risk management, cases requiring review and judgment by risk management personnel are automatically routed to the annotation platform.
Taking inspiration from active learning
(
Settles 2009
)
, we prioritize the annotation of samples that are most informative for model improvement. Specifically, when the conclusions provided by the LLM do not meet expectations, these cases are flagged as high-value samples for annotation. Otherwise, The LLM-generated conclusions are directly adopted as final judgment for the case, enhancing operational workflow efficiency. Also note that the acceptance rate is naturally a good measure of model performance.
This selective annotation process ensures that we focus resources on the most informative data points, thereby maximizing the efficiency of the annotation effort.

2.3.2.
Selection-over-Creation

In addition, to further reduce annotation costs, we adopted a “selection-over-creation” annotation strategy. In general, it is intuitive that making selections is simpler than creating new content. Therefore, we first employed LLM to analyze and identify risk factors in current cases, after which experts selected a confidence set (with potential modifications and extensions) to form the dataset for model fine-tuning. During the fine-tuning process, we utilized a combined strategy of SFT and DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
. For SFT, the expert-annotated confidence set served as supervised labels, enabling the LLM to familiarize itself with and mimic expert analytical patterns. For DPO, preference sample pairs were used to explicitly suppress erroneous assertions. Notably, given the critical importance of precision in risk investigation scenarios, DPO was specifically designed to mitigate false positives.

Your task is to "reverse-engineer" and "recreate" your initial, complete internal thought process as the expert, based on the final review conclusion (which risks were accepted and which were rejected). This process must be presented as a first-person soliloquy and must sound like a genuine expert analyzing data in real-time.

# Input Information

1.
input data

2.
accepted risks
: List of risk factors you ultimately confirmed and accepted.

3.
rejected risks
: List of risk factors you initially suspected but ultimately ruled out.

# Core Requirements for the Soliloquy

Your soliloquy must be a coherent narrative that skillfully integrates the following two thought processes:

1. For Accepted risk factors (Confirmation Logic):

- Describe how you identified the relevant anomalous data.

- Demonstrate how you connected the clues and applied risk management knowledge to confirm their riskiness.

- Use affirmative, confident language (e.g., "This point is suspicious," "This confirms my suspicion").

2. For Rejected risk factors (Exclusion Logic):

- Must reflect the "suspect first, rule out later" process.

- Step 1: Acknowledge surface anomaly. First, acknowledge why this point "appeared" to be a risk (e.g., "When I first saw this huge amount of orders, I thought it was abnormal…").

- Step 2: Seek plausible explanation. Describe your process of seeking more contextual information to validate this suspicion (e.g., "But I won’t jump to conclusions yet; let me look at this user’s background…").

- Step 3: Find exclusion evidence. Clearly identify the specific data point or business knowledge that led you to dismiss the initial suspicion (e.g., "Ah, I see the user segment is ’Verified Enterprise Customer.’ This amount is routine for their procurement, so that’s fine.").

- Step 4: State exclusion conclusion. Clearly state that you have ruled out this risk factor.

# Output Format Requirements

1. Soliloquy First, Summary Later: Output the full personal soliloquy first.

2. Clear Separation: After the soliloquy, use ‘—’ as a separator.

3. Final Conclusion: After the separator, list only the **
accepted
** risk factors to form the final risk assessment report.

2.3.3.
Suspect-Then-Rule-Out

Compared to the analytical conclusions, the expert reasoning process underlying case investigation is more crucial for LLM learning. However, explicitly requiring experts to articulate their analytical processes imposes a significant burden. To further reduce annotation costs, we leveraged the robust reasoning capabilities of LLMs to simulate the reasoning processes for preference sample pairs. Specifically, for generating reasoning processes, we meticulously designed a “suspect-then-rule-out” logical framework (see the detailed prompt in the block
“Prompt of Suspect-then-Rule-Out”
). The LLM was prompted to first generate the risk factors identified in the initial analysis, followed by self-questioning to eliminate “invalid” risks from the initial assessment. This process emulates the cognitive evolution of LLMs from their pre-trained reasoning patterns toward expert-level analytical methodologies, the efficacy of which will be substantiated in the experimental results section below.

2.3.4.
Bi-cycle

The data flywheel operates through a dual-loop architecture centered around model-generated conclusions that drive operational efficiency. The primary workflow directly leverages validated LLM outputs to accelerate daily case processing, with two synergistic cycles ensuring continuous improvement:
(1) Training Cycle (red cycle in Figure
3
): Cases where LLM-generated explanations fail to meet expert expectations are prioritized as high-value samples for annotation. These curated samples undergo iterative DPO fine-tuning, with each model update progressively enhancing output reliability and reducing dependency on expert labeling through improved risk assessment precision.
(2) Evaluation Cycle (green cycle in Figure
3
): Expert-validated outputs generate real-time accuracy metrics, with model acceptance rates serving as quantitative performance indicators. This evaluation framework directly informs subsequent training iterations, creating closed-loop optimization.
This self-reinforcing mechanism achieves dual optimization through active learning prioritization and automated performance monitoring. By integrating operational workflows with model evolution, the architecture greatly reduces annotation costs while accelerating expert preference alignment through targeted sample selection and metric-driven refinement.

2.4.
R&R Module

The Reflect & Refine (R&R) module is designed to address two critical challenges in LLM-based risk investigation: (1) mitigating hallucinations caused by domain knowledge misalignment, and (2) enabling rapid adaptation to emerging risk patterns. The functionality of R&R module is shown in the appendix
B
.

2.4.1.
Knowledge Retrieval

We leveraged the knowledge of business logic and risk patterns from our domain KB for reflection and refinement. The retrieval approach for business logic knowledge is relatively straightforward, where relevant business logic knowledge is retrieved directly through keyword matching based on the specific business scenario to which the case belongs. For the retrieval of risk patterns, semantic vectors are constructed from the initial risk factors, followed by similarity-based pattern retrieval through semantic matching.

2.4.2.
Reflect & Refine

We first employ LLM to conduct fact verification on the risk factors generated by the base model. This step is crucial because we observed that, due to the diversity of input data formats, the model does not always adhere strictly to factual accuracy in its analysis.
Subsequently, we retrieve business logic directly from the KB while using fact-checked risk factors as keys to retrieve relevant risk patterns from the knowledge base, along with their associated contextual background and tips.
Finally, we tasked the LLM with evaluating each fact-checked risk factor in light of the knowledge retrieved. The detailed prompt is shown below. This evaluation involves evaluating potential misjudgments, relevance to the scenario, precision of risk descriptions, and the presence of other related risk factors.

2.4.3.
Hotfix

Given the dynamic nature of e-commerce risk management requirements and the continuous emergence of new business scenarios, risk investigation LLMs must possess sustainable adaptation capabilities to accommodate evolving business logic. To mitigate the latency inherent in model retraining cycles, we leverage the post-hoc R&R module for real-time hotfixes. This approach operates through two mechanisms: First, when novel business logic emerges, we promptly augment the domain KB with relevant expertise, ensuring that the reflection process can retrieve the most up-to-date information during subsequent analyses. Second, for risk management policy adjustments, such as tightening or relaxing controls for specific scenarios, experts manually calibrate risk pattern thresholds within the KB. This enables the LLM to automatically align with the latest expert cognition during its reflection phase, thereby achieving agile policy enforcement without requiring full model retraining.

3.
Experiments

This section details the empirical evaluation of our proposed method. We aim to answer the following research questions (RQs):

•

RQ1 How does our fine-tuned, CoT-based model perform against zero-shot LLMs in identifying risks?

•

RQ2 Does our proposed Reflect-and-Refine (R&R) module significantly improve analytical accuracy and reduce false positives compared to single-pass approach?

•

RQ3 What are the individual contributions of the key components of the R&R module, namely the knowledge retrieval and the reflection step?

•

RQ4 Do human experts perceive the final, refined output from the SHERLOCK framework as more accurate, trustworthy, and helpful for decision-making?

3.1.
Experimental Setup

3.1.1.
Dataset

We conducted our experiments on an anonymized real-world dataset of e-commerce transactions from JD.com. The dataset comprises 5,000 orders collected over one month using the aforementioned annotation platform. A key characteristic of our operational data is its multi-modal nature. To construct a comprehensive input for our model, we consolidate features from three primary sources for each transaction:

•

Tabular Data: This forms the core of the order information, containing structured key-value pairs such as user profile details (user_id, registration_date, user_type), order specifics (order_amount, sku_category), and payment information.

•

Graph-based Features: To capture complex, latent relationships indicative of coordinated fraud, we extract features from an internal device-sharing and transaction graph. These features are represented as a set of triplets in the format (src, dst, relation), such as (user_a, user_b, shipping_phone).

•

Unstructured Text: Raw text data from sources like user-provided notes on an order or summaries of recent customer service interactions provide crucial context regarding user intent and history.

To make this multi-modal data accessible to a text-based LLM, we developed a structured serialization format. All features are converted into a single, coherent textual input using clear headings and Markdown-style formatting. This method preserves the distinct nature and origin of each data modality, allowing the model to better reason about the provided information.

3.1.4.
Evaluation Benchmarks

To validate the quality of the generated risk factors, we developed an evaluation benchmark combining expert annotations and automated LLM review. We first curated an evaluation dataset of 1,000 samples covering diverse risk scenarios, each manually verified by domain experts. These samples were carefully selected to represent both challenging and routine cases.
For each case, experts were tasked with labeling core risk factors (decisive factors for risk assessment) and relevant risk factors (supportive factors that provide contextual value and require attention). DeepSeek-R1 was then used to classify the LLM-generated risk factors, with each point categorized as a core risk factor, relevant risk factor, or noise, based on expert-annotated risk factors. In this paper, we introduced SHERLOCK, a novel framework designed to enhance the capabilities of LLMs in the domain of e-commerce risk management. Our experiments demonstrated that SHERLOCK significantly improves the precision of risk assessments, reduces hallucinations, and enhances the trustworthiness of LLM-generated conclusions. The framework’s ability to rapidly adapt to emerging risk patterns through real-time knowledge updates and post-hoc reflection ensures its practical applicability in dynamic e-commerce environments.

The deployment of SHERLOCK on JD.com’s platform has already yielded substantial improvements in operational efficiency, reducing case investigation times by 387% and increasing expert acceptance rates to 82%. These results underscore the framework’s potential to transform risk management workflows by combining the reasoning capabilities of LLMs with structured domain knowledge and expert cognition. Future work will focus on expanding the KB to cover additional risk scenarios and further optimizing the R&R module for real-time performance. # Task Description

- Risk Feature Calculation Code: Identifies how risk-related features are computed.

1.
Parse Feature Calculations
:

– Identify variables representing risk features (e.g., transaction_count, avg_amount).

– Note any preprocessing, thresholds, or transformations applied to these features.

2.
Extract Discrimination Logic
:

– Analyze conditional statements (e.g., if clauses, loops) in the model code.

– Focus on logical connectors: Flag AND/OR conditions that combine multiple features (e.g., if (A > X AND B < Y)).

– Map how features interact within these logical groupings.

3.
Synthesize Risk Patterns
:

– Group conditions linked by AND into a single coherent pattern if possible (e.g., “High risk if both A and B are true”).

– Treat OR as separate patterns unless they share a common theme (e.g., “Underage user OR unverified email”).

– Describe patterns concisely, emphasizing thresholds and feature relationships.

...
]
