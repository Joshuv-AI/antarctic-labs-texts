Trauma-induced crown dilaceration of permanent maxillary central incisors: A case report with CBCT follow-up
Authors: Fereshteh Hayatimotlagh, Saba Khorram, Zahra Sadeghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic dental injuries to primary teeth may result in developmental disturbances in the permanent successors because of the close anatomical relationship between primary tooth apices and developing permanent tooth germs. Injuries occurring during crown formation may interfere with normal odontogenesis and lead to crown dilaceration. A 7-year-old healthy boy presented with abnormal eruption of the permanent maxillary central incisors. At 2.5 years of age, he had sustained avulsion of both primary maxillary central incisors and crown fractures of the primary lateral incisors, followed by extraction of residual root fragments under general anesthesia. Cone-beam computed tomography examination at 6.5 years revealed early abnormal crown morphology of the permanent maxillary central incisors. Follow-up imaging at 7 years confirmed bilateral crown dilaceration, more pronounced on the left side, while root morphology remained normal. Nine months after eruption, the left permanent central incisor developed extensive structural breakdown with pulpal exposure and was considered nonrestorable; therefore, extraction was performed. The contralateral incisor remained asymptomatic and continues under periodic follow-up. The findings were consistent with trauma-related crown dilaceration associated with injury during early crown mineralization. Preservation of Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath may explain the normal root development despite severe coronal deformity. Cone-beam computed tomography provided valuable 3-dimensional information for diagnosis, longitudinal monitoring, and treatment planning. Early trauma to primary incisors may result in crown dilaceration of permanent successors. Introduction

Traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) to primary teeth are common during early childhood, particularly between 1 and 3 years of age, when children are more susceptible to falls and facial injuries [1]. Because of the close anatomical relationship between the apices of primary teeth and the developing permanent tooth germs, traumatic injuries to primary teeth may interfere with normal odontogenesis and lead to developmental disturbances in the permanent successors [2,3]. Among TDIs, avulsion, intrusion, and crown fractures of primary incisors are considered severe injuries and may result in enamel hypoplasia, eruption disturbances, positional abnormalities, arrested root development, or crown and root dilaceration in the permanent dentition [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Crown dilaceration refers to an abnormal angulation between the crown and root portions of a developing tooth and is strongly influenced by the stage of odontogenesis at the time of trauma [7]. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides 3-dimensional assessment of tooth morphology and spatial orientation and may facilitate early diagnosis of developmental disturbances that are difficult to evaluate on conventional 2-dimensional radiographs [4,6,8]. This report describes bilateral crown dilaceration of permanent maxillary central incisors following trauma to primary teeth, with longitudinal CBCT follow-up.

Case presentation

A 7-year-old medically healthy boy was referred for evaluation of abnormal eruption of the permanent maxillary central incisors.

Trauma history (detailed)

At 2.5 years of age, the patient sustained traumatic dental injuries resulting in avulsion of both primary maxillary central incisors and crown fractures of the primary lateral incisors. Extraction of residual root fragments was performed under general anesthesia. No radiographic documentation from the time of injury was available.

Following the trauma, the child was periodically monitored for the development and eruption of the permanent maxillary incisors. At approximately 6.5 years of age, the patient underwent a cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan to evaluate the developing permanent teeth. The CBCT revealed early signs of abnormal crown morphology, prompting continued follow-up.

At around 7 years of age, the patient returned after clinical exposure of a portion of the permanent crown, consistent with eruptive progression. Follow-up CBCT confirmed crown dilaceration of the permanent maxillary central incisors, with more pronounced deformity on the left side (Table 1).

Clinical examination

Clinical examination revealed partial eruption of both permanent maxillary central incisors with abnormal crown morphology and altered eruption trajectory. The left central incisor demonstrated more severe coronal deformity compared with the contralateral tooth. Surrounding soft tissues appeared normal, with no evidence of swelling or sinus tract formation. Oral hygiene was fair, and no significant periodontal abnormalities were observed at the time of examination.

Differential diagnosis

Differential diagnosis included developmental malformation unrelated to trauma, Turner tooth, and other odontogenic developmental disturbances. However, the history of severe trauma to the primary incisors during crown formation, together with the characteristic crown-root angulation observed on CBCT, supported the diagnosis of trauma-induced crown dilaceration.

Clinical outcome

Nine months after eruption, the left permanent maxillary central incisor developed extensive carious destruction with pulpal exposure and associated pain. Clinical and radiographic assessment demonstrated severe structural compromise of the crown, resulting in an unfavorable restorative prognosis. Following multidisciplinary consultation, the tooth was considered non-restorable and extraction was performed. Healing was uneventful. The contralateral incisor remained asymptomatic and continues under periodic clinical and radiographic follow-up.

Clinical outcome

The left permanent central incisor subsequently developed extensive caries with pulpal exposure and associated pain. Radiographic assessment revealed compromised coronal structure without periapical involvement. After multidisciplinary consultation, the tooth was deemed nonrestorable and extracted. Healing was uneventful. The contralateral incisor remained asymptomatic and continues under periodic radiographic surveillance.

Discussion

Traumatic dental injuries to primary teeth may lead to developmental disturbances in permanent successors, particularly when trauma occurs during active odontogenesis [9]. In the present case, the findings were consistent with trauma-related crown dilaceration of the permanent maxillary central incisors following avulsion of the primary predecessors during early childhood. The stage of tooth development at the time of injury plays an important role in determining the resulting deformity [10]. Trauma occurring during crown mineralization may displace the already mineralized portion of the developing crown while allowing subsequent tooth development to continue in an altered direction [11]. In the present case, the deformity was limited to the crown portions, whereas root morphology remained relatively normal. Preservation of Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath may explain the continuation of normal root formation despite severe coronal alteration [12]. CBCT examination provided valuable 3-dimensional assessment of crown morphology, spatial orientation, and adjacent anatomical structures. Compared with conventional radiographs, CBCT enabled more accurate visualization of the crown-root relationship and facilitated longitudinal monitoring during eruption [13]. Bilateral crown dilaceration of permanent maxillary central incisors is relatively uncommon [14]. Previous studies have reported that traumatic injuries to primary incisors may result in developmental disturbances ranging from enamel defects to severe morphological abnormalities in the permanent dentition [15]. The present case supports existing evidence regarding the long-term effects of early childhood dental trauma on permanent tooth development. Management of crown dilaceration depends on the severity of the deformity, esthetic considerations, eruption status, and long-term prognosis. Early diagnosis is important because these teeth may present restorative, orthodontic, and functional challenges during growth and development.

Conclusion

This case suggests an association between early trauma to primary incisors and subsequent crown dilaceration of the permanent successors. Despite significant coronal deformity, root development remained relatively normal, possibly because Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath was preserved during odontogenesis. CBCT provided valuable 3-dimensional information for diagnosis, follow-up, and treatment planning.
