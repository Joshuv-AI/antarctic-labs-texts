A biophysical approach to the design of networks of communication systems
Rodrigo Almeida, Ana Filipa Valente, Rui Dilão

Abstract
Inspired by the growth dynamics of the protist Physarum polycephalum , we use a formalism that describes adaptive incompressible Hagen-Poiseuille flows on channel networks to identify graphs connecting different nodes within Euclidean space. These graphs are either suboptimal or optimal in terms of their length. Occasionally, we obtain graph tree configurations that are topologically equivalent to Steiner trees. This approach is employed to design communication systems, such as railway networks, motorways, or fibre webs. As a proof of concept, we explicitly apply this framework to the Portuguese railway network.



1
Introduction

Several classes of equations utilising Hagen-Poiseuille (H-P) flows have been developed to describe the growth of the protist
Physarum polycephalum
, commonly known as slime mould. Despite lacking a neural system or centralised control, this organism exhibits advanced behaviour, such as selecting the shortest path between food sources in mazes
[
Nakagaki et al, 2000
]
. With multiple food sources,
Physarum
can construct networks with comparable efficiency, fault tolerance, and cost to artificial networks
[
Tero et al., 2007
,
Tero et al., 2010
]
. Various biological experiments indicate that the network tree structures of slime mould often resemble Steiner trees. Algorithms have been created using adaptive H-P type flows to interpret both
Physarum
and Steiner tree formation
[
Tero et al., 2010b
,
Sun & Halgamuge, 2016
,
Almeida, 2021
,
Hsu et al., 2022
]
. The Steiner tree represents a network optimisation problem in graphs and has significant applications in physics, biology, and engineering
[
Sun et al., 2016
,
Zhu et al., 2018
]
.

The survival of multicellular and multinucleated organisms relies on their network of veins. A vein network must efficiently transport and distribute resources and nutrients throughout the body for optimal survival, providing short pathways between important locations. To ensure transport efficiency, a biological network should be as short as possible to minimise the costs of building the network and energy dissipation. Furthermore, biological networks need to be robust and redundant to prevent failure if a channel is damaged. In the case of
Physarum
, its vein network adaptability is vital for foraging and survival.

Previously developed models, based on the phenomenology of adaptive H-P flows to study the formation and optimisation of network paths of
Physarum
, relied on deterministic formalism to solve systems of ordinary differential equations on graphs
[
Tero et al., 2006
,
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
]
. These graphs cover bounded regions of Euclidean space. The vertices of the graph can represent branching points of veins or edges, as well as sources or sinks of an incompressible fluid. Given the initial inflows and outflows at the sources and sinks, along with the total fluid volume in the network, and assuming that the radius of the network veins is elastic and can adapt as a function of the flux, the flow on the graph reaches a steady state where some veins transport fluid while others do not. By maintaining constant inflows and outflows over time, a steady state is achieved with a fixed subgraph or tree geometry. The steady states are not unique and depend on the initial conditions of the adapted conductivities. A particularity of this approach is that if one of the connections in the steady-state subgraph is inactivated, another graph link is formed, illustrating the adaptability and resilience of this adaptation process
[
Valente et al., 2024
]
. This geometric construction mimics several natural systems sensitive to the spatial distribution of sources, sinks, and local flows.

In this paper, we analyse extremal length graphs connecting
n
points in Euclidean space using the properties of adaptive incompressible H-P flows that preserve fluid volume in the network
[
Almeida, 2021
,
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
,
Valente, 2023
,
Valente et al., 2024
]
. To simulate communication systems and achieve a level of variability that may describe the resilient properties of natural flows, we have introduced several stochastic actualisation processes over time, leading to suboptimal or optimal solutions for graph networks. In all the cases tested, we have consistently obtained steady solutions that are topologically equivalent to Steiner tree solutions, which can then be further optimised relative to the graph length. We have derived suboptimal graph solutions for an ideal Portuguese railway network connecting all the main cities and land and sea borders.

2
Finding paths of extremal lengths

Consider a point lattice within a bounded region of Euclidean space of dimension
\mathcal{N}
. The point lattice can be represented by an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},E)
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the set of vertices and
E
is the set of edges. Given a bounded domain
\mathcal{D}
of Euclidean space, we can construct the graph
\mathcal{G}
through a Delaunay triangulation of a set of points with coordinates
(x_{i}^{1},...,x_{i}^{\mathcal{N}})
, with
i=1,...,N
. Suppose
N
is sufficiently large and well distributed within
\mathcal{D}
. In that case, we can make the area of triangles sufficiently small and approximate any Euclidean path by a collection of straight edges
(i,j)\in E
connecting nodes
i
and
j
. The graph
\mathcal{G}
is connected, having a single component. The formalism established here is very general, though we assume that
\mathcal{N}=2
.

From the graph
\mathcal{G}
, we choose
n<<N
points. The set of
n
points may represent the sources and sinks of a fluid injected into the network. Thus, the graph illustrates a network of channels through which an incompressible fluid circulates. Fluid is injected from the sources and exits through the sinks. The inflow and outflow are balanced, resulting in zero net flow.
Our primary objective is to investigate these types of flows, further assuming that the edges of
\mathcal{G}
can adjust such that they are capable of either conducting or not conducting the fluid.

The question is, under what conditions does this flow adapt to an optimised network comprising a subset of conducting edges that connect the
n
points of the graph
\mathcal{G}
?

For example, consider the case where
n=3
in a bounded region of the Euclidean plane. The three points are arranged in a triangular configuration. Two possible graphs connect the three points, as illustrated in figure
1
. The coordinates of the three points are

p_{1}=(0,0),\ \ p_{2}=(1,0),\ \ p_{3}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right).

(1)

A tree or closed graph can connect these three points, as depicted in figure
1
.

By definition, the Steiner tree of a set of points on the Euclidean plane is a tree of minimal length that connects all the points. The graph in figure
1
a) has length
L_{a}=3
, and in b),
L_{b}=3\sqrt{7}/4\approx 1.98
. The graph in figure
1
b) represents the tree of minimal lengths connecting the three points — the Steiner tree connecting these points. In the Steiner tree, there exists a fourth point with coordinates
p_{S}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\right)
. The challenge in constructing Steiner trees is that, in some instances, there are additional points — Steiner points — that, along with the initial points, span the tree of minimal length.

Figure 1:
Two possible graphs connecting the three points (
1
). a) A graph connecting the three points in the plane. b) The Steiner tree of minimal length connecting the three points in the plane. In this case, the tree has an additional point — the Steiner point
p_{S}
.

There are two issues in constructing a Steiner tree. The first is determining the number of Steiner points that organise the Steiner tree. The second is identifying which edges connect the initial data points to the Steiner points. If we know these for a configuration of
n
points, the coordinates of the Steiner points can be easily obtained by solving linear systems of equations involving the unknown coordinates of the Steiner points
[
Valente, 2023
]
.

The tree connecting the three points can also represent many physical, social, or biological systems. For example, it can act as a model of a road network linking three cities. Assume each road is used by travellers moving from city
i
to city
j
, and the number of travellers going from
i
to
j
, where
i\neq j
, is roughly the same for every
(i,j)
pair. For the road network shown in the graph of figure

1
b), the average distance between two cities is
L_{b}=1.98
, whereas for the network in figure
1
a), the average distance is
L_{a}=1
. From a communication perspective, the configuration in figure
1
a) is more beneficial for users due to its lower transportation costs. However, its construction cost is higher. In section
4
, we define the optimisation criteria for analysing communication systems.

3
Adaptive incompressible Hagen-Poiseuille flows on graphs

Specific systems, such as blood vessels or
Physarum
, can be modelled by a class of equations that describe the steady flow of viscous incompressible fluids in networks made up of straight channels with multiple sources and sinks, and adaptive conductivities
[
Rubinow, 1973
,
Tero et al., 2006
,
Valente et al., 2024
]
. The flow is similar to Hagen-Poiseuille flow, and the fluid volume within the network stays constant
[
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
]
.

A network of veins is represented by an undirected graph subset of
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},E)
.
The network has
K
sources and
R
sinks located at fixed nodes, and
n=K+R
. The fluid flux from outside the network to the inside (and vice versa) at node
j
is denoted by
S_{j}
. If the network has a source at node
j
, then
S_{j}>0
; if it has a sink at node
j
,
S_{j}<0
; otherwise,
S_{j}=0
. Given that the fluid is incompressible,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=\sum_{j:\text{sources}}S_{j}+\sum_{j:\text{sinks}}S_{j}=0,

and the volume of fluid in the system remains constant.

The steady state of the flow within the network is determined by Kirchhoff’s law, which states that flux is conserved at each node
j
,

\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E}Q_{ij}=\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E}D_{ij}\frac{(p_{i}-p_{j})}{L_{ij}}=S_{i}\quad,i=1,...,N,

(2)

where
p_{i}
is the pressure at node
i
,
L_{ij}
is the length of the graph edge connecting nodes
i
and
j
, and
D_{ij}
is the conductivity of the edge
(i,j)
.

The edges represent elastic cylindrical channels that can expand or contract transversely to the flow. The edge connecting node
i
to node
j
has a fixed length
L_{ij}
and a variable radius
r_{ij}
; the edge
(i,j)
has (variable) conductivity
D_{ij}
given by
D_{ij}=\pi r_{ij}^{4}/8\eta
, with
\eta
the dynamic viscosity of the fluid. The volume of the channel connecting node
i
to node
j
is given by
V_{ij}=\pi r_{ij}^{2}L_{ij}=\sqrt{8\pi\eta}L_{ij}\sqrt{D_{ij}}
,
[
Landau & Lifchitz, 1971
]
. As the fluid is incompressible, the volume of fluid in the network remains constant and is given by

V=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}V_{ij}=\beta\sum_{(i,j)\in E}L_{ij}\sqrt{D_{ij}},

where
\beta=\sqrt{8\pi\eta}
.

The formalism developed in

[
Almeida and Dilão, 2022
]
states that the conductivity of the network’s channels follows the adaptation law

\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{D_{ij}}=\alpha\frac{g(Q_{ij})}{\sum_{(k,m)\in E}L_{km}\,g(Q_{km})}-\sqrt{D_{ij}}\quad,\quad(i,j)\in E,

(3)

where
g(\cdot)
is an arbitrary function describing the elasticity of the channels,
\alpha=V/\beta
, and
t
is a dimensionless time.
The function
g(\cdot)
, is obtained by minimising the dissipated energy per unit time of the H-P flow,
\mathcal{P}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}Q_{ij}^{2}L_{ij}/D_{ij}
, relative to
\sqrt{D_{ij}}
, resulting in

g(Q_{ij})=Q_{ij}^{2/3}.

(4)

To derive the steady-state solution of the adaptive incompressible H-P flow on a graph, we choose a set of
n
sources and sinks, and we initialise all the edges of the graph
\mathcal{G}
with conductivities
D_{ij}(t=0)>0
. These conductivities remain positive for all the edges of
\mathcal{G}
.
To describe the temporal evolution of the radii of the channels/edges of
\mathcal{G}
, we first solve the linear equations outlined in equation (
2
) relative to the pressures. The fluxes
Q_{ij}
are then determined by the same equations (
2
). The channel conductivities are adapted in accordance with equations (
3
)-(
4
). These equations are integrated using the Euler method with an integration time step of
\Delta t
. These steps are repeated over time, with
\Delta t
time intervals.

Suppose the sources and sinks remain constant over time. In that case, the adaptation process converges to a steady state of the channel conductivities, and a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
is formed with conductivities exceeding a certain threshold. This steady state depends on the initial conductivities, and the subgraph may be disconnected,
[
Almeida, 2021
]
.

To simulate communication systems where the roles of sources or sinks change over time, the conductivity values
D_{ij}
do not settle at a stable value and vary with each iteration. However, the shape of the steady-state graph maintains a fixed geometry.

To simulate all possible itineraries between the nodes of a network and determine a steady-state path of the embedded subgraph in the initial graph
\mathcal{G}
, we utilise four different stochastic algorithms to update the conductivities of the channels and to alter the roles of the source/sink characteristics of the nodes. We consider the following updating algorithms:

1.

Random pair
: At each iteration, a source (with
S_{i}=I_{0}
) and a sink (with
S_{j}=-I_{0}
) are selected randomly from among the
n
sites.

2.

Random half
: At each iteration,
n/2
sources (each with
S_{i}=I_{0}/(\frac{n}{2})
) and
n/2
sinks (each with
S_{j}=-I_{0}/(\frac{n}{2})
) are randomly selected from the
n
sites; if
n
is an odd number, one site is considered inactive (
S_{j}=0
).

3.

Random random
: At each iteration,
1\leq n_{so}\leq n-1
sources are randomly selected from the
n
sites (each with
S_{i}=I_{0}/n_{so}
), and the remaining
n-n_{so}
sites act as sinks (each with
S_{j}=-I_{0}/(n-n_{so})
).

4.

Random source
: At each iteration, a source (with
S_{i}=I_{0}
) is randomly selected from the
n
sites, while the remaining
n-1
sites serve as sinks (each with
S_{j}=-I_{0}/(n-1)
).

Different initial conductivity values lead to distinct steady-state graph or tree shapes. A stochastic search will generate various trees with different shapes and lengths. All simulations were conducted
N_{\text{runs}}
times, each with unique random initial conditions for every configuration with
n
sites.

The adaptive H-P flow equations require at least one source node and one sink node for a tree to form. In this paper, for a configuration with
n
sites, each site is equivalent to every other site, making each site equally likely to be a source or a sink. Different stochastic algorithms may be employed to determine which nodes function as sources and which nodes act as sinks at each iteration step.

4
Optimisation criteria

Two metrics will characterise each steady-state subgraph: its length
L
, and the average length
\nu
between all pairs of sites,
(i,j)
.

The length
L
of a graph is defined by

L=\sum_{(i,j)\in E^{\prime}}L_{ij},

(5)

where
E^{\prime}
is the set of effectively conducting edges; that is, edges with conductivity values above a certain threshold,
E^{\prime}=\{(i,j)\in E:D_{ij}>D_{\text{thresh}}\}
, with
D_{\text{thresh}}=10^{-4}
.

The average length
\nu
between pairs of sites is given by

\nu=\frac{1}{\left({}^{n}_{2}\right)}\sum^{\left({}^{n}_{2}\right)}_{(i,j)\in E^{\prime}}L_{s_{i}s_{j}}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum^{\left({}^{n}_{2}\right)}_{(i,j)\in E^{\prime}}L_{s_{i}s_{j}},

(6)

where
L_{s_{i}s_{j}}
is the shortest path length between the two sites
i
and
j
, with
D_{ij}>D_{\text{thresh}}
.

From the point of view of the user, an optimal subgraph that connects all the sources and sinks should exhibit a low
\nu
value, while a subgraph with a low
L
has lower construction and maintenance costs.

Our aim is to create algorithms that help with decisions about the design of communication systems such as roads, railways, or fibre networks.

6
Conclusions

We applied a formalism describing adaptive incompressible Hagen-Poiseuille flows in channel networks within
\mathcal{N}
-dimensional Euclidean spaces to identify graphs connecting a finite set of nodes. These graphs can represent communication systems and Steiner tree problems. The networks are embedded in a predefined graph
\mathcal{G}
obtained through Delaunay triangulation from a
\mathcal{N}
-dimensional point lattice. The area of the Delaunay triangles can be sufficiently small to enable our system to reach a continuous limit. This embedding makes the adaptation process unidimensional along the graph’s edges. The procedure is computationally efficient, as multiple copies for various initial conditions can be computed, allowing the selection of suboptimal or optimal subgraph and tree solutions that algebraic methods can further optimise. This approach is particularly advantageous as a practical method for searching for approximate solutions to Steiner tree configurations.

We have applied this adaptive incompressible Hagen-Poiseuille formalism to the design of communication systems or networks of veins in organisms. The solutions obtained are either suboptimal or optimal, minimising the lengths of node connections or the cost of transportation. These solutions emerge within a sufficiently large set of steady-state solutions of the adaptive incompressible Hagen-Poiseuille flows. Furthermore, the suboptimality of the solutions highlights the adaptability and resilience of graph networks against perturbations. The approach to designing communication systems presented here offers a complementary and computationally efficient algorithm to explore and develop adaptive network routing

[
Kelly, 1991
]
.
