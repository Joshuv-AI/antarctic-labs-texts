Abstract Argumentation with Subargument Relations
Beishui Liao \affiliations Zhejiang University

Abstract
Dung’s abstract argumentation framework characterises argument acceptability solely via an attack relation, deliberately abstracting from the internal structure of arguments. While this level of abstraction has enabled a rich body of results, it limits the ability to represent structural dependencies that are central in many structured argumentation formalisms, in particular subargument relations. Existing extensions, including bipolar argumentation frameworks, introduce support relations, but these do not capture the asymmetric and constitutive nature of subarguments or their interaction with attacks. In this paper, we study abstract argumentation frameworks enriched with an explicit subargument relation, treated alongside attack as a basic relation. We analyse how subargument relations interact with attacks and examine their impact on fundamental semantic properties. This framework provides a principled abstraction of structural information and clarifies the role of subarguments in abstract acceptability reasoning.



1
Introduction

Dung’s abstract argumentation framework has become the standard abstraction
for nonmonotonic reasoning with arguments, precisely because it separates
argument evaluation from the internal structure of arguments.
By representing arguments as abstract entities related only through attacks,
Dung’s theory provides a uniform semantic foundation that applies across
domains, logics, and application contexts
[
10
]
.
This deliberate abstraction, however, also entails a systematic loss of
structural information about how arguments are constructed and how conflicts
interact with that structure.

In structured argumentation formalisms such as ASPIC
+
and DeLP, this lost
information is reintroduced indirectly
[
15
,
11
]
.
Subarguments are used to determine when attacks are induced, propagated, or
lifted to larger arguments, and notions such as rebuttal or undercut rely
implicitly on internal structure.
As a result, structural dependencies influence acceptability only through
derived attack relations.
While effective for concrete instantiations, this approach conflates conflict
and structure at the abstract level, making it difficult to distinguish
whether a semantic outcome is due to genuine conflict or merely to structural
propagation.

This paper starts from the observation that extension-based equivalence
[
5
]
does
not imply structural or explanatory equivalence.
Different subargument configurations may collapse into the same Dung
framework, yielding identical extensions while differing in how attacks are
grounded, how defence propagates, and how acceptance can be justified.
Such differences become particularly salient in settings concerned with
explainability, modularity, and dynamic updates
[
12
]
, where understanding
why

an argument is accepted matters as much as
whether
it is accepted.

To address this gap, we take a different abstraction stance.
Rather than encoding structure into attacks or committing to a particular
logical instantiation, we treat subargument relations as a primitive component
of abstract argumentation, orthogonal to conflict.
This yields a minimal extension of Dung’s framework in which direct attacks
represent explicit conflict, while subargument relations represent structural
dependency.
Crucially, attacks on subarguments are not assumed to automatically induce
attacks on superarguments at the representation level; such effects are
captured semantically instead.

Building on this separation, we develop structure-sensitive notions of
defence, admissibility, and fixpoint semantics that preserve the core
architecture of Dung’s theory.
Defence is grounded in committed subarguments rather than inferred attack
patterns, and standard semantic guarantees, including monotonicity and the
existence of grounded extensions, are retained.
At the same time, we show that structural information cannot be recovered once
it is forgotten: although subargument relations can be erased without
affecting extensions, they cannot be encoded back into attack-only frameworks
without loss.
This establishes a strict increase in representational expressiveness beyond
Dung frameworks.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 recalls basic notions of abstract argumentation, focusing on Dung
frameworks and standard acceptability semantics that will serve as a
baseline throughout the paper.
Section 3 presents a motivating example that exposes a representational
limitation of attack-only abstraction, independently of any particular
semantics.
Section 4 introduces abstract argumentation frameworks with explicit
subargument relations. In particular, Section 4.1 defines the
representational framework, while Section 4.2 develops structure-sensitive
notions of defence, admissibility, and fixpoint semantics, addressing the
grounding and propagation issues identified earlier.
Section 5 analyses the effect of forgetting structure via a formal
projection to Dung frameworks. It establishes a negative expressiveness
result: although extensions are preserved, structural information about
grounding, propagation, and justification is irretrievably lost.
Section 6 interprets this loss from a principles-based and explanatory
perspective, identifying which structural principles are satisfied by the
proposed framework and violated by attack-only abstraction.
Finally, Section 7 situates the contribution with respect to related work
and outlines directions for future research.

2
Preliminaries

We recall basic notions of Dung’s abstract argumentation, which will serve as
the semantic baseline throughout the paper
[
10
]
.
We also fix notation for reachability and closure operators that will be used
in later sections.

2.1
Dung argumentation frameworks

A
Dung argumentation framework
(AF) is a pair

\mathcal{G}=(A,\mathit{Att}),

where
A
is a finite set of arguments and
\mathit{Att}\subseteq A\times A
is
a binary attack relation.
A set
E\subseteq A
is
conflict-free
if there are no
a,b\in E
such that

(a,b)\in\mathit{Att}
.
An argument
a\in A
is
defended
by
E
if for every
b\in A
with

(b,a)\in\mathit{Att}
there exists
c\in E
such that
(c,b)\in\mathit{Att}
.
A set
E
is
admissible
if it is conflict-free and defends all its
elements;
E
is
complete
if
E
is admissible and each argument
a\in A
that is defended by
E
is in
E
. Furthermore, a maximal complete extension is called a
preferred
extension, and a minimal extension is called the
grounded
extension. Finally, a conflict-free set
E
of arguments is a stable extension, if and only if every argument in
A\setminus E
is attacked by
E
.

We write
\sigma(\mathcal{G})
for the set of extensions of
\mathcal{G}

under a semantics
\sigma
, where
\sigma\in\{\mathsf{adm},\mathsf{cmp},\mathsf{grd},\mathsf{prf},\mathsf{stb}\}
, denoting admissible, complete, grounded, preferred, and stable, respctively.

2.2
Characteristic function and fixpoints

The
characteristic function
of
\mathcal{G}=(A,\mathit{Att})
is the operator

\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{G}}:2^{A}\to 2^{A},\quad\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{G}}(E)=\{\,a\in A\mid a\text{ is defended by }E\,\}.

It is monotone w.r.t. set inclusion and thus admits a least fixpoint, which
coincides with the grounded extension of
\mathcal{G}
.
Moreover,
E
is a complete extension of
\mathcal{G}
iff

E=\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{G}}(E)
.

2.3
Graph reachability

Given a directed graph relation
R\subseteq A\times A
, we write
R^{\ast}
for
its reflexive and transitive closure.
For
x\in A
, we use the shorthand

\mathsf{Reach}_{R}(x)=\{\,y\in A\mid(x,y)\in R^{\ast}\,\},

i.e., the set of nodes reachable from
x
via
R
.

3
A Motivating Example

We illustrate why collapsing subargument structure into attacks is
insufficient to capture how conflicts are grounded and how their effects
propagate, even when extension-based outcomes coincide.
The example shows that different structural configurations may induce the
same Dung framework while supporting different explanations of acceptance and
different patterns of potential propagation.

The setting.

Consider a set of arguments

A=\{a,b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},b_{5}\}.

Intuitively, the arguments
b_{1},\dots,b_{5}
form a chain of increasingly complex
arguments, where each argument extends the previous one.
Formally, assume a structural dependency such that
b_{i}
is a subargument of

b_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,4
.

In addition, assume two direct attacks:

a
attacks the minimal argument
b_{1}
, and the maximal argument
b_{5}

attacks
a
.
No other direct attacks are present. Here, we say that an argument
a
directly attacks another argument
b
, if and only if there exists no subargumet of
b
, such that
a
directly attacks this subargument.

Abstraction by attacks alone.

If the structural dependencies between the
b_{i}
are represented by induced
(or indirect) attacks, the resulting Dung framework contains the same argument
set
A
and the attack relation

\{(a,b_{1}),(a,b_{2}),(a,b_{3}),(a,b_{4}),(a,b_{5}),(b_{5},a)\}.

At this level of abstraction, the subargument chain among the
b_{i}
is no
longer represented explicitly, and its effect is captured only through the
resulting attack pattern.

This observation highlights a first limitation of attack-only abstraction.
Arguments that play a purely structural role become indistinguishable from
arguments that are directly involved in conflict, and different abstract
frameworks with distinct subargument relations may collapse into the same
Dung argumentation framework
[
15
]
.
As a result, structural differences between arguments are erased, even though
they may give rise to different justifications or patterns of dependency.

Structural grounding of defence.

From a structural perspective, the acceptability of
b_{5}
depends on the
acceptability of its subarguments, including
b_{1}
.
Since
a
attacks
b_{1}
, and
b_{1}
is a subargument of
b_{5}
, the conflict
initiated by
a
structurally undermines
b_{5}
through its internal
dependencies.
In this sense, the attack on
b_{1}
affects not only the minimal argument but
also all of its superarguments.

Although
b_{5}
counterattacks
a
, this counterattack operates only at the level
of
b_{5}
itself, the maximal argument with respect to the subargument relation.
From a structural perspective, however, the acceptability of
b_{5}
depends on
the acceptability of its subarguments, including
b_{1}
.
The attack from
a
targets this structurally crucial subargument, yet this
attack is not addressed at the level where it arises, namely by any argument
that directly counteracts the conflict on
b_{1}
itself.
As a result, the defence of
b_{5}
is established solely by counterattacking
a
,
without requiring any response to the attack on the subargument
b_{1}
on which

b_{5}
depends.

This distinction is invisible at the Dung level, where defence is evaluated
exclusively in terms of attacks between arguments.
As a result, attack-only abstraction does not distinguish between defence that
is grounded in accepted subarguments and defence that merely neutralises an
attacker without resolving the underlying structural conflict.

Propagation beyond direct conflict.

The example further illustrates a difference in how the effects of a conflict
may propagate once structural dependencies are taken into account.
If the status of an intermediate argument such as
b_{3}
changes, this change
may affect the acceptability of its superarguments
b_{4}
and
b_{5}
due to the
underlying subargument relations, even in the absence of any additional direct
attacks.

Under attack-only abstraction, however, this potential for propagation is
entirely lost.
Since
b_{3}
is not related to
b_{4}
or
b_{5}
in terms of the attack relation,
it cannot influence the status of any other argument.

Thus, although attack-only abstraction yields a well-defined extension, it
fails to represent which arguments are structurally dependent on others and
therefore potentially affected by changes in their status
[
7
]
.

Implications.

Taken together, the observations above show that attack-only abstraction
conceals essential structural information.
Although different configurations may yield the same set of extensions at the
Dung level, they differ in how conflicts are grounded, how defence is justified,
and how the effects of attacks may propagate through structural dependencies.
These differences are not artefacts of a particular semantics, but arise at
the level of representation itself.

This indicates that the limitation does not lie in Dung’s evaluation
mechanism, but in the expressive power of an abstraction that represents
conflict without structure.
To make these distinctions explicit, an abstract framework is needed in which
structural dependency is represented independently of conflict, rather than
encoded indirectly through induced attacks or semantic conventions.

The next section introduces such a framework by extending abstract
argumentation with subargument relations as a primitive component.
This extension preserves the core architecture of Dung’s theory while making
it possible to reason explicitly about structure-sensitive defence,
justification, and propagation.

4
Subargument Frameworks and Structure-sensitive Semantics

The motivating example in Section
3
reveals a limitation of
attack-only abstraction that arises prior to any semantic evaluation.
When structural dependencies between arguments are collapsed into induced
attacks, essential information about how conflicts are grounded and how their
effects may propagate is lost.
Addressing this limitation requires a representational refinement rather than
a change of semantics.

In this section, we introduce an abstract argumentation framework that makes
structural dependency explicit while preserving the core abstraction of
Dung’s theory.
The key idea is to separate two orthogonal dimensions of argumentation:

conflict
, represented by direct attacks, and
structure
, represented
by subargument relations.
By treating subarguments as a primitive component of the framework, structural
information is retained at the abstract level instead of being encoded
indirectly through attack patterns.

Concretely, the framework introduced in this section is designed to address three representational deficiencies identified in Section 3: (i) the lack of a notion of defence grounded in subarguments, (ii) the inability to represent structural propagation beyond direct conflict, and (iii) the collapse of structurally distinct configurations under attack-only abstraction.

4.1
Argumentation Frameworks with Subargument Relations

Definition 1

(Subargument Argumentation Framework)
.

A
Subargument Argumentation Framework
(SAF) is a triple

\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub}),

where:

•

A
is a finite set of arguments;

•

\mathit{Att}\subseteq A\times A
is a binary relation
representing
direct attacks
between arguments;

•

\mathit{Sub}\subseteq A\times A
is a binary relation
representing the
subargument relation
, where
(a,b)\in\mathit{Sub}
means that
a
is a subargument of
b
.

The subargument relation
\mathit{Sub}
is assumed to be acyclic. Meanwhile, if
(a,b)\in\mathit{Att}
, then there exists no
b^{\prime}\in A
such that
(b^{\prime},b)\in\mathit{Sub}
and
(a,b^{\prime})\in\mathit{Att}
.

The introduction of a subargument relation at the abstract level does not
commit to any particular structured argumentation formalism.
Rather, it isolates structural dependency as a representational component
that can be shared across different instantiations.
At this stage, the framework specifies
where
structural information is
located, without yet determining
how
it affects acceptability or
defence.
These issues are addressed in the following section.

Having introduced a framework in which conflict and structure are
represented by distinct relations, we now clarify how this design departs
from common abstraction practices in structured argumentation.

In many structured argumentation formalisms, attacks directed at
subarguments are systematically lifted to attacks on the corresponding
superarguments
[
15
,
11
]
.
When such formalisms are abstracted, this lifting is typically realised by
expanding the attack relation, so that all structure-induced interactions
are encoded directly into
\mathit{Att}
.
As a result, the abstract framework no longer distinguishes between
attacks targeting a specific component of an argument and attacks
targeting the argument as a whole.

In the present framework, we deliberately refrain from such encodings.
The attack relation
\mathit{Att}
is restricted to represent direct
conflicts only, without any form of structural propagation.
Subargument relations are captured separately by
\mathit{Sub}
and are
not derived from attacks.
This choice ensures that structural dependencies are not prematurely
collapsed into the conflict relation.

Importantly, this separation does not deny that attacks on subarguments
may affect the acceptability of superarguments.
Rather, it postpones the treatment of such effects to the semantic level,
where they can be addressed in a principled and transparent manner.
By keeping conflict and structure independent at the abstract level, the
framework preserves the information required to reason about defence and
commitment without conflating distinct justificatory roles.

In summary, conflict and structure are treated as orthogonal dimensions of
abstract argumentation.
This orthogonality provides the foundation for the structure-sensitive
notions of defence and admissibility developed in the next section.

By distinguishing conflict from structure at the representational level, the
framework introduced in this section provides the basis for defining
structure-sensitive notions of defence and admissibility, which we develop
next.

4.2
Structured-sensitive Defence

The framework introduced in Section
4.1
makes structural
dependencies between arguments explicit.
However, as illustrated by the motivating example in
Section
3
, representing structure alone is not sufficient:
the central difficulty lies in how defence and acceptability should take
structural dependencies into account.

In particular, Section
3
shows that counterattacking an
attacker at the surface level does not necessarily resolve a conflict that is
grounded in an attacked subargument.
This observation motivates the need for a notion of defence that is sensitive
to subargument structure, while remaining compatible with the core semantic
architecture of Dung’s theory.

We first address the defence-grounding problem identified in
Section
3
by refining the notion of defence so that attacks
on subarguments cannot be neutralised without being structurally addressed.

Definition 2

(Structure-sensitive defence)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
and let
E\subseteq A
.
An argument
a\in A
is
defended by
E
if for every argument

x\in Sub^{\ast}(a)
and every argument
b\in A
such that

(b,x)\in\mathit{Att}
, there exist an argument
c\in E
and an argument

b^{\prime}\in Sub^{\ast}(b)
such that
(c,b^{\prime})\in\mathit{Att}
.

This definition quantifies defence over the entire subargument structure of
an argument.
In particular, it rules out vacuous defence of a superargument whose
subarguments remain undefended, thereby aligning defence with structural
commitment.

4.3
Admissibility and Fixpoints

We next revisit conflict-freeness and admissibility in the presence of
subargument relations.
Since accepting an argument implicitly commits one to accepting its
subarguments, conflicts must be evaluated relative to the full structural
closure of arguments.

The resulting notion of admissibility internalises a commitment to
subarguments: accepting an argument entails accepting its structural basis.

Definition 3

(Structure-aware conflict-freeness)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
.
Two arguments
a,b\in A
are
in conflict
if there exist

x\in Sub^{\ast}(a)
and
y\in Sub^{\ast}(b)
such that

(x,y)\in\mathit{Att}
.
A set
E\subseteq A
is
conflict-free
if no two arguments in
E
are
in conflict.

This notion ensures that conflicts arising at any structural level are
taken into account, even when attacks are defined only as direct conflicts.

Definition 4

(Admissible set)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
.
A set
E\subseteq A
is
admissible
if:

1.

E
is conflict-free; and

2.

every argument
a\in E
is defended by
E
.

Under this definition, admissibility internalises subargument commitment.
An argument can be admitted only if its entire subargument structure is
protected against attack, even though subargument closure is not imposed
as an explicit syntactic requirement.

Despite this refinement, the overall fixpoint-based architecture of Dung’s
semantics is preserved.
Let

\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)=\{\,a\in A\mid a\text{ is defended by }E\,\}.

Definition 5

(Characteristic function)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
.
The
characteristic function
of
\mathcal{F}
is the operator

\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}:2^{A}\to 2^{A}
defined by

\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)=\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E).

A crucial observation is that, under structure-sensitive defence,

\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
is subargument-closed.
Hence, no additional closure operation is required at the semantic level.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
be an SAF and
E\subseteq A
.
Then
\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)=\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
is
subargument-closed, i.e.,

a\in\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)\ \Rightarrow\ \mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)\subseteq\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E).

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
and let
a^{\prime}\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)
.
We show that
a^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
.

Fix any argument
b\in A
such that
(b,a^{\prime})\in\mathit{Att}
.
Since
a^{\prime}\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)
,
b
is an attacker of a subargument of
a
.
By the definition of (structure-sensitive) defence, the fact that
a
is
defended by
E
entails that every attack on any subargument of
a
is
countered from
E
.
Hence, there exists
c\in E
such that there exists
b^{\prime}\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(b)
and
(c,b^{\prime})\in\mathit{Att}
.
This is exactly the defence condition for
a^{\prime}
with respect to
E
.
Since
b
was arbitrary,
a^{\prime}
is defended by
E
, i.e.,

a^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
.
Therefore,
\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)\subseteq\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
, and

\mathsf{Def}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
is subargument-closed.
∎

Proposition 2
.

The operator
\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}
is monotone.

Proof.

If
E\subseteq E^{\prime}
, any counterattack witnessing the defence of an argument
by
E
is also available from
E^{\prime}
, which yields

\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)\subseteq\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}(E^{\prime})
.
∎

Definition 6

(Complete extension)
.

A set
E\subseteq A
is a
complete extension
of

\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
if it is admissible and contains
every argument that it defends.

Theorem 1

(Fixpoint characterisation)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
and let
E\subseteq A
.
The following are equivalent:

1.

E
is a complete extension of
\mathcal{F}
;

2.

E
is conflict-free and
E=\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
;

3.

E
is a conflict-free fixpoint of
\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Corollary 1

(Grounded extension)
.

The operator
\mathsf{F}_{\mathcal{F}}
has a least fixpoint, which
corresponds to the grounded extension of
\mathcal{F}
and is contained in
every complete extension.

This section shows that refining defence to account for subargument
structure does not disrupt the fixpoint-based backbone of abstract
argumentation.
Instead, it yields a semantics that is structurally more informative while
remaining a conservative extension of Dung’s theory.

By a slight abuse of notation, we use
\sigma(\mathcal{F})
to denote the set of extensions of
\mathcal{F}
under the semantics
\sigma
.

Although the structure-sensitive semantics resolves the grounding and propagation issues identified earlier, it raises a further question: if these structural distinctions are semantically meaningful, can they be faithfully recovered once structure is abstracted away? The next section shows that the answer is negative.

5
Forgetting Structure and Structural Expressiveness

The introduction of subargument relations raises a natural question:
if structural information can be ignored without affecting extensions, does it
provide any genuine expressive benefit at the abstract level?
This section addresses this question by analysing the effect of
forgetting

structure and by identifying what is lost when structural dependencies are
collapsed into attack-only representations.

We show that, although subargument relations can be forgotten in a way that
preserves extension-based semantics, this preservation comes at a fundamental
cost.
Different frameworks with distinct structural configurations may induce the
same Dung argumentation framework and therefore yield identical extensions,
while differing in how defence is grounded, how conflicts propagate, and how
arguments are structurally justified.
These differences cannot be recovered from the attack relation alone.

The results in this section establish a negative but essential conclusion:
the expressive power of attack-only abstraction is strictly weaker than that of
frameworks that represent structure explicitly.
This limitation is not a matter of semantic choice, but a consequence of
information loss at the level of abstraction itself.

5.1
Forgetting the subargument relation

We begin by formalising the process of forgetting subargument relations.
Intuitively, forgetting collapses all structure-induced interactions into a
single attack relation, yielding a standard Dung argumentation framework.

Definition 7

(Forgetful projection)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
be a SAF.
The
forgetful projection
of
\mathcal{F}
is the Dung framework

\Gamma(\mathcal{F})=(A,\mathit{Att}^{\Gamma}),

where

\mathit{Att}^{\Gamma}=\{\,(a,b)\mid\exists b^{\prime}\in Sub^{\ast}(b)\text{ such that }(a,b^{\prime})\in\mathit{Att}\,\}.

The projection
\Gamma(\mathcal{F})
captures the cumulative effect of all
direct attacks on subarguments, without retaining information about the
structural level at which such attacks occur.

We begin by showing that forgetting structure does not affect extensions.

Proposition 3

(Forgetting preserves extensions)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
and let

\Gamma(\mathcal{F})
be its forgetful projection.
For every Dung semantics

\sigma\in\{\mathsf{adm},\mathsf{cmp},\mathsf{grd},\mathsf{prf},\mathsf{stb}\}
, we have

\sigma(\mathcal{F})=\sigma(\Gamma(\mathcal{F}))
.

Proof.

The construction of
\mathit{Att}^{\Gamma}
ensures that any attack on an
argument or its subarguments in
\mathcal{F}
is reflected as an
attack in
\Gamma(\mathcal{F})
.
Conversely, any defence in
\mathcal{F}
against attacks on subarguments is
mirrored by a defence in
\Gamma(\mathcal{F})
against the corresponding
projected attacks.
Since the characteristic function in both settings coincides on defended
arguments, the resulting extensions are identical under all standard Dung
semantics.
∎

Proposition
3
confirms that subargument relations do not
increase the discriminative power of abstract semantics when only the
resulting extensions are considered.

5.2
Expressiveness beyond extensions

We now turn to the expressive power of the framework.
While forgetting preserves extensions, it does not preserve structure.
In particular, distinct subargument frameworks may collapse into the same
Dung framework under forgetful projection.

Theorem 2

(Strict increase in structural expressiveness)
.

There exist two SAFs

\mathcal{F}_{1}=(A,\mathit{Att}_{1},\mathit{Sub}_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}_{2}=(A,\mathit{Att}_{2},\mathit{Sub}_{2})

such that:

1.

\mathcal{F}_{1}\neq\mathcal{F}_{2}
; and

2.

\Gamma(\mathcal{F}_{1})=\Gamma(\mathcal{F}_{2})
.

\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
can be mapped to the same Dungs AAF, but are not equivalent at the structural level, in the sense that they induce different patterns of defence and structural commitment.

Definition 8

(Structural propagation reach)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
be a SAF and let
x\in A
.
The
structural propagation reach
of
x
in
\mathcal{F}
is defined as

\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\;=\;\{\,y\in A\mid x\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(y)\,\}.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)
contains exactly those arguments
whose acceptability may depend on the status of
x
due to structural
dependency, i.e., the superarguments of
x
.

Definition 9

(Attack-based propagation reach)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(A,\mathit{Att})
be a Dung AAF and let

x\in A
.
The
attack-based propagation reach
of
x
in
\mathcal{G}
is defined as

\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)\;=\;\{\,y\in A\mid\text{there exists a directed path from $x$ to $y$ in }\mathit{Att}\,\}.

Theorem 3

(Non-encodability of structural propagation)
.

Let
\Gamma
be the forgetful projection defined in
Definition
7
.
There exists a subargument argumentation framework

\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
and an argument
x\in A
such that

\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\;\neq\;\mathsf{Reach}_{\Gamma(\mathcal{F})}(x).

Consequently, structural propagation in subargument frameworks cannot be
encoded in Dung frameworks via forgetting the subargument relation.

Proof sketch.

Consider a framework
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
with

A=\{a,b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},b_{5}\},

where
(b_{i},b_{i+1})\in\mathit{Sub}
for
i=1,\dots,4
, so that

b_{1}\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(b_{5})
.
Assume the only direct attacks are
(a,b_{1})\in\mathit{Att}
and

(b_{5},a)\in\mathit{Att}
.

Let
x=b_{3}
.
By Definition
8
, since
b_{3}\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(b_{4})
and

b_{3}\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(b_{5})
, we have

\{b_{4},b_{5}\}\subseteq\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{F}}(x).

Now consider the forgetful projection

\Gamma(\mathcal{F})=(A,\mathit{Att})
.
In
\Gamma(\mathcal{F})
, the argument
b_{3}
does not participate in any attack,
and there is no attack path starting from
b_{3}
.
Hence, by Definition
9
,

\mathsf{Reach}_{\Gamma(\mathcal{F})}(x)=\{x\}.

Therefore,

\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\neq\mathsf{Reach}_{\Gamma(\mathcal{F})}(x)
,
which shows that the structural propagation induced by subargument relations
cannot be captured by forgetting structure.
∎

6
Principles and Explanatory Perspective

The negative results of Section 5 show that the loss incurred by forgetting structure is not reflected at the level of extensions. To understand what is lost and why this loss matters independently of semantic choice, we now turn to a principles-based analysis.

6.1
Principles for Structure-aware Argumentation

The results of the previous sections establish that explicitly representing
subargument structure strictly increases expressive power, even when
extension-based semantics are preserved under abstraction.
In this section, we analyse this increase in expressive power from a
principles-based perspective.

The principles introduced here do not impose additional normative constraints
on argumentation semantics.
Rather, they make explicit several structural assumptions that are implicitly
relied upon in structured reasoning but are violated when structure is
forgotten.
Each principle is shown to be satisfied by the semantics developed in this
paper, while failing under attack-only abstraction.

Principle 1

(Separation of Conflict and Structure)
.

An argumentation framework satisfies the
Separation of Conflict and
Structure
principle if conflict relations and structural dependency
relations are represented by independent primitives, and neither can be
derived from the other.

Theorem 4
.

The framework
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
satisfies
Principle
1
.

Proof.

By Definition
1
,
\mathit{Att}
represents direct attacks only
and is not closed under structural propagation.
The subargument relation
\mathit{Sub}
is introduced as a primitive and is
not definable from
\mathit{Att}
.
Hence changes in
\mathit{Sub}
do not affect
\mathit{Att}
, and vice versa,
establishing independence between conflict and structure.
∎

Principle 2

(Structure-sensitive Defence)
.

A semantics satisfies
Structure-sensitive Defence
if an argument is
defended only when all attacks against its subarguments are neutralised.

Theorem 5
.

The defence notion introduced in
Definition
2
satisfies
Principle
2
.

Proof.

By Definition
2
, an argument
a
is defended by a set
E
only
if for every attacker
b
of
a
, either
b
or one of its subarguments is
attacked by some argument in
E
.
Since attacks against any
x\in Sub^{\ast}(a)
are explicitly considered,
defence cannot be established without addressing subargument-level
conflicts.
∎

Principle 3

(Commitment-based Admissibility)
.

A semantics satisfies
Commitment-based Admissibility
if accepting an
argument entails a commitment to defending its subarguments.

Theorem 6
.

Every admissible set under the proposed semantics satisfies
Principle
3
.

Proof.

Let
E
be an admissible set and
a\in E
.
By admissibility,
a
is defended by
E
.
By Theorem
5
, all attacks against subarguments of
a

must be neutralised by
E
.
Thus accepting
a
entails a commitment to its subargument structure.
∎

Note that this principle does not require subarguments of
a
to belong to

E
, but only that conflicts targeting them are defended.

Principle 4

(Conservative Extension)
.

A framework is a conservative extension of Dung argumentation if it preserves
all Dung semantics under a suitable projection.

Theorem 7
.

The proposed framework satisfies
Principle
4
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3
, forgetting the subargument relation
preserves all standard Dung semantics.
Hence the framework does not alter extension-based outcomes and is a
conservative extension of Dung argumentation.
∎

The discussion so far suggests that even when extension membership is
preserved by forgetting structure, an important gap remains at the level of

justificatory information
.
In a subargument framework, the acceptability of an argument may depend on
which
subarguments
are attacked and how these attacks are defended.
Such information is invisible in plain Dung frameworks, where conflicts are
only represented between whole arguments.

We formalise this gap via an explanation principle.

Definition 10

(Local justification)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
be a SAF,
let
E\subseteq A
, and let
a\in E
.
A set
J\subseteq E
is a
local justification of
a
in
E
if:

(J1)

J
is subargument-closed (w.r.t.
\mathit{Sub}
), i.e.,
if
x\in J
then
\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(x)\subseteq J
;

(J2)

\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)\subseteq J
; and

(J3)

J
defends the whole subargument closure of
a
, i.e., for every

x\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)
and every
b\in A
with
(b,x)\in\mathit{Att}
,
there exists
c\in J
such that either

(c,b)\in\mathit{Att}
, or there exists
b^{\prime}\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(b)
such that
(c,b^{\prime})\in\mathit{Att}
.

A local justification
J
is
minimal
if no strict subset of
J
is a
local justification of
a
in
E
.

Definition 11

(Explanation function)
.

Fix a semantics
\sigma
. An
explanation function
for
\sigma
maps each
pair
(E,a)
with
E\in\sigma(\mathcal{F})
and
a\in E
to a minimal local
justification
\mathsf{Expl}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\sigma}(E,a)
.

Principle 5

(Subargument-based Justification)
.

A semantics
\sigma
satisfies the
Subargument-based Justification

principle if for every framework
\mathcal{F}
, every extension

E\in\sigma(\mathcal{F})
, and every argument
a\in E
, there exists a
minimal local justification of
a
in
E
(equivalently, an explanation
function exists).

Intuitively, Principle
5
requires that whenever an argument is
accepted, there is an explicit
local witness
—closed under subarguments
and sufficient for defending the whole subargument closure—explaining why its
acceptance is warranted.

Theorem 8

(Satisfaction of Subargument-based Justification)
.

For any semantics
\sigma
defined in Section
4.2
based on
subargument-closed admissibility and the structure-sensitive defence operator,
Principle
5
holds.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
and let
E\in\mathsf{Sem}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{F})
.
By definition of
\sigma
(in particular, admissibility),
E
is subargument-closed
and defends each of its members (in the structure-sensitive sense).
Fix
a\in E
.

Define

J_{0}\ :=\ \mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)\ \cup\ \{\,c\in E\mid\exists x\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a),\exists b\in A:(b,x)\in\mathit{Att}\ \text{and $c$ is a defender of $x$ against $b$}\,\}.

Since
E
is subargument-closed,
\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)\subseteq E
and thus

J_{0}\subseteq E
.
Moreover, closing
J_{0}
under
\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}
(still within
E
) yields a
set
J\subseteq E
satisfying (J1)–(J2).
Finally, (J3) holds because for every attack on any
x\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)
,
admissibility of
E
guarantees the existence of a defender in
E
, and by
construction such defenders are included in
J
(or their relevant subarguments
are included via closure).

Thus
J
is a local justification of
a
in
E
.
Existence of a minimal one follows by finiteness of
A
and standard subset
minimisation.
∎

The key point is that Principle
5
is not determined by the
attack graph alone: it depends on the subargument closure
\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}(a)

that must be included in any local justification.

Theorem 9

(Forgetting structure is explanation-losing)
.

There is no explanation function that can be defined solely on the forgetful
projection
\Gamma(\mathcal{F})
and that satisfies
Principle
5
for all subargument frameworks
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof sketch.

We use indistinguishability under forgetting.
By the structural expressiveness result of Section
5.1
,
there exist two distinct frameworks

\mathcal{F}_{1}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub}_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}_{2}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub}_{2})

such that they have the same forgetful projection:

\Gamma(\mathcal{F}_{1})=\Gamma(\mathcal{F}_{2})
,
and (by extension preservation of
\Gamma
) they induce the same
\sigma
-extensions.

Choose an argument
a\in A
such that
\mathit{Sub}_{1}^{\ast}(a)\neq\mathit{Sub}_{2}^{\ast}(a)
.
Such an
a
must exist since
\mathcal{F}_{1}\neq\mathcal{F}_{2}
while the attack
part is identical.
Let
E
be any common
\sigma
-extension containing
a
.

By Definition
10
(J2), every local justification of
a
in
E

must contain
\mathit{Sub}_{i}^{\ast}(a)
in
\mathcal{F}_{i}
.
Hence any minimal local justification in
\mathcal{F}_{1}
differs from any minimal
local justification in
\mathcal{F}_{2}
(at least on the required subargument
closure).
Therefore, any explanation function satisfying Principle
5

must output different explanations for
(E,a)
on
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
.

However, if an explanation mechanism only has access to the forgetful
projection
\Gamma(\mathcal{F})
, then it cannot distinguish
\mathcal{F}_{1}
from

\mathcal{F}_{2}
and must return the same output on both, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 2

(Attack-only encodings cannot be justificatorily faithful)
.

Even when
\Gamma(\mathcal{F})
preserves extension membership, it cannot
preserve subargument-based justifications in the sense of
Principle
5
.

Together, these principles clarify the sense in which representing structure
explicitly yields more informative and more faithful abstractions, without
departing from the core semantic commitments of Dung’s theory.

While the principles above clarify what is preserved and what is violated by attack-only abstraction, they do not yet explain how structure-sensitive reasoning can be operationalised. We address this question next by decomposing acceptability into conflict-handling and status-dependent components.

6.2
Explanation and Status Propagation

This section provides an explanatory perspective on the framework and semantics
developed in the previous sections.
Rather than introducing a new semantics or additional technical machinery, we
propose a conceptual decomposition that clarifies how structure-sensitive
acceptability arises from explicit subargument relations.

The key distinction is between
conflict-handling arguments
(or briefly
\mathsf{CH}
-arguments), which
participate directly in attack relations, and
status-dependent
arguments
(or briefly
\mathsf{SD}
-arguments), whose acceptability is inherited through structural dependency.
This decomposition does not alter the semantics defined in
Section
4.2
; instead, it makes explicit how defence and
acceptability propagate once conflict has been resolved at structurally
relevant points.

Example (Conflict-handling vs. status-dependent arguments).

Consider the SAF introduced in the proof of Theorem
3
.
In this framework, only arguments
a
,
b_{1}
, and
b_{5}
participate directly
in conflicts. The remaining arguments
b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}
neither attack nor are
attacked by any argument, and their acceptability is entirely determined by
the status of their subarguments. Accordingly, they are status-dependent
arguments, while
\{a,b_{1},b_{5}\}
form the conflict-handling core.

Viewed in this way, the distinction between
\mathsf{CH}
-arguments and

\mathsf{SD}
-arguments explains why structural information cannot be
recovered from attack-only abstraction.
When structure is forgotten, arguments that merely inherit their status become
indistinguishable from arguments that actively resolve conflicts, even though
they play fundamentally different roles in justification and propagation.

Definition 12

(Conflict-handling and status-dependent arguments)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
be an SAF.
An argument
a\in A
is
conflict-handling
if it participates in a
direct conflict, i.e.,

a\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}\quad\text{iff}\quad\exists b\in A\;\big((a,b)\in\mathit{Att}\ \lor\ (b,a)\in\mathit{Att}\big).

An argument is
status-dependent
if it is not conflict-handling:

A_{\mathsf{SD}}\;=\;A\setminus A_{\mathsf{CH}}.

In this definition,
A_{\mathsf{CH}}
denotes the set of
\mathsf{CH}
-arguments, while
A_{\mathsf{SD}}
denotes the set of
\mathsf{SD}
-arguments.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{CH}
-arguments are those that actively

handle conflicts
at the abstract level, whereas
\mathsf{SD}
-arguments
do not engage in direct conflicts and should receive their status through
their structural dependencies.

The proposed evaluation proceeds in two steps.
First, we compute extensions on the core
\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
Second, we propagate acceptance to superarguments whose subargument
requirements are met.

Definition 13

(Subargument closure induced by a core set)
.

Let
E\subseteq A_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
The
status-lift
of
E
to all arguments is

\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)\;=\;\{\,a\in A\mid Sub^{\ast}(a)\cap A_{\mathsf{CH}}\subseteq E\,\}.

The set
\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
contains precisely those arguments
whose
conflict-relevant
subarguments are accepted in the core.
In particular, any
\mathsf{SD}
-arguments is accepted if none of its
core subarguments is rejected.

Definition 14

(Conflict-handling core framework)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,Att,Sub)
be an SAF, and
let
A_{\mathsf{CH}}\subseteq A
be the set of
\mathsf{CH}
-arguments.
Let
\Gamma(\mathcal{F})=(A,Att^{\Gamma})
be the forgetful projection of

\mathcal{F}
as defined in Definition 7.
The
conflict-handling core framework
induced by
\mathcal{F}
is the
Dung argumentation framework

\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}=(A_{\mathsf{CH}},Att^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{CH}}),

where

Att^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{CH}}=Att^{\Gamma}\cap(A_{\mathsf{CH}}\times A_{\mathsf{CH}}).

Definition 15

(Core-based semantics)
.

Given a Dung semantics
\sigma
,
A set
S\subseteq A
is a
\sigma
-extension induced by the core

if there exists
E\in\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}})
, such that

S\;=\;\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E).

We write
\sigma_{\mathsf{core}}(\mathcal{F})
for the set of all such
S
.

Definition
15
makes the intended asymmetry explicit:
extensions are computed on conflict-handling arguments, while the remaining
arguments receive their status through structural dependency.
This captures a natural explanatory separation: core arguments determine

how conflicts are resolved
, and superarguments inherit acceptability
once their conflict-relevant bases are secured.

Theorem 10

(Core-first computation and lifting)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
be an SAF.
The following hold.

(1)

(
Completeness is core-determined
)
A set
S\subseteq A
is a complete extension of
\mathcal{F}
(under the
structure-aware semantics of Section
4.2
) iff there exists
a complete extension
E
of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
such that

S=\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E).

Equivalently,

\mathsf{cmp}(\mathcal{F})=\big\{\ \mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)\ \bigm|\ E\in\mathsf{cmp}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}})\ \big\}.

(2)

(
Grounded extension by core fixpoint
)
Let
G_{\mathsf{CH}}
be the grounded extension of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
Then the grounded extension
G
of
\mathcal{F}
exists and is given by

G=\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(G_{\mathsf{CH}}).

Proof.

We use two key observations.

(i) Attacks only come from the core.

According to Definition
12
, no argument in
A_{\mathsf{SD}}
attacks or is attacked.
Hence every direct attack
(b,x)\in\mathit{Att}
satisfies

b\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}
and
x\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
In particular, when evaluating structure-sensitive defence
(Definition
2
), only core arguments can occur as attackers.

(ii) Defence depends only on core subarguments.

Fix
E\subseteq A_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
For any
a\in A
, consider any attack in the
sense of the structure-aware semantics against a subargument

x\in Sub^{*}(a)
. By (i), such an
x
must lie in
A_{\mathsf{CH}}
and the
attacker must also lie in
A_{\mathsf{CH}}
. Therefore, whether
a
is
(defended/acceptable) w.r.t.
E
depends only on which
core

subarguments of
a
are in
E
.

(1) Completeness is core-determined.

(
\Rightarrow
).

Let
S\subseteq A
be complete under the structure-aware semantics of
Section 4.2 and set
E:=S\cap A_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
We show that
E
is a complete extension of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
and
that
S=\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
.

First,
E
is conflict-free in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
Indeed, if
(c,d)\in Att^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{CH}}
with
c,d\in E
, then by the
definition of
Att^{\Gamma}
(Definition 7) there exists some

y\in Sub^{*}(d)
such that
(c,y)\in Att
. By (i),
y\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
Since
d\in S
and
S
is conflict-free under the structure-aware semantics,
no accepted argument in
S
can have an accepted core subargument that is
directly attacked by an accepted core argument; hence such
c
and
d

cannot both belong to
S
, contradicting
c,d\in E\subseteq S
.

Second, every
e\in E
is defended by
E
in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
Let
(c,e)\in Att^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{CH}}
. Then there exists

y\in Sub^{*}(e)
with
(c,y)\in Att
. Since
e\in S
and
S
is admissible,
the attack
(c,y)
is countered by some
t\in S
. By (i),
t\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}
,
so
t\in E
, and
t
defeats
c
already at the core level, hence
E

defends
e
in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.

Third,
E
contains all core arguments defended by
E
in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
If
e\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}
is defended by
E
in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
,
then by (ii) the same defence suffices for
e
w.r.t.
S
in
\mathcal{F}
,
so by completeness of
S
we have
e\in S
, i.e.,
e\in E
.

Thus
E
is complete in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
Finally, by Definition 13 and (ii), the membership of any
a\in A
in a
complete extension is determined exactly by whether all its core
subarguments are accepted in
E
, hence
S=\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
.

(
\Leftarrow
).

Let
E\in\mathsf{cmp}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}})
and put
S:=\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
.
We show that
S
is complete in
\mathcal{F}
.

Conflict-freeness.
Any conflict under the structure-aware semantics
must arise from a direct attack on some core subargument (by (i)); but if

a,b\in S
generated such a conflict, then the corresponding core arguments
would yield an induced conflict in
Att^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{CH}}
, contradicting
that
E
is conflict-free.

Admissibility.
Let
a\in S
and consider any direct attack
(c,x)\in Att

with
x\in Sub^{*}(a)
. By (i),
c,x\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}
and thus
c\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
Since
a\in S=\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
, all core subarguments of
a
(in
particular
x
) are in
E
. Hence the attack on
x
corresponds to an induced
attack on the core level, so
c
attacks (at least) the relevant core part
of
a
in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
. Because
E
is admissible (as part of
completeness), there exists
t\in E
that counterattacks
c
in

\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
, i.e.
(t,c)\in Att^{\Gamma}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
By Definition 7, this yields a direct counterattack against a core
subargument of
c
in
\mathcal{F}
, and thus
t\in S
defends
a
in

\mathcal{F}
. Therefore
S
is admissible.

Completeness.
Let
a\in A
be defended by
S
in
\mathcal{F}
.
By (ii), this defence depends only on the core part
E=S\cap A_{\mathsf{CH}}
,
so all core subarguments of
a
are defended by
E
at the core level.
Since
E
is complete in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
, these core components
are in
E
, hence
a\in\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)=S
by Definition 13.
So
S
contains all arguments it defends, i.e.
S
is complete.

This establishes (1).

(2) Grounded extension by core fixpoint.

Let
G_{\mathsf{CH}}
be the grounded extension of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.
By (1),
\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(G_{\mathsf{CH}})
is a complete extension of

\mathcal{F}
. Moreover, since grounded extensions are the
\subseteq
-least
complete extensions, the correspondence in (1) preserves minimality, hence
the grounded extension
G
of
\mathcal{F}
exists and equals

G=\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(G_{\mathsf{CH}})
.
∎

Example (Status determination by core arguments).

We illustrate how the core–lifting perspective explains
structure-sensitive acceptability without introducing a new semantics.

Consider the framework

F=(A,Att,Sub),

where

A=\{a,b,c\},\quad Att=\{(a,b)\},\quad Sub=\{(b,c)\}.

Arguments
a
and
b
are conflict-handling arguments, since they
participate in a direct attack, whereas
c
is status-dependent:
it does not attack or get attacked by any argument, but structurally
depends on
b
.

The conflict-handling core is therefore

F_{\mathsf{CH}}=(\{a,b\},\{(a,b)\}).

Under grounded (and complete) semantics,
F_{\mathsf{CH}}
has the unique
extension

E=\{a\},

since
b
is attacked by
a
and remains undefended.

Lifting
E
to the full framework yields

\mathrm{Lift}_{F}(E)=\{x\in A\mid\mathrm{Sub}^{*}(x)\cap A_{\mathsf{CH}}\subseteq E\}=\{a\}.

In particular,
c\notin\mathrm{Lift}_{F}(E)
, because its core
subargument
b
is not accepted in
E
.

This example shows that the status of a status-dependent argument
is not determined by the presence or absence of direct attacks,
but is inherited from the acceptability of its conflict-handling
subarguments. The rejection of
c
thus follows from structural
dependency alone, without requiring any additional conflicts.

Proposition 4

(Monotonicity of lifting w.r.t. core sets)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})
be an SAF.
Let
C_{1},C_{2}\subseteq A_{\mathsf{CH}}
be two core (conflict-handling) sets such that

C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
.
Then the corresponding lifted sets satisfy

\mathsf{Lift}(C_{1})\subseteq\mathsf{Lift}(C_{2}).

Proof.

By definition, an argument
a\in A
belongs to
\mathsf{Lift}(C)
if and only if
there exists a subargument
a^{\prime}\in A_{\mathsf{CH}}
such that

(a^{\prime},a)\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}
and
a^{\prime}\in C
.

Assume
a\in\mathsf{Lift}(C_{1})
.
Then there exists
a^{\prime}\in C_{1}
with
(a^{\prime},a)\in\mathit{Sub}^{\ast}
.
Since
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
, we also have
a^{\prime}\in C_{2}
.
Hence
a\in\mathsf{Lift}(C_{2})
.

Therefore,
\mathsf{Lift}(C_{1})\subseteq\mathsf{Lift}(C_{2})
, establishing the
monotonicity of the lifting operation with respect to core sets.
∎

Corollary 3

(Least lifted extension induced by a least core extension)
.

Fix a Dung semantics
\sigma
and let
\mathcal{F}=(A,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Sub})

be an SAF.
Assume that the conflict-handling core framework
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}

admits a least
\sigma
-extension
E_{\min}
with respect to set inclusion, i.e.,

E_{\min}\in\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}})\quad\text{and}\quad\forall E\in\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}),\;E_{\min}\subseteq E.

Then
\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E_{\min})
is the least element of

\sigma_{\mathsf{core}}(\mathcal{F})
, that is,

\forall S\in\sigma_{\mathsf{core}}(\mathcal{F}),\quad\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E_{\min})\subseteq S.

In particular, this holds for grounded semantics, where
E_{\min}
is the unique
grounded extension of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}}
.

Proof.

Let
S\in\sigma_{\mathsf{core}}(\mathcal{F})
.
By Definition 15, there exists some
E\in\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{CH}})

such that
S=\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)
.
By assumption,
E_{\min}\subseteq E
.
By Proposition 4 (monotonicity of lifting),

\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E_{\min})\subseteq\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E)=S.

Hence
\mathsf{Lift}_{\mathcal{F}}(E_{\min})
is the least element of

\sigma_{\mathsf{core}}(\mathcal{F})
.
∎

This core–lifting perspective does not introduce a new semantics. Rather, it offers an explanatory decomposition of the structure-sensitive semantics defined earlier, separating the resolution of conflicts at the
core level from the propagation of acceptability through structural dependencies.
Seen in this light, it also becomes clear why structural information cannot be recovered from attack-only abstractions: once structure is forgotten,
the link between core conflicts and their structural consequences is lost. This observation directly underpins the non-encodability results established in Section 5.

7
Related Work and Conclusion

The present work is situated at the intersection of abstract and structured
argumentation.
Its primary contribution does not lie in introducing new attack types or
new semantics, but in making an explicit modelling choice at the abstract
level concerning the representation of internal argument structure.
This section clarifies how this choice relates to, and differs from,
existing lines of research.

Structured argumentation formalisms.

Structured argumentation frameworks such as ASPIC+, DeLP, and deductive
argumentation systems provide rich internal representations of arguments
based on underlying logical languages, inference rules, and defeat types
[
6
]
.
Within these formalisms, subarguments arise naturally from the construction
of arguments, and attacks on subarguments typically induce attacks on their
superarguments by definition.
When such systems are abstracted, this induced behaviour is usually
compiled into the attack relation, yielding a standard Dung framework.

In contrast, the present work does not commit to any particular structured
formalism or logical language.
Instead of deriving subargument relations from syntactic constructions, we
treat subarguments as a primitive abstract relation.
This allows us to reason about structural dependency independently of the
mechanisms that generate arguments, and to compare structurally distinct
frameworks even when they collapse to the same Dung abstraction.
Our approach is therefore orthogonal to existing structured formalisms and
can be seen as operating at a meta-abstract level above them.

Bipolar and support-based argumentation frameworks.

Bipolar argumentation frameworks extend Dung’s model by introducing an explicit support relation alongside attack
[
3
,
1
]
.
These frameworks are typically motivated by the need to capture positive
interactions between arguments and to study their semantic impact.
Although support relations are sometimes informally linked to the notion of
subarguments, they are conceptually different.
Support represents a justificatory relation between arguments, whereas
subargument relations capture structural dependency between components of a
single argument.

The framework proposed in this paper does not introduce support as an
additional interaction type.
Instead, it separates conflict from structure, and leaves the effect of
structure on acceptability to be determined by semantics.
As a result, subargument relations cannot be reduced to support relations,
nor do they play the same role in evaluating extensions.

Semi-structured and abstract extensions.

Closer in spirit to our work are semi-structured approaches that aim to
bridge the gap between abstract and structured argumentation.
In particular, the general semi-structured formalism proposed by Baroni,
Giacomin, and Liao
[
4
]
provides a unifying
framework in which abstract and structured systems can be compared through
a common interface.
That work focuses on identifying minimal structural components sufficient
to recover key properties of argumentation semantics.

Our contribution differs in focus and granularity.
Rather than proposing a general interface for multiple formalisms, we
isolate a specific structural relation (subargumenthood) and study its role
as a primitive component of abstract argumentation.
The results in Section
5
show that this choice yields
a strictly richer representational layer while remaining a conservative
extension of Dung semantics.

Explanation and justification in argumentation.

Explanation has recently emerged as a central topic in abstract argumentation, with several approaches emphasising that extension membership alone is
insufficient to account for why an argument is accepted or rejected
[
13
,
14
,
8
,
2
]
.
Recent surveys and frameworks for explanation and justification highlight the need to capture the internal reasons underlying acceptability
[
9
]
.
Our results provide a structural perspective on this issue: we show that structure-sensitive defence and justification cannot, in general, be recovered
from attack-only representations, even when they preserve extensions.
This places subargument structure as a key ingredient for explanation-oriented
argumentation semantics.

Induced attacks versus primitive structure.

A recurring theme in the literature is whether structural effects should be
compiled into the attack relation or represented explicitly.
Most existing abstractions adopt the former strategy, treating induced
attacks as part of the abstract framework.
The present work adopts the latter strategy: induced interactions are not
encoded at the abstract level, but are recovered semantically through
structure-sensitive defence.

This modelling choice underlies the main technical results of the paper.
It explains both why standard extension-based semantics are preserved and
why structurally distinct frameworks may nevertheless collapse under
forgetful projection.
In this sense, the present framework complements existing approaches by
making explicit a dimension of argumentation that is usually implicit or
discarded in abstraction.

This paper has examined the role of structural dependency in abstract
argumentation and argued that conflict alone is insufficient to capture how
arguments are justified, defended, and affected by change.
Starting from a motivating example, we showed that attack-only abstraction may
preserve extensions while nevertheless obscuring where conflicts arise, how
defence is grounded, and how the effects of attacks propagate through argument
structure.

To address this limitation, we introduced abstract argumentation frameworks
with subargument relations and developed structure-sensitive notions of
conflict-freeness, defence, and admissibility.
Crucially, these notions preserve the core fixpoint-based architecture of
Dung’s theory, demonstrating that explicit structural representation can be
incorporated without abandoning the foundations of abstract argumentation.

Our analysis of forgetting structure established a negative but essential
result: although subargument relations can be erased without changing
extensions, they encode information that cannot be recovered from attack-only
frameworks.
This loss was further characterised through a set of principles capturing
separation of conflict and structure, structure-sensitive defence, and
commitment to subarguments, clarifying the expressive advantage of the proposed
framework.

The perspective developed in this paper opens several directions for future
work.
One natural avenue is to study algorithmic and complexity aspects of
structure-sensitive semantics.
Another is to investigate how explanation and dynamic update can be analysed
when structural dependencies are made explicit.
Finally, connecting the abstract treatment of subarguments developed here with
concrete structured formalisms may provide further insight into how different
levels of abstraction interact in argumentative reasoning.
