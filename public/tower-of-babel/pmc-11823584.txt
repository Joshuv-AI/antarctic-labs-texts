In vitro analysis of compressive strength of root dentin on application of intracanal medicaments for different time periods
Authors: Kushal Kumar Ghosh, Sayantan Mukherjee, Paromita Mazumdar, Sahil Ali, Lovely Das
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Extended application of intracanal medicaments such as calcium hydroxide (CH) and triple antibiotic paste (TAP) is documented to reduce dentin’s resistance to fracture.Aim:This study intended to evaluate the resistance to fracture of root dentin treated with different medicaments – CH, TAP, CH with chlorhexidine (CHX) and lycopene (LP), and chitosan acetate (CA) – over 7, 14, and 42 days.Materials and Methods:Seventy-five single-rooted human mandibular premolars were biomechanically prepared and categorized into five groups based on the medicament used, with further subdivision based on application duration. Fracture resistance was tested after each time interval.Statistical Analysis:Within-group comparisons were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), while inter-group comparisons used a one-way ANOVA test at a 5% significance level.Results:At 7 days, no statistically notable differences were found among the groups. However, by day 14 and day 42, the groups treated with CH and TAP exhibited significantly reduced fracture resistance compared to the other groups.Conclusion:Combining LP with CH and CHX, or using CA, did not reduce radicular dentin fracture resistance, unlike TAP and CH. These findings suggest LP and CA as viable alternatives, potentially minimizing the adverse effects of traditional treatments.

INTRODUCTION

Calcium hydroxide (CH) is a widely used intracanal medication. Due to its solubility in water, salt, Ringer’s solution, local anesthetic, and hydroxyl ions, its mechanism of action against tissues and bacteria involves the dissociation of calcium and hydroxyl ions. CH has been thoroughly researched for its antimicrobial effectiveness against Enterococcus faecalis and Candida albicans. Research by Vaghela et al.[1] and Sinha et al.[2] has compared various formulations of CH with chlorhexidine (CHX) as intracanal medicaments, demonstrating their effectiveness in disinfecting dentinal tubules. CH is often used for extended periods when treating teeth with significant periapical radiolucencies or immature traumatized teeth.

Gher et al.[3] reported a decrease in the dentin’s resistance to fracture after root canal therapy, with most root fractures (71%) occurring after endodontic treatment. Factors contributing to these fractures in treated teeth include (i) structural loss due to decay, (ii) the effects of access cavity preparation, and (iii) alterations in dentin’s physical properties caused by various endodontic materials such as filling materials, intracanal medications, and irrigants. Some theories suggest that the application of CH medication may alter dentin’s physical characteristics, leading to increased fracture risk.[4]

Hoshino et al.[5] investigated the use of triple antibiotic paste (TAP), which combines ciprofloxacin, metronidazole, and minocycline. This combination exhibits strong antimicrobial and antibacterial properties.[6] TAP is effective as an intracanal medicament, particularly against E. faecalis.[7] TAP has certain drawbacks such as tooth discoloration, and heightened radicular dentin demineralization on long-term use, leading to a considerable reduction in fracture resistance and microhardness.[8]

Antioxidants such as lycopene (LP) and grape seed extract improve enamel composite resin bonding by neutralizing free radicals.[9] CH breaks down into calcium and hydroxyl ions, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that combat E. faecalis.[9] LP, found in tomatoes and other fruits, acts as a potent antioxidant by quenching singlet oxygen and activating antioxidative enzymes.[10] Madhusudhana et al.[11] demonstrated that adding LP to CH and CHX mitigates reductions in dentin fracture resistance.

Chitosan, another innovative substance, has demonstrated encouraging outcomes for endodontic dressings. Research indicates that it works well against E. faecalis.[12]

The dentinal surface’s resistance to collagenase breakdown is enhanced by chitosan treatment. Chitosan, the main component of crustacean exoskeletons, is a nontoxic cationic biopolymer that is typically derived from chitin through alkaline deacetylation.[13] In addition, chitosan has chelating properties, is biocompatible, and exhibits antibacterial activity against various microbial organisms. This makes it an appropriate intracanal medication that does not significantly compromise the integrity of root dentin.[14]

The study aimed to analyze the compressive strength of root dentin on the application of different intracanal medicaments for different periods.

The null hypothesis proposed that there would be no variation in the compressive strength of root dentin irrespective of the type of intracanal medicament that has been applied or the period for which the intracanal medicament has been applied in the root canal.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Institutional Ethical approval was obtained. The sample size was determined to be 75 (each group having 15 samples) using the G*Power software (v3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Single-rooted permanent mandibular premolars with fully developed roots and single root canal, extracted for orthodontic treatment reasons below 30 years of age were included in this study. Teeth with caries cracks on the surface of the root, previous restoration or endodontically treated teeth, teeth with severe anatomic variation, and curved canals (>5º), calcification, root resorption, and noncarious lesions were excluded from the study.

Sample preparation

The teeth were subsequently cleared of any calculus or soft-tissue debris with a hand scaler (Hu-Friedy 13/14 Gracey Curette) and stored in a 10% neutral-buffered formalin solution for at least 2 weeks.

Root canal preparation

Preoperative intraoral periapical radiographs were taken from mesiodistal and buccolingual angles to assess the presence and patency of a single canal. The specimens were prepared using a safe-sided diamond disc [Figure 1a] to a length of 13 mm from the apex. The working length was established by observing the tip of a size 15 K-file (Dentsply, Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) protruding beyond the apical foramen [Figure 1b] and deducting 1 mm from the file’s length. The root canals were subsequently prepared mechanically using the Protaper Gold rotary file system up to file #F3 [Figure 1c]. Between each file, irrigation was performed using 1 ml of 3% sodium hypochlorite solution (NaOCl) (Prime Dental Products, India) [Figure 1d]. After instrumentation, the smear layer was eliminated using 10 ml of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (Prime Dental Products, India), followed by an additional 10 ml of 3% NaOCl. Finally, a rinse with 10 ml of saline was administered.

Procedural steps included: (a) Decoronation of samples below cementoenamel junction, (b) working length determination, (c) canal preparation, (d) intra-canal irrigation, (e) drying with a paper point, (f) medicament application using Lentulo spiral, (g) mounting in acrylic blocks, (h) fracture resistance testing

Experimental groups

The teeth were randomly allocated into five groups of 15 each based on the type of intracanal medicament used. Subsequently, each group was further divided into three subgroups of five teeth each, depending on the storage period: 7 days, 14 days, and 42 days, respectively.

After drying the canals with paper points [Figure 1e], the medicaments were delivered into the canal spaces using a sterile Lentulo spiral [Figure 1f], inserted to 1 mm short of the working length, and condensed up to the level of the cementoenamel junction. Samples were then incubated at 37°C under 100% humidity for durations specified by respective subgroups. Apices were sealed by applying a light-cured bonding agent. All canals were sealed with glass ionomer cement at the orifice.

Teeth were further covered with additional silicone light body material and embedded in self-curing acrylic resin, with 1 mm protruding above the resin [Figure 1g]. Subsequently, a compressive force was applied until fracture occurred at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min using a universal testing machine [Figure 1h].

Statistical analysis

Data collected were organized in a spreadsheet using Microsoft Excel 2021, followed by statistical analysis performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Parametric tests were used for inferential statistics on normally distributed data. Within-group comparisons utilized repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc Bonferroni’s test. Inter-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test. A significance level of P ≤ 0.05 was adopted.

RESULTS

Table 1 represents the compressive strengths of different samples for the three time periods. For the CH group, the mean fracture resistance at the 14th day and at the 42nd day was significantly lesser than on the 7th day, respectively (P = 0.0065 and P = 0.0023, respectively). Furthermore, the mean fracture resistance on the 42nd day was significantly lesser than on the 14th day (P = 0.0049). For both LP and chitosan groups, the fracture resistance decreased over time, but the difference was not meaningful.

DISCUSSION

To ensure sample consistency, single-canal mandibular premolar teeth with single roots and canals were selected and sourced from patients under 30 years old to minimize age-related degradation in dentin strength.[15]

This storage media, i.e., 10% neutral-buffered formalin served to disinfect the samples and preserve dentin properties, minimizing postextraction changes.[16] Such meticulous standardization aimed to enhance the reliability of the study.

Torres et al.[17] compared various methods for placing CH, concluding that the Lentulo spiral was superior in paste placement at different canal depths. Hence, it was utilized in this study [Figure 1f].

Several in vitro studies, including that by Rahimi et al. (2013),[18] highlighted the potential detrimental effects of long-term CH application on root dentin compressive strength. The flexural strength of dentin relies on the hydroxyapatite crystals and collagen network bond facilitated by acidic proteins and proteoglycans. CH’s alkalinity disrupts this bonding, potentially weakening the dentin.[19]

This study revealed a gradual decrease in mean fracture resistance with the application of CH as an intracanal medicament, from 7 to 42 days [as shown in Figure 2]. These findings corroborate Andreasen et al.’s[19] study, indicating increased root fracture incidence with prolonged CH use.

Similarly, the TAP groups exhibited decreased fracture resistance over time [as shown in Figure 2]. The acidity of TAP causes demineralization of the surface resulting in a decrease in dentin’s inorganic content, as demonstrated in research conducted by Yassen et al. (2015)[20] and Yeung et al.[21] Furthermore, TAP causes less pronounced modifications in the chemical makeup of radicular dentin as compared to CH, indicating its potential for use in endodontic procedures. TAP includes minocycline, which chelates calcium, thereby potentially contributing to demineralization.[22]

On the contrary, the application of LP showed no significant effect on fracture resistance [as shown in Table 1], consistent with findings reported by Madhusudhana et al.[11] The antioxidative properties of LP may counteract the detrimental effects of ROS generated by CH and CHX combinations, thereby helping to preserve dentin integrity. In addition, chemical cross-linking of dentin collagen with carboxymethyl chitosan, as proposed by Shrestha et al.,[23] may enhance the fracture resistance of the collagen matrix. These findings underscore the potential of novel intracanal medicaments and collagen-stabilizing agents in endodontic treatments, offering insights into preserving dentin structure and improving treatment outcomes.

In this study, a gradual decline in mean fracture resistance was observed in the chitosan groups over time [as shown in Figure 2], although statistically insignificant, unlike the significantly reduced fracture resistance in the TAP and CH groups compared to the chitosan group. The chelating ability of chitosan on dentin, possibly through the bridge or pendant model,[23] may contribute to this decrease in strength. However, further research is needed to elucidate this mechanism fully.

In addition, a putty-based wash was applied [Figure 1g] to mimic the physiological spacing and cushioning effects of the periodontal ligament, following Coolidge’s study.[24] Decoronation was performed [Figure 1a] to eliminate crown weakness as a potential confounding factor, consistent with Olcay et al.’s[25] emphasis on the role of coronal structure in root fracture susceptibility.

Overall, the findings reject the null hypothesis, suggesting variation in root dentin compressive strength due to intracanal medicament type and duration of application.

The study’s strengths include a comprehensive evaluation of various intracanal medicaments on dentin fracture resistance across multiple time intervals, rigorous sample selection criteria ensuring consistency, standardized preparation and testing procedures, and robust statistical analyses. However, limitations include it in vitro design, and limited sample size, and thereby requires the need for further research to fully understand the mechanisms behind the observed changes.

CONCLUSION

Based on the constraints of this study, it can be inferred that the combination of chitosan and LP, along with CH and CHX, did not result in the reduction of fracture resistance of root dentin after 7, 30, or 42 days, unlike TAP and CH.
