Single-cell transcriptomics identifies adipose tissue CD271+ progenitors for enhanced angiogenesis in limb ischemia
Authors: Oto Inoue, Chiaki Goten, Daiki Hashimuko, Kosei Yamaguchi, Yusuke Takeda, Ayano Nomura, Hiroshi Ootsuji, Shinichiro Takashima, Kenji Iino, Hirofumi Takemura, Manasi Halurkar, Hee-Woong Lim, Vivian Hwa, Joan Sanchez-Gurmaches, Soichiro Usui, Masayuki Takamura
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryTherapeutic angiogenesis using mesenchymal stem/stromal cell grafts have shown modest and controversial effects in preventing amputation for patients with critical limb ischemia. Through single-cell transcriptomic analysis of human tissues, we identify CD271+ progenitors specifically from subcutaneous adipose tissue (AT) as having the most prominent pro-angiogenic gene profile distinct from other stem cell populations. AT-CD271+ progenitors demonstrate robust in vivo angiogenic capacity over conventional adipose stromal cell grafts, characterized by long-term engraftment, augmented tissue regeneration, and significant recovery of blood flow in a xenograft model of limb ischemia. Mechanistically, the angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors is dependent on functional CD271 and mTOR signaling. Notably, the number and angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors are strikingly reduced in insulin-resistant donors. Our study highlights the identification of AT-CD271+ progenitors with in vivo superior efficacy for limb ischemia. Furthermore, we showcase comprehensive single-cell transcriptomics strategies for identification of suitable grafts for cell therapy.Keywords: single-cell transcriptomics, therapeutic angiogenesis, cell therapy, CD271, adipose tissue, insulin resistance, peripheral artery disease, critical limb ischemia

Introduction

Critical limb ischemia (CLI), the end stage of peripheral artery disease, is increasingly prevalent, in part due to the worldwide increase in obesity and diabetes.1 Prognosis for patients with CLI is poor, comparable to malignant diseases.2 One of the fatal features of CLI is atherosclerotic calcification, a condition difficult to treat even with currently available revascularization techniques such as catheter interventions and bypass surgery.1 Consequently, at least one in three CLI patients is not eligible for revascularization therapies.3 To combat this, the strategy of inducing neovascularization in ischemic tissues, known as therapeutic angiogenesis, has emerged as an attractive alternative therapy for CLI.4

Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells with regenerative capacities were discovered from multiple human organs including “harvestable” tissues such as bone marrow (BM), subcutaneous adipose tissue (AT), and umbilical cord blood (UCB).5 The main therapeutic mechanism associated with cell-therapy-induced neovascularization is thought to be through secretion of growth factors (i.e., vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF], HGF, SDF-1) and extracellular vesicles.6,7 Preclinical studies of cell-based therapeutic angiogenesis showed favorable effects in animal models7,8,9,10,11 but have translated poorly in clinical trials.4,7 For CLI, unsorted BM mononuclear cells are the most intensively evaluated in clinical trials. However, a meta-analysis including 19 randomized controlled trials shows favorable effects on surrogate endpoints (e.g., pain score, pain-free walking distance, ankle brachial index) but not on primary endpoints of amputation-free rate and complete ulcer healing.12 Prospective isolation of specific cell populations from BM mononuclear cells, based on CD133+ or CD34+ cells, preclinically reported to be angiogenic,10,13 also did not lower the risk of limb amputation.14,15,16 Thus, a suitable cell population(s) optimal for therapeutic angiogenesis to achieve limb salvation has yet to be identified.

Single-cell transcriptomic technologies have revolutionized the capacity to identify lineages in tissue homeostasis and markers of specific stem cell populations.17,18,19,20,21 However, application of single-cell transcriptomic analysis in cell therapy has been limited to only chimera antigen receptor T (CAR T) cell therapy for lymphoma, where important cell populations for treatment response were assessed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq).22,23

In this study, we took advantage of available scRNA-seq datasets from different human tissues to screen for cell-surface markers that allow the segregation of a highly angiogenic cellular subpopulation. We identified CD271+ cells specifically from AT (named AT-CD271+) as having a distinct angiogenic gene profile with promising in vivo angiogenic capacity. Mechanistically, a functional CD271/mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) axis is necessary for full in vivo angiogenic capacity. Furthermore, with sufficient AT-CD271+ progenitors from metabolically healthy cardiovascular disease (CVD) donors, the therapeutic potentials proved markedly superior over conventional cell grafts for patients suffering from CLI.

Results

Adipose tissue lineage-negative cells show a distinct angiogenic gene profile among human stem cell sources

There is a strong need to identify the most suitable cell source for therapeutic angiogenesis. To search for subpopulations with pro-angiogenic capacity, we explored single-cell gene expression profiles from readily available human stem cell sources. We analyzed available scRNA-seq datasets from BM,24 UCB,25 and stromal vascular fraction (SVF) of subcutaneous AT26 from healthy donors (n = 2/tissue, Figure 1A and Table S1). We performed dimension reduction with t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) and cell clustering of 47,035 cells (Figures 1A, 1B, and S1). To annotate clusters, marker genes for each cluster were collated manually based on the Human Cell Atlas and Human Protein Atlas markers (proteinatlas.org, Figure S1).19,27,28 We removed mature immune and endothelial cells, i.e., lineage-positive (Lin+) cells,27 from further analysis to enrich for stem/progenitor cell populations. Since immune and endothelial cell markers differ between tissues and CD31+ cells are reported to be angiogenic only in BM,11 we used a well-established but distinct set of lineage markers in each of these tissues11,29,30 (Figure 1A). Overall, we processed 18,053 Lin− cells (BM, 9,289 cells; UCB, 1,932 cells; AT, 6,832 cells; Figure 1B).

The main mechanism of cell-based therapeutic angiogenesis has been reported to be paracrine activities of injected cells.6,32 Thus, to further characterize angiogenic gene profiles of stem cell sources, we used a gene set of reported pro-/anti-angiogenic paracrine factors released from tissue stem cells.6 We updated the list with 38 well-known secreted pro-/anti-angiogenic factors33,34,35 (Table S2). Hierarchical clustering of BM, UCB, and AT Lin− cells based on expression of pro-/anti-angiogenic factor genes clearly indicated AT cells have a distinctive gene expression profile with prominent expression of pro-angiogenic factors including MMP2, CXCL12, VEGFs, and IGF1 (Figure 1C). Consistently, gene enrichment analysis resulted in pro-angiogenic pathways significantly enriched in AT compared to BM or UCB (Figure 1D). On the other hand, anti-angiogenic factors such as TIMP2 and TIMP3 were also upregulated in AT (Figure 1C). Therefore, we hypothesized that AT would be an attractive cell source if pro-angiogenic cells could be segregated from TIMP2/3-expressing anti-angiogenic cells.

Adipose tissue scRNA-seq analysis filtered 30 surface markers of angiogenic cells

To delineate angiogenic populations within the AT, we analyzed Lin−CD34+ progenitors on available scRNA-seq datasets from six non-diabetic human subcutaneous AT SVF26 (Figure 2A). Louvain clustering divided AT Lin−CD34+ progenitor cells8,36 into three progenitor groups (Figures 2B and S2A). To understand the nature of each of these clusters, we analyzed the top ten marker genes of each cluster (Figure 2C). Progenitor 1 cluster was characterized by higher expression of adipocyte lineage markers (APOE and CEBPD) and progenitor 2 by mesenchymal/fibrotic markers (FBN1, MFAP5, and FN1), while progenitor 3 expressed some adipocyte lineage markers common to progenitor 1, including APOD, but also non-adipocyte markers MYOC and IGFBP3. Among these three clusters, pathway enrichment analysis found that progenitors 1 and 3 were dominated by genes involved in sprouting angiogenesis and lipogenesis (Figure 2D). Based on these analyses, we termed progenitors 1 and 3 a pro-angiogenic cluster and progenitor 2 cluster an anti-angiogenic cluster. Consistently, from the six human AT clusters, pro-/anti-angiogenic factor expressions clearly segregated into pro- and anti-angiogenic clusters (Figure S2B). Among the 38 genes used in this cell-source screening (Figure 1C and Table S2), 22 were differentially expressed between pro-/anti-angiogenic clusters. As expected, the pro-angiogenic cluster showed higher expression of 12 pro-angiogenic factors, while all five anti-angiogenic factor genes were highly expressed in the anti-angiogenic cluster. Interestingly, in the anti-angiogenic cluster, several pro-angiogenic factors, such as CTGF and VEGFA, were more highly expressed than in the pro-angiogenic cluster (Figure S2B), suggesting that identity of a highly pro-angiogenic population requires further delineation strategies.

To obtain intact cell populations for cell therapy, purification procedures utilizing surface markers are necessary. Thus, we next focused on identifying the surface markers specifically expressed in the pro-angiogenic cluster that would be suitable for downstream flow-cytometric isolation. Based on 2,621 differentially expressed genes between pro-/anti-angiogenic clusters (Figures 2E and S2C; Table S3), a total of 30 surface-protein-encoding genes (Table S4) were identified. We concluded that these 30 surface proteins may potentially lead to the specific isolation of pro-angiogenic cells.

Next, we determined which marker from the filtered 30 surface-protein-encoding genes would be the most suitable for cell-graft preparation for therapeutic angiogenesis. We therefore evaluated the number of cells that express each of the 30 individual surface proteins enriched in the pro-angiogenic cluster (Figures 2A and 2F). From this analysis, five surface markers isolated pro-angiogenic cells with over 95% purity (Figure 2G). Among them, CD271 (low-affinity nerve growth factor receptor [LNGFR]) emerged as the top-ranked surface marker, closely followed by VCAM1 (vascular cell adhesion molecule 1) and ABCB11 (ATP-binding cassette subfamily B member 11). From the cells detected to express CD271, 98% were on the pro-angiogenic cluster (Figure 2G). This is appreciably higher compared to Lin−CD34+ progenitors (progenitors 1, 2, and 3) and other previously known AT progenitor markers37 such as CD105, CD90, and CD73 (Figure 2G). Out of the cells expressing CD271, less than 3% were within the anti-angiogenic cluster, which was the lowest contamination rate among all studied markers (Figure 2G). Thus, we identified surface markers that may efficiently select for pro-angiogenic cells.

Adipose tissue CD271+ cells are highly angiogenic

Next, we evaluated the top three surface markers identified in our analysis, aiming to find which cell population showed the highest angiogenic potential. We noted that CD271+, VCAM1+, and ABCB11+ cell subpopulations are distinct, with very few cells carrying two or all three markers (Figure 3A). In particular, over 80% of CD271+ cells were VCAM−/ABCB11−. Therefore, we performed pathway enrichment analysis between single positive subpopulations. Strikingly, CD271+VCAM−ABCB11− cells showed pro-angiogenic pathway enrichment over CD271−VCAM+ABCB11− or CD271−VCAM−ABCB11+ cell populations (Figure 3B). Direct comparison between CD271+ and CD271− populations showed that CD271+ cells have higher expression of many key pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., MCP1, CXCL12, CTGF, and VEGFA, Figure 3C) and lower expression of anti-angiogenic factors TIMP2 and TIMP3. Additionally, none of the pro-angiogenic factors were upregulated in CD271− cells (Figure 3C). This is in sharp contrast to the pro-angiogenic versus anti-angiogenic clusters defined above (Figure S2B). Altogether, these analyses suggest enhanced capability to isolate angiogenic cells from Lin−CD34+ AT using CD271 selection (Figure 3C).

We next asked whether it is possible to obtain a sufficient number of CD271+ cells from AT for therapeutic angiogenesis. We found that the number of CD271-expressing cells were at least ten times higher in AT than in BM or UCB (Figure 3D). Consistently, we detected CD271 protein in at least 5%–10% of total SVF cells of human subcutaneous AT (Figure 3G); in contrast, it was previously reported that less than 0.1% of individual BM cells expressed CD271 protein.38 From these data, we concluded that AT is the most suitable tissue for harvesting a sufficient number of CD271+ cells.

Although AT carried higher numbers of CD271+ cells than either BM or UCB, we assessed whether CD271+ cells are pro-angiogenic, independent of tissue source. As expected, AT-CD271+ cells showed strong enrichment in pro-angiogenic pathways when compared to AT-CD271− cells (Figure 3E). BM-CD271+ cells, in contrast, were characterized by pathways related to the cell cycle and proliferation (Figure 3E), consistent with a previous report that BM-CD271+ cells possess clonogenic and multi-lineage differentiation capacities.38 On the other hand, UCB-CD271+ cells showed upregulation of immune-system-related pathways when compared to UBC-CD271− (Figure 3E). These transcriptomic analyses indicated that although CD271+ cells are available from different tissues, AT-CD271+ cells exhibit a distinct gene profile.

It is noteworthy that several subpopulations of BM, including CD34+,14 CD133+,16 and CD31+11 populations, were reported to be angiogenic and utilized in clinical trials. Thus, we compared transcriptomic profile of AT-CD271+ cells to these three BM subpopulations (Figure 3F). We found that AT-CD271+ cells consistently showed enrichment of pathways related to cell differentiation, while BM subpopulations were characterized by involvement with immune systems. More importantly, AT-CD271+ cells demonstrated enrichment of pro-angiogenic pathways (e.g., activating endothelial proliferation and VEGF signaling) when compared to each BM subpopulation. Altogether, these transcriptomic data suggest that AT-CD271+ cells may have higher angiogenic potential than candidates already in clinical trials.

AT-CD271+ progenitors are culture expandable with classical mesenchymal stem cell capacity

It is known that culture-expandable mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-like cells can be generated from unsorted AT progenitors and are commonly referred to as adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs).39 To evaluate human AT-CD271+ stem cell characteristics, we sorted and established in vitro cultures of CD271+ and CD271− cells from subcutaneous AT of human healthy subjects (Figure 3G). CD271 expression is detected in Lin−CD34+ AT mesenchymal progenitors but is also detected in MCAM+CD34− pericytes (Figure S2E). However, since the bulk of angiogenic potential resides in the CD34+ population,8 we focused on comparing CD271+ to CD271− cells in Lin−CD34+ fraction (Figure 3G).

Both CD271+ and CD271− cells were adherent and proliferative with similar spindle-shaped morphology (Figure 3H). As expected, CD271+ cells showed higher CD271 mRNA expression (Figure S2D). CD271+ cells have enhanced proliferative capacity compared to CD271− cells as determined by colony-formation assay where CD271+ cells generated larger and more dense colonies (Figure 3H) in significantly higher number (Figure 3I). Both cell types readily differentiated into adipocyte and osteoblast lineages (Figure 3J). Collectively, these in vitro assays characterized CD271+ progenitors as culture expandable with classical ADSC morphology and capacities.39 Because CD271 expression is shared between AT progenitors and pericytes based on scRNA-seq analysis (Figure S2E), we further examined pericytic marker expressions in cultured CD271+ and CD271− cells. Interestingly, CD271+ progenitors showed higher expressions of pericytic markers MCAM and NESTIN compared to CD271− cells (Figure S2F). This suggests that CD271+ progenitors may possess pericytic characteristics in addition to ADSC functions.

In previous reports, CD271+ cells from BM and AT showed reduced surface CD271 expression during early in vitro passaging but maintained 0.5%–5% of CD271-expressing cells upon further passaging.38,40 We confirmed that approximately 5%–10% of our sorted AT-CD271+ cells retained surface CD271 protein expression after eight passages (Figure S3C).

CD271+ progenitors demonstrate enhanced in vitro and in vivo angiogenic capacity with long-term engraftment

To better understand the functional potential of AT-CD271+ cells, we first assessed and compared the in vitro angiogenic capacity of AT-CD271− and AT-CD271+ progenitors using a well-established tube-formation assay40 (Figure 4A). We co-cultured either human AT-CD271− or CD271+ progenitors with human umbilical cord vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) in Matrigel 3D culture. As a positive control, HUVECs, plated alone in EGM2 medium for 8 h, demonstrated vigorous tube formation, as assessed by tube-segment lengths and total mesh area (defined as the area enclosed by tube segments) (Figures 4A and 4B). Co-culture of CD271+ progenitors with HUVECs (in M199 medium) promoted tube formation with higher segment length, with total mesh area comparable to EGM2 medium control (Figures 4A and 4B). However, co-culturing HUVECs with CD271− progenitors do not induce tube formation above that of the basal control medium M199 (Figures 4A and 4B). To further evaluate how many progenitors contributed to tube formation, we labeled AT progenitors with PKH-26 living cell dye prior to co-culture. Strikingly, CD271+ progenitors produced more total nodes and nodes containing CD271+ progenitors compared to CD271− progenitors (Figures 4C and 4D). These in vitro results support the more pronounced pro-angiogenic activities of AT-CD271+ progenitors compared to AT-CD271− progenitors.

Next, we examined the in vivo angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors in a xenograft model of limb ischemia with special focus on cell retention (engraftment, Figure 4E). For this, we injected PKH-labeled human CD271+ or CD271− progenitors (0.5 million cells/mice) directly into limb muscles of immunocompromised mice subjected to left femoral artery ligation. Cell-treated limbs were harvested at days 7, 14, and 35 after fluorescence-conjugated isolectin perfusion to detect capillaries with blood flow.9,11,41

We observed that CD271+ progenitors clearly engrafted better than CD271− progenitors in ischemic limb, with 2–3 times higher PKH+ cell numbers (Figures 4F and 4G). The enhanced differences in engraftment capacity of AT-CD271+ progenitors persisted for the full 5 weeks of the study. Further, immunofluorescent analysis of tissue sections shows that AT-CD271+ progenitors localize around lectin+ capillaries (Figure S3A). To clarify whether injected CD271+ progenitors were trans-differentiating to endothelial cells, we performed flow-cytometric analysis of PKH+ cells from digested limb muscles 14 days after cell engraftment (Figure S3B). We confirmed that only 6% of engrafted CD271+ progenitors expressed the endothelial marker CD31, which suggested that the majority of the injected cells did not undergo differentiation into mature endothelial cells (Figure S3C). On the other hand, isolated PKH+ cells maintained expression of human CD271 and typical markers of cultured ADSCs37 (human CD90 and CD105, Figure S3C). These findings confirmed that engrafted CD271+ progenitors preserve their stem cell marker expressions and likely acted as stem/progenitor cells to support intrinsic regeneration.

Correlating with higher survival of engraftment, CD271+ progenitor therapy induced enhanced revascularization after limb ischemia. This is evidenced by 3-fold higher capillary density (lectin+ and mouse CD31+, Figure S3D) than that induced by CD271− progenitors (Figure 4G). Strikingly, CD271+ progenitors successfully led to recovery of blood flow in treated limb to a level comparable to healthy limbs, as determined by both imaging and Doppler blood flow analysis (Figures 4H and 4I).

To confirm tissue-reparative capacity of CD271+ progenitors, we incorporated in vivo 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) labeling into our xenograft protocol. Since neovascularization and blood flow recovery were favorably accomplished by day 14 in our model, we evaluated EdU incorporation on day 7 when tissue neovascularization was in progress. CD271+ progenitor transplantation led to approximately four times higher proliferation of host cells (largely composed of skeletal muscle cells and endothelial cells and identified as PKH−) than treatment with CD271− progenitors (Figures 4J and 4K). We also observed that engrafted PKH+ cells were EdU− in this assay (Figure 4J). Collectively, the data from our xenograft model provide promising translational in vivo angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors with long-term engraftment and enhanced host cell proliferation.

Full angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors requires functional CD271 and mTOR signaling

We sought to better understand the molecular mechanism that promotes the angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors. CD271 signaling is dependent on multiple co-receptors that induce multiple and even opposing effects42 (Figure 5A). Using scRNA-seq analysis of human AT, we found that human adipose cells exclusively express TRKB (tyrosine receptor kinase B), which activates the mTOR pathway important for cell survival43 (Figure 5B). We confirmed that CD271+ progenitors maintained CD271 and TRKB expression after plating (Figure 5C). Consistently, we observed higher expression of mTOR signaling pathway components in CD271+ progenitors (Figure 5D). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that CD271-ligand interactions would activate the mTOR signaling pathway and boost the angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors.

CD271 is known to interact with four ligands, NGF (nerve growth factor), BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), NT3 (neurotrophin 3), and NT4/5.42 To identify which of the four ligands is expressed in human AT, we revisited the GTEx consortium31 transcriptomic data. We find that NT3 and especially NGF are highly expressed in human AT while NT4/5 and BDNF have very low expression (Figure 5E). As we are focusing on cell therapy on ischemic limb, we also evaluated expression of NT3 and NGF in human limb tissue and both were expressed, especially in limb nerve and arteries. In a publicly available compendium of single-cell transcriptome data, NGF and NT3 were both expressed in mouse AT MSCs and limb skeletal muscle satellite cells (Figure S4A).44 To further identify important CD271 ligands in the context of ischemic limb, we analyzed RNA-seq datasets of human limb muscles (gastrocnemius) obtained from 16 CLI patients and 15 healthy adults.45 We found that NGF expression was maintained in CLI patients, while NT3 was significantly downregulated (Figure 5F). These data suggest that NGF may play a role in the regulation of CD271+ cells.

To evaluate whether NGF activates mTOR signaling through CD271, CD271+ and CD271− progenitors were treated with NGF (100 ng/mL, 1 h) and Akt/mTOR signaling analyzed by western blot. NGF treatment strongly induced phosphorylation of Akt-S473, mTOR-S2448, and S6K-Thr421/Ser424 in CD271+ cell cultures. In contrast, in CD271− progenitors, phosphorylation of Akt-S473 and S6K-Thr421/Ser424 was low (Figure 5G). These findings demonstrated that NGF strongly activates mTOR signaling in CD271+, but not in CD271− progenitors.

To determine the necessity of the CD271/mTOR axis for in vivo angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors, we knocked down mTOR in CD271+ progenitors using small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Figures 5H and 5I). Downregulation of mTOR resulted in dramatically reduced S6K activation upon NGF stimulation (Figure 5H), with downregulation of target gene HIF1A (Figure 5I). Next, we tested AT-CD271+ progenitors depleted of mTOR in our xenograft model. As expected, the si-mTOR CD271+ progenitors showed significantly impaired in vivo angiogenic capacity including decreased neovascularization and graft survival (Figures 5J and 5K). From these results, we conclude that a functional NGF/CD271/mTOR axis is necessary for full angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors.

To validate the relationship between CD271 receptor function and angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors, we investigated mouse AT including from mice carrying a CD271 mutation. First, we revisited scRNA-seq datasets of SVF cells from neonatal mouse inguinal fat pads46 to assess whether mouse CD271+ progenitors have an angiogenic gene profile. scRNA-seq analysis subclustered SVF cells into ten clusters (Figures S4B and S4C). CD271+ progenitors were distributed predominantly with Schwann cells, pericytes, and Lin− progenitor clusters (Figure S4C). Consistent with human CD271+ progenitor gene expression profiles (Figure 3C), mouse CD271+ showed upregulation of pro-angiogenic factors including Igf1 and Cxcl12 and downregulation of anti-angiogenic factors Timp2 and Timp3 (Figure S4D). Furthermore, mouse AT-CD271+ progenitors showed enrichment of angiogenesis-related gene ontology (GO) terms (endothelial cell proliferation and VEGF signaling pathway, Figure S4E). We asked whether a functional CD271 protein is necessary for the angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors in mice. We used mice lacking exon 3 of the CD271 gene, which encodes three of the four necessary cysteine-rich repeats in the extracellular domain. The loss of exon 3 generated an in-frame mutant CD271 protein with loss of NGF binding affinity,47 which resulted in a 2- to 3-fold reduction in sensitivity to NGF in neural tissue cells48 (Figure S4F). Since current commercially available antibodies are incapable of isolating CD271+ progenitors from mouse AT, we established cultured mouse ADSCs from whole SVF, which contain CD271+ progenitors, from wild-type littermates (control) and CD271 mutant mice (CD271Mut). Based on the angiogenic factor profile from mouse scRNA-seq (Figure S4D), we found that CD271Mut ADSCs have significantly lower expression of these angiogenic factors than control cells (Figure S4G). Moreover, Mtor, Hif1a, and Vegfa were also downregulated in CD271Mut ADSCs (Figure S4G). These findings suggest that the CD271 mutation affects gene expression of mTOR signaling and downstream expression of angiogenic genes.

We also examined whether mutant CD271 impairs in vivo angiogenic capacity of mouse ADSCs. Compared to control ADSCs, CD271Mut ADSC transplants demonstrated reduced cell engraftment (Figures S4H and S4I), impaired in vivo angiogenesis (Figure S4J), and failed blood flow recovery (Figures S4K and S4L). These data suggest that normal CD271 activity is required for CD271+ cells to have full angiogenic capacity. In sum, our data highlight an important biological function of CD271 and propose the concept that CD271 is not solely a surface marker for cell selection.

Insulin-resistant patients have decreased CD271+ progenitor numbers with poor therapeutic potential

Next, we asked whether sufficient AT-CD271+ progenitors are available from patients to be used in clinical settings. First, we analyzed patient-derived AT samples. We evaluated the number of CD271+ progenitors in subcutaneous AT from 38 CVD patients with various pathological conditions and diseases (Table S4). The frequency of CD271+ progenitors in SVF cells (CD271+/SVF) ranged from 0.2% to 40% and was not significantly affected by donor gender, body mass index, or age (Figure 6A). This is clearly favorable for cell preparation from a variety of donors. Intriguingly, the number of CD271+ progenitors is negatively correlated with the insulin resistance score (HOMA-IR, Figure 6A). Consistently, CD271+ progenitor number is reduced by approximately 75% in patients with insulin resistance (HOMA-IR > 2.9)49 as determined by flow cytometry (Figure 6B). Because immune cells accumulate in insulin-resistant AT, we additionally calculated the percentage of CD271+ progenitors in Lin−CD34+ cells and found that CD271+/Lin−CD34+ SVF is also significantly reduced in patients with insulin resistance (Figure 6B). To validate the unexpected finding from our 38 CVD patient cohort with an orthogonal method, we analyzed available subcutaneous AT scRNA-seq datasets from lean healthy (insulin-sensitive) subjects versus obese type 2 diabetic (insulin-resistant) subjects (Figures 6C and S5A).26,50 We found a significant reduction in the number of CD271-expressing cells in AT from insulin-resistant subjects (Figures 6D and 6E). Furthermore, pro-angiogenic factor genes such as CXCL12, IGF1, and MCP1 were strongly upregulated in CD271+ cells of insulin-sensitive donors compared to those from insulin-resistant donors (Figure S5B). GO enrichment analysis shows decreased angiogenesis-related terms in CD271+ progenitors from insulin-resistant donors when compared to CD271+ progenitors from insulin-sensitive donors (Figure 6F and Table S6).

Since our results show that CD271+ progenitors have positive therapeutic effects in our mouse ischemic limb model, we hypothesized that CD271+ progenitor therapy is more beneficial than conventional unsorted ADSCs in our model. Hence, we prepared paired unsorted ADSCs and sorted CD271+ progenitors from five insulin-resistant and five insulin-sensitive patient subgroups from our 38-patient cohort (Figures 6A and 6B) to test therapeutic effectiveness in our ischemic limb model (Figure 6G). As shown in Figure 6H, sorted human CD271+ progenitors showed markedly enhanced engraftment over conventional ADSCs from the same donors. Notably, cell therapy with CD271+ progenitors of metabolically healthy donors resulted in improved engraftment compared to CD271+ progenitors from insulin-resistant donors (Figure 6H). Interestingly, although CD271+ progenitors from both insulin-resistant and metabolically healthy donors increased capillary density compared to respective unsorted cells, CD271+ progenitors of metabolically healthy donors achieved strikingly higher capillary density than those from insulin-resistant donors (Figure 6I). Notably, conventional unsorted ADSCs from insulin-resistant donors showed capillary density equivalent to PBS injections, suggesting a very limited effect of this population (Figure 6I). Finally, CD271+ progenitors from insulin-sensitive donors achieved full recovery of blood flow (Figure 6J), while CD271+ progenitors from insulin-resistant donors were less effective but still achieved a remarkable >85% recovery. These data further highlight the promising efficacy of CD271+ progenitor therapy for ischemic limb, even from insulin-resistant donors, albeit therapy from insulin-sensitive donors may lead to better long-term outcomes.

Preliminary positive impacts of autologous CD271+ progenitors in a trial outcome of SVF therapy for CLI

To further investigate the therapeutic potential of CD271+ progenitors in humans, we retrospectively evaluated data from a cell therapy trial (multicenter, SVF transplantation) for four patients with no-option CLI51 (Figures 7A and 7B). During the 1-year follow-up period, patients received no interventional treatments (e.g., catheter intervention, surgery). Medications were already optimized prior to SVF therapy under consensus guidelines, and no further changes were made.52 Of the four patients within our trial who underwent SVF therapy (Table S7), two exhibited complete recovery confirmed by ulcer disappearance and concomitant relief of pain (Figures 7D and 7E). We also observed angiographic improvements of blood flow characterized by newly generated capillaries and venous return increase 1 year after cell therapy (Figure 7D and Video S1). However, two other cases resulted in major amputation or ulcer recurrence without significant improvements in angiographic evaluation at the 1-year follow-up (Figure 7D and Video S1). All four patients had HOMA-IR within normal range (min.–max. 0.3–2.0, mean 1.4, Table S7). Based on the above results, we hypothesized that successful recovery at the 1-year follow-up is correlated with the number of CD271+ progenitors initially injected. Thus, we retrospectively evaluated the potential impact of injected CD271+ progenitors on efficacy of SVF therapy (Figure 7C) and stratified the patients based on recurrence or amputation outcome, then revisited the injected cellular composition. Although patient number in this analysis is very small, we observed that our primary outcome does not correlate with total SVF or with the Lin−CD34+ cell number injected (Figure 7F). In particular patient 2, who required amputation, had received the highest dose of total SVF cells and CD34+ cells compared to those of the patients who recovered, suggesting that high doses of CD34+ cells were insufficient for preventing amputation. However, both patients who completely recovered from ulcers without recurrence (patients 1 and 4) were injected with a higher number of CD271+ cells (Figure 7F). As an example, patient 1, who recovered from CLI, had an approximately 200 times higher number of CD271+ progenitors injected than patient 3 who presented with recurrent ulceration (Figures 7C and 7G). The positive correlation between full recovery and CD271+ progenitors injected was striking, especially when we compared the number of injected CD271+ progenitors per ulcer area (n = 6 ulcer lesions, Figure 7G). Collectively, our findings from this subanalysis of SVF therapy for CLI, despite the limited number of study subjects, would support the potential therapeutic effects of CD271+ progenitors in humans.

Discussion

Despite decades of research in therapeutic angiogenesis, the ideal cell population for grafting was elusive, as evidenced by controversial effects on amputation prevention in 19 randomized controlled trials (837 patients) using five cell types.12 Traditionally, the identification of cell populations suitable for cell therapy had depended on biased selection of markers. This potential hit-and-miss strategy is time consuming and costly and allows the analysis of only a limited number of markers.53 In this study, we took advantage of current single-cell genomic approaches to simultaneously explore all known/detected surface markers. Based on these unbiased analyses of available scRNA-seq datasets, we found that AT-CD271+ progenitors were predominantly pro-angiogenic, with less than 3% of anti-angiogenic cells. We suggest that application of single-cell transcriptomics for cell therapy can greatly improve the discovery of better cell markers for targeted therapies of interest.

The relationship between CD271 expression and cell angiogenic capacity has been poorly established. A previous report demonstrated that CD271+ progenitors secrete angiogenic factors in vitro, although the CD271+ progenitors did not demonstrate significant changes in tube-formation capacity,40 in contrast to our in vitro findings (Figures 4A–4D). This discrepancy could be simply due to differences in methodological details such as markers used for isolation (we established cultures from Lin−CD34+CD271+ sorting, while Smith et al.40 used CD271+ sorting), cell isolation methodology differences (flow sorting in our case vs. MACS isolation) (Figure 3G), or extracellular matrix support (we used serum-reduced Matrigel to avoid growth factors found in normal Matrigel). In another report, limited angiogenesis was observed in an in vitro HUVEC tube-formation assay using conditioned medium from CD271+ progenitors.54 Our CD271+ cell/HUVEC co-culture supports the importance of cell-to-cell interactions between CD271+ progenitors and target cells.40,54 We also observed an unexpectedly higher number of well-distributed CD271+ progenitors at nodes of HUVEC tube formation, suggesting that CD271+ progenitors directly supported tube-forming HUVECs (Figure 4C). Furthermore, in our xenograft model, CD271+ progenitors were found surrounding capillaries and labeled by perfused lectin (Figures 4F, 4G, and S3A). This indicates the close proximities between “leaky” capillaries and engrafted cell grafts. We also noted that AT-CD271+ progenitors possessed unexpected pericytic gene signatures (Figures S2E and S2F). As AT progenitors are known to be pericytic and important for maintaining vasculature integrity,36 the pericytic gene profile of AT-CD271+ progenitors might be responsible for higher engraftment and angiogenic capacity over other cell sources. From these observations, we conclude that engraftment capability and cell-cell interactions between cell grafts and the regenerating (immature) capillaries could be important for CD271+ progenitors to exert angiogenic functions.

CD271, also known as LNGFR, is a transmembrane receptor originally found to be a marker for neural lineage cells. CD271 is known to activate mTOR through cooperation with co-receptors including TRKB, also known as NTRK2 (neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 2)42,55 a co-receptor which we found to be abundantly expressed in the majority of CD271+ progenitors from human SVF cells. Data from human multi-organ transcriptomics,31 including from CLI patients,45 point to an important role of NGF, and we clarified the existence and importance of the NGF/CD271/mTOR axis in CD271+ progenitors. The negative effects of our si-mTOR studies, however, may reflect general blunted impacts, since the mTOR pathway is a well-established major signaling hub for multiple factors.56,57 Future studies should assess whether neurotrophic factors may enhance the angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors and whether neurotrophin pretreatment or controlled CD271 overexpression might be preconditioning strategies for improving cell grafts.

We found that angiogenic AT-CD271+ progenitor populations were reduced in patients with insulin resistance, although the causal relationship between AT-CD271+ progenitor decrease and insulin resistance remains to be clarified. Interestingly, changes in systemic and local58 level of NGF have been reported to closely relate to insulin resistance and diabetes.59 Moreover, blunted mTOR signaling in AT can lead to insulin resistance.43,60 Taken together, future investigations will assess protective roles of the NGF/CD271/mTOR axis in AT against insulin resistance.

Although preclinical studies suggested promising efficacy of cell therapy, clinical trials have shown inconsistent results. This might be explained by numerical or functional differences of cell grafts among CVD patients.61,62 Our retrospective analysis of the SVF therapy trial for terminal CLI51 supports a positive correlation between complete recovery and high numbers of injected CD271+ progenitors. Importantly, we determined that approximately 300,000–500,000 AT-CD271+ progenitors per lesion area (CD271+ cells/ulcer cm2) as the dose that seems to be necessary for favorable outcome. Given the higher incidence of metabolic disorders in CVD patients, our findings that decreased cell number and functional impairment of CD271+ progenitors in insulin-resistant donors should be taken into therapeutic consideration. We and others have reported altered gene expression and impaired angiogenic effects of ADSCs derived from type 2 diabetic patients compared to ADSCs from nondiabetics.63,64 In this study, we found that HOMA-IR can be used as a surrogate marker to predict the quantity and quality of CD271+ progenitors in AT. Taken together, donor selection with lower HOMA-IR, not only for autologous therapy but also for allograft (cell bank) development, might be beneficial for avoiding ineffective therapeutic interventions.

Our single-cell transcriptomics from multiple stem cell sources captured AT as a better cell source for angiogenic cell populations than current BM mononuclear cells. Furthermore, we showed that AT CD34+ selection did not increase the quantity of the appropriate angiogenic cell population when compared to unsorted SVF (Figure 2G). Although CD271+ progenitors showed angiogenic gene profiles more positive than the other candidate cell populations we screened (ABCB11 and VCAM1) or previously reported (BM-CD34+,15 CD133+,16 and CD31+11), we did not compare functional angiogenic capacity directly in this study. However, we showed significantly enhanced in vivo angiogenic capacity of AT-CD271+ cells over CD271− cells (Figures 4F–4I) and, importantly, also over conventional ADSCs (Figures 6H–6J). Conventional ADSCs are known to be equally or more angiogenic compared to the other MSC types from BM or UCB.65,66 Therefore, our in vivo data suggested that AT-CD271+ progenitors have distinctly enhanced angiogenic capacity compared to conventional AT-, BM-, and UCB-MSCs, which are the vast majority of cell grafts tested in clinical trials. Although our finding supports the benefit of CD271+ progenitor transplantation over current cell therapy, further studies to compare CD271+ progenitors and other potential subpopulations in vitro and in vivo is important toward further developing the most suitable strategy for therapeutic angiogenesis. More importantly, as demonstrated in this study (Figures 6 and 7), the feasibility/accessibility of these cell populations in human donors should be tested to successfully translate our findings into clinical trials.

In summary, we applied single-cell transcriptomics for CLI cell therapy purposes and identified a human stem cell population that is readily available and possesses a very strong angiogenic gene profile. CD271+ progenitors can be harvested from human subcutaneous AT and expanded in vitro to obtain sufficient quantity for cell grafts. This cell population exhibits promising angiogenic capacities, including long-term engraftment and augmentation of host tissue cell proliferation in cell therapy. In CVD patients with systemic insulin resistance, the number and angiogenic capacity of CD271+ progenitors were significantly decreased. CD271+ progenitors obtained from insulin-sensitive donors, in contrast, demonstrated the highest in vivo angiogenic capacity and consistently rescued ischemic limb. Our data highlight the potential screening benefits using single-cell technologies and evident benefits of CD271+ progenitor transplantation for improving current therapeutic angiogenesis. Future studies and clinical trials focusing on CD271+ progenitor therapy in larger cohorts will provide the necessary clinical evidence for efficacy and dosing requirements.

Limitations of the study

In this study, the correlation between the expression levels of CD271 mRNA and protein in each cell was not directly analyzed. As we detected relatively higher percentage of CD271+ (CD271-protein-expressing) cells by flow cytometry from human Lin− SVF than we observed in SVF scRNA-seq analysis, there is a possibility that sorted CD271+ cells did not perfectly represent CD271-expressing cells defined in our scRNA-seq analysis. Although we confirmed higher mRNA expression of CD271 in sorted CD271+ progenitors compared to CD271− progenitors in vitro (Figure S2D), further studies using single-cell multiomics technologies, such as CITE-seq,67 would be helpful toward validating our results.

Here, we used surgical resection of the femoral artery in nude mice to mimic CLI. Although this method is extensively validated for limb ischemia research, CLI has multiple etiologies including atherosclerosis, autoimmune disease, or Buerger’s disease (smoking). Future studies will have to assess whether CD271+ progenitor therapeutics is efficacious across different etiologies of CLI (e.g., apolipoprotein E knockout, low-density lipoprotein receptor knockout).

In mouse ADSC experiments, we did not sort CD271+ cells from CD271Mut mice because of difficulties in delineating mutated CD271 with currently available monoclonal antibodies.

Experimental model and subject details

Human adipose tissue samples

In the study of patient-derived AT and SVF, eligible patients were adult males or females with CVD who underwent elective cardiac surgery at Kanazawa University Hospital (Japan), including coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and cardiac valve replacement/plastic surgeries. Major exclusion criteria were acute emergency diseases, including acute myocardial infarction, active and acute infectious disease, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (HbA1c > 8.0%), hypo/hyperinsulinemia (<2.2 or >13.0 μU/mL), systemic inflammatory disease (autoimmune disease), and history of irradiation therapy at tissue harvest sites. Thirty-eight patients were enrolled between April 2017 and February 2022 (Table S5). This study was registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network Center (UMIN, Tokyo, Japan) Clinical Trials Registration System as UMIN000020764. After enrollment with written informed consent, clinical and laboratory variables were collected preoperatively, and all data were blinded for the researchers who performed further experiments.

The detailed protocol and eligibility for the clinical trial of SVF therapy for CLI are published,51,70 Briefly, this was a multicenter clinical trial (UMIN000010143) to investigate the feasibility and safety of SVF injection for CLI patients with no option for treatment.51 Autologous SVF from liposuction was transplanted to the ulcer lesions via intra-muscular injections. The safety, feasibility, and the efficacy including hard outcomes, including survival rate and amputation free rate in 6 months, of SVF therapy were investigated. In this study, we sub-analyzed 4 patients (Table S7) on whom we performed cell therapy in our institution (Kanazawa University) with 1-year follow-up data. On the remaining samples after SVF injection, we analyzed cell number and their viability by automated cell counter (NucleoCounter NC-100, M&S TechnoSystems, Inc., Osaka, Japan). Surface antigen expressions were evaluated by flow cytometry with the same protocol below.

The studies using human samples subjects was performed according to Declaration of Helsinki principles and approved by the Research Ethics Board of the University of Kanazawa. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to inclusion in the study. All animal experiments were approved by the Institution for Experimental Animals of the Kanazawa University Advanced Science Research Center.

Mice

All animal protocols were approved and performed according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Kanazawa University, which strictly conforms to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Athymic nude mice for xenograft experiments were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Strain #002019). Heterozygous Ngfrtm1jae47 were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Strain #002213) and crossed two- or three-times with C57BL/6J females before generating homozygous Ngfrtm1Jae (CD271Mut) mice. Homozygous Ngfrtm1Jae and controls mice were used at 6–8-week-old) before they develop peripheral nervous denervation and cutaneous ulcers (16 weeks old).

Cell lines

Human and mice ADSCs were established by tissue digestion and adherent culture. Human or mice CD45− SVF cells were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/cm2. Cells were cultured in standard conditions (5% carbon dioxide, 37°C) in DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific) solution. At 24 h after seeding, non-adherent cells were removed by washing the plates, and then medium was changed every three days. ADSCs were harvested at sub-confluency with trypsin (0.05% trypsin-EDTA; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells at the second passage and thereafter were replated at a density of 3,000 cells/cm2.

Method details

Single-cell transcriptome datasets and analysis

We used scRNA-seq datasets of AT,26,46,50 BM,24,71 and UCB.25,69 Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) accession numbers of revisited datasets are listed in Table S1. For quality control, cells expressing <200 genes and with a mitochondrial content >5% were removed. For batch integration of multiple scRNAseq datasets, Harmony was used to remove the batch effect.72 After data normalization, dimension reduction (tSNE) and clustering with using a Louvain algorism were performed. Marker gene analysis and differential expression (DE) analysis were performed using non-parametric approach, Venice73 or DESeq2. The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to list GO Biological Process terms.74 Cluster annotation was determined according to marker genes for clusters identified by non-parametric approach, Venice and collation with overlapping genes from the Human Cell Atlas and previously published scRNA-seq reports.19,21 These analyses were performed in BioTuring Browser 3.75

Adipose tissue processing

Unnecessary subcutaneous adipose tissues (1–2 g) discarded during making surgical incision were obtained. SVF cells were isolated from adipose tissue using a procedure established by Zuk et al.39 with some modifications. Briefly, adipose tissue was digested by Liberase TM Research Grade collagenase solution (0.4 units/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min using gentleMACS Dissociator (“ATDK” program, Miltenyi Biotec). Then, the digested solution was filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences) to remove undigested connective tissue debris. After centrifugation (400g, 10 min at room temperature), the supernatant containing mature adipocytes was discarded. The pellet was lysed to deplete erythrocytes with BD Pharm Lyse (2mL for 1 min at room temperature, BD Biosciences) and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 2.0% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The pellet was resuspended in 150 μL of autoMACS Running buffer (Miltenyi) and counted with a hemocytometer in triplicate.

Flow cytometry and sorting

SVF cells were separated into CD45+ and CD45− cells using microbeads and autoMACS pro separator (Miltenyi) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CD45− cells were incubated with Fc Block (BD Biosciences) prior to staining. Then, cells were labeled with following antibodies: human CD31-FITC (BD Biosciences, clone WM59, used in 1:20 dilution), CD271-APC (Miltenyi, cloneME20.4–1.H4, 1:20), and CD34-peridinin chlorophyll protein (PerCP) (BD Biosciences, clone 8G12, 1:20) for 15 min at 4°C. Then, cells were washed twice in PBS with 2.0% FBS. Flow cytometric data were acquired by using Accuri C6 (BD Biosciences). Before immunophenotyping, the instrument performance was validated by using BD Accuri Spherotech Validation Beads (BD Biosciences). A total of 30,000–100,000 events were acquired at slow flow rate. Acquired data were analyzed using FlowJo software X 10.0.7 (Tree Star Inc.). Isotype-matched negative controls or fluorescence-minus controls were used to define the threshold for each specific signal and establish the appropriate gate for positive cells.

Differentiation assays

For adipogenic differentiation assay, hMSC Adipogenic Differentiation Medium BulletKit (Lonza, PT-3004) was used according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, human ADSCs were plated in density of 2.1 x 104 cells/cm2 in 24 well-plates. Upon 90–100% confluence, cells were started to be cultured with adipogenic induction media for 3 days, followed by maintenance media cultivation for 2 days. After 3 cycles of this induction culture, cells were cultured with maintenance media for 7 days, then fixed with 4% PFA (paraformaldehyde, Sigma) for 15 min, stained by Oil Red O staining (Sigma), and imaged by microscope.

For osteogenic differentiation assay, hMSC Osteogenic Differentiation Medium BulletKit (Lonza, PT-3002) was used according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Human ADSCs were plated in density of 3.1 x 103 cells/cm2 in 24 well-plates. Upon 60–70% confluence, cells were started to be cultured with osteogenic induction media for 21 days. Media was changed twice per week. Then, cells were fixed with 4% PFA (paraformaldehyde, Sigma) for 1 min, stained by alkaline phosphatase (ALP)-based immunohistochemical staining (N-Histofine New Fuchsin Substrate Kit, NIC-415161F, Cosmo Bio LTD), and imaged by microscope.

RNA interference

Cultured CD271+ progenitors at 20–30% confluence were transfected with 60 nM SignalSilence mTOR siRNA I (CST, #6381) or control siRNA (CST, #6568) using lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. After 48 h of incubation, reagent-containing medium was removed and changed to complete culture medium. At day 2–3 from transfection, cells were used for assays.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from ADSCs using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Total RNA (100 ng) was used to generate cDNA using the TaqMan Universal Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis was performed on an ABI Prism 7300 sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems). The TaqMan probes (Applied Biosystems) used in this study were listed in Table S8. Relative expression was assessed by comparative CT method, correcting for amplification efficiency of the primers, and performed in duplicate.

Western blotting

CD271+ progenitors were washed twice with PBS and cultured with serum-depleted media (DMEM/F12 and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic) for 1 day. Then, human NGF-β (Sigma) was added to culture media (100 ng/mL). After 1-h, total cell extracts were obtained by using RIPA lysis buffer containing Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail and PhosSTOP (Roche Diagnostics). Cell lysates were centrifugated at 12,000 RPM for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected. From them, protein concentration was analyzed by Bradford Protein Assay (Bio-Rad). Proteins were separated by using 8–10% of SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, and incubated (4°C, overnight) with primary antibodies listed in the key resource table. Then, membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (room temperature, 1 h). Image development was obtained by ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad).

Colony-forming unit assay

Cultured AT-CD271+ or CD271− progenitors at passage 4 were used for colony-forming unit (CFU) assays. Cells were detached and plated at a density of 20 cells/cm2 in 10 cm dishes. Half the medium was changed at day 7 after plating. Adherent cell clusters of greater than 50 cells were counted at day 14 with Giemsa staining. The experiments were performed in triplicate for each sample.

Tube formation assay

Human umbilical vascular endothelial cells (HUVEC, Lonza, CC2517) were maintained in culture using Endothelial Cell Growth Medium (EGM2, Lonza). At 70–80% confluence, 26,000 HUVEC cells were replated into 24 well-plates according to manufacturer’s instruction (Endothelial Tube Formation Assay, CBA-200, Cell biolabs). For well coating, we used growth factor reduced-Matrigel with density of 200μL/cm2 (Corning, #356231). Human CD271+ or CD271− progenitors were labeled by PKH as described in cell therapy method section and co-cultured with HUVEC in 1:2 ratio (13,000 cells). Co-culture samples and negative control samples were maintained by M199 media without cocktail of angiogenic growth factor supplements. As positive control samples, HUVEC were maintained in EGM2 media. After 8 h, bright field and immunofluorescent images were acquired (Keyence, BZ-X810). Images were processed and analyzed through ImageJ Angiogenesis Analyzer plug-in. Node is defined as a cell pixel having at least 3 neighbors. Segment is defined as binary line linked with two nodes. Mesh area is defined as area enclosed by segments.76 Data were obtained as average of 5 pictures per sample and statistically analyzed in 4 samples per group tested in duplicates.

Cell therapy model of limb ischemia

6- to 8-week-old athymic nude mice (Jackson Laboratory) were subjected to hindlimb ischemia. Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg). The proximal portion of the left femoral artery was resected. After 24 h, 0.5 million human ADSCs at passage 4–6 were prepared for injection. In brief, ADSCs were harvested by trypsinization, labeled with Paul Karl Horan (PKH)-26 cell linker dye (2 μL/mL, 3 min in room temperature, Sigma), and resuspended in PBS (5.0 × 105 cells in 60 μL with 27 gauge syringes). Labeled ADSCs were immediately injected intra-muscularly at three different sites (gastrocnemius, gracilis, and quadriceps muscles, 20 μL per injection). Sham-operated mice were injected with PBS and used as a control group. At day 7, 14, and 35 after cell therapy, mice were anesthetized and subjected to laser Doppler analysis for blood flow, followed by harvest of gastrocnemius muscles.

Flow cytometry of ADSC treated limb

At 14 days after cell therapy in xenograft model, gastrocnemius muscle was harvested and digested using gentleMACS (Miltenyi) with “m_muscle_01” program and Liberase TM solution. Single cells from digested limb were filtered through 40μm cell strainer and labeled with immunofluorescence-conjugated antibodies; mice CD31-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BD Bioscience, clone MEC 13.3, 1:20), human CD31-FITC (BD Biosciences, clone WM59), human CD271-APC (Miltenyi, cloneME20.4–1.H4, 1:20), human CD90-APC (BD Biosciences, clone 5E10), and human CD105-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BD Biosciences, clone 266, 1:20) for 15 min at 4°C. Flow cytometric data was acquired by similar methods described above in flow cytometry and sorting section.

Blood flow measurement

Blood flow was assessed using Laser Doppler blood flow imager (OZ-2 LCB, OMEGAWAVE, Japan) at day 1, 3, and 14 after induction of limb ischemia. Before scanning, mice were placed on a heating plate at 37°C for 15 min, followed by anesthetization using intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (20 mg/kg). The signal intensities basically reflecting laser scattering by red blood cells within circulation were obtained as blood flow value. The mean blood flow value was acquired in region of interest (ROI) capturing the whole mouse lower limb. The ratio of blood flow values of the ischemic (left) limb to the healthy31 limb were calculated as blood flow recovery to avoid data variations caused by ambient light and temperature.8,9

In vivo proliferation assay

EdU solution (5mg/2mL) was administered via intra-peritoneal injection at day 6 after ADSC therapy. At day 7, the gastrocnemius muscles were harvested and processed to frozen sections. EdU positive nucleus were detected using EdU in vivo imaging kits (BCK488-IV-IM, baseclick) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The number of EdU+ and DAPI+ nucleus was counted in frozen sections in duplicate.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue vascularization and ADSCs engraftment were evaluated at day 14 and 35. Mice were anesthetized and injected with 50 μg of FITC-conjugated isolectin B4 (FL-1201, Vector Laboratories) via tail vein. After 10 min, mice were perfused with PBS for 3 min to remove unbound isolectin B4 in the blood vessels, and perfusion-fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The gastrocnemius muscles were removed, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C overnight, and incubated in 30% sucrose solution for 24 h. Frozen sections of the tissue were obtained at 10 μm thickness and counterstained with DAPI. Capillary density and engraftment of injected ADSCs were analyzed by counting FITC and PKH positive cells, respectively, in five randomly selected fields per section. The experiments were performed in duplicate or triplicate for each human ADSCs sample.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± SEM, and statistical analysis was performed with Prism 9 software (GraphPad, USA). Group comparisons were performed using paired or unpaired t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson correlation for normally distributed data (Shapiro–Wilk normality test) or Spearman correlation for nonnormally distributed data. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.005 and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.001. All reported statistical tests were two-tailed.
