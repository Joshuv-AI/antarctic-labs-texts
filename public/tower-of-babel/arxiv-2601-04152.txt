1.
Introduction

The classical singularity theorems of general relativity show that under appropriate energy and causality conditions, spacetime geodesics are incomplete
[
7
]
. What these theorems do
not
decide by themselves is whether the singular boundary (associated with this geodesic incompleteness) is hidden behind a trapped region or is (at least locally) visible. The problem of finding suitable conditions that lead to the formation of locally naked singularities is still a vastly open, and typically quite challenging pursuit. Here we address a related problem of finding obstructions for a locally visible singularity in a certain class of spacetimes to be globally visible, in the sense that any null geodesic issuing from the locally naked singularity cannot escape to future null infinity. Note that in particular such obstructions do not claim anything about cosmic censorship
[
12
]
directly, since one already assumes the existence of a past-singular boundary of the spacetime under consideration. However these obstructions and the methods used to derive them may be of independent interest in order to obtain a better understanding of certain aspects of naked singularities. In particular the conditions presented here will demonstrate how the behavior of curvature and shear along null geodesics emanating from such singularities affect their global visibility. The methods rely heavily on various notions from Lorentzian causality theory, for which the unfamiliar reader is referred to
[
10
]
. Note that such methods have already been employed in the past by Tipler
[
13
,
15
,
14
]
, Mingarelli
[
9
]
, and Hawking and Penrose
[
6
]
in various different contexts.

1.1.
Setup and guiding idea

Fix a future-directed, affinely parametrized null geodesic
\gamma:I\to M
, and choose an auxiliary null vector field
n\in\Gamma(TM)
with
g(k,n)=-1
, where
k
is the tangent to
\gamma
. The
screen bundle
of
M
is the
m\coloneqq N-1
-dimensional vector bundle over
M
with the underlying set
S_{\lambda}:=\{X:\ g(X,k)=g(X,n)=0\}
. The deformation of a thin null congruence around
\gamma
is encoded by the
optical endomorphism

B:S_{\lambda}\to S_{\lambda}
, defined by
(J^{\perp})^{\prime}=B(J^{\perp})
for screen-projected Jacobi fields
J^{\perp}
. It splits as,

\displaystyle B=\frac{1}{m}\theta\,\mathrm{Id}+\sigma+\omega,

where
\theta
is the expansion,
\sigma
the trace-free shear, and
\omega
the twist. Throughout we work in the twist-free case (
\omega\equiv 0
) and impose the null energy condition (NEC), i.e.,
\mathrm{Ric}(k,k)=T(k,k)\geq 0
.

Let
\Pi:TM\to S_{\lambda}
be the orthogonal projection to the screen-bundle. Then the trace of the optical evolution identity
\nabla_{k}B=-B^{2}-\Pi\circ R(\cdot,k)k
yields the Raychaudhuri equation

(1.1)

\theta^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{m}\theta^{2}-\|\sigma\|^{2}-\mathrm{Ric}(k,k),

where
(\cdot)^{\prime}
denotes differentiation with respect to the affine parameter
\lambda
(we shall use this notation throughout the paper). Let
D(\lambda):\mathcal{\mathcal{}}S_{\lambda_{0}}\to\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
be the
Jacobi map
that sends initial screen vectors at
\lambda_{0}
to the corresponding Jacobi fields at
\lambda
. To extract a
linear
equation from the Raychaudhuri equation, we rescale the congruence’s
m
-volume
\mathcal{A}(\lambda):=|\det D(\lambda)|
(with
D^{\prime}=BD
,
D(\lambda_{0})=\mathrm{Id}
) by,

\displaystyle u(\lambda):=\mathcal{A}(\lambda)^{1/m}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\theta=m\,\frac{u^{\prime}}{u}.

Differentiating with respect to
\lambda
and using (
1.1
) cancels the quadratic term and yields the scalar ODE

(1.2)

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{m}f\,u=0,\qquad f:=\|\sigma\|^{2}+T(k,k).

Equation (
1.2
) is the backbone of our analysis. It reduces the geometric focusing problem to a second-order Sturm-type equation along
one
null geodesic (which is part of a congruence).

1.2.
Zero of
u
, conjugate points, and pre-horizon placement

The existence of a first zero of
u
has two immediate consequences. First,
u(\lambda_{*})=0
if and only if
\det D(\lambda_{*})=0
, i.e. there is a nontrivial screen Jacobi field
J
with
J(\lambda_{*})=0
(a conjugate point). Second,
\theta=m\,u^{\prime}/u\to-\infty
as
\lambda\to\lambda_{*}
, which implies that the focusing is genuine and not a coordinate artifact. If in addition the spacetime is asymptotically flat and its future null infinity satisfies certain properties (as made precise in Theorem
6.7
), and moreover the null geodesic (and the congruence thereof) is past-incomplete, i.e., it terminates at a singularity in the past, and the first zero occurs at
\lambda_{*}<\lambda_{\mathrm{AH}}
(where
\lambda_{\mathrm{AH}}
is the first parameter where the outgoing expansion
\theta_{+}
of a suitable congruence vanishes), and moreover the segment up to
\lambda_{\mathrm{AH}}
is regular and untrapped, then we shall show the singularity is
not globally visible
along
\gamma
.

1.3.
Two possible obstructions to global visibility

We provide two different routes to force a pre-horizon zero of
u
:

(1)

Barrier route: On any interval where
f\geq 0
and the affine-linear
barrier

b(\lambda)=u(\lambda_{0})+u^{\prime}(\lambda_{0})(\lambda-\lambda_{0})
stays positive, a weighted inequality of the form

\frac{1}{m}\int(\lambda_{1}-s)\,f(s)\,b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s>u(\lambda_{0})+u^{\prime}(\lambda_{0})(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})

forces
u(\lambda_{1})<0
, thereby guaranteeing a zero of
u
in
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
.

(2)

Sturm/Prüfer route: Writing
q:=f/m
, if
\int_{c}^{d}\sqrt{q(\lambda)}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda>\pi
on some subinterval
(c,d)
contained in the regular region, then the solution of (
1.2
) has a zero of
u
in
(c,d)
.

Both tests require a condition on
f
along a single chosen geodesic. Recall that all of this holds under the twist-free evolution along
\gamma
along with the NEC. The auxiliary null vector field
n
only determines the screen and eventually drops out of all invariants. Moreover, affine reparametrizations leave the criteria unchanged.

2.
Geometric framework and preliminaries

Let
(M,g)
be a time-oriented Lorentzian manifold. Fix a future-directed null geodesic congruence with tangent
k
, affinely parametrized by
\lambda
so that
\nabla_{k}k=0
along each geodesic of the congruence. Choose an auxiliary future-directed null vector field
n
with
g(k,n)=-1
. The
screen bundle
at parameter
\lambda
is the
m
-dimensional vector bundle with the underlying set,

\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}:=\{X\in T_{\gamma(\lambda)}M:\ g(X,k)=g(X,n)=0\},\qquad m:=N-1,

with inner product
h:=g|_{\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}}
. Let
\Pi:TM\to\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
be the
orthogonal projector
.

Definition 2.1

(Optical map and scalars)
.

If
J
is a Jacobi field along a geodesic, its screen projection
J^{\perp}:=\Pi\circ J
satisfies
(J^{\perp})^{\prime}=B(J^{\perp})
, defining the
optical endomorphism

B:\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}\to\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
. One may decompose

B=\frac{1}{m}\theta\,\mathrm{Id}+\sigma+\omega,\qquad\theta:=\mathrm{tr}B,\quad\mathrm{tr}\sigma=0,\quad\omega^{\top}=-\omega.

We assume that the congruence is
twist-free
(
\omega\equiv 0
) throughout. Define
\|\sigma\|^{2}:=\mathrm{tr}(\sigma^{\top}\sigma)\geq 0
.

Remark 2.2

(Energy condition)
.

Under the NEC,
T(X,X)\geq 0
for every null
X
. Einstein’s equations in
(N+1)
dimensions yield
\mathrm{Ric}(k,k)=T(k,k)\geq 0
.

2.1.
The Raychaudhuri equation

For the sake of completeness, we first establish the optical evolution identity, then derive the Raychaudhuri equation by taking the screen bundle trace.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
R(U,V)W:=\nabla_{U}\nabla_{V}W-\nabla_{V}\nabla_{U}W-\nabla_{[U,V]}W
be the Riemann curvature operator for
U,V,W\in\Gamma(TM)
. Along each geodesic
\gamma
with tangent
k
, one has,

(2.1)

\nabla_{k}B=-B^{2}-\Pi\circ R(\,\cdot\,,k)k\big|_{S}.

Proof.

Fix a geodesic
\gamma:I\to M
and a screen Jacobi field
J
along it, i.e.
J(\lambda)\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
and
g(J(\lambda),k)=g(J(\lambda),n)=0
for all
\lambda\in I
. The Jacobi equation is given by
J^{\prime\prime}=R(k,J)k
. Projecting both sides with
\Pi
yields
\Pi\circ J^{\prime\prime}=\Pi\circ R(k,J)k
. Since
J^{\perp}:=\Pi\circ J
and
\Pi
is parallel along
k
on
S
(we identify screen fibers by parallel transport), the left hand side equals
(J^{\perp})^{\prime\prime}
. By definition
(J^{\perp})^{\prime}=B(J^{\perp})
. It follows that,

(J^{\perp})^{\prime\prime}=B^{\prime}(J^{\perp})+B\big((J^{\perp})^{\prime}\big)=B^{\prime}(J^{\perp})+B^{2}(J^{\perp}).

Hence for every screen vector field of the form
J^{\perp}
we have

B^{\prime}(J^{\perp})+B^{2}(J^{\perp})=\Pi\,R(k,J)k.

As the vector fields
J^{\perp}
span
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
(by choosing initial data for two linearly independent screen Jacobi fields and propagating), we can identify operators acting on
S
to get

\nabla_{k}B=-B^{2}-\Pi\circ R(\,\cdot\,,k)k\big|_{S}.

This completes the proof.

∎

Proposition 2.4
.

With
m=N-1
and
\omega=0
, one has,

(2.2)

\theta^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{m}\theta^{2}-\|\sigma\|^{2}-\mathrm{Ric}(k,k).

Proof.

Take the screen bundle trace of equation
2.1
gives,

\mathrm{tr}(\nabla_{k}B)=-\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})-\mathrm{tr}\big(\Pi\circ R(\,\cdot\,,k)k\big).

Since
\mathrm{tr}(\nabla_{k}B)=\nabla_{k}(\mathrm{tr}B)=\theta^{\prime}
, it remains to evaluate
\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})
and the curvature trace. Write
B=(\theta/m)\,\mathrm{Id}+\sigma
. It follows that,

\displaystyle B^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{m}\theta\,\mathrm{Id}+\sigma\Big)^{2}=\frac{\theta^{2}}{m^{2}}\mathrm{Id}+\frac{2\theta}{m}\sigma+\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(B^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta^{2}}{m^{2}}\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Id})+\frac{2\theta}{m}\mathrm{tr}(\sigma)+\mathrm{tr}(\sigma^{2})=\frac{\theta^{2}}{m}+\|\sigma\|^{2},

Here we have used
\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Id})=m
and
\mathrm{tr}(\sigma)=0
. For the curvature, orthogonal projection does not affect the trace on
S
, and hence,

\mathrm{tr}\big(\Pi\circ R(\,\cdot\,,k)k\big)=\mathrm{tr}\big(R(\,\cdot\,,k)k|_{S}\big)=\mathrm{Ric}(k,k).

Therefore
\theta^{\prime}=-\theta^{2}/m-\|\sigma\|^{2}-\mathrm{Ric}(k,k)
as claimed.

∎

2.2.
Jacobi map, rescaling, and linearization

Definition 2.5

(Jacobi map and beam
m
-volume)
.

Fix
\lambda_{0}
and identify
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda_{0}}
with
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
by parallel transport along
k
. The
Jacobi map

D(\lambda):\mathcal{S}_{\lambda_{0}}\to\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
sends initial screen vectors to the corresponding Jacobi field values at
\lambda
, and satisfies
D(\lambda_{0})=\mathrm{Id}
,
D^{\prime}=BD
. Define the unsigned
m
-volume density
and
beam scale

\mathcal{A}(\lambda):=|\det D(\lambda)|,\qquad u(\lambda):=\mathcal{A}(\lambda)^{1/m}.

Lemma 2.6
.

For
u>0
, one has,

\theta=\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}=m\,\frac{u^{\prime}}{u}.

Proof.

Liouville’s formula for matrix-valued ODEs gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\det D=\mathrm{tr}(B)\,\det D=\theta\,\det D.

Taking the absolute value and using that
\mathcal{A}=|\det D|
, we obtain
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\theta\mathcal{A}
wherever
\det D\neq 0
(hence
\mathcal{A}>0
). Since
\mathcal{A}=u^{m}
, differentiating with respect to
\lambda
gives,

\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{(u^{m})^{\prime}}{u^{m}}=m\,\frac{u^{\prime}}{u}.

Combining the two expressions yields the required identity.

∎

Proposition 2.7
.

On any interval where
u>0
, one has,

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{m}\,f\,u=0,\qquad f:=\|\sigma\|^{2}+T(k,k)\ (\geq 0\ \text{under NEC}).

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.6
,
\theta=m\,u^{\prime}/u
. Differentiating with respect to
\lambda
gives,

\theta^{\prime}=m\left(\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{u}-\frac{(u^{\prime})^{2}}{u^{2}}\right).

Inserting into the Raychaudhuri equation
2.2
gives,

m\left(\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{u}-\frac{(u^{\prime})^{2}}{u^{2}}\right)=-\frac{1}{m}\theta^{2}-\|\sigma\|^{2}-T(k,k)=-\frac{1}{m}\,m^{2}\frac{(u^{\prime})^{2}}{u^{2}}-\|\sigma\|^{2}-T(k,k).

The terms in
(u^{\prime}/u)^{2}
cancel exactly, leaving

m\,\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{u}=-\big(\|\sigma\|^{2}+T(k,k)\big).

Multiplying by
u
gives
u^{\prime\prime}+(f/m)\ u=0
with
f:=\|\sigma\|^{2}+T(k,k)\geq 0
by NEC.

∎

3.
The barrier route to conjugacy

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u
solve
u^{\prime\prime}+(f/m)\,u=0
with
f\in C^{0}([\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}])
. Then for any initial data
u(\lambda_{0})=u_{0}>0
,
u^{\prime}(\lambda_{0})=u_{0}^{\prime}
, one has,

(3.1)

u(\lambda_{1})=u_{0}+u_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})-\frac{1}{m}\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda_{1}-s)\,f(s)\,u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

Integrating the ODE from
\lambda_{0}
to
t
gives,

u^{\prime}(t)-u_{0}^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{m}\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{t}f(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Integrating in
t
from
\lambda_{0}
to
\lambda_{1}
then gives,

u(\lambda_{1})-u_{0}=u_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})-\frac{1}{m}\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{\lambda_{1}}\left(\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{t}f(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}t.

The integration domain is the triangle
\{(\!s,t):\lambda_{0}\leq s\leq t\leq\lambda_{1}\}
. Swapping the order (Fubini/Tonelli applies since the integrand is continuous) gives,

\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{\lambda_{1}}\left(\int_{t=\;s}^{\lambda_{1}}\mathrm{d}t\right)f(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda_{1}-s)\,f(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Substituting this into the previous equation yields the claimed identity.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

If
f\geq 0
and
u\geq 0
on an interval
I
, then
u^{\prime\prime}=-(1/m)fu\leq 0
on
I
. In particular, if
u>0
on
[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]
, then
u
is concave on that interval.

Proof.

Immediate from the ODE and the nonnegativity of
f
and
u
.

∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
b(s):=u_{0}+u_{0}^{\prime}(s-\lambda_{0})
and assume
f\geq 0
on
[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]
,
u_{0}>0
,
u_{0}^{\prime}<0
, and
b>0
on
[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]
. Then,

(a)

u\geq b
on
[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]
, and,

(b)

if

\frac{1}{m}\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda_{1}-s)\,f(s)\,b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\ >\ u_{0}+u_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}),

then
u(\lambda_{1})<0
. Hence there exists
\lambda_{*}\in(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
with
u(\lambda_{*})=0
.

Proof.

(a) Define
w:=u-b
. Then
w(\lambda_{0})=w^{\prime}(\lambda_{0})=0
. On any subinterval where
u\geq 0
, we have

w^{\prime\prime}=u^{\prime\prime}-b^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{1}{m}fu-0\leq 0,

so
w
is concave. If
w
becomes negative first at
\hat{\lambda}\in(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}]
, by concavity one has
w(\hat{\lambda})=\min w<0
and
w^{\prime}(\hat{\lambda})=0
, but then the graph of
w
would lie strictly below the tangent at
\lambda_{0}
(which is identically zero) even for
\lambda
near
\lambda_{0}
, contradicting
w(\lambda_{0})=w^{\prime}(\lambda_{0})=0
. Hence
w\geq 0
and
u\geq b
.

(b) Inserting
u\geq b
into the inequality
3.1
gives,

u(\lambda_{1})\leq u_{0}+u_{0}^{\prime}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})-\frac{1}{m}\int_{\lambda_{0}}^{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda_{1}-s)\,f(s)\,b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

If the right-hand side is negative, then
u(\lambda_{1})<0
. Since
u(\lambda_{0})=u_{0}>0
and
u
is continuous, the intermediate value theorem guarantees a zero in
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})
.

∎

4.
The Sturm route to conjugacy

For this section we write the Raychaudhuri equation as
y^{\prime\prime}+q(\lambda)y=0
with
q(\lambda):=f(\lambda)/m\geq 0.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
j\in\{0,1\}
, if
y_{j}^{\prime\prime}+q_{j}y_{j}=0
with continuous
q_{j}
, then

\big(y_{0}y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{0}^{\prime}y_{1}\big)^{\prime}=(q_{0}-q_{1})\,y_{0}y_{1}.

Proof.

Differentiating
y_{0}y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{0}^{\prime}y_{1}
and using
y_{j}^{\prime\prime}=-q_{j}y_{j}
gives,

(y_{0}y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{0}^{\prime}y_{1})^{\prime}=y_{0}^{\prime}y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{0}^{\prime\prime}y_{1}-y_{0}^{\prime}y_{1}^{\prime}+y_{0}y_{1}^{\prime\prime}=-(-q_{0}y_{0})y_{1}+y_{0}(-q_{1}y_{1})=(q_{0}-q_{1})y_{0}y_{1}.

∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
q_{1}\geq q_{0}\geq 0
on
[\alpha,\beta]
. If a nontrivial solution
y_{0}
of
y_{0}^{\prime\prime}+q_{0}y_{0}=0
has consecutive zeros at
\xi<\eta
, then any solution
y_{1}
of
y_{1}^{\prime\prime}+q_{1}y_{1}=0
has a zero in
(\xi,\eta)
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that a solution
y_{1}
has no zero in
(\xi,\eta)
. Multiply by
-1
if necessary so that
y_{1}>0
there. Between consecutive zeros,
y_{0}
has constant sign. Replace
y_{0}
by
-y_{0}
if needed so
y_{0}>0
on
(\xi,\eta)
. Define the Wronskian-like quantity
W:=y_{0}y_{1}^{\prime}-y_{0}^{\prime}y_{1}
. By
Lemma

4.1
,
W^{\prime}=(q_{0}-q_{1})y_{0}y_{1}\leq 0
, so
W
is nonincreasing. We then evaluate one-sided limits at the endpoints. Since
y_{0}(\xi)=0
and
y_{0}^{\prime}(\xi)>0
(a simple zero with sign change from negative to positive), we have
W(\xi)=-y_{0}^{\prime}(\xi)y_{1}(\xi)<0
. Similarly,
y_{0}(\eta)=0
and
y_{0}^{\prime}(\eta)<0
, hence
W(\eta)=-y_{0}^{\prime}(\eta)y_{1}(\eta)>0
. But a nonincreasing
W
cannot go from negative to positive, which proves the claim.

∎

Theorem 4.3
.

If
y
is a nontrivial solution of
y^{\prime\prime}+qy=0
with continuous
q
, then between two consecutive zeros of
y
, every linearly independent solution has exactly one zero.

Proof.

Fix consecutive zeros
\xi<\eta
of
y
. By
Theorem

4.2
applied with
q_{0}=q_{1}=q
, every solution has at least one zero in
(\xi,\eta)
. If a linearly independent solution
\tilde{y}
had two or more zeros there, then the Wronskian
W=y\tilde{y}^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\tilde{y}
would vanish at two points and hence be identically zero (for instance by Abel’s identity for continuous
q
), implying linear dependence. Thus exactly one zero occurs.

∎

4.1.
Prüfer transform and integral criterion

Definition 4.4

(Prüfer coordinates)
.

Assume
q\in C^{1}([a,b])
and
q>0
on
[a,b]
. For any nontrivial solution
y
, define functions
\rho>0
and a continuous angle
\vartheta
by,

y\coloneqq\rho\sin\vartheta,\qquad\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{q}}\coloneqq\rho\cos\vartheta,

with the branch fixed by
\vartheta(a)=0
if
y(a)=0
and
y^{\prime}(a)>0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

With
q\in C^{1}
and
q>0
, one has,

\vartheta^{\prime}=\sqrt{q}+\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\,\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta,\qquad\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho}=-\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\,\sin^{2}\vartheta.

Proof.

Differentiating
y=\rho\sin\vartheta
gives,

y^{\prime}=\rho^{\prime}\sin\vartheta+\rho\vartheta^{\prime}\cos\vartheta.

But
y^{\prime}=\sqrt{q}\,\rho\cos\vartheta
by definition, so,

(4.1)

\rho^{\prime}\sin\vartheta+\rho\vartheta^{\prime}\cos\vartheta=\sqrt{q}\,\rho\cos\vartheta.

Differentiating
y^{\prime}/\sqrt{q}=\rho\cos\vartheta
gives,

\frac{y^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{q}}-\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q\sqrt{q}}\,y^{\prime}=\rho^{\prime}\cos\vartheta-\rho\vartheta^{\prime}\sin\vartheta.

Since
y^{\prime\prime}=-qy=-q\rho\sin\vartheta
and
y^{\prime}=\sqrt{q}\,\rho\cos\vartheta
, this becomes,

(4.2)

-\sqrt{q}\,\rho\sin\vartheta-\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\,\rho\cos\vartheta=\rho^{\prime}\cos\vartheta-\rho\vartheta^{\prime}\sin\vartheta

Treating equations (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) as a linear system in the unknowns
\rho^{\prime}
and
\vartheta^{\prime}
, we multiply (
4.1
) by
\sin\vartheta
and (
4.2
) by
\cos\vartheta
, and add them to eliminate
\rho^{\prime}
, giving,

\rho\vartheta^{\prime}(\cos^{2}\vartheta+\sin^{2}\vartheta)=\sqrt{q}\,\rho\cos^{2}\vartheta-\sqrt{q}\,\rho\sin^{2}\vartheta-\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\,\rho\cos^{2}\vartheta.

It follows that,

\vartheta^{\prime}=\sqrt{q}\,(\cos^{2}\vartheta-\sin^{2}\vartheta)-\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\cos^{2}\vartheta=\sqrt{q}\,\cos(2\vartheta)-\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\frac{1+\cos(2\vartheta)}{2}.

Equivalently, using
\cos(2\vartheta)=1-2\sin^{2}\vartheta
and
\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta=\sin(2\vartheta)/2
, one simplifies to,

\vartheta^{\prime}=\sqrt{q}+\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta.

To obtain
\rho^{\prime}/\rho
, we multiply (

) by
\cos\vartheta
and (
4.2
) by
\sin\vartheta
and subtract them, eliminating
\theta^{\prime}
. This gives,

\rho^{\prime}\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta+\rho\vartheta^{\prime}\cos^{2}\vartheta=\sqrt{q}\,\rho\cos^{2}\vartheta,

-\sqrt{q}\,\rho\sin^{2}\vartheta-\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\,\rho\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta=\rho^{\prime}\cos^{2}\vartheta-\rho\vartheta^{\prime}\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta.

Adding the two equations to eliminate
\vartheta^{\prime}
and solving for
\rho^{\prime}/\rho
yields,

\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho}=-\frac{q^{\prime}}{2q}\sin^{2}\vartheta.

This completes the proof.

∎

Lemma 4.6
.

For

with
q>0
and

,

\vartheta(b)-\vartheta(a)\ \geq\ \int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda-\frac{1}{4}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{|q^{\prime}|}{q}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Proof.

From

Integrating from

∎

.

Proposition 5.2
.

\nabla_{k}k=\nabla_{\Omega^{-2}\partial_{v}}(\Omega^{-2}\partial_{v})=\Omega^{-4}\nabla_{\partial_{v}}\partial_{v}+\Omega^{-2}(\partial_{v}\Omega^{-2})\,\partial_{v}=\Omega^{-4}\cdot 2(\partial_{v}\log\Omega)\partial_{v}-2\Omega^{-4}(\partial_{v}\log\Omega)\partial_{v}=0.

The

-part contributes nothing because

Definition 7.5

Lemma 7.6

Let
p=(u,v,\omega)\in M
with

Proof.

Lemma 7.9
.

In
3+1

\mathrm{Ric}_{ab}=\nabla_{a}\phi\,\nabla_{b}\phi.

Proof.

Taking the trace of

gives,

. We rewrite

Substituting
R=(\nabla\phi)^{2}

Contracting with
k^{a}k^{b}
and using
g(k,k)=0
yields (
7.8
).
