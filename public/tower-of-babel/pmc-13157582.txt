Proteomic and Super‐Resolution Analyses of Murine Brown Adipocyte‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles
Authors: Menchus Quan, Yi‐Kai Liu, Maryam Mahmoodi, Muhammad Asif, Madeline Kay, Yongjie Yang, Fang Huang, Weiguo Andy Tao, Shihuan Kuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThermogenic brown adipocytes (BAs) are not only an energy sink but also regulate systemic metabolic health through secreted factors and extracellular vesicles (EVs). Here, we investigate the baseline profiles BA‐EVs and how cold‐induced thermogenesis alters the proteome of BA‐EVs. We first established and validated transgenic mouse models expressing human CD63 (hCD63)‐GFP for visualisation and isolation of EVs exclusively from adipocytes and BAs. We then used super‐resolution microscopy to compare the subcellular localisation and maturation of EVs in adipocytes grown in culture or directly isolated from mice, revealing distinct features. We then used immunoprecipitation to enrich BA‐EVs and employed mass spectrometry to define the EV‐proteomes. We discovered that cold stimulation elevated secretion of mitochondrial and lysosomal proteins in BA‐EVs. We validated the presence and activity of creatine kinase B (CKB) in BA‐EVs. We finally showed that BA‐EVs promote thermogenic gene expression in white adipocytes, but cold stimulation surprisingly blunted EV's effect in up‐regulating Prdm16 expression. These data establish a novel system for analysing EV contents in vivo and provide insight into how EV proteomes dynamically adapt to non‐shivering thermogenesis.

Introduction

Thermogenic brown adipose tissue (BAT) is crucial for maintaining body temperature in both rodents and infant humans, especially during times of cold exposure (Himms‐Hagen 1990). Additionally, BAT and its secretome have been shown to have the potential to regulate overall energy expenditure and the metabolic state of other tissues (Wankhade et al. 2016; Singh et al. 2021; Cheong and Xu 2021). In adult humans, although only small amounts of BAT are present, increased amounts of BAT are strongly correlated with better metabolic health (Seale and Lazar 2009), suggesting that the tissue may exert beneficial effects through communication with other metabolic organs. The crosstalk is often mediated by secreted factors of BAT, termed “Batokines” that include cytokines, lipids, hormones, and extracellular vesicles (EVs). Notable examples of batokines include: IL‐6, a key regulator of inflammation and hepatic metabolism (Qing et al. 2020); 12,13‐diHOME, a bioactive lipid that increases skeletal muscle fatty acid and glucose uptake (Pinckard et al. 2021); and myostatin, which impairs skeletal muscle capacity (Kong et al. 2018).

EVs secreted by BAT have been demonstrated to have both local and systemic impacts on various cell populations throughout the body (Ghesmati et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2020; Rosina et al. 2022). It has been reported that administration of BAT‐derived EVs was capable of ameliorating metabolic dysfunction in a mouse model of diet‐induced obesity by reducing fat mass gain and adiposity (Zhou et al. 2020). EVs exert their biological function through their carried cargos (contents), such as DNA, RNA, lipids, proteins, and even fragmented organelles (Kalluri and LeBleu 2020; Wang et al. 2020). The most well‐studied cargo of these EVs is miRNAs, short (20–24 base pairs) double‐stranded RNAs that regulate the stability of mRNAs (Shang et al. 2023). It has been reported that adipocyte‐specific deletion of DICER, an enzyme required for the generation of miRNAs, disrupts metabolic homeostasis in mice (Mori et al. 2014). Emerging studies have demonstrated that during cold‐induced thermogenesis (CIT), BAT ejects damaged mitochondria through EVs in order to maintain sufficient thermogenic capacity (Rosina et al. 2022). The protein content of EVs is quite variable and is highly influenced by the general state of the secreting cell, and there is evidence that the sorting of these proteins into EVs for secretion is a deliberate process (Dixson et al. 2023). EVs secreted during a state of CIT have been shown to have a wide range of beneficial effects on a variety of cell populations (Wankhade et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2020; Du et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2023). Therefore, the identification of EV proteins secreted during CIT has the potential to reveal novel regulators of metabolic states of tissues throughout the body.

Previous studies have attempted to identify EV proteins secreted by BATs and brown adipocytes (BAs) (Zhou et al. 2020; Rosina et al. 2022). Due to the difficulty of isolating EVs from the relatively small BAT, these studies relied on ex vivo culture to obtain a sufficient amount of EVs for analysis, potentially shifting the EV proteome away from the true physiological state (Dufau et al. 2021). Even if EVs were isolated from the BAT, the resulting EVs do not necessarily reflect those of BAs, as BAT contains a multitude of EV‐secreting cell populations (Bertola et al. 2022). Although these studies do identify differences between the EV proteome of BAT during room temperature and CIT, there is no way to discriminate the cellular origin of the EV proteins. Identification of EV proteins secreted from BAs allows for a more precise understanding of how non‐shivering thermogenesis affects the secretome of this population and its effects on the microenvironment and distal tissues. In this study, we developed a novel method for isolating and studying the EVs secreted specifically by BAs. Our proteomic analysis revealed novel cold‐induced BA‐EV proteins with metabolic effects on recipient cells.

Results

A Mouse Model for Labelling Adipocyte‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles in vivo

Adipose tissue is a highly heterogeneous tissue comprised of adipocytes, immune cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and mesenchymal stem cells (Bertola et al. 2022; Maniyadath et al. 2023). To specifically mark EVs in adipocytes, we used Adipoq‐Cre mice to drive the expression of transgenic human CD63 (hCD63)‐GFP fusion protein in Rosa26LSL‐hCD63‐copGFP mice (Men et al. 2019). In the resulting transgenic mouse model that we coined Ad/hCD63, expression of Cre recombinase is controlled by the regulatory sequence of the adipocyte‐specific adiponectin (Adipoq) gene (Figure 1A). Cre expression leads to the removal of the stop codon flanked by loxP sites (LSL) and subsequent expression of hCD63‐copGFP in adipocytes (Figure 1A). As CD63 is an integral membrane protein of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) that give rise to secreted vesicles, adipocyte‐derived EVs should be marked by GFP in the Ad/hCD63 reporter mouse model (Figure 1A).

To validate the labelling efficiency and specificity of the Ad/hCD63 reporter mice, we first examined tissue sections using immunofluorescence microscopy to confirm the extensive punctate expression patterns of GFP in both WAT and BAT of the Ad/hCD63 mice (Figure 1B, Figure S1B). The punctate pattern of GFP reflects the well‐known MVB distribution patterns in cells. As a control, GFP was not detectable in any adipose tissues of the WT mice that lack the AdipoqCre or RosaLSL‐hCD63‐copGFP transgene (Figure 1B). We then stained the WAT sections with an hCD63 antibody and detected a very high degree of colocalisation of hCD63 and GFP only in the Ad/hCD63 but not WT mice (Figure 1B), indicating that the GFP marks MVBs. To determine if the GFP puncta are located in adipocytes, we dissociated cells from the inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) of Ad/hCD63 mice and collected the floating cells that are filled with a single large lipid droplet, characteristic of mature adipocytes (Figure 1C). Punctate GFP signal was readily detectable in these lipid‐filled (marked by LipidTox) adipocytes (Figure 1D). Notably, the GFP and hCD63 signals overlap perfectly within individual mature adipocytes (Figure 1E). These data demonstrate that GFP signal in our Ad/hCD63 mice efficiently marks MVBs in adipocytes.

To determine the adipocyte‐specificity of the reporter, we examined the GFP signal in non‐adipocyte tissues. GFP signal was rarely observed in cross‐sections of skeletal muscles (Figure 1F). The few GFP puncta observed in the muscle sections were located in the interstitium or associated with vasculature‐like structures (Figure 1F), based on a chain of DAPI‐marked nuclei. These observations suggest that the GFP puncta in the muscle are located within perivascular adipocytes or represent adipocyte‐derived circulating EVs. To further confirm the specificity of hCD63 in EV labelling, we isolated EVs from the iWAT of WT and Ad/hCD63 mice via ultracentrifugation. We found that hCD63 was only detected in TSG101‐expressing EVs of Ad/hCD63 WAT but not in WT EVs (Figure S1A). Together, these data establish the utility and specificity of the adipocyte hCD63‐GFP reporter mice.

We further examined GFP and hCD63 expression in cultured adipocytes. Stromal vascular fraction (SVF) cells isolated from iWAT of Ad/hCD63 mice did not express GFP, suggesting that the reporter does not mark adipo‐progenitors (or preadipocytes). We then differentiated SVF cells into lipid droplet‐laden adipocytes. The presence of copGFP was primarily observed in close proximity to lipid droplets (stained with Oil Red) but not in regions devoid of lipid droplets, confirming that only differentiated adipocytes, but not undifferentiated SVF cells, express copGFP (Figure 1G). The high degree of colocalisation between hCD63 and copGFP immunofluorescence signals further confirms that the GFP signal marks MVBs (Figure 1H). Taken together, these data demonstrate that the Ad/hCD63 model allows for the specific labelling and visualisation of hCD63‐GFP fusion protein in mature adipocytes.

Super‐Resolution Imaging to Characterise MVBs in Adipocytes

To explore the structures and maturation of MVBs labelled by GFP in the Ad/hCD63 adipocytes, we performed super‐resolution microscopy on in vitro differentiated adipocytes (Figure 2A) and mature adipocytes isolated from reporter mice (Figure 2B), using a custom‐built direct stochastic single molecule localisation microscopy (3D SMLM) system (Huang et al. 2016). in vitro differentiated adipocytes are characterised by their small size (typically < 20 µm in diameter) and multiple small lipid droplets (Figure 2A). These immature adipocytes contained numerous intracellular hCD63 puncta that were evenly distributed in the cytoplasm surrounding the nucleus of adipocytes (Figure 2A). In contrast, mature adipocytes isolated from iWAT were much larger (∼200 µm in diameter), and these mature adipocytes contained hCD63 puncta that were localised in small pockets in the cell (Figure 2B).

High magnification imaging of the hCD63 puncta in immature adipocytes revealed structures with morphology and size resembling lysosomes (Barral et al. 2022; Pols and Klumperman 2009), where hCD63 puncta are found primarily around the outside perimeter and absent in the lumen (Figure 2C). We also observed hCD63 puncta in structures resembling MVBs and late endosomes (Pols and Klumperman 2009; von Bartheld and Altick 2011), where the hCD63 puncta were completely packed within the intraluminal space (Figure 2D). These intraluminal vesicles, if transported to the outer cell membrane, fuse with the plasma membrane and are released as exosomes (Wu et al. 2015).

Zooming into individual pre‐exosomal vesicles in mature adipocytes revealed that they are surrounded evenly by hCD63 puncta (Figure 2E). Interestingly, the size distribution suggests that there are two populations of pre‐exosomal vesicles, one with a median diameter of around 50 µm and the other population with a median diameter of around 125 µm, presumably representing different EV subtypes or maturation stages (Figure 2E). The presence of hCD63 within these intracellular structures indicates the proper subcellular localisation of the transgenic protein. These results represent one of the first direct visualisations of the maturation of exosomes within different compartments of adipocytes.

We further compared the distribution of CD63 in lysosomes of immature adipocytes and mature adipocytes in vivo, and observed several significant differences. The first is that the average area of these hCD63+ lysosomes was significantly larger in mature adipocytes in comparison to the immature adipocytes (Figure 2F). The next major difference is the hCD63 puncta localisation in the lysosomes. In the immature adipocytes, hCD63 puncta were very intense and more numerous, forming continuous boundaries at the periphery of lysosomes (Figure 2G). In contrast, the hCD63 puncta in the mature adipocytes isolated from the reporter mice were sparsely distributed peripherally on lysosomes (Figure 2G). These observations indicate that in earlier stages of adipocyte development, represented by the cultured immature adipocytes, a large amount of CD63 is integrated into the formation of many early, small lysosomes. At a later developmental stage, represented by mature adipocytes in vivo, CD63 makes up a smaller proportion of the lysosomal membrane and may be correlated with the decreased metabolic activity of the mature white adipocytes when compared to their more active progenitor state. This is supported by a recent study demonstrating that de novo lipogenesis, which is most active during earlier stages of adipocyte development, is the primary contributor to lysosome membrane expansion (Rowland et al. 2023). In summary, our SMLM imaging reveals a link between CD63 distribution, lysosomal biogenesis, lysosomal morphology, and adipogenesis.

Immunoaffinity‐Based Enrichment of BAT‐EVs and MS‐Analysis of Their Proteomes

With the novel adipocyte EV reporter mice, we next investigated how CIT affects exosomal protein content. We focused on proteins since adipocyte exosomal miRNAs are relatively well‐known (Kalluri and LeBleu 2020; Liu et al. 2020; Skotland et al. 2017), but exosomal proteins are less investigated despite their known function in mediating intercellular communication on both a local and systemic level (Wang et al. 2020; Leow et al. 2022; Luo et al. 2023). To explore the EV proteome of thermogenic adipocytes during CIT, we created the Ucp1‐Cre/Rosa26hCD63‐copGFP (Ucp/hCD63) reporter mouse model to specifically label EVs derived from BAs in vivo. The Ucp/hCD63 mice were then subjected to cold treatment (CT) for 7 days before EV isolation from the interstitial fluid of BAT. This was performed as the abundance of BAT‐EVs is far higher compared to plasma, and is more likely to reveal low abundance proteins in EVs. Due to the highly vascularised nature of BAT, we rationalise that these interstitial EVs are representative of systemically circulating EVs.

To enrich BAT‐EVs, we took advantage of immunoaffinity‐based isolation using Dynabeads conjugated with anti‐hCD63 antibodies (Figure 3A). Fluorescence (Figure 3B) and scanning electron microscopy (Figure 3C) confirmed that the particles isolated displayed the expected GFP fluorescence and were of the expected size (50–200 nm). Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of Dynabeads‐enriched EVs from BAT of wild‐type and Ucp/hCD63 mice demonstrated a specific enrichment of proteins in samples from Ucp/hCD63 mice (Figure 3D). As expected, hCD63 was only detected in the Ucp1/hCD63 samples and no hCD63 peptides were detected in the MS analysis of WT samples (Figure 3E). Additionally, we found that the abundance of hCD63 EVs was significantly increased in the BAT of Ucp/hCD63 mice exposed to CT compared to that of mice kept at room temperature (RT), suggesting that cold exposure increases BA‐EV secretion (Figure 3E). A cursory analysis of EV proteins that were only identified in Ucp/hCD63 samples showed that the majority were metabolism and mitochondrial proteins (Figure 3D). Thus, we established an efficient method of isolating and analysing EVs from UCP1‐expressing thermogenic adipocytes in vivo.

BAT‐EVs Are Enriched With Lysosome and Mitochondrial Proteins

We then compared the proteome of EVs isolated from BAT of Ucp1/hCD63 mice kept at RT or CT for 1 week (Figure 4A). In total, 1073 proteins were detected across samples (Figure 4B, Table S1). Cellular component analysis of the proteins detected in RT and CT EVs reveals very different protein compositions (Figure 4C, D). The RT EV‐proteins detected contain 45% proteins as exosomal proteins (Figure 4C), supporting the validity of our approach. The remaining half of the RT EV‐proteins are structure proteins such as cytoskeleton, intermediate filament, cornified envelope, and desmosome (Figure 4C). Interestingly, CT altered the EV‐protein composition significantly. With lysosome (32%), mitochondrion (16.2%) and mitochondrial inner membrane (MIM, 3.6%) proteins together accounting for more than half of the proteins (Figure 4D). These proteins were not detected in the RT EV proteome. While the exosome proteins were reduced to 32% and structural (cytoskeleton) proteins were reduced to 19% (Figure 4D). These data demonstrate that cold alters BAT‐EV proteome to promote secretion of lysosomal and mitochondrial proteins.

The large proportion (32%) of CT‐induced EV proteins associated with lysosomes (Figure 4C) is interesting, as the secretion of lysosomal proteins in EVs during CIT has never been reported. Lysosome regulation is crucial for BAT thermogenesis, as it regulates both autophagy and lipid metabolism (Buratta et al. 2020). Several lysosomal proteins (ATP6V0D1, 1, CTSZMP1, and CTSZ) were found to be enriched in the BAT EVs of CT mice. Of these, Clathrin (CLTA)‐mediated endocytosis of lipoproteins and subsequent catabolism is crucial for the intake of triglycerides and cholesterol uptake in BAs; this process is vital for efficient thermogenesis in brown fat (Wade et al. 2021). LAMP1 is one of two major proteins that make up the majority of proteins in lysosome membranes and is often used as a marker for both lysosome biogenesis and degradative autophagy (Eskelinen 2006). Autophagy is vital for CIT by facilitating fatty acid oxidation, but also mitochondrial turnover, known as mitophagy (Yau et al. 2021). When either of these processes is perturbed, there are significant disruptions in the thermogenic efficiency of BAT. ATP6V0D1 is a subunit of vacuolar H+‐ATPase that is upregulated in white adipocytes of obese mice (Galber et al. 2021). This ATP6V0D1 is a key regulator of adipogenesis, and its loss in adipocytes leads to severe lipodystrophy and severe metabolic issues (Zapata et al. 2022). CTSZ is a member of the lysosomal cysteine proteases and is a direct target of PPARα/γ; its function is necessary for thermogenesis in both human and murine BAT, as its loss leads to decreased OCR and more interestingly, the decrease of nuclear‐encoded mitochondrial genes (Shen et al. 2020). The enrichment of lysosomal proteins in cold‐induced EV proteome, therefore, suggests a key role of this organelle in thermogenesis.

Another class of proteins in CT‐induced EVs was mitochondrial proteins, accounting for ∼20% of the proteins detected (Figure 4C). Ejection of damaged mitochondria from thermogenic adipocytes has been shown to be a vital process for efficient thermogenesis (Rosina et al. 2022; Leow et al. 2022; Aquilano et al. 2023). Ejection of damaged mitochondria has also been observed in cardiomyocytes, BAs, and mesenchymal stem cells in response to insufficient lysosomal mitophagy (Rosina et al. 2022; Aquilano et al. 2023; Phinney et al. 2015; Bartelt and Weber 2020). EV secretion of mitochondrial proteins not only serves as a mitochondrial quality control mechanism but may also signal to recipient cells. For example, mitochondria ejected from stressed adipocytes can subsequently be taken up by cardiac cells and precondition the cardiomyocytes to lipotoxic and ischemic stress (Crewe et al. 2021).

Cold Treatment Promotes Secretion of Proteins through BAT‐EVs

We further analysed the BAT‐EV proteomes of RT and CT groups. Using p < 0.05 and fold change (FC) > 2 as cutoff (Figure 4B), we found that 96 proteins were differentially expressed between the two groups (Figure 4E, Table S2). These differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) include 65 upregulated and 31 downregulated by CT (Figure 4B), suggesting that CT mainly induces secretion of proteins while suppressing the secretion of a small set of proteins.

We then performed pathway analysis of the EV proteins upregulated or downregulated by the CT. Biological pathway analysis of the upregulated EV proteins showed that they were involved in metabolic processes such as the small molecule, carboxylic acid, and lipid/fatty acid metabolic processes (Figure 5A). Pentose‐phosphate shunt and NADH regeneration were also among the top upregulated pathways (Figure 5A). These results are consistent with cold‐induced glycolysis and thermogenesis. The downregulated proteins are primarily enriched in epidermis development and cardiac muscle function and contraction (Figure 5B). These results suggest that CT suppresses crosstalk between BAs and skin and cardiac muscles.

The predominant enrichment of metabolism‐related proteins in the cold‐induced EV proteome (Figure 5A) is consistent with the cold‐induced adaptation of mitochondrial functions. One of the most upregulated proteins was SLC27A2, a membrane‐bound long‐chain fatty acid (LCFA) transporter (Wang et al. 2024). LCFAs are positive regulators of thermogenesis, in that they are not only utilised directly for β‐oxidation to fuel thermogenesis in adipocytes but also act as ligands for PPAR activation (Shin 2022). EVs from adipocytes have been frequently observed to have metabolism‐altering effects on recipient cells; it is likely that the cargo secreted in CIT BAT EVs promotes lipolysis and fatty acid mobilisation, since circulating lipids are important for maintaining thermogenesis (Zhou et al. 2020; Quan and Kuang 2020; Mei et al. 2022).

We further looked into the specific mitochondrial proteins in the EVs that may represent adaptation to CIT. We compared the DEPs in our EV analysis against those found in cold‐induced transcriptomic changes of whole BAT (GSE70437) and proteomic changes of BAT mitochondria (PXD044866) (Qiu et al. 2023; Marcher et al. 2015) (Figure 5C). This led to the identification of 17 proteins that were found across all data sets (Figure 5C). The majority of these proteins, listed in Table 1, are involved in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism (Figure 5D). Among these, mitochondrial pyruvate carrier 1 (MPC1) deletion in BAT impaired thermogenesis by preventing complete glucose oxidation in BAs (Panic et al. 2020). Phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1) displays differing functions dependent upon adipose tissue type (Yoshida et al. 2022). Deletion of PGAM1 from all adipose tissue leads to the whitening of BAT and the beiging of WAT. Interestingly, exosomal PGAM1 from tumours drives local angiogenesis, suggesting that it may play a similar role during BAT thermogenesis since the creation of local blood vessels is heavily correlated with cold adaptation (Luo et al. 2023). Progranulin (GRN) is a key regulator of lysosomal function and immune regulation. Regulation of immune cells is vital for proper adipocyte thermogenesis, as crosstalk between thermogenic cells and resident immune cells is vital for regulating processes such as inflammation, neurotransmitter levels, tissue innervation, and tissue expansion (Bertola et al. 2022; Kao et al. 2017).

Creatine Kinase B (CKB) Is Secreted by Thermogenic Adipocytes During CIT

Among the cold‐induced BAT‐EV mitochondrial proteins (Table 1), the mitochondrial creatine kinase CKB caught our attention as it is highly expressed in BAT, where it mediates UCP1‐independent thermogenesis through futile creatine cycling (Rahbani et al. 2021; Streijger et al. 2009). The expression of CKB has been shown to increase during CIT in transcriptomic analyses of whole BAT and proteomic analyses of isolated mitochondria. CKB was also identified in the human BAT secretory proteome (Deshmukh et al. 2019), but its presence in EVs has not been reported. We used immunofluorescence staining to confirm that CKB puncta co‐localised with copGFP signals, indicating the presence of CKB within EVs (Figure 6A). Quantification indicates that CT significantly elevated the co‐localisation of copGFP and CKB (Figure 6B). Our results demonstrate that BA‐EVs are a major source of CKB secretion in response to CIT.

Interestingly, it was reported that adipocyte‐secreted CKB did not have any extracellular activity in vitro (Rahbani et al. 2021). We then examined if the EV‐associated CKB is biologically active, via a creatine kinase activity assay. We induced differentiation of immortalised murine brown preadipocytes, then treated differentiated cells with or without an adrenergic agonist CL316,243 (abbreviated as CL316) to stimulate thermogenesis. We then isolated the cell lysates from the BAs, EVs from the conditioned media (CM), and EV‐depleted CM from both treatment conditions. Both cell lysates and EVs displayed creatine kinase activities, while the EV‐depleted CM did not (Figure 6C), indicating that bioactive secreted CKB is present only within EVs or creatine kinase activity relies on the EVs. Interestingly, CL316 stimulation only elevated intracellular creatine kinase activity in mBAT cells, without affecting creatine kinase activity of the secreted EVs (Figure 6C). As thermogenic stimulation leads to phosphorylation of CKB (p‐CKB) at Thr133 that reduces creatine affinity (Wang et al. 2024), the reduced CKB activity in the EVs compared to cell lysates suggests that the p‐CKB is selectively packed in EVs.

BAT‐EVs Increase the Expression of Thermogenic Genes in White Adipocytes in vitro

Multiple studies have demonstrated the potent effects of EVs from BAs on white adipocytes, but whether cold‐stimulation enhances or reduces such effects is unclear. To address this question, we isolated EVs from in vitro differentiated BAT cells treated with vehicle control or CL316, and incubated differentiated 3T3‐L1 adipocytes with the EVs (Figure 6D). We observed that EVs from both CL316‐treated and untreated BAs increased expression of various thermogenesis genes (Ppargc1a, Pparg, Prdm16, Ucp1, and Hsl) in white adipocytes. Interestingly, CL316 stimulation not only failed to enhance the activity of BA‐EVs but also reduced BA‐EV's activity in upregulating the levels of Prdm16 (Figure 6E). This observation is consistent with previous studies showing a similar suppression of thermogenesis by EVs from thermogenic adipocytes (Rosina et al. 2022). We also examined whether EV treatment increased the creatine kinase activity in WAT cells and observed that both control and CL316‐treated BAT EVs suppressed creatine kinase activity (Figure 6F). This may be explained by the fact that thermogenic adipocytes preferentially eject stressed mitochondria in EVs, which can diminish the mitochondrial function of recipient cells.

Discussion

Isolating EVs from a specific cell type in vivo is especially challenging due to the fact that most tissues contain heterogeneous cell populations, almost all of which secrete EVs that would be co‐isolated using traditional methods (Kalluri and LeBleu 2020; Gurung et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2024). Due to the high efficiency and specificity of the Adipoq‐Cre and Ucp1‐Cre expression in adipocyte and thermogenic adipocyte populations, our mouse model avoids this problem (Liu et al. 2017). There are reports of several non‐adipocyte cells that express Adipoq (cardiomyocytes, skeletal muscle, and osteoblast progenitors) (Fordjour et al. 2023; Adem et al. 2021; Luo et al. 2020). In our Ad/hCD63 mice, we did not observe any hCD63‐GFP in multiple skeletal muscles outside of structures resembling blood vessels, indicating that the level of Adipoq promoter activity was not sufficient to drive expression of hCD63‐GFP. Ucp1‐Cre mouse reporter models have demonstrated expression in the central nervous system, kidney, adrenal glands, and hypothalamus (Knights et al. 2020; Rahbani et al. 2024). Because of our methodology of isolating EVs directly from BAT, the contributions of these cell types are unlikely to interfere with our results.

The most common approach for producing EV samples is to use either ex vivo or in vitro methods to generate CM that are then used for EV isolation. This then introduces the issue that the content of secreted EVs is likely to change due to the cells being kept in typical cell culture conditions to generate EVs for analysis (Rosina et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2021). We demonstrate that our reporter model and EV isolation technique allow for efficient and specific isolation of adipocyte and thermogenic adipocyte‐derived EVs immediately and directly from tissue, allowing for a more representative analysis of in vivo conditions.

Currently, there are several transgenic mouse models utilising Cre recombinase‐based systems to label exosomes in vivo. Fordjour et al. generated a mouse model that expresses CD81‐mNeonGreen in the presence of Cre recombinase and utilised the model to investigate the contribution of hepatic and neuronal cells to plasma and cerebrospinal fluid exosome contents (Maqdasy et al. 2022). Other transgenic mouse models have used luciferase or fluorescently labelled CD63 to track exosomes in vivo, although CD63 is a useful exosome marker, the use of other exosome markers such as CD9, TSG101, and ALIX for transgenic exosome labelling could yield useful models for studying EV subpopulations (Gurung et al. 2021; Loo et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2013). Another potential application of creating these new transgenic models is for studying exosomes in cell types that have low expression of CD63 on their EVs or whose intracellular function may be perturbed by CD63 overexpression.

Our proteomic analysis of EVs secreted during CIT corroborates previous reports demonstrating the ejection of mitochondria by thermogenic adipocytes but also reveals that lysosomal proteins and previously unreported mitochondrial proteins are also secreted. The enrichment of mitochondrial and lysosomal proteins in our cold‐induced EV proteome could be caused by CIT‐stressed thermogenic adipocytes attempting to outsource mitophagy to other cells in a similar manner to mesenchymal stem cells in a CD63‐dependent manner (Phinney et al. 2015). The enrichment of ubiquitin proteins (UBA52, UBB, UBC, and Rps27a) in the cold‐induced EV proteome suggests that sorting mitochondria into secretory vesicles is accompanied by ubiquitination status. These proteins that were identified in our dataset suggest that EVs released by BAT during CIT may provide a unique snapshot into the metabolic state of the cell, as we identified unique proteins not found in other ‐omics analyses of BAT components. As shown in other studies, the EVs of thermogenic adipocytes in vivo display the ability to affect distal tissues. It is likely that these mitochondrial and lysosomal proteins would have an impact on recipient cells, KEGG analysis of our cold‐induced EV protein showed enrichment in PPARγ signalling and metabolic pathways. The uptake of these bioactive proteins would contribute to increased catabolism and mitochondrial respiration (Muthu et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2024). They also serve as a method for thermogenic adipocytes to rid themselves of contents, and this process is important for their proper function. Additionally, the maintenance of a proper microenvironment within BAT during thermogenesis is also crucial (Bertola et al. 2022; Rezaie et al. 2022). We found that several metabolic enzymes and solute carriers were significantly enriched in CIT BAT EVs; considering the evidence that BAT‐derived EVs tend to have an effect of increasing energy expenditure in recipient cells, our EV proteome data suggest that the metabolites produced by this increased metabolic activity may be used to fuel BAs during thermogenesis.

We not only identified CKB as an EV protein from BAs, but also demonstrated the extracellular activity of EV‐associated CKB. CKB has been identified as a crucial component of CIT in adipocytes (Rahbani et al. 2021). Deletion of adipocyte UCP1 does not completely ablate the ability of mice to maintain body temperature; however, co‐deletion of CKB and UCP1 in adipocytes does lead to complete failure to maintain body temperature during cold exposure (Song et al. 2021). Other studies have found that CKB and TNAP are responsible for 40% of heat production during CIT (Sun et al. 2021). The reason why CKB would be secreted in BAT EVs is unclear. BAT EVs have been shown to repress the levels of inflammatory cytokines in both liver and visceral adipose tissue (Zhou et al. 2020). Meanwhile, in WAT, the repression of CKB expression directly leads to increased inflammation; this effect is observed in both human and murine tissues (Maqdasy et al. 2022). Because increased inflammation leads to decreased thermogenic capacity of adipose tissues, CKB delivered by EVs may be responsible for the anti‐inflammatory activity of BAT EVs as a mechanism to maintain a thermogenic microenvironment.

Another role that CKB may have during thermogenesis is controlling the flux of extracellular phosphocreatine. Metastatic colon cancer cells are able to release CKB in the hypoxic hepatic microenvironment to stimulate the production of extracellular phosphocreatine to use as a fuel source (Loo et al. 2015). It would be prudent to investigate if BAs perform a similar function in vivo. Considering that maintenance of creatine and phosphocreatine levels is important for maintaining adipocyte thermogenesis, BAT cells during CIT could be utilising secreted CKB to increase the availability of phosphocreatine for futile creatine cycling.

Because of the strong body of evidence suggesting that the BAT secretome has beneficial effects on overall metabolic health, there is great interest in identifying particularly effective components of the secretome for combatting obesity and metabolic disorders (Cheong and Xu 2021; Ghesmati et al. 2023; Du et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2013). There is evidence that ablation or reduction of secreted factors from healthy BAT leads to metabolic dysfunction, while activation of existing BAT leads to better overall metabolic health (Seale and Lazar 2009). Pharmaceutical researchers have already developed novel therapeutics based on EVs due to their low immunogenicity and high bioavailability (Muthu et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2024; Rezaie et al. 2022). Even if EVs derived from cells prove to be too difficult to develop into anti‐obesity drugs, EV‐like methods of delivery, such as liposomes or nanoparticles, can be used instead to deliver cargo to targeted cells. Additionally, further characterisation of the BAT EV proteome may yield new insights into the function of proteins as secreted factors and their potential as therapeutics.

Limitations of the Study

Immunoprecipitation of CD63+ EVs is likely to have caused a biased selection of EV subtypes. Studies have demonstrated that multiple cell types have uneven distribution of tetraspanins across EV subtypes (Mathieu et al.2021; Mizenko et al.2021). It is likely that CD63+ EVs do not represent the entirety of EVssecreted. Additionally, the strategy of examining EVs isolated directly from BAT may not entirely reflect the content of systemically circulating BAT EVs. The usage of the Ucp1‐cre to drive hCD63‐GFP reporter in UCP1‐expressing brown and beige adipocytes may also introduce confounding variables. It was reported that Ucp1‐cre hemizygotes and homozygotes express extra levels of UCP1 and the insertion of the transgene in Chromosome 1 affected expression of several nearby genes to induce ectopic effects in adipose and non‐adipose tissues (Halurkar et al. 2025). Our in vitro results of treating 3T3‐L1 white adipocytes with EVs from BAT progenitor cell lines may not be fully representative of in vivo biological phenomena. Lastly, we provided evidence that bioactive CKB is exported in BAT EVs, but a more thorough examination of EV CKB's function in recipient cells in vivo and in vitro was not explored in this study.

Methods

Ethical Statement

This research complies with all relevant ethical regulations. All procedures involving animals were done in compliance with National Institutes of Health and Institutional guidelines with approval by the Purdue Animal Care and Use Committee.

Animals

The mouse lines used in this study were of a C57BL/6J background. AdipoqCre (JAX stock #010803) and Ucp1‐Cre(JAX stock #024670) mouse stocks were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Mouse genotyping was performed by genomic DNA isolation from ear and was screened by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using primers and the program following the protocols provided by the supplier. Mice were housed and maintained in the animal facility with free access to water and standard rodent chow food (2018 Teklad Global 18% Protein Rodent Diets) and were housed under 12‐h light/dark cycle, at 22°C, and 45% humidity on average. For all animal‐based experiments, at least three pairs of gender‐matched littermates at the age of 2–5 months were used, both males and females were used. Mice were anesthetised with 3% isoflurane followed by euthanasia by cervical dislocation.

Cold and Thermoneutral Exposure Experiments

Cold exposure experiments were performed on 2‐ to 4‐ month‐old adult mice in a temperature‐controlled chamber (Memmert, HPP260). For chronic cold exposure, mice were individually caged with minimal bedding and food and water ad libitum at 6°C for 1 week.

in vitro Cell Cultures

Isolated primary WAT SVF cells and BA cell lines (a gift from Dr. Shingo Kajimura) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium F12 (DMEM F12, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, HyClone) at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 days, followed by replacing with fresh medium every 2 days. When 80% confluency was reached, cells were switched to adipogenic induction media. DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 2.85 µM insulin, 0.3 µM dexamethasone, 1 µM rosiglitazone, and 0.63 mM 3‐isobutyl‐methylxanthine) for 4 days, and then differentiated in differentiation medium (DMEM, 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 200 nM insulin, and 10 nM T3) for 3–4 days until mature adipocytes were obtained. To induce thermogenic stimulation in vitro differentiated BAT cells were treated with 10 µM CL316,243 for 12 h.

Collection of EVs From Brown Adipocyte Cells in vitro

CL316,243 stimulated and control BAT cells were switched to respective serum free differentiation media and cultured for 24 h. CM was then collected, and differential centrifugation was performed: 300 × g for 5 min, 2000 × g for 10 min, 10,000 × g for 20 min, and 100,000 × g for 70 min. EVs were resuspended in PBS, and protein content was then assessed using a BCA assay.

Collection of EVs From Brown Adipose Tissue

Twenty‐five milligrams of RT and CT BAT tissue were washed with PBS and minced into 1 mm3 pieces. The pieces were then digested in Collagenase I (1.25 mg/mL) and Dispase II (2.4 mg/mL) for 1 h at 37°C with shaking every 10 min. The digestion was then neutralised by adding an equal volume of DMEM F12 and filtered through a 70 µM cell strainer. This was then filtered through a 0.22 µM syringe filter and subjected to differential centrifugation: 300 × g for 5 min, 2000 × g for 10 min, 10,000 × g for 20 min. The 10,000 × g supernatant was then diluted by 10‐fold with PBS supplemented with 1% BSA and incubated with Dynabeads M‐280 Streptavidin beads coated with biotinylated anti‐hCD63 (Ancell, Cat No. 215‐030) antibodies overnight at 4°C. Beads with captured EVs were then fixed with 4% PFA and used for immunofluorescence staining, scanning electron microscopy, and MS analysis

Collection of EVs From White Adipose Tissue

IWAT tissues were collected and washed with PBS, minced into 1 mm3 pieces. Minced pieces were digested in Collagenase I (1.25 mg/mL) and Dispase II (2.4 mg/mL) for 1 h at 37°C. The digestion was neutralised by adding complete DMEM and filtered through a 70 µm filter. The filtrate was subjected to sequential centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, 2000 × g for 10 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was filtered through 0.22 µm filter and filtrated was ultracentrifuges at 100,000 × g for 70 min. The supernatant was aspirated, and pellet was washed with PBS and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min. Supernatant was aspirated and palleted EVs were used for western blotting.

Western Blot Analysis

Total EV proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP‐40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate). Protein concentration was determined by the BCA protein assay Reagent (Thermo Scientific, cat# 23225). Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to the PVDF membrane. After blocking with 2% BSA, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies; anti‐CD63 (Biotin) (Ancell‐215‐030) and TSG101 (Cell Signaling‐72312) overnight at 4°C. Membranes were washed in TBST (0.1% Tween‐20) followed by secondary antibody incubation for 1 h/overnight. Finally, membranes were washed, and immunodetection was performed using enhanced Western Blotting Chemiluminescence Luminol Reagent (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat#sc‐2048).

Total RNA Extraction and Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol reagent (Sigma–Aldrich, cat#T9424) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA was treated with RNase‐free DNase I to remove contaminating genomic DNA. The purity and concentration of the total RNA were determined by a spectrophotometer Nanodrop 2000c (Thermo Fisher). One microgram of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed using random primers with M‐MLV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, cat#28025021). Real‐time PCR was carried out in a Roche Light Cycler 480 PCR System with SYBR Green Master Mix. The 2‐ΔΔCt method was used to analyse the relative changes in each gene's expression normalised against 18S expression. The following amplification primers (Sigma) were used: 18s (Forward, AGTCCCTGCCCTTTGTACACA; Reverse, CGATCCGAGGGCCTCACTA), Pparg (Forward, GGAAGACCACTCGCATTCCTT; Reverse, GTAATCAGCAACCATTGGGTCA) Ppargc1a (Forward, TATGGAGTGACATAGAGTGTGCT; Reverse, CCACTTCAATCCACCCAGAAAG), Ucp1 (Forward, AGGCTTCCAGTACCATTAGGT; Reverse, CTGAGTGAGGCAAAGCTGATTT), Hsl (Forward, TTCTCCAAAGCACCTAGCCAA; Reverse, TGTGGAAAACTAAGGGCTTGTTG), Prdm16 (Forward, CCACCAGCGAGGACTTCAC; Reverse, GGAGGACTCTCGTAGCTCGAA).

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence was performed in cultured WAT SVF cells, isolated mature WAT cells, and EVs captured on Dynabeads. Briefly, samples were fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min, quenched with 100 nM glycine for 10 min, and permeabilised and blocked in blocking buffer (PBS containing 5% goat serum, 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.2% Triton X‐100, and 0.1% sodium azide) for 1 h at RT. Samples were subsequently incubated with the following primary antibodies: hCD63 (1:1000, Ancell, Cat No. 215‐030), CKB (1:300, Proteintech, Cat No. 18713‐1‐AP), diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, samples were incubated with secondary antibodies and DAPI (Invitrogen, cat#D1306) for 1 h at RT. Mature WAT were stained with HCS LipidTOX (Invitrogen, cat#H34477) and DAPI.

Single Molecule Localisation Microscopy (SMLM)

The custom‐built direct stochastic SMLM (3D SMLM) system is equipped with a 100×/1.35‐NA silicone oil immersion objective lens (UPLSAPO100XS, Olympus America), a laser line at wavelengths of 642 nm (2RU‐VFL‐P‐2000‐642‐B1R, MPB Communications), a deformable mirror to compensate for system and sample induced wavefront aberrations, an sCMOS camera (Orca‐Flash4.0v3, Hamamatsu) with an effective pixel size of 120 nm. The excitation laser is in a highly inclined and laminated optical sheet (HILO) imaging modality. Additionally, a transmitted Köhler illuminator inside the microscope stand equipped with a motorised shutter illuminated the sample between acquisition cycles for focus stabilisation. The system is designed to work as a biplane‐based setup to enable 3D image reconstruction. The whole system is controlled by a custom‐written program in LabVIEW (National Instruments).

SMLM Data Acquisition and Postprocessing

The sample, differentiated SVF and isolated mature white adipocytes in imaging buffer (10% wt/vol glucose in 50 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, 2.5 µL BME, 10 µL glucose oxidase, pH 8.0), was first excited with the 642‐nm laser at a low intensity of ∼50 W/cm2 to find a region of interest. Before fluorescence imaging, bright‐field images of this region were recorded over an axial range from −1 to +1 µm with a step size of 100 nm as reference images for focus stabilisation. Then the blinking data were collected at a laser intensity of 4–6 kW/cm2 and a frame rate of 50 Hz. To obtain a low density of molecules, 2000 frames per cycle and 50 cycles were collected. To pinpoint single‐molecule positions with high precision and minimum bias, we use In‐Situ INSPR to generate an in situ PSF for each detection FOV. INSPR allows us to directly reconstruct 3D PSF from the blinking dataset and reconstruct super‐resolution images.

Isolation of EV Peptides From Dynabeads

The Dynabeads beads with captured EVs were resuspended in 50 µL of lysis solution containing 12 mM sodium deoxycholate, 12 mM sodium lauroyl sarcosinate, 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer (TEAB), 10 mM tris(2‐carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), and 40 mM chloroacetamide (CAA) for phase transfer surfactant (PTS)‐aided enzymatic digestion. The samples were incubated at 95°C for 10 min with shaking to perform protein denaturation, reduction, and alkylation. The samples were then diluted five‐fold with 50 mM TEAB and digested with MS‐grade trypsin (Thermo Scientific) at 1:50 (w/w) enzyme‐to‐protein ratio at 37°C for overnight with shaking. On the next day, the beads were removed by a magnetic separation rack, and the samples were acidified with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to a final concentration of 1% (v/v). Ethyl acetate solution was then added at a 1:1 ratio to the samples, and the mixture was vortexed for 2 min and then centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 2 min to obtain aqueous and organic phases. The organic phase (top layer) was aspired, and the aqueous phase was collected. This surfactant removal step was repeated one more time. The aqueous phase was dried in a SpeedVac and desalted using in‐house prepared C18 StageTips (Empore SDB‐XC). Peptide concentration was determined using the Pierce Quantitative Colorimetric Peptide Assay (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. 0.5 µg of the peptide from each sample was aliquoted and dried in a SpeedVac for LC‐MS/MS analysis.

LC‐MS/MS

Dried peptides were dissolved in 0.1% FA and loaded onto Evotips Pure (EvoSep) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The Evosep One LC system (EvoSep) coupled with a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker) was used to analyse peptide samples. The 60 SPD method was used with the PepSep C18 column (8 cm x 150 µm, 1.5 µm; Bruker) connected to a Captive Spray source (Bruker) equipped with a ZDV Sprayer 20 µm emitter (Bruker). The mobile phases included 0.1% FA in LC–MS grade water as Solvent A and 0.1% FA in acetonitrile as Solvent B. The timsTOF HT was operated in dia‐PASEF mode with variable window widths covering an m/z range from 300 to 1200. The ion mobility (IM) range was set to 1.5 and 0.6 V/cm2. The accumulation and ramp times were set to 100 ms, and the cycle time was 1.38 s for 12 dia‐PASEF scans. The collision energy was set from 59 eV at 1.6 V/cm2 to 20 eV at 0.6 V/cm2. The TIMS dimension was calibrated using three Agilent ESI tuning mix ions (622.0289, 0.9848 V/cm2; 922.0097, 1.1895 V/cm2; 1221.9906, 1.3820 V/cm2) in positive mode. All samples were run in the same batch, for the LC‐MS/MS, we evaluated the carryover effect and did not observe any.

Raw Data Analysis

The raw files were searched against the mouse database using a directDIA (library‐free) method in Spectronaut v18 software (Biognosys). In Pulsar search engine, the workflow setting is: trypsin/P as specific digest type, 5 minimal peptide length, 52 maximum peptide length, carbamidomethyl at cysteine as fixed modification, acetyl protein N‐term and oxidation at methionine as variable modifications, and 5 as maximum variable modifications. The FDR at PSM, peptide, and protein group were set to 0.01. Retention time prediction with local regression calibration was used. The identification was determined with mutated decoy generation and dynamic size at 0.1 fractions of library size. The quantitation was performed at the MS2 level, and cross‐run normalisation was enabled. Q value and no imputing were used as data filtering.

All MS data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the jPOST partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD062866.

Bioinformatics Analysis

The data were analysed in the Perseus software (version 2.0.7.0) using the quantified intensities extracted from Spectronaut search results. The intensities were log2‐transformed, and the quantifiable proteins were set as 70% identified in at least one condition. The imputation was performed by replacing the missing values of intensities to a normal distribution with a downshift of 1.8 SDs and a width of 0.3 SDs. In the volcano plots, the significantly changed proteins were identified by the p value and FC. The vertical dotted line represents the FC cutoff of 2 or 0.5 (log2(difference) = ±1). The horizontal dotted line represents the two‐sample t test p value cutoff of 0.05 (−log10(p value = 2). Visualisations of volcano plots and heatmap were created using R (version 4.2.2).

Gene Ontology and Biological Process Analysis

Funrich was used for cell compartment and biological process analysis and data visualisation. The FDR values were reported with the processes with an FDR cutoff of 0.05.

Cell Culture Treatments for 3T3‐L1 Adipocytes

When 80% confluency was reached, 3T3‐L1 cells were switched to adipogenic induction media. DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 2.85 µM insulin, 0.3 µM dexamethasone, 1 µM rosiglitazone, and 0.63 mM 3‐isobutyl‐methylxanthine for 4 days, and then DMEM (10% FBS, 1% P/S, 200 nM insulin, and 10 nM T3) for 3–4 days until mature adipocytes were obtained. At this point, adipocytes were treated with serum‐free differentiation medium containing 25 µg/mL of mBAT EVs, CL316‐stimulated mBAT EVs, or equivalent volume of PBS for 48 h.

Creatine Kinase Activity Measurement of Cells and EVs

Creatine kinase activity was assessed using a Creatine kinase activity assay kit (Sigma–Aldrich, MAK116). Treated 3T3‐L1 adipocyte cell lysates and differentiated BAT cell EVs (ruptured with sonicator) in 50 mM KPO4 pH 7.5 buffer were processed according to instructions from the manufacturer. Creatine kinase activity (units/L) was calculated for samples using the equation ([(A340)40 min − (A340)20 min]×150)/[(A340)calibrator − (A340)blank].

Scanning Electron Microscopy

Dynabeads with captured EVs fixed in 4% PFA were fixed for 1 h in 1% osmium tetroxide and then washed three times in PBS. Samples were then incubated in ascending ethanol solutions: 50% 10 min, 70% 10 min, 80% 10 min, 95% 10 min, and 100% 15 min. Samples were then air‐dried and mounted on a coverslip and attached to an SEM stage with carbon paste. A coating of 2–5 nm gold–palladium alloy was applied by sputtering and imaged using a Teneo Volumescope FEG. SEM was performed using 5.0 kV.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least thrice, and the number of replicates is provided in the figure legends. Statistical analyses and graphs were generated using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism, version 9. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using a two‐tailed unpaired t test with the following significance indicators: *p < 0.05.

Gene Ontology and Biological Process Analysis

ShinyGO 0.76 was used for biological process analysis and data visualisation. The FDR values were reported with the processes with an FDR cutoff of 0.05.
