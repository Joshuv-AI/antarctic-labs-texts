Long cervical fusions: are multiple segmental plates less likely to fail than a single long plate?
Authors: Gregory Mundis, Hani Malone, Gautham Prabhakar, Fernando Rios, Austin Williams, Darryl D’Lima, Stephen Stephan, Robert Eastlack
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is widely performed for cervical pathology, yet multilevel anterior constructs are associated with higher rates of dysphagia, pseudoarthrosis, and mechanical failure—particularly at the caudal segment. These complications may arise from stress concentration and load transfer through long anterior cervical plates. Segmentally separated fixation, utilizing multiple short plates rather than a single long plate, may reduce these risks by limiting exposure, shortening operative time, and distributing mechanical loads more evenly. The purpose of this study was to compare the biomechanical behavior of multilevel ACDF constructs stabilized with multiple segmental plates versus a single long plate.MethodsTwelve fresh-frozen human cadaveric cervical spines (C4–C7) underwent ACDF using either a single long plate or multiple segmental plates. Bone quality was quantified via C7 trabecular Hounsfield units on computed tomography (CT), and specimens were randomized by density. Identical constructs were also tested in polyurethane foam cervical models to minimize biological variability. Specimens were mounted to multiaxial test platforms and subjected to fatigue cycling under flexion-extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation. Construct stiffness was recorded at baseline and after 1,500 cycles in each motion plane. Stiffness loss was analyzed with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and normalized to bone density.ResultsInitial construct stiffness did not differ between groups (P=0.86). After cyclic loading, multiple segmental plates demonstrated significantly greater stiffness retention in axial rotation (51%±10% vs. 88%±4% loss; P<0.001) in synthetic models. No significant differences were found for flexion-extension or lateral bending. In cadaveric specimens, normalized stiffness loss was comparable between groups, likely reflecting greater biological variability.ConclusionsSegmentally separated anterior fixation for multilevel ACDF preserves rotational stiffness at the caudal segment more effectively than long single-plate constructs in controlled models. This suggests a potential mechanical advantage that may translate to reduced stress concentration and lower failure risk in multilevel anterior cervical reconstructions. Further in-vivo and clinical correlation studies are warranted to validate these biomechanical findings and assess long-term fusion outcomes.

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is one of the most frequently performed surgeries for the treatment of cervical spine disorders, but it is not without complications. Utilizing this technique over multiple consecutive levels has been associated with greater exposure-related risks, such as dysphagia (1-3). In addition, longer constructs have also been associated with greater mechanical failure rates (4,5). The rates of pseudoarthrosis, subsidence, fracture, and fixation failure are highest at the caudal levels of multilevel anterior cervical reconstructions due to mechanical stress concentration in this region, which may be a result of loadbearing transfer from cephalad segments to caudal segments through single plate fixation.

Additionally, the application of long anterior cervical plates requires a more extensive exposure, and can be a technically challenging aspect of the procedure that adds considerably to the operative time. Larger exposure and increased operative times have been independently associated with greater rates of dysphagia (1). While the majority of patients recover from postoperative dysphagia, chronic dysphagia can develop in as high as 21% of patients (2). In efforts to mitigate this significant complication, newer anterior cervical reconstruction devices have been designed in a manner that allows for separate segmental fixation, either with smaller anterior plates that can be “stacked” with separated constructs at each level, or in the form of integrated interbody devices (3).

The use of segmentally separated anterior cervical fixation for ACDF has the potential advantage of requiring less exposure often required when applying long plates. This allows more efficiency in applying the fixation given the relative ease of placing single level plates versus long plates, thereby potentially mitigating the increased levels of dysphagia in multilevel reconstruction (6,7). Importantly, segmentally separated fixation may also provide for a beneficial change in the biomechanical environment at the caudal aspect of longer constructs, at which failures are more likely (8,9). Because the caudal segment would have no load sharing impact from cephalad levels when utilizing segmentally separated fixation, these lower segments should theoretically behave more like single level ACDFs, and potentially suffer less risk of mechanical failure, excessive loading, and stress concentration (9).

Alternative strategies for anterior cervical fixation continue to evolve. Zero-profile stand-alone cages have gained popularity due to their reduced risk of dysphagia but may carry a higher risk of subsidence and pseudoarthrosis in multilevel constructs. Hybrid constructs, such as combining stand-alone cages at certain levels with segmental plates at others, aim to balance biomechanical stability with soft tissue preservation. Additionally, novel three-dimensional (3D)-printed porous titanium implants offer promising load distribution and osteointegration characteristics, though long-term outcomes are still under investigation. This study focuses on comparing segmental versus long plate constructs, but future work should evaluate these alternatives within a comprehensive biomechanical and clinical framework (10-13).

To our knowledge, no prior studies have compared the biomechanical properties of multiple segmental plates versus a single long plate fixation for multilevel ACDF. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the biomechanical differences at the caudal aspect of multilevel ACDFs when utilizing multiple segmental plates versus a single long anterior cervical plate. We hypothesized that multiple segmental plates would be able to retain significantly more stiffness than the long single plate construct. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-3/rc).

Methods

Fresh-frozen human cadaveric cervical spines (n=12) were stabilized from C4 to C7 with either a single traditional long cervical plate or multiple segmental plates. The medical history of each donor was reviewed to exclude trauma, malignancy, or metabolic disease that might otherwise compromise the mechanical properties of the cervical spine. Plain film radiographs were taken and specimens with any obvious radiographic or visible flaws (particularly osteophytes, disc narrowing, or joint arthrosis) were excluded and replaced. Bone density was assessed by measuring the Hounsfield units (HUs) on computed tomography (CT) scans using a simple region-of-interest (ROI) attenuation measurement in the trabecular bone of the C7 vertebra on three axial cuts which were then averaged (Table 1). The HU values were measured in the midbody cut, caudal to the cephalad endplate and cephalad to the caudal endplate (Figure 1). The ROI was expanded to be as large as possible within the vertebral body, but it did not include any cortical bone, such as the lateral walls or endplates, or over areas of attenuation heterogeneity, in order to avoid distortion of attenuation measurements. Specimens were randomly divided into two groups, and the equivalence of groups was verified by comparing mean HU’s. Given the challenges and inconsistencies associated with bone and other connective tissues within cadaveric specimens and the potential for confounding impact on biomechanical testing, we also compared these anterior cervical constructs in the setting of sawbone models of the cervical spine. The screw size utilized were all 3.5 mm × 16 mm variable angle screws. Following discectomy, a titanium interbody cage was inserted into the disc space at each surgical level. Cage height was determined using trial spacers to achieve an optimal press-fit, providing appropriate distraction and contact with the endplates without over-distraction. This approach was used consistently across specimens to simulate clinical technique and ensure uniformity in construct stiffness assessment. The cage height was matched to the intervertebral space height and both 20 mm × 15 mm × 7 mm and 20 mm × 15 mm × 6 mm cages were used. In a pilot study, eight cervical spine polyurethane foam models were also tested. All specimens were mounted on a multiaxial robotic arm using the following fatigue protocols.

Sawbones specimens

One of the two cervical spine anterior constructs, long single plate or segmental multiple plates, were installed in polyurethane foam cervical spine models (sawbones). The models were modified only leaving C4 through C7 and were then potted in 3M Scotch-Weld two-part epoxy (Figure 2). The C4 end of the specimen was attached to the tool head of a Universal Robotics UR6 robot (Figure 3) and a MicroScribe digitizer was utilized to determine the coordinated of the center of the C7 vertebrae. This point is used as the center of rotation for the testing. Total force and moments across the construct were measured over the duration of testing using the FT300 force/torque sensor from Universal Robotics. Specimens were visually inspected and photographed before and after testing. The testing protocol was conducted as follows:

Cadaveric specimens

Cervical spine cadaver specimens were screened so that chosen specimens would have no previous spinal injuries or conditions that would affect bone quality or density. Once acquired, one of the two anterior constructs were randomly implanted on the specimens which were then dissected so that only vertebrae C4 through C7 remained, maintaining the integrity of the posterior ligamentous complex and the posterior longitudinal ligament (Figure 4). Two screws were inserted into the anterior base of C7 and two screws were inserted into the anterior top of C4. Vertebrae C4 and C7 were potted using methyl methacrylate bone cement. Once the material was dry, the construct was mounted to a multiaxial servohydraulic test platform (AMTI VIVO) where the center of vertebrae C7 was found using a MicroScribe digitizer. This point was used as the center of rotation during the test. The AMTI VIVO was programmed so that the specimen would experience the following forces: ±3 NM axial, ±3 NM flexion, and ±2 NM lateral. The specimen was then tested by applying 1,500 cycles of each motion in each direction.

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed in each fatigue protocol using 1-way analysis of variance to determine whether stiffness retention differed among multiple segmental plates or a single long plate in each of the three motions. The percentages of reduction in stiffness of each specimen were normalized by the average C7 HUs, and paired t-tests were used to detect statistically significant differences in loss of stiffness between baseline and after each fatigue protocol. P values less than 0.05 were considered significant. A post hoc power analysis was performed using the observed standard deviations in construct stiffness. With a sample size of 12 specimens, the study had 70% power to detect a large effect size (Cohen’s d =1.0) at an alpha level of 0.05. This suggests that while major differences could be detected, more subtle differences between groups may not have reached statistical significance.

Results

We monitored the rotational stiffness of each specimen over the course of each test. The reduction in stiffness at the end of testing quantified the loss of fixation stability. The mean absolute starting stiffness in the artificial cervical spine models in all three motions was comparable between both groups (P=0.86, Figure 5). The retention of stiffness at the caudal segment was significantly better (P<0.001, Figure 6) in the multiple segmental plate group in axial rotation with only a 51% (±10%) reduction from baseline stiffness at completion of the fatigue protocol, as compared to an 88% (±4%) reduction in stiffness within the single long plate group. There were no significant differences on flexion-extension or lateral bending between groups (Table 2).

Discussion

The primary goal of this study was to compare the ability of multiple segmental plates in retaining stiffness at the caudal aspect of long ACDFs to the traditional single long plate construct. Our results suggest that individual segmental plates in the setting of multilevel anterior cervical fusions retain rotational stiffness at the caudal aspect of the construct more effectively than long single plate constructs.

Multilevel ACDF is broadly employed for a range of cervical pathology, but has historically led to higher rates of pseudoarthrosis, mechanical failure, and dysphagia (1). Traditional constructs have included long plates with interbody cages or structural bone graft, while more recently, fixation has also included separate segmental fixation systems or all-in-one cage-plate devices. Longer cervical fusions (3 or more levels), requiring long plates, more frequently fail at the caudal levels, leading to kyphosis, nonunion, and other undesirable outcomes (4). These complications are likely influenced by load sharing and transfer of stresses from the cephalad to the caudal most segments through these long plates. The use of segmentally separated fixation for long anterior cervical fusions is hypothesized to reduce this transfer of stress to the caudal aspects of the reconstruction, and thus reduce the risk and rate of failure after multilevel cervical anterior fusions. To date, no study has been performed to document or quantify the biomechanical benefit of multiple segmental plates over traditional long cervical plates for long cervical fusions.

These complications have significant impact on clinical outcomes and may require revision surgery that results in excessive resource utilization and potential risks of added adverse events (14-16). In addition, the long exposure and application of long anterior plates are associated with higher approach-related complications (17). Zero profile stand-alone cage implants were introduced to the market as a potential advantageous alternative in reducing rates of postoperative dysphagia (18,19). Other benefits with smaller profile constructs include reduced operative time, decreased postoperative pain and blood loss (20,21). However, a critical consideration of zero profile stand-alone cages is the higher subsidence rate. Jin et al. demonstrated that overall subsidence rate was higher in the stand alone group than conventional cage and plate construct in their cohort of seventy four patients undergoing ACDF (22). Ahn et al. echo similar results in their finite element modeling showing that a zero profile stand alone cage without a plate leads to higher subsidence rate, thereby interfering with disc stability and potentially incurring a higher reoperation rate (21). Multilevel segmental plate fixation confers a similar low-profile footprint while retaining superior biomechanical characteristics. In contradistinction to long plate constructs, multiple plate constructs demonstrate superior stiffness during fatigue testing in finite element analysis (8). In addition screws in segmental plating experience lower axial forces and thus decreased risk of screw pull out postoperatively (9).

As demonstrated in the cervical spine polyurethane foam models, our results show that multiple segmental plates retain more stiffness at the caudal aspect of the construct, especially in axial rotation. The observed superiority of the multiple segmental plate construct in retaining stiffness during axial rotation—compared to the lack of significant differences in flexion-extension and lateral bending—may be attributed to the mechanical behavior of cervical constructs under torsional load. Axial rotation generates more complex multidirectional shear forces across the interbody and fixation interfaces. In a long single plate construct, this torsional load may concentrate at the caudal-most fixation points, increasing the risk of screw loosening or micro-motion. Segmental plates distribute rotational forces more evenly across each level, reducing localized stress and enhancing resistance to torsional fatigue. In contrast, flexion-extension and lateral bending loads are more linearly distributed and may not benefit as substantially from multilevel force dispersion. This biomechanical rationale aligns with prior finite element studies demonstrating enhanced screw stability and reduced micromotion with segmental plating under rotational loads (5-8). Although not statistically significant, the single long plate group in the cadaveric specimen showed a greater bone density, making the similar retention of stiffness between both cadaveric groups more impressive on the multiple segmental plate group. Huang et al. (8) found that the maximum stress of the cage was higher in single plate fixation compared to multiple plates. They propose that that the multiple plates allow for more screws which could decrease stress on the cage via load sharing (5). The use of a titanium interbody cage significantly influences the stiffness of the construct, particularly when a good press-fit is achieved. This mechanical contribution of the cage may account for the observed differences in stiffness between cadaveric and synthetic sawbone models. In cadaveric specimens, variability in anatomy and bone quality can reduce the cage’s contribution compared to sawbones, where material uniformity and precise fit may enhance construct stability. These factors likely explain the differential stiffness outcomes observed and underscore the need for future studies.

While the use of polyurethane sawbones models provides a controlled and reproducible environment for biomechanical testing, we acknowledge their limitations in replicating the viscoelastic properties and complex soft tissue interactions of the in vivo cervical spine. However, the sawbones model was chosen to eliminate variability in bone quality, which can confound stiffness measurements in cadaveric specimens. This allowed for a more focused comparison of construct mechanics. Our findings from the sawbones testing were used primarily to supplement the cadaveric data and to isolate the effects of instrumentation techniques under consistent boundary conditions. Another limitation of this study is the lack of in vivo validation or retrospective clinical correlation. While the biomechanical models allowed us to control for anatomical and construct variables, they do not capture patient-specific factors such as biological healing response, comorbidities, or loading conditions during daily activity. Although we normalized biomechanical outcomes using HU measurements to account for bone density differences, we did not adjust for other potentially confounding variables such as age, sex, or medical history. Given the small sample size, controlling for these factors was not statistically feasible, which may influence the interpretation of cadaveric stiffness outcomes. Future studies with larger sample sizes should include multivariate adjustment to improve the robustness of biomechanical normalization.

Although our study demonstrated biomechanical advantages of multiple segmental plates—particularly in axial rotation—this was not correlated with actual fusion outcomes, which are the ultimate determinant of clinical success. Fusion is influenced by numerous biological and patient-specific factors that cannot be modeled in sawbones or cadaveric specimens. Prior studies have shown that greater construct stability can promote osseous fusion and reduce pseudoarthrosis risk, particularly at the caudal end of multilevel fusions where nonunion is most common (23). A key limitation of our study is the small sample size in the cadaveric component (n=12), which may limit the statistical power to detect more subtle biomechanical differences. While trends were observed, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. One potential concern with the use of multiple segmental plates is the risk of screw loosening due to micromotion at individual segmental interfaces. Although our study did not directly evaluate screw backout or loosening, prior biomechanical analyses have suggested that segmental constructs may actually reduce peak axial force per screw by distributing the load across more anchor points (8,9). This could, in theory, mitigate the risk of screw fatigue and pullout compared to long single plates, where stress is more concentrated at terminal screws. Nevertheless, clinical validation is necessary to determine whether this theoretical advantage translates into lower hardware complication rates.

Conclusions

In this study, multiple individual segmental plates demonstrated a potential biomechanical advantage in retaining rotational stiffness at the caudal aspect of multilevel anterior cervical constructs, particularly in a controlled sawbones model. These results suggest a trend that warrants further investigation through larger biomechanical studies and clinical outcome analyses to determine whether this construct translates into improved fusion rates or reduced complication profiles. Although we did not demonstrate these differences between groups in the cadaveric arm, they were present when the constructs were tested in a more consistent testing model. These results support the need for clinical studies comparing both constructs. This may lead to a change in practice resulting in a reduction in mechanical and fusion complications related to long anterior cervical fusion treated with single plates, which may also reduce the need for revision or additional surgical approaches after such surgery.
