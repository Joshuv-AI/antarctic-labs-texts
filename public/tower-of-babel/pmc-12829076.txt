Introduction

Vascular anomalies are congenital and neonatal abnormalities that are categorized into vascular tumors and vascular malformations [1]. Hemangiomas are endothelial tumors marked by heightened cellular proliferation, usually manifesting shortly after birth and exhibiting rapid growth. These tumors are most prevalent in infancy, particularly in the craniofacial region [2].

Intrinsic vascular anomalies originating from the internal jugular vein are uncommon and frequently manifest as a mass in the neck region [3]. The rarity of intrinsic internal jugular vein anomalies, along with the established associations of facial hemangiomas with other vascular anomalies, underscores the significance of this case report. It provides important insights into the clinical presentation, diagnosis, and management of these complex vascular lesions in a young patient.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old Iranian female patient, with no significant medical history other than a substantial cutaneous hemangioma situated on the right side of her face, attended the dental clinic for standard dental operations. The patient exhibited no symptoms and had no previous history of bleeding diseases, cardiovascular abnormalities, or surgical operations. Physical exam showed a big, well-defined hemangioma on the right cheek and around the eyes. There were no symptoms of infection, pulsatility, or bruit. Patient’s initial hematologic and coagulation profiles were normal.

The attending dentist ordered a computed tomography (CT) angiography of the head and neck arteries to check the vascular architecture and rule out any underlying arteriovenous abnormalities. This was because the hemangioma was close to major arterial structures and there was a risk of bleeding during the procedure. CT angiography showed two major problems with the blood vessels: the right internal jugular vein (IJV) was missing from the jugular foramen to the thoracic inlet, and the left internal jugular vein had two separate venous channels that kept their flow and size.

The lack of the right IJV and the presence of two left IJVs are unusual congenital vascular variations (Fig. 1).

The findings are significant in relation to facial hemangiomas, as they may indicate underlying developmental anomalies of the branchial arch derivatives. Recognizing these variations is essential for procedural planning, particularly in interventions related to central venous access, head and neck surgery, or embolization therapy. There was no need for rapid action. The dental operation was postponed until a multidisciplinary review could be carried out. The patient was sent to vascular surgery and interventional radiology for further tests and long-term monitoring.

Discussion

Our case involves a 17-year-old female patient with a cutaneous hemangioma on her right cheek. Unexpected findings from CT angiography showed significant congenital vascular variations of the internal jugular veins (IJVs): a missing right IJV from the jugular foramen to the thoracic inlet and a duplicated left IJV. These findings are significant as they indicate developmental defects of the branchial arch derivatives, requiring a multidisciplinary evaluation and prolonged surveillance.

Finding intrinsic vascular problems with the internal jugular vein is quite unusual. Duggal et al. [4] documented a case of intrinsic vascular anomaly originating from the internal jugular vein, manifesting as a mass at the base of the neck. In a similar vein, Anajar et al. [3] discussed an internal jugular vein cavernous hemangioma. Venous malformations (VMs) are often the most prevalent form of vascular malformation; nevertheless, the occurrence of venous anomalies in deep veins, such as the internal jugular vein, is regarded as “extremely rare.” These studies emphasize the atypical characteristics of our patient’s IJV results and the scarcity of literature about such particular deep venous abnormalities. The total absence of the right IJV and the duplication of the left IJV further emphasize the singularity of this instance.

The significant vascular abnormalities observed in our patient’s IJVs, although being venous, correspond with the assertion that “vascular anomalies are the most frequent anomaly associated with cutaneous hemangioma–vascular complex syndrome” [5]. The presence of the hemangioma on the face of a young child necessitates a thorough evaluation to exclude related anomalies, as conducted in our study.

The recognized “unusual congenital vascular variations” of the IJVs and their possible correlation with developmental abnormalities of the branchial arch derivatives align with the comprehension of vascular development. Vascular anomalies are often regarded as congenital defects arising from atypical development of vascular structures during embryogenesis and fetal development. The embryonic development of the cerebral arteries and the aortic arch is a complex and time-sensitive process. Arrest or occlusion in this development during specific embryonic stages (for example, between 4.5 and 6.5 weeks of gestation for carotid and vertebral arteries originating in the aortic arch) can lead to various anomalies, including agenesis or hypoplasia of major vessels [6]. The internal jugular vein itself is a major derivative of the branchial arch system. Therefore, the agenesis of the right IJV and duplication of the left IJV in our patient strongly suggest a perturbation during these critical periods of vascular embryogenesis.

The use of CT angiography in our case was crucial for revealing these unexpected IJV anomalies. Imaging plays a vital role in the diagnosis of vascular anomalies. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is often considered the most informative technique for documenting the nature and extent of venous anomalies [7]. Pascual-Castroviejo et al. [5] recommend that patients with hemangiomas of the face, scalp, neck, and chest undergo CT or MR studies, preferably with MR angiography, to include intracranial and extracranial vessels as well as the aortic arch. In some reported cases, even advanced imaging can be challenging; Anajar et al. [3] noted that neither ultrasound nor CT scan initially enabled the diagnosis in their case of IJV cavernous hemangioma, possibly due to thrombosis. The case of Duggal et al. [4] also required surgical exploration and histopathology for a definitive diagnosis, despite imaging suggestive of a mass. These examples emphasize that while imaging is essential, the diagnosis of rare deep vascular anomalies can be complex and may require further investigation.

The range of internal jugular vein (IJV) disorders encompasses developmental defects, as exemplified by the presented example, and acquired lesions, such as malignancies. Two recent case reports highlight the intricacies of managing space-occupying IJV lesions: Puppala et al. [8] described an uncommon instance of IJV venous malformation treated with surgical excision and ligation to address the etiology and prevent complications, while Balcıoğlu et al. [9] examined a substantial right IJV hemangioma excised through a mini J-sternotomy approach, necessitating synthetic graft reconstruction to prevent venous hypertension. Unlike these surgical examples, the present case pertains to a rare developmental defect characterized by complete agenesis of the right IJV and duplication of the left IJV, identified accidentally in a patient principally exhibiting a facial hemangioma. This underscores the necessity of identifying atypical venous pathways prior to procedures, emphasizing the significance of thorough vascular imaging in patients with substantial head and neck vascular lesions to prevent complications during interventions such as central venous cannulation or surgeries in the head and neck area.

The decision to postpone the dental operation and refer the patient for a multidisciplinary review, further tests, and long-term monitoring is highly appropriate and aligns with recommended practices for complex vascular anomalies. Given the rarity of internal jugular vein anomalies, “therapeutic strategies remain unclear.” However, a close interdisciplinary cooperation of all involved disciplines is essential for diagnosing, evaluating, and treating these diseases. To ensure that complicated patients get the best care, it is important to use specialized clinics and “angioma boards.” Patients with PHACE syndrome require multidisciplinary management and follow-up involving a team of specialists, including pediatricians, neurologists, dermatologists, surgeons, and ophthalmologists. The existence of both a face hemangioma and uncommon IJV abnormalities in our patient highlights the necessity for a thorough, coordinated strategy to assess the complete scope of the vascular anomalies and devise suitable therapy.

Conclusion

This case of internal jugular vein anomalies in a young patient with a facial hemangioma highlights the necessity of comprehensive vascular assessment in patients with cutaneous vascular lesions. The lack of the right internal jugular vein and the duplication of the left vein underscore atypical congenital variations and indicate a more profound embryological disruption of branchial arch derivatives. The findings underscore the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and advanced imaging in procedural planning, particularly for patients with complex vascular anatomy. Timely identification and integrated management are crucial for guaranteeing safe interventions and ongoing oversight.
