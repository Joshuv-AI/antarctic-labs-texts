Interaction Between Operator and Assistant Certification Is Associated With Local Recurrence After Laparoscopic Rectal Cancer Surgery: An Ancillary Analysis of the EnSSURE Study
Authors: Yusuke Sakimura, Hiroyuki Bando, Koya Hida, Tatsunari Fukuoka, Tohru Funakoshi, Fuminori Teraishi, Kay Uehara, Yuji Inoue, Nobuaki Suzuki, Nobuki Ichikawa, Hiroaki Iijima, Akinobu Taketomi, Yoshiharu Sakai, Takeshi Naitoh, Ichiro Takemasa, the EnSSURE study group collaboratives within the Japan Society of Laparoscopic Colorectal Surgery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLocal recurrence (LR) after laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery remains a major clinical concern. While surgical expertise is important, the impact of combined operator and assistant certification on local recurrence remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study analyzed 2825 patients with stage II–III rectal cancer who underwent laparoscopic resection in the EnSSURE cohort. The primary endpoint was the 3‐year cumulative incidence of LR, treating death and distant metastasis as competing events. The primary analysis used a Fine–Gray model including an interaction term between operator and assistant certification based on the Endoscopic Surgical Skill Qualification System. Secondary analyses included a four‐group Fine–Gray analysis (qualified operator and assistant [QQ], qualified operator only [QN], qualified assistant only [NQ], and neither qualified [NN]). A cause‐specific Cox model was used for sensitivity analysis.ResultsDuring a median follow‐up of 55 months, LR occurred in 125 patients (4.4%). A significant interaction between operator and assistant certification was observed (subdistribution hazard ratio [sHR] 0.43, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.20–0.95; p = 0.038), whereas neither was independently associated with LR. In the four‐group analysis, the risk of LR was significantly higher in the QN, NQ, and NN groups compared with the QQ group (QN: sHR 2.37; NQ: sHR 2.12; NN: sHR 2.18). Results were consistent in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsA significant interaction between operator and assistant certification was associated with a lower risk of LR, suggesting that surgical outcomes may depend on team composition rather than individual qualifications alone.Trial RegistrationUMIN Clinical Trials Registry System: UMIN000040645 (June 3, 2020)Keywords: clinical competence, credentialing, laparoscopic surgery, local recurrence, rectal cancer, risk factors

Introduction

Local recurrence after laparoscopic radical resection for rectal cancer (LR) remains a major concern because it is associated with poor survival and prognosis [1, 2]. Compared with colon cancer, rectal cancer has a higher risk of local recurrence, and laparoscopic rectal resection is technically demanding [2, 3, 4]. Several clinicopathological factors including advanced tumor stage, positive resection margins and low tumor location have been reported as risk factors for LR [2, 5, 6, 7, 8]. In contrast, preoperative radiation therapy (pre‐RT) and rectal washout are known to reduce the risk of LR [9, 10]. Several multi‐institutional prospective studies have indicated noninferiority or no significant difference in the rate of LR of laparoscopic rectal resection compared with open surgery [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. These studies were conducted in highly selected settings involving expert surgeons, raising concerns regarding their generalizability to real‐world practice.

Surgical quality is influenced not only by the primary operator but also by the entire surgical team. However, the impact of surgeon and assistant expertise on LR remains unclear. Previous analyses from the EnSSURE study demonstrated that the presence of either a qualified operator alone or a qualified assistant alone, based on the Endoscopic Surgical Skill Qualification System (ESSQS) of the Japan Society for Endoscopic Surgery (JSES), was not associated with a lower risk of LR [17]. These findings suggest that individual certification alone may not be sufficient to influence oncological outcomes.

Given these observations, it remains unclear whether the combined presence of both a qualified operator and a qualified assistant influences LR. We therefore hypothesized that a synergistic interaction between operator and assistant certification may affect LR.

Methods

Patients

This study was a retrospective observational study with data from the EnSSURE study, in which patient data were retrospectively gathered from 56 member institutions of Japan Society of Laparoscopic Colorectal Surgery (JSLCS) [17]. This group was composed of 31 university hospitals and cancer centers, and 25 other hospitals. The data included patient background, preoperative diagnosis, neoadjuvant therapy, operation details, complications, pathology, adjuvant therapy and follow‐up. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: patients with a histopathological diagnosis of rectal adenocarcinoma before surgery; patients with tumors located between the rectosigmoid colon and lower rectum; patients with clinical stage II or III disease; and patients who planned surgery with laparoscopy. The exclusion criteria of EnSSURE study were as follows: patients with synchronous or metachronous cancers within 5 years excluding in situ cancer; robotic surgery; multiple intra‐abdominal procedures during surgery; inappropriate registration of the clinical report form; ulcerative colitis‐associated cancers. To conduct this ancillary analysis, the cases with missing data for pre‐ and intraoperative variables, non‐R0 resection, D0 or D1 lymphadenectomy, conversion to open surgery, postoperative diagnosis of stage IV, no surveillance after surgery and refusal of the patient to participate in this study were excluded (Figure 1).

Definition

Tumor stage and location, grade of lymphadenectomy, and residual tumor status were defined according to the eighth edition of the Japanese Classification of Colorectal Carcinoma [18]. The Endoscopic Surgical Skill Qualification System (ESSQS) of the Japan Society for Endoscopic Surgery (JSES) is a certification system designed to objectively assess laparoscopic surgical skills and ensure the ability to supervise and instruct other surgeons. Certification requires submission of unedited surgical videos, which are evaluated by multiple expert reviewers based on standardized criteria. Applicants are required to have substantial surgical experience prior to certification. The certification does not restrict the types of procedures that surgeons can perform or the institutions in which they practice. In addition, certification must be periodically renewed to maintain quality standards. The operator was defined as the primary surgeon, and assistants included the first and second assistant and scopist. If any assistant was board‐certified, the procedure was considered to involve a qualified assistant. The indications for pre‐RT and surgical approach were determined under discussion by surgeons, other professionals and patients. The follow‐up period was at least 3 years after surgery. LR was defined as recurrence at the anastomotic site, regional lymph nodes or other tissue, and the modality for the diagnosis was based on each hospital. The primary endpoint of this study was the 3‐year cumulative incidence of LR after laparoscopic resection, considering death and distant metastasis as competing events.

Statistics

To evaluate the primary hypothesis, a Fine–Gray model including an interaction term between operator and assistant certification was performed. As a secondary analysis, patients were categorized into four surgeon–assistant qualification groups (qualified operator and qualified assistant [QQ], qualified operator and nonqualified assistant [QN], nonqualified operator and qualified assistant [NQ], and nonqualified operator and nonqualified assistant [NN]) and the Fine–Gray competing risk model treating death and distant metastasis as competing events was conducted.

Differences among groups were assessed using Gray's test. Subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for all models. Patients without LR were censored at the last follow‐up, and death and distant metastasis were treated as competing events. The covariates included the preoperative and operative variables, namely, age, body mass index (BMI; dichotomized at 22 kg/m2 according to the standard body weight in Japan [19]), sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status, preoperative obstruction, clinical T stage, clinical N stage, tumor location, preoperative radiation therapy, operation with anastomosis, lateral lymphadenectomy, institution type, institution caseload and operator and assistant certification status. The variables from the obtained data were chosen based on a previous report of the risk factors for LR and categorized into binary variables. Each variable and surgical outcome were compared between qualification groups using Pearson's χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were conducted with R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Sensitivity Analysis

Several prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings. First, a cause‐specific Cox proportional hazards model including the interaction term between operator and assistant certification was applied, censoring patients at the time of competing events. Second, the primary interaction analysis was repeated using a multivariable Fine–Gray model in which institutional caseload was modeled as a continuous variable instead of the predefined categorical institutional caseload variable. Third, the primary interaction analysis was repeated using a more parsimonious multivariable Fine–Gray model including 12 prespecified clinically relevant variables to evaluate the potential influence of model complexity. Finally, assistant certification was redefined according to the certification status of the first assistant alone, and both the primary interaction analysis and the secondary four‐group Fine–Gray analysis were repeated using the revised definition.

Patients were categorized into four groups according to surgeon–assistant qualification status: QQ (n = 650, 23.0%), QN (n = 981, 34.7%), NQ (n = 607, 21.5%), and NN (n = 587, 20.8%). Qualified operators (QQ and QN) were more frequently involved in patients with younger age, higher BMI, lower rectal tumors, more advanced clinical T and N stages, preoperative radiotherapy, non‐anastomotic procedures, and lateral lymph node dissection. QQ procedures were more commonly performed in academic institutions and cancer centers. There were no differences in pathological stage or adjuvant chemotherapy among the groups. The median follow‐up period was 55 months.

Cumulative Incidence of Local Recurrence

The cumulative incidence of LR is shown in Figure 2. The 3‐year cumulative incidence of LR was 2.73% in the QQ group, 5.94% in the QN group, 4.35% in the NQ group, and 4.87% in the NN group. The incidence differed significantly among the four groups (Gray's test, p = 0.040), with the lowest incidence observed in the QQ group.

Primary Analysis: Interaction Between Operator and Assistant Certification

In multivariable analysis using the Fine–Gray competing risks model including an interaction term (Table 2), neither operator nor assistant certification alone was significantly associated with LR. However, a significant interaction between operator and assistant certification was observed (sHR 0.43, 95% CI 0.20–0.95; p = 0.038), indicating a synergistic effect.

Sensitivity Analysis

The cause‐specific Cox proportional hazards model yielded findings consistent with those of the primary Fine–Gray analysis (HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.20–0.94; p = 0.035; Table S1). When institutional caseload was modeled as a continuous variable, the primary interaction analysis remained statistically significant (sHR 0.42, 95% CI 0.19–0.93, p = 0.033; Table S2). Similarly, the primary interaction analysis remained statistically significant in the more parsimonious multivariable model including 12 prespecified clinically relevant variables (sHR 0.44, 95% CI 0.20–0.97, p = 0.042; Table S3). When assistant certification was restricted to the certification status of the first assistant, the primary interaction analysis remained statistically significant (sHR 0.43, 95% CI 0.20–0.96, p = 0.038; Table S4A). The results of the secondary four‐group analysis were also consistent with the primary analysis, with the QQ group continuing to demonstrate the lowest risk of LR (Table S4B).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that a significant interaction between operator and assistant certification was associated with a lower risk of LR after laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery. Notably, neither operator nor assistant certification alone was significantly associated with LR. Only their combination was associated with a lower risk of LR, supporting the presence of a synergistic interaction between the two roles. These findings suggest that surgical outcomes may depend on the interaction between team members rather than individual qualifications alone.

These findings highlight the importance of team‐based surgery. Laparoscopic rectal surgery is a technically demanding procedure that requires precise coordination between the operator and assistant. A qualified assistant contributes not only to technical support but also to intraoperative decision‐making and optimal exposure, while a qualified operator ensures appropriate procedural execution. The synergistic effect observed in this study may reflect the integration of these complementary roles.

The finding that the QQ group was associated with a lower risk of LR is consistent with this interpretation. Previous studies have reported that the presence of a qualified operator or assistant alone is associated with improved operative and short‐term outcomes, such as reduced blood loss, shorter operative time, lower conversion rates, and fewer postoperative complications [17, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. However, no clear association with oncological outcomes has been demonstrated. Our findings extend these observations by suggesting that a combination of qualified operator and assistant may be required to influence long‐term oncological outcomes such as LR. Furthermore, the significant interaction between operator and assistant certification supports the presence of a synergistic effect beyond individual qualifications.

To further examine the robustness of the primary findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which assistant certification was defined according to the certification status of the first assistant alone, and the interaction remained statistically significant. This finding suggests that the first assistant may play an important role in the observed synergistic effect between operator and assistant certification. However, because assistant certification in the primary analysis was defined based on certification of any assisting member, the potential contributions of the second assistant and scopist cannot be excluded.

One possible explanation for the lower risk of LR observed in the QQ group is that institutions capable of assembling ESSQS‐certified operators and assistants may also have more established multidisciplinary treatment pathways, standardized perioperative management, and greater institutional experience, all of which could contribute to improved oncological outcomes. Although our analyses adjusted for institution type and institutional case volume, residual confounding related to unmeasured institutional characteristics cannot be excluded. Thus, the presence of ESSQS‐certified operators and assistants may represent not only individual technical expertise but also a broader institutional environment associated with high‐quality rectal cancer care.

Another possible explanation is that the combination of ESSQS‐certified operators and assistants may contribute to improved surgical quality. This improvement may be reflected by better circumferential resection margin (CRM) status and/or total mesorectal excision (TME) quality, both of which are well‐established pathological indicators associated with LR after rectal cancer surgery [27, 28]. The recent Japanese PRODUCT trial further highlighted the importance of standardized pathological assessment of CRM and TME quality in laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery [29]. However, because information on CRM status and TME quality was not collected in the EnSSURE database, we were unable to determine whether these pathological factors mediated the association between operator–assistant certification and a lower risk of LR.

These findings have potential clinical implications. The combination of operator and assistant certification was associated with a lower risk of LR in this study. However, because of the observational design, these findings should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of a causal effect, and further prospective studies are needed before recommendations regarding optimal team composition can be made. The combination of ESSQS‐certified operators and assistants may be particularly relevant when organizing surgical teams for technically demanding cases, such as low rectal tumors or clinically advanced disease. These findings may have implications for surgical education, as the presence of certified surgeons in both operator and assistant roles could contribute not only to technical performance but also to intraoperative guidance and team coordination.

There are several limitations in this study. First, its retrospective design may have introduced selection bias and residual confounding, despite multivariable adjustment. Although we adjusted for institution type and institutional caseload, residual confounding due to unmeasured institutional characteristics or differences in treatment policies cannot be completely excluded. In addition, although the number of LR events was limited relative to the number of adjustment variables in the primary model, a sensitivity analysis using a more parsimonious model yielded consistent results. Nevertheless, the possibility of model overfitting cannot be completely excluded. Second, certification status may not fully reflect actual surgical skill, and some non‐certified surgeons may possess equivalent expertise. Third, we were unable to account for intraoperative role changes, including situations in which the operator and assistant exchanged roles or a supervising surgeon assumed the operator's role during technically demanding portions of the procedure. Such unrecorded changes may have resulted in misclassification of the actual operating team and could have attenuated the observed association between ESSQS certification and LR. Fourth, the EnSSURE database did not include information on CRM status or TME quality, both of which are established indicators of surgical quality and are closely associated with LR. Consequently, we were unable to determine whether the observed association between operator–assistant certification and a lower risk of LR was mediated through improvements in surgical quality reflected by these pathological factors. This is an important limitation of the present study, as it precludes evaluation of the potential mechanism underlying the observed association. Finally, although ESSQS certification was treated as a binary variable in the present study, heterogeneity may exist among certified surgeons with respect to years since certification, cumulative operative experience, and surgical case volume. These surgeon‐specific characteristics were not available in the EnSSURE database and therefore could not be evaluated. Future studies are warranted to determine whether these factors further modify the association between ESSQS certification and oncological outcomes.

In conclusion, a significant interaction between operator and assistant certification was associated with a lower risk of LR, highlighting the potential importance of the interaction between operator and assistant certification, rather than certification of individual team members alone.
