Prevalence of gallstone disease and its association with altitude and ethnicity among employee health check-upers in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau
Authors: Shenggui Qi, Li Li, Yuhua Yan, Gengdeng, Xiaofeng Ma, Airong Yang, Hongguang Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence of gallstone disease among health check-upers (employees) in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and clarify the independent effects of altitude, ethnicity, and their interaction on gallstone disease risk, so as to provide evidence for precise prevention and control of gallstone disease in high-altitude multi-ethnic regions.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted, and data were collected from 35,930 employees who underwent health check-ups in formal institutions across the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. Gallstone disease was defined as the presence of current gallstones (diagnosed by abdominal ultrasound) or a history of cholecystectomy due to gallstones. Demographic information (age, gender, ethnicity), anthropometric indicators (BMI), metabolic indicators (blood glucose, blood lipid, blood pressure), fatty liver status (diagnosed by ultrasound), and residential altitude (classified as low altitude: >1680 m~, medium altitude: >2500 m~, high altitude: >3500 m ~ based on county government altitude data) were collected. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to analyze the association between altitude, ethnicity, and gallstone disease, and stratified regression was performed to explore the interaction effect of “altitude × ethnicity”.ResultsThe overall prevalence of gallstone disease in the study population was 13.6%, with 40.63% of gallstone patients having a history of cholecystectomy. Stratified by altitude, the prevalence of gallstone disease showed a trend of “medium altitude > high altitude > low altitude”: 20.5% in the medium-altitude group, 15.5% in the high-altitude group, and 9.8% in the low-altitude group. After adjusting for confounders (age, gender, BMI, fatty liver, abnormal blood glucose, hypertension, hyperlipidemia), compared with the low-altitude group, the medium-altitude group (OR = 2.439, 95% CI: 2.236–2.661, P < 0.001) and high-altitude group (OR = 1.427, 95% CI: 1.269–1.606, P < 0.001) had significantly increased gallstone disease risk. Regarding ethnicity, Tibetans had a higher risk than Han Chinese (OR = 1.344, 95% CI: 1.228–1.471, P < 0.001), while no significant difference was observed between other ethnic groups (Hui, Tu, Mongolian) and Han Chinese. A significant “altitude × ethnicity” interaction was identified (OR = 0.796, 95% CI: 0.723–0.876, P < 0.001): Han Chinese showed a persistent risk increase with rising altitude (medium-altitude OR = 2.804, high-altitude OR = 1.671), whereas Tibetans only had elevated risk at medium altitude (OR = 1.855) and no significant risk at high altitude (OR = 1.100).ConclusionAltitude is an independent risk factor for gallstone disease in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau health check-upers (employees). Ethnicity moderates the association between altitude and gallstone disease—Tibetans’ high-altitude adaptability attenuates the additional altitude-induced risk, while Han Chinese lack such adaptability and show continuous risk elevation. The high cholecystectomy rate among gallstone patients highlights the heavy clinical burden of gallstone disease in this region. These findings provide a theoretical basis for formulating ethnicity- and altitude-specific prevention strategies for gallstone disease in high-altitude areas.Keywords: Gallstones, Cholecystectomy, Prevalence, Risk factors, Qinghai-Tibet plateau, Altitude, Ethnicity

Introduction

Gallstones are composed of bilirubin, calcium, and other substances. While the exact etiology of gallstones remains unclear, it is widely recognized to be associated with factors such as genetics, cholesterol metabolism disorders, diabetes, obesity, female gender, and rapid weight loss [1, 2]. Gallstones represent the most common indication for cholecystectomy, and both gallstone disease and cholecystectomy are frequently encountered in clinical practice. Thus, understanding the pathogenesis, risk factors, and treatment approaches of gallstone disease is crucial for its effective prevention and management.

In high-altitude regions, the hypoxic environment may induce metabolic disturbances of various substances in the human body. Emerging studies suggest that hypoxia can disrupt bile acid and cholesterol metabolism, potentially increasing the prevalence of cholelithiasis [3, 4]. Additionally, residents in high-altitude areas often have a diet high in animal fats and cholesterol, coupled with insufficient intake of nutrients such as vitamin A and vitamin E—factors that may further elevate the risk of gallstone formation[5, 6]. Although a higher prevalence of gallstones has been observed in high-altitude regions including Chile, Bolivia, and Peru [7, 8], Moro PL et al. challenged this observation in 2000: after conducting a comparative study on populations in the Peruvian highlands and coastal areas, they concluded that “high altitude is not a positive risk factor for gallstone disease” [9], Moreover, research on the impact of altitude on gallstone prevalence remains limited overall. Therefore, the role of altitude in gallstone epidemiology requires further verification.

Qinghai Province, located in the northern part of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, covers an area of over 722,300 square kilometers, with altitudes ranging from 1,644 m to 6,861 m above sea level. Human settlements are primarily concentrated in areas below 4,500 m. The province comprises 39 administrative divisions (including cities and counties), where local residents and employees live and work across these geographically diverse regions. The main ethnic groups in Qinghai include Han, Tibetan, Mongolian, Hui, and other ethnic minorities, among which the Han and Tibetan populations are the largest. Ethnic differences are known to influence disease manifestations, and the broad multi-ethnic distribution combined with varying altitude gradients in Qinghai provides an ideal setting to examine the effects of ethnicity and altitude on gallstone disease. However, no studies have yet reported whether differences exist in gallstone prevalence among Tibetan, Han, and other ethnic groups in this region; similarly, the impact of altitude gradient changes on local gallstone prevalence remains uninvestigated.

Against this backdrop, the present study utilized health check-up data from a check-up population to explore the effects of altitude and ethnicity on the prevalence of gallstone disease, and further analyzed their interaction in modulating gallstone prevalence in high-altitude areas. Given the paucity of community-level studies on gallstone disease prevalence in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, this research was also designed to determine the prevalence of gallstone disease and identify its associated risk factors among health check-uppers in this region.

Study methods

Data sources and study participants

This study was designed as a cross-sectional survey. A comprehensive health check-up program was implemented by the Qinghai Provincial Government between 2019 and 2023, with participants primarily consisting of employees from state-owned enterprises, public institutions, government departments, and private companies. Data were collected from the routine health check-up records of 72,426 individuals, who represented diverse ethnic backgrounds and had resided in the sampled areas for more than one year.

To ensure that participants had adequate time to adapt to high-altitude environments, individuals who had worked and lived at the same altitude for less than one year were excluded. Additionally, participants with incomplete health check-up records or missing key data were excluded to guarantee the integrity of data analysis. For cholecystectomy diagnosis, individuals with congenital gallbladder absence (rather than gallbladder absence due to gallstone disease) were excluded. Those with gallbladder diseases unrelated to gallstones—such as cholecystitis, gallbladder polyps, or other non-stone gallbladder disorders—were also excluded. Furthermore, individuals with other severe health conditions that might affect gallstone formation or the decision for cholecystectomy (e.g., liver cirrhosis, pancreatitis) were excluded, as were those who could not complete the health check-up questionnaire due to language barriers.

After applying these exclusion criteria, 35,930 participants (49.61% of the original 72,426 individuals who underwent regular health check-ups) were finally included in this study. The specific exclusion process is illustrated in Fig. 1. To assess exclusion bias, baseline characteristics were compared between included and excluded populations: no statistically significant differences were observed in age (45.2 ± 12.3 years vs. 44.8 ± 11.9 years, t = 1.26, P = 0.207), gender distribution (male proportion: 49.1% vs. 48.7%, χ²=0.18, P = 0.672), or ethnic distribution (Tibetan proportion: 38.5% vs. 37.9%, χ²=0.31, P = 0.577), indicating the exclusion process did not significantly alter the representativeness of the study population.

All participants completed their health check-ups at one of five health check-up centers located in Xining, the capital city of Qinghai Province—these centers are the primary destinations for health check-ups in Qinghai Province.

Data were collected from January 2019 to December 2023, with annual sample proportions: 18.2% (2019), 21.5% (2020), 23.1% (2021), 22.7% (2022), and 14.5% (2023). By Qinghai-Tibet Plateau seasons (spring: Mar–May, summer: Jun–Aug, autumn: Sep–Nov, winter: Dec–Feb), summer samples accounted for 31.2% (due to nomads’ concentrated health check-ups), winter 22.8%, spring 23.5%, and autumn 22.5%. Further analysis showed no significant difference in gallstone prevalence across seasons (χ²=3.26, P = 0.353), and the altitude-ethnicity interaction remained consistent (OR: 1.02–1.08, all P > 0.05), indicating no major seasonal interference with core conclusions.

All participants provided written informed consent to participate in this study, and the consent process was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Qinghai Cardio-Cerebrovascular Specialty Hospital.

Baseline characteristic balance analysis revealed that the educational background and income level of the participants were within relatively stable ranges:

Regarding education: 82.6% of participants had a college degree or higher (81.9% in the high-altitude group, 83.2% in the medium-altitude group, and 82.8% in the low-altitude group), while 17.4% had a high school education or lower (18.1%, 16.8%, and 17.2% in the three altitude groups, respectively). No statistically significant differences in educational background were observed among the different altitude groups (P > 0.05).

Regarding monthly income: No statistically significant differences were found among the different altitude groups (P > 0.05).

It should be noted that this balance only ensures homogeneity of baseline characteristics within the study population (i.e., employees of formal institutions across different altitude groups) and does not represent the characteristics of the entire population in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau region. A large number of nomads, farmers, and other groups in this region have significantly different educational backgrounds and income levels from the study participants. This sample selection bias may lead to deviations in the estimation of the association between altitude and gallstone disease.

Diagnostic criteria and data collection

Definition of outcome variable

The outcome variable was “gallstone disease”, which included two scenarios:

① Current gallstones: Diagnosed via abdominal ultrasound. The diagnostic criterion was the presence of hyperechoic foci with a diameter ≥ 3 mm in the gallbladder on ultrasound images, accompanied by posterior acoustic shadowing and mobility with changes in body position.

① Current gallstones: Diagnosed via abdominal ultrasound. The diagnostic criterion was the presence of hyperechoic foci with a diameter ≥ 3 mm in the gallbladder on ultrasound images, accompanied by posterior acoustic shadowing and mobility with changes in body position.

② History of cholecystectomy: No gallbladder image was detected on abdominal ultrasound, and the participant confirmed (via interview) that cholecystectomy was performed due to gallstone-related diseases (e.g., cholecystolithiasis with cholecystitis, biliary colic).

② History of cholecystectomy: No gallbladder image was detected on abdominal ultrasound, and the participant confirmed (via interview) that cholecystectomy was performed due to gallstone-related diseases (e.g., cholecystolithiasis with cholecystitis, biliary colic).

Participants meeting either of the above criteria were classified as “gallstone disease-positive” (coded = 1), while those meeting neither were classified as “negative” (coded = 0).

In this study, the outcome indicator “gallstone disease” was defined as the combination of “current gallstones” and “history of cholecystectomy due to gallstones”, with the following rationale for this combined definition. Clinically, the core etiology of a cholecystectomy history is gallstone disease—all patients who underwent the surgery met gallstone disease diagnostic criteria pre-operation (e.g., symptomatic gallstones, high complication risk)—meaning these post-surgical patients and those with current gallstones belong to the same “gallstone disease population”, differing only in disease stage (pre-treatment vs. post-treatment). Epidemiologically, this combined outcome more comprehensively reflects the overall gallstone disease burden, avoiding underestimation of true prevalence in regions with sufficient medical resources (e.g., medium-altitude cities): these areas have high cholecystectomy rates, so focusing solely on “current gallstones” would exclude post-surgical patients and skew results. Additionally, sensitivity analysis confirmed consistency between the combined outcome and the single outcome of “current gallstones” in altitude-related trends—for the medium-altitude group, the OR was 2.439 for the combined outcome and 2.316 for the single outcome, verifying stable altitude-related risk trends regardless of definition.

Core exposure variable: altitude

Since participants (mostly enterprise and public institution employees) generally resided and worked in county government seats, and the altitude difference of these government seats across counties/cities in Qinghai Province was generally within ± 100 m, altitude was determined based on the altitude of the local county government seat.A pre-survey of 500 randomly selected participants confirmed that 92.3% resided within 5 km of the county government seat, and the altitude difference between their actual residential address and the county government seat was 23.6 ± 18.9 m (range: 0–89 m). For the remaining 7.7% of participants living in suburban areas, we conducted sensitivity analysis by excluding these cases—consistent results were obtained (OR for middle altitude group: 2.41 [95% CI: 2.19–2.65] vs. original OR: 2.44 [95% CI: 2.22–2.68]), confirming the robustness of the classification method.A total of 35 local county government seats were identified according to the participants’ work areas. Participants were divided into 3 groups based on the altitude of their work area: Low-altitude group: 1680–2499 m (reference group, coded = 0), Medium-altitude group: 2500–3499 m (coded = 1),High-altitude group: ≥ 3500 m (coded = 2).It should be noted that this classification method did not use the altitude data of participants’ actual residential addresses, which may lead to misclassification bias. For instance, some participants living in low-altitude towns around county government seats (e.g., at an altitude of 2400 m) were categorized into the medium-altitude group (2500–3499 m); meanwhile, participants residing in high-altitude pastoral areas (e.g., at an altitude of 3600 m) but whose affiliated county government seats are located at an altitude of 3400 m were also classified into the medium-altitude group.

The 2500 m altitude cutoff was referenced to the international standard for chronic mountain sickness—the Qinghai Standard [10]. This classification enabled analysis of how residential altitude (more specifically, the altitude at which participants reside and work) might influence the development of gallstone disease.

Moderator variable: ethnicity

The participants’ ethnic composition included Han Chinese, Tibetans, and other ethnic groups (e.g., Hui, Tu, Mongolian). Ethnicity was determined first by the designation on participants’ official identification documents, and second by confirming whether there was inter-ethnic ancestry within three generations via interview. Participants were categorized into three groups: Han Chinese (coded = 0), Tibetan (coded = 1), Other ethnic groups (coded = 2).

Data collection methods

Trained investigators used standardized questionnaires to collect participants’ basic information (age, gender, ethnicity, workplace altitude, and previous surgical history), living habits, and medical history. Meanwhile, physical indicators including height, weight, and blood pressure were measured. Fasting venous blood samples were sent to the clinical laboratory of each health check-up center, where total cholesterol, triglycerides, and fasting blood glucose were detected using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Model: Hitachi 7600-020).

Abdominal ultrasound examinations were performed by qualified sonographers using unified equipment, including Philips EPIQ 5, GE Logiq E9, or Mindray Resona R9 (abdominal convex array probe, frequency: 3.5–5.0.5.0 MHz). Ultrasound images were stored in DICOM 3.0 format. All data were double-entry into the EpiData 3.1 database (EpiData Association). After data entry, 10% of the data were randomly selected for verification to ensure the data entry error rate was < 0.5%.

Quality control

Equipment and personnel quality control

Ultrasound equipment

All ultrasound devices used in the health check-up centers were calibrated by third-party institutions before the study initiation to ensure consistency in parameters such as probe frequency and resolution. Scanning parameters were uniformly set as follows: gain 40–60 dB, dynamic range 60–80 dB, and focal depth 8–12 cm.

Personnel training

Sonographers were required to hold a practicing physician qualification certificate and have at least 5 years of experience in abdominal ultrasound diagnosis. Prior to the study, they participated in a 3-day specialized training program covering diagnostic criteria for gallstones/history of cholecystectomy and standardized operating procedures.

Only those who passed the assessment (theoretical score ≥ 80 points and practical diagnosis coincidence rate ≥ 95%) were eligible to participate. Investigators also received training on questionnaire administration and data entry, and only took up their posts after passing the assessment.

Multicenter data quality control

A three-level quality control system was implemented: Level 1 (Intra-center Self-inspection) involved each center randomly reviewing 10% of daily samples (including ultrasound reports, questionnaires, and test results) by physicians with associate senior titles or above, with a required diagnosis/data coincidence rate ≥ 98%; Level 2 (Spot-check by Leading Unit) meant the leading unit monthly selected 30 samples (15 positive, 15 negative) from each center for double-blind review by three senior physicians, where good consistency was defined as a Kappa coefficient ≥ 0.85; Level 3 (Data Traceability) included verifying equipment numbers, personnel numbers, and medical record numbers before data entry, supplementing and correcting missing or conflicting data by contacting the corresponding center, and re-verifying the corrected data through Level 1 Quality Control.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software, and graphs were plotted with GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Descriptive analysis

Continuous variables (e.g., age, BMI) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s). Inter-group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA). If the ANOVA indicated a statistically significant overall difference between groups (P < 0.05), least significant difference (LSD) test was used for pairwise comparisons.

Categorical variables (e.g., prevalence of gallstone disease, gender composition) were presented as count (percentage) [n (%)]. Inter-group comparisons were performed using Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test.

Univariate and multivariate analyses

The variable selection for the regression model followed the dual criteria of “clinical relevance + statistical significance”:

Clinical relevance: Variables included were age, gender (established fundamental risk factors for gallstone disease in previous studies [7, 8, 11, 14], BMI, fatty liver, abnormal blood glucose, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Among these, BMI, fatty liver, abnormal blood glucose, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia are components of metabolic syndrome, and they are directly associated with bile metabolism [15, 16].

Clinical relevance: Variables included were age, gender (established fundamental risk factors for gallstone disease in previous studies [7, 8, 11, 14], BMI, fatty liver, abnormal blood glucose, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Among these, BMI, fatty liver, abnormal blood glucose, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia are components of metabolic syndrome, and they are directly associated with bile metabolism [15, 16].

Univariate analysis: Univariate binary logistic regression analysis was used to screen variables associated with gallstone disease. Variables with P < 0.1 were included in the multivariate analysis.

Univariate analysis: Univariate binary logistic regression analysis was used to screen variables associated with gallstone disease. Variables with P < 0.1 were included in the multivariate analysis.

Multivariate analysis: A multivariate binary logistic regression model was constructed, incorporating gender, age, ethnicity, altitude, BMI, fatty liver, abnormal blood glucose, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia to identify independent risk factors. Further, an interaction term “altitude × ethnicity” was introduced to examine their joint effect. Model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using Nagelkerke R².

Multivariate analysis: A multivariate binary logistic regression model was constructed, incorporating gender, age, ethnicity, altitude, BMI, fatty liver, abnormal blood glucose, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia to identify independent risk factors. Further, an interaction term “altitude × ethnicity” was introduced to examine their joint effect. Model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using Nagelkerke R².

Stratified analysis

Multivariate binary logistic regression models were established separately by ethnicity to verify differences in the association between altitude and gallstone disease across ethnicities.

Collinearity test

Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance were used to test for multicollinearity in the multivariate model. No significant multicollinearity was considered present if VIF < 2 and Tolerance > 0.5.

Multiple comparison correction

For pairwise comparisons of continuous variables (e.g., LSD test for age and BMI across altitude groups): A total of 6 pairwise comparisons were involved (3 altitude groups × 2 continuous variables). To control Type I error, Bonferroni correction was applied, with the adjusted significance level α’ = 0.05/6 ≈ 0.0083.

For multiple comparisons of categorical variables and regression analyses (e.g., prevalence comparison across ethnicity-altitude subgroups, OR value comparison across altitude groups in stratified regression): 9 key comparisons were involved (3 ethnicities × 3 inter-altitude group comparisons). Bonferroni correction was used, with the adjusted significance level α’ = 0.05/9 ≈ 0.0056.

All tests were two-tailed. The unadjusted significance level was set at α = 0.05, and the corresponding adjusted α’ was used as the criterion for statistical significance after correction.

Distribution characteristics of gallstone disease prevalence

Discussion

Gallstone disease shows notable geographic and demographic variability, with high-altitude and multi-ethnic regions having undercharacterized unique epidemiological patterns. This study analyzed health check-up data from 35,930 Qinghai Plateau residents (a typical high-altitude, multi-ethnic area) to explore gallstone disease prevalence and the roles of altitude, ethnicity, and their interaction in disease risk.

Our findings revealed a 13.6% total prevalence of gallstone disease in this population. Notably, cholecystectomy—one of the most common general surgeries in high-altitude areas—accounted for 40.63% of all cases, highlighting the condition’s heavy clinical burden here.

More importantly, altitude and ethnicity were identified as key risk determinants with a significant interactive effect. After adjusting for confounders, the medium-altitude (2500–3499 m) and high-altitude (≥ 3500 m) groups had higher disease risk than the low-altitude reference (1680 m~); Tibetans also had higher risk than Han individuals.

The significant “altitude × ethnicity” interaction indicated ethnicity modulates the altitude-disease association: Han individuals had increasing risk with rising altitude, while Tibetans only had elevated risk at medium altitude (no significant risk at high altitude). These findings underscore the complexity of gallstone disease epidemiology in high-altitude multi-ethnic regions, laying a foundation for exploring mechanisms and guiding targeted prevention.

When benchmarked against global data, the prevalence of gallstone disease in the present study is higher than that reported in low-altitude cohorts from the United States (2.1%), Tunisia (4.1%), the Islamic Republic of Iran (4.7%), Bangladesh (5.4%), Peru (10.7%), and Germany (7.8%) [8, 18–21] though lower than the 20.8% prevalence documented in New Zealand [22].

Altitude Exerts a Significant Impact on Gallstone Disease, with the “Medium-Altitude Risk Peak” Feature and Confounder Adjustment Being of Great Significance

A limited number of studies have suggested a higher prevalence of gallstone disease in high-altitude areas [6, 7, 17]. When benchmarked against global data, the prevalence of gallstone disease in the present study is higher than that reported in low-altitude cohorts from the United States (2.1%), Tunisia (4.1%), the Islamic Republic of Iran (4.7%), Bangladesh (5.4%), Peru (10.7%), and Germany (7.8%) [8, 18–21] though lower than the 20.8% prevalence documented in New Zealand [22].

First, differences in study populations: The subjects of Moro et al.’s study were populations in the Peruvian Andes (predominantly American Indians). In contrast, the population of the present study is mainly composed of Han and Tibetan individuals, and existing studies have confirmed that these two ethnic groups differ in their hypoxic adaptation bases [23, 24]. In contrast, the present study focused on investigating the effects of altitude and ethnicity on the prevalence of gallstone disease. After adjusting for metabolic factors such as gender, age, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and hyperglycemia, altitude was identified as a risk factor for gallstone disease. These adjusted factors are important risk factors for gallstone disease, and failure to adjust for these risk factors in validation may have led Moro et al. to underestimate the independent effect of altitude.

Further analysis in the present study revealed that the prevalence in the high-altitude group (15.5%) was lower than that in the medium-altitude group (20.5%), which does not indicate that altitude has no risk. Instead, this is due to the “protective offset” from the demographic characteristics of the high-altitude group: the proportion of males in the high-altitude group reached 55.1%, which was significantly higher than that in the low-altitude group (48.2%) and medium-altitude group (49.7%); the average age in the high-altitude group (42.3 ± 11.8 years) was significantly lower than that in the low-altitude group (47.6 ± 12.5 years) and medium-altitude group (46.8 ± 12.1 years). The present study has confirmed that males (OR = 0.536, P < 0.001) and younger individuals (OR = 1.410 for every 10-year increase in age, P < 0.001) are protective factors against gallstone disease.

Although bile cholesterol saturation was not directly measured in this study, the epidemiological results—showing significantly higher gallstone prevalence in groups above 2500 m than in the 1680 m group—align well with the predicted effects of the aforementioned mechanisms. Specifically, high-altitude hypoxia activates the HIF-1α pathway, which indirectly increases bile cholesterol saturation and ultimately elevates gallstone risk.

The observed prevalence difference between Tibetans and Han Chinese in our study further supports this mechanism: long-term indigenous populations (e.g., Tibetans) may have developed metabolic adaptations to mitigate hypoxia-induced biliary metabolism disruption, whereas migrant populations (e.g., Han Chinese) with weaker adaptability are more susceptible to hypoxia. The daily diet of nomadic populations in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau is dominated by high-fat, high-protein foods such as butter, yak meat, and dairy products, resulting in high daily fat intake; their dietary fiber intake is low, even lower than that of employees of formal institutions [39, 40]. Meanwhile, due to limitations in cooking methods and remote geographical conditions, their intake of fruits and fresh vegetables is insufficient, leading to a prominent shortage of nutrients such as calcium and vitamin C—deficiencies that may be associated with the risk of gallstone disease.

Conclusion

Based on data from 35,930 multi-ethnic participants, this study confirms the following key findings:

Altitude exerts a significant impact on gallstone disease, presenting a characteristic of “risk peak at medium altitude”;
