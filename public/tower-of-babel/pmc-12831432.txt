Introduction

The strawberry (Fragaria spp.) plant is widely recognized as a suitable fruit crop for cultivation in highland regions, particularly at elevations between 1000 and 1500 meters above sea level [1]. However, identifying specific diseases affecting strawberry plants remains a persistent challenge for farmers, particularly in large-scale or labor-constrained operations [2]. In this context, integrating robotic arms equipped with intelligent vision systems offers a compelling solution for automated plant health monitoring. These systems can be deployed in controlled environments (e.g., greenhouses) and open-field agriculture, enabling consistent, non-invasive inspection of plant foliage with high spatial and temporal precision. In real-world settings, robotic arms provide several advantages: they can operate continuously under varying conditions, minimize human labor and error, and enable early, localized detection of foliar diseases. This facilitates timely intervention, reduces chemical usage, and contributes to higher crop yield, improved quality, and sustainable farming practices. Traditionally, the inspection of strawberry foliar diseases has relied heavily on manual observation by experienced agricultural workers or plant pathologists. This approach typically involves visual assessment of leaf discoloration, spots, mold, or other physical symptoms indicative of disease [3]. While such methods have been widely practiced, they are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and highly subjective. The accuracy of manual inspection often depends on the inspector’s expertise, and it may lead to inconsistent or delayed diagnoses, especially under conditions of large-scale farming or limited access to skilled personnel [4]. In recent years, Machine Learning (ML) techniques have been increasingly explored in agricultural diagnostics as an alternative to manual inspection. These methods typically rely on handcrafted features extracted from leaf images to train predictive models. However, they present several limitations: The manual feature extraction process requires domain expertise and often fails to capture plant disease’s complex and varied patterns. Such models also lack generalization across different environments and are sensitive to variations in lighting, cultivar types, and disease stages. Their reliance on limited datasets and extensive preprocessing can hinder scalability, real-time performance, and overall reliability in practical applications [5]. These challenges have motivated a shift towards more advanced and automated solutions, particularly those based on deep learning architectures that can learn hierarchical features directly from raw image data, eliminating the need for manual feature engineering.

An Effective detection and management of strawberry leaf diseases are critical to minimizing agricultural losses and sustaining the long-term health of strawberry cultivation. Hence, visual feature extraction via deep learning effectively distinguishes various plant diseases [6–9]. For example, Wu et al. [10] modified the ResNet model to classify diseases in strawberries effectively. Additionally, Bera et al. [11] developed the PND-Net by integrating Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), presenting better performance on the discrimination of numerous diseases. However, these approaches have architectural limitations in plant disease analysis. CNNs, with their fixed receptive fields and local filtering, often miss fine-grained or irregular lesion patterns and lose global context. GANs, while useful for data augmentation, may generate unrealistic textures and lack semantic control, which can compromise model reliability. Both architectures generally lack pixel-level localization, limiting their effectiveness in tasks requiring precise spatial interpretation. Moreover, Wang et al. [12] presented MatDet, a model tailor for determining tomato maturity under challenging conditions. Liu et al. [13] optimized YOLO’s multi-scale feature extraction to improve tomato pest and disease assessment. Furthermore, Li et al. [14] leveraged deep transfer learning for pest and disease detection in tea plants. Meanwhile, Liu et al. [3] employed spatiotemporal fusion attention techniques to improve multi-source vegetable disease identification. The detection models, while fast, often struggle with detecting small or overlapping disease regions due to coarse grid-based predictions. Though effective in capturing dynamic patterns, Spatiotemporal attention networks can be computationally intensive and less suited for real-time agricultural deployment.

This study presents an efficient deep learning framework, Strawberry Leaf Disease Inspection (SLDI), for real-time identification of strawberry foliar diseases. The SLDI pipeline integrates a YOLO-NAS object detection backbone with a Transformer-based encoder to combine rapid and precise localization with enhanced contextual understanding of disease patterns. To further improve feature representation, the architecture incorporates Receptive Guided Channel Attention (RGCA), Deep Context Aggregator (DCA), and a novel Multi-Scale Feature Fusion Module (MSFF). These complementary features are fused through dedicated modules (M1, M2, and M3) as illustrated in Fig. 1, ensuring robust detection under challenging conditions such as occlusions, small-scale lesions, and variable environments. The trained SLDI model has been deployed on a robotic arm equipped with an RGB camera, enabling automated, contactless, and real-time inspection of strawberry plants in agricultural settings. This integration of deep learning and robotics provides a scalable, sustainable solution for early disease detection and smart crop management.

Related work

Early and accurate recognition of plant diseases is essential for effective crop management and ensuring global food security. Based on an in-depth literature review, plant disease detection methods can be broadly categorized into CNN-based and transformer-based approaches. A comprehensive analysis of these methods is presented in the following subsections.

CNN-based object detectors

Previous research has thoroughly investigated the agriculture domain analysis of strawberry plant diseases [15–17]. Ramdani and Suyanto [18] utilized CNNs to recognize strawberry plants diseases and compared VGG-16, ResNet-50, and G-Net, with ResNet-50 to evaluate the highest accuracy. Another research study [19] utilized a diverse selection of crop leaves from the PlantVillage dataset and employed CNN architectures involving InceptionV3, InceptionResNetV2, MobileNetV2, and EfficientNetB0 to detect plant diseases. Similarly, [20] proposed a CNN algorithm based on MobileNetV3-Large and EfficientNet-B0 to detect three diseases of the strawberry leaf, where the large network achieved an accuracy of 92.14%. These studies highlight the effectiveness of different CNN architectures in accurately detecting plant diseases, including those specific to strawberries.

In the current studies, researchers explored either directly or fine-tuned CNN architectures to identify plant diseases. For instance. Xiao et al. [21] used and compared ResNet50, VGG16, and GoogLeNet to classify three strawberry diseases, where ResNet50 attained the highest precision of 99.60%. To identify four grape leaf diseases, [22] compared UnitedModel with VGGNet, GoogLeNet, DenseNet, and ResNet. They found that UnitedModel achieved an accuracy of around 99.17%. Khan et al. [58] trained Siamese network using Google street imagery and achieved an average accuracy of 93.2% in health assessment of Eucalyptus trees. Atila et al. [23] applied a pre-trained Inception-V3 model to identify six classes of plant disease, achieving an accuracy of 94.20%. They also created BR-CNN, incorporating a Binary Relevance (BR) multi-label learning algorithm with a lightweight NasNet to detect 10 plant diseases. However, these models are susceptible to over-fitting when applied to small target datasets or when they have many parameters. BR-CNN demonstrated an identification accuracy of 99.17%, surpassing VGGNet by 4.89%.

Despite the excellent accuracies attained with large-scale datasets, deep learning networks trained on a small number of annotated plant leaf disease images frequently perform unsatisfactorily due to the few-shot problem, specific to this domain. Wu et al. [24] introduced a model that combines VGGNet, GoogLeNet, and ResNet to find fine-grained assessment of the severity of bacterial spot diseases in peppers. This approach overcame the limitations of individual CNNs by combining the extracted features, attaining an optimal precision of 95.34%. Wang et al. [25] developed a model incorporating GoogLeNet and DCGAN to identify five common tomato diseases, achieving a precision of 94.33%. Although CNNs have shown remarkable success in detecting plant disease, however, they often grapple with challenges such as architectural complexity, high computational demands, and susceptibility to both under and over-fitting, mainly when dealing with limited data sets or complex disease patterns. However, conventional CNNs suffer from fixed receptive fields that limit multi-scale lesion detection and exhibit over-sensitivity to lighting and scale variations common in field conditions. These drawbacks motivate our inclusion of grouped attention mechanisms to dynamically adapt receptive fields.

Transformer-based object detectors

In precision agriculture, transformer-based networks have emerged as a powerful alternative to Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), effectively leveraging the fusion of RGB, multispectral, and hyper-spectral data to enhance agricultural monitoring tasks. Shen et al. [26] introduced Agricultural Aerial Transformer (AAFormer), a model that employs data fusion and attention mechanisms to enhance the accuracy of crop classification and disease detection, outperforming traditional methods. AAFormer’s scalability and efficiency mark a significant advancement in precision agriculture. Chen et al. [27] demonstrated that a hybrid model, integrating CNNs and Vision Transformers, yields superior accuracy in classifying tomato leaf diseases. Rachman et al. [28] proposed an enhanced Vision Transformer model designed explicitly for plant disease recognition. The model optimizations focus on capturing intricate patterns and features in plant images, increasing accuracy and efficiency. Yu et al. [29] presented a novel approach that combined deep attention-based learning in multiple instances with Vision Transformers. This method enhances crop disease classification by focusing on relevant image regions and capturing complex patterns. Borhani et al. [30] used a deep learning methodology with Vision Transformers to classify plant diseases automatically. By using the algorithm’s ability to identify intricate patterns in visual data, it surpasses the effectiveness of conventional methods in recognizing plant diseases with a high degree of accuracy.

Vision Transformers (ViT) [31] has opened new avenues, overcoming a few limitations. Researchers are increasingly adopting advanced ViT architectures [32, 33] for dense object detection tasks. Jiang et al. [34], for example, enhanced the YOLO model by integrating ViT, creating a transfer-learning framework for detecting cabbage diseases in field conditions. Similarly, Ye et al. [35] developed a tomato leaf disease recognition system by combining multi-scale features with transformer-based attention mechanisms. To improve crop disease detection comprehensively, it’s crucial to integrate local and global feature extraction, leveraging the strengths of CNNs and transformers. Recent efforts have explored hybrid architectures that merge CNNs’ robust local feature capture with transformers’ global contextual understanding. Guo et al. [36, 37] demonstrated how integrating CNNs with transformers can overcome the performance limitations of CNNs alone, without extensive pre-training. Despite these advances, hybrid models have predominantly been explored in various domains involving image classification, detection, and segmentation [38, 39].

Several studies [40, 41] have explored the application of Multi-scale Vision Transformers and Swin Transformers for crop disease recognition. By using their ability to analyze images at multiple scales and capture fine-grained details, these models surpass traditional methods in both accuracy and performance. Ye et al. [35] introduced Tswin-F, a novel Transformer-based tomato leaf disease detection network. The network employs bilateral local attention and self-supervised learning to capture spatial image connections. Furthermore, it includes a Feature-Fuse Local Attention (FFLA) structure to address the problem of increasing attention distances in deep Transformer models. Tswin-F has shown high precision in 10-class and 13-class tomato leaf disease datasets. Li et al. [42] introduced plant-based MobileViT (PMVT), a computationally efficient model for real-time plant disease detection based on MobileViT. PMVT is designed to be lightweight and attain high accuracy, which makes it appropriate for use on resource-constrained mobile devices. In another study by [28], The main goal was to enhance the ViT Base (B) transfer learning model for rice disease identification. The model’s capacity to beat conventional CNN models like VGG, MobileNet, and EfficientNet was proved by the researchers via meticulous reconfiguration, layer augmentation, and hyperparameter optimization, especially when handling unbalanced datasets. Yu et al. [43] proposed a novel transformer block designed for the recognition of fine-grained plant disease. This block combines transformer architecture for global context modeling, soft split token embedding for capturing local details, and inception architecture with cross-channel feature learning to enrich feature representation. The model achieved state-of-the-art accuracy on multiple plant disease datasets. The downside of using only transformer detectors is their high computational cost and latency. They also frequently perform poorly when localizing small objects, a result of their global self-attention handling features at too coarse a level.

To address this, hybrid CNN-transformer approaches, such as RGCA+DCA, provide a good trade-off. Such methods successfully merge local feature sensitivity with global context, incurring minimal overhead.

Comparative analysis

CNNs and ViTs address plant-health imagery from opposite ends of the modeling spectrum, each with well-documented trade-offs. CNNs exploit local convolutions and translation-equivariant pooling, making them parameter-efficient and effective on modest, class-imbalanced agricultural datasets. These same inductive biases, however, keep the effective receptive field largely local; capturing long-range context or lesions that span multiple scales typically requires auxiliary structures such as feature-pyramid networks or atrous spatial-pyramid pooling. Pooling layers further introduce a loss of spatial resolution, which can blur fine lesion boundaries. ViTs, by contrast, replace convolutions with self-attention over non-overlapping image patches, allowing global context aggregation from the first layer. This comes at a cost of self-attention scales quadratically with the number of tokens, inflating memory and compute demands, while the absence of strong locality biases means ViTs rely on substantial pre-training or large, diverse datasets to learn spatial regularities. Positional encoding must also be tuned carefully poor choices can degrade fine spatial detail. Finally, although ViTs can model multi-scale cues implicitly, practical deployments often add pyramid or windowed attention modules to curb computation. Overview

The proposed system combines channel guided attention and contextual aggregator based feature extraction to get optimal features. Later these mature descriptors is forward to YOLO-NAS to perform the training and test in real-time agricultural farming using robotic arm. Our features extracted modules are deviates from the standard channel attention and transformer network due to the inherent positional awareness embedded within the deep features through convolution and pooling operations. The process begins with normalizing the position-aware embedded patches generated from the channel output. Then, inside a high-dimensional space (, multi-head attention sub-layers project queries and keys into various sub-spaces (). It allows the attention function to be executed in parallel over many sub-spaces, collecting a variety of feature correlations while avoiding over-fitting. The resulting attention features from each subspace are then merged to provide a richer representation. Subsequently, non-linear transformations are introduced by a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) sub-layer. Two linear connections with ReLU activation functions make up this sub-layer, which efficiently processes the attention’s subspace properties and improves the expressiveness of the model. Layer normalization and residual connections are features of both the MLP and the multi-head attention sub-layers. By stabilizing intermediate activations, layer normalization helps save over-fitting and training time. Residual connections, on the other hand, facilitate gradient flow in deeper networks, alleviating the vanishing gradient problem. This refined feature representation, obtained through the Transformer encoder, forms a strong foundation for subsequent tasks like feature fusion and detection within the overall architecture. Multi-scale feature fusion (MSFF) ()

The feature extraction network produces the disease characteristics, which then extracts them from processed pictures and displays them as various feature maps (abbreviated as ). Generally, the shallow feature map produced during the first stage of the feature extraction network has less impact on target categorization and provides little semantic information about the target. However, the broader receptive area represented by the feature values of individual pixels in the output feature map makes it more difficult to discern small objects in the deep feature map as the network layers become deeper.

To address this issue, the MSFF approach adds cross-stage branches to the MSFF and PAN propagation channels. High-level semantic feature maps are built by FPN in different sizes using a top-down architecture and lateral connections. Due to the long travel from low-level feature maps to high-level feature maps in FPN caused by the CNN’s numerous layers, target contour edges and other low-level characteristics are largely lost in the high-level feature map. PAN raises FPN by using a bottom-up strategy to mitigate this.

Our approach effectively integrates feature maps from different stages using the routes generated by FPN and PAN, improving feature expressiveness for tiny objects such as strawberry leaf diseases. PAN uses a hierarchical fusion mechanism in its bottom-up approach. N3 is created by downsampling the low-level feature map P2, combining it with P3, and then starting again.

This process is repeated by down-sampling N3 and fusing it with to generate N4 and incorporating P2 into the creation of N4, the resulting feature map benefits from enhanced low-level information. Similarly, P3 is directly involved in the generation of N5, ensuring that low-level features are preserved and strengthened throughout the fusion process, as mentioned in Fig. 3.

Multi-scale features are fused using MSFF. FPN and PAN create two feature enhancement paths, forming a multi-branch structure to improve feature representation further

First, in the MSFF, the feature map from the higher-level route is scaled using bi-linear interpolation to fit into the lower-level path dimensions of each cross-stage branch. Then, we repeatedly merge components of the scaled feature map with those of the lower-level route feature map. A single feature map with high-level and fine-grained features is produced because the information from several phases is integrated. A convolutional layer is appended at the end further to enhance the representational capacity of the fused feature map.

Strawberry leaf disease inspection (SLDI) ()

The SLDI algorithm uses YOLO-NAS detection layers [44] for bounding box regression and category prediction. To predict the disease categories within each likely bounding box, it combines feature information and anchor settings with multi-scale feature maps from the MSFF stage. A multi-label classification procedure is then used, with the sigmoid function producing confidence scores for each prediction. Boxes with confidence ratings that surpass a predetermined threshold are further refined using non-maximum suppression. Lastly, the identified object boxes and disease classes are shown.

Performance metrics

The Mean Average Precision (mAP) statistic is often used to evaluate object-detecting algorithms. This measure is computed by comparing predicted bounding boxes to their corresponding ground truth bounding boxes. The Intersection Over Union (IoU) is an important component of this comparison since it evaluates the overlap between the anticipated bounding box and the ground truth.

Precision is the proportion of true positives out of all the positive predictions made by a model. It measures how accurate the model’s positive predictions are.

Recall is the proportion of true positive samples that the model correctly identified as positive out of all the true positive samples. In other words, recall measures how well the model is able to find or detect the positive samples. A higher recall value indicates the model is able to correctly identify a larger proportion of the actual positive cases.

The F1-score combines these two factors into a single value that indicates how well the model can accurately predict outcomes. An improved ability to balance accurately detecting instances that are positive with reducing false positives is shown by a higher F1 score for the model. To summarise, the F1-score evaluates a classification model’s overall efficacy by considering its precision (accuracy in producing positive predictions) and recall (ability to identify positive occurrences). It is a useful metric for evaluating how well a model can predict different classes.

Discussion

Crop diseases are serious risks to agriculture due to their detrimental impacts and rapid spread, resulting in large losses in productivity and quality. Modern technologies are needed to address these issues and provide quick and reliable identification, which is a critical responsibility in agriculture. Prompt detection and identification enable farmers to take appropriate action, reducing losses and maximizing resource utilization.

Our proposed SLDI method utilized in this study significantly increases real-time disease diagnosis. Our suggested model surpasses current cutting-edge algorithms by establishing a remarkable balance between detection accuracy and speed. However, given the diversity of disease species and the apparent resemblance in colour and texture of certain leaf diseases, there is room to increase overall detection accuracy.

The proposed strawberry leaf disease inspection model is integrated into a robotic system for real-time disease inspection. The experiments are conducted in a controlled lab environment with infected leaves of strawberry plants. The robotic system, equipped with the detection model, demonstrates high efficiency in detecting strawberry diseases in real-time. However, the study has some limitations. The performance of the model has only been evaluated under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully represent real-world field environments. Variations in lighting, background noise, leaf orientation, and natural disease progression in outdoor settings could affect detection accuracy. Additionally, the dataset used may not cover all possible disease types or stages, potentially limiting the model’s generalizability to unseen cases.

Further more, Our long-term research objective is to implement the SLDI on IoT devices as well to provide ongoing disease monitoring in greenhouses. For example, SLDI can work with an integrated IoT based system to let farmers know when the disease detection reaches a certain level so they can take preventive measures to reduce crop losses. Notably, the number of model parameters is strictly limited when the model is integrated into hardware devices. To preserve the model’s detection performance, we concentrate on parameter optimization in our upcoming research phase. In agricultural contexts, striking this balance is essential for practical implementation, especially regarding enhancing monitoring accuracy.

Conclusion

This study presents SLDI, a novel disease identification method that consists of two parallel encoders: Receptive Guided Channel Attention (RGCA) and a Deep Context Aggregator (DCA). The RGCA enhances the representation of spatial features by focusing on channel-wise attention, while the DCA captures long-range dependencies and contextual information within the image. These complementary feature sets are then fused using a dedicated Feature Fusion Network. The fused features are subsequently processed by a prediction head to perform the final output. The evaluations carried out on the PlantVillage and PlantDoc datasets confirm the ability of SLDI to outperform existing methods, achieving a precision of 91.10%, a recall of 88.50%, and a real-time frame rate of 76.50 fps.

To facilitate the transition from research to practical application, SLDI has been implemented on a robot-based system for real-time disease detection. Preliminary tests within a lab controlled environment have demonstrated promising outcomes. Future research endeavors will concentrate on optimizing the system for deployment in greenhouse environments, incorporating additional disease datasets of other crops, and investigating advanced robotic manipulation techniques to further enhance disease management practices. The successful implementation of SLDI has the potential to significantly increase disease control strategies and contribute to the advancement of sustainable agriculture.
