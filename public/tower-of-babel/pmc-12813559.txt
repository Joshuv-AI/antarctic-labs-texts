A Case of a Right Gastric Artery Aneurysm Caused by the Collateral Flow Changes Due to the Coexistence of Median Arcuate Ligament Syndrome and a Celiac Artery Anomaly
Authors: Yamato Tsunoda, Hirotatsu Sato, Tetsuo Sonomura, Yuichi Miyake, Naoki Nishioka, Fumihiro Tanaka, Kodai Fukuda, Ryuta Okuhira, Nobuyuki Higashino, Atsufumi Kamisako, Akira Ikoma, Hiroki Minamiguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A man in his 60s presented with severe epigastric pain. Abdominal computed tomography revealed an aneurysm of the right gastric artery, which branched from an aberrant gastroduodenal artery originating from the axis of the splenic artery. Other vascular anomalies included a left gastric artery arising directly from the aorta and several additional arterial variations. Computed tomography also revealed stenosis of the axes of the celiac artery and left gastric artery. The aneurysm was thought to be caused by the stenosis due to median arcuate ligament syndrome. Transcatheter arterial embolization was successfully performed. This case highlights that median arcuate ligament syndrome combined with vascular anomalies can alter hemodynamics and lead to the development of uncommon gastric artery aneurysms.

Introduction

Median arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS) is a condition in which the median arcuate ligament and both the right and left diaphragmatic crura compress the celiac artery (CA) and the celiac plexus. Symptoms include chronic postprandial abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and unintentional weight loss [1]. Additionally, stenosis of the CA can result in aneurysms. MALS-related visceral artery aneurysms are most frequently reported in the pancreaticoduodenal arcades (PDAs), whereas occurrence in the right gastric artery (RGA) is rare [2, 3]. We treated a case of a ruptured RGA aneurysm associated with MALS. An anomalous left gastric artery (LGA) branching from the aorta was considered to contribute to the occurrence of the aneurysm. To our knowledge, this is the first case of an RGA aneurysm caused by the coexistence of MALS and this vascular anomaly. This case aims to raise awareness that in patients with MALS combined with vascular anomalies, hemodynamic changes can lead to aneurysm formation in unusual sites, such as the RGA.

Case Report

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and the accompanying images. The patient was a male in his 60s who presented with severe epigastric pain and visited another hospital 3 days after the onset of symptoms. He had a history of chronic hepatitis B, a submucosal gastric tumor, and ulcerative colitis, which was in remission. Abdominal CT revealed a hematoma around the gastric antrum, irregular dilation of the RGA within the hematoma, and hemorrhagic ascites. He was admitted to the original hospital and treated conservatively. Although his symptoms remained stable, abdominal CT performed on day 9 of hospitalization revealed an aneurysm of the RGA (Figure 1).

He was then transferred to our hospital for TAE as definitive treatment was considered necessary. A 4 Fr sheath (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted into the left femoral artery and a 4 Fr pigtail catheter (Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) was used to perform CT during aortography to obtain thinner and clearer images. CT revealed the aneurysm of the RGA, which branched from the aberrant gastroduodenal artery (GDA) originating from the axis of the splenic artery. Other anomalies included a LGA arising directly from the aorta, an absent common hepatic artery (CHA), a left hepatic artery arising from the LGA, and a right hepatic artery arising from the SMA (Figure 2a). CT also revealed stenosis of the axes of the CA and LGA (Figure 2b‒e). The stenosis was more severe in the LGA, which was located more cranially, and exhibited the hook sign, which is characteristic of MALS [4]. Both stenoses were considered to be attributable to compression by the median arcuate ligament.

Angiography of the CA using a 4 Fr 1CJ catheter (Medikit) confirmed the aneurysm of the RGA (Figure 3). Using two microcatheters (Carnelian HF 2.7 Fr/2.9 Fr and Carnelian Marvel S 1.6 Fr/1.8 Fr; Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) via the GDA as a tri-axial catheter system, we performed TAE using the isolation technique with 11 microcoils (Target Tetra; Stryker Neurovascular, CA, USA). Because the aneurysm was considered to be a pseudoaneurysm, the isolation technique was selected. Postprocedural angiography from the CA and LGA showed the aneurysm had disappeared (Figure 4). There were no complications during or after the procedure. The patient was discharged 3 days after TAE. Given the patient's preference, surgical decompression of the median arcuate ligament is not planned, and the patient will be followed with imaging to monitor for recurrence or new aneurysm formation.

Discussion

In patients with MALS, aneurysms are commonly observed around the PDA. These aneurysms develop due to increased blood flow through the PDA from the SMA, caused by stenosis of the CA [5].

Gastric artery aneurysms (GAAs) are rare, accounting for only 4% (including gastroepiploic artery aneurysms) of all visceral artery aneurysms (VAAs) [6]. GAAs are rare in patients with MALS; we are aware of only one reported case [7]. Normally, the LGA branches from the CA and the RGA branches from a branch of the CA such as the proper hepatic artery. Therefore, stenosis of the CA does not cause an increase in blood flow through the gastric arteries. In our case, however, the LGA and CA were stenotic, with more severe stenosis of the LGA. Therefore, this case indicates the blood flow increased through the RGA from the CA and led to the formation of the aneurysm. The report we mentioned earlier is one that, similar to our case, suggests that an RGA aneurysm can develop as a result of hemodynamic changes [7]. In that case, reduced flow in the LGA due to MALS led to increased flow through the RGA, which arose from the CHA, which in turn arose from the SMA. These findings reinforce the concept that MALS can alter hemodynamics and redirect blood flow to collateral arteries, potentially resulting in aneurysm formation in unusual sites beyond the PDA.

According to the Adachi classification, the absence of the CHA, a left hepatic artery branching from the LGA, a right hepatic artery branching from the SMA, and an RGA branching from the aberrant GDA originating from the axis of the splenic artery corresponds to type VI group 28, which accounts for 0.4%. Additionally, an LGA branching directly from the aorta corresponds to type II groups 12‒17, which accounts for 6.4% [8]. Our case exhibited rare type VI group 28 elements and relatively rare type II elements, making precise classification difficult. Such anomalies were not identified in a study of 876 patients lacking a CHA, suggesting that the features of our case are extremely rare, and this is the first reported case to the best of our knowledge [9]. However, the formation of GAAs does not require such rare anomalies. An LGA branching directly from the aorta (a type II element) and a hepatomesenteric variant contributed to the formation of the RGA aneurysms in our case and the case we mentioned earlier, respectively [7].

The causes of VAAs, including GAAs, include atherosclerosis, inflammation, connective tissue diseases such as Marfan syndrome and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, collagen diseases, fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD), and segmental arterial mediolysis (SAM) [10, 11]. However, these causes are unlikely in our case. Although atherosclerotic findings were present, they were mild. There were no findings suggestive of inflammation. Additionally, there was no family history or other findings (such as tall stature or skin fragility) associated with connective tissue disease and no other symptoms indicative of collagen disease. The patient's biological sex and age were not typical for FMD, and there were no renal artery lesions associated with FMD. Although SAM is possible, given its presentation, which includes solitary or multiple VAAs and dissecting hematomas in middle-aged and older adults, it is rare [11]. Because aneurysms were reported in 47.6% of patients with MALS, it is more reasonable to consider that the aneurysm in our case was associated with MALS rather than with rare diseases such as SAM and the coincidental coexistence of MALS [3].

We treated a case of a ruptured RGA aneurysm caused by the coexistence of MALS and rare vascular anomalies. This case indicates that MALS coexisting with vascular anomalies could lead to uncommon VAAs, highlighting the need for clinicians to consider the possibility of aneurysm formation in unusual sites beyond the PDA in patients with MALS.
