Factors associated with idle peripheral intravenous catheters: a point-prevalence study using the Donabedian model
Authors: Poonam Joshi, Rashmita Devi, Madhu Madhu, Sharmila Jayarani J, Soniya C, Surya Kant Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to determine the point prevalence of idle peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) and identify associated factors using the Donabedian framework (structure, process, outcome) in a tertiary care setting.ResultsAmong patients with PIVC (n = 118), 35 (29.7%) had an idle catheter. Documentation gaps were evident: only 67.8% had a clear indication for continued use, and only 22.9% had a documented removal plan. Precautionary “just-in-case” placement (28.0% of PIVCs) was strongly associated with idle status (adjusted OR 7.76, 95% CI 2.02–29.78, p = 0.003). Conversely, having a documented indication for continued use was associated with lower odds of idleness (adjusted OR 0.037, 95% CI 0.010–0.136, p < 0.001). These findings highlight critical and modifiable process failures within the Donabedian framework. While the cross-sectional design precludes causal conclusions, these associations suggest that interventions should focus on enforcing documentation protocols, reducing precautionary insertions, and implementing standardized removal pathways to enhance patient safety and resource utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07821-7.Keywords: Idle peripheral intravenous catheters, Point-prevalence study, Donabedian model, Documentation practices, Catheter management, Healthcare quality, Resource utilization

Introduction

Peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) are among the most frequently used medical devices in hospitalized patients worldwide. PIVCs are short, 2–6 cm catheters inserted into superficial veins of the hand or arm for short-term intravenous therapy, whereas peripherally inserted central catheters are longer catheters threaded into central veins for extended use [1, 2]. This study focuses on PIVCs, which have different indications, protocols, and complications than central lines.

An idle PIVC, defined as an intravenous catheter that remains in situ without active use for medication administration or fluid infusion for 24 h or more, represents a significant clinical and operational concern [3]. Although often essential for treatment, idle catheters are associated with unnecessary patient discomfort, phlebitis, infiltration, and an increased risk of healthcare-associated bloodstream infections, potentially leading to prolonged hospitalization and increased costs [4].

The practice of inserting PIVCs “just-in-case” is well documented, particularly in acute and emergency settings [5, 6]. However, the persistence of idle catheters in inpatient wards highlights a critical gap between evidence-based standards and clinical practice. For instance, the Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice recommend the prompt removal of PIVCs when they are no longer medically necessary [7]. However, studies have reported that 30–50% of PIVCs remain unused in various clinical environments, indicating a systemic issue that requires structured analysis [8, 9].

To systematically identify the underlying causes of this discrepancy, the present study employed Donabedian’s model of healthcare quality, which examines care through the interrelated lenses of structure, process, and outcome [10]. This framework is particularly suited to quality improvement research as it connects foundational hospital systems and resources (structure) with the actions and decisions of healthcare providers (process) to determine the resulting clinical and efficiency outcomes (outcome). While the prevalence of idle PIVCs has been described, there remains a paucity of studies applying a holistic framework like Donabedian’s, to identify the specific, modifiable structural and process factors that drive this outcome in defined clinical settings.

Against this backdrop, we conducted a point-prevalence study in a tertiary care facility with two primary objectives: First, to determine the prevalence of idle PIVCs among patients admitted to the medical and surgical units. Second, to investigate current documentation and monitoring practices related to PIVC use, thereby identifying targets for improvement guided by the Donabedian model. We hypothesized that a significant proportion of PIVCs in our setting would be idle and that modifiable process factors, specifically inadequate documentation and discretionary “just-in-case” placement, would be associated with the idle status.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

This single-center, cross-sectional point-prevalence study was conducted over a 24-hour period in September 2024 across the medical and surgical wards of a tertiary care facility. The study setting included wards for internal medicine, general surgery, gynecology, pediatrics, orthopedics, and super-specialty units (cardiology, neurology, and neurosurgery intensive care units). All patients present in the selected wards during the 24-hour study period were screened for eligibility. Patients were included if they provided written informed consent for the inspection of their IV catheter site(s). Patients who were temporarily absent from the ward for diagnostic or therapeutic procedures at the time of data collection were excluded.

Study size

The sample size was calculated based on a reported international point prevalence of idle PIVCs of 16% [11]. For a 95% confidence level and 5% absolute precision, the minimum required sample size was 211. Accounting for a 20% potential non-response rate, the final target sample size was 253. Ultimately, 256 patients were enrolled in the study.

Variables

Data were collected on patient demographics (age and sex), clinical characteristics (primary diagnosis, department, and duration of stay), and PIVC-specific details. PIVC-specific variables included the date and site of insertion, documented purpose, site condition (e.g., signs of inflammation, dressing integrity), and documentation of the indication for continued use and plans for removal.

The primary outcome was the status of the PIVC, classified as idle or in use. For this study, an idle PIVC was operationally defined as a peripheral intravenous catheter through which no intravenous fluid, medication, or blood product had been administered and which was not being used for continuous monitoring in the preceding 24 h, as verified through medical records and nurse verification.

All variables were prospectively mapped to the Donabedian framework for analysis based on consensus among the research teams. Structural factors included ward characteristics and institutional context (e.g., admitting facilities and place of insertion). Process factors encompassed clinician actions and documentation practices (e.g., documentation of indication, review of necessity within 24 h, “just-in-case” placement, and patient communication). The outcome was idle PIVC status, conceptualized as a process-sensitive quality indicator that reflects the quality of preceding structures and processes. While not a direct patient-centered outcome (such as infection or mortality), idle PIVCs are associated with patient discomfort, resource waste, and increased risk of downstream complications, making them a clinically relevant target for quality improvement. Data sources/measurement

Data were collected via two primary methods: (1) direct bedside observation of the catheter and patient interview, and (2) review of the corresponding medical and nursing records. A structured data collection form was developed using the Google Forms platform to ensure standardized data entry. The content validity of the tool was assessed by a panel of five experts, comprising three senior infection control nurses and two consultant physicians, yielding a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 0.92. The form was pilot-tested on ten patients, who were not included in the main study, to ensure its clarity. The complete observation checklist is provided as Supplementary Material 1.

To minimize observer bias and ensure a true point-prevalence snapshot, a team of five trained researchers collected data simultaneously across all study wards during a coordinated 4-hour block on the study day.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC/AIIMSKALYANI/2024/081). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, and all data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality of the participants.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25.0). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, or as median and interquartile range (IQR) if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %).

Variable selection for multivariable modeling proceeded as follows: First, all candidate predictors from the data collection form were examined using univariable logistic regression. Variables with a univariable association of p < 0.20 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model (n = 6). To avoid overfitting given the number of outcome events (n = 35), we applied the “10 events per variable” rule as a guiding principle, limiting the final model to four predictors. The final set was selected based on clinical relevance, framework alignment (prioritizing modifiable process factors), and examination of correlation matrices to avoid collinearity (all pairwise correlations < 0.5). The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated for each predictor in the final model; all VIF values were < 2.0, indicating no multicollinearity.

The enter method was used for multivariate analysis, wherein all selected variables were entered into the model simultaneously. The results of the final multivariable model are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The overall goodness of fit of the final model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Statistical significance for the final multivariable model was set at a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Results

Participant and PIVC characteristics

Among the 256 surveyed patients, 118 (46.1%) had a peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) and comprised the analytic cohort. Patients with PIVC had a mean age of 43.61 ± 19.85 years and a median hospital stay of 5 days (IQR 3–10). Most patients were admitted for medical (54.2%) or surgical (42.4%) management. Catheters were predominantly inserted in wards (95.8%) by nursing officers, most commonly in the dorsum of the hand (46.6%), and were most frequently 20-gauge (44.1%). Overall, 35 of the 118 PIVCs (29.7%) were idle (Table 1).

Healthcare professionals’ observations and process factors

The necessity of catheterization was reviewed within 24 h in 66.9% of the cases. A precautionary “just-in-case” rationale was documented for 28.0% of catheters. Clinical signs of local inflammation, such as redness, swelling, or tenderness, were present in 8.5% of cases, whereas patient-reported discomfort was noted in 22.0%. Documentation practices revealed significant gaps: a clear indication for continued catheter use was documented in 67.8% of cases, but only 22.9% of patients had a documented plan for catheter removal if it became unnecessary (Table 2).

Factors associated with idle catheter status

The results of the logistic regression analysis identifying factors associated with idle PIVC status are presented in Table 3. The final model demonstrated an adequate fit (Hosmer-Lemeshow χ²= 0.71, p = 0.871). Precautionary “just-in-case” catheter placement was associated with significantly higher odds of catheter idleness (aOR 7.76; 95% CI 2.02–29.78; p = 0.003). Conversely, having a documented indication for continued IV catheterization was associated with lower odds of idleness (aOR 0.037; 95% CI 0.010–0.136; p < 0.001). Informing the patient about the catheter’s purpose or status was included in the model but was not statistically significant (aOR 0.331; 95% CI 0.054–2.039; p = 0.233). Discussion

This study employed the Donabedian model to systematically identify factors contributing to the presence of idle PIVCs in a tertiary care setting. The prevalence of idle PIVCs was 29.7%, which is substantially higher than the 16% reported in a large international study by Alexandrou et al. (2015) [12], but aligns closely with rates found in other single-center analyses and emergency department settings [9]. Replication of these findings nearly a decade after Alexandrou’s international study demonstrates that this problem persists and warrants continued attention. More importantly, our model-based analysis identified key modifiable factors associated with an idle status, offering a targeted roadmap for interventions.

Structural factors: the foundation for practice

Our findings highlight the critical structural gaps that enable the persistence of idle PIVCs. The most prominent factor was the absence of enforced standardized protocols for documentation and removal planning. Our results showed that approximately two-thirds of PIVCs had a documented indication for continued use and less than one-fourth had a documented removal plan. This lack of systematic guidance represents a structural failure that directly contravenes the Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice [13]. This finding concurs with several studies that identified significant documentation gaps in diverse settings, suggesting that this is a widespread structural issue rather than one that is limited to specific patient populations [14, 15]. While we mapped variables to the Donabedian framework, other structural elements, such as staffing patterns, training availability, and the design of the electronic health record, specifically whether it includes mandatory fields for indication and removal planning, likely influenced both process measures and outcomes and warrants future investigation.

Process factors: the actions and decisions of clinicians

Our analysis identified two powerful opposing process factors. The association between “just-in-case” placement and idle PIVCs reveals a persistent clinical culture that prioritizes potential future access over current evidence-based practice. This practice is likely driven by workflow pressures, fear of difficult venous access, and a defensive mindset.

Conversely, the association between documented indications for use and reduced likelihood of idleness represents a highly actionable finding. This suggests that institutions with poor documentation practices are likely to have high idle rates, making documentation a useful screening indicator, even if it is not the direct causal agent. Requiring documentation of indication likely exerts its effect not through the act of writing but by forcing a cognitive reassessment of necessity and creating an audit trail that enables accountability. In this sense, documentation serves as both a marker of the underlying care processes and a potential lever for change. The suboptimal rates of necessity review and patient communication about catheter status further illustrate process-level inconsistencies that contribute to the prolonged catheter retention.

An important finding with direct implications for intervention design is that the majority of the PIVCs were inserted by nurses in our sample. This suggests that nurse-driven protocols for daily review and prompt removal could be particularly effective, as the same professional group responsible for insertion is empowered to remove the unnecessary catheters. Several studies have demonstrated that empowering nurses to remove idle PIVCs without requiring physician orders significantly reduces idle rates and associated complications [16–18].

An important consideration in the idle PIVC debate is the trade-off between the risks of maintaining an unused catheter versus the risks and patient burden of repeated insertions. While each PIVC insertion causes patient discomfort and anxiety, consumes staff time, and carries its own risks of insertion failure or early complications, cumulative evidence suggests that the prompt removal of unnecessary catheters remains the safest strategy [19, 20]. An integrative review demonstrated that the risk of phlebitis increases with the duration of catheter dwell time [21]. Furthermore, another study emphasized that the risk of infection is primarily a function of the ongoing presence of the catheter rather than insertion events [22]. Moreover, from a patient experience perspective, retaining a catheter that restricts movement or causes persistent discomfort without a clear therapeutic benefit is difficult to justify.

Outcomes: prevalence and complications

The primary negative outcome measured was the idle PIVC itself, representing a direct resource waste and an avoidable patient risk [23]. We acknowledge that idle PIVC status is an intermediate outcome rather than a direct measure of patient harm. However, given the established links between prolonged catheter dwell time and complications, including phlebitis and bloodstream infection, reducing idle catheter rates is a reasonable proxy for reducing these clinically meaningful end points [24, 25]. We observed a relatively low incidence of local complications, such as phlebitis and infiltration, which was lower than that reported in a previous study [26] and comparable to the findings of a recent study [27]. It is possible that our point-prevalence design underestimated the complication rates that developed over time or that the high idle rate preceded a future increase in complications, a relationship that a longitudinal study could clarify.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. Given the cross-sectional design, the associations identified should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as causal. The temporal relationship between documentation practices and catheter idleness cannot be definitively established, and unmeasured confounding factors may influence the observed associations. Interventional studies are needed to determine whether modifying these factors reduces idle PIVC rates. Although the sample size was adequate for the primary objective, it limited more complex subgroup analyses and raised the possibility of overfitting the multivariable model. Conducting the study at a single center may affect generalizability; however, the diagnostic approach using the Donabedian model is widely applicable. Social desirability bias may have influenced the documentation or responses during the observation. We did not collect data on which arm the PIVC was inserted or patient hand dominance, factors that may influence both patient discomfort and the functional impact of idle catheters. Catheters placed in the dominant arm may interfere with activities of daily living to a greater extent, potentially amplifying the negative patient experience associated with the unnecessary catheter retention. Future research should incorporate these variables to better characterize the full burden of the use of idle devices. We did not assess healthcare professionals’ knowledge or attitudes, which are important structural and process-related areas for future research. Furthermore, while documentation quality is associated with idle rates, unmeasured structural factors (e.g., staffing, electronic health record design, availability of written protocols) likely influence both documentation practices and idle rates, and our findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this point-prevalence study, guided by the Donabedian model, revealed that idle PIVCs are a prevalent and modifiable issue in our tertiary care setting. The analysis identified a critical interconnection between structural gaps and process failures that drove this outcome. Specifically, the absence of enforced protocols (structure) enables the high-risk practice of precautionary “just-in-case” catheter placement and leads to inadequate documentation, both of which are strongly associated with idle PIVC status. Conversely, the simple process of documenting a clear indication of use was associated with markedly lower odds of idleness. While causal conclusions require interventional studies, these findings identify specific measurable targets for quality improvement initiatives, including nurse-driven removal protocols, documentation mandates, and reduction of precautionary insertions.
