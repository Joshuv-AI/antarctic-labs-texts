Mendelian randomization study on the association of circulating ketone bodies with lung cancer and respiratory diseases
Authors: Xisha Tang, Huijia Zhuang, Hai Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The liver produces various ketone bodies (KBs) including 3-Hydroxybutyrate (3-OHB), acetoacetate (AcAc), and acetone, with 3-OHB being the major component. Previous studies have shown that KBs protect against respiratory diseases; however, there is no evidence of a genetic link. To avoid biases existing in traditional observational studies, a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was carried out to investigate genetic causation and novel therapeutic uses for KBs. This study used databases from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and single nucleotide polymorphisms as instrumental variables for KBs from a recently published metabonomics study (n = 121,584) and respiratory diseases [lung cancer, n = 85,716; asthma, n = 127,669; chronic bronchitis, n = 450,422; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), n = 468,475; FEV1/FVC < 0.7, n = 353,315] from their publicly available GWAS, respectively. Strong sets of instrumental variables (P < 5 × 10− 8) were selected, with inverse-variance weighted as the primary MR method. Sensitivity analyses included Cochran’s Q test, MR Egger, MR-PRESSO, leave-one-out test, and funnel plots. The Steiger test and reversed MR were used to exclude reverse causality. Additionally, independent replication MR studies were conducted using databases from another large public GWAS and similar methods as described above. After MR analyses and sensitivity filtering, we discovered a protective effect of 3-OHB on lung cancer (odds ratio [OR] = 0.771; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.648–0.916; PFDR=0.006), small cell carcinoma (OR = 0.485, 95% CI = 0.301–0.781, PFDR=0.006), asthma (OR = 0.585, 95% CI = 0.395–0.867, PFDR=0.010), chronic bronchitis (OR = 0.753, 95% CI = 0.570–0.994, PFDR=0.045), COPD (OR = 0.690, 95% CI = 0.535–0.890, PFDR=0.008) and lung function (OR = 0.970, 95%CI = 0.950–0.990, PFDR =0.008). In summary, our findings suggest that 3-OHB acts as a protective factor against lung cancer and respiratory diseases. However, heterogeneity implies that other mechanisms may also be involved in COPD improvement by 3-OHB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81591-9.

Introduction

Lung cancer and chronic respiratory diseases have significantly increased the economic burden on populations globally and present serious threats to quality of life. Chronic lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and asthma are common conditions influenced by various genes. They are the 3rd leading cause of global mortality, accounting for 7% of deaths globally in 20171; specifically, COPD was the 6th largest cause of mortality in the United States in 20202. COPD is a condition characterized by chronic airflow obstruction caused by chronic bronchitis and emphysema3. Relative to healthy lungs, forced expiratory volume in one second after using bronchodilators /forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) ratio<0.7 indicates airflow obstruction, which is an important diagnostic criterion of COPD4. Besides chronic lung diseases, lung cancer is another significant threat among respiratory disorders, constituting a primary cause of cancer-related deaths globally that affect individuals of both sexes. Lung cancer manifests in two primary forms, i.e., non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (80–85%) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) (10–15%). NSCLC further comprises subtypes including squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, and lung large cell carcinoma5. Previous studies have established a comprehensive framework for cancer therapies, which encompass surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and pharmacological hormone therapy6. As cancer is fundamentally a genetic disease driven by oncogenic mutations, treatments that target these altered genes and their associated signaling pathways will continue to be crucial. However, to effectively tackle the complexities of cancer, a diverse range of innovative approaches will likely be necessary in the near future.

Notably, numerous studies have identified systemic inflammation and metabolic reprogramming as shared characteristics of lung cancer and respiratory diseases7–9. The modulation of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation observed in asthma and COPD supports the notion of targeting metabolic pathways as a therapeutic strategy for respiratory conditions. In addition, the downregulation of glycolysis has shown beneficial effects in animal models of various chronic respiratory diseases10. Energy metabolism reprogramming, which supports rapid cell growth and proliferation through metabolic adjustments, is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer. A growing body of evidence indicates that tumor metabolism is essential not only for carcinogenesis and cellular survival but also for influencing immune responses through the production of metabolic byproducts such as acetate, lactate, PGE2, and arginine11. Ketone bodies (KBs), which are derived from lipids and synthesized by the liver through the beta-oxidation of free fatty acids, are endogenous substrates that provide a clean source of energy. They rank among the most significant metabolic substrates in the body. The synthesis of KBs is used as a marker for the rate of beta-oxidation. Ketone bodies have long been considered pathogenic markers and mediators due to their relationship with diabetic ketoacidosis. Nonetheless, important exceptions include their capacity to act as antiepileptic agents in drug-resistant epilepsy, and their usage by the brain as an alternative fuel source when the body is starving or fasting12,13. Therefore, KBs have exponentially increased in the identified molecular targets and treatment applications over the past decade. KBs are composed of 3-hydroxybutyrate (3-OHB), acetoacetate (AcAc), and acetone. Among them, 3-OHB is the most abundant stable, and significant KB that circulates in the human body12. The ketogenic diet (KD), exogenous ketone esters, intermittent fasting, and exercise are all effective methods that increase endogenous ketone levels. KD has been discovered to have therapeutic potential in various diseases, including obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases14,15, neurodegenerative diseases16, cancer, and more17. One of the key reasons for its therapeutic effects is the endogenous increase in 3-OHB, rather than AcAc and acetone. As the end product of fatty acid beta-oxidation, increased levels of 3-OHB often indicate enhanced fat utilization as an energy substrate to meet the high energetic requirements associated with increased KB production after tissue damage or inflammation. More interestingly, accumulating evidence suggests that KBs are closely associated with multiple respiratory diseases18. A serum metabolomic study found decreased fatty acid beta-oxidation metabolism and 3-OHB levels in children with cystic fibrosis19. After normalization based on healthy controls, serum 3-OHB levels are significantly lower in acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) patients than in those with stable COPD20. Moreover, KD is a valuable nutritional tool to prevent infection and severe health consequences due to COVID-1921. In acute COVID-19 and acute respiratory distress syndrome patients, however, 3-OHB is at extremely high levels in the plasma samples22,23. A metabolomic analysis by Bruzzone et al. found abnormally high levels of KBs (AcAc, 3-OHB, and acetone) in COVID-19 patients, implying that they are biomarkers of hepatic glutathione synthesis and oxidative stress24. A recent MR analysis also revealed a positive correlation between plasma 3-OHB and the risk of severe COVID-1925. Despite evidence from conventional observational studies, randomized controlled trials, and metabolomics studies, the results obtained so far are unclear on the use of KBs in the treatment of respiratory disorders due to limited sample sizes, potential confounding biases, and implementation challenges. Therefore, this work aims to investigate the probable causal effects of ketone bodies (KBs) on lung cancer and respiratory disorders, and provides genetic evidence for their use in the prevention and treatment of these diseases. This is significant, as advancements in understanding the complex genetic biology of lung cancer and respiratory diseases may accelerate the development of better treatment options.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a new statistical technique that uses genetic instrumental variables (IVs) as tools to explore the causal effects of an exposure on an outcome of interest, hence reducing biases from reverse causality and confounding factors26. MR analysis capitalizes on the random inheritance of genetic instruments during meiosis, which safeguards against confounding factors such as lifestyle, environmental influences, and behavioral traits. This methodological approach effectively mitigates potential confounding issues commonly encountered in observational studies across diverse disease domains27,28. With increasing genetic developments, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) at present offer many potential techniques for MR analysis. Previous MR investigations addressed the causal association of KBs with cognitive impairment, and the risk of severe COVID-1925,29. Nonetheless, whether KBs have a causal relationship with lung cancer or chronic respiratory disorders remains elusive. Herein, we carried out MR methods based on diverse assumptions for validity and assessed evidence of the causal role of KBs in relation to lung cancer and respiratory diseases.

Methods

Study design

A two-sample MR analysis was performed to examine the causal relationships between KBs and lung diseases. This MR analysis complied with the STROBE-MR guidelines (strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology–Mendelian Randomization)30. The MR design was based on three core assumptions (Fig. 1): (1) IVs must have a strong correlation with KBs (assumption 1); (2) IVs must be independent of any potential confounding factors (assumption 2); (3) IVs must influence the outcome solely via the exposure (assumption 3). Assumption 2 and assumption 3, known as the independence of horizontal pleiotropy, could be tested using various statistical techniques.

All analyses were conducted utilizing TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.7), Mendelian Randomization (version 0.8.0), and MR-PRESSO package (version 1.0) within R Software version 4.3.1 (https://www.R-project.org), along with the LDSC software. No further ethical approval was sought since we used openly available summary data and participants provided informed consent during the original trials. The GWAS summary-level statistics necessary for these MR analyses were obtained from a publicly accessible GWAS website (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/).

Summary statistics from a study by Demenais et al. were used for the primary MR analysis of asthma among individuals of European ancestry34. The GWAS summary-level data for COPD and chronic bronchitis were obtained from the National Bioscience Database Center (NBDC) human database, encompassing a significant sample size of 497,000 individuals35. FinnGen, a major public-private collaboration, aims to collect and analyze genome and health data from 500,000 participants in the Finnish biobank including males and females36. Additionally, GWAS summary data pertaining to FEV1/FVC < 0.7 were obtained from a study by Higbee et al., involving a total sample size of 353,315 individuals. Besides, bias was minimized by targeting any potential overlap within populations.

Genetic IVs for KBs

An array of quality control criteria were employed to select suitable genetic IVs. In the initial MR analysis, SNPs with a p-value below 5 × 10− 8 (significance threshold) were selected as genetic IVs significantly associated with the exposure of interest29. Subsequently, to ensure the independence of the genetic IVs used for the KBs, all IVs were subjected to linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (R²<0.001 and clumping window size = 10,000 kb) to minimize the effect of correlated SNPs. European samples from the 1000 Genomes Project acted as the reference panel for LD calculations37. Meanwhile, we produced the F-statistic [(N–2) R²/(1–R²)], which indicated the reliability of each IV and suggested the percentage of variance explained by IVs, with N representing the effective sample size of GWAS38. F-statistic>10 was used to prevent weak instrument bias39. We further identified the SNPs associated with the outcome and removed those SNPs with a p-value of less than 5 × 10− 540. Subsequently, harmonization procedures were adopted to synchronize alleles of exposure-SNPs and outcome-SNPs and eliminate palindromic SNPs with unknown effective alleles. To ensure the specificity of the identified SNPs, we conducted a thorough search using PhenoScanner and LDlink to verify the absence of pleiotropic effects on other phenotypes at the genome-wide significance level, as such influences could potentially skew our results (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/; https://ldlink.nih.gov/?tab=home)41,42. Additionally, we used the Steiger test to see whether the observed causalities were biased by reverse causation43.

MR analysis

A two-sample MR technique was used to investigate the potential causal relationship between genetically predicted KBs and respiratory diseases. Assuming uncorrelated genetic IVs, the inverse variance weighted (IVW) approach was asymptotically similar to the broadly used two-stage least squares estimate in individual-level data analyses. Thus, we initially used the conventional IVW method, selecting between random-effects as well as fixed-effects IVW models based on a heterogeneity test44. The random-effects IVW model in instances of heterogeneity.

To increase the robustness of the findings, we adopted additional causal analysis approaches, including MR-Egger analysis, MR-PRESSO test, weighted median analysis, and weighted mode analysis45–47. All of these supplemental MR approaches make distinct assumptions regarding the validity of the genetic IVs. Two methods including the MR-PRESSO test and MR-Egger regression46,47 were used to mitigate possible horizontal pleiotropy. In MR-Egger regression, the intercept term acted as a tool to determine whether directional horizontal pleiotropy plays a role in shaping the results of MR analysis. Of note, the MR-PRESSO test is a technique designed to detect and adjust outliers. Subsequently, another MR analysis was carried out after excluding outlier SNPs identified by the MR-PRESSO test with p-values below the set threshold. The weighted median approach, which is only second to the IVW in terms of statistical efficacy, provides true causal estimates if at least half of the contributing information originates from valid genetic variations. The weighted mode approach provides an unbiased effect estimate when the most common causal effect estimate is in line with genuine causal effect despite the majority of instruments being erroneous analysis approaches. The Cochran-Q test identified heterogeneity in SNPs with p-value < 0.05 and I2 > 25%48. Leave-one-out (LOO) analysis is employed to identify SNPs that may significantly influence the results, helping to validate the robustness of the conclusions. Funnel plots are also used to detect heterogeneity, and symmetry plots indicate the absence of heterogeneity. The reverse MR utilized a similar method as described before. The Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction among MR analyses individually was used to adjust for multiple testing. A FDR-corrected p-value (PFDR) threshold of < 0.05 was used to define strong evidence supporting the analyses49.

A reverse MR approach was also used to investigate the potential causal link between lung illnesses and levels of KBs, following a similar approach as the initial MR analysis. Finally, replication MR experiments were carried out using different GWAS databases to increase the robustness of the results.

Results

MR analysis exploring the relevance of KB levels to lung cancers

First, we used the GWAS database of plasma KBs published in 2022 and conducted MR analysis on two independent lung cancer GWAS databases, respectively. After following the steps for instrument selection, we identified 19 3-OHB-associated SNPs, 7 AcAc-associated SNPs, and 12 acetone-associated SNPs as the genetic IVs. Additional File 2 Table S1 illustrates the details of these SNPs. The F-statistics of IVs exceeded 10, indicating a minimal likelihood of weak IV bias. Greater plasma 3-OHB levels under genetic prediction had an obvious connection to lowering the risk of lung cancer (Odds Ratio (OR) = 0.771, 95% Confidence Interval (95% CI) = 0.648–0.916, PFDR = 0.006) and SCLC (OR = 0.485, 95% CI 0.301–0.781, PFDR = 0.006), but not in squamous cell lung cancer (OR = 0.890, 95% CI 0.664–1.193, PFDR = 0.437) and lung adenocarcinoma (OR = 0.813 95% CI 0.640–1.033, PFDR = 0.121) (Fig. 2). Both AcAc and acetone levels have no causal relationship between lung cancer and the three categories of lung cancer (Additional File 2, Figure S1). To validate our findings, a replication MR analysis was conducted using additional 3-OHB and lung cancer GWAS data33. Our replication MR results support the conclusions drawn above (Figs. 2 and 3).

The Cochran Q-derived p-value indicated the absence of heterogeneity. Furthermore, a pleiotropy test with the MR-Egger intercept observed the intercept p-value above 0.05, suggesting no evidence of pleiotropy in these findings (Additional File 2, Table S2). Moreover, the MR-PRESSO method observed no significant directional pleiotropy in the correlations between KBs and lung cancer (P = 0.892 for lung cancer, P = 0.644 for SCLC, P = 0.311 for squamous cell lung cancer, and P = 0.260 for lung adenocarcinoma). Additionally, the LOO analysis indicated the absence of high-influence SNPs that might disrupt the collective effect estimates (Additional File 1, Figure S2). The distribution of SNPs in the funnel plot displays vertical lines as the axis of symmetry, indicating a symmetrical arrangement. This symmetry suggests that there is no directional horizontal pleiotropy among the SNPs, thereby enhancing the robustness of the MR estimation (Additional File 1, Figure S3). Simultaneously, we employed the Steiger test and reverse MR analysis43 to assess whether the observed causal relationships were confounded by reverse causation. Our findings indicate no evidence of a causal relationship between lung cancer and 3-OHB levels (Additional File 1, Figure S4), with all p-values from the Steiger test being < 0.001.

MR analysis exploring the relevance of KB levels to respiratory diseases

The open GWAS database for 3-OHB (met-d-bOHbutyrate) was exclusively used to prevent bias from data overlap. After removing outliers (rs429358, rs585188) calculated by MR-PRESSO outlier test, there was evidence for a protective impact of 3-OHB levels on asthma (OR = 0.585, 95% CI 0.395–0.867, PFDR = 0.010), COPD (OR = 0.716, 95% CI 0.562–0.912, PFDR = 0.008), chronic bronchitis (OR = 0.714, 95% CI 0.533–0.955, PFDR = 0.045) and FEV1/FVC < 0.7 (OR = 0.978, 95% CI 0.959–0.998, PFDR = 0.008) (Fig. 4). Additional File 2, Table S3 shows the details of the SNPs selected as the genetic IVs in this MR analysis. A pleiotropy test using the MR-Egger intercept identified the intercept p-value greater than 0.05, suggesting no indication of pleiotropy in these data. Nevertheless, significant heterogeneity was observed in the SNP-specific causal estimates for COPD, as demonstrated by the considerable Cochran’s Q statistic values. This suggests a violation of the fundamental MR assumptions for certain SNPs. (Additional File 2, Table S4). The results of LOO analysis and funnel plots were summarized in Additional File 1, Figure S3 and Figure S4. Consistent with the results of lung cancer, both AcAc and acetone levels had no causal relationship between these lung diseases. To validate our findings, we performed a replication MR study using another lung disease database (FinnGen consortium), before performing a meta-analysis (Fig. 5). We did not identify GWAS data for FEV1/FEV in the FinGen Consortium. Therefore, we performed a meta-analysis only with asthma, chronic bronchitis, and COPD. The combined research of the FinnGen consortium and lung disease GWAS data demonstrated a genetic liability to greater levels of plasma 3-OHB and predicted a lower risk of asthma, chronic bronchitis, and COPD. Concurrently, a reverse MR experiment was performed, which demonstrated no causative relationship between these lung diseases and 3-OHB levels. (Additional File 1, Figure S5). In addition, the p-values of the Steiger test were all <0. 001. STROBE-MR Checklist was summarized in Additional File 3.

Discussion

In the present MR research, increased 3-OHB significantly increased FEV1/FVC, whereas no significant effect was observed for AcAc and acetone levels. Moreover, this increase in 3-OHB levels corresponded with a decrease in the risk of lung cancer and respiratory diseases including chronic bronchitis, COPD, and asthma. These results are in line across multiple replication MR analyses. Nonetheless, it is important to exercise caution when drawing causal inferences from these findings due to the considerable heterogeneity in the causal estimates of individual variants. This suggests the need for careful consideration, since some IVs may have violated MR assumptions. Additional genetically associated systems may also be involved, which influence lung health and the outcome of these lung diseases.

Systemic inflammation and lipid metabolism disorders are common in patients with lung diseases and lung cancer50–53. A recent randomized clinical trial showed that omega-3 fatty acid supplementation may contribute to positive daily protein balance in patients with COPD54. KBs, which are key products of fatty acid metabolism, may provide therapeutic potential. KBs are synthesized in the liver and used by extrahepatic organs. The pathways of ketogenesis and ketolysis are illustrated in Additional File 1, Figure S6. In this metabolic pathway, 3-hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase (HMGCS2) catalyzes the key step in ketogenesis, whereas 3-oxoacid CoA-transferase 1 (OXCT) is the critical enzyme responsible for catalyzing the initial and rate-limiting step of ketolysis55. The enzyme-meditated down-regulation of ketogenesis and up-regulation of ketolysis will decrease the circulating KB levels. Based on our Mendelian findings, we further sought to investigate whether KBs could also prevent or treat lung cancer and respiratory diseases.

Metabolic abnormalities have been detected in human and animal models of lung cancer. As an important energy substrate other than glucose, KBs are linked to lung cancer. Studies have hypothesized that KBs can act as an alternative energy source for cancer cells, thereby supporting their survival and multiplication56. Lu et al. discovered that OXCT1 regulates the SREBP1-TRIM21-p65 axis in NSCLC via KBs homeostasis, potentially promoting tumor initiation and progression57. Allen et al. discovered that KD alone does not cause any inhibit tumor growth relative to control diet58. Nonetheless, other studies suggest that KBs protect against lung cancer. One recent study discovered that HMGCL was downregulated in lung cancer, causing larger tumor sizes and lower overall survival times59. KD limits the intake of carbohydrates, which can increase the body’s levels of KBs, thus playing a key role in the treatment of those diseases. Zhang et al. discovered that KD can increase cisplatin efficiency in NSCLC60. Similarly, our MR study revealed that 3-OHB levels were negatively associated with the hazard of lung cancer, but not AcAc and acetone. This suggests that 3-OHB plays a predominant role among KBs. Furthermore, we found for the first time that 3-OHB plays a protective role in lung cancer and subtypes of SCLC. We found no evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity. SCLC, which accounts for 13–15% of lung cancer cases, is classified as an aggressive neuroendocrine carcinoma, whose onset is strongly attributed to smoking. SCLC has a poor prognosis, as demonstrated by a 5-year survival rate of only 3% in patients with metastatic disease and substantial resistance to current treatment regimens. Consequently, a better knowledge of the risk and underlying mechanisms of SCLC is important as it can provide possibilities for early and more beneficial treatment intervention. A few studies only focus on the metabonomic aspects of this specific subtype of lung cancer due to the low prevalence of SCLC. Therefore, this MR study may provide novel insights for future research on SCLC. Additionally, we could not observe any genetic correlation between KBs and squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma. Therefore, we hypothesize that the clinical observations may be due to confounding factors or different types of tumor and animal models utilized.

Besides lung cancer, chronic respiratory diseases significantly increase the economic burden on populations globally and reduce their quality of life. These chronic respiratory diseases, including asthma, chronic bronchitis, and COPD have common significant characteristics, including chronic airway inflammation61. Researchers speculate that KBs exert protective effects against these diseases. Besides their role as an energy source, KBs are increasingly recognized for their regulatory effects on immune cell function, with 3-OHB predominantly causing an anti-inflammatory response62. An animal experiment discovered that providing a KD diet to obese asthma mice for 3 weeks increased the concentrations of 3-OHB and reduced airway hyperresponsiveness63. A high-resolution NMR-based serum metabolomics study revealed that serum 3-OHB increased after inhalation of budesonide and salbutamol in asthmatic children during acute exacerbation64, suggesting that 3-OHB could act as one of the efficacy indicators for therapeutics for asthma. In overweight individuals with asthma, increased 3-OHB levels using alternate-day caloric restriction are linked to decreased oxidative stress and inflammation, as well as improved clinical outcomes65. Moreover, a KD-mediated strategy to improve asthma control and lung function is currently under investigation in 15 adults with asthma (see NCT06151405). Patients diagnosed with advanced COPD and chronic respiratory failure show lower concentrations of 3-OHB than their counterparts without chronic respiratory failure, thereby suggesting a potential alteration in lipolysis between the two66. Besides chronic diseases, KBs can protect against acute lung infection by impeding tissue necrosis, phagocyte activation, and inflammation56,67. An animal experiment revealed that KD and 3-OHB supplementation can improve CD4+ T cell metabolism and function during severe respiratory infections, hence reducing mortality from SARS-CoV-2; this highlights the potential of 3-OHB as a carbon source for enhanced T cell responses in viral lung infections18. However, a recent MR study found that 3-OHB levels positively correlate with the risk of severe COVID-1925. Peng et al. discovered that patients with acute exacerbations of COPD have considerably higher serum 3-OHB levels than those with stable COPD20. However, we found the protective effect of 3-OHB on asthma, chronic bronchitis, and COPD. Notably, caution is necessary when concluding a direct causal relationship between 3-OHB and COPD due to the high heterogeneity in our results stemming from pleiotropy. To explore this, we compared different MR methods under different validity assumptions. The attenuation of some results when using the MR-Egger, weighted median, and MR-PRESSO methods indicates that some IVs may be related to COPD via pathways other than 3-OHB, a known issue in MR research. Nonetheless, we propose that 3-OHB could act as a protective factor in the early stages of respiratory diseases, with plasma 3-OHB potentially acting as a serum marker for disease progression after diagnosis.

Recent research is increasingly aimed at elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying lung cancer and various pulmonary diseases. MR analysis, a statistical method that employs genetic instrumental variables to explore potential causal relationships, can address the limitations inherent in observational studies. For instance, Li et al. showed that elevated levels of cathepsin H correlate with increased overall risk of lung cancer, adenocarcinoma, and lung cancer in smokers28. Zhang et al. demonstrated that COPD is a risk factor for the development of both lung adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma through MR analysis. This suggests a potential shared mechanism underlying these respiratory diseases68. Interestingly, Yu et al. investigated the relationships between 1400 serum metabolites and lung cancer; however, they did not clarify the association between KBs and lung cancer69. This omission may stem from variations in the selection thresholds for instrumental variables or from a limited sample size. Nevertheless, their findings highlight the crucial role of metabolic dysregulation as a key pathogenic mechanism in the development of lung cancer.

Metabolic reprogramming plays a crucial role in various cancers and chronic diseases. The oxLDL/CD36 signaling pathway in macrophages is linked to dysregulated mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism, which drives chronic inflammation and contributes to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis70. KAT6A influences the effector response of CD4 + T cells via glucose metabolism, modulating the progression of autoimmune diseases71. Beyond chronic diseases, tumor progression is closely linked to alterations in metabolic processes. In addition to the role of ketone bodies, various forms of metabolic reprogramming have been investigated in cancer. Wang et al. revealed that NSCLC tumor tissues demonstrate increased acetate uptake, with elevated dietary acetate intake facilitating tumor immune evasion11. Furthermore, a large Japanese case-control study reported that plasma levels of branched-chain amino acids were negatively associated with the risk of colorectal adenoma in male72. These studies collectively demonstrate that tumor metabolic reprogramming, encompassing glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid oxidation, and other metabolic pathways8, is a crucial adaptive change that enhances tumor survival and immune evasion, diverging from normal physiological processes. In addition, research conducted by Xu et al. employed an integrated approach of transcriptomics and proteomics to identify genes related to alternative polyadenylation in lung adenocarcinoma, investigating the association between apaQTL/eQTL SNPs and the risk of developing this malignancy73. In future, we aim to investigate whether key enzymes in the KBs metabolism pathway are potentially associated with lung cancer and respiratory diseases via proteomics.

In summary, the development of targeted therapies that address metabolic abnormalities presents significant potential. Tumor recurrence and metastasis critically affect long-term survival rates in postoperative cancer patients; nonetheless, many individuals experience local or distant metastases following major surgery74. Anesthetic drugs are frequently administered during the perioperative phase, and emerging research indicates that their use may impact tumor recurrence and metastasis. Additionally, anesthesia has the potential to induce metabolic reprogramming within the body; however, the extent to which this reprogramming influences tumor recurrence and metastasis remains a topic of debate. This uncertainty presents opportunities for further investigation in this area.

This MR study has significant strengths. First, it represents the first MR analysis that elucidates the correlation between genetically influenced KB levels in circulation and the risk of lung cancer and various lung diseases. Secondly, the use of the newest and most thorough GWAS database for KBs enabled a more precise estimation of causality between circulating ketones and lung diseases. Thirdly, we adopted rigorous control measures in selecting eligible SNPs. We also ensured the use of an F-statistic greater than 10 to prevent weak instrument bias. Eventually, the inclusion of multiple sensitivity analyses improved the robustness and stability of the MR findings.

However, this study has a few noteworthy limitations. Firstly, our findings are primarily based on European ethnicity, which may not be entirely applicable to other racial groups with different lifestyles and cultural backgrounds. Secondly, we cannot exclude horizontal pleiotropy in our data despite conducting various procedures to detect outliers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the triangulating evidence in this MR study revealed evidence of the causal effect of 3-OHB on lung cancer and several lung diseases. Our findings suggest that increasing 3-OHB levels reduces the incidence of lung cancer, asthma, COPD, and chronic bronchitis. Moreover, the methods of increasing KBs, including ketosis, exercise, and intermittent fasting, are well tolerated in the human body. Therefore, 3-OHB holds significant treatment potential in respiratory system diseases as an endogenously produced clean energy substrate.
