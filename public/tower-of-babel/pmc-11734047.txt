Giant Cardiac Myxoma as a Cause of Stroke
Authors: Nuno Cotrim, Ana Rita Veiga, Bruno Castilho, Marisa Peres, Vítor Martins
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiac myxomas are the most common primary intracardiac tumors and are histologically benign. However, they are potentially dangerous because of the risk of systemic embolism. Echocardiography is the key diagnostic tool for atrial myxomas, allowing for the identification of the tumor, as well as determining its location, shape, size, and connections with adjacent cardiac structures. Surgical resection is curative in most patients, with tumor relapse and recurrent embolism being relatively rare.Key Words: cardiac tumors, echocardiography, embolism, left atrial myxoma

A 67-year-old diabetic man was admitted to the emergency department with a sudden onset of dysarthria and central facial paralysis. He was diagnosed with an acute ischemic stroke caused by the occlusion of the right middle cerebral artery. The patient underwent successful endovascular therapy (Figure 1D), resulting in complete symptom resolution (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 0). During the physical examination, auscultation revealed an early diastolic sound (“tumor plop”) followed by a soft diastolic murmur. A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a large (53 × 48 mm), mobile, globular, pedunculated mass in the severely dilated left atrium (95 mL/m2), adjacent to the interatrial septum, with heterogeneous echogenicity.1 Protrusion into the left ventricular inflow was noted during diastole (Figure 1A, Video 1). A contrast study showed contrast perfusion within the mass, suggesting a cardiac polypoid myxoma (Figure 1B, Video 2).2 The patient was scheduled for cardiac surgery, during which a large globular mass was successfully extracted without complications (Figure 1C).3 Histopathologic examination confirmed the diagnosis of cardiac myxoma.

Take-Home Messages

•Myxomas, though benign cardiac tumors, can still cause embolic events because of tumor fragment detachment, thrombus formation, or infected vegetation, with larger and irregular tumors posing a higher risk.

Giant Myxoma in the Left Atrium Causing Acute Ischemic Stroke

(A) Apical 4-chamber view revealing a large mass (53 × 48 mm) in the left atrium (white arrow), adjacent to the interatrial septum, with heterogeneous echogenicity. (B) Apical 4-chamber view with echocardiographic contrast agent revealing contrast perfusion of the mass in the left atrium (white arrow). (C) Intraoperative image of the large and irregularly shaped cardiac myxoma (white arrow). The features discussed in A, B, and C suggest that the cardiac mass is a myxoma. (D) Cerebral angiography (left) pre– and (right) post–endovascular thrombectomy. On the left, a thrombus is seen on the right middle cerebral artery (yellow arrow). On the right, the resolution of the occlusion is noted (red arrow). (E) Transverse cross section of a cerebral computed tomography scan performed 5 days after successful endovascular therapy revealing an ischemic lesion in the territory of the right middle cerebral artery, with cortical-subcortical hypodensity in the right frontal, insular, and parietal regions (pink arrow). There is a confluent petechial hemorrhagic transformation in the right frontal area.
