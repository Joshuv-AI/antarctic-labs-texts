Stability and List-Replicability for Agnostic Learners
Ari Blondal, Shan Gao, Hamed Hatami, Pooya Hatami

Abstract
Two seminal papers–Alon, Livni, Malliaris, Moran (STOC 2019) and Bun, Livni, and Moran (FOCS 2020)–established the equivalence between online learnability and globally stable PAC learnability in binary classification. However, Chase, Chornomaz, Moran, and Yehudayoff (STOC 2024) recently showed that this equivalence does not hold in the agnostic setting. Specifically, they proved that in the agnostic setting, only finite hypothesis classes are globally stable learnable. Therefore, agnostic global stability is too restrictive to capture interesting hypothesis classes. To address this limitation, Chase et al. introduced two relaxations of agnostic global stability. In this paper, we characterize the classes that are learnable under their proposed relaxed conditions, resolving the two open problems raised in their work. First, we prove that in the setting where the stability parameter can depend on the excess error (the gap between the learner’s error and the best achievable error by the hypothesis class), agnostic stability is fully characterized by the Littlestone dimension. Consequently, as in the realizable case, this form of learnability is equivalent to online learnability. As part of the proof of this theorem, we strengthen the celebrated result of Bun et al. by showing that classes with infinite Littlestone dimension are not stably PAC learnable, even if we allow the stability parameter to depend on the excess error. For the second relaxation proposed by Chase et al. , we prove that only finite hypothesis classes are globally stable learnable even if we restrict the agnostic setting to distributions with small population loss.

Introduction

We follow the standard PAC learning framework for
binary classification
as, for example, described in
[
SSBD14
]
. In this model, a learner receives a sample of i.i.d.
examples
from an unknown distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
X\times\{0,1\}
, where
X
is the domain set, and
\{0,1\}
represents the two possible
labels
in binary classification. The learner’s goal is to produce a
hypothesis

h:X\to\{0,1\}
that minimizes the
population loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\coloneqq\Pr_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}].

Here, and throughout the paper, we use boldface letters to denote random variables and use the notation
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathcal{D}
to express that
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is a random variable distributed according to
\mathcal{D}
.

Formally, a
learning rule
is a (randomized) function
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
that maps any sample

S\in(X\times\{0,1\})^{*}\coloneqq\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}(X\times\{0,1\})^{n}
to a hypothesis
\bm{\mathcal{A}}(S)\in\{0,1\}^{X}
. Thus, for any given sample
S
,
\bm{\mathcal{A}}(S)
is a random variable taking values in
\{0,1\}^{X}
.

Throughout this paper, all learning rules are assumed to be randomized. We consistently use
X
to denote the domain,
\{0,1\}
to represent the two possible labels, and
\mathcal{D}
always refers to a distribution over
X\times\{0,1\}
. For an integer
n>0
, we use
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Given a
hypothesis class

\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
, the goal of PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) learning is for the learner to produce, with high probability, a hypothesis whose population loss is close to the best achievable within
\mathcal{H}
, defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})\coloneqq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h).

A class
\mathcal{H}
is
PAC learnable
if there is a learning rule
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
and a function
n(\epsilon,\delta)
such that for any
\epsilon,\delta>0
,

\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S}))\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\epsilon]\geq 1-\delta\ \text{ where }n=n(\epsilon,\delta).

PAC learning is studied in the
realizable case
, where we assume
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})=0
, and the
agnostic case
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})>0
.

Stability.

Replicability is a fundamental principle of the scientific method. A study is replicable if it consistently yields the same results when repeated with new data drawn from the same distribution or source. In recent years, machine learning has seen a growing need to address the replication crisis
[
Bal23
,
Bak16
]
. Impagliazzo, Lei, Pitassi, and Sorrel
[
ILPS22
]
initiated a formal theoretical framework for studying replicability in machine learning. Since their work, a rapidly growing body of research has emerged that introduced various notions of replicability. These works and subsequent research showed that many of these notions of replicability are essentially equivalent. Furthermore, they established deep connections to other foundational concepts in learning theory, such as differential privacy
[
CMY23
,
BGH+23
,
KKMV23
,
GKM21a
,
GKM21b
,
CCMY24
]
. Additionally, a growing body of work has explored replicability in many data analysis and learning settings
[
ILPS22
,
BGH+23
,
KVYZ23
,
EKK+23
,
EKM+23
,
EHKS24
,
KKL+24
,
KKMV23
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the notion of replicability where the learning algorithm is expected to often produce the same predictor when applied to two independent and identically distributed inputs. This concept was first introduced under the term
global stability
in
[
BLM20
]
and has since been refined and explored in subsequent works
[
GKM21a
,
KKMV23
,
CMY23
,
CCMY24
]
. We start by defining stability and global stability.

Definition 1.1

(Stability)
.

Given a function
\rho:(0,1)\rightarrow(0,1)
, a learning rule
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
is a
\rho
-stable learner
for a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
if the following holds.
For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
n=n(\epsilon)
such that for every realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
, there exists a hypothesis
h
satisfying

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\epsilon

and

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}\end{subarray}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})=h]\geq\rho(\epsilon).

(1)

Similarly, we call
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
a
\rho
-stable agnostic learner
for
\mathcal{H}
, if there exists
n=n(\epsilon)
such that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
X\times\{0,1\}
, there exists a hypothesis
h\in\{0,1\}^{X}
that satisfies (
1
) and

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\epsilon.

(2)

To simplify terminology, we use the term
\rho
-stable
to describe a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
with a
\rho
-stable learner
. Likewise, we call
\mathcal{H}

agnostically
\rho
-stable
if it has a
\rho
-stable agnostic learner.

Definition 1.2

(Global Stability)
.

We say that a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is
globally stable
if it is
\rho
-stable for a fixed constant
\rho\in(0,1)
. Similarly, a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is
agnostically globally stable
if it is agnostically
\rho
-stable for such a constant.

In short, global stability requires the stability parameter in
Equation

1
to be uniform, meaning it must not depend on
\epsilon
.

Bun, Livni, and Moran
[
BLM20
]
showed that in the realizable setting, global stability is fully characterized by bounded Littlestone dimension (
Definition

1.12
). This result, combined with the seminal works of
[
Lit88
,
ABL+22
]
, shows that global stability is equivalent to online learnability and approximately private learnability, as well as, some other notions of replicability
[
KKMV23
,
GKM21a
,
BGH+23
]
.

In contrast, the agnostic setting reveals a different picture. Chase, Chornomaz, Moran, and Yehudayoff
[
CCMY24
]
proved the following characterization using a topological approach.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
CCMY24
]
)
.

A hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically globally stable if and only if
\mathcal{H}
is finite.

This striking result shows that agnostic global stability is far more restrictive than its realizable counterpart. Since finite classes are trivially stable,
Theorem

1.3
shows that agnostic global stability is too restrictive to lead to interesting learnability phenomena. To remedy this, Chase
et al.

[
CCMY24
]
introduced two relaxations of agnostic global stability and proposed a study of which hypothesis classes can be learned under these relaxed notions of stability.

Excess-error dependent stability.

The first suggested relaxation, coincides with our definition of
\rho
-stability in
Definition

1.1
. A hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is called
excess-error dependent stable
if it is agnostically
\rho
-stable for some
\rho:(0,1)\to(0,1)
. Here the excess-error refers to the parameter
\epsilon
in
Eq.

2
.

Our main theorem provides a complete characterization of such classes. We show that a hypothesis class is
\rho
-agnostically stable learnable for some
\rho
if and only if it has a bounded Littlestone dimension.
We denote the Littlestone dimension of
\mathcal{H}
as
\ldim(\mathcal{H})
.

Theorem 1.4

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a binary concept class.

(i)

If
\ldim(\mathcal{H})=\infty
, then
\mathcal{H}
is not
\rho
-stable for any
\rho:(0,1)\to(0,1)
.

(ii)

If
\ldim(\mathcal{H})<\infty
, then
\mathcal{H}
is agnostically
\rho
-stable for some
\rho:(0,1)\to(0,1)
.

Note that
Theorem

1.4
(i) states that if
\ldim(\mathcal{H})=\infty
, then even in the realizable case, we cannot achieve
\rho
-stability for any
\rho:(0,1)\to(0,1)
. This strengthens the result of Bun, Livni, and Moran
[
BLM20
]
, which only overrules
\rho
-stability when
\rho>0
is a fixed constant.

Combined with the work of
[
ABL+22
]
,
Theorem

1.4
implies that agnostic
\rho
-stability is equivalent to global stability, as well as to approximate private learnability and online learnability.

Class-error dependent stability.

In many practical learning scenarios, while we cannot assume realizability, we may have prior knowledge that the hypothesis class performs reasonably well. This corresponds to a more restricted version of agnostic learning, where the learning task is limited to distributions
\mathcal{D}
that satisfy
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\gamma
for some small
\gamma>0
.

Definition 1.5

(Class-error Dependent Stability)
.

Let
\gamma\in[0,1]
be a fixed constant. We say
\mathcal{H}
is
\gamma
-agnostically globally stable if there exists a constant
\rho>0
and a learning rule
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
such that the following holds. For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
n=n(\epsilon)
such that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\gamma
, there exists a hypothesis
h
satisfying

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\epsilon,

and

\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})=h\right]\geq\rho.

The case
\gamma=0
corresponds to the realizable case, where Bun, Livni, and Moran
[
BLM20
]
show that global stability is fully characterized by bounded Littlestone dimension. On the other hand,
\gamma=1
corresponds to the agnostic case, where
Theorem

1.3
shows that only finite classes are agnostically globally stable.

Chase
et al.

[
CCMY24
]
ask which hypothesis classes are
\gamma
-agnostically globally stable for all sufficiently small
\gamma
. Our next theorem shows that the realizable case,
\gamma=0
, is the only scenario in which infinite hypothesis classes can be
\gamma
-agnostically globally stable.
Therefore, the relaxation of agnostic global stability to
\gamma
-agnostic global stability does not lead to any generalization, as only finite hypothesis classes can be
\gamma
-agnostically globally stable if
\gamma>0
.

Theorem 1.6
.

If a class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
is
\gamma
-agnostically globally stable for some
\gamma>0
, then
\mathcal{H}
is finite.

Proof.

Assume towards a contradiction that an infinite
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
is
\gamma
-agnostically globally stable for some
\gamma>0
, and let
\rho>0
,
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
, and
n(\cdot)
be as in
Definition

1.5
.

Pick any
x^{*}\in X
, and let
b^{*}\in\{0,1\}
be such that the subclass
\mathcal{H}^{*}\coloneqq\{h\in\mathcal{H}:h(x^{*})=b^{*}\}
is infinite. Let
\gamma^{\prime}\coloneqq\min\{\gamma,\frac{1}{10}\}
. We obtain a contradiction with
Theorem

1.3
by showing that
\mathcal{H}^{*}
is agnostically globally stable despite being infinite.

Given
\epsilon>0
and access to a distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
X\times\{0,1\}
, let
n\coloneqq n(\epsilon\gamma^{\prime})
, and define the distribution

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\coloneqq\gamma^{\prime}\mathcal{D}+(1-\gamma^{\prime})\mathbf{1}_{(x^{*},b^{*})},

which corresponds to sampling from
\mathcal{D}
with probability
\gamma^{\prime}
, and sampling
(x^{*},b^{*})
with probability
1-\gamma^{\prime}
. Consider the learning rule
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
described as follows:

1.

Given a sample
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
, independently replace each example in
\bm{S}
with
(x^{*},b^{*})
with probability
1-\gamma^{\prime}
. Let
\bm{T}
denote the resulting modified sample.

2.

Output
\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{T})
.

Note that
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(\bm{S})
with
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
has the same distribution as
\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{T})
with
\bm{T}\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}
.

Since every
h\in\{0,1\}^{X}
with
h(x^{*})=b^{*}
, satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(h)=\gamma^{\prime}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\gamma^{\prime}
, we have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\gamma^{\prime}
. Therefore, by our choice of
n\coloneqq n(\epsilon\gamma^{\prime})
and our assumption of the
\gamma
-agnostic global stability of
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
on

\mathcal{H}
, there exists
h^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{X}
with

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(h^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})+\epsilon\gamma^{\prime}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(\bm{S})=h^{*}\right]=\Pr_{\bm{T}\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}}\left[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{T})=h^{*}\right]\geq\rho.

(3)

If
h^{*}(x^{*})\neq b^{*}
, then since
\gamma^{\prime}<\frac{1}{10}
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(h^{*})\geq 1-\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma^{\prime}+\epsilon\gamma^{\prime}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})+\epsilon\gamma^{\prime},

which contradicts the first inequality in
Equation

3
. Therefore,
h^{*}(x^{*})=b^{*}
, and consequently, we have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(h^{*})=\gamma^{\prime}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h^{*})
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})=\gamma^{\prime}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
. Replacing these in
Equation

3
shows

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H}^{*})+\epsilon\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(\bm{S})=h^{*}\right]\geq\rho.

Therefore, the infinite class
\mathcal{H}^{*}
is agnostically globally stable, contradicting
Theorem

1.3
.
∎

Relation to list replicability.

In learning theory, stability is more useful when paired with a guarantee that the learner typically outputs a hypothesis with a low population loss. However, the definition of global stability (
Definition

1.2
) only requires that the learner outputs a low-error hypothesis with some probability
\rho>0
, allowing the learner to output hypotheses with large population loss with probability
1-\rho
.

To address this limitation, Chase
et al.

[
CMY23
]
proposed a seemingly stronger notion than stability, termed
list replicability
, which ensures both low population loss and stability of the output hypothesis.

Definition 1.7

(List Replicability)
.

Given a function
L:(0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, we say that a learner
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
is an
L
-list-replicable learner
for a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
if the following holds. For every
\epsilon,\delta>0
, there exists
n=n(\epsilon,\delta)
such that for every realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
, there exists a list of
L=L(\epsilon)
hypotheses
h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}
satisfying

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{i})\leq\epsilon\ \text{ for all }1\leq i\leq L

and

\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})\in\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\}]\geq 1-\delta.

(4)

Similarly,
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
is an
agnostic
L
-list-replicable learner
for
\mathcal{H}
if there exists
n=n(\epsilon,\delta)
such that for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
X\times Y
, there exists a list of
L=L(\epsilon)
hypotheses
h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying (
4
) and

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{i})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\epsilon\ \text{ for all }1\leq i\leq L.

Similar to stability, we define the notions of
global list-replicability
and
agnostic global list-replicability
to describe the uniform case where the learner in
Definition

1.7
exists for a fixed constant
L>0
independent of
\epsilon
.

It is worth noting that
Equation

4
easily implies stability, as there must exist some
i\in[L]
with

\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})=h_{i}]\geq\frac{1-\delta}{L}.

Surprisingly, Chase, Moran, and Yehudayoff
[
CMY23
]
showed that the converse is also true: global stability implies global list-replicability.

Theorem 1.8

(
[
CMY23
]
)
.

For any fixed constant
L>0
, a hypothesis class is
L
-list-replicable if and only if it is
\rho
-stable for all
\rho<\frac{1}{L}
.

Analogous to
Definition

1.5
, given a parameter
\gamma\in[0,1]
, we refer to a class
\mathcal{H}
as
\gamma
-agnostically list-replicable if we relax the requirement of the agnostic global list-replicability to only consider distributions
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})\leq\gamma
.

The foregoing relaxations of global list-replicability were proposed in
[
CCMY24
]
, where they asked for a characterization of hypothesis classes that can be agnostically learned under these notions, termed excess-error dependent and class-error dependent list-replicability.

Our next theorem extends
Theorem

1.8
to show that these new notions coincide with their stability counterparts. Consequently,
Theorem

1.4
and
Theorem

1.6
completely resolve the questions posed by
[
CCMY24
]
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Consider a parameter
\gamma\in[0,1]
.

(i)

Given
L:(0,1)\to\mathbb{N}
, if a class
\mathcal{H}
is
\gamma
-agnostically
L
-list replicable, then it is
\gamma
-agnostically
\rho
-stable for any
\rho:(0,1)\to[0,1]
satisfying
\rho(\epsilon)<\frac{1}{L(\epsilon)}
for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

(ii)

Given
\rho:(0,1)\to(0,1]
, if a class
\mathcal{H}
is
\gamma
-agnostically
\rho
-stable, then it is
\gamma
-agnostically
L
-list replicable, for
L(\epsilon)\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\rho(\epsilon/4)}\right\rfloor
.

1.1
Preliminaries: VC Dimension, Uniform Convergence, and Littlestone Dimension

This section outlines a few key concepts and results from learning theory. More specifically, we state the connections between PAC learnability, VC dimension, and uniform convergence, and we state the definition of the Littlestone dimension. For a detailed exposition, see
[
SSBD14
]
.

A fundamental result of learning theory is that a class
\mathcal{H}
is PAC-learnable if and only if it satisfies the
Uniform Convergence
property. For a sample of
m
examples
S\in(X\times\{0,1\})^{m}
, and a hypothesis
h:X\to\{0,1\}
, let

\mathcal{L}_{\bm{S}}(h)\coloneqq\Pr_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim S}[h(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}],

denote the
empirical population loss
of
h
with respect to
S
.

Definition 1.10

(Uniform Convergence)
.

A binary hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
has the
Uniform Convergence
property if, for any
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists
n(\epsilon,\delta)
such that for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[|\mathcal{L}_{\bm{S}}(h)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)|<\epsilon\text{ for all }h\in\mathcal{H}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

The fundamental theory of PAC learning states that the Uniform Convergence property and, consequently, PAC-learnability are characterized by having a finite Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension.

Definition 1.11

(VC dimension)
.

The VC dimension of a binary hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is the size of the largest subset
X^{\prime}
of
X
such that, for every binary labelling of
X^{\prime}
, there is a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
consistent with that labelling. Such a set
X^{\prime}
is said to be
shattered
by
\mathcal{H}
. If arbitrarily large sets can be shattered, the VC dimension is defined to be
\infty
.

The Littlestone dimension relaxes the VC dimension by shattering decision trees instead of sets. A
mistake tree
of depth
d
over a domain
X
is a complete binary tree of depth
d
with the following properties:

•

Each internal node in the tree is labelled by an element
x\in X
.

•

Each edge is labeled by a binary value
b\in\{0,1\}
where
b=0
indicates a left child and
b=1
indicates a right child.

Every
root-to-leaf path
in the tree is described by a sequence
(x_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(x_{d},b_{d})
where
x_{i}\in X
is the label of the
i
th internal node on the path and
b_{i}
specifies whether the path moves to the left or right child at each level.

We say that a mistake tree is
shattered
by a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
if for every root-to-leaf path
(x_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(x_{d},b_{d})
where
x_{i}\in X
and
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, there exists a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
with
h(x_{i})=b_{i}
for all
i\in[d]
.

Definition 1.12

(Littlestone Dimension)
.

The Littlestone dimension of a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\ldim(\mathcal{H})
, is the largest integer
d
such that there exists a mistake tree of depth
d
shattered by
\mathcal{H}
.

We always have
\VCdim(\mathcal{H})\leq\ldim(\mathcal{H})
, since every shattered set
X^{\prime}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}
gives rise to a mistake tree of depth
d
where all nodes at level
i
are labelled with
x_{i}
. This tree is clearly shattered by
\mathcal{H}
.

This result generalizes the equivalence between global stability and global list replicability of

For the reader’s convenience, we recall the statement of the theorem.

The proof of (i) is straightforward. Given

Theorem

(i): Stability implies finite Littlestone dimension

By the equivalence of stability and list-replicability established in

(i) is equivalent to the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\ldim(\mathcal{H})=\infty
, then
\mathcal{H}
is not
L
-list replicable for any

.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of

, which uses a classical result of Shelah
[
She90
]
connecting the Littlestone dimension to the threshold dimension.

Definition 3.2

(Threshold dimension)
.

The
threshold dimension
of
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
is the largest
k
such that there exists a set of inputs
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subseteq X
and classifiers
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
satisfying

h_{t}(x_{i})=1\iff i\geq t\qquad\text{ for all }i,t\in[k].

We refer the reader to
[
ALMM19
]
for an accessible proof of the following result of Hodges
[
Hod97
]
, which provides effective bounds for a qualitative result of Shelah
[
She90
]
. Shelah proved that any class
\mathcal{H}
with infinite Littlestone dimension also has an infinite threshold dimension.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
Hod97
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
has
\ldim(\mathcal{H})=d
, its threshold dimension is at least
\lfloor\log d\rfloor
.

Throughout the proof, we will use the following observation stating that without loss of generality we may ignore the order of examples in a sample.

Remark 3.4
.

Since the population loss does not depend on the order of the examples in
S
, and the examples are drawn independently from
\mathcal{D}
, in the context of PAC learning and stability, we may assume that the learning rule disregards the order of the examples in any sample
S\in(X\times\{0,1\})^{n}
. In other words, the learning rule is invariant under the permutations of the examples in any given sample. As a result, we often treat a sample
S
as a multiset rather than a sequence.

Next, we prove a lemma to decrease the probability of failure (i.e.,
\delta
) in the definition of list replicability to a small function of the sample size
n
and the population regret
\epsilon
.

Lemma 3.5

(Boosting success probability)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is
L
-list-replicable for some
L:(0,1)\to\mathbb{N}
. For every
C>1
, there exists a learning rule
\mathcal{A}
and a sample complexity
n_{C}:(0,1)\to\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds. For every
\epsilon>0
and every realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
, there exists a list of
L=L(\epsilon)
hypotheses
h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}
satisfying

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{i})\leq\epsilon\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq L

and

\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})\in\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\}]\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{n^{C}}\ \ \text{ where }n=n_{C}(\epsilon).

Proof.

Define
\delta_{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{16L}
. By our assumption, there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(\epsilon)
and a learning rule
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
such that for any realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
, there exists a list
h_{1},\dots,h_{L(\epsilon)}
of hypotheses satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{i})\leq\epsilon
for all
i
and

\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{0}}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}(\bm{S})\in\{h_{1},\dots,h_{L}\}]\geq 1-\delta_{0}=1-\frac{1}{16L}.

(9)

Since
\delta_{0}
is fixed,
n_{0}
depends only
\epsilon
.

Let
k>0
be an integer to be determined later. We define a new learning rule
\mathcal{A}
that uses samples of size
kn_{0}
. Given a sample
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\in((X\times\{0,1\})^{n_{0}})^{k}
, the learner
\mathcal{A}
outputs the most frequent hypothesis produced by the
k
independent runs
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S_{k})
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be any realizable distribution, and let
h_{1},\dots,h_{L(\epsilon)}
be as above. By
Equation

9
, there exists some
j^{*}\in[L]
such that

\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{0}}}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\bm{S})=h_{j^{*}}]\geq\frac{1}{2L}.

Consider
\bm{S}=(\bm{S}_{1},\ldots,\bm{S}_{k})\sim(\mathcal{D}^{n_{0}})^{k}
. For every
i\in[k]
, define the indicator variable
\bm{E}_{i}
and
\bm{B}_{i}
as

•

\bm{E}_{i}=1
iff
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{i})=h_{j^{*}}
;

•

\bm{B}_{i}=1
iff
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{i})\notin\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\}
.

The variables
\bm{E}_{1},\ldots,\bm{E}_{k}
are independent Bernoulli variables with
\Ex[\bm{E}_{i}]\geq\frac{1}{2L}
. Similarly,
\bm{B}_{1},\ldots,\bm{B}_{k}
are independent Bernoulli variables with
\Ex[\bm{B}_{i}]\leq\delta_{0}\leq\frac{1}{16L}
. Define
\bm{E}\coloneqq\sum\bm{E}_{i}
and
\bm{B}\coloneqq\sum\bm{B}_{i}
.
Applying Hoeffding’s inequality, we have

\Pr[\bm{E}\geq\frac{k}{4L}]\geq 1-\Pr[|\bm{E}-\Ex[\bm{E}]|\geq\frac{k}{4L}]\geq 1-e^{-\Omega(k/L^{2})}

and

\Pr[\bm{B}\leq\frac{k}{8L}]\geq 1-\Pr[|\bm{B}-\Ex[\bm{B}]|\geq\frac{k}{16L}]\geq 1-e^{-\Omega(k/L^{2})}.

When both events occur, the output of
\mathcal{A}
, the most frequent hypothesis, must come from the list
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\}
. We may now choose
k
such that
\delta\coloneqq 2\cdot e^{-\Omega(k/L^{2})}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{(n_{0}k)^{C}}=\frac{\epsilon}{n^{C}}
, concluding the proof.
∎

4
Theorem

1.4
(ii): Stability from finite Littlestone dimension

In
[
BLM20
]
, Bun, Livni, and Moran showed that every class with finite Littlestone dimension has a
globally
stable learner in the
realizable case
.

Theorem 4.1

(Global Stable Learning from Finite Littlestone Dimension,
[
BLM20
]
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
satisfy
\ldim(\mathcal{H})\leq d
. Then there exists a sample complexity
n:(0,1)\to\mathbb{N}
and a learning rule
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
such that for every
\epsilon>0
, and every realizable
\mathcal{D}
, there exists a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
with

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\epsilon\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})=h]\geq\frac{1}{(d+1)2^{2^{d}+1}}\ \text{ where }n=n(\epsilon).

We show that the algorithm of
[
BLM20
]
is already essentially an agnostic
\rho
-stable learner for these classes for some
\rho:(0,1)\rightarrow(0,1)
. For a minor technical reason, in the following lemma, we require that the population loss of the distribution
\mathcal{D}
is bounded away from
1
. The constant
2/3
in the statement of the lemma is quite arbitrary and can be replaced by any larger constant strictly less than
1
.

Lemma 4.2

(Stable Agnostic Learning of Classes with Finite Littlestone Dimension)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
satisfy
\ldim(\mathcal{H})\leq d
.
There exists a learning rule
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
, a stability parameter
\rho:(0,1)\to(0,1)
, a sample complexity
n:(0,1)\to\mathbb{N}
, such that for every
\epsilon>0
and every distribution
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})<\frac{2}{3}
, there exists a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
with

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\varepsilon\ \ \text{and }\ \ \Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})=h]\geq\rho(\varepsilon).

Moreover,
\rho
is given explicitly by
\rho(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{(d+1)2^{2^{d}+1}4^{n(\varepsilon)}}.

Proof.

Let
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
be a globally stable learner for
\mathcal{H}
in the realizable case as guaranteed by
Theorem

4.1
, and let
n\coloneqq n_{\ref{thm:global_stable}}(\epsilon/2)
where
n_{\ref{thm:global_stable}}(\cdot)
is the sample complexity in
Theorem

4.1
. Note that
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
is intended for realizable distributions, but our samples come from a non-realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

Fix a hypothesis
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
with
\gamma\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\leq\frac{3}{4}
. Note

\Pr_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[\bm{y}=h^{*}(\bm{x})]=1-\gamma,

and let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be the distribution obtained by conditioning
\mathcal{D}
on the event that the example is consistent with
h^{*}
. We have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(h^{*})=0
and thus
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
. By our choice of
n
and
Theorem

4.1
, there is a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
with

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(h)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \Pr_{\bm{S}\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})=h]\geq\frac{1}{(d+1)2^{2^{d}+1}}.

Since
\gamma\leq\frac{3}{4}
, we have

\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})=h]\geq(1-\gamma)^{n}\Pr_{\bm{S}\sim(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{S})=h]\geq\frac{(1-\gamma)^{n}}{(d+1)2^{2^{d}+1}}\geq\frac{1}{(d+1)2^{2^{d}+1}4^{n}}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)

\displaystyle=\Pr_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}\ |\ h^{*}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}]+\Pr_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[h^{*}(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(h)+\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h^{*})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{H})+\epsilon.\qed
