Exploring the link between IgE-mediated inflammation and cardiorenal function in adults living with and without HIV
Authors: Phinnoty Mwansa, David Chisompola, Stella P Mainga, Joreen P Povia, Sepiso K Masenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunoglobulin E (IgE) is traditionally recognized for its role in allergic response. However, emerging evidence suggests it may also influence cardiovascular, renal, and immune function. Understanding how IgE relates to endothelial function, electrolytes, renal markers, inflammatory mediators, and sociodemographic factors could provide insight into integrated cardiorenal-immune pathways and inform risk stratification people living with or without HIV.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 221 adults (median age 49 years, 68.3% female, 64.7% HIV-positive) at the medical clinic of Livingstone University Teaching Hospital. We collected data on sociodemographic characteristics, and performed diagnostic assessments for cardiometabolic health and inflammatory markers, including total IgE. To identify factors independently associated with IgE levels, we employed multiple linear regression models. Given the potential for HIV to modulate immune responses, all analyses were adjusted for relevant confounders, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsIn multivariable analysis of the overall cohort, log-transformed IgE levels were independently associated with serum creatinine (β = 2.26, 95% CI: 0.80–3.73, p = 0.003), plasma chloride (β = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.02–0.21, p = 0.020), flow-mediated dilation (FMD) (β = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.001–0.066, p = 0.043), and peripheral neuropathy (β = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.16–1.45, p = 0.014). However, after false discovery rate (FDR) correction, only serum creatinine remained significantly associated with IgE. In analyses restricted to people living with HIV, several factors remained independently associated with IgE after FDR adjustment, including serum creatinine (β = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.45–3.97, p < 0.001), plasma chloride (β = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.05–0.23, p = 0.003), salt taste preference (β = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.09–0.41, p = 0.003), and FMD (β = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.01–0.07, p = 0.011). Aldosterone demonstrated an inverse association with IgE (β = −1.22, 95% CI: −2.16 to − 0.27, p = 0.013). Other associations, including inflammatory and hematologic markers, were attenuated after correction for multiple testing.ConclusionIgE levels in this HIV-endemic cohort were most consistently associated with renal function, with serum creatinine emerging as the only robust correlate after multiple testing correction. Additional associations with endothelial function, electrolytes, and behavioral factors were observed, particularly among people living with HIV, but remained exploratory. These findings suggest that IgE reflects a broader state of systemic immune activation rather than a specific driver of cardiorenal dysfunction. Longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify causality and determine any prognostic relevance.Keywords: Endothelial dysfunction, IgE, FMD, Cardiovascular disease, Allergic inflammation, Electrolytes, HIV

Introduction

Immunoglobulin E (IgE) is traditionally associated with allergic disease. However, growing evidence indicates broader roles in immune-mediated cardiovascular and renal pathology. Population-based studies have reported associations between elevated serum IgE and coronary heart disease, hypertension, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, while selective IgE deficiency has also been linked to adverse vascular outcomes, suggesting a complex relationship between IgE and cardiovascular health [1, 2].

Mechanistically, IgE-mediated effects are thought to operate through Fc epsilon receptor I (FcεRI) signaling on mast cells and other immune cells, promoting perivascular inflammation, cytokine release, and adventitial immune activation (3,4). These processes have been implicated in endothelial dysfunction and hypertension, positioning IgE as a potential marker of immune–vascular interactions.

Flow-mediated dilation (FMD) is a non-invasive measure of endothelium-dependent vasodilation and is widely used as a functional surrogate of vascular health; however, direct evidence linking IgE to impaired FMD remains limited and inconsistent [3].

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection provides a relevant biological context in which to examine IgE-related pathways. HIV is characterized by persistent immune activation and chronic inflammation, even among individuals receiving effective antiretroviral therapy [4, 5]. Studies from African settings have documented elevated IgE levels in people living with HIV, with higher concentrations observed in advanced disease stages [6]. This immune activation is accompanied by increased inflammatory biomarkers, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), which independently predict cardiovascular risk and have also been linked to renal dysfunction [4, 5, 7]. Inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) have been implicated in IgE regulation in autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, with coordinated expression of these mediators reported in systemic lupus erythematosus and related disorders [8].

In parallel, electrolyte homeostasis plays a central role in cardiovascular and renal physiology and is increasingly recognized as a modulator of immune signaling. However, the relationships between electrolyte balance, IgE levels, and immune activation have not been systematically examined, particularly in HIV-endemic populations [9]. Sociodemographic and behavioral factors influence immune function and cardiovascular risk, yet their contribution to IgE variation and IgE-related cardiorenal associations in African settings remains poorly characterized [10]. Limited regional data exist on how these determinants shape IgE profiles and related health outcomes in resource-limited settings where HIV prevalence is high and traditional cardiovascular risk factors are evolving [4, 6].

Despite emerging mechanistic and epidemiological evidence, important gaps persist. Direct links between IgE and functional vascular outcomes such as FMD are not well defined, and the extent to which HIV infection modifies IgE-related immune, vascular, and renal pathways remains unclear [3, 7, 8]. Addressing these gaps may improve understanding of immune-mediated cardiovascular and renal risk in HIV-endemic settings.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine associations between serum IgE levels and endothelial function, inflammatory markers, electrolytes, and sociodemographic factors in a cohort attending a medical clinic at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital, and to assess whether these relationships differ by HIV status, with relevance to cardiovascular and renal risk stratification.

Methods

Study design, population and setting

This was a cross-sectional study in which participants were consecutively recruited from individuals attending a routine medical clinic at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital between October 1, 2023, and June 1, 2024.

Livingstone University Teaching Hospital is a tertiary referral centre serving the Southern and Western Provinces of Zambia. The hospital offers comprehensive services to people living with HIV (PLHIV) or without HIV. Study participants were drawn from the outpatient medical clinic, which screens roughly 3,800 individuals. The hospital provides standard healthcare, HIV services, including ART follow-up, counselling, and treatment of opportunistic infections, making it an appropriate environment to explore factors associated with the inflammatory marker IgE in a population with a high HIV burden such as Zambia.

Data collection

Sociodemographic and clinical data were systematically collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform. Trained research personnel gathered information on age, sex, marital status, smoking status, employment status, and medical history (including HIV, hypertension, and cardiovascular conditions) through standardized structured interviews and medical record reviews. HIV status and ART regimen details were confirmed from clinical records.

Eligibility

Participants were aged ≥ 18 years with written informed consent, including people with HIV (PWH; n = 143, 64.7%) on stable antiretroviral therapy ≥ 12 months and virologically suppressed (viral load < 50 copies/mL), people without HIV (PWOH; n = 78, 35.3%) confirmed with negative HIV tests, and those with (n = 64, 29.0%) or without hypertension. Exclusions comprised diabetes, pregnancy, severe renal/hepatic disease, active infections, or serious comorbidities to minimize inflammatory confounds.

Sample size and sampling

A purposive sampling technique was employed to recruit participants efficiently from the outpatient clinic. This non-probability method maximized enrollment of eligible adults attending routine checkups, targeting those with stable HIV (on antiretroviral therapy ≥ 12 months, virologically suppressed) or without HIV, and with or without hypertension.

Sample size calculation used G*Power for multiple linear regression to detect a medium effect size (f²=0.15) at significance level α = 0.05, statistical power of 90%, and up to 12 predictors, yielding a minimum required n = 158. The final cohort of n = 221 exceeded this threshold, providing adequate statistical power for the primary analysis. However, subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously due to reduced effective sample size and potential model instability.

Biomarker assessments

Following an overnight fast, venous blood was collected by standard venipuncture into multiple appropriately labelled evacuated tubes to ensure accurate assay results. Tubes and approximate draw volumes were selected based on the analytes of interest:

EDTA tubes (purple cap): used for haematological parameters, including complete blood count (WBC, RBC, lymphocyte count), red blood cell indices (MCV, MCH), and haemoglobin measurement, as EDTA prevents clotting and preserves cellular morphology.

Fluoride-oxalate tubes (grey cap): used for fasting glucose analysis because sodium fluoride inhibits glycolysis, stabilizing glucose in whole blood.

Plain serum tubes or serum separator tubes (SST): collected for lipid profile tests (total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL, LDL, VLDL, and HDL cholesterol) with subsequent serum separation following clotting, as well as for tests requiring serum input.

Lithium heparin tubes (green top): drawn for biochemical analyses including electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride) and for preparation of plasma used in clinical chemistry and selected immunoassays, since lithium heparin provides plasma with minimal interference in ion measurements. Plasma and serum were promptly separated by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Plasma cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and hormonal markers, such as aldosterone and angiotensin II, were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Elabscience®, China). Routine clinical chemistry analyses were performed using automated clinical chemistry analyzers for fasting glucose and lipid panel components. Electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride) and hematologic parameters, including complete blood counts, red blood cell indices, haemoglobin, and lymphocyte count, were measured using automated haematology analysers.

Flow-mediated dilatation (FMD) assessment

FMD of the brachial artery was assessed using high-resolution ultrasound in accordance with established international guidelines [11]. Participants rested in the supine position in a temperature-controlled room for at least 10 min before measurements. The brachial artery was imaged longitudinally above the antecubital fossa using a high-frequency linear-array transducer.

Baseline arterial diameter was recorded during end-diastole. An air-filled cuff was placed on the forearm and inflated to suprasystolic pressure for 5 min to induce arterial occlusion. Upon cuff deflation, reactive hyperemia was induced, and continuous ultrasound images were acquired to capture the maximal post-occlusion arterial diameter.

Automated edge-detection and diameter analysis were performed using Cardiovascular Suite 4 software (https://www.quipu.eu/cardiovascularsuite4/). The peak brachial artery diameter following cuff release was identified by the software.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Continuous variables were assessed for distributional properties using graphical methods (histograms and Q–Q plots). Due to evidence of non-normality, particularly for immunoglobulin E (IgE), data were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Given the markedly right-skewed distribution of IgE, a logarithmic transformation (log-IgE) was applied prior to regression analyses to improve normality and stabilize variance. The transformed variable was used as the dependent outcome in all multivariable models.

Associations between log-IgE and clinical, biochemical, and sociodemographic variables were assessed using multiple linear regression models. Variables included in the models were selected based on biological plausibility, prior literature, and univariate associations (p < 0.05), alongside clinically relevant covariates.

Model assumptions were formally evaluated. Normality of residuals was assessed using graphical methods, and homoscedasticity was tested using the Breusch–Pagan / Cook–Weisberg test. There was no evidence of heteroskedasticity (χ²(1) = 0.01, p = 0.9117), supporting the assumption of constant variance. Model fit and influence diagnostics were also examined to ensure robustness of the estimates.

Two multiple linear regression models were fitted. Model 1 was conducted in the overall study population. Model 2 was a subgroup analysis restricted to participants living with HIV and included the same predictors as Model 1 to assess whether the observed associations were consistent within the HIV-positive subgroup. Regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and corresponding p-values were reported. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using StatCrunch and GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with a threshold of > 10 indicating significant collinearity, leading to exclusion of selected variables. To account for multiple testing across predictors included in the regression models, p-values were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Both unadjusted and FDR-adjusted p-values are reported, and primary interpretation was based on FDR-corrected results.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study included 221 participants with a median age of 49 years (IQR: 40–59). The majority were female (68.3%) and unemployed (66.3%). Approximately half were not married (56.3%). The cohort had a high HIV prevalence of 64.7%, reflecting the endemic nature of HIV in the region. Hypertension was present in 29.0% of participants, with hypertensive heart disease in 10.2%. Smoking history was uncommon (4.24%), while tuberculosis history was reported in 13.8%. Peripheral neuropathy was present in 47.5% of participants.

Anthropometric and metabolic parameters showed a median BMI of 25.5 kg/m2 (IQR: 21–30), indicating overweight status in the cohort. Fasting glucose was within normal range at 4.8 mmol/L (IQR: 4.4–5.5). Lipid profiles showed median total cholesterol of 4.39 mmol/L (IQR: 4–5), triglycerides of 0.79 mmol/L (IQR: 0.6–1.8), LDL of 3.01 mmol/L (IQR: 2.4–3.7), VLDL of 0.36 mmol/L (IQR: 0.3–0.5), and HDL cholesterol of 1.18 mmol/L (IQR: 0.99–1.4).

Haematological parameters revealed median lymphocyte count of 1.8 × 109/L (IQR: 1.5–2.4), WBC of 3.4 × 109/L (IQR: 2.7–4.3), RBC of 4.65 × 10¹²/L (IQR: 4.25–5.05), haemoglobin of 13.4 g/dL (IQR: 13–15), MCV of 93 fL (IQR: 88–99), and MCH of 29.1 pg (IQR: 27–31).

Electrolyte measurements showed median sodium of 145 mmol/L (IQR: 144–146), chloride of 102 mmol/L (IQR: 100–105), and potassium of 4.0 mmol/L (IQR: 3.8–4.3), all within normal physiological ranges. Hormonal and inflammatory markers demonstrated wide variability: aldosterone 165 pg/mL (IQR: 84-6144), angiotensin II 1669 pg/mL (IQR: 44-4885), IFN-γ 1517 pg/mL (IQR: 28-2257), IL-1 (IQR: 2.7–211), IL-5 (IQR:12.83–1370) and IL-6 86 pg/mL (IQR: 0.8–109), reflecting heterogeneous inflammatory states within the cohort (Table 1).

Factor associated with IgE: multiple linear regression analysis (Model 1)

In this multivariable regression analysis, several clinical and biochemical parameters were evaluated for their independent association with the outcome. Notably, peripheral neuropathy (β = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.16 to 1.45, p = 0.014), serum creatinine (log-transformed) (β = 2.26, 95% CI: 0.80 to 3.73, p = 0.003), plasma chloride (β = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.21, p = 0.020) and flow-mediated dilation (FMD) (β = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.001 to 0.066, p = 0.043) showed a strong and statistically significant association with IgE. Discussion

This cross-sectional study of 221 adults in an HIV-endemic Zambian setting identified associations between serum IgE levels and vascular, immune, renal, electrolyte, behavioral, and sociodemographic factors. Specifically, IgE was associated with flow-mediated dilation, plasma chloride, serum creatinine, and the presence of peripheral neuropathy. These findings add to growing evidence that IgE reflects broader systemic immune and cardiometabolic processes beyond its classical role in allergic disease, particularly in populations characterized by a high burden of infection and chronic inflammation. However, after adjustment for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate approach, only serum creatinine remained significantly associated with IgE, while the other associations were attenuated and should be interpreted as exploratory.

A key observation of this study is the positive association between IgE and FMD, indicating that higher IgE levels were associated with higher endothelial-dependent vasodilation. This direction of association does not support endothelial dysfunction, rather, it suggests that IgE may be linked to adaptive or context-dependent vascular responses in the setting of immune activation. Experimental evidence indicates that IgE can modulate vascular function through FcεRI-mediated activation of mast cells and subsequent release of cytokines and vasoactive mediators, which may influence endothelial signaling pathways [12]. However, clinical data linking IgE to functional vascular outcomes remain limited and inconsistent, and the present findings should be interpreted as associative rather than mechanistic. This unexpected direction of association may also reflect residual confounding or measurement variability and should be interpreted cautiously.

The most robust and consistent finding in this study was the association between IgE and serum creatinine, which remained significant after false discovery rate correction. This relationship suggests a potential link between immune activation and renal function. Chronic inflammation has been implicated in the pathogenesis of kidney dysfunction, particularly in people living with HIV, where immune activation persists despite viral suppression [13]. Elevated IgE levels may reflect immune-mediated processes that contribute to renal injury or altered renal clearance mechanisms. However, whether IgE plays a direct role in renal pathology or simply reflects underlying inflammatory burden cannot be determined from this study.

An important contextual factor in interpreting these findings is the markedly elevated IgE levels observed in this cohort, which are substantially higher than typical reference ranges in non-atopic populations. In sub-Saharan Africa, such elevations are commonly driven by chronic exposure to helminthic and other parasitic infections, which induce strong Th2 immune responses and polyclonal IgE production [14]. The absence of direct measurement of parasitic infection in this study represents a major limitation, as helminth exposure is likely a primary determinant of IgE levels in this setting. Consequently, IgE in this population may function more as a non-specific marker of immune activation shaped by environmental and infectious exposures, rather than a biomarker of cardiometabolic disease.

The observed associations between IgE and electrolyte-related variables, particularly plasma chloride and potassium, are novel and warrant cautious interpretation. Electrolytes play an emerging role in immune regulation, with potassium flux recognized as a key regulator of inflammasome activation and cytokine release [15]. However, direct links between electrolyte balance and IgE regulation remain poorly characterized, and these findings should be considered exploratory until validated in mechanistic studies.

IgE was also associated with markers of immune activation, including IL-1 and lymphocyte count in unadjusted analyses, although these associations did not persist after correction for multiple testing. IL-1 is a central mediator of inflammatory pathways implicated in cardiovascular disease, and its inhibition has been shown to reduce cardiovascular events in high-risk populations [16]. However, the relationship between IL-1 and IgE likely reflects shared upstream immune activation pathways rather than a direct regulatory interaction, particularly in the context of chronic infection and immune dysregulation.

In people living with HIV, several associations, including those with creatinine, chloride, salt taste preference, and FMD remained significant after correction for multiple testing. This suggests that HIV-related immune activation may amplify the relationships between IgE and cardiorenal parameters. Persistent immune activation and inflammation are well-established contributors to cardiovascular and renal disease in HIV, even in individuals receiving effective antiretroviral therapy [17]. Within this framework, IgE may represent an additional marker of immune dysregulation rather than a causal factor.

Behavioral and sociodemographic factors, including smoking and salt taste preference, were also associated with IgE in subgroup analyses. Smoking is known to modulate immune responses and has been associated with altered IgE levels and increased cardiovascular risk [18]. The association with salt taste preference is novel and may reflect dietary influences on immune function, although this remains speculative and requires further investigation.

Overall, the findings of this study support a conceptual framework in which IgE reflects integrated immune–cardiorenal interactions, particularly in environments characterized by high infectious burden and chronic immune activation. However, the attenuation of most associations after multiple testing correction highlights the importance of cautious interpretation and underscores the exploratory nature of these findings.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, rather than clinically actionable. While IgE demonstrated associations with vascular, renal, and inflammatory markers, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference regarding causality or temporal relationships. Accordingly, IgE cannot be considered a biomarker of cardiovascular or renal risk based on the present data.

The observed relationships likely reflect broader immune activation states, particularly within an HIV-endemic setting characterized by persistent inflammation and high infectious burden. Importantly, the markedly elevated IgE levels observed in this cohort strongly suggest underlying exposure to helminth or other parasitic infections, which are well-established drivers of IgE production in sub-Saharan Africa. This substantially limits the specificity of IgE as a cardiometabolic marker in this context.

Although the association between IgE and IL-1 aligns with established inflammatory pathways implicated in cardiovascular disease, therapeutic implications should be interpreted with caution. Interleukin-1 inhibition has demonstrated cardiovascular benefit in selected high-risk populations [16], but these findings cannot be extrapolated to IgE-mediated processes without direct evidence.

Overall, these findings underscore the need for integrated immune–cardiorenal research frameworks, particularly in populations with overlapping burdens of infectious and non-communicable diseases. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are required to determine whether IgE has prognostic value or reflects broader immune dysregulation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, and the single time-point assessment of FMD may be influenced by short-term physiological or environmental factors. Total IgE was measured rather than antigen-specific IgE, which may mask heterogeneous effects. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including parasitic infections, lifestyle, body composition, and genetic variation, cannot be excluded. The sample was drawn from a single HIV-endemic African setting, limiting generalizability, and the study relied on surrogate markers rather than clinical cardiovascular or renal outcomes. A further limitation is the substantial variability observed in aldosterone and angiotensin II concentrations, which may reflect both biological fluctuation and potential measurement variability inherent to these biomarkers. Although samples were processed under standardized conditions using validated assays, residual variability cannot be fully excluded. To mitigate this, aldosterone was log-transformed and angiotensin II was excluded from multivariable models following multicollinearity assessment. Nonetheless, these factors should be considered when interpreting findings related to renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system markers.

A key limitation of this study is the absence of direct assessment of parasitic or helminthic infections, which are highly prevalent in this setting and represent a major determinant of circulating IgE levels. The markedly elevated IgE concentrations observed in this cohort likely reflect, at least in part, underlying parasitic exposure. As such, parasitic infections constitute a significant unmeasured confounder that may influence both IgE levels and the observed associations with vascular, renal, and inflammatory markers. The lack of parasitic screening limits the specificity of IgE as a biomarker and constrains causal interpretation of the findings. Future studies incorporating direct measures of parasitic burden and immune profiling will be essential to disentangle these relationships.

Conclusion

This study reveals that IgE is associated with selected cardiorenal and immunological markers in an HIV-endemic population, although most associations did not remain robust after correction for multiple testing. The consistent association with renal function suggests that IgE may reflect broader systemic immune activation rather than a specific pathophysiological driver. These findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Longitudinal and mechanistic studies are required to clarify causality and potential clinical relevance.
