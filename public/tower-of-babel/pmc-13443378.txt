Bilateral transcutaneous auricular nerve stimulation promotes motor function recovery after stroke: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jia Wang, Qiuyan Liu, Yifan Zhou, Chao Peng, Xiaoyang Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVagus nerve stimulation (VNS) has emerged as a promising adjunctive approach for stroke rehabilitation. However, current clinical applications are predominantly limited to invasive VNS implantation or unilateral transcutaneous auricular nerve stimulation (taNS), often characterized by small sample sizes and a primary focus on upper limb motor recovery. Although emerging evidence suggests that bilateral taNS (BtaNS) may confer superior neuromodulatory effects compared with conventional left-sided unilateral taNS (LtaNS), its efficacy and safety profile for comprehensive post-stroke limb motor rehabilitation remain largely unexplored.ObjectivesTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of BtaNS combined with routine rehabilitation in promoting motor recovery in post-stroke patients with hemiplegia.DesignA prospective, double-blinded, three-arm randomized controlled trial.MethodsPatients with subacute or chronic stroke were recruited from the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2025 and December 2025. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: BtaNS, LtaNS, or a control group. All patients received routine rehabilitation therapy targeting both upper and lower limb motor function. Additionally, patients in the taNS groups received either LtaNS or BtaNS (stimulation parameters: 20 Hz frequency, 300 µs pulse width, intensity individually adjusted within a range of 0.5–1.0 mA) for 30 minutes twice daily, six days per week, over a four-week period. The primary outcome measures included the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity (FMA-UE), Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Lower Extremities (FMA-LE) and the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT). Secondary outcomes encompassed the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), the Modified Barthel Index (MBI), and low-frequency oscillations measured via functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). Adverse events were monitored to assess safety throughout the treatment period.ResultsA total of 111 patients were enrolled in this study, of whom 5 withdrew and 4 were lost to follow-up, resulting in 102 participants completing the trial (34 per group). Compared with the control group, both the LtaNS and BtaNS groups exhibited significantly greater improvements in the FMA-UE, FMA-LE, and WMFT scores at all post-intervention assessment time points. Moreover, the BtaNS group demonstrated superior motor recovery relative to the LtaNS group. In addition, BtaNS was associated with significantly greater improvements in BBS and MBI scores at 4 weeks post-intervention and at follow-up compared with both the LtaNS and control groups. Subgroup analyses indicated that patients with a shorter time since stroke onset and those diagnosed with hemorrhagic stroke exhibited more pronounced improvements. No serious adverse events occurred; only mild, transient skin irritation was observed.ConclusionBtaNS is a safe and well-tolerated intervention that demonstrates superior efficacy compared to LtaNS in enhancing motor recovery, balance, and independence in activities of daily living following stroke. The underlying mechanism may be associated with increased bilateral cortical engagement.RegistrationThis study was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2400093825.

1. Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide and represents a major public health burden, particularly in aging societies.1,2 Advances in acute stroke management have substantially reduced mortality. However, a large proportion of stroke survivors continue to experience persistent neurological impairments. 3 Among these, motor deficits—especially of the upper limbs—are the most common and disabling, severely limiting patients’ independence in activities of daily living and overall quality of life. Motor recovery after stroke is often incomplete, with more than half of survivors failing to regain adequate upper limb function, even after receiving routine rehabilitation. 4 This limited recovery not only restricts functional independence but also imposes substantial socioeconomic and caregiving burdens on patients, families, and healthcare systems. The primary goal of post-stroke rehabilitation is to facilitate adaptive neuroplasticity that supports functional reorganization and behavioral recovery. However, spontaneous post-stroke plasticity is frequently insufficient or maladaptive, particularly during the subacute and chronic stages of recovery. 5 Without appropriate modulation, these maladaptive processes may reinforce abnormal movement patterns and compensatory strategies, exacerbate interhemispheric imbalance, and ultimately lead to long-term disability. Therefore, there is a critical need for adjunctive therapeutic strategies that can enhance the effectiveness of routine rehabilitation by promoting more favorable neurophysiological responses.6–8

To enhance neurorehabilitation outcomes, adjunctive neuromodulatory interventions such as vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) have gained attention. VNS has received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical use in epilepsy and treatment-resistant depression. 9 Recent studies have further demonstrated its potential to enhance motor function recovery in patients with stroke. 10 While traditional VNS requires surgical implantation, limiting accessibility and posing surgical risks, left-sided transcutaneous auricular stimulation (taNS) has emerged as a non-invasive alternative that targets the auricular branch of the vagus nerve. 11 Currently, there is ongoing controversy regarding the terminology of transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation and transcutaneous auricular nerve stimulation, because the innervation of the ear is highly complex and not exclusively mediated by the vagus nerve. In addition, due to the complexity of the anatomical structure, many of the therapeutic mechanisms of taNS remain unclear, and whether its effects are truly mediated through vagal pathways is still speculative. Therefore, whether the mechanisms of transcutaneous auricular stimulation described in this paper are consistent with the effects of direct vagus nerve stimulation requires further investigation.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that taNS paired with physical rehabilitation can significantly improve upper limb motor function after stroke. 12 In 2021, Dawson et al. conducted a series of randomized, double-blind clinical trials showing that implanted VNS combined with motor training significantly improved upper limb motor function in patients with ischemic stroke compared with sham VNS combined with motor training.13,14 Since then, increasing attention has been directed toward the application of non-invasive taNS for the recovery of post-stroke motor dysfunction. Bashar et al. subsequently reported in a clinical trial that closed-loop motor-activated auricular vagus nerve stimulation (MAAVNS) significantly improved upper limb Fugl–Meyer motor scores and enhanced quality of life in stroke patients. 15 However, the overall evidence remains heterogeneous across studies. While some randomized controlled trials have demonstrated significant improvements in motor outcomes, others have reported modest or inconsistent effects, particularly at long-term follow-up or under varying stimulation protocols. For example, a systematic review by Li et al. reported that although short-term motor improvements were observed, the overall effect size was limited and not consistently sustained over time. 16 Similarly, another study by Li et al. highlighted that differences in stimulation timing, patient characteristics, and rehabilitation paradigms may contribute to variability in clinical outcomes. 17 Moreover, previous studies suggest that bilateral transcutaneous auricular nerve stimulation (BtaNS) activation may provide greater sensory afferent input to brainstem pathways, thereby potentiating the therapeutic effects of VNS. Compared with unilateral stimulation, bilateral auricular stimulation appears to produce more pronounced neuromodulatory benefits. 18 However, the optimal laterality of taNS remains a subject of ongoing debate.

Recent neuroimaging evidence suggests that the effects of vagus nerve stimulation may depend on the interaction between stimulation side and lesion location. For instance, a functional MRI study by Peng et al. demonstrated that ipsilesional unilateral stimulation preferentially enhances activation of the affected motor cortex, whereas bilateral stimulation may lead to a relative “signal dilution” effect. 19 These findings highlight the importance of considering laterality-specific neuromodulation strategies in stroke rehabilitation. Nevertheless, bilateral stimulation may offer distinct advantages at the network level. By simultaneously engaging afferent inputs from both auricular branches of the vagus nerve, bilateral taNS may facilitate more balanced interhemispheric activation and promote large-scale motor network reorganization. Given that post-stroke motor impairment is closely associated with interhemispheric imbalance, bilateral stimulation may provide complementary benefits beyond focal cortical activation.

Despite promising findings, most clinical trials have focused on upper limbs, used unilateral taNS, involved small sample sizes, and included limited patient populations. There remains a critical gap in high-quality randomized trials evaluating the efficacy and safety of bilateral taNS in patients at least 14 days post-stroke, which represents a key subacute window for rehabilitation gains. 12 To address this gap, we conducted a double-blind, randomized controlled trial enrolling patients with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke at least 14 days post-onset. We compared the outcomes of BtaNS, left transcutaneous auricular nerve stimulation (LtaNS), and sham stimulation, all combined with routine rehabilitation training. This study aimed to evaluate whether BtaNS leads to greater improvements in motor function recovery than unilateral taNS and the control interventions, while maintaining a favorable safety profile in stroke populations.

2. Subjects and methods

2.1. Study design

The study was a three-group, double-blind, randomized controlled trial. Stroke patients were enrolled and randomly assigned (1:1:1) to one of three groups: bilateral taNS, left-sided taNS, or control. Assessments were conducted at baseline, after 2 weeks of treatment, after 4 weeks of treatment, and at an 8-week follow-up. All patients signed informed consent forms. Both the participants and the outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation throughout the study period. This project was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (approval No. IIT-2024 (736)) on October 7, 2024 (Additional file 1) and registered with Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration No. ChiCTR2400093825) on December 12, 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Good Clinical Practice and the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki. Deidentified individual participant data will be shared; related documents, such as the study protocol and statistical analysis plan, will also be made available. The data will become accessible within five years. Researchers may request access through the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.

2.2. Subjects

Study participants were recruited from January 1, 2025, to December 31, 2025, at The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. All eligible participants (aged 18–65 years) had a confirmed diagnosis of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke made by qualified clinical physicians according to the Chinese Guidelines for Stroke Prevention and Treatment (2021). To be included in the trial, participants were required to be in the recovery phase of stroke, with a time since stroke onset greater than two weeks, stable vital signs, and no evidence of disease progression. A face-to-face screening process, administered by the clinical trial coordinator, was utilized to ensure adherence to the study criteria. 2.3. Randomization and masking

Participants were enrolled by trained rehabilitation specialists after confirming eligibility and obtaining informed consent. Randomization was performed using a stratified block randomization method, with stratification based on stroke type (ischemic or hemorrhagic) to ensure balanced allocation across clinically relevant subgroups. The randomization sequence was generated using a computer-based random number generator (SPSS version 26.0) by an independent statistician who was not involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment. A permuted block design with a fixed block size of 6 was used to ensure equal allocation across the three groups (BtaNS, LtaNS, and control). Group assignments were placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by a study coordinator. Investigators opened the envelopes in sequence after participant enrollment to determine group allocation.

To maintain blinding, all taNS devices used in the study were pre-programmed in visually and functionally identical A/B modes by a third-party technician not involved in patient care or assessment. This ensured that participants, therapists, device operators, and outcome assessors remained blinded to group allocation. All participants, regardless of group assignment, wore the same taNS headset and followed identical stimulation procedures in appearance and timing. For the control group, no electrical current was delivered (0 mA), while in the LtaNS group, only the left ear received active stimulation. Only the unblinded supervisor had access to the randomization key and could perform emergency unblinding if medically necessary.

2.4. Intervention

All participants received a standardized 4-week intervention program consisting of twice-daily sessions, six days per week, conducted in an inpatient rehabilitation setting. Each session included taNS (or sham stimulation) followed immediately by routine rehabilitation therapy. The taNS intervention was delivered using a non-invasive auricular electrical nerve stimulation device (JY-VNS-200; Jingyi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Jiangxi, China, Figure 2(a)). The device utilized two carbon-impregnated silicone electrodes mounted on a headphone-like apparatus, targeting the cymba conchae and cavum conchae regions of the auricle, which are innervated by the auricular branch of the vagus nerve (Figure 2(b)). In the BtaNS group, active stimulation was administered bilaterally to both ears. In the LtaNS group, active stimulation was delivered to the left ear only, while the right ear received sham stimulation (0 mA). The control group wore the same device with identical electrode placement and session duration but received no electrical stimulation (0 mA on both sides). The stimulation parameters were standardized as follows: frequency of 20 Hz, pulse width of 300 µs, and square wave pulses. Current intensity ranged from 0.5 to 1 mA and was individually adjusted in 0.5 mA increments to achieve a mild but tolerable sensation without discomfort. Each stimulation session lasted 30 minutes. Prior to each session, the electrode sites were cleansed with alcohol to reduce impedance and optimize conductivity. Participants were continuously monitored for behavioral responses and discomfort.Figure 2.The schematic diagram of bilateral transcutaneous auricular nerve stimulation implementation. Immediately following each stimulation session, all participants received a 30-minute session of routine rehabilitation therapy. The rehabilitation program targeted both upper and lower limb motor function and consisted of a combination of task-oriented training, postural control exercises, balance training, gait training, and sensory integration therapy. The specific content was individualized according to each patient’s functional status but followed a predefined standardized protocol to ensure consistency across participants. Each participant received two stimulation sessions per day, and each session was paired with one rehabilitation session. Therefore, all participants underwent two rehabilitation sessions per day, six days per week, for four consecutive weeks, ensuring that rehabilitation dosage was equivalent across all groups. All rehabilitation sessions were delivered by licensed physiotherapists with at least 5 years of experience in stroke rehabilitation. Therapists received standardized training prior to study initiation to ensure uniform implementation of the rehabilitation protocol. To maintain double-blind conditions, the taNS device was pre-set in visually indistinguishable A/B modes by a third-party technician not involved in treatment delivery or outcome assessment. All procedures, including device handling and monitoring, were identical across groups. Treatment adherence was monitored using daily treatment logs, and all sessions were supervised by senior rehabilitation staff to ensure protocol compliance and treatment fidelity. The rehabilitation protocol was kept consistent across all groups to minimize potential confounding effects and to isolate the contribution of taNS to functional recovery.

2.5. Outcome measures

Outcome assessments were performed at four time points: baseline (prior to treatment), after 2 weeks, after the 4-week intervention, and at the 8-week follow-up. The primary outcomes were motor function of the upper and lower limbs, evaluated using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity (FMA-UE; score range 0–66) and the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Lower Extremity (FMA-LE; score range 0–34), and the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), which assesses upper limb function through 15 timed tasks (total score range 0–75). Higher scores in both the FMA and WMFT indicate better motor performance. The FMA is a widely validated quantitative measure of sensorimotor recovery following stroke, while the WMFT has been confirmed to be reliable and valid for upper extremity functional evaluation in stroke rehabilitation. 20 The FMA is considered highly sensitive to motor recovery, particularly during the subacute stage after stroke. However, its responsiveness may be reduced in later stages or in patients with mild impairment due to potential ceiling effects. The secondary outcomes included the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), which measures balance ability on a 0–56 scale (higher scores indicate better balance); and the Modified Barthel Index (MBI), which assesses performance in daily activities with scores ranging from 0 to 100.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) imaging was used to explore cortical changes in brain regions associated with motor control. fNIRS data were collected before and after the 4-week intervention, targeting areas such as the primary motor cortex (M1), supplementary motor area (SMA), premotor cortex (PMC), and sensorimotor cortex (SMC). Resting-state fNIRS data were collected in a subset of participants before and after the intervention to explore cortical hemodynamic changes associated with taNS. Due to equipment availability and patient feasibility, fNIRS assessments were performed in a subgroup of participants (BtaNS: n = 6; LtaNS: n = 7; Control: n = 6). Participants were included based on their ability to complete the imaging protocol with acceptable data quality, without predefined clinical selection criteria.

Data acquisition was performed using a continuous-wave, multichannel near-infrared optical imaging system (NirScan-6000A; HuiChuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Danyang, China). The system consisted of 15 light sources and 16 detectors, forming 48 measurement channels. The source–detector distance was approximately 3 cm, and optodes were positioned according to the international 10/20 system, covering bilateral frontal and temporal cortical regions. Changes in oxyhemoglobin (HbO) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) concentrations were recorded in real time. Raw optical signals were processed and analyzed using NirSpark software (HuiChuang Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China). The preprocessing pipeline included motion artifact correction, band-pass filtering, conversion of optical density signals to hemoglobin concentration changes (hemodynamic signals), and block averaging. Feature extraction focused on low-frequency oscillations of HbO signals. In addition, a general linear model (GLM) was applied to estimate beta coefficients, which reflect condition-related hemodynamic response magnitude across channels. Given the resting-state design and the relatively small sample size, the fNIRS analysis was considered exploratory. Therefore, the results are presented primarily in a descriptive manner, and no formal statistical inference was performed.

Safety outcomes included the monitoring and documentation of all adverse events (AEs) throughout the trial. The assessed AEs included ear-related symptoms (such as ear pain, pruritus, and skin redness), as well as headache, dizziness, nausea, constipation, and other systemic events. Vital signs such as heart rate and blood pressure were measured before and after stimulation sessions using a patient monitor. For each day, the values obtained from the two sessions were averaged to calculate a daily mean. These daily mean values were then used for subsequent analysis to reflect overall hemodynamic responses during the intervention period. Additionally, the integrity of the skin at stimulation sites was observed for signs of irritation (e.g., redness, blistering, pain), and patient-reported discomfort was documented to assess treatment tolerability.

2.6. Sample size

The required sample size was calculated based on effect sizes reported in prior randomized controlled trials investigating vagus nerve stimulation for post-stroke motor recovery. Referring to the VNS-REHAB trial conducted by Dawson et al., which reported a between-group effect size (Cohen’s d) of approximately 0.632 for upper limb motor function improvement, the corresponding effect size was converted to Cohen’s f of 0.316 for use in a three-arm comparison model. 13 Using G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany), with a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and power (1–β) of 80%, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 34 participants per group. We acknowledge that the effect size derived from comparisons between active VNS and sham stimulation may not directly correspond to differences between bilateral and unilateral stimulation. The latter comparison may yield a smaller effect size due to both groups receiving active intervention. However, evidence from non-invasive taNS studies, such as the pilot trial by Badran et al., which reported a comparable effect size (d ≈ 0.63) for motor recovery outcomes, provides additional support for the chosen estimate. 15 To account for an estimated 10% dropout rate, the target sample size was increased to 38 participants per group, resulting in a total of 114 participants across the three study arms (BtaNS, LtaNS, and control). This sample size ensures adequate statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences in FMA outcomes among groups over time.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviations), and categorical variables as counts and/or frequencies. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For data that were normally distributed with homogeneous variances, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to compare group differences over time, followed by Bonferroni correction for post hoc multiple comparisons when appropriate. Baseline differences among groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, with LSD or Games–Howell tests applied for post hoc comparisons depending on variance homogeneity. For data that did not meet normality or homogeneity assumptions, repeated-measures data were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE), with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. Baseline differences were assessed using nonparametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis test), followed by Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc comparisons when significant differences were observed. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, while ordinal data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Additionally, two-way ANOVA was used to examine the effects of relevant variables on treatment outcomes across groups. The percentage of population in bar charts were used to illustrate group differences in balance and activities of daily living (ADL) at week 12. The final behavioral outcome analysis was performed using a per-protocol approach, including only participants who completed the intervention and follow-up assessments with available behavioral outcome data. Participants who withdrew or were lost to follow-up were not included in the final behavioral analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0.

3. Results

3.1. Study participants

A total of 114 participants were assessed for eligibility, of whom 3 were excluded (2 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 1 declined to participate). The remaining 111 participants underwent randomization with allocation concealment and were equally assigned to three groups: BtaNS (n = 37), LtaNS (n = 37), and control (n = 37). During the intervention and follow-up period, 5 participants withdrew from the study, including two from the BtaNS group (one due to early discharge, one due to treatment intolerance), one from the LtaNS group (due to adverse event unrelated to intervention), and two from the control group (one due to recurrent stroke, one voluntarily withdrew). In addition, 4 participants were lost to follow-up due to loss of contact (1 in the BtaNS group, 2 in the LtaNS group, and 1 in the control group). Ultimately, behavioral outcome data were available for 34 participants in each group. Therefore, the final behavioral outcome analysis was conducted using a per-protocol approach based on the 102 participants who completed the intervention and follow-up assessments; the 9 participants who withdrew or were lost to follow-up were not included in the final behavioral analysis. A subset of participants additionally completed resting-state fNIRS assessments, including 6 participants in the BtaNS group, 7 in the LtaNS group, and 6 in the control group. 3.3. Functional outcomes

At baseline, there were no significant differences among the three groups in scores of the FMA-UE, FMA-LE, WMFT, BBS, or MBI (all P > 0.05). Following the intervention, a clear time-by-group interaction was observed. Both the BtaNS and LtaNS groups showed significant improvements over time in FMA-UE, FMA-LE, and WMFT scores. In contrast, the control group showed minimal changes in these measures. At 2 and 4 weeks, the LtaNS group showed significantly greater improvements than the control group in most motor outcomes (P < 0.05). 3.4. Subgroup analysis

To further explore factors influencing the efficacy of taNS on motor recovery, subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, disease duration, and stroke etiology using the WMFT time and score as dependent variables (Figure 7, Supplementary Table 3). For WMFT time, both the LtaNS and BtaNS groups exhibited significantly shorter task completion times compared with the control group across all subgroups (P < 0.001). The treatment benefit of bilateral taNS remained consistent regardless of age (<45, 45–60, or ≥60 years), but a significant interaction was observed for time since stroke onset (P for interaction = 0.006) and stroke etiology (P for interaction = 0.018). Patients with a shorter disease duration (≤1 month) and those with hemorrhagic stroke demonstrated greater improvements in WMFT time reduction, suggesting that earlier intervention and hemorrhagic etiology are associated with a greater treatment response. Importantly, baseline functional scores, including FMA-UE, FMA-LE, WMFT score, and WMFT time, were comparable across the disease duration and stroke etiology subgroups, suggesting that the observed subgroup differences were unlikely to be driven by baseline functional imbalance. 4. Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to assess the efficacy and safety of BtaNS in enhancing motor function recovery in patients with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke. Our findings demonstrate that taNS significantly enhances motor function recovery in patients during the recovery phase of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, and that BtaNS produces superior improvements in motor performance, balance, and activities of daily living compared with LtaNS or sham stimulation. The fNIRS demonstrated increased HbO signal changes in the primary motor cortex, premotor cortex, and supplementary motor area (Figure 9). In recent years, VNS has been applied to a wide range of neurological disorders, including epilepsy, traumatic brain injury (TBI), Parkinson’s disease, and stroke.22–25 However, the traditional cervical VNS approach involves surgical implantation, which carries risks such as vocal cord paralysis, dysphagia, and wound infection, with nearly 50% of patients requiring revision surgery due to battery exhaustion or hardware failure. 26 As a safer, noninvasive alternative, taNS stimulates the auricular branch of the vagus nerve via the ear, and growing evidence supports its neuromodulatory effect on key central brain structures involved in stroke recovery.27,28 Furthermore, literature indicates that activating the vagus nerves in both ears can increase sensory input to the brainstem nerve conduction pathway and enhance the therapeutic effect of vagus nerve electrical stimulation. Notably, the efficacy of bilateral stimulation appears superior to that of unilateral stimulation.18,29 This new non-invasive BtaNS technology is expected to be used in the treatment of patients after stroke.

Although patients in the BtaNS, LtaNS, and control groups all demonstrated functional improvements after treatment, the magnitude of improvement in functional outcomes was significantly greater in patients receiving taNS, with the effects being more pronounced in the BtaNS group than in the LtaNS group. These findings indicate that the addition of taNS provides greater therapeutic benefits than routine rehabilitation therapy alone, and that BtaNS confers superior advantages compared with unilateral stimulation. Our data support the view that the synergistic effect of taNS combined with physical rehabilitation depends on neural plasticity, a time-dependent phenomenon, and that the therapeutic benefits persist for up to two months after cessation of stimulation. 30 Previous studies have reported cumulative effects of taNS in stroke patients.21,31 However, these investigations were limited to unilateral vagus nerve stimulation and primarily focused on upper limb recovery, with little attention paid to bilateral stimulation or lower limb outcomes. Other studies have explored the effects of LtaNS in patients with subacute ischemic stroke, reporting an approximately 46% difference in FMA-UE recovery at 3 months following 16 consecutive days of taNS. 21 Another study demonstrated an improvement of approximately 28% after 10 treatment sessions in stroke patients. 31 In the present study, after 4 weeks of LtaNS and BtaNS treatment, the degree of improvement observed at 1 month was highly encouraging. Specifically, FMA-UE and FMA-LE scores increased by 11.3 and 6.8 points from baseline in the LtaNS group, and by 15.8 and 10.5 points, respectively, in the BtaNS group. Moreover, during the 8-week follow-up period, BBS and MBI scores in the LtaNS group continued to increase by 2.7 and 2.4 points compared with the end of the 4-week treatment period, while corresponding increases of 3.1 and 2.2 points were observed in the BtaNS group. From the second week onward, both the BtaNS and LtaNS groups showed significantly greater improvements in FMA-UE, FMA-LE, and WMFT scores compared with the control group. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that early rehabilitation strategies combined with taNS are effective in promoting functional recovery in stroke patients.

The resting-state fNIRS results provide neurophysiological evidence for differential modulation of intrinsic activity within core motor-related cortical regions—namely the M1, PMC, and SMA—across the control, LtaNS, and BtaNS groups. Resting-state fNIRS has been increasingly used to characterize motor-network organization and recovery-related changes in M1/PMC/SMA after stroke.32,33 Before the intervention, HbO signal levels within these regions were relatively limited and broadly comparable among the three groups. After the intervention, all groups demonstrated increases in HbO signals involving M1, PMC, and SMA. However, the magnitude, spatial extent, and hemispheric distribution of these changes differed substantially. In the control group, post-intervention HbO signal changes within M1/PMC/SMA were modest and spatially restricted, suggesting that routine rehabilitation alone yields limited modulation of baseline motor-network activity. This interpretation is consistent with contemporary models of stroke recovery emphasizing that functional gains depend on the degree of motor-network reorganization rather than isolated changes in a single cortical site. 34 In contrast, the LtaNS group exhibited greater increases in HbO signal changes within these regions, but the pattern appeared relatively lateralized, indicating that unilateral stimulation may preferentially augment motor-network engagement within a dominant hemisphere. Prior clinical studies of unilateral taNS in stroke have also demonstrated meaningful motor improvement, supporting the capacity of taNS to enhance recovery-related neuroplasticity beyond rehabilitation alone. 21 Notably, the BtaNS group showed the most robust and widespread post-intervention HbO signal changes across M1, PMC, and SMA, characterized by greater amplitude and a more symmetrical bilateral distribution of HbO signals. Compared with both the control and LtaNS groups, BtaNS was associated with broader patterns of hemodynamic changes and improved interhemispheric balance within core motor areas.

Interhemispheric imbalance is a well-recognized feature of post-stroke motor impairment, and recent neurophysiological work combining fNIRS and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has highlighted the relevance of interhemispheric measures for understanding and predicting motor recovery. Given the complementary roles of M1 in motor output, PMC in motor set and sensorimotor integration, and SMA in motor initiation/coordination at the network level, a more bilateral and integrated recruitment of these regions may reflect a more coordinated pattern of motor-network reorganization. 35 This neurophysiological profile provides a plausible basis for the superior motor recovery observed in the BtaNS group relative to unilateral stimulation and rehabilitation alone, aligning with broader evidence that VNS-based neuromodulation can potentiate recovery processes after stroke.36,37 It is important to note that the optimal laterality of stimulation remains controversial. While some studies suggest that ipsilesional unilateral stimulation may more effectively target the affected motor cortex, bilateral stimulation may exert broader network-level modulation. 19 In our study, the more symmetrical and widespread cortical activation observed in the BtaNS group may reflect enhanced interhemispheric coordination rather than focal activation alone. This distinction may help reconcile differing findings across studies and suggests that the therapeutic effects of taNS may depend on both focal and network-level mechanisms.

It is important to interpret these findings within the context of established theories of post-stroke recovery. Classical models emphasize the “focalization” hypothesis, whereby functional recovery is associated with a shift in cortical activity toward the ipsilesional hemisphere. In contrast, the present study observed more symmetrical patterns of cortical hemodynamic changes in the BtaNS group. Rather than contradicting the focalization model, this pattern may reflect enhanced interhemispheric coordination and network-level reorganization. It is possible that bilateral neuromodulation facilitates interhemispheric communication, thereby supporting functional recovery through mechanisms that extend beyond focal cortical hemodynamic changes. However, given the exploratory nature of the fNIRS analysis and the absence of statistical testing, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further studies are needed to clarify the relationship between bilateral modulation, interhemispheric balance, and focal recovery processes.

From a neurophysiological perspective, taNS is hypothesized to modulate afferent pathways projecting to brainstem structures such as the nucleus tractus solitarius, which may in turn influence broader neuromodulatory systems, including the locus coeruleus.11,38 Evidence from invasive vagus nerve stimulation studies suggests that such pathways may be associated with norepinephrine release and processes related to synaptic plasticity, motor learning, and cortical reorganization. However, whether taNS engages these mechanisms to the same extent remains speculative. 39 Preclinical studies of invasive vagus nerve stimulation have reported anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects in models of TBI, including modulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and pyroptosis.40–42 These findings may provide indirect and hypothesis-generating support for potential mechanisms of transcutaneous auricular nerve stimulation, although direct evidence in taNS remains limited. Bilateral stimulation may amplify these effects by simultaneously engaging vagal afferent inputs from both sides, thereby facilitating more coordinated and robust network-level plasticity.18,43

An important consideration is the temporal relationship between stimulation and motor activity. Previous studies, such as the VNS-REHAB and MAAVNS trials, have emphasized the importance of precise temporal pairing between vagus nerve stimulation and motor task execution to induce spike-timing-dependent plasticity.13,15 In contrast, the present study adopted a sequential “stimulation-then-rehabilitation” protocol, which does not provide exact temporal coupling. This difference may influence the underlying mechanisms of action. One possible explanation is that taNS may exert its effects through neuromodulatory priming. By increasing the release of neuromodulators such as norepinephrine and acetylcholine, taNS may enhance cortical excitability and create a favorable neural environment for subsequent motor training.44,45 In this context, physical rehabilitation may benefit from a preconditioned neural state, even in the absence of precise timing. However, the lack of task-synchronized stimulation may limit the engagement of timing-dependent plasticity mechanisms, and therefore the observed effects should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should explore closed-loop or task-triggered taNS paradigms to better align with established neuroplasticity principles.

Recent evidence has raised important questions regarding the optimal laterality of taNS. In particular, Peng et al. reported that ipsilesional unilateral stimulation preferentially enhanced hemodynamic responses in the affected motor cortex, and in some cases showed greater focal cortical engagement compared with bilateral stimulation. 19 These findings suggest that laterality-matched stimulation may provide more targeted neuromodulation. However, our findings appear to differ, as bilateral stimulation resulted in greater functional improvement compared with unilateral stimulation. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in outcome measures and underlying mechanisms. While Peng et al. focused on neuroimaging-based cortical activation, the present study evaluated longitudinal functional recovery. 19 It is possible that ipsilesional stimulation enhances focal cortical excitability, whereas bilateral stimulation may facilitate broader network-level reorganization, including interhemispheric balance, which is critical for post-stroke recovery. Furthermore, differences in stimulation paradigms, including timing relative to motor training and cumulative treatment exposure, may also contribute to the divergent findings. Importantly, an alternative explanation should also be considered. Our study did not incorporate lesion laterality into the study design or analysis, and therefore could not differentiate the effects of ipsilesional, contralesional, or bilateral stimulation. As highlighted by Peng et al., the interaction between stimulation side and lesion location may critically influence both cortical activation patterns and therapeutic outcomes. 19 The absence of lesion-based stratification in our study may have masked potential advantages of ipsilesional stimulation in specific subgroups, and thus limits the interpretation of our findings regarding the relative superiority of bilateral stimulation. Taken together, these results suggest that the optimal stimulation strategy remains unresolved and is likely dependent on lesion-specific characteristics as well as therapeutic objectives—whether targeting focal cortical activation or promoting large-scale functional recovery. Future studies incorporating lesion-informed stratification and direct comparisons of different laterality paradigms are warranted to clarify these issues.

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the therapeutic benefits of bilateral taNS were generally preserved across different age groups and stroke etiologies, indicating a stable and broadly applicable treatment effect. Notably, greater improvements were observed in patients with a shorter disease duration as well as in those with hemorrhagic stroke, suggesting that both intervention timing and underlying lesion characteristics may influence responsiveness to neuromodulatory therapy. Accumulating evidence indicates that the post-stroke brain undergoes a temporally constrained window of heightened neuroplasticity, during which rehabilitation and neuromodulatory interventions are particularly effective in promoting functional reorganization and recovery.6,46 Importantly, the more pronounced response observed in hemorrhagic stroke may reflect fundamental differences in pathophysiology. Hemorrhagic lesions are often characterized by secondary inflammatory injury and mechanical compression, while a substantial proportion of neural structures remain anatomically preserved but functionally suppressed. 47 Given that taNS can enhance neuroplasticity while exerting anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects through vagal afferent pathways, patients with hemorrhagic stroke may retain a greater capacity for neuromodulation-induced recovery. Together, these findings are consistent with previous neuromodulation-based rehabilitation studies reporting time-dependent effects, highlighting that both preserved neural architecture and early intervention contribute to optimized post-stroke recovery outcomes.48,49

An important consideration is the potential influence of recovery stage on outcome responsiveness. The present study included patients from ≥14 days post-stroke, encompassing both subacute and early chronic phases. It is well recognized that neuroplasticity and responsiveness to rehabilitation are greater during the subacute stage, whereas recovery tends to plateau in later stages. In addition, commonly used motor scales such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment may exhibit reduced sensitivity or ceiling effects in patients with milder impairment or during chronic stages of recovery. This may influence the magnitude of detectable change and should be considered when interpreting the results. Notably, our subgroup analysis showed that patients with shorter disease duration demonstrated greater functional improvement, supporting the stage-dependent responsiveness of rehabilitation interventions. Future studies should consider stratified designs or stage-specific inclusion criteria to further clarify these effects.

Safety is a critical consideration when applying neuromodulation therapies in stroke populations. In the present study, bilateral taNS was well tolerated, with no serious adverse events related to stimulation. Cardiovascular parameters remained stable throughout the intervention period, and only mild, transient skin irritation was reported in a small number of participants. These findings are consistent with previous systematic reviews demonstrating the favorable safety and tolerability profile of taNS across neurological populations.27,50

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the sample size was sufficient to detect significant differences in primary outcomes, larger multicenter trials are needed to explore individual variability and identify predictors of treatment response. In addition, although the 8-week follow-up provided a useful mid-term perspective, it was insufficient to determine the long-term retention of motor-network reorganization; future studies with longer follow-up periods, such as 3–6 months, are needed to evaluate the durability of clinical and neurophysiological effects. Second, given the limited sample size, we did not perform formal statistical analyses for the fNIRS data. Therefore, these findings are presented as descriptive and exploratory outcomes, future studies incorporating larger sample size and formal statistical analyses may offer deeper mechanistic insights. Third, stimulation parameters were standardized rather than individualized, personalized or closed-loop taNS protocols may further enhance therapeutic efficacy and warrant future investigation. Fourth, this study mainly used the FMA and WMFT to assess post-stroke motor recovery, and did not employ neurophysiological measurements to evaluate muscle activation patterns. Future studies should incorporate precise kinematic assessments and surface electromyography to better characterize the mechanisms underlying motor improvement. Importantly, although a double-blind design was implemented, participants in the control group received 0 mA and did not experience the tingling sensation associated with active stimulation (0.5–1.0 mA). Therefore, it is possible that some participants could deduce their group allocation, which represents a potential limitation of blinding. The results in this section should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the absence of an active control group represents an important limitation, as it may affect the interpretation of intervention-specific effects.

5. Conclusion

BtaNS is a safe and well-tolerated non-invasive intervention that demonstrates overall superior efficacy compared with LtaNS in improving motor recovery, balance, and functional independence following stroke. Although not all outcome measures showed consistent between-group differences at every time point, the overall pattern of results favored bilateral stimulation across most functional domains. The enhanced therapeutic effects of BtaNS may be attributed not only to increased bilateral cortical engagement, but also to improved interhemispheric balance and more effective large-scale motor network reorganization, together with greater cumulative afferent input to brainstem pathways. These findings support the clinical potential of BtaNS as a promising adjunctive strategy in stroke rehabilitation. While the present study provides robust evidence for both upper and lower limb functional improvements, further research is warranted to elucidate the precise neurophysiological mechanisms and to confirm the long-term durability and generalizability of these effects in larger, multicenter populations.
