Uncertainty estimation and probabilistic skull shape reconstruction using bayesian neural networks
Authors: Jianning Li, Agniva Sengupta, Stefan Zachow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
3D shape reconstruction is an active area of research and a fundamental problem in computer vision, with growing applications in the medical domain, where it enables the recovery of missing or fine anatomical structures. Numerous approaches have been proposed for medical shape reconstruction, emphasizing accurate and anatomically plausible reconstructions. However, 3D shape reconstruction remains an inherently ill-posed problem, meaning (1) both neural network-based methods and conventional shape modeling approaches naturally introduce uncertainty in their predictions, and (2) multiple anatomically plausible reconstructions exist for a given partial or low-resolution input. While the uncertainty aspects have been widely explored in general computer vision, they remain relatively under-explored in the context of medical shape reconstruction. In this paper, we developed a 3D Bayesian U-Net and investigated its use for uncertainty estimation and probabilistic reconstructions across three key tasks: cranial reconstruction, facial reconstruction, and skull shape super-resolution. Our findings show that the Bayesian model is able to produce a range of anatomically plausible skull reconstructions while capturing natural skull variations arising from the learned weight uncertainty. Notably, these variations are primarily expressed through differences in bone thickness, which aligns with anatomical expectations, particularly relevant in real-world applications like cranial implant design. Additionally, we propose a principled framework to study the relationship between weight uncertainty and reconstruction uncertainty by analyzing the learned posterior distribution of the weights, demonstrating that our Bayesian U-Net achieves comparable reconstruction performance to a deterministic U-Net baseline while providing reliable uncertainty estimates. Our study also reveals a clear cross-task uncertainty pattern, where tasks with stronger structural constraints, like super-resolution, yield lower predictive uncertainty, while less constrained tasks, like facial reconstruction, result in higher uncertainty. Introduction

In recent years, geometric deep learning has opened new frontiers in tasks involving the reconstruction of shape from partial or degraded data. These deep learning based advancements have enabled more accurate and efficient modeling and subsequent reconstruction of complex shapes, which is essential for a variety of applications ranging from virtual reality to clinical decision-making. However, reliable recovery of fine-grained anatomical details from sparse, noisy, or incomplete inputs remain a key challenge. While advancements have certainly been made in the direction of such fine-grained shape recovery, understanding and quantifying the uncertainty of the recovered shape is crucial, especially in high-stakes environments like healthcare, where the accuracy and error-bounds of a prediction are highly desired to be interpretable.

Uncertainty is a fundamental aspect of 3D shape reconstruction tasks, including shape completion and shape super-resolution, where the goal is to infer missing or fine-grained geometric details from incomplete or low-resolution data. Due to the ill-posed nature of these problems, multiple plausible estimations may exist for a given input1–4, a characteristic also observed in real-world applications like autonomous driving5,6. Methods including Monte Carlo dropout7,8, deep ensembling9, and Bayesian neural networks10,11 have been widely studied to quantify uncertainty and generate probabilistic predictions. Such uncertainty-aware frameworks have also been frequently used in the traditional sense, either for fitting statistical shape models (SSMs) to target shapes in the presence of heavy outliers by an expectation maximization (EM)-like scheme12, often with a thresholding in the EM to detect outliers13, or by sequential Bayesian optimization, termed Gaussian process morphable model (GPMM), that aims to estimate the posterior distribution of the target shape, irrespective of outlying pathologies14. Bayesian frameworks have been applied to various craniofacial applications to address uncertainty. For instance, Zhao et al.15 utilized Bayesian convolutional neural networks for automated MRI brain extraction in primates, while Kohl et al.16 introduced a probabilistic U-Net to handle label ambiguity in the segmentation of lung nodules. Other applications include Bayesian-optimized CNNs for classifying the primary tumor origin of brain metastases17 and Bayesian deep learning for accelerated MRI signal reconstruction18. In forensics, Claes et al.19 proposed a Bayesian approach for craniofacial reconstruction, focusing on estimating soft-tissue facial morphology from skull data. However, these works either focus on segmentation, classification, signal-level restoration, or forensic soft-tissue prediction.

The shape completion and super-resolution concepts from computer vision have been extensively applied to medical imaging for skull reconstruction, aiming to restore missing surfaces or recover detailed skull geometry using learning-based approaches20–22. Early work in this area treated skull shape completion as a surface interpolation problem, using methods such as radial basis functions (RBFs) to interpolate the missing skull surface23. Over time, SSMs24 and deep learning-based methods have gained popularity, where traditional U-Net-based architectures21,25–28, Transformer networks29,30, as well as generative models, such as generative adversarial networks (GANs)31,32 and diffusion models33,34 have been extensively employed. These methods have demonstrated success in producing anatomically plausible skull reconstructions. Super-resolution has been predominantly applied in the imaging domain, such as computed tomography (CT)35,36 and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)37–40, to improve diagnostic accuracy and support downstream clinical tasks, such as detection and segmentation. Popular methods include implicit neural representations41–44, GANs45 and diffusion models46. A limited number of studies have explored super-resolution of anatomical shapes, typically representing them as voxel grids22,47. However, these approaches have largely focused on generating a single, fixed reconstruction, without accounting for the inherent uncertainty associated with the shape reconstruction process. This highlights two significant gaps (1) there is a lack of studies exploring uncertainty estimation in skull shape completion and super-resolution. (2) anatomically plausible shape variations and alternative reconstructions improve both the interpretability of the deep-learning-based reconstruction process and the robustness of their outcome - an aspect that is particularly important in clinical applications, such as accounting for bone thickness variations in cranial implant design48–50. Furthermore, assessment of the uncertainty-aware reconstructions is also a tricky task. Point- and voxel-wise metrics e.g., Chamfer Distance (CD), Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), and Hausdorff Distance (HD), are insufficient or even misleading, as they penalize anatomically plausible variations that deviate from a single ground-truth27. Instead, evaluation should prioritize anatomical plausibility, quantify the reconstructions variations, and reward appropriate uncertainty, meaning that a model should be considered effective if its uncertainty estimates reflect natural anatomical and clinical variations i.e., high uncertainty in ambiguous regions and low uncertainty in deterministic regions. Expert-based, domain-specific and qualitative evaluations are desired.

In this paper, we address these gaps by investigating uncertainty estimation and probabilistic shape reconstruction using Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) across three skull reconstruction tasks, including cranial reconstruction, facial reconstruction, and skull shape super-resolution. Note that, we use the umbrella term skull reconstruction to refer to the three related tasks: skull shape completion, which involves restoring missing facial or cranial bones, and skull shape super-resolution, which focuses on recovering fine geometric details of the skull, including the facial region. Each of these tasks can be framed as a completion or inpainting problem. Our goal is to develop a probabilistic model based on BNNs that not only produces a range of anatomically plausible skull reconstructions, but also quantifies the uncertainty inherent in the reconstruction process. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to specifically target the reconstruction of the skull bony structure for cranioplasty and implant design using Bayesian neural networks, integrating three interrelated tasks: skull shape reconstruction (cranial and facial) and super-resolution. We fill a critical gap by shifting the focus to rigid anatomical shape completion, directly translating weight uncertainty into clinically relevant geometric variations like bone thickness. Our work marks a significant step toward bridging the gap between advanced shape completion methods in computer vision and the practical necessity of uncertainty estimation in craniofacial shape reconstruction applications.

3D Bayesian U-Net

The diagram of the 3D Bayesian U-Net used in our work, consisting of a deterministic downsampling path and a Bayesian upsampling path.

As shown in Fig. 1, the BNN is implemented as a 3D convolutional U-Net, incorporating Bayesian layers via TensorFlow Probability (TFP) to capture uncertainty in the learned parameters of the upsampling path. The downsampling path consists of four stages of standard 3D convolutions, each including convolutional layers, group normalization, and max pooling with deterministic weights, followed by an upsampling path with 3D deconvolutional layers, group normalization, and 3D Bayesian convolutions with probabilistic weights. The number of the trainable parameters of the Bayesian U-Net is 1924964. The penultimate layer involves a Bayesian convolution with a sigmoid activation to generate a segmentation mask. The prior distribution of the Bayesian convolutional weights is implemented as a standard Gaussian. For each weight in the weight space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {W}$$\end{document} of a specific layer, we do:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \theta _{i,j,k} = \mu _{i,j,k} + \lambda \sigma _{i,j,k}, \qquad (i,j,k) \in \mathcal {W} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} acts as a scaling factor over the standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{i,j,k}$$\end{document}, effectively defining the deformation domain. Weight uncertainty propagates throughout the network, influencing the uncertainty in the final reconstruction. However, this relationship is complex and varies depending on the specific layer. Using Eq. (8), we can study their relationship in a principled manner by examining how reconstruction changes induced by weight perturbations correspond to the high-variation regions identified through Eq. (7).

Experiments and results

We investigated uncertainty estimation in three well-known ill-posed tasks using the Bayesian U-Net: two shape completion tasks (cranial reconstruction, facial reconstruction), and a super-resolution task (skull shape super-resolution). The experiments were conducted on a dataset derived from the AutoImplant challenge27. In the cranial reconstruction task, a complete skull is reconstructed from a defective skull with a partially missing cranium. The facial reconstruction task restores missing facial bone structures, while the super-resolution task enhances the skull’s voxel resolution from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$128\times 128\times 64$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256\times 256\times 128$$\end{document}. In all tasks, the skulls are represented as binary voxel grids, with ’1s’ being the skull surface and ’0s’ being the background. These tasks aim to generate anatomically plausible skull reconstructions, quantify regions of high uncertainty (based on Eq. (7)), and analyze their anatomical relevance (using Eq. (8)). Evaluation strategies

We evaluate the performance of our Bayesian U-Net by: (1) quantifying weight and reconstruction uncertainty and analyzing their cross-task correlations, and (2) Visually inspecting the anatomical conformity of the reconstructions and high uncertainty areas obtained by perturbing intermediate Bayesian layers (8th Bayesian layer for the cranial reconstruction and super-resolution task and 7th Bayesian layer for the super-resolution task), and (3) comparing the reconstruction performance of the Bayesian U-Net with a deterministic U-Net baseline and several variants with identical network depth and topology, and (4) examining voxel-wise uncertainty–error correlation and conducting an uncertainty calibration study.

The visual inspection of the Bayesian U-Net’s performance and the anatomical conformity of the high-uncertainty regions was primarily conducted by the lead author (J. Li), who possesses extensive domain knowledge in craniofacial reconstruction and learning-based shape modeling20–22. Furthermore, to facilitate an intuitive understanding of how the network’s predictive uncertainty is physically reflected in the skull geometry, we designed a visual user interface (UI). This tool (publicly available at https://git.zib.de/jli/uncertainty-aware-skull-reconstruction/) renders the complex probabilistic outputs dynamically so that even non-domain experts can easily grasp and interpret them. Within the UI, the high uncertainty areas are manipulated in a controlled manner, as detailed in “Uncertainty estimation” Uncertainty Estimation, dynamically illustrating geometric variations in bone thickness and structural prominences. The 7th and 8th Bayesian layers (as indicated in Fig. 1) were selected for visual analysis as they represent an intermediate stage in the decoder path of the network. At this level, the learned features are sufficiently abstract to capture the global anatomical structure of the skull while still retaining enough spatial detail to influence fine-grained geometric reconstructions. This choice is further supported by the layer-wise analysis of average weight variance (Table A.1), which shows a consistent trend across all tasks, with higher uncertainty in early layers that gradually decreases toward deeper layers, while intermediate layers maintain moderate uncertainty. This indicates that uncertainty is distributed across the network, and that reconstruction variability arises from the aggregated effect of uncertainty throughout the network rather than from any single layer. Cranial reconstruction

The Bayesian U-Net was trained using 100 paired defective-healthy skulls from the AutoImplant dataset. Figure 2 presents qualitative results on two test cases not included in the training set. It includes sagittal-view uncertainty maps and skull reconstructions, displayed in both sagittal and 3D views, under a varying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} (Eq. (8)). The uncertainty map is obtained via 20 stochastic forward passes and is defined as a voxel-wise variance map that measures how much the model’s predictions fluctuate during multiple runs (in our case, 20 forward runs). Specifically, each voxel in the defective region is predicted multiple times as a probability value in the range ([0, 1]), and uncertainty is quantified based on the consistency of these predictions: regions with consistent probabilities exhibit low variance (low uncertainty), whereas regions with greater variability in predicted probabilities show higher variance (high uncertainty), thereby indicating where the model is less confident i.e., high uncertainty regions, in the reconstruction. As shown in Fig. 2, the first uncertainty map displays low uncertainty along the border regions of the outer implant surface, suggesting that the model is confident in maintaining a smooth transition between the implant and the surrounding skull, which is an essential aspect for anatomical plausibility of the reconstructed skull. The non-border regions and the inner implant surface exhibit higher uncertainty, corresponding to greater variations in the surface curvatures and implant thickness. The variations in thickness and curvature reflect the design considerations of individual cranial implants used in clinical practice48. In the second example, the reconstructed implant from the mean weight (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 0$$\end{document}) exhibits a mismatch on the upper part of the implant’s outer surface, which is corrected by perturbing the mean weight (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 1, 2, 3$$\end{document}), highlighting the advantages of uncertainty-aware reconstruction of cranial implants. Similar variations in thickness and curvature are observed as in the first example.

Quantitative evaluation, uncertainty analysis, and calibration

Here we present a comprehensive quantitative evaluation of the proposed Bayesian U-Net, including both reconstruction performance and uncertainty-related analyses. Specifically, we compare the model against a deterministic U-Net baseline using Dice, 95 percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) and average surface distance (ASD) to assess reconstruction accuracy. In addition, we evaluate the quality of the estimated uncertainty by analyzing its correlation with reconstruction error. Finally, we assess the reliability of the probabilistic predictions using uncertainty calibration metrics e.g., expected calibration error (ECE) and reliability diagram. All experiments are conducted on the cranial reconstruction task, as it is the most clinically relevant and closely aligned with existing work on cranial reconstruction22. The test set from the AutoImplant challenge containing 100 test cases are used for the experiments.

For a fair comparison, a deterministic U-Net baseline is constructed to mirror the proposed Bayesian U-Net, ensuring structural parity in terms of network depth, filter configurations and overall encoder–decoder design (refer to Fig. 1). The primary difference lies in the upsampling path, where the Bayesian U-Net employs probabilistic layers based on Conv3DFlipout to model weight uncertainty, while the deterministic U-Net uses standard Conv3D layers. Besides, the deterministic U-Net optimizes only the Dice loss during training while the Bayesian U-Net optimizes both a Dice loss and a KLD loss. We evaluate four model variants to disentangle the effects of stochastic weight sampling and Bayesian regularization: (1) a deterministic U-Net baseline, (2) a stochastic U-Net using Conv3DFlipout layers without the KLD term, (3) a fully Bayesian U-Net trained from scratch with KLD regularization, and (4) a Bayesian U-Net initialized from the stochastic model and further optimized with KLD term. For the Bayesian models, predictions are obtained as the mean over 20 Monte Carlo forward passes. All methods are evaluated on the reconstructed defect region i.e., the implants obtained by subtracting the input defective skulls from full skull predictions. The results are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 7. The results reveal several important trends. First, introducing stochasticity via Flipout layers without KL regularization (Exp 2) improves performance over the deterministic baseline (Exp 1), which can be attributed to noise injected into the weights during training acting as an implicit regularizer. This stochasticity prevents overfitting, encourages better generalization, and provides an ensemble-like effect, where multiple forward passes approximate a lightweight ensemble whose averaged predictions improve accuracy. This is particularly beneficial for cranial reconstruction, where missing regions are inherently ambiguous and deterministic models tend to overfit specific patterns, while stochastic models remain more flexible. Second, the Bayesian model trained from scratch with KL divergence (Exp 3) underperforms, suggesting that the KL term can hinder early optimization by overly constraining the weights. Finally, initializing the Bayesian model from a pretrained stochastic model and then introducing KL regularization (Exp 4) yields the best performance across all metrics, indicating that decoupling representation learning from posterior regularization enables both strong reconstruction and well-regularized uncertainty.

We evaluate the relationship between predictive uncertainty and reconstruction error within the ground-truth implant region, using the best-performing Bayesian model in Exp. 4. Voxel-wise uncertainty is quantified as the variance across 20 stochastic forward passes, and the voxel-wise error map is obtained by comparing the binarized mean prediction with the binary ground truth. The results reveal a clear dependence of calibration quality on the training strategy. The Bayesian model trained without KLD regularization (Exp. 2) exhibits the poorest calibration (weighted Global ECE: 0.0650, per-case: 0.0646 ±0.0158), indicating that stochasticity alone is insufficient to produce reliable probability estimates. Introducing KLD regularization improves calibration, with the model trained from scratch (Exp. 3) achieving better performance (weighted Global ECE: 0.0534, per-case: 0.0554 ± 0.0054). The pretrained-and-continued Bayesian model (Exp. 4) further improves calibration (weighted Global ECE: 0.0493, per-case: 0.0488 ± 0.0176), achieving the best overall balance. The results indicate the pretrained Bayesian model (Exp. 4) benefits from both improved reconstruction performance and effective uncertainty modeling.

Discussion and conclusion

In this exploratory work, we investigated uncertainty estimation in skull shape completion and super-resolution tasks using a Bayesian U-Net, which allows for both quantifying reconstruction uncertainty and generating a range of anatomically plausible skull reconstructions. Additionally, we introduced a pipeline to examine the empirical relationship between weight uncertainty within the Bayesian network and the resulting reconstruction variability by analyzing the posterior weight distributions of selected layers. Through our experiments, we visualized these weight distributions and assessed how they influence reconstruction outcomes, highlighting their role in capturing natural anatomical variations. Notably, our Bayesian U-Net consistently revealed thickness variations across these tasks, aligning closely with clinical observations, particularly in cranial reconstruction. The Bayesian model achieves comparable reconstruction performance to the deterministic U-Net baseline in terms of Dice and surface distance metrics, while additionally providing meaningful voxel-wise uncertainty estimates that correlate with reconstruction errors and highlight anatomically challenging regions. Building on this foundation, future work could involve a systematic investigation of all Bayesian layers to better understand their individual contributions to reconstruction variability. Furthermore, the scale of this study remains limited (i.e., 100 training and 100 test cases per task) compared to large clinical datasets, potentially affecting its generalizability. Given the inherent variability in cranial anatomy and imaging protocols, further evaluation on external, multi-center cohorts is necessary to assess robustness under domain shifts. The methods can also be used for other uncertainty-critical tasks like craniofacial reconstruction (skull to facial skin) in future work. Additionally, we observed clear differences in the levels of weights and predictive uncertainty across tasks: facial reconstruction exhibited the highest uncertainty, reflecting the greater ambiguity, variability, and degrees of freedom in reconstructing detailed facial features and facial morphologies from partial skull input; skull shape super-resolution showed the lowest uncertainty, likely due to the strong anatomical constraints (or shape prior) from the low-resolution input guiding the network towards a consistent global shape reconstruction; cranial reconstruction fell between the two. These findings highlight how the nature and constraints of each task directly influence the Bayesian model’s weight uncertainty and its confidence in reconstructions.
