Mechanisms underlying the influence of skin properties on a single cavitation bubble in low-frequency sonophoresis
Authors: Hao Wu, Cheng Zhou, Yuanyuan Li, Yongzhen Jin, Xiaochen Lai, Claus-Dieter Ohl, Dachao Li, Haixia Yu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a safe and effective method for systemic transdermal drug delivery (TDD), sonophoresis has drawn much attention from researchers. Despite numerous studies confirming cavitation as the main reason for sonophoresis, the effect skin has on cavitation bubble dynamics remains elusive due to the difficulty of experimental challenges. For a start, we reveal how single cavitation bubble (SCB) dynamics are affected by skin properties, including elasticity, hydrophilicity and texture. We use polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) to simulate human skin and record the temporary evolution of SCBs with synchronous ultrafast photography. The influences of skin properties on SCBs are concluded: 1) SCBs collapse later near walls with better elasticities and generate microjets with higher speed; 2) SCBs collapse later near hydrophilic walls with slower microjets; and 3) the existence of a texture structure on walls also delays the time of bubble collapse near them and slows the velocities of microjets (v) during collapses.Keywords: Acoustic cavitation, Sonophoresis, Skin-like wall, Single acoustic cavitation bubble

Introduction

Numerous techniques allow for interstitial fluid (ISF) transdermal extraction and transdermal drug delivery (TDD) [1], [2], [3]. As an important active method, ultrasound has been demonstrated to enhance the penetration of various drugs and ISF across human skin; this method is referred to as sonophoresis [4]. Historically, transdermal enhancement of ultrasound was first reported by Fellinger and Schmid [5], who successfully introduced hydrocortisone ointment into knuckles for the clinical treatment of chronic rheumatoid arthritis in 1954. In the following decades, researchers have explored various applications based on sonophoresis, such as pain-releasing applications by Griffin [6], [7] and TDD for local anesthetics by Benson et al. [8] and Tachibana et al. [9] In 1995, Mitragotri et al. [10] first reported that low-frequency ultrasound (20 kHz) can induce significant transdermal transport of proteins, including insulin (molecular weight, ∼6000), interferon γ (∼17,000), and erythropoietin(∼48,000). In addition, Mitragotri et al. [11] proposed a potential method for noninvasive detection based on ultrasonic skin permeabilization to analyse ISF extracted across the skin with a vacuum. Moreover, application examples are transcutaneous immunization [12], gene therapy [13], [14] and noninvasive transdermal monitoring. Although these early works already revealed the possibilities of sonophoresis, its efficiency still did not meet expectations for clinical applications due to a lack of knowledge regarding the mechanisms of sonophoresis.

Since the 1990 s, systematic research studies on sonophoresis varying the parameters, frequency, intensity and energy density of the applied ultrasound [4], [15], [16], the distance between the skin and the ultrasonic horn [17], and the properties of the ultrasonic coupling agent [10], [18], [19], [20], have been carried out. As a result, the efficiency of sonophoresis increased drastically. In particular, low-frequency sonophoresis (LFS), with ultrasound frequencies ranging from 20 kHz to 100 kHz, leads to a thousand times greater skin permeability than sonophoresis in the MHz range [15], [21]. These intriguing reports have brought worldwide attention to LFS, and numerous related studies have been conducted [22], [23], [24]. As cavitation activity is much more violent in LFS, the main mechanism of sonophoresis is believed to be cavitation [19].

The effects of ultrasound on tissues can be classified into thermal and nonthermal [25], [26]. Ultrasound-induced skin heating causes the fluidization of stratum corneum (SC) lipids and leads to the transdermal permeation enhancement of molecules, which is a thermal effect. As an important nonthermal effect, ultrasonic cavitation improves skin permeability through shock waves and liquid microjets both acting on the SC. Thus, cavitation and especially inertial cavitation has attracted considerable attention in LFS applications [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]. The effects of various influencing factors, such as the acoustic pressure, ultrasonic frequency, size of bubbles and physical properties of the liquid medium have been investigated through experiments and simulations for single and multi-bubble cavitation [30], [31], [34], [35], [36]. Moreover, mechanical routes by which cavitation bubbles may enhance skin permeability were proposed by Tezel et al. [37] and Collis et al. [38], as shown in Fig. 1. However, to date, the effect of skin boundary properties on the dynamics of cavitation bubbles and the efficiency of skin permeabilization in LFS has not been systematically investigated, and the mechanism underlying the influence of skin properties on a micro-sized single cavitation bubble (SCB) in LFS remains partly unclear.

As the foundation of cavitation phenomenon, cavitation bubble dynamics have drawn much attention from researchers [39], [40], especially the interactions of cavitation bubbles and biological tissue. To assess these processes, Kodama et al. [41] conducted one of the first experiments on the dynamics of cavitation bubbles attached to a gelatin surface and observed that the bubble tended to move away from the elastic boundary but a liquid microjet formed inside the bubble penetrated the gelatin wall.

Subsequently, effects of boundary properties, such as the elastic property [42], [43], [44] and the wall wettability [45], on single cavitation bubbles were also widely investigated with laser or spark-generated bubbles. Brujan et al. [46], [47] conducted a thorough study of the interaction of laser-induced cavitation bubbles with polyacrylamide hydrogels (PAA) of different elasticities and observed the strong relationship of the bubble dynamics on the elasticity of the boundary and the distance to the surface. Other experiments were also conducted to examine the effects of different boundaries, such as rubber [48] and silicon gel [43], [49], on the bubble dynamics. Furthermore, the interactions of cavitation bubbles with a thin elastic membrane were investigated by Shaw et al. [50] and Orthaber et al. [51] and similar bubble dynamics to those near the thick elastic wall were observed. Lyubimova et al. [45] experimentally investigated the dynamics of vapor–gas bubbles arising in distilled water under the action of ultrasound, near and on the surface of solid plates with various hydrophobic properties (contact angle) and found a significant effect of surface properties on the dynamics of bubbles oscillating near and on a solid surface under the influence of ultrasound. Howerer, the single laser or spark-generated bubbles in these works are much larger than those in the applications of LFS, and the size and position of the gas-bubbles in these works cannot be controlled. These conclusions connot be directly used in LFS applications. In parallel to the experimental works mentioned above, theoretical studies have also been carried out on a single cavitation bubble near an elastic wall and play an important role in this field. Klaseboer et al. [52], Curtiss et al. [53] and Wang et al. [54] regarded the elastic wall as a second fluid and solved the equations with the boundary integral method (BIM). Moreover, they found good qualitative agreement with the experiments and captured the main features of the bubble dynamics near elastic boundaries. Recently, Zhang et al. [39] established a novel theory for the dynamics of oscillating bubbles that can simultaneously take into consideration the effects of boundaries, bubble interaction, ambient flow field, gravity, bubble migration, fluid compressibility, viscosity, and surface tension while maintaining a unified and elegant mathematical form. This theory has shown advantages over some classical theoretical models and can thus potentially be used in applications of ultrasonic cavitation bubbles.

In this work, we study the dynamics of SCBs near elastic boundaries based on a new experimental system that allows controlled studies of ultrasound-induced cavitation from a single and well-defined microbubble with high-speed photography. Elastic boundaries with various properties, such as elasticity, wettability, and texture, are fabricated to simulate some of the properties of a skin. They are referred to as skin-like walls (SLWs) in this work. The experimental study shows that the skin properties affect the dynamics of the SCB and the interaction between the bubble and the boundary induced by cavitation, particularly as a function of the elasticity.

From a strong relationship between the skin properties, the dynamics of SCBs, and the impact of SCBs on the SLW, some potential mechanisms for how sonophoresis based on SCBs is influenced by skin properties are concluded. Since cavitation has been widely regarded as the main reason for LFS and SCB is the basic unit of the cavitation phenomenon, this work can help to reveal the basic mechanisms of sonophoresis regarding how the efficiency of LFS is influenced by skin properties.

Methods

Experimental setup

The dynamic behaviors of single acoustic cavitation bubbles near SLWs were studied in a water tank by a synchronously controlled high-speed photography system according to previously published methods [55], [56]. SLWs with different properties (Young's modulus, wettability and surface depression) made of PDMS have the same thickness of 4 mm. An ultrahigh-speed camera (FASTCAM SA-X2, Photron, Japan) fitted with an inverted fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer A1, Zeiss, Germany) was used to make optical recordings at 0.48 million frames per second. Sufficient illumination was achieved by using a high-power halogen lamp (HAL100, Zeiss, Germany).

The SLWs attached vertically to the upper surface of a fixed plate were placed in filtered, deionized, and degassed water. Single bubbles (original radius of R0 = 24 μm) were generated near SLWs from a regulated coflow micropipette injector [55]. The initial distance from the centre of the original single bubble before ultrasonic excitation to the surface of the SLW was controlled and defined as L, and the distance parameter γ0 is defined as L/R0. As it has been concluded that the cavitation phenomenon of a single bubble near a rigid wall is most intense at a proper value of γ0 (γ0 = 2) [57], the value of L0 was controlled at approximately 48 μm.

As a wide range of skin elasticity, we chose a elasticity range of SLWs from 0.04 to 1.30 MPa in present work according to previous experimental works. [43], [47], [51] SLWs with various elasticities were fabricated from PDMS with different temperatures and curing times. We controlled the elasticity of SLWs by different combinations of temperatures and curing times which are listed in Table S1 in the Supplementary Material. The skin texture/structures on the wall were fabricated by soft contact lithography.

A 20-kHz focused low-frequency transducer connected to a commercial high-power ultrasonic generator (Yitian Tec. Co., Baoding) was used to produce ultrasound for single-bubble cavitation excitation. A hydrophone (TC4013, RESON, Denmark) was used for ultrasonic intensity measurement.

Materials

Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), octyl glucoside (OG), and propylene glycol (PG) were obtained from Solarbio Company (Beijing, China). Allura red (red food coloring) and bisbenzimide (blue fluorochrome, molecular weight: 534) were obtained from Yuanye Company (Shanghai, China). Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) was obtained from Dow Company (Michigan, US).

Young's modulus and wettability measurements

In this article, the Young’s modulus (E) of the elastic walls was measured with a tensile testing machine (ZQ-990B, Zhiqu Co., China) by creating stress–strain plots several times (n = 8) and calculating the average values of their slopes. The wettability of the surfaces was characterized by a contact angle measuring instrument (SDC-200S, SINDIN, China) to measure the contact angle through the liquid, where liquid–vapor interfaces meet the surfaces.

Treatment of skin samples by LFS

Skin tissues were treated with a SonoPrep ® ultrasound device at medium intensity for approximately 3 min. Following the treatment, skin tissues were fixed in 10 % formalin overnight and embedded in paraffin. Then, tissue sections of these samples were stained with Allura red for histological examination. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used for skin ultrastructural analysis and QD penetration.

Analysis

Images of bubble margins were obtained by using a high-speed camera for data analysis. In this work, the velocity v of the bubble margin was calculated by the displacement between two adjacent frames and their time interval [55], [56]. We define the direction pointing toward the wall as the positive of v. The definition of the time zero (t = 0), time of bubble collapse tc and velocities of the microjets v (v1, v2, v3) have been described in our previous work [55], [56].

Results and discussion

Low-frequency ultrasound-induced localized transport regions (LTRs) in skins with different properties are manifested at different length scales (Supplementary Fig. S1 (A and B)). These regions show obviously different sonophoresis results among the different skins. Due to the certain similarities between polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and human skin, particularly in terms of certain physical and mechanical properties, such as flexibility, softness, biocompatibility, water repellence, gas permeability and chemical stability, PDMS has been considered a suitable material for skin-related uses [58], [59]. In addition, PDMS can be easily molded, shaped, and fabricated into various forms, and it has great transparency with some transparent formulations. Thus, PDMS was chosen as a good material to fabricate skin-like walls (SLWs) in the present work. In this section, experiments on how skin properties affect the efficiency of LFS in skin permeabilization are conducted. Since cavitation has been regarded as the main reason for LFS, experiments on SCBs near SLWs are performed. We record and analyze the effects on SCBs near SLWs fabricated with PDMS. Here, the SLW-parameters elastic modulus, wettability, and surface structure are varied separately.

Influence of wall elasticity on a SCB in an acoustic field

Temporal evolutions of SCBs near SLWs with different elasticities are recorded and analyzed (see videos of temporal evolutions of SCBs near different elastic walls in the supplementary files Video 1 to Video 6). Typical characteristics of bubble dynamics in different frames are chosen and compared to those for SCBs near SLWs with different elasticities, as shown in Fig. 2(a)-(f). The duration of bubble movement to the wall increases as Young's modulus decreases (frame 1 in Fig. 2(a-f)), and the same trend occurs for the time of bubble collapse (frame 2 in Fig. 2(a-f)). As Young’s modulus E decreases, bubbles show higher collapse intensity during their first collapse (frame 3 in Fig. 2(a-f)) and different shapes after the first collapse (frame 4 in Fig. 2(a-f)). In the experiments using SLWs with lower values of E, it takes longer for the bubbles to rebound from the walls after their first collapse (frame 4 in Fig. 2(a-f)) and to show drop-like shapes with tips that are directed toward the walls and have different tip angles (Fig. 2(g)). The differences in the bubble morphology during the first collapse reflect the gradually decreasing pressure between the bubbles and SLWs with decreasing values of E because of wall movement. In addition, there are no obvious changes in bubble dynamics during the following two collapses for various walls, and the time interval between each bubble collapse remains approximately 50 μs, but the collapse intensity varies. These results are in good agreement with the existing numerical results [60] which found that the bubbles in an ultrasonic field would collapse with liquid jets toward the wall near bio-materials with lower elasticities (22.6 kPa and 44.7 kPa) and would collapse and break into two with the top bubble being much larger than the bottom one in near bio-materials with higher elasticities (240 kPa and 790 kPa) (as shown in frame 3 in Fig. 2(a-f) in the present work).

Fig 2(h) shows the time of bubble collapse (tc) with E of the SLWs. The value of tc, which directly affects the efficiency of acoustic cavitation, increases with decreasing E of the SLWs. As the value of E decreases from 1.3 MPa to 0.04 MPa, tc increases from 91.5 μs to 92.6 μs, 96.6 μs, 101.1 μs, 111.4 μs, and 114.7 μs, which is longer than the time of bubble collapse (tc = 85.4 μs) near a rigid wall (see Video 7 of the temporal evolution of SCBs near a rigid wall in the supplementary).

The velocities of the microjets during bubble collapse in our experiments are analyzed and shown in Fig. 2(i) to quantify the impact effect of SCBs on the walls. In Fig. 2(i), the velocities of the first, second and third microjets (v1, v2, and v3, respectively) during SCB collapse near various walls are shown. The velocities of the first microjets vary from 15 to 30 m/s. For SCBs collapsing near the rigid wall, the velocity of the microjet (v) decreases successively from the first to the third collapse (25.2 m/s, 10.7 m/s and 7.8 m/s, respectively). In contrast, the velocity of the microjet (v) near the elastic walls increases obviously from the first to the third collapse. In addition, for SCBs collapsing near the elastic walls, the velocity of the first microjet (v1) increases as E decreases, and the same situation occurs in the second (v2) and third microjets (v3).

The main mechanical reason for these results can be the differences in the flow field near SLWs with different viscosities. The movement of a SLW would affect the flow field between the wall and the bubble and the influence is directly related to the moving intensity. Compared to a bubble near a rigid wall, the pressure difference between the bubble and the SLW remains in a relatively small range because of the fluid–structure interaction between the elastic wall and the nearby flow field, which slows the speed of the bubble moving to the wall and prolongs the time of bubble collapse. This effect is more pronounced for SLWs with lower values of E. In addition, the liquid pressure field between the bubble and the SLW decreases with the movement of the wall (the opposite direction to the bubble) during the expansion of the bubble. This will increase the pressure difference between the opposite sides of the bubble, facilitating a higher velocity of the microjet during bubble collapse near the SLW. Therefore, the smaller the value of E is for a SLW, the easier the wall movement, and the greater the velocity of the microjet.

These results are in good agreement with the results of a previous numerical study [60]. In this numerical research, it was also found that the gas bubble in an ultrasonic field near bio-materials tended to collapse earlier as the Young’s modulus of the bio-materials increased, and the maximum jet velocity decreased obviously as the value of materials’ Young’s modulus increased from 22.6 kPa to 790 kPa.

Influence of surface wettability of a SLW on a SCB

A SLW made from PDMS (E = 0.04 MPa) is inherently hydrophobic, as shown in Fig. 3(a). Fig. 3(b) shows the same PDMS wall after hydrophilic treatment. Fig. 3(c and d) compares the individual bubble dynamics near SLWs with different wettability. The bubbles are initiallly near the hydrophobic and hydrophilic walls with the same size and relative position (frames 0 of Fig. 3(c and d)). When the bubbles move close enough to the walls, they exhibit different triangle shapes that look inverted between them because of the difference in walls’ wettability. In the case near the hydrophobic wall, the bubble's tip is directed toward the wall with a small contact angle (frames 1 of Fig. 3(c)), but in the opposite direction as shown in frames 1 of Fig. 3(d) near the hydrophilic wall with a larger contact angle.

Comparison of individual bubble dynamics near elastic walls (E = 0.04 MPa) with different wettability ((c) the hydrophobic wall and (d) the hydrophilic wall). (e) Time of bubble collapse (tc) near two elastic walls (E = 0.04 MPa and E = 1.3 MPa) with different wettability (n = 10). (f) Velocities of the microjets (v) during the first, second and third collapses of the bubbles near elastic walls with different wettability (n = 10).

However, due to the difference in the adsorption energy of the hydrophobic and hydrophilic walls for bubbles, the bubble collapses on the hydrophobic wall earlier with higher intensity (frames 2 and 3 of Fig. 3(c and d)). After the first collapse, the bubble rebounds and moves farther from the hydrophobic wall (frame 4 of Fig. 3(c and d)). Then, the bubble near the hydrophobic wall continues to move more intensely during the second collapse (frames 5 and 6 of Fig. 3(c and d)) and rebounds to a farther distance (frames 7 of Fig. 3(c and d)) than the bubble near the hydrophilic wall. The same situation occurs during the third collapse, as shown in frames 8 and 9 of Fig. 3(c and d).

The time of bubble collapse (tc) and the velocity of microjets during collapses (v) are analysed based on the experiments of SCBs near two SLWs (E = 0.04 MPa and E = 1.3 MPa) with different wettability. As shown in Fig. 3(e), the value of tc is larger after hydrophilic treatment of the SLW with a lower value of E (E = 0.04 MPa). The same situation occurred for the wall with a higher value of E (E = 1.3 MPa), and the difference in tc is much greater between the hydrophobic and hydrophilic walls. Compared to the hydrophobic wall, the contact angle of the bubble on the hydrophilic wall is relatively large, so the stretching of the bubble along the horizontal direction of the wall is easily affected by liquid extrusion. This is specifically manifested as the liquid pressure on the vertical direction of the bubble wall being small and the adsorption capacity of the wall with respect to the bubble being high. For these reasons, the shape of the bubble attached to the hydrophilic wall changes, and finally, the collapse time of the bubble is delayed. Fig. 3(e) also shows the time of bubbles’ attachment on the SLWs before their first collapses (ta), it is found that the bubble on hydrophobic wall is more likely to leave the wall under low-frequency ultrasonic irradiation, which certifies the stronger adsorption capacity of the hydrophilic wall for bubbles in the present work.

Fig. 3(f) shows the velocities of microjets (v) during the first, second and third collapses of bubbles near elastic walls with different wettability. The values of v for the three collapses near hydrophilic walls show a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. During the second collapse, with a higher value of v, the bubble exhibits a flat shape and oscillates on the wall for a long time (see supplementary file Video 8). This seriously limits the energy accumulation before the third collapse because of the strong adsorption capacity of the hydrophilic wall. As a result, the value of v during the third collapse decreases compared to that of the second collapse. In addition, the velocity of every microjet near the hydrophilic wall is less than that of the same microjet near the hydrophobic wall, which is also because of the strong adsorption capacity of the hydrophilic wall.

Influence of skin texture on a SCB

Skin texture is one of the most important features of the skin surface, which differs for people of various sexes and ages and even at different positions on each person; these differences are mainly reflected in texture width and depth. Figure 4(a) shows anterior forearm skin as an example. The skin surface is uneven and complex in shape, the texture width of the skin in this area is 20 ∼ 120 μm, and the depth is 10 ∼ 80 μm. Due to the variability and structural complexity of skin texture, it is difficult to control the relative positions of the bubble and each groove on the skin surface, even for a simplified model of skin texture with straight lines (as shown in Fig. 4(b)). To reveal the influencing mechanisms of the skin texture on the SCB during sonophoresis applications, we research the kinetics of SCBs near SLWs with grooves with depths of 40 μm and 10 μm and widths of 25 μm, 37.5 μm, 50 μm, 62.5 μm, and 75 μm. Fig. 4(c) shows the relative position of the bubble and the elastic wall, and Fig. 4(d) depicts the horizontal cross section of Fig. 4(c) from the center of the bubble. As shown in Fig. 4(d), the width and depth of the groove are defined as w and h, respectively, and Sin, Ssi and Sout represent the inner underside, the inner side, and the outer plane of the groove, respectively.

(a) Photomicrograph of anterior forearm skin: (b) simplified structure diagram of the skin texture; (c) relative position of the bubble and the elastic wall with a straight groove in three-dimensional space; (d) horizontal cross section of Fig. 4(c) from the center of the bubble and the details of the groove on the elastic wall.

Fig. 5(a-f) show a comparison of the dynamic behaviours of SCBs near elastic walls without a groove and with grooves of different sizes (h = 40 μm; w = 0, 25 μm, 37.5 μm, 50 μm, 62.5 μm, and 75 μm). The bubbles are originally the same size near the wall with the same value of γ0 (frame 0 of Fig. 5(a-f)). Compared to the bubble near an elastic wall without any grooves, the bubble near a grooved wall collapses later and less intensely; it also has the tendency to collapse later if the wall has narrow grooves (frames 1–3 of Fig. 5(a-f)). In addition, the position where the bubble first collapses is closer to the inner underside Sin of the groove as w increases (frame 3 of Fig. 5(b-f)), and the bubbles exhibit different shapes, since the grooves with various widths are restrictive (frame 4 of Fig. 5(a-f)). After 50 μs and 100 μs, the bubbles collapse for the second and third times, respectively, which are like the first collapse because of the restriction of the grooves (frames 5, 6 and 7–9 of Fig. 5(a-f)). These results suggest that the bubble near a narrower groove collapses later and farther from the inner underside. Therefore, the microjet near a narrower groove will generate less impact on the wall directly.

Comparison of the dynamic behaviors of SCBs near elastic walls without a groove (a) and with grooves of different widths (b) w = 25 μm, (c) w = 37.5 μm, (d) w = 50 μm, (e) w = 62.5 μm, and (f) w = 75 μm (the depth of the grooves h = 40 μm). (g) Time of bubble collapse vs. w (n = 10); the velocities of the microjets during (h) the first, (i) second and (j) third collapses of the bubble vs. w (n = 10).

The collapse time of the bubble (tc) and the velocities of the microjets during the three collapses (v) are analysed, as shown in Fig. 5(g, h, i and j). With increasing groove width w, the collapse time of bubbles becomes significantly shorter. However, compared to the bubble near the wall without a groove, the collapse time is significantly increased. Therefore, the existence of a groove structure will affect the collapse time of the cavitation bubble, thereby affecting the cavitation efficiency, and the wider the groove width is, the smaller the effect. Regarding the velocities of the microjets, the existence of the groove will obviously reduce the value of v for each subsequent collapse (Fig. 5(h, i and j)). Furthermore, the value of v has an increasing trend with increasing w during the second and third collapses (Fig. 5(i and j)) because of the decreasing restriction of the wider groove on the bubble. However, there is no significant correlation between v and the groove width w during the first collapse. In addition, the value of v during the second collapse is greater than that during the third collapse because of the restriction of the groove when the bubble expands and moves in the groove during the second and third collapses, which will consume the bubble energy.

To continue to analyze the influence of the groove depth on SCBs and cavitation efficiency to further explore the intrinsic mechanisms of LFS, elastic walls with two different groove depths and two values of E were prepared and used for experiments. Fig. 6(a-d) show the comparisons of the dynamics of the SCBs near the SLWs (E = 0.12 MPa, E = 1.3 MPa) with two different grooves (w = 50 μm, h = 40 μm and 10 μm). It is shown that the SCBs near the shallower grooves behave more like the SCBs near an elastic wall without a groove and are almost unrestricted by the grooves compared to that of the elastic wall with deeper grooves, although the shallower grooves do change the time of bubble collapse and the intensity of collapse. As shown in Fig. 6(e), as the groove’s depth increases, the value of tc increases for the elastic walls with different values of E because of the increasing restriction of the bubbles due to the presence of the groove. The value of v (Fig. 6(f)) increases from the first to the third collapse near the elastic walls with shallower grooves, which agrees with the results for an elastic wall without a groove. As a result of the restrictive difference of the grooves, the value of v (v2, v3) increases as the value of h decreases. These results reflect that the depth of the groove is also a main factor that affects the activities of the SCB and the cavitation efficiency.

Conclusions

In the present work, experimental studies are presented to investigate the influence of skin properties on the dynamics of a single bubble near the skin wall in an ultrasonic field. Elastic walls were fabricated with PDMS to simulate human skin with different characteristics (elasticity, wettability, and skin texture properties) and are referred to as SLWs. The analysis of the bubble collapse time and velocities of microjets during collapse, which are two main representations of cavitation efficiency, are presented to quantify the dynamic behaviours of cavitation bubbles. The main conclusions and new findings of the present work are summarized as follows:

1.A single bubble collapses later near a SLW with more elasticity (a lower value of E) and has a higher value of v during each collapse. The value of v increases over time from the first to the third collapse.

2.The hydrophilic property of SLWs delays the time of bubble collapse near them and slows the microjet velocity (v) during collapse

3.The existence of texture/structure or grooves of a SLW affects the dynamic characteristics of a SCB near the SLW, including delaying the time of bubble collapse and slowing the microjet velocity (v) during collapse. The deeper and narrower the grooves are, the more significant the effects are.

These conclusions are the main influence mechanisms of skin properties on a SCB in an ultrasonic field, which may be important for sonophoresis applications. According to the conclusions of this work, as SCBs collapse later near a SLW with more elasticity (a lower value of E) and has a higher value of v during each collapse, if a patient’s skin has good elasticity, then the intensity of ultrasound should be properly reduced to lower the microjets’ speed for minimizing unwanted tissue injury, with a prolonged treatment time in low-frequency ultrasound-assisted drug delivery. If a patient’s skin has a complex texture/structure, the time of SCB collapse near them would be delayed and the microjet velocity (v) during collapses would be slowed according to the results in present experiments. Then, the intensity of ultrasound and the treatment time should be properly increased, and the patient’s skin should ideally be hydrophobic.

In the present work, only the cavitation of a single bubble near SLWs is considered, and the study of bubble groups will be carried out in the future. A more detailed study of fast boundary flows and their timing during the jetting collapses is still needed, as our experiments were not developed to analyse all issues. More experiments considering triangular grooves and semicircular grooves will be carried out, as only simple square grooves are considered in present work. Finally, additional insights are also expected from advanced numerical simulations. [39].
