Benchmarking YOLOv8 to YOLOv13 for robust hand gesture recognition in human–robot interaction
Authors: Yifang Gao, Wei Luo, Shunshun Zhang, Nur Syazreen Ahmad, Xiaojun Wang, Patrick Goh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReal-time and accurate hand gesture detection is essential for safe and intuitive Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), enabling robots to interpret non-verbal cues and respond appropriately in dynamic environments. This research evaluates the effectiveness of YOLOv8n through YOLOv13n models in recognizing static hand gestures from the TSL detection dataset, which includes 5469 grayscale images across 31 gesture classes. The models underwent training with uniform data augmentation protocols and were assessed using object detection metrics including precision, recall, and mean average precision computed at an IoU threshold of 0.50 as well as over the interval from 0.50 to 0.95. The evaluation of computational efficiency involved metrics such as how fast the model infers, its frame rate, size and the total training duration. YOLOv9t exhibited the most robust detection accuracy across all evaluated metrics, achieving the highest mean mAP at 0.50 (0.990), mAP at 0.50 to 0.95 (0.876), precision (0.975), and recall (0.966). In contrast, YOLOv10n achieved the lowest inference latency (0.7 ms). These findings highlight the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency in gesture detection and show that YOLOv9t and YOLOv10n represent strong choices for accuracy and latency-critical applications, respectively.Keywords: Static hand gesture analysis, YOLO-based visual detection, Interactive robotics, Low-latency inferenceSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The development of intelligent systems increasingly depends on effective human–robot interaction (HRI), with collaborative, service, and assistive robots now deployed in domestic, industrial, and healthcare domains1–4. As demand grows for natural communication, robots must accurately interpret non-verbal cues such as gestures5,6. In real-world HRI, fast and reliable recognition of hand gestures is critical for fluent interaction and safe operation. Missed or misclassified gestures can disrupt task coordination, frustrate users, or even cause harm in shared or assistive environments7,8.

Many robotic systems still rely on handcrafted rules or wearable sensors, which can be intrusive, error-prone, or unsuitable in unconstrained settings. Vision-based methods, particularly those using deep learning, offer a promising alternative. Among CNN-based approaches, YOLO models are notable for their effectiveness in real-time detection tasks such as pose estimation and gesture recognition9–11. Earlier YOLO versions (e.g., v3–v5) have been used in hand detection and gesture recognition with good accuracy, but often face trade-offs: some lack the speed for real-time use, while others generalize poorly under variable lighting, occlusion, or unusual hand poses12–16.

The evolution from YOLOv8 to YOLOv13 has brought architectural advances such as multi-task learning, anchor-free detection heads, stronger transfer learning, and better scalability on low-power hardware17–22. These improvements may enhance detection accuracy and efficiency under challenges common in HRI, such as partial hand views, motion blur, and non-standard gesture angles. To our knowledge, no comprehensive evaluation of the most recent YOLO models for gesture-based HRI has yet been published.

This study addresses that gap by empirically evaluating five recent YOLO architectures for static hand posture detection. Experiments use the TSL Detection dataset, a curated benchmark of grayscale gesture images simulating real-world scenarios. We compare detection metrics (mAP, precision, recall), resource measures (model size), runtime (latency, FPS), and training behavior (epochs to convergence) to determine which YOLO variant is best suited for gesture-based HRI under diverse conditions.

Development of YOLO object detectors and their role in human–robot interaction

YOLO-based frameworks have shaped real-time vision systems, especially in tasks requiring both speed and precision. In HRI, where rapid recognition of human motion is critical, YOLO offers a strong balance between accuracy and responsiveness. Given the cross-disciplinary scope of this work—spanning robotics, computer vision, and human-centered AI—we provide a brief overview of YOLO’s progression, establishing the rationale for the architectures examined here. While many sub-variants exist, we focus on the canonical milestones most relevant to gesture detection in HRI.

Evolution of YOLO: from YOLOv1 to YOLOv13 for real-time hand detection

First introduced in 2015, YOLOv1 brought a new paradigm to object detection by merging the processes of classification and spatial localization within an end-to-end regression formulation. This integration simplified the detection pipeline and set the stage for subsequent one-stage detector designs9. This innovation also enabled real-time inference speeds previously unattainable with multi-stage detectors like R-CNN. YOLOv1 laid the groundwork for efficient gesture recognition, although its coarse localization and limited generalization restricted its use in dynamic HRI settings23. YOLOv2 (2016) introduced major revisions, including batch normalization24, higher input resolutions, and anchor-based bounding box priors25. These changes improved spatial precision and robustness, extending YOLO’s use to tasks like hand detection and pose tracking. YOLOv3 (2018) added residual connections and multi-scale predictions, greatly improving small object detection–important for hand pose estimation where fingers occupy small regions26. YOLOv4 (2020), the first release not authored by Redmon, integrated the CSP backbone, feature aggregation modules, Mish activation, and mosaic augmentation10,27,28. These improvements enhanced representation, convergence, and generalization, particularly for gestures with subtle finger movements.

YOLOv5 (2020) was released by Ultralytics without an academic paper but quickly gained traction due to its PyTorch implementation, modular design, and ease of deployment29. It became a popular baseline for real-time hand tracking and HRI prototypes. YOLOv6 (2022) emphasized hardware-aware optimization, introducing the EfficientRep backbone, Rep-PAN neck, anchor-free detection, and a decoupled head30,31. These features reduced computation while improving robustness under occlusion. YOLOv7 (2022) further refined memory use and backpropagation efficiency with E-ELAN11,32, leading to faster convergence and stronger multi-scale detection. Around the same time, models such as YOLOX33 and YOLOR34 also emerged, targeting speed and generalization on edge GPUs.

YOLOv8 (2023) brought re-parameterized architectures, anchor-free heads, and stronger augmentation17, outperforming earlier versions in accuracy and throughput. More recent releases–YOLOv918, YOLOv1019, YOLOv1120, YOLOv1221,35, and YOLOv1322–further advanced accuracy, adaptability, and real-time performance. YOLOv9 introduced unified multi-task learning for detection and keypoints. YOLOv10 added domain adaptation layers and attention-based segmentation. YOLOv11 improved self-supervised learning and lightweight deployment for partially labeled data. YOLOv12 integrated dynamic re-parameterization and fine-grained feature fusion, while YOLOv13 adopted transformer-inspired modules and spatiotemporal attention for high frame-rate, low-latency gesture recognition. Together, these innovations establish YOLO as a cornerstone for responsive perception in human–robot interaction.

YOLO-oriented hand detection in human–robot interaction

Vision-based deep learning has become central to advancing HRI, particularly for gesture-driven communication and control36. YOLO architectures are notable for balancing detection accuracy and speed, making them effective for real-time hand detection. Their use has been validated in applications such as localization, segmentation, and hand–object interaction.

Zhao et al.37 applied YOLOv3 in smart home environments for static and dynamic gesture recognition, achieving mAP@[0.50:0.95] of 98.1%. Lee et al.38 used YOLOv4 for mobile-based hand pose detection in cluttered scenes, reporting 93.6% accuracy. Kim et al.39 integrated YOLOv4 with a robotic manipulator for hand–object interaction under occlusion, with precision ranging from 75.2% to 88.4%. Subedi et al.40 optimized YOLOv5 for hand tracking in collaborative assembly, achieving mAP@0.50 above 99%. Tan et al.41 showed that YOLOv7 reached 96.3% precision and 91.5% recall in occlusion-heavy tasks while supporting intention recognition across hand sizes, poses, and lighting conditions. Xu et al.42 demonstrated that YOLOv8n achieved AP of 98.4% (IoU = 0.5) with real-time responsiveness in wearable AR-based HRI. While YOLOv1–YOLOv8 have been widely studied for gesture-based hand detection, models from YOLOv9 to YOLOv13 remain largely unexplored, with no systematic benchmarking yet reported in HRI.

Methodology

Dataset creation and preprocessing

This study used the publicly available TSL detection dataset43 from Roboflow Universe, released under the Creative Commons BY 4.0 license. The dataset contains grayscale images of hand gestures representing alphabet finger spelling in American Sign Language (ASL). Each image includes one labeled gesture, annotated across 31 classes covering letters (A–Z), space, and some variants (e.g., S and S–, C and C–).

All labels were reformatted into YOLO annotation style for training. Quality control was done through manual inspection in CVAT (Computer Vision Annotation Tool), an open-source system for reviewing and refining detection labels. To improve robustness, data augmentations such as flipping, rotation, lighting variation, and background overlays were applied. The final dataset contained 5469 images: 4794 for training, 497 for validation, and 178 for testing. The 31 classes were relatively balanced, with no severe class imbalance. All images were resized to pixels before training.

Model selection and training

From the YOLOv8–YOLOv13 families, six lightweight models were selected for their balance between accuracy and efficiency. The models were compared on three aspects: network depth, parameter count (in millions), and computational cost (GFLOPs). GFLOPs indicate the number of floating-point operations, in billions, required per second. Table 1 summarizes the main specifications of each model.

To ensure fair comparison, all models were trained with the same protocol on the same dataset. Experiments used the Ultralytics YOLO framework (v8.3.63) with Python 3.11.13 and PyTorch 2.6.0, running on an NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB, CUDA 12.4). Training lasted up to 600 epochs with early stopping after 50 epochs of no improvement. Input resolution was fixed at pixels, with a learning rate of 0.01 and a batch size of 16. This setup follows Ultralytics defaults and provides a reproducible baseline for model comparison. Pretrained weights (e.g., yolov10n.pt, yolov13n.pt) were taken from the official Ultralytics repository and fine-tuned on the TSL dataset. Larger models (e.g., YOLOv10m, YOLOv11l) were excluded due to higher memory and power demands, which limit real-time use on edge hardware. This decision is consistent with prior work 44, which recommends lightweight YOLO variants to sustain high frame rates with low power use in real-time vision tasks.

During training, models were periodically evaluated with standard detection metrics: precision (P), recall (R), and mean average precision (mAP). These were calculated under an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.50 and averaged across thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95. As shown in Eq. 1, precision is the proportion of correctly identified gestures among all predicted gestures. Recall (Eq. 2) is the proportion of true gestures in the dataset that were successfully detected. Mean average precision (Eq. 3) summarizes detection performance by averaging results across all gesture categories.

Equations (1)–(3) define TP as correctly identified gestures, FP as incorrectly identified gestures, and FN as missed detections, with N representing the total number of gesture classes. Confusion matrices were then applied to evaluate per-class precision and recall, covering gesture categories as well as background. In HRI, accurate gesture recognition must be balanced with low false positives to ensure safe interactions. Computational efficiency was assessed using inference time (ms), frames per second (FPS), training duration (epochs), and model size (MB). For HRI tasks, these performance measures play a key role in enabling real-time execution on low-power or resource-limited edge platforms.

Evaluation of detection metrics

The detection performance of YOLOv8 through YOLOv13 in recognizing hand gestures for human–robot interaction is analyzed here, considering precision, recall, mAP at IoU 0.50, and mAP averaged across IoUs from 0.50 to 0.95. Results are summarized in Table 2, all models achieved perfect or near-perfect mAP@0.50 (0.995) on their top-performing gesture classes, indicating strong baseline detection ability. However, mAP at 50 to 95, which provides a stricter assessment of localization quality, revealed more noticeable differences. YOLOv9t achieved the highest per-class mAP at 50 to 95, with G- and C- reaching 0.995 and 0.989, respectively. YOLOv10n followed closely with similarly strong scores on G- (0.995) and C- (0.990), showing high spatial precision on well-structured gestures. YOLOv11n and YOLOv12n maintained good performance across most classes but exhibited slight drops on more articulated gestures like S- (0.949 and 0.962, respectively), suggesting some sensitivity to occlusion or finger curvature. Notably, YOLOv13n delivered consistently high mAP at 50 to 95 across varied gestures–0.987 on G-, 0.964 on I-, and 0.951 on C-–indicating balanced generalisation and robust localization across open-palm, curved, and finger-extended classes. Although YOLOv8n showed perfect recall on its top classes, its mAP at 50 to 95 for C- (0.974) and I- (0.970) was slightly lower than the newer models. Overall, YOLOv9t and YOLOv10n achieved the highest per-class localization accuracy, while YOLOv13n offered the most balanced and reliable detection across diverse gesture types.

Computational efficiency

Beyond detection accuracy, computational efficiency is essential for real-time HRI. This section evaluates YOLOv8–v13 models in terms of latency, FPS, and size to assess suitability for edge deployment. Model sizes ranged from 4.50 MB (YOLOv9t) to 5.98 MB (YOLOv8n), with all under 6 MB, suggesting compatibility with resource-constrained devices such as the NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX. YOLOv13n (5.17 MB) and YOLOv12n (5.22 MB) offered strong accuracy while remaining lightweight, making them well suited for systems requiring both efficiency and precision.

Inference speed directly affects responsiveness in gesture-based HRI. Here, inference time refers to per-frame latency, including preprocessing, model inference, and post-processing. YOLOv10n was fastest at 0.7 ms, followed by YOLOv8n (1.2 ms), YOLOv11n (1.3 ms), YOLOv9t (1.9 ms), YOLOv12n (2.0 ms), and YOLOv13n (2.2 ms). The slightly higher latency of YOLOv13n was due to longer inference and post-processing, but it still exceeded 450 FPS and trained with stable convergence over 184 epochs.

YOLOv8n achieved solid accuracy (mAP@0.50 = 0.986) and fast inference but showed less stable training and a lower mAP@[0.50:0.95] (0.829). YOLOv9t reached the highest overall accuracy (mAP@0.50 = 0.990, mAP@[0.50:0.95] = 0.876, precision = 0.975, recall = 0.966), detecting subtle or occluded gestures more reliably. YOLOv13n also stood out for consistent performance, making it a reliable choice for long-term robotic use.

Although latencies were measured on an NVIDIA A100 GPU (FP32 peak 19.5 TFLOPS), the small sizes of these models suggest promise for edge devices. Prior benchmarks 45 showed YOLO nano variants sustaining 30–40 FPS at 640640 on the Jetson Orin NX (FP32 1.88 TFLOPS) using FP16 and TensorRT. By analogy, YOLOv10n, YOLOv11n, and YOLOv12n may also support real-time embedded HRI with efficient resource usage. Error analysis

Table 4 shows the error breakdown for YOLOv9t, averaged across three random seeds (mean ± standard deviation). Three error types were identified following the COCO-style taxonomy, which ensures each prediction is uniquely assigned to one category: (i) missed detections, (ii) false positives, and (iii) localization errors. On average, localization errors were the most frequent (11.2%), while missed detections (3.4%) and false positives (2.6%) were less common.

Qualitative inspection confirmed that these errors arise in intuitive situations. Missed detections often happened when the hand was cropped at the image boundary or partly hidden, causing the model to overlook the gesture. False positives mainly occurred when gestures looked visually similar—for example, a curved “C” gesture sometimes resembled a closed “O”. Localization errors were most prominent: bounding boxes frequently failed to align well with hand contours when fingers were bent or rotated, leading to boxes that were either too tight or extended beyond the hand.

These error patterns have practical implications for HRI. Missed or misclassified gestures, even if relatively rare, can interrupt smooth communication by causing the robot to ignore or misinterpret commands. Localization errors, though less likely to break recognition entirely, reduce spatial precision and may hinder downstream tasks such as pointing or tracking. Overall, the analysis highlights two key areas for improvement: better handling of occlusion and gesture similarity, and stronger spatial modeling to improve bounding box accuracy.

Comparative analysis with prior research

Earlier studies on real-time detection often used YOLO-based frameworks as their main architecture. Table 5 summarizes the performance of YOLOv8–v13 models retrained and evaluated in this work. All models were fine-tuned from their official pretrained weights on the same dataset with consistent settings to ensure fair comparison.

Sen et al. 46 pruned a YOLOv5-s model, achieving precision of 0.951, recall of 0.931, and mAP@0.50 of 0.938. However, its mAP@[0.50:0.95] was lower (0.672), and inference was slower at 16.1 ms per frame (62 FPS). By contrast, YOLOv9t and YOLOv13n in this study achieved higher accuracy across all metrics and exceeded 200 FPS with models under 6 MB. Chen and Tian 47 proposed a YOLOv5 with CBAM for cluttered environments. While effective for noisy backgrounds, it reached only 0.756 mAP@0.50 and 15.6 ms per frame (64 FPS), making it slower and less accurate than even the smallest models here, such as YOLOv10n and YOLOv8n, which achieved sub-millisecond latency (2 ms) and mAP@0.50 above 0.98. Raj et al.’s ISL study 48 used YOLOv7 for gesture detection, reporting mAP@0.50 of 0.947, precision of 0.944, and recall of 0.929. While stronger than YOLOv4-based works, its latency (14.5 ms, 69 FPS) was much higher than YOLOv10n (0.7 ms, 1429 FPS). Aziz et al. 49 reported 0.933 mAP@0.50 with YOLOv4 but a latency of 66.7 ms (15 FPS). Similarly, Zhuang et al.’s YOLOv4-MBG 50 improved detection under motion blur (mAP@0.50 = 0.968) but was slower at 38.2 ms (26 FPS). These works show progress over early YOLO versions but fall short of real-time needs in HRI.

Overall, YOLOv8–v13 models in this study consistently achieved real-time inference (> 400 FPS), high accuracy (mAP@0.50 > 0.986), and compact sizes (< 5.98 MB). Among them, YOLOv10n delivered the fastest inference (0.7 ms), while YOLOv9t reached the highest mAP@[0.50:0.95] (0.876). All results were obtained using an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Latency values in other studies may differ due to hardware, batch size, or deployment optimizations (e.g., TensorRT, quantization).

In addition to YOLO-based architectures, prior research has extensively benchmarked alternative paradigms such as two-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-CNN51) and Transformer-based models (e.g., DETR52) on standard benchmarks like COCO. Reported results indicate that Faster R-CNN-FPN (42M parameters) achieves an AP of 40.2 with 26 FPS, while DETR-R50 (41M parameters) attains an AP of 42.0 with 28 FPS. By contrast, published evaluations of the lightweight YOLOv8–v13 nano variants show that, despite containing fewer than 3.2M parameters, they achieve comparable accuracy (AP at 50 to 95 around 41–42) while sustaining over 500 FPS on modern GPUs. These comparisons situate YOLO within a broader landscape of detection paradigms and highlight its ability to deliver competitive accuracy at a fraction of the computational footprint.

Conclusion and limitations

This study evaluated six YOLO variants (YOLOv8 to YOLOv13) on the Roboflow TSL dataset. YOLOv9t achieved the highest detection accuracy, with mean scores of 0.990 mAP@0.50, 0.876 mAP@[0.50:0.95], precision of 0.975, and recall of 0.966. YOLOv13n showed balanced performance, combining high precision (0.964), strong recall (0.953), and stable convergence, making it suitable for general-purpose gesture recognition. YOLOv10n achieved the lowest inference latency of 0.7 ms per frame (about 1429 FPS), making it the most efficient model for latency-critical tasks. These findings highlight the trade-off between accuracy, processing speed, and model complexity in HRI-focused deployments. Compared with earlier YOLO versions and prior gesture recognition studies, the newer variants (YOLOv9–YOLOv13) demonstrate clear improvements in both accuracy and inference efficiency, reinforcing their suitability for vision-based perception modules in interactive robotics. From a deployment perspective, model choice should reflect system priorities: YOLOv9t is best for maximum accuracy, YOLOv10n for latency-critical applications such as mobile or embedded robots, and YOLOv13n as a reliable general-purpose model. YOLOv8n and YOLOv12n remain competitive lightweight options, offering strong accuracy with small model sizes for resource-constrained platforms.

This study also has several limitations. First, the TSL dataset, though clean and well annotated, is relatively small and does not capture real-world challenges such as motion blur or dynamic lighting. Its focus on static gestures further limits generalization. Second, although the models are compact and potentially suitable for embedded use, their performance on actual hardware was not tested. Future work will address these gaps by incorporating live video evaluations, benchmarking on physical robotic platforms, and comparing more against non-YOLO models. These steps will provide a clearer view of deployment feasibility and support the design of robust, low-latency gesture recognition systems for interactive robotics.
