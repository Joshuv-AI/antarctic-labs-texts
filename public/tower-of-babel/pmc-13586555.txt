A Beginner's Guide to Study the Role of Extracellular Vesicles in Coagulation
Authors: Rienk Nieuwland, Jillian W P Bracht, Joost C M Meijers, Johannes Thaler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis guide provides an overview of information that is needed to understand and study the coagulant functions of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in human body fluids. Although EV research has expanded rapidly, the physiological functions of EVs remain incompletely understood, and coagulation is one of the first biological functions that was attributed to EVs. Since coagulation is a complex balance between coagulation promoting and inhibiting pathways, for clarity and to reduce complexity we focus exclusively on how EVs contribute to coagulation‐promoting pathways in physiological conditions. We explain what coagulation is, and how coagulation contributes to haemostasis and host defence, followed by an overview of historical evidence showing that extracellular vesicles initiate and promote coagulation. In addition, we explain how extracellular vesicles provide a coagulation‐promoting surface, and expand the classical “envelope model”. Finally, we summarize how the procoagulant activities of EVs can be measured, and provide guidance to improve the reproducibility of such measurements.

Introduction to Human Haemostasis and Coagulation

Haemostasis

Tissue injury results in blood loss and increases the risk of infection, that is of microorganisms such as bacteria entering the blood. Consequently, a wound must be sealed rapidly, and haemostasis is the encompassing term of all cellular and biochemical processes that contribute to wound sealing and healing (Figure 1). Upon vascular damage, platelets adhere, secrete their granule contents, and aggregate thereby forming a platelet plug. In the literature, the process of platelet adhesion, secretion and aggregation is called “primary haemostasis”, and coagulation is called “secondary haemostasis”.

Coagulation

Coagulation is the process that leads to conversion of fibrinogen, one of our most abundant plasma proteins, into insoluble fibrin strands. Fibrin acts as a natural “band‐aid”, which traps bacteria and glues platelets and other cells together, thereby sealing the wound (Figure 1). Coagulation requires coagulation factors, calcium ions, a membrane surface, and an initiator, which all are present in blood except the initiator. The coagulation factors in blood are predominantly present as inactive precursors called zymogens, which can interact with membranes. Many activated coagulation factors are serine proteases that activate other coagulation factors by proteolytic cleavage. In contrast, the so‐called cofactors have no enzymatic activity themselves but they strongly enhance the activity of other coagulation factors by promoting their proteolytic activity and assembling protease and substrate. These interactions are predominantly calcium ion‐ dependent and require both a membrane surface modification and gamma carboxylation of specific coagulation factors, resulting in assembly of coagulation factor complexes and activation of coagulation factors.

To understand the contribution of extracellular vesicles (EVs) to coagulation, we will describe coagulation using the cell‐based model (Hoffman and Monroe, 2001). This model provides insight in how coagulation works, but to which extent this model also applies to EVs, requires additional studies. In this cell‐based model, coagulation proceeds in three spatially organized and partially overlapping phases: initiation, amplification, and propagation.

Initiation starts when membranes expose tissue factor (TF). TF is a transmembrane receptor that binds circulating (activated) coagulation factor (F) VII(a). The complex of TF/FVII(a), also called “extrinsic tenase complexes” (Figure 2, top, left), initiates local generation of minute amounts of FXa and thrombin.

These minute amounts of thrombin amplify coagulation by activating platelets and the key cofactors FV and FVIII (Figure 2, bottom panel, from left to right). Platelet activation results in exposure of phosphatidylserine (PS). In non‐activated platelets, PS is present in the inner leaflet of the membrane, but upon activation PS evenly distributes in the inner and outer leaflets (Nagata et al., 2016). The vitamin K‐dependent coagulation factors FII, FVII, FIX and FX contain N‐terminal γ‐carboxyglutamic acid (Gla)‐rich domains. The Gla residues bind calcium ions, which stabilize a membrane‐binding conformation and mediate interactions with the negatively charged head groups of PS. Hydrophobic residues within the Gla domain additionally insert into the phospholipid bilayer. Together, these interactions anchor and concentrate the coagulation factors on the membrane surface, thereby facilitating the assembly of coagulation factor complexes (Dahlback, 2000; Heemskerk et al., 2002).

During propagation, both intrinsic tenase complexes (FIXa and its cofactor FVIIIa) and prothrombinase complexes (FXa and its cofactor FVa) are formed on the PS‐exposing membrane surface. This formation results in a burst of thrombin generation and fibrin formation. Importantly, this surface‐oriented organization concentrates coagulation factors on a membrane surface, thereby accelerating the enzymatic coagulation reactions by multiple orders of magnitude compared to reactions in solution (Spronk et al., 2003).

This surface‐centric model naturally extends to EVs, which can contribute to coagulation by (i) providing a procoagulant PS‐exposing membrane surface and (ii) TF or extrinsic tenase complexes. Thus, EVs can contribute to initiation, amplification and propagation of coagulation. Platelet‐derived EVs, which are commonly present in blood, can expose PS (without detectable TF), and are best considered as procoagulant membrane platforms. As such, platelet EVs primarily support amplification and propagation of coagulation by providing the negatively charged surface required for assembly and function of the intrinsic tenase and prothrombinase complexes (Tripisciano et al., 2017; Guerreiro et al., 2024; Tripisciano et al., 2020).

On the other hand, EVs from for example, amniotic fluid, mother's milk, saliva and urine initiate coagulation because they expose extrinsic tenase complexes (Hu et al., 2022; Thaler et al., 2024). When added to recalcified plasma, body fluid–derived EVs exposing extrinsic tenase complexes can induce fibrin formation almost immediately. These EVs act as potent triggers of FXa generation, such that downstream propagation via the FVIIIa/FIXa (intrinsic tenase) complex becomes largely dispensable. Consistent with this, we previously observed that even in severe FVIII deficiency, salivary EVs can provide sufficient procoagulant activity to prevent oropharyngeal bleedings (Thaler et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that, in an EV‐based view of coagulation, the classical phase concept (initiation, amplification, propagation) remains useful, but the boundaries between phases can be context‐dependent and may be less distinct when strong EV‐associated extrinsic tenase activity is present.

The spatial separation between PS‐exposing EVs in blood and body fluid–derived EVs that expose extrinsic tenase complexes aligns with the physiological compartmentalization of TF. In physiological conditions, TF is constitutively produced by cells surrounding the vessel wall, specifically adventitial fibroblasts and pericytes, which together form a “haemostatic barrier” that is exposed to the circulating blood upon vascular injury (Furie and Furie, 2008; Mackman, 2006). TF is also abundantly produced in highly vascularized organs such as the brain, heart and lungs (Furie and Furie, 2008; Osterud and Bjorklid, 2006), and is exposed by EVs in body fluids other than blood. Only in pathological conditions, such as in patients with sepsis or cancer, also blood can contain TF‐exposing EVs, and the presence of such EVs is associated with an increased risk of developing thrombosis, that is, blood clots within the intact blood vessel (Tesselaar et al., 2007; Thaler et al., 2012; van Es et al., 2018; Bharthuar et al., 2013; Thaler et al., 2014; Hell et al., 2021).

Historical Evidence of Extracellular Vesicle Involvement in Coagulation

To explain the long and fascinating history of discoveries showing that EVs promote and initiate coagulation, it is important to remember that most of this work was performed before the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) introduced the term “extracellular vesicles” (van der Pol et al., 2016). Before its introduction, researchers were often unaware of each other's work, and standardization of nomenclature was a prerequisite to unify the field. Something comparable happened in the history of EVs and coagulation. For nearly a century, studies were performed on different continents and results were published in different languages, nomenclature changed over time, and during the first decades EVs remained unknown until Peter Wolf first described “platelet dust” or platelet‐derived EVs in 1967 (Wolf, 1967).

With our current knowledge of EVs, we can now explain results from the very first observational and interventional studies that were performed already in the 1930s. From an educational perspective, these studies illustrate the surprising ability of EVs to initiate coagulation and to stop bleeding. This hitherto virtually undescribed part of EV history is summarized in Section 2.2, which is preceded by an overview of discoveries showing that human blood EVs can promote coagulation by providing a membrane surface in Section 2.1.

Coagulation‐Promoting Extracellular Vesicles in Human Blood

EVs can provide a coagulation‐promoting surface that supports formation of tenase complexes and prothrombinase complexes (Section 1), and in 1946 Chargaff and West showed that cell‐free human plasma contains an undescribed subcellular factor (i.e., a factor smaller than platelets, the smallest cells present in human blood) that promotes coagulation (Chargaff and West, 1946). In 1967, this subcellular factor was identified as “platelet dust” or platelet‐derived EVs, which proved that endogenous blood EVs can provide a coagulation‐promoting surface (Figure 2) (Wolf, 1967). Since the 1980s, research focused on how “platelet dust” promotes coagulation. These studies showed that platelet EVs bind coagulation factors V and VIII, which are both coagulation factors that are not carboxylated, with high affinity (Lentz, 2003). This efficient binding or “docking” of coagulation factors can now be explained by changes in the distribution of membrane phospholipids such as PS (Section 1).

The relevance of a membrane surface to support coagulation in vivo became clear in 1979, when the underlying cause of a life‐long bleeding tendency was discovered (Weiss et al., 1979). The patient, suffering from what is now known as “Scott syndrome”, had an inherited bleeding disorder that was caused by a phospholipid‐scramblase‐1 (PLSCR1) deficiency (Figure 3). Due to this deficiency, PS cannot be transported (“flipped”) from the inner to the outer leaflet of the phospholipid bilayer (membrane) of platelets. This deficiency results in reduced exposure of PS on activated platelets and their EVs (Toti et al., 1996). Consequently, coagulation factors cannot bind efficiently and coagulation becomes impaired, which leads to a bleeding phenotype and underscores the relevance of a procoagulant membrane surface.

Phospholipids are the main component of eukaryotic cell membranes. Membranes contain different types of phospholipids, and negatively charged (anionic) phospholipids such as phosphatidylserine are exclusively present in the inner leaflet of the phospholipid bilayer of resting cells. To maintain this “phospholipid symmetry”, the membranes contain a family of phospholipid‐specific transporter proteins, called (from left to right) flippases (ATP‐dependent), floppases (ATP‐dependent), and scramblases (ATP‐independent). Exposure of PS by blood EVs may have been overestimated due to pre‐analytical and analytical challenges. The pre‐analytical challenge is to prepare truly cell‐free plasma and serum, since remaining cells can fragment during freeze‐thaw cycles (please see also Section 5.3). Particularly platelets, the smallest type of cell present in any human body fluid and only marginally larger than EVs, are difficult to separate from EVs by centrifugation. Remaining platelets can fragment, thereby producing PS‐exposing platelet fragments which can be easily misidentified as PS‐exposing “platelet microparticles”. In many of the early studies flow cytometry was used to detect “platelet microparticles”, which encountered two analytical challenges. Firstly, a commonly applied size gating strategy to select EVs proved wrong and selected platelet‐sized events rather than EVs (Bettin et al., 2023). Secondly, in 2018 nearly all flow cytometers were unable to measure EVs of 300 nm, and thus these instruments were too insensitive to measure the bulk of EVs (van der Pol et al., 2018). Taken together, in many studies the exposure of PS by blood EVs has probably been overestimated. This is confirmed by a more recent lipidomic analysis of EVs from human body fluids, which shows that blood‐derived EVs contain about 0.1 mol% PS, whereas EVs from other body fluids as human semen and milk contain over 50 mol% PS (Gross et al., 2024).

So the question is whether contemporary methods can confirm the exposure of PS by platelet‐derived EVs. To answer this question, we collected EDTA plasma from healthy donors, and measured EVs in the size range of 200 nm to 1 µm by flow cytometry. EVs from platelets (CD61+), leukocytes (CD45+) and erythrocytes (CD235a+) exposed detectable levels of PS, unpublished observation). Thus, various populations of blood‐derived EVs expose PS (Tripisciano et al., 2017; Van Der Meijden et al., 2012).

Coagulation‐Initiating Extracellular Vesicles in Body Fluids Other than Blood

Persons with severe haemophilia suffer from a hereditary coagulation factor defect, which results in prolonged and unstoppable bleedings. The Austrian paediatrician Alphons Solé was looking for a therapy, and in 1934, in a German‐language publication which was not cited for nearly 60 years, he reported that tamponades soaked in human milk effectively stop external bleeding in persons with severe haemophilia. Recently, we confirmed this haemostatic potential of human milk, and showed that (cell‐free) human milk efficiently initiates clotting of blood and plasma even at over 4,000‐fold dilutions (Hu et al., 2020). This haemostatic potential of human milk is solely explained by the presence of EVs exposing extrinsic tenase complexes (Hu et al., 2022).

From 1926 onwards, studies were published showing that also human amniotic fluid, saliva, urine, and semen initiate blood clotting (Meyer, 1926; Hunter, 1928; Grunke, 1935; Huggins and Neal, 1942). The coagulant potential of these body fluids is solely explained by the presence of EVs exposing extrinsic tenase complexes (Figure 2) (Thaler et al., 2024). There are differences in the extrinsic tenase activity of these body fluids, and for example semen triggers coagulation even at a 10,000‐fold dilution (Huggins and Neal, 1942). The physiological relevance of these findings may be that these body fluids are located outside the circulation and can come into contact with blood when epithelial or vascular barriers are damaged. EVs exposing functional extrinsic tenase complexes may therefore act as local endogenous haemostatic agents and enable rapid initiation of coagulation at such sites. Whether this concept applies equally to all of these body fluids remains unknown. This extrinsic tenase activity may also play a role in cell signalling. For example, extrinsic tenase complexes cleave and thereby activate protease‐activated receptor (PAR)‐2, which is involved in regulation of the epithelial barrier function, and free FVIIa dampens inflammation via endothelial protein C receptor and PAR1 (Kondreddy et al., 2018; Le Gall et al., 2016).

Although compelling evidence shows that the procoagulant activity of body fluids other than blood is explained by the presence of EVs exposing extrinsic tenase complexes, evidence was lacking that such EVs really contribute to haemostasis in vivo. Recently, we showed that the absence of functional extrinsic tenase complexes on salivary EVs is associated with the occurrence of mucosal bleedings (and the presence of extrinsic tenase complexes on salivary EVs limits mucosal bleedings), confirming that interactions between blood and other body fluids (i.e., body fluids containing highly coagulant EVs exposing extrinsic tenase complexes) likely also contributes to haemostasis in vivo (Thaler et al., 2024). Of note, not all physiological body fluids contain a strong coagulation‐promoting activity. As reviewed in detail elsewhere, normal cerebrospinal fluid and follicular fluid have no or only limited haemostatic potential, and bile and follicular fluid inhibit coagulation (Thaler et al., 2024). Lymph contains most coagulation components albeit at lower concentrations than in plasma and lymph does not promote coagulation under physiological conditions (Miller et al., 2000; Zhang et al., 2021.) Thus, the presence of coagulation‐promoting EVs differs between body fluids and remains unknown for several compartments.

Extracellular Vesicles Promote and Initiate Coagulation

EVs can provide a coagulation‐promoting (or procoagulant) PS‐rich surface, and initiate coagulation by exposing extrinsic tenase complexes. In Section 3.1, we explain the concept of membrane asymmetry which is needed to understand how cells and EVs propagate coagulation, and we explain the envelop model, and how this model is currently being expanded by including EVs (Section 3.2).

Notably, PS exposure and TF/extrinsic tenase complexes are not the only mechanisms by which EVs may influence coagulation. EV‐associated polyphosphate and other negatively charged components can support FXII‐dependent contact activation, and EVs can interact with neutrophil extracellular traps and extracellular DNA (Tripisciano et al., 2017; Shim et al., 2021; Whitefoot‐Keliin et al., 2024). EVs may also transfer regulatory molecules, including miRNAs, that alter the procoagulant properties of recipient cells (Li et al., 2020). These mechanisms have mainly been studied in experimental and pathological conditions and are not discussed further in this guide.

Regulation of Membrane Asymmetry

Based on the estimated concentration and size distribution of EVs in body fluids, EVs can provide a substantial membrane surface area to promote coagulation (Nieuwland and Siljander, 2024; Arraud et al., 2014). The membrane surrounding cells, organelles, and EVs is a bilayer of phospholipids. This bilayer contains multiple different types of phospholipids. In resting (unstimulated) cells, these different types of phospholipids are not symmetrically distributed within the membrane. The inner membrane leaflet contains PS, phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylinositol, the outer membrane leaflet contains phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin (Sakuragi and Nagata, 2023). This non‐symmetrical distribution of phospholipids is known as “membrane asymmetry”, and explains why membranes from resting (unstimulated) cells do not expose PS, and hence do not provide a suitable “docking surface” for coagulation factors.

The phospholipid asymmetry is actively maintained in cells by lipid transporters that translocate phospholipids between the leaflets (Figure 3), which is a common hallmark of plasma membranes, Golgi membranes, and endosomal membranes (Clarke et al., 2020). Membrane asymmetry contributes to selective transport of molecules across the membrane, membrane potential, transmembrane signalling, and membrane fusion events (Caputo et al., 2025). More information on membrane asymmetry and its regulation and functions have been extensively reviewed elsewhere, and are considered to be beyond the scope of this guide (Fadeel and Xue, 2009). To which extent lipid transporters are present in EVs and can regulate phospholipid distribution, is unknown.

When platelets are activated or when nucleated cells undergo apoptosis, the phospholipid composition in the membrane becomes symmetrical, meaning that the different types of phospholipids redistribute, which results in equal quantities of PS molecules in the inner and outer leaflet of the phospholipid bilayer (Bevers et al., 1983). Due to externalization and exposure of PS, PS‐exposing membrane provide a suitable “docking surface” to which coagulation factors can bind and on which tenase and prothrombinase complexes can assemble (Nagata et al., 2016). There is ample evidence in the literature that EVs can expose PS (Gross et al., 2024; Flaskamp et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2018; Matsumura et al., 2019).

Most studies on EV‐associated coagulation have focused on larger EVs, historically termed microparticles or microvesicles. In contrast, the contribution of small EVs, which according to MISEV2023 are generally described as EVs <200 nm in diameter (Welsh et al., 2024), is less clear. Studies using differential centrifugation showed that procoagulant phospholipid activity in human plasma is more pronounced in fractions enriched in larger EVs, although activity is also detectable in fractions enriched in smaller EVs (Nielsen et al., 2018). Similarly, both larger and smaller endothelial cell‐derived EV fractions can expose functional TF and promote coagulation, although the strongest effect was observed for the larger EV fraction (Holnthoner et al., 2017). Importantly, differential centrifugation enriches rather than completely separates EVs according to size. Thus, small EVs may contribute to coagulation, but their specific contribution remains difficult to determine.

Extracellular Vesicles Expand the ‘Hemostatic Envelope Model’

TF is constitutively expressed by extravascular and perivascular cells, whereas FVII circulates in the blood. In the traditional “hemostatic envelope model” (please see also the last paragraph of Section 1), TF and its ligand factor FVII are supposed to be spatially separated by the intact vessel wall. Only when a vessel becomes damaged, the blood‐borne FVII will encounter extravascular TF, which leads to formation of extrinsic tenase complexes and initiation of (extrinsic) coagulation.

Recent findings, however, show that at least part of the TF‐rich envelope surrounding blood vessels is present already as preformed extrinsic tenase complexes (Hoffman et al., 2007). Consequently, vascular injury is apparently not a requirement for formation of active extrinsic tenase complexes. Secondly, it is now clear that also EVs in body fluids other than blood expose extrinsic tenase complexes (Figure 2) (Thaler et al., 2024). These fluids are located outside the vascular system, and therefore such extrinsic tenase complex‐exposing EVs should be considered as components of the haemostatic envelope, because likely they enable faster and more efficient initiation of coagulation when a blood vessel is damaged and extravasating blood contacts these body fluids.

Measuring Coagulation

The contribution of EVs to coagulation depends on their ability to (i) provide a procoagulant PS‐rich surface for the adhesion and formation of coagulation factor complexes, and (ii) expose TF and/or functional extrinsic tenase complexes. A recent interlaboratory comparison study showed that the sensitivity and specificity of functional coagulation assays that measure the TF / extrinsic tenase activity of EVs outperform antigen assays detecting the presence of TF (Mackman and Sachetto, 2024; Bonifay et al., 2024; Nieuwland et al., 2019; Bonifay et al., 2024; Sachetto et al., 2023). This difference in sensitivity and specificity is explained by the fact that the coagulation process itself has many amplification loops (Spronk et al., 2003), whereas detection of antigens on EVs suffers from the limited availability of the epitopes of interest (Bonifay et al., 2024). Moreover, the presence of antigens on EVs is not necessarily related to their functional activity, since for example in several studies a lack of correlation between functional TF and TF antigen levels was found (Mackman and Sachetto, 2024; Sachetto et al., 2023; Mackman et al., 2022; Sachetto and Mackman, 2024). Therefore, we will focus mainly on functional coagulation assays. Figure 4, shows four functional assays that quantify the procoagulant activity of EVs. By definition, functional coagulation assays are bulk assays that are performed in suspension, that is, assays that measure the contribution of all EVs that are present in a sample of interest. This is important, because in such assays also the potential contribution of the small EVs can be taken into account, which are the most abundant EVs and potentially provide the largest surface area supporting coagulation.

Firstly, the prothrombinase assay is a functional assay that measures the efficacy of EVs to enable formation of prothrombinase complexes (Amiral and Seghatchian, 2015). The formation of these complexes depends on the biologically active PS that is provided by EVs, and this assay quantifies the amount of PS that is available but is independent from the presence of TF and/or extrinsic tenase complexes.

Secondly, there are multiple functional assays that measure and quantify the presence of TF and/or preformed extrinsic tenase complexes on EVs. These assays measure either generation of FXa (Hu et al., 2022; Tesselaar et al., 2007; Hisada et al., 2016; Khorana et al., 2008), thrombin (Tripisciano et al., 2017; Kristensen and Nybo, 2023; Osterud et al., 2022; Gheldof et al., 2013; Hell et al., 2017), or fibrin (van Es et al., 2018; Thaler et al., 2014; Humphries et al., 2021), or clotting of whole blood (Thaler et al., 2024; Holnthoner et al., 2017; Hell et al., 2017; Berckmans et al., 2011; Santucci et al., 2000; Butenas et al., 2005). These assays all rely on the same principle, namely that EVs exposing TF and/or extrinsic tenase complexes are the rate‐limiting step in initiating coagulation, whereas all other coagulation components are present.

Functional coagulation assays can be performed either using purified reagents which are added to the EVs, or they can be performed in plasma itself, either to study the ability of the endogenous plasma EVs to affect coagulation (i.e. without addition of exogenous EVs), or after the addition of isolated (exogenous) EVs if they are the subject of interest, and such EVs have been isolated from biofluids for example by size exclusion chromatography (Boing et al., 2014), differential centrifugation (Nielsen et al., 2018), or immunocapture (Franco et al., 2020).

Commonly used activity assays measure generation of FXa or FIIa (thrombin) (Bonifay et al., 2024). Activated coagulation factors, such as FXa and thrombin, are enzymes, and the activities of these enzymes can be measured using chromogenic substrates for the determination of FXa generation and prothrombinase activity, and a fluorochrome for the determination of the thrombin generation potential of plasma, respectively.

There are several experimental possibilities, for example some researchers first concentrate EVs in a plasma sample by centrifugation to measure FXa generation, but in essence the principle of the assay remains the same, that is, the conversion of a chromogenic substrate or fluorochrome by factor Xa or thrombin (Bonifay et al., 2024; Bonifay et al., 2024). Also, the clotting time in cell free plasma can be measured. The plasma clotting time (or PCT) has been used for decades, and measures the ability of formed thrombin to convert fibrinogen, that is, the natural substrate of thrombin, into insoluble fibrin. This assay is also known as the “fibrin‐generation test”. To ensure specificity, it is recommended to perform these activity assays in the presence of inhibitory antibodies against factor VIIa or TF (Bonifay et al., 2024).

Guidance, Considerations and Recommendations

This section contains an overview of educational information and methodological guidelines, which are both needed to understand the principles of measuring the coagulant function of EVs.

Plasma or Serum

Collected plasma contains an ex vivo added anticoagulant. When blood is collected without an anticoagulant, the blood will clot, and the collected blood fluid that is collected after clotting has completed, is called serum. Serum is unsuitable as a starting material to study coagulation, because coagulation factors and fibrinogen are consumed by coagulation activation, and platelets and other cells become activated and release de novo EVs (Flaujac et al., 2025).

Anticoagulation of Blood

Calcium‐Ion Chelating Anticoagulants

Collection of blood and preparation of plasma requires anticoagulation. Blood and plasma contain predominantly inactive precursors of coagulation factors, which are also known as zymogens. FII, FVII, FIX, and FX undergo a posttranslational modification, that is, the addition of a carboxyl group to some of their glutamic acid residues by a vitamin K‐dependent liver enzyme called gamma‐glutamyl carboxylase. In turn, this modification facilitates the binding of zymogens to the PS‐exposing membrane surface, which is essential to become activated. Since the binding of zymogens to PS is calcium ion‐dependent, several commonly used anticoagulants chelate calcium ions. This chelation prevents binding of zymogens to PS, and thus prevents formation of both tenase and prothrombinase complexes, and thereby also inhibiting thrombin generation and formation of fibrin. Additional information about the effects of on blood collection for EV research can be found elsewhere (Nieuwland and Siljander, 2024; Dhondt et al., 2023).

Citrate (usually 0.32% trisodium citrate) is the most commonly used anticoagulant when collecting blood or plasma for coagulation studies. Citrate mildly chelates calcium ions, thereby reversibly inhibiting the binding of coagulation factors to the membrane (Lima‐Oliveira et al., 2021).

The anticoagulant used for blood collection can influence the measured concentration and procoagulant activity of EVs. A study comparing anticoagulants in commercially available blood collection tubes reported differences in EV measurements by both flow cytometry and a PS‐dependent prothrombinase assay. In particular, ACD (acid‐citrate‐dextrose) reduced in vitro vesiculation caused by blood handling and storage (Gyorgy et al., 2014). Another alternative may be CTAD (Citrate‐Theophylline‐Adenosine‐Dipyridamole)‐containing tubes, in which platelet activation is prevented by multiple platelet‐inhibiting compounds (Ulbing et al., 2026). Thus, the type of anticoagulant should be considered when comparing functional EV coagulation studies.

Another alternative may be CTAD (Citrate‐Theophylline‐Adenosine‐Dipyridamole)‐containing tubes, in which platelet activation is prevented by multiple platelet‐inhibiting compounds (Ulbing et al., 2026). Thus, the type of anticoagulant should be considered when comparing functional EV coagulation studies.

Other Anticoagulants

There are also commercially available blood collection tubes used for functional coagulation studies that contain anticoagulants not chelating calcium ions. The two most commonly used are heparin and hirudin.

Heparin binds antithrombin, which is a plasma protein that inhibits coagulation (Gray et al., 2012). When heparin binds to antithrombin, the affinity of antithrombin for both FXa and thrombin increases, which results in accelerated inhibition of both FXa and thrombin. Heparin is commonly used during surgery, and heparin‐anticoagulated plasma can be used for EV coagulation studies by degrading heparin by addition of heparinase, a heparin‐degrading enzyme, or by separating EVs from the heparin‐containing plasma (Hirsh et al., 2001, Garcia et al., 2012). Future Directions

During the last decade, EV research primarily focused on improving detection and isolation, understanding their contribution to intercellular communication, and exploring EVs as biomarkers for diseases such as cancer and cardiovascular disease. When going back in time, however, the original functions attributed to EVs were cellular waste management and coagulation. These early discoveries were made when studying “physiological models”, maturing sheep erythrocytes in the case of exosomes, and blood plasma from healthy humans in the case of microvesicles (called “platelet dust” upon their discovery). Thus far, the role of EVs in physiology is understudied, and why human body fluids such as milk, saliva, and urine contain EVs is unclear. For example, both saliva and urine contain EVs, but it seems difficult to image that the EVs present in such “waste fluids” contribute to intercellular communication. The fact that these EVs expose extrinsic tenase complexes suggests that they more likely contribute to conserved protection processes such as haemostasis. Understanding how EVs contribute to coagulation may therefore provide insight into one of their most fundamental physiological functions.

Still, there are many unanswered questions. For example, whereas the extrinsic tenase activity of human milk EVs is detectable when diluted over 2 000‐fold, but bovine milk contains no detectable coagulant activity. Thus, there are considerable interspecies differences which need further investigations. It is also still largely unknown from which cells the TF‐exposing EVs in human body fluids originate, where the associated FVII(a) comes from, and how functional extrinsic tenase complexes are assembled on these EVs. More generally, the contribution of EVs to coagulation will depend on their cellular origin, molecular composition, and biological environment. Although in this guide we have focused on the coagulation‐promoting functions of EVs in human blood, it needs to be mentioned that under physiological conditions EVs may also inhibit coagulation and promote fibrinolysis (Lacroix et al., 2007).

Looking back in evolution may provide another perspective. In evolutionarily distant organisms such as horseshoe crabs, coagulation is part of innate immunity and can be directly triggered by microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide (Sachetto and Mackman, 2023). Also in humans, infection and inflammation can induce monocytes to express TF, produce FVII(a) and release procoagulant EVs (Drake et al., 1989; Tsao et al., 1984; Hisada et al., 2022). When excessive, as during sepsis, this response contributes to pathological systemic coagulation activation (Semeraro et al., 2015). This raises the speculative possibility that the association between coagulation and innate host defence has become compartmentalized during evolution. Whereas TF/FVII(a)‐exposing EVs in blood are mainly associated with pathological conditions, such EVs are constitutively present in several physiological body fluids. Here, these EVs may provide local protection by promoting haemostasis when epithelial barriers become damaged and blood encounters the respective body fluid. Human milk may be an extreme example of such a system, providing large amounts of highly procoagulant EVs to the gastrointestinal tract of the new‐born. Whether this proposed link between EV‐mediated coagulation, haemostasis, and innate defence indeed represents a conserved protective mechanism remains to be investigated.
