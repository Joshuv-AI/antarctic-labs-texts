Differences in psychological well-being, physical activity, and perception of the value of physical education among Italian middle and high school teachers: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Sonia Angilletta, Enzo Iuliano, Goran Kuvacic, Nicola Luigi Bragazzi, Marco Alessandria, Andrea De Giorgio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Research shows that teachers with high self-efficacy and emotional stability are better able to manage lessons, respond to student behavior, and apply proactive, student-centered approaches. In addition, teachers’ emotional well-being enhances their teaching effectiveness and has a positive impact on students’ perceptions of their competence, with positive emotions increasing student satisfaction and engagement. In this study, we want to examine the perception that middle and high school physical education teachers have of the importance of physical education, as well as their level of physical activity and psychological well-being.MATERIALS AND METHODS:Italian physical education teachers (total: 267; F = 193; M = 74; mean age 42.69 ± 9.05 years) completed an online survey that included sociodemographic data, perceptions of the importance of physical education, the International Physical Activity Questionnaire—Short Form (IPAQ-SF), Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21), and the Italian version of the Oxford Happiness Inventory (OHI).RESULTS:Male teachers were significantly more likely to rate physical education (PE) lessons as very important (P = 0.001) and reported lower levels of anxiety (P = 0.026), depression (P = 0.019), and stress (P < 0.001). Younger teachers also rated PE as more important (P < 0.001). Older age was associated with less depression (P = 0.003) and stress (P < 0.001). Moderately or highly physically active teachers had significantly lower anxiety and depression scores than non-active teachers (P < 0.05).CONCLUSION:These findings highlight the need to support female and older teachers to recognize the value of exercise and address their mental health needs/issues. Promoting physical activity among teachers can increase their psychological well-being and improve teaching effectiveness and student outcomes.

Introduction

The school teacher is someone who should take care of the students and not just their cultural education. A holistic approach to education involves addressing every facet of a person’s growth, such as emotional, cognitive, and behavioral aspects.[1,2,3,4] Among the various subjects, physical education (PE) is of crucial importance as it concerns all areas of the human being, including the body. According to the UNESCO declaration,[5] PE is “[…] the most effective means of providing all children and young people with the skills, attitudes, values, knowledge, and understanding for lifelong participation in society.” Therefore, teachers who specialize in a particular area can have a major impact on how the subject is taught and how students gain insight into the subject.[6] The influence of a teacher depends not only on their skills but also on their psychological and emotional state. It has been shown that teachers with high self-efficacy and emotional stability are better able to manage lessons, deal with student misbehavior, and use proactive, student-centered strategies. They also tend to build positive relationships with students and create a supportive learning environment.[7,8] The literature has also shown that teachers’ emotional well-being influences their teaching effectiveness and students’ perception of their competence. In particular, positive emotions can increase students’ satisfaction with collaboration and their willingness to engage and vice versa.[9] Moreover, teachers’ psychological characteristics, such as emotion regulation and satisfaction, are crucial for their effectiveness, retention, and interpersonal relationships, for delivering high-quality instruction, and for maintaining positive interactions with students and colleagues.[10] In light of the above, it is important to emphasize that students see their teachers as role models to emulate and aspire to. PE teachers, but also those who teach other subjects, need to set an example of the importance of physical activity (PA). According to the WHO, inactivity is a major risk factor for non-communicable diseases, and studies show that physically active children and adolescents are more likely to maintain these practices throughout their lives, thus reducing their risk of developing chronic diseases.[11] In addition, PA is closely linked to improved cognitive abilities. Research shows that it stimulates neurogenesis[12,13] and improves cognitive function.[14,15] No less importantly, it reduces anxiety, stress, and depression and improves emotional well-being.[16,17] However, despite the extraordinary importance of PE, it has been shown that this subject is not considered particularly central to education, often even by the teachers themselves.[18] The aim of this study was to investigate the differences in psychological well-being, PA, and perception of the value of PE among Italian middle and high school teachers. To this end, we included people with a master’s degree in PE who have followed a specific pathway, required in our country, Italy, to qualify to teach PE in middle and high schools. Through a questionnaire given to the teachers, we investigated (i) their opinion on the perception of PE in middle and high school by the general population and other specialized teachers, (ii) their level of PA, and (iii) their psychological well-being (depression, anxiety, and stress) and their satisfaction.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

In this cross-sectional study, self-report tools were used to assess the general perception of the importance of PE in schools and to investigate how teachers of other subjects perceive the importance, level of PA, and psychological well-being and satisfaction of PE teachers in middle and high schools.

Study participants and sampling

Two hundred sixty-seven PE teachers from Italy working with students aged 11–19 years, aged 42.69 ± 9.05 years (female: 193; male: 74), voluntarily participated in this study. Teachers were asked to complete an online survey consisting of several sections. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was designed and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the eCampus University Ethics Board.

Data collection tools and technique

An online survey with 41 items was developed based on prior research on perceptions of PE importance.[18] The survey consisted of five parts.

Sociodemographic information. Gender and age were collected using an informal questionnaire. Following this part, two questions were asked about the views of the general population and teachers of other subjects about PE in middle and high school.Importance of PE. Teachers’ perceptions of PE in middle and high school were assessed with two questions/items: “How would you rate the general perception of the importance of PE in all grades?” and “How would you rate the perception of teachers of other subjects regarding the importance of PE in all grades?” The answers were given using a 3-point Likert system: 1 (very important), 2 (somewhat important), and 3 (not at all important).Level of PA. The IPAQ-SF—that is, the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form — was used.[19] It consists of seven open-ended questions about the activities participants have done in the last 7 days: Weekly PA was measured in minutes per week and minutes per day and then converted to MET-min (metabolic equivalent × minutes) per week. Participants were thus categorized as Physically inactive: if not moderate or vigorous; Normally active: 3 or more days of vigorous-intensity activity of at least 20 min per day or 5 or more days of moderate-intensity activity and/or walking of at least 30 min per day or 5 or more days of any combination of walking, moderate-intensity or vigorous-intensity activities achieving a minimum T = total PA of at least 600 MET-min/week; Highly active: at least 3 days (20 min minimum, achieving a minimum total PA of at least 1500 MET-min/week or 7 or more days of any combination of walking, moderate-intensity or vigorous-intensity activities achieving a minimum total PA of at least 3000 MET-min/week).Anxiety, depression, and stress. The Depression Anxiety Stress Scales Short Version (DASS-21) was used. This questionnaire is a shortened version of the DASS developed by Lovibond and Lovibond with 42 items.[20] This self-report instrument comprises three subscales (i.e., depression, anxiety, and stress) with seven items each. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale to assess the extent to which participants have experienced the emotional state in the past week. The scale ranges from 0 (strongly disagree) to 3 (strongly agree). The score of each subscale is calculated as the sum of the seven items of the subscale multiplied by 2. Consequently, the score of each subscale ranged from 0 (best score) to 42 (worst score). The survey was conducted online via a Google module platform. Participants were randomly recruited via social networks and word of mouth. When participants expressed interest in participating in the study, they received a formal email with the consent form and a detailed description of the study, including the objective, a description of the survey, instructions on how to complete it, information on the processing of personal data and the contact details of the study leaders. In this email, participants were also informed that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time before completing and submitting the survey. At the bottom of the email was a hyperlink labeled “I agree to participate,” which redirected participants to the survey webpage on the Google platform. After completing the survey, participants were able to submit their answers by clicking the “Submit” button, which was taken as confirmation of final consent to participate. The information received from participants was collected and analyzed anonymously in accordance with European data protection guidelines. The link to the survey website was available to participants throughout the month of May 2024.

Ethical considerations

The data collected in this study was collected after obtaining informed consent from adult participants and involved only self-administered questionnaires. In accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki, and since no biological material was collected, we secured approval from the local ethics committee.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed for three subgroups of the DASS-21 questionnaire (depression, anxiety, stress) and their severity levels both for the total sample and for the three groups of participants classified according to PA (inactive, normally active, or very active). An ordinal logistic regression analysis was performed to analyze the relationship between the items (measured on an ordinal 3-point Likert scale) assessing the importance of exercise and three independent variables: Gender, age, and International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) classification. The same model was used, with the dependent variable replaced by the subgroups of the DASS-21 questionnaire (anxiety, depression, and stress) and then by the scores of the OHI, both measured on an ordinal scale. In each of these analyses, the proportional odds assumption was checked, and the results of the Pearson test and the deviance test were considered to assess the goodness of fit using the pseudo-R-squared value (Nagelkerke method). After the non-normal distribution was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used to determine the differences between the groups with different PA levels in the total score of the DASS-21 questionnaire, anxiety, depression, stress, and the total score of the OHI questionnaire using the Dunn test for multiple comparisons. The α was set at 0.05. The data were processed with the IBM SPSS Statistic 29.0.1.0 version. (Armonk, New York, United States; ISIN: US4592001014).

Importance of PE

The logistic regression analysis addressing the first question item, “What do you think is the general perception of the importance of PE in schools at all levels?”, revealed that the model was statistically significant (χ²(3) = 26.570, P < 0.001) with Pseudo R2 of 0.130 indicating a modest explanatory power, and both Pearson (χ²(251) = 239.931, P = 0.681) and Deviance test (χ²(251) = 171.474, P = 1.000) indicating a good fit of the model to the data. Gender emerged as a significant predictor (B = 1.273, OR = 3.573 [CI95% = 1.662-7.682], P = 0.001), meaning that males were approximately 3.57 times more likely than females to rate PE as very important. Age was also a significant predictor (B = -0.057, OR = 0.944 [CI95% = 0.913–0.977], P < 0.001), indicating that with each additional year of age, the odds of rating PE as very important decreased by about 5.6%. IPAQ classification was not significant (B = - 0.119, OR = 0.888 [CI95% = 0.609-1.295], P = 0.537). For the second item, “What do you think is the perception of teachers of other disciplines regarding the importance of PE in schools at all grade levels?” the model was not statistically significant (χ²(3) = 3.413, P = 0.332).

Discussion

This study investigated the views of middle and high school PE teachers on the importance of this subject in school. We also investigated both their levels of PA and their psychological well-being (i.e., depression, anxiety, stress, and happiness). The results we obtained provide important information about how PE teachers perceive the importance of this subject and how this perception may be at least partially influenced by personal characteristics. In particular, the results showed that male teachers were significantly more likely than female teachers to rate PE as very important: the former were about 3.57 times more likely than female teachers to rate PE as very important. This gender difference is consistent with the literature, which suggests that male teachers place a higher value on PE due to social norms that associate masculinity with PA and sport.[6] The younger the male teachers were, the more importance they placed on PE, with this importance decreasing by 5.6% for each additional year of age. These findings may reflect generational shifts in attitudes toward holistic education, where younger educators may be more attuned to the benefits of PA for cognitive and emotional development.[2,3] Interestingly, the level of PA had no significant influence on teachers’ perceptions of the importance of PE. This suggests that teachers recognize the educational value of PE regardless of their own activity level. However, the modest explanatory power of the model suggests that other unmeasured factors may influence these perceptions. In terms of psychological well-being, male teachers reported significantly lower levels of anxiety, depression, and stress compared to female teachers. These findings are consistent with the literature showing gender differences in educators’ mental health, with women often reporting higher levels of stress and emotional exhaustion.[7,8] In addition, age was found to be a protective factor. Older participants reported lower levels of depression and stress, which may be due to the greater experience and coping mechanisms developed over the years,[10] enabling them to cope better with the demands and challenges of the job. Examination of levels of PA showed that normally or highly active teachers had significantly lower total scores on the DASS-21 than physically inactive teachers. Significant differences were observed especially in the anxiety and depression subscales, suggesting that higher levels of PA are associated with better mental health outcomes. These results are consistent with the literature emphasizing the positive impact of PA on reducing anxiety and depression symptoms.[16,17] However, no significant differences in stress subscale or happiness levels were found between the different PA groups. This contradicts the literature, which instead points to a relationship between PA and happiness. For example, An and co-workers[23] showed that PA was significantly associated with happiness in young, middle-aged, and older adults. On the other hand, the lack of significant results for happiness in our study suggests that PA may not have a decisive influence in PE teachers. Therefore, further research is needed to explore and deepen our understanding of the interaction between happiness and PA specifically in this population.

Limitations and recommendation

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, it has several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the variables. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine the direction of the observed relationships. Second, the use of self-report tools may lead to biases such as social desirability or recall errors, especially when reporting levels of PA and mental state. The use of objective measures of PA and clinical assessments of mental health could improve the accuracy of the data. Third, the sample consisted of PE teachers from Italy, which may limit the generalisability of the results to other cultural or educational contexts. Cultural attitudes toward sports and gender roles can vary considerably from country to country.[18] Future studies should consider more diverse samples to increase external validity. Furthermore, the study did not consider other potential confounding variables such as workload, classroom environment, or personal stress factors that may influence both perceptions of the importance of sport and psychological well-being.

Conclusion

To summarize, the study reveals important gender- and age-related differences in the perception of the importance of sport among prospective PE teachers, as well as correlations between the level of PA and mental health. The findings highlight the need to support female and younger teachers to recognize the value of PE and to address teachers’ mental health. Promoting physical activity among teachers themselves can positively affect their mental well-being, which in turn may enhance their teaching effectiveness and student outcomes.[9]
