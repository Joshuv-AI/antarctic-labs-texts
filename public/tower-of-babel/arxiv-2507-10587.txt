Anthropomimetic Uncertainty: What Verbalized Uncertainty in Language Models is Missing
Dennis Ulmer, Alexandra Lorson, Ivan Titov, Christian Hardmeier

Abstract
Human users increasingly rely on natural language interactions with large language models (LLMs) in order to receive help on a large variety of tasks and problems. However, the trustworthiness and perceived legitimacy of LLMs is undermined by the fact that their output is frequently stated in very confident terms, even when its accuracy is questionable. Therefore, there is a need to signal the confidence of the language model to a user in order to reap the benefits of human-machine collaboration and mitigate potential harms. Verbalized uncertainty is the expression of confidence with linguistic means, an approach that integrates perfectly into language-based interfaces. Nevertheless, most recent research in natural language processing (NLP) overlooks the nuances surrounding human uncertainty communication and the data biases that influence machine uncertainty communication. We argue for anthropomimetic uncertainty , meaning that intuitive and trustworthy uncertainty communication requires a degree of linguistic authenticity and personalization to the user, which could be achieved by emulating human communication. We present a thorough overview over the research in human uncertainty communication, survey ongoing research, and perform additional analyses to demonstrate so-far overlooked biases in verbalized uncertainty. We conclude by pointing out unique factors in human-machine communication of uncertainty and deconstruct anthropomimetic uncertainty into future research directions for NLP. kaleidophon/anthropomimetic-uncertainty

Introduction

In March 2025, Columbia Journalism Review conducted a study to test whether different artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots could accurately identify and cite news articles
(
Jaźwińska and Chandrasekar 2025
)
.
Given a snippet from a news article, the chatbots were tasked to identify information such as date, headline and publisher.
Not only did the authors find that across tested chatbots, many gave incorrect but plausible-looking answers, but they also noticed that these answers were given with complete confidence.
For instance, ChatGPT answered the prompt incorrectly 67% of times, hedging its answer using phrases such as “it appears” or “it’s possible” in only 7.5% of responses, and never declining a single answer.
Thus, while chatbots remain a powerful and personalized tool, their replies often remain misleading or wrong.
This investigation above gives a glimpse into the risks of AI in the (current) form of (conversational) LLMs as a tool. Potential exposure is high—for instance,
Eloundou et al. 2024
estimated that across 1016 tested occupations, LLMs are relevant to an average of
14\%
of tasks per occupation, with signs of increasing adoption in some fields
(
Humlum and Vestergaard 2024
)
, and some freelance work being increasingly substituted
(
Teutloff et al. 2025
)
.
Likewise, LLMs are being considered more and more for education
(
Kasneci et al. 2023
)
, and used for everyday tasks
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
.

Human users have a tendency to perceive language models as other humans
(
Miller 2019
;
DeVrio et al. 2025
)
.
This is problematic since LLMs are known to hallucinate, i.e. produce plausible-looking but wrong information that is hard to detect
(
Guerreiro et al. 2023
;
Ye et al. 2024
)
and tend to present false information with unwarranted confidence
Krause et al. 2023
;
Yona et al. 2024
;
Xiong et al. 2024
.
This mimics a strategy used by uncooperative human speakers, but without any intent to deceive, and reveals a deeper challenge: the usual cognitive mechanisms against being accidentally or intentionally misinformed are less effective in human–machine interactions.
Users can only assess the model’s output, with no access to a reasoning process or any communicative intent.
Relying on the assessment of content is difficult as users increasingly treat LLMs as sources of information
Gude 2025
, and thus, lack the necessary background knowledge to evaluate its plausibility.
This risks the loss of trust, which can be hard to recover from
(
Dhuliawala et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024b
)
, as well as potential negative outcomes.
Consistent uncertainty communication can be seen as form of building extrinsic trust
Jacovi et al. 2021
,
but despite growing attention in NLP in the form of
verbalized uncertainty

(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Mielke et al. 2022
)
, its roots in human uncertainty communication are often overlooked, and their implications on human-AI communication and collaboration underexplored:
As shown in
Figure

1
, we discuss in our work how human uncertainty is influenced by factors such as prior knowledge, speaking habits and hierarchy between the speaker and listener, while machine uncertainty is shaped by conversational context, data biases, and its alignment procedure.

Contributions.

This work argues for
anthropomimetic uncertainty
: The adherence of language models to norms in human uncertainty communication that is user-centric in order to improve reliability, trustworthiness and personalization.
We contribute to this by first giving a detailed account of the nuances in human uncertainty communication.
Secondly, we contrast this account with the current state of verbalized uncertainty research in NLP, demonstrating that most works still rely on insufficient prompting methods and numerical uncertainty expressions.
Thirdly, we explore some shortcomings of verbalized uncertainty next, showing how it is heavily influenced by conversational context and domain, and exhibits extreme variability as well as inconsistent calibration.
Fourthly, we discuss unique challenges in human-machine uncertainty communication such as anthropomorphization and sycophancy.
Lastly, we propose a list of actionable research directions towards anthropomimetic uncertainty, like choosing the right way to communicate uncertainty, a call to rethink calibration, personalization of uncertainty, and its role with other factors in building user trust.

Human Communication of Uncertainty

Although predictive uncertainty can be represented in a variety of ways
(
Hoarau et al. 2024
)
, humans are already used to express and interpret uncertainty verbally, making a potentially more intuitive medium.
This section is therefore dedicated to first gain a better understanding of the standards and mechanisms in human uncertainty communication.
We distinguish two
registers
of verbalizing uncertainty, namely using numerical expressions (
100 %
,
The chance of this is 6/10
) and verbal expressions (
I am very certain
), where words such as
certain
,
might
or
maybe
are referred to as epistemic markers.

Human communication is generally understood as the intentional exchange of relevant propositions
Grice 1975
;
Sperber and Wilson 1995
—a proposition being the core meaning or claim expressed by a sentence, which can be evaluated as true or false. For communication to succeed, both speaker and addressee must invest effort in producing and interpreting messages, guided by cooperative principles in which speakers aim to be informative, truthful, relevant, and clear in their contributions
(
Grice 1975
)
. This cooperative effort comes with benefits: the addressee gains true and relevant information, while the speaker achieves their goal of eliciting an effect or response in the addressee
Sperber et al. 2010
. A key aspect of cooperative speaker behavior is acknowledging uncertainty when the speaker is unsure about the truth of a proposition. While a speaker asserting a proposition in “
Mo will pass the exam
” expresses some level of confidence in Mo passing the exam, they can express uncertainty via verbal uncertainty expressions (e.g.,
It is possible that Mo will pass the exam
) and numerical expressions (e.g.,
There is a 40% chance Mo will pass the exam
).

Thus, comprehension involves a tentative stance of trust
Holton 1994
, such that the addressee is prepared to revise their beliefs based on the speaker’s contribution. This trust is advantageous given the fact that communication is so ubiquitous
Sperber 2001
;
Bergstrom et al. 2006
. However, the interests of speaker and addressee rarely align perfectly: while providing true and relevant information will meet the addressee’s expectations, the speaker may instead choose the message that is most likely to produce the desired effect—for example, convincing the addressee—regardless of its truth. This includes under- or overstating one’s confidence. This divergence of interest makes it necessary for the addressee to exercise caution in order to avoid being misled. As
Sperber et al. 2010
argue, addressees engage in
epistemic vigilance
while processing the speaker’s message, a cognitive capacity that helps them guard against both accidental and intentional misinformation. According to
Sperber et al. 2010
, the credibility of communicated information depends primarily on two factors: the perceived reliability of the source and the plausibility of the content itself. Thus, comprehension should not be equated with belief, nor does it entail adopting a purely skeptical stance.
In the following sections, we examine how uncertainty is expressed, how it can lead to miscommunication due imprecision, why speakers may strategically adjust their confidence, how listeners interpret these cues, and what this means for human-machine interaction.

2.1
Verbal uncertainty expressions

One way of expressing uncertainty is through a diverse set of verbal uncertainty expressions involving epistemic markers that range from factive verbs (
notice
,
think
,
believe
), modal verbs (
must
,
might
), to adverbs (
probably
,
certainly
) and that can be used to form increasingly complex expressions (e.g.,
a slight yet plausible chance
). Their meaning is often captured by mapping them onto numerical probabilities, intervals, or thresholds
(
Lichtenstein and Newman 1967
;
Beyth-Marom 1982
;
Wesson and Pulford 2009
;
Lassiter 2010
;
Vogel et al. 2022
)
.
1
1

1

For instance, quantitative semantic approaches propose that for any verbal uncertainty expression, there exists a probability threshold in the range
[0,1)
, such that an utterance is true if the probability of the described event exceeds this threshold
Lassiter 2010
;
Lassiter 2017
;
Moss 2015
;
Swanson 2006
;
Yalcin 2007
;
Yalcin 2010
.

However, their meaning is inherently imprecise and context-dependent, lacking consistent interpretation across speakers and situations.
For example, empirical studies suggest that interlocutors interpret
(
Mosteller and Youtz 1990
)
and produce
(
van Tiel et al. 2022
)
the epistemic marker
possible
to refer to a wide range of probabilities: 17%–89%, with an average estimate of around 42%. There are various contextual factors shown to influence the production and interpretation of verbal uncertainty expressions, including event severity and base rates: participants interpreted verbal uncertainty expressions as indicating higher likelihoods for more frequent events compared to less frequent ones
Wallsten et al. 1986
. When base-rates were controlled, these expressions also signaled greater likelihood for events with more severe consequences relative to those with less severe outcomes
Weber and Hilton 1990
;
Bonnefon and Villejoubert 2006
;
Harris and Corner 2011
.
Despite their imprecision, verbal uncertainty expressions help speakers approximate their level of certainty, as they rarely have precise probabilities in mind about the truth of a proposition.

2.2
(Inter-)personal Motivations

Epistemic markers further add to the complexity of uncertainty communication, having long been recognized as tools for either strengthening or hedging the assertive force of an utterance
(
Fraser 1975
;
Holmes 1982
, e.g.,)
. Strengthening
You misunderstood this paragraph
would involve high certainty markers (in bold), e.g.,
I
am sure
that you misunderstood this paragraph

Hyl 1999
;
Hyland 2005
; and hedging, low certainty markers, e.g.,
You
might
have misunderstood this paragraph

(
Holtgraves and Perdew 2016
)
. A speaker may choose to strengthen a statement to persuade the addressee, or to hedge in order to appear polite
(
Brown and Levinson 1978
)
. The extent to which speakers engage in polite behavior depends on social hierarchies or relative power dynamics: speakers are generally more likely to use hedging when addressing someone of higher social status, such as a manager, teacher, or parent
(
Brown and Levinson 1978
;
Holtgraves 2010
;
Holtgraves 2014
;
Holtgraves and Perdew 2016
;
Juanchich and Sirota 2015
)
. Speakers are more likely to hedge when discussing serious consequences such as poor financial decisions, than when describing less severe outcomes like a car breaking down
(
Juanchich and Sirota 2015
)
. This is taken into account by addressees:
possible
was found to be interpreted as indicating higher numerical probabilities when used to describe a more severe condition (e.g., deafness) compared to a less severe but equally prevalent condition (e.g., insomnia)
(
Bonnefon and Villejoubert 2006
)
.

Whether speakers choose to strengthen or hedge their statements is also shaped by how much commitment and accountability they are willing to assume for the claim, balancing persuasive intent with the potential social consequences of being wrong. The stronger the expression of commitment (e.g.,
I am sure that the deadline is tomorrow
), the more the speaker endorses the claim. This in turn increases scrutiny if proven wrong, while hedging can help speakers avoid negative social consequences and preserve credibility
(
Ochs 1976
)
. Research shows that although confident speakers seem persuasive at first, their credibility declines when proven inaccurate, whereas hedged statements foster more resilient trust
(
Tenney et al. 2007
;
Tenney et al. 2008
;
Vullioud et al. 2017
)
.
Independent of the motivation, miscommunication may occur if the addressee fails to take into account the speaker’s perspective in choosing a given verbal uncertainty expression or by ignoring the speaker-addressee dynamics.
Importantly, LLMs might be intuitively perceived by users to follow similar motivations, but we argue later in
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
how their uncertainty communication can lead to miscommunication through biases in their training.

2.3
Numerical Expressions

Given the complexity of producing and interpreting verbal expressions of uncertainty, numerical formats may appear to offer a more precise, and less error-prone way to communicate uncertainty. Numerical expressions include percentages, fractions, decimals, and ratios, which may convey relatively precise information with specific values (e.g.,
a 65% probability
) or relatively imprecise estimates in the form of ranges (e.g.,
60% to 70%
). Previous studies found a tendency that speakers prefer verbal and addressees numerical expressions of uncertainty
(
Wallsten et al. 1993
;
Erev and Cohen 1990
)
. However, preference for one form over the other has been shown to vary depending on the domain and context. For example, in medicine
Juanchich and Sirota 2020
and intelligence analysis
Barnes 2016
, contexts which require a relatively high level precision and where miscommunication comes with dire consequences, speakers tend to prefer numeric uncertainty expressions.
2
2

2

It should be noted however that standardized usages of verbal expressions exists in these fields, see e.g.
European Commission 2009
;
UK Ministry of Defence 2023
.

Such domain-specific differences are often linked to the severity of potential outcomes, with more serious consequences prompting a stronger preference for precise numeric formats
Juanchich and Sirota 2020
.

Although numerical expressions of uncertainty may seem precise, numbers like 60 or 65 in
there is a 65% chance of rain
often represent approximations, as multiples of 5 are generally treated as round numbers
Ruud et al. 2014
;
Cummins 2015
;
Jansen and Pollmann 2001
;
Krifka 2007
. Speakers frequently round figures for simplicity (e.g., saying 50 instead of 47), and such numbers are typically understood as approximate. In contrast, non-round numbers like 52 imply precision; referring to 52 as 50 is usually acceptable, but the reverse can seem misleading
Lasersohn 1999
.
Speakers often use round numbers when precision is unnecessary, aligning with Grice’s maxim to be only as precise as needed: round numbers allow speakers to remain informative without being overly exact
Grice 1975
;
Gibbs Jr and Bryant 2008
;
Van Der Henst and Sperber 2004
. Unnecessary precision, in contrast, may come across as pedantic or socially inappropriate
Beltrama et al. 2023
;
Cotterill 2007
;
Lin 2013
;
McCarthy and Carter 2006
. Speakers also use round numbers when the exact value is unknown to signal reduced commitment, as they are generally interpreted as approximate unless explicitly marked (e.g., with
exactly
)
Krifka 2007
;
Lasersohn 1999
.
This suggests that even when uncertainty is expressed numerically, the communicative use of round numbers introduces a layer of imprecision that to some extent parallels verbal uncertainty expressions.
Numerical expressions are not necessarily the silver bullet for uncertainty communication:
Especially in machines they can imply precision when it is not warranted, and might be misinterpreted in listeners with low numeracy skills
(
Zikmund-Fisher et al. 2007
;
Galesic and Garcia-Retamero 2010
)
.

From Human to Human-Machine

Section

2
has painted an extensive picture of of human uncertainty communication, where speakers don’t just communicate their lack of knowledge, but also consider the social hierarchy between people, the type of person they address, and express politeness or commitment.
Contemporary language technology also mimics a conversational setting, making many of these considerations become blurry.
In this section, we explore the current state of research and highlight some technical caveats to the uncertainty communication of LLMs, before also exploring unique notions compared to the human communication of uncertainty.

3.1
Background

Baan et al. 2023
define uncertainty as the lack or limit of knowledge of an agent interacting with the world.
In machine learning, the most prominent paradigms to
quantify
this uncertainty are the frequentist and Bayesian one.
From the frequentist point of view, probabilities correspond to the relative frequency of an event, under repetitions of an experiment
(
Willink and White 2012
)
.
Uncertainty can then be quantified through
confidence
,
3
3

3

Another way is through the set / interval size in conformal prediction
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2023
;
Campos et al. 2024
)
.
where some score
\hat{p}\in[0,1]
is obtained (corresponding to the model probability of the most likely outcome or class).
The quality of an uncertainty estimate is assessed through
calibration

(
Guo et al. 2017
)
.
Given a prediction
\hat{y}

p(y=\hat{y}\mid\hat{p})=\hat{p},

(1)

i.e. if we observe
\hat{p}=0.45
, then
\hat{y}
should be the correct prediction for an average 45 out of 100 times under repetitions of the experiment.
In contrast, the Bayesian perspective on probabilities views probabilities as degrees of belief in the likelihood in an event instead of simple relative frequencies
(
Good 1971
)
.
Uncertainty is then expressed in posterior distributions over predicted quantities.
Bayesian methods also commonly decompose total uncertainty into
data
(aleatoric) and epistemic (model) uncertainty
(
Shafer 1976
;
Shafer 1978
;
Der Kiureghian and Ditlevsen 2009
;
Hüllermeier and Waegeman 2021
)
, where data uncertainty refers to unresolvable ambiguity or noise in the data, such as inherent randomness.
In contrast, model uncertainty concerns the lack of knowledge about the best model parameters, which can usually be corrected by increasing the amount of training data.
4
4

4

However, practical decompositions are often unreliable
(
Mucsányi et al. 2024
)
, do not account for all sources of uncertainty
(
Gruber et al. 2023
)
, and both uncertainties can appear in (ir-)reducible forms
(
Baan et al. 2023
;
Ulmer 2024
)
.

Uncertainty in Modeling Language.

In addition, NLP models are also faced with uncertainty contained in human language.
Language is often fundamentally underspecified
(
Pustejovsky 2017
)
, context-dependent, imprecise and ambiguous
(
Kennedy 2011
)
, creating uncertainty in the input data and modeling:
For instance, saying
she didn’t make it
does not specify whether she didn’t manage to complete something (like a race) or didn’t create something (like a homemade vase),
I saw the person with the telescope
is ambiguous about which person possesses the instrument, and
glass
can refer to container or content depending on the context (
I drank the glass
vs.
I filled up the glass
).
Compared to purely statistical ways to quantify uncertainty, verbalized uncertainty presents a completely new paradigm.
As we will see next, it can serve two different roles, namely separate communication or joint quantification and communication of uncertainty.
In the former case, we directly ask the model to verbalize its uncertainty, while in the latter case we might obtain uncertainty estimates from another source, and then train the model to verbalize them, hoping for generalization.

Bias in RLHF.

The last step of the contemporary model training pipeline is RLHF, where models are typically taught human preferences about generations based on ordered output pairs.

Tian et al. 2023
noticed that while the log-probabilities of models after RLHF are calibrated worse than those of the base models, (numerical) verbalized uncertainties actually become calibrated better.
This training step might still include negative biases for verbalization, though:

Zhou et al. 2024b
observed that models—even when explicitly prompted—tend to not generate epistemic markers, and when they do, they show a preference for strengtheners, resulting in overconfident generations.
Interestingly, they also show that this bias is less pronounced in unaligned models.
Importantly, they find reward models to predict higher rewards for plain statements than for statements with strengtheners, and even give negative rewards for statements with weakeners.
They conclude that there is a negative bias towards uncertainty in the human preference data.
Similarly,
Leng et al. 2025
look at a reward model and a model trained with DPO, and find in both cases that models are biased towards high-certainty responses, and that adding high-confidence statements can make models prefer the rejected response in preference pairs.
All of this implies that the RLHF step further amplifies biases in verbalization that lead to overconfidence, due to the fact that a preference for confident statements is seen as preferable by human annotations.

3.5
Human-Machine Pragmatics

Language generation by LLMs however does not occur in a vacuum, but in interaction with human users.
12
12

12

Or with other LLMs
(
Park et al. 2023
;
Stengel-Eskin et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Yoffe et al. 2024
, inter alia)
.

Therefore, additional pragmatic concerns arise in this novel setting, which we study here.

Anthropomorphization.

As we interact with chatbots through chat interfaces akin to those we use with other humans, we might be tempted to ascribe human traits, emotions, and a sense of agency to the models.
This
anthropomorphization
has been observed in users, media reports, and even AI researchers themselves
(
Abercrombie et al. 2021
;
Shardlow and Przybyła 2024
)
.
While the literature has pointed out different effects of this
(
Schneider et al. 2018
;
Troshani et al. 2021
)
, specifically concerning trust-building
(
Natarajan and Gombolay 2020
;
Cohn et al. 2024
;
Basoah et al. 2025
)
.

Colombatto et al. 2025
for instance show that users’ willingness to accept the systems’ advice is primarily based on attributions of intelligence. While humans also have been shown to use relatively consistent vocabularies of epistemic markers
(
Clark 1990
;
Dhami and Mandel 2022
)
,
this is likely not true for LLMs, as evidenced by the analyses in
Section

3.3
.
Taking it further, the tendency of LLMs to present false information with unwarranted confidence is similar to uncooperative human speakers, except without any underlying intent to deceive.
The problem is that the standard way of dealing with this is to engage in epistemic vigilance, see
Section

2
, which is much less effective in human–machine interactions due to the black-box nature of LLMs.
As user increasingly treat LLMs as sources of information, spotting falsehoods either requires unavailable background knowledge
Gude 2025
, or critically engaging with the output with through external resources.
However, research suggests that although users are aware of hallucinations, they only tend to scrutinize more carefully in high-stakes situations, where errors have greater perceived consequences
(
Lee et al. 2025b
)
.

Sycophancy.

As LLM-based chatbots are trained to be helpful and supportive, they can also behave in a manner that is too agreeable with the user, reinforcing unhealthy user behaviors or being misleading. This effect has already been studied repeatedly
(
Cotra 2021
;
Perez et al. 2023
;
Sharma et al. 2024
;
Cheng et al. 2025
)
, including in the context of uncertainty quantification
(
Sicilia et al. 2025
)
and the (negative) impact on user trust
(
Carro 2024
)
.

Zhou et al. 2024b
;
Leng et al. 2025
both demonstrated that reward models used for RLHF show biases towards certain responses, most likely since they appear more helpful to human annotators.
Thus, without correction, models tend to downplay their uncertainty in order to increase their perceived helpfulness
(
Dahlgren Lindström et al. 2025
)
.
At the same time,
Steyvers et al. 2025
have demonstrated that humans struggle to reliable estimate when an answer might be wrong, which can damage trust and lead to worse outcomes in the long-term
(
Dhuliawala et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024b
)
.
This training artifact parallels the confirmation bias seen in human reasoning, where speakers tend to select arguments that support their own views when trying to persuade others
Sperber 2001
;
Mercier and Sperber 2009
.
While LLMs are not driven by social motives like persuasion or reputation management, they are optimized to align with user inputs and preferences.
Consequently, they may unintentionally replicate this bias by consistently producing agreeable content that reinforces existing beliefs.
This deviation from human communication introduces a distinct epistemic risk: the cooperative and non-confrontational tone of AI-generated responses may be perceived as trustworthy, potentially lowering users’ epistemic vigilance and critical engagement.

4
Anthropomimetic Uncertainty

Finally, our core thesis is that language models should better imitate human uncertainty communication, which we term
anthropomimetic uncertainty
.
13
13

13

From
anthropo-
(human) and
-mimetic
(imitative). The term has already been used in the context of robotics, see e.g.
Holland et al. 2006
;
Diamond et al. 2012
.

We believe that verbalized uncertainty creates am exciting but incomplete paradigm to do so, and discuss some necessary prerequisites towards anthropomimetic uncertainty in the following.
Importantly, we do
not
to advocate for measures that increase anthropomorphization in language models.
Rather, we accept that through their conversational use, some degree of anthropomorphization is probably inevitable, and that risks should be mitigated through anthropomimetic uncertainty.

Consistency.

Our analyses in
Section

3.4
revealed that our tested models are heavily biased by conversational context and subject when it comes to their expression of uncertainty, and its calibration.
We propose that anthropomimetic uncertainty should aim for a consistent way to use uncertainty expressions across conversations.
It is known that uncertainty communication (and the interpretation thereof) differs between people
(
Beyth-Marom 1982
;
Wesson and Pulford 2009
;
Vogel et al. 2022
;
Dhami and Mandel 2022
)
, however individuals usually have a consistent way to express different level of confidence
(
Clark 1990
;
Dhami and Mandel 2022
)
.
This implies that a model should use similar expressions when referring to events of similar estimated probability.

Personalization.

If the goal is to avert negatives consequences, we have to recognize the fact that different people will act differently upon receiving the same hedged response.
Indeed, tolerance for risk differs for instance based on demographic factors or personality traits
(
Mishra and Lalumière 2011
;
Brooks and Williams 2021
;
Pavlíček et al. 2021
)
.
To account for these factors, uncertainty communication could be adapted to specific users and problems
(
Chakraborti et al. 2025
)
, for instance by incorporating feedback about past decisions over time, and by taking into account prior knowledge the user might have.
This idea is adjacent to the
interactive learning
described by
Kirchhof et al. 2025
, where a model learns to ask clarification questions in order to reduce uncertainty.

Choice of Register.

The current choice of verbalization register in research works is dominated by numerical expressions (see
Figure

2(a)
), likely since calibrating and evaluating numerical expressions is more straightforward, with a fluent expression of uncertainty being especially challenging.
However, it is not necessarily the case that fluent, linguistic expressions of uncertainty are better—humans seem to have a preference to communicate their uncertainty in words, but receive it in numerical form, with the former being more imprecise by nature
(
Wallsten et al. 1993
;
Dhami and Mandel 2022
)
.
Additionally, numerical expressions distinguish between external (“It is 60 % certain”) vs. internal expressions (“I am 60 % certain”) of uncertainty.
Løhre and Teigen 2016
found that while external expressions are deemed more trustworthy, internal expressions are seen as more agreeable and engaging.
However, as was previously mentioned in
Section

2.3
, while numerical expression on the surface appear more precise, they might be similarly imprecise, especially for events for which probabilities cannot be precisely computed
(
Teigen 2023
)
—users may perceive numerical outputs as more authoritative or grounded than they truly are, given that LLMs do not have access to the
actual
likelihood of an event.
14
14

14

At the same time, studies have found that linguistic expressions are processed more intuitively, while numerical expressions elicit a more logical thinking
Windschitl and Wells 1996
;
Liu et al. 2020
.

This suggests that while numerical expressions may technically be more feasible, their seeming objectivity can be misleading; conversely, the imprecision of verbal expressions may be a deliberate feature, indicating that the uncertainty being expressed is itself imprecise.
Thus, fluent verbal expressions might be preferable where absolute precision is not paramount, but methodological challenges on how to learn and evaluate them remain, with first works in this direction being
Yona et al. 2024
;
Stengel-Eskin et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
.
Therefore, the best choice of register—and when to switch—remains another open research topic, intertwined with the previous points of consistency and personalization.

Multilinguality.

A glaring shortcoming of the research around verbalized uncertainty is its focus on English, with the exception of
Krause et al. 2023
.
Given that LLMs’ knowledge is not consistent across languages
(
Kassner et al. 2021
;
Fierro et al. 2025
)
, neither are accuracy and verbal calibration
(
Krause et al. 2023
)
.
More importantly, language is likely to bias expressions of uncertainty similarly to the other factors discussed in
Sections

3.4
and
3.3
.
This is in addition to the fact that ways to express uncertainty vary greatly across languages
(
Vold 2006
;
Ruskan and Šolienė 2017
;
Mulder et al. 2022
;
Riccioni et al. 2022
)
.

Explaining Uncertainty.

To increase trust, models should ideally not only be able to express their uncertainty accurately, but also explaining the reasons why, be it for instance because of a difficult or unusual input.
While a number of works have started exploring this direction
(
Chen and Ji 2022
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Thuy and Benoit 2024
;
Watson et al. 2023
;
Steyvers et al. 2025
)
, there remain significant challenges with explaining model predictions
(
Madsen et al. 2024
)
, which are only exacerbated by the absence of a gold standard for uncertainty quantification in LLMs.

Beyond Classical Calibration.

Previous research has often been focused on the concept of probabilistic calibration in
Equation

1
, aiming to align confidence with the probability of correctness on a response.
15
15

15

Other notions of probabilistic calibration have also been considered, see e.g.
Perez-Lebel et al. 2023
;
Si et al. 2022
.

But since LLMs often generate their answers stochastically, some works also have investigated calibration with respect to their answer distribution
(
Yona et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
.
In the case of verbal expressions, the mapping to probabilistic confidences is non-trivial, and so the question also remains whether
exact
calibration is even necessary.
Nevertheless, some works have explored the calibration of a listener or receiver acting upon a response
(
Stengel-Eskin et al. 2024
;
Yona et al. 2024
;
Steyvers et al. 2025
)
.
Future work should consider the calibration towards outcomes induced by a listener, and develop notions of linguistic calibration that don’t rely on probabilities.

Trust beyond Uncertainty.

While a number of works have already explored the relationship between uncertainty and trust between humans and AI
(
Kapoor et al. 2024
;
Dhuliawala et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024b
)
, trust is not exclusively built on the expression of uncertainty in responses.
Indeed,
Zhou et al. 2024a
found that humans are also influenced by factors such as subject matter, prior interactions, warmth, and humanlikeness.
Future research should therefore also aim to understand the role of uncertainty in lieu with other factors to influence human trust.

Related Work

Due to the overview over the most closely related works in
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
, we only provide a brief overview over other related topics here.

Uncertainty Quantification in LLMs.

The recent attention to the quantify the uncertainty in language models also has produced a variety of surveys on the topics, often with specific angles:
While
Geng et al. 2024
;
Shorinwa et al.
;
He et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
provide a general overview over available methods,
Baan et al. 2023
give a general introduction into the fundamentals and sources of uncertainty,
Xie et al. 2024
specifically focus on the black-box setting, and
Huang et al. 2024
write from a decidedly theoretical angle.
Additionally,
Campos et al. 2024
provides an overview over conformal method in NLP.
For practical comparisons, works that benchmark verbalized uncertainty against other methods include for instance
Vashurin et al. 2025
;
Sung et al. 2025
.
Furthermore, several works have tried to redefine the kinds of uncertainties in LLMs
(
Baan et al. 2023
;
Beigi et al. 2024
;
Kirchhof et al. 2025
)
.

Learning to Abstain or Clarify.

Instead of expressing an response with uncertainty, models might simply abstain from answering, or ask for clarifications.
Learning to reject uncertain inputs has already been studied in machine learning
(
Fumera and Roli 2002
;
Herbei and Wegkamp 2006
;
Geifman and El-Yaniv 2019
;
Hendrickx et al. 2024
)
, and has recently also been attracting increasing attention in the context of LLMs, where it might be part of the behavior of the model
(
Arditi et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2025a
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Jain et al.
)
or additional classifiers that detect inputs for which responses should be refused
(
Grattafiori et al. 2024
;
Sharma et al. 2025
)
.
16
16

16

In these cases, refusal is usually motivated by security concerns rather than questions of uncertainty. An overview over potential reasons is given by
Brahman et al. 2024
.

Alternatively, a model might also reduce its uncertainty by asking additional clarification questions, see e.g.
Rahmani et al. 2023
;
Manggala and Monz 2023
;
Tix and Binsted 2024
;
Testoni and Fernández 2024
;
Zhang and Choi 2024
.

6
Conclusion

In our work, we gave a holistic account of the verbal communication of uncertainty.
By giving a detailed insights about verbalized uncertainty between humans, we were able to point out important gaps and lacks of nuance in the current NLP literature.
Furthermore, we demonstrated empirically that the current human-machine communication of uncertainty is heavily influenced by data and contextual biases.
Based on these insights, we outline actionable research directions towards anthropomimetic uncertainty, uncertainty communication behaviors in language models that build trust by emulating human uncertainty communication.

Limitations

Simulating Conversational Context.

The conversational contexts in
Section

3.4
are based on specific, hand-crafted prompts.
Due to the sensitivity of LLMs towards specific prompts, this might potentially bias or distort results
(
Leidinger et al. 2023
;
Sclar et al. 2024
;
Mizrahi et al. 2024
;
Xia et al. 2025
)
.
However, the variability between models and between settings is nevertheless striking and to the best of our knowledge has hitherto not been reported in the literature.

Epistemic Markers in Context.

Our analyses in
Section

3.3
rely on a custom list of epistemic markers and their annotations as strengtheners or weakeners.
However, this comes with two limitations:
To the best of our knowledge, there is no agree-upon canonical list of markers, and there is ample room for disagreement regarding their role as strengtheners and weakeners.
In addition, the same word might function as an epistemic marker in one context, but not in another (take for instance the “shall” in legal texts in
Figure

3(b)
).
Therefore, these results should be interpreted with the appropriate amount of caution.

Focus on English.

In order to point out problems with verbalized uncertainty in its current form, we focused our analyses on English.
While the use of epistemic markers in verbal expressions of uncertainty has been studied in other languages
(
Vold 2006
;
Ruskan and Šolienė 2017
;
Mulder et al. 2022
;
Riccioni et al. 2022
)
, there exist only limited work in NLP
(
Krause et al. 2023
)
.
Since uncertainty might be expressed entirely differently across languages, some of the conclusions from this work might only transfer to a very limited degree.
