Eco-friendly and efficient synthesis of 1,4-dihydropyridines using a recyclable l-arginine-modified magnetic natural phosphate nanocatalyst
Authors: Manuchehr Rahimi, Zahra Lasemi, Mohsen Zeeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A magnetic natural phosphate functionalized with arginine (MNP‑Arg) was synthesized and fully characterized using FTIR, XRD, XRF, SEM/EDS, VSM, and elemental analysis, which confirmed the successful functionalization and magnetic behavior of the synthesized catalyst. MNP‑Arg efficiently catalyzed the synthesis of 1,4‑dihydropyridines (1,4‑DHPs) in ethanol at room temperature under mild conditions, affording diverse products in 86–98% yield within 10–20 min using only 20 mg of catalyst. The catalyst was readily recoverable by magnetic separation and maintained activity for nine consecutive cycles, demonstrating high efficiency, recyclability, and improved environmental compatibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1038/s41598-026-51129-2.

Introduction

In light of the environmental impacts associated with industrialization and the increasing demand for efficient chemical manufacturing, green chemistry has become a central strategy for designing sustainable synthetic processes1–4. In this context, heterogeneous catalysis plays an important role because it can improve reaction efficiency and selectivity while enabling easier catalyst recovery and reducing waste generation5–7. In particular, reusable immobilized catalysts are widely investigated as they can lower both operating costs and environmental burden by minimizing catalyst loss and avoiding repeated synthesis of fresh catalytic materials8.

Beyond conventional catalyst design, nanostructured materials have emerged as promising platforms for catalysis. Their high surface-to-volume ratio, size-dependent reactivity, and tunable surface chemistry can enhance catalytic performance even with relatively small amounts of material9. To further align catalytic processes with green chemistry principles, the selection of catalyst supports and functional components is equally critical.

Natural phosphates have attracted considerable attention because they are low-cost, naturally abundant, and environmentally benign resources. While NP are primarily used in the fertilizer sector, they have also been effectively applied as catalysts—either alone or with convenient modifications—in a variety of organic reactions10. These reactions include Knoevenagel condensation, Click reaction, transesterification reaction, Michael addition, Diels–Alder reaction, Claisen-Schmidt, Suzuki coupling reactions, Beckmann rearrangement, as well as synthesis of 5-arylhydantoins, xanthenes, and α-hydroxyphosphonates11–15. This solid catalyst has several advantages: it is inexpensive, readily available, stable, non-toxic, environmentally friendly, and recoverable and reusable14.

Among modern directions in heterogeneous catalysis, magnetic heterogeneous catalysts have received growing interest. Compared with conventional separation methods such as centrifugation or filtration, magnetic recovery can simplify downstream processing, limit mechanical losses of the catalyst, and improve recyclability—key aspects for reducing process waste16–21. However, Fe-based magnetic nanoparticles may undergo aggregation, which can diminish active surface area and compromise stability. Therefore, appropriate surface modification and immobilization strategies are often employed to improve dispersion and preserve nanoparticle functionality, especially for Fe3O4-based systems22,23.

In addition to the support, the nature of the functionalizing ligand strongly affects catalytic behavior. Amino acids are useful organic ligands for the functionalization of solid surfaces because they contain multiple anchoring sites and can tune the local chemical environment around the catalytic centers. Among amino acids, arginine (Arg) is particularly attractive due to its natural origin, non-toxicity, availability, and biological relevance. Structurally, arginine contains a carboxyl group, an α-amino group, and a guanidine moiety, enabling versatile interactions with solid surfaces and promoting the formation of stable organic–inorganic hybrid catalysts. Such arginine-functionalized materials like Cu@Zeo-Arg24, GO-Arg25, Cu(II)@Fe3O4@SiO2-l-Arg26, gC3N4@L-Arg27, Fe3O4 @l-Arg-CD-Cu(II)28, and Fe3O4@SiO2@l-Arg29 have been reported to enhance performance in various catalytic applications.

From an organic synthesis perspective, nitrogen-containing heterocycles remain highly valuable scaffolds due to their pharmaceutical importance and diverse biological activities30. In particular, 1,4-dihydropyridines (1,4-DHPs) are compounds of considerable significance in medicinal chemistry and materials research, being associated with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antitumor, antihypertensive, and analgesic properties31. Traditional routes to 1,4-DHP derivatives often rely on condensation-based multicomponent reactions, typically involving electron-deficient building blocks such as acetylenic esters and malononitrile, together with aldehydes and amines32. While many catalytic methods have been developed to improve yield and reaction efficiency using catalysts such as zeolite-SO3H33, MCQD-AP-Ugi34, Sm/ZrO235, KF/Al2O336, Pyrrolo[3,4-b]pyridine37, Cu@Fe3O4/SiO2@MWCNTs38, SDS39, [3,6-DOMDA]OTf40, Al(DS)341, DBU42, CuI43, CMC-g-poly(AA-co-AMPS)/Fe3O444, AlCl3.6H2O45, Ch-Rhomboclase NCs46, l-proline47, PEG-40048, Fe3O4@SiO2@-NH249, Fe3O4@SiO2@sulfated boric acid50 some approaches still suffer from drawbacks including harsh conditions, catalyst toxicity, and limited reusability. Consequently, it is crucial to design efficient and eco-friendly catalytic systems that provide 1,4-DHPs under milder conditions while minimizing waste and simplifying catalyst recovery.

In this study, we report a novel, to the best of our knowledge, unreported hybrid catalytic system by functionalizing magnetic natural phosphate with arginine (MNP–Arg). Although magnetic heterogeneous catalysts and amino-acid–functionalized supports have been studied separately, combining a naturally derived phosphate mineral framework, Fe-containing magnetic particles for facile recovery, and arginine as a biomolecule-based multidentate ligand is new. Arginine is used not only as an additive but as a structurally active anchor: its carboxyl, amino, and guanidinium groups enable strong surface attachment and rational tuning of catalyst-reactant interactions to promote a sustainable one-pot four-component synthesis of 1,4-DHPs. The proposed strategy couples (i) a naturally derived phosphate support, (ii) magnetic Fe-containing nanoparticles for straightforward separation, and (iii) arginine as a flexible, functional ligand that can improve uniformity and catalytic interaction with reactants. The catalytic system operates with a two-phase concept: the stationary phase consists of natural phosphate bearing magnetic particles and surface-bound arginine, while the solvent and reactants constitute the mobile phase. Owing to arginine’s multiple functional groups, its attachment to the catalyst surface can create a more favorable local environment for the multicomponent transformation. Meanwhile, magnetic nanoparticles enable rapid catalyst retrieval and recycling, which supports greener operation by reducing catalyst loss and minimizing purification steps. Thus, we report the synthesis, characterization, and catalytic application of the arginine-functionalized magnetic natural phosphate (MNP-Arg) for the efficient synthesis of 1,4-DHPs in a sustainable one-pot four-component process.

Experimental

Materials and methods

The natural phosphate was sourced from the extracted ore located in Yazd, Iran. The solvents and chemicals employed in this research include Arginine amino acid, FeCl2.4H2O, FeCl3.6H2O, ammonia (25%wt), 3-chloropropyl trimethoxysilane (CPTMS), aromatic aldehydes, malononitrile, anilines, and diethyl acetylenedicarboxylate, all of which were sourced from the Merck Company. All analytical instruments are contained within the supplementary information document.

Preparation of NP

The NP was obtained from extracted ore in Yazd, Iran. The phosphate particles were refluxed and washed with water, then calcined at 700 °C for 5 h. After this initial calcination, they were washed again and recalcined at 700 °C for 2.5 h before being used as a catalyst11,13. The chemical composition was analyzed using XRF, revealing a Ca/P molar ratio of 1.62.

Synthesis of magnetic natural phosphate (MNP)

A combination of NP (1 g), FeCl₂·4H₂O (0.6 g) and FeCl₃·6H₂O (1.5 g) was created by dispersing the components in ethanol and subjecting them to sonication until a uniform mixture was achieved. Following this, the mixture was refluxed for 1 h, after which 10 mL of 25% NH₃·H₂O was added and stirred for an additional hour. Upon cooling, the resulting black precipitate was extracted using an external magnet. Finally, the solution was washed several times with deionized water and ethanol to ensure complete neutralization. The resulting MNP was then dried in an oven at 60 °C, resulting in a black powder.

Preparation of MNP-Arg nanocatalyst

A solution containing 1 g of MNP was prepared in 50 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 2.60 g (15 mmol) of Arg was introduced into this solution, and the resulting mixture was allowed to reflux for a duration of 48 h. Upon completion of the refluxing process, the reaction mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature. The catalyst was then extracted using an external magnet and subsequently washed with ethanol and water. Any unbound arginine was eliminated through the use of a Soxhlet extractor. In conclusion, the arginine-functionalized MNP (MNP-Arg) was collected and dried in a vacuum oven.

Synthesis of products 5a–t using MNP-Arg

To a combination of malononitrile (1 equivalent), diethyl acetylenedicarboxylate (DEAD, 1 equivalent), aromatic aldehydes (1 equivalent), and anilines (1 equivalent) in ethanol (10 mL), 20 mg of MNP-Arg catalyst was introduced, and the mixture was agitated at 25 °C for the specified time period. Upon completion of the reaction (as observed through TLC), the magnetic catalyst was removed from the reaction mixture with the aid of a magnet, and the solvent was subsequently evaporated to obtain the corresponding product. The resulting solid products were then recrystallized from ethanol to achieve a higher purity of 1,4-DHPs.

Results and discussion

The NP utilized in this research was sourced from the Yazd region of Iran. As illustrated in Fig. 1, before its application, NP necessitates preliminary processing, which includes crushing and washing. For effective use in heterogeneous synthesis, the NP undergoes treatment through methods such as attrition, sifting, calcination, and washing. A sample with a grain size ranging from 100 to 500 mm was extracted, subjected to washing with water, calcined at a temperature of 700 °C for a duration of 5 h, then washed once more, calcined again at 700 °C for 2.5 h, and finally ground to a size of 50 to 110 mm to produce the NP catalyst. Subsequently, the magnetic natural phosphate catalyst (MNP) was synthesized by reacting the NP with FeCl3·6H2O and FeCl2·4H2O salts in ethanol. The desired Arginine-modified magnetic NP (MNP-Arg) was then obtained through the reaction of MNP with Arg in deionized water at 90 °C for 48 h. The formation of the amino acid-modified magnetic nanocatalysts was investigated using various analytical techniques, including FTIR, XRF, XRD, EDS, SEM, VSM, and elemental analysis.

Catalyst characterization

The chemical composition of NP was analyzed utilizing an XRF spectrometer, revealing the following percentages: CaO (48.53%), P2O5 (37.61%), Fe2O3 (5.26%), SiO2 (4.26%), MgO (1.16%), Al2O3 (0.19%), Na2O (0.28%), MnO (0.08%), K2O (0.06%), along with other metals present in quantities less than 5 ppm11,13,51.

The FTIR spectra for NP, MNP, Arg, and MNP-Arg are illustrated in Fig. 2. The FT-IR curve of NP displays prominent peaks at 1077, 1043, and 878 cm−1, which can be attributed to the vibrational modes of P–O bands within the PO43− groups of the apatite structure52,53. This is comparable to the natural phosphates found in Morocco54 and Tunisia55. In the spectrum of MNP, the peak observed at 584 cm−1, which is linked to the Fe-O stretching vibrations, verifies that the surface of NP has been effectively coated with the magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles34. The FT-IR profile of the Arg amino acid exhibits prominent signals within the range of 3200–3600 cm−1, which correspond to the stretching vibrations of amine and hydroxyl groups, as well as a peak at 2919 cm−1, indicative of C–H stretching vibrations24. Additionally, there are peaks at 1680 cm−1, representing C=O stretching vibrations, 1610 cm−1 for C=N stretching vibrations, and 1386 cm−1 associated with bending vibrations of C–H56. In addition, the FT-IR spectrum of MNP-Arg, which is a magnetic nanoparticle supported by arginine, reveals extra signals at 2919 cm−1 (C–H stretching vibrations), 1680 cm−1 (C=O stretching vibrations), 1610 cm−1 (C=N stretching vibrations), and 1386 cm−1 (bending vibrations of C–H). These findings confirm the effective modification of the MNP surface through its reaction with arginine.

The XRD patterns for NP, MNP, and MNP-Arg are presented in Fig. 3. The patterns for every sample, which span a 2θ range from 5 ° to 90°, confirm their crystalline characteristics. The XRD pattern of NP shows relatively sharp reflections that correlate well with standard references (JCPDS 15-876)57,58. The presence of extra peaks can be linked to other minerals, including siliceous minerals (quartz), calcite, and dolomite12. The MNP’s diffraction peaks closely match the reference XRD pattern for Fe3O4 nanoparticles (JCPDS No. 65-3107), confirming the presence of a cubic iron oxide structure with crystallographic planes corresponding to (111), (220), (311), (400), (422), (440), and (531)59. The XRD pattern of arginine supported on magnetic natural phosphate (MNP-Arg) exhibits all the distinctive reflections of NP and MNP, demonstrating that the crystal structure of MNP remained intact during the incorporation of arginine. The results obtained from the Debye–Scherrer equation indicate the crystallite sizes of three different materials: NP, MNP, and MNP-Arg. The crystallite size for NP was estimated to be 56 nm, which suggests a relatively smaller crystallite structure. In contrast, MNP exhibited a larger crystallite size of 76 nm, indicating that the magnetic modification may have contributed to increased crystallinity. On the other hand, MNP-Arg, which involves the arginine modification, showed a crystallite size of 73 nm. This size is slightly smaller than that of MNP but larger than NP, suggesting that the modification with arginine may influence crystallite growth to some extent. Overall, the results reveal distinct differences in crystallite sizes among the three materials, reflecting the impact of both magnetic enhancement and chemical modification on the structural characteristics of the phosphate materials.

The morphology and elemental composition of the NP, MNP, and MNP-Arg catalysts were analyzed using SEM (Fig. 4) and EDS (Fig. 5) techniques. SEM image of NP shows porosity and a heterogeneous microstructure. The results align well with earlier studies12,49. The average particle diameters range from about 100 to 500 nm. It is also evident that the microsphere shapes contain fine white particles dispersed on the surface, and some particle aggregations are observed. The EDS analysis of the NP reveals the expected elements such as Ca, P, O, Si, Mg, Fe, and Al. The SEM image of the MNP shows that the surface is completely coated with Fe3O4 nanoparticles, which appear either amorphous or spherical in shape. Additionally, the increased percentage of Fe found in the EDS analysis of the MNP confirms the successful magnetization of the NP. The SEM image of MNP-Arg reveals that the catalyst’s surface remained largely unchanged throughout the modification process involving arginine. Furthermore, the detection of nitrogen and carbon elements in the EDS analysis substantiates the effective modification of the MNP surface with the arginine amino acid. The average particle size of MNP-Arg is approximated to be 50 nm. Furthermore, ICP analysis revealed that iron was loaded onto the MNP-Arg surface at approximately 4.64 mmol/g.

To assess the sizes and distributions of particles in the liquid phase, DLS analysis was conducted based on the intensity of the scattered light. Figure 6 illustrates that the average particle size for MNP-Arg is 87 nm, indicating a narrow particle size distribution.

The quantity of Arg that has been bonded to the surface of the MNP was assessed through CHN analysis. The presence of carbon (10.92%) and nitrogen (8.25%) in the MNP-Arg structure validates the effective modification of the MNP catalyst with the Arg amino acid. It has been determined that around 1.47 mmol/g of Arg is attached to the MNP surface.

VSM analysis characterized the magnetic properties of the magnetic nanostructures. At room temperature and in a 5T external magnetic field, MNP exhibited a saturation magnetization (Ms) of 15.98 emu/g, while MNP-Arg showed a lower Ms of 9.89 emu/g (Fig. 7; Table 1). This decrease in Ms is attributed to the arginine coating; however, the MNP-Arg retained sufficient magnetic properties for efficient separation. The coercive field (Hc) and residual magnetism (Mr) can be determined from the interception of the magnetic curve with the applied field and the magnetic axes, respectively. The values for coercivity (Hc) and remanent magnetization (Mr) for MNP and MNP-Arg were obtained from Fig. 7 and are summarized in Table 1. Specifically, MNP has a coercivity of 2.41 Oe and a remanent magnetization of 0.31 emu/g, while MNP-Arg has a coercivity of 8.80 Oe and a remanent magnetization of 0.21 emu/g. The remanence ratio (Mr/Ms) is 0.019 for MNP and 0.021 for MNP-Arg, which indicates that both synthesized catalysts exhibit superparamagnetic-like behavior.

Study of catalytic activity

MNP-Arg catalyst was investigated for the synthesis of 1,4-DHPs via a four-component reaction. The reaction of 4-bromobenzaldehyde (1 mmol), malononitrile (1 mmol), aniline (1 mmol), and DEAD (1 mmol) in 10 mL of ethanol at 25 °C was chosen as a model reaction to produce the compound 5 f. As shown in Table 2, the reaction was evaluated under various conditions, including NP, MNP, MNP-Arg, unsupported arginine, and catalyst-free conditions (entries 1–5). The results show that the model reaction yielded 75% in the presence of non-bonded arginine as a homogeneous catalyst. Although the yield of the product 5f was obtained in 45% and 57% in the presence of NP, MNP catalysts, respectively. Furthermore, a minimal quantity of product was produced under free-catalyst conditions. The reaction yield improved by 72% with the introduction of the MNP-Arg catalyst, likely attributable to the presence of the supported Arg amino acid on the MNP surface. The model reaction was examined with varying amounts of MNP-Arg, leading to the conclusion that 20 mg of MNP-Arg represented the optimal quantity. Increasing the catalyst amount beyond this threshold did not influence the reaction yield (entries 5–10). To evaluate the impact of solvents on the model reaction, a range of solvents was analyzed. The highest yield of 98% was recorded when ethanol was utilized as the green solvent (entry 8 in comparison to entries 11–16). Moreover, raising the reaction temperature did not alter the yield of compound 5f; however, it did reduce the duration required for the reaction to reach completion (entry 8 in comparison to entries 17–18).

It appears that the MNP-Arg catalyst is capable of promoting the nucleophilic attack of malononitrile, leading to the formation of alkylidene malononitrile (I) through the activation of the methylene group. Anilines were introduced to acetylenedicarboxylate esters, resulting in a zwitterionic intermediate (II), which subsequently undergoes a Michael addition with intermediate (I). The latter, through the migration of a hydrogen atom, yields intermediate (III). The intramolecular addition of the amino group to the cyano triple bond generates intermediate (IV), which then tautomerizes to yield product 5 and simultaneously regenerates MNP-Arg within the reaction mixture (Fig. 8).

The recovery and reuse of catalysts are essential for both economic and environmental sustainability. This research investigated the reusability, leaching, and recovery of a catalyst employed in a model reaction. The MNP-Arg catalyst successfully synthesized 1,4-DHPs as a heterogeneous catalyst and was readily recycled using an external magnet. It preserved its catalytic performance over nine cycles (Fig. 9). Following each cycle, the recovered catalyst underwent washing, drying, and was subsequently reused in the next reaction. The slight decrease in yield of the target product across nine cycles reflects the catalyst’s durability and minimal loss of activity. Utilizing an external magnet for catalyst separation streamlines the recycling process, rendering it suitable for scale-up and continuous operation.

XRD analyses (Fig. 10) indicate that MNP-Arg, after being reused for nine cycles, closely resembles its fresh counterpart, implying that there has been minimal alteration in its surface properties. This observation suggests that the surface characteristics of the catalyst have largely remained stable. The consistent XRD patterns further demonstrate that the arginine modification on the MNP retains its stability under the tested reaction conditions. This stability is a vital aspect for the economic feasibility and sustainability of the catalyst, as it minimizes the requirement for new material and diminishes waste production. The results derived from the Debye–Scherrer equation indicate a notable difference in crystallite sizes between fresh MNP-Arg and reused MNP-Arg after nine cycles. The fresh MNP-Arg exhibits a crystallite size of 73 nm, suggesting a relatively stable crystalline structure at the outset. In contrast, the reused MNP-Arg shows a slightly reduced crystallite size of 70 nm. This decrease in crystallite size upon reuse may imply that the recycling process has caused some minor structural changes, potentially due to factors such as mechanical stress, solvent interactions, or slight aggregation. Despite the reduction, the crystallite size remains within a close range, indicating that the material retains much of its crystalline integrity even after multiple cycles of use. Such findings are crucial for understanding the stability and performance of MNP-Arg in practical applications, particularly in fields where consistent reusability is essential.

The MNP-Arg catalyst’s leaching was assessed during the synthesis of product 5f under optimized conditions. After 7 min (total reaction time 15 min), the catalyst was removed magnetically. GC analysis showed an 85% yield. The remaining mixture was stirred for 15 min without the catalyst, and subsequent GC analysis showed no significant yield change, indicating catalyst stability in the reaction. Additionally the Fe content in the structure of the reused MNP-Arg, after nine cycles, was determined by ICP analysis to be approximately 4.18 mmol/g, confirming the catalyst’s consistency and stability during the recovery process.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this work reports the synthesis of a new eco-friendly amino acid–functionalized magnetic nanocatalyst (MNP-Arg) by anchoring arginine onto magnetic natural phosphate. The magnetic phosphate support was prepared by reacting natural phosphate with Fe3+ and Fe2+ salts in ethanol, and the resulting magnetic natural phosphate was subsequently functionalized with arginine in deionized water at 90 °C for 48 h. The successful construction of MNP-Arg was confirmed by FTIR, XRF, XRD, EDS/SEM, VSM, and elemental analysis, which collectively evidenced both the incorporation of arginine and the retention of the magnetic properties required for efficient catalyst handling. The catalytic performance of MNP-Arg was evaluated in the synthesis of 1,4-DHPs. Under mild conditions using ethanol as the solvent and 20 mg of catalyst at room temperature, the target products were obtained in 10–20 min with a high isolated yield (86–98%). Importantly, the external magnetic field enabled easy separation of the catalyst from the reaction mixture, improving operational simplicity and process sustainability. Moreover, MNP-Arg could be reused for nine consecutive cycles without a significant loss of activity, supporting its robustness and potential applicability for greener and more cost-effective synthetic protocols. Future work will focus on advancing catalytic platforms based on magnetic natural phosphate by immobilizing metal ions within the phosphate framework and anchoring new organic ligands on the catalyst surface. These next-generation materials will be benchmarked against the current MNP-Arg system using standardized metrics for catalytic activity, selectivity, and recyclability. In parallel, the substrate scope will be systematically broadened and optimized to include structurally diverse 1,4-DHP derivatives (and potentially other biologically relevant heterocycles), while more rigorous recyclability and operational stability studies will be conducted to substantiate performance for continuous or large-scale applications.
