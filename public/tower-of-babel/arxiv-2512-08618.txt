Nonequilibrium Photocarrier and Phonon Dynamics from First Principles: a Unified Treatment of Carrier-Carrier, Carrier–Phonon, and Phonon–Phonon Scattering
Stefano Mocatti, Giovanni Marini, Giulio Volpato, Pierluigi Cudazzo, Matteo Calandra

Abstract
We develop a first-principles many-body framework to describe the dynamics of photocarriers and phonons in semiconductors following ultrafast excitation. Our approach incorporates explicit ab initio light–matter coupling and first-principles collision integrals for carrier–carrier, carrier–phonon, and phonon–phonon scattering. It also yields time-dependent quasiparticle and phonon frequency renormalizations, along with light-induced coherent atomic motion. The equations of motion are solved in a maximally localized Wannier basis, ensuring gauge-consistent scattering integrals and ultradense momentum sampling, thereby enabling direct comparison with pump–probe experiments. The method can be coupled to constrained density-functional theory to access light-induced structural phase transitions at longer times after the light pulse. We showcase the capabilities and predictive power of this framework on MoS 2 and h-BN monolayers. For MoS 2 , we resolve photoinduced renormalizations of electronic and lattice properties, ultrafast carrier relaxation, hot-phonon dynamics, and displacive coherent atomic motion. Including carrier–carrier scattering is crucial to obtain realistic photocarrier equilibration times, while omitting phonon–phonon scattering leads to incorrect long-time lattice thermalization and a factor of two larger A 1g coherent phonon damping time. For h-BN, we quantify photoinduced changes in the electronic, optical, and lattice responses in quasi-equilibrium, demonstrating a fluence-dependent enhancement of screening and melting of excitonic features.

II
Theoretical framework

II.1
Equilibrium structure in the absence of an external drive

We start from the full quantum Hamiltonian of electrons and nuclei. At equilibrium, we employ the Born–Oppenheimer (BO) factorization of the total wavefunction,

\Phi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},t)=\Psi(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{R},t)\,\chi(\mathbf{R},t),

(1)

where
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{R}
are collective electronic and nuclear coordinates, respectively.
\Psi(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{R},t)
is the electronic many-body state at fixed nuclear configuration, and
\chi(\mathbf{R},t)
is the nuclear many-body state.

We focus on crystalline solids in which nuclei oscillate about equilibrium positions
\mathbf{R}_{i}^{0}
repeated periodically. The composite index
i\!\equiv\!(I,a)
labels unit cell
I
and atom
a
within the cell, so that
\mathbf{R}_{i}^{0}=\mathbf{R}_{I}+\bm{\tau}_{a}
, with
\mathbf{R}_{I}
a Bravais lattice vector and
\bm{\tau}_{a}
the basis position. The number of atoms per cell is
N_{\text{at}}
.

Equilibrium positions are defined by vanishing forces on the BO energy surface
E(\mathbf{R})
:

\mathbf{F}_{i}=-\left.\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{i}}E(\mathbf{R})\right|_{\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{0}}=0.

(2)

We write the instantaneous nuclear coordinates as displacements from equilibrium,

\mathbf{R}_{i}=\mathbf{R}_{i}^{0}+\mathbf{Q}_{i},

(3)

with
\mathbf{Q}_{i}
the ionic displacement. We impose Born–von Kármán boundary conditions over
N
unit cells of volume
\Omega
, obtaining a total volume
V=N\Omega
. From now on, unless otherwise stated, we work in Rydberg atomic units (Ry a.u.), i.e.,
\hbar=2m_{e}=e^{2}/2=4\pi\epsilon_{0}=1
.

II.2
Harmonic phonon Hamiltonian at equilibrium

To describe nuclear motion at equilibrium, we adopt the harmonic approximation, expanding
E(\mathbf{R})
to second order in ionic displacements. The resulting lattice dynamics is set by the force constant matrix in reciprocal space,

C_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{I}e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{I}}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}E(\mathbf{R})}{\partial R_{Ia\alpha}\,\partial R_{0b\beta}}\right|_{\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{0}},

(4)

where Greek indices denote cartesian components.

For each wavevector
\mathbf{q}
, the
N_{m}=3N_{\text{at}}
normal mode frequencies
\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
and eigenvectors
e^{a\alpha}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
follow from the dynamical matrix

D_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{C_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q})}{\sqrt{M_{a}M_{b}}},

(5)

with
M_{a}
the mass of atom
a
, via diagonalization:

D_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}=\sum_{a,b,\alpha,\beta}\big(e^{a\alpha}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\big)^{*}D_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q})\,e^{b\beta}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}=\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}\big(\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\big)^{2}.

(6)

The normal modes define phonon creation and annihilation operators,
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
and
\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
, satisfying canonical commutation relations (Appendix
A
). From these, the nuclear displacement and momentum operators can be defined:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{Ia\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{M_{a}N}}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu}\frac{e^{a\alpha}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}{\sqrt{\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}}e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{I}}\,\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}\nu}+\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}{\sqrt{2}},

(7)

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{Ia\alpha}

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{\frac{M_{a}}{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu}\sqrt{\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}\,e^{a\alpha}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{I}}\,\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}\nu}-\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(8)

It is convenient to introduce reciprocal-space displacement and momentum operators,

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}\nu}+\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}{\sqrt{2}},

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}

\displaystyle=i\,\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}\nu}-\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}{\sqrt{2}},

(10)

which obey canonical commutation relations, moreover their Hermitian conjugates satisfy
\hat{Q}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}=\hat{Q}_{-\mathbf{q}\nu}
and
\hat{P}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}=\hat{P}_{-\mathbf{q}\nu}
.

In this basis, the harmonic nuclear Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{BO}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\Bigg[\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}\,\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\,\hat{P}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{P}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}+\hat{Q}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\frac{D_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}}}\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\Bigg]

(11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\left(\hat{n}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(12)

with
\hat{n}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
the phonon number operator.

The harmonic frequencies
\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}
are computed within density-functional perturbation theory (DFPT)
7
using a semilocal exchange–correlation functional, and already include the adiabatic renormalization due to electron–phonon interactions
16
.

If the harmonic Hamiltonian is dynamically unstable, one may construct an auxiliary, positive-definite harmonic Hamiltonian through a full quantum anharmonic minimization, e.g., by employing the stochastic self-consistent harmonic approximation (SSCHA)
100
, which then replaces the original harmonic Hamiltonian. The role of anharmonicity in nonequilibrium dynamics is discussed in Sec .
II.7
.

II.3
Electronic Hamiltonian at fixed nuclei

We begin by choosing an appropriate single-particle basis for the electrons. Since our starting point is a density-functional theory (DFT) calculation, we use the Kohn–Sham (KS) states
\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}(\mathbf{r},\sigma)
and band energies
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\text{KS}}
, where
\mathbf{k}
is the crystal momentum and
n
the band index. This choice allows us to define second-quantized operator
\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n}
(
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}
), obeying the canonical anticommutation relations (see Appendix
A
), that annihilates (creates) an electron in the
n
th Kohn–Sham orbital with momentum
\mathbf{k}
.

In this basis, the auxiliary Kohn–Sham Hamiltonian is diagonal:

\begin{gathered}\hat{H}_{\text{KS}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},n}\varepsilon^{\text{KS}}_{\mathbf{k}n}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\end{gathered}

(13)

with

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\text{KS}}_{\mathbf{k}n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}|\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{2}+\hat{V}_{\text{H}xc}+\hat{V}_{ext}+\hat{V}_{ion}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}\rangle,

(14)

and where
\hat{V}_{\text{H}xc}
is the (semilocal) Hartree plus exchange–correlation potential. The external potential is

\hat{V}_{ext}({\mathbf{r}})=-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{n}}Z_{i}v(|\hat{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{R}^{0}_{i}|)

(15)

and the ionic potential is

\hat{V}_{ion}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N_{n}}Z_{i}Z_{j}v(|\mathbf{R}^{0}_{i}-\mathbf{R}^{0}_{j}|).

(16)

Because we employ a pseudopotential framework,
Z_{i}
is the pseudo-ionic atomic number for the
i
th atomic species, and
N_{n}
is the number of ions in the crystal.
The potential
v(|\mathbf{r}|)=\frac{2}{|\mathbf{r}|}
is the bare Coulomb potential, and
\mathbf{R}^{0}_{i}
denote the ionic equilibrium positions, determined by the zero-force condition in the absence of an external drive (see Eq. (
2
)).

II.3.1
Electronic Hamiltonian at equilibrium

We discuss the equilibrium electronic Hamiltonian before the action of the external drive (
t<0
). To define the bare single particle Hamiltonian matrix from the Kohn-Sham eigenvalues, we subtract the Hartree, exchange-correlation potential, i.e.,

\bar{h}_{\mathbf{k}nm}=\delta_{nm}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\text{KS}}-\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}|\hat{V}_{\text{H}xc}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}m}\rangle

(17)

and obtain the bare electron single-particle Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}_{sp}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},n,m}\bar{h}_{\mathbf{k}nm}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m}.

(18)

The
ab initio
many-body Hamiltonian of the electronic system at equilibrium is composed of three terms:

\hat{H}_{e}=\hat{H}_{sp}+\hat{H}_{ee}+\hat{H}_{ep}.

(19)

The electron–electron interaction part is

\begin{gathered}\hat{H}_{ee}=\dfrac{1}{2N}\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k,k^{\prime},q}\\
n,n^{\prime},m,m^{\prime}\end{subarray}}}v_{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}k-qk^{\prime}+q}}^{nn^{\prime}mm^{\prime}}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}+q}m^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k-q}m},\end{gathered}

(20)

where the matrix element of the bare Coulomb potential is defined as

v_{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}k-qk^{\prime}+q}}^{nn^{\prime}mm^{\prime}}=\matrixelement{\psi_{\vb{k}n}\psi_{\vb{k'}n'}}{v(|\hat{\vb{r}}-\hat{\vb{r}}'|)}{{\psi_{\vb{k-q}m}\psi_{\vb{k'+q}m'}}}.

(21)

The electron–phonon interaction part is

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{ep}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k,q}\\
n,m,\nu\end{subarray}}}

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{mn}^{\nu}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k+q}m}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n}(\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}\nu}),

(22)

involving the bare electron–phonon matrix element defined as

\bar{g}_{mn}^{\nu}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=\sum_{a,\alpha}\dfrac{{e}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{a\alpha}}{{\sqrt{2M_{a}\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}}}}\bar{{d}}^{a\alpha}_{mn}(\mathbf{k,q}),

(23)

where the bare deformation potential is obtained from

\bar{{d}}^{a\alpha}_{mn}(\mathbf{k,q})=\sum_{I}e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{I}}\matrixelement{\psi_{\vb{k+q}m}}{\bar{d}_{Ia\alpha}(\hat{\vb{r}})}{\psi_{\vb{k}n}}

(24)

with

\bar{d}_{Ia\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=\dfrac{\partial V_{ext}({\mathbf{r}})}{\partial Q_{Ia\alpha}}.

(25)

II.3.2
Non-equilibrium Hamiltonian

We assume that the external drive couples directly only to the electronic degrees of freedom. This is the case for a time-dependent uniform external electric field
\mathbf{E}(t)
with photon energies in the eV range, inducing dipolar electronic excitations. The ions are perturbed only indirectly, via the field-induced electronic response. We thus first consider the electrons at clamped nuclei and analyze their dynamics. The dynamics of phonons and ions, triggered by the drive-induced change in the electronic state, will be treated in Secs.
II.5
–
II.8
.

The perturbed nonequilibrium electronic Hamiltonian is obtained by adding a time-dependent light–matter interaction term
\hat{H}_{drive}(t)
. This term follows from the minimal-coupling substitution. In the Coulomb gauge and within the dipole approximation, the second-quantized light–matter interaction reads

\hat{H}_{drive}(t)=\sum_{\mathclap{\mathbf{k},n,m}}\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m},

(26)

where we introduce the bare Rabi frequency matrix

\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)=\sqrt{2}\mathbf{E}(t)\cdot\mathbf{\cal D}_{\mathbf{k}nm},

(27)

and the electric-dipole matrix elements

\mathbf{\cal D}_{\mathbf{k}nm}=\matrixelement{\psi_{\vb{k}n}}{\hat{\vb{r}}}{\psi_{\vb{k}m}}.

(28)

The full nonequilibrium electronic Hamiltonian at clamped nuclei is therefore

\hat{H}_{el}(t)=\hat{H}_{sp}+\hat{H}_{ee}+\hat{H}_{ep}+\hat{H}_{drive}(t).

(29)

II.4
Electronic Bloch equations

The time evolution of observables is governed by quantum thermal averages computed from the system’s density matrix. We introduce the time-dependent nonequilibrium many-body density-matrix operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
, which describes the state of electrons and phonons. Its time dependence arises from the time-dependent Hamiltonian. In the Schrödinger picture, the nonequilibrium average of an operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
is defined as

\langle\hat{\cal O}\rangle_{t}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{\cal O}].

(30)

Its time derivative satisfies:

\displaystyle i\dfrac{d\langle\hat{\cal O}\rangle_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle[\hat{\cal O},\hat{H}]\rangle_{t}.

(31)

To track the dynamics of the electronic subsystem, we evaluate the nonequilibrium electronic occupations
f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)
and microscopic dipole polarizations
p_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)
, defined as

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n}\rangle_{t},

(32)

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n}\rangle_{t}\quad{\rm for}\quad n\neq m.

(33)

Using Eqs. (
29
)–(
33
), the time evolution of these quantities reads

\displaystyle i\frac{df_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle[\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{sp}+\hat{H}_{drive}]\rangle_{t}+

(34)

\displaystyle\langle[\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{ee}+\hat{H}_{ep}]\rangle_{t},

\displaystyle i\dfrac{dp_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle[\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{sp}+\hat{H}_{drive}]\rangle_{t}+

(35)

\displaystyle\langle[\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{ee}+\hat{H}_{ep}]\rangle_{t}.

To derive the equations of motion (EOMs), we evaluate the equal-time commutators in the expressions above. These fall into two categories: single-particle commutators, arising from the quadratic Hamiltonians
\hat{H}_{sp}
and
\hat{H}_{drive}(t)
, which can be computed analytically; and many-body commutators, involving
\hat{H}_{ee}
and
\hat{H}_{ep}
, which are more complex to evaluate.

II.4.1
Single-particle terms

The single-particle commutators can be evaluated directly using the equal-time canonical anticommutation relations (see Eq. (
150
)). This yields

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\langle[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}m}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{sp}]\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\bar{h}_{\mathbf{k}nm}[f_{\mathbf{k}m}(t)-f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)]+

\displaystyle\hskip-80.0pt\sum_{\mathclap{m^{\prime}\neq m}}\bar{h}_{\mathbf{k}nm^{\prime}}\,p_{\mathbf{k}m^{\prime}m}(t)-\sum_{\mathclap{n^{\prime}\neq n}}\bar{h}_{\mathbf{k}n^{\prime}m}\,p_{\mathbf{k}nn^{\prime}}(t).

(36)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\langle[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}m}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{drive}]\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)\left[f_{\mathbf{k}m}(t)-f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)\right]+

\displaystyle\hskip-80.0pt\sum_{\mathclap{m^{\prime}\neq m}}\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm^{\prime}}(t)\,p_{\mathbf{k}m^{\prime}m}(t)-\sum_{\mathclap{n^{\prime}\neq n}}\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}n^{\prime}m}(t)\,p_{\mathbf{k}nn^{\prime}}(t).

(37)

The single-particle contributions to the equation of motion for the electronic occupations are obtained by setting
n=m
in Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
). All terms derived from the single-particle commutators involve the bare single-particle Hamiltonian matrix eigenvalues and Rabi frequencies. However, physically meaningful equations must depend on dressed electronic degrees of freedom. Therefore, Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
) alone are insufficient to fully capture the system’s dynamics. The required dressing is provided by many-body effects due to electron–electron and electron–phonon interactions.

II.4.2
Many-body terms

The evaluation of the many-body commutators in Eqs. (
34

35

;
6
, involving operators of increasing complexity.

To truncate this hierarchy, two main strategies are typically employed. The first is to evaluate the commutators explicitly and apply a suitable truncation to the electron–electron and electron–phonon terms

;
129
;
121
.

The second approach relies on the nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) formalism

;
141
;
138
, which introduces lesser and greater Green’s functions and self-energies. These enter a nonequilibrium Dyson equation used to determine dressed propagators from a closed expression for the self-energy, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(a). A Feynman diagrammatic expansion is then employed, together with the Kadanoff–Baym ansatz (KBA)
60
and the Markov approximation, to obtain a computationally tractable scheme (see Appendix
B
) that naturally accounts for both quasiparticle renormalization and interaction-vertex screening.

The net result of the NEGF formalism is that the many-body commutator can be decomposed into two contributions:

\displaystyle\langle[\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{ee}+\hat{H}_{ep}]\rangle_{t}=\Delta_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)+i\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t).

(38)

The first term,
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)
, represents the time-dependent quasiparticle renormalization due to many-body interactions. The second term,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)
, is a collision integral that captures scattering-induced changes in occupations and polarizations arising from carrier-carrier and carrier–phonon processes.

The specific form of these two contributions depends on the approximation scheme used to truncate the BBGKY hierarchy. In general, both terms can be separated into electron–electron and electron–phonon components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}^{ee}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)+\mathcal{I}^{ep}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t),

(39)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{ee}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)+\Delta^{ep}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t).

(40)

In what follows, we denote diagonal terms using a single band index for notational simplicity.

II.4.3
Dressing of single particle terms

The renormalization term originates from the singular part of the electron self-energy
141
;
138
, which collects all time-local contributions to quasiparticle properties. In its exact form, this singular component includes the Hartree–Fock term, the electron–phonon tadpole, and the Debye–Waller contributions
1
;
84
;
141
. Additional static terms may also appear, depending on the chosen quasiparticle approximation, as shown in Eqs. (
166
) and (
167
).

The renormalization term can be written as a commutator with a time-dependent effective single-particle Hamiltonian, in close analogy with Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
); see also Appendix
B
. Its explicit expression reads

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)=\langle[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}m}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{\Delta}]\rangle_{t},

(41)

where

\hat{\Delta}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},n,m}\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m}

(42)

is a single-particle operator constructed from the time-local, singular self-energy
\Sigma(t)
.

Given the structure of the commutators in Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
), the net effect of the renormalization term is to dress the single-particle part of the electronic Hamiltonian. In other words, the quadratic part of the Hamiltonian becomes

\bar{h}_{\mathbf{k}nm}+\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)\;\to\;\bar{h}_{\mathbf{k}nm}+\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)+\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t).

(43)

Consider now this expression at equilibrium in the absence of the external perturbation, i.e., at
t=0
. Under these assumptions, the bare Rabi frequency vanishes and the renormalization term dresses the bare single-particle Hamiltonian matrix. Within the quasiparticle approximation on the Kohn–Sham basis, the dressed single-particle Hamiltonian becomes diagonal:

h_{\mathbf{k}nm}=\bar{h}_{\mathbf{k}nm}+\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}nm}(0)=\delta_{nm}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{0},

(44)

where we defined the equilibrium dressed single-particle electronic eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{0}
.
Out of equilibrium (
t>0
), the renormalization dresses both the eigenvalues and the Rabi frequency through the diagonal and off-diagonal components of the self-energy variation, respectively. We define the time-dependent change of the self-energy as

\Delta\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)=\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)-\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}nm}(0).

(45)

Using Eqs. (
43
)–(
45
), we obtain the following expressions for the dressed time-dependent electronic quantities:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{0}+\Delta\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}n}(t),

(46)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)

\displaystyle=\bar{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)+\Delta\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t).

(47)

Thus, in the single-particle commutators of Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
), combined with the many-body renormalization term in Eq. (
41
), renormalization effects can be included by replacing the bare Hamiltonian matrix and Rabi frequencies with their time-dependent dressed counterparts given in Eqs. (
46
) and (
47
). Thus, we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}m}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{sp}+\hat{H}_{drive}+\hat{\Delta}]\rangle_{t}=\sum_{\mathclap{m^{\prime}\neq m}}{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm^{\prime}}(t)p_{\mathbf{k}m^{\prime}m}(t)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathclap{n^{\prime}\neq n}}{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}n^{\prime}m}(t)\,p_{\mathbf{k}nn^{\prime}}(t)+\left[\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}m}(t)\right]p_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)

\displaystyle+{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t)\left[f_{\mathbf{k}m}(t)-f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)\right]

(48)

The single-particle contribution to the electronic occupations is found by setting
n=m
. In this case, the only non-vanishing terms are the first two on the right-hand side, leading to

\langle[\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n},\hat{H}_{sp}+\hat{H}_{drive}+\hat{\Delta}]\rangle_{t}=2\operatorname{Im}\sum_{\mathclap{m^{\prime}\neq m}}{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}nm^{\prime}}(t)p_{\mathbf{k}m^{\prime}m}(t)

(49)

In the next section, we detail the approximations used to construct the time-dependent quasiparticle self-energy variation.

II.4.4
Dressing: electron-electron

We focus on the electron–electron contribution to the self-energy. This is treated within the GW approximation
52
;
3
, where the bare Coulomb interaction is screened by a frequency-dependent dielectric function computed within the random-phase approximation (RPA).

In our framework, the time-local component of the self-energy is identified with the time-dependent Hartree term plus the static GW contribution, namely, the screened-exchange and Coulomb-hole terms known collectively as the COHSEX approximation
52
;
14
. We denote these as
\Delta\Sigma^{\text{H}}(t)
and
\Delta\Sigma^{\text{GW}}(t)
, respectively.

These contributions correspond to the first and third diagrams in Fig.
2
(b). Their time dependence arises from the evolution of electronic occupations and polarizations, which modify both the charge density and the static screening. The explicit expressions for
\Delta\Sigma^{\text{H}}(t)
and
\Delta\Sigma^{\text{GW}}(t)
are given in Eqs. (
172
), (
C
), and (
179
).

We emphasize that the Hartree self-energy includes only inhomogeneous (
\mathbf{G}\neq 0
) contributions, as the homogeneous (
\mathbf{G}=0
) component vanishes due to overall charge neutrality. Consequently, the Hartree variation contributes only in the presence of local-field effects.

To capture the time dependence of the screened Coulomb interaction, we adopt a time-dependent static-screening approximation. In this approach, the screened interaction is assumed to take the time-local form

W(t,t^{\prime})=W_{s}(t)\,\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(50)

where
W_{s}(t)
is the statically screened Coulomb interaction evaluated at time
t
. This quantity is computed as

W_{s}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)=\int d\mathbf{r}_{1}\,v(|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{1}|)\,\epsilon^{-1}_{s}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t),

(51)

with
\epsilon^{-1}_{s}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)
denoting the time-dependent static inverse dielectric function in real space, evaluated within the RPA. Its time dependence reflects the underlying evolution of the electronic density. The construction of the time-dependent RPA screening is described in Appendix
D
.

II.4.5
Dressing: electron-phonon

We approximate the electron–phonon contribution to the electron self-energy using the Fan–Migdal (FM) approximation
39
;
97
;
47
. Within this framework, the bare electron–phonon coupling matrix in Eq. (
23
) is replaced by the statically screened electron–phonon vertex

{g}_{mn}^{\nu}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=\sum_{a,\alpha}\dfrac{{e}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{a\alpha}}{{\sqrt{2M_{a}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}}}d^{a\alpha}_{mn}(\mathbf{k,q}),

(52)

where the dressed deformation potential in reciprocal space is defined as

{d}_{mn}^{a\alpha}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=\matrixelement{\psi_{\vb{k+q}m}}{ \sum_{I}e^{-i\vb{q}\cdot\vb{R}_{I}}d_{Ia\alpha}(\vb{\hat{r}})}{{\psi_{\vb{k}n}}},

(53)

and the real-space deformation potential reads

d_{Ia\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=\int d\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\,\bar{d}_{Ia\alpha}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\,\epsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}}).

(54)

The dressed deformation potential is typically computed within DFPT
7
using a semilocal exchange–correlation kernel. As a result, the underlying static dielectric matrix includes both RPA screening and DFT exchange–correlation effects. The static dielectric matrix employed for the electron–electron self-energy is instead computed within the RPA alone; see Eq. (
51
).

This difference stems from practical considerations: the screened electron–phonon matrix elements are conventionally extracted from the self-consistent Kohn–Sham potential in DFPT. While, in principle, one could go beyond this level by incorporating GW corrections to the electron–phonon vertex
68
, such treatments remain computationally demanding and are not explored here.

A further distinction between Eqs. (
51
) and (
54
) concerns the treatment of time dependence. Whereas the statically screened Coulomb interaction
W_{s}(t)
accounts for time-dependent screening effects arising from evolving occupations, the deformation potential is assumed to be time independent. In principle, this time dependence could be included by evaluating the dielectric matrix at each time step using instantaneous time-dependent DFT states; however, we leave this extension for future work.

The time-local electron–phonon self-energy is thus approximated by a time-dependent FM self-energy in the quasiparticle approximation, denoted
\Delta\Sigma^{\text{FM}}(t)
. This contribution corresponds to the sixth diagram in Fig.
2
(b). We also include the electron–phonon tadpole term, represented by the second diagram in Fig.
2
(b), which accounts for renormalizations induced by coherent atomic displacements. This term, denoted
\Delta\Sigma^{\text{AM}}(t)
, will be discussed in Sec.
II.8
.

An additional time-local singular self-energy from electron–phonon interaction is the Debye–Waller term. Although this term should, in principle, be included, we neglect it for practical reasons.

The explicit expressions for the tadpole and FM self-energies are given in Eqs. (
175
) and (
C
).

II.4.6
Collision Integrals

The collision term in Eq. (
38
) arises from all self-energy contributions beyond the singular part. It accounts for quasiparticle scattering and incoherent processes in the dynamics of the electronic state, thereby inducing the decay of quasiparticle populations and the dephasing of the system’s polarization.

Within the NEGF formalism
60
;
86
;
141
, the scattering term is expressed as a time convolution between the greater/lesser components of the self-energy and the corresponding Green’s functions, as given in Eq. (
165
). While this expression is formally exact, it has limited practical utility, as it depends on the full two-time structure of the Green’s functions and therefore does not yield a closed equation for the single-time electronic observables of interest.

To obtain a tractable description, we adopt the KBA
60
in combination with the quasiparticle approximation for the electron propagator. This procedure yields a closed equation of motion, given a suitable form of the self-energy
86
;
138
. However, the resulting scattering integral retains memory effects, as its evaluation requires access to the system’s history at all preceding times.

A further simplification comes from the Markov approximation, which assumes that scattering at a given time depends only on the system’s instantaneous state. This approximation eliminates memory effects and yields time-local collision integrals
86
;
138
.

By combining the KBA with the Markov approximation and specifying a closed form for the self-energy, we derive explicit expressions for the scattering integrals. In the following, we present the self-energy approximations adopted in this work and the corresponding expressions for the scattering terms.

II.4.7
Collision: carrier-carrier

To approximate the collision integrals arising from electron–electron interactions, we adopt the time-local form of the screened Coulomb interaction introduced in Eq. (
50
) and express the self-energy in terms of the statically screened, time-dependent interaction. Specifically, we employ the second-order Born approximation
50
, wherein the bare Coulomb interaction is replaced by its statically screened counterpart. This approach corresponds to evaluating the fourth and fifth diagrams in Fig.
2
(b). Together with the third diagram, these terms represent the leading-order contributions to the electron self-energy in a perturbative expansion in powers of
W_{s}
. The first-order term is purely singular and yields only quasiparticle energy renormalizations, whereas the second-order terms incorporate retardation effects and contribute to scattering processes via the collision integrals.

Within these approximations, the diagonal electron–electron collision integral takes the form
86
;
142
;
138

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{k}n}^{ee}(t)=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\pi}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{q}\\
n^{\prime},m,m^{\prime}\end{subarray}}}\big|W_{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}k-qk^{\prime}+q}}^{nn^{\prime}mm^{\prime}}(t)-W_{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}k^{\prime}+qk-q}}^{nn^{\prime}m^{\prime}m}(t)\big|^{2}\big[f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}(t)(1-f_{\mathbf{k-q}m}(t))(1-f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}+q}m^{\prime}}(t))

(55)

\displaystyle-f_{\mathbf{k-q}m}(t)f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}+q}m^{\prime}}(t)(1-f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t))(1-f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}(t))\big]\delta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)+\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}(t)-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}+q}m^{\prime}}(t)-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k-q}m}(t)),

where we introduce the time-dependent screened Coulomb matrix element,

W_{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}k^{\prime\prime}k^{\prime\prime\prime}}}^{nn^{\prime}mm^{\prime}}(t)=\matrixelement{\psi_{\vb{k}n}\psi_{\vb{k'}n'}}{W_s(\hat{\vb{r}},\hat{\vb{r}}',t)}{{\psi_{\vb{k''}m}\psi_{\vb{k'''}m'}}},

(56)

with

The off-diagonal collision integral is approximated using a relaxation-time ansatz (RTA):

(57)

where

Under these assumptions, the electron–electron relaxation rate reads:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}n}^{ee}(t)=\dfrac{\pi}{2N^{2}}\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{q}\\

\displaystyle\big|W_{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}k-qk^{\prime}+q}}^{nn^{\prime}mm^{\prime}}(t)-W_{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}k^{\prime}+qk-q}}^{nn^{\prime}m^{\prime}m}(t)\big|^{2}\big[f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}(t)(1-f_{\mathbf{k-q}m}(t))(1-f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}+q}m^{\prime}}(t))+

II.4.8
Collision: carrier-phonon

The explicit form of the electron–phonon collision term is derived from the full FM self-energy. II.5
Phonon Hamiltonian

In Sec.
II.2
, we introduced the harmonic phonon Hamiltonian at equilibrium within the BO approximation. This allowed us to define creation and annihilation operators for the normal modes of vibration from the Hessian of the BO energy surface; see Eqs. (
5
)–(
12
). However, to account for electron–phonon coupling, we must instead start from the bare phonon Hamiltonian.

The first step is to derive a quadratic bare phonon Hamiltonian starting from Eq. (
12
). In principle, this can be achieved by removing from the force constant matrix in Eq. (
4
) the electron–phonon self-energy evaluated in the adiabatic approximation
16
;
47
.

Diagonalizing the resulting matrix yields the crystal elastic tensor and the bare phonon frequencies. However, as shown in Refs.
16
;
10
;
17
;
139
, the adiabatic electron–phonon self-energy can be replaced by the static screened–screened approximation:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Pi}_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q})=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k},n,m}

\displaystyle d^{a\alpha}_{mn}(\mathbf{k,q})d^{b\beta}_{mn}(\mathbf{k,q})\times

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left[\dfrac{f_{\mathbf{k}n}^{0}-f_{\mathbf{k+q}m}^{0}}{\varepsilon^{0}_{\mathbf{k}n}-\varepsilon^{0}_{\mathbf{k+q}m}+i\eta}\right]

(64)

without introducing overscreening. Thus, we define the bare force constant matrix as

\bar{C}_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q})=C_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q})-\widetilde{\Pi}_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q}),

(65)

The bare phonon Hamiltonian is then obtained by replacing the BO dynamical matrix in Eq. (
11
) with the bare one, as defined through Eqs. (
5
), (
6
), and (
65
). The resulting expression reads

\hat{H}_{h}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{q}\\
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\end{subarray}}}\Bigg[\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{P}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{P}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}+\hat{Q}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\dfrac{\bar{D}_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}}}\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\Bigg].

(66)

A key difference relative to the BO harmonic Hamiltonian in Eq. (
12
) is that the bare phonon Hamiltonian is not diagonal in the normal-mode indices and includes quadratic terms with two creation or two annihilation operators. The full phonon Hamiltonian is obtained by combining Eqs. (
22
) and (
66
), resulting in

\hat{H}_{ph}=\hat{H}_{h}+\hat{H}_{ep},

(67)

where
\hat{H}_{ep}
describes the coupling between electrons and phonons.

Anharmonic phonon–phonon interactions can also be incorporated into the formalism. This will be the topic of Sec.
II.7
.

II.6
Phonon Bloch equations

We employ the same density-matrix formalism used for electrons in Sec.
II.4
. We track the time evolution of phonon degrees of freedom via the nonequilibrium phonon occupations
n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)
, defined as

n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)=\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\rangle_{t}.

(68)

The EOMs for the phonon occupations follow from Eqs. (
31
), (
67
), and (
68
), and take the form

\displaystyle i\dfrac{dn_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)}{dt}=\langle[\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu},\hat{H}_{h}]\rangle_{t}+\langle[\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu},\hat{H}_{ep}]\rangle_{t}.

(69)

The first commutator yields quadratic terms in second-quantized operators. Hence, we obtain nonequilibrium averages of the type
\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}
,
\langle\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}
, and
\langle\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{-q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}
. This indicates that Eq. (
69
) alone is not sufficient to determine the EOMs for phonon occupations and should be coupled to the corresponding equations for these additional averages.

The second commutator, involving the many-body electron–phonon Hamiltonian, leads to a BBGKY hierarchy, analogous to the electronic case. This many-body problem can be addressed using Green’s-function techniques
47
;
141
, which yield the EOMs for the phonon occupations
138
.

In principle, one can derive EOMs for averages involving two creation or two annihilation operators, which give rise to effects such as phonon squeezing
59
;
9
;
67
and time-dependent oscillations in thermal diffuse scattering
150
;
38
. However, since we are primarily interested in phonon relaxation, the impact of such correlations can be neglected. Therefore, we restrict our treatment to the evolution of the phonon occupation alone.

Here we summarize the main outcomes of the many-body treatment of Eq. (
69
). The commutator with the electron–phonon interaction Hamiltonian can be split, similarly to Eq. (
38
), into two contributions:

\langle[\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu},\hat{H}_{ep}]\rangle_{t}=\Delta^{pe}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)+i\mathcal{I}^{pe}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t),

(70)

where the first term corresponds to a many-body renormalization and the second term is a collision integral. The explicit forms of
\Delta_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)
depend on the self-energy approximation adopted to handle the many-body hierarchy.

II.6.1
Dressing of dynamical matrix

The renormalization term, as in the electronic case, originates from the singular (time-local) part of the phonon self-energy
141
;
138
. It can be expressed as a commutator between the phonon-occupation operator and an effective Hamiltonian contribution:

\Delta_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{pe}(t)=\langle[\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu},\hat{\Delta}]\rangle,

(71)

with

\hat{\Delta}=\sum_{\mathbf{q},\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\Pi_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(t)\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}.

Here,
\Pi(t)

D_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(t)=\bar{D}_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}+2\sqrt{\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}}\,\Pi_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(t).

(73)

An explicit form for the time-dependent dynamical matrix follows once an approximation for the phonon self-energy is specified. We treat it via the screened-screened electron-hole bubble approximation

;
10
;
17
;
139

2

Within this approximation, the time-local phonon self-energy is given by the adiabatic electron–hole bubble
\Pi^{\text{EH}}(t)
, whose full expression is provided in Eq. (
181
). At equilibrium (
t=0

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(0)=\sum_{a,b,\alpha,\beta}\dfrac{(e^{a\alpha}_{\mathbf{q}\nu})^{*}}{\sqrt{2M_{a}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}}}\widetilde{\Pi}_{a\alpha b\beta}(\mathbf{q})\dfrac{e^{b\beta}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{2M_{b}\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}}},

where

D_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(0)=\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}\left[\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}\right]^{2}.

(75)

This result implies that the time-dependent dynamical matrix can be written as

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(t)=\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}[\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}]^{2}+2\sqrt{\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}^{0}}\,\Delta\Pi^{\text{EH}}_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(t),

(76)

where
\Delta\Pi(t)=\Pi(t)-\Pi(0)
denotes the nonequilibrium correction to the self-energy. The time-dependent phonon frequencies are then obtained by diagonalizing
D_{\mathbf{q}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(t)

To avoid this complication, we adopt the no mixing mode approximation, in which the normal-mode basis is fixed and only the diagonal part of the self-energy is retained. The time-dependent phonon frequencies thus reduce to

\displaystyle[\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)]^{2}=\left[\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}\right]^{2}+2\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}\,\Delta\Pi^{\text{EH}}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t),

(77)

namely, the dressed dynamical matrix remains diagonal at all times. Hence, by neglecting the time evolution of quadratic phonon averages other than the occupations, the commutators involving the harmonic Hamiltonian combined with the renormalization term in Eq. (

II.6.2
Phonon-carrier scattering

The scattering integral originates from all phonon self-energy contributions other than the singular part. As in the electronic case, an explicit expression can be derived within the NEGF approach

We approximate the phonon self-energy with the electron-hole bubble with two statically screened vertices

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{pe}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)=-\dfrac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k},m,n}|g_{mn}^{\nu}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})|^{2}\big[n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)(f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)-f_{\mathbf{k+q}m}(t))-f_{\mathbf{k+q}m}(t)(1-f_{\mathbf{k}n}(t))\big]\delta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k+q}m}(t)-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)-\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)),

(78)

where the screened electron–phonon matrix elements

II.7
Anharmonic effects

Up to now, we considered a harmonic phonon Hamiltonian coupled to electrons through Eq. (
67
). While the harmonic approximation provides a solid foundation for lattice dynamics, real materials inevitably exhibit anharmonic effects. These are key to understanding many phonon-related phenomena, especially phonon transport, thermal expansion, and phase transitions
165
;
40
;
12
.

For nonequilibrium phonon dynamics, anharmonicity plays a crucial role. It provides the microscopic mechanism behind intrinsic phonon–phonon scattering, which is essential for bringing the phonon subsystem to thermal equilibrium. This is particularly relevant for the relaxation of nonequilibrium phonon distributions generated by electron–phonon interactions. For instance, after an ultrafast electronic excitation, the energy transferred to the lattice initially populates specific phonon modes via carrier–phonon scattering
106
, and the subsequent redistribution of this energy among all phonon modes occurs primarily through anharmonic phonon–phonon scattering processes
18
.

To include anharmonicity, we expand the BO energy up to fourth order in the atomic displacement operators
95
;
82
;
109
, obtaining the effective anharmonic Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{pp}=\hat{H}^{(3)}+\hat{H}^{(4)},

(79)

where

\hat{H}^{(n)}=\dfrac{2^{n/2}N}{n!N^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{q}_{1},..,\mathbf{q}_{n}\\
\nu_{1},..,\nu_{n}\end{subarray}}}\Phi^{(n)}_{\nu_{1}..\nu_{n}}(\mathbf{q}_{1},..,\mathbf{q}_{n})\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{q}_{1}\nu_{1}}..\hat{Q}_{\mathbf{q}_{n}\nu_{n}}.

(80)

Here, we introduce the third- and fourth-order anharmonic matrices in the normal-mode basis
95
;
82
;
109
. The total phonon Hamiltonian is thus obtained by combining Eqs. (
11
) and (
79
). This clarifies why we treat anharmonic effects as on top of the results obtained in Sec.
II.6
. While the electron–phonon coupling problem is solved from the bare harmonic dynamical matrix and deformation potential, the anharmonic part is defined from a BO energy expansion to fourth order, based on the equilibrium quadratic Hamiltonian in Eq. (
11
).

At this point, we distinguish two regimes: perturbative and nonperturbative. Which case applies depends on the anharmonicity of the lattice potential, the temperature range considered, and the strength of quantum ionic effects; this is generally system-specific.

In the perturbative case, anharmonic effects are relatively small. The harmonic dynamical matrix is positive definite, so the BO energy can be expanded directly in terms of atomic displacements. The anharmonic matrices can be obtained either via DFPT
110
or finite-difference methods
152
. From these, one can estimate phonon linewidths and shifts using the anharmonic self-energy
82
;
109
.

In the nonperturbative case, anharmonicity is strong enough to render the harmonic dynamical matrix unstable, and a direct expansion is no longer possible. Here, one can employ the SSCHA
100
and perform a full quantum anharmonic minimization. This yields a new set of equilibrium positions and renormalized phonon frequencies (the SSCHA frequencies). The anharmonic matrices are then extracted via stochastic sampling of the free energy using an effective quadratic density matrix. This approach allows computation of nonperturbative linewidths and shifts as well
100
.

In this work, we focus on the perturbative case for simplicity and defer the nonperturbative extension to future work. Moreover, for practical reasons, we retain only the third-order terms in Eq. (
79
). Even though fourth-order terms can yield significant phonon shifts, phonon linewidths are entirely determined by third-order terms
82
;
109
.

Within this framework, the relevant self-energy is the phonon bubble (PB), see Fig.
2
(c), which gives a time-dependent correction to the BO phonon frequencies via the real part of
\Pi^{\text{PB}}(t)

82
;
109
;
100
; see Eq. (
C
). This correction is included in the quasiparticle and no mixing mode approximations. The resulting time-dependent phonon frequencies are then obtained from Eq. (
77
) by adding
\Pi^{\text{PB}}(t)
to
\Delta\Pi^{\text{EH}}(t)

\big[\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)\big]^{2}=\big[\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}\big]^{2}+2\omega_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{0}[\Delta\Pi^{\text{EH}}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)+\Pi^{\text{PB}}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)].

(81)

Anharmonicity also introduces phonon–phonon scattering, leading to an additional collision integral. This term, derived from Fermi’s golden rule for three-phonon processes, reads
165
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{pp}(t)=-\dfrac{\pi}{N}\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{q^{\prime}},\mathbf{q^{\prime\prime}}\\
\nu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\big|\Phi^{(3)}_{\nu\nu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q^{\prime}},-\mathbf{q^{\prime\prime}})\big|^{2}\big[n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)(n_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}(t)+1)+n_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}\nu^{\prime\prime}}(t)(n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)-n_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}(t))\big]\times

(82)

\displaystyle\delta({\omega}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)-{\omega}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}(t)-{\omega}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime\prime}\nu^{\prime\prime}}(t))+2\big|\Phi^{(3)}_{\nu\nu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q^{\prime}},-\mathbf{q^{\prime\prime}})\big|^{2}\big[n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)(n_{\mathbf{q^{\prime}}\nu^{\prime}}(t)-n_{\mathbf{q^{\prime\prime}}\nu^{\prime\prime}}(t))

\displaystyle-n_{\mathbf{q^{\prime\prime}}\nu^{\prime\prime}}(t)(1+n_{\mathbf{q^{\prime}}\nu^{\prime}}(t))\big]\delta({\omega}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)+{\omega}_{\mathbf{q^{\prime}}\nu^{\prime}}(t)-{\omega}_{\mathbf{q^{\prime\prime}}\nu^{\prime\prime}}(t))\bigg\}.

Again, this scattering term can be linked to the imaginary part of the phonon bubble self-energy via the RTA, as discussed in Appendix
E

\dfrac{dn_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{pe}(t)+\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{pp}(t),

(83)

where the explicit expressions for the collision integrals are given in Eqs. (
78

II.8
Coherent atomic motion

In the previous sections, we derived the nonequilibrium EOMs for the coupled electron–phonon system, which in principle allow us to track its time evolution under the discussed approximations. At that stage, we neglected the possibility of light-induced coherent atomic motion. In other words, we assumed that the nonequilibrium thermal averages of ionic displacements and momenta, defined in Eqs. (
7
) and (
8

To describe the time evolution of atomic displacements and momenta, we start from Eqs. (

\displaystyle Q_{Ia\alpha}(t)

(84)

\displaystyle P_{Ia\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{P}_{Ia\alpha}\rangle_{t}.

(85)

We restrict our analysis to situations where the crystal periodicity is preserved, assuming
Q_{Ia\alpha}=P_{Ia\alpha}=0
for all
I\neq 0

\displaystyle M_{a}\dfrac{d^{2}Q_{Ia\alpha}(t)}{dt^{2}}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{J,b,\beta}C^{0}_{Ia\alpha Jb\beta}Q_{Jb\beta}(t)

\displaystyle-\int d\mathbf{r}\bar{d}_{Ia\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\Delta n(\mathbf{r},t),

(86)

where the crystal elastic tensor is defined as

\displaystyle C^{0}_{Ia\alpha Jb\beta}=

\displaystyle\left.\dfrac{\partial^{2}E_{nn}}{\partial Q_{Ia\alpha}\partial Q_{Jb\beta}}\right|_{\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{0}}

(87)

\displaystyle+\int d\mathbf{r}\ n^{0}(\mathbf{r})\left.\dfrac{\partial^{2}V_{ext}(\mathbf{r})}{\partial Q_{Ia\alpha}\partial Q_{Jb\beta}}\right|_{\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{0}},

(88)

with

denoting the nuclear–nuclear interaction energy and

is defined in Eq. (

We now analyze the nonequilibrium density variation

The term

\Delta n_{1}(\mathbf{r},t)

We then assume that the variation

\Delta n_{2}

II.8.1
Carrier occupations contribution

We evaluate the ionic force arising from
\Delta n_{1}(\mathbf{r},t)
, that is, the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
II.8
) due to
\Delta n_{1}(\mathbf{r},t)

7
;
16

\Delta f_{\mathbf{k}n}

To this end, we start from the photoinduced force term, see Eqs. (
II.8
) and (
89

F_{Ia\alpha}(t)=-\int d\mathbf{r}\,\bar{d}_{Ia\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\,\Delta n_{1}(\mathbf{r},t).

(90)

For simplicity, and to highlight the connection with a time-independent DFPT framework, we assume a quasistationary electronic density variation. This assumption is well justified for times larger than tens of femtoseconds, since the density varies rapidly during the pulse and only slowly after carrier thermalization. This means that the force expression we obtain will accurately describe the ionic motion on its characteristic timescale (hundreds of femtoseconds) after carrier thermalization.

We relabel the time-independent density variation after carrier thermalization as
\Delta n(\mathbf{r})=\Delta n_{1}(\mathbf{r},t)
. We express the density variation in the KS basis as

\displaystyle\Delta n(\mathbf{r})\simeq\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathclap{\mathbf{k},n}}\Big[

\displaystyle\Delta f_{\mathbf{k}n}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}+f_{\mathbf{k}n}^{0}\Delta\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}^{*}(\mathbf{r})\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle+f_{\mathbf{k}n}^{0}\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}^{*}(\mathbf{r})\Delta\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}(\mathbf{r})\Big],

(91)

accounting for both the change in electronic occupations and in the single-particle orbitals. This differs significantly from standard many-body treatments, where the single-particle orbitals are kept fixed and only changes in the occupations are considered. Here, instead, we also include the change in the single-particle orbitals induced by an out-of-equilibrium electronic occupation set.

We now express the change of the electronic orbitals using linear-response theory

\displaystyle\Delta n(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\simeq\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathclap{\mathbf{k},n}}\Delta f_{\mathbf{k}n}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\int d\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\chi_{0}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Delta V_{\text{KS}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

where we used the definition of the static irreducible density–density response function

\displaystyle\chi_{0}(\mathbf{r,r^{\prime}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k,k^{\prime}}\\

\displaystyle\dfrac{f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}n}-f^{0}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\text{KS}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}^{\text{KS}}}\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}^{*}(\mathbf{r})\psi_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})

(94)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\delta n(\mathbf{r})}{\delta f_{\mathbf{k}n}}=\int d\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\chi_{0}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\frac{\delta V_{\text{KS}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\delta f_{\mathbf{k}n}}+|\psi_{\mathbf{k}n}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\delta V_{\text{KS}}(\mathbf{r})}{\delta f_{\mathbf{k}n}}=

For this reason, our quasi-equilibrium treatment goes beyond state-of-the-art Ehrenfest dynamics, in which the orbitals are kept fixed. It is worth noting that vertex screening yields quantitative differences in the real-time ionic forces, with important consequences for the phonon dynamics.

The result in Eq. (

The electron–electron collision integral is associated with two distinct scattering processes, illustrated in Fig.

III.3
Time evolution of the nonequilibrium state

The evolution of the nonequilibrium state is governed by the time evolution of the system’s dynamical variables, including electron and phonon occupations, microscopic polarizations, and atomic positions and momenta. The simulation proceeds through three main steps:

(i)

At time
t=t_{i}
, self-energy corrections are computed (see Appendix
C
). These are used to evaluate the renormalized electron and phonon energies and Rabi-frequency corrections (Eqs. (
46
), (
47
), (
81
)). The rates, i.e., the time derivatives of the dynamical variables, are then calculated (Eqs. (
63
)–(
62
), (
83
), and (
113
)).

(ii)

The rates are used to update the dynamical variables at
t_{i+1}=t_{i}+\Delta t
via an explicit time-integration algorithm.

(iii)

The updated variables at
t_{i+1}
are used to compute and store selected physical observables.

These steps are repeated until the system reaches a quasi-equilibrium state.

Step (i) involves evaluating momentum averages over the first Brillouin zone. However, the presence of
\delta
functions and the Cauchy principal part
\mathcal{P}
makes these expressions well defined only in the continuum limit. To regularize them on finite grids, we introduce the regularized forms:

\delta(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\dfrac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},\quad\mathcal{P}\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)=\dfrac{x}{x^{2}+\sigma^{2}},

(134)

with
\sigma

In step (ii), the dynamical variables are propagated to
t_{i+1}
using their values and derivatives at
t_{i}
. We employ explicit Runge–Kutta (RK) schemes, including second-order (RK2), fourth-order (RK4), and the adaptive fifth-fourth-order Dormand–Prince method (RKDP54)
33
. Unlike RK2 and RK4, RKDP54 adjusts the timestep
\Delta t_{i}
dynamically to maintain the local error below a specified threshold, estimated from the difference between fourth- and fifth-order solutions. Implementation details are provided in Appendix
H
.

In step (iii), relevant physical observables are computed. For example, the macroscopic polarization is

(135)

(136)

where

III.4
Optimization and computational performance

The explicit time integration of the EOMs can become significantly computationally demanding. The most time-consuming part of the algorithm is the evaluation of the collision integrals, which involve reciprocal-space integrations over the BZ and summations over Wannier bands and phonon branches.

Each carrier-carrier collision integral requires two BZ integrations and three summations over Wannier bands. This must be performed
N\times N_{w}
times to obtain the collision rates for every electronic state. Consequently, each term in the summation must be evaluated and accumulated a total of
N^{3}\times N_{w}^{4}
times.

The carrier-phonon collision integrals, by contrast, involve a single BZ integration and two summations over Wannier bands (Wannier bands and phonon branches). Evaluating these rates requires summing
2N^{2}\times N_{w}^{2}\times N_{m}
contributions.

Finally, phonon–phonon collision integrals involve two BZ integrations and two summations over phonon modes. However, due to the presence of a Kronecker
\delta
in the definition of the third-order anharmonic matrix (Eq. (
III.1
)), the number of BZ integrations is effectively reduced to one. As a result, computing these rates involves summing
N^{2}\times N_{m}^{3}
terms.

The computational cost can become substantial when using fine reciprocal-space grids or a large number of Wannier bands or phonon modes. Therefore, it is essential to adopt strategies that reduce the number of floating-point operations, thereby minimizing computational time and improving the efficiency of time propagation.

The most immediate optimization is to discard all terms where the Dirac
\delta
function is violated significantly. Specifically, we ignore contributions for which the modulus of the Dirac delta argument exceeds
3\sigma
.

Further efficiency is obtained by neglecting contributions where the scattering amplitude, set by the squared modulus of the static Coulomb screening, electron–phonon interaction, or third-order anharmonic matrix elements, is below a chosen threshold. Another optimization leverages symmetries in the definition of the collision integrals.

For example, the computational cost of carrier-carrier collision integrals can be reduced (assuming time reversal symmetry) by exploiting their invariance under the transformation
\mathbf{k}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
,
n\leftrightarrow n^{\prime}
,
m\leftrightarrow m^{\prime}
, and
\mathbf{q}\to-\mathbf{q}
. This symmetry allows us to limit the summation over
n^{\prime}
to
n^{\prime}\leq n
and restrict the indices of
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
to those less than or equal to the corresponding indices of
\mathbf{k}
.

Similar symmetries hold for carrier- and phonon-phonon collision integrals. However, since these are significantly less expensive than carrier-carrier contributions, we do not apply these optimizations to them.

Crystalline symmetries can also be used to restrict computations to the irreducible wedge of the BZ. However, the presence of an external electric field may break some spatial symmetries, preventing their application to collision-integral evaluations. For this reason, we do not exploit spatial symmetries and instead compute rates explicitly for all
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{q}
points.

To enhance performance, we parallelize our implementation using the Message Passing Interface (MPI), distributing both computational workload and memory across
n
MPI tasks. A natural choice is to distribute over reciprocal-space points in the BZ. By analyzing the structure of the matrix elements defining the scattering amplitudes in Eqs. (
131
)–(
III.2
), we find that distributing memory over
\mathbf{q}
points is more efficient than that on
\mathbf{k}
points, as it reduces communication overhead. As a result, matrix elements corresponding to electron–phonon interactions, oscillator strengths, and anharmonic matrices, interpolated on a double
\mathbf{k}
–
\mathbf{q}
grid of dimension
N\times N
, are distributed among the
n
MPI tasks in
\mathbf{q}
-chunks. Thus, each process handles a fraction of matrix elements on a double-grid of dimension
N\times N/n
. This ensures that the total memory required to store matrix elements remains independent of the number of MPI tasks, improving scalability.

For rate calculations, all summations over
\mathbf{q}
are performed in chunks by each task, with the final result obtained via a synchronous MPI reduction. Conversely, summations over
\mathbf{k}
, which yield a
\mathbf{q}
-dependent quantity, are computed by each task and collected using a synchronous MPI gather. The current parallelization strategy is schematically depicted in Fig.
6
(a). Systematic improvements are possible, for instance, through multiple MPI layers or a hybrid MPI+OpenMP approach. These modifications would be straightforward to implement and represent a promising future development.

Next, we analyze how the choice of EOMs integration algorithm affects computational workload and precision. As discussed above, the primary cost arises from rate evaluations. Thus, it is crucial to develop strategies that allow for large timesteps while maintaining high accuracy. In this context, adaptive-timestep algorithms offer a favorable trade-off between efficiency and precision. To validate this, we compare the performance of the three RK schemes we implemented (see Appendix
H

We evaluate accuracy by comparing results to a reference set of occupations at 90 fs,
f^{r}_{\mathbf{k}n}
and

\epsilon_{\text{tol}}=10^{-11}
; see Appendix
H

f_{\mathbf{k}n}
and

\varepsilon_{el}=\max_{\mathbf{k},n}|f_{\mathbf{k}n}-f^{r}_{\mathbf{k}n}|,\quad\varepsilon_{ph}=\max_{\mathbf{q},\nu}|n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}-n^{r}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}|.

(141)

The overall error is defined as
\max(\varepsilon_{el},\varepsilon_{ph})

Figure 7:

Finally, we note that the stepsize, whether fixed or adaptive, is primarily determined by the equation for electronic polarization (see Eq. (
63
)). The polarization exhibits a rapidly oscillating component due to the term proportional to the difference between electronic eigenvalues. Moreover, in the presence of an oscillating electric field, the Rabi term oscillates at frequencies on the order of fractions of a petahertz. To resolve this fast oscillatory behavior, the timestep must be sufficiently small to keep the propagation error below a given threshold. Conversely, in the absence of an oscillating polarization, the timestep would be determined by the much slower timescale associated with the collision integrals. As a result, when an oscillating polarization is present, the collision integrals must be evaluated far more frequently than would otherwise be required, substantially increasing computational overhead.

This represents a typical example of a problem involving a nonstiff (slow) component, determined by the scattering integrals, and a stiff (fast) component, arising from the rapidly oscillating polarization. Methods designed to disentangle these two timescales are commonly referred to as multirate infinitesimal (MRI) methods
127
;
126
. In such frameworks, the fast component of the equation is integrated with a small timestep, while the slow component is treated through an effective equation using a much larger timestep.

This approach significantly reduces the number of collision-integral evaluations while preserving an accurate evolution of the fast oscillating polarization. MRI methods have already been applied to real-time electron and phonon dynamics in a semiclassical Boltzmann framework
161
, demonstrating clear advantages over standard RK methods. Implementing MRI methods thus represents a promising avenue for future developments.

In summary, photoexcitation rapidly produces a quasi-thermal electronic distribution via carrier–carrier scattering, while carrier–phonon collisions allow for energy transfer to a restricted set of phonon modes, generating strong nonequilibrium hot spots. On the picosecond timescale, combined carrier–phonon and phonon–phonon processes redistribute this energy, leading to lattice thermalization. The associated ionic forces exhibit a fast rise due to carrier heating and a slower relaxation governed by the electronic temperature decrease, and they coherently drive the

V
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a first-principles many-body framework to describe the coupled dynamics of photocarriers, phonons, and ions in semiconductors following ultrafast excitation. Our approach incorporates explicit light–matter coupling together with
ab initio
carrier–carrier, carrier–phonon, and phonon–phonon collision integrals, thereby treating electron and phonon interactions on the same footing in real time.

The time evolution of the density matrix is performed in a maximally localized Wannier basis, ensuring gauge-consistent evaluation of scattering integrals and enabling ultradense sampling of electron and phonon momenta. This implementation allows for direct, parameter-free comparison with realistic pump–probe experimental conditions. Furthermore, the method can be coupled to constrained density-functional theory to access light-induced structural phase transitions at longer times after the light pulse.

Key advances relative to previous approaches include: (i) the equal-footing treatment of carrier–carrier, carrier–phonon, and anharmonic phonon–phonon scattering channels; (ii) the inclusion of time-dependent electronic screening in the nonequilibrium dynamics; (iii) the incorporation of quasiparticle-energy and Rabi-frequency renormalizations in the real-time evolution; and (iv) the first-principles calculation of coherent, damped nuclear oscillations driven by displacive forces, going beyond state-of-the-art Ehrenfest dynamics in which electronic orbitals are typically kept fixed.

We showcased the capabilities and predictive power of this framework on MoS
2
and h-BN monolayers. For MoS
2
, we resolved photoinduced renormalizations of electronic and lattice properties, ultrafast carrier relaxation, hot-phonon dynamics, and displacive coherent phonon motion, highlighting the crucial role of scattering channels in setting the relevant time scales. For h-BN, we quantify photoinduced changes in the electronic, optical, and lattice responses in quasi-equilibrium after photoexcitation, demonstrating a fluence-dependent enhancement of screening and melting of excitonic features. Taken together, these results establish our approach as a versatile and quantitatively predictive platform for interpreting and guiding ultrafast experiments in semiconductors.

Kadanoff-Baym equations

We briefly introduce the electronic Kadanoff-Baym equations (KBE). The starting point is the electronic single-particle Green’s function:

G_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t,t^{\prime})=\left\langle T_{c}\left[\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}m}(t^{\prime})\right]\right\rangle,

(153)

where the creation and annihilation operators (see Sec.
II.3
) are in the Heisenberg representation and

and
t^{\prime}
lie on the Schwinger-Keldysh contour

;
141
. Here,
\langle\ \rangle
denotes a thermal average over the system’s initial state, and
T_{c}
is the time-ordering operator along the contour.

We can define different Green’s function components: the lesser and greater parts

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}nm}^{>}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i\langle\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m}(t)\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}(t^{\prime})\rangle,

(154)

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{k}nm}^{<}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\ i\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}n}(t)\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}m}(t^{\prime})\rangle,

(155)

and the retarded and advanced components

\displaystyle G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta(t-t^{\prime})\left[G^{>}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t,t^{\prime})-G^{<}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t,t^{\prime})\right],

(156)

\displaystyle G^{A}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left[G^{R}_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t^{\prime},t)\right]^{\dagger}.

(157)

The self-energy
\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}nm}(t,t^{\prime})
is defined as the irreducible part of the Green’s function
30
and defines the following Dyson equation:

G_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime})=\left[G^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}+G^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot G_{\mathbf{k}}\right](t,t^{\prime}),

(158)

where we used matrix notation for the band indices, and the "
\cdot
" indicates implicit time convolutions along the contour. The non-interacting Green’s function
G^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}
is obtained from Eq. (
153

h_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
. This Hamiltonian may include an explicit time-dependent part due to external driving, see Eq. (
26
).

The greater and lesser components of the self-energy can be extracted from the general structure of any function defined on the contour:

\displaystyle F(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta(t-t^{\prime})F^{>}(t,t^{\prime})+\theta(t^{\prime}-t)F^{<}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+F^{\delta}(t)\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(159)

where
F^{\delta}(t)
is a time-local term accounting for a possible singular part. The retarded and advanced components are then given by

\displaystyle F^{R/A}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\pm\theta(\pm t\mp t^{\prime})\left[F^{<}(t,t^{\prime})-F^{>}(t,t^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+F^{\delta}(t)\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(160)

In the presence of an explicitly time-dependent perturbation, the EOMs for the contour single-particle Green’s function coincide with the electronic Kadanoff-Baym equations

30

145

77

\begin{gathered}\Big[i\partial_{t}-h_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\Big]G_{\mathbf{k}}^{\lessgtr}(t,t^{\prime})=\Big[\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}^{R}\cdot G_{\mathbf{k}}^{\lessgtr}+\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\lessgtr}\cdot G_{\mathbf{k}}^{A}\Big](t,t^{\prime}),\end{gathered}

(161)

Following the same conventions as in Eq. (
158
), we use matrix notation for the band indices and denote time convolutions along the contour with "
\cdot
". The electronic occupations and polarizations are obtained from
G^{<}_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime})
by evaluating it at equal times, i.e., by setting
t^{\prime}=t

p_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=iG^{<}_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t),

(162)

33

The equation of motion for
p_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
can be derived from Eq. (

G^{<}

t^{\prime}=t

(163)

where we defined the quasiparticle Hamiltonian
h^{qp}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)

h^{qp}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=h_{\mathbf{k}}(t)+\Sigma^{\delta}_{\mathbf{k}}(t),

(164)

with
\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\delta}(t)

\mathcal{I}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}d\bar{t}\left[\Sigma^{>}(t,\bar{t})G^{<}(\bar{t},t)-\Sigma^{<}(t,\bar{t})G^{>}(\bar{t},t)\right].

(165)

For the exact self-energy, the singular part corresponds to the Hartree-Fock self-energy. However, depending on the chosen approximation, it may also include additional time-local contributions. In fact, the self-energy can be written exactly as

\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime})=\delta(t-t^{\prime})\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{add}}(t)+\Delta\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime}),

(166)

\Delta\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime})=\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime})-\delta(t-t^{\prime})\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{add}}(t)

\Delta\Sigma^{\lessgtr}_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime})=\Sigma^{\lessgtr}_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime}).

(167)

Therefore, we can include in the singular part of the self-energy additional static Hermitian contributions without changing the scattering integral in Eq. (
165

(168)

where we have explicitly written the band indices and denoted the equilibrium quasiparticle energies by
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{0}

\Delta\Sigma^{\delta}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=\Sigma^{\delta}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)-\Sigma^{\delta}_{\mathbf{k}}(0).

\displaystyle\Delta\Sigma_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\text{H}}(t)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k^{\prime}},n^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\widetilde{v}^{nn^{\prime}nn^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}kk^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{0})\Delta f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}n^{\prime}}(t),

(174)

the Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential and

Time-dependent RPA screening

The statically screened Coulomb interaction is time-dependent through the static RPA dielectric function (see Eq. (

\displaystyle\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{GG^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},t)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k},n,m}

\displaystyle\times\left(\rho^{\mathbf{G}}_{nm}(\mathbf{k,q})\right)^{*}\rho^{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}_{nm}(\mathbf{k,q}),

The RPA dielectric matrix then reads:

(185)

We separate the response function into equilibrium (time-independent) and nonequilibrium (time-dependent) parts:

\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{GG^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},t)=\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{GG^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},0)+\Delta\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{GG^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},t),

(186)

(188)

(189)

From Eq. (

Appendix G
Anharmonic effects on coherent phonons

We start from a model phonon Hamiltonian composed of the equilibrium BO harmonic part and the third-order anharmonic contribution:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{BO}+\hat{H}^{(3)}_{pp},

(201)

as defined in Eqs.(
12

Our goal is to derive the equation of motion for the atomic displacement in reciprocal space, see Eq.(
9

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}Q_{\nu}(t)}{dt^{2}}=

\displaystyle-\big[\omega^{0}_{\mathbf{0}\nu}\big]^{2}Q_{\nu}(t)-

(202)

68

(203)

Substituting this into Eq.(
202

II.8.3
). In the following, we derive the contribution from coherent motion to the displacement–displacement correlation due to anharmonicity.

To this end, we consider the EOMs for the displacement–displacement correlation function:

\displaystyle\langle Q_{\mathbf{q}\nu}Q_{-\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}=\dfrac{1}{2}\big[

\displaystyle\langle a_{\mathbf{q}\nu}a_{\mathbf{-q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}+\langle a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{-q}\nu}a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle+\langle a_{\mathbf{q}\nu}a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}+\langle a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{-q}\nu}a_{\mathbf{-q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}\big].

\langle\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\rangle_{t}=\delta_{\mathbf{q0}}\langle\hat{a}_{\mathbf{0}\nu}\rangle_{t}
.

Within this approximation, third-order correlations are factorized into products of second- and first-order terms, and we retain only the phonon occupation among quadratic correlations:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{-q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{-q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}=0,

(205)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{q}\nu}^{\dagger}\rangle_{t}-\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}=\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}n_{\mathbf{q}\nu}(t).

(206)

204

&-\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{N}}\sum_{\nu^{\prime\prime}}\Phi^{(3)}_{\nu\nu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime\prime}}(-\mathbf{q},+\mathbf{q},0)\\

Relation between electric field and laser fluence

We assume that the incoming electric field is linearly polarized in the
xy
plane, uniform and non-zero within a planar region of area
S
. Its time dependence is described by

\mathbf{E}(t)=\mathbf{E}_{0}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(t-t_{0})^{2}}{2\Delta t^{2}}\right]\sin(\omega t),

(219)

where
\mathbf{E}_{0}=E_{0}\bm{\xi}
, with
\bm{\xi}
a unit vector lying in the
xy
plane.

The instantaneous energy density
u_{e}(t)
stored in the electric field is

u_{e}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{0}[E(t)]^{2}.

(220)

From this expression, the infinitesimal energy
dU(t)
transmitted during a time interval
dt
through an infinitesimal surface element
dS
reads

dU(t)=\frac{1}{2}c\epsilon_{0}[E(t)]^{2}\,dS\,dt.

(221)

Assuming that the electric field is homogeneous within the region of area
S
, the laser fluence, defined as the total transmitted energy per unit area, is obtained by integrating over time:

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\frac{U}{S}=\frac{1}{2}c\epsilon_{0}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}[E(t)]^{2}\,dt

(222)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}c\epsilon_{0}\Delta tE_{0}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-x^{2}}\sin^{2}(\omega\Delta tx-\omega t_{0})\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}c\epsilon_{0}\Delta tE_{0}^{2}\left[1-e^{-(\omega\Delta t)^{2}}\cos(2\omega t_0)\right].

For typical values of
\omega
and
\Delta t
, the condition
\omega\Delta t\gg 1
holds, leading to the simplified expression

F\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}c\epsilon_{0}\Delta tE_{0}^{2}.

(223)
