Clinical outcomes and severity of laboratory-confirmed RSV compared with influenza, parainfluenza and human metapneumovirus in Australian children attending secondary care
Authors: Mohinder Sarna, Huong Le, Belaynew Wasie Taye, Kathryn Glass, Avram Levy, Peter Richmond, Hannah C Moore
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute lower respiratory infections (ALRIs) are a major contributor to the global infectious disease burden and a common cause of hospitalisation for children under 2 years. We compared clinical severity in children hospitalised with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), parainfluenza virus (PIV), human metapneumovirus (hMPV) and influenza virus (IFV).MethodsWe used a probabilistically linked population cohort born in Western Australia between 2010 and 2020 and hospitalised before the age of 2 years. Outcomes compared included length of hospital stay (LOS), admission to intensive care unit (ICU), need for respiratory support (RS), complex hospital course (RS, death, ICU admission or LOS >75th percentile), 7-day and 30-day mortality, hospital-in-the-home care, 30-day all-cause and ALRI-specific readmissions and emergency department presentations 14 days prior to hospitalisation. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes, and negative binomial regression was used for discrete count variables. Incidence rates, time to RS and time to readmissions were calculated using survival analysis techniques.ResultsThe final cohort included 210 997 hospitalised children under 24 months of age for a total of 315 769 admissions. Infants hospitalised before 6 months had the highest rates for all virus-specific hospitalisations, particularly RSV hospitalisations (50.4 per 1000 child-years (95% CI 48.7 to 52.1)). Infants <6 months had higher odds of an ICU admission (adjusted OR (aOR) 2.39, 95% CI 1.36 to 4.19) and RS (aOR 4.68, 95% CI 2.95 to 7.44) and a complex hospital course (aOR 2.69, 95% CI 2.13 to 3.42) with RSV and four times higher hazards of requiring RS earlier (adjusted HR (aHR) 4.06, 95% CI 2.59 to 6.36). An ALRI-coded 30-day readmission was recorded in 10%–24% of virus-specific hospitalisations.DiscussionYoung infants have a more severe and complex hospital course with RSV hospitalisation compared with hospitalisation with other respiratory viruses and should be prioritised for prevention measures such as the single-dose monoclonal antibody, nirsevimab.Keywords: Clinical Epidemiology, Respiratory Infection

Introduction

Acute lower respiratory infections (ALRIs) account for a large proportion of the global infectious disease burden and are a common cause of hospitalisation for children under the age of 5 years.1 The majority of ALRIs are due to respiratory viruses, most commonly respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), parainfluenza virus (PIV), human metapneumovirus (hMPV) and influenza virus (IFV), with RSV infections contributing the highest burden of hospitalisation in children under 2 years of age.2 3

While there is wide variability in the severity of the disease and most hospitalised children will have a short hospital stay, a proportion of children with ALRI have a longer and more complex hospital course, which may include admission to intensive care (ICU) or mechanical ventilation. The incidence of readmission, 7-day and 30-day mortality, admission to the ICU and ventilatory requirements following the initial hospitalisation have not been determined in Australian children for these respiratory viruses. Previous studies have examined one or more risk factors associated with the incidence of these clinical outcomes, the majority of them in other countries4,6 or in adult populations.7 8 Analyses from Australian data have been in relatively small cohorts or case series,9,11 focused solely on preterm children12 or have used International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems-10th revision-Australian Modification (ICD-10-AM) diagnostic codes to identify RSV infection.10 While there are a number of studies from other countries, health systems differ and costs are country-specific. These are important clinical outcomes that add to the cost of infection and underpin the cost-effectiveness modelling of any virus-specific intervention. Moreover, the implementation of immunisation programmes is country specific, and there is, therefore, a need for country-specific epidemiological and clinical data.

Identifying patients most at risk of a complex hospital course in a timely fashion may also provide guidance to clinicians regarding admission, level of care and appropriate length of stay in the hospital. With the implementation of monoclonal antibody nirsevimab programmes in the USA, UK, Europe and Australia, identifying children with a more complex and severe hospital course is essential to prioritise populations and inform targeted vaccination strategies. Our aim was to compare the age-specific hospitalisation burden and clinical outcomes of laboratory-confirmed RSV in Western Australian (WA) children attending secondary care with those of PIV, hMPV and IFV. Our hypothesis was that clinical outcomes and care requirements of hospitalisation with RSV are higher than those of other respiratory viruses of interest.

Methods

Setting and study design

WA covers the western third of Australia with a population of approximately 2.3 million residents, of which Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander people comprise 6.4% of the population. We conducted a whole-of-population-based cohort study using probabilistically linked administrative data in the Respiratory Infections Linked Data Platform13 of all births in WA between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2020 with follow-up to 31 March 2022.

Data sources

Data sources pertinent to this study include the Midwives Notifications System, comprising state-wide perinatal information on >99% of births in WA,14 birth and death registries, the Hospital Morbidity Data Collection, the Emergency Department (ED) Data Collection and routine respiratory pathogen testing from the state’s sole public pathology provider, PathWest (online supplemental figure 1). Data were probabilistically linked using best practice protocols by Data Services at the WA Department of Health.15

Hospital admission and ED presentation records with an admission/presentation and discharge date between 1 January 2010 and 31 March 2022 were included to match the same time period as routine laboratory data were available (specimen collection date between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2021). We included all admissions in children aged <2 years with any diagnosis using ICD-10-AM diagnosis codes. As per previous analyses, interhospital transfers were collapsed into a single episode.16 17 From here, we refer to hospital admissions and ED presentations as secondary care episodes.

Positive and negative RSV, PIV, hMPV and IFV results from PCR testing were included.18 For this analysis, we grouped subtypes of the four virus types together. In previous analyses, our team has documented a broad range and sometimes non-specific ICD-10-AM codes assigned to discharge diagnoses of hospitalisations with respiratory viral infections and often found with laboratory-confirmed hospitalisations associated with these four viruses.19,21 In light of our previous work, we have opted in this analysis to temporally link laboratory testing records with secondary care episodes of children with a specimen collection date within 4 days of hospital admission or ED presentation.13

Outcome measures and covariates

We defined our analytical cohort as children who were admitted with a laboratory-confirmed RSV, PIV, hMPV or IFV hospitalisation episode from birth up to 2 years of age. We estimated the overall rate in those hospitalised at least once of the first virus-specific hospitalisation. Children who were admitted twice with the same virus but at different ages were included, with a minimum 30-day time period between admissions, after which the second admission was considered a new virus-specific episode and unrelated to the index admission. If a child before age 2 years had an RSV-confirmed hospitalisation at one age and a subsequent IFV-confirmed hospitalisation at another age, both hospitalisations were included. Our primary clinical outcomes associated with each virus-specific admission were median length of hospital stay (LOS) in days, admission to ICU, median LOS in ICU in hours, the need for respiratory support, requirement for non-admitted acute care in the patient’s home (hospital-in-the-home (HITH) care), median length of home care in days, 7-day and 30-day mortality, all-cause and ALRI-specific readmissions and the number of ED presentations prior to hospitalisation within a 14-day period. Readmissions were defined as admissions postdischarge of the virus-specific episode within a 30-day period. An ED presentation that resulted in an admission was counted as a hospital admission. A complex hospital course was defined as LOS >75th percentile of total LOS, admission to ICU, death during an admission or requirement for continuous respiratory support (CVS)/invasive ventilation, similar to that described in Tang et al.4 The need for respiratory support was presented as CVS or any respiratory support, the latter of which included invasive and non-invasive respiratory support, based on Australian Classification of Health Interventions procedure codes, 12th edition (online supplemental text 1, table 1). Respiratory support was assessed as both a binary variable (ie, was respiratory support required? (yes/no)) and also as a discrete count variable representing the number of days of respiratory support for those children who required it. The duration of respiratory support was calculated from the time of admission to the earliest date ventilatory support was begun. Time to the first ALRI readmission was counted in days from the day of discharge of the ‘index’ hospitalisation to the day of subsequent admission within 30 days. An ALRI readmission was identified using ICD-10-AM codes in principal and 20 additional diagnoses (online supplemental table 1). The total time for outcomes such as hours in the ICU, hours of ventilatory support and total LOS was calculated prior to collapsing transfers. All analyses were stratified by age at the time of admission.

We included sociodemographic and child risk factors based on published literature and data availability.1321,24 These included the index of socioeconomic disadvantage (quintiles)25 and the remoteness index (major city, inner regional, outer regional, remote and very remote).26 Child variables included age at admission (<6 months, 6–11 months and 12–23 months), Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander status, derived using a validated algorithm,27 gender, gestational age (<28 weeks, 28–31 weeks, 32–36 weeks and ≥37 weeks) and seasons of admission (Spring: September–November; Summer: December–February; Autumn: March–May and Winter: June–August).

Exclusions

We excluded all stillbirths, births with gestational age missing or <20 weeks, birthweight <400 g or missing and any missing data on covariates of interest to conduct a complete case analysis. We also removed hospital admissions for injuries or trauma (ICD-10-AM S and T codes). Finally, our analysis was focused on single infections, so all hospitalisations with co-detections were excluded.

Statistical analysis

As our outcomes of interest were focused on hospitalised children, we calculated virus-specific rates per 1000 hospitalised child years at risk (rather than per population) stratified by age group at admission using survival analysis techniques. Time-at-risk for all hospitalised children was calculated from the date of birth to the earliest of either age at admission, death or end of the study period. Logistic regression models were developed to assess the association between virus-specific hospitalisations and individual dichotomous categorical clinical outcomes, adjusted for gestational age at birth, gender, age at admission, Aboriginal status, remoteness of residence and socioeconomic status. Effect estimates were presented as adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% CI.

Hospital LOS, LOS in ICU, length of respiratory support and length of HITH care were evaluated as continuous variables and presented as median and IQR (25% and 75th percentile, IQR). Adjusted incidence rate ratios (aIRRs) were calculated using negative binomial regression. In calculating days of respiratory support, 1 day was added to the time if respiratory support was started and stopped on the date of admission. The number of ED visits before admission and all-cause and ALRI readmissions postdischarge were presented as the number of children and visits. Time to ALRI readmission and time to respiratory support were analysed as time-to-event variables using survival analysis techniques, plotted as cumulative hazard functions, and adjusted HRs (aHRs) calculated, with hospitalisations with PIV as the reference category. Time to respiratory support was censored at 15 days in order to count events related to the initial RSV infection and exclude time contributed from possible secondary infections. All analyses were performed using STATA software, V.17.

Patient and public involvement

The RSV+Community Reference Group at the Kids Research Institute Australia is a group of 10 mothers, including an Aboriginal Health Liaison Officer, whose children have lived experience of RSV. The group was set up in 2021 and meet quarterly, with the purpose of providing a voice in the design and direction of our projects. The forum is also a way of keeping members up to date on the progress of projects.

Results

Cohort description and virus-specific incidence of hospitalisation

The birth cohort comprised 368 830 children born between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2020, from which 2520 (0.7%) stillbirths, 46 (0.01%) children with infant weight <400 g or missing, 23 (0.01%) children with missing gestational age and 649 (0.2%) children with missing data on socioeconomic indexes for areas (SEIFA) and/or the Accessibility and Remoteness Index for Australia (ARIA) were excluded. Our final birth cohort comprised 365 592 children (online supplemental figure 1). The outcome data comprised 1 838 346 records of ED presentations and hospital admissions merged with PathWest laboratory testing data to identify virus-positive children. From these data, we excluded 41 790 ED records (3.3% of all ED presentations) outside of the study period and 37 540 (6.7%) injury and trauma admissions. A further 1495 (0.09%) admissions were removed because of co-detections. Our final analytical cohort consisted of 210 997 children under 24 months of age who were hospitalised at least once for a total of 315 769 admissions during the study period. Overall, 7.7% of the cohort of hospitalised children under 24 months were Aboriginal and 14.4% were born preterm (<37 weeks gestation) (table 1). Of admitted children, 25 893 (8.2%) admissions had a laboratory test for the four viruses of interest; 8804 (98.9%) were positive (RSV: n=6118; PIV: n=1183; IFV: n=575 and hMPV: n=928).

There were 6110 first-time hospitalisations for RSV in children with any hospitalisation under 24 months of age, resulting in an RSV-specific hospitalisation rate of 26 per 1000 child-years (95% CI 25.4 to 26.7; table 2). This was considerably higher than the hospitalisation rate for PIV (5 per 1000 child-years, 95% CI 4.8 to 5.3), hMPV (3.9 per 1000 child-years, 95% CI 3.7 to 4.2) and IFV (2.5 per 1000 child-years, 95% CI 2.3 to 2.7). Infants hospitalised <6 months had the highest incidence rates for all virus-specific hospitalisations, and the rate was considerably higher for RSV compared with the other viruses (table 2). The median age at first virus-specific admission was 4.9 months (IQR 2.0–12.3) for RSV, 9.6 months (IQR 4.1–15.1) for PIV, 9 months (IQR 4.7–15.1) for hMPV and 9.2 months (IQR 4.6–15.4) for IFV. When comparing selected demographic characteristics between virus-specific hospitalisations, extremely preterm children, Aboriginal children and children in regional locations had higher odds of an RSV hospitalisation compared with a PIV or hMPV hospitalisation (online supplemental table 2). There were also differences between seasons of admission, with PIV, hMPV and IFV hospitalisations more likely in spring compared with RSV hospitalisations (online supplemental figure 2). Few children died during an admission (n=8). There were no deaths 7 days postdischarge and 1 death 30 days postdischarge.

Evaluation of severity markers

For infants admitted in their first 6 months of life, clinical outcomes for RSV-hospitalisations were by far the greatest in number (7.6%, n=258 admitted to ICU; 17.9%, n=608 required any form of ventilatory support, 1.9%, n=65 had CVS, 48.4%, n=1648 of infants met the criteria for a more complex hospital course (table 3). A small proportion (n=12, 0.4%) of infants hospitalised <6 months with RSV had follow-up HITH care postdischarge (table 3). Infants hospitalised <6 months had higher odds of ICU admission (adjusted OR (aOR) 2.39, 95% CI 1.36 to 4.19) with RSV compared with hospitalisation with PIV. Respiratory support was highest for hospitalisations with RSV (aOR 4.68, 95% CI 2.95 to 7.44] and IFV (aOR 5.76, 95% CI 2.66 to 12.46). Compared with PIV, infants hospitalised <6 months with RSV (aOR 2.69, 95% CI 2.13 to 3.42), hMPV (aOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.33 to 2.23) or IFV (aOR 2.83, 95% CI 2.01 to 4.00]) had higher odds of a complex hospital course (table 3). We were unable to compare the need for CVS and HITH care between hospitalisation episodes for all viruses as there were few infants needing CVS or home care if hospitalised with PIV, hMPV or IFV.

In contrast to young infants, ICU admissions and the need for CVS were more prevalent with hospitalisations with PIV and hMPV in older children admitted 12–23 months compared with RSV hospitalisations (table 3). The median LOS was similar for all four viruses in each age group and approximated 2 days (online supplemental table 3). Infants hospitalised <6 months with a diagnosis of RSV had 1.3 times longer hospital LOS (aIRR 1.31, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.42) compared with a diagnosis of PIV.

All-cause readmissions postdischarge were highest for hospitalisations with PIV in all age groups and readmissions following an influenza hospitalisation in older age groups >6 months (table 4). ALRI-coded readmissions following the initial hospitalisation were more prevalent following hospitalisation with RSV in infants hospitalised at ages 6–11 months (aHR 2.74, 95% CI 1.39 to 5.43) (online supplemental figure 3 and table 4). ED presentations up to 14 days prior to admission were relatively common and comprised 30%–48% of presentations associated with a virus-specific admission (online supplemental table 5).

Infants admitted <6 months of age had four times higher hazards and required RS earlier when hospitalised with RSV (aHR 4.06, 95% CI 2.59 to 6.36) and twice higher hazards when hospitalised with hMPV (aHR 2.43, 95% CI 1.39 to 4.25, figure 1, table 5) when compared with the time to respiratory support when hospitalised with PIV. We observed a levelling off between 5 and 10 days followed by a second increase in the cumulative hazard for infants who required respiratory support 10 days postadmission. Discussion

Comparison of clinical outcomes of RSV hospitalisation to that of three other common viruses affecting young children showed that in addition to a younger age at hospitalisation for RSV compared with PIV, hMPV and IFV, RSV hospitalisations were also associated with higher ICU attendance, greater use of respiratory support and a more complex hospital course, particularly in children hospitalised <6 months. Beyond the infancy period, older children hospitalised with PIV, hMPV and IFV infections were more likely to have a severe course than older children with RSV, as well as infants hospitalised <6 months old with the same infections. The median LOS was similar across all four viruses. HITH follow-up care was relatively uncommon and most often administered to infants <6 months of age hospitalised for an RSV infection. All-cause readmissions were common, as were readmissions for ALRI.

Our results support prior research4 28 that shows that RSV hospitalisations can cause more severe disease in infants compared with other respiratory viruses. While we did not have information on clinical signs and symptoms associated with each virus-specific hospitalisation, our findings correlate with the known propensity of RSV for causing obstruction of distal airways.29 30 RSV is mostly associated with the clinical presentation of bronchiolitis, with young children at greater risk of severe disease.29 Early-life RSV-ALRI impairs lung growth and development and is also associated with lower spirometric lung function in later life.31 Our results show infants hospitalised with RSV are more likely to require ventilatory support and require it earlier. The levelling off between days 5 and 10 in <6-month-old children followed by a second hump could possibly reflect different populations of children, with the first increase being children requiring respiratory support related to the initial RSV infection, and the second increase in cumulative hazard 10 days postadmission being children requiring respiratory support because of possible secondary infections. In contrast, influenza infection is more often associated with acute respiratory distress syndrome, which is characterised by alveolar damage, although some ICU admissions with influenza are for non-respiratory complications such as seizures or encephalopathy. The need for respiratory support was higher for PIV and hMPV in children at older ages, further highlighting not only the severity of RSV in infancy but also the importance of prevention measures for other respiratory viruses that are not yet on the vaccine prevention pathway. Lower morbidity for hospitalisations due to influenza infection could also be partly attributed to influenza vaccination in children older than 6 months through a nationally funded universal influenza vaccination programme, although vaccine uptake in recent years in Western Australian children has been suboptimal at 21.5% for children aged 6 months–5 years.32

Readmissions following the initial virus-specific hospitalisation in our cohort were relatively common and showed that children continue to be impacted after discharge following the initial hospitalisation. High readmission rates have been shown in other studies; several of these analyses conducted in preterm children12 33 34 or children with underlying medical comorbidities.35 A 1-year prospective study of 297 children <5 years of age with RSV-ALRI readmissions showed respiratory readmission after the initial RSV hospitalisation was common (estimated to be 13.1 per 100 patients (95% CI 9.5 to 17.5) and that 25% of children who were readmitted had an underlying chronic medical condition.36 Our study also showed that children who were admitted for the virus-specific hospitalisations under study were significant users of emergency services prior to admission.

Quantifying the burden of disease and the full economic cost of these diseases, including the complexity of the initial hospitalisation and frequency of subsequent readmissions, is essential to guide decision-making around efforts at implementing or designing prevention measures. Influenza remains the only respiratory virus with a nationally funded universal vaccination programme. A single-dose monoclonal antibody, nirsevimab, providing passive immunisation against RSV, and a maternal RSV vaccine, have completed phase III trials and are now approved in several countries, including the USA,37 38 UK,39 Europe40 and Australia.41 Nirsevimab has been rolled out for the European and US winter (December 2023–February 2024), and initial results from the NIRSE-GAL study show it is 87% effective against severe RSV-related lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) requiring respiratory support and 90% against RSV-related LRTI hospitalisations.42 WA commenced a universal funded programme for the administration of nirsevimab in April 2024 for the Southern winter. Programmes targeted at high-risk populations have also commenced in Queensland and New South Wales. Finally, efforts at vaccine development for PIV and hMPV are at various phases of clinical trials.43 44 While RSV causes up to 80% of bronchiolitis in infants, in immunocompromised children, the landscape is much more heterogeneous, with other respiratory viruses contributing to the burden of respiratory disease. In future work, we are exploring specific risk factors for admission with RSV, work that we hope will inform decision-making and vaccine policy development to target children most in need.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has many strengths, including the use of 10-year statewide population data on hospitalisations, ED presentations and pathology testing results to confirm virus-specific infection, rather than the reliance on ICD-10-AM coding, which is known to underestimate the burden of virus-specific infection. These data were linked with birth cohort data, which included sociodemographic and perinatal factors. Importantly, our study includes all children born during this time period, in contrast to some previous studies that have focused on specific subgroups such as preterm children12 45 or children admitted to the paediatric ICU.5

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. We were missing data on the date of death for some children and have calculated a proxy date of death for children with missing dates of death using the death registration year, although it should be acknowledged that mortality from RSV in WA is rare. Additionally, we did not have information on influenza vaccination status for children older than 6 months of age. Our study included the early years of the pandemic (2020 and 2021), and rates of respiratory virus hospitalisations were lower in the second half of 2020/early 2021 as a result of border closures and non-pharmaceutical interventions. We also know that not all respiratory infection-coded admissions are tested for viruses, so our sample size was limited to those who underwent laboratory tests. Our study also did not capture those tested in the community by private pathology providers prior to or following hospital presentation. Furthermore, testing practices vary according to the age of the child, geographical location, season of admission and the presence of comorbid conditions. However, we do not believe that these variable testing rates (potentially under-reporting of virus-specific hospitalisations), and decreases in the incidence rate during the pandemic would significantly impact our findings comparing the clinical outcomes and admission-related factors between these four viruses. We did not distinguish between the different forms of ventilatory support (invasive vs non-invasive). We were also missing the date of ventilatory support for 11.5% of children under the age of 2 years. Finally, we only examined single infections in our analysis and excluded detections of more than one virus or viral-bacterial co-detections.

Conclusions

Our study confirms the high burden of RSV in hospitalised children. For children under the age of 6 months, the trajectory of their hospital course is associated with a higher risk of an ICU admission, respiratory support and a more complex hospital course than for hospitalisations with other viruses under study. Given the propensity to have more severe disease, young infants should be prioritised for RSV prevention measures, particularly to provide protection for infants admitted in the winter months when hospitalisations are most common. Additionally, prevention measures for PIV and hMPV should be explored, especially beyond the infant period.
