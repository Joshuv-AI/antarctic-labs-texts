HLA-DQB1-mediated B cell-epithelial crosstalk drives EBV-associated inflammation in Hunner-type interstitial cystitis
Authors: Shaik Ismail Mohammed Thangameeran, Jia-Fong Jhang, Yuan-Hong Jiang, Hann-Chorng Kuo, Chih-Wen Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHunner-type interstitial cystitis (HIC) is a chronic condition marked by persistent pain and inflammation. To elucidate its immunogenetic drivers, we integrated bulk RNA sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets with targeted major histocompatibility complex (MHC) sequencing. Transcriptomic analysis revealed selective expansion of B cells and epithelial cells, with strong enrichment of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) response signatures. CellChat and NicheNet modeling uncovered bidirectional communication wherein B cells secrete IL-1β, FGF2, LIF, and TNFSF9, activating prostaglandin synthesis, matrix metalloproteinases, and stress genes in epithelial cells. In turn, epithelial BMP4, TGF-β2, and SHH modulate B cell survival. SCENIC regulatory network analysis identified IRF8 as the top B cell regulator; its regulon controls HLA-DQB1, CD40, and CIITA, linking EBV latency to heightened antigen presentation. Among differentially expressed genes, HLA-DQB1 was the most strongly induced in EBV+ HIC (∼1,000-fold), emerged as the most frequently mutated gene in targeted MHC sequencing, and ranked as a high-confidence IRF8 target. Notably, the evolutionarily constrained variant rs1049133 (A>G) lies within a low-entropy HLA-DQB1 domain, underscoring functional importance. Our integrated analysis supports a model where IRF8-driven, EBV-infected B cells perpetuate HIC via variant HLA-DQB1-mediated antigen presentation and epithelial cytokine loops, highlighting a tractable axis for precision therapy.Keywords: MT: Bioinformatics, Epstein-Barr virus, EBV, HLA-DQB1, Hunner-type interstitial cystitis, B cell-epithelial crosstalk

Introduction

Interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) is a chronic bladder condition causing persistent pain and discomfort in the bladder. It is commonly associated with urinary frequency and urgency that significantly impacts the quality of life of the affected individuals. IC/BPS is classified into two subtypes: Hunner-type IC (HIC), affecting 5%–10% of patients, which is characterized by the presence of Hunner’s lesions (distinct, inflamed patches or ulcers on the bladder wall), and non-Hunner IC (NHIC), accounting for approximately 90% of the IC/BPS patients and characterized by bladder wall glomerulations. HIC is generally more prevalent in older individuals and predominantly affects females. Histologically, HIC is marked by chronic inflammation, epithelial denudation, and expansion of lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, including a notable presence of clonal B cells, which may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of the disease.1

Although various studies have explored the underlying mechanisms of HIC, its etiology remains poorly understood, and effective treatment options are limited. Our recent works have highlighted the involvement of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) as a potential etiological factor observed in HIC.2,3 EBV is a highly pervasive gammaherpesvirus, infecting over 95% of the global population and establishing lifelong persistence primarily within B cells. EBV is notable for its ability to induce both latent and lytic infection cycles, which allow it to evade immune responses to persist within host cells. EBV is etiologically linked to various malignancies, including Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, and certain gastric cancers. The virus expresses distinct latency-associated genes in different cell types, adapting to B cells, epithelial cells, and T/natural killer (NK) cells, and contributes to tumor formation through multiple mechanisms, including immune modulation and promotion of cell survival and proliferation.4 Given the capacity of EBV to transform B cells and modulate immune responses, it is plausible that EBV infection may directly influence the inflammatory milieu of HIC by altering the behavior of B cells and their interactions with other cell types, particularly bladder epithelial cells.

In the context of HIC, EBV infection appears to contribute to chronic inflammation within the bladder by activating inflammatory pathways and producing inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), commonly seen in HIC bladder tissues.3 Additionally, EBV-infected B cells in HIC have been found to induce BDNF, a factor linked to nerve hyperplasia and bladder pain. These findings suggest that EBV may play a crucial role in promoting the persistent bladder inflammation and nerve-related symptoms that characterize HIC.2 While the potential role of EBV in driving chronic inflammation and nerve hyperplasia in HIC is compelling, understanding the interplay between various cellular populations within the bladder microenvironment is crucial for understanding the complex pathogenesis of this condition. In particular, the dynamic interactions between EBV-infected B cells and bladder epithelial cells require detailed exploration, as these interactions may underpin the inflammatory milieu characteristic of HIC.

To address the gap in understanding the immune dysregulation in HIC, we integrated our previously used bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset (BioProject: PRJNA813893) and publicly available single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) on HIC (GEO: GSE175526) to understand the transcriptional changes in the HIC microenvironment. In addition to that, a very recent genome-wide association study (GWAS) identified a significant association between histocompatibility leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II alleles HLA-DQB1 and HLA-DPB1 variants and IC/BPS in Japanese patients, providing strong evidence for the immunogenetic contribution to disease risk.5 These findings guided our focus on targeted sequencing of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) locus in a Taiwanese HIC cohort to evaluate whether similar patterns of variation could be observed. We combined transcriptional and genomic data to explore cellular heterogeneity and intracellular communications within the HIC microenvironment.

While a previous study on this particular scRNA-seq dataset extensively characterized the immune landscape in HIC and NHIC, the interactions between B cells and structural cells, such as epithelial cells, remain largely unexplored.6 This is particularly relevant as B cells, influenced by EBV—a B cell-transforming virus that establishes lifelong latency within host B cells—could play a critical role in shaping the HIC microenvironment.7 Although the scRNA-seq data (GEO: GSE175526) does not differentiate EBV+ patients, our bulk RNA-seq data identify a higher subset of HIC cases potentially associated with EBV infection. These complementary datasets allowed us to hypothesize that EBV-associated mechanisms may drive B cell-mediated immune dysregulation in HIC.

Results

Upregulation of EBV-associated genes in pseudobulk analysis with enriched B cell and epithelial cell proportions in HIC scRNA-seq data

The available scRNA-seq dataset was first pooled together into their corresponding groups, and the HIC group (n = 2) was given primary importance. This aggregation enabled pseudobulk analysis, which provides a robust framework to detect group-level transcriptional differences while mitigating the noise inherent in single-cell data. Pseudobulk analysis highlighted the significant upregulation of EBV-associated genes in the HIC samples compared with the control samples. Key genes, including AKT2, CHUK, E2F3, HLA-C, HLA-DQA2, MAP3K7, MAP2K7, NFKB1, NFKB2, PIK3CD, PSMD4, RBPJ, RELA, STAT3, TAB2, and TRAF3, exhibited changes in expression levels, with log2 fold changes (FC) ranging from −2 to 2. Notably, the upregulation of CHUK, STAT3, and NFKB1 in HIC samples underscore their potential role in driving immune dysregulation8,9 (Figure 1A). Furthermore, pathway enrichment analysis of the top 20 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways enriched in B cells with pseudobulk analysis10 of the scRNA-seq data revealed many similar enriched pathways as observed in bulk RNA-seq data (Figure 1B), including viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and chemokine signaling pathway. The KEGG pathway visualization highlights the molecular interactions and signaling cascades activated during EBV infection, and the KEGG map reveals EBV-associated genes in the HIC samples, except for a few genes suggesting the role of EBV-associated mechanisms in shaping the molecular and cellular environment of HIC (Figure 1C).

Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was performed for the scRNA-seq dataset to understand the overview of cellular heterogeneity across conditions (Figures 1D–1F). Upon the dimensionality reduction, the cell-type proportion was calculated, in which we found an enriched proportion of B cells (12.7%) and epithelial cells (16.3%) in the HIC group when compared to the control group (B cells, 11.4%; epithelial cells, 9.8%). This suggests an immune-dysregulated microenvironment in the HIC group (Figure 1G).11

Bulk RNA-seq data highlights distinct immune dysregulation beyond EBV

RNA-seq analyses provided a comprehensive understanding of the molecular landscape of HIC (n = 12, HIC; n = 10, control), highlighting distinct transcriptional profiles between HIC and control samples. Principal-component analysis (PCA) of bulk RNA-seq data revealed clear transcriptomic differences between the HIC and the control samples, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 42% and 14% of the variance, respectively. HIC samples clustered distinctly from control samples, reflecting substantial differences in global gene expression profiles (Figure 2A).

We then compared HIC samples that tested positive for EBV (HIC/EBV+) (n = 9) with those that tested negative (HIC/EBV−) (n = 3) to understand their gene activity. The top 20 upregulated genes include HLA-DQB1 and HLA-DQA1 as upregulated genes in HIC/EBV+ patients. These two genes belong to MHC class II and are involved in antigen presentation, suggesting immunostimulatory activity.12 HLA-DQB1 was highly upregulated in HIC/EBV+ compared to HIC/EBV−, with a fold change of ∼1,000×. While the p value did not reach statistical significance due to high variability, the magnitude of upregulation suggests a potential biological role. Previous studies have demonstrated the role of HLA-DQB1 and other HLA class II genes in EBV-related immune response. Notably, HLA-DQB1 serves as a co-receptor for EBV gp42-mediated entry into B cells, emphasizing the functional interlink between EBV and the HLA-DQ locus in infection susceptibility and immune modulation.13 Additionally, SPRR2A, S100A7A, and TMPRSS11D were upregulated (Figure 2B). The upregulation of these three genes is linked to epithelial stress, inflammatory cytokine production, and inflammatory cell recruitment.14,15,16

To further investigate the immune landscape in HIC, we performed immune cell deconvolution of the RNA-seq data using CIBERSORTx for the RNA-seq data to understand the immune cell proportions between HIC (n = 14) and control samples (n = 11). Memory B cells (EBV reservoir) were significantly enriched in HIC samples alongside other immune populations such as naive B cells, CD4+ memory T cells, monocytes, and M1 macrophages, which exhibited distinct distribution patterns (Figures 2C and 2D).

Pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) highlighted immune-related and inflammatory pathways as central features of HIC. The enrichment of the EBV pathway suggests that viral proteins may interact with host cytokines and immune receptors, amplifying inflammatory signals and contributing to the chronic immune activation observed in HIC (Figure 2E).17

CellChat identifies CD74-APP as a key interaction and highlights bidirectional B cell-epithelial cell communication in the HIC microenvironment

Since B cells and epithelial cells are significantly enriched in HIC, and EBV-associated mechanisms are implicated in shaping the disease pathology, it is crucial to investigate the communication between B cells and epithelial cells in the HIC microenvironment. CellChat was used to study intracellular communication.18 The normalized interaction weights revealed significantly higher interaction weights for T cells, tissue stem cells, epithelial cells, B cells, NK cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells (Figures 3A and 3B). Further examining the immune-epithelial interactions, the analysis showed robust two-way communication between immune cells, such as B cells, T cells, NK cells, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and neutrophils. This elucidates that epithelial cells not only receive signals from immune cells but are also actively involved in sending signals back to the immune cells, ensuring the bidirectional immune-epithelial crosstalk within the HIC microenvironment (Figure 3C). To understand the interconnection of B cells and epithelial cells with other cell types, we analyzed the global interaction network that highlighted the significantly higher interaction of B cells and epithelial cells with endothelial cells, underscoring their roles in modulating vascular and immune responses in HIC19 (Figures 3D and 3E).

CellChat also provided us with the ligand-receptor interaction network among different cell types. The CD74-APP (amyloid precursor protein) pair appeared to be the top-ranked interaction based on normalized contributions, underscoring its central role in the HIC microenvironment (Figure 3F). CD74, a receptor primarily involved in antigen presentation and immune activation, is expressed in B cells, DCs, and macrophages.20,21 Our CellChat analysis showed a predominant expression of CD74 in B cells, while its ligand, APP, is highly expressed in epithelial cells. This interaction highlights a critical axis of immune-epithelial communication, linking antigen presentation pathways in B cells to inflammatory signaling cascades in epithelial cells (Figure 3G). The communication network of the top three ligands, APP, IL-1, and CXCL, is provided in Figures S2A–S2C. The APP signaling network plot demonstrates a multitude of signaling among all the cell types; of these, four major structural cells (endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, epithelial cells, and fibroblasts) are demonstrated to send signals compared to receiving signals from the other cell types.

In addition to the immune-to-epithelial signaling, our analysis revealed the possibility of reverse communication from epithelial cells to B cells, showing different possible communication links involving COL1A2, AREG, COL9A3, APP, COL1A1, NAMPT, COL6A2, SDC4, EGFR, CD74, SDC1, and INSR, underscoring the active role of epithelial cells in modulating B cell activity (Figure S2D).22

IL-1B and LIF drive B cell-to-epithelial crosstalk, while BMP4 and TGFB2 mediate epithelial-to-B cell signaling in HIC

To explore the intercellular communication dynamics within the HIC microenvironment, we integrated CellChat and NicheNet analyses.23 While CellChat provided a global overview of cell-cell communication networks, identifying key pathways such as the CCL and IL signaling, NicheNet enabled us to gain more insights into the downstream effects of these interactions, since NicheNet has the leverage to fix the sender and receiver cells. With our initial analysis of setting sender cells as B cells and receiver cells as epithelial cells, we identified the top 20 upstream ligands with significant expression (p < 0.01, log2FC > 0.25) across all cell types, providing an overview of the most prominent signaling candidates (Figure S3A). In B cells, LIF, TNFSF9, IL-1B, and PRRG4 exhibited relatively higher expression levels, suggesting their prominent functions in immunomodulation and inflammation (Figure 4A). To investigate the top 20 upstream ligands in epithelial cells, we refined the criteria of sender cells as epithelial cells and receiver cells as B cells. Upon which the top 20 significant upstream ligands were identified and in epithelial cells with BMP4, SHH, TGFB2, and CCL2 showing higher expression levels indicating their potential role in mediating inflammatory and stress response in the HIC microenvironment (Figure 4B).

Focusing on B cell-to-epithelial cell signaling, we prioritized ligands derived from B cells and mapped their corresponding receptor counterparts expressed on epithelial cells. A detailed heatmap visualization of the ligand-receptor pairs revealed notable interactions involving the highly expressed B cell-specific ligands (Figure 4C). Notably, signaling through LIF-LIFR, TNFSF9-TNFRSF9, IL-1R2, and IL-1R1 was prominent. ADRB2 and PRRG4 interacting with CADM4 emerged as key mediators of epithelial responses. Additional interactions included FGF2 with receptors such as FGFR1, NRP1, and SDC4, along with SDC3 and SDC1. ANGPTL4 showed strong associations with SDC4, SDC3, SDC1, and SDC2, as well as with receptors including LPL, CDH11, CDH5, ITGA5, and TIE1. These interactions suggest a multifaceted regulatory network, emphasizing the critical role of B cells in modulating epithelial cell function within the HIC microenvironment.

To further understand the downstream effects of these ligand-receptor interactions, we identified target genes in epithelial cells that are predicted to be regulated by B cell-derived ligands (Figure S3B). IL-1B exhibited broad regulatory effects as seen in CellChat with numerous target genes, including PTGS2, a key mediator of inflammation; BMP2, which is involved in epithelial repair and fibrosis; and GBP1, known for its role in host defense and immunoregulation. Additionally, matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP-1), a gene critical for extracellular matrix remodeling, and PTX3, a key component of the innate immune response, were also identified as downstream targets. Other significant genes regulated by IL-1B include TNFRSF11B, associated with osteoprotective signaling, and EGR2, a transcription factor (TF) involved in stress and differentiation responses. Similarly, FGF2 emerged as another key ligand with extensive regulatory influence, targeting genes such as EGR1, which plays a pivotal role in cellular stress responses; PTGS2, further reinforcing its involvement in inflammatory signaling; and HES1, a regulator of epithelial homeostasis and differentiation (Figure 4D).

We extended our NicheNet analysis in the context of MHC class II molecules as HLA-DQB1 and HLA-DQA1 were upregulated in EBV+ HIC samples (Figure 2B). While MHC class II components do not function as direct ligands in the classical sense, their presentation of antigens can activate CD4+ T cells, which then secrete cytokines and growth factors that drive epithelial responses. Intriguingly, the predicted target genes for HLA-DQB1 include IL1B and TNF (Figure 4E), suggesting a feed-forward loop in which antigen presentation by B cells (via HLA-DQB1) ultimately promotes elevated cytokine production in epithelial cells. These findings not only highlight IL-1B and FGF2 as central mediators of epithelial inflammation and remodeling but they also underscore the potential of HLA-DQB1-driven antigen presentation by B cells to shape epithelial cell cytokine profiles (e.g., IL1B, TNF).

SCENIC expressed ERG and SOX17 as top regulators, SPIB and IRF8 in B cells, and FOSL1 and IRF6 in epithelial cells

Given the pseudobulk analysis findings highlighting the significant upregulation of EBV-associated genes (Figure 1A) coupled with the enrichment of B cells and epithelial cells (Figure 1G), we streamlined our analysis to explore the key transcriptional regulators and gene networks in B cells and epithelial cells that might be influenced by EBV. We employed SCENIC (single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering),24 a computational framework designed specifically to reconstruct gene regulatory networks (GRNs) to study the transcriptional regulations in B cells and epithelial cells in particular. The boxplots represent the number of cells per regulon and regulons per cell, emphasizing the distribution of active regulons across cells and ensuring increased transcriptional complexity within the HIC microenvironment (Figure 5A).

Upon filtering with the relative activity score, ERG and SOX17 emerged as the top regulons corresponding to the endothelial cells with a relative activity score of 4.76 and 4.5, respectively. SPIB and IRF8 exhibit enriched activity in B cells (relative activity score 2.02 and 1.79), suggesting their pivotal role in B cell activation. Conversely, FOSL1 and IRF6 emerged as the key regulators in the epithelial cells (relative activity score 2.23 and 2.12), which might link the epithelial response in the HIC microenvironment (Figures 5B and 5C). A list of all the top regulators with the cell type and relative activity score is given in Table S1. Scatterplots depicting cell-type-specific regulators based on regulon specificity score (RSS) in B cells ranked SPIB and IRF8 as top TFs, further underscoring their pivotal roles in B cell activation and immunoregulation (Figure 5D). The SCENIC analysis revealed HLA-DQB1 as one of the significant high-confidence targets of IRF8 with a net enrichment score of 3.44, supported by motif enrichment analysis, aligning with its critical role in the MHC class II complex. In addition to HLA-DQB1, IRF8 was found to regulate other key genes such as CD40, a co-stimulatory molecule critical for B cell-T cell interactions and cytokine production. To further validate IRF8 as a direct transcriptional regulator of MHC class II-associated genes, we explored publicly available IRF8 chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data and functional regulatory annotations. Notably, ChIP-seq data from THP-1 monocytes (GEO: GSE123872) demonstrate that IRF8 binds proximal to the CIITA promoter, consistent with its known role in driving MHC class II expression. In parallel, integrated analysis of ENCODE candidate cis-regulatory elements, H3K27Ac histone marks, and ReMap 2022 TF binding peaks revealed that IRF8 occupies putative enhancer or promoter regions of both HLA-DQB1 and CD40 (Figures S5A–S5C). These binding regions are conserved and exhibit chromatin signatures indicative of active regulatory function. These data provide supporting evidence of IRF8-mediated transcriptional control of key immunoregulatory genes, reinforcing its central role in modulating the MHC class II pathway in inflammatory contexts.

To further explore the cellular specificity of IRF8 expression, we performed protein expression analysis using lysate from EBV-immortalized B cells (lymphoblastoid cell lines [LCLs]) generated using the EBV B95-8 prototype strain (2089), whereas no detectable expression was observed in bladder epithelial cell lines (RT4 and HT1376) (Figure 5E). This observation confirms that IRF8 is not broadly expressed across cell types, but instead exhibits a B cell-restricted expression pattern, likely induced during EBV transformation. These results support our SCENIC-based inference that IRF8 is a key transcriptional regulator in B cells and further suggest that IRF8 expression is dependent on EBV-driven reprogramming, rather than being a feature of epithelial cells.

This regulatory link supports the idea that IRF8 may orchestrate MHC class II-related antigen presentation in EBV-infected B cells, potentially contributing to chronic immune activation in HIC. Given the regulation of HLA-DQB1 by IRF8, along with our bulk RNA-seq findings, we next assessed the genomic status of HLA-DQB1 to further explore its role in HIC pathogenesis.

HLA-DQB1 emerges as a key genetic hotspot in targeted MHC region sequencing

Identifying HLA-DQB1 and HLA-DQA1 as the upregulated genes in EBV+/HIC patients from the top 20 DEGs heatmap underscores their critical role in driving immune responses. To further investigate the genetic basis of these transcriptional changes, targeted sequencing of the MHC regions was performed on HIC patients (n = 24). Initial analyses of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and their proportions across the MHC regions revealed that while the MHC class II region had the highest total number of SNVs, the proportion of SNVs relative to the total variants was highest in the MHC class I region (89.4%), followed by the MHC class II region (87%) and the MHC class III region (82.1%) (Figure 6A). Next, we mapped the distribution of SNVs across MHC regions (Figure 6B) and then we narrowed down to the genes that show specific SNVs in all the patients. The genes that contained SNVs in all the patients include HLA-F, HLA-G, HLA-A, HLA-C, HLA-B, MICA, MICB, and TAP2 in the MHC class I region; for the MHC class II region, the genes included HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DQA2, HLA-DQB2, HLA-DOA, HLA-DPA1, and HLA-DPB1; and the MHC class III region showcased the greatest number of genes with SNVs common among all the patients: MIC, MCCD1, DDX39B, ATP6V1G2, NFKBIL1, PRRC2A, CSNK2B, MPIG6B, DDAH2, CYP21A2, PRRT1, PPT2, AGPAT1, PPT-EGFL8, AGER, and NOTCH4 (Figure S4A). Based on this, we next mapped the distribution of SNVs in genes with 100% detection in all the patients, and we found that HLA-DQB1 was the top gene that had the highest number of SNVs (Figure 6C). In addition to HLA-DQB1, recurrent SNVs were also observed in HLA-DPB1 (Figures 6C and S4A), which has been implicated in IC/BPS. Their GWAS identified amino acid variants in HLA-DPB1 as significantly associated with disease risk in Japanese cohorts, aligning with our detection of HLA-DPB1 mutations in all 24 HIC patients.5 To assess disease specificity, we additionally compared HIC (n = 24) with a matched Taiwan Biobank control cohort (n = 50) across 618 Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database (dbSNP)-indexed sites (build: GRCh37/hg19) using Fisher’s exact test with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR); 220/618 sites met FDR <0.05, with the burden concentrated at HLA-DQB1 and corroborating signals at HLA-B and HLA-A (Figure 6D). Per-SNP counts, carrier proportions, p values, and FDRs for all analyzed sites are provided in Table S2. Additionally, Table S3 presents the de-identified per-sample matrix for HIC and Taiwan Biobank controls documenting the raw call set underpinning the case-control analysis in Figure 6D. The distribution of SNVs, transitions, and transversions in HLA-DQB1 is shown in Figure 6E, which depicts the transition/transversion (Ts/Tv) ratio of 2.86, reflecting the mutational characteristics of this HLA-DQB1.

To explore the above observations further, we assessed the relative frequencies of insertions, deletions, and SNVs across the MHC class II genes (Figures S4B–S4D). The results showcased that SNVs dominate the mutation landscape in HLA-DQB1 compared to the other structural variants. Given the pronounced SNV burden and its previously observed transcriptional upregulation in HIC/EBV+ patients, we next explored the evolutionary constraints on HLA-DQB1 using Evo-Designer.25 In Evo-Designer, the Score a Sequence tool was used to score against a multi-species genomic alignment to evaluate evolutionary constraints. Evo-Designer analysis identified rs1049133 (chr6:32662070 A>G) as the most functionally constrained SNV within a low-entropy region (0.06683) of HLA-DQB1 followed by rs1049055 (chr6:32666610 C>T) with an entropy of 0.6613, suggesting strong evolutionary conservation (Figures 6F and S4E). This presence of a constrained variant in a gene critical for antigen presentation further supports its potential role in immune dysregulation associated with HIC. The accumulation of rs1049133, combined with the increased expression of HLA-DQB1 in HIC/EBV+ patients, highlights the significance of HLA-DQB1 in the disease pathogenesis. Collectively, these data direct us to investigate the functional and structural analyses of HLA-DQB1 in more detail, especially given the pronounced SNV burden and its previously observed transcriptional upregulation in HIC/EBV+ patients.

Discussion

Our study, using a multi-omics approach including scRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq, and targeted sequencing of the MHC region combined with CellChat, NicheNet, SCENIC, and Evo2 analysis shows that HIC is an inflammatory disease caused by complex interactions between immune cells and epithelial cells. In our pseudobulk analysis of scRNA-seq data, we found that genes related to EBV were highly expressed in HIC samples. In particular, CHUK, NFKB1, and STAT3 were highly expressed in HIC, and these genes are key regulators of immunoresponse and chronic inflammation. These results are consistent with the known role of EBV in long-term inflammatory diseases, denoting the fact that the virus can remain in B cells and influence immune pathways (Figures 1B and 1C).4,7

In addition, we found that HIC samples had a larger number of B cells and epithelial cells in our cell-type proportion analysis depicting that these two cell types contribute to HIC. This is in accordance with the previous reports showcasing the abundance of B cells and plasma cells in HIC lesions, where B cell expansion is often witnessed (Figures 1D–1G).1 The increased B cell population, together with higher expression of EBV-related genes, suggests that EBV infection might be helping B cells survive or function differently in the HIC bladder microenvironment.26 Previous studies have shown that EBV-infected B cells can evade the immune system and release inflammatory cytokines, potentially resulting in tissue damage.27 Our data, showing enrichment of inflammatory pathways such as viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction further solidifies this mechanism (Figure 1B). Moreover, the expansion of epithelial cells in HIC may indicate a reactive response to ongoing mucosal damage. Epithelial denudation is a hallmark of HIC lesions, yet our finding suggests that certain epithelial subsets might be actively proliferating or stressed under constant inflammatory pressure. Their crosstalk with virus-modulated B cells could be critical in conserving inflammation that results in chronic pain and bladder dysfunction characteristics of HIC.

Our bulk RNA-seq data reinforced the association between EBV and HIC. Notably, gene-encoding MHC class II molecules, particularly HLA-DQB1 and HLA-DQA1, were significantly upregulated in EBV+/HIC patients (Figure 2B). MHC class II molecules are essential for antigen presentation to CD4+ T cells, and their epithelial upregulation suggests persistent immunostimulation via viral and potentially self-antigens. This aberrant antigen presentation can perpetuate chronic T cell activation, sustained cytokine production, and bladder inflammation, amplifying epithelial injury and tissue remodeling.3,28 Additionally, non-professional antigen-presenting epithelial cells, such as those in the gut, lung, and bladder, are now recognized as active participants in local immunoregulation, where dysregulated MHC class II expression exacerbates chronic inflammation and barrier disruption, as observed in mucosal diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease and chronic airway inflammation.29

We propose that in HIC, epithelial stress exposes intracellular or viral antigens, which are continuously presented by B cells and epithelial cells, reinforcing a self-sustaining immune activation loop. This is supported by the significant enrichment of memory B cells in HIC samples, as revealed by CIBERSORTx deconvolution (Figures 2C and 2D). Since EBV is a B cell-transforming virus capable of maintaining a long-term latent reservoir in B cells, the expansion of memory B cell pools in HIC may provide a mechanistic link between viral persistence and sustained immune activation. The expansion of memory B cell observation aligns with the previously documented lymphoplasmacytic infiltration characteristic of HIC, which suggests EBV-infected or otherwise activated inflammatory signaling through cytokine secretion and antigen presentation.30

Our data also showed upregulation of stress-associated genes, including SPRR2A, S100A7, and TMPRSS11D (Figure 2B). SPRR2A is increasingly recognized as a stress-induced barrier repair protein that enhances epithelial resilience under injury, promotes wound healing via the epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and suppresses p53 activity to facilitate epithelial proliferation and migration during stress.31,32 S100A7 functions as a pro-inflammatory alarmin that not only recruits immune cells but also enhances epithelial barrier protein expression, as seen in upper airway epithelial cells, linking barrier modulation and inflammation directly to epithelial cell signaling.33 Furthermore, TMPRSS11D, a transmembrane serine proteasome, plays a critical role in airway epithelial remodeling, proteolytic activation, and epithelial-pathogen interactions under inflammatory stress, with its autocleavage mechanism enhancing local protease activity and potentially increasing epithelial vulnerability.34 Collectively, the upregulation of these markers signifies epithelial barrier compromise, active remodeling, and immune cell recruitment, facilitating increased antigen exposure and driving the persistence of local inflammation.

These processes together establish a vicious cycle of immune-epithelial crosstalk in HIC, wherein epithelial stress exposes antigens, B cells and epithelial cells perpetuate antigen presentation via MHC class II pathways, and the immune response further damages the epithelial barrier, reinforcing the chronicity of the disease.

Beyond the EBV-related pathway (Figure 2E), our enrichment analysis highlighted several key immunoregulatory pathways that likely contribute to the sustained immune dysregulation observed in HIC. Notably, we observed enrichment of T helper 1 (Th1) and Th2 cell differentiation and Th17 cell differentiation pathways, implicating polarized T helper responses in disease pathogenesis. Th1 cells promote cytotoxic and macrophage-mediated immune responses through IFN-γ, while Th2 cells mediate humoral immunity and fibrotic remodeling via IL-4 and IL-13.35 Th17 cells, in contrast, produce IL-17 and IL-22, promoting neutrophil recruitment and epithelial barrier disruption.36 These findings are consistent with a recent study that demonstrated that bladder tissues from IC/BPS patients exhibit a Th1/Th17-polarized cytokine environment that drives epithelial apoptosis and tight junction loss, contributing to urothelial dysfunction.37

In addition, we found enrichment of the chemokine signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways, supporting the notion of sustained chemotactic gradients and ligand-receptor interaction that drives immune cell infiltration and communication within the bladder microenvironment.38,39 These are consistent with the prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltration observed in HIC.

We also identified enrichment of the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling, a central regulator of pro-inflammatory gene transcription, including TNF, IL-1B, and IL-6, all of which amplify immune response and epithelial stress.9,40 Furthermore, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway was enriched, reflecting continuous cytokine-mediated signaling cascades that reinforce T cell differentiation and potentially antiviral immunity, particularly in the context of EBV persistence.41,42

Together, these findings highlight a complex immune signaling landscape in HIC involving T helper cell polarization, chemokine-driven immune cell recruitment, and sustained pro-inflammatory signaling, which collectively drive the chronic immune-epithelial crosstalk central to disease progression.

Our CellChat analysis reveals a robust epithelial-to-B cell communication axis mediated by APP-CD74 (Figure 3F). CD74 is usually known as a protein involved in antigen presentation and is highly expressed in B cells and other antigen-presenting cells.43 While CD74 is traditionally viewed as a receptor found on immune cells, several studies have shown that CD74 is upregulated in epithelial tissues during inflammation, including models of IC.44,45 A recent study demonstrated that APP interacts with CD74 in endothelial cells during kidney injury, promoting inflammation and tissue remodeling. Although specific to the renal environment, it supports the broader role of APP-CD74 signaling beyond the nervous system, potentially influencing immune responses and tissue repair.46

CD74 engagement by its ligands typically activates classic pro-survival and pro-inflammatory pathways. For example, CD74 ligation in gut epithelium drives PI3K/AKT and ERK1/2 signaling to promote epithelial proliferation.22 Similarly, in colonic cells, CD74 activation induces AKT phosphorylation and Bcl-2 upregulation, enhancing cell survival.47 Concurrently, CD74 can trigger NF-κB mediated inflammation: binding of a pathogen-derived ligand (Helicobacter pylori urease B) to epithelial CD74 induces IκBα degradation to NF-κB activation and IL-8 secretion.48 In fibrotic epithelia, CD74 signaling also converges on tissue-remodeling pathways. For instance, deletion of CD74 in polycystic kidney epithelium normalizes aberrant ERK and NF-κB activation and suppresses TGF-β-driven fibrosis.49

Equally important, our reverse CellChat analysis (Figure S2D) reveals a B cell-to-epithelial signaling axis, where B cells actively contribute to the epithelial inflammatory environment. This demonstrates a bidirectional APP-CD74 interaction between epithelial cells and B cells, with both cell types expressing CD74 and APP. Moreover, work in Caco-2 enterocytes has shown that epithelial APP forms a functional complex with CD36 and, upon ligand engagement, drives up Src kinase levels, downregulates the tight-junction protein occluding, and boosts IL-6 secretion.50 This work underscores the broader capacity of APP in epithelial cells to couple external cues to kinase-dependent barrier remodeling and pro-inflammatory cytokine mechanisms that could influence downstream of epithelial APP-CD74 engagement. This suggests not only intercellular signaling for epithelial APP to B cell CD74 but also a potential for autocrine or paracrine CD74-mediated signaling within epithelial cells.

In the HIC context, B cells exhibit clonal expansion and may harbor latent EBV, which supports persistent immunostimulation. The co-upregulation of CD74 and HLA-DQB1 in B cells suggests enhanced antigen-presenting potential. Our findings indicate that epithelial cells expressing APP may engage in bidirectional signaling with CD74+ B cells, thereby amplifying local inflammation and epithelial stress response. Mechanistically, the CD74-APP interaction appears to amplify inflammatory signals and maintain B cell-epithelial cell communication; meanwhile, the epithelial cells actively emit ligands such as COL1A2 and NAMPT that further reinforce immune cell activation (Figure 3G).51,52 By influencing B cell survival and antigen-presenting capacity through both CD74 and HLA-DQB1, EBV may leverage these pathways to maintain a pro-inflammatory environment favorable for viral latency.53

Our combined CellChat and NicheNet analysis gives strong evidence that B cells and epithelial cells in HIC communicate through two-way signaling. When defining B cells as the ligand criteria, IL-1B and LIF were identified as important ligands that may create a pro-inflammatory environment affecting epithelial cells (Figure 4C). IL-1β is well known for causing strong inflammatory responses by increasing prostaglandin production, chemokine release, and MMP activity in epithelial cells. Thus, the IL1B-IL1R interaction we found in our data may explain why epithelial damage and pain are common in HIC.54,55 However, epithelial cells produced ligands such as BMP4 and TGFB2, which are shown to regulate B cell activity (Figure 4B). Both of these molecules are important for tissue repair and remodeling, suggesting a compensatory and controlling immune activity in the HIC microenvironment.56

One very interesting finding is that MHC class II molecules, especially HLA-DQB1, seem to affect the production of important cytokines like IL-1B and TNF in epithelial cells (Figure 4E). This suggests that antigen presentation by B cells may lead to a continuous cycle, where activated T cells or other immune cells increase stress signals in epithelial cells. This kind of feedback loop might explain why HIC lesions do not respond well to normal anti-inflammatory treatments, as antigen presentation and cytokine-driven epithelial damage keep the disease process going. Overall, these two-way signaling pathways show that the HIC environment is much more interactive than a simple case of immune cells attacking the epithelium. Instead, B cells and epithelial cells are constantly communicating in ways that influence inflammation and tissue health.

Our SCENIC analysis identified several important TFs that may control different parts of the inflammation and tissue remodeling processes in HIC. Among them, ERG and SOX17 were found to be key regulators in endothelial cells, suggesting that the endothelium is not only a passive barrier but also actively involved in chronic inflammation (Figure 5B). These TFs are usually linked to vascular stability and new blood vessel formation, so their high activity in HIC may be connected to abnormal blood vessel growth or endothelial dysfunction in inflamed bladder tissue.

In B cells, SPIB and IRF8 were the most prominent TFs, with IRF8, in particular, governing key antigen-presenting genes such as HLA-DQB1 (Figures 5C and 5D). Notably, IRF8 is necessary for EBV lytic replication. EBV has been shown to manipulate IRF8 expression in B cells to facilitate viral latency and support cell survival; IRF8 is required for EBV reactivation and regulation of apoptosis in infected B cells.57 EBV has been shown to modulate IRF8 expression in B cells to promote proliferation and survival, thereby strengthening the notion of the capacity of EBV to drive sustained immune activation.58 Furthermore, the protein expression of IRF8 is observed in the LCLs but not in the bladder epithelial cells, strengthening the context of IRF8 in terms of EBV-modulated B cells (Figure 5E). For epithelial cells, FOSL1 and IRF6 were identified as major regulators (Figure 5C). Both of these TFs are known to react to stress and inflammation.59,60 Overall, these TFs provide a clear understanding of how immune cells and structural cells interact in HIC. These findings would be very useful for identifying potential drug targets for treating the disease, and IRF8 and FOSL1 seem to be especially interesting for developing new therapies.

Our targeted sequencing of the MHC genomic region found a high number of SNVs in HLA-DQB1, making it a possible mutation hotspot in HIC (Figures 6B and 6C). In addition, the matched case-control comparison (HIC n = 24 vs. controls n = 50) across 618 MHC SNPs identified 220 FDR-significant sites (Benjamini-Hochberg-FDR <0.05), with the burden centered at HLA-DQB1 (Figure 6D; Table S2), supporting locus-level enrichment beyond baseline population variation. This large number of variants, together with our earlier results showing increased HLA-DQB1 expression in EBV+ HIC samples, suggests that HLA-DQB1 plays an important role in long-term immune activation. MHC class II molecules are necessary for CD4+ T cell responses, and changes in HLA-DQB1 might affect how antigens are presented, leading to stronger or abnormal immune reactions in the bladder.28 Notably, our genomic sequencing revealed high SNV burden not just in HLA-DQB1 but also in HLA-DPB1 (Figures 6C and S4A), coinciding with the recent GWAS findings that reported amino acid substitutions in both genes confer genetic susceptibility to IC/BPS. These shared results across Taiwanese and Japanese populations suggest a conserved immunogenetic axis in East Asian cohorts. Given the role of these molecules in antigen presentation, the observed variants may alter peptide binding or immune cell interactions, contributing to disease pathophysiology.5 Because of extensive linkage disequilibrium within the MHC, we interpret these genetic signals at the locus level (e.g., HLA-DQB1) rather than assign causality to individual SNPs.

We also found that the Ts/Tv ratio in HLA-DQB1 was high, suggesting that these mutations may not happen randomly but might be influenced by immune system pressures or viral infection (Figure 6E).61 This stress could come from long-term EBV infection, which is known to keep B cells activated or from processes similar to autoimmunity, where bladder antigens (possibly caused by epithelial damage) are constantly being presented to the immune system.7,62 In either case, HLA-DQB1 mutations might change how antigens are bound, leading to stronger T cell responses, similar to what happens in other diseases linked to MHC class II polymorphisms.63

Notably, the identification of rs1049133 (chr6:32662070 A>G) as a functionally constrained SNV in HLA-DQB1 strengthens the argument that this gene plays a crucial role in immunoregulation (Figure 6F). The evolutionary conservation of rs1049133 suggests it may impact antigen presentation, with its enrichment in HIC patients pointing to a role in sustained immune activation. The rs1049133 variant in HLA-DQB1, while identified in all our HIC patients, is not disease specific. As a conserved SNV in an immune-related gene, it may influence antigenic interactions and immune persistence. Further functional and structural studies of HLA-DQB1 in the context of rs1049133 are needed to clarify its role in HIC pathology.

While our previous studies and the present integrated analysis clearly implicates a mechanism that drives chronic inflammation in HIC, it is important to note that a recent study in a Japanese cohort reported an absence of detectable EBV transcripts in HIC samples despite prominent B cell expansion.64 The study demonstrated that the abnormal expression of APRIL (TNFSF13) and BAFF (TNFSF13B) were abnormally expressed in bladder tissue from HIC patients and were associated with B cell accumulation, clonal expansion, and tertiary lymphoid structure formation, proposing a central role for BAFF/APRIL signaling in driving local immunopathology. While these findings suggest a non-viral mechanism of B cell activation, our CellChat analysis also detected the expression of TNFSF13 and TNFSF13B in B cells (Figure S2D). However, these ligands were not among the dominant outgoing signals, and their overall contribution to epithelial-immune crosstalk in our dataset appeared limited compared with other pathways. This discrepancy may reflect immunological heterogeneity across cohorts, potentially influenced by differences in EBV status, patient ancestry, or disease progression. Collectively, these observations support the concept that HIC encompasses diverse immunopathological subtypes, wherein either viral latency or cytokine-driven B cell signaling may dominate depending on the context. This highlights the importance of ancestry-specific immunogenetic modeling, particularly in the HLA-DQB1 region. Future studies with larger, ethnically diverse cohorts and standardized protocols will be essential to reconcile these differences and clarify the role of EBV in HIC pathogenesis.

A major strength of this study is its multi-omics design, integrating bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, CellChat, NicheNet, SCENIC, MHC sequencing, and Evo2 SNV analysis to investigate immune-epithelial interactions in HIC. This approach revealed links between HLA-DQB1 variants, immune signaling, and EBV-associated inflammation. Targeted MHC sequencing further supported the role of HLA-DQB1 in chronic inflammation, suggesting the potential for precision therapies.

However, limitations remain. EBV status was defined using bulk RNA-seq, while the scRNA-seq dataset lacked EBV-specific annotation, emphasizing the need for cell-level viral detection (e.g., EBER/RNAscope in situ hybridization) and spatial localization of viral and host transcripts. The cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation, and functional studies such as peptide-binding assays or B cell co-culture with B cell-restricted IRF8 perturbation are needed to validate findings, alongside EBV-permissive/humanized in vivo models. A larger, more diverse cohort would also improve generalizability.

In summary, our multi-omics analysis implicates EBV-infected B cells in driving chronic inflammation in HIC through sustained immune-epithelial crosstalk. Further studies exploring EBV gene expression in B cells and its effects on epithelial homeostasis may help identify therapeutic targets to disrupt inflammation and enhance patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital ethics committee (institutional review board no. IRB108-45-A), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient selection and bladder tissue sampling

The diagnosis of HIC was confirmed in patients with IC/BPS who exhibited Hunner’s lesion on office cystoscopy. IC/BPS was diagnosed according to European Association of Urology guidelines, requiring chronic (>6 months) pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort perceived to arise from the bladder, accompanied by at least one other urinary symptom such as persistent urgency or frequency. A Hunner’s lesion was defined as a circumscribed, reddened mucosal area with radiating small vessels and a central coagulum or scar. All HIC patients had previously failed oral therapies (e.g., analgesics, antimuscarinics) and electrocauterization, and subsequently underwent partial cystectomy with augmentation enterocystoplasty. Full-thickness bladder specimens, including mucosa, submucosa, muscle, and serosa were excised from Hunner’s lesion area for transcriptomic analysis.65,66

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq data (BioProject: PRJNA813893) were processed via Galaxy.67 Quality control was performed using FastQC (version 0.11.9) and summarized with MultiQC (version 1.9). The average GC content (48%–51%) and read length (151 bp) reflected high-quality sequencing. Duplication rates ranged from 56.9% to 70.6%, with minimal sequence failure (∼27%). Sequencing depth ranged from 20 to 33 million reads per sample. Trimmomatic (version 0.39) was used for adapter and low-quality base trimming. Cleaned reads were aligned to hg38 using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1), and gene expression quantified via featureCounts (version 2.0.3). DEGs (adjusted p < 0.05) were identified using DESeq2 (version 2.11.40.8) and analyzed for Gene Ontology and KEGG enrichment using clusterProfiler (version 4.14.4).

Immune cell deconvolution

Immune cell composition in HIC (n = 14) and control (n = 11) samples was analyzed using the CIBERSORTx platform.68 This tool deconvoluted RNA-seq data into 22 immune cell subtypes, including B cells (naive, memory, plasma), T cells (CD4+, CD8+, T regulatory cells, T follicular helper, γδ), NK cells, monocytes, macrophages (M0, M1, M2), DCs, mast cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils. Immune cell proportions and comparative plots were visualized directly within the CIBERSORTx interface.

scRNA-seq data analysis: Quality and clustering

scRNA-seq data from HIC (n = 2), NHIC (n = 3), and control (n = 2) samples (GEO: GSE175526) were processed using Seurat (version 5.1.0) in R. Cells with >20% mitochondrial genes were excluded using PercentageFeatureSet. Filtering thresholds were set at >200 and <5,000–7,500 for nFeature_RNA and <50,000–75,000 for nCount_RNA (Figures S1A–S1C). Data were normalized, and the top 3,000 variable genes were selected using SelectIntegrationFeatures. Integration was performed via FindVariableFeatures, FindIntegrationAnchors, and IntegrateData. Cell types were annotated with SingleR69 using the Human Primary Cell Atlas reference. UMAPs were generated to visualize clusters and annotations, and split by condition for comparison.

Pseudobulk analysis, KEGG pathway, and molecular interaction mapping

scRNA-seq data from HIC (n = 2) and control (n = 2) samples were aggregated at the sample and cell-type level using Seurat’s AggregateExpression function. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (adjusted p ≤ 0.05, log2FC > 0) using size factor normalization. Functional enrichment was conducted using clusterProfiler and enrichKEGG, with gene symbols converted to Entrez IDs via bitr for KEGG compatibility.

EBV+ vs. EBV− classification

A total of 12 HIC samples were classified as EBV+ (n = 9) or EBV− (n = 3) based on the expression levels of EBV-associated transcripts, such as EBER, EBNA1, LMP1, and BRLF1. Samples showing detectable levels of either one of the EBV transcripts were considered positive samples, and samples showing undetectable or negative expression were considered negative samples (Table S4).

Transcriptional regulatory network analysis

SCENIC was used to reconstruct GRNs and identify the TFs driving cellular heterogeneity in HIC samples. Genes expressed in ≥1% of cells were retained using geneFiltering. Co-expression modules were inferred with GENIE3, and TF-target regulons were identified via RcisTarget based on motif enrichment. Regulon activity was scored per cell using AUCell.

Cell-cell communication and ligand-receptor analysis

CellChat and NicheNet were used to characterize intercellular communication between B cells and epithelial cells in the HIC microenvironment. CellChat identified overexpressed genes and ligand-receptor pairs using the CellChatDB.human database, with communication probabilities and pathway-level interactions computed via computeCommonProb and computeCommunProbPathway and network aggregation through aggregateNet. NicheNet predicted ligand-receptor interactions and downstream target genes by filtering genes expressed in ≥10% of cells, identifying DEGs in epithelial cells (FindMarkers, adjusted p ≤ 0.01, |log2FC| ≥ 0.5), and ranking ligands based on regulatory potential using predict_ligand_activities.

Genomic analysis of MHC variants in HIC patients

Genomic DNA from 24 HIC samples was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit. DNA quality was assessed with the QuantiFluor dsDNA System and Qubit 4. Libraries were prepared using the Celero EZ DNA-Seq Kit, and the MHC region was captured with the BioDynami MHC Capture Kit. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeqX Plus (151-bp paired end reads). Raw reads were quality checked with FastQC version 0.11.9, trimmed with TrimGalore version 0.9.7, and aligned to GRCh38 using BWA-MEM version 0.7.15. Variants were called with GATK version 4 HaplotypeCaller and filtered for high-confidence SNVs. Variants were classified by MHC class (I, II, III), and metrics such as SNV proportions, Ts/Tv ratios, and allele frequencies were calculated. For presentation and reporting, genomic coordinates used in Figure 6D and Table S2 are in GRCh37/hg19 after liftover from GRCh38; statistical comparison was rsID based. For disease-specificity assessment, we incorporated a matched control cohort from the Taiwan Biobank (n = 50) using publicly available germline variant calls. We harmonized at the analysis level as follows: (1) genome-build harmonization to GRCh37/hg19 for reporting (liftover from GRCh38 where required); (2) dbSNP rsID and REF/ALT orientation alignment with multiallelic decomposition and left-normalization (bcftools norm); and (3) restriction of inference to sites within our targeted MHC panel that were callable (non-missing) in both cohorts. For each retained dbSNP-indexed site (618 total), we tabulated carriers (≥1 alternate allele) and noncarriers in HIC (n = 24) and controls (n = 50), computed carrier proportions, and tested group differences with two-sided Fisher’s exact test in R; FDR was controlled across sites using Benjamini-Hochberg.

Evolutionary constraint analysis (Evo2 designer)

To investigate evolutionary constraints in HLA-DQB1, a composite haplotype incorporating 27 SNVs identified across all HIC patient samples was generated. The reference HLA-DQB1 FASTA sequence (chr6:32,659,000–32,669,000; GRCh38) was retrieved and processed in Biostrings. The modified haplotype was analyzed using Evo2 Designer’s Score a Sequence tool.

IRF8 protein expression

Protein lysates from RT4 and HT1376 bladder epithelial cells, as well as EBV-transformed LCLs (prototype B95.8), were separated on 8% SDS-PAGE and transferred using semi-dry blotting (10 V, 60 min). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in 0.1% PBS with Tween 20 and then probed overnight at 4°C with anti-IRF8 rabbit monoclonal antibody (E6J8Q, Cell Signaling, catalog no. 83413; 1:1,000) followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch; 1:5,000, 1.5 h, room temperature). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase was used as a loading control (mouse monoclonal clone 2D2, Origene; 1:2,000) with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch; 1:5,000). Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), Supersignal West Pico Plus ECL (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 34580), or Western Lightning ECL Pro (PerkinElmer, catalog no. NEL113001EA), depending on signal intensity requirements.
