Protocol: Psychosocial Interventions Addressing Mental Health Outcomes Among Hospital-Based Healthcare Workers in United States Hospitals: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Harly M. Blumhagen, Brandy R. Maynard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare workers employed in hospital settings face significant occupational stressors that place them at elevated risk for a range of mental health outcomes, including burnout, depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, secondary traumatic stress, compassion fatigue, and moral injury. These challenges carry serious consequences for healthcare worker well-being, patient safety, care quality, and workforce retention. Despite growing recognition of this crisis and recent federal legislation allowing funding to address healthcare worker mental health, no comprehensive synthesis exists identifying which psychosocial interventions are most effective for this population in the United States.ObjectivesThis systematic review and meta-analysis aims to examine the effects of psychosocial interventions on mental health outcomes among healthcare workers employed in United States hospital settings, to identify moderators or intervention effectiveness, and to provide actionable evidence to inform program development, resource allocation, and policy implementation.MethodsWe will search eight electronic databases and complete an extensive grey literature search to identify studies published from 2010 to present. Eligible study designs include randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cluster randomized controlled trials (CRCTs), and quasi-experimental designs (QEDs) with a control group examining psychosocial interventions delivered within or through hospital-affiliated systems in the United States. Participants will include hospital-based healthcare workers who provide direct clinical care. Primary outcomes include burnout, depression symptoms, anxiety symptoms, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), secondary traumatic stress, vicarious trauma, compassion fatigue, and moral injury, assessed using validated measures. Secondary outcomes include turnover intention and turnover rates, extracted when reported. Two reviewers will independently screen studies and extract data. Risk of bias will be assessed using the ROB 2 tool for RCTs and the ROBINS-I V2 tool for QEDs. Where sufficient data are available, meta-analyses will be conducted using a random-effects model with robust variance estimation (RVE) to account for dependent effect sizes. Heterogeneity will be assessed using the Q statistic and I2 statistic, interpreted alongside 95% prediction intervals and visual inspection of forest plots. Moderator analyses will explore intervention type, healthcare worker role, treatment duration, delivery format, and facilitator type as potential sources of heterogeneity.

Background

The Problem, Condition, or Issue

Healthcare workers are frequently exposed to high levels of occupational stress due to the demands of their caregiving roles. Long shifts, repeated exposure to illness and death, and the risk of verbal or physical assault from patients or their families are just a few of the challenges they face (Hinderer et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2019; Orrù et al., 2021). While the psychological toll of having a career in the military has long been recognized, growing attention is now being given to the mental health challenges faced by other helping professions, including emergency responders, law enforcement officers, and healthcare workers (Maguen et al., 2025). As a result of sustained exposure to stressors, healthcare professionals are at increased risk for a range of adverse mental health outcomes, including burnout, anxiety, depression, secondary traumatic stress (STS), vicarious trauma (VT), compassion fatigue (CF), moral injury, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). As one of the most widely studied and prevalent outcomes, burnout has received particular attention in the literature as both a consequence and indicator of chronic occupational stress in healthcare settings.

Symptoms of Burnout, Depression, and Anxiety

Burnout among healthcare workers is defined as “an occupational syndrome characterized by a high degree of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization (i.e., cynicism), along with a diminished sense of personal accomplishment at work” (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2022, p. 7). Even outside of extraordinary events, burnout has been a widespread issue, with 35% to 54% of U.S. physicians and nurses reporting symptoms (National Academies of Sciences et al., 2019). Recent nationwide data indicators suggest burnout among healthcare workers has increased in 2022 from 2018, with 45.6% of healthcare workers reporting feeling burned out (Nigam et al., 2023). Several factors contribute to burnout in healthcare workers including harassment, violence, and discrimination, poor work-life balance, excessive workloads and hours, barriers to receiving support for mental health, poor care coordination, burdensome administrative processes, health misinformation, politicization of science and public health, and unrealistic expectations placed on healthcare workers by healthcare systems and individuals (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2022).

Rates of anxiety and depression symptoms among healthcare workers have been widely studied across various countries, particularly in the context of recent global health crises. These investigations consistently highlight the elevated mental health risks faced by healthcare professionals (Li et al., 2021; Marvaldi et al., 2021; Olaya et al., 2021; Sahebi et al., 2021; Salari et al., 2020). In a nationwide study of more than 10,000 healthcare workers, Fond et al. (2022) reported a probable depression prevalence rate of 30%. Key organizational contributors to depressive symptoms among healthcare workers include burnout, workplace bullying, and limited decision-making latitude (Fond et al., 2022). Individual level risk factors for depressive symptoms in healthcare workers include female sex, caregiving responsibilities at home, a history of recurrent major depression, chronic illness, and engagement in health risk behaviors (Fond et al., 2022). While contextual stressors like the COVID-19 pandemic and rising stigma against medical professionals may have exacerbated mental health issues, earlier evidence suggests that high rates of depression among healthcare workers predate the pandemic. For instance, Mata et al. (2015) reported 28.8% of resident physicians experienced either depression or depressive symptoms.

More recent evidence continues to emphasize the mental health burden within this group. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Sialakis et al. (2023) found pooled prevalence rates of 33.8% for depressive symptoms and 41.3% for anxiety symptoms among healthcare workers. Similarly, data from the 2022 General Social Survey indicated that 57% of healthcare workers reported symptoms of anxiety and 34.4% reported symptoms of depression (Nigam et al., 2023). Research on healthcare worker mental health has grown exponentially in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, reflecting both heightened awareness and a growing need for systemic intervention.

These high rates of psychological distress have serious implications not only for the health and well-being of clinicians themselves but also for the safety and quality of patient care. Healthcare workers who are burned out, or have poorer mental health, are less likely to provide quality care than those who do not report these concerns. For example, nurses with poor mental health are 62% more likely to report making medical errors than those with better mental health (Melnyk et al., 2021). The consequences of burnout among medical professionals extend beyond individual well-being, as it has been linked to unprofessional behavior, poorer patient outcomes, increased medical errors, and substantial financial burdens (National Academies of Sciences et al., 2019). For instance, burnout has been significantly associated with higher rates of urinary tract infections and surgical site infections (Cimiotti et al., 2012). Notably, in hospitals where burnout was successfully reduced, thousands of infections were presumably prevented, leading to cost savings of approximately $68 million (Cimiotti et al., 2012). Additionally, burnout often results in healthcare workers reducing their working hours, exacerbating workforce shortages (Dyrbye et al., 2021). The financial impact of physician burnout in the U.S. is substantial, with estimated costs ranging from $2.6 billion to $6.3 billion (Han et al., 2019).

Taken together, the toll of burnout and mental health challenges is not confined to patient care or organizational costs, it also contributes to a growing crisis in workforce retention and recruitment. It was reported in 2021 that nearly 30% of healthcare workers were considering leaving the profession, with approximately 60% attributing their decision to work-related mental health challenges (American Hospital Association, 2021). Recent indicators suggest that 44.2% of healthcare workers reported an intention to pursue a new job, an increase of 27.7% since 2018 (Nigam et al., 2023). In 2022, 60% of nurses reported an increased workload since the pandemic, and more than one-quarter indicated plans to leave the profession or retire within five years (Smiley et al., 2023). These trends could contribute to a severe nursing shortage, posing a significant threat to the U.S. healthcare system (American Hospital Association, 2021). Beyond nursing, a projected shortage of 122,000 physicians across both primary and specialty care is expected by 2034 (Association of American Medical Colleges, 2021). In response, Congress has recently reviewed legislation aimed at addressing this shortfall by expanding residency positions for physicians (U.S. Congress, 2023). The growing shortages of healthcare workers across the country may lead to reduced access to quality healthcare, especially in areas that are already strained, such as rural locations and organizations that specifically treat underserved and marginalized populations (Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform, 2022). In addition to burnout, depression, and anxiety, healthcare workers may also experience other forms of psychological distress that stem from exposure to the suffering of others.

Secondary Traumatic Stress, Vicarious Trauma, and Compassion Fatigue

Secondary traumatic stress, vicarious trauma, and compassion fatigue are terms often used to describe the mental health effects experienced by healthcare workers because of their work. This group of terms have been referred to as empathy-based stress, defined as “experience of adverse psychological and/or physical reactions to trauma exposure at work, resulting from empathetic engagement following trauma exposure” (Rauvola et al., 2019, p. 300). Since the inception of these terms, they have significantly overlapped and have lacked clear distinction. Secondary traumatic stress and compassion fatigue were originally synonymous, with Figley (1995) asserting that the terms could be used interchangeably, and that both terms refer to “the natural consequent behaviors and emotions resulting from knowing about a traumatizing event experienced by a significant other– the stress resulting from a helping or wanting to help a traumatized or suffering person” (Figley, 1995, p. 7). Since this time, secondary traumatic stress has primarily been referred to as the psychological and physiological responses to being exposed to the traumatic experiences of others while working in a helping profession, with symptoms closely mirroring those of PTSD (Rauvola et al., 2019). In some instances, compassion fatigue is still used interchangeably with STS, while in others it is conceptualized as a progression of losing one’s empathy or experiencing emotional exhaustion due to the cumulative effects of being exposed to the suffering, stress, and trauma of patients (Zhang & Dator, 2025). Vicarious trauma has remained more consistent and refers specifically to the psychological effects of working with traumatized individuals, such as a helper experiencing a change in core beliefs about the world due to the result of their work (McCann & Pearlman, 1990).

It is essential to understand the interconnectedness of these terms when conducting this review, as primary study authors may use these terms interchangeably with one another. One study may measure “vicarious trauma,” the same way another author is measuring “secondary traumatic stress.” The overlap of these terms can create difficulty in quantifying the effects of these phenomena across studies.

The current literature is rife with identifying risk and protective factors that may lead to healthcare workers experiencing higher levels of compassion fatigue, secondary traumatic stress, vicarious trauma, and burnout. Personal characteristic risk factors associated with these outcomes include lower levels of resilience, having less years of experience, fewer post-graduate qualifications, attachment anxiety, passive coping styles, experiencing physical symptoms, being a nurse or a woman, neuroticism, and having a personal trauma history (Benfante et al., 2020; Harker et al., 2016; Leung et al., 2023; West, 2015; Yu et al., 2016). Organizational or work factors associated with increases in these outcomes include lower level of manager support, subjective time pressure at work, work-family relationships, poor group cohesion, negative co-worker relationships, higher number of hours worked per working shift, perception of greater workload, and greater difficulty in managing patient’s perceived stress (Hinderer et al., 2014; Hunsaker et al., 2015; Kleiner & Wallace, 2017; Li et al., 2014; Morando et al., 2024). Results seem to indicate that years of experience influence these outcomes, with studies overall indicating that younger nurses with less experience are more at risk for outcomes related to secondary traumatic stress, while nurses with more years of experience are more likely to report burnout (Hegney et al., 2014; Kelly et al., 2015). It is possible there may be a peak in which healthcare workers have enough experience to gain mastery in their profession, before ultimately entering a period in which they experience greater burnout, indicating that years of experience and negative outcomes may not be a linear relationship, but may change over time.

Exposure-related factors have also been explored in the literature, indicating that healthcare workers who are frontline workers, or who have been faced with patients’ physical pain, psychological suffering, and death are more likely to develop STS (Benfante et al., 2020; Meyer et al., 2015). Stress exposure in general is associated with increased burnout (Meyer et al., 2015). Burnout and STS have also been associated with higher anxiety and depression symptoms in healthcare workers (Hegney et al., 2014). Though these terms seem to be associated in many ways, there is evidence that the studies account for unique variance from one another (Meadors et al., 2009). Another related, but not synonymous mental health phenomenon in healthcare workers, is moral injury.

Moral Injury

Moral injury can arise when individuals are unable to reconcile their own actions, or the actions of others, with their previously held moral beliefs, making it difficult to integrate these experiences into their existing moral framework (Litz et al., 2009). This difficulty in integrating distressing experiences into one’s moral framework often leads to intense feelings of shame and guilt, and may manifest in dysfunctional behaviors such as social withdrawal (Litz et al., 2009). Factors that may lead to moral injury within the healthcare setting include the obligation to prioritize work tasks over human dignity, rendering unnecessary medical treatments, restraining patients, a lack of autonomy in determining the treatment plan, conflicts with higher-ranking treatment professionals about the care provided, and institutional policies that conflict with individual morals (Choe et al., 2015). Half of healthcare workers report experiencing events that could lead to moral injury, and around 7% screen positive for clinically significant symptoms—rates comparable to those observed in military veterans (Maguen et al., 2025). In addition to experiencing effects related to secondary, or empathy-based strain, healthcare workers may also be at risk for developing PTSD.

Post Traumatic Stress Disorder

Healthcare workers have primary exposures to trauma that may result in PTSD. Unlike secondary or vicarious trauma, which arises from indirect exposure through others’ experiences, healthcare workers may also encounter primary, direct trauma exposure in the course of their work, which can result in PTSD. Primary trauma exposure in hospital settings may include direct life-threatening experiences such as physical or sexual assault by patients or their families, as well as direct exposure to death and dying, which meets the DSM-5 Criterion A exposure requirements for a PTSD diagnosis (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Studies exploring the prevalence of PTSD among healthcare workers indicate that PTSD rates may vary by profession and specialty. Vance et al. (2021) found that 56.4% of emergency room residents had experienced trauma at work, with 19% screening positive for PTSD (Vance et al., 2021). Other studies have reported PTSD prevalence rates of 15.8% among emergency room physicians, 17% among trauma surgeons, and 15% among non-trauma surgeons (DeLucia et al., 2019; Jackson et al., 2019). Critical care nurses employed in the ICU have a higher prevalence rate of PTSD (24-29%) when compared to other nurses (14%) (Mealer et al., 2007, 2009). Studies specifically examining PTSD rates among other direct patient care providers, including social workers, child life specialists, hospital chaplains, and medical technicians, are noticeably lacking.

Current Context

In March 2020, the healthcare workforce was abruptly thrust into the COVID-19 pandemic with little time to prepare for the immense strain it would place on the healthcare system. Hospitals were forced to suspend non-emergent care and elective procedures while simultaneously managing a surge in uninsured patients, resulting in substantial financial losses (American Hospital Association, 2020). These financial challenges, coupled with the urgent need to provide emergency care for COVID-19 patients, led to organizational policies that significantly impacted the occupational lives of healthcare workers.

Beyond the usual stressors of the profession, healthcare workers faced deteriorating working conditions, financial insecurity, heightened concerns about being infected or infecting loved ones, disruptions in services, and COVID-19-related discrimination and stigma (Garcia-Iglecias et al., 2022; Mattila et al., 2021). Recognizing the toll of the pandemic, researchers extensively examined its impact on healthcare workers’ mental health, leading to multiple systematic reviews on the risk factors for and prevalence of mental health disorders among this population (Chigwedere et al., 2021; De Pablo et al., 2020; Ghahramani et al., 2023; Saragih et al., 2021; Vizheh et al., 2020). Risk factors included both pandemic-specific challenges—such as inadequate personal protective equipment, extended shifts, and history of quarantine—and broader vulnerabilities that may persist beyond the pandemic (Chigwedere et al., 2021). These include being female, working in the nursing profession, inadequate training, limited experience, and lack of social support (Chigwedere et al., 2021). Mental health concerns for healthcare workers across reviews included increased rates of insomnia, depression symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and trauma symptoms (Ghahramani et al., 2023; Saragih et al., 2021; Vizheh et al., 2020).

Although the pandemic intensified the strain on healthcare workers’ mental health, the need for support existed long before this crisis. The pandemic merely amplified awareness of the issue, highlighting its urgency for both the individual healthcare workers affected and the healthcare infrastructure. While the pandemic affected hospital systems worldwide, several contextual factors specific to the United States are particularly relevant to this review. First, the United States operates a predominantly for-profit, privatized hospital system. Under this structure, patients may incur significant financial debt to receive care (Aborode et al., 2025). This factor may contribute to the emotional burdens of healthcare workers, who have reported increased moral distress when caring for patients who forego care due to cost (Keilman et al., 2023). Physicians also carry substantial insurance-related administrative burdens, possibly creating occupational stressors that may differ meaningfully from those experienced by healthcare workers in countries with universal healthcare systems (Blumenthal et al., 2024; Himmelstein et al., 2020; Hurst et al., 2005; Rao et al., 2017). These structural differences may influence both the nature and severity of mental health challenges faced by US hospital workers and the types of psychosocial interventions that are feasible to implement within this context. Second, healthcare systems in other developed nations typically operate within policy environments that provide greater institutional support for workers, including universal healthcare coverage which is not tethered to employment and stronger labor protections related to wages, leave policies, and workplace harassment, which may buffer against some of the occupational stressors faced by US hospital workers (Henderson, 2023). The International Trade Union Confederation (ITUC) annually rates labor rights across countries on a scale of 1 to 5+, where 1 indicates only sporadic violations of rights and 5+ indicates no guarantee of rights due to the breakdown of the rule of law (International Trade Union Confederation, 2025). While similarly developed nations such as Italy, Australia, the Netherlands, Finland, France, and Germany are rated between one and two, the United States received a score of four, indicating systematic violation of rights (International Trade Union Confederation, 2025). This suggests that workers in the United States may have fewer protections in domains such as collective bargaining and fundamental labor rights compared to workers in similarly developed nations, with potential implications for working conditions and occupational wellbeing. Third, the United States scores among the highest globally on individualism as a cultural dimension (Hofstede, 2001), which may contribute to differences in perceptions of mental health stigma, lower rates of help-seeking, and cultural norms that emphasize self-reliance – patterns that are documented among US healthcare workers (Boehm & Simmons, 2025). These cultural factors may influence both the acceptability and effectiveness of psychosocial interventions in US hospital workplaces.

Taken together, these structural, institutional, and cultural factors suggest that the occupational experiences of US hospital workers, and the interventions most likely to be effective and feasible for this population, may differ meaningfully from those in other developed nations, informing the decision to restrict this review to hospital-based healthcare workers employed in the United States.

The Intervention and How It Might Work

Theoretical Foundation

Rauvola et al. (2019) developed an empathy-based stress model that captures the mechanisms which may lead to secondary traumatic stress, empathetic strain, and other poor occupational and mental health outcomes such as burnout, depression, anxiety, turnover, and performance concerns. The empathy-based stress model posits that the primary mechanism by which empathy-based strain occurs in the workplace is (1) second-hand trauma exposure - witnessing or hearing about trauma, and (2) empathetic engagement as a function of individual differences, emotional norms, and expectations (Rauvola et al., 2019). This model emphasizes that it is not only the secondhand trauma exposure that is important but also that during the secondhand trauma exposure, the individual was expected to engage in an empathetic response. Factors anticipated to moderate the effects of this exposure and empathetic response include contextual factors such as emotional display norms and expectations, form and frequency of trauma exposure, and support. Individual factors that may moderate this response include sociodemographics, empathy-relevant individual differences, personality, and coping styles. Empathy-based strain is said to result from the exposure, empathetic response, and moderators, and this strain ultimately leads to poor occupational health and wellbeing outcomes (Rauvola et al., 2019).

While the empathy-based stress model was developed primarily to explain empathy-based strain outcomes, the broader occupational stress processes it describes, including the role of trauma exposure, individual moderators, and social support, are relevant to the full range of mental health outcomes examined in this review, including burnout, depression, anxiety, and PTSD. Moral injury in healthcare work represents a related but distinct pathway; while it arises from perceived violations of one’s moral framework rather than empathetic engagement per se, the same moderating factors, particularly social support, institutional context, and individual coping, are relevant to its development and resolution (Litz et al., 2009). One framework that Rauvola et al. (2019) utilized in developing their model is the Job Demands-Resources model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007), which further explains how psychosocial interventions targeting resources such as social support and coping capacity may buffer against burnout and psychological distress more broadly.

Psychosocial interventions may target various points along this process to interrupt the development of empathy-based strain and its downstream effects on mental health and occupational wellbeing. Though some factors may be more stable, such as personality traits, sociodemographics, or emotional display norms, psychosocial interventions can effectively target more modifiable areas, such as coping styles, by teaching adaptive coping strategies and enhancing social support systems. These interventions may be more preventative in nature and may enhance the resilience of healthcare workers to better withstand the stresses of their job. Interventions may also seek to reduce the effects of empathy-based strain by being implemented after exposure, such as providing peer support or referral to an Employee Assistance Program (EAP). In sum, these interventions may be either preventative or reactive in nature, with the preventative interventions aiming to enhance resilience and coping prior to events to decrease the chance of harmful effects developing as a result of stressful exposures, and reactive interventions that are put in place to assist healthcare workers in coping if harmful effects have already occurred.

Several categories of psychosocial interventions have been identified in the literature as commonly implemented in hospital settings to address healthcare worker mental health, including peer support programs, employee assistance programs, resilience-building trainings, mindfulness-based interventions, and psychotherapy-based approaches (Anger et al., 2024; Hoedl et al., 2024). The following sections provide an overview of each of these intervention types and examples of their use.

Multicomponent Peer Support Programs

A number of healthcare organizations have attempted to address the poor mental health of healthcare workers through the design and implementation of on-site programs that often address organizational policies and implement peer support programs. Peer support programs may be offered on-site and include employees who have received basic training in supporting fellow colleagues (Lane et al., 2018). Training to be a peer volunteer may encompass leadership skills, communicative and relational ability, active listening, identification of alarming signs, techniques for stress reduction, and psychoeducation about peer support and the second victim phenomenon (Carbone et al., 2022). These programs, initially conceptualized to address the “second victimhood” phenomenon of being a medical professional, in which a medical error by a medical professional results in harm or death to a patient, often address mental health of employees following distressing patient-related events beyond medical errors (Wu, 2000). As these programs are all unique, three examples will be provided for insight into these programs.

The Resilience in Stressful Events (RISE) program implemented by Johns Hopkins Hospital, aims to support healthcare workers and promote a culture of safety (Connors et al., 2021). This program is an interdisciplinary, voluntary team of healthcare professionals that are trained to provide peer support to co-workers who have been affected by stressful patient-related events. Volunteers have an on-call rotation that makes the service available to employees 24 hours per day, seven days a week. The goal is for affected individuals to have a phone conversation within 30 minutes of the event, and a face-to-face discussion as soon as is convenient. Affected individuals are provided with a supportive, safe, and confidential environment to share anything about the event that affected them. Volunteers are trained in delivering psychological first aid, providing brief emotional support, and sharing resources (Connors et al., 2021). Programs in other places have also incorporated a similar model.

The forYou program implemented at the University of Missouri Health Care system, is a rapid response team of peer volunteers with a three-tiered integration model designed to provide support to employees (Scott et al., 2010). The purpose is to check in with employees following any event that may cause a “second victim” response. The first tier is basic emotional first aid provided at the local or department level. If a greater need for support is identified, the affected individual will receive tier two support, which involves instant, one-to-one peer support from a trained peer supporter. If needed, the individual is also provided with internal support to navigate the institutional investigation, risk management, and legal process procedures. The third tier includes support from chaplains, social workers, EAP personnel, and clinical health psychologists. A goal for the program is to ensure that employees who may experience an event that could result in secondary victimhood is fast-tracked to receive aid as quickly as possible (Scott et al., 2010).

The CopeColumbia program was developed in response to the COVID-19 pandemic (Mellins et al., 2020). This program aims to provide peer psychological support, mitigate emotional fatigue, and enhance resilience in healthcare employees. This program consists of faculty volunteers including psychiatrists and psychologists, who facilitated the program and have experience in clinical and research mental health treatment, particularly Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), group therapy, physician mental health, trauma, anxiety disorders, and organizational psychology and leadership during crises. Training for peer volunteers included (1) emotional themes likely to arise such as stress, anxiety, grief, and anger; (2) evidence-based suggested facilitator responses rooted in well-established peer support and psychotherapy models. Sessions are offered in groups, individual sessions, and town halls (Mellins et al., 2020).

All three programs report positive effects of their respective interventions for their healthcare workers. Strengths of these programs may include the camaraderie between coworkers who have experienced or witnessed similar challenges (Agarwal et al., 2022) The intervention is typically readily available following a distressing event due to being located onsite and volunteers being employed at the facility. These programs may also reduce the stigma surrounding healthcare workers’ mental health and normalize stressful reactions to patient-related events. While these programs may be less common, employee assistance programs are a more common resource offered to healthcare workers.

Employee Assistance Programs

Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs) are “voluntary, work-based programs that provide free and confidential assessments, short-term counseling, referrals, and follow-up services for employees dealing with personal and/or work-related issues” (U.S. Office of Personnel Management, nd). EAPs are among the most common forms of mental health support available to healthcare workers, with healthcare organizations utilizing them since the 1970s (Couser et al., 2023). The number of sessions provided to employees by their organization, may vary, but 3-6 sessions is common, and the average number of sessions utilized from a global report of EAPs was 5.4 sessions (Attridge, 2024). These programs can be delivered on-site, off-site, or through a hybrid model (Couser et al., 2023). Research has shown that EAPs are linked to reduced stress, lower anxiety, improved mood, and the development of new skills (Couser et al., 2023). Additionally, EAPs have been associated with enhanced work performance, increased organizational commitment, greater job satisfaction, and stronger social support networks (Chen et al., 2021). Despite these positive outcomes, there is evidence suggesting that EAPs may not be fully utilized (Doran, 2022). Langlieb et al. (2021) propose several strategies to maximize the effectiveness of EAPs, including: (1) early surveillance of employee mental health to identify at-risk individuals and track prevalence rates, (2) enhancing the understanding of workplace mental health interventions and developing assessments to measure their quality and effectiveness, and (3) increasing awareness of EAP availability and effectiveness to ensure employees are fully informed and encouraged to make use of these valuable resources (Langlieb et al., 2021). Although healthcare workers reported poorer mental health and increased symptoms during the COVID pandemic, EAP usage in the post-COVID era has remained low (4.25%) (Martin et al., 2024).

Resiliency-Building Trainings

Another intervention explored to support healthcare worker mental health is resiliency-based programs, which aim to enhance resilience—the ability to “withstand, bounce back from, and work through challenging circumstances or events at work” (Hayes et al., 2020, p. 4). These programs focus on building individuals’ resilience to help them better cope with the challenges of healthcare professions. While some resiliency-based programs have shown no statistically significant improvements in well-being or resilience, others have demonstrated notable benefits, including reduced stress, emotional exhaustion, and anxiety, enhanced compassion for others, and presenteeism (Henshall et al., 2023; Mistretta et al., 2018). The content of these programs varies, with some emphasizing goal setting, processing and letting go after medical errors, and cultivating gratitude, while others integrate resilience with mindfulness or self-compassion practices (Bird & Pincavage, 2016; Franco & Christie, 2021; Mistretta et al., 2018). The programs differ in length, with some offering brief, one-day workshops, and others extending over six weeks. Additionally, the delivery method varies, with studies examining the effectiveness of both in-person and virtual formats. Some resiliency-based programs incorporate mindfulness, while others do not have an explicit mindfulness component. Mindfulness-based practices have been heavily explored in a variety of formats in healthcare worker populations but also vary in their delivery approach and measurement.

Mindfulness-Based Programs

Mindfulness is defined as “awareness of one’s internal states and surroundings.” (Association, nd-a). Mindfulness-based programs operate on the assumption that increasing an individual’s ability to observe their thoughts and emotions will lead to a decrease in psychological suffering. Studies have examined both full length, eight-week mindfulness-based stress reduction programs, and shortened versions of this intervention (Mackenzie et al., 2006; Morrow et al., 2022; Shapiro et al., 2017). Overall, results seem to indicate mindfulness as a promising intervention, with reductions in burnout, perceived stress, anxiety, exhaustion, compassion fatigue, and experiential avoidance, and increases in quality of life, self-compassion, and job related personal accomplishment among healthcare worker populations (Duarte & Pinto-Gouveia, 2016; Mackenzie et al., 2006; Morrow et al., 2022; Pascual et al., 2023; Shapiro et al., 2017). Outcomes of interest differ across studies, with the most common outcomes being stress or perceived stress, burnout, compassion, compassion fatigue, life satisfaction, and mindfulness levels. Studies have examined various delivery methods including in person, mobile application, and virtual reality methods (Duarte & Pinto-Gouveia, 2016; Pascual et al., 2023; Wylde et al., 2017). Study designs appear to be split, with pre-post quasi-experimental designs and RCTs being common. Studies have also linked an increase in nurse state mindfulness to an increase in patient satisfaction, indicating that these interventions may benefit both healthcare workers, and subsequently, their patients (Saban et al., 2021). The delivery of these mindfulness-based interventions has varied from self-guided practices, led by a trained staff member, or delivered by expert professionals. In addition to standalone mindfulness-based programs, some interventions incorporate mindfulness as one component within a broader treatment strategy—for example, psychotherapy approaches that include mindfulness as a taught skill.

Psychotherapy-Based Interventions

Recent studies have started to explore the effectiveness of interventions grounded in psychotherapy. While not exhaustive, some of the psychotherapeutic modalities investigated include Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), and Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR). Findings suggest that ACT-based interventions have been linked to improvements in stress, burnout, work presence, treatment efficacy, psychological distress, mindfulness skills, and values-based behavior (Barrett & Stewart, 2021; Gaupp et al., 2020; Prudenzi et al., 2022). CBT-based interventions have been associated with reductions in stress, burnout, and insomnia (Amra et al., 2023; Barrett & Stewart, 2021). EMDR, which can be administered either as a full protocol by a mental health clinician or in an abbreviated form by peer volunteers, has shown promise in both treating and preventing poor mental health outcomes in healthcare workers (Fogliato et al., 2022; Shapiro, 2017). Additionally, a recent protocol exploring self-directed bilateral stimulation processing—known as the Self-Care Traumatic Episode Protocol (STEP)—has demonstrated significant reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress among healthcare workers in a randomized controlled trial (Moench & Billsten, 2021).

While it makes logical sense that healthcare workers experiencing poor mental health symptoms may be best served by clinical treatments, the issue of implementing such treatments is typically feasibility. These treatments may cost more and be less feasible to integrate. In an attempt to address these concerns, one ACT-based workshop demonstrated that the cost of implementing the workshop led to both improved outcomes for patients, and improved presenteeism in workers, ultimately resulting in a return of investment for the employers being compensated ten times what was spent on the workshop (Gaupp et al., 2020; Caille et al., 2023, have proposed a research design specifically with feasibility in mind to integrate EMDR sessions provided by trained clinicians to healthcare employees (Caille et al., 2023). Results of the designed study are not yet available; however the detailed protocol is available and could act as a blueprint for healthcare organizations to test the feasibility of integrating this intervention into practice. Research involving psychotherapeutic modalities should continue to address feasibility concerns including time commitments, costs, and ensuring the intervention can persist outside of study settings.

Why It Is Important to Do This Review

In 2022, Congress passed the Lorna Breen Health Care Provider Protection Act. This law dictates that the Department of Health and Human Services must award grants to hospitals, medical professional associations, and other healthcare entities specifically for programs to promote mental health and resilience in healthcare providers (U.S. Congress, 2022). This review aims to inform the strategic and effective use of these resources by guiding decision makers in awarding funds to the most appropriate programs, as well as supporting the development of evidence-based Request for Proposals (RFPs) that direct future grant funding toward interventions with the strongest demonstrated effectiveness.

Research highlights a clear need for mental health support among healthcare workers, including high rates of burnout and symptoms of depression, anxiety, and trauma across multiple studies and healthcare settings (National Academies of Sciences et al., 2019; Nigam et al., 2023; Sialakis et al., 2023). However, there is currently no comprehensive synthesis identifying which psychosocial interventions are most effective in addressing this need. While previous reviews have broadly examined interventions for healthcare worker mental health (Anger et al., 2024; Chigwedere et al., 2021; Hoedl et al., 2024), this review adopts a more targeted approach in both scope and research design. It focuses specifically on including interventions intended to support healthcare workers during routine conditions, rather than solely during extraordinary events such as pandemics. The absence of a comprehensive, targeted synthesis is particularly consequential given that poor mental health among this population can contribute to critical workforce shortages and threaten the sustainability of the U.S. healthcare system.

Existing Reviews

A recent review by Anger et al. (2024) synthesized evidence-based interventions and resources to improve healthcare workers’ mental health. While similar in scope, their review had broad inclusion criteria, encompassing all study designs, all mental health-related interventions, and healthcare workers across various settings. This broad scope makes it difficult to determine which psychosocial interventions are most effective for improving mental health outcomes specifically among healthcare workers in hospital settings. As a result, the findings offer limited guidance for practitioners and policymakers seeking to implement targeted, evidence-based strategies tailored to particular professional roles, intervention formats, and hospital contexts. In contrast, the proposed review will take a more focused approach, limiting studies to RCTs and Quasi-Experimental Designs (QEDs), examining only psychosocial interventions, and restricting the setting to inpatient hospitals. This narrower scope is expected to enhance homogeneity among included studies, providing a clearer picture of the current evidence specific to hospital-based healthcare workers.

Other recent existing reviews have focused on the mental health of healthcare workers, specifically considering either the COVID-19 pandemic, or broadly during any pandemic (Chigwedere et al., 2021; De Pablo et al., 2020; Ghahramani et al., 2023; Saragih et al., 2021; Vizheh et al., 2020; Zace et al., 2021). This emphasis on pandemic-related contexts limits the applicability of their findings to more routine hospital environments and may overlook evidence about psychosocial interventions that can support healthcare workers’ mental health outside of crisis conditions. A search was conducted in the Campbell protocol and PROSPERO registries to identify any in-progress reviews with a similar scope, and none were identified.

By systematically identifying and evaluating the most effective psychosocial interventions for improving mental health among hospital-based healthcare workers, this review aims to equip healthcare administrators, policymakers, and funders with actionable evidence to inform program development, resource allocation, and policy implementation. Ultimately, these insights can support the strategic investment of resources—such as those authorized under the Lorna Breen Health Care Provider Protection Act—to strengthen the well-being of the healthcare workforce, improve care quality, and mitigate workforce shortages.

Methods

The conduct and reporting of this systematic review will follow the Campbell Standards (Aloe et al., 2024; Higgins et al., 2023). The reporting of this review will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021).

Criteria for Considering Studies for This Review

Types of Studies

When assessing the effects of an intervention, it is crucial to rely on evidence that offers the strongest potential to attribute observed outcomes directly to the intervention, rather than to external or confounding factors. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental designs (QEDs) with a control group will be included in this review due to their established strength in supporting causal inference. By limiting the scope to these rigorous study designs, the review enhances both the internal validity of its findings and the potential to synthesize results meaningfully across studies.

Eligible control conditions include no treatment or usual care groups, waitlist control conditions, attention control, or placebo interventions. In addition, control groups must be distinct from the intervention group, have no or minimal exposure to the active components of the intervention, and be assessed at the same time points and using the same outcome measures as the intervention group. Studies in which both groups receive an active psychosocial intervention will be excluded, as the absence of an inactive or minimal-treatment comparison group limits the ability to estimate absolute intervention effects.

Types of Participants

The population for this review is healthcare workers employed in hospital settings. Healthcare worker is defined as any person employed in a hospital setting who provides direct clinical care for the physical or mental well-being of patients. Healthcare workers should be employed in a hospital physically located within the United States. The location has been restricted to the United States due to the unique contextual factors of the US healthcare system including its predominantly for-profit system, comparatively limited worker protections, and cultural norms around individualism and help-seeking that may influence both the nature of occupational stressors and the acceptability and feasibility of psychosocial interventions. These factors depart meaningfully from the universal healthcare systems of other developed nations and may bring about unique stressors for employees. As medical students, residents, nursing students, and students of other healthcare worker professions provide direct patient care through clinical rotations, they will be included in this review. As this review focuses on interventions delivered within or through hospital-affiliated systems, participants are expected to be actively working at the time of the intervention. Healthcare workers on sick leave are not explicitly excluded; however, given the hospital-based delivery context of eligible interventions, it is anticipated that included studies will predominantly enroll actively working healthcare workers. In situations in which studies include some participants that match criteria, for example a subset of hospital healthcare workers, these studies will be included if information can be extracted for the results pertaining explicitly to this subset. If the results for hospital healthcare workers are mixed with other populations, and their results cannot be extracted separately, those studies will be excluded from this review.

Study participants excluded from this review include healthcare workers who are not employed in hospital settings (e.g. outpatient primary care settings, nursing homes, urgent cares, or clinics), as well as non-healthcare staff such as administrative professionals, financial services, or janitorial staff. Volunteers or other unpaid individuals will also be excluded.

Types of Interventions

This review will focus on psychosocial interventions designed to address psychological and social factors to improve the mental health of healthcare workers. There is no standard definition for psychosocial interventions. This review will adopt a definition of psychosocial that has been employed by other reviews, which includes any interventions in which the primary mechanism of action is through psychological or social processes, examples of which may include therapeutic work, social support, and educational processes (Hoedl et al., 2024). Interventions in this review will not be restricted based on the delivery method. An intervention can be delivered by individuals such as mental health professionals or peers or can be offered through other methods such as online programming or mobile apps. Examples of existing interventions that might be included are peer support groups, psychoeducation, employee assistance programs, mindfulness practice, and psychotherapy-based interventions.

Though prior reviews have provided evidence synthesis related to interventions which may be effective during crisis contexts, this review will not exclude interventions based on crisis context; however, crisis context will be coded during data extraction and reported as part of the descriptive summary of program characteristics, so that readers can understand the context for which the intervention was developed and tested. A potential concern in intervention studies is the lack of completeness in describing the intervention that limits the replicability of the program. Studies reporting interventions that are insufficiently described to allow replication will be included in the review. We will systemically extract data noting the availability of program materials and note these findings in the description of each intervention.

Interventions that will be excluded include pharmacological interventions, purely physical health interventions, general educational interventions with no therapeutic or social support components, and technological tools with no psychosocial components – such as those that track mood, sleep, etc. Interventions that address organizational or policy-related changes as their sole mechanism of action, without a distinct psychosocial component, will also be excluded. Multicomponent interventions that include organizational elements alongside a clearly described psychosocial component will be considered eligible if the psychosocial component is specifically designed to address one of the targeted mental health outcomes and an eligible outcome is reported per outcome criteria.

Types of Outcome Measures

Primary Outcomes

Studies must report at least one of the following primary outcomes to be included in the review: post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, secondary traumatic stress (STS), vicarious trauma (VT), compassion fatigue, moral injury, burnout, depression symptoms, and anxiety symptoms. Only outcomes assessed using standardized, validated measures will be included. For each outcome domain, eligible measures must have demonstrated reliability and validity. Studies relying solely on single, non-validated items to assess primary outcomes will be excluded. A list of eligible instruments for each outcome domain will be compiled prior to screening using PsycTESTS (American Psychological Association, nd-b) and the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) database and will be documented in the data extraction form (COSMIN, nd). Given the overlap among these constructs and the potential for measures to be used interchangeably or labeled inconsistently (e.g., the same instrument reporting outcomes as either secondary traumatic stress or compassion fatigue), outcomes will be categorized based on the primary measurement tool used.

In the event that intervention studies do not pre-designate a primary outcome, all outcomes relevant to this review’s primary and secondary outcome domains will be extracted regardless of how the study authors designated them. Where multiple outcomes of the same domain are reported within a single study, we will extract all of the available outcome data that meets outcome criteria. See analysis section for details about how this data will be handled in the meta-analysis.

Secondary Outcomes

The following secondary outcomes will be extracted from included studies if reported, however the presence or absence of these outcomes does not influence study inclusion. These outcomes include turnover intention and turnover rates. Secondary outcomes were selected to document effects related to the ongoing concern of healthcare workforce shortages. Outcomes such as quality of life, resilience, overall well-being, mental health stigma, and work climate, while potentially relevant, are considered beyond the scope of this review.

Duration of Follow-Up

We will include outcomes measured at post-intervention and all follow-up time points.

Types of Settings

This review focuses on psychosocial interventions that are integrated into, or delivered through, hospital-affiliated systems, with the goal of identifying practices that can be embedded into hospital organizations as part of routine employee or staff support services. Therefore, only interventions that are formally affiliated with a hospital or health system will be included. For the purposes of this review, hospital-affiliated refers to programs that are administered, funded, coordinated, or endorsed by a hospital or healthcare institution, and are intended to support individuals within the hospital workforce (e.g., clinicians, nurses, support staff).

Examples of eligible interventions include employee assistance programs operated by or in partnership with the hospital, on-site peer support initiatives led or sanctioned by the hospital, or mental health programs implemented within hospital premises for hospital staff. Interventions that are community-based and not institutionally connected to a hospital—for instance, support groups offered independently in the community without hospital oversight or integration—will be excluded.

Search Methods for Identification of Studies

We will conduct a comprehensive search to identify both published and unpublished studies, drawing on multiple electronic databases, grey literature sources, trial registries, relevant websites, reference lists of prior reviews and studies, and consultations with subject matter experts (MacDonald et al., 2024). The search strategy is designed to capture studies addressing primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes, including turnover rates or intention, will be extracted from included studies when reported but will not be used as search terms. The search strategy and its results will be documented in detail to enable the development of a transparent flow chart outlining the study selection process. The search will be restricted to studies disseminated in 2010 or later to reflect contemporary interventions. We will restrict studies to those published in English, as resource constraints prevent the review team from conducting searches in or translating studies published in other languages. We acknowledge that this restriction represents a potential source of language bias, as relevant studies published in other languages may be excluded. Though studies will be restricted to those published in English, English-language database filters will not be applied during the search, as doing so may unintentionally exclude potentially relevant studies at the search stage. A sample search strategy for the MEDLINE(OVID) database is located in Appendix A.

Electronic Searches

We will search MEDLINE (OVID), Scopus, CINAHL (EBSCO), ProQuest Sociological Abstracts, APA PsychInfo (EBSCO), ProQuest Dissertation and Theses Global, and PTSDpubs electronic databases. We will also search the Web of Science Core Collection, which includes scholarly journals, books, and proceedings from the Science Citation Index, Social Sciences Citation Index, Arts and Humanities Citation Index, and Emerging Sources Citation Index.

Searching Other Resources

In addition to searching electronic databases, we will examine ClinicalTrials.gov and relevant grey literature from key governmental and organizational sources, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), American Hospital Association, NIH RePORTER, and American Nurses Association. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we will also screen the reference lists of all included studies and perform forward citation searches using Google Scholar to identify additional eligible studies that have cited the included works. Additionally, we will contact subject matter experts to identify any unpublished or ongoing studies that may meet inclusion criteria.

Data Collection and Analysis

Selection of Studies

One reviewer will conduct the initial searches for all sources and will save the results of each source in an electronic format to EndNote reference management software (Clarivate, 2024). The reviewer will then upload these references into Rayyan, an electronic platform for systematic review management (Ouzzani et al., 2016). Though Rayyan offers integrated artificial intelligence functionality that allows reviewers to prioritize or exclude studies based on likelihood of inclusion, review authors will not exclude any studies without manual review of the title and abstract. Automated deduplication features in Rayyan will be used to resolve duplicate studies. References that are determined to be 97% or greater similarity, or an exact DOI match, will be auto-resolved, and any remaining duplicates will be manually resolved by one reviewer. Following de-duplication, one reviewer will remove results that are obviously ineligible through title and abstract screening (non-empirical report, book review, editorial, not human subjects, etc.). The full text of all remaining references will be retrieved and uploaded into Rayyan. Two reviewers will then independently screen each of the reports for eligibility using a structured screening form. Decisions will be automatically tracked in the Rayyan system, and any disagreements will be discussed and resolved through consensus.

Data Extraction and Management

Two reviewers will independently extract data from all reports of the included studies using a structured data extraction form. When multiple reports pertain to the same study, they will be collated to ensure comprehensive and non-duplicative data extraction. Extracted data will include bibliographic details, source descriptors, study methods and procedures, contextual factors, intervention characteristics, sample demographics, settings outcome measures, and effect sizes.

To ensure clarity and consistency, the data extraction form will be pilot-tested on a sample of three studies. This process will help refine the tool and establish a shared understanding among reviewers. Following the pilot, two reviewers will independently extract data from 100% of the included studies. Any discrepancies in the extracted data will be discussed and resolved through consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, a third member of the review team will be consulted to resolve the discrepancy.

Assessment of Risk of Bias in Included Studies

Two review authors will independently assess risk of bias for all included studies. Any disagreements in risk of bias judgments between the two review authors will be resolved through discussion and consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, a third member of the review team will be consulted. We will use ROB 2: A revised Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized controlled trials to assess risk of bias in randomized controlled trials (Sterne et al., 2019). The ROB 2 assesses risk of bias through evaluating a study’s biases arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. Reviewers will use this standardized tool to assign judgments of low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias. We will use the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies – of Interventions Version 2 (ROBINS-I V2) to assess risk of bias in included quasi-experimental designs (Sterne & Higgins., 2025). The ROBINS-I V2 will assess for risk of bias related to confounding variables, selection of participants into the study, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. The standardized tool will be used to assign each study as low risk, moderate risk, serious risk, or critical risk of bias. We will report the outcomes of the risk of bias assessments for each domain, as well as the overall risk of bias assigned to each study.

Measures of Treatment Effect

In addition to providing a descriptive summary of each included study, we will estimate effect sizes for all outcomes of interest (Borenstein et al., 2009). For randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental designs (QEDs), we will quantify effect sizes using appropriate metrics based on outcome type. For continuous outcomes, we will report standardized mean differences using Hedges’ g, which includes a correction for small sample bias. For dichotomous outcomes, we will report odds ratios. We will also report 95% confidence intervals for all effect sizes. All effect sizes will be coded so that a positive value reflects a favorable outcome for the treatment or intervention group compared to the comparison group.

Unit of Analysis Issues

To ensure that each included study represents an independent finding, we will carefully review all records to identify multiple reports stemming from the same underlying study. When duplicate or companion reports are identified, we will designate one as the primary source and link the others as supplementary reports. Data from all reports will be used to inform coding and data extraction to ensure completeness, but the study will be counted only once in analyses.

We do not anticipate crossover designs. For studies with clustering (e.g., participants randomized by hospital, department, etc.), we will follow the procedures in the What Works Clearinghouse Procedures and Standards Handbook, Version 5.0 (What Works Clearing House, 2022). We will adjust effect size estimates and standard errors following WWC guidance, using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) when clustering was not accounted for in original analyses.

For outcomes measured at multiple time points, we will compute separate effect sizes for each time point and synthesize like time points across studies.

Criteria for Determination of Independent Findings

The primary outcomes of this review are burnout, secondary traumatic stress, vicarious trauma, compassion fatigue, moral injury, anxiety symptoms, depression symptoms, and PTSD. Due to conceptual overlap, as well as overlap in the measures used to measure these concepts, we anticipate separating outcomes related to empathy-based strain, including secondary traumatic stress, compassion fatigue, and vicarious trauma into an “empathy-based strain” domain. These outcomes will be categorized according to the measurement tool utilized by the study authors. Other outcomes, including moral injury, depression, anxiety, burnout, and PTSD are anticipated to remain distinct and each of these outcome domains will be analyzed separately.

It is anticipated that some included studies may involve more than one intervention or include multiple comparison groups. In studies with multiple interventions, we will select the intervention most directly aligned with the review’s inclusion criteria (e.g. psychosocial component), as defined in this protocol. In studies with multiple comparison groups, preference will be given to comparison condition in line with the majority of included studies or the more active condition (e.g., treatment as usual over waitlist).

If multiple validated measures assess the same construct, we will include all measures and use robust variance estimation (RVE; Hedges et al., 2010; Tipton, 2015) to account for the statistical dependency among effect sizes arising from multiple measures within the same study.

Dealing With Missing Data

If missing data is present, review authors will contact primary study authors to attempt to collect the missing data. Review authors will document contact attempts and allow four weeks for primary study authors to provide the missing data to review authors before concluding that the primary authors will not provide the information. All studies will be included in the review despite missing data, but only studies with sufficient effect size data reported will be included in the meta-analysis.

Assessment of Heterogeneity

To assess the degree of heterogeneity among included studies, review authors will perform a Q test to compare the observed variance to what would be expected due to sampling error alone. A statistically significant Q test (p < .05) will be interpreted as indicating the presence of heterogeneity beyond sampling error (Cochran, 1954). Review authors will use the I2 statistic to determine the percentage of variation explained by heterogeneity, rather than chance. We will consider the I2 statistic alongside the 95% prediction interval and visual inspection of forest plots to judge the clinical and statistical relevance of any observed heterogeneity (Higgins et al., 2003). Given that some degree of heterogeneity is expected across studies due to differences in intervention type, participant characteristics, and outcome measurement, the presence of heterogeneity will be addressed through the use of a random-effects model and explored through planned moderator analyses as described below.

Assessment of Reporting Biases

To assess the presence of publication bias, we will generate a funnel plot to visually examine potential asymmetry that may suggest missing studies (Egger et al., 1997). Additionally, for studies with available protocols or trial registrations, we will compare the registered outcomes with the published results to evaluate selective outcome reporting.

Data Synthesis

Following the estimation of individual study-level effects, we will conduct separate meta-analyses for each outcome of interest. Given that compassion fatigue, secondary traumatic stress, and vicarious trauma are often measured using similar standardized instruments, we will combine these constructs under a single outcome category termed ‘empathy-based strain’ when appropriate. To calculate a pooled effect size across studies, we will use inverse-variance weighting. Each study’s effect size will be weighted by the inverse of its variance, such that more reliable estimates contribute more heavily to the overall summary. The pooled effect size will then be computed as the weighted average of individual effect sizes.

We plan to use a random-effects model to account for expected heterogeneity across studies, as differences in study, intervention and participant characteristics are likely to lead to variability in effect sizes (DerSimonian & Laird, 1986). This approach assumes that the true effects may vary across studies and provides more conservative estimates that better reflect this diversity. Studies will be pooled separately based on design, with randomized controlled trials (RCTs) analyzed independently from quasi-experimental designs (QEDs), unless sufficient homogeneity justifies pooling them to enhance statistical power.

Meta-analyses will be conducted using the ‘robumeta’ package in R (Fisher et al., 2017), employing robust variance estimation (RVE) with small-sample corrections (Hedges et al., 2010) when multiple effect sizes are reported within a single study. RVE will be used as the primary method for handling dependent effect sizes throughout all meta-analyses in this review, as it appropriately adjusts for nonindependent sampling errors due to correlated outcomes from the same study.

Subgroup Analysis and Investigation of Heterogeneity

If the number of included studies allows, we will perform a moderation analysis to investigate whether study characteristics or methods are associated with effect size. If data is available to conduct a moderation analysis, meta-regression will be used for continuous outcomes and Analysis of Variance will be used to analyze categorical outcomes (Borenstein et al., 2009). Moderation variables of interest include study design (RCT or QED), duration of treatment, type of healthcare worker targeted, delivery of the intervention including format (in-person, online, virtual reality) and intervention facilitator (licensed mental health professional, peer, other).

Study design will be examined as a potential source of heterogeneity to determine whether observed treatment effects are influenced by the type of research design. If study design accounts for a significant proportion of the variance, it may suggest that differences in outcomes are due more to methodological factors than the intervention itself, raising questions about the true effectiveness of the intervention.

Given that healthcare workers occupy diverse roles within the healthcare system and may respond to stress differently, professional role may influence intervention outcomes. If the data permit, we will explore the type of healthcare worker (e.g., nurses, physicians, support staff) as a potential moderator of intervention effects.

The format of the intervention (face-to-face versus online) may also impact intervention effectiveness. For instance, face-to-face interventions may foster stronger interpersonal connections and allow facilitators to tailor content in real time based on participant reactions. In contrast, online interventions, while potentially less personal, may offer greater flexibility—an important consideration for hospital-based healthcare workers who often work long or irregular shifts.

Additionally, we will investigate whether the type of facilitator moderates intervention outcomes. We hypothesize that interventions delivered by trained mental health professionals may differ in effectiveness compared to interventions delivered by peers or non-clinical facilitators.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the overall findings under more stringent methodological conditions. First, we will re-run all meta-analyses excluding studies identified as outliers based on visual inspection of forest plots and compare the resulting estimates to those from the full sample. Second, we will re-run all meta-analyses excluding studies at high risk of bias, specifically, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) rated as high risk of bias using the ROB2 tool, and quasi-experimental designs (QEDs) rated as critical risk of bias using the ROBINS-I V2 tool, and compare the resulting effect size estimates to those from the full sample. If pooled effect sizes remain stable after these exclusions, this will provide evidence that the overall findings are robust. Meaningful differences in effect size estimates between the full and restricted samples will be interpreted as indicating that results should be treated with caution.

Treatment of Qualitative Research

We do not plan to include qualitative research.

Summary of Findings and Assessment of the Certainty of the Evidence

A formal certainty of evidence assessment using the GRADE framework will not be conducted in this review. While this review aims to inform practice and policy, it does so by synthesizing evidence to guide program development and resource allocation decisions rather than by generating specific treatment recommendations – the contexts for which GRADE was specifically developed (Schünemann et al., 2013). The findings of this review are intended to provide insight into which psychosocial interventions show promise for supporting healthcare worker mental health in hospital settings, and to inform decisions about program investment implementation. The trustworthiness of findings will instead be addressed through domain-level risk of bias reporting using the ROB 2 and ROBINS-I V2 tools, combined with planned sensitivity analyses excluding high risk or bias studies, which provide readers with the methodological transparency needed to appropriately contextualize the evidence.
