A fractal-like configuration of point-line pairs for the minimal distance problem
Alexander Logunov, Dmitrii Zakharov

Abstract
. We show that for every n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} there is a collection of points p 1 , … , p n p_{1},\ldots,p_{n} and lines ℓ 1 , … , ℓ n \ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n} in the unit square such that for any i i we have p i ∈ ℓ i p_{i}\in\ell_{i} and the distance from p i p_{i} to any other line ℓ j \ell_{j} is at least c ​ n γ − 1 cn^{\gamma-1} for some universal constants c , γ > 0 c,\gamma>0 . This is better than a trivial construction by a polynomial factor.



1.
Introduction

In
[
2
]
, Cohen, Pohoata and the second author considered the following problem about collections of point-line pairs. For an integer
n\geqslant 2
, what is the largest
\delta>0
such that there are points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in[0,1]^{2}
and lines
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
p_{i}\in\ell_{i}
and for every
i\neq j
we have
d(p_{i},\ell_{j})\geqslant\delta
? By taking
p_{i}=(i/n,0)
and
\ell_{i}=p_{i}+(0,1)\mathbb{R}
it is easy too see that
\delta\geqslant 1/n
. On the other hand, by tiling
[0,1]^{2}
with
\frac{1}{[\sqrt{n-1}]}\times\frac{1}{[\sqrt{n-1}]}
-squares we see that among any
n
points in the unit square there is a pair at distance at most
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{[\sqrt{n-1}]}
. So using that
d(p_{i},\ell_{j})\leqslant d(p_{i},p_{j})
this leads to the upper bound
\delta\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{[\sqrt{n-1}]}
.

In
[
2
]
, authors managed to improve this simple upper bound to
\delta\leqslant n^{-2/3+o(1)}
. As a corollary, they obtained a new upper bound for Heilbronn’s triangle problem which asks to determine the smallest number
\Delta(n)
such that among any
n
points in unit square there exists a triangle with area at most
\Delta(n)
. The connection between these two problems can be seen as follows: given a set of
n
points in
[0,1]^{2}
we can find a collection of at least
n/10
disjoint pairs
\{p_{i},q_{i}\}
among these points so that
d(p_{i},q_{i})\leqslant 2n^{-1/2}
for all
i
. Let
\ell_{i}
be the line passing through
p_{i},q_{i}
and note that if
d(p_{i},\ell_{j})\leqslant\delta
for some
i\neq j
then the points
p_{i},p_{j},q_{j}
form a triangle of area at most
\Delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}d(p_{i},\ell_{j})d(p_{j},q_{j})\leqslant n^{-1/2}\delta
. So better upper bounds on the minimal distance
\delta
lead to better upper bounds on the area of smallest triangle
\Delta
. In particular, the upper bound
\delta\leqslant n^{-2/3+o(1)}
gives
\Delta\leqslant n^{-7/6+o(1)}
.
This estimate improves on previous work on Heilbronn’s triangle problem by Roth
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
, Komlós–Pintz–Szemerédi
[
3
]
and Cohen, Pohoata and the second author
[
1
]
and reaches a certain ‘high-low’ limit of the method.

Many authors (see e.g. the table on page 34 in
[
1
]
) have guessed that the bound
\Delta(n)\lesssim n^{-3/2}
should hold based on the following heuristic.
Let
\ell_{j}=\overline{p_{j}q_{j}}
be lines defined as above and let
H_{j}\subset[0,1]^{2}
be the strip of points
x\in[0,1]^{2}
at distance at most
\leqslant K/n
to
\ell_{j}
. If the points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
were uniformly distributed in
[0,1]^{2}
then we would expect that about
|H_{j}|n\sim K
of them would land in the strip
H_{j}
. So for
K\gg 1
there should be lots of points in the strip
H_{j}
and so we should be able to find a triangle with area at most
2n^{-1/2}(K/n)\lesssim n^{-3/2}
. One way to turn this idea into a rigorous proof is to show that in the setup in the first paragraph, we have the upper bound
\delta\lesssim n^{-1}
. Because of this, it seemed plausible to the authors of
[
2
]
that the upper bound
\delta\leqslant n^{-1+o(1)}
holds. In Appendix B of
[
2
]
, they further note that this estimate, if true, would also imply non-trivial upper bounds for Heilbronn’s problem for
k
-gons.

In this note, we show that this is not the case, namely that there are collections of point-line pairs with
\delta\gtrsim n^{\gamma-1}
for some (small) constant
\gamma>0
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist constants
c,\gamma>0
such that for any
n\geqslant 2
there are points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in[0,1]^{2}
and lines
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
such that
p_{i}\in\ell_{i}
and
d(p_{i},\ell_{j})\geqslant cn^{\gamma-1}
for any
i\neq j
.

Similar lower bounds hold for higher dimensional variants of this problem. For example, the same proof works for collections of point-line pairs in
[0,1]^{d}
for any
d\geqslant 2
, we omit the details since they are essentially identical. On the other hand, Theorem
1.1
does not imply anything new about Heilbronn’s triangle problem. However it does hint at the possibility that there could be better lower bound constructions: the current best lower bound
\Delta(n)\gtrsim\frac{\log n}{n^{2}}
due to Komlós–Pintz–Szemerédi
[
4
]
is a slight improvement over the ‘easy’ lower bound
\Delta(n)\gtrsim n^{-2}
due to Erdős.

In the next section we present the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The proof consists of two parts: first, we use the probabilistic method to beat the lower bound by a logarithmic factor and second, we use a recursive construction to amplify this improvement into a polynomial gain. In particular, the set of points and lines we construct is essentially a self-affine fractal. Self-affine fractals might be an interesting special class of configurations for further study of the minimal distance problem.

2.
Proof

To ease the recursive construction it is convenient to introduce the following notation (very similar to the one used in
[
2
]
).
Let
\Omega=[-1,1]^{2}\times[-1,1]
, where we view
\Omega
as the configuration space of incident point-line pairs in the plane. Namely, for
(p,\theta)\in\Omega
let
\ell=\ell(p,\theta)=p+(1,\theta)\mathbb{R}
be the line with slope
\theta
through
p
. So we can identify a subset
X\subset\Omega
with the point-line configuration
\{(p,\ell(p,\theta)),~~(p,\theta)\in X\}
.

For
\omega=(x,y,\theta),\omega^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\in\Omega
define the vertical distance
d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=|y-y^{\prime}-\theta^{\prime}(x-x^{\prime})|
. For
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega
,
d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
measures the vertical distance from the point
p=(x,y)
to the line
\ell^{\prime}=\ell^{\prime}(p^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
, with
p^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. Since
|\theta^{\prime}|\leqslant 1
it coincides with the euclidean distance up to a constant factor.
For a point-line configuration
X\subset\Omega
define the
minimal distance
of
X
as

d(X)=\min_{\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}\in X}d(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).

For
\delta>0
define
n(\delta)
to be the largest integer
n
such that there exists
X\subset\Omega
with
d(X)\geqslant\delta
and
|X|=n
. With this notation, it is easy to see that Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

There exist constants
\gamma,c>0
such that
n(\delta)\geqslant c\delta^{-1-\gamma}
for all
\delta\in(0,1)
.

Theorem
2.1
now follows the following two lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that
n(\delta)\geqslant c_{1}\delta^{-1}\log 1/\delta
for all
\delta\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a constant
C_{2}\geqslant 1
such that for any
w,\delta\in(0,1)
such that
\delta\leqslant w^{2}
we have
n(\delta)\geqslant w^{-1}n(C_{2}w)n(\delta/w^{2})
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
w\in(0,1)
be such that
\log(1/C_{2}w)\geqslant ec_{1}^{-1}C_{2}
holds. Then for any
\delta\leqslant w^{2}
we have

n(\delta)\geqslant w^{-1}n(C_{2}w)n(\delta/w^{2})\geqslant c_{1}C_{2}^{-1}w^{-2}\log(1/C_{2}w)n(\delta/w^{2})\geqslant ew^{-2}n(\delta/w^{2}).

Now fix
w
and let
\delta\in[w^{2m+2},w^{2m}]
for an integer
m\geqslant 1
, then iterating this inequality
m
times gives

n(\delta)\geqslant e^{m}w^{-2m}n(\delta/w^{2m})\geqslant e^{m}w^{-2m}\geqslant e^{m}w^{2}\delta^{-1}\geqslant c\delta^{-1-\gamma}

for some
c=c(w)>0
and
\gamma=\gamma(w)>0
.

\Box

In the remainder of the section we prove the two lemmas.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

If is enough to prove the lemma for all sufficiently small
\delta\leqslant\delta_{0}
(for
\delta>\delta_{0}
we can just choose
c_{1}
small enough). For
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\theta\in[-1,1]
let
T(p,\theta)=p+(1,\theta)\mathbb{R}+\{0\}\times[-\delta,\delta]
, i.e.
T(p,\theta)
is a strip of vertical width
2\delta
through
p
at slope
\theta
.

Let
N=[0.1\delta^{-1}(\log 1/\delta)]
.
Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}\in[0,1]^{2}
be a uniformly random and mutually independent collection of points and let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}\}
.
For each point
p_{i}
, it suffices to show that the probability that there exists an angle
\theta\in[-1,1]
such that
T(p_{i},\theta)\cap P=\{p_{i}\}
is at least
1/2
(for
\delta\leqslant\delta_{0}
). Indeed, by linearity of expectation this would give a collection of
N/2
points among
p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}
so that for every
p_{i}
in the collection there is an angle
\theta
such that
T(p_{i},\theta)\cap P=\{p_{i}\}
. In particular, this gives
n(\delta)\geqslant N/2\geqslant c\delta^{-1}\log 1/\delta
.

Fix an index
i
.
Let
Q_{i}=p_{i}+[-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{N}},\frac{1}{4\sqrt{N}}]^{2}
.
Note that for
i^{\prime}\neq i
we have
p_{i^{\prime}}\in Q_{i}
with probability at most
|Q_{i}|\leqslant\frac{1}{4N}
. So by the union bound we have

\Pr[Q_{i}\cap P\neq\{p_{i}\}]\leqslant(N-1)|Q_{i}|\leqslant 1/4.

So with probability at least
3/4
there are no points inside
Q_{i}
besides
p_{i}
. So it suffices to show that with probability at least
3/4
there exists a slope
\theta
such that
(T(p_{i},\theta)\setminus Q_{i})\cap P=\emptyset
.

Let
P^{\prime}=P\setminus\{p_{i}\}
.
Denote
M=[\frac{1}{8\delta\sqrt{N}}]
and let
\theta_{j}=8j\delta\sqrt{N}
, for
j=1,\ldots,M
. Note that for any
j\neq j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
we have
T(p_{i},\theta_{j})\cap T(p_{i},\theta_{j^{\prime}})\subset Q_{i}
. We now estimate the probability that
P^{\prime}\cap(T(p_{i},\theta_{j})\setminus Q_{i})=\emptyset
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
.
For a region
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the probability that
A\cap P^{\prime}=\emptyset
is equal to
(1-|A\cap[0,1]^{2}|)^{N-1}
. By mutual independence of the collection
\{p_{1}\ldots,p_{N}\}
, this also holds if
A
is a random set depending only on
p_{i}
. Since
T(p_{i},\theta)
has vertical width
2\delta
, we have
|T(p_{i},\theta)\cap[0,1]^{2}|\leqslant 2\delta
. So we can choose pairwise disjoint sets
A_{j}\subset[0,1]^{2}
of area
2\delta
so that
A_{j}
contains
T(p_{i},\theta_{j})\cap[0,1]^{2}\setminus Q_{i}
.

Let
Z_{j}=1_{A_{j}\cap P^{\prime}=\emptyset}
be the indicator of the event that
A_{j}\cap P^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}Z_{j}=(1-2\delta)^{N-1}\geqslant\exp(-2\delta(N-1))\geqslant\exp(-2\delta\times 0.1\delta^{-1}\log(1/\delta))\geqslant\delta^{0.2}.

Denote
q=(1-2\delta)^{N-1}
. So the sum
Z=\sum_{j=1}^{M}Z_{j}
has expectation
\mathbb{E}Z=Mq
. Since
M=[\frac{1}{8\delta\sqrt{N}}]\sim(\delta\log(1/\delta))^{-1/2}\gg\delta^{-0.2}
, we have
Mq>16
for
\delta\leqslant\delta_{0}
. We note that the variables
Z_{j}
are negatively correlated: for
j\neq j^{\prime}
the product
Z_{j}Z_{j^{\prime}}
is the indicator of the event that
P^{\prime}\cap(A_{j}\cup A_{j^{\prime}})=\emptyset
and so
\mathbb{E}Z_{j}Z_{j^{\prime}}=(1-4\delta)^{N-1}\leqslant q^{2}=(\mathbb{E}Z_{j})(\mathbb{E}Z_{j^{\prime}})
. For
j=j^{\prime}
we have
\mathbb{E}Z_{j}Z_{j^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}Z_{j}=q
.
We use this to estimate the variance of
Z
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}Z=\mathbb{E}Z^{2}-(\mathbb{E}Z)^{2}=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{M}\left(\mathbb{E}Z_{j}Z_{j^{\prime}}-(\mathbb{E}Z_{j})(\mathbb{E}Z_{j^{\prime}})\right)\leqslant Mq.

By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\Pr[|Z-\mathbb{E}Z|\geqslant\mathbb{E}Z/2]\leqslant 4\frac{\operatorname{Var}Z}{(\mathbb{E}Z)^{2}}\leqslant\frac{4}{Mq}\leqslant 1/4.

We conclude that for every
i
, the probability that there exists
\theta
with
T(p_{i},\theta)\cap P=\{p_{i}\}
is at least
1/2
which in turn implies that
n(\delta)\geqslant N/2\geqslant c\delta^{-1}\log 1/\delta
, as desired.

\Box

Fix
w>0
. For
(p_{0},\theta_{0})=(x_{0},y_{0},\theta_{0})\in\Omega
let us define an affine map
\psi^{(w)}_{p_{0},\theta_{0}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

\psi^{(w)}_{p_{0},\theta_{0}}(x,y)=(x_{0}+wx,y_{0}+w\theta_{0}x+w^{2}y)

and extend it to a map on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
by

\psi^{(w)}_{p_{0},\theta_{0}}(x,y,\theta)=(x_{0}+wx,y_{0}+w\theta_{0}x+w^{2}y,\theta_{0}+w\theta).

We note that if
|x_{0}|,|y_{0}|,|\theta_{0}|\leqslant 1/2
and
w\leqslant 1/2
, then
\psi^{(w)}_{p_{0},\theta_{0}}(\Omega)\subset\Omega
, i.e. we get a well-defined map on the configuration space of point-line pairs. Roughly speaking, the map
\psi^{(w)}_{p_{0},\theta_{0}}
sends a point line pair inside
[-1,1]^{2}
to a point-line pair inside of a
w\times w^{2}
rectangle centered at
p_{0}
and inclined by slope
\theta_{0}
.

The rescaling map
\psi=\psi^{(w)}_{p_{0},\theta_{0}}
interacts well with the distance
d
: a straightforward computation shows that for
\omega=(x,y,\theta),\omega^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
we have

d(\psi(\omega),\psi(\omega^{\prime}))=w^{2}|y-y^{\prime}-\theta^{\prime}(x-x^{\prime})|=w^{2}d(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).

In particular, for any
X\subset\Omega
we have
d(\psi(X))=w^{2}d(X)
. Now we use the rescaling maps to prove Lemma
2.2
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Fix a sufficiently large constant
C
and let
w\in(0,1/4C^{2})
. Let
X_{0}\subset\Omega
be a configuration of size
k=n(4C^{2}w)
such that
d(X_{0})\geqslant 4C^{2}w
. Then
X_{1}=\psi^{(1/2)}_{(0,0),0}(X_{0})\subset[-1/2,1/2]\times[-1/4,1/4]\times[-1/2,1/2]
is a configuration with
d(X_{1})\geqslant C^{2}w
.
Write
X_{1}=\{(p_{1},\theta_{1}),\ldots,(p_{k},\theta_{k})\}
.
For
|j|\leqslant w^{-1}
let
p_{i,j}=p_{i}+(0,Cjw^{2})
. Note that if
C\geqslant 4
then
p_{i,j}\in[-1/2,1/2]^{2}
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,k
and
|j|\leqslant w^{-1}
we then have an affine map
\psi_{i,j}=\psi_{p_{i,j},\theta_{i}}^{(w)}:\Omega\to\Omega
.

Now given an arbitrary
X\subset\Omega
we define a new configuration
\tilde{X}
by

\tilde{X}=\bigcup_{i\in[k],|j|\leqslant w^{-1}}\psi_{i,j}(X).

We claim that this is a disjoint union and for an appropriate
C
we have
d(\tilde{X})\geqslant w^{2}\min(d(X),1)
. Given this, we can finish the proof as follows: by construction, we have
|\tilde{X}|=k(2[w^{-1}]-1)|X|\geqslant n(4C^{2}w)w^{-1}|X|
. For
\delta\leqslant w^{2}
and
w\leqslant 1/4C^{2}
we then get

n(\delta)\geqslant|\tilde{X}|\geqslant n(4C^{2}w)w^{-1}|X|\geqslant n(4C^{2}w)w^{-1}n(\delta/w^{2}).

Now it remains to show that for any
(\tilde{p},\tilde{\theta})\neq(\tilde{p}^{\prime},\tilde{\theta}^{\prime})\in\tilde{X}
we have
d((\tilde{p},\tilde{\theta}),(\tilde{p}^{\prime},\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}))\geqslant w^{2}\min(d(X),1)
. We can write

\tilde{\omega}=\psi_{i,j}(p,\theta),\quad\tilde{\omega}^{\prime}=\psi_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(p^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}),

for some
i,i^{\prime}\in[k]
,
|j|,|j^{\prime}|\leqslant w^{-1}
and
(p,\theta),(p^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\in X
. We split into three cases:

•

Suppose that
i\neq i^{\prime}
. One can check that we have
\tilde{p}=\psi_{i,j}(p)\in p_{i}+[-w,w]\times[-2Cw,2Cw]
and
\tilde{p}^{\prime}=\psi_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})\in p_{i^{\prime}}+[-w,w]\times[-2Cw,2Cw]
. Furthermore, we have
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}\in\theta_{i^{\prime}}+[-w,w]
and so we get

\displaystyle d((\tilde{p},\tilde{\theta}),(\tilde{p}^{\prime},\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\geqslant d((p_{i},\theta_{i}),(p_{i^{\prime}},\theta_{i^{\prime}}))-(4C+4)w

\displaystyle\geqslant d(X_{1})-(4C+4)w\geqslant(C^{2}-4C-4)w\geqslant w

for
C\geqslant 5
.

•

Suppose that
i=i^{\prime}
but
j\neq j^{\prime}
. In this case, one can check that for
p=(x,y)
and
p^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

d((\tilde{p},\tilde{\theta}),(\tilde{p}^{\prime},\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}))=|Cw^{2}(j-j^{\prime})+w^{2}(y-y^{\prime}-\theta^{\prime}(x-x^{\prime}))|\geqslant Cw^{2}-4w^{2}\geqslant w^{2},

where we used
|j-j^{\prime}|\geqslant 1
and
C\geqslant 5
.

•

Finally, suppose that
i=i^{\prime}
,
j=j^{\prime}
and
(p,\theta)\neq(p^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
. Then we know that

d((\tilde{p},\tilde{\theta}),(\tilde{p}^{\prime},\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}))=w^{2}d((p,\theta),(p^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}))\geqslant w^{2}d(X).

Putting the three cases together this implies the desired bound on
d(\tilde{X})
which completes the proof.

\Box
