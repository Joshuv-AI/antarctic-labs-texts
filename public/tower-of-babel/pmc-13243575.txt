Deep learning-based pathomics signature predicts prognosis and treatment response in gastric cancer: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Hao Wang, Hao Li, Keru Ma, Genshen Mo, Meihong Yan, Xinyue Zhang, Haonan Xie, Yuze Huang, Huiying Li, Yingwei Xue, Peng Han, Shenghan Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe existing TNM staging system provides insufficient prognostic information in gastric cancer (GC) patients. This study aims to establish a pathomics signature of GC (PSGC) that uses deep learning (DL) to directly analyze H&E slides for predicting GC outcomes. We propose a multi-scale graph neural network with gated attention mechanism for multi-instance learning (MS-GMIL) for the construction of PSGC. Moreover, transcriptomic data investigated the possible pathophysiological mechanisms of the PSGC. The PSGC was identified as an independent prognostic factor in all cohorts. Patients with stage II and III GC, along with a high PSGC, showed considerable benefits from chemotherapy and an effective response to immunotherapy. The primary histological features underlying the PSGC were tumor cell anaplasia, intraepithelial neoplasia, tumor-stroma fibrosis, and intestinal epithelial metaplasia. Moreover, the PSGC was associated with cell cycle regulation, drug resistance pathways, and mechanisms of cancer progression. The PSGC functions as a valuable tool in clinical decision-making for the management of GC, providing insights into the underlying pathogenic mechanisms.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Gastroenterology, Oncology

Introduction

Despite significant advancements in reducing the incidence and mortality rates of gastric cancer (GC), the disease continues to impose a considerable global health burden1. Prognostic evaluation and therapeutic decision-making for GC remain predominantly guided by the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system2. However, substantial variability in clinical outcomes has been found among all patients with the same TNM stage undergoing similar intervention protocols3. This inconsistency indicates that the current TNM classification does not provide adequate prognostic precision, emphasizing the urgent need for novel biomarkers to improve prognosis assessment, personalized therapeutic strategies, and optimize clinical outcomes for GC patients.

Recent progress in molecular omics has led to the identification of several molecular markers related to the prognosis and progression of GC, i.e., microsatellite instability (MSI), tumor mutation burden, PD-L1 expression, and Epstein–Barr virus infection status4. However, the broad clinical application of these biomarkers is hindered by challenges such as sample integrity, technical requirements, time limitations, and financial constraints. Moreover, research findings indicate that when considered individually, driver mutations and RNA signatures are weak prognostic indicators and do not provide sufficient guidance for clinical decision-making5,6. Therefore, a rapid, dependable, and cost-efficient approach is still required to improve accessibility for routine clinical applications7.

The examination of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides, a basic and crucial aspect of solid tumor diagnosis, has been transformed by the advancement of whole-slide digital scanning and artificial intelligence (AI)8,9. Integrating AI with digital H&E imaging facilitates the automated and objective extraction of quantitative pathological data, a discipline known as pathomics10. Studies have demonstrated that pathomics can predict molecular alterations and assess patient prognosis8,9, presenting a cost-efficient and effective approach for personalized prognostic evaluation and clinical decision-making. Based on this, our study hypothesized that pathomics analysis of digital H&E-stained images could provide significant insights into prognosis and treatment response in patients with GC.

Despite these technological advancements, substantial challenges remain in translating pathomics into clinical practice. One of the main barriers is the limited interpretability of DL models, commonly known as the “black-box” issue11,12. Moreover, the generalizability of these models is often restricted due to their dependence on the size and heterogeneity of training datasets13. Other main challenges include overfitting, inconsistencies in reproducibility, extensive computational demands, and ethical concerns in medical applications14,15.

To overcome these limitations, we propose a multi-scale graph neural network with gated attention mechanism for multi-instance learning (MS-GMIL), designed to learn discriminative representations from whole slide images (WSIs). A multicenter study was then conducted to develop and verify a pathomics signature of GC (PSGC), integrating MS-GMIL and traditional machine learning approaches to directly predict overall survival (OS) from histopathology images. Visualization techniques also identified recurring pathological traits associated with patient outcomes. Furthermore, Shapley Additive explanation (SHAP) and pathogenomics analyses were employed to interpret the PSGC from various perspectives. This novel PSGC has the potential to provide more comprehensive prognostic data for patients and further advance precision medicine in GC.

Results

Patient characteristics

Table 1 provides a summary of the characteristics of all patients in both study cohorts. Among the 3138 patients who participated in the study, the median age [interquartile range (IQR)] was 60 years (IQR: 52 to 66), and 71.64% (2248/3138) were male. A considerable portion of patients (69.50%, 2181/3138) were diagnosed with advanced disease. In the HMUCH cohort, the median follow-up time was 92.33 months (IQR: 47.67 to 104.4), with a 5-year OS rate of 72.79% (95% CI: 70.37% to 75.29%). Conversely, the TCGA-Stomach adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD) cohort exhibited a median follow-up time of 19.9 months (IQR: 12.18 to 31.4), with a documented 5-year OS rate of 45.66% (95% CI: 36.23% to 57.54%).

PSGC construction

Figure 1 depicts the workflow for developing and interpreting the PSGC. A LASSO-Cox regression model with tenfold cross-validation was employed in the training cohort to establish the PSGC. The final PSGC, which functions as an integrated risk factor, was developed based on 11 pathomics features using an optimal λ value (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Then, the coefficients of all features were calculated, and the PSGC was constructed. Prognostic value of the PSGC

The optimal cutoff value was determined to be −1.37 based on the highest standardized log-rank statistic (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). Based on this, patients from both cohorts were classified as low and high PSGC groups. Supplementary Table 1 presents the detailed associations of patient characteristics across various cohorts of PSGC. In the high PSGC group, patients were generally older, and this group also exhibited a higher proportion of T3-T4 and N3 tumors, alongside poorer histological differentiation.

The K-M analysis (Fig. 2A) revealed significant variations in OS rates between the training and validation cohorts for both the low- and high-PSGC groups. Additionally, the analyzes of restricted mean survival time (RMST) consistently demonstrated that patients in the low-PSGC group had a survival advantage at various time points (Table 2). Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyzes indicated that the PSGC was independently correlated with OS in the training cohort, with these results corroborated in the validation cohorts (Supplementary Table 2).

An E-value sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding was carried out for the adjusted HR of the PSGC across the five cohorts (Supplementary Table 3). When stratified according to patient characteristics, the PSGC remained an important prognostic indicator across all the subgroups (Supplementary Table 4), highlighting its robustness as a prognostic marker.

Predictive performance of the PSGC

The time-dependent ROC curve of the PSGC exhibited satisfactory performance for estimating OS across both cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, the calibration curves illustrated good concordance between predicted and actual survival outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Decision curve analysis (DCA) further demonstrated that using the PSGC for OS prediction provided greater net clinical benefit compared to the treat-all and treat-none strategies across all evaluated cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 3), highlighting the clinical relevance and potential applicability of the pathomics signature in prognostic evaluation.

Incremental value of the PSGC added to the TNM stage

The incorporation of the PSGC into the TNM staging system led to a composite model that achieved significantly higher C-index values compared to the TNM system alone, a trend consistently observed across the validation datasets (Supplementary Table 5). Comparative evaluation of the Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROCs) across the three models revealed superior discriminative capabilities of the integrated model for predicting OS in both cohorts (Fig. 2B). In addition, the combined model surpassed the TNM-based model in terms of predictive accuracy (Fig. 2C) and provided more net clinical benefit across a wide range of possible decision values (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Finally, the integrated model displayed improvements in net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) relative to the TNM staging system at various periods in both datasets (Table 3). These findings suggest that the integrated model significantly improves the accuracy of survival outcome prediction over the traditional TNM staging method, underscoring the potential of the PSGC to offer more prognostic insights to the TNM staging system for GC.

PSGC and adjuvant chemotherapy response

To assess the predictive capacity of the PSGC for adjuvant chemotherapy response, the correlation between the PSGC and survival outcomes was investigated in GC patients with stage II and stage III disease, both those who received and those who did not receive postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. The analysis indicated that adjuvant chemotherapy did not significantly extend survival in patients with stage II and stage III GC (Supplementary Fig. 4), underscoring the limitation of TNM staging alone in identifying GC patients who may benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. In comparison, the PSGC demonstrated a notable correlation with OS, regardless of chemotherapy treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Notably, patients with higher PSGC levels who received adjuvant chemotherapy showed a favorable prognosis, while those with lower PSGC levels did not experience similar enhancements (Fig. 3A, B). Drug sensitivity predictions indicated that individuals in the high PSGC group exhibited resistance to chemotherapy agents (Fig. 3D, E). These findings suggest that the PSGC could serve as an effective tool for identifying stage II and III GC patients who are likely to benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy therapy.

PSGC and potential immunotherapy response

Predictive modeling indicates that patients with a high PSGC are more inclined to demonstrate favorable responses to immunotherapy compared to those with a low PSGC (Supplementary Fig. 6A). This observation was further corroborated by analysis using the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm (149/246 vs. 48/172; odds ratio [OR], 0.253; 95% CI, 0.162 to 0.391) and the ImmuCellAI algorithm (144/246 vs. 31/172; OR, 0.157; 95% CI, 0.095 to 0.253) (Supplementary Fig. 6B, C). Based on these results, the submap algorithm further validated that the high PSGC group is more likely to respond to anti-PD-1 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6D). Specifically, patients with a high PSGC score may exhibit a partial response (PR) to anti-PD-1 treatment, while those with a low PSGC score may demonstrate progressive disease (PD) to the same treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6E).

Interpretation of the PSGC

The SHAP value was utilized to analyze the model’s predictions, offering in-depth insights into how the effect of various features affects its decisions. As illustrated in the SHAP summary plot (Fig. 4A), feature17 was identified as the most prominent feature for the PSGC, followed by feature8, feature3, and feature34 in terms of their importance. Among the 11 pathological features analyzed, feature10 contributed the least.

Interpretation of the pathomics signature.

A SHAP values for the individual features of pathomics signature. B Representative H&E slide and their predicted heatmaps. C Unsupervised clustering of patches with the highest predictive probability for prognosis. D Proportion of patches with two opposite predictions in each cluster.

To visualize and interpret the regions most closely associated with patient outcomes, prediction heatmaps (Fig. 4B) were generated. These heatmaps indicated that nuclear pleomorphism, high cellularity, and abnormal structural characteristics were linked to a higher risk of adverse outcomes. In contrast, regions associated with low risk exhibited smaller, more regular cellular structures. For each patient, the ten patches with the highest predictive probability for poor prognosis and the 10 patches with the lowest predictive probability were identified. Unsupervised clustering was performed using the K-means and t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) algorithms to classify these patches into ten distinct clusters (Fig. 4C). Clusters 6 and 7 were associated with poor prognosis, while Clusters 0 and 2 suggested a favorable prognosis (Fig. 4D).

Poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, marked by tumor-stroma fibrosis and extratumoral lymphocyte infiltration, was found to be associated with a higher risk of adverse prognosis (Clusters 6 and 7). Conversely, favorable prognostic results were mainly related to moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas exhibiting intraepithelial neoplasia and intestinal-type epithelial metaplasia. They exhibited inflammatory infiltration at the tumor-stromal infiltration, as observed in Clusters 0 and 2 (Fig. 5A). In addition, integrating representative histopathological features with corresponding gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)-derived heatmaps indicated that these regions received increased attention weights (Fig. 5B), implying that such histological characteristics could be critical interpretative elements of the pathomic signature derived from the predictive model. For instance, a greater degree of tumor-stroma fibrosis was observed in the high-risk group. Grad-CAM visualization results demonstrated that the model’s attention was strongly directed towards these tumor-stroma fibrosis areas, suggesting that tumor-stroma fibrosis may be the key histological basis for the model to identify high-risk states.

Association of the PSGC and biological features

To clarify the potential biological processes associated with the PSGC, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out (Fig. 6A). The analysis indicated that GC samples with lower PSGC scores were significantly enriched for DNA repair, cellular proliferation, and metabolism-related pathways. Conversely, higher PSGC scores were mainly associated with activating pathways involved in myogenesis, angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and other invasion-related mechanisms.

To create a gene co-expression network relevant to the PSGC, the 5000 genes with the highest variability, based on median absolute deviation, were chosen for analysis. Then, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was applied, where a soft thresholding power of 16 was determined as optimal for establishing scale-free topology (Fig. 6B). This process led to the classification of genes into five different modules, each designated by a unique color (Fig. 6C). Genes not clustered within defined modules were categorized into the grey module and were excluded from further analyzes.

Subsequently, the eigengene of the selected traits and modules was correlated to assess module-trait relationships. This analysis suggested a strong correlation between the yellow module and the PSGC (Fig. 6D). Meanwhile, module enrichment analyzes indicated that genes within the yellow module significantly enriched pathways associated with proliferation and metabolism (Fig. 6E).

Association of the PSGC and tumor immunophenotype

The xCell algorithm was employed to thoroughly evaluate the relationship between the PSGC and tumor immunophenotype (Fig. 6F). Consistent with biological observations, the analysis indicated a positive correlation between myocytes, neurons, and various stromal cells with the PSGC. There were notable variations in the proportions of several endothelial and dendritic cells between these two subgroups (Fig. 6G).

Discussion

Precise prognosis prediction is crucial for effective risk stratification and management of GC patients in clinical settings. This study developed and validated an interpretable MS-GMIL-driven pathomics signature using 11 features derived from digital H&E-stained slides. Our results confirmed the prognostic significance of the PSGC across various follow-up periods, with the predictive performance of the PSGC surpassing previously reported models16,17. The results indicated that PSGC, when grouped at the optimal cutoff value, could significantly distinguish patient prognoses, and this survival difference was thoroughly validated across all cohorts. Additionally, the consistent results across cohorts suggested that PSGC was superior to TNM staging in prognosis prediction. These results indicate that PSGC has a notable degree of robustness and generalization ability.

To the best of our knowledge, this study presents the largest investigation to date. Compared to previous studies16,17, the PSGC exhibits outstanding predictive performance across all four external datasets. Meanwhile, integrating the PSGC with the TNM staging system markedly enhances prognostic prediction over using the TNM staging system in isolation. These findings illustrate that the pathomics feature could enable more precise risk stratification, thus contributing to more personalized intervention of GC patients in clinical practice. Additionally, we noted heterogeneity in external cohorts, particularly that The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort had shorter follow-up times than others; however, the PSGC remained well validated in TCGA. These results suggest that PSGC can be applied to diverse GC cohorts even under real-world clinical heterogeneity, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the current results. This indicates that the model’s predictive power arises from the identification of prognostic markers instead of relying on long-term follow-up, thus potentially increasing translational relevance in current healthcare settings.

Advanced GC often sees improved prognosis with postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy2. Nevertheless, many patients do not gain significant benefits from this treatment, indicating that the TNM staging system may not accurately predict survival outcomes individually3. The findings of this study suggest that patients with a high PSGC are more likely to benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy, whereas those with a low PSGC receive limited therapeutic advantages. Existing studies have indicated that perioperative chemotherapy can enhance overall survival in patients with GC18,19. In breast cancer, there is increasing evidence that computational pathology can predict the response to neoadjuvant therapy20. Therefore, future investigations will explore the potential of PSGC in neoadjuvant therapy, which is also more consistent with the clinical practice standards of most patients. Additionally, the results of this study demonstrate that patients with a high PSGC may exhibit a greater response to anti-PD-1 treatment. This implies that the PSGC could function as a crucial tool for identifying patients who are more likely to benefit from adjuvant therapy, thus improving risk stratification and ultimately guiding individualized treatment decision-making in advanced GC. Notably, chemotherapy drugs can improve the immunogenicity of TME, target and modulate the functions of various immune cells, and diminish the immunosuppressive state in the TME21. Some studies have also indicated that the combined application of immunotherapy and chemotherapy can significantly enhance patient prognosis22,23. This suggests that patients with a high PSGC may derive greater benefits from the combined effects of chemotherapy and immunotherapy, indirectly increasing its clinical value. Moreover, the results of algorithms utilized in public databases, such as Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and TIDE, are largely hypothetical and lack validation against real-world treatment data. Additional prospective studies are necessary to confirm the impact of PSGC on treatment efficacy.

Recently, there has been an increasing number of studies using deep neural network-based approaches to predict survival outcomes directly from histopathological images24–29. Although DL has shown remarkable performance in medical image analysis, its “black-box” nature poses significant challenges to its acceptance by clinicians and researchers. This challenge may constrain its applicability in high-level decision processes, particularly in oncological prognosis and treatment response predictions11.

Unlike traditional DL models that often lack interpretability, the current method integrates the predictive capabilities of DL with the interpretability provided by the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method. This integration improves the interpretability of the model, enabling an evaluation of the importance of each input variable through SHAP values. Additionally, the Grad-CAM technique was also employed to visualize the regions that most influence the model, aiding in the identification of key morphological traits related to survival outcomes.

After analyzing the deep histological features in this study, several key factors were identified as vital for prognosis prediction, including mature glandular structures with intraepithelial neoplasia, intestinal epithelial metaplasia, and tumor-stroma inflammatory cell infiltration, alongside poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma with tumor-stroma fibrosis and lymphocyte infiltration. Such features encompass spatial localization, organizational structure, and cellular interaction relationships. Recent studies have indicated that tumor-stroma fibrosis correlates with poor survival30,31. In contrast, intraepithelial neoplasia and intestinal epithelial metaplasia are often associated with Helicobacter pylori infection, which typically exhibits a more favorable prognosis32. These results underscore the high interpretability and clinical reliability of the proposed pathomics feature, which are crucial for clinical acceptability.

In this study, a thorough analysis of transcriptome associations was also conducted, laying a molecular biological foundation for the model. GSEA suggested that differentially expressed genes between the high- and low-PSGC groups were mainly enriched in proliferation, metabolism, immune dysregulation, and EMT pathways. In line with these findings, significant differences in immune and stromal cell infiltration were observed between the two groups. Specifically, the high-PSGC group exhibited a considerably higher stromal infiltration, particularly within endothelial cells. Enhanced stromal infiltration is associated with the promotion of tumor progression and resistance to treatment, thus contributing to a poor prognosis33. Furthermore, the cell cycle tends to play a crucial role in the prognosis of GC, as indicated by the enrichment of gene sets associated with E2F, MYC, and G2M checkpoints. These findings underscore the ability of targeting cell cycle regulators for GC treatment.

One major challenge in extracting deep prognostic characteristics lies in efficiently capturing informative content from histological images. Traditional feature extraction methods often rely on sampling from tumor regions24,27,34, risking the overlooking of critical prognostic information within the tumor microenvironment. Further studies have employed random single-patch sampling across the WSI, an approach that disrupts the spatial context between patches and may compromise the integrity of spatially relevant information35,36. Other approaches have utilized handcrafted feature-based methodologies, focusing on pathologist-annotated regions of interest (ROIs) within WSIs. Such approaches employ specialized tools to extract predefined features from the selected ROIs to develop pathogenic features37–39, so they could be time-consuming, subjective, and lack reproducibility40, and the predefined image features have limited capacity to represent comprehensive image information.

In addition to its interpretative advantages, this study presents the MS-GMIL pipeline designed to overcome the intrinsic challenges of analyzing large-scale and heterogeneous tumor histopathology images. Utilizing the MS-GMIL pipeline, we cropped patches of varying scales from the WSIs of 3138 patients. The features of multi-scale patches were then fused through attention mechanisms and concatenation, allowing the model to capture fine-grained cellular information while preserving macroscopic tissue structural features. This enhances the robustness of the model in histological analysis and avoids the defects of single-scale information. By employing graph neural network (GNN) modules for feature propagation and aggregation in graph structures, we can more effectively model the spatial dependencies between varying regions of pathological images. Graph modeling can reflect the overall characteristics of the tissue microenvironment. Moreover, the attention mechanism is utilized to weight the contributions of different patches, minimizing key lesion areas while suppressing irrelevant or redundant information. By leveraging multi-head attention to fuse multi-scale features, the model effectively aggregates important regions at various levels, leading to better interpretability. Our results suggest that the MS-GMIL pipeline possesses enhanced representational capabilities and can adapt to pathological sections with distinct tissue structures and staining conditions. For tumors with tissue heterogeneity, MS-GMIL can more effectively capture the balance between local and global features.

Despite the promising results presented in this research, several limitations have been identified. Firstly, due to the retrospective design, there are certain inherent biases and hidden confounders. For instance, all patients at all centers underwent CT and enhanced CT scans preoperatively, while only some underwent Fluorodeoxyglucose-Positron Emission Tomography (FDG-PET) and laparoscopy, potentially leading to an underestimation of the staging. However, it is unlikely that unaccounted confounders alone could fully account for the observed results, as indicated by the substantial E-values in the primary analyzes. Secondly, the TCGA cohort had a shorter follow-up period compared to the other four cohorts, which restricts the ability to assess disease progression and introduces potential age-related biases, thus impacting model performance evaluation. Additionally, the potential association between PSGC status and response to adjuvant therapy was derived from a small-sized subgroup analysis that did not control key confounders (ECOG, lymph node yield, margin status, peritoneal involvement). We plan to obtain GC data from more centers in the future to perform larger-scale subgroup analyzes and fully control confounding factors. Thirdly, although the pathomics feature was developed and externally verified using images from patients in various countries and hospitals, further validation is necessary to ensure its robustness against variations in patient demographics, sample preparation, and image acquisition practices encountered in clinical settings globally. Current research does not sufficiently examine PD-L1, MSI, and MMR status, which are crucial for understanding perioperative chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy41. While engaging in PSGC may suggest the effectiveness of immunotherapy, there is still a lack of correlation results between PSGC and PD-L1, MSI, and MMR status. In the future, we intend to fully gather information on these biomarkers to improve the clinical applicability of PSGC. Finally, although we compared the predictive capabilities of traditional TNM staging, a direct comparison with established pathological grading systems (e.g., tumor grading, Laurent classification) needs to be conducted. Considering clinical applicability, future studies could minimize computational requirements through model quantification or knowledge distillation, and extend this approach to in-hospital pathology information systems to enhance its value for clinical decision-making.

This study successfully developed and validated a PSGC derived from the MS-GMIL analysis of H&E-stained slides for direct prognostication of GC outcomes. The integration of this signature significantly enhances the prognostic stratification provided by the traditional TNM staging system and may help identify patients most likely to benefit from adjuvant therapeutic interventions, thereby contributing to more personalized and evidence-based clinical decision-making. Nonetheless, further validation through large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies is required to validate its clinical use and generalizability.

Methods

Ethics concern

This prognostic study was approved by the respective institutional ethics committees of our Hospital (KY2024-22) and cooperative hospitals (GZRYS-115, K-2024-03-005, KY2024-1). Each participant provided written consent, including permission to use clinical data and tissue specimens in scientific research. All human-related procedures adhered to the ethical standards established in the Declaration of Helsinki42. The study adheres to the guidelines outlined in the Recommendations for Tumor Marker Prognostic Studies (REMARK) and the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD)43,44. The work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria45.

Study design and participants

This retrospective multicenter analysis involved evaluating 3138 H&E-stained slides across five different patient cohorts to achieve broad representation and strengthen the applicability of the results. The cohorts were methodically categorized into a single training cohort and four separate validation cohorts.

Figure 1A shows the number of patients in each center. Specifically, the training cohort comprised 1257 patients diagnosed at our Hospital (HMUCH) between January 2014 and December 2016. The validation cohort 1 comprised 752 patients diagnosed at the cooperative hospitals (HMUSH) between January 2014 and December 2016. The validation cohort 2 included 540 patients from the cooperative hospitals (QMUSH) diagnosed from January 2013 to December 2017. The validation cohort 3 included 319 patients from the cooperative hospitals (YKPH) diagnosed from January 2013 to December 2017. Finally, 270 patient cases obtained from TCGA, spanning from January 2002 to April 2015, were included as validation cohort 4 to further reinforce the reliability of the analysis.

Participants were selected according to the following criteria: (1) ≥18 years age group, (2) histopathologically confirmed GC with R0 surgical resection, (3) a minimum postoperative survival of 90 days to minimize potential bias associated with surgical outcomes46,47, (4) no previous diagnosis of other tumors, and (5) availability of comprehensive clinicopathological data and follow-up records. Individuals who underwent neoadjuvant therapy were excluded to avoid confounding effects on the histological characteristics observed in H&E-stained slides and patient prognosis. Chemotherapy regimens include oxaliplatin plus capecitabine (XELOX) or oxaliplatin plus S-1 (SOX).

For the TCGA-STAD cohort, the baseline and prognostic data were retrieved from the National Cancer Institute Genomic Data Commons Data Portal. For the remaining four cohorts, detailed baseline data were systematically gathered, including patient age, sex, tumor differentiation grade, Lauren classification, invasion depth, lymph node involvement, TNM staging, receipt of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy and follow-up records. Follow-up time was calculated from the surgery date to the most recent follow-up, with survival status noted at the final follow-up. Moreover, OS was defined as the interval from surgical intervention to either death or the last documented follow-up.

Standard postoperative chemotherapy consisted of a combination regimen administered over 6 months following curative surgery. Patients who were unable to complete the full course but received a minimum of 3 months of treatment were still classified within the chemotherapy group.

Acquisition of H&E-stained histopathology images

High-quality H&E-stained digital histopathological images for the TCGA cohort were retrieved from the respective data portal. Slides from the remaining four cohorts were obtained through routine histopathological processing, which included fixation in 4% neutral buffered formaldehyde, paraffin embedding, sectioning (4 µm thickness), and staining with H&E (Fig. 1B). After quality assessment, the selected sections were digitized via the Aperio AT2 scanner (Leica Biosystems, Germany) at 20X (0.5 μm/pixel resolution). All digital images were stored in SVS file format and managed using Aperio ImageScope (v12.4.6).

Image preprocessing

To process WSIs within a DL framework, each WSI is approximately 10 gigapixels in size, and the images were first divided into non-overlapping patches of 512 × 512 pixels at 20×. Images patches with an average Canny edge count greater than the defined cutoff value of 2 were excluded to eliminate non-informative background regions48. Afterwards, the Macenko method was employed to normalize stain variability across tiles, and then z-score normalization of the RGB channels was performed to achieve a standardized distribution of pixel intensities49. A variety of data augmentation techniques was applied, including horizontal and vertical flipping, mirroring, blurring, controlled color distortions, and progressive sprinkles, to improve model generalizability and reduce overfitting.

Construction of the PSGC

The LASSO Cox regression model is widely considered a robust approach for conducting survival analysis in high-dimensional data34,37. This model incorporates an L1 penalty, which drives the coefficients of features closer to zero (Fig. 1D). The penalty parameter, λ, also called the tuning constant, regulates the strength of the penalty. A decrease in λ diminishes the penalty, allowing more predictors to be integrated into the model.

In this study, a tenfold cross-validation with minimum criteria was employed to evaluate the optimal value of λ. The partial likelihood of deviance was analyzed in the training cohort to identify the most prognostic features and formulate a calculation formula for the PSGC. The PSGC for the validation group was then computed directly using a formula derived from the training cohort.

Prognostic value of the PSGC

In the training cohort, the optimal threshold for the PSGC was determined by applying maximally selected rank statistics, and it was then used in the validation cohorts. Patients were classified into groups with high or low PSGC scores based on this threshold for prognostic evaluation. OS differences between these groups were investigated by performing survival curve analysis and calculating the RMST60. A subgroup analysis also examined potential variability across different patient subgroups. Moreover, a sensitivity analysis with E-value61 was performed to assess the possible influence of unmeasured confounding factors.

Performance evaluation of the PSGC

The discriminative capability was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the concordance index (C-index). Calibration assessments were carried out using plots to compare the predicted survival probabilities with the corresponding observed outcomes. In addition, DCA was employed to evaluate the net clinical benefit associated with the PSGC at various threshold probabilities62.

The clinical application was further examined by measuring its incremental value over the TNM staging system based on several measures, including discrimination, calibration, clinical benefit, IDI, NRI, and prediction error analysis63.

Association between the PSGC and treatment response

For this analysis, two publicly available drug response repositories, GDSC and Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP), were utilized (Fig. 1E). The oncoPredict package was employed to predict drug sensitivity profiles64–66. Ridge regression was utilized to estimate the IC50 for each sample, and the reliability of the predictive model was assessed through tenfold cross-validation.

RNA-seq data from the PRJEB25780 study, which included 45 GC patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy, were gathered to predict immunotherapy response67. The submap algorithm was used to analyze the consistency between the patients with different responses and pathomics signature subgroups68. In addition, the ImmuCellAI and the TIDE tools were utilized to predict the relationship between PSGC subgroups and immunotherapy responses69,70.

Interpretation of the PSGC

To address the challenge of black-box predictions in DL models, SHAP, a game-theoretic method, was employed to evaluate the contribution and significance of each feature with the model’s predictions71. This methodology enables a deeper understanding of the model’s reasoning, offering both global and local interpretations of the predicted results from the trained models.

Prediction heatmaps were produced to identify pathomorphological features correlated with prognosis. The pathological morphological features were independently evaluated by two highly experienced pathologists who were blinded to the model output, patient prognosis, or other clinical information. To ensure consistency and reliability, the assessments were subsequently verified by a senior pathology expert (Huiying Li). Furthermore, Grad-CAM was utilized to generate heatmaps on the tiles72, emphasizing crucial regions that affected network predictions. This visualization technique leveraged gradient information from the final convolutional layer of the current DL network, offering a visual explanation that helps to understand the model’s decision-making process.

Molecular insights into the PSGC

When analyzing the training cohort, mRNA sequencing was carried out on 193 samples, and 1 µg RNA/sample was utilized for library construction using the NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. RNA isolation, library construction, and mRNA sequencing were performed by Novogene (Beijing, China). The resulting data were submitted to the Gene Expression Omnibus repository (GSE184336 and GSE179252 accession numbers)73. In addition, transcriptome data from 225 samples of the TCGA-STAD group were collected using the TCGAbiolinks package74.

To create the meta-cohort for bioinformatics analysis, the ComBat method was employed to correct batch effects from non-biological technical discrepancies between the HMUCH and TCGA-STAD cohorts75. WGCNA was conducted to identify gene modules associated with the PSGC76. Gene enrichment analysis was also performed to investigate the biological processes linked to the PSGC77,78. The xCell tool was utilized to measure the immunophenotypic characteristics of the GC samples79.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were analyzed using the independent samples (unpaired) t-test. Nevertheless, non-normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables were evaluated using either Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square (χ²) test. The Kaplan–Meier method was utilized to create survival curves, and groups were compared using the log-rank test. To determine the independent prognostic value of the PSGC, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied, and the results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In addition, correlation analyzes were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a p-value less than 0.05 indicating a significant threshold. Statistical analysis was conducted via R software (v4.0.5), while DL analyzes were carried out via Python software (v3.7.12). The computing platform is equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 6430 processor and an NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU, running the Ubuntu 24.04 LTS operating system.
