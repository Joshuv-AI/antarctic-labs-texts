Construct Validity of the GENDER-Q Face and Neck Scales in 4890 Transgender and Gender Diverse Adults
Authors: Anne F Klassen, Manraj N Kaur, Charlene Rae, Caleb Haley, Fermín Capitán-Cañadas, Daniel Simon, Luis Capitán, Jens U Berli, Tim C van de Grift, Margriet G Mullender, Thomas Satterwhite, Gerhard S Mundinger, Gabriel A Del Corral, Christodoulos Kaoutzanis, Alireza Hamidian Jahromi, Michael Alperovich, Fan Liang, Danny A Young-Afat, Sylvie D Cornacchi, Andrea L Pusic, Shane D Morrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGender-affirming facial interventions aim to align facial features of people who identify as transgender or gender diverse (TGD) with their gender identity. GENDER-Q is a comprehensive patient-reported outcome measure with 11 scales that measure satisfaction with the face overall, facial features, specific facial areas, and laryngeal prominence (Adam's apple).ObjectivesThe aim of the study was to examine construct validity for the face and neck scales in an international sample of people who identify as TGD.MethodsAn international cross-sectional survey collected GENDER-Q data between February 2022 and March 2024. Participants were aged 18 years and older and had sought or received gender-affirming care within the past 5 years. Predefined hypotheses for expected group-level differences and correlations between GENDER-Q scales were tested using independent t-tests, analysis of variance, and Pearson correlation coefficients. The COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) guideline was followed to establish construct validity (ie, ≥75% hypotheses confirmed).ResultsA total of 4890 participants completed the GENDER-Q Face Overall scale and additional scales as relevant. The sample identified as man (33%), woman (37%), nonbinary (24%), and other gender (6%). The percentage of hypotheses that met the COSMIN criteria of evidence of construct validity ranged from 78% to 100% for the 11 scales. As hypothesized, participants who had undergone gender-affirming facial procedures reported significantly higher satisfaction with appearance scores than those who wanted surgery or required revision surgery.ConclusionsGENDER-Q scales met COSMIN criteria for hypothesis-based construct validity. GENDER-Q can be used to inform clinical care and to advance research regarding gender-affirming facial interventions.Level of Evidence: 3 (Therapeutic)

The face plays a central role in how gender is perceived and how people are socially recognized in their daily life. Unlike other aspects of appearance that can be concealed, facial features are always visible and are evaluated by others. As such, the face is closely linked to one's experience of being appropriately gendered. In people who identify as transgender or gender diverse (TGD), incongruence between facial appearance and gender identity can be a source of gender dysphoria and social distress.1-3

Gender-affirming facial interventions aim to align a person's appearance with their gender identity. A range of interventions are available that include minimally invasive procedures (eg, fillers and toxins) and surgery (eg, forehead contouring, jaw and chin shaping, rhinoplasty, and chondrolaryngoplasty).4 Such interventions aim to improve gender congruence by aligning facial appearance with one's gender identity.1,5 Research has demonstrated that gender-affirming facial interventions are associated with improvements in psychological well-being, patient satisfaction, and health-related quality of life (HRQL).1,5-8 As the evidence base for gender-affirming care (GAC) has expanded, facial gender-affirming surgery has gained recognition as medically necessary care rather than a merely cosmetic intervention.6,9,10

Given the inherently subjective nature of facial appearance, outcomes of gender-affirming interventions need to include the patient perspective alongside traditional outcome measures. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) represent indispensable tools for capturing outcomes that matter most to patients. Our team previously developed the FACE-Q Aesthetics PROM to evaluate outcomes of surgical and minimally invasive facial aesthetic procedures from the patient perspective.11-15 This PROM is now widely used in facial aesthetics.16 However, FACE-Q was developed and validated in cisgender populations and, despite being used to evaluate outcomes in a range of gender-affirming surgical and nonsurgical interventions, it may not adequately capture all relevant concepts unique to TGD people's experiences with GAC.17-22

To address the need for a PROM specifically for GAC, our team developed the GENDER-Q.23-25 This PROM includes 11 independently functioning scales that measure satisfaction with the face overall, facial features, specific facial areas, and laryngeal prominence (Adam's apple). To inform the development of GENDER-Q scales, cognitive debriefing interviews were conducted with 21 patient participants who provided feedback on the relevance of content from existing FACE-Q Aesthetic scales. Concept elicitation interviews were also conducted with 84 TGD people from 4 countries. Together, the qualitative data were used to create a set of scales that were refined iteratively with input from 50 experts and 14 patient participants.24 The GENDER-Q international field test study was then conducted, which involved 5497 TGD individuals. Psychometric analyses demonstrated robust content validity, internal consistency, and test–retest reliability of the 11 scales forming the GENDER-Q Face and Neck domain.25 However, assessment of construct validity was lacking.

Construct validity refers to the ability of an instrument to measure its intended construct, which is essential for confirming the instrument's clinical value. The aim of this study was to assess construct validity of the GENDER-Q Face and Neck scales using data from the GENDER-Q field test study. Specifically, we tested a series of a priori hypotheses that examined expected group-level differences and evaluated correlations between GENDER-Q Face and Neck scales and GENDER-Q HRQL scales.

METHODS

Ethics Approval

Development and validation of the GENDER-Q adhered to the established international guidelines and recommendations for PROM development.26-28 The cross-sectional GENDER-Q international field test took place between February 2022 and March 2024.25 The present study used a sub-set of participants from the larger GENDER-Q field test who completed the GENDER-Q Face Overall scale and other GENDER-Q scales. Ethics approvals were obtained from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (#13171) and participating sites. Participants provided informed consent prior to survey completion.

Data Collection

Detailed data collection methods for the GENDER-Q field test study are described elsewhere.25 Participants were recruited from clinics, social media, Trans PULSE Canada participants and a crowdsourcing platform (ie, Prolific Academic). Data were collected using a REDCap survey with versions in English, Danish, Dutch, and Canadian French.29 Participants were eligible for this study if they were aged 18 years or older, had sought or received GAC within the last 5 years (eg, hormones, surgery, and minimally invasive procedures), and had completed the Face Overall scale. Participants recruited in the first Prolific pilot test completed all Face and Neck scales. After that pilot test, all remaining GENDER-Q participants were asked to complete 4 scales (Face Overall, Facial Features, Upper Face, and Eyebrows), but only participants who wanted to have or had undergone a surgical or minimally invasive procedure for facial features/areas completed the associated scales for the specific parts of the face addressed. For the Adam's Apple scale, in the first Prolific pilot test, participants completed the scale if they wanted to have or had surgery for laryngeal prominence. After the pilot test, for the Adam's Apple scale, participants assigned male sex at birth who wanted or had undergone surgery and anyone who wanted to look more feminine were asked to complete that scale. Supplemental Figure 1 shows recruitment for each scale.

Measures

The face scales measure satisfaction (ie, extremely dissatisfied, very dissatisfied, somewhat dissatisfied, somewhat satisfied, very satisfied, extremely satisfied) with how the face, facial feature, or facial area currently looks. The Adam's Apple scale measures how bothered someone is (extremely, very, somewhat, a little, and not at all) by how their Adam's apple currently looks. Items for the 11 scales cover concepts that patients used to describe their appearance (eg, size, shape, fullness, gender-affirming, attractive, photographs, profile, and up close). To score each scale, the raw total score was converted into a score that ranges from 0 to 100 based on the Rasch-transformed logit scores. Higher scores for the facial scales indicate more satisfied and the Adam's Apple scale indicates being less bothered. Missing scores were imputed using the within-person mean imputation for participants who completed at least 50% of a scale's items. Given that the percentage of cases with missing data in each scale was minimal (2%-4% of cases), and items within the scale measure a similar construct, imputing using the mean was deemed to be appropriate.25 Sensitivity analyses were performed using complete cases to assess stability of results.

Variables used to test hypotheses of expected group-level differences included the following: gender identity, hormone use (ie, type and length of time taking), status of facial interventions (ie, surgery and/or minimally invasive procedures) for each facial area (ie, not sure, don’t want, want, had, and had and need more), complications (yes/no), and amount of facial hair (none, a little, a moderate amount, and a lot). Participants were also asked to rate their satisfaction (as above) with the following: height and prominence of their cheekbones, jawline from different angles, size of nose, evenness of nostrils, and shape and fullness of lips. Other items asked participants to rate how often (ie, never, rarely, sometimes, often, and always) they love the way they look, feel affirmed in their gender, and feel attractive. For the Adam's Apple scale, participants were asked how much (ie, not at all, a little, somewhat, and very) they were bothered with its prominence, noticeability, and being misgendered due to it. For correlation hypotheses, the following GENDER-Q scales were used: Gender Dysphoria, Social Acceptance, Psychological Distress, Psychological Well-being, and Sexual Well-being.

Statistical Analysis

Construct validity hypotheses were predefined based on expected group-level differences and correlations between scales, with at least 75% of accepted hypotheses considered sufficient evidence of construct validity according to COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) criteria (Table 1).26 The hypotheses were developed based on clinical expertise. Expected group-level differences were assessed using independent T-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc Bonferroni tests or the equivalent nonparametric test. Cohen's d and Eta-squared (η2) were calculated for significant T-tests and ANOVAs, respectively. All effect sizes were hypothesized to be at least small in size (d ≥ 0.2, η2 ≥ 0.01).30,31 Correlations between GENDER-Q scales were interpreted as follows: <0.3 dissimilar constructs, 0.3-<0.5 related but dissimilar constructs, and ≥0.5 similar constructs. Normality was assessed both graphically and statistically based on kurtosis (absolute < 7) and skewness (absolute < 2).32 Statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with statistical significance P ≤ .05. The focus of the construct validity analysis was on the consistency of the overall results based on the direction magnitude and statistical significance of the tests, rather than the individual P-values.

RESULTS

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Supplemental Table 1 shows participant demographic and clinical characteristics for the 4890 GENDER-Q field test participants who completed the Face Overall scale. Participants ranged from 18 to 83 years of age (mean = 32.8; SD = 12.3). Most participants resided in the United States (44%) and Canada (32%), were White (78%), and had a college/university degree or higher (57%). Supplemental Table 1 also provides the sample demographic and clinical characteristics by scale for the Face and Neck domain; the sample size varied by scale due to branching logic.

Of the 4890 participants, 25% (1225) reported that they had no previous facial procedures and wanted to have at least one gender-affirming intervention (ie, surgery and/or a minimally invasive procedure) to change the appearance of their face. The specific types of interventions participants had received were as follows: 80% (472) had surgery, 5% (29) had a minimally invasive procedure, 14% (84) had both, and 2 participants did not specify the procedure type.

GENDER-Q Face and Neck Scale Scores

Figure 1 shows the GENDER-Q Face and Neck scale mean scores by procedure status. For all 11 scales, participants who wanted to have a procedure to change the appearance of their face or neck scored lower than those who had undergone a procedure. For the Face Overall, Facial Features, and specific facial areas scales, those who had previously undergone interventions, but reported that they wanted more, had lower scores than those who reported that they did not want any more facial interventions.

Construct Validity

Table 1 shows the construct validity hypotheses tested and which hypotheses were accepted for each scale. Overall, construct validity results were stable in the complete-case analysis. The percentage of accepted hypotheses ranged from 78% to 100%, demonstrating sufficient evidence of construct validity for each scale. Detailed results for expected group-level differences hypothesis testing are included in Supplemental Table 2. All expected group-level differences hypotheses were accepted for the Face Overall, Facial Features, Upper Face, Eyebrows, Nose, Nostrils, Chin, and Adam's Apple scales. The number of expected group-level differences hypotheses rejected for the other scales were as follows: 3 for Cheeks, 2 for Lips, and 1 for Jawline. Among individuals without facial interventions, participants who reported estrogen use for 2 years or more did not report higher satisfaction scores for Cheeks (P = .80) or Lips (P = .25) compared with those who reported <2 years of use. Similarly, participants who reported testosterone use for 2 years or more did not report higher satisfaction with Cheeks (P = .06) or Jawline (P = .35) compared with those who reported <2 years of use. Additionally, no differences in Cheeks and Lips scale scores were observed between participants who had facial surgery and those who had a minimally invasive procedure (P = .07 and P = .71, respectively).

Detailed results for correlation hypothesis testing are shown in Supplemental Table 3. All correlational hypotheses were accepted for the Facial Features, Eyebrows, and Nostrils scales. The number of correlational hypotheses rejected for the other scales were as follows: 3 for Face Overall, 1 for Upper Face, 1 for Cheeks, 2 for Nose, 1 for Lips, 2 for Chin, 1 for Jawline and 1 for Adam's Apple. Most hypotheses that were not met were weaker than expected, including correlations between Social Acceptance and the Chin scale; Sexual Well-being and the Nose, Lips, and Chin scales; Psychological Well-being and the Nose scale; and Body Image and the Adam's Apple scale. Hypotheses about correlation between the Face Overall scale and Gender Dysphoria, Psychological Distress, and Psychological Well-being scales were higher than expected. Correlations between Body Image and the Upper Face, Cheeks, and Jawline scales were also higher than expected. These findings indicated that the constructs were more strongly related than hypothesized.

DISCUSSION

The GENDER-Q Face and Neck scales evidenced strong construct validity in an international sample of people who identified as TGD, meeting COSMIN guidelines for all 11 scales.26 Importantly, participants who underwent facial procedures reported higher satisfaction with appearance scores than participants who wanted but had not yet obtained facial interventions. These results demonstrate the GENDER-Q Face and Neck scales' ability to produce meaningful, patient-focused data about the effects of gender-affirming facial interventions.

Previous systematic reviews of PROMs used in GAC, including facial gender-affirming interventions, have consistently shown that few instruments were developed specifically for TGD populations.33-37 Most PROMs used to evaluate treatment outcomes were originally developed in cisgender populations and measure generic constructs rather than outcomes prioritized by TGD individuals. In a review of peer-reviewed literature on facial feminization surgery and patient-reported outcomes up to 2019, 21 facial feminization studies were identified.35 This review identified the use of a single TGD-specific PROM (ie, Transgender Congruence Scale). An updated review of literature on facial feminization surgery for TGD patients published in January 2021 found 15 articles which used 12 PROMs.38 None of the 12 instruments used demonstrated strong psychometric properties in patients undergoing facial feminization surgery.

In contrast, the GENDER-Q was developed using extensive input from both clinical experts and TGD adults with lived experience and has been shown to demonstrate high content validity, meaning that it measures relevant outcomes for the TGD population.24,25 Since validation of a PROM is an ongoing process with evidence accumulating over time and across multiple studies, the construct validity results presented in this study add to other published development and validation findings.24,25 Construct validity is a key measurement property of a PROM as it provides evidence that an instrument measures the theoretical concept it proports to measure.26 With evidence of construct validity, the GENDER-Q Face and Neck scales allow for incorporation of the patient's perspective into outcome assessment for any type of surgical or minimally invasive gender-affirming facial procedure. Such a PROM is needed given the subjective nature of facial appearance and the multi-procedural, region-specific approach often required in gender-affirming facial care.

This study has key implications for the field of facial GAC. Participants who underwent gender-affirming facial procedures reported significantly higher scores than participants who had not yet undergone these procedures. These findings are aligned with published studies that show the benefits associated with this form of care for patients who desire these interventions.1,5-8 Future studies of gender-affirming facial procedures can use the GENDER-Q Face and Neck scales to measure outcomes across gender identities to accurately measure appearance of the face overall, specific facial areas and laryngeal prominence. Potential clinical applications of these scales include using them preoperatively to identify areas of concern for patients. The GENDER-Q can also be used in research to evaluate the effectiveness of different surgical techniques or staging protocols, and to evaluate outcomes of minimally invasive procedures. Importantly, GENDER-Q's modular design provides flexibility for clinicians and researchers to select only the scales relevant to the outcome(s) of interest, thereby minimizing patient burden.

This study has some limitations. The data collected from participants were self-reported and were not verified clinically. Accordingly, some categories used in the analysis may be influenced by factors unrelated to objective treatment outcomes. For example, participants' self-reported intervention status for each facial area (ie, not sure, don’t want, want, had, had and need more) may reflect a range of considerations, including personal preferences, access to care, and treatment goals. The sample was composed mostly of people who are White and from North America. Further research is needed to translate, culturally adapt and validate versions for use in linguistic and cultural contexts beyond the current English, Danish, Dutch, and Canadian French versions. Given the length of the GENDER-Q survey, we were limited in the number of clinical questions we could ask, particularly about types of facial interventions and complications associated with such procedures. Several hypothesized differences and associations were not observed or were weaker in magnitude than anticipated. It is possible these findings relate to the heterogeneity in the sample, and the limited information we were able to collect to understand motivations for facial interventions and revisions. Another limitation is that most of the participants who had facial interventions wanted to look more feminine. Future research could examine construct validity in individuals undergoing facial masculinization.

CONCLUSIONS

The GENDER-Q Face and Neck scales provide a comprehensive set of scales that can be used to accurately measure outcomes of gender-affirming facial interventions, both surgically and minimally invasive, from the patient's perspective. By generating high-quality patient-centered outcomes data related to facial GAC, the GENDER-Q Face and Neck scales can inform patients, providers, and policymakers alike regarding the expected outcomes associated with facial interventions. Future research is needed to support interpretation of scores for GENDER-Q scales, including establishing normative values and determining minimally important differences. Until such benchmarks are established, changes in scores should be interpreted cautiously.
