1.
Introduction

Dimensionality reduction techniques are some of the most powerful tools for designing efficient algorithms in computational linear algebra, numerical optimization, and related areas. A fundamental primitive in this context is matrix approximation, where given a large matrix
A
, we seek to efficiently construct a smaller matrix
\tilde{A}
that approximately preserves the linear algebraic properties of
A
such as its rank, norm, singular values, etc. Most of these properties can be naturally encapsulated by a single one, the
subspace embedding
property, which requires that
\|\tilde{A}x\|\approx_{\epsilon}\|Ax\|
for all vectors
x
of appropriate dimension and a given
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Here, we use
a\approx_{\epsilon}b
to denote
(1-\epsilon)b\leq a\leq(1+\epsilon)b
. Subspace embeddings have become a workhorse of computational linear algebra, leading to fast algorithms for linear regression, low-rank approximation, clustering, linear programming, and more (see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
for comprehensive overviews).

A key advantage of subspace embeddings is that they can be both simple and efficient: an approximation
\tilde{A}
obtained by multiplying
A
with a sparse random matrix will, under appropriate conditions on sparsity and dimension, with high probability attain the subspace embedding property in nearly linear time. Such sparse random matrices are often called
oblivious
, since their distribution need not depend on the input
A
. The first such distribution was proposed by Clarkson and Woodruff
[
5
]
. Concretely, given
n\geq d\geq 1
, a matrix
\Pi\in\R^{m\times n}
with exactly one uniformly placed non-zero random sign entry in each column (CountSketch) is an oblivious subspace embedding (OSE), i.e., for every
A\in\R^{n\times d}
,
\tilde{A}=\Pi A
satisfies the subspace embedding property with good probability, as long as
m\geq O(d^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
(see
[
6
]
for a simple proof). The sparsity pattern of the CountSketch implies that
\Pi A
can be computed in
O(\nnz(A))
time, i.e., proportional to the number of non-zero entries in
A
.
While this running time is the best one could hope for, the embedding dimension
m
incurs an undesirable quadratic dependence on
d
. Shortly after this first result, Nelson and Nguyen
[
6
]
conjectured that a linear dependence on
d
is possible without sacrificing too much in sparsity.

1.1.
Our Results

The main result of this paper is the following theorem, which resolves the conjecture of Nelson and Nguyen up to sub-polylogarithmic factors in
d
when
\epsilon
is inverse polynomially bounded in
d
.

Theorem 1.2

(Nelson-Nguyen up to sub-polylogarithmic factors)
.

For any
n\geq d
and
\epsilon\geq d^{-O(1)}
, there is a random
\Pi\in\R^{m\times n}
with
\tilde{O}(\log(d)/\epsilon)
1
1

1

Here and throughout, the notation
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
hides only sub-polylogarithmic factors in
d
, i.e.,
\log^{o(1)}(d)
.
non-zeros per column and dimension
m=\tilde{O}(d/\epsilon^{2})
such that for any
A\in\R^{n\times d}
, with probability
1-d^{-O(1)}
,
\|\Pi Ax\|\approx_{\epsilon}\|Ax\|
for all
x\in\R^{d}
.

A more detailed version of our result is provided in Theorem
2.1
, which gives a parameterized family of subspace embedding guarantees that offer different trade-offs between the dimension and the sparsity of
\Pi
, including one that exactly attains the optimal dimension but with a slightly worse sparsity bound. Carefully balancing those trade-offs, we recover Theorem
1.2
.

A key novelty in our analysis is a matrix concentration technique we call
iterative decoupling
, which provides fine-grained bounds on higher-order trace moments of random matrices. In this approach, we recursively reduce the order of the matrix moment we wish to bound by decoupling the matrix power into a product of independent copies. While decoupling is a ubiquitous technique in probability theory, it tends to result in an exponential blow up when applied to a product of sufficiently many components. However, we show that when deployed recursively through a careful schedule, it can lead to improved bounds for the higher-order trace moments that naturally arise in matrix concentration analysis. In order to deploy this technique, we further develop several higher-order moment bounds for norm quantities associated with subspace embeddings, which should be of independent interest.

Implications for Linear Regression.

As discussed, subspace embeddings have numerous algorithmic implications in linear algebra and optimization (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
for detailed overviews). The most immediate one arises in the context of linear regression, where one often deals with minimizing a generalized least squares objective of the form
\|Ax-b\|^{2}+g(x)
over some domain
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\R^{d}
, where
A\in\R^{n\times d}
,
b\in\R^{d}
,
g(x)
is, e.g., the scaled
l_{1}
-norm (Lasso regression,
[
17
]
), and
\mathcal{C}
is, e.g., the
l_{p}
-norm ball
[
18
]
, non-negativity constraints
[
19
]
, or
\R^{d}
(see
[
20
]
for further discussion).

When
n\gg d
, we can reduce this optimization problem to solving a much smaller instance by computing
\tilde{A}=SA
and
\tilde{b}=Sb
for
S\in\R^{m\times n}
. If
S
is a subspace embedding for the extended data matrix
[A\mid b]\in\R^{n\times(d+1)}
, then this smaller instance retains an
\epsilon
-approximation of the objective, since
\|\tilde{A}x-\tilde{b}\|\approx_{\epsilon}\|Ax-b\|
for all
x
. It follows that an
\epsilon
-approximate solution to the sketched problem yields an
O(\epsilon)
-approximate solution to the original problem (e.g., see
[
9
]
).

Applying Theorem
1.2
together with the above argument, we obtain the fastest sub-current matrix multiplication time reduction of size
\tilde{O}(d/\epsilon^{2})
for this class of linear regression objectives.

Corollary 1.3

(Fast reduction for linear regression)
.

Given
A\in\R^{n\times d}
,
b\in\R^{n}
,
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, constraint set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\R^{d}
, and regularizer
g:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\R_{\geq 0}
, let
f(x)=\|Ax-b\|^{2}+g(x)
for
x\in\mathcal{C}
. We can reduce the task of finding
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
f(\hat{x})\leq(1+\epsilon)\min_{x\in\mathcal{C}}f(x)
to an instance
\tilde{A}\in\R^{m\times d},\tilde{b}\in\R^{m},\tilde{\epsilon}=\Theta(\epsilon)
of the same task where
m=\tilde{O}(d/\epsilon^{2})
in time
\tilde{O}(\nnz(A)\log(d)/\epsilon)
with probability
1-d^{-O(1)}
.

In addition to the above listed prior works on oblivious subspace embeddings, we can compare this guarantee to other approaches, which use information about the matrix
A
to optimize the running time, via techniques such as leverage score sampling
[
21
,
22
,
9
,
10
]
. Unfortunately, all of these reductions incur additional
\poly(d/\epsilon)
costs, including the
d^{\omega}
cost of matrix multiplication (here,
\omega\approx 2.372
denotes the current matrix multiplication exponent
[
23
]
). The best known
\tilde{O}(d/\epsilon^{2})
reduction of this type attains
O(\nnz(A)\log(d)+d^{\omega}+d^{2}\log^{3}(d)/\epsilon)
running time
[
10
]
.

1.2.
Further Related Work

A number of subspace embedding techniques have been considered in addition to sparse random matrices. In particular,
[
24
]
was the first to propose the concept of a subspace embedding, and showed that it can be attained using the fast Johnson-Lindenstrauss transform introduced by
[
25
]
, which is closely related to the subsampled randomized Hadamard transform
[
26
]
. For example, in the latter, we first apply an orthogonal transformation that randomly mixes the rows of the input
A\in\R^{n\times d}
, and then we select a uniformly random sample of
m
rows from the transformed matrix. A yet different approach by
[
27
]
uses i.i.d. importance sampling to select a size
m
row sample from the original input, without the transformation step. Here, the importance weights are chosen based on the approximate leverage scores of the rows
[
28
,
29
]
. Interestingly, unlike sparse random matrices, the i.i.d row sampling approaches cannot attain linear dependence of
m
on
d
due to the coupon collector problem (they require at least
m\geq d\log d
). However,
[
30
]
showed that leverage score sampling can be incorporated into sparse random matrices, which has led to fast algorithms for subspace embeddings in certain regimes
[
10
]
, as mentioned earlier.

Finally, there have been works analyzing sparse random matrices as embeddings with somewhat different and incomparable claims
[
31
,
32
,
30
,
33
]
. Most notably,
[
31
]
obtained a linear in
d
sparse subspace embedding under additional assumptions on the aspect ratio and leverage score distribution of the input, whereas
[
32
]
showed that an
O(d/\epsilon^{2})
-dimensional embedding with sparsity
O(\log(d)/\epsilon^{2})
satisfies the lower
\epsilon
-approximation, i.e.,
(1-\epsilon)\|Ax\|\leq\|\Pi Ax\|
.

2.1.
Main Result

The matrices
\Pi
that we work with are part of the well studied family of subspace embeddings termed OSNAP (
[
6
,
10
]
). Given dimensions
m
and
n
, and a sparsity parameter
p\in[0,1]
, these embeddings are defined as
m\times n
random matrices formed by stacking
pm
many
(1/p)\times n
CountSketch matrices vertically followed by scaling with
1/\sqrt{pm}
, leading to a construction with
pm
non-zero entries per column (where we assume that
pm
divides
m
). In other words, each column of
\Pi
is a stack of
pm
many subcolumns each of length
1/p
with exactly one
\pm 1/\sqrt{pm}
entry in a random position in each subcolumn. We give a formal definition in Section
3.5
.

Following usual practice (
[
6
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
), we frame our subspace embedding result in terms of bounding the extreme singular values of matrices of the form
\Pi U
for
n\times d
matrices
U
with orthonormal columns. In particular, for given

, we show,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Pb(1-\varepsilon\leq s_{\min}(\Pi U)\leq s_{\max}(\Pi U)\leq 1+\varepsilon)\geq 1-\delta,

for any
n\times d
matrices
U
with orthonormal columns when dimension
m
and sparsity
pm
are above specified thresholds. Here,
s_{\min}
and
s_{\max}
denote the smallest and largest singular values.

We now state our parameterized family of theorems that establish (
2.1
) for different values of
m
and

, explaining the trade-off between dimension and sparsity that arises from these guarantees.

2.2.
Overview of the Techniques

In this section, we provide an overview of our key technical contributions that lead to the proof of Theorem
2.1
. First, we give a very high-level proof sketch (Section
2.2.1
), and then we explain our key technique, iterative decoupling, in greater depth (Sections
2.2.2
and
2.2.3
), with further details provided in Section
2.2.4
. We use the symbols
C,c,C_{1},c_{1},\ldots
to denote absolute constants whose values can change from line to line.

2.2.1.
High-level proof sketch

We start by reducing the problem of showing (
2.1
) to a bound on the moments, following
[
6
,
10
]
and then using Markov’s inequality. Concretely, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\left(\E[\tr((\Pi U)^{T}(\Pi U)-I_{d})^{2q}]\right)^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{for}\quad q=\log(d/\delta).

Here
\tr(\cdot)
is the normalized trace
\tr(M)=\frac{1}{d}\Tr(M)
. For convenience, we work with a scaled version of
\Pi
so that all of its entries are in
\{0,1,-1\}
, i.e.,
S:=\sqrt{pm}\Pi
. The equivalent requirement is

\displaystyle\left(\E[\tr((SU)^{T}(SU)-pm\cdot I_{d})^{2q}]\right)^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq pm\varepsilon.

Standard calculations (see
[
6
,
9
,
10
]
) show that to get optimal requirement of the embedding dimension
m
and the sparsity level
pm
as conjectured in
[
6
]
, it suffices to get the bound

\displaystyle\left(\E[\tr((SU)^{T}(SU)-pmI_{d})^{2q}]\right)^{\frac{1}{2q}}=O(\sqrt{pmpd+q^{2}})

since
\sqrt{pmpd+q^{2}}=O(pm\varepsilon)
when
m\geq c_{1}d/\varepsilon^{2}
and
pm\geq c_{2}q/\varepsilon
, and recall that
q=\log(d/\delta)
.

It was observed by
[
8
]
and
[
10
]
that by using decoupling, one has

\displaystyle\left(\E[\tr((SU)^{T}(SU)-pmI_{d})^{2q}]\right)^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq C\left(\E[\tr|(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)|^{2q}]\right)^{\frac{1}{2q}}=C\lVert(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)\rVert_{2q}

for some constant
C
where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are i.i.d. copies of
S
and we define the absolute value
|M|=\sqrt{M^{T}M}
and the norm

\displaystyle\lVert M\rVert_{q}=\begin{cases}\big(\E[\tr|M|^{q}]\big)^{\frac{1}{q}}&\text{if }1\leq q<\infty\\
\|\lVert M\rVert_{op}\|_{\infty}&\text{if }q=\infty\end{cases}

Therefore, the OSE problem is reduced to proving

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lVert(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)\rVert_{2q}=O(\sqrt{pmpd+q^{2}})

For convenience, we define

\displaystyle K=

\displaystyle K(m,d,p,q):=\left(pmpd+(pm)^{1/(q)}q(pd+q)\right)^{1/2}\approx\sqrt{pmpd+q^{2}}

to be the almost correct moment bound for
\lVert(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)\rVert_{2q}
(for technical reasons, we introduce some extra errors in
K
, but these errors are not significant). To better understand
K
, we observe that if the embedding dimension and sparsity satisfy the assumptions from Conjecture
1.1
, then using
q=\log(d/\delta)
, we have
pmpd=(pm\varepsilon)^{2}=Cq^{2}
, so
K\approx\sqrt{pmpd}\approx q
.

Thus, our goal is to show

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lVert(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)\rVert_{2q}\leq CK.

Moment estimates obtained from iterative decoupling.

Using our iterative decoupling technique (explained in the following sections), we obtain a sequence of moment estimates of the following form,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lVert(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)\rVert_{2q\cdot 2^{k}}\leq C_{k}(K+K^{\alpha_{k}}(q^{2}R(I_{d}))^{1-\alpha_{k}}),\qquad\alpha_{k}=\max\Big\{0,\frac{2k-1}{2k+1}\Big\}

where
k=0,1,2,...
corresponds to the number of steps of iterative decoupling, and
R(I_{d})
is a quantity that arises from Gaussian universality analysis (which we will ignore for now). Note that when
k=0
, the second term in the bound is of the order at least
q^{2}
, whereas

(under the conjectured sparsity), so the second term dominates and the estimate is suboptimal.

2.2.2.
Towards iterative decoupling: Intuition in a general random matrix model

Before formally explaining the idea of iterative decoupling that we use to obtain the sequence of moment estimates (
2.6
), we first explain the motivation for iterative decoupling in a simplified setting. Suppose that we have a symmetric random matrix
M=\sum_{l}Z_{l}
where
Z_{1},...,Z_{n}
are independent random matrices and we want to estimate the norm
\lVert M\rVert_{2q}
(here we consider a general situation where
M
need not be OSNAP).

First attempt: Naïve full decoupling.

We first examine a simple and naïve strategy of decoupling higher-order moments, to see how it fails. We have the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\lVert M\rVert_{2q}^{2q}

\displaystyle=\tr(M^{2q})=\lVert M^{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert(\sum\limits_{l}Z_{l})^{2q}\rVert

\displaystyle=\Big\|\underbrace{\sum\limits_{(l_{1},...,l_{q}):\exists k_{1},k_{2}(k_{1}\neq k_{2},l_{k_{1}}=l_{k_{1}})}Z_{l_{1}}\cdots Z_{l_{q}}}_{T_{\text{diagonal}}}+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{(l_{1},...,l_{q}):\forall k_{1},k_{2}(k_{1}\neq k_{2}\Rightarrow l_{k_{1}}\neq l_{k_{1}})}Z_{l_{1}}\cdots Z_{l_{q}}}_{T_{\text{off-diagonal}}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\lVert T_{\text{diagonal}}\rVert_{2}+\lVert T_{\text{off-diagonal}}\rVert_{2})^{2}

By decoupling
[
34
]
and Jensen’s inequality, letting
Z_{1,l_{i}},Z_{2,l_{i}},...
denote i.i.d. copies of
Z_{l_{i}}
we have

\displaystyle\lVert T_{\text{off-diagonal}}\rVert_{2}\leq\Big\|\sum\limits_{(l_{1},...,l_{q}):\forall k_{1},k_{2}(k_{1}\neq k_{2}\Rightarrow l_{k_{1}}\neq l_{k_{1}})}Z_{1,l_{1}}\cdots Z_{q,l_{q}}\Big\|_{2}\leq C({q})\lVert M_{1}\cdots M_{q}\rVert_{2}

where
M_{1},...,M_{q}
are i.i.d. copies of
M
and the constant
C({q})
is a rapidly growing function of
q
,

\displaystyle C(q)=2^{q}\bigl(q^{\,q}-1\bigr)\bigl((q-1)^{\,q-1}-1\bigr)\times\cdots\times 3

Since
M_{1},...,M_{q}
are independent, the quantity

\displaystyle\lVert M_{1}\cdots M_{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\E\tr((M_{1}\cdots M_{q})^{T}M_{1}\cdots M_{q})

is completely determined by the second moments of
M
which can be calculated explicitly.
Therefore, by estimating the term

\lVert T_{\text{diagonal}}\rVert_{2}

we can get a bound for
\lVert M\rVert_{2q}
. However, there are two issues:

(1)

First, the term
\lVert T_{\text{diagonal}}\rVert_{2}

is very complicated, as it has many different types of components, e.g., terms with different numbers of collisions of indices, which makes it difficult to control.

(2)

Second, the constant
C(q)
blows up very rapidly when
q\to\infty
, and it is far too large even when
q
is logarithmic in the dimensions of
M
(which is the case in the OSNAP analysis).

Alternative approach: Moment bounds via Gaussian universality.

Another approach to bounding the higher-order moments of
M
is via Gaussian universality analysis, i.e., comparing these moments to the moments of a corresponding Gaussian random matrix.
We state here the universality result of Brailovskaya and van Handel, which forms the starting point of our analysis.

Proposition 2.4

(Theorem 2.9,
[
15
]
)
.

Let
M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
be a sum of independent symmetric matrices
Z_{i}
. Let
G
be a gaussian random matrix with the same mean and covariance profile as
M
. Then,

\left\lvert\lVert M\rVert_{2q}-\lVert G\rVert_{2q}\right\rvert\leq C\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert Z_{i}\rVert_{2q}^{2q}\right)^{\frac{1}{2q}}.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
q=2^{k}
. Let
M_{1},M_{2}
be i.i.d. copies of
M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
and
G_{1},G_{2}
be i.i.d. copies of the corresponding gaussian models for
M
. We obtain

(2.7)

\displaystyle\lVert M\rVert_{2q}\leq\lVert G\rVert_{2q}+C\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}=\Theta+C\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}

and we write
\Theta=\lVert G\rVert_{2q}
as the moment estimate from the gaussian model. In many cases, e.g., the OSNAP case and other models with sparse entries, the error term
\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}
dominates
\Theta
, so it would be desirable to improve this error term. More precisely, our goal is to show

\displaystyle\lVert M\rVert_{2q}\leq O(\Theta+\text{error})

with the error term as small as possible. (In our OSNAP model,
\Theta=\sqrt{pmpd}
and we want to show that the error term is as small as
q
so that
\Theta+\text{error}=O(\sqrt{pmpd}+q)=O(\sqrt{pmpd+q^{2}})
).

Iterative decoupling to fine-tune universality error.

We now show how to improve the term
\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}
by iterative decoupling. In the first step, we write

\displaystyle\lVert M\rVert_{2q}=

\displaystyle\lVert M^{2}\rVert_{q}^{1/2}=\Big\|\sum_{l}Z_{l}^{2}+\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}Z_{l_{1}}\cdot Z_{l_{2}}\Big\|_{q}^{1/2}\leq{\Big\lVert\!\!\!\underbrace{\sum_{l}Z_{l}^{2}}_{(\text{diagonal term})}\!\!\!\Big\rVert_{q}^{1/2}}\;+\;{\Big\lVert\!\!\!\underbrace{\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}Z_{l_{1}}\,Z_{l_{2}}}_{(\text{off-diagonal term})}\!\!\!\Big\rVert_{q}^{1/2}}

Since the diagonal term is a sum of independent random matrices, we can use standard tools such as matrix Bernstein or Matrix Rosenthal to get an easy (but suboptimal) bound. Fortunately, the diagonal term is generally of smaller order than
M^{2}
(because most terms
Z_{l_{1}}Z_{l_{2}}
are in the off-diagonal term), so these suboptimal bounds are sufficient for our purpose. Thus, suppose that we have a bound
\lVert\sum_{l}Z_{l}^{2}\rVert\leq\Theta^{2}
.

For the off-diagonal term, we use decoupling and obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}Z_{l_{1}}\cdot Z_{l_{2}}\rVert_{q}\leq C\lVert M_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}

Now, we condition on
M_{2}
and use Proposition
2.4
to obtain

\displaystyle\lVert M_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}\leq\lVert G_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}+\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(M_{1}M_{2}|M_{2})\rVert_{2q}\,q^{2}

where

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{q}(M_{1}M_{2}|M_{2})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E[\tr(|Z_{1,i}\cdot M_{2}|^{q})|M_{2}]\right)^{1/q}

For the term
\lVert G_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}
, we condition on
G_{1}
and use Proposition
2.4
again to obtain

\displaystyle\lVert G_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}\leq\lVert G_{1}\cdot G_{2}\rVert_{q}+\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(G_{1}M_{2}|G_{1})\rVert_{q}\,q^{2}

In summary, we have

\displaystyle\lVert M_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}\leq

\displaystyle\lVert G_{1}\cdot G_{2}\rVert_{q}+\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(G_{1}M_{2}|G_{1})\rVert_{q}q^{2}+\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(M_{1}M_{2}|M_{2})\rVert_{q}\,q^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Theta^{2}+\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(G_{1}M_{2}|G_{1})\rVert_{q}q^{2}+\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(M_{1}M_{2}|M_{2})\rVert_{q}\,q^{2}

where we use Hölder inequality to claim that
\lVert G_{1}\cdot G_{2}\rVert_{q}\leq\Theta^{2}
.

For the sake of this illustration, assume that
\lVert R_{q}(M_{1}M_{2}|M_{2})\rVert
and
\lVert R_{q}(G_{1}M_{2}|G_{1})\rVert
are easier to estimate (we show this is true for the OSNAP model) and we have

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(M_{1}M_{2}|M_{2})\rVert_{q}\leq\Theta\cdot\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lVert R_{q}(G_{1}M_{2}|G_{1})\rVert_{q}\leq\Theta\cdot\tilde{R}_{2q}(M).

Putting this all together, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert M\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lVert\sum_{l}Z_{l}^{2}\Big\rVert_{q}^{1/2}\;+\;{\Big\lVert\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}Z_{l_{1}}\,Z_{l_{2}}\Big\rVert_{q}^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Theta+(\Theta^{2}+\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(G_{1}M_{2}|G_{1})\rVert_{q}q^{2}+\lVert\tilde{R}_{q}(M_{1}M_{2}|M_{2})\rVert_{q}q^{2})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\Theta+(\Theta^{2}+\Theta\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}+\Theta\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2})^{1/2}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Theta+2\sqrt{\Theta}\sqrt{\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}}

Comparing the last line with the initial moment estimate (
2.7
) from universality, we see that the error term is changed from
\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}
to
\sqrt{\Theta}\sqrt{\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}}
. In other words, square root of
\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}
is replaced by square root of
\Theta
and this is an improvement when
\tilde{R}_{2q}(M)q^{2}
is of higher order than
\Theta
.

To carry this step further, we can choose to not apply the universality result to
\lVert M_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}
, but do decoupling on it again. More precisely, we condition on
M_{1}
and write

\displaystyle\lVert M_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}

\displaystyle=\lVert M_{2}\cdot M_{1}\cdot M_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q/2}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq{\Big\lVert\underbrace{\sum_{l}Z_{l}\cdot M_{1}\cdot M_{1}\cdot Z_{l}}_{(\text{diagonal term})}\Big\rVert_{q/2}^{1/2}}\;+\;{\Big\lVert\underbrace{\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}Z_{l_{1}}\cdot M_{1}\cdot M_{1}\cdot Z_{l_{2}}}_{(\text{off-diagonal term})}\Big\rVert_{q/2}^{1/2}}

By conditioning on
M_{1}
, we can still estimate the diagonal term by matrix Bernstein or Matrix Rosenthal. And for the off-diagonal, we have, by decoupling,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}Z_{l_{1}}\cdot M_{1}\cdot M_{1}\cdot Z_{l_{2}}\right\rVert_{q/2}=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\lVert\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}Z_{l_{1}}\cdot M_{1}\cdot M_{1}\cdot Z_{l_{2}}|M_{1}\rVert_{q/2}\right\rVert_{L_{q/2}(\Pb)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\lVert\lVert M_{3}M_{1}M_{1}M_{4}|M_{1}\rVert_{q/2}\right\rVert_{L_{q/2}(\Pb)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert M_{3}M_{1}M_{1}M_{4}\rVert_{q/2}

where we define
\lVert A|B\rVert_{2q}=(\E[(\tr|A|^{2q})|B])^{\frac{1}{2q}}
for any random matrices
A
and
B
.

Then we can use Proposition
2.4
conditionally (first on
M_{1}
and
M_{3}
, and then on
M_{1}
) to estimate
\lVert M_{3}M_{1}M_{1}M_{4}\rVert_{q/2}
. In the next step, we will further condition on
M_{3}M_{1}M_{1}
and decouple
M_{4}
to get a moment of the form
\lVert M_{5}M_{1}M_{1}M_{3}M_{3}M_{1}M_{1}M_{6}\rVert_{q/4}
. The calculations become complicated here so we do not go into details in this overview. But finally we will further improve the moment estimate for
\lVert M\rVert_{2q}
after each step. Figure

We can see that this new method avoids the issues of the naïve full decoupling method we described previously. First, in the naïve full decoupling method, the diagonal term

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{(l_{1},...,l_{q}):\exists k_{1},k_{2}(k_{1}\neq k_{2},l_{k_{1}}=l_{k_{1}})}Z_{l_{1}}\cdots Z_{l_{q}}

consists of collision terms that arise in power
2q
and these are very complicated and hard to deal with. However, in this new method the diagonal terms at each step, e.g.,

\displaystyle\sum_{l}Z_{l}^{2}\text{\ \ \ \ and \ \ \ \ }\sum_{l}Z_{l}\cdot M_{1}\cdot M_{1}\cdot Z_{l},

only include collision terms that arise in power
2
.
By conditioning on appropriate random matrices, these diagonal terms are sums of independent random matrices and therefore are easy to analyze.

Second, in the nav̈e full decoupling method, the constant factor
C(q)
depends on the moment
q
and since we need to choose
q=\log(d/\delta)
, this constant blows up rapidly. In our new method, after each time of decoupling, we still collect a constant factor, so our moment estimate for
\lVert M\rVert_{2q}
still includes a factor
C_{k}
. However, this constant
C_{k}
depends only on the number of steps
k
for which we do iterative decoupling (after which, we stop and apply the universality result), and we have seen that we can choose
k
however we wish, e.g., a fixed large constant or a function growing extremely slowly with
q
, so that the factor
C_{k}
does not blow up.

2.2.3.
Iterative Decoupling for OSNAP

We now specialize this paradigm to our specific setting. For convenience, let
X_{1}=S_{1}U
,
X_{2}=S_{2}U
, and
Y=X_{1}^{T}X_{2}
. Recall that in step 2 of the general iterative decoupling argument, we condition on
M_{1}
and decouple
M_{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\lVert M_{1}\cdot M_{2}\rVert_{q}\leq\text{(diagonal term)}+\lVert M_{3}M_{1}M_{1}M_{4}\rVert_{q/2}^{\frac{1}{2}}

And in the next step, we will further condition on
M_{3}M_{1}M_{1}
and decouple
M_{4}
.
To simplify the argument (in the specialized situation for
Y
), we work with a matrix of the form
VY
and its norm
\lVert VY\rVert_{2q}
where
V
is a fixed matrix, and then we will plug in the random matrices that we condition on in each step into
V
. In this way, we can do iterative decoupling by induction.

We start from some initial moment estimate results derived by applying Proposition
2.4
and improve this moment estimate iteratively (see Figure
3
for an overview of the steps). More precisely, assume that at step
k
we have the moment estimate

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{2q}\leq\Phi_{k}(V)

Then in step
k+1
, we write

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{4q}=

\displaystyle(\E\tr((Y^{T}V^{T}VY)^{2q}))^{\frac{1}{4q}}=\lVert Y^{T}V^{T}VY\rVert_{2q}^{1/2}

Now,
Y^{T}V^{T}VY=X_{2}^{T}X_{1}V^{T}VX_{1}^{T}X_{2}
contains degree two terms in both
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, which are independent. Thus, we can decouple the two
X_{1}
factors while conditioning on
X_{2}
and then decouple the two
X_{2}
factors similarly. This gives us the estimate

\displaystyle\lVert Y^{T}V^{T}VY\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\text{diagonal terms}\rVert_{2q}+4\lVert X_{2}^{T}X_{1}V^{T}VX_{3}^{T}X_{4}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\text{diagonal terms}\rVert_{2q}+4\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q}

Then, by conditioning on
Y_{1}
, we can estimate
\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q}
by applying the previous moment estimate to
\lVert WY_{2}\rVert_{2q}
where
W=Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V
.

We get,

\displaystyle\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q}\leq\lVert\Phi_{k}(W)\rVert_{L_{2q}}=\lVert\Phi_{k}(Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V)\rVert_{L_{2q}}

Plugging this into the previous inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{4q}=

\displaystyle(\E\tr((Y^{T}V^{T}VY)^{2q}))^{\frac{1}{4q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert Y^{T}V^{T}VY\rVert_{2q}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lVert\text{diagonal terms}\rVert^{1/2}_{2q}+2\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lVert\text{diagonal terms}\rVert^{1/2}_{2q}+2\lVert\Phi_{k}(Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V)\rVert_{L_{2q}}^{1/2}

After estimating the diagonal terms and simplifying
\lVert\Phi_{k}(Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V)\rVert_{L_{2q}}
, the last line will become a function of
V
, and we denote this function by
\Phi_{k+1}(V)
.

\lVert Y\rVert_{2q}\leq CK

)

to obtain an estimate for the moment of
Y=X_{1}^{T}X_{2}

To illustrate our argument, we show details of the dynamics in this iterative process in the first two steps. In step 0, we apply Proposition
2.4
in two steps as described in Section
2.2.2
and obtain an upper bound

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{2q}=

\displaystyle\lVert VX_{1}^{T}X_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\lVert VG_{1}^{T}G_{2}\rVert_{2q}+q^{2}R(V))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\sqrt{pmpd}\lVert V\rVert+q^{2}R(V))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(K\lVert V\rVert+q^{2}R(V))

where
R(V)
is a function of
V
(see Lemma
4.1
for details the precise definition of
R
). Plugging in
V=I_{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert Y\rVert_{2q}=

\displaystyle\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\lVert G_{1}^{T}G_{2}\rVert_{2q}+q^{2}R(I_{d}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\sqrt{pmpd}+q^{2}R(I_{d}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(K+q^{2}R(I_{d}))

where we can bound
R(I_{d})\approx(pm)^{1/q}\sqrt{pd+q}
(see Lemma
4.2
for details).

Right at this stage, we are able to obtain the first of our family of results, Corollary
2.2
. Recall from Section
2.2.1
that we want
\lVert Y\rVert_{2q}
to be
O(pm\varepsilon)
. It suffices to have
m\geq c_{1}d/\varepsilon^{2}
and
pm\geq q/\varepsilon
for
K
to be
O(pm\varepsilon)
. For the second term,
q^{2}(pm)^{1/q}\sqrt{pd+q}
to be
O(pm\varepsilon)
, we need
pm\geq(q^{5/2}/\varepsilon)+q^{4}
. This bound is similar to
[
10
, Theorem 7]
, with slightly worse powers of
\log
in the sparsity but much easier to obtain.

Next, in step 1, following the framework previously described, we have

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{4q}\leq

\displaystyle(\lVert\text{diagonal terms 1}\rVert_{2q}+4\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q})^{1/2}

Then we set
W=Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V
and apply the previous moment estimate to
\lVert WY_{2}\rVert_{2q}
when conditioning on
W
as we described before. We have

\displaystyle\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q}=

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert WY_{2}|W\rVert_{2q}\rVert_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert C(K\lVert W\rVert+q^{2}R(W))\rVert_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert C(K\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V\rVert+q^{2}R(Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V))\rVert_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(K\lVert Y_{1}\rVert_{2q}\lVert V\rVert^{2}+q^{2}\lVert R(Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V))\rVert_{L_{2q}}\right)

where we recall the definition
\lVert WY_{2}|W\rVert_{2q}=(\E[(\tr|WY_{2}|^{2q})|W])^{\frac{1}{2q}}
and use independence between
W
and
Y_{2}
.

The key reason that each time the moment estimate can be improved is because we can “replace”
Y_{1}
in
\lVert R(Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V))\rVert_{L_{2q}}
by the desired factor

K
.
More precisely, (see Lemma
5.4
) we have

\displaystyle\lVert R(Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V)\rVert_{L_{2q}}\leq K\lVert V\rVert R(V)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{4q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\lVert\text{diagonal terms 1}\rVert_{2q}+4\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\lVert\text{diagonal terms 1}\rVert_{2q}+C\left(K\lVert Y_{1}\rVert_{2q}\lVert V\rVert^{2}+q^{2}K\lVert V\rVert R(V)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\lVert\text{diagonal terms 1}\rVert_{2q}+C\left(K^{1/2}\lVert Y_{1}\rVert_{2q}^{1/2}\lVert V\rVert+K^{1/2}(q^{2}\lVert V\rVert R(V))^{1/2}\right)

In addition, we can bound

\displaystyle\lVert\text{diagonal terms 1}\rVert_{2q}^{1/2}\leq(K+\sqrt{K}\sqrt{\lVert Y\rVert_{2q}})\lVert V\rVert

by using standard tools such as Matrix Rosenthal (see Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
for details).

Plugging in
V=I_{d}
, and simplifying,

\displaystyle\lVert Y\rVert_{4q}\leq C\left(K+K^{1/2}(q^{2}R(V))^{1/2}\right)

From the last line, we see that after first time decoupling, we can replace square root of the bad factor
q^{2}R(I_{d})
by square root of the good factor
K
.

The calculations of the later steps become more complicated and the formal inductive argument is shown in the proof of Lemma
5.3
. In general, for large
k
, after
k
iterative decoupling steps, our moment bound becomes

\displaystyle\lVert Y\rVert_{2q\cdot 2^{k}}\leq C_{k}(K+K^{\frac{2k-1}{2k+1}}(q^{2}R(I_{d}))^{\frac{2}{2k+1}})

See Lemma
5.5
for details. Note that this formula gives a weaker result than we have seen for
k=1
because in the formal iterative decoupling argument in Lemma
5.3
, we introduce some artificial extra terms in the moment bound
\Phi_{k}
when
k
is small to to keep a convenient form of
\Phi_{k}
for all

, but these minor modifications do not affect the long run behavior.

2.2.4.
Obtaining the Subspace Embedding Guarantee

Recall that to get the OSE property, it suffices to require

\displaystyle C\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}\rVert_{2q}\leq pm\varepsilon

where
q=\log(d)
and the constant
C
comes from the initial decoupling step.

Since our iterative decoupling attains the bound

\displaystyle\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}\rVert_{2q}\leq\lVert Y\rVert_{2q\cdot 2^{k}}\leq C_{k}(K+K^{\frac{2k-1}{2k+1}}(q^{2}R(I_{d}))^{\frac{2}{2k+1}})

it suffices to require

(2.9)

C^{\prime}_{k}(K+K^{\frac{2k-1}{2k+1}}(q^{2}R(I_{d}))^{\frac{2}{2k+1}})\leq pm\varepsilon

Since
K\approx\sqrt{pmpd+q^{2}}
, roughly speaking we need

\displaystyle C^{\prime}_{k}\Big(\sqrt{pmpd+q^{2}}+\big(\sqrt{pmpd+q^{2}}\big)^{\frac{2k-1}{2k+1}}\big(q^{2}R(I_{d})\big)^{\frac{2}{2k+1}}\Big)\leq pm\varepsilon.

When
pmpd\geq q^{2}
, this requirement becomes

\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}_{k}\Big(\sqrt{pmpd}+\big(\sqrt{pmpd}\big)^{\frac{2k-1}{2k+1}}\big(q^{2}R(I_{d})\big)^{\frac{2}{2k+1}}\Big)\leq pm\varepsilon

Under optimal embedding dimension,
m=\theta\frac{d}{\varepsilon^{2}}
with
\theta=O(1)
, we have
pm\varepsilon=C^{\prime\prime\prime}\sqrt{pmpd}
, and so (
2.9
) can only be satisfied when
C^{\prime\prime}_{k}=O(1)
and

\displaystyle pm\varepsilon=C^{\prime\prime\prime}\sqrt{pmpd}\geq q^{2}R(I_{d})

leading to the same sparsity guarantee that we would attain using the initial moment estimate. This is why we need to trade off the embedding dimension in order to improve the sparsity via iterative decoupling.

Formally, by Thoerem
2.1
, when
m=\theta\cdot(d+\log(d/\delta))/\varepsilon^{2}
, the requirement (
2.9
) reduces to

\displaystyle{pm}\geq{\exp(\exp(c_{\ref{thm:osedecoup}.3}k))}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{1+\frac{1}{\log(d/\delta)}}}\Big(\theta\log(d/\delta)+\frac{\log(d/\delta)^{5/2}}{\theta^{k/2-1/4}}\Big)+\frac{\log(d/\delta)^{4}}{\theta^{k+1/2}}\right)

In order to remove the extra
\log(d/\delta)
factors, we need
\theta^{k/2-1/4}
to cancel out with
\log(d/\delta)^{5/2}
and
\theta^{k+1/2}
to cancel out with
\log(d/\delta)^{4}
, and this works only when
\theta
grows with
\log(d/\delta)
(but it may grow very slowly).

be a vector with
\lVert v^{T}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}=1
. Let
q\geq 1
be an integer. Then there exists constants
c_{\ref{lem:rowbounds}.1},c_{\ref{lem:rowbounds}.2}>0
such that,

\E\left[\lVert e_{1}^{T}X\rVert^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq c_{\ref{lem:rowbounds}.1}(\sqrt{pd}+\sqrt{q})

and,

\E\left[\lVert e_{1}^{T}Xv\rVert^{2q}_{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq c_{\ref{lem:rowbounds}.2}(\sqrt{p}+\sqrt{q})

Proof.

Note that
S_{1,1}
(which is the
(1,1)
th entry of
S
) is non-zero when the one hot distribution on the column submatrix
S_{[1:m/s]\times{1}}
has it’s non zero entry on the first row. The probability that this happens is
1/(m/s)=s/m=p
. Moreover, the columns of
S
are independent. Thus, we conclude that for looking at the distribution of
e_{1}^{T}X
we may assume that
S
has independent

\pm 1
entries sparsified by independent Bernoulli
p
random variables, i.e.,
s_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}\xi_{i,j}
where
\delta_{i,j}
are Bernoulli random variables taking value 1 with probability
p\in(0,1]
,
\xi_{i,j}
are random variables with
\Pb(\xi_{i,j}=1)=\Pb(\xi_{i,j}=-1)=1/2
and the collection
\{\delta_{i,j},\xi_{i,j}\}_{i\in[m],j\in[n]}
is independent.

Now,
\lVert e_{1}^{T}X\rVert
is a convex 1-Lipschitz function of the entries of the first row of
S
, and

\displaystyle E_{S}[\lVert e_{1}^{T}X\rVert]

\displaystyle\leq E_{S}[\lVert e_{1}^{T}X\rVert^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{pd}

By
[
35
, Theorem 6.10]
, we have, for
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\Pb_{S}(\lVert e_{1}^{T}X\rVert\geq\sqrt{pd}+\lambda)\leq\exp(-c_{1}\lambda^{2})

for some
c_{1}>0
. Similarly, since
\lvert e_{1}^{T}Xv\rvert
is a convex and 1-Lipschitz as a function of the entries of the first row of
S
,

\displaystyle\Pb_{S}(\lvert e_{1}^{T}Xv\rvert\geq\sqrt{p}+\lambda)\leq\exp(-c_{1}\lambda^{2})

Thus
\lVert e_{1}^{T}X\rVert
and
\lvert e_{1}^{T}Xv\rvert
have a subgaussian tail. The claims now follow by a standard moment computation of subgaussian random variables.

∎

Corollary 3.15

(Norm of a Random Row in OSNAP)
.

Let
S
be a fully independent unscaled OSNAP distribution with parameter
p
. Let
U
be an
n\times d
matrix such that
U^{T}U=I
. Let
\mu
be a random variable uniformly distributed in
J\subset[m]
and independent of
S
and
G
. Then, there exists
c_{\ref*{lem:rownormbound}}>0
such that for any positive integer
q>0
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\mu,S}[\lVert e_{\mu}^{T}SU\rVert^{q}]^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq c_{\ref*{lem:rownormbound}}\sqrt{\max\{pd,q\}}

Proof.

By Hölder’s inequality, it suffices to prove these bounds for moments of the order of the smallest even integer bigger than
q
, so without loss of generality, we may assume that
q
is an even integer.

\displaystyle\E_{\mu,S}[\lVert e_{\mu}^{T}SU\rVert^{q}]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{j\in J}\E_{S(t)}[\lVert e_{j}^{T}SU\rVert^{q}]

\displaystyle=\E_{S}[\lVert e_{1}^{T}S(t)U\rVert^{q}]

since rows of
S(t)U
are identically distributed. The claim now follows from Lemma
3.14
.

∎

4.
Initial Moment Estimate

Recall that, to get the optimal embedding dimension and sparsity requirement, it suffices to bound

(4.1)

\displaystyle\lVert(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)\rVert_{2q}=O(\sqrt{p\max\{m,q\}p\max\{d,q\}+q^{2}})

To this end, we design the following iterative decoupling method to get almost optimal estimate for
\lVert(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)\rVert_{2q}
. For convenience, let
Y=X_{1}^{T}X_{2}
. Briefly speaking, we work with the matrix with the form
VY
and its norm
\lVert VY\rVert_{2q}
where
V
is a fixed matrix. We start from some initial moment estimate results derived by applying Proposition
2.4
and improve this moment estimate iteratively. Each time, we write

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{4q}=

\displaystyle(\E\tr((Y^{T}V^{T}VY)^{2q}))^{\frac{1}{4q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert Y^{T}V^{T}VY\rVert_{2q}^{1/2}

and then use decoupling to estimate

\displaystyle\lVert Y^{T}V^{T}VY\rVert_{2q}\leq\lVert\text{diagonal terms}\rVert_{2q}+\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q}

Then, by conditioning on
Y_{1}
, we can estimate
\lVert Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}VY_{2}\rVert_{2q}
by applying the previous universality result to
\lVert WY_{2}\rVert_{2q}
where
W=Y_{1}^{T}V^{T}V
. By using some basic tools, each time the universality result is improved.

By using this method, we can iteratively improve the moment estimate for
VY
. And in the final estimate, we will plug in
V=\operatorname{Id}
to obtain an estimate for the moment of
Y=X_{1}^{T}X_{2}
, which is what we need for the analysis of OSE properties.

To make the above argument precise, we start with the following initial moment estimate, which follows directly from Proposition
2.4
.

Lemma 4.1

(Initial Moment Estimate)
.

Let
S
be an

p

Let

\displaystyle R_{1,2r}(V)=(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}(\lVert VX_{1}^{T}Z_{2,(l,\gamma)}U\rVert_{2r}^{2r}))^{1/(2r)}

, and

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{\ref{lem:initialuniv}}(\sqrt{p\max\{m,q\}p\max\{d,q\}}\lVert V\rVert+q^{2}R_{2,2r}(V)+q^{2}R_{1,2r}(V))

We have
\lVert VY\rVert_{2q}=\lVert VX_{1}^{T}X_{2}\rVert_{2q}

for some fixed matrix

2.4
to

\displaystyle\lVert VW^{T}X_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lVert VW^{T}G_{2}\rVert_{2q}+c_{2}(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}(\lVert VW^{T}Z_{2,(l,\gamma)}U\rVert_{2r}^{2r}))^{1/(2r)}q^{2}

and conditioning on

\displaystyle\lVert VY\rVert_{2q}=

\displaystyle\lVert VX_{1}^{T}X_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\xi

.

Formally, we have the following argument.

\displaystyle f_{2}(W)=

\displaystyle\lVert VW^{T}X_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\lVert VW^{T}G_{2}\rVert_{2q}+c_{2}(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}(\lVert VW^{T}Z_{2,(l,\gamma)}U\rVert_{2r}^{2r}))^{1/(2r)}q^{2}

\displaystyle\lVert VX_{1}^{T}G_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle(p\sum\limits_{i\in[m]}\lVert Ve_{i}\rVert^{2}_{l_{2}([d])})\lVert W_{4}^{T}\operatorname{Id}_{d\times d}W_{4}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p\lVert V\rVert_{F}^{2})\lVert W_{4}^{T}W_{4}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(p\lVert V\rVert^{2}d)\lVert W_{4}^{T}W_{4}\rVert_{2q}

Now we have finished bounding the first terms in the RHS of the matrix Rosenthal inequality. We will move on to bound the second term

\displaystyle(2q)\cdot(\E\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\lVert W_{4}^{T}(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)^{T}V^{T}V(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)W_{4}\rVert_{2q}^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

\displaystyle(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\lVert W_{4}^{T}(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)^{T}V^{T}V(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)W_{4}\rVert_{2q}^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

\displaystyle(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\lVert e_{\mu_{1,(l,\gamma)}}^{T}V^{T}Ve_{\mu_{1,(l,\gamma)}}W_{4}^{T}u_{l}u_{l}^{T}W_{4}\rVert_{2q}^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

\displaystyle(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}\E_{\mu_{1,(l,\gamma)}}((\lVert Ve_{\mu_{1,(l,\gamma)}}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})\lVert W_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

\displaystyle(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert W_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

In summary, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}W_{4}^{T}(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)^{T}V^{T}V(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)W_{4}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}((pd\lVert V\rVert^{2})\lVert W_{4}^{T}W_{4}\rVert_{2q}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert W_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

for any fixed matrix
W_{4}
.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}W_{3}^{T}(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)^{T}V^{T}V(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)W_{3}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}((pd\lVert V\rVert^{2})\lVert W_{3}^{T}W_{3}\rVert_{2q}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert W_{3}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

for any fixed matrix
W_{3}

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}W_{3}^{T}(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)^{T}V^{T}V(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)W_{4}\rVert_{2q}^{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lVert\Lambda W_{4}(\Lambda W_{4})^{T}\rVert_{2q}^{q}\lVert\Lambda W_{3}(\Lambda W_{3})^{T}\rVert_{2q}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(c_{1}((pd\lVert V\rVert^{2})\lVert W_{4}^{T}W_{4}\rVert_{2q}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert W_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\cdot\left(c_{1}((pd\lVert V\rVert^{2})\lVert W_{3}^{T}W_{3}\rVert_{2q}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert W_{3}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}\right)^{q}

Step 2: plugging in

and

and uncondition on

and

.

plugging in

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\E_{X_{4}}\left(c_{1}((pd\lVert V\rVert^{2})\lVert X_{4}^{T}X_{4}\rVert_{S_{2q}}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert X_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}\right)^{2q}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\E_{X_{3}}\left(c_{1}((pd\lVert V\rVert^{2})\lVert X_{3}^{T}X_{3}\rVert_{S_{2q}}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert X_{3}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}\right)^{2q}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\E_{X_{4}}\left(c_{1}((pd\lVert V\rVert^{2})\lVert X_{4}^{T}X_{4}\rVert_{S_{2q}}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert X_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}\right)^{2q}

Using triangle inequality for

\displaystyle\E_{X_{3},X_{4}}\lVert\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}X_{3}^{T}(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)^{T}V^{T}V(Z_{1,(l,\gamma)}U)X_{4}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}\left((pd\lVert V\rVert^{2})\lVert X_{4}^{T}X_{4}\rVert_{2q}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}\E_{X_{4}}(\lVert V\rVert^{2}\lVert X_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}\right)

\displaystyle c_{1}\lVert V\rVert^{2}\left(pd\lVert X_{4}^{T}X_{4}\rVert_{2q}+(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}\E_{X_{4}}(\lVert X_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}\right)

To bound the second term

\displaystyle(2q)\cdot(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}\E_{X_{4}}(\lVert X_{4}^{T}u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

\displaystyle(\sum\limits_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\frac{1}{d}(c_{3}(pd+q)\lVert u_{l}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}^{2})^{2q})^{1/(2q)}

\displaystyle=

This motivates the next lemma.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
S
be an
m\times n
matrix distributed according to the fully independent unscaled OSNAP distribution with parameter
p
. Let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
be i.i.d. copies of
S
. Let
u\in\R^{n}
and
v\in\R^{n}
be a vector with
\lVert u\rVert_{l_{2}([n])}=\lVert v\rVert_{l_{2}([n])}=1
. Let
q\geq 1
be an integer. Then there exists a constant
c_{\ref{lem:scalarnorm}}
such that

\displaystyle\lVert\langle S_{1}u,S_{2}v\rangle\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}\leq c_{\ref{lem:scalarnorm}}q

Proof.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle S=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{s}\xi_{(l,\gamma)}e_{\mu_{(l,\gamma)}}e_{l}^{\top}

We can rewrite
S
as

\displaystyle S=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\zeta_{(j,i)}(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{s}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\}}(\mu_{(j,\gamma)}))e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{s}\sum_{i\in[(m/s)(\gamma-1)+1:(m/s)\gamma]}\zeta_{(j,i)}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\}}(\mu_{(j,\gamma)}))e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}

where
\zeta_{(j,i)}
are i.i.d. random signs.

In fact, we can set
\xi_{(l,\gamma)}=\zeta_{(l,\mu_{(l,\gamma)})}
and recover the original definition.

Define
\eta_{i,j}=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{s}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\}}(\mu_{(j,\gamma)})
.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\langle S_{1}u,S_{2}v\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r}(\sum_{j_{1}}(S_{1})_{r,j_{1}}u_{j_{1}})(\sum_{j_{2}}(S_{2})_{r,j_{2}}v_{j_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r}(\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}(S_{1})_{r,j_{1}}\cdot u_{j_{1}}\cdot(S_{2})_{r,j_{2}}\cdot v_{j_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r}(\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}\zeta_{1,r,j_{1}}\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}\cdot u_{j_{1}}\cdot\zeta_{2,r,j_{2}}\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}\cdot v_{j_{2}})

Let
\iota
be the default embedding

\iota:[m]\times[n]\to[mn]

defined by

\iota(i,j)=n\,(i-1)+j.

We define random vectors
W_{1}\in\R^{mn}
and
W_{2}\in\R^{mn}
such that

\displaystyle W_{1}(\iota(i,j))=\zeta_{1,i,j}

and

\displaystyle W_{2}(\iota(i,j))=\zeta_{1,i,j}

Let
A
be an
mn\times mn
block diagonal matrix whose
r
th block along the diagonal is

\displaystyle A_{r}=

\displaystyle\diag(\eta_{1,r,1},...,\eta_{1,r,n})u\cdot(\diag(\eta_{2,r,1},...,\eta_{2,r,n})v)^{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{1,r,1}u_{1}\\
\eta_{1,r,2}u_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{1,r,n}u_{n}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{2,r,1}v_{1}\\
\eta_{2,r,2}v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{2,r,n}v_{n}\end{pmatrix}^{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{1,r,1}u_{1}\,\eta_{2,r,1}v_{1}&\eta_{1,r,1}u_{1}\,\eta_{2,r,2}v_{2}&\cdots&\eta_{1,r,1}u_{1}\,\eta_{2,r,n}v_{n}\\
\eta_{1,r,2}u_{2}\,\eta_{2,r,1}v_{1}&\eta_{1,r,2}u_{2}\,\eta_{2,r,2}v_{2}&\cdots&\eta_{1,r,2}u_{2}\,\eta_{2,r,n}v_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\eta_{1,r,n}u_{n}\,\eta_{2,r,1}v_{1}&\eta_{1,r,n}u_{n}\,\eta_{2,r,2}v_{2}&\cdots&\eta_{1,r,n}u_{n}\,\eta_{2,r,n}v_{n}\end{pmatrix}

Therefore, direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\langle S_{1}u,S_{2}v\rangle=W_{1}^{T}AW_{2}

In fact, since
A
is block diagonal, we may write

\displaystyle W_{1}^{T}AW_{2}=\sum_{r\in[m]}\;\bigl(W_{1}^{r}\bigr)^{T}A_{r}W_{2}^{r},

where
W_{1}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the subvector of
W_{1}
corresponding to the indices
\iota(r,1),\dots,\iota(r,n)
and similarly for
W_{2}^{r}
.

For a fixed
r
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl(W_{1}^{r}\bigr)^{T}A_{r}W_{2}^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n}\Bigl[W_{1}^{r}\Bigr]_{j_{1}}\,\Bigl[A_{r}\Bigr]_{j_{1},j_{2}}\,\Bigl[W_{2}^{r}\Bigr]_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{n}\zeta_{1,r,j_{1}}\Bigl(\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}u_{j_{1}}\,\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}v_{j_{2}}\Bigr)\zeta_{2,r,j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}\zeta_{1,r,j_{1}}\,\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}\,u_{j_{1}}\,\zeta_{2,r,j_{2}}\,\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}\,v_{j_{2}}.

Summing over all
r
gives

\displaystyle W_{1}^{T}AW_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\;\bigl(W_{1}^{r}\bigr)^{T}A_{r}W_{2}^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}\zeta_{1,r,j_{1}}\,\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}\,u_{j_{1}}\,\zeta_{2,r,j_{2}}\,\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}\,v_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle S_{1}u,S_{2}v\rangle.

Conditioning on
\mu_{1,(l,\gamma)}
and
\mu_{2,(l,\gamma)}
(and therefore conditioning on
A
) and using iterated expectation, Lemma
3.5
implies

\displaystyle\lVert W_{1}^{T}AW_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}\leq c_{1}(\sqrt{2q}\lVert\lVert A\rVert_{F}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}+2q\lVert\lVert A\rVert\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)})

for some constant
c_{1}
.

To estimate
\lVert\lVert A\rVert_{F}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}
, we observe that

\displaystyle\lVert A\rVert_{F}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{r,j_{1},j_{2}}(\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}u_{j_{1}}\,\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}v_{j_{2}})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}\sum_{r}(\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}u_{j_{1}}\,\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}v_{j_{2}})^{2}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}u_{j_{1}}^{2}v_{j_{2}}^{2}(\sum_{r}\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}^{2}\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}^{2})

We further observe that

\displaystyle(\sum_{r}\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}^{2}\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}^{2})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma=1}^{s}\sum_{r\in[(m/s)(\gamma-1)+1:(m/s)\gamma]}\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}^{2}\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma=1}^{s}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mu_{1,r,j_{1}}=\mu_{2,r,j_{1}}\}}

Since
\{\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mu_{1,r,j_{1}}=\mu_{2,r,j_{1}}\}}\}_{r=1}^{2}
are independent Bernoulli-
p
random variables, by Lemma 2 in
[
39
]
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\sum_{r}\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}^{2}\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}^{2}\rVert_{L_{q}}=\lVert\sum_{\gamma=1}^{s}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\mu_{1,r,j_{1}}=\mu_{2,r,j_{1}}\}}\rVert_{L_{q}}\leq c_{2}(q)

for some constant
c_{2}
.

Therefore, by triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert A\rVert_{F}\rVert_{L_{2q}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert A\rVert_{F}^{2}\rVert_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}u_{j_{1}}^{2}v_{j_{2}}^{2}\lVert(\sum_{r}\eta_{1,r,j_{1}}^{2}\eta_{2,r,j_{2}}^{2})\rVert_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{2}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}u_{j_{1}}^{2}v_{j_{2}}^{2}q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{2}q

Next, we have

\displaystyle\lVert A\rVert=

\displaystyle\max\limits_{r}\lVert A_{r}\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\limits_{r}\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{1,r,1}u_{1}\\
\eta_{1,r,2}u_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{1,r,n}u_{n}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{2,r,1}v_{1}\\
\eta_{2,r,2}v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{2,r,n}v_{n}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert\cdot\lVert v\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

almost surely.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lVert W_{1}^{T}AW_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}\leq c_{1}(\sqrt{2q}\sqrt{c_{2}q}+2q\cdot 1)\leq c_{3}q

∎

We now proceed to bound the moment
\lVert\lVert(S_{1}U)^{T}(S_{2}U)u\rVert_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}
or equivalently,

\displaystyle\lVert\langle X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v,X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rangle\rVert_{L_{q}(\Pb)}

for
X_{i}=S_{i}U
by formally describing the decoupling steps used to bound the latter.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
S
be an
m\times n
matrix distributed according to the fully independent unscaled OSNAP distribution with parameter
p
. Let
U
be an arbitrary
n\times d
deterministic matrix such that

. Let
X=SU
. Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be i.i.d. copies of
X
. Let
u\in\R^{d}
be a vector with
\lVert u^{T}\rVert_{l_{2}([d])}=1
. Let
q\geq 1
be an integer. Then there exists a constant
c_{\ref{lem:x1x2unorm}}
such that

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}u\rVert_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}\leq c_{\ref{lem:x1x2unorm}}\left(\sqrt{pmpd}+(pmd)^{\frac{1}{q}}(\sqrt{pdq}+q)\right)

Proof.

Recall that for a matrix
S
with the fully independent unscaled OSNAP distribution we have
S=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}Z_{(l,\gamma)}
, for independent summands
Z_{(l,\gamma)}
as described in Definition
3.10

\displaystyle\langle X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v,X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\langle X_{1}^{T}Z_{2,(l,\gamma)}Uv,X_{1}^{T}Z_{2,(l,\gamma)}Uv\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l,\gamma)}X_{2}v,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l,\gamma)}X_{2}v\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(l_{1},\gamma_{1})\neq(l_{2},\gamma_{2})\\
(l_{3},\gamma_{3})\neq(l_{4},\gamma_{4})\end{subarray}}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}Z_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})}Uv,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})}Uv\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l,\gamma)}Z_{2,(l,\gamma)}Uv,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l,\gamma)}Z_{2,(l,\gamma)}Uv\rangle

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}u\rVert_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}

\displaystyle=\left(\E\left[\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}u\rVert_{2}^{2q}\right]\right)^{1/2q}

\displaystyle=\left(\E\left[\left(\langle X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v,X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rangle\right)^{q}\right]\right)^{1/2q}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\E\left[\left(Y_{1}+Y_{2}+Y_{3}\right)^{q}\right]\right)^{1/2q}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\E\left[Y_{1}^{q}\right]\right)^{1/2q}+\left(\E\left[Y_{2}^{q}\right]\right)^{1/2q}+\left(\E\left[Y_{3}^{q}\right]\right)^{1/2q}

First we consider the
Y_{1}

\displaystyle Y_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}v^{T}U^{T}Z^{T}_{2,(l,\gamma)}X_{1}X_{1}^{T}Z_{2,(l,\gamma)}Uv

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}v^{T}U^{T}e_{l}e_{\mu_{2,(l,\gamma)}}^{T}X_{1}X_{1}^{T}e_{\mu_{2,(l,\gamma)}}e_{l}^{T}Uv

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\lVert e^{T}_{\mu_{2,(l,\gamma)}}X_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}\langle u_{l},v\rangle^{2}

where
\{u_{l}\}_{l\in[n]}
are the rows of
U
.

We condition on
X_{1}
and evaluate
\E\left[Y_{1}^{q}|X_{1}\right]

are independent, so we can use the following version of Rosenthal’s inequality,

Lemma 8.3

(Theorem 14.10 from
[
35
]
)
.

Let
R=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Z_{i}
where
R_{1},...,R_{n}
are independent and nonnegative random variables. Then the exists a constant

q\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle(\E(R^{q}))^{1/q}\leq 2\E(R)+c_{\ref*{lem:rosenthal}}q(\sum_{j\in[n]}\E[R_{j}^{q}])^{1/q}

Hence,

\displaystyle(\E[R^{q}])

\displaystyle\leq 2^{q}\left(2^{q}(\E[R])^{q}+c_{\ref*{lem:rosenthal}}^{q}q^{q}(\sum_{j\in[n]}\E[R_{j}^{q}])\right)

In our case,

\displaystyle\E[Y_{1}|X_{1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\langle u_{l},v\rangle^{2}\E\left[\lVert e^{T}_{\mu_{2,(l,\gamma)}}X_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\langle u_{l},v\rangle^{2}(s/m)\sum_{j\in[(m/s)(\gamma-1):(m/s)\gamma]}\lVert e^{T}_{j}X_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[n]}p\cdot\langle u_{l},v\rangle^{2}\lVert X_{1}\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=p\lVert X_{1}\rVert_{F}^{2}

since
\sum_{l\in[n]}\langle u_{l},v\rangle^{2}=\lVert Uv\rVert_{2}^{2}=1
.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\langle u_{l},v\rangle^{2q}\E\left[\lVert e^{T}_{\mu_{2,(l,\gamma)}}X_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2q}\big|X_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[n],j\in[m]}p\langle u_{l},v\rangle^{2q}\lVert e^{T}_{j}X_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2q}

\displaystyle\leq pm\lVert e^{T}_{I}X_{1}\rVert^{2q}

where
I
is a integer valued random variable uniformly distributed in
[m]
independent of all existing random variables (the reason for expressing the sum in this way is to later use an existing result to control the moments of this quantity after unconditioning on
X_{1}
)

By Lemma
8.3
,

\displaystyle\E\left[Y_{1}^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\E\left[Y_{1}^{q}|X_{1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[4^{q}(p)^{q}\lVert X_{1}\rVert_{F}^{2q}+(2c_{\ref{lem:rosenthal}}q)^{q}pm\lVert e^{T}_{I}X_{1}\rVert^{2q}\right]

\displaystyle\text{and, }\E\left[Y_{1}^{q}\right]^{1/2q}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{p}\E\left[\lVert X_{1}\rVert_{F}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}+\sqrt{2c_{\ref{lem:rosenthal}}q}(pm)^{1/2q}\E\left[\lVert e_{I}^{T}X_{1}\rVert^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

Since the rows of
X_{1}
are identically distributed,
\E\left[\lVert e_{I}^{T}X_{1}\rVert^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}=\E\left[\lVert e_{1}^{T}X_{1}\rVert^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}
.
By Lemma
3.13
,
\E\left[\lVert X_{1}\rVert_{F}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq c_{\ref{lem:xfrobnorm}.1}(\sqrt{pmd}+\sqrt{q})
, and by Lemma
3.14
,
\E\left[\lVert e_{1}^{T}X_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq c_{\ref{lem:rowbounds}.1}(\sqrt{pd}+\sqrt{q})
. Finally we get,

\displaystyle\E\left[Y_{1}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\sqrt{pmpd}+(pm)^{1/2q}(\sqrt{pdq}+q)\right)

Here we have absorbed the
\sqrt{pq}
term coming from
2\sqrt{p}\E\left[\lVert X_{1}\rVert_{F}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}
into the
\sqrt{pdq}+q
term by adjusting
C_{1}
.

Moving on to the
Y_{2}
term,

\displaystyle Y_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l,\gamma)}X_{2}v,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l,\gamma)}X_{2}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}v^{T}X_{2}^{T}e_{\mu_{2,(l,\gamma)}}u_{l}^{T}u_{l}e_{\mu_{2,(l,\gamma)}}^{T}X_{2}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\lVert u_{l}\rVert^{2}(X_{2}v)^{2}_{\mu_{1,(l,\gamma)}}

As in the previous case we condition on
X_{2}
and evaluate
\E[Y_{2}^{q}|X_{2}]
using Rosenthal’s inequality.

\displaystyle\E[Y_{2}|X_{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\lVert u_{l}\rVert^{2}\E\left[(X_{2}v)_{\mu_{1,(l,\gamma)}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\lVert u_{l}\rVert^{2}(s/m)\sum_{j\in[(m/s)(\gamma-1):(m/s)\gamma]}(X_{2}v)_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[n]}p\cdot\lVert u_{l}\rVert^{2}\lVert X_{2}v\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=pd\lVert X_{2}v\rVert_{2}^{2}

since
\sum_{l\in[n]}\lVert u_{l}\rVert^{2}=d
, and,

\displaystyle\sum_{(l,\gamma)\in\Xi}\lVert u_{l}\rVert^{2q}\E\left[(X_{2}v)_{\mu_{1,(l,\gamma)}}^{2q}|X_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\in[n],j\in[m]}p\lVert u_{l}\rVert^{2q}(X_{2}v)_{j}^{2q}

\displaystyle\leq dpm(X_{2}v)_{I}^{2q}

where
I
is a integer valued random variable uniformly distributed in
[m]
independent of all existing random variables.
By Lemma
8.3
,

\displaystyle\E\left[Y_{2}^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\E\left[Y_{2}^{q}|X_{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[4^{q}(pd)^{q}\lVert X_{2}v\rVert_{2}^{2q}+(2c_{\ref{lem:rosenthal}}q)^{q}pmd(X_{2}v)_{I}^{2q}\right]

\displaystyle\text{and, }\E\left[Y_{2}^{q}\right]^{1/2q}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{pd}\E\left[\lVert X_{2}v\rVert_{2}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}+\sqrt{2c_{\ref{lem:rosenthal}}q}(pmd)^{1/2q}\E\left[(X_{2}v)_{I}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

Since the rows of
X_{2}
are identically distributed,
\E\left[(X_{2}v)_{I}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}=\E\left[(X_{2}v)_{1}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}
. By Lemma
3.13
,
\E\left[\lVert X_{2}v\rVert^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq c_{\ref{lem:xfrobnorm}.2}(\sqrt{pm}+\sqrt{q})
, and by Lemma
3.14

\E\left[(X_{2}v)_{I}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}\leq 2c_{\ref{lem:rowbounds}.2}\sqrt{q}
. Finally we get,

\displaystyle\E\left[Y_{2}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}(\sqrt{pmpd}+\sqrt{pdq}+(pmd)^{1/2q}\cdot q)

Now recall the
Y_{3}
term,

Y_{3}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(l_{1},\gamma_{1})\neq(l_{2},\gamma_{2})\\
(l_{3},\gamma_{3})\neq(l_{4},\gamma_{4})\end{subarray}}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}Z_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})}Uv,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})}Uv\rangle

We wish to look at
\E\left[Y_{3}^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}
, and we evaluate it by first conditioning on
\mathcal{Z}_{1}:=\{Z_{1,(l,\gamma}\}_{l\in[n],\gamma\in[pm]}
, i.e. as
\E\left[\E\left[Y_{3}|\mathcal{Z}_{1}\right]^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}
.

For fixed
(l_{1},\gamma_{1}),(l_{2},\gamma_{2})
, let

h_{2}(Z_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})=\sum_{(l_{3},\gamma_{3})\neq(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}Z_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})}Uv,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})}Uv\rangle

Clearly
h_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a deterministic multilinear function after conditioning on
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
. Then,

Y_{3}=\sum_{(l_{1},\gamma_{1})\neq(l_{2},\gamma_{2})}h_{2}(Z_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})

By Lemma
A.1
,

\displaystyle\E[\lvert Y_{3}\rvert^{q}]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\E\left[\left\lvert 2\sum_{(l_{1},\gamma_{1}),(l_{2},\gamma_{2})\in\Xi}h_{2}(Z^{\prime}_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})+h_{2}(Z_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z^{\prime}_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})\right\rvert^{q}\Bigg|\mathcal{Z}_{1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\left\lvert 2\sum_{(l_{1},\gamma_{1}),(l_{2},\gamma_{2})\in\Xi}h_{2}(Z^{\prime}_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})+h_{2}(Z_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z^{\prime}_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})\right\rvert^{q}\right]

Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{(l_{1},\gamma_{1}),(l_{2},\gamma_{2})\in\Xi}h_{2}(Z^{\prime}_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(l_{3},\gamma_{3})\neq(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}\left(\sum_{(l_{1},\gamma_{1})\in\Xi}Z^{\prime}_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})}\right)Uv,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}\left(\sum_{(l_{2},\gamma_{2})\in\Xi}Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})}\right)Uv\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(l_{3},\gamma_{3})\neq(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}X_{3}v,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}X_{2}v\rangle

where
X_{3}
is an independent copy of
X_{2}
. So we get,

\displaystyle\E[\lvert Y_{3}\rvert^{q}]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\left\lvert 2\sum_{(l_{1},\gamma_{1}),(l_{2},\gamma_{2})\in\Xi}h_{2}(Z^{\prime}_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})+h_{2}(Z_{2,(l_{1},\gamma_{1})},Z^{\prime}_{2,(l_{2},\gamma_{2})})\right\rvert^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\left\lvert 2\sum_{(l_{3},\gamma_{3})\neq(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}X_{3}v,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}X_{2}v\rangle+\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}X_{2}v,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}X_{3}v\rangle\right\rvert^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\left\lvert\sum_{(l_{3},\gamma_{3})\neq(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}h_{1}(Z_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})},Z_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})})\right\rvert^{q}\right]

where,

h_{1}(Z_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})},Z_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})})=2\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}X_{3}v,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}X_{2}v\rangle+2\langle U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{3},\gamma_{3})}X_{2}v,U^{T}Z^{T}_{1,(l_{4},\gamma_{4})}X_{3}v\rangle

is a deterministic multilinear function in its inputs after conditioning on
X_{2}
and
X_{3}
. Applying Lemma
A.1
again and proceeding similarly, we get,

\displaystyle\E[\lvert Y_{3}\rvert^{q}]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\left\lvert 4\left(\langle X_{4}^{T}X_{3}v,X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rangle+\langle X_{1}^{T}X_{3}v,X_{4}^{T}X_{2}v\rangle+\langle X_{4}^{T}X_{2}v,X_{1}^{T}X_{3}v\rangle+\langle X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v,X_{4}^{T}X_{3}v\rangle\right)\right\rvert^{q}\right]

Thus,

\displaystyle\E[Y_{3}^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\E[\langle X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v,X_{3}^{T}X_{4}v\rangle^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle=4\E[\E[\langle X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v,X_{3}^{T}X_{4}v\rangle^{q}|X_{1},X_{2}]]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle=4\E[\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rVert^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}\E[\langle u,X_{3}^{T}X_{4}v\rangle^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle=4\E[\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rVert^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}\E[\langle X_{3}u,X_{4}v\rangle^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\sqrt{q}\E[\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rVert^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

where
u\in\R^{d}
is some fixed unit vector and the last inequality follows from Lemma
8.1
.

Putting the estimates for the
Y_{1},Y_{2}
and
Y_{3}
terms together, we get,

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}u\rVert_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(\sqrt{pmpd}+(pm)^{1/2q}(\sqrt{pdq}+q))

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{2}(\sqrt{pmpd}+\sqrt{pdq}+(pmd)^{1/2q}\cdot q)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+C_{3}\sqrt{q}\E[\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rVert_{2}^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}(\sqrt{pmpd}+(pmd)^{\frac{1}{2q}}(\sqrt{pdq}+q))+C_{3}\sqrt{q}\E[\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rVert_{2}^{q}]^{\frac{1}{2q}}

Let
C_{5}>0
be a constant which satisfies
C_{4}+C_{3}\sqrt{C_{5}}<C_{5}
and after fixing such a
C_{5}
let
C_{6}>0
be chosen so that it satisfies
C_{4}+C_{3}\sqrt{C_{5}}+C_{3}\sqrt{C_{6}}\leq C_{6}
.

Assume that
\E[\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}v\rVert_{2}^{q}]^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq C_{5}\sqrt{pmpd}+C_{6}(pmd)^{1/q}(\sqrt{pdq}+q)
. This is true when
q=2
for
C_{5}\geq 1
since
\E[X_{2}^{T}X_{1}X_{1}^{T}X_{2}]=pmpdI_{d}
. Then,

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}u\rVert_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}(\sqrt{pmpd}+(pmd)^{\frac{1}{2q}}(\sqrt{pdq}+q))

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{3}\sqrt{q}\sqrt{C_{5}}(pmpd)^{1/4}+C_{3}\sqrt{q}\sqrt{C_{6}}(pmd)^{1/2q}(\sqrt{pdq}+q)^{\frac{1}{2}}

Note that
q^{\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{pdq}+q)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq(\sqrt{pdq}+q)
. So,

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}u\rVert_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}\sqrt{pmpd}+C_{3}\sqrt{q}\sqrt{C_{5}}(pmpd)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\qquad+(C_{3}\sqrt{C_{6}}+C_{4})(pmd)^{1/2q}(\sqrt{pdq}+q)

If

, then
C_{4}\sqrt{pmpd}+C_{3}\sqrt{q}\sqrt{C_{5}}(pmpd)^{1/4}\leq(C_{4}+C_{3}\sqrt{C_{5}})\sqrt{pmpd}\leq C_{5}\sqrt{pmpd}
by our choice of
C_{5}
and
(C_{3}\sqrt{C_{6}}+C_{4})\leq C_{6}
by our choice of

, so,

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}u\rVert_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\sqrt{pmpd}+C_{6}(pmd)^{1/2q}(\sqrt{2pdq}+2q)

. In this case,

\displaystyle\lVert\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}u\rVert_{2}\rVert_{L_{2q}(\Pb)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}\sqrt{pmpd}

\displaystyle\qquad+(C_{3}\sqrt{C_{6}}+C_{4}+C_{3}\sqrt{C_{5}})(pmd)^{1/2q}(\sqrt{pdq}+q)

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\sqrt{pmpd}+C_{6}(pmd)^{1/2q}(\sqrt{2pdq}+2q)

by our choice of

and

∎

Appendix A
Decoupling

In this section, we provide formal proofs of the decoupling steps mentioned in Section
2.2
. We start with a general decoupling lemma that will later be specialized to the specific cases required.

A.1.
General Decoupling Lemma

Lemma A.1

(General Decoupling)
.

For a natural number
n
, let
\mathcal{Z}:=\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
be a collection of zero mean independent random variables taking values in some Euclidean space
\R^{N}
. Let
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}:=\{Z^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
be an independent copy of
\mathcal{Z}
. Let
h:\R^{N}\times\R^{N}\to\R^{M}
be a multilinear function, and let
\lVert\cdot\rVert
be any norm on
\R^{M}
such that
\E\left[\left\lVert h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right]<\infty
for
i\neq j
. Let
\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a convex nondecreasing function such that
\E\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right)\right]<\infty
for
i\neq j
. Then,

\E\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i,j\in[n],i\neq j}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right)\right]\leq\E\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert 2\sum_{i,j\in[n]}h(Z_{i},Z^{\prime}_{j})+h(Z^{\prime}_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right)\right]

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{w}^{T}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
be a vector of independent random variables such that
\Pb(w_{i}=1)=\Pb(w_{i}=0)=1/2
and
\mathbf{w}
is independent from
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and
\{Z^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
. Then, whenever
i\neq j
,
\E[\mathbf{1}_{w_{i}\neq w_{j}}]=1/2
. So we have,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{n}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})=2\E_{\mathbf{w}}\left[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{w_{i}\neq w_{j}}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right]

By Jensen’s inequality
[
40
, Lemma 4.5, p.86]

\displaystyle\E\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i,j\in[n],i\neq j}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{Z}}\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert 2\E_{\mathbf{w}}\left[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{w_{i}\neq w_{j}}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right]\right\rVert\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\mathcal{Z}}\E_{\mathbf{w}}\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{w_{i}\neq w_{j}}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\mathbf{w}}\E_{\mathcal{Z}}\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{w_{i}\neq w_{j}}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right)\right]

Fix
\mathbf{w}
, and let
J=\{j\in[n]|w_{j}=1\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{Z}}\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{w_{i}\neq w_{j}}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{Z}}\left[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i\in J,j\in[n]\backslash J}2h(Z_{i},Z_{j})+\sum_{i\in[n]\backslash J,j\in J}2h(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right\rVert\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{Z}}\Biggl[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{i\in J,j\in J^{C}}2\left(h(Z_{i},Z_{j})+h(Z_{j},Z_{i})\right)\right\rVert\right)\Biggr]

The key observation for decoupling is that the collection of random variables
\mathcal{Z}_{J}:=\{Z_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is independent of the collection
\mathcal{Z}_{J^{C}}:=\{Z_{j}\}_{j\in J^{C}}

, so the above expectation does not change if the collection
\mathcal{Z}_{J}
is replaced by
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}_{J}:=\{Z^{\prime}\}_{j\in J}
thought of as a subset of the collection
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{Z}_{J},\mathcal{Z}_{J^{C}}}\Biggl[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{(i,j)\in J\times J^{C}}2(h(Z_{i},Z_{j})+h(Z_{j},Z_{i}))\right\rVert\right)\Biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{Z^{\prime}}_{J},\mathcal{Z}_{J^{C}}}\Biggl[\Phi\left(\left\lVert\sum_{(i,j)\in J\times J^{C}}2(h(Z_{i}^{\prime},Z_{j})+h(Z_{j},Z_{i}^{\prime}))\right\rVert\right)\Biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{Z^{\prime}}_{J},\mathcal{Z}_{J^{C}}}\Biggl[\Phi\Biggl(\Biggl\|\sum_{(i,j)\in J\times J^{C}}2(h(Z_{i}^{\prime},Z_{j})+h(Z_{j},Z_{i}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad+\E_{\mathcal{Z}_{J},\mathcal{Z^{\prime}}_{J^{C}}}\biggl[\sum_{(i,j)\notin J\times J^{C}}2(h(Z_{i}^{\prime},Z_{j})+h(Z_{j},Z_{i}^{\prime}))\biggr]\Biggr\|\Biggr)\Biggr]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{Z^{\prime}}_{J},\mathcal{Z}_{J^{C}}}\E_{\mathcal{Z}_{J},\mathcal{Z^{\prime}}_{J^{C}}}\Biggl[\Phi\Biggl(\Biggl\|\sum_{(i,j)\in J\times J^{C}}2(h(Z_{i}^{\prime},Z_{j})+h(Z_{j},Z_{i}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{(i,j)\notin J\times J^{C}}2(h(Z_{i}^{\prime},Z_{j})+h(Z_{j},Z_{i}^{\prime}))\Biggr\|\Biggr)\Biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{Z^{\prime}},\mathcal{Z}}\Biggl[\Phi\Biggl(\Biggl\|\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[n]}2(h(Z_{i}^{\prime},Z_{j})+h(Z_{j},Z_{i}^{\prime}))\Biggr\|\Biggr)\Biggr]

where in the third equality the expectations we add are all
0
by multilinearity of
h
and the zero mean property of
Z_{i}
, and the inequality step follows by taking the expectations outside using Jensen’s inequality.
∎

A.2.
Decoupling for General Random Matrices

Next, we use Lemma
A.1
to bound
\lVert X^{T}X\rVert_{2q}
when
X
is a sum of independent random matrices.

Lemma A.2

(Decoupling)
.

Let
X=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}Z_{l}
where
Z_{1},...,Z_{L}
are independent
m\times d
random matrices.
Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent copies of
X
. Let
q
be a positive integer. Then we have

\displaystyle\lVert X^{T}X\rVert_{2q}\leq\lVert\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}(Z_{l})^{T}Z_{l}\rVert_{2q}+2\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}+X_{2}^{T}X_{1}\rVert_{2q}

Proof.

By definition, for any symmetric matrix
M
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert M\rVert_{2q}=

\displaystyle\big(\E(\tr|M|^{2q})\big)^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\E(\tr(\sqrt{(M)^{2}})^{2q})\big)^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\E(\tr((M)^{2})^{q})\big)^{\frac{1}{2q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\E(\tr((M)^{2q})\big)^{\frac{1}{2q}}

Therefore, since
X^{T}X
,
\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}(Z_{l})^{T}Z_{l}
, and
X_{1}^{T}X_{2}+X_{2}^{T}X_{1}
are symmetric, we have
\lVert X^{T}X\rVert_{2q}=\big(\E(\tr((X^{T}X)^{2q})\big)^{\frac{1}{2q}}
,
\lVert\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}(Z_{l})^{T}Z_{l}\rVert_{2q}=\big(\E(\tr((\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}(Z_{l})^{T}Z_{l})^{2q})\big)^{\frac{1}{2q}}
, and
\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}+X_{2}^{T}X_{1}\rVert_{2q}=\big(\E(\tr((X_{1}^{T}X_{2}+X_{2}^{T}X_{1})^{2q})\big)^{\frac{1}{2q}}
.

Since
X=\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z_{l}
. Then we have

\displaystyle X^{T}X=

\displaystyle(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}(Z_{l})^{T})(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z_{l})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}(Z_{l})^{T}Z_{l}+\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}Z_{l_{2}}

By triangle inequality for norms, we have

\displaystyle\lVert X^{T}X\rVert_{2q}\leq\lVert\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}(Z_{l})^{T}Z_{l}\rVert_{2q}+\lVert\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}Z_{l_{2}}\rVert_{2q}

Now,
\left\lVert\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}Z_{l_{2}}\right\rVert_{2q}=\E\left[\left\lVert\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}Z_{l_{2}}\right\rVert_{S_{2q}}^{2q}\right]^{\frac{1}{2q}}
, where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{S_{2q}}
denotes the normalised Schatten norm (see Section
3.1
).

By Lemma
A.1
,

\displaystyle\E\left[\left\lVert\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}Z_{l_{2}}\right\rVert_{S_{2q}}^{2q}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\left\lVert\sum\limits_{l_{1},l_{2}\in[L]}2(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}Z^{\prime}_{l_{2}}+2(Z^{\prime}_{l_{2}})^{T}Z_{l_{1}}\right\rVert_{S_{2q}}^{2q}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\left\lVert 2\left(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z_{l}\right)^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z^{\prime}_{l}\right)+2\left(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z^{\prime}_{l}\right)^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z_{l}\right)\right\rVert_{S_{2q}}^{2q}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[\left\lVert 2X_{1}^{T}X_{2}+2X_{2}^{T}X_{1}\right\rVert^{2q}_{S_{2q}}\right]

Taking the
2q
-th root gives,
\lVert\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}Z_{l_{2}}\rVert_{2q}\leq 2\lVert X_{1}^{T}X_{2}+X_{2}^{T}X_{1}\rVert_{2q}
.

∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
X=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}Z_{l}
where
Z_{1},...,Z_{L}
are independent
m\times d
random matrices.
Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent copies of
X
. Let
V_{1},V_{2}
be a
d\times m
(deterministic) matrices, and
V_{3}
be a
d\times d
(deterministic) matrix. Let
q\geq 1
be an integer. Then we have

\displaystyle\lVert V_{1}^{T}X^{T}V_{3}XV_{2}\rVert_{2q}\leq\lVert\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}V_{1}^{T}Z_{l}^{T}V_{3}Z_{l}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}+4\lVert V_{1}^{T}X_{1}^{T}V_{3}X_{2}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}

where
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent copies of
X
.

Proof.

Since
X=\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z_{l}
. Then we have

\displaystyle V_{1}^{T}X^{T}V_{3}XV_{2}=

\displaystyle V_{1}^{T}(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}(Z_{l})^{T})V_{3}(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z_{l})V_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}V_{1}^{T}(Z_{l})^{T}V_{3})(\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}Z_{l}V_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}V_{1}^{T}(Z_{l})^{T}V_{3}Z_{l}V_{2}+\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}V_{1}^{T}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}V_{3}Z_{l_{2}}V_{2}

By triangle inequality for norms, we have

\displaystyle\lVert V_{1}^{T}X^{T}V_{3}XV_{2}\rVert_{2q}\leq\lVert\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}V_{1}^{T}(Z_{l})^{T}V_{3}Z_{l}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}+\lVert\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}V_{1}^{T}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}V_{3}Z_{l_{2}}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}

Applying Lemma
A.1
as in the proof of Lemma
A.2
, we see that

\displaystyle\lVert\sum\limits_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}}V_{1}^{T}(Z_{l_{1}})^{T}V_{3}Z_{l_{2}}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert V_{1}^{T}X_{1}^{T}V_{3}X_{2}V_{2}+V_{1}^{T}X_{2}^{T}V_{3}X_{1}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert V_{1}^{T}X_{1}^{T}V_{3}X_{2}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}+2\lVert V_{1}^{T}X_{2}^{T}V_{3}X_{1}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}

\displaystyle\leq 4\lVert V_{1}^{T}X_{1}^{T}V_{3}X_{2}V_{2}\rVert_{2q}

∎

A.3.
Decoupling for OSNAP

In the case where
S
has the fully independent unscaled OSNAP distribution as described in Definition
3.10
, we have,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}\xi_{l,\gamma}e_{\mu_{(l,\gamma)}}e_{l}^{T}

\displaystyle=:\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}Z_{l,\gamma}

where
\{\xi_{l,\gamma}\}_{l\in[n],\gamma\in[s]}
is a collection of independent random variables with
\Pb(\xi_{l,\gamma}=1)=\Pb(\xi_{l,\gamma}=-1)=1/2
,
\{\mu_{l,\gamma}\}_{l\in[n],\gamma\in[s]}
is a collection of independent random variables such that each
\mu_{l,\gamma}
is uniformly distributed in
[(m/s)(\gamma-1)+1:(m/s)\gamma]
and
e_{\mu_{(l,\gamma)}}
and
e_{l}
represent basis vectors in
\R^{m}
and
\R^{n}
respectively.

Recalling that our goal is to look at the moments of
(SU)^{T}(SU)-pmI_{d}
, we observe that,

\displaystyle U^{T}S^{T}SU-pm\cdot I_{d}

\displaystyle=U^{T}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}Z_{l,\gamma}\right)^{T}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}Z_{l,\gamma}\right)U-pm\cdot I_{d}

Observe that for
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in[pm]
with
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}
,
e_{\mu_{(l,\gamma)}}
and
e_{\mu_{(l,\gamma^{\prime})}}
have disjoint supports, which means
e_{\mu_{(l,\gamma)}}^{T}e_{\mu_{(l,\gamma^{\prime})}}=0
. Thus, we also have
Z_{l,\gamma}^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}}=0
for any
l,l^{\prime}\in[n]
. So we get,

\displaystyle U^{T}S^{T}SU-pm\cdot I_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}U-pm\cdot I_{d}

Separating the cases where
l=l^{\prime}
and
l\neq l^{\prime}
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle U^{T}S^{T}SU-pm\cdot I_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}U-pm\cdot I_{d}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,l^{\prime}=1\\
l\neq l^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}U

Notice that
Z_{l,\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}=e_{l}e_{l}^{T}
, so,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}U-pm\cdot I_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}e_{l}e_{l}^{T}U-pm\cdot I_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}e_{l}e_{l}^{T}\right)U-pm\cdot I_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}I_{d}-pm\cdot I_{d}=0

where we used the fact that
\sum_{l=1}^{n}e_{l}e_{l}^{T}=I_{n}
and
U^{T}I_{n}U=U^{T}U=I_{d}
.

To analyze the off-diagonal term, we use the decoupling results developed previously,

Lemma A.4

(Decoupling)
.

When
S
has the fully independent unscaled OSNAP distribution, we have

\displaystyle\E[\tr(U^{T}S^{T}SU-pm\cdot I_{d})^{2q}]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\tr\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,l^{\prime}=1\\
l\neq l^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}U\right)^{2q}\right]

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\E[\tr(U^{T}S^{T}SU-pm\cdot I_{d})^{2q}]

\displaystyle\leq\E_{S,S^{\prime}}\left[\tr\left(2\left((S^{\prime}U)^{T}SU+(SU)^{T}S^{\prime}U\right)\right)^{2q}\right]

where
S^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
S
.

Proof.

Consider the independent tuples

\displaystyle\{(Z_{1,1},Z_{1,2},\ldots,Z_{1,pm}),\ldots,(Z_{n,1},Z_{n,2},\ldots,Z_{n,pm})\}=\{\widetilde{Z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{Z}_{n}\}

Then,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,l^{\prime}=1\\
l\neq l^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}U=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,l^{\prime}=1\\
l\neq l^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{n}h(\widetilde{Z}_{l^{\prime}},\widetilde{Z}_{l})

for a multilinear function
h
. Using Lemma
A.1
,

\E\left[\tr\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,l^{\prime}=1\\
l\neq l^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}U\right)^{2q}\right]\leq\E\left[\tr\left(\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{n}2h(\widetilde{Z}_{l^{\prime}},\widetilde{Z}_{l})+2h(\widetilde{Z}_{l},\widetilde{Z}_{l^{\prime}})\right)^{2q}\right]

Observing that,

\displaystyle\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{n}h(\widetilde{Z}_{l^{\prime}},\widetilde{Z}_{l})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma}^{T}Z_{l,\gamma}U

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}=1}^{pm}U^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}}^{T}\right)\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{pm}Z_{l,\gamma}U\right)

\displaystyle=(S^{\prime}U)^{T}(SU)

since
Z_{l,\gamma}^{T}Z_{l^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}}=0
whenever
\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}
concludes the proof.
∎
