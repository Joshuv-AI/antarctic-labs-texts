CK1-induced TERT phosphorylation mediates mitochondrial translocation to inhibit cardiomyocyte senescence and alleviate myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury
Authors: Zheng Wan, Yi Lu, Hui Wang, Yanchun Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCellular senescence is an outcome of mitochondrial dysfunction occurring during myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MIRI). Mitochondrial telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) is responsible for cardiac protection and can improve MIRI. In this study, the effects of TERT on cardiomyocyte senescence after MIRI were evaluated.MethodsInfarct area was examined by EVANS BLUE and TTC combined staining in MIRI mice. HE staining evaluated myocardial injury. Ultrasound electrocardiogram was used to detect cardiac function in mice. mRNA expression of TERT and CK1 was assessed utilizing RT-qPCR. Protein levels of SA-β-gal, TERT, p-TERT and CK1 were measured by Western blot. Mitochondrial membrane potential was determined by JC-1. Immunofluorescence staining detected the expression and localization of p-TERT. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) determined interaction between CK1 and TERT. Changes in senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) were detected by ELISA assay. Mouse primary cardiomyocytes were exposed to oxygen and glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) condition. Cell viability was assessed by CCK-8.ResultsIn MIRI mice, cardiomyocytes showed increased levels of mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular senescence. After MIRI, mitochondrial aggregation of p-TERT diminished in cardiomyocytes, whereas nuclear aggregation increased. TERT depletion exacerbated myocardial damage in MIRI mice. TERT silencing advanced MIRI progression by enhancing mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular senescence in cardiomyocytes. CK1 phosphorylated TERT at serine 227 and controlled mitochondrial relocation of TERT. CK1 alleviated MIRI and reduced cellular senescence by phosphorylating TERT in mice.ConclusionCK1 regulates TERT phosphorylation and mitochondrial translocation to suppress mitochondrial dysfunction and cell senescence after MIRI, which provides new therapeutic targets for MIRI treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10565-026-10172-5.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a heart disease caused by myocardial ischemia. Current standard clinical care often involves reperfusion therapy to restore blood flow to the infarcted area; however, this intervention can paradoxically lead to myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MIRI) (Liu et al. 2023). MIRI refers to the phenomenon in which, despite the restoration of blood flow to ischemic myocardium after reperfusion, structural damage is paradoxically exacerbated. This leads to increased cell death, expansion of the infarct size, and further impairment of cardiac function (Lønborg 2015). MIRI seriously restricts the success rate of treatment for patients with AMI, and currently, there are no highly effective measures to prevent MIRI (Sánchez-Hernández et al. 2020). Therefore, the research on the pathogenesis of MIRI and preventive drugs has become a research hotspot in recent years. Cell senescence is a permanent state of cell cycle arrest, which can lead to decreased tissue regeneration potential and function, inflammation and tumorigenesis (Shmulevich and Krizhanovsky 2021). Senescent cells produce senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which affects the tissue microenvironment and surrounding cells by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors (Birch and Gil 2020). The accumulation of cardiovascular-related senescent cells is an important risk factor for cardiovascular diseases such as vascular aging, atherosclerotic plaque formation, and myocardial infarction (Suda et al. 2023). Studies have found that eliminating age-related aging before myocardial infarction can improve the prognosis of elderly mice (Dookun et al. 2020). Furthermore, inhibiting hypoxia-induced cell senescence in human cardiomyocytes can mitigate myocardial ischemia and reperfusion injury in patients undergoing cardiac surgery (Lewinska et al. 2020). Therefore, exploring myocardial cell senescence in MIRI and early intervention to reduce cell senescence are of great significance for the prevention and treatment of MIRI.

Cell senescence is an irreversible process of cell stagnation, determined by a variety of complex mechanisms, including telomere wear, mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic disorders, loss of protein homeostasis, and epigenetic changes (Gao et al. 2022). Telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) is a component of the telomerase complex that extends the telomeric repeat sequences at chromosome ends, thereby safeguarding against telomere attrition (Dratwa et al. 2020). TERT is mainly distributed in the nucleus of cells with high replication capacity, maintaining telomere length and preventing aging (Liu et al. 2024). Notably, TERT is also present in mitochondria. Research has confirmed that mitochondrial TERT plays a role in cardiac protection and can ameliorate MIRIMIRI (Ale-Agha et al. 2021). In addition, inhibiting the mitochondrial translocation of TERT and reducing its mitochondrial expression can promote cell mitochondrial damage and cell senescence (Cui et al. 2023). Those studies indicated that TERT exerts a protection role after MIRI.

Emerging evidence shows that the phosphorylation of TERT at serine 227 is associated with both TERT phosphorylation and TERT nuclear translocation (Li et al. 2024; Chung et al. 2012). Several non-canonical functions of TERT appear to be closely linked to its activity within mitochondria (Burkatovskii et al. 2024). For example, TERT overexpression has been documented to elevate TERT protein levels within mitochondria and enhance cellular viability following oxidative stress (Indran et al. 2011). However, it has not been reported whether serine 227 phosphorylation is also associated with mitochondrial translocation of TERT. We previously conducted an online prediction of the TERT protein sequence using the Group-based Prediction System (GPS) 6.0 and found that the serine 227 phosphorylation prediction score was the highest, suggesting a potential relationship with casein kinase 1 (CK1). CK1 is a kind of serine/threonine protein kinase with unique physical and chemical properties, which is involved in the regulation of gene expression, vesicle transport, DNA damage repair, and cell cycle regulation (Schittek and Sinnberg 2014). The phosphorylation regulation of downstream target proteins by CK1 is beneficial to the recovery of MIRI and reduces infarct size (Hirschhäuser et al. 2021). Moreover, the phosphorylation regulation of CK1 can be involved in the translocation of mitochondrial proteins (Gerbeth et al. 2013; Bordin et al. 2002). Therefore, we speculate that the protective effect of CK1 on MIRI may be related to the mitochondrial translocation of TERT.

Based on these findings, we hypothesize that during MIRI, CK1 enhances the phosphorylation modification of the cytoplasmic TERT serine 227 site, thereby promoting the mitochondrial translocation of TERT. Elevated TERT expression in mitochondria can inhibit mitochondrial dysfunction and cell senescence in cardiomyocytes, ultimately improving MIRI. Our study may offer novel insights and potential targets for MIRI treatment.

Materials and methods

Animal model construction

Male C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old, weighing 23–28 g) sourced from Beijing HFK Bioscience (Beijing, China) underwent a one-week acclimation period prior to initiation of experiment. For each independent experimental batch, separate and parallel Sham and IRI groups were established. Mice were divided into six groups (n = 5/group): Sham group, ischemia/reperfusion injury (IRI) group, IRI + oe-NC + sh-NC group, IRI + overexpression plasmid for CK1 (oe-CK1) + sh-NC group, IRI + short hairpin RNA for TERT (sh-TERT), IRI + oe-CK1 + sh-TERT group. Adenovirus expressing sh-TERT, oe-CK1 and negative controls (oe-NC, sh-NC) were designed and synthesized by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Specifically, the sh-TERT adenovirus was used to knock down TERT expression, allowing us to investigate the functional role of TERT in the context of IRI. Conversely, the oe-CK1 adenovirus was employed to overexpress CK1, enabling assessment of its specific effects on the observed processes. The corresponding negative controls (oe-NC, sh-NC) are non-targeting constructs, which are crucial for distinguishing the specific effects of TERT knockdown or CK1 overexpression from any potential non-specific responses triggered by the viral vector or the transduction procedure itself. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane throughout surgical procedure. An adenovirus solution was prepared at a concentration of 2.5 × 1011 PFU/ml. Following a left thoracotomy, the heart was carefully removed from the cardiac chamber to enable the adenovirus injections. Using a 50 μl needle (Hamilton Co. Reno, NV, USA), 30 μl of adenovirus was administered at three sites within the left ventricular (LV) free wall. One week after intramyocardial adenovirus administration, mice underwent either IRI surgery or sham surgery in accordance with established protocols (He et al. 2023). The left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) was occluded for 30 min and then reperfused for 24 h. To elaborate, mice were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (120 mg/kg) and xylazine (4 mg/kg), intubated, and ventilated with 100% oxygen using a MiniVent mouse ventilator (Model 845, Harvard Apparatus). The surgical platform maintained body temperature at a suitable level. Following a left thoracotomy to expose the heart, LAD artery was ligated 1 mm below the tip of the left atrial appendage using a 7–0 silk suture (US Surgical Corp., Norwalk, Connecticut) placed over a segment of PE-10 tubing for 30 min. Ischemia was verified by ST elevation on the surface electrocardiogram and visible blanching. Reperfusion was initiated by removing the ligature, and the chest was subsequently closed layer by layer. The sham-operated group underwent same procedure, with exclusion of the ligation step. This research was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee, adhering to the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by National Institutes of Health (National Institutes of Health, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Detection of cardiac function

Cardiac function was evaluated using the Vevo 3100 high-resolution imaging system (MS-550D; VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) featuring a 30 MHz sensor for transthoracic echocardiography. Computer algorithms were utilized to calculate left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), fractional shortening (LVFS), and end-systolic diameter (LVESD).

EVANS BLUE and tetrazolium chloride (TTC) combined staining

Myocardial infarction induced by I/R was evaluated utilizing TTC-Evans blue staining. Following 24 h of reperfusion, heart was promptly removed. After irrigating the heart with saline, 0.2 mL of 1% Evans blue dye was introduced via aortic root. Hearts were then transversely sectioned into 1-mm-thick slices, encompassing 5 sections per heart starting from the apex. Tissue sections were placed in a 1% TTC solution (prepared in PBS, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. The area at risk was determined by the lack of blue dye staining. Subsequently, the total LV area, AAR, and TTC-negative staining area (representing the infarcted myocardium) were quantified using Image J software (NIH).

Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Following fixation in 10% formalin at 4 °C overnight, cardiac tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 4-μM slices, deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated using different ethanol concentrations, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min and eosin for 2 min. Subsequently, stained tissue sections were observed under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blot

Tissues and cells were lysed utilizing RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to extract proteins. Protein concentration was assessed with BCA kit (Beyotime). Subsequently, 20 μg of protein was subjected to electrophoresis on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were then incubated at 4 °C overnight after treatment with 5% BSA, using specified primary antibodies: SA-β-gal (ab616, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), TERT (PA5-116,024, 1:1000, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), phosphorylated-TERT serine 824 (PA5-99,474, 1:1000, Invitrogen), phosphorylated-TERT serine 227 (PA5-38,817, 1:1000, Invitrogen), CK1 (PA5-17,536, 1:1000, Invitrogen), p16 (PA5-20,379, 1:1000, Invitrogen), p21 (MA5-42,680, 1:1000, Invitrogen), Lamin B (PA5-19,468, 1:1000, Invitrogen), COX IV (PA5-29,992, 1:1000, Invitrogen). Following a 1-h incubation at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (#7074, 1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, CST, MA, USA), membranes were visualized utilizing an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Beyotime). Image J software (NIH) was utilized to assess signal density, with β-actin serving as internal reference.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cells and tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Total RNA was utilized to produce complementary DNA (cDNA) employing HiScriptTM First-strand CDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China). Prism 7000 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) in combination with Super Script III Platinum SYBR Green One-Step qRT-PCR Kit (Invitrogen) was employed to ascertain relative RNA levels through RT-PCR, employing GAPDH as an internal reference. Primer sequences were: TERT F: 5′-GGATTGCCACTGGCTCCG-3′, R: 5′-TGCCTGACCTCCTCTTGTGAC-3; CK1 F: 5′-GACAACAGGACAAGGCAAC-3′, R: 5′-TTGTTGCAGCCTTTAGTCC-3. mRNA levels were calculated utilizing 2−∆∆Ct method.

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) by flow cytometry

The MMP of cardiomyocytes was measured using the fluorescent cationic dye JC-1. In healthy mitochondria with high MMP, JC-1 forms aggregates that emit red fluorescence (around 590 nm). In depolarized mitochondria with low MMP, JC-1 remains in its monomeric form, emitting green fluorescence (around 529 nm). The ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity is therefore a quantitative indicator of MMP. Briefly, after the indicated treatments, cardiomyocytes were washed once with ice-cold PBS. The cells were then incubated with JC-1 staining solution (Beyotime) in the dark at 37 °C for 20 min. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with the provided JC-1 staining buffer (1X) to remove excess dye. Finally, the cells were resuspended in fresh culture medium. Fluorescence was immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA). For analysis, the FL1 (green fluorescence, monomers) and FL2 (red fluorescence, aggregates) channels were used, and the MMP was expressed as the ratio of the median fluorescence intensity of red aggregates to green monomers.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cellular senescence was evaluated using the Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining kit (Beyotime), following the provided guidelines. Initially, cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and then subjected to an overnight incubation with the staining solution at 37 °C. The resulting images were examined using a microscope (Olympus).

For the detection of the expression and localization of p-TERT, cells and frozen tissue sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Following a 30-min block with 5% BSA, samples were exposed overnight at 4 °C to primary antibodies directed against TERT serine 227 (PA5-38,817, 1:1000, Invitrogen). Next, samples underwent staining with Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen) for 1 h. Subsequently, immunofluorescence microscopic images were captured utilizing a confocal microscope system (Olympus).

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) detection

Intracellular ATP levels were assessed with an ATP content assay kit (Jiancheng Corporation, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) following provided guidelines. To summarize, heart tissues were lysed in ATP lysis buffer. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and its absorbance at 560 nm was measured for analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Protein lysates from cardiomyocytes were incubated with either anti-CK1 antibody (PA5-17,536, Invitrogen) or anti-IgG antibody (R5130, Sigma). Following overnight incubation at 4 °C, Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose (Santa Cruz, DBA, Milan, Italy) was introduced to the samples and incubated at 4 °C for 6 h. The immunoprecipitated protein complex was separated using SDS-PAGE and analyzed via western blot to assess TERT expression.

Detection of aging-related secretory phenotype by Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA assay was utilized to analyze the alterations in senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Following homogenization and centrifugation of mouse tissues at 12,000 × g for 15 min, cell cultures of primary mouse cardiomyocytes were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min in a 4 °C centrifuge, and the resulting supernatant was collected. Levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, TGF-β, CCL2, and CCL5 were assessed utilizing ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Abingdon, UK) in accordance with specific protocols. Absorbance readings at 450 nm were measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Subcellular fractionation and Western blot analysis

Nuclear and mitochondrial fractions were isolated sequentially. Briefly, cells were lysed in hypotonic buffer and homogenized. Nuclei were pelleted by low-speed centrifugation (800 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The resulting post-nuclear supernatant was subjected to high-speed centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) to pellet the mitochondrial fraction. Proteins were extracted from the isolated nuclear and mitochondrial pellets using RIPA buffer with sonication. For western blot analysis, 25% of the mitochondrial or nuclear protein lysate and 2.5% of the corresponding non-organellar lysate were loaded per lane. Blots were probed for TERT and phospho-TERT (Ser227). Fraction purity was confirmed using the nuclear marker Lamin B and the mitochondrial marker COX IV.

Isolation and culture of mouse primary cardiomyocytes

Male C57BL/6 mice (1-day-old; weighing 1.5–3 g) were used to extract primary cardiomyocytes based on established procedures (Wu et al. 2020). The mice were sourced from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). It is important to note that these neonatal mice were used exclusively for primary cell culture and were not the subjects of the MIRI model described elsewhere in the study. The MIRI model was established using adult mice. Briefly, neonatal mice were euthanized humanely, and their hearts were swiftly excised, minced, and enzymatically digested using collagenase type 2 (Sigma) to isolate cardiomyocytes. After a short digestion period, tissue fragments were moved to a sterile surface for brief settling. Cells obtained from the digested tissue supernatant underwent pre-plating for 1 h to remove fibroblasts and endothelial cells. The remaining supernatant, containing cardiomyocytes (10,000–12000 cells/cm2), was subsequently seeded in DMEM (Invitrogen) supplemented with 20% FBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. By the fourth day following isolation, primary neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes displayed spontaneous beating.

To administer oxygen and glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) treatment, cardiomyocytes were incubated in glucose-free DMEM medium (Invitrogen) and exposed to a hypoxic environment with 5% CO2 and 95% N2 at 37 °C for 3 h. Subsequently, the glucose-free medium was substituted with a normal medium, and cells were further cultured under normoxic conditions at 37 °C with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 for 24 h. In control group, cells were sustained without OGD treatment.

To transfect cells, the open reading frame of TERT or CK1 was inserted into pcDNA3.1 vector (oe-TERT, oe-CK1, GenePharma, Shanghai, China). These plasmids were then transfected into cardiomyocytes using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) for 48 h. Subsequent exposure to OGD/R was carried out after cell transfection.

Cell counting kit-8 assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Mouse primary cardiomyocytes were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. Following specified treatments, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was applied to each well and incubated for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Berkeley, CA, USA).

Cell site mutation

Based on the general protocol outlined in the QuickChange II site-directed mutagenesis kit manual (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA), various mutations of TERT serine 227 were generated. The TERT gene within the pET-11a plasmid served as the template for producing mutant plasmids via PCR. Subsequently, both wild-type and mutant TERT serine 227 plasmids were introduced into E. coli BL21(DE3) and cultivated in 3.6 L of auto-induction medium supplemented with 50 µg ml-1 ampicillin at 37 °C for 3 h before adjusting the incubation temperature to 25 °C. The culture was then sustained for 14 h prior to cell collection.

Isolation of cardiomyocytes and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for mitochondrial morphology assessment

Isolation of mouse cardiomyocytes

Hearts were rapidly excised from euthanized mice and immediately perfused retrogradely through the aorta with calcium-free perfusion buffer (in mM: 113 NaCl, 4.7 KCl, 0.6 KH₂PO₄, 0.6 Na2HPO4, 1.2 MgSO4, 12 NaHCO3, 10 KHCO3, 10 HEPES, 30 Taurine, 10 2,3-Butanedione monoxime, 5.5 Glucose; pH 7.4, 37 °C) for 4–5 min. Digestion was initiated by switching to perfusion buffer supplemented with 1 mg/mL Collagenase Type II and 50 µM CaCl₂. Upon tissue softening, the ventricles were dissected, minced, and gently triturated. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 100 µm nylon mesh. Cardiomyocytes were allowed to settle by gravity, washed, and gradually re-introduced to calcium (up to 1.2 mM) to restore calcium tolerance. Cell viability was assessed via trypan blue exclusion, and only preparations with > 70% rod-shaped, quiescent cells were used.

Sample preparation for TEM

Isolated viable cardiomyocytes were pelleted by gentle centrifugation. The pellet was immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 4–6 h. Following primary fixation, samples were washed three times in cacodylate buffer and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in the same buffer for 1.5 h at 4 °C. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, 100%), the cells were infiltrated and embedded in pure epoxy resin (Epon 812). Polymerization was conducted at 60 °C for 48 h.

Transmission electron microscopy

Ultra-thin Sects. (70–90 nm) were cut using a diamond knife on an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7) and collected on copper grids. Sections were contrasted with 2% uranyl acetate for 15 min followed by Reynolds’ lead citrate for 5 min. Grids were examined using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7800) operating at 80 kV. For each experimental group, at least 20–30 cardiomyocyte cross-sections from 3–5 independent biological replicates were systematically imaged. Mitochondrial morphology within the perinuclear and interfibrillar regions was qualitatively assessed, and representative micrographs were captured.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was employed for statistical analysis. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. When experimental groups was two, results were analyzed utilizing Student’s t-test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was conducted to determine differences more than two groups. p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Mitochondrial dysfunction and cell senescence increased in cardiomyocytes of MIRI mice

First, we established an MIRI in vivo model with C57BL/6 mice via LAD occlusion for 30 min as described in Methods. Compared to sham group, myocardial function was damaged after IRI with decreased LVEF and LVFS and increased LVESD, which were recovered after 7 days (Fig. 1A and Table 1). Additionally, EVANS BLUE and TTC combined staining showed that ischemia area in mouse heart tissue was increased compared to sham group (Fig. 1B). HE staining revealed that in the myocardial tissues of the Sham group, cells were neatly arranged and intact, showing no signs of pathological alterations like necrosis, hyperplasia, or inflammatory infiltration. Inflammatory cell infiltration was identified based on the presence of clusters of mononuclear cells, predominantly lymphocytes and macrophages, within the myocardial interstitium. Conversely, in the IRI group, myocardial fibers appeared fragmented and disordered, with visible gaps, significant bleeding, and prominent cell necrosis accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 1C). Meanwhile, the activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) was greatly increased in IRI mice, as well as the levels of senescence-related proteins (p16, p21) and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, TGF-β, CCL2, CCL5) (Fig. 1D and 1E). Furthermore, compared with sham group, the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was lower in cardiomyocytes of IRI group (Fig. 1F). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that mitochondria in cardiomyocytes of IRI groups exhibited progressive structural abnormalities over time, including swelling, cristae disruption, and loss of matrix density, compared to the intact and densely packed cristae observed in the sham group (Fig. 1G). Knockdown of TERT promoted the development of disease in MIRI mice

Next, we investigated the effect of TERT on MIRI in vivo. In the absence of ischemic injury, we transfected mouse heart tissue with three distinct sh-TERT constructs and assessed TERT knockdown efficiency. As shown in Fig. 3A, both RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that transduction of all three sh-TERT significantly reduced TERT expression at the mRNA and protein levels compared to the non-targeting control (sh-NC). Among them, sh-TERT-3 demonstrated the highest knockdown efficacy and was therefore selected for all subsequent functional experiments. Mice were subjected to different groups: sham group, IRI group, IRI + sh-NC group, IRI + sh-TERT group. Transfection of sh-TERT significantly reduced the expression of TERT in mouse heart tissue (Fig. 3B). Discussion

Myocardial senescence is intricately linked to the onset and progression of cardiovascular conditions (Chen et al. 2022). Research has demonstrated a significant accumulation of senescent cells in affected cardiac tissues, leading to structural and functional changes in the heart (Mesquita et al. 2021; Lyu et al. 2018). At present, the molecular pathways underlying cardiac senescence are largely unknown. Evidence suggests that cellular senescence in cardiovascular diseases is linked with mitochondrial dysfunction (Camacho-Encina et al. 2024). Moreover, telomerase enzyme TERT can enhance mitochondrial functionality (Ahmed et al. 2008; Haendeler et al. 2009; Singhapol et al. 2013). Traditionally, TERT was thought to be present solely in stem cells and rapidly dividing cells. Yet, recent years have revealed its presence in tissues with minimal proliferation, such as heart and vasculature (Zurek et al. 2016). Multiple studies have highlighted the protective role of TERT in the cardiovascular system. For example, cardioprotective effects of TERT are apparent through diminished cardiomyocyte apoptosis in transgenic mice with TERT overexpression (Oh et al. 2001). Additionally, enhanced TERT expression in heart via adeno-associated viruses leads to reduced infarct size and improved survival rates in mice (Bär et al. 2014). In this paper, our experimental results presented that mitochondrial dysfunction and myocardial senescence occurred in cardiomyocytes of MIRI mice. Moreover, we found that TERT depletion greatly promoted MIRI development via inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and cell senescence in cardiomyocytes. Therefore, the telomerase protein TERT appears to exert beneficial effects after MIRI by improving mitochondrial function and reducing cell senescence. Notably, while a therapeutic strategy targeting TERT post-MIRI is valuable, our focus on the CK1/TERT pathway as a preventive target is justified by its potential to preemptively enhance cardiac resilience, potentially mitigating the initial onset of senescence and dysfunction triggered by ischemic injury, which aligns with the paradigm of ischemic preconditioning.

Our study identifies a previously uncharacterized axis involving CK1-mediated phosphorylation of TERT at Ser227 as a critical regulatory mechanism governing mitochondrial TERT localization and cardioprotection during MIRI. While mitochondrial translocation of TERT has been linked to cellular stress responses, the upstream signaling governing this process in post-mitotic cardiomyocytes remained poorly defined. Notably, the canonical pathway described in proliferative cells involves Akt-mediated phosphorylation of TERT-Ser227, which promotes its mitochondrial import under growth or survival signals (Jeong et al. 2015). Our data reveal a divergent paradigm in the context of I/R injury, where TERT-Ser227 phosphorylation and mitochondrial accumulation are diminished, coinciding with reduced CK1 expression. This suggests that CK1, rather than Akt, serves as the predominant physiological kinase regulating this specific modification in cardiomyocytes under ischemic stress. Furthermore, although oxidative stress can induce mitochondrial TERT translocation in certain settings (Burkatovskii et al. 2024), our findings indicate that such signals are insufficient when CK1 activity is compromised. Thus, we propose that the CK1-pTERT(Ser227) axis operates as a parallel and indispensable regulatory pathway that may act as a mandatory checkpoint, permitting or facilitating mitochondrial trafficking of TERT specifically in response to metabolic and ischemic insults. This mechanism adds a crucial layer of understanding to the stress-responsive regulation of mitochondrial telomerase and highlights CK1 as a potential therapeutic target for modulating mitochondrial function and cellular senescence in ischemic heart disease.

Interestingly, multiple studies have indicated that TERT is not only present in the nucleus but also localizes within mitochondria, which are regions devoid of telomeres (Singhapol et al. 2013; Santos et al. 2006). Oxidative stress, known to culminate in cell apoptosis, has been linked to elevated TERT localization within mitochondria (Burkatovskii et al. 2024). In contrast to its nuclear counterpart, TERT localized within mitochondria has been shown to counteract the detrimental effects of TERT deficiency on infarct size and the recovery of left ventricular function following MIRI (Ale-Agha et al. 2021). In the current investigation, it was found that in MIRI mice, the levels of p-TERT decreased, with a concomitant reduction in mitochondrial accumulation of p-TERT and an increase in nuclear accumulation, suggesting a cardioprotective role for mitochondrial TERT. Previous studies showing TERT-mediated cardioprotection often involved chronic models where TERT may have had time to localize to mitochondria, where it is known to reduce oxidative stress and support function (Ale-Agha et al. 2021; Marinaccio et al. 2025). In contrast, our acute OGD/R model rapidly disrupts the regulation of TERT. Our data show that during acute injury, phosphorylation of TERT at Ser227 (by CK1) decreases, reducing its mitochondrial translocation and increasing nuclear accumulation. Simply overexpressing wild-type TERT (oe-TERT) does not restore this lost mitochondrial targeting, explaining its lack of protection in vitro. The critical role of localization is confirmed by our in vivo data: overexpressing CK1 restored TERT phosphorylation/mitochondrial localization and reduced injury, an effect reversed by TERT knockdown. Therefore, TERT’s protection depends on its CK1-mediated phosphorylation and mitochondrial delivery, which is impaired during acute IRI. This clarifies why increasing TERT protein alone was insufficient in our setting.

Casein kinase 1 (CK1) is a kind of serine/threonine protein kinase with unique physical and chemical properties. Its family members share highly conserved sequences and are widely distributed in the cytoplasm, nucleus, cell membrane, and cytoskeleton of eukaryotic organisms, including mammals and yeast (Knippschild et al. 2005). CK1 has a variety of substrate binding sites and is involved in the regulation of cell life processes. It plays an important role in Wnt and Hedgehog (Hh) signaling regulation, circadian rhythm regulation, membrane transport, cytoskeleton maintenance, cell division, DNA repair and nuclear localization (Jiang 2017). Notably, it has been proposed that the phosphorylation of connexin 43 by CK1 is essential for the cardioprotective effects of ischemic preconditioning (Hirschhäuser et al. 2021). In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that CK1 can phosphorylate TERT at serine 227 and facilitate its mitochondrial translocation, thereby mitigating MIRI and reducing cell senescence.

The observed recovery of myocardial function seven days post-MIRI is a recognized phenomenon in murine models and can be attributed to several intrinsic repair mechanisms. While acute IRI induces cardiomyocyte death, inflammation, and profound contractile dysfunction, the subsequent days activate compensatory pathways that facilitate tissue remodeling and functional improvement. A key contributor is the dynamic regulation of autophagy (Tang et al. 2025). Following IRI, autophagy is typically upregulated as an adaptive response, clearing damaged organelles and protein aggregates, thereby promoting cardiomyocyte survival and mitigating initial injury. This carefully balanced autophagic flux is crucial for the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria (mitophagy) and the reduction of oxidative stress, creating a cellular environment conducive to repair. Furthermore, this recovery phase involves a shift from pro-inflammatory to pro-resolving signaling, reduction of interstitial edema, and potential recruitment of resident progenitor cells contributing to tissue repair. The murine heart’s inherent regenerative and remodeling capacity, particularly within this short-term timeframe, thus allows for partial functional recovery, as evidenced by the improvement in LVEF and LVFS. However, it is important to note that this recovery may not indicate complete histological restoration, and the long-term outcomes often involve maladaptive remodeling.

While our findings highlight the CK1/TERT axis as a promising preventive target against MIRI by preemptively enhancing mitochondrial resilience and attenuating cellular senescence, the translational application of directly targeting CK1 requires careful consideration. As CK1 is a pleiotropic enzyme with a wide array of substrates and fundamental roles in diverse cellular processes, systemic modulation, particularly via overexpression, risks significant off-target effects and potential toxicity (Schittek and Sinnberg 2014; Knippschild et al. 2014). This presents a challenge for clinical feasibility, as there are currently no well-characterized, cardiac-specific CK1 activators reported; most pharmacological research on CK1 has centered on inhibitor development for conditions like cancer (Perez et al. 2011; Li et al. 2021). Therefore, future therapeutic strategies should aim for greater precision. Potential avenues include the development of small molecules or peptides that specifically enhance the CK1-TERT interaction or promote TERT phosphorylation at serine 227 without broadly activating other CK1-dependent pathways. Alternatively, downstream effectors of mitochondrial TERT could serve as more specific drug targets. Thus, while our study provides a crucial proof-of-concept for the cardioprotective role of the CK1/TERT pathway, its clinical translation hinges on subsequent research dedicated to achieving cardiac-specific targeting and defining a safe therapeutic window for intervention.

In conclusion, we found that CK1 phosphorylated TERT to induce mitochondrial translocation of TERT, thereby improving mitochondrial dysfunction and cell senescence after MIRI. It is important to note, however, that while our study demonstrates a correlation between CK1 overexpression and TERT phosphorylation and mitochondrial localization, direct experimental evidence to fully establish causality remains limited. Our paper might provide a reliable theoretical basis for the clinical treatment of MIRI.
