Gradient-based filtering under misspecification: Stability and error bounds
Simon Donker van Heel, Rutger-Jan Lange, Bram van Os, and Dick van Dijk

Abstract
Can stochastic gradient methods track a moving target? We study the problem of tracking multidimensional time-varying parameters under noisy observations and possible model misspecification. Gradient-based filters update the time-varying parameters using the gradient of a postulated objective function. A natural filtering objective is the logarithm of the postulated observation density, which gives rise to the widely used class of score-driven filters. As in the optimization literature, these filters come in two forms: explicit filters evaluate the gradient at the predicted parameter, whereas implicit filters evaluate it at the updated parameter. For both filter types, we derive novel sufficient conditions for exponential stability of the filtered parameter path, showing that stability can be guaranteed independently of the data-generating process. Under mild additional moment conditions on the data-generating process, we also obtain finite-sample and asymptotic mean squared error bounds relative to the pseudo-true parameter path. For implicit filters, these guarantees hold under weak parameter restrictions. For explicit filters, they additionally require Lipschitz continuity of the score and a sufficiently small learning rate. Simulation studies support our theoretical findings and show that implicit gradient filters outperform explicit ones in both accuracy and stability.

1
Introduction

In a wide range of disciplines, from economics and finance to engineering and climate science, variables exhibit characteristics that change over time. Such fluctuations can be represented by unobserved states or parameters and tracked by filtering techniques that alternate between prediction and update steps. These steps yield state or parameter estimates from lagged and contemporaneous information, respectively, as in
Kalman
’s (
1960
) classic approach and its successors in the state-space literature (e.g.,
Durbin and Koopman, 2012
).

In this paper, we study gradient-based filters for tracking vectors of unobserved parameters. These filters update the parameter estimates using the gradient of a stochastic objective function. Using the postulated observation log density as the objective yields the widely used class of score-driven (SD) filters
(
Artemova et al., 2022
;
Harvey, 2022
)
. As in the optimization literature, they come in two variants: explicit methods evaluate the gradient at the predicted parameter, as in classical stochastic approximation (e.g.,
Robbins and Monro, 1951
), whereas implicit methods evaluate the gradient at the updated parameter, as in implicit gradient methods (e.g.,
Rockafellar, 1976
) and recent work on proximal methods
(
Toulis and Airoldi, 2017
;
Toulis et al., 2021
)
.

Our theoretical contribution is twofold. First, we establish new sufficient conditions for the exponential stability of (multivariate) implicit and explicit SD filters (Theorem
1
). Exponential stability means that any two filtered parameter paths, started from different initial values but driven by the same data, converge toward each other exponentially fast. Because our conditions depend only on the postulated density, they are verifiable in practice, readily interpretable, and applicable under arbitrary misspecification, holding uniformly over all data-generating processes (DGPs). Second, for both filter types, we combine stability with mild conditions on the DGP to obtain finite-sample and asymptotic upper bounds on the mean squared errors (MSEs) of the filtered and predicted paths relative to the pseudo-true parameter path (Theorem
2
).

Our approach differs in several respects from the literature on SD models, also known as dynamic conditional score
(
Harvey, 2013
, DCS;)
and generalized autoregressive score
(
Creal et al., 2013
, GAS;)
models. First, we allow the researcher’s SD filter to be misspecified, possibly severely so, rather than assuming that the true time-varying parameter follows SD dynamics. Second, while recent work allowing for misspecification focuses on guarantees at a
single
time step (e.g.,
Creal et al., 2024
;
Gorgi et al., 2024
), to our knowledge ours is the first paper to give performance guarantees for the
entire
filtered or predicted parameter path. Third, while the literature has focused on explicit SD filters, we also consider the implicit SD filter recently introduced by
Lange et al. (2026)
and show that it offers stronger stability and performance guarantees than the standard explicit version. For a more detailed comparison with the literature, see Section
1.1
.

Our first result (Theorem
1
) underpins large-sample theory for the maximum-likelihood estimator (MLE;
Straumann and Mikosch, 2006
) of the filter’s static (hyper-)parameters, including consistency and asymptotic normality. Although explicit SD filters are commonly estimated by maximum likelihood, existing stability results are often limited to the scalar case (e.g.,
Harvey and Lange, 2018
;
Blasques et al., 2022
;
D’Innocenzo, 2026
), while in the multivariate setting available analytical conditions tend to be overly restrictive (see
Pötscher and Prucha, 1997
, sec. 6.4
;
Artemova, 2025
, p. 5
). For implicit SD filters, the stability result of
Lange et al. (2026)
relies on the assumed concavity of the postulated log observation density. By contrast, Theorem
1
provides two new verifiable sufficient conditions for the stability of multivariate SD filters: one each for the implicit and explicit versions. These conditions are formulated without imposing concavity and guarantee stability of the filtered path for every data sequence, meaning that we can remain entirely agnostic about the DGP. Under correct specification, when both the filter and the DGP are score driven with the same static parameters, exponential stability implies asymptotic recovery of the true time-varying parameter. Under misspecification, exact recovery need not hold, which motivates our second contribution.

Our second result (Theorem
2
) provides formal guarantees for the tracking accuracy of SD filters under misspecification. The behavior of misspecified stochastic gradient methods is an important concern in statistics (e.g.,
Liang and Su, 2019
), machine learning (e.g.,
Cutler et al., 2023
), and time-series analysis (e.g.,
Brownlees and Llorens-Terrazas, 2024
;
Lange, 2024a
;
Beutner et al., 2026
), yet it remains relatively underexplored. For example,
Koopman et al. (2016)
study misspecified explicit SD filters by simulation, but do not provide formal guarantees. Under misspecification, exact recovery is no longer possible, so the natural target becomes the
pseudo
-true state. This only requires mild assumptions on the DGP, such as the existence of a pseudo-true state with finite-variance increments. For both implicit and explicit filters, Theorem
2
establishes finite-sample and asymptotic guarantees by bounding the MSE of the entire filtered and predicted parameter paths relative to the pseudo-true path. The asymptotic bounds can often be minimized analytically with respect to tuning parameters such as the learning rate; in a special case related to the Kalman filter, the resulting minimized bound is tight.

A central insight from both Theorems
1
and
2
is that explicit SD filters require substantially stronger assumptions than implicit SD filters. In particular, theoretical guarantees for misspecified explicit SD filters require the score to be Lipschitz continuous in the parameter of interest. Under twice differentiability, this requires the Hessian of the postulated log density to be bounded. Although this Lipschitz assumption is standard in the optimization literature (e.g.,
Nesterov, 2018
), it is often overlooked in the SD literature and rarely mentioned in the
{\sim}450
papers listed on
gasmodel.com
. Yet it is frequently violated in practice, even in relatively simple settings such as a Poisson model with time-varying log-intensity. Our theory identifies Lipschitz continuity as a sufficient condition for stability of misspecified explicit filters, while simulation results suggest that it may even be close to necessary in practice. When it fails and the true state is sufficiently volatile, explicit filters may become unstable or even diverge, whereas implicit filters remain accurate. This mirrors well-known results for stochastic gradient methods, where implicit procedures enjoy stronger stability under weaker assumptions
(
Toulis and Airoldi, 2017
;
Toulis et al., 2021
)
.

In three Monte Carlo experiments, we assess SD filters in terms of stability, theoretical MSE bounds, and empirical MSEs. First, we consider a linear setting with high-dimensional states, as in
Cutler et al. (2023)
, where the Lipschitz condition holds and performance guarantees are available for both the explicit and implicit versions. Compared with three recent alternatives, the implicit SD filter achieves the lowest theoretical and empirical MSEs across all time steps, state dimensions, and observation dimensions, and is the only filter whose performance improves as the Lipschitz constant increases. Notably, our MSE bounds are up to three orders of magnitude smaller than those in
Cutler et al. (2023)
. Second, we examine nine nonlinear settings studied by
Koopman et al. (2016)
. Finite MSE bounds are available for all DGPs under the implicit SD filter, whereas for the explicit SD filter such guarantees hold only in the two cases where the score is Lipschitz continuous. In the remaining seven DGPs, when the true state is sufficiently volatile, the explicit SD filter becomes unstable and sometimes diverges. This finding adds nuance to
Koopman et al.
’s (
2016
) conclusion that misspecified explicit SD filters accurately track unobserved states, which appears to hold only when the score is Lipschitz continuous or the state volatility is low. Third, we analyze the widely used time-varying Poisson count model, for which the Lipschitz condition fails. All examined variants of the explicit filter diverge, whereas the implicit filter remains stable.

1.1
Contribution relative to existing work

Our work relates to time-varying parameter models, observation-driven time-series models, and stochastic gradient methods for tracking moving targets. A common focus in this literature is the stability and accuracy of noisy updates, especially under misspecification. We discuss three closely related strands of work on observation-driven models: (i) stability and invertibility, (ii) pseudo-true targets and optimality, and (iii) recent extensions of the score-driven framework.

First, we connect to the literature on stability and invertibility of observation-driven models. The seminal contribution of
Straumann and Mikosch (2006)
studies invertibility as a crucial ingredient for likelihood-based inference. Many later contributions on score-driven filters, such as
Blasques et al. (2022)
,
Blasques et al. (2024)
, and
D’Innocenzo (2026)
, formulate invertibility conditions for
scalar
time-varying parameters through contraction conditions whose expectations are taken under the
true
stationary law of the data, which is generally unknown. By contrast, our main stability result is stated directly in terms of the
filter
itself: the conditions are verifiable from the filter’s parameters and the postulated density alone, apply in any number of dimensions, and remain meaningful under arbitrary misspecification. For explicit score-driven filters, our Lipschitz-type condition implies stability uniformly over any realized data sequence without requiring assumptions on the data-generating process. Standard affine filters such as GARCH arise as special cases, and existing multivariate models such as
D’Innocenzo et al. (2023)
also fit within our framework. For implicit score-driven filters, the stability guarantees are stronger still, as they hold uniformly over all realized data sequences without requiring a Lipschitz condition.

Second, we contribute to the recent literature on misspecification and optimality in score-driven models.
Gorgi et al. (2024)
show that the expected score-driven update at a given time step moves the parameter toward a pseudo-true target defined by conditional Kullback–Leibler divergence, while
Creal et al. (2024)
obtain improvement results for observation-driven updates based on moment conditions. These results concern a
single update
at a fixed point in time. By contrast, we analyze the
entire
filtered and predicted parameter paths and derive finite-sample and asymptotic error bounds relative to a time-varying pseudo-true path. In our discrete-time setting with fixed observation intervals, exact recovery of the pseudo-true path is generally impossible, so our error bounds do not vanish asymptotically, much as in the classic discrete-time Kalman filter, albeit here for a broader class of gradient-based filters. This contrasts with
Beutner et al. (2026)
, who obtain convergence towards the pseudo-true state under
infill asymptotics
, where the number of observations per unit of time diverges, making exact recovery possible. To our knowledge, the discrete-time pathwise error bounds for both explicit and implicit score-driven filters developed here are new.

Third, we relate to recent work modifying the basic score-driven setting.
Blasques et al. (2023)
propose quasi score-driven models, which leave the explicit filtering recursion unchanged but replace the log likelihood used to estimate static parameters with a quasi log likelihood. Thus, from a filtering perspective, quasi score-driven filters fall within our explicit class. By contrast,
Lange et al. (2026)
use the same log likelihood for filtering and estimation, but replace the explicit gradient with an implicit one. Relative to their work, we retain the log-likelihood objective but develop theory for both explicit and implicit filters in parallel. For the explicit class, our stability and pathwise error-bound results are new. For the implicit class, our pathwise error bounds are new, and we strengthen the stability result of
Lange et al. (2026, Thm. 1)
by allowing non-concave observation log densities and deriving sharper contraction rates.

2
Implicit and explicit score-driven filters

2.1
Problem setting

For
t=1,\ldots,T
, the
n\times 1
observation
\text{{{y}}}_{t}
is drawn from a true conditional observation density
p^{0}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{\psi}^{0},\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
, where
\bm{\vartheta}_{t}
is a time-varying parameter vector taking values in some parameter space
\mathbf{\Theta}^{0}
,
\bm{\psi}^{0}
is a vector of static shape parameters, and
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is the information set at time
{t-1}
. The dependence on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
allows for exogenous variables and lags of
\text{{{y}}}_{t}
. For brevity, we suppress the dependence on
\bm{\psi}^{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, and for discrete observations interpret
p^{0}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\vartheta}_{t})
as a probability rather than a density. The researcher-postulated density, typically misspecified, is denoted by
p(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t})
, where
\bm{\theta}_{t}\in\mathbf{\Theta}
is a parameter vector in a non-empty convex parameter space
\mathbf{\Theta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
(e.g., the positive quadrant). Additional dependence of
p(\cdot\mid\bm{\theta}_{t})
on a static shape parameter
\bm{\psi}
and exogenous variables is allowed but suppressed for brevity.

2.2
Filtering algorithm

Score-driven filters are gradient-based methods whose filtering objective is based on the postulated log density of the observations
(
Lange et al., 2026
)
. Like
Kalman
’s (
1960
) filter, SD filters can be viewed as alternating between update and prediction steps. The updated and predicted states represent the researcher’s estimates of the time-varying parameter based on the contemporaneous information set
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and the lagged information set
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, respectively. For
t=1,\ldots,T
, the implicit SD (ISD) and explicit SD (ESD) updates are given by

\displaystyle\text{ISD update: }\qquad\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}^{\textnormal{im}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{\textnormal{im}}\,+\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}^{\textnormal{im}}),

(1)

\displaystyle\text{ESD update: }\qquad\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}^{\textnormal{ex}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}}\,+\,\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{H}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable positive-definite learning-rate matrix (i.e.,
\mathbf{H}_{t}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
, meaning
\mathbf{H}_{t}
is symmetric and
\text{{{v}}}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{t}\text{{{v}}}>0
for all
\text{{{v}}}\neq\text{{{0}}}_{k}
),
\nabla:=\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}
is the gradient with respect to the second argument of
\ell(\text{{{y}}}\mid\bm{\theta})
, and
\ell(\text{{{y}}}\mid\bm{\theta}):=\log p(\text{{{y}}}\mid\bm{\theta})
. Thus,
\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}\mid\bm{\theta})
is the score. Although the learning-rate matrix may differ across methods, i.e., we may allow
\mathbf{H}_{t}^{j}
with
j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\}
, we suppress the superscript for simplicity. In (
1
), the score is evaluated at the updated state, which makes the method implicit. By contrast, (
2
) is directly computable because the score is evaluated at the predicted state. As we show in Section
2.3
, the implicit update (
1
) is the first-order condition for an optimization problem, whereas the explicit update (
2
) solves a linearized version of the same problem. Interestingly,
Kalman
’s (
1960
) update can be written in either form, albeit with different learning rates (see Appendix
B.1
).

Turning to the prediction step, both filters use the linear first-order specification

\text{prediction step: }\qquad\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{j}\;=\;(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{\Phi})\;\bm{\omega}\,+\,\mathbf{\Phi}\,\bm{\theta}_{t-1\mid t-1}^{j},\qquad j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\},

(3)

for
t=1,\ldots,T
. Here,
\bm{\omega}
is a
k\times 1
vector,
\mathbf{\Phi}
a
k\times k
matrix, and
\mathbf{I}_{k}
the
k\times k
identity. Although
\bm{\omega}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}
may differ across filters, i.e., we may allow
\bm{\omega}^{j}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}^{j}
with
j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\}
, we suppress superscripts when convenient.
The initialization at time zero is taken as given, and we assume throughout that the prediction step maps
\mathbf{\Theta}
into itself. If
\mathbf{\Theta}\neq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, this can often be ensured by restricting
\bm{\omega}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}
; for example, if
\mathbf{\Theta}
is the positive quadrant, it suffices to take
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbf{\Theta}
,
\mathbf{\Phi}
diagonal, and
\mathbf{O}_{k}\preceq\mathbf{\Phi}\preceq\mathbf{I}_{k}
. Although (
3
) could be generalized to allow nonlinear or higher-order dynamics, we do not pursue this for tractability.

2.3
Reformulation as optimization-based filters

We reformulate the ISD update (
1
) as a multivariate optimization problem:

\text{ISD update: }\qquad\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}^{\textnormal{im}}=\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbf{\Theta}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\left\{\ell\left(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t\mid t-1}\right\|_{\mathbf{P}_{t}}^{2}\right\},

(4)

where
\mathbf{P}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
is the penalty matrix and
\|\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}_{t}}=\text{{{v}}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t}\text{{{v}}}
is a squared weighted norm. The first-order condition for (
4
) is
\text{{{0}}}_{k}=\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}})-\mathbf{P}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}-\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{im}})
. Rearranging gives
\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}=\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{im}}+\mathbf{P}_{t}^{-1}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}})
, which recovers (
1
) when
\mathbf{P}_{t}
equals the inverse learning-rate matrix. Hence, we set
\mathbf{P}_{t}:=\mathbf{H}_{t}^{-1}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
throughout. Thus, (
1
) is shorthand for optimization (
4
): the ISD update maximizes the most recent log-likelihood contribution subject to a weighted quadratic penalty centered at the one-step-ahead prediction. In Section
3
, Assumption
1
will ensure a smooth unique interior maximum, so that (
1
) and (
4
) are equivalent.

In optimization (
4
), we may assume
\mathbf{\Theta}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
to rule out boundary solutions and linearly approximate
\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta})
around the prediction to obtain the ESD update (
2
):

\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}^{\textnormal{ex}}=\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\left\{\underbrace{\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}})+(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}})^{\top}\,\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}})}_{\textnormal{linear approximation of }\ell\left(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}\right)\text{ at }\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t\mid t-1}\right\|_{\mathbf{P}_{t}}^{2}\right\}.

(5)

The corresponding first-order condition,
\text{{{0}}}_{k}=\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}})-\mathbf{P}_{t}(\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t}-\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t-1})
, can be rearranged as
\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{ex}}=\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}}+\mathbf{P}_{t}^{-1}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}})
, thereby recovering (
2
) with
\mathbf{P}_{t}^{-1}=\mathbf{H}_{t}
.

The full optimization (
4
) has two advantages over its linearized counterpart (
5
). First, it guarantees

\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t})\geq\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1}),

so the implicit update cannot worsen the fit of the postulated log density. The explicit update (
2
) provides no such guarantee; see Appendix
C.1
. Second, the linearization in (
5
) makes it harder to impose constraints on the parameter space
\mathbf{\Theta}
. We therefore assume throughout that the ESD filter uses
\mathbf{\Theta}^{\textnormal{ex}}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
. For intrinsically bounded parameters, such as volatility or correlation, ESD filters often rely on link functions mapping
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to the admissible space. These links introduce additional nonlinearity and may complicate the filter’s theoretical analysis. By contrast, our theory for the ISD filter allows a general state space
\mathbf{\Theta}^{\textnormal{im}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, so link functions are not required, though they may still be used.

2.4
Static (hyper-)parameter estimation

The static (hyper-)parameters of both filters can be estimated by maximum likelihood (ML). Suppose we use a static learning-rate matrix (i.e.,
\mathbf{H}^{j}_{t}=\mathbf{H}^{j}=(\mathbf{P}^{j})^{-1}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
for all
t
and
j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\}
) and collect all static parameters into a column vector
\bm{\upsilon}^{j}:=(\text{vech}(\mathbf{H}^{j})^{\top},\bm{\omega}^{j^{\top}},\text{vec}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{j})^{\top},\bm{\psi}^{j^{\top}})^{\top}
with
j\in\{\text{im},\text{ex}\}
, where vech(
\cdot
) and vec(
\cdot
) denote the half-vectorization and vectorization matrix operations, respectively. Following
Creal et al. (2013)
, the static parameters are estimated as

\hat{\bm{\upsilon}}^{j}:=\underset{\bm{\upsilon}^{j}\in\mathbf{\Upsilon}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell\left(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t\mid t-1},\bm{\psi}^{j}\right),\qquad j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\}.

(6)

Here,
\mathbf{\Upsilon}
is the subset of the static-parameter space for which
\mathbf{H}^{j}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
and
\bm{\psi}^{j}
lies in its permissible domain. While the logarithmic density
\ell(\cdot\mid\cdot):=\log p(\cdot\mid\cdot)
in (
6
) typically matches that used in the filter (
1
) or (
2
), we can also allow the estimation criterion to differ from the logarithmic density used in the update.
Following
Blasques et al. (2023)
, we refer to this as a quasi score-driven specification. Since our theory depends on the density used in the filtering recursion rather than on the static-parameter estimation criterion, our stability results and error bounds for the explicit class cover quasi score-driven filters as well.

3
Stability of score-driven filters

We study the exponential stability (e.g.,
Guo and Ljung, 1995
) and the closely related concept of invertibility (e.g.,
Straumann and Mikosch, 2006
) of score-driven filters. These properties are important in their own right and also underpin maximum-likelihood estimation of static parameters (e.g.,
Blasques et al., 2022
). Exponential stability means that, given identical data, filtered paths originating from different starting points converge exponentially fast over time. We focus on a particularly strong form of exponential stability that holds uniformly over all initializations as well as data sequences, which is especially relevant under possible filter misspecification.

Definition 1

(Exponential stability)
.

Consider two starting points,
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
, and the corresponding paths,
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}\}
and
\{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\}
, generated by the same data
\{\text{{{y}}}_{t}\}
and the same filter (i.e., with identical static parameters). Here,
\bm{\theta}_{t}
may denote either the filtered state
\bm{\theta}_{t|t}
or the predicted state
\bm{\theta}_{t+1|t}
. For a given set of static parameters
\bm{\upsilon}\in\mathbf{\Upsilon}
, the filter is said to be
exponentially stable
if there exist a weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
and a contraction coefficient
\tau<1
such that, uniformly over all
\bm{\theta}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{0}\in\mathbf{\Theta}
and all data
\{\text{{{y}}}_{t}\}
,

\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}\;\leq\;\tau^{t}\,\|\bm{\theta}_{0}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2},\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

(7)

Exponential stability implies asymptotic forgetting of the initialization: for any given data sequence, the distance between any two paths converges to zero. Hence all paths are asymptotically equivalent, in the sense that they converge to the same data-driven path up to differences that vanish asymptotically. In many standard filters, moreover, the predicted and filtered paths are linked by one-step recursions, so that stability of either path typically implies that of the other. We introduce the concept of exponential stability to emphasize that this is a pathwise property of the
filter
itself, rather than a property of the DGP: (i) a filter may be exponentially stable for some values of the static parameters, but not for others; (ii) under misspecification, the asymptotic filtered path need not coincide with the true parameter path; and (iii) if the data are not stationary and ergodic, the asymptotic path need not be stationary and ergodic either.

Condition (
7
) is stronger than most conditions in the literature (e.g.,
Straumann and Mikosch, 2006
;
Blasques et al., 2018
;
D’Innocenzo, 2026
), as it requires a “sure” contraction between any two paths, uniformly over all initializations and data sequences. Some flexibility remains, as
\mathbf{W}
is not fixed a priori; i.e., any positive definite matrix yielding the contraction in (
7
) is admissible. Later, we will choose
\mathbf{W}
to match the natural geometry of the filter.

Remark
1
shows that our new stability concept in Definition
1
leads to classic stability conditions for affine filters. For such filters, standard Lyapunov theory (e.g.,
Stein, 1952
) guarantees that an appropriate weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
exists if and only if the usual spectral-radius condition holds.

Remark 1

(Exponential stability of affine filters)
.

Many familiar filters, such as GARCH, are formulated in terms of predictions. Consider therefore the affine prediction filter

\bm{\theta}_{t+1|t}=(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{\Phi})\bm{\omega}+\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\text{{{f}}}_{t},

(8)

which is affine in
\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}
provided that
\text{{{f}}}_{t}
is a “forcing term” that depends
only
on the data (e.g., realized moments). Then, for two predicted paths
\{\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}\}
and
\{\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}\}
driven by the same data sequence,

\bm{\theta}_{t+1|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1|t}=\mathbf{\Phi}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}).

By Definition
1
, the predicted path is exponentially stable if there exists a weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
such that
\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}<1
, where
\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|_{\mathbf{W}}:=\sup_{\text{{{v}}}\neq\text{{{0}}}_{k}}\|\mathbf{\Phi}\,\text{{{v}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}/\|\text{{{v}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}
is the matrix norm induced by the weighted vector norm. By classic Lyapunov theory (e.g.,
Stein, 1952
), this requirement is
equivalent
to the usual spectral-radius condition
\varrho(\mathbf{\Phi})<1
.
Thus, for well-known affine filters such as standard GARCH
(
Bollerslev, 1986
)
, Poisson count filters
(
Davis et al., 2003
)
, integer GARCH
(
Ferland et al., 2006
)
, and ACD filters
(
Engle and Russell, 1998
)
, Definition
1
recovers the familiar spectral-radius stability condition; see also
Brandt (1986)
and
Bougerol and Picard (1992)
. Score-driven filters, however, are generally not affine in this sense, because the forcing term
\text{{{f}}}_{t}
in (
8
) then relates to the
score
, which depends not only on the data but also on the state itself. Definition
1
therefore extends the familiar stability concept to a broader class of possibly non-affine filters.

To derive tractable sufficient conditions for the exponential stability of SD filters, we now impose regularity conditions on the postulated log density.
Assumption
1
(a) denotes the Hessian matrix of the postulated log density with respect to
\bm{\theta}
as
\bm{\mathcal{H}}(\text{{{y}}},{\bm{\theta}}):=\nabla^{2}\ell(\text{{{y}}}\mid\bm{\theta})
. We assume this Hessian to be well defined, that is, the log density is twice differentiable, although not necessarily continuously so. Parts (b)–(c) are needed to analyze the full optimization problem (
4
), but not its linearized version (
5
). The reason is that the linearized problem is already strictly concave as
\mathbf{P}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
, and always admits interior solutions as
\mathbf{\Theta}^{\text{ex}}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Assumption 1

(Regularity conditions)
.

Let the penalty matrix
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{H}^{-1}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
be static.

a.

The Hessian
\bm{\mathcal{H}}(\text{{{y}}},{\bm{\theta}})
is well defined and satisfies
\alpha\mathbf{I}_{k}\preceq-\bm{\mathcal{H}}(\text{{{y}}},{\bm{\theta}})\preceq\beta\mathbf{I}_{k}
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbf{\Theta},\text{{{y}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and some
\alpha\in(-\infty,\beta]
, while
\beta\in(0,\infty]
may be unbounded; and

b.

Optimization (
4
) is strictly concave, implying
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{im}})>\alpha^{-}:=\max\{0,-\alpha\}
; and

c.

Optimization (
4
) admits an interior solution
\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}\in\mathbf{\Theta}^{\textnormal{im}}
for all
t
.

Part (a) imposes a Hessian bound, as is standard in the analysis of stochastic gradient methods (e.g.,
Chen et al., 2020
, Ass. 3.1
). We use a one-sided version of this condition (allowing
\beta=\infty
), applied uniformly over all
\text{{{y}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
rather than in expectation, since the DGP is unspecified. Under concavity of
\ell(\text{{{y}}}|\bm{\theta})
, the negative Hessian is positive semi-definite, so
\alpha\geq 0
. If
\alpha>0
, then
\alpha
is the strong-concavity parameter. If the postulated log density is (locally) non-concave, then
\alpha<0
. Writing
\alpha=\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-}
, where
\alpha^{+}:=\max\{0,\alpha\}
and
\alpha^{-}:=\max\{0,-\alpha\}
, the quantity
\alpha^{-}
measures the maximal violation of concavity and is assumed to be bounded in part (a), i.e.,
\alpha^{-}<\infty
. If the gradient is Lipschitz continuous, the negative Hessian is bounded above by
\beta<\infty
, so the Lipschitz constant
L:=\max\{\alpha^{-},\beta\}
is finite.

Part (b) restricts the penalty matrix
\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{im}}
by requiring its smallest eigenvalue to exceed
\alpha^{-}
, which ensures that the objective in optimization (
4
) is strictly concave. Since
\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{im}}=(\mathbf{H}^{\textnormal{im}})^{-1}
, this is equivalent to
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H}^{\textnormal{im}})<1/\alpha^{-}
, which under concavity reduces to the trivial requirement
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H}^{\textnormal{im}})<\infty
. Part (c) is needed to analyze optimization (
4
) through its first-order condition (
1
).

Our first main result, Theorem
1
, involves the induced matrix norm

\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\mathbf{W}}=\|\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}^{-1/2}\|
for any positive-definite matrix
\mathbf{W}
and square real matrix
\mathbf{A}
of the same dimension (e.g.,
Jungers, 2009
, p. 39
). Here
\|\mathbf{A}\|=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})}
is the spectral norm of
\mathbf{A}
, and
\mathbf{W}^{1/2}
is the unique positive-definite square root of
\mathbf{W}
.

Theorem 1

(Exponential stability of misspecified SD filters)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then the filters
\{\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t|t}\}

are exponentially stable (see Definition

) with weight matrix
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{H}^{-1}
if the static-parameter vector
\bm{\upsilon}\in\mathbf{\Upsilon}
is such that
\tau^{j}<1
for
j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\}
, where

\displaystyle\tau^{\textnormal{im}}

\displaystyle:=\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}\;\left(1-\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})+\alpha^{+}}+\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})-\alpha^{-}}\right)^{2},

(9)

\displaystyle\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}

\displaystyle:=\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}\;\left(1-\min\left\{\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}-\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},\,2-\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}\right)^{2}.

Remark 2

(Interpretable conditions)
.

If the intuitive conditions (i)
\alpha\geq 0
and (ii)
\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|_{\mathbf{P}}\leq 1
hold, with at least one holding strictly, then
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}<1
and the ISD filter is exponentially stable. For the ESD filter, the same conclusion additionally requires
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})\leq 2/\beta
.

In Appendices
A.1
–
A.4
, we present several intermediate results leading to the proof of Theorem
1
. Intuitively, the conditions
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}<1
and
\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}<1
define subsets of the static-parameter space on which the ISD and ESD filters are exponentially stable, with
\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}<1
potentially more restrictive, as discussed below.
These stability results are uniform over admissible subsets of the static-parameter space, rather than tied to a single parameter value. Thus, much like the spectral-radius condition
\varrho(\mathbf{\Phi})<1
, the conditions
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}<1
and
\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}<1
identify nonempty (and typically sizeable) regions of the static-parameter space where the corresponding filter is exponentially stable. We exploit the freedom in Definition
1
to choose the weight matrix by taking
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{H}^{-1}
, which is consistent with the natural geometry of the filter and yields the new contraction rates (
9
)–(
10
).

In sum, Theorem
1
provides a new multivariate stability result for both explicit and implicit filters that is easily verifiable and agnostic about the DGP. Indeed, the conditions
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}<1
and
\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}<1
depend only on filter parameters, namely
\alpha,\beta,\mathbf{P}
, and
\mathbf{\Phi}
, and impose no restrictions on the DGP. Under these conditions, Theorem
1
guarantees that, for any data sequence, all filtered paths converge toward one another, so any limiting path is unique. Relative to
Lange et al. (2026, Thm. 1)
, the ESD stability result is new, while the ISD result is strengthened by allowing possibly non-concave log densities and providing the sharper contraction rate (
9
).

Generally, our filters are score driven and the DGP is unknown; we now highlight a consequence of Theorem
1
for the special case of correct specification.

Remark 3

(Correct specification)
.

If the contraction conditions
\tau^{\textnormal{im}},\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}<1
in Theorem
1
hold and the data sequence
\{\text{{{y}}}_{t}\}
is stationary and ergodic (SE), then the limiting filtered path is also SE, provided its dependence on past data remains measurable (for details, see
Krengel, 1985
, Prop. 4.3
;
Brandt, 1986
, Thm. 1
;
Bougerol, 1993
, Thm. 3.1
). Moreover, if both the filter and the DGP are score driven and share the same observation density and the same static parameters
\bm{\upsilon}\in\mathbf{\Upsilon}
, then
\|\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t|t-1}-\bm{\vartheta}_{t}\|\to 0
exponentially fast
(as
t\to\infty
)
uniformly over all data
\{\text{{{y}}}_{t}\}
and all initializations at time zero. Thus, the true path is perfectly recovered in the limit. This convergence is pathwise, rather than merely almost sure, because Definition
1
is formulated uniformly over all data sequences and initializations; this is stronger than the almost-sure convergence commonly reported in the literature (e.g.,
Blasques et al., 2018
, Rem. 3.1
).

We now discuss the contraction conditions
\tau^{\textnormal{im}},\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}<1
in more detail. As noted in Remark
2
, if the postulated log density
\ell(\text{{{y}}}|\bm{\theta})
is (strictly) concave in
\bm{\theta}
(i.e.,
\alpha\geq 0
), then simple restrictions on
\mathbf{\Phi}
and
\mathbf{P}
suffice to ensure exponential stability of the ISD filter. More generally,
\alpha<0
or
\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|_{\mathbf{P}}>1
may still be allowed provided
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}<1
. The case
\beta=\infty
is not problematic, since
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}
does not depend on
\beta
.
For the ESD filter, by contrast, large values of
\beta
can be problematic. The second argument of
\min\{\cdot,\cdot\}
in equation (
10
) is
2-\beta/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})
, which must be nonnegative for stability. Using
1/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})
, this requirement becomes
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})\leq 2/\beta
. Similar conditions are standard for explicit gradient methods; see, for example,
Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004)
(around Eq. 9.17),
Nesterov (2018)
(around Eqns. 1.2.18–22), and
Wu and Su (2023)
. Thus, for ESD filters, the surprise is not that a learning-rate restriction is needed, but rather that such a simple and interpretable condition guarantees stability even in our dynamic and stochastic setting.

Another notable implication of
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})\leq 2/\beta
is that the learning rate must shrink to zero as
\beta\to\infty
. In Section
5
, we find that misspecified ESD filters with
\beta=\infty
and a positive learning rate can be made to diverge by increasing the volatility of the true state process. This suggests that
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})\leq 2/\beta
is close to being necessary for exponential stability of the ESD filter in the sense of Definition
1
. Although weaker notions of stability may still be available for some ESD filters with
\beta=\infty
, establishing them seems to require some knowledge of the DGP, which we do not assume. Instead, we seek a strong form of exponential filter stability that holds uniformly over
any
DGP. Theorem
1
is robust in this sense: its conclusion cannot fail even if the data
\{\text{{{y}}}_{t}\}
are generated with the express purpose of making the filters diverge. The resulting conditions are intuitive, readily verifiable, and not overly stringent when the DGP is unknown.

4
(Non-)asymptotic MSE bounds for score-driven filters

This section provides formal theoretical guarantees for the tracking accuracy of score-driven (SD) filters, even when misspecified. To this end, we must reintroduce some consideration of the true process. To measure performance, we consider the weighted mean squared filtering error
\textnormal{MSE}^{\mathbf{W}}_{t\mid t}:=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}]
and the weighted mean squared prediction error
\textnormal{MSE}^{\mathbf{W}}_{t\mid t-1}:=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}]
, where
\mathbf{W}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
is a positive-definite weight matrix and
\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}
denotes the pseudo-true parameter defined below.
The introduction of a
weighted
MSE allows for some flexibility even if we are ultimately interested in the usual case
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
. For example, the usual MSE can be bounded as follows:
\textnormal{MSE}^{\mathbf{I}_{k}}_{t|t}\leq 1/(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}))\textnormal{MSE}^{\mathbf{P}}_{t|t}
.

Definition 2

(Pseudo-true parameter)
.

Consider a true distribution
p^{0}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\vartheta}_{t})

p(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{t})

p(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\cdot)=p^{0}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\cdot)
, then
\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}=\bm{\vartheta}_{t}
.

To track the pseudo-true parameter path
\{\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\}
, we require three moment conditions. Although these conditions are almost entirely nonrestrictive, they will, along with Assumption
1
, be sufficient to derive MSE bounds for misspecified SD filters.

Assumption 2

may be arbitrarily small. To obtain a finite MSE bound, we then require
a<1
to ensure
a\,c<1
. For the ISD filter, this can be achieved if
\alpha>0
, which essentially means that each observation must carry a minimum (non-zero) amount of information. Thus, ISD filters can successfully track unit-root
(pseudo-)
true states (i.e., achieving an asymptotically bounded MSE) if the postulated density is strongly log concave. For ESD filters, as before, we additionally require
\beta<\infty
and
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})<2/\beta
.

4.1
Special case: Known observation density and linear state dynamics

If the DGP is known, our error bounds can be improved in a manner analogous to the derivation of the classic Kalman filter. As in the classic setting, we assume linear state dynamics, but we deviate by allowing non-Gaussian state innovations and a general observation density.

Assumption 3

(Known state-space model with linear state dynamics)
.

For all
t=1,\ldots,T
, let the true density
p^{0}(\cdot|\cdot)
coincide with the postulated density
p(\cdot|\cdot)
, while the true state evolves as

\bm{{\vartheta}}_{t}=(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{0})\,\bm{\omega}_{0}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\bm{\vartheta}_{t-1}+\bm{\xi}_{t}

where
\bm{\xi}_{t}\sim\textnormal{i.i.d.}\,(\text{{{0}}}_{k},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\xi})

\sigma_{\xi}^{2}:=\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\xi})<\infty

\bm{\omega}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and autoregressive matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
are known.

When Assumption

\bm{\omega}=\bm{\omega}_{0}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}
.
In this case, the pseudo-true and true parameters coincide; that is,
\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}=\bm{\vartheta}_{t}
for

. However, as the DGP is a state-space model, our SD filters remain misspecified.

5
Simulation studies

We present three simulation studies comparing SD filters in terms of stability and performance. Specifically, we investigate a linear setting with high-dimensional states as in
Cutler et al. (2023)
(Section
5.1
), nine nonlinear DGPs with univariate states as in
Koopman et al. (2016)
(Section
5.2
), and the popular dynamic Poisson count model (Section
5.3
).

5.1
Least-squares recovery as in
Cutler et al. (2023)

Observation equation.
Following
Madden et al. (2021, pp. 453–4)
and
Cutler et al. (2023, pp. 41–2)
, we investigate a high-dimensional linear model. Conditional on the true latent state
\bm{\vartheta}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},

each observation
\text{{{y}}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is independently drawn from a Gaussian distribution
\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{A}\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\mathbf{\Sigma})
. The (static) matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is generated using
Haar-distributed orthogonal matrices to ensure that it has rank
k\leq n
, with
singular values equally spaced in the interval
[\sqrt{\alpha},\sqrt{\beta}\,]
, where
0<\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
. As we will see, this corresponds with the notation in Assumption
1
. The covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is set as
\sigma^{2}/(n\beta)\mathbf{I}_{n}
with
0<\sigma^{2}<\infty
, ensuring that
\sigma^{2}<\infty
matches the notation in Assumption
2
(b).

State transition.
The latent state
\bm{\vartheta}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
evolves according to a random walk
\bm{\vartheta}_{t+1}=\bm{\vartheta}_{t}+\bm{\xi}_{t}
,
where
\bm{\xi}_{t}
is i.i.d. and non-Gaussian, drawn uniformly from the surface of a
k
-dimensional sphere with radius
\sigma_{\xi}>0
, aligning with the notation in Assumption
3
. The initial state
\bm{\vartheta}_{0}
is drawn from the surface of a sphere with radius
10
.

Postulated density.
Following
Cutler et al. (2023)
, we are interested in least-squares recovery, meaning that all directions are equally important. Therefore, we postulate a Gaussian density with the identity as the covariance matrix:

\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta})=-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}-\text{{{y}}}_{t}\|^{2}-\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi).

(15)

The Hessian with respect to
\bm{\theta}
is constant at
\bm{\mathcal{H}}=-\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
. Due to the construction of
\mathbf{A}
, the eigenvalues of
-\bm{\mathcal{H}}
lie in
[\alpha,\beta]
with
0<\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
, consistent with the notation in Assumption
1
(a).
Although the logarithmic observation density (
15
) is technically misspecified, the pseudo-true parameter still equals the true parameter (i.e.,
\bm{\vartheta}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star},\forall t
). Moreover, taking the postulated covariance matrix to be a
scalar multiple
of the identity would lead to identical updates, differing only in the learning rate. Parameter
\sigma^{2}
matches the notation in Assumption
2
(b), as
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\vartheta}_{t})\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\mathbf{A}\bm{\vartheta}_{t})\|^{2}=\tr(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{A})\leq\beta\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\sigma^{2}<\infty
.

Filter specification.
Our filters are initialized at the origin and use the identity mapping for their prediction steps; that is,
\bm{\theta}^{j}_{0|0}=\text{{{0}}}_{k}
and
\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t+1|t}=\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t|t}
with
j\in\{\text{ex, im}\}
. Following
Cutler et al. (2023)
, we consider learning-rate matrices that are scalar multiples of the identity.
The ESD update with
\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\textnormal{ex}}=\eta^{\textnormal{ex}}\mathbf{I}_{k}
and
\eta^{\textnormal{ex}}>0
then equals

\text{ESD update:}\qquad\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{ex}}\;=\;\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}}+\eta^{\textnormal{ex}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}}),

where
\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t-1})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}})
is the (explicit) score. For the ISD update, the first-order condition
\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}=\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{im}}+\eta^{\textnormal{im}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}})
can be solved for
\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}
to yield

\displaystyle\text{ISD update:}\qquad\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\eta^{\textnormal{im}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{im}}+\eta^{\textnormal{im}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\text{{{y}}}_{t}\right),

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1}+\eta^{\textnormal{im}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\eta^{\textnormal{im}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{im}}),

which is a shrunken version of the ESD update with shrinkage coefficient
\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\eta^{\textnormal{im}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}
. This ensures that the ISD update remains bounded as the learning rate
\eta^{\textnormal{im}}
increases.

Error bounds.

For both filters in this setup,
Theorem
2
holds with
c^{\text{im}}=c^{\text{ex}}=1
and

\displaystyle a^{\textnormal{ex}}

\displaystyle=(1-\min\{\alpha\eta^{\textnormal{ex}},2-\beta\eta^{\textnormal{ex}}\})^{2}+(\chi\,\beta\,\eta^{\textnormal{ex}})^{2},

\displaystyle b^{\textnormal{ex}}

\displaystyle=(1+1/\chi^{2})\sigma^{2}\eta^{\textnormal{ex}},

\displaystyle d^{\textnormal{ex}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\xi}^{2}/\eta^{\textnormal{ex}}.

\displaystyle a^{\textnormal{im}}

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha\eta^{\textnormal{im}})^{-2},

\displaystyle b^{\textnormal{im}}

\displaystyle=a^{\textnormal{im}}\sigma^{2}\eta^{\textnormal{im}},

\displaystyle d^{\textnormal{im}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\xi}^{2}/\eta^{\textnormal{im}}.

These values can be substituted into equations (
13
)–(
14
) to obtain finite-sample and asymptotic error bounds in the
\mathbf{P}^{j}
-norm, respectively.
As we are interested in least squares, we multiply these bounds by
1/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}^{j})=\eta^{j}
to obtain (Euclidean) MSE bounds. We then minimize these MSE bounds by selecting the learning rates
\eta^{j}
for
j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\}
. For the ISD filter, we use the analytic minimizer
\eta^{\textnormal{im}}_{\star}=1/\rho^{\textnormal{im}}_{\star}
given in Appendix
B.3
. For the ESD filter, we numerically minimize the MSE bound with respect to both the learning rate
\eta^{\textnormal{ex}}
and the free parameter
\chi
.

Three alternative tracking algorithms.

5.3
Poisson count model with various link and scaling functions

DGP.
We consider count observations
y_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
generated by a dynamic Poisson distribution
p^{0}(y_{t}|\mu_{t})={\mu_{t}^{y_{t}}\exp(-\mu_{t})}/{y_{t}!}
with mean
\mu_{t}=\exp(\vartheta_{t})
, where
\mu_{t}>0
is strictly positive due to the exponential link. The latent process
\{\vartheta_{t}\}
follows linear dynamics
\vartheta_{t}=0.98\vartheta_{t-1}+\xi_{t}
with Gaussian increments
\xi_{t}\sim\text{i.i.d. }\mathrm{N}(0,\sigma_{\xi}^{2})
.
This model, along with slight variations, has been extensively studied in the literature, with notable applications including the modeling of U.K. van-driver deaths
(
Harvey and Fernandes, 1989
;
Durbin and Koopman, 1997
;
Durbin and Koopman, 2000
)
.

ESD filter.
Explicit SD filters for the Poisson count model
have been considered in
Davis et al. (2003, sec. 2.3)
,
Gorgi (2018, sec. 5.2)
and
Gorgi et al. (2024, sec. 4.3)
. In line with this literature, we assume that the Poisson distribution and the exponential link
\mu_{t}=\exp(\vartheta_{t})
are known to the researcher, such that they can be used to calculate the score,
y_{t}-\exp(\theta)
, and Fisher’s information quantity,
\exp(\theta)
. We follow the literature in considering several
scaling
options by multiplying the score by the inverse Fisher information to the power
\zeta\in\{0,1/2,1\}
. However, since the resulting scaled score
\exp(-\zeta\theta)\,[y_{t}-\exp(\theta)]
fails to be Lipschitz continuous in
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
for any scaling
\zeta\in\{0,1/2,1\}
, neither stability nor performance guarantees can be established for the ESD filter.

ISD filter.
For the ISD filter, we consider only a static learning rate. While the Poisson distribution is again assumed to be known, we explore scenarios where the relationship between
\vartheta_{t}
and
\mu_{t}
is unknown. Specifically, we consider two cases in which the researcher postulates either (i) an exponential link
\mu^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}=\exp(\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t})
, which is correct, or (ii) a quadratic link
\mu^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}={(\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t})}^{2}
, which is misspecified.

The parameter space
\Theta
is
\mathbb{R}
under the exponential link and

6
Conclusion

This paper established theoretical guarantees for the stability and filtering performance of multivariate explicit and implicit score-driven filters under possible misspecification. We derived practical sufficient conditions for exponential stability of the filter, uniformly over all data sequences, and combined these conditions with mild assumptions on the DGP to obtain finite-sample and asymptotic MSE bounds between filtered and pseudo-true paths. These bounds can be minimized analytically, facilitating the tuning of static
(hyper-)
parameters such as the learning rate. Monte Carlo studies validated the theory and highlighted the advantages of implicit filters. In a high-dimensional linear model, the proposed bounds sharpened existing results by up to three orders of magnitude. Consistent with the theory, misspecified explicit filters may diverge when finite MSE bounds are unavailable, whereas implicit filters remain stable and accurately track the pseudo-true path.

with real eigenvalues. Because we work exclusively with real-valued matrices, symmetry is sufficient to guarantee that all eigenvalues are real.

First, focusing on the maximal eigenvalue of
\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}
, we can bound it by using Lemma
1
in Appendix
A.2
as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=1+\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=1-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t})\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq 1-\min\left\{\frac{\lambda_{\min}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},\frac{\lambda_{\min}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t})}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}\quad\text{\footnotesize by Lemma~\ref{lem3}}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\min\left\{\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}\quad\text{\footnotesize as $\alpha\leq-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}$ by Assumption~\ref{ass1}(a)}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}+\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}.

(A.9)

The first and second lines use
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{A})=1+\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
and
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})=-\lambda_{\min}(-\mathbf{A})
, respectively, for an arbitrary matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
. The inequality in the third line holds because
1-x
is decreasing in
x
and we can lower bound
x=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t})\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})
by Lemma
1
in Appendix
A.2
with
\mathbf{A}=-\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{t}^{\textnormal{ex}}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{P}
. The inequality in the fourth line holds by Assumption
1
(a). The final equality follows by
\alpha=\alpha^{+}-\alpha^{-}
(where
\alpha^{+}:=\max\{0,\alpha\}
and
\alpha^{-}:=\max\{0,-\alpha\}
) and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})>0
.

Second, focusing on (the negative of) the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}
, we again use Lemma
1
as follows:

\displaystyle-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}(-\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t})\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=-1+\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t})\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq-1+\max\left\{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},\frac{\lambda_{\max}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t})}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}\quad\text{\footnotesize by Lemma~\ref{lem3}}

\displaystyle\leq-1+\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\quad\text{\footnotesize by Assumption~\ref{ass1}(a)}

\displaystyle=1-\left(2-\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right).

(A.10)

The first and second lines use
-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A})=\lambda_{\max}(-\mathbf{A})
and
\lambda_{\max}(-\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{A})=-1+\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
, respectively, for arbitrary matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
. The inequality in the third line uses Lemma
1
in Appendix
A.2
with
\mathbf{A}=-\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{t}^{\textnormal{ex}}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{P}
. The inequality in the fourth line holds by Assumption
1
(a), which says
\lambda_{\max}(-\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{t}^{\textnormal{ex}})\leq\beta
. The final line, which holds trivially, is used below.

Third, combining (
A.8
) with bounds (
A.9
) and (
A.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t\mid t}}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{\top}}\right\|_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}),-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})\right\}\text{\footnotesize by \eqref{eq: eigenvalue bounds}}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\min\left\{\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}-\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},2-\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}\text{\footnotesize by \eqref{new result1} and \eqref{new result2}}

(A.11)

This concludes the proof.
∎

A.4
Proof of Theorem

The proof of Theorem

Proof.

Let
f^{j}_{t}:\mathbf{\Theta}\rightarrow\mathbf{\Theta}
denote the update function at time
t
for
j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\}
. For example, for the ISD update we have
\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}=f^{\textnormal{im}}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1})=f^{\textnormal{im}}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}|\text{{{y}}}_{t},\mathbf{P})=\underset{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbf{\Theta}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\{\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta})-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t\mid t-1}\|_{\mathbf{P}}^{2}\}
. Because the proof structure is not contingent on whether the update is explicit or implicit, we suppress the filter-type superscript in the proof below and use
f_{t}
and
\bm{\theta}_{t|t}
.

Next, let
g_{t}^{\text{{{a}}}}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for some
\text{{{a}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
g_{t}^{\text{{{a}}}}(u):=\langle\text{{{a}}},f_{t}(u\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+(1-u)\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1})\rangle
,
u\in[0,1]
and
\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1},\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}\in\mathbf{\Theta}
are two predictions. By convexity of the parameter space
\mathbf{\Theta}
, we have
u\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+(1-u)\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}\in\mathbf{\Theta},\forall u\in[0,1]
, such that
g_{t}^{\text{{{a}}}}
is well defined.

For both the ISD and ESD update mappings, we have that the Jacobian of the update mapping
f_{t}
is well-defined if the Hessian of the log-likelihood exists, see again Lemma
2
. In addition, if
f_{t}(\bm{\theta})
is differentiable in
\bm{\theta}
everywhere, then
g_{t}^{\text{{{a}}}}(u)
is differentiable in
u
everywhere. In sum, Assumption
1
implies
g_{t}^{\text{{{a}}}}(u)
is differentiable and hence continuous everywhere. Therefore, by the mean-value theorem, we have
\forall\text{{{a}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
that
\exists\,u^{\star}\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\langle\text{{{a}}},\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\text{{{a}}},f_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1})-f_{t}(\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1})\rangle

\displaystyle=g_{t}^{\text{{{a}}}}(1)-g_{t}^{\text{{{a}}}}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}g^{\text{{{a}}}}_{t}(u)}{\mathrm{d}u}\Big\rvert_{u=u^{\star}}(1-0)

\displaystyle=\langle\text{{{a}}},\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1})\rangle,

(A.12)

where
\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}):=\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\Big\rvert_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}}
is the
k\times k
Jacobian of the update function
f_{t}
evaluated at the midpoint
\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}:=u^{\star}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+(1-u^{\star})\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}
.

Next, we use our result with
\text{{{a}}}=\mathbf{P}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t})/\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\|_{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. This yields

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\|_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{P}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t})/\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\|_{\mathbf{P}},\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{P}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t})/\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\|_{\mathbf{P}},\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\|^{-1}_{\mathbf{P}}\,\langle\mathbf{P}^{1/2}(\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}),\mathbf{P}^{1/2}\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\|^{-1}_{\mathbf{P}}\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\|_{\mathbf{P}}\|\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})(\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1})\|_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|_{\mathbf{P}}\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}\|_{\mathbf{P}},

(A.13)

where the second line uses the mean-value theorem (
A.12
) above, the fourth uses the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
\|\mathbf{P}^{1/2}\text{{{x}}}\|=\|\text{{{x}}}\|_{\mathbf{P}},\forall\text{{{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and the final line uses the submultiplicative property (
A.1
) of the
\mathbf{P}
-weighted matrix norm.

Using the prediction step (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t}\|_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|_{\mathbf{P}}\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t|t-1}\|_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|_{\mathbf{P}}\|\mathbf{\Phi}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1|t-1})\|_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|_{\mathbf{P}}\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|_{\mathbf{P}}\|\bm{\theta}_{t-1|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1|t-1}\|_{\mathbf{P}},

(A.14)

using again the submultiplicative property (
A.1
) of the
\mathbf{P}
-weighted norm. The result indicates that the update-to-update mapping at time
t
is contractive in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{P}}
if
\|\mathcal{J}_{f_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|_{\mathbf{P}}\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|_{\mathbf{P}}<1
.

In Lemma
2
, we derived bounds for the ISD updates (
A.7
) and ESD updates (
A.11
). With slight abuse of notation, we have
\mathcal{J}_{f^{j}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}):=\frac{\mathrm{d}f^{j}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\Big\rvert_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t\mid t}}{\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}^{\top}}\Big\rvert_{\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}}
for
j\in\{\textnormal{im},\textnormal{ex}\}
. Substituting these bounds into equation (
A.14
), we have

\displaystyle\|\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}_{t|t}\|_{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\tau^{j}}\,\|\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t-1|t-1}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}_{t-1|t-1}\|_{\mathbf{P}},\quad\text{where}

(A.15)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}

\displaystyle:=\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|_{\mathbf{P}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})+\alpha^{+}}+\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})-\alpha^{-}}\right),

(A.16)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}}

\displaystyle:=\|\mathbf{\Phi}\|_{\mathbf{P}}\left(1-\min\left\{\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}-\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},2-\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}\right),

(A.17)

such that the ISD and ESD update-to-update mappings at time
t
are contractive if
\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}<1
and
\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}}<1
, respectively. Because
\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}},\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}}\geq 0
(see again the proof of Lemma
2
), these conditions are equivalent to
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}<1
and
\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}<1

1
.

Since
\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}},\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{ex}}},\|\cdot\|\geq 0
, squaring both sides of (
A.15

\|\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{j}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}_{t|t}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}\leq(\tau^{j})^{t}\|\bm{\theta}^{j}_{0|0}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}_{0|0}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}},

where
\bm{\theta}^{j}_{0|0},\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}_{0|0}\in\mathbf{\Theta}
are two starting points. Hence for each filter, Definition
1
is satisfied with
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{P}
. Thus, under the (sufficient) condition
\tau^{j}<1
, it follows that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\bm{\theta}^{j}_{t|t}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}_{t|t}\|_{\mathbf{P}}^{2}=0,

for any starting points
\bm{\theta}^{j}_{0|0}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{j}_{0|0}
and any data sequence
\{\text{{{y}}}_{t}\}
, and this convergence to zero is exponentially fast. Finally, for any two positive-definite matrices
\mathbf{W},\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}

\|\cdot\|

A.5
Proof of Theorem
2

The proof of Theorem
2
makes use of Lemma
2
in Appendix
A.3
.

Proof.

Here, we derive the values of
a,b,c,d
given in Table
1
in terms of other quantities defined in Assumptions
1
–
2
. Specifically, we derive
a
and
b
for the ISD and ESD updates, and
c
and
d
for the prediction step.

Preliminaries.
Throughout this proof, we make use of two different expectation operators. First, we use the unconditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
, which acts on
\{\text{{{y}}}_{t}\}
using the true densities
\{p^{0}(\cdot\mid\bm{\vartheta}_{t})\}
and then on the true state path
\{\bm{\vartheta}_{t}\}
using its joint density. Second, we use the conditional expectation operator that acts only on
\text{{{y}}}_{t}
given a particular state
\bm{\vartheta}_{t}
. That is,
\underset{\text{{{y}}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot]:=\int\cdot\,p^{0}(\text{{{y}}}|\bm{\vartheta}_{t})d\text{{{y}}}
. By the tower property, it follows that
\mathbb{E}[\underset{\text{{{y}}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot]]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
.

Young’s inequality.
For both the ESD update and the prediction step, we make use of Young’s inequality. Specifically, we use that for any
\text{{{u}}},\text{{{v}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and any positive definite
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
:

\displaystyle\|\text{{{u}}}+\text{{{v}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{u}}}+\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{u}}}\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}+2\langle\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{u}}},\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{v}}}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\text{{{u}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+\|\text{{{v}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+2|\langle\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{u}}},\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{v}}}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|\text{{{u}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+\|\text{{{v}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+2\|\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{u}}}\|\|\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{v}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\text{{{u}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+\|\text{{{v}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\|\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{u}}}\|^{2}+(1/\epsilon^{2})\|\mathbf{W}^{1/2}\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon^{2})\|\text{{{u}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+(1+1/\epsilon^{2})\|\text{{{v}}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2},

(A.18)

for any
\epsilon>0
, where in the fourth line we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and in the fifth line we used Young’s inequality for products (i.e.,
2xy\leq\epsilon^{2}x^{2}+1/\epsilon^{2}y^{2}
for all
x,y\geq 0,\epsilon>0
).

Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
For the prediction step, we also use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. For any
\text{{{u}}},\text{{{v}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{u}}}+\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{u}}}\|^{2}+\|\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}+2\langle\text{{{u}}},\text{{{v}}}\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{u}}}\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[|\langle\text{{{u}}},\text{{{v}}}\rangle|]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{u}}}\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{u}}}\|\|\text{{{v}}}\|]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{u}}}\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}]+2\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{u}}}\|^{2}]\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{u}}}\|^{2}]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\text{{{v}}}\|^{2}]}\right)^{2},

(A.19)

where the third line uses the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the penultimate line uses the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for random variables:
|\mathbb{E}[XY]|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}
for scalar-valued random variables
X,Y
, which we use with
X=\|\text{{{u}}}\|
and
Y=\|\text{{{v}}}\|
.

Analysis of ISD update step.
The first-order condition of the ISD update for a static penalty matrix
\mathbf{P}
reads:

\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}=\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}+\mathbf{P}^{-1}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}).

We move
\mathbf{P}^{-1}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t})
to the left-hand side, pre-multiply both sides by the symmetric square root of the penalty matrix, denoted
\mathbf{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and subtract
\mathbf{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}-\mathbf{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})
from both sides to obtain

\mathbf{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})-\mathbf{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t})-\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}))=\mathbf{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})+\mathbf{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}).

(A.20)

Using Riemann integrability of the Hessian of the log-likelihood function, we may write

\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t})-\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})=\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}),

where
\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}:=\int_{0}^{1}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta})}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\right|_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\theta}\,=\,u\,\bm{\theta}_{t|t}\,+\,(1-u)\,\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{d}u
is the average Hessian between
\bm{\theta}_{t|t}
and
\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}
. Substituting this result into equation (
A.20
) produces

(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})\mathbf{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})=\mathbf{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})+\mathbf{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}),

where by Assumption
1
(b),
\mathbf{P}\succ\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\Rightarrow\mathbf{I}_{k}\succ\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\Rightarrow\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
, such that taking the inner product on both sides gives

\|\mathbf{P}^{1/2}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}_{(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2}}=\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}+\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}+2\langle\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star},\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\rangle.

Next, we take the unconditional expectation on both sides and use that
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}]\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P}^{-1})\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})
by Assumption
2
(b) and that
\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star},\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\rangle]=\mathbb{E}[\underset{\text{{{y}}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star},\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\rangle]]=\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star},\underset{\text{{{y}}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})]\rangle]=0
by the tower property and the fact that
\underset{\text{{{y}}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})]=\text{{{0}}}_{k}
because the pseudo-true parameter uniquely maximizes the expected log likelihood (see Definition
2
). This produces

\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{P}^{1/2}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}_{(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}]+\sigma^{2}/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}).

(A.21)

For the left-hand side, we may use the following lower bound

\lambda_{\min}((\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2})\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}\leq\|\mathbf{P}^{1/2}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}_{(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2}},

as
\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
, it follows that
\lambda_{\min}((\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2})=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2}>0
. Using the lower bound in (
A.21
) and dividing both sides by
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2}
gives

\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}]\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{-2}\left(\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}]+\sigma^{2}/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})\right).

Using the same methodology as in the proof of Lemma
2
, equation (
A.7
), we may obtain:

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{-1}\leq 1-\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})+\alpha^{+}}+\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})-\alpha^{-}}.

(A.22)

As both sides are nonnegative, we may square both sides. Using the definition of the
\mathbf{P}
-weighted MSE produces the final result:

\text{MSE}^{\mathbf{P}}_{t|t}\leq a\text{MSE}^{\mathbf{P}}_{t|t-1}+b,

where
a=\left(1-\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})+\alpha^{+}}+\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})-\alpha^{-}}\right)^{2}
and
b=a\sigma^{2}/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})
, which confirms the expressions for the ISD filter in Table
1
.

Analysis of ESD update step.
The ESD update reads:

\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}=\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}+\mathbf{P}^{-1}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}).

Pre-multiplying both sides by
\mathbf{P}^{1/2}
and subtracting
\mathbf{P}^{1/2}\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}
on both sides yields

\mathbf{P}^{1/2}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t})=\mathbf{P}^{1/2}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t})+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}).

Computing the squared norm on both sides and taking an unconditional expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}]+2\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}),\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\rangle]+\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}].

(A.23)

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
A.23
), we may write

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}),\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\rangle]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1})-\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}),\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\rangle]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}),\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\rangle]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}_{2\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}}],

(A.24)

where the first equality uses the tower property together with
\underset{\text{{{y}}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})]=\text{{{0}}}_{k}
from Assumption
2
(b), and that
\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable. The second equality uses Riemann integrability of the Hessian to write

\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1})-\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})=\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}),

where
\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}:=\int_{0}^{1}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta})}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\right|_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\theta}\,=\,u\,\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}\,+\,(1-u)\,\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{d}u
is the average Hessian between
\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}
. Equation (
A.24
) technically contains an abuse of notation as the Hessian matrix typically is not positive definite; below, however, this term will be combined with others, resulting in a proper norm.

For the final term on the right-hand side of (
A.23
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1})-\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})+\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\chi^{2})\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1})-\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}^{2}+(1+1/\chi^{2})\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=(1+\chi^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}\right]+(1+1/\chi^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1+\chi^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}}\right]+(1+1/\chi^{2})\sigma^{2}/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}),

(A.25)

where the second line uses Young’s
inequality as specified in (
A.18
) with
\epsilon=\chi
,
\text{{{u}}}=\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1})-\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})
,
\text{{{v}}}=\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})
and
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}
, the third uses the definition of
\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}
and the fourth that
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}^{-1}}]\leq\sigma^{2}/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})
by Assumption
2
(b).

Substituting (
A.24
) and (
A.25
) into equation (
A.23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}_{2\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}}]

\displaystyle\quad+(1+\chi^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}}\right]+\frac{(1+1/\chi^{2})\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{P}+2\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}+(1+\chi^{2})\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}}]+\frac{(1+1/\chi^{2})\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{P}^{1/2}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t})\|^{2}_{(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2}}]

\displaystyle\quad+\chi^{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{P}^{1/2}(\bm{\theta}_{t\mid t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{\star}_{t})\|^{2}_{(\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2}}]+\frac{(1+1/\chi^{2})\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}.

(A.26)

The two weighted norms on the right-hand side of (
A.26
) can be bounded by the largest eigenvalues of their weight matrices. For the first weight matrix, the bound on
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})
from (
A.11
) in Lemma
2
has nonnegative right-hand side under Assumption
1
, so squaring yields

\lambda_{\max}\left((\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2}\right)\leq\left(1-\min\left\{\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}-\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},2-\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}\right)^{2}.

For the second weight matrix, using again inequality (
A.11
) in Lemma
2
, we also have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})=\lambda_{\max}((\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})^{2})

\displaystyle=\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}),-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})-1,1-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2})\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\max\left\{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})-\alpha}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})},\frac{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})-\alpha}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}-1,1+\frac{\beta-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\frac{-\alpha}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})},\frac{-\alpha}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}^{2}=\frac{L^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})^{2}},

using
\beta>0
,
\alpha^{-}/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})\geq\alpha^{-}/\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})
, and
L:=\max\{\alpha^{-},\beta\}
. In the penultimate line,
-\alpha/\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})
cannot be the largest of the three numbers, so that it can be dropped. Combining these results, we obtain the final result:

\text{MSE}^{\mathbf{P}}_{t|t}\leq a\text{MSE}^{\mathbf{P}}_{t|t-1}+b,

where
a=\left(1-\min\left\{\frac{\alpha^{+}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})}-\frac{\alpha^{-}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})},2-\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}\right\}\right)^{2}+\frac{\chi^{2}L^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})^{2}}
and
b=\frac{(1+1/\chi^{2})\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})}
, which confirms the expressions for the ESD filter in Table
1
.

Appendix B
Further theoretical results

B.1
Kalman filter as ISD and ESD update

Although the ISD and ESD filters generally produce different outcomes, they yield identical results (albeit with different learning rates) in the case of a Gaussian observation density where the mean is a linear transformation of the parameter being tracked.

Example 1

(
Kalman
’s (
1960
) level update as both ISD and ESD)
.

Consider the observation
\text{{{y}}}_{t}=\text{{{d}}}+\mathbf{Z}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
, where
\text{{{y}}}_{t},\text{{{d}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
,
\bm{\vartheta}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\sim\textnormal{i.i.d.}\,\mathrm{N}(\text{{{0}}}_{n},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})
with
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}\succ\mathbf{O}_{n}
.
Consider Kalman’s predicted and updated covariance matrices
\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1},\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
, which are both
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
measurable.
Assume the postulated density
p(\cdot|\cdot)
matches the true density
p^{0}(\cdot|\cdot)
. Then,
Kalman’s level update can be interpreted as (i) an ISD
update as in equation (
1
), with the learning-rate matrix
\mathbf{H}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t}=\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}
; and (ii) an ESD update as in equation (
2
), with
\mathbf{H}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}=\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t}
.
The implicit learning rate exceeds the explicit one, in that
\mathbf{H}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t}\succeq\mathbf{H}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}
, since
\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\succeq\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t}
.
This dual interpretation suggests that generalizations of the Kalman filter may be constructed from either its ISD or ESD representation, as in
Lange (2024a)
and
Ollivier (2018)
, respectively.

Proof.

Linear Gaussian observation equation.
Observations
\text{{{y}}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are generated by

\text{{{y}}}_{t}=\text{{{d}}}\,+\,\mathbf{Z}\,\bm{\vartheta}_{t}\,+\,\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},\qquad\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\sim\text{i.i.d.}\,\mathrm{N}(\text{{{0}}}_{n},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}),

where
\text{{{d}}},\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
,

\bm{\vartheta}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}\succ\mathbf{O}_{n}
. If the postulated density
p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})
matches the true density
p^{0}(\cdot|\bm{\vartheta})
, then the log-likelihood function and score are

log density:

\displaystyle\ell(\text{{{y}}}|\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto-\smash{\frac{1}{2}}(\text{{{y}}}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta}),

score:

\displaystyle\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}|\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta}).

(B.1)

Let the predicted parameter
\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be given and fixed, which is used to compute both the ISD update (
1
) and the ESD update (
2
); hence, we omit the superscript on

Kalman’s covariance update.
Let Kalman’s predicted and updated covariance matrices
\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
and
\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
be given and fixed. They are related via

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}-\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1},

\displaystyle=[(\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1})^{-1}+\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}]^{-1}.

(B.2)

The first line gives the standard covariance-matrix updating step; see
Harvey (1989, p. 106)
or
Durbin and Koopman (2012, p. 86)
. The second line is used below and can be found in several places (e.g.

) below).

Kalman update as ISD update.
We take the ISD update (
1
) with

and substitute the score (

) to yield

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}),

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\,\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}).

Pre-multiplying by
(\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle\small\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}

\displaystyle\small=(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}+(\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1})^{-1})^{-1}[(\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1})^{-1}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\text{{{d}}})],

\displaystyle\small=(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}+(\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1})^{-1})^{-1}[\big\{(\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1})^{-1}+{\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}}-{\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}}\big\}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\text{{{d}}})],

\displaystyle\small=\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}+(\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1})^{-1})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}),

\displaystyle\small=\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}),

where the last line, which follows from a standard matrix-inversion lemma (see equation (
B.3
) below), is the standard Kalman-filter level update; see e.g.
Harvey (1989, p. 106)
or
Durbin and Koopman (2012, p. 86)
.

Kalman update as ESD update.
We take the ESD update (
2
) with
\mathbf{H}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t}=\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t}
and substitute the score (
B.1
) to yield

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t}\,\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t}\,\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}),

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{Kalman filter covariance update}}{=}

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+((\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1})^{-1}+\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Z})^{-1}\,\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{P}^{\textnormal{\tiny KF}}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}(\text{{{y}}}_{t}-\text{{{d}}}-\mathbf{Z}\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}),

where the last line, which follows from the same matrix-inversion lemma (see equation (
B.3
) below), is again the standard form of Kalman’s level update (see references given above).

Matrix-inversion lemmas.
The above derivation relied on two matrix-inversion lemmas for positive-definite matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\succ\mathbf{O}_{k}
and an arbitrary (size-compatible) matrix
\mathbf{C}
:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{B}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top})^{-1},

(B.3)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{C})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^{-1}.

(B.4)

While the second identity is a special case of the Woodbury matrix identity (e.g.
Sherman and Morrison, 1950
and
Henderson and Searle, 1981
), a simple proof for the first identity is hard to find. A short proof of both identities can be found in
Lange (2024b)
.
∎

B.2
Minimizing MSE bound of ISD filter w.r.t. Young’s parameter

For the ISD filter when Assumption
2
holds but Assumption
3
does not, the MSE bound contains a single free parameter,
\epsilon>0
, which can be analytically optimized.

Corollary 1

(Optimal Young’s parameter for the ISD filter)
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
2
hold and assume that sufficient condition (
9
) for stability in Theorem
1
is met, implying
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}=ac/(1+\epsilon^{2})<1
for some
\epsilon>0
. For
0<\tau^{\textnormal{im}}<1
, the value of the Young’s parameter
\epsilon^{2}>0
that minimizes the asymptotic MSE bound (
14
) is then given by

\epsilon_{\star}^{2}=\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})(\|\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{\Phi}\|\,s_{\omega}+q)^{2}}}}<\infty.

Proof.

Computing the bound.
Consider the ISD filter when Assumption
2
holds, but Assumption
3
does not. In addition, let
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}=ac/(1+\epsilon^{2})<1
(see equation (
9
)) and define
\tilde{d}:=d/(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})
, where
d
is as in Table
1
. Unlike
d
,
\tilde{d}
is independent of
\epsilon^{2}
.
As we must ensure
a\,c<1
to guarantee finite asymptotic MSE bounds, it is convenient to take
\epsilon^{2}:=(\kappa(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}))/\tau^{\textnormal{im}}
, such that
ac=\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1+\epsilon^{2})=\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\kappa(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})<1
,
\forall\kappa\in(0,1)
. Intuitively,
\kappa
controls the extent to which
\epsilon^{2}
closes the “gap” between
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}
and unity; i.e., the denominator in the asymptotic MSE bound reads
1-ac=1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}-\kappa(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})=1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}-\kappa+\kappa\tau^{\textnormal{im}}=(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})(1-\kappa)\in(0,1)
.

The asymptotic MSE bound
\forall\kappa\in(0,1)
is

\displaystyle\lim\sup_{t\to\infty}\textnormal{MSE}^{\mathbf{P}}_{t|t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{b+ad}{1-ac}

\displaystyle=\frac{b+a\tilde{d}(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})}{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1+\epsilon^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{b+a\tilde{d}(1+\frac{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\kappa(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})})}{(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})(1-\kappa)}

\displaystyle=\frac{b+a\tilde{d}}{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}\frac{1}{1-\kappa}+\frac{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}a\tilde{d}}{(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}}\frac{1}{\kappa(1-\kappa)}

\displaystyle=A\frac{1}{1-\kappa}+B\frac{1}{\kappa(1-\kappa)},

(B.5)

where the second line uses the definition of
\tilde{d}
and
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}
and the third line uses our specification of
\epsilon^{2}
in terms of
\kappa
. From the final line, it follows that
A,B\geq 0
are given by

A:=\frac{b+a\tilde{d}}{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}},\quad B:=\frac{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}a\tilde{d}}{(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}}.

(B.6)

For future reference,

\frac{A}{B}=\frac{\frac{b+a\tilde{d}}{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}}{\frac{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}a\tilde{d}}{(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}}}=\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}\frac{b+a\tilde{d}}{a\tilde{d}}.

(B.7)

Minimizing the bound.
If
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}=0
, we have that
\epsilon=\infty
minimizes the MSE bound, as can be seen in the second line of (
B.5
). We therefore proceed with the case
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}>0
, which implies
B>0
as
\tau^{\textnormal{im}}=ac/(1+\epsilon^{2})\neq 0\Rightarrow a\neq 0
and
q>0\Rightarrow\tilde{d}>0
; see (
B.6
). Equation (
B.5
) illustrates that as
A,B>0
, we have that
\kappa\in(0,1)
should not approach either boundary too closely.

Minimizing the bound (
B.5
) with respect to
\kappa
yields the following first-order condition:

0=\frac{A}{(1-\kappa)^{2}}+\frac{B(2\kappa-1)}{\kappa^{2}(1-\kappa)^{2}},

where we may multiply by
\kappa^{2}(1-\kappa)^{2}\in(0,1)
to obtain

0=A\,\kappa^{2}+2B\,\kappa-B,

which is a quadratic equation in
\kappa
, the solution of which reads

\kappa_{\pm}=\frac{-2B\pm\sqrt{4B^{2}+4AB}}{2A}.

Because
A
and
B
are positive, we have
\kappa_{+}>0
and
\kappa_{-}<0
. Only the positive solution is of interest. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\kappa_{\star}=\kappa_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+4AB}}{2A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+4AB}}{2A}\times\underbrace{\frac{2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+4AB}}{2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+4AB}}}_{=1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4AB}{4AB+2A\sqrt{4B^{2}+4AB}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4AB}{4AB+\sqrt{(4AB)^{2}+16A^{3}B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+A/B}},

which shows that
\kappa_{\star}\in(0,1/2)
.

Using the expression for
A/B
in (
B.7
) and
\epsilon^{2}=\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}\kappa
, we obtain

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\star}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}\kappa_{\star}=\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+A/B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\sqrt{(\tau^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}+\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})\frac{b+a\tilde{d}}{a\tilde{d}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\sqrt{(\tau^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}+\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})(\frac{b}{a\tilde{d}}+1)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\sqrt{(\tau^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}+\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})\frac{b}{a\tilde{d}}+\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})\frac{b}{a\tilde{d}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}}}{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\sqrt{\tau^{\textnormal{im}}+\tau^{\textnormal{im}}(1-\tau^{\textnormal{im}})\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{P})\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{P})(\|\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{\Phi}\|\,s_{\omega}+q)^{2}}}},

where the final line uses the values of
b
and
d
for the ISD filter from Table
1
. Coerciveness (the bounds tend to infinity as
\kappa
approaches either zero or one) and continuity imply that this unique stationary point is, in fact, a global minimum.
∎

B.3
Minimizing MSE bound of ISD filter w.r.t. learning rate

Under Assumptions
2
–
3
, the (Euclidean) MSE bound for the ISD filter derived from Theorem
2
contains no free parameters, although some freedom remains in selecting the penalty matrix. If we take the penalty matrix to be a scalar multiple of the identity, the value of this multiple that minimizes the asymptotic MSE bound can be derived analytically.

Corollary 2

(Optimal penalty parameter for the ISD filter)
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
(a,b), and
3
hold. Furthermore, assume that (i) the postulated log-likelihood contribution is strongly concave (i.e.,
\alpha>0
), (ii) the penalty matrix is a scalar multiple of the identity matrix (i.e.,
\mathbf{P}=\rho\mathbf{I}_{k}
for some
\rho>0
), and (iii)
\sigma,\sigma_{\xi}>0
such that
b,d>0
. Then the value of
\rho>0
that minimizes the asymptotic (Euclidean) MSE bound for the ISD filter is

\rho_{\star}=\frac{\sigma^{2}\left(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}\right)-\alpha^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+\sqrt{\left(\alpha^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}-\sigma^{2}\left(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}\right)\right)^{2}+4\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}}{2\alpha\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}<\infty.

Proof.

Using the result of Theorem
2
and that
\mathbf{P}=\rho\mathbf{I}_{k}
, we have that
a=\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho+\alpha}\right)^{2}\in(0,1)
and
b=a\rho^{-1}\sigma^{2}
for the ISD filter with strong concavity (
\alpha>0
). In addition, when the DGP is a known state-space model (Assumption
3
holds) and again using
\mathbf{P}=\rho\mathbf{I}_{k}
, we have that
c=\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{P}}^{2}=\|\mathbf{P}^{1/2}\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\mathbf{P}^{-1/2}\|^{2}=\|\rho^{1/2}\mathbf{I}_{k}\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\rho^{-1/2}\mathbf{I}_{k}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}
and
d=\rho\sigma^{2}_{\xi}
. Substituting these into the asymptotic filter
\mathbf{P}
-weighted MSE bound in (
14
) and converting to the (Euclidean) MSE bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\;\textnormal{MSE}_{t\mid t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho}\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\;\textnormal{MSE}^{\mathbf{P}}_{t\mid t}\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{b+ad}{1-ac}=\frac{a\sigma^{2}/\rho^{2}+a\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{1-a\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}/\rho^{2}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{a^{-1}-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}/\rho^{2}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{\left(\frac{\rho+\alpha}{\rho}\right)^{2}-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}+\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{(\rho+\alpha)^{2}-\rho^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}},

where the second line first multiplies by
a^{-1}/a^{-1}=1
and subsequently by
\rho^{2}/\rho^{2}=1
.

To optimize the bound with respect to
\rho
, we consider the first-order condition:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\rho}\frac{\sigma^{2}+\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{(\rho+\alpha)^{2}-\rho^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}}

(B.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\rho\sigma_{\xi}^{2}((\rho+\alpha)^{2}-\rho^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})-(\sigma^{2}+\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2})(2(\rho+\alpha)-2\rho\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})}{((\rho+\alpha)^{2}-\rho^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\rho\sigma_{\xi}^{2}((\rho+\alpha)^{2}-\rho^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})-(\sigma^{2}+\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2})(2(\rho+\alpha)-2\rho\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})}{\rho^{4}(a^{-1}-c)^{2}},

where the second line multiplies with
\frac{1}{\rho^{4}\rho^{-4}}=1
followed by using the definition of
a
and
c
. Since
a>0
(as it is a squared quantity), the contraction condition
ac<1
implies that the denominator in (
B.8
) is positive, as
ac<1\Rightarrow 1-ac>0\Rightarrow a^{-1}-c>0\Rightarrow\rho^{4}(a^{-1}-c)^{2}>0
. This means that to solve the first-order condition, we need to set the numerator equal to 0, i.e.,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\rho\sigma_{\xi}^{2}((\rho+\alpha)^{2}-\rho^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})-(\sigma^{2}+\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2})(2(\rho+\alpha)-2\rho\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\rho\sigma_{\xi}^{2}(\rho+\alpha)^{2}-2\rho^{3}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}-\sigma^{2}(2(\rho+\alpha)-2\rho\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})-2\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}(\rho+\alpha)+2\rho^{3}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\rho\sigma_{\xi}^{2}(\rho+\alpha)^{2}-2\sigma^{2}(\rho+\alpha)+2\rho\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}-2\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}(\rho+\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\rho^{3}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+2\rho\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha^{2}+4\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha-2\rho\sigma^{2}-2\sigma^{2}\alpha+2\rho\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}-2\rho^{3}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}-2\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\rho\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha^{2}+4\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha-2\rho\sigma^{2}-2\sigma^{2}\alpha+2\rho\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}-2\rho^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{2}(2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha)+\rho(2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha^{2}-2\sigma^{2}(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}))-2\sigma^{2}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\rho^{2}+B\rho+C,

which yields a quadratic equation in
\rho
with coefficients
A=2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha
,
B=2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha^{2}-2\sigma^{2}(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})
and
C=-2\sigma^{2}\alpha
and solution

\rho_{\pm}=\frac{-B\pm\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}}{2A}.

Because
AC=-4\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma^{2}\alpha^{2}<0
and
A=2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha>0
, we have that
\rho_{-}<0
and
\rho_{+}>0
, and the latter is therefore the solution to the minimization problem at hand. Specifically, the final result reads

\displaystyle\rho_{\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{+}=\frac{-2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha^{2}+2\sigma^{2}(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})+\sqrt{4\left(\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha^{2}-\sigma^{2}(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})\right)^{2}+16\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma^{2}\alpha^{2}}}{4\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})-\alpha^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+\sqrt{\left(\alpha^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}-\sigma^{2}(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})\right)^{2}+4\alpha^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma^{2}}}{2\alpha\sigma_{\xi}^{2}},

where the second line multiplies by
(1/2)/(1/2)=1
and rearranges. Because
A=2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\alpha>0
, we have that the numerator of (
B.8
) is a convex parabola with largest root
\rho_{\star}
, which means that it becomes negative for
\rho\in(0,\rho_{\star})
and positive for
\rho\in(\rho_{\star},\infty)
. Combined with the fact that the denominator of (
B.8
) is always positive, we have that the bound is decreasing in
\rho
on the interval
(0,\rho_{\star})
and increasing on the interval
(\rho_{\star},\infty)
. In sum, this means that the stationary point
\rho_{\star}
is indeed the global minimum.
∎

B.4
Minimized MSE bound of ISD filter can be tight

Example
2
below illustrates a case in which the minimized asymptotic MSE bound for the ISD filter is tight, as it equals the steady-state variance of the Kalman filter. The optimal learning-rate selection reveals a connection with the Kalman gain.

Example 2

(Tight MSE bounds of ISD filter)
.

Consider an AR(1) model with additive observation noise, defined by the observation equation
y_{t}=\vartheta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t},
where
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\textnormal{i.i.d.}\,\mathrm{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
with
\sigma_{\varepsilon}>0
, and linear first-order state dynamics
\vartheta_{t+1}=(1-\phi_{0})\omega_{0}+\phi_{0}\vartheta_{t}+\xi_{t},
where
\xi_{t}\sim\textnormal{i.i.d.}\,\mathrm{N}(0,\sigma_{\xi}^{2})
with
\sigma_{\xi}>0
.
Let the learning rate be positive and constant,
\mathbf{H}_{t}=\mathbf{P}_{t}^{-1}=\rho^{-1}=\eta>0
, for all
t
. As in
Kalman
’s classic setting, let Assumption
3
hold; that is, the true density
p^{0}(\cdot|\cdot)
coincides with the postulated density
p(\cdot|\cdot)
,
and the parameters
\omega_{0}
and
\phi_{0}
are known to the researcher. Then, the asymptotic MSE bound

\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\textnormal{MSE}_{t\mid t}\leq\frac{\eta^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{\xi}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}}{2\eta\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\eta^{2}+(1-\phi_{0}^{2})\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}},

is minimized when the learning rate
\eta
is chosen as

\eta_{\star}=\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)+\sqrt{\sigma_{\xi}^{4}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}+2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(1+\phi_{0}^{2}\right)}}{2}.

For the local-level model, where
\phi_{0}=1
, the minimizer
\eta_{\star}
simplifies to

\eta_{\star}=\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\xi}^{4}+4\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\xi}}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{4}_{\xi}}{\sigma^{4}_{\varepsilon}}+4\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\xi}}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}}\right).

As it turns out,
\eta_{\star}
equals the steady-state variance of prediction errors in the Kalman filter, as given in
Durbin and Koopman (2012, p. 37)
and
Harvey (1989, p. 175)
, where
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}/\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}
is the signal-to-noise ratio.
Substituting the minimizer
\eta_{\star}
into the ISD update, we obtain the exact form of Kalman’s level update with the (optimal) Kalman gain. Since the Kalman filter is optimal for this model, our minimized MSE bound is tight.

Proof.

For readability, we omit all superscripts
im
. Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, the asymptotic (Euclidean) MSE bound for the ISD filter is obtained by substituting
a=\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho+\alpha}\right)^{2}
,
b=a\rho^{-1}\sigma^{2}
,
c=\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2}
, and
d=\rho\sigma^{2}_{\xi}
in equation (
14
), and multiplying by
\eta
to obtain

\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\textnormal{MSE}_{t\mid t}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{\xi}\rho^{2}}{\alpha^{2}+2\alpha\rho+(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})\rho^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{\xi}}{\alpha^{2}\eta^{2}+2\alpha\eta+(1-\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}\|^{2})},

(B.9)

where
\eta:=1/\rho
. In the context of Example
2
,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}=\phi_{0}
,
\alpha=-\nabla^{2}\ell(y_{t}|\theta_{t|t-1})=1/{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}
and
\sigma^{2}=\sup_{t}\underset{y_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}(\|\nabla\ell(y_{t}|\vartheta_{t})\|^{2})=1/{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}
. To see this, consider the postulated log-likelihood function

\ell(y_{t}|\theta_{t|t-1})=-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})-\frac{(y_{t}-\theta_{t|t-1})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

Taking the first derivative with respect to
\theta_{t|t-1}
, the score is

\nabla\ell(y_{t}|\theta_{t|t-1})=\frac{y_{t}-\theta_{t|t-1}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

The second derivative with respect to
\theta_{t|t-1}
is

\nabla^{2}\ell(y_{t}|\theta_{t|t-1})=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

Thus,
\alpha=1/{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}
. Now, for
y_{t}\sim\mathrm{N}(\vartheta_{t},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
, the score evaluated at the true parameter is

\nabla\ell(y_{t}|\vartheta_{t})=\frac{y_{t}-\vartheta_{t}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}},

and taking the squared norm, we obtain

\|\nabla\ell(y_{t}|\vartheta_{t})\|^{2}=\frac{(y_{t}-\vartheta_{t})^{2}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}}.

Taking the expectation with respect to
y_{t}
, we get:

\underset{y_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\|\nabla\ell(y_{t}|\vartheta_{t})\|^{2}\right)=\frac{\underset{y_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}[(y_{t}-\vartheta_{t})^{2}]}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}}=\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

Thus,
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}
.
Next, we substitute
\mathbf{\Phi}_{0}=\phi_{0}
,
\alpha=1/\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}=1/\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
in equation (
B.9
), and subsequently multiply the numerator and denominator by
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}>0
, to obtain

\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\textnormal{MSE}_{t\mid t}\leq\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{\xi}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}}{\eta^{2}+2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta+(1-\phi_{0}^{2})\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}}.

(B.10)

If
\eta=0
, we obtain an upper bound equal to
\sigma_{\xi}^{2}/(1-\phi_{0}^{2})
, which is the unconditional variance of the true state,
\vartheta_{t}
. If
\eta=\infty
, we obtain an upper bound equal to
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
, which is the conditional variance of the observations. We hope to find an (optimal) learning rate
\eta>0
that minimizes the upper bound, yielding a lower value than either
\eta=0
or
\eta=\infty
.

To this end, we compute the derivative (with respect to
\eta
) of the bound (
B.10
) and equate it to zero:

\frac{\left[2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta\right]\left[2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta+\eta^{2}+\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\right]-\left[\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta^{2}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\right]\left[2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+2\eta\right]}{\left(\eta^{2}+2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta+(1-\phi_{0}^{2})\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\right)^{2}}=0.

We will find that there exists a unique value of
\eta>0
that solves this equation. Moreover, this unique value
\eta>0
will turn out to deliver a lower MSE bound than either
\eta=0
or
\eta=\infty
. Because the bound is continuously differentiable in
\eta\geq 0
, this means that we will have found the global minimum.

Given that the denominator above is positive,
finding the stationary point is equivalent to solving

4\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\eta^{2}+2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta^{3}+2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{6}\eta\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)-\left(2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\eta^{2}+2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta^{3}+2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{6}+2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\eta\right)=0.

Canceling out
2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\eta^{3}
, using that
4\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\eta^{2}-2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\eta^{2}=2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\eta^{2}
, and that
2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{6}\eta(1-\phi_{0}^{2})-2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\eta=\eta\,(2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{6}\eta(1-\phi_{0}^{2})-2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4})
, combining the terms we get a quadratic equation in
\eta
:

2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\eta^{2}+\eta\left(2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{6}\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)-2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\right)-2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{6}=0.

Dividing each term by
2\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}>0
leads to

\eta^{2}+\eta\left(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)-\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\right)-\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}=0.

Solving for
\eta_{\star}
using the quadratic formula, we obtain

\eta_{\pm}=\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)\pm\sqrt{D}}{2},

where
D=(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1-\phi_{0}^{2})-\sigma_{\xi}^{2})^{2}+4\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}=\sigma_{\xi}^{4}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}(1-\phi_{0}^{2})^{2}+2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1+\phi_{0}^{2})
. Because
\sqrt{D}>\sigma_{\xi}^{2}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)
, we have
\eta_{+}>0
and
\eta_{-}<0
. Only the positive solution is of interest. Specifically, we have

\eta_{\star}=\eta_{+}=\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)+\sqrt{\sigma_{\xi}^{4}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}\left(1-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}+2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(1+\phi_{0}^{2}\right)}}{2}.

In the case of a local-level model (i.e.,
\phi_{0}=1
), we have
a\times c=a\times\phi_{0}=\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho+\alpha}\right)^{2}<1
as
\alpha=1/{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}
. Thus, the optimal learning rate reduces to

\eta_{\star}=\frac{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\xi}^{4}+4\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}}{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\xi}}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{4}_{\xi}}{\sigma^{4}_{\varepsilon}}+4\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\xi}}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}}\right),

(B.11)

which is the steady-state covariance associated with predictions in the Kalman filter, where
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}/\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}
is the signal-to-noise ratio.

Substituting
\eta_{\star}
from equation (
B.11
) back into the bound (
B.10
), which was to be minimized, we can confirm (after some algebra) that the unique stationary point yields a value that does not exceed the value at either endpoint (i.e., at
\eta=0
or
\eta=\infty
). By continuous differentiability of the MSE bound in
\eta\geq 0
, the stationary point yields the global minimum as desired.
∎

C.4
Computing the ISD update

Standard Newton-Raphson iterates for solving the ISD update (
4
) with
\mathbf{P}_{t}=\mathbf{P}
read

\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}\;\leftarrow\;\bm{\theta}_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}\,+\,\big[\mathbf{P}-\nabla^{2}\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t})\big]^{-1}\;\big[\,\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t})\,-\,\mathbf{P}\,(\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}-\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1})\,\big],

(C.1)

where
\nabla^{2}:=\nabla\nabla^{\top}=(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta})^{\top}

denotes the Hessian operator.
The algorithm may be initialized with
\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}\leftarrow\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1}
. The inverse exists as
\mathbf{P}-\nabla^{2}\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t})
is positive definite because of Assumption
1
(b). Adding a standard line search is typically helpful.

For high-dimensional problems, it may be beneficial to employ an algorithm that avoids large-matrix inversions, such as the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) algorithm (e.g.,
Liu and Nocedal, 1989
). When computational efficiency is critical, algorithm (
C.1
) may be terminated after a single NR iteration, in which case the output (after one iteration) reads
\bm{\theta}_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{im}}+[\mathbf{P}-\nabla^{2}\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1})]^{-1}\nabla\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{im}}_{t\mid t-1})
. This “1NR” version is similar to the
explicit
update (
2
) in being computationally inexpensive; however, it is based on a quadratic (rather than linear) approximation of
\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta})
around the prediction, which is advantageous when
\bm{\theta}\mapsto\ell(\text{{{y}}}_{t}|\bm{\theta})
exhibits strong curvature. On the other hand, additional iterations typically provide additional precision; hence, depending on the available computer power, researchers may decide to execute more or fewer iterations of algorithm (
C.1
).

C.5
Details for Section
5.3

Here, we provide additional implementation details for the ESD and ISD filters, as well as the MSE bounds for the latter from Section
5.3
.

ESD filter.
For the ESD filters with
exponential
link functions,
\mu^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t}=\,\exp(\theta^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t})
, we define the learning rates as
\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\textnormal{ex}}=\eta^{\textnormal{ex}}\,\mathcal{I}(\theta_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{ex}})^{-\zeta}
, where
\mathcal{I}(\theta)=\exp(\theta)
represents the Fisher information and
\zeta\in\{0,1/2,1\}
determines the scaling of the score function. The ESD update becomes

\theta^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t}=\theta^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t-1}+\eta^{\textnormal{ex}}\,\exp(-\zeta\theta^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t-1})(y_{t}-\exp(\theta^{\textnormal{ex}}_{t|t-1})).

Combined with the prediction step (
3
), this leads to the standard score-driven prediction-to-prediction recursion (e.g.,
Creal et al., 2013
, p. 779
).

ISD filter.
The ISD filter with
exponential
link function,
\mu^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}=\,\exp(\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t})
, and static learning rate,
\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\textnormal{im}}=\eta^{\textnormal{im}}
for all
t
, has update

\displaystyle\theta_{t\mid t}^{\textnormal{im}}

\displaystyle=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\left\{\ell\left(y_{t}\mid\theta\right)-\frac{1}{2\eta^{\textnormal{im}}}\left(\theta-\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t\mid t-1}\right)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\left\{y_{t}\,\theta\,-\exp(\theta)-\log(y_{t}!)-\frac{1}{2\eta^{\textnormal{im}}}\left(\theta-\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t\mid t-1}\right)^{2}\right\},

which is solved numerically using a standard Newton-Raphson algorithm (see Appendix
C.4
).
The ISD filter with
quadratic
link function,
\mu^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}=\,(\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t})^{2}
, and static learning rate, has an implicit update that can be solved analytically as

\displaystyle\theta_{t\mid t}^{\textnormal{im}}

\displaystyle=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\left\{\ell\left(y_{t}\mid\theta\right)-\frac{1}{2\eta^{\textnormal{im}}}\left(\theta-\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t\mid t-1}\right)^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\left\{y_{t}\log(\theta^{2})-\theta^{2}-\log(y_{t}!)-\frac{1}{2\eta^{\textnormal{im}}}\left(\theta-\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t\mid t-1}\right)^{2}\right\}.

Taking first-order conditions with respect to
\theta
, and evaluating at
\theta=\theta_{t\mid t}^{\textnormal{im}}
, yields

\frac{2y_{t}}{\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}}-2\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}-\frac{1}{\eta^{\textnormal{im}}}\left(\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}-\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t\mid t-1}\right)=0.

Next, we assume
\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}>0
, which is without loss of generality,
as the case
\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}=0
(which occurs when
y_{t}=\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1}=0
) will be automatically covered below.
As
\eta^{\textnormal{im}}>0
, we can then multiply both sides by
\eta^{\textnormal{im}}\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}>0
to obtain a quadratic equation in
\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}
as follows:

-(1+2\eta^{\textnormal{im}})(\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}+\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1}\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}+2\eta^{\textnormal{im}}y_{t}=0.

Solving for
\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}
by the usual formula yields two potential solutions:

\theta_{t|t}^{\textnormal{im}}=\frac{-\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1}\pm\sqrt{(\theta_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}+8(1+2\eta^{\textnormal{im}})\eta^{\textnormal{im}}y_{t}}}{-2(1+2\eta^{\textnormal{im}})}.

Multiplying the numerator and denominator by
-1
and taking only the nonnegative solution, we obtain the ISD update

\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}=\frac{\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1}+\sqrt{(\theta_{t|t-1}^{\textnormal{im}})^{2}+8\eta^{\textnormal{im}}(1+2\eta^{\textnormal{im}})y_{t}}}{2(1+2\eta^{\textnormal{im}})}\geq 0.

This solution yields
\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t}=0
if and only if
\theta^{\textnormal{im}}_{t|t-1}=y_{t}=0
, such that the limiting case is correctly covered.

MSE bounds.
The gradient noise (in Assumption
2
) of the ISD filter with quadratic link can be computed as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\textnormal{d}\ell(y_{t}|\theta_{t})}{\textnormal{d}\theta_{t}}\right)^{2}\mid_{\theta_{t}=\theta_{t}^{\star}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{2y_{t}}{\theta_{t}}-2\theta_{t}\right)^{2}\mid_{\theta_{t}=\theta_{t}^{\star}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{4y_{t}^{2}}{\theta_{t}^{2}}-8y_{t}+4\theta_{t}^{2}\right)\mid_{\theta_{t}=\theta_{t}^{\star}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{4y_{t}^{2}}{\mu_{t}}-8y_{t}+4\mu_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[4(\mu_{t}+1)-8\mu_{t}+4\mu_{t}\right]
