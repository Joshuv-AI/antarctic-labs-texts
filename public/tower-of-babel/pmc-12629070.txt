Ethnoentomology (1952–2024): scientific production, emerging trends and research gaps
Authors: Johnson Andrade Junior, Paulo Roberto Ramalho Silva, Roseli Farias Melo de Barros
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEthnoentomology, as an interdisciplinary field, explores the interactions between humans and insects from cultural, ecological, and social perspectives, fostering dialogue between traditional knowledge and modern science. Despite its relevance, a comprehensive bibliometric overview of its scientific production has been lacking.MethodsA bibliometric analysis was carried out on the Ethnoentomology literature published between 1952 and 2024, using the Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases. Searches were performed in Portuguese, English, and Spanish using the terms “ethnoentomol*”, “etnoentomol*” and their variations. Articles directly addressing Ethnoentomology were included, while unrelated studies and duplicates were excluded. The data were analysed to identify publication trends, leading countries, authorship networks, and major research themes.ResultsA total of 1,077 articles were identified, of which 375 met the inclusion criteria after duplicate removal. Publications showed continuous growth over time, with Brazil and other megadiverse countries standing out as the main contributors. Brazil ranked first, particularly in the Northeast Region, followed by studies from Mexico, the United States, India, and Spain. Among the most prominent authors are Costa Neto, Posey, and Monserrat, whose contributions were fundamental to the consolidation of Ethnoentomology as an autonomous field. Collaboration networks revealed increasing interdisciplinarity, encompassing themes such as food security, environmental conservation, and sustainability. Across the analysed studies, 1,043 insect species were recorded for different purposes, predominantly alimentary, medicinal, and utilitarian. Species such as Apis mellifera (Linnaeus, 1758), Rhynchophorus palmarum (Linnaeus, 1758), and Periplaneta americana (Linnaeus, 1758) stand out for their versatility and frequency of citation, emphasising the multifunctional value of insects in cultural contexts.ConclusionsEthnoentomology plays a strategic role in valuing ancestral knowledge and promoting sustainable practices, reinforcing its importance for biodiversity conservation and local livelihoods. Strengthening research networks and integrating local knowledge into academic and policy agendas are essential for advancing this field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-025-00831-y.Keywords: Ethnoscience, Entomology, Bibliometrics, Traditional knowledge, Edible insects, Sustainability

Introduction

Insects play significant roles in different cultures, influencing religions, mythologies, rituals, beliefs, and magical-medicinal systems among various peoples [1, 2]. In Asian countries such as China, India, and Thailand, the consumption of insects as a food source has been an established practice for centuries [3, 4]. In addition, traditional management strategies, such as vegetation diversification for pest control, were already employed by Indigenous communities long before the advent of modern agricultural practices [5–7].

Traditional knowledge about insects — encompassing medicinal uses, folk classifications, and cultural practices — constitutes one of the cornerstones of Ethnoentomology [8]. Its relevance is illustrated by regional studies [9, 10] that document Indigenous and popular uses of insects in medicinal and cultural contexts. By integrating traditional knowledge with scientific methods, the field broadens our understanding of the natural world and contributes to innovative and sustainable solutions to contemporary environmental and food challenges.

Historically, the thematic foundations of Ethnoentomology were being explored long before the term itself became established within ethnobiological sciences (Ethnobiology: Ethnobotany/Ethnozoology and Ethnoecology). The conceptual consolidation of “Ethnoentomology” occurred only after decades of research on human–insect interactions conducted in various disciplinary contexts, including medical entomology, agronomy, ecology, and anthropology.

In the mid-twentieth century, topics such as malaria and other insect-borne diseases, agricultural pest control, and pollination ecology were central to public health and agricultural agendas, particularly in the context of global malaria eradication campaigns and the Green Revolution. Many of these studies, although not explicitly using the term “ethnoentomology,” addressed human–insect relationships in ways that are now central to the field. This historical trajectory helps explain the low number of publications that explicitly used the term between the 1950 s and 1980 s, and it contextualises the temporal patterns observed in the bibliometric analysis.

Despite its growing relevance, scientific production in Ethnoentomology remains insufficiently systematised at the global scale. In this context, bibliometric analysis represents a fundamental tool for mapping the evolution of the field, identifying research gaps and emerging trends, and elucidating collaboration networks and academic impacts [11, 12]. Such an approach is particularly pertinent in emerging disciplines, where the establishment of quantitative metrics provides a solid foundation for strategic development and knowledge consolidation — as illustrated by bibliometric studies on edible insects [13] and trends in tropical insect science publications [14].

Given this scenario, the general objective of this study is to map scientific production in Ethnoentomology between 1952 and 2024, based on the Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases, through bibliometric indicators. Specifically, it aims to: (i) quantify scientific production over time; (ii) identify the main authors, institutions, and countries active in the field; and (iii) highlight emerging trends and existing gaps in the literature. By adopting a comprehensive bibliometric approach, this study provides a structured view of the historical evolution of Ethnoentomology, highlights its current challenges, and discusses future perspectives, contributing to the consolidation of the field as an interdisciplinary scientific domain with broad social, environmental, and economic applications.

Methods

This research adopts a descriptive approach with both qualitative and quantitative focuses, based on a bibliometric analysis of scientific production in Ethnoentomology.

Data collection

The study covered the period between 1952 and 2024 and included articles published in English, Portuguese, and Spanish. Searches were carried out in January 2025 through institutional access (@ufpi.edu.br) to the CAPES Journal Portal.

Three main databases were used: Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, and Google Scholar. WoS and Scopus were selected due to their broad coverage and well-established relevance in bibliometric research [15–18]. Records from Google Scholar were obtained using the Publish or Perish software (PoP, version 8.17.4863), which is widely recognised for enabling the structured collection of metadata and citation indicators in bibliometric studies [19].

Search strategies were developed using the descriptors “ethnoentomol”* and “etnoentomol”*, including hyphenated variations, applied to the title, abstract, and keyword fields of each database.

It is important to emphasise that the search strategy focused exclusively on identifying publications that explicitly used the terms “ethnoentomology”, “ethno-entomology”, “etnoentomologia”, or “etno-entomologia” in titles, abstracts, or keywords. Broader descriptors such as “ethnozoology” or “ethnobiology” were not systematically included, even though these fields often contain information relevant to Ethnoentomology. This methodological decision was deliberate, as the aim was to examine the historical trajectory and growth of Ethnoentomology as an autonomous scientific discipline, rather than to compile information on human–insect interactions scattered across related fields. Consequently, records that did not explicitly refer to Ethnoentomology were excluded during the screening process, even when they presented conceptual overlaps with the field.

Selection and eligibility

The screening of studies was carried out by reading titles and abstracts, with the aim of including only those directly related to the research topic. Duplicates and documents that did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded. In total, 375 articles were included in the final analysis.

The process of identification, screening, and inclusion followed a flow diagram adapted from the PRISMA model (Fig. 1), which details the number of records retrieved, duplicates removed, and studies included.

Data organisation and analysis

The records were exported in BiBTeX (.BIB) format and organised in Microsoft® Excel® (Version 2306). Subsequently, the data were integrated and analysed in R (version 4.3.3+), via RStudio (2024.12.0 + 467), using the Bibliometrix package and the Biblioshiny interface. The function mergeDbSources() was applied to integrate the databases and remove duplicates.

The analyses included: temporal evolution of publications; identification of the most productive authors, institutions, countries, and journals; co-authorship and co-citation networks; and keyword analysis to detect emerging themes and trends.

Based on the information extracted from the analysed articles, the uses attributed to insects were organised into eight non-exclusive categories, which reflect the cultural, symbolic, and practical dimensions associated with these organisms. These categories — alimentary, medicinal, pest, offensive, ritualistic, symbolic, utilitarian, and ludic — were defined from the Ethnoentomology and Ethnozoology literature, aiming to encompass both material and immaterial aspects of their uses. Each category is briefly described below, outlining the criteria used for classification.

Alimentary

Includes the direct consumption of insect eggs or oothecae, larvae, pupae, and adults, prepared in different ways or consumed raw. It excludes derivative products such as honey or wax. This category reflects traditional entomophagy practices documented in different cultural contexts.

Symbolic

Refers to cultural, spiritual, or cosmological associations attributed to insects, regardless of material use. It includes meanings related to social values, collective identities, omens, mythologies, or artistic representations, reflecting the incorporation of these organisms into symbolic systems.

Utilitarian

Covers the use of insects or their products as sources of raw materials or services, excluding alimentary or medicinal purposes. Typical examples include the production of silk, dyes, waxes, or lacquers; traditional technologies such as glues or instruments; and artistic or material applications such as paintings, sculptures, and handicrafts. It also includes management practices and the indirect use of insects for human benefit.

Ritualistic

Comprises uses prescribed in ceremonies, rites of passage, shamanic practices, religious festivities, or other ritual contexts. It includes both the direct use of insects and the incorporation of their forms, behaviours, or products into symbolic or performative elements, according to local cosmologies and traditions.

Pest

Includes species recognised by communities as harmful to agriculture, public health, or domestic environments. It encompasses insects that damage crops, infest spaces, or act as disease vectors, often associated with direct negative impacts on human activities.

Medicinal

Encompasses the therapeutic use of insects or their products, administered internally or externally. It includes whole insects, parts, eggs, larvae, and traditional preparations (e.g., teas, infusions, poultices or decoctions) or secretions used for curative, prophylactic, or symptomatic purposes, according to local knowledge systems. It comprises, for example, the use of honey, propolis, wax, or other apicultural products, as well as insect parts or secretions applied in traditional healing practices.

Offensive

Refers to insects identified by their ability to cause pain, discomfort, or injury through bites, stings, haematophagy, or the release of irritating substances. It includes species that pose physical risks or cause discomfort, regardless of intentional use.

Ludic

Encompasses recreational or entertainment-related uses, particularly in childhood or community contexts. It includes non-utilitarian interactions such as games, behavioural observation, keeping insects as “pets”, or other culturally significant playful practices.

The use of bibliometric methods is justified by their ability to reveal research patterns, gaps, and developmental trends in emerging scientific domains, thus providing a comprehensive overview of field evolution. Bibliometric approaches have been successfully applied in various areas, such as sustainable rural tourism [20], transformative learning [21], sonodynamic therapy [22], and network meta-analysis [23, 24].

Results and discussion

Annual scientific production

The bibliometric analysis of scientific production in Ethnoentomology between 1952 and 2024 revealed significant growth in the volume of publications, highlighting the consolidation of the field as a scientific discipline over recent decades (Fig. 2). This expansion accompanies the increasing appreciation of interdisciplinary themes that connect the biological, social, and environmental sciences, reflecting broader shifts in academic and scientific priorities.

Annual scientific production in Ethnoentomology (1952–2024)

During the initial period (1952–1981), only seven articles were recorded, with long intervals of stagnation. This scenario can be explained by the lack of recognition of Ethnoentomology as an autonomous field, as studies on human–insect interactions were generally framed within broader disciplines such as Anthropology, Entomology, and Ethnobiology. In this context, the pioneering work of Wyman and Bailey (1952), Native Navaho methods for the control of insect pests, stands out as one of the earliest formal records in the area. The study documented traditional Navajo practices for insect management, laying the groundwork for Ethnoentomology, even though the term itself was not yet widely used.

Between the 1980 s and the early 2000 s, there was a moderate increase in publications (41 in total), albeit with marked fluctuations. This growth is associated with the strengthening of Ethnobiology and the expansion of academic debates on traditional knowledge related to insects. A milestone of this period was the work of Posey (1986), which identified Ethnoentomology as a specific branch of Ethnobiology and promoted its recognition as an autonomous area of study. The systematic adoption of the term encouraged researchers to incorporate it into their investigations, consolidating the field in the international academic landscape.

The year 2003 marked a turning point, with 11 published articles. This leap reflects the consolidation of the field and can be attributed to factors such as the valorisation of traditional knowledge, the development of new methodologies, and the expansion of interdisciplinary approaches. It also coincided with a marked increase in the academic impact of ethnoentomological studies, as evidenced by higher citation rates and growing scholarly interest.

The appreciation of traditional and Indigenous knowledge has been one of the main driving forces behind this trend. Local knowledge systems play an essential role in understanding biodiversity and ensuring sustainable resource management [25]. This perspective has been reinforced by studies highlighting the importance of biocultural memory as a foundation for conservation strategies [26] and expanding the concept of biocultural sustainability by emphasising its methodological and applied dimensions [27]. Collectively, these approaches have stimulated research documenting and analysing cultural uses of insects, whether for food, medicine, or pest control.

Methodological advances and greater access to digital databases have also played a decisive role. The integration of refined quantitative and qualitative approaches, combined with the increasing availability of digital repositories, has facilitated both the production and dissemination of ethnoentomological knowledge [28]. Subsequent studies reinforced this perspective by demonstrating how methodological systematisation and meta-analytical approaches enhance scientific rigour [29, 30]. In addition, the relevance of ethnozoological methods has been emphasised for consolidating Ethnobiology and its subfields, broadening their visibility within both the Biological and Social Sciences [31].

Another decisive factor has been the growing interdisciplinarity of Ethnoentomology. The field increasingly intersects with biodiversity conservation, food security, and public health, thereby broadening its scope and social relevance. Early studies already highlighted the importance of Ethnobiology in shaping conservation strategies [32], while more recent research demonstrates the direct applicability of ethnoentomological knowledge to agroecological management and pharmacological innovation [33].

From 2012 onwards, scientific output in Ethnoentomology accelerated significantly, accompanied by a substantial rise in citation rates. This trend is associated with the increasing incorporation of ethnoentomological knowledge into debates on agroecology and sustainability [34]. By documenting and valuing traditional perspectives on insects, the field contributes directly to biodiversity conservation while also generating innovative approaches to food security and environmental resilience. Tropical regions, where cultural and biological diversity converge, have consolidated themselves as leading centres of ethnoentomological research.

Finally, research policies focused on biodiversity and sustainability, particularly in megadiverse countries, have reinforced this trajectory. Early contributions already emphasised that investment in ecological and ethnobiological studies is essential to foster the development of fields such as Ethnoentomology [35]. More recent evidence supports this view, showing that international funding has increasingly strengthened conservation research in megadiverse countries [36], while other studies highlight that scientific output remains insufficient in biodiversity-rich regions, underscoring the importance of targeted investment to expand research capacity and knowledge generation [37].

Thus, the continuous strengthening of Ethnoentomology not only demonstrates its consolidation as an established scientific discipline but also reveals its strategic role in mediating between traditional knowledge and modern science. This integration has proven fundamental for biodiversity conservation, the development of sustainable practices, and the creation of innovative solutions in fields such as food security, public health, and biotechnology. Looking to the future, prospects point to further expansion, driven both by the urgency of addressing biodiversity loss and by the recognition of traditional knowledge as a key element in conservation and environmental management strategies.

Most relevant authors in Ethnoentomology (1952–2024)

Among the most prominent names, Costa Neto, Posey, and Monserrat stand out for the volume of publications produced over the decades. Their contributions have positioned them at the forefront of scientific production in Ethnoentomology, directly influencing the conceptual and methodological development of the discipline. The number of works and frequency of citations indicate that they have become essential references for the consolidation of the field.

The temporal analysis of these authors’ output (Fig. 4) shows that, although there was already consistent production in the early decades, a significant increase occurred from the 2000 s onwards. This growth coincides with the rising global interest in topics such as edible insects and food sustainability, which have gained prominence over the past two decades. The scientific contributions of Costa Neto and Posey, in particular, reflect this movement and reinforce their roles as central figures in the expansion of Ethnoentomology.

Authors’ scientific output in Ethnoentomology (1952–2024)

The rise in publications after 2003 also reflects a growing trend of collaboration among researchers in the field. As Ethnoentomology became more consolidated as a scientific discipline, there was greater demand for interinstitutional and interdisciplinary work. The formation of academic cooperation networks expanded the thematic reach of studies and amplified their scientific impact, contributing to the diversification and internationalisation of the field.

Scientific production by country

Scientific production in Ethnoentomology is distributed across different continents, with the highest concentration in South America (153 publications), followed by North America (69), Asia (64), and Europe (59) (Fig. 5). This geographical distribution highlights the consolidation of the field across diverse academic contexts and underscores the importance of international cooperation for its advancement.

Publications by continent in Ethnoentomology (1952–2024)

The countries that have contributed most significantly to the development of Ethnoentomology are Brazil, Mexico, the United States, India, and Spain. Brazil stands out clearly as the leader, with 127 published articles, followed by Mexico (32), the United States (32), India (29), and Spain (15). These figures reflect the leading role of these nations in building and disseminating ethnoentomological knowledge, particularly concerning cultural, ecological, and dietary interactions between humans and insects.

In Brazil, academic production spans multiple thematic areas. Studies on Ethnobiology and traditional knowledge predominate (28 articles), followed by research on environmental perception and education (22), insect diversity and conservation (20), and cultural and traditional practices (18). Research on the alimentary uses of insects (15 articles) and public health issues (12) also stand out, addressing both medicinal uses and the role of species as disease vectors. The country’s biological richness and the deep knowledge held by local and Indigenous communities are central factors in the consolidation of Ethnoentomology. Understanding these knowledge systems is essential not only for cultural appreciation but also for developing sustainable management strategies, thereby reinforcing the role of Ethnoentomology in bridging traditional knowledge and contemporary conservation approaches [38].

In Mexico, research is mainly focused on traditional knowledge (10 studies) and alimentary uses of insects (8), highlighting the nutritional importance of various species. Other relevant themes include environmental education (6), cultural traditions (5), biodiversity conservation (2), and public health (1), with emphasis on studies addressing Chagas disease. The consolidation of Ethnobiology in the country, supported by the establishment of specialised academic institutions over the past 135 years, has contributed significantly to the advancement of Ethnoentomology as a scientific field [39].

In the United States, research emphasises Ethnobiology and traditional knowledge (7 studies), alongside investigations into cultural and artistic traditions involving insects (7), encompassing mythology, visual arts, and Indigenous knowledge. Alimentary uses appear in four studies, while public health and insect diversity/conservation are each addressed in three studies. This development is supported by a structured interdisciplinary research system that integrates Anthropology, Biology, and Environmental Sciences, backed by substantial scientific and institutional investments. Indigenous Peoples’ ecological knowledge has assumed a central role in shaping interdisciplinary research agendas on sustainability and environmental governance, reinforcing Ethnoentomology as an applied, integrative discipline [40].

India, with 29 publications, focuses mainly on the alimentary use of insects and their role in the subsistence of rural communities. Spain, with 15 studies, emphasises cultural traditions and biodiversity. Other European countries, such as France, Germany, England, and Italy, also contribute, albeit on a smaller scale.

By contrast, scientific production in Africa and Oceania remains limited, despite the vast reservoir of traditional insect knowledge in these regions. Structural challenges — such as limited financial resources, reduced institutional infrastructure, and insufficient integration between academic science and local knowledge — help explain this gap. Bibliometric studies show that, although collaboration between African and Western countries in entomological research is increasing, many projects still lack effective local participation, either in authorship or at the institutional level [41, 42]. Strengthening international research networks has been highlighted as a key strategy to improve connectivity and knowledge exchange, fostering the inclusion of local perspectives and enhancing the global relevance of Ethnoentomology [43].

In summary, the geographical distribution of scientific production in Ethnoentomology demonstrates that, while countries in the Global South concentrate much of the biocultural potential, actual scientific output still depends on institutional investment, research policies, and international collaborations. This scenario reinforces the strategic importance of integrating academic science and traditional knowledge as a foundation for conservation, sustainability, and innovation.

Leading sources

Among the main publication outlets, the Journal of Insects as Food and Feed stands out as a central journal for research in Ethnoentomology (Fig. 6). Studies published in this journal have addressed themes such as anthropo-entomophagy in Latin America [44], ethnoentomological knowledge in peri-urban areas of Mexico [45], and the use of ghost moths by Indigenous peoples [46]. More recent contributions have broadened the global debate on edible insects, examining food security perspectives [47], estimating insect consumption patterns on a global scale [48], and conducting taxonomic analyses of edible species present in rural Mexican diets [49]. Collectively, these studies illustrate how Ethnoentomology bridges traditional knowledge with contemporary challenges in sustainability and nutrition.

Leading publication sources in Ethnoentomology, 1952–2024

Equally relevant is the Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine (JCR ≈ 3.7), whose prominence illustrates the interdisciplinary nature of Ethnoentomology. Beyond food, this journal has served as an important platform for research on the medicinal and cultural dimensions of human–insect interactions. Recent publications have updated the diversity of species consumed as food and highlighted their nutritional value [50], explored the cultural dimensions of anthropo-entomophagy among Amazonian Indigenous peoples [51], and demonstrated the medicinal potential of insects in traditional practices [52], including ethnoveterinary applications [53]. These contributions exemplify how Ethnoentomology integrates ancestral knowledge with contemporary challenges, revealing the multiple ways in which humans interact with insects.

Acta Scientiarum – Biological Sciences (JCR 0.45) and Ethnobiology Letters (JCR 0.9) have also played important roles in consolidating the field by publishing studies that integrate biological diversity and cultural practices. Research published in Acta Scientiarum has documented local perceptions of beetles in Bahia, Brazil [54], and investigated university students’ understanding of insects in educational contexts [55]. Ethnobiology Letters, in turn, has emphasised interdisciplinary approaches in times of rapid environmental change [56] and reinforced the role of diverse conservation practices for sustainability [57]. Together, these journals highlight the value of combining ecological and ethnobiological perspectives in the study of human–insect relationships.

The relevance of Latin American research is further illustrated by the Annals of the Brazilian Academy of Sciences (JCR 1.5) and the Journal of Ethnobiology (JCR 2.2), which demonstrate the strong role of regional contributions to Ethnoentomology, particularly in tropical contexts. For example, studies with the Paiter Suruí have documented a complex ancestral diet that includes multiple insect species [58], while other research has emphasised the nutritional value of these organisms as a food resource, despite persistent cultural taboos [59]. These findings reinforce how Ethnoentomology links biodiversity and cultural traditions, underscoring its strategic importance for conservation and the sustainable management of resources.

Cited species and their uses

The insect species recorded in the analysed studies were classified into eight main categories of use: alimentary, medicinal, utilitarian, ritualistic, symbolic, ludic, offensive, and pests (Fig. 8).

Insect species by use category (1952–2024)

The alimentary category comprised the largest number of records (n = 649), highlighting the central role of insects as nutritional resources across different cultures. Examples include the larvae of Rhynchophorus palmarum (C. Linnaeus, 1758) (Coleoptera: Dryophthoridae), which are widely consumed by Indigenous peoples of the Amazon and South America, either raw or prepared in various ways, and are valued for their high energy content [60–62]. Tenebrio molitor Linnaeus, 1758 (Coleoptera: Tenebrionidae) is consumed in both larval and adult stages as an alternative protein source [47, 63, 64]. Workers and pupae of Oecophylla smaragdina (Fabricius, 1775) (Hymenoptera: Formicidae) are part of the diet of communities in Southeast Asia, where they are eaten raw or seasoned [65–67]. For Apis mellifera (Linnaeus, 1758) (Hymenoptera: Apidae), the alimentary records refer to the consumption of eggs, larvae, and pupae, which are rich in proteins and micronutrients, a practice observed in several regions [47, 62, 68–70].

The symbolic category (n = 239) encompasses a wide range of cultural and mythological representations. In European traditions, A. mellifera often symbolises labour, order, and communal virtues — a recurrent motif in religious and artistic iconography, such as in Florence [71]. In the South American Gran Chaco, S. cancellata features in Indigenous narratives (e.g. Mocoví myths) and Wichí textile repertoires, symbolising the ambivalence between fertility and devastation, and acting as a social and moral marker [72]. In Malay communities, popular beliefs associate the moth Lyssa zampa (Butler, 1869) (Lepidoptera: Uraniidae) with omens — from “bad luck” to the arrival of relatives or ancestral spirits — illustrating how the same taxon may hold different meanings across ethnic groups [73]. In sub-Saharan Africa, Cetoniinae beetles such as Diplognatha gagates (Forster, 1771) (Coleoptera: Scarabaeidae) appear in recent ethnographies as feared “night visitors”, sometimes associated with disease or misfortune and requiring ritual management; this symbolic role of scarab beetles has been discussed in comparative syntheses across the continent [74].

The utilitarian category included 233 species associated with the production of material goods such as silk, waxes, resins, dyes, glues, and other resources, highlighting the role of insects as providers of non-food and non-medicinal raw materials. A. mellifera stands out for the use of its wax and propolis in the manufacture of candles, waterproofing agents, utensils, musical instruments, paintings, sculptures, and various technological and artisanal applications [74–77]. Trigona spinipes (Fabricius, 1793) (Hymenoptera: Apidae), traditionally managed by Indigenous peoples in Brazil, provides cerumen for sealing utensils, constructing artefacts, and ornamental uses — practices documented among the Pankararu and Kayapó peoples [78–80]. Bombyx mori Linnaeus, 1758 (Lepidoptera: Bombycidae) represents a classic example of insect domestication for silk production, with great cultural and economic importance, particularly in Asia and Europe [71, 81]. In addition, Dactylopius coccus Costa, 1829 (Hemiptera: Dactylopiidae), the cochineal, is known for producing carminic acid, a natural dye widely used in the food, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical industries [47].

The ritualistic category (n = 176) brings together species associated with spiritual, religious, or ceremonial practices, highlighting the symbolic dimension of insects within cosmologies and traditional rituals. Examples include the grasshopper Schistocerca cancellata (Serville, 1838) (Orthoptera: Acrididae), used in fertility and protection rituals by communities in the South American Chaco [72]. Among groups in Papua New Guinea, such as the Onabasulu, larvae of Rhynchophorus bilineatus J. Faust, 1899 (Coleoptera: Dryophthoridae) are collected on a large scale and prepared during annual festivals, in which their collective consumption follows social and marital rules, playing a central role in community cohesion [82]. Among the Korowai, similar rituals connect the construction of ceremonial houses with the sago cycles and the ritual distribution of the “sago grubs” [83]. Among the Kaiowá, wasp species are used in initiation and learning rituals, where controlled stings transmit strength, endurance, and hunting skills to young men [84]. In Australian Aboriginal societies, native bee wax is traditionally used to mould ritual figures and perform magical practices [85]. Ethnographic records also show that African peoples attribute protective or offensive spiritual roles to swarms of A. mellifera, including ceremonial uses to “send” bees against enemies [74].

The pest category gathered 161 species recognised for their negative impacts on agriculture, public health, and domestic environments, demonstrating the multiple ways in which insects affect human activities. Endoxyla cinereus (Tepper, 1890) (Lepidoptera: Cossidae), known as the “giant wood moth”, is an emblematic example: although traditionally consumed by Australian Indigenous peoples, it is considered a forest pest due to the damage it causes to host trees [86]. S. cancellata, the South American locust, has historically been feared for its migratory outbreaks in the Gran Chaco, where it devastates crops and forage plants [72]. In Brazil, T. spinipes, despite being an important stingless bee for pollination, is frequently reported as a pest in passionfruit crops due to the damage it causes to flower buds [87]. Synanthropic species such as Musca domestica Linnaeus, 1758 (Diptera: Muscidae) and P. americana are associated with disease transmission in urban and rural environments [88, 89].

The medicinal category (n = 150) includes species widely used in the production of therapeutic substances, notably A. mellifera and Tetragonisca angustula (Latreille, 1811) (Hymenoptera: Apidae), whose products — honey and propolis — are employed to treat respiratory, dermatological, gastric, and rheumatic conditions, as well as the use of bee stings in popular healing practices [68, 75, 87, 90, 91]. Periplaneta americana (Linnaeus, 1758) (Blattodea: Blattidae) is traditionally used in Asian medical systems, particularly Traditional Chinese Medicine, being prepared as decoctions, powders, or extracts to treat gastrointestinal, respiratory, and inflammatory disorders [92–94]. The oil of R. palmarum is used as a cough remedy among Amazonian Indigenous peoples [62]. Formica rufa Linnaeus, 1761 (Hymenoptera: Formicidae) appears in Scandinavian ethnotherapeutic records, especially for the use of formic acid in folk medicine and in the production of medicinal beverages [95, 96].

The offensive category included 103 species recognised for their harmful or nuisance potential to humans, mainly haematophagous insects, disease vectors, or species with aggressive defensive behaviour. Among Dipterans, Aedes aegypti (Linnaeus, 1762) (Diptera: Culicidae) stands out as a vector of arboviruses such as dengue, Zika, and chikungunya, and is widely recognised by communities as dangerous and bothersome [88, 89]. Culicidae species in general were cited in different ethnoentomological contexts as causing discomfort, disease transmission, or sleep disturbances. In the case of A. mellifera, although highly valued for its products, its presence was reported as offensive due to its intense defensive behaviour — when disturbed, bees sting and release alarm pheromones that attract other workers, increasing the risk of multiple stings [87].

The ludic category (n = 93) highlights insects as elements of recreation, games, and traditional play in different cultures, revealing the close symbiotic relationship between humans and arthropods in childhood and community contexts. In Brazil, Orthopteran species such as Gryllus domesticus Linnaeus, 1758 (Orthoptera: Gryllidae) and various grasshoppers are used as toys: children tie strings to grasshoppers to pull them along the streets or keep crickets in containers to listen to their songs. These practices also appear in traditional songs and games, such as “Brincadeira do grilo” and “Lagarta pintada”, reinforcing cultural values and social development [92, 97, 98]. In Asian contexts, the aquatic beetle Cybister tripunctatus (Olivier, 1795) (Coleoptera: Dytiscidae) is traditionally used in roulette-type games in South Korea [99]. Among the Sateré-Mawé in the Amazon, the bullet ant Paraponera clavata (Fabricius, 1775) (Hymenoptera: Formicidae) features in dances and playful rituals that combine ludic and spiritual dimensions (Costa Neto, 2003). Similarly, in Upper Rio Negro communities, a wide diversity of insects — including ants, beetles, mantises, dragonflies, and cicadas — is incorporated into games and cultural activities, evidencing an ethnoentomological repertoire linked to playfulness [100].

In total, 1,043 species were recorded, distributed across 159 families and 20 taxonomic orders. Among these, Hymenoptera (254 species), Coleoptera (251), and Lepidoptera (182) were the most represented (Fig. 9), highlighting the strong association between taxonomic diversity and the variety of uses identified. A detailed list of species and their respective categories of use is provided in Additional file 1. These findings reinforce the multifunctional role of insects in human cultures.

This study presents some limitations that should be taken into consideration. Although the inclusion of indexed databases and Google Scholar broadened the scope of the search, part of the relevant literature — such as books, theses, and non-indexed regional publications — may not have been fully captured. In addition, the temporal delimitation up to 2024, while providing an updated overview, should be periodically revised to incorporate new contributions. Regarding the linguistic scope, the inclusion of Portuguese, English, and Spanish covered the main languages used in Ethnoentomological research and ensured wide coverage of scientific production. However, studies published in other languages may still be under-represented. Despite these limitations, the analysis provides a comprehensive and representative overview of the scientific landscape of Ethnoentomology.

Conclusions

By exploring the complex interactions between humans and insects from cultural, ecological, and social perspectives, Ethnoentomology establishes itself as a key discipline for understanding traditional practices and their applications in contemporary sustainability contexts.

This bibliometric analysis not only mapped the evolution of scientific production on the subject but also highlighted the continuous growth of the field and its consolidation as an interdisciplinary area. The findings reveal the increasing recognition of traditional knowledge and its integration with contemporary scientific approaches, particularly in areas such as food security, environmental conservation, and public health.

A significant concentration of publications originates from countries with high biodiversity, such as Brazil, highlighting the central role of local knowledge in building innovative solutions to global environmental and food challenges. The centrality of key authors and journals, as well as the mapping of collaborative research networks, demonstrates the structural development of the field. The integration of Anthropology, Biology, and Environmental Sciences illustrates the potential of Ethnoentomology to generate applied knowledge and foster dialogue between modern science and ancestral wisdom.

Another relevant finding is the remarkable diversity of recorded species (over 1,000, distributed across 20 orders and 159 families), associated with multiple uses — mainly alimentary, medicinal, and utilitarian. Species such as A. melífera, R. palmarum and P. americana, stand out for their versatility, illustrating the multifunctional role of insects in human cultures — ecological, economic, and symbolic.

Despite these advances, the study revealed important gaps, especially in regions with vast traditional knowledge but low representation in indexed scientific production. This underscores the need to expand collaborative research networks, strengthen science policies, and promote the effective integration of local knowledge into academic and institutional contexts.

Building on this foundation, future research can further enrich the field by expanding the analytical scope to include disciplines and thematic areas which, although not explicitly using the term “ethnoentomology”, address human–insect interactions from complementary perspectives. Fields such as medical entomology, agricultural sciences, pollination ecology, and anthropology have historically generated rich bodies of knowledge that intersect with ethnoentomological themes. Incorporating these perspectives into future bibliometric studies can strengthen comparative analyses, highlight interdisciplinary connections, and contextualise the development of Ethnoentomology within broader scientific landscapes — without deviating from this study’s focus on the consolidation of Ethnoentomology as a distinct discipline.

Therefore, the results presented here contribute to a better understanding of the current scientific landscape of Ethnoentomology and provide strategic insights to guide future research. Strengthening the field requires expanding interdisciplinary studies, fostering community engagement in the scientific process, and recognising the intrinsic value of traditional knowledge in building sustainable solutions.

Ultimately, this study reinforces the scientific and societal relevance of Ethnoentomology as a discipline capable of bridging traditional knowledge and modern science, positioning it as an essential field for addressing global socio-environmental challenges and advancing the construction of sustainable societies.
