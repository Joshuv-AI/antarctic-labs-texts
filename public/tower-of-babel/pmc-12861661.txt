In medicine, good things don’t come to those who wait
Authors: Andrew Duncombe
Published: 2025

Welcome to this issue of Future Healthcare Journal, where we highlight common illnesses affecting millions in the UK alone with chronic distressing symptoms, largely without a proven pathophysiological basis. A variety of phrases have been used to describe these, including medically unexplained symptoms, persistent physical symptoms and functional disorders, with symptom-based disorders now being the preferred term. The spectrum includes fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome/ME, post-COVID and irritable bowel syndrome, and many disorders overlap all medical specialties. Most of us will have some family members or friends suffering from one of these. I am delighted to welcome Theresa Barnes as our guest editor for this themed issue and I commend her editorial, where she signposts and discusses the expert opinion papers that we have commissioned on this crucial topic.

Reading the patient perspectives in the issue and listening to friends’ testimonies, I was struck by how much waiting is a key frustration of these patients’ lives: waiting for a diagnosis, waiting for explanations, waiting for recognition, waiting for support and treatment, and waiting in hope for recovery. Apart from the human cost of the suffering to patients and their families, most of those affected are of working age, so the economic cost of lost productivity is substantial. Given all this, it is perhaps most surprising that there has been so little high-quality research in this area, nor the funding to facilitate this. We hope you gain new insights from reading these articles in this issue.

On the waiting theme, in the UK, we still have the shocking statistic that >7 million people are waiting for elective treatment on the NHS. So we also publish an important paper from healthcare provision modelling experts,1 describing the relationship between numbers on waiting lists and times to first consultation. They show that to achieve the NHS statutory target of 92% of patients (currently around 60%) waiting <18 weeks for first elective treatment, the current waiting list will need to halve. The government has set a goal of achieving this target within the lifetime of this parliament, which means that the waiting list will need to fall by approximately 1 million patients per year, representing a major challenge for health services. Furthermore, the authors show that there are large differences in the size of the task, both by health regions and by specialty. The Midlands requires the greatest absolute reduction over this period (764,000), which they suggest will be a particular challenge owing to current workforce shortages with this region having the highest vacancy rates in acute medical staff, at 6.3% at end 2024. By specialty, the largest absolute reduction required is for trauma and orthopaedics, which is estimated to require a 519,000 reduction to achieve the target. It is sobering to remind ourselves that behind every one of these vast numbers is a patient in need, and it is surprising to me that there is not more public anger about the current situation. The authors suggest that, in the short term, extra priority could be given to those specialties where waiting appears to result in greater patient harm.

Continuing with the waiting theme, we know that early diagnosis is crucial in obtaining better outcomes in a range of cancers. This is particularly true in lung cancer, which is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, responsible for nearly 2 million deaths annually. A team from Greater Manchester2 have created a one-stop lung cancer clinic providing a multidisciplinary service of thoracic surgeons, anaesthetists, clinical oncologists, physicians, specialist nurses alongside prehabilitation, tobacco dependency and frailty specialists, to support treatment decisions in a single clinic visit. This one-stop model of care has delivered significant pathway improvements with fewer clinic visits and increased the uptake of optimisation interventions, leading to significantly reduced 12-month mortality of 3.5% from 12.8%. If replicated across an entire healthcare system, large numbers of patients could benefit from longer survival. We see so many examples in FHJ of such clear evidence-based local transformational change that I find it exasperating that we are still so poor at rolling out such improved care models to the wider service for the benefit of all.

At FHJ, we have a longstanding interest in the debate about generalism versus specialism in medicine. In ‘Jack of all trades’,3 we feature a personal view of a trainee experience of the general internal medicine higher specialist training programme in the UK. The authors argue that this is a surprising gap in service provision since we have long accepted the importance of generalists in both paediatrics and geriatrics but have neglected those aged between 18 and 80, despite them being the majority of the population. They argue that this has become more urgent due to the rise of multimorbidity in a less elderly age group. In a challenge to the titular aphorism, the authors argue why can they not be considered master of many rather than none.

Recommended vaccinations during pregnancy are safe and can prevent serious illness in both pregnant women and newborns. Despite this, uptake remains low in England, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged groups and some ethnic minority communities. In England, the NHS recommends vaccinations during pregnancy to protect the mother and the infant against pertussis (whooping cough), influenza and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). Razai et al4 explored the factors that underly low uptake in two major cities in England. They found considerable variations in practice in both primary and secondary care, with only isolated pockets of good quality care. They identified lack of digital record continuity and administrative burden, insufficient training of midwives and lack of knowledge in pregnant women as key hindrances. They suggest that system-level improvements such as integrated data access, protected training time for midwives, clear documentation protocols, and tailored outreach are needed to strengthen maternal vaccination services.

Following on from our March 2025 issue detailing the climate health emergency, Thomas et al5 highlight an underrecognised and worsening issue of light pollution especially at night – the world is getting brighter at night by around 10% per year. Such artificial light at night (ALAN) is not only wasted energy, but there is increasing evidence of adverse effects on human health such that we should regard ALAN as a pollutant. ALAN, even at low intensities, interferes with normal circadian patterns of melatonin production, which interacts with immune system functions. Some studies have even shown correlation of ALAN with incidence of certain cancers, particularly breast and prostate. Although no causal connection has yet been found, further research seems warranted. Concern is not just related to the intensity of this artificial light, but also its wavelength predominance at the more harmful blue end of the spectrum due to the preferential use of LED, an issue in common with the night-time use of digital screens including smartphones. Interestingly, the principal driver of increasing use of outdoor ALAN appears to be public safety, but there is no convincing evidence that its use has reduced criminality at night. The authors argue cogently for more research in this neglected area and succinctly suggest that we should shine the light only when and where it is needed.
