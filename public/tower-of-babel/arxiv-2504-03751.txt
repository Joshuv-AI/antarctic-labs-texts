Revolutionizing Fractional Calculus with Neural Networks: Voronovskaya-Damasclin Theory for Next-Generation AI Systems
Rômulo Damasclin Chaves dos Santos, Jorge Henrique de Oliveira Sales

Abstract
This work introduces rigorous convergence rates for neural network operators activated by symmetrized and perturbed hyperbolic tangent functions, utilizing novel Voronovskaya-Damasclin asymptotic expansions. We analyze basic, Kantorovich, and quadrature-type operators over infinite domains, extending classical approximation theory to fractional calculus via Caputo derivatives. Key innovations include parameterized activation functions with asymmetry control, symmetrized density operators, and fractional Taylor expansions for error analysis. The main theorem demonstrates that Kantorovich operators achieve o ⁡ ( n − β ⁡ ( N − ε ) ) o(n^{-\beta(N-\varepsilon)}) convergence rates, while basic operators exhibit 𝒪 ⁡ ( n − β ​ N ) \mathcal{O}(n^{-\beta N}) error decay. For deep networks, we prove 𝒪 ⁡ ( L − β ⁡ ( N − ε ) ) \mathcal{O}(L^{-\beta(N-\varepsilon)}) approximation bounds. Stability results under parameter perturbations highlight operator robustness. By integrating neural approximation theory with fractional calculus, this work provides foundational mathematical insights and deployable engineering solutions, with potential applications in complex system modeling and signal processing. Keywords: Voronovskaya expansions, Symmetrized neural networks, Fractional calculus, Kantorovich operators.

Mathematical Foundations of Symmetrized Activation Functions

Definition 2.1

(Perturbed Hyperbolic Tangent)
.

The
perturbed hyperbolic tangent
activation function is defined as:

g_{q,\lambda}(x):=\frac{e^{\lambda x}-qe^{-\lambda x}}{e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x}},\quad\lambda,q>0,\;x\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

This function generalizes the standard hyperbolic tangent function, which is recovered when
q=1
. The parameter
\lambda
controls the steepness of the function, while
q
introduces asymmetry. The behavior of
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is crucial in neural network approximation as it allows for controlled deformations, enhancing the flexibility of the activation function in modeling complex patterns.

Lemma 2.1

(Positivity and Normalization of Density Function)
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
q,\lambda>0
, the density function
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
satisfies:

M_{q,\lambda}(x)>0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}M_{q,\lambda}(x)\,dx=1.

(3)

This lemma ensures that
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
is a valid probability density function, which is essential for the construction of neural network operators that approximate continuous functions.

Proof.

The density function
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
is defined as:

M_{q,\lambda}(x):=\frac{1}{4}\left[g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\right].

(4)

Since
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is strictly increasing, we have:

g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)>g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(5)

Therefore,

M_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{4}\left[g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\right]>0.

(6)

To show that the integral of
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
over the entire domain is 1, consider:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}M_{q,\lambda}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\right]\,dx.

(7)

Splitting the integral into two parts, we get:

\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)\,dx-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\,dx\right).

(8)

Using the substitution
u=x+1
in the first integral and
v=x-1
in the second integral, we have:

\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{q,\lambda}(u)\,du-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{q,\lambda}(v)\,dv\right).

(9)

Since
u
and
v
are dummy variables, the integrals are equivalent and cancel each other out:

\frac{1}{4}\left(\lim_{x\to\infty}g_{q,\lambda}(x)-\lim_{x\to-\infty}g_{q,\lambda}(x)\right).

(10)

Given that
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
is a strictly increasing function that tends to 1 as
x\to\infty
and to -1 as
x\to-\infty
:

\frac{1}{4}[1-(-1)]=\frac{1}{4}\times 2=1.

(11)

Thus, the integral of
M_{q,\lambda}(x)
over the entire domain is 1, concluding the proof. This normalization property is fundamental for ensuring that the density function can be used in probabilistic interpretations within neural network frameworks.
∎

3
Main Results

3.1
Basic Operators

Theorem 3.1

(Approximation by Operators)
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
(bounded and continuous), and
0<\varepsilon\leq N
. Then:

1.

The following approximation holds:

B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right),

(12)

where
B_{n}
is a linear operator and
B_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})
denotes the operator applied to monomials shifted by
x
.

2.

If
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
, then:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,\quad 0<\varepsilon\leq N.

(13)

Proof.

To prove the theorem, we use Taylor’s theorem to expand
f
around
x
and analyze the asymptotic properties of the operators
B_{n}
. Let
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
, and expand
f
around
x
as:

f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right),

(14)

where the remainder term
R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)
is given by:

R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\int_{x}^{\frac{k}{n}}\frac{\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\left[f^{(N)}(t)-f^{(N)}(x)\right]\,dt.

(15)

We substitute this expansion into the definition of the operator
B_{n}
:

B_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\Phi(nx-k),

(16)

where
\Phi
is a weight function with appropriate support. Substituting, we get:

B_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\Phi(nx-k).

(17)

Define the error term:

R:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}R_{N}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\Phi(nx-k).

(18)

To estimate
R
, we consider two cases:

Case 1:
\left|\frac{k}{n}-x\right|<\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}
.

Within this interval,
\Phi(nx-k)
has significant support, and the regularity of
f^{(N)}
implies:

|R|\leq 2\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{1}{N!n^{\beta N}}.

(19)

Case 2:
\left|\frac{k}{n}-x\right|\geq\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}
.

Outside the principal support,
\Phi(nx-k)
decays exponentially, so:

|R|\leq\frac{4\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{n^{N}\lambda^{N}}\left(q+\frac{1}{q}\right)e^{2\lambda}e^{-\lambda\left(n^{1-\beta}-1\right)}.

(20)

Combining both cases, we obtain:

|R|=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(21)

Substituting
R
back into the expansion, we prove the approximation:

B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(22)

Finally, when
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\ldots,N
, we have:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0,

(23)

as indicated, completing the proof.
∎

3.2
Kantorovich Operators

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
, and
0<\varepsilon\leq N
. Then:

1.

C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+o\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N-\varepsilon}\right).

(24)

2.

When
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\ldots,N
, we have:

\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N-\varepsilon}}\left[C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,\quad 0<\varepsilon\leq N.

(25)

Proof.

We start by expressing
C_{n}(f,x)
as:

C_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\Phi(nx-k).

(26)

Given
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
, we can use the Taylor expansion of
f
around
x
:

f\left(t+\dfrac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\dfrac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(t+\dfrac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+\int_{x}^{t+\dfrac{k}{n}}\left(f^{(N)}(s)-f^{(N)}(x)\right)\dfrac{\left(t+\dfrac{k}{n}-s\right)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\,ds.

(27)

Substituting this expansion into the expression for
C_{n}(f,x)
, we get:

\displaystyle C_{n}(f,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\dfrac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+

(28)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(nx-k)\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\int_{x}^{t+\frac{k}{n}}\left(f^{(N)}(s)-f^{(N)}(x)\right)\frac{\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-s\right)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\,ds\right)\,dt\right).

Define the remainder term
R
as:

R:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(nx-k)\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\int_{x}^{t+\frac{k}{n}}\left(f^{(N)}(s)-f^{(N)}(x)\right)\frac{\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-s\right)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}\,ds\right)\,dt\right).

(29)

We now analyze the magnitude of
R
in two cases:

Case 1:
\left|\frac{k}{n}-x\right|<\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}

In this case, the distance between
\frac{k}{n}
and
x
is small. We can bound
R
as follows:

|R|\leq 2\left\|f^{(N)}\right\|_{\infty}\dfrac{\left(\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N}}{N!}.

(30)

Case 2:
\left|\frac{k}{n}-x\right|\geq\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}

In this case, the distance
\left|\frac{k}{n}-x\right|
is larger, and we exploit the decay properties of the function
\Phi
to estimate
R
. More precisely, we use the exponential behavior of the associated decay function, which allows us to impose an upper bound on
|R|
:

|R|\leq\dfrac{2^{N}\left\|f^{(N)}\right\|_{\infty}}{n^{N}N!}\left[\dfrac{T}{e^{2\lambda n^{1-\beta}}}+\dfrac{\left(q+\dfrac{1}{q}\right)}{\lambda^{N}}2e^{2\lambda}N!e^{-\lambda\left(n^{1-\beta}-1\right)}\right].

(31)

To understand this bound, consider the asymptotic expansion of
f(x)
in a Taylor series around
k/n
:

f(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(m)}(k/n)}{m!}(x-k/n)^{m}+R_{N},

(32)

where the remainder term
R_{N}
satisfies:

R_{N}=\frac{f^{(N)}(\xi)}{N!}(x-k/n)^{N},\quad\text{for some }\xi\in(k/n,x).

(33)

Given that
|x-k/n|\geq 1/n^{\beta}
, we have:

|R_{N}|\leq\frac{\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{N!}\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N}.

(34)

Moreover, the decay properties of
\Phi
introduce an additional exponential suppression term, leading to the refined bound:

|R|\leq\dfrac{2^{N}\left\|f^{(N)}\right\|_{\infty}}{n^{N}N!}\left[\dfrac{T}{e^{2\lambda n^{1-\beta}}}+\dfrac{\left(q+\dfrac{1}{q}\right)}{\lambda^{N}}2e^{2\lambda}N!e^{-\lambda\left(n^{1-\beta}-1\right)}\right].

(35)

This ensures that the remainder term exhibits exponential decay in addition to polynomial suppression.

Now, combining the two cases discussed in the proof, we obtain the following uniform estimate for
|R|
:

|R|\leq\dfrac{4\left\|f^{(N)}\right\|_{\infty}}{N!}\left(\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N}.

(36)

To obtain the final asymptotic order of the remainder term, we apply a refined estimate that considers an arbitrary parameter
\varepsilon>0
, ensuring that

|R|=o\left(\left(\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{n^{\beta}}\right)^{N-\varepsilon}\right),

(37)

which completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

3.3
Convergence of Operators in Deep Learning

Theorem 3.3

(Convergence of Operators in Deep Learning)
.

Let
f
be a continuous and bounded function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{N}
a deep neural network with
L
layers, where each layer uses the activation function
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
. If
\lambda
and
q
are chosen to optimize convergence, then the output of the network
\mathcal{N}(f,x)
approximates
f(x)
with an error bound of:

\|\mathcal{N}(f,x)-f(x)\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{L^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(38)

Proof.

Consider a deep neural network
\mathcal{N}
with
L
layers. Each layer
l
applies a transformation followed by the activation function
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
. The output of layer
l
can be expressed as:

\mathbf{z}^{(l)}=g_{q,\lambda}(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{z}^{(l-1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)}),

(39)

where
\mathbf{W}^{(l)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{(l)}
are the weights and biases of layer
l
, respectively.

To analyze the error propagation through the layers, we use the Taylor expansion for the activation function
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
around a point
x_{0}
:

g_{q,\lambda}(x)=g_{q,\lambda}(x_{0})+g_{q,\lambda}^{\prime}(x_{0})(x-x_{0})+\frac{g_{q,\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)}{2}(x-x_{0})^{2},

(40)

where
\xi
is between
x
and
x_{0}
.

The error in each layer can be expressed in terms of this Taylor expansion. Let’s denote the error at layer
l
as
e^{(l)}
. We have:

e^{(l)}=\mathbf{z}^{(l)}-\mathbf{z}^{(l-1)}=g_{q,\lambda}(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{z}^{(l-1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)})-g_{q,\lambda}(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{z}^{(l-2)}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)}).

(41)

Using the Lipschitz property of the activation function
g_{q,\lambda}
, we can bound the error propagation:

\|e^{(l)}\|\leq K\|\mathbf{W}^{(l)}e^{(l-1)}\|,

(42)

where
K
is the Lipschitz constant of
g_{q,\lambda}
.

For a network with
L
layers, the total error is a combination of the errors from each layer. We can express this as:

\|\mathcal{N}(f,x)-f(x)\|\leq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|e^{(l)}\|.

(43)

Given that each layer’s error decreases as
\frac{1}{l^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}
, we have:

\|e^{(l)}\|\leq\frac{C}{l^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}},

(44)

where
C
is a constant that depends on the network parameters.

Therefore, the total error is bounded by:

\|\mathcal{N}(f,x)-f(x)\|\leq C\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{l^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}.

(45)

As the number of layers
L
increases, the sum
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{l^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}
converges to a finite constant. Therefore, the error decreases according to the rate:

\|\mathcal{N}(f,x)-f(x)\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{L^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right),

(46)

proving the theorem.
∎

Fractional Perturbation Stability

Theorem 4.1

(Stability Under Fractional Perturbations)
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
, and
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
be the perturbed hyperbolic tangent activation function defined by:

g_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{e^{\lambda x}-qe^{-\lambda x}}{e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x}},\quad\lambda,q>0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(47)

For any small perturbation
|q-1|<\delta
, the operator
C_{n}
satisfies the stability estimate:

\|C_{n}(f,x;q)-C_{n}(f,x;1)\|\leq\frac{\delta}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty},

(48)

where
0<\varepsilon\leq N
and
\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|f^{(N)}(x)|
.

Proof.

To begin, consider
\Phi_{q,\lambda}(z)
as the density function derived from the perturbed activation function
g_{q,\lambda}(x)
. The operator
C_{n}
is defined as:

C_{n}(f,x;q)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\Phi_{q,\lambda}(nx-k).

(49)

Next, we expand
\Phi_{q,\lambda}(z)
around
q=1
using the first-order Taylor expansion:

\Phi_{q,\lambda}(z)=\Phi_{1,\lambda}(z)+\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\bigg|_{q=1}\right)(q-1)+\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2}).

(50)

Thus, the perturbed operator can be written as:

C_{n}(f,x;q)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\left[\Phi_{1,\lambda}(nx-k)+\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\bigg|_{q=1}\right)(q-1)+\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2})\right].

(51)

The difference between
C_{n}(f,x;q)
and
C_{n}(f,x;1)
is given by:

C_{n}(f,x;q)-C_{n}(f,x;1)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\left[\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\bigg|_{q=1}\right)(q-1)+\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2})\right].

(52)

Let us focus on the first-order perturbation term. The remainder term involving
\mathcal{O}((q-1)^{2})
contributes at a higher order in
(q-1)
, which is negligible for small
|q-1|
. Therefore, we estimate the perturbation as:

\|C_{n}(f,x;q)-C_{n}(f,x;1)\|\leq\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\,dt\right)\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\bigg|_{q=1}\right|(q-1).

(53)

Assuming
\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{q,\lambda}}{\partial q}\right|
is bounded, we have:

\|C_{n}(f,x;q)-C_{n}(f,x;1)\|\leq\frac{\delta}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty},

(54)

where
\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|f^{(N)}(x)|
represents the supremum norm of the
N
-th derivative of
f
.

Thus, we have established the desired stability estimate.
∎

Generalized Voronovskaya Expansions

Theorem 5.1

(Generalized Voronovskaya Expansion)
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
N=\lceil\alpha\rceil
,
\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}
,
f\in AC^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
0<\beta<1
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large. Assume that
\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,[x,\infty)}
and
\|D_{x-}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,(-\infty,x]}
are finite. Then:

B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})(x)+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(55)

When
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\dots,N
:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(56)

Proof.

Using the Caputo fractional Taylor expansion for
f
:

f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\frac{k}{n}}\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(t)-D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right)dt.

(57)

Substitute this expansion into the definition of the operator
B_{n}
:

B_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\Phi(nx-k),

(58)

where
\Phi(x)
is a density kernel function. Substituting
f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)
, we separate the terms into two contributions:

Main Contribution:

The first
N
terms of the Taylor expansion yield:

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})(x),

(59)

which captures the local behavior of
f
in terms of its derivatives up to order
N
.

Error Term:

The remainder term involves the fractional derivative
D_{*x}^{\alpha}
and can be bounded as:

R=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(nx-k)\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\frac{k}{n}}\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(t)-D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right)dt.

(60)

Bounding the Remainder:

•

For
|k/n-x|<1/n^{\beta}
: The kernel
\Phi(nx-k)
has significant support, and the fractional regularity of
f
ensures:

|R|\leq\frac{\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty}}{n^{\alpha\beta}}.

(61)

•

For
|k/n-x|\geq 1/n^{\beta}
: The exponential decay of
\Phi(nx-k)
ensures that contributions from distant terms are negligible:

|R|\leq\frac{\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty}}{n^{\alpha\beta}}.

(62)

Combining both cases, the error term satisfies:

|R|=o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(63)

Conclusion:

Substituting the bounds for the main contribution and error term into the expansion for
B_{n}(f,x)
, we conclude:

B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}B_{n}((\cdot-x)^{j})(x)+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right).

(64)

Moreover, when
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\dots,N
:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[B_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(65)

This completes the proof.
∎

6
Symmetrized Density Approach to Kantorovich Operator Convergence in Infinite Domains

Theorem 6.1

(Convergence Under Generalized Density)
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\Phi(x)
be a symmetrized density function defined by:

\Phi(x)=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(x)+M_{1/q,\lambda}(x)}{2},\quad M_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{4}\left(g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\right),

(66)

where
g_{q,\lambda}
satisfies
|g_{q,\lambda}(x)|\leq Ce^{-\gamma|x|}
for constants
C,\gamma>0
. Then the Kantorovich operator
C_{n}
satisfies:

C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(n^{-\beta}\right)^{N}\right).

(67)

Moreover, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the remainder can be refined to:

C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+o\left(n^{-(N-\varepsilon)}\right).

(68)

Proof.

By definition of the Kantorovich operator:

C_{n}(f,x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)dt\right)\Phi(nx-k).

(69)

Expand
f
using Taylor’s theorem around
x
up to order
N-1
:

f\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+R_{N}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right),

(70)

where the remainder
R_{N}
satisfies:

\left|R_{N}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{N!}\left|t+\frac{k}{n}-x\right|^{N}.

(71)

Substituting into
C_{n}(f,x)
:

\displaystyle C_{n}(f,x)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}dt\,\Phi(nx-k)

(72)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}R_{N}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)dt\,\Phi(nx-k).

The
j=0
term recovers
f(x)
due to
\sum_{k}\Phi(nx-k)=1
. Thus:

C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)+\mathcal{R}_{N},

(73)

where
\mathcal{R}_{N}
is the integrated remainder term.

Decay of
\Phi
:
By the exponential decay of
g_{q,\lambda}
, there exist
C^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>0
such that:

\Phi(nx-k)\leq C^{\prime}e^{-\gamma^{\prime}|nx-k|}.

(74)

Case 1:
|k/n-x|<n^{-\beta}
.
Let
\delta=t+\frac{k}{n}-x
. Since
|t|\leq 1/n
, we have
|\delta|\leq n^{-\beta}+n^{-1}
. Bounding
\Phi(nx-k)
by 1:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{N}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{N!}\sum_{|k/n-x|<n^{-\beta}}n\int_{0}^{1/n}\left(n^{-\beta}+n^{-1}\right)^{N}dt

(75)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{N!}\left(2n^{-\beta}\right)^{N}\cdot 2n^{1-\beta}\cdot n^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\beta N}\right).

Case 2:
|k/n-x|\geq n^{-\beta}
.
Using exponential decay of
\Phi
:

|\mathcal{R}_{N}|\leq\frac{\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{N!}\sum_{|k/n-x|\geq n^{-\beta}}n\int_{0}^{1/n}\left(1+|k/n-x|\right)^{N}dt\cdot Ce^{-\gamma|nx-k|}\leq C^{\prime\prime}e^{-\gamma n^{1-\beta}}.

(76)

Combining both cases, the total remainder satisfies:

\mathcal{R}_{N}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\beta N}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\gamma n^{1-\beta}}\right)=o\left(n^{-(N-\varepsilon)}\right)\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

(77)

The refined expansion including the
j=N
term follows from moment estimates on
C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{N}\right)(x)
, which decay as
n\to\infty
due to the operator’s regularization properties.
∎

7
Vonorovskaya-Damasclin Theorem

Theorem 7.1

(Vonorovskaya-Damasclin Theorem)
.

Let
0<\beta<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
f^{(N)}\in C_{B}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\Phi(x)
be a symmetrized density function defined as:

\Phi(x)=\frac{M_{q,\lambda}(x)+M_{1/q,\lambda}(x)}{2},\quad M_{q,\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{4}\left(g_{q,\lambda}(x+1)-g_{q,\lambda}(x-1)\right),

(78)

where
g_{q,\lambda}(x)=\dfrac{e^{\lambda x}-qe^{-\lambda x}}{e^{\lambda x}+qe^{-\lambda x}}
is the perturbed hyperbolic tangent function with
\lambda,q>0
. Assume the Caputo derivatives satisfy:

\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,[x,\infty)}+\|D_{x-}^{\alpha}f\|_{\infty,(-\infty,x]}<\infty\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha>0,

(79)

where
N=\lceil\alpha\rceil
. Then the Kantorovich operator
C_{n}
satisfies:

\displaystyle C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\dfrac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)

(80)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\infty}\left(D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(t)-D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right)\frac{(t-x)^{\alpha-1}}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\,dt

\displaystyle+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}}\right),

where
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrarily small. Furthermore, if
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
j=1,\dots,N
, then:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\left[C_{n}(f,x)-f(x)\right]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(81)

Proof.

For
f\in C^{N}(\mathbb{R})
, we use the Caputo fractional Taylor expansion around
x
:

f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\left(\frac{k}{n}-x\right)^{j}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\frac{k}{n}}\left(\frac{k}{n}-t\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(t)-D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right)dt.

(82)

The remainder term is controlled by the Hölder continuity of
D_{*x}^{\alpha}f
, implied by the boundedness assumption.

Substitute into
C_{n}(f,x)
:

C_{n}(f,x)=\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}C_{n}\left((\cdot-x)^{j}\right)(x)}_{\text{Main Terms}}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}n\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}R_{N}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)dt\,\Phi(nx-k)}_{\text{Remainder }\mathcal{R}_{N}}.

(83)

The
j=0
term equals
f(x)
due to
\sum_{k}\Phi(nx-k)=1
.

Split
\mathcal{R}_{N}
into near and far terms relative to
x
:

Case 1:
|k/n-x|<n^{-\beta}
.
Using the Hölder condition for
D_{*x}^{\alpha}f
:

\displaystyle\left|R_{N}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\left\|D_{*x}^{\alpha}f-D_{*x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right\|_{\infty}\int_{x}^{t+k/n}\left(t+\frac{k}{n}-u\right)^{\alpha-1}du

(84)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\left(n^{-\beta}+n^{-1}\right)^{\alpha}.

Summing over
k
in this region:

|\mathcal{R}_{N}|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\left(n^{-\beta}\right)^{\alpha-\varepsilon}.

(85)

Case 2:
|k/n-x|\geq n^{-\beta}
.
Using exponential decay of
\Phi
:

\displaystyle\Phi(nx-k)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-\gamma|nx-k|}\leq Ce^{-\gamma n^{1-\beta}}

(86)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{N}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f^{(N)}\|_{\infty}}{N!}\sum_{|k/n-x|\geq n^{-\beta}}n\int_{0}^{1/n}\left(1+|k/n-x|\right)^{N}dt\cdot Ce^{-\gamma n^{1-\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime}e^{-\gamma n^{1-\beta}}.

Combine both cases:

\mathcal{R}_{N}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\beta(\alpha-\varepsilon)}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\gamma n^{1-\beta}}\right)=o\left(n^{-\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\right).

(87)

When
f^{(j)}(x)=0
for
1\leq j\leq N
, the polynomial terms vanish, leaving only the fractional remainder and exponential decay terms.

Multiplying by
n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}
:

n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\mathcal{R}_{N}\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime}n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}\cdot n^{-\beta(N-\varepsilon)}+C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}n^{\beta(N-\varepsilon)}e^{-\gamma n^{1-\beta}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(88)

This completes the proof.
∎
