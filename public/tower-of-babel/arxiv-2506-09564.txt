Slowly oscillating periodic solutions in a nonlinear Volterra equation with non-symmetric feedback
Quentin Griette, Franco Herrera

Abstract
In this work we study a nonlinear Volterra equation with non-symmetric feedback that arises as a particular case of the Gurtin-MacCamy model in population dynamics. We are particularly interested in the existence of slowly oscillating periodic solutions when the trivial stationary state is unstable. Here the absence of symmetry of the nonlinearity prevents the use of many traditional strategies to obtain a priori estimates on the solution. Without a precise knowledge of the period of the solution, we manage to prove the forward invariance of a carefully constructed set of initial data whose properties imply the slowly oscillating character of all continuations. We prove the existence of periodic solutions by constructing a homeomorphism between our set and a convex subset of a different Banach space, thereby showing that it possesses the fixed-point property. Finally, in a singular limit of a parameter, we show that this periodic solution converges to the solution of a well-known discrete difference equation. We conclude the paper with some numerical simulations to illustrate the existence of the periodic orbit as well as the singular limit behavior.

Introduction

In this work we are interested in the existence of slowly oscillating solutions for the Volterra-type integral equation

b(t)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{1-\varepsilon/2}^{1+\varepsilon/2}f\big(b(t-s)\big)\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}f\big(b(s)\big)\mathrm{d}s,

(1.1)

where
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
is a scaling parameter and
f
is a negative feedback nonlinearity, i.e. a continuous function satisfying
xf(x)<0
(see Figure
1
below).

This equation arises naturally as a particular case of the Gurtin-MacCamy equation

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}+\partial_{a})u(t,a)=-\mu u(t,a),&t>0,~a>0\\
u(t,0)=f\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma(a)u(t,a)\mathrm{d}a\right),&t>0,\quad u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}\in L^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\end{cases}

(1.2)

that describes a theoretical population structured by chronological age. Equation (
1.2
) has been proposed by Gurtin and MacCamy
[
8
]
and has been extensively studied since then, see e.g. Webb
[
33
]
, Thieme
[
31
]
, Inaba
[
10
]
, Magal and Ruan
[
17
]
, and the references therein. Recently, Ma and Magal
[
16
]
studied the global asymptotic stability of the unique positive stationary solution of the model (
1.2
) with a Ricker’s-type nonlinearity
f(u)=\alpha ue^{-u}
. They showed that the global asymptotic stability holds whenever
1<\alpha<e^{2}
(the stationary solution does not exists for
\alpha<1
) and that a Hopf bifurcation may occur at
\alpha=\alpha^{\star}>e^{2}
depending on the function
\gamma
; and for each
\alpha^{\star}>e^{2}
they provide a
\gamma
for which a Hopf bifurcation effectively occurs at
\alpha=\alpha^{\star}
. By reducing (
1.2
) to a nonlinear convolution equation such as (
1.1
), Herrera and Trofimchuk
[
9
]
proved the global asymptotic stability of the unique equilibrium under more generic assumptions on
f
, namely that
f
is unimodal function with a unique nontrivial equilibrium. In that work, the authors showed that the asymptotic stability can be obtained just from the analysis of the one-dimensional map associated to the equation, as requiring
f
to have a negative Schwarzian derivative, which are independent of the kernel
\gamma
involved, so they can be called
absolute convergence conditions
; as well as other conditions related to particular forms of the kernel. We explain the link between (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) in Section
8
. In this work, we are not concerned with the global asymptotic stability of the stationary state in (
1.1
), but with the existence of periodic solutions of maximal period, whose existence is suggested by the result of Ma and Magal
[
16
]
at least in some cases.

Equation (
1.1
) is also an instance of nonlinear Volterra equation that are also interesting on their own. These equations, that can be written as

b(t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\beta(a)f(b(t-s))\mathrm{d}s

(1.3)

for a kernel
\beta
normalized by
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\beta(s)\mathrm{d}s=1
, have indeed attracted an independent interest, see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
23
]
or
[
25
]
for an integro-differential version; we also refer to
[
19
,
17
]
and the encyclopedia
[
7
]
for further references and historical notes.

Slowly oscillating solutions to delay differential equations are particular solutions that have a minimal period that is somehow maximal with respect to the considered problem. This notions can be tracked back to the late ’70s with the works of Kaplan and Yorke ’75
[
13
]
, Nussbaum ’79
[
26
]
, Walther ’79 and ’81
[
32
]
. For a fixed delay, slowly oscillating solutions are solutions with the property that the distance between two consecutive zeros is greater than the delay.
For delay-differential equations that are more complex, this notions has to be adapted to the specific equation considered. In the context of delay differential equations with dynamic delays (the delay being a part of the differential equation), Arino, Hadeler and Hbid
[
1
]
proposed the following definition: the distance between the left-hand side of two consecutive intervals on which the solution is identically zero, has to be no less than the maximal value that can be taken by the delay. Such a strong definition would correspond in our case to solutions for which the distance between two zeros is no less than
1+\varepsilon/2
; however it can be proved that such solutions do not exist for (
1.1
).
In this work we will call
slowly oscillating solution
a solution that changes sign only once on any interval of size
1-\varepsilon/2
(see the precise statement in Definition
2.1
).
Remark that the notion of slowly oscillating
solution
should not be confused with that of slowly oscillating
function
, which is a completely different notion independent of delay differential equations.

To the extent of our knowledge, the existence of slowly oscillating solutions for nonlinear Volterra equations such as (
1.1
) with negative feedback has only been established under strong symmetry assumptions on both the function
f
and the solution.
Nussbaum
[
24
]
proved in 1978 that, under some conditions, nonlinear delay-differential equations with several delays may possess slowly oscillating solutions under the assumption that all nonlinearities are
odd
; in our context, this amounts to assuming that the function
f
in (
1.1
) is odd.
Chow, Diekmann and Mallet-Paret
[
4
]
proved the existence and uniqueness of periodic solutions of fixed period 2, more precisely of sine-type solutions.
More recently, Breda et al.
[
3
]
showed, by means of numerical tools, the existence of a bifurcation in this type of renewal equations and also the existence of periodic solutions. Kennedy
[
14
]
proved the existence of periodic solutions for a slight generalization of (
1.1
) under symmetric conditions for
f
.

In this work, we present a result of existence for a periodic, slowly oscillating orbit of (
1.1
) without any symmetric condition over
f
by means of a fixed-point-argument. Our argument is inspired by Arino, Hadeler and Hbid
[
1
]
, although the method we use turns out to be quite different. The core of our argument is the study of the first-return or Poincaré map for the equation acting on an appropriate set of initial data that is consistent with slowly oscillating solutions.
We chose to work with the set
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
of functions over
[-1-\varepsilon/2,0]
that are bounded by a constant, positive on a large interval and change sign exactly once between
-\varepsilon
and
0
in a non-degenerate manner (see the precise definition in Assumption
2.2
).
This set shares some similarities with a cone but fails to be convex, which complicates the application of fixed-point theorems (in particular, we cannot apply the Schauder fixed-point Theorem directly).
We first prove that the Poincaré map is well-defined, continuous and leaves
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
positively invariant by the Poincaré map. A key argument in our proof is the comparison with an eigenvector of the linearised equation of (
1.1
) far away from the zeros of the solution, which is made possible by the fact that the integral operator is monotone whenever the solution does not change sign in the whole domain of integration; and when it does, a steepness argument shows that the solution is allowed to cross any eigenvector only once.
In the process we show that any initial data in
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
yields a slowly oscillating solution of (
1.1
).
Then, we prove that our set
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
possesses the fixed point property (see
[
6
]
) by constructing a homeomorphism to a convex subset of a different Banach space. This finishes the proof of existence of a slowly oscillating periodic solution of (
1.1
).

We also consider the singular limit of the constructed periodic orbit of (
1.1
) for a non-trivial subset of admissible
f
and show that, when
\varepsilon\to 0
, it converges to a solution of the limit discrete-difference equation

b(t)=f\big(b(t-1)\big)

(1.4)

that is slowly oscillating (see Ivanov and Sharkovsky
[
11
]
for detailed results on the solutions of (
1.4
)).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present our main assumptions and results. Section
3
gives a treatment of the linearized equation around 0 and the existence of the principal eigenvalue in the space of 2-periodic sine-type functions. In Section
4
we present a first result concerned to the norm-preserving property of the extension for any initial data. Section
5
is devoted to the definition and main properties of the Poincaré map, such as its continuity and compactness. In section
6
we present some a priori estimates for the continuation of an initial data in the phase space, as well as its connection with a convex set in a different Banach space. Section
7
is devoted to the proofs of our mains results. In Section
8
we show how our result can be fitted in the setting of the Gurtin-MacCamy population model. Finally, in Section
9
we illustrate our results by means of numerical simulations where some phenomena can be observed, such as the Gibbs phenomenon in the non-monotone case.

A first upper bound

Looking at the definition of
x_{b}
in (
2.1
), we suspect its norm is controlled by the properties of
f
. In particular, by Assumption
2.1
there exists a positive constant
a_{0}
(as small as we want) such that
f
is strictly decreasing on
[-a_{0},a_{0}]
and

|f(x)|\geq\max\{f(-a_{0}),|f(a_{0})|\}=\max_{y\in[-a_{0},a_{0}]}|f(y)|,\quad\,^{\forall}|x|\geq a_{0},

(4.1)

and also a constant
A_{0}>0
such that

|f(x)|<|x|,\quad\,^{\forall}|x|\geq A_{0}.

(4.2)

Now, the aforementioned property can be stated formally as follows.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
R\geq\max_{|x|\leq A_{0}}|f(x)|
. Then, for every continuous function
b\colon[-1-\varepsilon/2,0]\to\mathbb{R}
with
\|b\|\leq R
, it follows
|x_{b}(t)|\leq R
for all
t\geq 0
(here,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the sup-norm).

Proof.

Let
R
as in the statement and
b
be such a continuous function. Then, for
t\in[0,1-\varepsilon/2]
,

|x_{b}(t)|\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}|f(b(s))|\mathrm{d}s\leq\max_{x\in[-R,R]}|f(x)|.

If
R\leq A_{0}
then clearly
|x_{b}(t)|\leq R
. On the other hand, if
R>A_{0}
, by (
4.2
) we deduce
|f(x)|\leq R
for
A_{0}\leq|x|\leq R
and therefore
|x_{b}(t)|\leq R
. Consequently, we have proved the result for
t\in[0,1-\varepsilon/2]
, and the general bound follows by induction.
∎

5
The Poincaré map

5.1
Assumptions and definition

For any
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
, we define

z_{1}(b):=\sup\{t\geq 0\colon x_{b}(s)<0~\forall s\in[0,t]\},

(5.1)

which corresponds to the first positive zero of
x_{b}
. Observe
z_{1}(b)
is well-defined and finite. Indeed
0
satisfies
x_{b}(0)<0
and therefore
z_{1}(b)>-\infty
. Let us show by contradiction that
z_{1}(b)<+\infty
. If
z_{1}(b)=+\infty
then
x_{b}(t)<0
for all
t\geq 0
; but then

x_{b}(1+\varepsilon/2)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}f(x_{b}(s))\mathrm{d}s>0

owing to the negative-feedback condition, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
z_{1}(b)
is well defined, and is possible to define
z_{j}(b)
as the
j^{\text{th}}
-positive zero of
x_{b}
in a similar fashion.

Remark 5.1
.

Observe that
z_{1}(b)\in(1-\tau-\varepsilon/2,1-\tau+\varepsilon/2)
. Indeed,

x_{b}(1-\tau-\varepsilon/2)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-\tau-\varepsilon}^{-\tau}f(b(s))\mathrm{d}s<0,

which implies that
x_{b}(t)<0
for all
t\in[0,1-\tau-\varepsilon/2]
and thus
z_{1}(b)>1-\tau-\varepsilon/2
. On the other hand,

x_{b}(1-\tau+\varepsilon/2)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-\tau}^{-\tau+\varepsilon}f(x_{b}(s))\mathrm{d}s>0,

as
-\tau+\varepsilon<1-\tau-\varepsilon/2
, and therefore
z_{1}(b)<1-\tau+\varepsilon/2
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
be given. If
x_{b}
is differentiable at
z_{1}(b)
, then
x^{\prime}_{b}(z_{1}(b))>0
. If
x_{b}
is not differentiable at that point, the two half-derivatives

\partial_{+}x_{b}(t):=\lim_{h\to 0,h>0}\dfrac{x_{b}\big(t+h\big)-x_{b}\big(t\big)}{h}\text{ and }\partial_{-}x_{b}(t):=\lim_{h\to 0,h<0}\dfrac{x_{b}\big(t+h\big)-x_{b}\big(t\big)}{h}

exist at
t=z_{1}(b)
, and both are positive:

\partial_{+}x_{b}\big(z_{1}(b)\big)>0\text{ and }\partial_{-}x_{b}\big(z_{1}(b)\big)>0.

Proof.

Since
b
is a continuous function on
[-1-\varepsilon/2,0]
, there are exactly two points where the derivative of
x_{b}(t)
might be discontinuous, that are
t=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
and
t=1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Since
z_{1}(b)<1-\tau+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\leq 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
(see Remark
5.1
), we have only two cases: either
x_{b}
is differentiable at
z_{1}(b)
, or
z_{1}(b)=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.

\bullet

If
x_{b}
is differentiable at
z_{1}(b)
, it is clear that

x_{b}^{\prime}(z_{1}(b))=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[f(x_{b}(z_{1}(b)-1+\varepsilon/2))-f(x_{b}(z_{1}(b)-1-\varepsilon/2))]>0

as
z_{1}(b)-1+\varepsilon/2\in(-\tau,z_{1}(b))
and
z_{1}(b)-1-\varepsilon/2<-\tau
.

\bullet
If
x_{b}
is not differentiable at this point, then necessarily
z_{1}(b)=1-\varepsilon/2
and we have

\displaystyle\partial_{+}x_{b}\big(z_{1}(b)\big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{h}f\big(x_{b}(s)\big)\mathrm{d}s-\frac{1}{h}\int_{{-\varepsilon}}^{-\varepsilon+h}f\big(b(s)\big)\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}f\big(x_{b}(h)\big)-f\left(b\left(-\varepsilon\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-1-\varepsilon/2}^{-1+\varepsilon/2}f\big(b(s)\big)\mathrm{d}s\right)-f\left(b\left(-\varepsilon\right)\right)\right],

and clearly
f\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-1-\varepsilon/2}^{-1+\varepsilon/2}f\big(b(s)\big)\mathrm{d}s\right)>0
since
b(s)>0
on
(-1-\varepsilon/2,-1+\varepsilon/2)
, while
f\left(b\left(-\varepsilon\right)\right)<0
. Similarly,

\partial_{-}x_{b}\big(z_{1}(b)\big)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[f\big(b(0)\big)-f(b(-\varepsilon))\right]>0.

This finishes the proof of Proposition
5.2

∎

Next, we can define the following operator

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}\subseteq C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,0])&\longrightarrow&C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,0])\\
b&\longmapsto&x_{b}(z_{1}(b)+1+\varepsilon/2+\cdot)=:\mathcal{F}(b),\end{array}

(5.2)

which is, actually, the Poincaré map, or first-return map, associated to (
1.1
).

5.2
Continuity and compactness of the Poincaré map

This paragraph is devoted to the continuity of the Poincaré operator. We first establish some properties related to the continuous dependence of
x_{b}
on the initial data
b
. More precisely the next lemma holds

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
R\geq\max_{|x|\leq A_{0}}|f(x)|
. For every
T>0
and
\eta>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that if
b,\psi\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
,
\|b\|,\|\psi\|\leq R
, with
\|b-\psi\|<\delta
then

|x_{b}(t)-x_{\psi}(t)|<\eta,\quad\forall~t\in[0,T].

Proof.

We will prove that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that if
\|b-\psi\|<\delta
then

|x_{b}(t)-x_{\psi}(t)|<\eta,\quad\forall~t\in[0,n(1-\varepsilon/2)],

by the means of induction. For the case
n=1
, as
f
is uniformly continuous on
[-R,R]
, there exists
\delta>0
such that if
|x-y|<\delta
with
x,y\in[-R,R]
then

|f(x)-f(y)|<\eta.

Thus, setting
\|b-\psi\|<\delta
we obtain

\displaystyle|x_{b}(t)-x_{\psi}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}|f(b(s))-f(\psi(s))|\mathrm{d}s\leq\eta,

(5.3)

for all
t\in[0,1-\varepsilon/2]
.

Next, let us assume that the result is true for certain
n\in\mathbb{N}
and we will prove it for
n+1
. So, from the induction hypothesis, we deduce the existence of
\overline{\delta}\in(0,\delta)
such that if
\|b-\psi\|<\overline{\delta}
then

|x_{b}(t)-x_{\psi}(t)|<\min\{\delta,\eta\}\quad\forall~t\in[0,n(1-\varepsilon/2)],

where
\delta
is as before. With this choice, as
|x_{b}(t)|,|x_{\psi}(t)|\leq R
for
t\in[0,n(1-\varepsilon/2)]
(see Proposition
4.1
) then

|x_{b}(t)-x_{\psi}(t)|\leq\eta,

for all
t\in[n(1-\varepsilon/2,(n+1)(1-\varepsilon/2)]
, as in (
5.3
).
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

The map
b\mapsto z_{1}(b)
is continuous from
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
into
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
and
\mu>0
. As a consequence of Proposition
5.2
, there exists
r\in(0,\mu/2)
such that
x_{b}(z_{1}(b)+r)>0
. Noting that

0<\min\left\{\min_{[0,z_{1}(b)-r]}-x_{b}(t),x_{b}(z_{1}(b)+r)\right\},

it is possible to apply the previous lemma in the interval
[0,z_{1}(b)+\mu]
and for a suitable
R
(since
b
is fixed), thus there exists
\delta>0
such that, if
\|b-\psi\|<\delta
,

x_{\psi}(t)<0\quad\forall~t\in[0,z_{1}(b)-r],\quad\text{and}\quad x_{\psi}(z_{1}(b)+r)>0,

thus
z_{1}(\psi)\in(z_{1}(b)-r,z_{1}(b)+r)
, and therefore
|z_{1}(\psi)-z_{1}(b)|<\mu
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
R\geq\max_{|x|\leq A_{0}}|f(x)|
. For every
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
,
T>0
and
\eta>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that if
b,\psi\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
,
\|b\|,\|\psi\|\leq R
, with
\|b-\psi\|<\delta
then

|x_{b}(t)-x_{\psi}(s)|<\frac{2R}{\varepsilon}|t-s|+\eta\quad\forall~t,s\in[0,T]~\text{with}~|t-s|<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Observe that if
|t-s|<\varepsilon
and, without loss of generality, if we assume that
t<s
then
s-\varepsilon/2<t+\varepsilon/2
. Thus, taking
\delta>0
given by Lemma
5.3
, we have

\displaystyle|x_{b}(t)-x_{\psi}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq|x_{b}(t)-x_{b}(s)|+|x_{b}(s)-x_{\psi}(s)|

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left|\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{s-1-\varepsilon/2}f(x_{b}(y))~dy-\int_{t-1+\varepsilon/2}^{s-1+\varepsilon/2}f(x_{b}(y))~dy\right|+\eta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(s-t)}{\varepsilon}\max_{|x|\leq R}|f(x)|+\eta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2R}{\varepsilon}(s-t)+\eta,

for
\|b-\psi\|<\delta
.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

The map
b\mapsto\mathcal{F}(b)
is continuous from
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
into
C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,0])
.

Proof.

Let
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
and
\eta>0
. Using the continuity of the map
b\to z_{1}(b)
we know there exists
\delta>0
such that if
\|b-\psi\|<\delta
then

|z_{1}(b)-z_{1}(\psi)|<\min\left\{\varepsilon,\frac{\eta\varepsilon}{4R}\right\},

where
R>0
is a suitable fixed constant which satisfies
R>\|b\|
and
R\geq\max_{|x|\leq A_{0}}|f(x)|
.

Moreover, using the previous lemma with, and taking
\delta
smaller if needed, it follows that for
\|b-\psi\|<\delta
and
t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,0]
, then

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(b)(t)-\mathcal{F}(\psi)(t)|

\displaystyle=|x_{b}(z_{1}(b)+1+\varepsilon/2+t)-x_{\psi}(z_{1}(\psi)+1+\varepsilon/2+t)|

\displaystyle<\frac{2R}{\varepsilon}|z_{1}(b)-z_{1}(\psi)|+\frac{\eta}{2}

\displaystyle<\eta,

which shows that
\|\mathcal{F}(b)-\mathcal{F}(\psi)\|\leq\eta
and therefore
\mathcal{F}
is continuous.
∎

Finally, another important property of the map
\mathcal{F}
is its compactness.

Theorem 5.7
.

The set
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{B}^{\alpha})
is compact for the topology of
C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,0])
.

Proof.

Let
(b_{n})_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
be a bounded sequence, say by
R\geq\max_{|x|\leq A_{0}}|f(x)|
. Observe that the sequence
(\mathcal{F}(b_{n}))_{n}
has almost everywhere uniformly bounded derivatives, that is, all but the points at which its derivative does not exists; indeed,

|\mathcal{F}(b_{n})^{\prime}(t)|=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|f(x_{b_{n}}(t-1+\varepsilon/2))-f(x_{b_{n}}(t-1-\varepsilon/2))|\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\sup_{x\in[-R,R]}|f(x)|\leq\frac{2R}{\varepsilon}.

This shows that
(\mathcal{F}(b_{n}))_{n}
is an equicontinuous and bounded family in
C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,0])
, and therefore it possesses a uniformly convergent subsequence by the Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem. Thus
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{B}^{\alpha})
is compact.
∎

6
Homeomorphisms and Invariance

6.1
A priori estimates for the extensions

By definition, for every
\delta_{0}>0
there exists an
\alpha_{0}>0
such that

\frac{f(x)}{x}\leq f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0}<0\quad\forall|x|\leq\alpha_{0}.

Hereinafter, we assume that
f^{\prime}(0)<-2
, and
\delta_{0},\varepsilon,\alpha
are sufficiently small so that the following is verified:

•

\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\geq 2
.

•

\alpha\lambda_{0}\leq\min\{\alpha_{0},a_{0}\}
, where
\alpha_{0}
is as in the previous note and
a_{0}
is as in (
4.1
).

Remark 6.1
.

The conditions imposed over
\varepsilon
and
\delta_{0}
also implies that

\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi\varepsilon}{2}\right)>1.

Indeed, as
\varepsilon\leq 1/4
then
\cos(\pi\varepsilon/2)\geq\cos(\pi/8)>1/2
. In general, this holds for every
\varepsilon<2/3
.

Remark 6.2
.

With this choice for
\alpha
, every continuous function
b\colon[0,2]\to\mathbb{R}
for which
\frac{b(t)}{\alpha\lambda_{0}\varphi_{0}(t)}\geq 1
for
t\in(0,2)\setminus\{1\}
, also satisfies
\frac{f(b(t))}{f(\alpha\lambda_{0}\varphi_{0}(t))}\geq 1
for
t\in(0,2)\setminus\{1\}
.

Indeed, let
t\in(0,1)
, then we have
b(t)\geq\alpha\lambda_{0}\varphi_{0}(t)>0
. If
b(t)\geq a_{0}
then
|f(b(t))|\geq|f(\alpha\lambda_{0}\varphi_{0}(t))|
(see (
4.1
)), and from the negative-feedback condition we get the claim. Reciprocally, if
b(t)\leq a_{0}
then the statement follows from the monotonicity and negative-feedback condition on
f
. An analogous argument proves the statement for
t\in(1,2)
.

The previous remark justifies the manipulations done in the next proposition. Mainly, it allows us to establish the order relation between
f(x_{b}(t))
and
f(\alpha\lambda_{0}\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t))
.

Proposition 6.3

(Estimates for
\tau\in(0,\varepsilon/2{]}
)
.

Let
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
with
\tau\in(0,\varepsilon/2]
. Then, the extension
x_{b}
verifies the following estimates:

\displaystyle x_{b}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\min_{\hat{\tau}\in[\tau,\varepsilon/2]}\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\hat{\tau}}(t),\quad t\in[0,1-\tau-\varepsilon/2];

(6.1)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{b}(t)}{\alpha\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(t)}

\displaystyle>1,\quad t\in(1-\tau-\varepsilon/2,1-\tau+\varepsilon/2)\setminus\{z_{1}(b)\};

(6.2)

\displaystyle x_{b}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t),\quad t\in[1-\tau+\varepsilon/2,2-\tau-\varepsilon];

(6.3)

\displaystyle x_{b}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(t),\quad t\in(2-\tau-\varepsilon,z_{1}(b)+1-\varepsilon/2];

(6.4)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{b}(t)}{\alpha\varphi_{0}^{-z_{2}(b)}(t)}

\displaystyle>1,\quad t\in(z_{1}(b)+1-\varepsilon/2,z_{1}(b)+1+\varepsilon/2)\setminus\{z_{2}(b)\}.

(6.5)

Proof.

To prove (
6.1
) let
t\in[0,1-\tau-\varepsilon/2]
. Using Assumption
2.1
and the previous remark we obtain

\displaystyle x_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}f(b(s))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}f\left(\max_{\hat{\tau}\in[\tau,\varepsilon/2]}\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\hat{\tau}}(s)\right)ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}\max_{\hat{\tau}\in[\tau,\varepsilon/2]}\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\hat{\tau}}(s)~ds

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0})\frac{\lambda_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\min_{\hat{\tau}\in[\tau,\varepsilon/2]}\varphi_{0}^{\hat{\tau}}(t),

and thus (
6.1
) follows. In addition, this inequality implies that

x_{b}(t)\leq\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t),\quad\forall t\in[0,1-\tau-\varepsilon/2],

(6.6)

due to the condition on
\delta_{0}
.

To prove (
6.2
) let
t\in(1-\tau-\varepsilon/2,1-\tau+\varepsilon/2)
. From the previous inequalities we have

\displaystyle x_{b}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[f(x_{b}(t-1+\varepsilon/2))-f(x_{b}(t-1-\varepsilon/2))]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[f(\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t-1+\varepsilon/2))-f(\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t-1-\varepsilon/2))]

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\frac{f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}[\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t-1+\varepsilon/2)-\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t-1-\varepsilon/2)]

\displaystyle=\alpha(f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0})\frac{\lambda_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}(\varphi_{0}^{\tau})^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\pi\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi\varepsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle>\alpha\pi.

This estimate on the derivative implies that
x_{b}^{\prime}(t)<\alpha(\varphi_{0}^{\eta})^{\prime}(t)
for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and
t
in this interval, thus the functions
x_{b}
and
\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\eta}
intersects at most once, and therefore we deduce (
6.2
).

For (
6.3
), let
t\in[1-\tau+\varepsilon/2,2-\tau-\varepsilon]
. As in the proof of (
6.1
), and using (
6.6
), we have

\displaystyle x_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}f(x_{b}(s))ds

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}f(\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(s))ds

\displaystyle\geq\frac{f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\alpha\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t).

Subsequently, for (
6.4
) consider
t\in(2-\tau-\varepsilon,z_{1}(b)+1-\varepsilon/2]
, and observe that

\displaystyle x_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{1-\tau-\varepsilon/2}f(x_{b}(s))ds+\int_{1-\tau-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}f(x_{b}(s))ds\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{1-\tau-\varepsilon/2}f\left(\alpha\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(s)\right)ds+\int_{1-\tau-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}f(\alpha\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(s))ds\right)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\frac{f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}\left(\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{1-\tau-\varepsilon/2}\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(s)ds+\int_{1-\tau-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(s)ds\right).

Now, if
z_{1}(b)\leq 1-\tau
, it is easy to see that
\varphi_{0}^{\tau}\leq\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}
in the first interval of integration. On the other hand, if
z_{1}(b)>1-\tau
then
\lambda_{0}(1+\delta_{0}/f^{\prime}(0))\varphi_{0}^{\tau}\leq\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}
in the aforementioned interval. In fact, due to the shape of the functions involved, it is straightforward to check they intersect at most once in
[1/2-\tau,1-\tau]
. Then, since

\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(1-\tau)=0>\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(1-\tau),

to get the claim it is enough to ensure that

\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(1-\tau-\varepsilon/2)\leq\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(1-\tau-\varepsilon/2).

This last inequality is equivalent to

\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\geq\frac{\varphi_{0}(1-z_{1}(b)-\tau-\varepsilon/2)}{\varphi_{0}(-\varepsilon/2)},

and since
z_{1}(b)\in(1-\tau,1-\tau+\varepsilon/2)
, the right-hand side is bounded above by
\frac{\varphi_{0}(\varepsilon)}{\varphi_{0}(\varepsilon/2)}<2
and this last inequality holds. Consequently

\displaystyle x_{b}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\frac{f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}\int_{t-1-\varepsilon/2}^{t-1+\varepsilon/2}\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\alpha\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(t),

and thus (
6.4
) follows.

Finally, for (
6.5
) we use an analogous steepness argument as for (
6.2
). Let
t\in(z_{1}(b)+1-\varepsilon/2,z_{1}(b)+1+\varepsilon/2)
, then we know that

x_{b}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[f(x_{b}(t-1+\varepsilon/2))-f(x_{b}(t-1-\varepsilon/2))].

We divide the proof in two cases to use the different information about
x_{b}
, especially as the estimates change at
1-\tau-\varepsilon/2
. If
t-1-\varepsilon/2>1-\tau-\varepsilon/2
then (
6.2
) and (
6.3
) yield

\displaystyle x_{b}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[f\left(\alpha\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t-1+\varepsilon/2)\right)-f(\alpha\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(t-1-\varepsilon/2))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\frac{f^{\prime}(0)+\delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}\left[\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t-1+\varepsilon/2)-\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(t-1-\varepsilon/2)\right],

6.2
Connection with a convex set

The notion of the set
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
comes from trying to identify a subsolution to the problem that allows us to construct an invariant set in which apply a fixed point argument. However, due to the flexibility allowed to the parameter
\tau
, this set fails to be convex. Thus, we are dealing with two different issues. It is necessary to take
\tau
in this way taking into account the lack of symmetry in the problem, because this may raise solutions with an a priori unknown half-period. On the other hand, one of the usual conditions in the fixed point theorems is the convexity of the set we are working on, a property which
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
does not possess.

One possibility to handle these problems is to use a topological degree argument, since this does not require to have a convex set, but instead consider an open set (see
[
34
]
). This additional requirement requires providing an stronger topology to the state space, as the
C^{1}
-topology. This will also carry several difficulties to define and prove the main properties of the Poincaré map.

One other way in which we can deal with this problem is to transform the set in one which turns out to be convex. Or, more generally, one which possesses the fixed point property. To do this, note that we can divide a function
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
in two parts, its positive part defined in
[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau]
, and its negative part defined in
[-\tau,0]
. Thus, by a simple change of variables we can regard these functions as defined on
[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2]
and
[-\varepsilon/2,0]
, respectively. That is, we transform the set
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
into one which does not include explicitly the value of
\tau
, and therefore in a convex set, and this is the approach we are going to take.

To formalize the aforementioned ideas, as when we define
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
, let us establish the following assumptions.

Assumption 6.1
.

Let
b_{1}\in C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2])
and
b_{2}\in C_{0}([-\varepsilon/2,0])
. The following conditions are satisfied:

i)

b_{1}(-\varepsilon/2)=0
.

ii)

There exists a unique
\tau\in(0,\varepsilon]
such that the following estimates hold

\displaystyle b_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{\tau}(h_{\tau}(t)),\quad\forall t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2],

\displaystyle b_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon}t\right),\quad\forall t\in[-\varepsilon/2,0],

where
h_{\tau}\colon[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2]\to[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau]
is given by
h_{\tau}(t)=t-(\tau-\varepsilon/2)(t+1+\varepsilon/2)
.

Assumption 6.2
.

Let
b_{1}\in C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2])
and
b_{2}\in C_{0}([-\varepsilon/2,0])
. The following conditions are satisfied:

i)

b_{1}\geq 0
and
b_{2}\leq 0
.

ii)

b_{1}(-\varepsilon/2)=0
.

Then, let
E=C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2])\times C_{0}([-\varepsilon/2,0])\times(0,\varepsilon]
, and we define the following sets for
\tau_{0}\in(0,\varepsilon/2)
fixed

\displaystyle B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}

\displaystyle=\{b\in C_{0}([-1-\varepsilon/2,0])\colon\text{Assumption \ref{as:Balpha} holds with}~\tau\geq\tau_{0}\},

(6.12)

\displaystyle U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}

\displaystyle=\{(b_{1},b_{2},\tau)\in E\colon\text{Assumption \ref{as:Ualpha} holds with}~\tau\geq\tau_{0}\},

(6.13)

\displaystyle V^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}

\displaystyle=\{(b_{1},b_{2},\tau)\in E\colon\text{Assumption \ref{as:Valpha} holds and}~\tau\in[\tau_{0},\varepsilon]\}.

(6.14)

Now, our aim is to prove that
B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
is homeomorphic to
V^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
, and to this end we define the maps
B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}\xrightarrow{\xi_{1}}U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}\xrightarrow{\xi_{2}}V^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
given by

\xi_{1}(u)=(v_{1},v_{2},\tau),\quad\text{and}\quad\xi_{2}(v_{1},v_{2},\tau)=(w_{1},w_{2},\tau),

with

\displaystyle v_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=u(h_{\tau}(t)),\quad t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2],

\displaystyle v_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=u\left(\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon}t\right),\quad t\in[-\varepsilon/2,0],

\displaystyle w_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=v_{1}(t)-\gamma_{\tau}(h_{\tau}(t)),\quad t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2],

\displaystyle w_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=v_{2}(t)-\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon}t\right),\quad t\in[-\varepsilon/2,0],

where
h_{\tau}
is as in Assumption
6.1
.

Proposition 6.5
.

The maps
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
are well defined.

Proof.

Let
u\in B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
and let
\tau\in[\tau_{0},\varepsilon]
be the unique zero of
u
. Since
u(t)\geq\gamma_{\tau}(t)
for
t\in[-1-\varepsilon,-\tau]
, and
u(t)\leq\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t)
for
t\in[-\tau,0]
, then

\displaystyle v_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{\tau}(h_{\tau}(t)),\quad\forall t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2],

\displaystyle v_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon}t\right),\quad\forall t\in[-\varepsilon/2,0],

where
\xi_{1}(u)=(v_{1},v_{2},\tau)
. Moreover,
v_{1}(-\varepsilon/2)=v_{2}(-\varepsilon/2)=u(-\tau)=0
and consequently,
\xi_{1}(u)=(v_{1},v_{2},\tau)\in U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
.

It is straightforward to check that
\xi_{2}
is well defined.
∎

Proposition 6.6
.

The maps
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
are continuous.

Proof.

Let
u\in B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
and
(u_{n})_{n}\subseteq B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
such that
u_{n}\to u
. In addition, let
\xi_{1}(u_{n})=(v_{1,n},v_{2,n},\tau_{n})
and
\xi_{1}(u)=(v_{1},v_{2},\tau)
.

First, let us prove
\tau_{n}\to\tau
. To do this, we first check that
(\tau_{n})_{n}
indeed converges. Let
(\tau_{m_{k}})_{k}
and
(\tau_{n_{k}})_{k}
two convergent subsequences and assume they converge to two different limits, say
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{1}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}
and therefore, for a fixed
\delta>0
and taking
k
sufficiently large such that
-\tau_{m_{k}}>-\tau_{0}-\delta
, then

u_{m_{k}}(t)\geq\gamma_{\tau_{m_{k}}}(t),\quad\forall t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau_{0}-\delta].

Noting that
\tau_{m_{k}}\to\tau_{0}
implies
\gamma_{\tau_{m_{k}}}\to\gamma_{\tau_{0}}
uniformly in this set, then passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
yields

u(t)\geq\gamma_{\tau_{0}}(t),\quad\forall t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau_{0}-\delta],

and since
\delta>0
is arbitrary it follows

u(t)\geq\gamma_{\tau_{0}}(t),\quad\forall t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau_{0}].

(6.15)

In a complementary fashion, making the same manipulations with the other subsequence, we obtain

u(t)\leq\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau_{1}}(t)\quad\forall t\in[-\tau_{1},0],

(6.16)

but this contradicts (
6.15
) since it states that
u
is positive on
(-\tau_{1},-\tau_{0})
, while the second asserts
u
is negative in the same interval. Thus, we deduce that
\tau_{0}=\tau_{1}
, and then the sequence
(\tau_{n})_{n}
is convergent.

Now, we assume that
\tau_{n}\to\tilde{\tau}
, and
\tilde{\tau}\neq\tau
; without loss of generality we take
\tilde{\tau}<\tau
. Then, as before we have

u(t)\geq\gamma_{\tilde{\tau}}(t)\quad\forall t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tilde{\tau}],

owing the definition
\tau
it follows

u(t)\leq\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(t)\quad\forall t\in[-\tau,0],

and this is also a contradiction. Consequently,
\tilde{\tau}=\tau
.

Next, observe that

\displaystyle|v_{1}(t)-v_{1,n}(t)|

\displaystyle=|u(h_{\tau}(t))-u_{n}(h_{\tau_{n}}(t))|

\displaystyle\leq|u(h_{\tau}(t))-u(h_{\tau_{n}}(t))|+|u(h_{\tau_{n}}(t))-u_{n}(h_{\tau_{n}}(t))|

and clearly the right-hand side converges uniformly to 0 since
\tau_{n}\to\tau
implies the uniform convergence of
h_{\tau_{n}}\to h_{\tau}
, and also thanks to the uniform convergence
u_{n}\to u
. In the same fashion, we can prove that
v_{2,n}\to v_{2}
uniformly, and for that reason
\xi_{1}
is a continuous function.

The continuity of
\xi_{2}
is straightforward since the maps
\tau\mapsto\gamma_{\tau}(h_{\tau})
and
\tau\mapsto\varphi_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon}\cdot\right)
are continuous.
∎

It is easy to see that the functions
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
are one-to-one, and therefore invertible on the sets
\xi_{1}(B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}})
and
\xi_{2}(U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}})
, which actually turn out to be
U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
and
V^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
, respectively. Moreover, the inverses are given by

\displaystyle\xi_{1}^{-1}(v_{1},v_{2},\tau)=u,\quad\text{and}\quad\xi_{2}^{-1}(w_{1},w_{2},\tau)=(v_{1},v_{2},\tau),

with

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}v_{1}(h_{\tau}^{-1}(t)),&t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau],\\
v_{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}t\right),&t\in[-\tau,0],\end{cases}

\displaystyle v_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=w_{1}(t)+\gamma_{\tau}(h_{\tau}(t)),\quad t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2],

\displaystyle v_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=w_{2}(t)+\alpha\varphi_{0}^{\tau}\left(\frac{2\tau}{\varepsilon}t\right),\quad t\in[-\varepsilon/2,0],

where
h_{\tau}^{-1}\colon[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau]\to[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\varepsilon/2]
is given by
h_{\tau}^{-1}(t)=\frac{t+(\tau-\varepsilon/2)(1+\varepsilon/2)}{1-\tau+\varepsilon/2}
, i.e.,
h_{\tau}^{-1}
is the inverse function of
h_{\tau}
.

Proposition 6.7
.

The maps
\xi_{1}^{-1}
and
\xi_{2}^{-1}
are continuous.

Proof.

Let
(v_{1},v_{2},\tau)\in U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
and
(v_{1,n},v_{2,n},\tau_{n})_{n}\subseteq U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
which converges to
(v_{1},v_{2},\tau)
. Also, let
u_{n}=\xi_{n}^{-1}(v_{1,n},v_{2,n},\tau_{n})
and
u=\xi_{1}^{-1}(v_{1},v_{2},\tau)
. We consider
I=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\colon\tau\geq\tau_{n}\}
, and the following analysis will be made for the elements of the sequence with index in
I
; for the elements with index in
\mathbb{N}\setminus I
it is done in a similar fashion.

Note that, if
t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau]
, we have

\displaystyle|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|

\displaystyle=|v_{1,n}(h_{\tau_{n}}^{-1}(t))-v_{1}(h_{\tau}^{-1}(t))|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{1,n}-v_{1}\|+|v_{1}(h_{\tau_{n}}^{-1}(t))-v_{1}(h_{\tau}^{-1}(t))|;

on the other hand, if
t\in[-\tau_{n},0]
then

\displaystyle|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|

\displaystyle=\left|v_{2,n}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau_{n}}t\right)-v_{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}t\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{2,n}-v_{2}\|+\left|v_{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau_{n}}t\right)-v_{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}t\right)\right|,

and from these estimates it is straightforward to check that taking the maximum over the interval under consideration and subsequently the limit as
n\to\infty
(
n\in I
) goes to 0. Furthermore, if
t\in[-\tau,-\tau_{n}]
it holds

\displaystyle|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|

\displaystyle=\left|v_{1,n}(h_{\tau_{n}}^{-1}(t))-v_{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}t\right)\right|,

and since all the functions involved are continuous there exists
t_{n}\in[-\tau,-\tau_{n}]
such that

\max_{t\in[-\tau,-\tau_{n}]}|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|=\left|v_{1}(h_{\tau_{n}}^{-1}(t_{n}))-v_{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}t_{n}\right)\right|,

from where

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to+\infty\\
n\in I\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[-\tau,-\tau_{n}]}|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|=\left|v_{1}(h_{\tau}^{-1}(-\tau)-v_{2}(\varepsilon/2))\right|=0,

owing to the assumptions about
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.

Thus, observing that

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,0]}|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|=\max

\displaystyle\left\{\max_{t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,-\tau]}|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|,\max_{t\in[-\tau,-\tau_{n}]}|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|,\right.

\displaystyle\left.\max_{t\in[-\tau_{n},-\tau]}|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|\right\},

we can conclude that

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to+\infty\\
n\in I\end{subarray}}\max_{t\in[-1-\varepsilon/2,0]}|u_{n}(t)-u(t)|=0.

This shows that
u_{n}\to u
uniformly and therefore
\xi_{1}^{-1}
is continuous.

The proof of the continuity for
\xi_{2}^{-1}
is the same as for
\xi_{2}
.
∎

Therefore, we have proved that
B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
is homeomorphic to
V^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}
.

6.3
Invariance of the state space

For a given
R>0
, let

\displaystyle B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}_{R}

\displaystyle=\{b\in B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}\colon\|b\|\leq R\},

\displaystyle U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}_{R}

\displaystyle=\{(b_{1},b_{2},\tau)\in U^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}\colon\|b_{1}\|,\|b_{2}\|\leq R\},

\displaystyle V^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}_{R}

\displaystyle=\{(b_{1},b_{2},\tau)\in V^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}\colon\|b_{1}\|,\|b_{2}\|\leq R\}.

The main motivation to define the sets
B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}_{R}
is given in the next lemma

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
R\geq\max_{|x|\leq A_{0}}|f(x)|
. Then, the set
B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}_{R}
is forward invariant under the operator
\mathcal{F}
, defined in (
5.2
), for every
\tau_{0}
such that

\tau_{0}\leq\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\lambda_{0}\varphi_{0}(\varepsilon/2)}{2R}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right),

(6.17)

where
\varepsilon,\delta_{0}
and
\alpha
are like at the beginning of this section.

Proof.

Let
b\in B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}_{R}
and
u=\mathcal{F}(b)
. Observe that
\|u\|\leq R
owing to Proposition
4.1
. On the other hand, since the estimates
x_{b}
verifies are the same in the two different cases for the associated
\tau
(see Propositions
6.3
and
6.4
), without loss of generality let
\tau\in[\tau_{0},\varepsilon/2]
be the associated zero of
b
given by the definition of
B^{\alpha,\tau_{0}}_{R}
. If
z_{2}(b)
is the second positive zero of
x_{b}
, is straightforward to check
z_{2}(b)\in(z_{1}(b)+1-\varepsilon/2,z_{1}(b)+1+\varepsilon/2)
. Then, note that
\tau_{u}:=z_{1}(b)+1+\varepsilon/2-z_{2}(b)
verifies
u(-\tau_{u})=0
. Thanks to the Proposition
6.3
, in order to ensure that
u\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}
, we just need to prove that

u(t)\geq\gamma_{\tau_{u}}(t),\quad\forall t\in[-\tau-z_{1}(b),-\varepsilon],

and, indeed, owing the estimates established there, it is enough to show

\displaystyle\alpha\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{\tau}(z_{1}(b)+1+\varepsilon/2+t)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{\tau_{u}}(t)\quad\forall t\in[-\tau-z_{1}(b),1-\tau-z_{1}(b)-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon];

\displaystyle\alpha\lambda_{0}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{f^{\prime}(0)}\right)\varphi_{0}^{1-z_{1}(b)}(z_{1}(b)+1+\varepsilon/2+t)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{\tau_{u}}(t)\quad\forall t\in[1-\tau-z_{1}(b)-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon,-\varepsilon].
