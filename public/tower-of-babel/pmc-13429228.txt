Elimination of the Opioid Sliding Scale Is Associated with Improved Opioid Stewardship Metrics
Authors: Daniel A. DeUgarte, Ross J. Fleischman, Rebecca Trotsky, Ghassan Moubarak, Danthanh Vu, Hugh Gordon, Peter Huynh, Rodolfo Amaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Hospitals traditionally use an opioid sliding scale, prescribing lower doses for pain scores of 4–6 and higher doses for pain score 7–10. The authors hypothesized that elimination of the opioid sliding scale and implementation of additional electronic health record (EHR) “nudges” could improve adoption of best practices and improve opioid stewardship metrics.Methods:Focus groups with key stakeholders were employed to design, implement, and evaluate the intervention. The intervention used the EHR to eliminate the conventional opioid sliding scale with a novel order set favoring non-opioids and setting a new single threshold for opioid administration (pain score of 5–10). Baseline and postintervention data for inpatients with hospital stay ≤ 30 days were extracted from the medical record. Subanalysis was performed for patients undergoing at least one operative procedure. Mean morphine milligram equivalents (MME) administered and prescribed at discharge were compared using analysis of variance.Results:This multicenter study included 60,464 inpatients for the baseline and postintervention time periods. Interventions were associated with a 31.4% (95% confidence interval [CI] 26.3%–36.5%) reduction in the mean inpatient MME administered and a 12.4% (95% CI 6.7%–18.3%) reduction in the mean MME prescribed. For the subset of patients who underwent an operative procedure (22.6% of the cohort), there was a 37.3% (95% CI 31.0%–43.7%) reduction in the mean inpatient MME administered and 12.1% (95% CI 8.3%–15.9%) reduction in MME prescribed.Conclusion:Elimination of the conventional opioid sliding scale and other EHR “nudges” have the potential to reduce opioids administered. These findings support safe pain management practices and demonstrate a scalable model for health system opioid stewardship.

The emphasis on pain assessment as the “fifth vital sign” likely contributed to overprescription of opioids and the subsequent epidemic. 1 There continues to be a misconception that Joint Commission mandates the use of a conventional opioid sliding scale (Table 1: A) with qualifiers (“mild,” “moderate,” and “severe”), an approach that can result in escalating doses of analgesics and opioids based on a subjective numeric scale. In fact, many opioid sliding scales double the opioid dose for subjectively “severe” pain vs. “moderate” pain when a lower dose would suffice. This is of particular concern given the widespread use of a numeric pain scale, which is highly subjective. For example, patients not infrequently will report high pain scores despite having no change in vital signs and no change in function. Given the limitations of the numeric pain scale, our health system planned to adopt a function-based pain scale, the Defense and Veterans Pain Rating Scale (DVPRS). 2, 3 The DVPRS defines “moderate” pain as a score 5 to 6, unlike the numeric pain scale, which defines “moderate” pain as pain score from 4 to 6. To avoid confusing qualifiers of “moderate” and “severe” pain with different pain scores, our health system proposed a novel approach that favored non-opioids, eliminated the conventional sliding scale (titrating doses to reported pain severity), and set a new single threshold for opioid administration (pain score of 5 to 10) (Table 1: B). Pain unrelieved by an initial opioid is addressed with a breakthrough opioid with clearly defined indications. Although other health systems have reported quality improvement projects to improve opioid-prescribing practices, none have reported the elimination of the opioid sliding scale. We hypothesized that elimination of the opioid sliding scale would be associated with a reduction in morphine milligram equivalents (MME) administered in the inpatient setting and prescribed at the time of discharge.

METHODS

Setting

The study was conducted at the Los Angeles County Department of Health Services (LAC-DHS), the second largest municipal health system in the United States composed of four hospitals, including two Level 1 trauma centers (600 and 373 + bed capacity),a community hospital (355 + bed capacity), and a rehabilitation hospital (150 + bed capacity). Nearly all specialty services are available through the health system (including trauma, burn, and renal transplant). A health system–wide pain management specialty workgroup updates expected practices and the electronic health record (EHR) to align with opioid stewardship principles, which are summarized in Table 2. Prior health system opioid stewardship efforts (2019–2021) demonstrated that a strategy of maximizing acetaminophen dosing and promoting single-agent opioids (for example, oxycodone) over combination opioid drugs (for example, hydrocodone-acetaminophen) reduced opioids prescribed. 4

Approach

Focus groups with key stakeholders (providers, nurses, pharmacists, and informaticists) were employed to design, implement, and evaluate the intervention. Approvals for changes were obtained from several health system committees: Pain Management, Critical Care, Anesthesia, Medication Safety, and Pharmacy and Therapeutics. An informatics optimization team was assembled to review and finalize order options, facilitate communication and education, anticipate and overcome barriers to implementation, and execute the intervention.

Intervention

From July 2022 through December 2023, we implemented systemwide EHR changes culminating in the elimination of the opioid sliding scale (Table 3). Nudge theory was used to influence physician prescribing practices to align with opioid stewardship principles. 5–8 “Good” analgesic practices were encouraged by building them into mandatory order sets with drop-down dose default selections that required simple click-acceptance. “Bad” analgesic practices were discouraged by making them more challenging (by removing associated order sentences, thereby necessitating manual input) and pop-up notifications/windows requiring providers to acknowledge best practices). Opioid sliding scales and qualifiers (“mild,” “moderate,” and “severe”) were eliminated to (1) encourage around-the-clock non-opioids when appropriate, (2) encourage acetaminophen as a first-line analgesic for any pain level, (3) encourage nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory agents as a second-line analgesic for any pain level, (4) define a pain score threshold of 5 to 10 for opioid analgesics, and (5) more clearly define breakthrough pain. The elimination of opioid sliding scales was a major shift in hospital culture and required systemwide education of physicians, nurses, and pharmacists. Nurse workflow was developed to provide guidance for scenarios when non-opioids were ordered around-the-clock (Supplemental Figure 1, available in online article) and PRN (Supplemental Figure 2). Pain management order sets were updated for all units: medical/surgical, pediatric, ICU, and postanesthesia care unit (PACU). Additional refinements to the order sets included addition of lower-dose opioid options for high-risk patients (for example, geriatric patients) and creating pop-up notifications (for example, reminding providers to avoid morphine in patients with renal dysfunction; co-prescribe naloxone for high-risk patients). Non-favored opioids that were discouraged included combination acetaminophen-opioid medications (to help maximize acetaminophen dosing) and tramadol (because of its unpredictable efficacy and higher risk of seizures and serotonin syndrome).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the mean morphine milligram equivalents (MME) administered to inpatients comparing the periods before and after the intervention. The secondary outcomes included the mean MME prescribed to outpatients and the nurse perception of the impact of the novel order set on pain management. We hypothesized that our intervention would be associated with a decrease in MME and an improvement in pain management. This quality improvement project was deemed by the John F. Wolf, M.D. Human Subjects Committee to be exempt from review and patient consent. SQUIRE reporting guidelines were followed.

Quantitative Analysis

Opioid metrics were extracted from the health system’s four hospitals using methodology previously described. Briefly, data were extracted from the health system EHR (Oracle Health – Cerner). We excluded patients with stays greater than 30 days to avoid outliers with a complex hospital course and confounding comorbidities. Opioid medications administered during the inpatient stay and prescribed at the time of discharge were captured. MME were estimated using MME calculators based on opioid amount and potency. 9 Inpatient MME exceeded 5,000 for 1 patients, and these outlier data were excluded from analysis. Data for discharge prescriptions of non-opioids (acetaminophen and ibuprofen), opioids (oxycodone, acetaminophen-hydrocodone, tramadol), and naloxone were also extracted. Results were summarized using descriptive statistics. The baseline period was defined as January 2022 to June 2022. The postintervention period was defined as January 2024 to June 2024. Outcomes were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Subanalysis was performed for patients undergoing at least one operative procedure. Summary statistics for continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation (SD). Mean percentage differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported using one-way analysis of variance.

Qualitative Analysis

The qualitative portion of the study evaluated nurse feedback on the implementation of the novel order set (and elimination of the opioid sliding scale). A convenience sample was performed with participant nurses recruited at daily nurse briefings and regular nurse staff meetings. Focus groups were conducted by nurse leaders/educators at all four hospitals until thematic saturation was reached. The following open-ended questions were asked: (1) What are your experiences with implementation of the novel order set and elimination of the opioid sliding scale? and (2) Have there been any challenges? Recurring comments were collected and analyzed by two nurse-leads and two physicians using inductive thematic analysis. These themes were further reviewed and refined with our health system Pain Management Committee (comprised of physicians, nurses, and pharmacists).

RESULTS

A total of 60,464 patients with hospital stay ≤ 30 days were admitted to four hospitals during the baseline and postintervention time periods. Mean age was 50 (SD 20) years, and 55.7% of patients were male. Results are summarized in Table 4. The postintervention cohort had a 31.4% (95% CI 26.3%–36.5%) reduction in the mean inpatient MME administered. There was a 13.6% (95% CI 8.6%–18.5%) reduction in the mean number of opioid pills prescribed and a 12.4% (95% CI 6.7%–18.3%) reduction in the mean MME prescribed.

A total of 13,691 patients (22.6% of all inpatients) underwent at least one operative procedure during the baseline and postintervention time periods. Mean age was 46 (SD 18) years, and 50.4% of patients were male. Results are summarized in Table 5. The postintervention cohort had a 37.7% (95% CI 31.0%–43.7%) reduction in the mean inpatient MME administered. There was a 14.5% (95% CI 10.9%–18.3%) reduction in the mean number of opioid pills prescribed and a 12.1% (95% CI 8.3%–15.9%) reduction in the mean MME prescribed. However, there were no significant differences in the MME prescribed in the PACU.

There were also significant increases in the percentage of patients prescribed non-opioids at discharge. For patients undergoing an operative procedure, the percentage of patients receiving non-opioid prescriptions increased from 61.5% to 67.2% for acetaminophen (p < 0.0001) and 24.0% to 30.9% for ibuprofen (p < 0.0001).

There was a significant reduction in the prescription of non-favored combination opioids (hydrocodone-acetaminophen) and tramadol. For patients undergoing an operative procedure, the total percentage of patients prescribed one of these two opioids decreased from 10.3% to 2.7% of patients (p < 0.0001). Oxycodone remained the preferred opioid and was prescribed to over 50% of patients undergoing an operative procedure.

There was also a significant increase in the patients who received a prescription for naloxone. For patients undergoing an operative procedure, the percentage of patients receiving a naloxone prescription increased from 5.5% to 16.7% (p < 0.0001).

The experiences and perceptions with elimination of the opioid scale and implementation of the novel approach (favoring non-opioids with a new pain threshold) were evaluated by nurse educators/leaders with focus groups composed of 2 to 10 nurses. Forty nurses participated in the focus groups. Feedback was reviewed at pain committee meetings. Themes emerged regarding ordering safety, simplification, and standardization; challenges with provider adherence and timely escalation; and culture change and nurse empowerment. Nurses felt that new order sets more clearly specified which analgesic to administer first and when to escalate to opioids. Despite these improvements, there were challenges with implementation; for example, when new or rotating providers continued to write ad hoc orders, nurses struggled to reconcile the orders. When the correct order set was used, nurses felt they sometimes got behind on controlling the patient’s pain because they weren’t allowed to escalate to intravenous (IV) opioids as quickly, and providers weren’t always responsive or willing to write one-time orders. Furthermore, the new order set did not address issues with pain management for those with chronic pain. Finally, nurses reported a feeling of empowerment to educate patients on the importance of maximizing non-opioid medications and a stepwise approach to managing pain that minimized the use of IV opioids. DISCUSSION

This study reports on the successful use of the EHR to eliminate the opioid sliding scale and implement a novel approach of pain management favoring non-opioids and setting a new pain threshold for opioids (pain score of 5). In addition, we describe the use of the EHR to “nudge” providers to select favored analgesics and reduce prescriptions of opioids. Some organizations have been successful with EHR implementation, but others have reported variable impact suggesting that an integrated approach with multiple stakeholders is often required for widespread adoption of best practices. 4, 10–15

It is important to note that there was significant resistance to the elimination of an opioid sliding scale, which was culturally embedded in hospital practice, with false reports that Joint Commission requires a sliding scale. To be clear, Joint Commission does NOT require a sliding scale for the management of pain. Joint Commission requires a comprehensive approach to pain assessment and management, emphasizing patient-centered care and safety. 16 Our goal was to identify a pain threshold affecting function and recovery for which opioids were justified. In preparation for a function-based pain scale (DVPRS), we selected a pain score of 5 as that threshold. This approach was quite simple, as it is binary—pain either reaches a threshold for an opioid or not. In addition, the approach avoids the confusion for nurses of what to do when a patient’s pain shifts from “moderate” to “severe” while awaiting an opioid to be administered and/or take effect. Furthermore, pain scores are not like glucose, which is an objective linear measurement. Therefore, a sliding scale like that used for management of hyperglycemia seems less appropriate for pain.

Some providers initially resisted adoption of the new orders, attempting to circumvent the elimination of an opioid sliding scale with a workaround that included order comments, which were inconsistent. These inconsistencies created confusion for nursing staff and burdened the pharmacy staff with the need to request order clarifications and corrections. After an initial failed rollout, the health system informatics group worked with physician, nursing, and pharmacy champions at each hospital to implement a staged rollout that was ultimately successful. Nonetheless, providers that are new to the health system must be educated to use mandatory order sets to comply with our comprehensive pain management approach. Our hope is that other hospitals will eliminate the opioid sliding scale, and our novel approach will become standardized and facilitate adoption across health systems. Another challenge with the stepwise approach to opioid escalation is the potential to get behind on a patient’s pain. However, isolated occurrences of inadequate or delayed pain management occurred prior to our intervention. Nurses are encouraged to escalate concerns to providers, who should evaluate patients whose needs are not met and provide one-time orders and/or adjust opioid dosing and pain management strategies accordingly.

Interestingly, the only metric that was not affected by the elimination of the opioid sliding scale was the MME prescribed in the PACU. This finding underscores that the PACU period opioid use is driven more by the immediate physiologic stress of surgery than prescribing practices alone. The period following recovery from an operative intervention is a unique setting that often necessitates opioids for rapid and reliable analgesia. Thus it is not surprising that opioid requirements were not decreased. Conversely, it is reassuring to know that setting a new threshold pain score for opioids (5 to 10) and eliminating an opioid sliding scale and qualifiers did not affect overall opioid use for this population at risk for poor pain control. Of note, our initial rollout of the PACU order set did not include breakthrough pain orders, and nurses noted inadequate pain control for some patients. The addition of breakthrough pain orders eliminated this issue, and nursing staff reported that the updated order worked well and was simple to follow.

The strength of this study is that it captures a high volume of cases across a large municipal health system using a centralized EHR in the setting of mandatory electronic prescribing. Limitations include the possibility that the observed improvements in opioid metrics occurred independent of the interventions described. Indeed, an abundance of literature and media advocating for multimodal pain management and reduction in opioid use may have contributed to some of the observed findings. However, it is important to note that the time period examined occurred long after these initiatives were commenced and certainly long after California state laws were passed mandating consultation with Prescription Drug Monitoring Programs (2018) and co-prescription of naloxone (2019). Furthermore, the intervention includes many plausible avenues for the reduction of opioid MME: (1) promoting use of non-opioids, (2) setting a higher threshold for opioids (pain score of 5 instead of 4), (3) eliminating PRN severe pain (which is historically associated with a double dose of opioids), and (4) discouraging IV opioids. Another limitation of the study is that the qualitative evaluation of the intervention was primarily limited to nursing staff. Although the intervention was designed iteratively with input from key stakeholders, including providers, nurses, and pharmacists, we did not obtain formal feedback from providers or patients. We felt that nurses on the front line would be best able to evaluate the impact of the intervention on the workflow as well as the management of patient pain.

The next step in our opioid stewardship efforts is to introduce and promote adoption of the DVPRS pain scale, which better evaluates the impact of pain on function than the subjective numeric pain scale. The DVPRS pain scale defines “moderate” pain affecting function as a pain score of 5 to 6, in contrast to the numeric pain scale, which defines “moderate” pain as a score of 4 to 6. For this reason, we eliminated qualifiers like “moderate” and set the threshold pain score for receiving an opioid as 5 to 10. Higher pain scores do not always require higher doses of opioids. In cases where pain is not controlled by an initial dose of opioids, a breakthrough opioid can be administered. Considerable effort was spent convening stakeholders to identify the optimal wording for PRN order options and comments so that nursing would have clear instructions on the stepwise administration of analgesics. Indeed, patients in our health system have learned that nurses can no longer “jump to” high-dose breakthrough pain IV opioids without first receiving non-opioids and oral opioids. Although there are some patients who require an individualized pain management approach, often with assistance from a pain specialist, the standardized order sets work well for the great majority of patients. Along these lines, our pain specialists advocated for the addition to the EHR of sub-dissociative ketamine (both IV and oral), which has been a useful adjunct in the management of complex pain. Ongoing monitoring and evaluation of these improvements are required to optimize comprehensive pain management.

CONCLUSION

Elimination of the opioid sliding scale and establishment of a new pain scale threshold for opioid analgesics were associated with a significant reduction in morphine milligram equivalents administered and prescribed and an overall improvement in the profile of analgesics prescribed at discharge. Hospital-based opioid stewardship interventions through the EHR can facilitate adoption of comprehensive pain management strategies and warrant further investigation.
