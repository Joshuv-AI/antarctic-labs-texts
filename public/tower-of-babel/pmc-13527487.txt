Validation of the Reaction Time-Based False-Positive Rate as a Reliability Index for Standard Automated Perimetry
Authors: Hiroki Nomoto, Sachiko Okuyama, Chota Matsumoto, Tairo Kimura, Keiji Yoshikawa, Shiro Mizoue, Mami Nanno, Shinji Kimura, Satoshi Inoue, Shunji Kusaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo validate a reaction time (RT)-based false-positive rate (RT-FP) for standard automated perimetry (SAP) and to determine the optimal RT listening window.DesignRetrospective cross-sectional study.ParticipantsA total of 1201 eyes from 607 glaucoma patients tested with the AIZE algorithm (imo perimeter, 24plus[1-2] pattern).MethodsReaction time–based FP rate was defined as the proportion of stimulus responses below a defined RT listening window, excluding catch trials. The effect of excluding responses at locations with estimated threshold ≤0, ≤5, ≤10, and ≤15 dB on the association between RT-FP and disease severity was evaluated using Spearman correlation with mean deviation (MD). Standard automated perimetry (SAP) is the standard method for quantifying visual field (VF) function in patients with glaucoma, optic nerve disease, and other conditions affecting the visual pathway.1 The clinical utility of SAP depends critically on the reliability of individual test results; unreliable tests may lead to misclassification of disease severity, inappropriate treatment decisions, or spurious detection of disease progression.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 To assist clinicians in assessing test reliability, SAP software provides 3 indices: the fixation loss rate, false-positive (FP) rate, and false-negative rate.4,8

The conventional FP rate is determined by the catch trial method, in which stimuli unlikely to be perceived are randomly interspersed during testing. A patient’s response to a catch trial is recorded as a FP, reflecting anticipatory button-pressing behavior (anticipatory responses) that can inflate sensitivity estimates and compromise the validity of VF results.5 Despite its widespread use, the catch trial method has several well-recognized methodological limitations.2,9

An alternative approach to detecting FP responses is to use response time (RT) data recorded during actual stimulus presentations. Responses occurring within an implausibly short interval after stimulus onset cannot arise from ordinary stimulus-driven perceptual responses and are more likely to reflect anticipatory button pressing. This concept is not novel: the Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm used in the Humphrey Field Analyzer (HFA: Carl Zeiss Meditec) has long incorporated RT data into its testing strategy, using patient-specific RT distributions to optimize interstimulus intervals and to flag short-latency button presses as FP responses.10, 11, 12 Importantly, because RT is measured for every stimulus presentation rather than a dedicated subset, an RT-based FP index would utilize orders of magnitude more data than the catch trial method. However, adaptive perimetric algorithms may generate a higher proportion of low-probability stimuli presentation at locations with severely reduced sensitivity, which can influence RT-based FP estimates independently of the patient’s true FP tendency. Whether appropriate location exclusion criteria can mitigate this algorithm-related influence requires investigation.

The AIZE (Ambient Interactive Zippy estimation by sequential testing) algorithm, implemented in the imo head-mounted perimeter (CREWT Medical Systems, Inc), is a SAP algorithm that uses spatial information from neighboring test locations to estimate thresholds efficiently.13,14 Because AIZE records the RT for every stimulus presentation, it provides a platform for developing and validating RT-based reliability indices. However, a comprehensive RT-based FP index for this platform has not been developed or clinically validated.

The purpose of this study was to (1) evaluate early RT listening windows of 0–100, 0 to 200, and 0 to 300 ms for defining RT-FP events by comparing their discriminative performance against the conventional catch-trial FP rate; (2) evaluate the influence of low-sensitivity test locations on RT-FP and determine whether excluding these locations reduces algorithm-related effects; and (3) validate the optimized RT-FP as a catch-trial–free RT-based reliability metric for SAP.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at 5 clinical sites in Japan between June 2016 and April 2017. We analyzed 1201 eyes from 610 patients who underwent VF testing with the 24plus(1-2) pattern. The 24plus(1-2) pattern is used to examine 76 test locations, combining the standard 24-2 grid with additional paracentral locations derived from the 10-2 pattern. This pattern was selected because prior work from our group demonstrated that glaucomatous VF damage frequently involves central locations not captured by the 24-2 grid alone, and that using the 24plus(1-2) pattern provides a more comprehensive characterization of glaucomatous damage across the central 24 degrees of the VF.15,16

All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation comprising best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure measurement, subjective refraction, slit–lamp biomicroscopy, dilated fundus examination with a fundus lens, and OCT. Glaucoma was defined as glaucomatous VF loss consistent with structural abnormalities of the optic disc and/or retinal nerve fiber layer on examination. Visual field loss was classified as glaucomatous when ≥1 of the following criteria was satisfied: (1) glaucoma hemifield test results outside normal limits; (2) a cluster of ≥3 contiguous nonedged points in the pattern probability plot of the upper or lower hemifield with P < 5%, including ≥1 point with P < 1%; or (3) a pattern standard deviation outside the 95% confidence interval of the age-corrected normal range. Participants were excluded if they had high myopia (refractive error worse than –6.0 D), a best-corrected visual acuity worse than logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution 0.3, and any ocular condition other than glaucoma known to affect VF, systemic disease likely to impair visual function, or a history of intraocular surgery during the study period. Additional exclusion criteria included a pupil diameter <2 mm, significant media opacity, failure to complete the full test, or missing reliability index data. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kindai University (approval number: 26-239). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design.

VF Testing

Visual field testing was performed using the head-mounted perimeter imo with the AIZE algorithm. Details of this device have been described previously.14 Briefly, the imo features independently isolated optical systems for the right and left eyes, enabling simultaneous binocular testing in a natural head position without a chin rest. The device replicates standard Goldmann stimulus conditions comparable to those of the Humphrey Field Analyzer (background luminance 31.5 asb, maximum stimulus luminance 10 000 asb, target size Goldmann III). Ambient Interactive Zippy estimation by sequential testing is a Bayesian threshold-estimating algorithm that combines the Zippy estimation by sequential testing framework with spatial prior information derived from neighboring test locations, enabling efficient threshold estimation with fewer presentations per location than conventional full-threshold methods.13 The stimulus presentation duration was 200 ms. The response window for threshold estimation, defined as the interval after stimulus onset during which a button press was attributed to the presented stimulus, defaulted to 1600 ms. This window was automatically shortened to a minimum of 1100 ms in patients who consistently responded quickly and could be extended to a maximum of 2400 ms according to the patient's response characteristics. For each stimulus presentation, AIZE records the stimulus location, stimulus intensity, patient response, and RT, defined as the elapsed time from stimulus onset to button press.

Reliability Indices

In the AIZE algorithm, FP catch trials consist of stimuli presented at an intensity 9 dB dimmer than the estimated threshold at each location and are administered at approximately 5% of all stimulus presentations; catch trials are not performed at locations with an estimated threshold of ≤10 dB. The conventional FP rate was calculated as the number of catch-trial responses divided by the total number of catch trials, expressed as a proportion. A conventional FP rate was used as the warning threshold, following the widely adopted clinical convention derived from the HFA software.2,17 Unlike the conventional FP rate, which is monitored and available to the examiner during the examination, the RT-FP index is calculated from the complete test record and is therefore available only after completion of the examination.

Reaction time–based FP rate was defined as the proportion of stimulus presentations during actual threshold testing (excluding catch trials) that elicited a button-press response below a predefined RT listening window. Three listening window values—100, 200, and 300 ms—were evaluated; the 200 ms window corresponds to that used in the HFA Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm algorithm.

To examine the influence of low-sensitivity test locations on RT-FP estimates, responses at locations with estimated threshold ≤0, ≤5, ≤10, and ≤15 dB were progressively excluded from the RT-FP calculation. Notably, the AIZE algorithm does not perform catch trials at locations with estimated threshold ≤10 dB; the ≤10 dB exclusion criterion therefore ensures that both indices are evaluated on the same set of test locations, providing an additional algorithmic rationale for this threshold.

Statistical Analysis

To account for intereye correlation within patients, regression-based eye-level analyses were performed using generalized estimating equations (GEEs) with an exchangeable working correlation matrix and the patient as the clustering unit.18 For correlation analyses, variables were rank-transformed before GEE linear regression, where the resulting standardized beta coefficient approximated Spearman rho. Generalized estimating equation logistic regression was used for binary outcomes, including whether each index exceeded its warning threshold. The coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated as a descriptive statistic to assess the relative variability of each FP index.

For analyses requiring resampling, patient-level cluster bootstrap resampling was performed with 10 000 iterations. In each iteration, patients were sampled with replacement, and all eligible eyes from selected patients were included to preserve within-patient intereye correlation. Two-sided P values were derived from the centered bootstrap distribution of the statistic of interest. This bootstrap procedure was used to compare the CV between the optimized RT-FP and conventional FP rate, to estimate 95% confidence intervals and pairwise differences for area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (AUCs), and to compare the strength of the association between each FP index and mean deviation (MD). A 2-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed to compare the discriminative accuracy of RT-FP at listening windows of 100, 200, and 300 ms against the conventional FP rate ≥15% as the reference standard. The AUC) was calculated for each listening window. For each location exclusion criterion (≤0, ≤5, ≤10, and ≤15 dB), the association between RT-FP and MD was calculated and compared with that of the conventional FP rate to evaluate the influence of low-sensitivity test locations on RT-FP estimates.

For the concordance analysis of the 2 measures, eyes were categorized into 4 groups based on the conventional FP rate (<15% vs. ≥15%) and the optimized RT-FP (<75th vs. ≥75th percentile, an exploratory threshold selected because no established clinical cutoff is currently available): group A (both low), group B (low conventional/high RT-FP), group C (high conventional/low RT-FP), and group D (both high). Clinical characteristics (MD, pattern standard deviation, and age) were compared across groups using GEE linear models with rank-transformed outcomes to account for both non-normality and intereye correlation, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. The relationship between RT-FP and age was assessed using the GEE-based rank correlation approach described earlier. To evaluate the independent effects of age and disease severity on each FP index, separate multivariable GEE models were fitted, adjusting for age, MD, and sex. Partial Spearman-type associations between the 2 FP indices were evaluated using multivariable GEE models on rank-transformed variables, sequentially controlling for MD alone and then for both MD and age. This association was also evaluated stratified by MD severity: early (MD > –6 dB), moderate (–12 less than MD ≤ –6 dB), and advanced (MD ≤ –12 dB). All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.11 (SciPy 1.13.1, pandas 2.3.3, statsmodels 0.14.6; Python Software Foundation).

Results

A total of 1201 eyes from 607 patients (594 [97.9%] bilateral) analyzed with the 24plus(1-2) pattern were included in the main comparative analysis. The overall median patient age was 66 years (interquartile range [IQR] 56–72), and 566 eyes (46.9%) were from male patients. The median MD was –3.11 dB (IQR –8.23 to –0.64 dB), and the median pattern standard deviation was 3.94 dB (IQR 2.17–8.56 dB). The median number of catch trials per test was 13 (IQR 11–16). RT Listening Window Selection

No individual test session averaged an RT ≤300 ms (median 550.2 ms; minimum 382.9 ms; Fig 1A), confirming that genuine perceptual responses in this population consistently exceeded the 300-ms threshold. Across all 3 listening windows, catch trial responses consistently showed substantially higher proportions of rapid responses than stimulus responses (Fig 1B), confirming that responses within each window are disproportionately concentrated among catch-trial FP responses. Using the conventional FP rate (≥15%) as the reference standard, ROC analysis showed that the 300-ms listening window discriminated tests with high FP rates most accurately (AUC = 0.777), significantly outperforming both the 200-ms (AUC = 0.724) and 100-ms (AUC = 0.707) windows (both P < 0.001, cluster bootstrap test). Optimization and Clinical Correlations of the RT-FP Index

Without sensitivity-based location exclusion, the unoptimized 300-ms RT-FP showed a markedly stronger association with MD than the conventional FP rate (rs = –0.185 vs. –0.081, P = 0.001, bootstrap), consistent with the influence of disproportionate subthreshold stimulus presentations at severely damaged locations (Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The unoptimized RT-FP was also weakly correlated with age (rs = +0.104, P = 0.0003), whereas the conventional FP rate showed no such association (rs = +0.021, P = 0.469). In a multivariable GEE analysis for the unoptimized measures, MD remained independently associated with the RT-FP (P < 0.001), whereas age and sex were not. The conventional FP rate was not independently associated with any covariate (Table 2, Panel A). To confirm whether the remaining concordance between the RT-FP and the conventional FP rate reflected genuine anticipatory button-pressing behavior rather than shared confounding by disease severity, partial Spearman correlations were computed (Table 2, Panel B). After controlling for MD severity alone, as well as combined with age, the robust correlation between the 2 indices remained virtually unchanged. Stratification by MD severity further confirmed the high consistency of this significant relationship across all disease stages, including advanced, moderate, and early VF loss (all P < 0.001; Table 2, Panel B). These findings demonstrate that an elevated RT-FP reflects genuine anticipatory behavior rather than a spurious association driven by underlying disease severity.

To determine whether the RT-FP reflects intrinsic individual RT characteristics rather than genuine anticipatory behavior, its relationship with overall RT metrics was examined. The per-test average RT showed no clinically meaningful correlation with the RT-FP (rs = 0.07; Fig 3A), and the mean RT of responses ≥300 ms was similarly unrelated (rs = 0.19; Fig 3B). Patients in the highest RT-FP quartile demonstrated average RTs comparable to those in the lowest quartile (560.9 vs. 545.8 ms), confirming that the RT-FP is not driven by a “fast-responder” patient trait. In contrast, the within-test RT standard deviation was strongly correlated with the RT-FP (rs = 0.68, P < 0.001; Fig 3C), with the highest RT-FP quartile showing markedly greater RT variability than the lowest (168.4 vs. 107.0 ms). Discussion

This study demonstrates that RT-FP, defined as the proportion of responses occurring within the 0 to 300 ms early listening window during SAP with the AIZE algorithm, provides a statistically stable RT-based reliability metric for SAP. The 300-ms RT listening window was validated through 4 converging lines of evidence, and clinical validation showed a lower CV, reduction of the algorithm-related influence of low-sensitivity locations via sensitivity-based location exclusion, and complementary concordance properties relative to the conventional catch-trial FP rate.

The 300-ms listening window was empirically derived by identifying the RT threshold that maximized the Spearman correlation with the conventional catch-trial FP rate among the evaluated listening windows (100, 200, and 300 ms), achieving peak agreement at 300 ms. This calibration approach is appropriate because both measures are intended to capture aspects of anticipatory or short-latency response behavior, although RT-FP should not be interpreted as a direct equivalent of the conventional catch-trial FP rate. Crucially, the conventional catch-trial method remains a practical reference standard for identifying this optimal threshold because blank catch trials directly define the conventional FP response used in SAP reliability assessment. Independent physiological grounds further corroborate this choice; the minimum reaction time for conscious visual detection is estimated to range from 150 to 200 ms,19 and our data confirm that the minimum per-session average RT among all patients remained well above the 300-ms threshold. Furthermore, 18.83% of the catch-trial responses occurred within 300 ms compared with only 1.99% of all stimulus responses, indicating that sub-300-ms responses are enriched among conventional catch-trial FP responses.

The validation of RT-FP is best understood in the context of the methodological limitations of the conventional catch-trial approach and the specific challenges that must be managed for RT-based indices. The sparse-sampling problem—whereby the conventional FP rate is derived from a limited number of catch trials per test—can produce estimates with wide confidence intervals.1,2,8,9,17,20, 21, 22 Reaction time–based FP rate, computed from all stimulus responses, typically numbering several 100 per test, may reduce this imprecision. Additionally, patients may learn to suppress responses during the interstimulus intervals used for catch trials, artificially lowering the measured conventional FP rate and causing underestimation of anticipatory response behavior.9 Because RT-FP requires no dedicated catch trials, it is not subject to this timing-based suppression. The use of the conventional FP rate as a reference standard is grounded in signal detection theory:23 responses to blank stimuli represent instances in which internal noise exceeds the observer's response criterion, a behavioral property independent of stimulus threshold, and therefore constitute a legitimate index of FP response tendency, although its precision is limited by sparse sampling.

Importantly, however, RT-FP events as defined in the present study are not equivalent to FP responses in this strict sense; rather, they represent short-latency responses occurring during stimulus presentations. McKendrick et al24 demonstrated that genuine stimulus-driven RTs are prolonged at lower-threshold locations, consistent with Piéron's law,25,26 indicating that short-latency responses (<300 ms) at these locations are unlikely to reflect ordinary stimulus-driven visual detections. In the present data, the proportion of short-latency responses increased across presentation categories with lower expected perceptibility, from 1.4% to 2.1% for suprathreshold presentations to 7.0% for subthreshold presentations (Fig S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Blank catch-trial presentations, which were not included in the RT-FP calculation, showed the highest short-latency fraction (18.8%) and were included only as a reference condition in which no stimulus was presented. These findings suggest that the RT-FP gradient across threshold levels reflects, at least in part, differences in the composition of responded presentations entering the RT-FP calculation, rather than a threshold-dependent change in FP behavior. The elevated RT-FP rate at low-sensitivity locations is therefore interpreted not as evidence of altered FP behavior, but as reflecting, at least in part, the AIZE algorithm’s tendency to present a higher proportion of subthreshold stimuli at those locations, thereby influencing the observed RT-FP rate independently of any change in the patient’s FP tendency. Table S1 shows this algorithm-related pattern directly. Subthreshold presentations became increasingly common as sensitivity declined, rising from <5% of presentations at locations with an estimated threshold ≥16 dB to 49.3% at locations ≤10 dB, and accounted for 64.5% of the short-latency responses recorded at ≤10 dB locations. Other factors, including delayed-response carry-over (“run-over”), may also contribute to RT-FP events and warrant further investigation. Excluding responses at locations with an estimated threshold ≤10 dB mitigated this algorithm-related influence and reduced the association between RT-FP and MD to a level comparable to that of the conventional FP rate.

The lower relative variability of the RT-FP (CV = 1.27 vs. 1.52) can be interpreted within a measurement precision framework. In the present cohort, the conventional FP rate was derived from a median of only 13 catch trials per test. The sampling variability inherent to a binomial distribution with only 13 observations is substantial: even 2 positive responses yield a 15.4% rate, crossing the standard clinical warning threshold by chance alone.1,2 The observed conventional FP rate in any given test therefore reflects the patient’s true FP tendency combined with a large random error component attributable to this sparse sampling. In contrast, the RT-FP, computed from the entire set of stimulus presentations—typically several 100 in the AIZE 24plus pattern—provides a more densely sampled RT-based measure of short-latency response behavior. The moderate correlation between the 2 indices (rs = 0.417, P < 0.001) is consistent with partial concordance between related reliability metrics, while also reflecting the measurement error inherent in a 13-trial sample. This is analogous to estimating a population proportion from 13 versus several hundred observations; the reliability of the estimate improves not because the underlying construct changes but because the expanded sample size minimizes sampling error.

In the univariate analysis, the optimized RT-FP displayed weak correlations with age (P < 0.001) and MD severity (P < 0.01), though the multivariable GEE analysis adjusted for age, sex, and MD demonstrated that none of these covariates were independently associated with the optimized RT-FP (MD: P = 0.209; age: P = 0.094; sex: P = 0.230). This lack of independent association mirrors the behavior of the conventional FP rate (MD: P = 0.688; age: P = 0.862; sex: P = 0.446), underscoring that the location exclusion technique substantially reduced the independent association of the optimized RT-FP with MD severity, as visually supported by Figure 2. Crucially, the fact that the univariate association between the optimized RT-FP and older age (P < 0.001) was attenuated to nonsignificance in the multivariable GEE model (P = 0.094) provides useful interpretive insight. This statistical mitigation indicates that the apparent relationship between advancing age and a higher RT-FP rate is largely attributable to the greater incidence and severity of advanced glaucoma among older patients, rather than reflecting a direct, age-dependent propensity for anticipatory button-pressing behavior. Partial Spearman correlation analysis further verified that the concordance between the optimized RT-FP and the conventional FP rate was not a spurious artifact driven by shared confounding; the correlation remained robustly unchanged after controlling for MD severity alone, as well as after additional adjustment for age (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, analysis stratified by glaucoma severity confirmed this consistency, maintaining significant correlations across eyes with early, moderate, and advanced VF loss (all P < 0.001). Collectively, these multivariable, partial, and stratified assessments indicate that the optimized RT-FP was not primarily driven by advanced glaucomatous damage and instead provides complementary information on short-latency response behavior.

To identify the optimal location exclusion criterion, we compared thresholds of ≤0, ≤5, ≤10, and ≤15 dB. Excluding locations with estimated threshold ≤10 dB most effectively reduced the MD–RT-FP correlation to the level of the conventional FP rate, whereas ≤0 dB and ≤5 dB exclusions yielded only partial improvements. The ≤15 dB criterion also achieved nonsignificant concordance with the conventional FP rate (bootstrap P = 0.30) but provided no additional benefit in reducing the algorithm-related MD association while further diminishing discriminative accuracy (AUC 0.770 vs. 0.776). Although the ≤10 dB criterion showed modestly lower AUC than the ≤0 dB (bootstrap P = 0.035) and ≤5 dB (bootstrap P = 0.027) exclusion criteria—reflecting a tradeoff between reducing the algorithm-related MD association and discriminative accuracy—its AUC did not differ significantly from either no exclusion (bootstrap P = 0.182) or the ≤15 dB criterion (bootstrap P = 0.542), and concordance with conventional FP was preserved (bootstrap P = 0.056). Despite a modest but statistically significant CV increase (P < 0.001), the CV of the optimized RT-FP remained substantially lower than that of the conventional FP rate (P < 0.001). Severely damaged locations have inherently greater threshold-estimation variability; furthermore, as described earlier, responses at these locations disproportionately originate from subthreshold stimulus presentations. These factors make such locations less suitable for RT-FP calculation and support their exclusion from the optimized RT-FP index. The specificity of the ≤10 dB exclusion criterion was further supported by a matched random-exclusion sensitivity analysis (Table S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). To determine whether the reduction in the RT-FP–MD association was simply due to reducing the number of analyzed presentations, the same number of responded stimulus presentations as those removed by the ≤10 dB exclusion was removed at random in each test session, irrespective of threshold. Matched random exclusion did not reproduce the reduction observed with the targeted ≤10 dB exclusion: the RT-FP–MD correlation remained close to the no-exclusion value (rs = –0.168), whereas the actual ≤10 dB exclusion reduced the correlation to rs = –0.092. This finding supports the interpretation that the effect of the ≤10 dB exclusion was specific to the low-threshold, subthreshold-enriched stimulus-response population rather than to nonspecific data reduction. Consistent with this interpretation, RT <300 ms responses were significantly more concentrated at below-threshold stimulus locations than RT ≥300 ms responses (10.3% vs. 2.8%; P < 0.001), further supporting the view that low-threshold locations are less suitable for RT-FP calculation. The distribution of optimized RT-FP rates across test sessions was markedly right-skewed (Fig S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), with 15.6% of tests showing no RT <300 ms responses. The right-skewed distribution of optimized RT-FP showed no clinically meaningful association with disease severity, with its MD correlation reduced to a level comparable to that of the conventional FP rate (rs = –0.081 vs. –0.081); after accounting for bilateral eye correlation, this association was not statistically significant (GEE, P = 0.209). These findings further support that the optimized RT-FP was less influenced by the extent of functional VF loss and can be interpreted as an RT-based reliability metric reflecting short-latency response behavior.

A potential concern with RT-based FP classification is that patients with constitutionally faster RTs might be systematically misclassified as unreliable, regardless of their true response behavior. Our data argue against this interpretation. Patients in the highest RT-FP quartile had per-test average RTs nearly identical to those in the lowest quartile (561 vs. 546 ms), and the mean RT of responses ≥300 ms was not shorter in high RT-FP patients. Indeed, the strongest RT correlate of the RT-FP was within-test RT standard deviation. This indicates that high RT-FP tests are characterized by high RT variability, consistent with a pattern in which short-latency responses (<300 ms) coexist with responses occurring at ordinary latencies. These findings support the validity of the 300-ms listening window as a separator between 2 behaviorally distinct response types rather than an arbitrary cutoff along a continuous distribution.

The 4-group concordance analysis provides evidence for the clinical value of the RT-FP as a complementary reliability index. The 75th percentile of the study distribution (2.4%) was used as the RT-FP classification threshold, which approximates the ROC-derived Youden-optimal cutoff of 1.9% while achieving higher specificity (0.80 vs. 0.70); the Youden index curve was relatively flat across this range, indicating limited sensitivity to the exact threshold (Fig S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Evaluation of the discordant groups further suggested that RT-FP and the conventional catch-trial FP rate provide nonidentical reliability information. Group C, characterized by elevated conventional FP but normal RT-FP, showed VF impairment comparable to that of group A. This pattern is compatible with the possibility that some elevated conventional FP rates may reflect sampling variability arising from the limited number of catch trials, although this interpretation requires confirmation using repeated testing. In contrast, group B showed elevated RT-FP despite a normal conventional FP rate and had significantly worse VF impairment than group A. These findings suggest that RT-FP may identify rapid-response behavior not captured by conventional catch trials; however, residual effects of disease severity or threshold uncertainty cannot be fully excluded in this cross-sectional data set. Because reliable VF measurements are essential for estimating rates of glaucomatous progression and for interpreting longitudinal functional outcomes, including vision-related quality of life, discordance between reliability indices may have practical implications for clinical follow-up and research analyses.27, 28, 29 Therefore, these discordant groups should be interpreted as evidence that the 2 indices provide complementary reliability information, rather than as definitive proof that 1 method is correct and the other is incorrect.

The interpretation of group D, in which both the conventional FP rate and RT-FP were elevated, also requires caution. These eyes did not show significantly worse VF loss than group A (median MD, –2.65 vs. –2.69 dB; GEE, P = 0.493), suggesting that the concurrent elevation of both indices was unlikely to be explained solely by disease severity. This concordant pattern is consistent with increased anticipatory response behavior, but direct confirmation using test-retest variability or an independent behavioral reference standard was not available in the present study. Therefore, prospective studies with larger samples and repeated examinations are needed to determine whether eyes in this group show reduced VF reproducibility or greater fluctuation in threshold estimates.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at 5 centers using a single perimeter (imo) and a single testing algorithm (AIZE). The RT-based approach requires that stimulus RTs be recorded with sufficient temporal resolution, and its applicability to other devices—including the HFA—depends on the institutional accessibility of raw response-timing metadata. Consequently, multiplatform validation across diverse patient populations is warranted before these findings can be universally generalized. Second, the conventional catch trial FP rate was used as the external criterion for validating the RT-FP. Although the conventional FP rate is subject to substantial measurement imprecision owing to the small number of catch trials, it remains conceptually valid as a reference because responses during blank catch trials correspond to FP responses under signal detection theory. The conventional FP rate therefore provides a legitimate benchmark for validating the RT-FP approach, although its precision is limited by sparse sampling. The sparse sampling of this reference standard may nonetheless have attenuated the observed agreement between the 2 measures, and validation against an independent behavioral criterion would further strengthen the evidence. Third, the location exclusion criterion (threshold ≤10 dB) was derived from the characteristics of the AIZE algorithm, which does not administer catch trials at those locations by design. Whether the same exclusion threshold is optimal for other perimetric algorithms requires separate investigation. Fourth, the present study did not include an experimental FP injection paradigm. Newkirk et al17 formally evaluated RT-window–based FP detection by artificially injecting FP responses at known rates and comparing the algorithm-reported FP rate with the known injected rate. They found that the Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm approach substantially underestimated FP errors, particularly among normal subjects. This finding is directly relevant to any RT-window–based approach and underscores an important distinction: the RT-FP index proposed in the present study was evaluated as a discriminative RT-based reliability metric, that is, its ability to distinguish tests with elevated versus normal conventional FP rates, rather than as a calibrated quantitative estimator of the absolute FP rate. This distinction highlights that discriminative performance and quantitative calibration are both important aspects of validating RT-window–based FP metrics. Therefore, whether RT-FP accurately estimates the absolute FP rate cannot be determined from the present data set, and formal validation against known injected FP rates will be required in future work. Finally, this study lacked longitudinal and test–retest data. The within-individual reproducibility of the optimized RT-FP across repeated examinations, as well as its sensitivity for detecting temporal shifts in patient reliability, remains an important area for future investigation.

In conclusion, ROC analysis validated an optimized RT-FP—defined by a 300-ms listening window with exclusion of responses at locations with estimated threshold ≤10 dB—as an RT-based reliability metric that showed a VF-severity association comparable to that of the conventional FP rate, lower relative variability than the conventional catch-trial approach, and no requirement for dedicated catch trials. An RT-FP exceeding the 75th percentile of the clinical distribution (2.4%) may serve as a practical reference point for identifying tests with elevated short-latency response activity, warranting clinical review of the corresponding VF results.
