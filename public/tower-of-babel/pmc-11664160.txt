Protocol for single sensillum recording from labial olfactory sensory fields in spotted lanternfly
Authors: Hany K.M. Dweck
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryMeasuring the electrophysiological responses of olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs) to odorants in spotted lanternfly (SLF) (Lycorma delicatula) is crucial for understanding how this invasive sap-feeding planthopper locates host plants, aggregates, and mates. Here, we present a protocol for single sensillum recording from SLF labial ORNs to measure their sensitivity, specificity, and response dynamics to odorants. We describe the steps for preparing odorant cartridges, mounting the labium, setting up the electrophysiology rig, recording neuronal responses to odorants, and analyzing data.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Dweck and Claire.1

Before you begin

Olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs) and their receptors detect odorants that mediate host plant finding, aggregation, and mating.2 Identifying these odorants for spotted lanternfly (SLF) is crucial for developing effective management strategies to limit its agricultural and ecological damage. SLF is a polyphagous planthopper that has recently spread across the United States and other countries.3 The spread of this insect is mediated, in part, via ORNs.1,4,5,6,7,8,9,10,11 ORNs in SLF are housed in structures mainly located on the antennae and labial sensory fields.1,12,13 However, the odorant receptive range of these ORNs remains largely undetermined. The following protocol provides step-by-step procedures for measuring and analyzing odorant response profiles of labial ORNs in SLF, aiming to foster research in this area across planthoppers, a group of over 9000 described species in the infraorder Fulgoromorpha, suborder Auchenorrhyncha.14 This protocol has also been adapted for antennal ORNs and is applicable to adults and nymphal instars of most planthopper species.

Preparation of odorant cartridges

Timing: 2 min for each odorant cartridge 1.Prior to beginning, prepare odorant cartridges (Scheme 1).a.Using clean forceps, place a 1.3 cm diameter disc of filter paper into the large end of a disposable borosilicate glass Pasteur pipette (2 mL volume) to prepare an odorant cartridge.b.Vortex a diluted odorant solution for a few seconds to ensure it is well-mixed and homogeneous.c.Pipette 10 μL on the filter paper disc.d.Cap the end of the large end Pasteur pipette with a 1000 μL pipette tip (without filter) to reduce odorant evaporation and to connect to the pulse flow output of the odorant delivery system.e.Prepare a cartridge with the diluent of each odorant as a control.Scheme 1Schematic drawing showing the loading of 10 μL of an odorant/solvent solution onto a 1.3 cm filter paper disc inside the large end of a Pasteur pipette Note: Depending on the volatility of the odorants, preparing new cartridges every three hours or at any specific interval is recommended. CRITICAL: Use a pair of clean gloves while preparing odorant cartridges and use pipette tips with filters for odorant/solvent pipetting.

Setting up equipment

Timing: 1–2 h 9.Set up the electrophysiology rig (Figure 2).a.Choose a room that has stable and suitable temperature and relative humidity to ensure consistent recording conditions and avoid damaging the equipment.Note: Isolation from electrical and mechanical noise sources, such as refrigerators, centrifuges, or other potentially interfering equipment, is crucial to avoid any electrical or mechanical interference during recording.b.Place the microscope at the center of an anti-vibration table (Figures 2A and 2B). This minimizes vibrations that could affect the precision of the recording.c.Secure a manual manipulator to the anti-vibration table (Figure 2B).d.Attach a stainless-steel shaft to the manipulator. This shaft will hold the tungsten reference electrode (Figure 2B).e.Connect two motorized micromanipulators to the anti-vibration table using stands:i.First Micromanipulator: Equip it with a holder for the recording electrode probe (Figure 2B).ii.Second Micromanipulator: Hold a glass tube (20 cm long, ∼4 mm inner diameter) that supplies continuous humidified air to the preparation at an airflow speed of 40 cm/s (Figure 2B).iii.Place the end of the glass tube at ∼1–2 cm from the preparation (Figure 2C). The end of the tube should have a hole to introduce odorized pulsed air into the continuous humidified air during odorant application.iv.Use a stimulus air controller to generate pulsed and continuous airflow (Figure 2A).v.Use an anemometer to accurately measure the air flow speed.f.Connect the recording electrode probe to an Intelligent Data Acquisition Controller (IDAC) system or another suitable amplifier/digitizer system. This system will capture and process the electrical signals from the ORNs.g.Link the IDAC system to the computer at the workstation. This connection allows for the transfer and analysis of data from the recording electrode.h.Ground the manual and motorized manipulators to the same location within the rig. Proper grounding is crucial to prevent electrical noise and interference in your recording.i.Install the appropriate acquisition software for the IDAC system on the computer. Check compatibility of the digital acquisition drivers with the operating system to ensure smooth operation of the IDAC system.Figure 2Electrophysiology rig setup(A) An overview of the rig.(B) A close-up view of the rig.(C) A preparation slide placed on the microscope stage with a low magnification (10x) objective in position.(D) The tip of the SLF mouthparts showing one of the placoid sensilla of the subapical labial sensory organ.(E) A close-up view showing the grounding electrode inserted into the abdomen and the recording electrode inserted into the labium. The nozzle of the continuous airflow is positioned 1 cm away from the preparation.(F) An animal preparation showing a tungsten recording electrode inserted into one of the placoid sensilla of the subapical labial sensory organ in SLF.

Step-by-step method details

Recording neuronal responses to odorants

Timing: 3–5 min for each odorant

Timing: 3–5 min for each odorant

This step aims to measure neuronal responses to each odorant.1.Place the preparation slide on the microscope stage with a low magnification (10x) objective in position (Figure 2C).2.Move the stage until the tip of the labium is in focus and centered in the field of view at low magnification.3.Switch to a high magnification (50x) objective to ensure that the sensory fields at the tip of the labium are visible (Figure 2D).Note: The tip of the labium contains the apical and subapical sensory fields, which bear olfactory sensilla.4.Start the signal-recording (AutoSpike) software and turn on the audio output to monitor neuronal firing activity.5.Insert the reference electrode into the abdomen using the low magnification objective (Figure 2E).Note: Use tungsten rods (127 μm diameter and 76.2 mm length) for both reference and recording electrodes. Sharpen the electrodes to approximately 1 μm diameter at the tip by dipping them repeatedly for several seconds in either a 10% KNO3 (∼1 M) solution or a 10% KOH (1.8 M) solution.CRITICAL: This solution requires a current of less than 10 mA to facilitate the sharpening process.6.Under high magnification (50x), use the motorized micromanipulator to carefully insert the recording electrode into one of the two placoid sensilla that form the subapical labial sensory field (Figure 2F).7.Listen for neuronal firing activity through the audio output of the IDAC system (Figure 2A) as you insert the recording electrode. This indicates that you have successfully targeted a neuron.8.Once a stable signal is established, connect the odorant cartridge to the pulse flow outlet of the stimulus controller system using a flexible tube.9.Insert the narrow end of the odorant cartridge into the hole of the 20 cm tube that supplies continuous humidified air to the preparation.10.For odorant application, press the pedal.Note: Pressing the pedal initiates the application of odorized pulsed air for 0.5 s and starts signal recording for 10 s or any desired period.CRITICAL: The introduction of odorized pulsed air will increase airflow speed from 40 cm/s to 50 cm/s. The duration of the pulsed air should be maintained at 0.5 s for standard recording.

Expected outcomes

Despite detailed morphological studies on the sensilla, including the olfactory sensilla, located on the antenna and labium—the main olfactory organs in spotted lanternfly (SLF)—our understanding of their functional characteristics remains limited.12,13 SLF is an invasive, polyphagous planthopper that poses a significant threat to the grape industry and various other sectors in the United States.3 This insect partly relies on its sense of smell (olfaction) to locate host plants, feeding sites, mating partners, and oviposition sites.1,4,5,6,7,8,9,10,11 However, key aspects of its olfactory system are not well understood, including the number of functional olfactory neurons, their odorant response profiles, sensitivity and specificity, and response dynamics. Understanding these aspects is crucial not only for gaining insights into how the olfactory system works in SLF and other planthoppers but also for the development of novel natural repellents and attractants. Such discoveries could play a significant role in mitigating the impact of this destructive insect on agriculture, residential areas, and forests.

The animal preparation and single sensillum recording steps outlined in this protocol enabled us to successfully, for the first time to our knowledge, record the spontaneous firing rates of neurons within the paired placoid sensilla of the subapical labial sensory organ in SLF (Figure 3A). By monitoring neuronal activity over an extended period (approximately 100 s), we were able to distinguish between two distinct populations of spikes, corresponding to two separate neurons (Figures 3A and 3B). This distinction was initially based on the differing amplitudes of the spikes and was further confirmed using the 'Create Amplitude Histogram' function of the AutoSpike software. In our single sensillum measurements in SLF using several odorants that were identified from SLF host plants, honeydew, or the insect itself,4,5,6,7,8,9,10,11 we observed that the A-neuron of the placoid sensilla exhibits robust excitatory responses to three structurally similar aldehydes (Figure 3C) and inhibitory responses to many other odorants (Figure 3D). By contrast, the B-neuron shows robust excitatory responses to a wide range of odorants, with inhibitory responses to few or none (Figure 3D). Additionally, these measurements revealed that the A and B neurons exhibit distinct excitatory odorant response profiles, even at the highest tested dilution (10⁻2), and that the responses in each neuron to odorants are dose-dependent (Figure 4). Limitations

This technique is undeniably powerful and versatile, offering substantial benefits for measuring neuronal spontaneous activities and their responses to various odorants in most insects. It also proves invaluable in educational settings, particularly in teaching laboratories, where it can be used to effectively demonstrate fundamental principles of sensory biology within a short time frame. However, despite its strengths, there are significant challenges that researchers may encounter, particularly when working with complex sensory structures.

One of the primary difficulties lies in improving the signal-to-noise ratio during recording, especially when dealing with sensilla that contain a large number of neurons. For example, the placoid sensilla of parasitoid wasps can house up to 30 neurons in some species.15,16 This densely packed neuronal environment can create substantial background noise, making it difficult to isolate and accurately measure the activity of individual neurons.

Another challenge arises when neurons are activated using their most potent ligands at higher concentrations. In such cases, the increased neuronal activity can result in an overwhelming number of spikes, making it exceedingly difficult to accurately count and analyze these spikes.
