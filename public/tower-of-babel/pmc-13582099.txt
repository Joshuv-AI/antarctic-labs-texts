Thinking as Relational Process and Pattern in Semiotic Cultural Psychology
Authors: Glen Rutherford, Sudarat Tuntivivat, Edris Alam, George Onyeukwu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study challenges traditional psychological notions of thinking as a static cognitive trait. Instead, we construe enduring thinking patterns to emerge persistently in a semiotic-relational process across three intertwined cultural-psychological lenses: worldviews (macro-level), mindsets (meso-level), and thinking styles (micro-level). The overarching field of Semiotic Cultural Psychology theory (SCPT) has been developed to focus on the embodied micro-mechanisms that bridge the intra- and interpsychological levels of reality through semiotic dynamics. Thinking as a semiotic relational process occurs in the dynamic sign-relations between the person observed, the observing person, and the “generalised other”. This dynamic semiotic process happens at the personal, “I-Me” meaning-making level and at the cultural, “We-Us” level. ‘Complex’ relational patterns such as mutuality & integration are conceived to develop in support-amplifying semiotic loops of enhancement, while ‘simpler’ relational patterns such as reductionism & fragmentation are considered to develop in control-dampening semiotic loops of constraint. Using Thai food systems as a theoretical case, we consider ways Thai actors move through shifting sign positions as well as their likely semiotic mediational responses to ‘crisis’ situations. Thinking, in our relational sense, is not something individuals possess but something they do together semiotically, within culture, and across the evolving dynamics of cultural life.

Introduction: From Individual Minds to Semiotic Relations

How people think is often treated as an individual cognitive property located within the individual’s mind, shaped by traits, abilities, or internal processes. In contrast, cultural psychology has argued that thinking is not simply “in the head” but emerges through participation in social and cultural semiotically organised systems of meaning-making (Valsiner, 2024, 2026). From this cultural psychology perspective, the self is not a fixed entity but a relational and dialogical process continuously formed through engagement with others, institutions, and cultural worlds (Yıldız, 2026).

Building on this tradition and Valsiner’s (2024) exposition on dynamic semiosis, this paper develops a semiotic-relational process approach to “thinking”. It argues that thinking is not a static attribute possessed by an individual but an emergent property of ongoing interactions between observed, observing and ‘generalised other’ sign relations and meaning-making systems within personal and cultural life (cf. Rutherford, 2026). To conceptualise this, the paper integrates three conceptual lenses, namely, worldviews (macro), mindsets (meso), and thinking styles (micro) as dynamically interconnected levels of sign relations. Across these lenses, thinking patterns are understood to arise in semiotic-relational1 movements rather than as discrete categories or stable traits (Wagoner & Carriere, 2020; cf., Carriere, 2024).

The field of Semiotic Cultural Psychology theory (SCPT) has been developed to focus on the embodied micro-mechanisms that bridge the intra- and inter-psychological levels of reality through semiotic dynamics (Salvatore et al., 2021; Kerušauskaitė, 2022; Salvatore, 2018; Rosa & Pievi, 2013). The focus on sensemaking as a field dynamic, in which meaning is generated by what kinds of transitions signs create with one another, raises questions about thinking as a process and not merely as content (Salvatore et al., 2021). To avoid the creation of confusion throughout this work, certain terms are repeated rather than synonymously categorised (e.g., ‘semiotic-relational process’ is used consistently instead of ‘process of relational communication’).

Central to this approach is a reformulation of thinking styles. Rather than treating styles such as analytical, holistic, or creative thinking as fixed cognitive types, the paper conceptualises them as patterns of semiotically mediated relational organisation of meaning-making. These patterns arise in two interlinked domains: a personal relational process domain (me, I, me-I sign-relations), in which individuals are observed, observing and mutually observing within socially shaped selves; and a cultural relational process domain (us, we, us-we sign-relations), through which collective meanings are observed, observing and mutually observing (cf. Rutherford, 2026). Thinking, in this sense, is co-constructed through recursive loops of semiotic mediation across personal and cultural domains (Bertau & Gonçalves, 2007; Winnicott, 1971).

We hypothesise that the simpler semiotic-relational thinking patterns of reductionism, fragmentation, linearity, homogeneity and centricity are single-loop meaning-making systems. These patterns adjust action to feedback within bounds to solve ‘tame’ problems (cf. Rittel & Webber, 1973). Conversely, we hypothesise that the ‘complex’ relational semiotic-relational patterns of mutuality, integration, nonlinearity, heterogeneity, and reflexivity are double-loop meaning-making systems. These patterns actively challenge underlying assumptions and semiotic constraints to solve ‘wicked’ problems (c.f. Rittel & Webber, 1973), thus reframing system goals to enhance effectiveness, efficiency and equity.

To illustrate and extend this framework, the paper engages Thai food systems as a culturally grounded site of analysis. Food practices provide a rich context in which personal preferences, social expectations, cultural traditions, and global influences intersect (Clark, 2025). By examining how food consumers, food producers, and food technologists navigate these relations, Thai food systems are not treated merely as empirical examples but as theoretical partners that reveal how culturally specific forms of semiotic relationality shape processes and patterns of thinking (Hyma & Pratley, 2026).

Our semiotic-relational process perspective contributes to cultural psychology (especially SCPT) in three ways. First, it reframes thinking from an individual cognitive function to a culturally and semiotically mediated process of signs of self/culture in relation to others/other cultures (cf., Mesquita, 2022). Second, it integrates systems thinking with SCPT by acknowledging how feedback, interdependence, and emergence operate in sign relations and meaning-making processes (e.g., Darnhofer, 2020). Third, it offers a semiotic-relational framework for analysing real-world cultural systems such as food, environment, and sustainability not as static structures but as ongoing patterns of thinking (e.g., West et al., 2024).

By shifting attention from what people think to how thinking is semiotically organised, the paper advances a process-orientated understanding of culture, mind, and cognition.

Theoretical Foundations: Culture, Self, and Process

From a cultural psychology perspective, especially a semiotic cultural psychology theory (SCPT) perspective, culture is not a static, “fixed” variable outside the person but is a dynamic, ongoing process of meaning-making mediated by signs. Meaning is persistently signified and reconstructed in irreversible time. Individuals as active agents create, reproduce, transform or cease cultural practices. In this way, culture is a “process” shaping human consciousness and social activity (e.g., Wagoner et al., 2021). As well, meaning is not solely cognitive; it is often effectively ‘charged with feeling’. Hence, culture provides a ‘semiotic order’ within which the self develops in a ‘meaningful’ context. Moreover, semiotic tools like signs, language, metaphors, and cultural artefacts ‘mediate’ psychological functions and, in so doing, transform the mind as a meaning-making system. This system, therefore, interrelates the internal world of the person with the external social environment (e.g., Valsiner 2018a).

Valsiner (2024) writes on dynamic semiosis:… the emergence of signs and their functions in the flow of meaning-making has not been covered by static semiotics. Hence there is a need for taking a developmental look at human signs-related actions … Semiotics as a science can acquire a focus on the reality of the human flow of experience that needs ordering. That happens by signs as they are used to organise the ongoing flow of human experience (p. ix).

In our view, Valsiner conceives thinking as dynamic semiosis with a microgenetic mechanism/method. Specifically, he argues that meaning making unfolds through two parallel, opposing processes, namely, schematization and pleromatization (Valsiner, 2006, as cited in Klempe & Lehmann, 2021). Schematization construes an object through pre-given categories and, in so doing, limits its affective and interpersonal richness into a point-like sign. Pleromatization, on the other hand, “hyper” generalizes the point-like sign into an affect-laden field-like sign (e.g., from a point-like sign as ‘smiling’ to a field-like sign as ‘happiness’). These two processes operate moment-by-moment and in irreversible time, with their products organized into temporary, flexible hierarchies in which signs regulate each other (cf., Valsiner, 2001). Moreover, schematization and pleromatization exist in semiotic symbiosis with each other. Humans make sense and meaning of our experiences. Our existence is a result of combining an overwhelming impression (from pleroma) and a delimited generalization (in schema). Those two constitute a dynamic movement in meaning-making in which the emotional nuances of our experiences and cognitive understandings come together. Our framework accepts this microgenetic dynamic as foundational to thinking as process. However, we extend it by showing how repeated cycles of contraction (schematization) and expansion (pleromatization) do not remain confined at the moment-by-moment level but are iteratively organised into stabilised, higher-order relational patterns. We call these thinking styles (micro), mindsets, (meso), and worldviews (macro). In our account, this developmental move from momentary opposition to structured organization is mediated by the I-Me-We-Us sign architecture and by the double loop/single loop learning distinction (Argyris & Schön, 1978). ‘Complex’ patterns like mutuality, integration, nonlinearity, heterogeneity, and reflexivity reflect sustained, iterated pleromatization, that is, repeated field-like expansion that resists premature closure into fixed categories. Alternatively, ‘simpler’ patterns like reductionism, fragmentation, linearity, homogeneity, and centricity reflect sustained schematization, in which point-like categorization dominates without being re-opened by a compensating expansive process. In this way, our eleven thinking patterns can be read as stabilised outcomes of asymmetries in the schematization–pleromatization dynamic over longer developmental, as well as microgenetic, time. Hence, we extend Valsiner’s model to the meso- and macro-scale (complex) “organisation” of thinking, rather than reformulating it.

The relational self, conceived as a dialogical and reflexive ‘process-structure’ (e.g., Valsiner, 2001), assumes that identity is not a static, internal core but rather a dynamic process involving an ongoing conversation among multiple ‘positions’ or ‘voices’ within the self, which are deeply embedded & intertwined in social relations. Influenced by George Herbert Mead’s I-Me dialectic and Mikhail Bakhtin’s dialogicality, this perspective postulates that the self is a “society of mind” where internal and external interactions continuously shape and negotiate personal and cultural identities. Therefore, the dialogical self is fundamentally a “relational self” because the I-Me internal dialogue means the self is connected to its social context (‘me’) while maintaining agentic, spontaneous movement (‘I’) (Hermans & Gieser, 2011).

Three SCPT Conceptual Lenses of “Thinking”

To close this gap between the abstract theoretical underpinning and the distinct relational structure of thinking, we offer a set of three SCPT lenses, each conceptualised differently but nested together: worldviews (macro-level), mindsets (meso-level), and thinking styles (micro-level).

First, a worldview refers to a comprehensive framework of cultural beliefs, values, and assumptions an individual and society use to interpret reality, understand their place in the world, and guide their actions (see Aerts et al., 1994, for comprehensive coverage of the concept of a ‘worldview’). A recent retrospective semiotic cultural psychology theory (SCPT) analysis investigates the stability of worldviews through their organising structures over time (Reho et al., 2026).

Secondly, a mindset is a particular network of orientations, common to a group of people or a community, that are socially informed and constitute a specific set of orientations in an SCPT meaning-making system. When combined with Richmond’s (2018) critical thinking skills, our process-orientated approach can be structurally topologised by Maruyama’s (1985) mindscapes, which are what we will explore. Maruyama talks about parts and holes (H-type vs. G-type), and Richmond talks about active movement and skills needed to deal with the relations. They are a synoptic illustration of the process of a meaning-making system evolving from a static, hierarchical control (H-type/static thinking) towards an emergent, support-amplifying system (G-type/dynamic thinking), a process that leads to the broader semiotic-relational process.

Maruyama (1985) identifies his four ‘mindscapes’ as H-type, I-type, S-type, and G-type. Simply, ‘H’ mindscapes are homogenistic, hierarchical, and classificational. The parts (e.g., employees) are subordinated to the whole (e.g., the firm). The firm has divisions, subdivisions and super-divisions. Jobs are categorised as categories, subcategories and super-categories. Each person in the same class must be treated equally. Business ecosystems are ‘zero-sum’ (one’s gain is another’s loss), and Newtonian physics applies.

For Maruyama (1985), ‘I’-mindscapes are heterogenistic, isolationistic, and random. The firm is considered the aggregate of individuals who mostly act independently. If employees’ interests coincide, they will work together. Employees tend to move from firm to firm for personal reasons at a given time. Business ecosystems are negative-sum (the collective value created by participants is less than the sum of their individual contributions), and 19th -century thermodynamics apply.

‘S’ mindscapes are heterogenistic, interactive, and pattern-maintaining. In the firm, heterogeneous individuals interact for mutual advantage. In a group context, members know each other’s talents and idiosyncrasies, adjusting themselves for mutual advantage. Non-hierarchical dynamics mean advantages of belonging outweigh the disadvantages. Business ecosystems are positive-sum, meaning that collaboration, knowledge sharing, and interdependence among participants increase the value of the overall market instead of fighting over market share. In other words, there is a win-win scenario for stakeholders. The principles of 1 st cybernetics apply (see Rutherford, 2026, for an outline of cybernetics).

‘G’ mindscapes are heterogenistic, interactive, and pattern-generating. Individuals interact for mutual benefit. Non-hierarchical interactions generate new diversity, new patterns, and new harmony. Individuals combine and recombine, and groups are less permanent than ‘S’ mindscapes. Business ecosystems are positive-sum (see above for S-type), and principles of 2nd cybernetics apply (see Rutherford, 2026).

Third, a thinking style is traditionally conceived as a relatively consistent, preferred method of processing information or tackling tasks (e.g., Sternberg, 1997) that can be adapted to a given situation. There are several types: analytical thinking, creative thinking, practical thinking, holistic thinking, and sequential thinking. Sternberg’s (1997) triarchic theory of intelligence identifies analytical, creative, and practical thinking. Richard Nisbett and colleagues have studied analytical and holistic thinking, finding that Westerners are relatively more analytical and East Asians are relatively more holistic in their thinking (Nisbett et al., 2001). However, in this paper we shift from a focus on traits to SCPT processes, wherein thinking styles are patterns of relational movement. Because of their pertinence to our ‘thinking as relational movement’, Richmond’s (2018) seven critical thinking skills (dimensions) are now elaborated.

Richmond (2018) identifies seven (7) critical thinking skills: dynamic thinking (vs. static thinking), systems-as-cause thinking (vs. systems-as-effect thinking), forest thinking (vs. tree-by-tree thinking), operational thinking (vs. correlational thinking), closed-loop (vs. straight-line thinking), quantitative thinking (vs. measurement thinking), and scientific thinking (vs. proving-the-truth thinking).

Simplifying Richmond (2018), dynamic thinking emphasises the unfolding and emerging nature of problems or issues. System-as-cause thinking emphasises processes and boundary conditions. Forest thinking emphasises wholes and integration. Operational thinking emphasises learning and structure (cf. Peter Senge’s, 1990 ‘learning organisation’). Closed-loop thinking emphasises ongoing feedback (cf. Chris Argyris’ single- and double-loop learning). Quantitative thinking accepts that all models are wrong, but some models are useful. Hence, one can always quantify but not always measure. Scientific thinking emphasises models are only entertainable hypotheses; that is, the “truth” is always contingent.

SCPT Relational Architecture of Thinking: I-Me-We-Us

Sign Relations

Rather than treating actors as isolated decision-makers, we see them as operating within culturally structured relations expressed through four core sign positions:“I” – intra-personal, orientated toward self-support.“Me” – inter-personal, orientated toward self-control in relation to others.“We” – intra-cultural, orientated toward sociocultural support.“Us” – intercultural, orientated toward sociocultural control across boundaries.

Therefore, this sign relation SCPT framework replaces the “isolated rational actor” model with a “socially situated meaning-making actor”, showing that decisions are shaped by where the individual is positioned within a cultural structure. The “I” focuses on the internal, subjective experience of the self. It is a spontaneous and creative agent. The “me” focuses on the self as an object shaped by social interaction and others’ perspectives. It answers the question “How am I perceived?” The “we” focuses on the collective social unit (e.g., a team, family, or community). It boosts social resilience through mutual support and a sense of belonging. Finally, the “us” focuses on the interactions across differing social units to control or navigate cultural-systems boundaries. It can adapt to new social contexts and mediates differing “we” groups.

In our view, thinking functions as a movement across loops through a “reflex arc”. The observed (object/context) social situation, social problem, or social environment is in constant flux in the act of being observed. The observing agent gathers data and engages with the social environment to make sense of it. Reflexive mediation (in the personal or cultural domains) bridges the observed and the observing by continuously interpreting one’s own interpretations and adapting their perspectives to the evolving situation. This creates a “dance of reflexive monitoring” where the act of knowing transforms the knowledge itself (cf. Valsiner, 2017).

SCPT Movement to Five Thinking Dimensions and Eleven Thinking Patterns

The foregoing analysis conceptualises thinking as a dynamic, semiotic-relational process unfolding across personal and cultural reflexive loops. However, these processes do not remain fluid indefinitely. Over time, recurrent patterns of coordination between the observed, the observer and mediation (in the interdependence of the ‘generalised other’ and the ‘dialogical self’) give rise to relatively stable – though always revisable – SCPT tendencies in how thinking proceeds. The relationship between the dialogical self and the generalised other is where the individual internalises societal norms and social perspectives to construct a dynamic, multi-voiced identity.

Therefore, these tendencies are not fixed cognitive traits. Rather, they represent emergent configurations of semiotic-relational processing (in SCPT). They are persistently imitative ways in which reflexivity resolves tension between stability and change, self and other, and part and whole.

In this sense, what we term thinking patterns (influenced by Maruyama’s S pattern-maintaining and G pattern-generating mindscapes) are best understood as stabilised orientations within the broader semiotic-relational (SCPT) process of thinking. They reflect how individuals and collectives tend to organise sign relations and meaning-making when engaging with complexity, particularly under conditions of uncertainty, disruption, or institutional constraint.

Accordingly, we recognise five dimensions of thinking, which are not presented as distinct categories but as dynamic polarities. The following five relational pattern dimensions are chosen to demonstrate the difference between “single-loop” and “double-loop” organisational learning. ‘Complex’ relational patterns such as mutuality, integration, nonlinearity, heterogeneity and reflexivity are conceived to develop in support-amplifying semiotic loops of enhancement (requiring double-loop learning) to address ‘wicked’ or ill-defined problems. In turn, ‘simpler’ relational patterns such as reductionism, fragmentation, linearity, homogeneity and centricity are considered to develop in control dampening semiotic loops of constraint (in single-loop feedback) to address ‘tame’ or well-defined problems.

Each of our eleven thinking patterns is not a discrete cognitive category an actor occupies but a stabilised tendency. In other words, these patterns represent a habitual asymmetry in how the schematisation–performatisation dialectic typically resolves for a given actor, in each cultural and societal context, across repeated microgenetic episodes and developmental cycles. The patterns themselves remain “live” within any single episode.

Reductionism–Mutuality

Reductionistic thinking narrows an episode by locating explanatory or practical significance in a discrete element, agent, cause, or variable. The resulting point-like sign makes the situation manageable by concentrating attention: one factor caused the problem, one actor is responsible, or one intervention is expected to produce the desired outcome. This concentration is not simply the denial of relationships. Rather, it is a temporary contraction of the relational field in which relationships become subordinate to an apparently decisive element. Mutualistic thinking keeps the field of relations active and asks how the elements of a situation are constituted through their interactions. An individual and a group, an institution and a community, or an environment and a society are understood not merely as separate entities that affect one another but as mutually shaping participants in a shared configuration. Mutuality therefore extends the unsettling generated by pleromatisation rather than allowing it to close around a single explanatory point. Hence, across repeated episodes, an actor may develop a tendency to repeatedly contract the field around salient elements or, alternatively, to sustain attention to the reciprocal relations through which those elements acquire their significance.

Fragmentation–Integration

Fragmented thinking emerges when a domain of experience is apprehended as sufficiently self-contained that its relations with other domains recede from consideration. The issue is therefore not that the actor cannot recognise connections, but that those connections are temporarily subordinated to the apparent integrity of the object, category, or problem. A point-like sign establishes a boundary around something such as health, tradition, environmental change, or market pressure, allowing it to be treated as an independent domain. Integrative thinking, by contrast, keeps that boundary permeable. A field-like sign reveals how the domain is constituted through its relations with neighbouring domains, so that what initially appeared to be a discrete problem becomes part of a wider configuration. Integration therefore does not replace fragmentation with its opposite; it sustains the relational opening through which the apparent separateness of domains can be reconsidered. Hence, across episodes, an actor may repeatedly move between these tendencies, with integration becoming stabilised where relational connections remain salient rather than being closed down again.

Linearity–Nonlinearity

Linear thinking stabilises experience around an apparently direct sequence in which one condition produces another: an increase in X is expected to generate an increase or decrease in Y. Such reasoning is not simply an inability to perceive complexity. Rather, it represents a temporary closure of the wider field of consequences around a tractable causal pathway. The point-like sign makes the relationship intelligible by holding other influences, feedback loops, delays, thresholds, and unintended effects in the background. Nonlinear thinking reopens that causal sequence to the wider dynamics in which it is embedded. A field-like sign brings feedback between causes and effects, temporal delays, reciprocal influence, tipping points, and emergent consequences into the episode. What matters is therefore not whether an actor knows that systems can be nonlinear, but whether the causal field remains open long enough for these relationships to influence judgement. Hence, repeated closure tends to stabilise linear reasoning, whereas sustained openness allows nonlinear patterns of causation to become the more habitual way of organising experience.

Homogeneity–Heterogeneity

Homogeneous thinking treats variation as secondary to a more encompassing similarity. People, practices, institutions, or situations are gathered under a common category and encountered as sufficiently alike for their internal differences to become analytically insignificant. A point-like sign consequently compresses diversity into a recognisable type: a population becomes “the community”, a practice becomes “the tradition”, or a set of responses becomes “how people behave”. Heterogeneous thinking interrupts this compression by making internal variation consequential. The field-like sign does not merely add more categories; it reveals that the category itself contains different positions, histories, meanings, interests, capacities, and responses. Heterogeneity therefore concerns the preservation of meaningful difference within an apparent whole.The distinction between the two tendencies lies in what happens to variation once it becomes visible: it may be absorbed back into a general category, or it may remain active in shaping interpretation. Repeatedly privileging one response over the other can consequently stabilise either a tendency toward homogeneity or a sensitivity to heterogeneity.

Centricity–Reflexivity

Centric thinking organises judgement from a relatively fixed vantage point. This may involve privileging one’s own experience, interests, knowledge, or values, but it may equally involve locating authority in another actor, institution, culture, or established framework. In either case, the standpoint from which the situation is interpreted becomes comparatively invisible as a condition of interpretation. A point-like sign therefore provides a stable centre from which the situation can be judged. Reflexive thinking occurs when that centre itself becomes available for examination. The field-like sign draws attention to the actor’s own position within the situation and to the ways in which the standpoint of observation participates in constructing what is being observed. Reflexivity consequently introduces self-implication: the question is no longer only What is happening? or Who is responsible, but also How am I positioned within what I am interpreting? Reflexive thinking is thus not the elimination of centricity, since every judgement begins from some situated position. Rather, it is the sustained capacity to recognise, interrogate, and potentially revise that position rather than allowing it to become an unquestioned centre.

The Five Dimensions as Sites of Tension

Taken together, the five dimensions should therefore not be understood as five pairs of mutually exclusive cognitive types. Each names a recurring site at which the schematisation–performatisation dialectic may temporarily stabilise. Reductionism and mutuality concern the contraction or expansion of relations; fragmentation and integration concern the closure or opening of boundaries between domains; linearity and nonlinearity concern the closure or opening of causal and temporal pathways; homogeneity and heterogeneity concern the compression or preservation of variation within categories; and centricity and reflexivity concern the concealment or examination of the position from which judgement is made. In each case, the two poles are possible resolutions of the same underlying tension rather than separate cognitive states. What becomes characteristic of an actor, group, institution, or culture is consequently not an exclusive possession of one pole but a recurring tendency to resolve the tension in one direction rather than another.

This research adopts a culturally grounded semiotic-relational process (SCPT) approach to thinking in Thai food systems. Instead of using actors as a paradigm example, the analysis assumes that all consumers, producers, and technologists of food are potential actors in a theoretical relationship with each other, the practices of which can be explored through the semiotic flows between four main sign positions in Thai Meaning-Making Systems:

“I” – intra-personal, orientated toward self-support.“Me” – interpersonal, orientated toward self-control in relation to others.“We” – intra-cultural, orientated toward sociocultural support.“Us” – inter-cultural, oriented toward sociocultural control across boundaries.

Within this framework, thinking patterns are analysed as moving through semiotic-relational processes of observed, observer, and generalised other. The developmental approach means that under conditions of complexity (e.g., ambiguity, wickedness, and/or uncertainty), at least some Thai food consumers, producers or technologists will likely remain at the personal ‘me’ and cultural ‘us’ positions of control and be semiotically constrained. Effective leaders, of course, can progress to the ‘I’ and ‘we’ positions of support and be semiotically enhanced. In complexity leaders, however, such actors will move to the I-me and us-we generalisation of other perspectives, balancing control and support dynamics as well as constraining and enhancing semiotic mediation.

Thinking Patterns as SCPT Processual Movements: Thai Food Systems as Exemplars

The following analysis confirms the nature of these movements in the Thai food system as a processual place. Beyond static examples, we explore how actors are impacted by the semiotic mediational processes of constraint and enhancement in situations that are ‘complex-wicked’ versus ‘simple-tame’. To show methodological caution, we acknowledge the theoretical-methodological nature of the Thai food cases. Since the example is hypothetical, the conclusions drawn from it necessarily remain hypothetical as well.

Since our eleven thinking patterns describe cultural-developmental tendencies in how the contracting-expanding tension is characteristically resolved, we now add an example. This worked microgenetic example demonstrates the dialectical process directly, showing how a single episode of Thai food-related decision-making moves through opposing orientations that transform (or regulate) one another in real time.

Analytical Implications

Thai food systems are understood not simply as material or institutional arrangements but as ongoing ‘hyper’ semiotic-relational (SCPT) processes of observing-observed, reflecting-reflected, and semiotically constraining-enhancing across selves and cultures.

We propose that complex relational thinking patterns such as mutuality, integration, nonlinearity, heterogeneity, and reflexivity are more likely to emerge in double-loop organisational learning and to be semiotically enhanced, while simpler relational thinking patterns such as reductionism, fragmentation, linearity, homogeneity, and centricity are more likely to occur in single-loop organisational learning and to be more semiotically constrained. On this account, the former set may be generally better suited to complex and messy situations, while the latter set may be better suited to simple or complicated situations. As the Thai food illustration is a theoretical-methodological example rather than empirical data, these associations remain propositions for future testing rather than demonstrated findings.

Our integration reframes thinking patterns as culturally and semiotically mediated ways of positioning the self in relation to others, institutions, and wider sign systems.

This theoretical analysis results in a set of propositions that can be tested in the context of the cognitive organisation of cultural actors in complex systems. The explanatory power of the framework comes from its ability to provide predictions about the organisation of thinking by cultural actors in pressurised situations. Reductionism, a science principle of breaking down a problem into its component parts to isolate and analyse, can be an effective tool but can often lack the ability to communicate emergent properties that are created only when parts of a system interact with one another dynamically (Gunn, 2024; Couch, 2023). A key change is the transition from constrained single-loop patterns (single components) to enhanced double-loop patterns (whole and systemic patterns) that is necessary to comprehend the more complex causal-interactive nature of a behaving system (Minati, 2024; Bickle et al., 2022). The Thai situation therefore serves as a theoretical methodological study of the process of culturally specific semiotic relationality in the formation of adaptive thinking patterns in real situations.

Discussion

Our Contribution

The theoretical contribution lies in shifting the analytical focus from what people think to how thinking is organised relationally and semiotically. Thinking patterns, in this view, are neither fixed traits nor normative categories but enduring tendencies (patterns) arising within culturally and semiotically structured processes. The eleven thinking patterns examined in this study – ranging from reductionism to mutuality, fragmentation to integration, and centricity to reflexivity – represent sign system orientations through which individuals and groups navigate complexity, negotiate meaning, and position themselves within moving cultural-psychological sign systems. One key aspect to novelty is the cohesive integration of various process-orientated theoretical strands. There is a successful convergence of foundational theories of cultural psychology and systems thinking by mapping worldviews, mindsets, and thinking styles as connected levels of sign relations. This is in line with semiotic cultural psychology theory (SCPT) that foregrounds the micro-mechanisms of the body located between intra- and inter-psychological levels of reality (Kerušauskaitė, 2022). This synthesis rethinks thinking as a dynamic process of co-construction of sign-making that involves continuous and constant transformation and organisation of cultural practices (Salvatore et al., 2021).

The illustrative theoretical case of Thai food systems illustrates the plausible empirical relevance of this framework, though as a hypothetical rather than empirically derived case, it cannot itself confirm this relevance. Among food consumers, food producers, and food technologists, thinking patterns are proposed to emerge as a situated practice shaped by shifting sign-system relations between self, culture, and their mediation. Decisions about food are not merely technical or economic choices but semiotic-relational acts mediated by cultural norms, embodied habits, and reflexive awareness of broader meaning-making systems. In a ‘crisis’ context, more integrative, mutualistic, nonlinear, heterogenous and reflexive patterns of thinking are theorised to be associated with a semiotically enhanced capacity to coordinate across multiple perspectives. However, more reductionistic, fragmented, linear, homogenous and self- or other-centric patterns reflect semiotically constrained systems, having simpler engagement with the generalised other and dialogical self.

Limitations

The study is conceptually rich and sets out to establish consistency among theories. One possible criticism is that the use of system thinking, semiotics, learning loops and cultural psychological modelling is too dense to clearly expound the central argument. However, the disunity of psychology is disputed, as one of the reasons for it may be the absence of an integrative theory or the difficulty in combining the various descriptive methods with a single explanatory theory (Venturelli, 2025; Reedy-Rogier et al., 2025). It is suggested that going forward, the interconnectedness of the three conceptual lenses (worldviews, mindsets and thinking styles) and the articulation of the reflexive I-Me and Us-We loops (Archer, 2010) should be graphically represented to reduce the conceptual complexity (see Fig. 3 below in Future Directions).

Moreover, the five polarities and the eleven thinking patterns have been chosen as axiomatic. Reductionist thinking is recognised as a key method for understanding complex problems efficiently and simplifying them into more manageable parts in science, but the assumption that ‘complex’ relational patterns (e.g., heterogeneity) are universally more adaptive than ‘simpler’ patterns needs empirical support (Gunn, 2024). Homogeneity, for example, plays an important role in ensuring consistency and internal validity in certain contexts (Cordova, 2025; Fonseca, 2024), while heterogeneity is crucial for examining the generalisability of results and contextualisation (Shim et al., 2014; Giraudeau et al., 2022). The effectiveness of a thinking pattern, therefore, depends on the problem context (tame vs. wicked), and empirical testing is required.

Of course, while the conceptual approach is examined theoretically with respect to Thai food systems actors, qualitative research with Thais and other cultural identities is required on this topic, among others.

Future Directions

Our SCPT approach offers new avenues for research and practice in cultural psychology, education, and sustainability by emphasising the cultivation of reflexive, integrative, and mutual patterns of thinking. For example, graduate students’ use of the 11 thinking patterns in their differing research contexts warrants examination. For instance, international business students could be studied to see how they behave semiotically in relation to thinking patterns across simple, complicated, and complex business situations. Most importantly, future studies should also be qualitative and comparative and cross-cultural, rather than theoretical discussions of the Thai food system. The studies should measure empirically the use of the thinking patterns in various cultural identities to test the generalisability of the double-loop/single-loop hypothesis. However, there is great cultural variation in the symptoms of psychological distress, and future studies need to consider the relationship between semiotic patterns and this variation (Feng et al., 2026). The reflexive models to use in such research should be team-based to enhance the trustworthiness of the findings, since reflexivity is known to be an important approach to understanding the power dynamics and researcher biases that exist within cross-cultural research (Rankl et al., 2021; Woodward & Ball, 2023; Setchell & Dalziel, 2019).

Conclusion

In the end, the present relational and semiotic (SCPT) understanding of cognition offers a strong alternative to the individualistic models which it replaces, as well as a culturally orientated approach to understanding social change. The framework highlights how ‘semiotic interaction with thought’ establishes a whole new way of practising applied fields such as social work (Guttman, 2024), urban governance, and global sustainability (Schuitmaker-Warnaar, 2021), as it puts the possibility of conceptualising and practising sign-making reflexively as a precondition for solving the world’s most complex and ‘wicked’ problems. Content integration and flexibility lessen the waning trend of interpretative, narrowly focused approaches and are essential to the successful balance of the macro and micro experiences of complex issues (Hasan, 2025; Usodo et al., 2023).

Ultimately, to understand thinking is to understand the semiotic-relational processes through which people come to know themselves, others, and the world. Thinking, in this sense, is not something individuals possess but something they do together semiotically, within culture, and across the evolving dynamics of cultural life.
