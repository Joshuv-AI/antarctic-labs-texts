Adipose tissue metabolic dysregulation in diet-induced obesity: insights from UHPLC-HRMS-based metabolomics
Authors: Hongying Cai, Daojie Li, Xiling Han, Yunsheng Han, Rui Zhang, Kun Meng, Peilong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity and related metabolic complications are associated with adipose tissue (AT) dysfunction, which contributes to metabolic inflexibility. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Our previous studies demonstrated that a high-fat diet (HFD) induces weight gain, increased fat weight, as well as significant metabolite alterations in serum, liver, and cecum in mice. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effect of HFD on AT metabolites to elucidate their role in obesity progression.MethodsTo establish a diet-induced obesity model, nine female Kunming mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 16 weeks and compared with nine control mice maintained on a normal diet. Subcutaneous abdominal adipose tissue samples (SAAT) were analyzed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS) coupled with orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA).ResultsBy metabolic profiling, 48 significantly different metabolites were identified, including phosphatidylcholines (PCs), lysophosphatidylcholines (LysoPCs), O-phosphoethanolamine, linoleic acid, alpha-linolenic acid, proline betaine, and 3-dehydroxycarnitine. These obesity-associated metabolites were mainly involved in glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids. Among these pathways, glycerophospholipid metabolism exhibited the most pronounced disruption.ConclusionsThese identified metabolites in SAAT may aid in understanding of the mechanism by which HFD promotes the progression of obesity and related diseases.

Introduction

Overweight and obesity are characterized by abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that increase the risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hepatic steatosis (Agha & Agha, 2017; Hotamisligil, 2017). By 2030, approximately 3.3 billion adults are expected to be overweight or obese, accounting for nearly 60% of the world’s adult population (González-Muniesa et al., 2017). Adipose tissue (AT) functions as a dynamic organ throughout the body, which secretes a wide range of adipokines, including hormones (e.g., adiponectin), pro-inflammatory cytokines, and other signal molecules (Kahn, Hull & Utzschneider, 2006). White adipose tissue (WAT), which constitutes the majority of body fat, accumulates predominantly in subcutaneous deposits and visceral depots surrounding abdominal organs and vasculature (Koenen et al., 2021). As the primary site for triglyceride (TG) storage, excessive WAT accumulation—particularly in visceral depots—represents a major risk factor for cardiometabolic disorders, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases (Koenen et al., 2021). Given its central role in metabolic pathophysiology, WAT has emerged as a critical research focus for understanding obesity-related diseases at both molecular and systemic levels.

Metabolomics is a sensitive and powerful analytical tool capable of quantitatively detecting hundreds of metabolites in biofluids, tissues, and cells. This approach holds great potential for early disease diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and elucidating pathogenic mechanisms (Johnson, Ivanisevic & Siuzdak, 2016; Gowda et al., 2008). During obesity, adipose tissue overproduces fatty acids, hormones, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may contribute to systemic insulin resistance (Kahn, Hull & Utzschneider, 2006). Studies have indicated that adipose tissue inflammation triggers lipolysis, fibrosis, and the release of free fatty acids, leading to ectopic lipid accumulation in the liver and skeletal muscle (Strissel et al., 2007; Suganami, Tanaka & Ogawa, 2012). Dysfunction of lipid metabolism in WAT may lead to lipotoxicity and is closely associated with metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (Carobbio, Pellegrinelli & Vidal-Puig, 2017; Longo et al., 2019). However, there is limited information regarding how WAT contributes to obesity development by changing metabolic processes.

In our previous study, a mouse model of high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity was successfully constructed. The results showed that HFD feeding resulted in significant increase in body weight gain, liver weight and fat weight compared with a normal control diet (Cai et al., 2020a). These phenotypic alterations were mainly ascribed to increased adipose tissue mass in HFD-fed mice (Cai et al., 2020a). Furthermore, serum and hepatic levels of TG, total cholesterol (TC), alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase were increased, along with hepatic lipid droplet accumulation (Cai et al., 2020a). UPLC-Q-TOF/MS analysis of liver and cecal contents revealed that HFD significantly altered 19 hepatic and 43 cecal metabolites. Notably, compared to lean mice, hepatic levels of chenodeoxycholic acid, taurochenodeoxycholate, and tauroursodeoxycholic acid were increased by 35.94-fold, 24.36-fold, and 18.71-fold, respectively. Similarly, cecal levels of phosphatidylinositols (PI(P-16:0/18:1(9Z)), phosphatidylglycerol (PG(19:0/16:0)), phosphatidylserine (PS(P-16:0/20:2(11Z,14Z)), phosphatidyl inositol (PI(22:1(11Z)/12:0)), and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE(21:0/0:0)) were increased by 884-fold, 640.96-fold, 226.63-fold, 210.10-fold, and 45.13-fold compared to lean controls (Cai et al., 2021). Serum metabolomics analysis further identified 55 significantly altered metabolites, including glycerophosphocholines, lysophosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin, serotonin, and indole-3-methyl aceta (Cai et al., 2022a). Most existing studies comparing lean and obese individuals employ metabolomic or lipidomic approaches to profile plasma/serum or limited numbers of WAT samples (Cirulli et al., 2019; Kučera et al., 2018; Nagao et al., 2018). However, the direct metabolic perturbations occurring within WAT during obesity remain incompletely understood. Given that WAT secretes both polar and non-polar metabolites exerting autocrine, paracrine, or endocrine functions (Petrus et al., 2020), elucidating its metabolic remodeling in obesity is essential for alleviating obesity-related complications. Accordingly, the present study utilized ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS)-based untargeted metabolomics to characterize HFD-induced metabolic perturbations in adipose tissue. By extending our previous observations in the liver, serum, and cecum (Cai et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2022a), we sought to explore whether analogous metabolic dysregulations occur in WAT. Collectively, our findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the pathogenesis of obesity progression.

Materials & Methods

Animals and diets

Specific pathogen-free female Kunming mice (7 weeks of age) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained on a standard rodent diet (Catalog no. 1233#). Following one week of acclimatization, eight-week-old mice were randomly assigned into two groups based on body weight (n = 9 per group). The control group (CT) received a standard diet, whereas the high-fat diet group (HF) was fed a specially formulated obesogenic diet for 16 weeks to induce obesity. The high-fat diet (HFD) composition was designed based on: (1) contemporary obesity research highlighting the role of sedentary lifestyles and fast food containing high levels of fat and glucose (Lu, Bi & Ning, 2016; Vandevijvere et al., 2015) and (2) a modified version of previously reported formulations (Yin et al., 2013). Specifically, the HFD consisted of 67% (w/w) standard chow (Catalog no. 1233#) supplemented with 10% (w/w) refined lard, 20% (w/w) sucrose, 2.5% (w/w) cholesterol, and 0.5% (w/w) sodium cholate (Keao Xieli Feed Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Sodium cholate was included to enhance lipid absorption (Zhang et al., 2013). All diets were purchased from Keao Xieli Feed Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Sample preparation

Following 16 weeks of HFD intervention, mice were fasted for 6 h and were euthanized via anesthesia by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering. After dissection, subcutaneous abdominal adipose tissue was immediately collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent metabolomics analysis. All animal experiments were conducted according to the requirements of the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Feed Research of Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS) and the Laboratory Animal Ethical Committee and its Inspection of the Institute of Feed Research of CAAS (AEC-CAAS-20090609).

Sample preparation for LC-MS/MS analysis

After weighing the sample (25 mg) into an EP tube, 500 µL of extract solution (methanol:acetonitrile:water = 2: 2: 1, with isotopically labelled internal standard mixture) was added. Under positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) ion modes, three internal standards were used per mode. In the positive ion mode, L-leucine-d3 (methyl-d3), trimethyl-d9-amine N-oxide, and nicotinamide-2,4,6-[d4] were used as internal standards, with retention times of 135.1207 s, 85.1322 s, and 127.0804 s, respectively. In the negative ion mode, L-leucine-d3 (methyl-d3), L-(+)-lactic acid-13C3 sodium salt, and anthranilic acid-[13C6] were used as internal standards, with retention times of 133.1062 s, 92.0345 s, and 142.0605 s, respectively. After homogenizing the samples at 35 Hz for 4 min, the samples were sonicated in ice water for 5 min. After 3 rounds of homogenization and sonication, the samples were incubated at −40 °C for 1 h, and centrifuged at 13,800 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to a fresh glass vial for analysis. To prepare the quality control (QC) sample, an equal aliquot of the supernatant from each sample was pooled.

LC-MS/MS analysis

LC-MS/MS analyses were conducted using a UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 µm) coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twenty-five mmol/L Ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia hydroxide solution in water (pH = 9.75) served as mobile phase A and acetonitrile served as mobile phase B. A linear gradient elution was applied at a flow rate of 0.50 mL/min: initial conditions were 95% B, held for 0.50 min; this was followed by a linear decrease to 65% B at 7.00 min, a further decrease to 40% B at 8.00 min (held until 9.00 min), and a rapid ramp back to 95% B at 9.10 min, which was maintained until 12.00 min for column re-equilibration. The auto-sampler temperature was set at 4 °C, and 3 µL was used for the injection. Using the Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer, MS/MS spectra were acquired in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode, controlled by Xcalibur acquisition software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In this mode, the acquisition software continuously evaluated the full scan MS spectra. The ESI source conditions were as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 30 Arb; auxiliary (Aux) gas flow rate, 25 Arb; capillary temperature, 350 °C; full MS resolution, 60,000; MS/MS resolution, 7,500; collision energy in NCE mode, 10/30/60; and spray voltage, 3.6 kV (positive) or −3.2 kV (negative), respectively. The raw data of untargeted metabolomics were deposited in OMIX, China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix/releaseList: accession number OMIX012556).

Data processing and annotation

To detect, extract, align, and integrate the raw UHPLC-HRMS data, an in-house program developed with R and based on XCMS was used to convert the raw data to mzXML format using ProteoWizard. Afterwards, an in-house MS2 database (BiotreeDB) was used for metabolite annotation, with a cutoff value set at 0.3.

Statistical analysis

Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant (OPLS-DA) and Student t-test were used for multidimensional analysis to identify discriminatory metabolites, which were defined as those with variable importance for the projection (VIP) >1 and p < 0.05. Multivariate statistical analysis of the final dataset was performed using SIMCA software package (V16.0.2; Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umeå, Sweden). To further elucidate the variables driving group classification and improve group separation, the metabolic profiles of the lean and obese groups were compared using OPLS-DA, and the OPLS-DA model was validated using the permutation test (n = 200). Seven-fold cross-validation was used to assess the robustness and predictive ability of the OPLS-DA model. In addition, public databases Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.kegg.jp) were utilized to search for contributing pathways of metabolites. MetaboAnalyst (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca) incorporates the high-quality KEGG metabolic pathway data for pathway analysis. Significant enrichment was considered only for functional categories and pathways with p < 0.05.

Results

Multivariate statistical analysis of adipose tissue metabolites

Adipose tissue plays a critical role in disease pathogenesis by regulating lipid metabolism and signaling pathways. Comprehensive metabolic profiling of adipose tissue is therefore essential for understanding these physiological processes. As previously reported (Cai et al., 2020a), mice fed with HFD exhibited a significant increase in AT weight compared to lean controls. Untargeted metabolomic analysis using UHPLC-HRMS revealed distinct metabolic profiles between the obese and lean groups, with substantial metabolic perturbation in SAAT metabolism. As shown in Figs. 1A and 1B, OPLS-DA score plots exhibited clear separation between obese and lean mice, indicating substantial systemic metabolic alterations induced by HFD. For positive ion-mode data, the OPLS model parameters were R2X = 0.447, R2Y = 0.991, and Q2 = 0.573. For negative ion-mode data, the model parameters were R2X = 0.384, R2Y = 0.987, and Q2 = 0.453. To prevent overfitting of the OPLS-DA models, the permutation test was performed. As shown in Figs. 1C and 1D, validation with 200 random permutation tests yielded intercepts of R2 = 0.93 and Q2 = −0.27 for the positive ion mode, and R2 = 0.97 and Q2 = −0.07 for the negative ion mode. The R2Y value of is original model was very close to 1, indicating that the established model was consistent with the actual sample data. The intercept of Q2 regression line with the vertical axis was less than zero. Additionally, as the retention of replacement gradually decreased, the proportion of the replacement Y variable increased, and the Q2 value of the random model gradually decreased. These results demonstrated that the OPLS-DA models exhibited good robustness and reliability, with no evidence of overfitting.

Metabolic analysis of adipose tissue metabolites in obese mice induced by HFD

Building on the OPLA-DA results, the VIP values were used to access the influence and interpretive power of each metabolite’s expression pattern on the classification and discrimination. Student’s t-test was further used to verify whether differences in metabolite levels between the two groups were significant. According to VIP > 1 and p < 0.05, there were 48 differentially abundant metabolite species between obese and lean SAAT samples. Among these, 42 metabolites were detected in positive ion mode and six in negative ion mode, as determined by UHPLC-HRMS spectral characteristics and information from existing metabolite and lipid structure databases. The results of differential metabolite screening were visualized using volcano plots to intuitively illustrate the overall distribution of metabolic perturbations between obese and lean mice. Compared to lean mice, eight metabolites were upregulated and 34 were downregulated in the positive ion mode (Fig. 2A), and two metabolites were upregulated and four were downregulated in negative ion mode (Fig. 2B), as shown in the volcano plots. As shown in Table 1, the significantly altered metabolites were identified in detail. These differentially abundant metabolites were classified into 11 superclass categories, including organoheterocyclic compounds, organic acids and derivatives, benzenoids, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic nitrogen compounds, hydrocarbon derivatives, alkaloids and derivatives, organoheterocyclic compounds, organic oxygen compounds, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, and hydrocarbons. As shown in Table 2, among the analyzed metabolites, 10 were elevated (fold change (FC) ≥ 1.36), while 38 were decreased (FC ≤ 0.79) in the obese group. These differentially abundant metabolites at the superclass level were classified into 11 categories, including organoheterocyclic compounds, organic acids and derivatives, benzenoids, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic nitrogen compounds, hydrocarbon derivatives, alkaloids and derivatives, organoheterocyclic compounds, organic oxygen compounds, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, and hydrocarbons. Compared to lean mice, HFD significantly altered metabolites associated with glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and taurine and hypotaurine metabolism. Notably, HFD predominantly impacted the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways. Specifically, O-phosphoethanolamine and four saturated fatty acids (SFAs), including PC(16:0_15:0), LysoPC(20:0), LysoPC(16:0), and LysoPA(18:0e), were significantly decreased (p < 0.05), whereas PC(16:0_P-16:0) was significantly increased (p < 0.05). Additionally, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), including LysoPC(18:2) and LysoPC(22:6) were significantly decreased. Furthermore, PC(20:2_14:0), PC(22:2_15:0), and PC(22:4_15:0) were decreased, whereas PC(20:4_15:0), PE(22:4_16:0), and 2-acetyl-1-alkyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine were significantly elevated (p < 0.05). The levels of alpha-linolenic acid (18:3), and linoleic acid (18:2), involved in biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids pathway, were significantly decreased. Additionally, sphinganine, stearoyl sphingomyelin, and SM(d18:1_18:1) (a type of sphingolipids (SM), a class of phosphosphingolipids) involved in sphingolipid metabolism were significantly elevated (p < 0.05), whereas SM(d17:1_24:1) and SM(d16:1_24:1) were significantly decreased (p < 0.05). Taurine involved in primary bile acid biosynthesis was significantly decreased (p < 0.05). The metabolite of 3-dehydroxycarnitine was significantly decreased in the obese group compared to lean controls. Other compounds, including proline betaine, tryptophyl-serine, N-acetyldopamine, trigonelline, 2-O-acetylarbutin, and 5-aminopentanamide, were also significantly reduced in obese mice (p < 0.05).

Metabolic pathway affected by HFD

The metabolic pathways with a significant difference in response to HFD in obese mice were accessed by KEGG annotation and MetaboAnalyst 3.0 (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) based on p < 0.05 and pathway impact value. To identify the metabolic pathways that exhibited a strong correlation with the differential metabolites, we conducted an additional topological analysis. The result indicated that the differential pathways between the lean and obese mice were mainly related to glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, linoleic acid metabolism, and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (Fig. 3). These pathways serve as the primary focus of the forthcoming discussion. A comprehensive view of metabolic pathway analysis is shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

White adipose tissue (WAT) in human body undergoes excessive expansion and accumulation in obesity (Blüher, 2019; Cai et al., 2020b). As a key regulator of energy homeostasis, WAT functions both as a metabolic rheostat for energy storage/release and as an endocrine organ that secretes leptin and other adipokines (Ahima et al., 1996), while releasing fatty acids into systemic circulation (Pocai et al., 2006). While lipids represent the primary components of WAT, the role of non-lipid/polar metabolites remains understudied (Kučera et al., 2018). Our previous work demonstrated that a 16-week HFD intervention induced metabolic perturbations in liver tissue, cecal contents (Cai et al., 2021), and serum (Cai et al., 2022a). Here, we employed UHPLC-HRMS to investigate the effects of HFD on SAAT in a mouse model of diet-induced obesity. Comparative metabolomic analysis revealed significant differences in adipose tissue metabolite profiles between HFD-fed and lean mice (Fig. 1). Notably, glycerophospholipid metabolism was identified as the core pathway perturbed by the HFD, which is consistent with our previous findings in liver, serum, and cecum (Cai et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2022a; Cai et al., 2022b). Additionally, several other key pathways were markedly perturbed, including sphingolipid metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, linoleic acid metabolism, and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism. Our findings are consistent with a previous study demonstrating that a 15-week HFD induces metabolic perturbations in glycerophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid (FA) metabolism and sphingolipid metabolism in the abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue of male C57BL/6 J mice (Yan et al., 2025). Mitochondrial energy metabolism and branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism were the most significant dysregulated pathways in WAT after 12 weeks of HFD in male C57BL/6 mice (Meierhofer, Weidner & Sauer, 2014). Metabolomic profiling of epididymal WAT revealed pronounced alterations in lipid, amino acid, carbohydrate, nucleotide, and energy metabolism in C57BL/6J mice fed a HFD for 6 weeks compared to those fed a low-fat diet (Cummins et al., 2014). Sex-specific metabolic differences have also been reported in previous studies. Ead et al. (2024), demonstrated via untargeted metabolomics (GC-TOF/MS) that 8 weeks of HFD exposure induced distinct metabolite profiles in the mesenteric adipose tissue (MAT) of mice. Fem perturbations in pathways involving alanine, aspartate, glutamate and beta-alanine metabolism, as well as linoleic and α-linolenic acid metabolism. In contrast, male KC mice displayed perturbations in alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, citric acid cycle (TCA cycle), and linoleic acid metabolism. These interstudy discrepancies studies may arise from variations in experimental design, including dietary composition, intervention duration, and adipose tissue depot specificity.

High-fat diet, obesity, and obesity-related disorders have been consistently associated with altered lipid profiles in both animal and human studies (Park, Sadanala & Kim, 2015). Our findings demonstrate that HFD feeding induces remodeling of membrane lipids, with significant alterations observed in membrane-associated lipids, including glycerophospholipids, lysophospholipids, and sphingolipids. Increased abundance of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and PUFAs within membrane phospholipids can enhance membrane fluidity, improve insulin sensitivity, and promote the recruitment of proteins such as glucose transporter 4 (Valenzuela & Videla, 2011). Our results indicated that HFD feeding decreases membrane fluidity within adipose tissue. These observations underscore that adipose tissue health depends not only on total fat storage capacity but also on lipid composition characteristics, including lipid class distribution, fatty acid chain length, and degree of unsaturation (Roberts, Koulman & Griffin, 2014). We subsequently characterized the significantly differentially abundant lipids. Specifically, two glycerophosphocholines (PCs), including PC(16:0_P-16:0) and PC(20:4_15:0) were significantly increased, while five PCs ((PC(20:2_14:0), PC(22:2_15:0), PC(20:2_15:0), PC(16:0_15:0), and PC(22:4_15:0)) were significantly decreased in obese mice compared to the lean mice (Table 2). PC, also called lecithin, is an important structural component of cell membranes and plasma lipoproteins (Niebergall et al., 2011). In terms of structure, PC consists of a glycerol core, with two long chain hydrophobic fatty acid molecules linked at the sn-1 and sn-2 positions, together with a hydrophilic phosphorylcholine head group (Liu et al., 2020). Particularly in adipocytes under obesity conditions, plasma membranes include phospholipids, with PC being the most abundant (Zeghari et al., 2000). A previous study reported that glycerophosphocholine level was elevated in epididymal fat pads from mice fed a HFD for 6 weeks (Cummins et al., 2014). In a LC-MS/MS study, 12 weeks of HFD feeding in male ApoE−/−mice increased total PC concentrations in epididymal WAT compared to chow-fed controls (Ding et al., 2023). Our observations of changes in membrane lipid levels may be associated with adipocyte hypertrophy and a relative reduction in membrane abundance per tissue mass due to enhanced neutral lipid (e.g., TG) storage.

Furthermore, several lysophospholipid species were significantly decreased in obese mice in the present study, including LysoPC(20:0), LysoPC(18:2), LysoPC(16:0), LysoPC(17:0), and LysoPC(22:6). Lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC) is produced by the removal of one fatty acid group from PCs. These molecules serve as specific biomarkers for metabolic disorders and significantly contribute to the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease by inducing inflammatory and autoimmune responses (Liu et al., 2014), with their altered levels being strongly associated with lipid dysregulation and obesity (Kim et al., 2020). Perturbations were also observed in SMs. SMs are the principal components of lipid rafts within the cell membrane (Lingwood & Simons, 2010). Alterations in sphingolipid levels may impact cell–cell communication (Hakomori & Igarashi, 1995). Notably, sphinganine, stearoyl sphingomyelin, and SM (d18:1_18:1) involved in sphingolipid metabolism were significantly increased in obese mice, while SM(d17:1_24:1) and SM(d16:1_24:1) were significantly decreased. A previous study demonstrated that dietary saturated fat enhances sphingolipid metabolism, which can result in obesity and hyperlipidemia (Chen et al., 2016). These results suggest that there may be different biological functions for different SM species, which need to be further explored. Perturbations in glycerophospholipid metabolism and sphingolipid metabolism indicate alterations in fluidity and stability of the phospholipid bilayer, as well as lipid absorption or metabolism. These associations warrant more detailed investigation in further studies.

Linoleic acid (LA) is a carboxylic acid, and an omega-6 PUFA. Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), the parent essential fatty acid of the omega-3 family, cannot be synthesized by humans or animals and must therefore be obtained from dietary sources (Tinoco, 1982). Omega-6 PUFAs, such as LA, found in vegetable oils, may contribute to obesity, whereas omega-3 PUFAs may exert a protective effect (DiNicolantonio & O’Keefe, 2017). This dichotomy is reflected in the association between chronic inflammation/metabolic disease and an unbalanced omega-6/omega-3 ratio (Miyamoto et al., 2019). In the present study, LA and ALA were significantly decreased in obese mice compared to the lean mice. Our results are consistent with a previous study demonstrating that 12 weeks of HFD feeding decreased LA and ALA concentrations in epididymal WAT (Illesca et al., 2020). Since LA and ALA are essential fatty acids from perturbations in their metabolic processes. A previous study reported that omega-3 PUFAs exert anti-inflammatory effects in brown adipose tissue of Ames dwarf mice (Darcy et al., 2020). A significant increase in ALA level (FA 18:3) was observed in adipose tissue samples after polydextrose (PDX) intervention, which was associated with a significant reduction in epididymal fat weight compared to the control group (Saarinen et al., 2010). Our findings contrast with a previous report showing elevated PUFA levels in inguinal WAT (Baiges-Gaya et al., 2021). A recent study found that LA and ALA were significantly increased in the mesenteric adipose tissue of both male and female mice fed a HFD for 8 weeks (Ead et al., 2024). This discrepancy can be explained by the fact that adipose tissue is the largest organ in the body, with its metabolic function varying not only between different anatomical regions, but also within them, implying a complex regulatory network.

Distinct metabolic profiles were observed between obese and lean mice, encompassing both lipid and non-lipid metabolites. Notably, reduced levels of 3-dehydroxycarnitine and proline betaine were identified in obese mice. As a carnitine derivative structurally similar to carnitine, 3-dehydroxycarnitine may share similar metabolic functions. A recent study found that carnitine lowered TC, low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C), and TG, and increased high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) levels (Askarpour et al., 2019), primarily through its role in facilitating mitochondrial fatty acid transport and subsequent β-oxidation (Flanagan et al., 2010). Our findings are consistent with a previous report demonstrating that HFD feeding significantly decreases various carnitine species (including butyryl-carnitine, hexanoylcarnitine, palmitoyl-L-carnitine, tetradecanoylcarnitine, and hexadecenoyl carnitine) in visceral adipose tissue (VAT) of male C57BL/6J mice (Airaksinen et al., 2018). L-carnitine supplementation in obese mice improves glucose tolerance and increases total energy expenditure (Power et al., 2007; Cave et al., 2008). The microbial metabolite 3-dehydroxycarnitine, produced by cecal Bacteroides, Parasutterella and Prevotella genera (Falony, Vieira-Silva & Raes, 2015), acts as an intermediate metabolite in the intestinal bacterial catabolism of L-carnitine to trimethylamine, and is more abundant in red meat relative to white meat (Koeth et al., 2014). This metabolite is involved in carnitine metabolism in red meat and has been implicated in colorectal cancer, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease (Rombouts et al., 2017). While its tissue-specific functions warrant further elucidation, the observed decrease in 3-dehydroxycarnitine suggests impaired fatty acid metabolism in obesity, potentially contributing to HFD-induced metabolic dysregulation. Stachydrine, also known as proline betaine, exhibits multiple therapeutic effects including anti-obesity, neuroprotective, nephroprotective (Ahmed, Manna & Borah, 2024), and cardioprotective properties, which are mediated through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic mechanisms and calcium handling modulation (He et al., 2024). In 3T3-L1 adipocytes, stachydrine promotes lipolysis while inhibiting lipid accumulation, and it also reduces weight gain and improves glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in a mouse model (Lee et al., 2022). These findings support proline betaine as a promising therapeutic candidate for metabolic syndrome (Ahmed, Manna & Borah, 2024). Proline betaine is a type of betaine derivative and a structural analog of betaine. Dietary betaine (BET, N,N,N-trimethylglycine) supplementation in HFD-fed mice increases adipose tissue betaine and butyrobetaine levels while improving adipose tissue function and hepatic health (Airaksinen et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2010). BET also modulates carnitine metabolism in the liver and muscle of obese mice (Pekkinen et al., 2013). Clinical observations correlate higher betaine intake with reduced adiposity and increased lean mass in both females and males (Gao et al., 2016), while polydextrose intervention increases plasma betaine and L-carnitine levels, which are inversely correlated with TG concentrations (Saarinen et al., 2010). Notably, the decrease in 3-dehydroxycarnitine may partially reflect reduced proline betaine levels, which is consistent with human studies demonstrating adiposity-reducing effects of betaine supplementation (Gao et al., 2019).

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study comprehensively characterizes the metabolic alterations in SAAT induced by HFD consumption 16 weeks. Our metabolomic analysis revealed significant perturbations in multiple lipid classes, including PCs, LysoPCs, O-phosphoethanolamine, linoleic acid, alpha-linolenic acid, proline betaine, and 3-dehydroxycarnitine. The most pronounced metabolic distinctions between obese and lean mice centered on five key pathways, including glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids. These pathway alterations likely impair adipose tissue function by modulating the fluidity and stability of phospholipid bilayer, and disrupting lipid absorption and metabolism. Together, our findings reveal novel metabolic perturbations in adipose tissue during the progression of HFD-induced obesity, highlighting depot-specific dysregulation in SAAT. The identified metabolic signatures not only advance our understanding of pathogenesis of obesity but also uncover potential therapeutic targets for metabolic intervention.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged and addressed. First, the exclusive reliance on a single mouse model limits the generalizability of these findings to human obesity. Second, the dysregulated metabolites identified here require further validation to assess their potential as diagnostic biomarkers for obesity. Third, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of high-fat diet on adipose tissue remain to be elucidated through the application of targeted molecular techniques. Fourth, the metabolic alterations induced by different high-fat diet components or in different genetic obesity models need further investigation. Future studies should focus on characterizing novel adipocyte metabolites and elucidating their molecular mechanisms in the pathogenesis of obesity.
