Microvascular dysfunction in chronic kidney disease: A feasibility study
Authors: Eli Bok, Natasha Wiebe, Michael D Nelson, Michael K Stickland, Aminu K Bello, Branko Braam, Stephanie E Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicrovascular dysfunction contributes to PAD progression, and its detection may facilitate earlier recognition and risk stratification. People with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) have a high burden of PAD, yet early detection is limited by disease‐related changes, such as medial calcification. Near‐infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) is a candidate microvascular tool for early PAD detection, but its feasibility in CKD is unknown.PurposeTo evaluate NIRS feasibility for assessing lower limb microvascular function in participants with CKD Stage 5 (dialysis and non‐dialysis) versus controls.MethodsUsing an ischaemic challenge, lower limb reperfusion kinetics of the tibialis anterior were assessed by NIRS in CKD participants and controls. Feasibility was defined as acceptability, technical limitations, interpretability, and safety; a priori analyses examined associations between participant characteristics and feasibility within CKD. NIRS indices were compared with brachial and popliteal hyperaemic velocity–time integral (VTI).ResultsThirty‐eight CKD and 20 controls were included. Among CKD participants, 79% of tests were acceptable versus 100% of controls, linked to higher ankle systolic blood pressure and BMI. Interpretability was reduced in 39% of CKD tests, unrelated to these characteristics. Popliteal VTI correlated moderately with NIRS reperfusion time from nadir to baseline (r = −0.64) and hyperemic AUC (r = 0.63); brachial VTI did not.ConclusionsNIRS was feasible when tolerated, but acceptability and interpretability limited its use in advanced CKD. Acceptability, but not interpretability, was limited by markers of vascular severity consistent with a calcified, poorly compressible vasculature. Moderate correlations with popliteal VTI support the potential utility of NIRS in this population.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, dialysis, feasibility, feasibility study, microvascular dysfunction, microvascular function, near‐infrared spectroscopy, peripheral arterial disease, skeletal muscle perfusion

INTRODUCTION

Assessing vascular function in severe chronic kidney disease (CKD) is more complicated than in the general population. Medial arterial calcification, highly prevalent in advanced CKD, stiffens the arterial wall and renders vessels poorly compressible, requiring supraphysiologic cuff pressures to achieve occlusion (Goodman et al., 2004; Leskinen et al., 2002). Volume overload and altered tissue composition degrade the optical and hemodynamic signals on which non‐invasive measures rely (Poeze, 2006), while uraemia and autonomic dysfunction blunt the vasomotor responses these tests provoke (Six et al., 2020). Therefore, approaches validated in healthier populations cannot be assumed to transfer to advanced CKD and their feasibility requires evaluation.

The burden of vascular disease in CKD makes this measurement gap an established research priority (Johansen et al., 2021). Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is common among people with CKD affecting 23%–46% of individuals (Cheung et al., 2000; Collins et al., 2012a; Fishbane et al., 1996; Garimella and Hirsch, 2014; Rajagopalan et al., 2006; Testa and Ottavioli, 1998). PAD is a predictor of cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality, non‐traumatic amputations, and foot ulcers (Bourrier et al., 2020; Collins et al., 2012a) and although CKD confers a markedly higher PAD risk than normal kidney function, detection often occurs only after complications have developed (Johansen et al., 2021). This late detection partly reflects the limitations of available screening tests. The ankle‐brachial index (ABI), the first‐line screening test for PAD, has low diagnostic accuracy when medial calcification renders vessels poorly compressible (Leskinen et al., 2002; Ogata et al., 2010). The toe‐brachial index (TBI) is recommended for those with an ABI > 1.4 in the general population, but its incremental accuracy in CKD is not established (Chen et al., 2021; Okamoto et al., 2006). Vascular imaging is often unavailable or infeasible, and the infra‐popliteal lesions that are more common among people with CKD present additional technical challenges (Chan et al., 2010; Garimella et al., 2012; Rudnick et al., 1995).

Microvascular disease often precedes large vessel disease (Olesen et al., 2020) and independently predicts complications. In the general population, a history of microvascular disease conferred a 3.7‐fold higher amputation risk, rising to 22.7‐fold when combined with PAD (Beckman et al., 2019). The microvasculature is therefore an appealing target for earlier detection. Direct methods of microvascular assessment are limited by operator skill, complex analysis and static, spatially restricted measurement (Cao et al., 2011; Figoni et al., 2006; Flammer et al., 2012; Theodorakopoulou et al., 2020). Near‐infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), combined with an arterial occlusion, offers a non‐invasive, cost‐effective, and relatively operator‐independent measure of microvascular function in muscle (Boezeman et al., 2016; Pellicer and Bravo, 2011; Rosenberry and Nelson, 2020; Scheeren et al., 2012; Vardi and Nini, 2008), with a reported sensitivity for diagnosing PAD of 71‐88% (Baker et al., 2017; Collins et al., 2012b; Manfredini et al., 2009; Tuesta et al., 2022). Whether it performs comparably in CKD is unknown (Theodorakopoulou et al., 2020). The same features that complicate vascular testing in advanced CKD, calcification, oedema, and hypertension, may decrease the accuracy of NIRS by attenuating the optical signal and impeding the ischaemic occlusion on which the technique depends (Poeze, 2006).

Our primary aim was therefore to evaluate the feasibility of using NIRS for assessing microvascular function in individuals with severe CKD and Kidney Failure without a history of severe PAD, using a healthy control group to verify the technique. Secondarily, we explored the correlation between NIRS‐derived reactive hyperaemia and a referent measure of microvascular function, the velocity–time integral (VTI) of hyperemic velocity at the brachial and popliteal arteries. We hypothesized that NIRS testing would be feasible in this population. We further anticipated that any barriers encountered would be informative about the vascular phenotype of advanced CKD, reflecting disease severity.

METHODS

Study design

This is a single‐centre cross‐sectional study designed to examine the feasibility of using continuous‐wave NIRS (CW‐NIRS) to assess microvascular function in the lower limbs of individuals with CKD G5 (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) ≤ 15 mL/min), or G5D (G5 and dialysis dependent). Enrolment occurred between October 2022 and October 2023. All participants gave informed written consent, and the study was approved by the University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board (Pro00115743). Study reporting follows the recommendations from the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross‐sectional studies (Vandenbroucke et al., 2007).

Recruitment and eligibility

Haemodialyses (HD) unit and CKD clinic staff helped identify patients based on eligibility criteria and obtained their consent to be contacted by the study coordinator. Study eligibility for those with CKD G5D was initiation of HD within the last 6 months. For those with non‐dialysis CKD, eligibility was an eGFR ≤ 15 mL/min per 1.73 m2 without any contraindication to an ischaemic challenge of the lower limb (lower limb wounds, limb pain at rest consistent with ischaemia, known arterial occlusion). The procedural control group was recruited via posters on University Campus. Eligibility for the procedural control group was based on the absence of characteristics that may complicate obtaining NIRS measurements, that is, non‐smokers without a history of hypertension, diabetes, or kidney disease.

Feasibility

Feasibility was determined by the following criteria, defined a priori as: Acceptability: ≤10% of tests using an ischaemic challenge as a provocative manoeuvre were terminated due to participant discomfort. Technical limitations:≤10% of tests were incomplete due to technical limitations (e.g., no calibration signal). Proportion of uninterpretable tests: ≤10% of completed tests were not interpretable (e.g., incomplete occlusion). Adverse events: a pre‐defined adverse event within 48 h of testing (e.g., test‐induced unfavourable symptoms or signs, such as pain, bruising, or functional impairment).

Data collection

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Demographic information, medical history and medication use were obtained from the electronic medical record and verified with the participant prior to the initial laboratory visit. All collected data were entered into the Research Electronic Data Capture System (REDCap 14.2.0 ©Vanderbilt University) (Harris et al., 2009, 2019). Participants completed a San Diego Claudication Questionnaire (Criqui et al., 1996).

Lab procedures

All participants were instructed to fast for a minimum of 6 h and abstain from caffeine, alcohol, smoking, and vasoactive drugs for 12 h prior to each visit (Thijssen et al., 2019). All laboratory visits were conducted in a temperature‐controlled and dimly lit environment. For reliability measures, individuals in the CKD group attended an additional session scheduled between ≥48 h and 14 days from the first visit at the same time of day. For participants with CKD G5d, vascular testing was scheduled on the second non‐HD‐day for consistency. The same trained kinesiologist (EB) conducted all tests throughout the study.

Ankle‐brachial and toe‐brachial index

The ABI was calculated as a ratio of systolic blood pressure (SBP) between the ankle and brachial artery (Aboyans et al., 2012). The SBP was measured using a Doppler probe (8 MHz, Smartdop XT, KOVEN Technology) following 10 min of supine resting. Higher ankle SBP from each side of either dorsalis pedis or posterior tibialis artery was divided by upper arm SBP (or SBP of the arm without dialysis access) (Gerhard‐Herman et al., 2017). The toe SBP was obtained by placing the cuff on the proximal phalanx in the big toe using photoplethysmographic probe of the Smartdop XT system. The limb with the lower TBI (if not available, lower ABI) was selected for the subsequent measurements.

Velocity–time integral

We selected reactive hyperaemia VTI as our referent measure of microvascular function to compare with NIRS indices of microvascular function. Brachial artery (BA) VTI measured during the first hyperemic heartbeat following the cuff release is a non‐invasive measurement of microvascular function with high predictive validity for cardiovascular events in the general population (Anderson et al., 2015; Anderson et al., 2011; Flammer et al., 2012) and in a Haemodialyses cohort (Ayer et al., 2019). With the participant remaining in the supine position, Duplex ultrasound (8L‐RS 4.0–13.0 MHz probe, GE, Vivid‐Q) was used to acquire B‐mode images for measuring vessel diameter and pulsed‐wave Doppler for measuring blood velocity. If the participant had an arteriovenous fistula or graft on the upper limb, the other arm was used. A manually inflated pneumatic cuff was placed on the forearm distal to the antecubital fossa. The supra‐systolic pressure applied during the ischaemic challenge was 50 mmHg above the brachial SBP for 5 min. The transducer was stabilized proximal to the cuff using a stereotactic probe holder while maintaining the insonation angle of 60° or less with the sample volume adjusted to the vessel diameter. The VTI (cm) was obtained using the commercially available software (FMD studio, version 4.5, Quipu, Pisa, Italy) by measuring the envelope of the first heartbeat of reactive hyperaemia following the period of BA occlusion (Philpott et al., 2009).

Lower limb vascular testing

To account for known blood flow differences in the upper versus the lower limb (Thijssen et al., 2011a), we also measured popliteal artery (PA) VTI as a referent. Following brachial artery reactivity testing, the participant was asked to switch to the lateral decubitus position with the legs supported by inflatable pillows. A rapid inflation cuff (E‐20, Hokanson, Bellevue, WA, USA) was placed on the belly of the anterior tibialis, 5 cm distal to the popliteal fossa. The ultrasound transducer was positioned perpendicularly to the artery being imaged, proximal to the popliteal fossa. The change in arterial diameter and shear rate stimulus were recorded following the same guideline used for BA‐reactivity (Thijssen et al., 2011b). The supra‐systolic pressure applied during ischaemia was 50 mmHg above the ankle SBP.

Near‐infrared spectroscopy

A CW‐NIRS device (Artinis Oxymon Mk III, Artinis Medical Systems, Elst) was used to measure muscle tissue oxygenation:oxygenated (O2Hb), deoxygenated (HHb), or total concentration of haemoglobin (tHb). In skeletal muscle, CW‐NIRS cannot distinguish haemoglobin from myoglobin; therefore, these chromophore signals reflect haemoglobin with a small, inseparable myoglobin contribution. The NIRS signals were collected at 10 Hz frequency and set to an arbitrary value of zero as a baseline signal (30 s prior to the cuff occlusion). We applied a Gaussian moving‐average filter with a 1‐s window to the NIRS signals before analysis, as previously described (Macnab et al., 2019; McCully et al., 1997). A single channel using one light emitter and one receiver optode was placed along the muscle belly of the tibialis anterior distal to the ultrasound transducer and pneumatic cuff. The interoptode distance was maintained at 50 mm and the maximum penetration depth of the light at approximately 25 mm with wavelengths of 761 and 846 nm. The NIRS device was set with a constant differential path length factor of 6.26 (Duncan et al., 1995). The participant's adipose tissue thickness under the probe was measured using the Doppler ultrasound and recorded in millimetres following the PA‐reactivity measurement. The NIRS measurement was obtained during the reactive hyperaemia protocol (PA‐reactivity). The resting baseline value was recorded for 1 min followed by 5 min of supra‐systolic cuff occlusion. Once the cuff was deflated, NIRS signals were recorded continuously for 5 min.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done in Stata/MP 18.0 (www.stata.com). Descriptive statistics were reported as counts and percentages, or medians and inter‐quartile ranges, as appropriate. The validity of NIRS parameters in CKD participants was assessed using Pearson's correlation with the referent standards, popliteal and brachial VTI. Test‐retest reliability was measured using the intraclass correlation (ICC) from a two‐way mixed effects single‐measurement model for absolute agreement in participants with CKD. Participant was treated as a random effect and visit number was a fixed effect. Since no NIRS metric achieved a correlation >0.70 and an ICC > 0.70, measures of diagnostic accuracy were not reported as per protocol.

Characteristics associated with feasibility

To examine whether participant characteristics were associated with feasibility, we compared CKD participants whose tests were terminated for discomfort with those whose were not, and those with uninterpretable tests with those without, on body mass index, ankle systolic blood pressure, and ABI. Comparisons used the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test. Given the low number of participants in each subgroup, these analyses were considered exploratory.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the feasibility of NIRS as a screening tool for subclinical microvascular dysfunction in the lower limbs of individuals with severe CKD and kidney failure. While NIRS is well‐established in other populations (De Blasi et al., 2005; Donati et al., 2016; Gargani et al., 2019; Soares et al., 2019), we found that its feasibility in advanced CKD was limited in ways that appear to reflect the severity of the underlying vascular disease. Discomfort from the ischaemic challenge reduced acceptability and occurred in participants with higher ankle SBP and BMI, characteristics consistent with vascular stiffness that is common in CKD. In addition, a substantial proportion of tests were uninterpretable, which was not explained by the characteristics we measured, suggesting a separate limitation whose basis—technical, physiological, or both—could not be fully resolved from the present data. In an exploratory analysis, several NIRS indices correlated with popliteal but not brachial VTI.

The discomfort associated with lower acceptability was linked to higher ankle SBP, higher BMI, and higher ABI. These associations are directionally consistent with the presence of stiff, poorly compressible arteries resulting from medial arterial calcification, a condition that is highly prevalent in this population (Goodman et al., 2004; Leskinen et al., 2002). From other studies, inflating the cuff to pressures as high as 425–500 mmHg may fail to fully compress arteries in the presence of calcification (Barr et al., 2010; Chandrappa and Deepak, 2010; Jeyaseelan et al., 1981). In contrast to acceptability, in this exploratory analysis, interpretability was not associated with ankle SBP, BMI, or ABI. The uninterpretable tests were not characterized by signal dropout or noise, but by structured, reproducible deviations from the expected occlusion–reperfusion curve, most commonly a paradoxical rise in tHb during occlusion, or a failure to return to baseline after 5 min of reperfusion. The uninterpretable tests observed in CKD participants were also qualitatively distinct from those in controls, who showed a brief, isolated signal drop that resolved into a typical curve, rather than the sustained deviations across the occlusion–reperfusion curve seen in CKD—suggesting these are not simply a higher rate of the same technical phenomenon. Their structured and recurrent nature, together with the standardized single‐operator protocol, argues against random technical failure. We speculate that the absence of association with macrovascular markers could suggest an origin in microvascular or fluid‐related physiology that those markers do not capture.

We used a lower limb arterial occlusion for several reasons. First, vascular dysfunction may be localised to specific areas of the vascular tree and peripheral vascular disease commonly affects the lower limbs. Second, unique to people with severe CKD and kidney failure, surgically created arteriovenous fistulas or grafts in the arm are types of HD access that alter upper‐limb hemodynamics and would limit the use of this technique on the upper limbs (Jindal et al., 2006). Third, to our knowledge, no other studies have applied lower‐limb arterial occlusion in individuals with kidney failure, though ischaemic provocation of the lower‐limb has been successfully used in non‐kidney failure population (Carasca et al., 2017; Gardner, 2014).

Though our findings are exploratory, we found that PA‐VTI correlated with several NIRS indices of microvascular function, whereas BA‐VTI did not. Various non‐invasive microvascular techniques are used to make inferences about microvascular function in other microvascular territories (e.g., retinal arterioles, skin) (Liew et al., 2008; Roustit and Cracowski, 2012). It is likely that heterogeneity in these endothelial phenotypes contributed to the lack of correlation with BA‐VTI (Aird, 2001). Studies have consistently shown significant differences in vascular function between the arm and the leg, attributed to distinct physiological roles and vascular adaptations in these regions (Newcomer et al., 2004; Padilla et al., 2009; Thijssen et al., 2011a). The lower limb is particularly prone to atherosclerotic burden and arterial stiffness in conditions such as CKD and PAD, which can independently affect microvascular function, further decoupling the relationship between BA‐VTI and NIRS parameters.

Studies using NIRS to examine microvascular dysfunction in people with CKD or kidney failure are limited. De Blasi et al. (2009) used NIRS with a venous occlusion of the lower limb in 20 participants undergoing Haemodialyses and observed a reduction in microvascular compliance during Haemodialyses, which was assessed by dividing the increase in total haemoglobin concentration—reflecting arterial blood influx—by the pressure applied during a series of venous occlusions. The underlying mechanisms driving these changes are unclear but could relate reduced intravascular volume from ultrafiltration, HD‐induced stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system, or changes in smooth muscle resulting from rapid changes in electrolytes. Uraemic factors are also likely to play a role. In one study, participants with early to advanced CKD (CKD stages 2–4) had a markedly blunted hyperemic response compared to age‐matched healthy controls, which significantly worsened in patient groups with more advanced CKD (Theodorakopoulou et al., 2023).

Our study has several limitations. We encountered technical challenges with PA‐VTI in CKD participants, which we speculate relate to calcification or other vessel‐wall changes affecting Doppler signal acquisition rather than procedural factors. However, despite the lower number of interpretable tests, we were able to detect a moderate correlation with several NIRS indices. We did not quantify potential confounders known to affect NIRS signal quality, such as skin melanin concentration (Matas et al., 2002) or fluid in the tissues associated with volume overload (Poeze, 2006), which could have introduced variability in NIRS measurements. Likewise, skin adipose tissue thickness is a known confounding factor in NIRS signal quality (Craig et al., 2017; Pirovano et al., 2021; van Beekvelt et al., 2001); however, it did not differ between groups, plausibly because the tibialis anterior, unlike more adiposity‐sensitive sites, accumulates comparatively little subcutaneous fat even at higher BMI, which may also explain why BMI was associated with discomfort, via occlusion mechanics, but not with interpretability, via optical signal attenuation. Finally, as our primary aim was to assess the feasibility of NIRS in CKD, we compared our feasibility parameters to those from a control group where this technique has been established. Without matching on important characteristics, we cannot make inferences about differences in microvascular function.

In summary, our study highlights the potential utility of NIRS for assessing microvascular dysfunction in people with kidney failure. Differences in measurement sites (forearm vs. lower limb), patient characteristics (e.g., blood pressure control), and procedural protocols require further evaluation prior to the implementation of NIRS in advanced CKD populations. Alternative manoeuvres that mitigate the discomfort of supra‐systolic occlusion, such as passive leg raise, may stimulate physiological responses in the lower limbs without requiring high‐pressure cuffs, thereby improving participant acceptability. Additionally, NIRS measurements conducted during exercise represent a minimally invasive approach that has demonstrated potential for assessing microvascular function in clinical and research settings (Alvares et al., 2020; Jones et al., 2016). Whether NIRS detects peripheral arterial disease not captured by ABI remains an open and important question, but one this study was not designed to answer; a direct comparison would require a reference standard for PAD and a population enriched for disease. Our findings define the feasibility barriers that must first be addressed. Lastly, integrating advanced imaging, or novel multi‐distance NIRS combined with diffusion correlation spectroscopy techniques might provide deeper insight. Such methodological refinements are necessary to improve the feasibility of NIRS and its concurrent validity prior to evaluating its prognostic utility in longitudinal studies.
