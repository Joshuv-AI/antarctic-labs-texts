New prognostic index for neoadjuvant chemotherapy outcome in patients with advanced high-grade serous ovarian cancer
Authors: Chuying Huo, Bin Wu, Dongdong Ye, Miaochun Xu, Shaolin Ma, Aoshuang Cheng, Yunyun Liu, Chunxian Huang, Yuhao Zhang, Zhongqiu Lin, Bowen Li, Huaiwu Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA validated prognostic index for the outcome of patients with advanced high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) remains elusive. To address this need, we developed an ovarian neoadjuvant chemotherapy prognostic index (ONCPI) to improve predictive accuracy.MethodsWe encompassed an analysis of the clinicopathological characteristics of patients with advanced HGSOC who were administered platinum-based NACT. Blood inflammatory composite markers were calculated and converted into binary values using optimal cutoffs. Omental hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides were selected for the assessment of chemotherapy response score (CRS), which served as a measure of NACT efficacy. Logistic regression analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression model were utilized to construct a prognostic index.ResultsMultivariate logistic analysis showed that both CRS and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) independently influenced the response to platinum-based chemotherapy. Meanwhile, Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analysis revealed that CRS score was significantly correlated with progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), and patients with high NLR showed poor OS. We further developed an ovarian neoadjuvant chemotherapy prognostic index (ONCPI) based on the CRS and NLR. The area under the curve (AUC) value of ONCPI was 0.771 (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.656–0.887) for the prediction of platinum resistance. This AUC value surpasses that of the individual NLR and CRS, which were 0.670 (P = 0.018, 95% CI: 0.547–0.793) and 0.714 (P = 0.003, 95% CI: 0.590–0.839), respectively. Moreover, survival analysis suggested that patients with ONCPI of 0 and 1 were significantly associated with improved PFS and OS.ConclusionsThe ONCPI emerges as a significant prognostic marker for predicting NACT outcome in advanced HGSOC patients and holds promise for integration into clinical practice and risk-stratified trial design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13324-0.Keywords: High-grade serous ovarian cancer, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Prognostic index

Introduction

Primary debulking surgery (PDS) followed by platinum-based chemotherapy has been globally approved as the standard treatment for high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) [1]. However, the majority of ovarian cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, typically presenting widespread pelvic and abdominal metastases. Meanwhile, extensive surgical intervention often corresponds with a proportional increase in perioperative complications, underscoring the necessity for innovative therapeutic strategies [2]. Over the past decade, in cases of unresectable disease and high perioperative risk, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by interval debulking surgery (IDS) has been recommended in clinical practice guidelines [3], which serves the purpose of reducing tumor burden, thereby enhancing the possibility of achieving R0 excision and minimizing perioperative complications [4]. Despite the high initial response rate to chemotherapy, a proportion of patients still experience relapse [5]. Therefore, there is a pressing demand for developing biomarkers and prognostic factors that can effectively assess the efficacy of treatments and enable the customization of individualized therapeutic strategies.

Various tumor and hematological markers have been used for prognostication in patients with advanced HGSOC [6, 7]. Multiple blood inflammatory composite markers, such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and systemic inflammation index (SII), have been extensively studied in HGSOC for their predictive value of patients’ survival, treatment response, and disease recurrence [8–11]. Mounting evidence suggests that the presence of an inflammatory microenvironment significantly impacts the malignant properties by modulating the biological mechanisms that are integral to tumorigenesis [12]. Changes in these blood inflammatory markers likely reflect uncontrolled inflammation, which may accelerate tumor growth and ultimately result in a poorer patient prognosis [9]. Early identification of such changes may facilitate timely diagnosis and treatment of the disease at an early stage. Thus, evaluation of inflammatory responses is fundamental for prognostication in HGSOC. In addition, the chemotherapy response score (CRS) has been recognized as a pathological tool for forecasting outcomes and informing treatment strategies in epithelial ovarian cancer [13]. The CRS system, initially introduced by Böhm et al., provides a simple and reproducible histopathological grading system [14]. Adhering to 2019 European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines for ovarian cancer, the three-tiered CRS system is now the recommended approach for evaluating chemotherapy responses and for predicting survival outcome among patients who have received NACT [15]. Specifically, CRS1 suggests a minimal chemotherapy response, characterized by few fibroinflammatory changes. CRS2 indicates a moderate response with visible tumor cells and degenerative changes. CRS3 signifies a near-complete response, marked by few scattered tumor foci and significant degeneration-associated changes [16]. Recent research has shown that compared to those with partial or no response, patients with complete response to chemotherapy correlate with notably enhanced survival rates [16]. However, both blood inflammatory markers and the CRS system have limited sensitivity and specificity in identifying patients who benefit from NACT-IDS treatment. Therefore, a more precise and objective system based on validated prognostic markers tailored to the neoadjuvant setting should be further explored.

In the present study, we developed an ovarian neoadjuvant chemotherapy prognostic index (ONCPI) model. Drawing upon objective histopathological and blood parameters readily evaluated in routine clinical practice, this model is designed to predict clinical outcome of advanced HGSOC patients who have undergone NACT followed by surgical resection.

Patients and methods

Patients

This retrospective, single-center study was carried out within the Department of Gynecologic Oncology, Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University in Guangzhou, China. The study cohort comprised patients with advanced HGSOC who had undergone platinum-based NACT prior to IDS and subsequent postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. The patient enrollment spanned from July 2013 to March 2022. This study has been reviewed and granted approval by the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (SYSKY-2023-963-01). Additionally, it has been registered for a clinical trial with the identifier NCT06120309. Eligibility for the study was determined by meeting specific inclusion criteria, which included: (1) a verified diagnosis of HGSOC confirmed by two experienced pathologists; (2) clinical stage III-IV according to the 2018 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) guideline; (3) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECGO) performance status rating between 0 and 1; (4) the absence of any previous anti-cancer therapy; (5) completion of a minimum of ≥ 3 cycles of platinum-based NACT, followed by IDS; and (6) the availability of comprehensive pre-treatment blood test results, along with clinical and imaging data. Exclusion criteria were applied to patients with different pathological types, those who did not receive NACT or IDS, those lacking complete pre-treatment data, and those who were lost to follow up. Ultimately, the study enrolled a total of 139 patients who fulfilled the eligibility criteria.

Clinical characteristics and laboratory testing

This study enrolled patients with advanced HGSOC who met the abovementioned inclusion criteria. The enrolled patients received NACT in a 3-week cycle for ≥ 3 cycles, followed by IDS upon completion of NACT. Computed tomography (CT) images of the included patients were collected before and after NACT. Various clinicopathological variables were retrospectively collected from the medical records, including age, FIGO 2018 clinical stage, histological grade, ECOG score, body mass index (BMI), and routine laboratory parameters. The routine blood tests and tumor marker measurements, including carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), human Epididymis Protein 4 (HE4), and inflammation-related serum biomarkers including neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, fibrinogen, and platelets, were conducted within three days before the first NACT. From these measurements, several ratios were calculated, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), fibrinogen-to-lymphocyte ratio (FLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII = platelet count × NLR). The calculation of Progression-Free Survival (PFS) commenced from the initial administration of Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy (NACT) and extended to the point of disease progression or the occurrence of death from any cause. A parallel approach was applied to the computation of Overall Survival (OS), which started from the first NACT dose and continued until the individual’s death from any cause. For patients who remained free from disease progression or had not succumbed to death by the December 2022 data cut-off, their last recorded follow-up timestamp was utilized for right-censoring in the analysis.

Radiologic assessment and pathological response evaluation

Radiologic assessment was conducted with computed tomography (CT) before and after NACT by a radiologist who was blinded to patients’ clinical information. The response evaluation was conducted according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST, version 1.1). The definitions of the criteria are as follows: Complete Response (CR) entails the disappearance of all target lesions, while Partial Response (PR) denotes at least a 30% decrease in the sum of diameters of target lesions compared to baseline. Progressive Disease (PD) involves at least a 20% increase in the sum of diameters of target lesions, with reference to the smallest sum on study, including the baseline if it’s the smallest. Stable Disease (SD) indicates neither sufficient shrinkage for PR nor sufficient increase for PD, with reference to the smallest sum diameters while on study [17]. For pathological evaluation, the omental specimens, which were excised during the IDS, underwent histological staining with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The slides were meticulously reviewed by two independent gynecologic pathologists, ensuring that they were unaware of the clinical data and each other’s findings. A consensus was diligently reached for the evaluation of each case. It is important to note that the pathology slide derived from omentum, typically the area exhibiting the most robust tumor viability, was specifically chosen for the CRS assessment. This selection was in accordance with the three-tiered CRS system recommended by 2019 ESMO guidelines for ovarian cancer [18]. The CRS scores are delineated as follows: CRS1 indicates equal to no or minimal tumor response; CRS2 signifies incomplete tumor response; CRS3 denotes complete or near-complete response, with minimal or no residual tumor remaining [18].

Statistical analysis

Chi-square tests or Fisher exact tests was used to compared the categorical variables which were described with proportions. Student’s t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were adopted to estimate the continuous variables, which were described with medians (range), medians with interquartile range (IQR), or mean with standard deviation (SD) if applicable. According to the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, the Youden index was used to define the optimal cutoff values for blood test results, and then each indicator was subsequently transformed into “High” and “Low” levels for binary assessment [19]. In this study, based on the ROC analysis, the optimal cutoff value for the NLR was set at 3.61. In addition, the clinical utility of the model based on the net benefit was validated using decision curve analysis (DCA) [19, 20]. To identify independent risk factors for patients’ response to platinum-based chemotherapy, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed. Cox proportional hazards regression model and Kaplan–Meier method was conducted for survival analyses. In regression analysis, multi-classification independent variable should be processed by setting dummy variables. The reported P value in this paper were two-tailed and considered statistically significant when less than or equal to 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA) and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBMCorp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline clinicopathological characteristics of advanced HGSOC patients

Overall, 465 patients with advanced ovarian cancer were enrolled in this study. To identify the independent risk factors and to construct the prognostic index for the outcome of NACT-IDS treatment in the advanced HGSOC patients, the eligible patients were selected based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. The exclusion criteria were as follows: other pathological types (n = 53), without NACT or IDS (n = 251), incomplete pretreatment data (n = 10), and lost to follow-up (n = 12). Finally, 139 advanced HGSOC patients who received NACT-IDS treatment with complete clinicopathological information were included (Fig. 1A). As shown in Table 1, the median age of the 139 patients was 57 years (range, 32–75 years). The BMI was 22.2 ± 3.4 kg/cm2. ECOG score was 0 in 116 patients (83.5%), and 1 in 23 patients (16.5%). Moreover, there were 50 (35.9%) patients with CRS1, 20 (20.9%) patients with CRS2 and 60 (43.2%) patients with CRS3. 119 (85.6%) underwent 3 cycles of NACT and most patients were clinical FIGO 2018 III stage. Given that the aim of our study was to explore a more effective indicator for predicting the efficacy of platinum-based NACT, we only included patients who underwent platinum-based NACT. 24.5% (34/139) of included patients received paclitaxel plus cisplatin, 47.5% (66/139) received paclitaxel plus carboplatin, 23.7% (33/139) underwent intraperitoneal exploration followed by chemotherapy with paclitaxel plus cisplatin, and then continued with paclitaxel plus carboplatin, and 4.3% (6/139) of patients were treated with a regimen containing doxorubicin/gemcitabine plus platinum. The outcome of IDS was evaluated by the extent of the residual disease after cytoreduction. According to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline, residual disease measuring less than 1 cm defines optimal cytoreduction [21]. In our study, 80.6% (112/139) of patients exhibited no residual disease (R0) after cytoreduction, while 15.8% (22/139) of patients had residual disease measuring greater than 0 but less than 1 cm (R1), which could be considered as optimal cytoreduction. However, three patients (2.2%) had residual disease exceeding 1 cm (R2) and two patients (1.4%) did not have specific information about residual lesions. Radiologic assessment revealed that 13 patients (7.2%) achieved a CR, 78 patients (43.1%) had a PR, 33 patients (18.2%) experienced SD, and 4 patients (2.2%) showed PD. Additionally, 11 patients (6.1%) were referred from other hospitals and had missing radiologic data prior to NACT. The adjuvant chemotherapy regimen was primarily the first-line combination of paclitaxel and platinum, with 22 patients (15.8%) also receiving bevacizumab during this phase. Following NACT-IDS and postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, treatment efficacy was assessed according to NCCN guidelines [21]. For primary tumor, 115 (82.7%) patients who relapsed 6 months or more after initial chemotherapy were termed platinum-sensitive. In contrast, 24 (17.3%) patients whose disease recurred in less than 6 months were classified as platinum-resistant. Of note, the median follow-up time was 41.0 months (IQR, 27.0–58.0 months), with 51.1% (71/139) patients experiencing recurrence and 23.0% (32/139) mortality. Additionally, we incorporated a waterfall plot of platinum-free interval (PFI) alongside individual patient profiles to elucidate the association between key risk factors and the efficacy of platinum-based chemotherapy (Fig. 1B). From the Fig. 1B, the CRS1 and high level of NLR both are commonly observed in platinum-resistant patients, while other clinicopathological features appear to lack a strong correlation.

CRS and NLR as independent risk factors for the efficacy of NACT-IDS treatment in advanced HGSOC patients

To identify independent risk factors for the efficacy of NACT-IDS treatment, we further analyzed the correlation between clinicopathological features, and chemotherapy response in advanced HGSOC patients (Tables 2 and Fig. S1). According to the ROC analysis, the optimal cutoff values for blood test results were defined based on the Youden index, and then each indicator was subsequently transformed into “High” and “Low” levels for binary assessment. The defined optimal cutoff values were displayed in Table S1. We took the presence of “platinum sensitive/platinum resistance” as the endpoint and set the step in probability of multivariate analysis as P < 0.050 and the exclusion probability as P > 0.050. Then we set dummy variables for “CRS”, “NACT regimens”, “Residual disease after IDS” and “Radiographic response”, with “CRS3”, “TP”, “R0” and “CR” as the reference classification, respectively. The univariate logistic analysis suggested that CRS1 (P = 0.002, odds ratio (OR): 6.588, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.033–21.346), CRS3 (P = 0.004, OR = 1 [reference]), neutrophil count (P = 0.016, OR = 3.111, 95% CI: 1.230–7.867), Radiographic response (CR: P = 0.022, OR = 1 [reference]), NLR (P = 0.028, OR = 3.582, 95% CI: 1.151–11.152) and SII (P = 0.014, OR = 3.160, 95% CI: 1.258–7.934) correlated with response to the platinum-based chemotherapy (Tables 2 and Fig. S1A). Furthermore, the multivariate logistic analysis revealed that CRS1 (P = 0.011, OR = 5.389, 95% CI: 1.480-19.621), CRS3 (P = 0.013, OR = 1 [reference]), and NLR (P = 0.034, OR = 4.818, 95% CI: 1.122–20.687) were significantly correlated with the efficacy of chemotherapy, which confirmed that both CRS and NLR were independent predictors of response to platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with advanced HGSOC (Tables 2 and Fig. S1B).

Further exploring the relationship between CRS and NLR, we found that 66.6% of platinum-resistant patients corresponded to CRS1, while 48.7% of platinum-sensitive patients were CRS3 (Fig. 2A). However, no discernible correlation emerged between CRS and other clinical characteristics of advanced HGSOC patients (Fig. 2B-D). We assessed the association between CRS and the platinum-free interval time in two representative cases (Case 1: platinum-resistant and Case 2: platinum-sensitive) (Fig. 2E). The results further demonstrated that CRS had a promising predictive value for platinum-based chemotherapy response. Moreover, we evaluated the relationship between NLR and diverse clinicopathological factors in our cohort, which showed that platinum-resistant patients tended to have higher NLR (Fig. S2A). Interestingly, NLR was associated with CRS. The higher NLR in CRS1 than in CRS3 suggested that the inflammatory conditions might lead to unresponsiveness to NACT treatment (Fig. S2B). However, there was no correlation between NLR and other clinical characteristics of advanced HGSOC patients (Fig. S2C-F). Taken together, these results highlighted the robust predictive value of CRS and NLR in determining the responsiveness of advanced HGSOC patients to platinum-based chemotherapy.

CRS and NLR as robust prognostic factors for advanced HGSOC patients in the NACT-IDS regimen

Numerous studies have linked chemotherapy-induced resistance to unfavorable long-term outcome in advanced HGSOC patients [22, 23]. Although our data indicated that CRS and NLR were independent risk factors for platinum-based chemotherapy efficacy, their prognostic significance in advanced HGSOC patients remained uncertain. Univariate and multivariate analyses of PFS using the Cox proportional hazards model were detailed in Table 3 and Fig. S3. After setting dummy variables and defining the reference classification, the univariate analysis revealed that CRS1 (P = 0.000, hazard ratio (HR) = 3.785, 95% CI: 2.092–6.847), CRS2 (P = 0.000, HR = 3.365, 95% CI: 1.814–6.240), CRS3 (P = 0.000, HR = 1 [reference]), neutrophil count (P = 0.004, HR = 2.009, 95% CI: 1.257–3.213), fibrinogen concentration (P = 0.049, HR = 1.666, 95% CI: 1.003–2.768) and NLR (P = 0.013, HR = 1.932, 95% CI: 1.150–3.247) were significantly associated with shorter PFS. Multivariable analysis confirmed that CRS1 (P = 0.000, HR = 3.420, 95% CI: 1.864–6.276), CRS2 (P = 0.000, HR = 3.366, 95% CI: 1.775–6.380), CRS3 (P = 0.000, HR = 1 [reference]) and higher neutrophil count (P = 0.034, HR = 1.708, 95% CI: 1.042–2.797) were associated with inferior PFS. Similarly, in univariate analysis of OS, CRS1 (P = 0.000, HR = 6.464, 95% CI: 2.392–17.468), CRS2 (P = 0.043, HR = 3.174, 95% CI: 1.038–9.704), CRS3 (P = 0.001, HR = 1 [reference]), R0 resection (P = 0.020, HR = 1 [reference]), R2 resection (P = 0.002, HR = 10.661, 95% CI: 2.419–46.982), neutrophil count (P = 0.000, HR = 4.210, 95% CI: 1.882-9.419), fibrinogen concentration (P = 0.026, HR = 2.280, 95% CI: 1.105–4.704), NLR (P = 0.002, HR = 9.304, 95% CI: 2.219–39.001) and SII (P = 0.002, HR = 3.101, 95% CI: 1.511–6.361) were related to inferior OS. These variables were then inputted a multivariable Cox hazard model. The analyses showed that CRS1 (P = 0.009, HR = 3.955, 95% CI: 1.419-11.026), CRS2 (P = 0.026, HR = 3.564, 95% CI: 1.163-10.922), CRS3 (P = 0.026, HR = 1 [reference]), R0 resection (P = 0.003, HR = 1 [reference]), R2 resection (P = 0.000, HR = 47.298, 95% CI: 6.316–354.180), and NLR (P = 0.016, HR = 5.978, 95% CI: 1.388-25.742) were independent prognostic factors for OS in advanced HGSOC patients (Table 4 and Fig. S4).

ONCPI as a new prognostic index for NACT-IDS treatment in patients with advanced HGSOC

Given the robust prognostic value of both CRS and NLR, we speculated that their combination might provide insight into NACT treatment resistance among advanced HGSOC patients, leading to the development of an ovarian neoadjuvant chemotherapy prognostic index (ONCPI). The ONCPI scoring system was established as follows: low NLR (≤ 3.61) was scored 0, high NLR (> 3.61) was scored 1 and CRS1, CRS2, CRS3 were scored 2, 1, 0, respectively. Therefore, the ONCPI score ranged from 0 to 3, i.e., Score 0: low NLR (≤ 3.61) + CRS3; Score 1: low NLR (≤ 3.61) + CRS2 or high NLR (> 3.61) + CRS3; Score 2: low NLR (≤ 3.61) + CRS1 or high NLR (> 3.61) + CRS2; Score 3: high NLR (> 3.61) + CRS1. Of the 139 evaluated patients, 27 (19.4%) had an ONCPI score of 0, 46 (33.1%) had an ONCPI score of 1, 28 (20.1%) had an ONCPI score of 2 and 38 (27.4%) had an ONCPI score of 3. Median PFS was 28.1 months (IQR, 17.2–41.4 months), 25.6 months (IQR, 15.1–33.2 months), 15.6 months (IQR, 12.0-25.1 months) and 13.1 months (IQR, 9.6–21.6 months) for the ONCPI score of 0, 1, 2, 3, respectively (P < 0.001). In addition, median OS was 40.5 months (IQR, 27.7–51.8 months), 30.5 months (IQR, 22.7–45.4 months), 29.6 months (IQR, 20.1–42.6 months) and 24.2 months (IQR, 12.0-38.4 months) for the ONCPI score of 0, 1, 2, 3, respectively (P < 0.001) (Table 5). The Kaplan-Meier survival curve showed that patients with ONCPI scores of 0 and 1 had significantly improved PFS and OS compared to those with the scores of 2 and 3 (Fig. 4A-B). Next, we investigated the predictive value of NLR and CRS in discriminating resistant patients. The area under the curve (AUC) of NLR was 0.670 (P = 0.018, 95% CI: 0.547–0.793), while the AUC of CRS was 0.714 (P = 0.003, 95% CI: 0.590–0.839). Intriguingly, the AUC of ONCPI score was 0.771 (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.656–0.887) (Fig. 5A). Additionally, our analysis revealed that the “ONCPI score” had a larger area under the decision curve compared to the area under the curve for any individual variable assessed, which indicated that ONCPI might have a higher clinical decision-making value (Fig. 5B). While the ONCPI model only marginally increased the AUC for predicting chemoresistance in patients with advanced HGSOC, it demonstrated a significantly higher specificity of 80%, maintaining a sensitivity level of 70%, which was superior to the specificity offered by the CRS. In contrast, the NLR showed the highest sensitivity in predicting platinum resistance, but its specificity was quite low, at only 41.1% (Fig. 5C, D). These results indicated that the ONCPI model incorporating CRS and NLR could be a promising diagnostic tool for predicting the chemotherapy response in advanced HGSOC patients in the NACT setting.

Discussion

Ovarian cancer stands as the foremost fatal gynecological cancer, distinguished by its high rate of mortality [24]. Current treatment options include surgery, drug treatment and radiotherapy [25]. However, it is crucial to note that platinum-based chemotherapy continues to be the fundamental treatment for ovarian cancer. NACT is a common approach to treating ovarian cancer patients with advanced disease or when primary surgery is either unfeasible or unsafe [5, 26]. Growing evidence endorses the benefits of NACT in advanced HGSOC patients, potentially leading to improved PFS and OS [27, 28]. In light of this, we undertook a retrospective study to identify the independent prognostic markers, and to develop a more precise, objective, and reproducible evaluation system to evaluate chemotherapy efficacy in advanced HGSOC patients who underwent NACT followed by IDS.

As we know, the CRS system is typically employed to evaluate the efficacy of NACT in advanced HGSOC patients [1]. Additionally, CRS serving as a pathological metric may help to assess the prognosis and steer treatment decisions for patients with epithelial ovarian cancer. In previous studies, the classification of CRS often groups CRS1 and CRS2 together as CRS1/2, with CRS3 as another category. CRS1/2 is typically associated with a poorer prognosis, while CRS3 related to better one. Betrian [15] et al. evaluated survival outcome according to the CRS (CRS1/2 and CRS3) and the number of NACT cycles in advanced ovarian cancer patients, discovering that CRS3 was associated with improved oncological outcomes, irrespective of the NACT cycle count. Liontos [6] et al. found that lymphocytic infiltration in pre-chemotherapy biopsies was associated with CRS3 post-NACT. Then they utilized Kaplan-Meier estimates to describe and visualize the effect of NLR and CRS on OS and PFS, and found that NLR was associated with OS, revealing that NLR was associated with OS, and CRS (CRS1/2) with PFS in advanced ovarian cancer patients treated with NACT. However, the 2019 ESMO ovarian cancer guidelines [18] highlighted the fundamental differences between CRS1 and CRS2 in assessing tumor chemosensitivity, with CRS1 indicating minimal response and CRS2 suggesting a moderate response. Therefore, the impact of the three-tiered CRS system on the efficacy of NACT efficacy and prognosis in advanced HGSOC patients should also be analyzed separately. In our cohort, we incorporated the NLR level and CRS assessment, including CRS1, CRS2, and CRS3 into logistic regression analysis. The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed a significant association between CRS and NLR with platinum-based chemotherapy efficacy. However, nearly 29.6% of CRS1 patients were recognized as platinum-sensitive, and some of them had a favorable prognosis. And the CRS only had a limited accuracy in predicting patients’ chemotherapy response (AUC = 0.714, sensitivity: 70% and specificity: 71.1%). These results demonstrated that CRS could be an appropriate predictor, but its accuracy in predicting the efficacy of NACT in advanced HGSOC patients needed further improvement.

Growing evidence shows that NLR is a potential prognostic marker in various cancers including HGSOC [29–32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated NLR level, either before or during NACT, may be associated with a poorer prognosis in ovarian cancer patients [6, 33, 34]. The biological basis of its prognostic value is believed to stem from its ability to reflect a systemic inflammatory state. Elevated NLR often signifies systemic inflammation, which has been implicated to critical processes in cancer, such as tumorigenesis, progression, and poor prognosis [35, 36]. The activation of inflammatory cells results in the generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, which are capable of causing DNA damage and leading to genomic instability, thus potentially triggering the onset of cancer [37]. These activated cells can also disrupt the mechanisms responsible for DNA repair [38]. Additionally, inflammatory conditions are known to boost the secretion of growth factors and cytokines. These substances can bestow a stem-like characteristic upon cancer precursor cells and also stimulate the overproduction of angiogenic factors, a process often referred to as the “angiogenic switch,” which aids in the advancement of tumors [38]. Among the inflammatory cells, neutrophils have garnered significant research attention, with a wealth of evidence highlighting their contribution to the enhancement of tumor growth and the progression of metastatic development [39]. Their ability to secrete proteases, particularly matrix metalloproteases, significantly enhances tumor invasion. Moreover, neutrophils are also involved in tumor progression by activating signal transducers and activators of transcription 3 (STAT3) and to promote neo-angiogenesis [40]. Recent studies have demonstrated that elevated inflammatory cells such as neutrophils in the tumor microenvironment are associated with resistance to chemotherapy and immunotherapy [41, 42]. These findings may explain why NLR is an important prognostic factor for advanced HGSOC patients. In our study, we observed that patients who were platinum-resistant tended to have high NLR, whereas those with lower NLR were generally more responsive to platinum-based chemotherapy, which aligned with the results of prior investigations in the field. Although the independent prediction of NLR was not confirmed in multivariate Cox regression analysis for PFS, low NLR significantly prolonged OS in advanced HGSOC patients. Therefore, the NLR level may serve as a key piece of information to aid clinical decision-making. These findings suggested that the CRS and NLR might be the potential independent prognostic factors for NACT-treated advanced HGSOC patients.

At present, a growing number of studies have investigated various combined indexes to predict the prognosis of ovarian cancer, typically integrating NLR with hematological markers, such as the plasma fibrinogen concentration and PLR characteristics [9, 33, 34]. Yang et al. [9] included 281 epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) patients who underwent surgery and developed a combined plasma fibrinogen and neutrophil lymphocyte ratio (F-NLR) score to predict EOC prognosis, finding that the pre-operative F-NLR score was a promising prognostic indicator for EOC patients. Zhao et al. [33] conducted a meta-analysis of 13 retrospective studies to evaluate the prognostic impact of NLR and PLR on ovarian cancer, supporting that NLR/PLR could be effective prognostic predictors for patients with ovarian cancer. These studies involved the total EOC or HGSOC patients. Although these combined indexes have demonstrated their influence on disease progression and response to treatment, there are no articles that have combine these two factors and quantify them in a scoring system to evaluate chemotherapy efficacy in advanced HGSOC patients receiving NACT. It is rare to find an index that encompasses both blood inflammatory composite markers and pathology index specifically in the NACT patient population of HGSOC. Therefore, we combined CRS with NLR levels, and quantified them in a scoring system, ultimately deriving the ONCPI predictive model. Although the combination led to a modest increase in the AUC of ROC analysis (AUC values for CRS, NLR, and ONCPI = 0.714, 0.670, and 0.771, respectively), our ONCPI model demonstrated a higher sensitivity and specificity in predicting the efficacy of platinum-based chemotherapy in ovarian cancer. High specificity is particularly crucial for the precise identification of patients with platinum resistance, who require regular monitoring and even early access to additional medication. At an equivalent sensitivity level, ONCPI demonstrated a higher specificity of 80% compared to CRS, and DCA analysis indicated that ONCPI has greater value in clinical decision-making. In contrast, while NLR exhibited the highest sensitivity in predicting platinum resistance, its specificity was relatively low at only 41.1%. Our findings underscore the advantage of ONCPI in providing a more reliable prediction with a better balance between sensitivity and specificity. Importantly, NLR as a readily available and cost-effective biomarker that could be easily obtained through routine testing of a patient’s blood. Meanwhile, CRS could be assessed by H&E staining of the omental tissue after NCAT-IDS treatments, sparing patients the burden of additional injuries and medical expenses. Therefore, the ONCPI model may offer an approach by providing a more comprehensive and intuitive assessment of host immune status and the tumor response to platinum-based chemotherapy and may hold potential benefits for risk stratification and identification of advanced HGSOC patients who will benefit from more aggressive therapeutic strategies or additional follow-up care.

Despite these promising findings, our study has several limitations, such as its retrospective nature, single-institution focus, and the modest scale of the sample size. Further validation in larger, prospective, and multi-center studies are warranted to confirm the predictive validity of the ONCPI score across a broader patient demographic.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the significance of CRS and NLR as independent prognostic factors. Moreover, by using these readily accessible clinical parameters, we have effectively developed a prognostic index tailored to advanced HGSOC patients undergoing NACT. The application of the ONCPI score offers potential to shape the course of future clinical trials and facilitate the development of personalized treatment strategies.
