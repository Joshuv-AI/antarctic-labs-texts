Perceptions of frail older adults and professionals on adherence to a multidomain community programme to prevent disability: a descriptive qualitative study of + AGIL Barcelona
Authors: Mercè Sitjà-Rabert, Sebastián Nazar-González, Lluvi Farré Montalà, Júlia Ollé-Gonzalez, Luís Soto-Bagaria, Laura Mónica Pérez, Sonia Baró Sabaté, Olga Güibas-Roura, Paula Espí-Vallvé, Andrea González, Marco Inzitari, Aida Ribera, Olga Canet-Vélez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSustained adherence is essential for the effectiveness of community-based multidomain interventions aimed at preventing disability in older adults with initial frailty. This study explores the perspectives of older adults and primary care professionals involved in the + AGIL Barcelona programme to understand the factors that facilitate or hinder sustained adherence and provide evidence for improving its implementation and scalability.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted in three primary care centres in Barcelona implementing the + AGIL programme. Participants were purposively selected to ensure information-rich perspectives. Six homogeneous focus groups were conducted with older adults meeting criteria for initial frailty (n = 22; mean age 82 years) and primary care professionals (n = 18), facilitated by two experienced qualitative researchers. Discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis. Rigour was ensured through independent coding, reflexive discussion, consensus, and data saturation confirmation.ResultsAdherence emerged as a multidimensional and context-dependent process shaped by professional, personal, and community-related factors. Empathetic communication, individualised exercise prescription, and group cohesion acted as key facilitators for motivation, autonomy, and the adoption of new healthy routines. Conversely, caregiving responsibilities, socioeconomic vulnerability, and limited access to community resources hindered the maintenance of behaviours over time. Organisational continuity and stronger healthcare–community coordination were perceived as essential for sustainability.ConclusionAdherence to multidomain interventions depends not only on programme content but also on delivery processes and contextual embedding within participants’ lived realities. Strengthening behavioural support and healthcare–community integration is vital to enhance long-term sustainability and scalability in primary care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-026-01484-0.Keywords: Frailty, Healthy ageing, Integrated care, Multidomain intervention, Experiences and perspectives, Adherence

Introduction

Population ageing poses critical challenges to health systems and social well-being [1]. The proportion of individuals aged 80 years and older in the European Union is projected to rise from 5.6% to 14.6% by 2100, reflecting a profound demographic shift accompanied by an increased prevalence of age-related health issues, particularly frailty induced by poor intrinsic capacity [2, 3].

Intrinsic capacity (IC) is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the sum of an individual’s physiological reserves, which interact with the environment to maintain functional status. Frailty, in contrast, represents a state of increased vulnerability resulting from a decline in IC, and it can potentially be reversed through timely identification and intervention [4, 5]. IC provides a holistic framework for proactively addressing functional decline and promoting healthy ageing. The Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework, developed by the WHO, promotes integrated interventions addressing multiple domains of intrinsic capacity to preserve IC and delay frailty progression [6]. Multidomain interventions combining exercise, optimised nutrition, pharmacological treatment, and social support have demonstrated efficacy in improving IC and reducing frailty [7–10].

The + AGIL Barcelona programme is a multidomain initiative targeting frailty in community-dwelling older adults through personalised interventions, including structured exercise sessions; education workshops on healthy habits (e.g. Mediterranean diet promotion, sleep hygiene, social isolation support); pharmacological optimization; and community-based follow-up, co-designed by healthcare teams, individuals, and caregivers [11–13]. Initially implemented in one area of Barcelona, + AGIL integrates primary care, geriatrics, and community resources, following a comprehensive geriatric assessment to design tailored care plans [6]. Currently, a stepped-wedge cluster randomised analysis is underway to evaluate the implementation process and assess the scalability of the programme to other areas of Barcelona (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06836167). This evaluation seeks to refine the intervention and ensure its broader applicability within diverse healthcare settings.

Adherence is critical to the success of interventions targeting frailty and IC. Defined as "the extent to which patient behaviours align with healthcare provider recommendations," adherence significantly influences therapeutic outcomes [14]. However, adherence rates in exercise-based interventions for older adults are low, with only 23% maintaining long-term engagement [15]. Poor adherence undermines the effectiveness of these interventions [8, 15], highlighting the urgent need to explore behaviour change strategies that can enhance long-term adherence in complex community-based interventions [16]. A previous qualitative study on the + AGIL programme explored the experiences of participants and professionals, identifying barriers and facilitators to the implementation of the programme, such as the perception of frailty and the need for technological support [17]. However, a significant gap remains regarding the dynamics of long-term adherence [18]. The present study expands this line of research by focussing specifically on adherence and its maintenance over time, examining how behavioural, personal, professional, and contextual factors influence the sustained participation of frail older adults in the + AGIL Barcelona programme. In addition, the geographical and social scope has been broadened by including three primary care centres located in neighbourhoods with diverse socioeconomic characteristics, providing a more representative view of the community context. The main objective of this study is to understand the factors that facilitate or hinder sustained adherence of frail older adults to a multidomain community-based programme, providing useful evidence to improve its implementation and scalability within primary care settings.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative descriptive design was chosen to explore the experiences and perspectives of older adults and healthcare professionals involved in the + AGIL Barcelona programme [19]. + AGIL is an implementation programme aimed at preventing disability in older adults with initial frailty or an initial reduction of the intrinsic capacity, through a multidomain approach based on an initial comprehensive geriatric assessment in primary care centres, co-designed with end-users and integrating primary and geriatric care with existing community resources [11]. The programme, included in the WHO as an example of an innovative field programme [20], started in 2016 in one primary care area of Barcelona and has been scaled-up in three new sites. The qualitative approach is suitable for understanding complex and multidimensional phenomena, such as adherence to multidomain interventions, within their specific contextual and experiential dimensions. This study was conducted following the COREQ checklist to ensure methodological rigour and transparency in reporting (Tong et al., 2007).

Context and participants

As mentioned, after being in place on the original site since 2016, the programme has been scaled-up to three new primary healthcare centres (PHCC) in Barcelona: CAP Barceloneta, CAP Vila Olímpica, and CAP Larrard, located in geographically and socioeconomically diverse neighbourhoods.

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling, based on predefined inclusion criteria and the researchers’ judgement of their potential to provide rich and relevant insights. This assessment was guided by established qualitative sampling principles, considering each participant’s direct experience with the + AGIL programme, willingness and communicative ability to articulate their perspectives, and diversity in functional status and age to ensure information-rich and varied cases [21, 22]. The selection also considered participants’ level of engagement, time invested in the programme’s implementation, and their ability to reflect on their experiences. All primary care professionals (PCP) involved in the programme at the intervention sites were invited to participate.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

Older adults: (1) meeting the criteria for initial frailty according to the Gerontopole Screening Frailty Tool, following the tool’s original design, the final frailty classification being determined by the physician’s clinical judgement after completing the initial screening items, (2) completing the + AGIL programme at any participating PHCCs, (3) being willing and able to express their experience during the programme, and (4) providing signed informed consent.

Older adults: (1) meeting the criteria for initial frailty according to the Gerontopole Screening Frailty Tool, following the tool’s original design, the final frailty classification being determined by the physician’s clinical judgement after completing the initial screening items, (2) completing the + AGIL programme at any participating PHCCs, (3) being willing and able to express their experience during the programme, and (4) providing signed informed consent.

Professionals: (1) employment as a primary PCP at any of the PHCCs where the programme was implemented, (2) active involvement in the + AGIL programme’s implementation, and (3) provision of signed informed consent.

Professionals: (1) employment as a primary PCP at any of the PHCCs where the programme was implemented, (2) active involvement in the + AGIL programme’s implementation, and (3) provision of signed informed consent.

All participants were personally invited via email or telephone and were provided with detailed information about the study’s objectives, procedures, and ethical safeguards.

Data collection

Six focus groups (FGs) were conducted between April and July 2024, including three with frail older adults (FOA) and three with PCP involved in the programme. Focus groups were homogeneous, with separate groups conducted for older adults and healthcare professionals; no mixed groups were held. The FGs were held at the respective PHCCs to provide a familiar environment for participants.

A semi-structured script (Table 1), developed by two experienced qualitative researchers (OCV, MSR), was used to guide the discussions. Each FG lasted 50–70 min, was audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. Field notes captured contextual details. Confidentiality and anonymity were ensured through transcript pseudonymization.

Each FG was facilitated by two researchers (SNG, JOG, OCV, OGR), including a moderator with expertise in qualitative methods (OCV, MSR).

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was performed following Braun and Clarke’s approach [23–25]. Transcripts were imported into ATLAS.ti software, version 24 for thematic analysis as outlined by Braun and Clarke [24]. Three researchers (OCV, MSR, and SNG) independently coded the data, and discrepancies were resolved through reflexive discussion until full consensus was reached on all units of analysis. Data saturation was confirmed through sequential analysis of each FG, ensuring no new themes emerged. Rigour was maintained through researcher reflexivity, transparent coding, and adherence to the COREQ checklist [26], ensuring consistency and reliability in the analytical process.

Results

The sample comprised 40 participants: 22 FOA and 18 PCP. Table 2 presents the characteristics of the study participants. A total of 40% were vulnerable to mild frailty, exhibiting a good level of autonomy (median Barthel Index: 95 [IQR 85–100]), and 86% were on polypharmacy.

Concerning the PCP involved in the study, 14 were women (78%) and four were men, with an average of 11.12 years of professional experience. The most represented profession in the focus groups was nursing (n = 4), followed by physiotherapy (n = 3), medicine (n = 3), and psychology (n = 3). Results of the focus groups

The FGs’ results are divided into two main themes: 1) factors influencing adherence and 2) the programme’s impact on behaviour change. The results for each theme are presented separately for each participant group: first, the perspectives of FOAs, followed by those of PCPs.

Perspective of frail older adults (FOA)

Theme 1: Results regarding factors influencing adherence

The factors influencing adherence are presented below, organised into four categories: characteristics of PCPs, community-related factors, aspects related to FOAs, and specific programme elements.

1. Characteristics of primary care professionals

FOA valued the kindness, empathy, and adaptability shown by PCP, emphasising their verbal and non-verbal communication during sessions and their ability to listen and tailor activities to individual needs.

- “The psychologist at the (…) is such a wonderful person. For us, as older adults, we don’t want to feel ridiculous. But she gives you confidence through her words. That is very valuable.” FOA, Participant B_u4.

-“It’s the way they explain things, the way they treat people. Their smile—I don’t know. They don’t say anything, but it says everything.” FOA, Participant B_u1.

-“It’s how they interact with the person, how they adjust the level (of the exercise) up or down depending on what the other person needs.” FOA, Participant VO_u2.

-“The professionals. From the two I’ve had, they listen, they are very kind, welcoming, and they always have time to listen to what you want to say.” FOA, Participant L_u10.

2. Community-related factors

Older adults highlighted the need for better coordination between primary care and community resources to maintain exercise habits. They appreciated local recreational options but often found them unsuitable or difficult to access.

-“I wish the primary care centre could coordinate, for example, with the local gym to create an exercise programme similar to what the +AGIL programme offers.” FOA, Participant VO_u2.

-"At [community centre], they offer yoga and Tai Chi. I’ve done Tai Chi for four years to practice the postures, but some of the postures there are not suitable for me." FOA, Participant VO_u4.

3. Related to characteristics of frail older adults

FOA valued that the programme was tailored to people of a similar age and condition, which made them feel understood and motivated.

“I think the fact of having to leave the house, to go to a place where there are people more or less your age, to do exercises that are beneficial.” FOA, Participant L_u1.

4. Related to the specific characteristics of the + AGIL programme

The sense of belonging generated within the group was considered one of the main facilitators of adherence. Shared experiences and social support fostered motivation and continuity. They also valued the material resources provided by the + AGIL programme.

-“Honestly, I’m very happy with what I’ve done here because I found, above all, a group where we were very, very united, and we all got along very well.” FOA, Participant VO_u6.

-“The [physiotherapist] gave me a little booklet, and I keep it on the sofa with the elastic band, just like she said. I just finished doing it now[...]. And with what the [physiotherapist] gave me, I feel great”. FOA, Participant B_u2.

The programme’s impact on behaviour change is organised into three categories: the new habits developed, improvement proposals, and barriers to maintaining learned habits.

1. New habits developed

FOA identified positive effects of the programme, both physical and emotional, which enhanced their motivation and supported the adoption of new habits.

Because before, when I went out, I used to think, today I have to go here, there, and plan how long I would walk, where I would stop and rest. But now I don’t think about it as much. I just say, okay, let’s go. For me, that’s a little miracle.” FOA, Participant B_u1.

FOA noted that recognising the programme’s benefits boosted their confidence, helping them face challenges with greater determination and independence.

-“I feel better. By doing the exercises, yes, definitely, because otherwise I was deteriorating. And now I can walk without a cane at home.” FOA, Participant B_u3.

Socialisation was a key motivator for adherence. FOA noted that comparing themselves with peers positively influenced their engagement and motivation.

-“You see other people who are in worse condition, and I say, well, I compare myself to them and think, I’m doing so little! They’re going here, they’re going there, and I feel a bit self-conscious. And I think, well, maybe I can do more too…” FOA, Participant VO_u1.

-“Bringing the groups together from time to time to see what one person has done, what another has done… just sharing simple information about what we’ve been doing because maybe in that information, someone might find something useful for themselves.” FOA, Participant VO_u6.

FOA reported that the programme positively impacted their daily lives. Some incorporated exercises into their routines, resumed previous activities, or improved balance, reducing fall risk.

-“Well, I’ve achieved something. I’ve managed to go back to the gym.” FOA, Participant L_u9.

-“... I used to fall constantly because of Parkinson’s, but since I started doing the balance exercises we learned here at home, I haven’t fallen again.” FOA, Participant VO_u6.

-“...and the squats, now when I get up and go to the kitchen and come back, I think, look, I’ve done a squat, and I’ll do two more.” FOA, Participant VO_u1.

However, FOA noted that sustaining habits were challenging due to a lack of community programmes tailored to their needs.

-“But I know that the exercise done there (in the community), even the one called exercise for the golden age, has nothing to do with what we do here (in the +AGIL programme), nothing at all.” FOA Participant VO_u1.

-“The types of exercises we do here (in the +AGIL programme) are not done anywhere else. The types of exercises.” FOA, Participant VO_u6.

2. Improvement proposals

FOA suggested increasing session frequency to boost the programme’s impact. Some were willing to pay for more sessions, whilst others stressed balancing intensity with sufficient rest.

-“If there were a place with two sessions a week that required payment, I would pay for it.” FOA, Participant L_u1.

-“Yes, but they’re very intense. Ten days is too little, and we all agree it’s not enough.” FOA, Participant VO_u6.

FOA proposed additional strategies, such as periodic follow-up calls and group reunions, to sustain motivation and connection after the programme.

-“It wouldn’t involve much more administrative work for the physiotherapist to call periodically and ask, 'How are you? Are you doing the exercises?' Because if I know they’re going to call me on a certain day, I might actually do some.” FOA, Participant VO_u1.

-“It’s not mandatory, but once a month or every two weeks, having a group meeting afterward.” FOA, Participant VO_u4.

3. Barriers to maintaining learned habits

FOA reported that following the programme’s guidelines at home was challenging without support or encouragement from others, which made it difficult to maintain the learned routines.

-“At home, it’s a disaster. I admit it. I try, but I do it wrong. I get tired, and in the end, I give up.” FOA, Participant B_u3.

-“Because even though I do it at home, I know I don’t do it right. If I have someone next to me, they will show me how to do it properly.” FOA, Participant B_u1.

Perspectives of primary care professionals (PCP)

Theme 1: Results regarding factors influencing adherence

The factors influencing adherence are presented below and organised into two categories: communication skills and characteristics of frail older adults.

1. Communication skills

PCP identified active listening as a communication skill to reach patients and promote small behavioural changes that support adherence.

- “I think listening to patients is key to reaching them and encouraging them to make small changes.” PCP, Participant B_pf2.

2. Characteristics of frail older adults

PCP emphasised the importance of promoting autonomy and recognised that family responsibilities, sociocultural factors, and socioeconomic vulnerability could strongly influence adherence.

-“In the context of the +AGIL programme specifically, I think one of the key factors we can use to reach them is autonomy.” PCP, Participant B_pf2.

-“They are people who are always looking after others, taking care of their family, their grandchildren, their children. They are women, right? Women who have been heavily burdened with these responsibilities.” PCP, Participant B_pf3.

-“These are people who, socioeconomically, have very few resources, and their lives are very unstable, right? … In theory, everything should be more stable by now, but they’re still, for example, taking care of a son who is a drug addict. They have a history and a heavy burden to carry.” PCP, Participant B_pf5.

Theme 2: Results regarding programme’s impact on behaviour change

The elements that impact behaviour change in older adults are presented below and organised into two categories: programme-related factors that facilitate new behaviour and barriers to maintaining learned habits.

1. Programme-related factors

PCP emphasised that decisions about the programme’s location and structure were key to ensuring continuity and adherence beyond the intervention period.

– “Offering sessions in different community facilities… could later facilitate continuity by linking it to those spaces.” PCP, Participant B_pf3.

PCP expressed differing opinions on whether the programme should be centralised in a single location or extended to multiple community spaces, citing benefits for both approaches.

-“If you open it up to the community, you also encourage people to go somewhere other than the CAP […] and they can see this as something different, more social, which could be beneficial for them.” PCP, Participant VO_pf4.

-“Of course, if you designate a single location where the sessions take place, people know they need to come here. Then, when the sessions start, you can also manage the resources more effectively.” PCP, Participant VO_pf4.

Some PCP suggested that organising sessions in a more continuous, cyclical format could enhance adherence and help participants maintain engagement over time.

-“Trying to create a more continuous cycle of healthy habit workshops, because the issue was that there would be one talk one month, then another about nutrition, and in the CAP, it felt too far apart. People didn’t remember.” PCP, Participant L_pf5.

2. Barriers to maintaining learned habits

PCP identified limited economic and social resources as key barriers to sustaining habits and attending community activities.

-“Or that it costs money… For many, for example, activities at the civic centre cost 70 euros for 10 sessions. And they told me, 'I can’t sign up for anything like that. ” PCP, Participant VO_pf3.

PCP identified distance as a barrier, especially in large neighbourhoods. Travelling often requires taxis or extra support, limiting accessibility and discouraging participation.

-“...this neighbourhood is so large that asking someone who lives on one end of the neighbourhood to come down to this library here becomes an issue. They get lost or have to take a taxi—it’s a problem.” PCP, Participant VO_pf3.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study revealing factors that affect adherence amongst FOA in maintaining new healthy ageing habits through a multidomain intervention programme. These findings include the characteristics of the PCP, the role of the community, factors related to the FOA, and specific factors influencing the multidomain intervention programme itself. Whilst a systematic review on adherence to physical activity programmes reported similar results, stating that older adults’ adherence to exercise programmes is associated with personal and programme-related factors [27], our study specifically addresses these factors within the FOA population. In this context, Bandura’s social cognitive theory offers a conceptual framework that resonates with our findings, identifying five fundamental components that determine behaviour change: personal factors (cognitive and emotional), external environmental factors, past behaviour, reinforcement (positive and negative), and modelling, which may influence adherence [28]. The role of the PCP is crucial for adherence to new habits, especially in physical activity.

Regarding the characteristics of PCP, we highlight two key aspects: personal attributes and technical skills. Our findings underscore the impact of PCPs, particularly competence, communication skills, and empathy, on patient adherence. On the other hand, technical skills also play a significant role. These results align with recent studies emphasising the significance of feedback, knowledge, and interpersonal skills amongst PCP [29]. According to social cognitive theory, this impact can be explained by the PCP’s role as a behavioural model, facilitating learning through observation and demonstrating skills and strategies that FOA can adapt to their context [28]. However, to what extent are PCPs being trained to optimally fulfil this role? In this regard, learning to enhance the "therapeutic alliance" emerges as a key concept [30], as it represents the collaborative relationship established between the professional and the patient, facilitating the change process and achieving therapeutic goals based on empathetic communication and strong interpersonal skills [29, 31]. In terms of technical skills, participants’ assessment of the professionals’ technical ability to tailor exercise sessions underscores the importance of the fundamental principle of individualization in exercise prescription. These findings suggest that individualization not only improves the effectiveness of the intervention by adapting to each FOA’s needs but also enhances adherence to the programme by increasing motivation and sustained commitment.

Regarding the role of the community, the connection between primary healthcare centres and community resources, such as gyms or social centres, was identified as a key element for the continuity of exercise programmes and the promotion of healthy habits. Few studies highlight the relevance of community resources in maintaining healthy habits; however, some research mentions aspects of the community environment, such as access to nature, recreational areas, and nearby stores, as both barriers and facilitators to behaviour change in nutrition, physical activity, and lifestyle [32, 33]. A previous study on the + AGIL programme highlighted that empowering participants and linking them with community resources are critical points for the success of interventions. However, participants and professionals in our study indicated that creating these links remains a challenge, particularly due to the lack of activities specifically adapted for FOA’s [13]. This gap underscores the importance of adapting community resources and fostering more effective coordination strategies between PHCC and these spaces to enhance long-term adherence.

Regarding factors related to FOA, certain characteristics of the programme participants were identified as influencing adherence. These factors can be physical, social, or psychological, as individuals themselves play a significant role in their adherence to new habits. Physically frail and less fit individuals tend to have poorer adherence, especially when experiencing poor health conditions such as pain and fatigue [34]. Regarding social factors, our study has demonstrated that having family responsibilities serves as a barrier to acquiring new habits. This could be explained by the fact that caregiving duties often take priority over engaging in physical activity [35]. Depending on the nature of these responsibilities, however, caregiving roles can sometimes involve increased physical activity [36]. Additionally, other research highlights that a key motivating factor for older adults is the desire to maintain their health and autonomy to avoid becoming a burden on their loved ones [37].

A low socioeconomic status or unwillingness to allocate resources to health improvement has also been reported as a barrier to maintaining physical activity. High financial costs and the lack of free or affordable spaces and activities for healthy habits represent significant barriers to adherence [18, 38]. These findings highlight the importance of implementing specific and feasible strategies for primary and community care programmes, combining different aspects related to increased economic support or subsidies to eliminate financial barriers [39], strengthening support networks and multisectoral collaboration and, in particular, training professionals and community agents to adapt interventions to socioeconomic and care challenges [18]. It is also important to adapt programmes to the sociocultural context of vulnerable groups and employ leaders who share their characteristics, created by co-design or community participation [40].

Finally, regarding psychological factors, whilst motivation alone may not be sufficient for older adults to acquire new habits, fostering their self-efficacy and autonomy can enhance their confidence in making changes [27, 29]. This can be achieved if professionals provide patients with tailored support, practical tools, and goals aligned with their individual needs [41]. Concerning the factors influencing the multidomain programme, our results show that access to physical materials, such as exercise booklets, is a key factor for adherence to physical activity programmes for older adults. For greater adherence, the literature highlights the importance of the presence of the professional in the programme follow-up, especially in this population, either with phone calls, WhatsApp, mobile app, DVD support, or any monitoring system that favours social interaction [17, 42]. In addition, regarding carrying out the programme in a group, previous studies have emphasised that social connection and group support are essential for the sustainability of these programmes [37, 43, 44].

In light of these findings, it is relevant to consider conceptual frameworks that can help interpret the complexity of adherence. Among them, the Behaviour Change Techniques (BCTs) taxonomy offers a structured classification of intervention components shown to enhance long-term engagement, including strategies such as action planning, goal setting, and feedback [45]. Although the identification of BCTs was not the focus of this study, several of our findings resonate with these techniques, suggesting that such approaches could contribute to improving adherence in multidomain community-based interventions like + AGIL.

One of the objectives of the + AGIL programme is to provide FOA with tools to acquire new healthy habits through a complex intervention based on integrated primary and geriatric care in the community. The results show that participants are acquiring and maintaining new habits for healthy ageing through the programme. However, they have identified challenges and proposed improvements, particularly related to the programme’s organisation, to enhance adherence outcomes. Among these proposed improvements, understanding and implementing techniques that facilitate behaviour change, such as those described in Michie’s taxonomy, could optimise the programme’s outcomes and adherence rates.

Within the participants’ and professionals’ narratives, we recognised correspondences with a number of BCTs from Michie’s taxonomy [45], which are also included amongst the 39 BCTs identified in Gilchrist’s review [46] on exercise interventions for older adults (Supplementary Appendix 1). This theoretical convergence is interesting, as whilst Gilchrist [46] focuses on BCTs explicitly described in interventions, our qualitative study illustrates how these techniques are experienced and valued through the narratives of participants and professionals. These narratives reflect not only what is strictly implemented in the intervention but also the valued elements within the programme’s context.

The concept of “context” has been widely used in research, though it often lacks a precise definition [47]. Some perspectives define it broadly as “everything else that is not the intervention” [48, 49].

From this, it can be inferred that, for both users and professionals, it is not only the intervention itself that generates behaviour change but also the underlying elements and the context in which it is developed. This highlights the importance of considering that BCTs can be applied not only within the strict boundaries of an intervention but also as an integral part of the broader environment that represents a health programme.

Whilst the effectiveness of multidomain interventions to support intrinsic capacity in older adults is not questioned, this study helps to focus on the elements that facilitate behaviour change, emphasising the relevance of individual context and perspective within the complexity of healthcare systems. In this regard, the focus groups provide deep, situated insights.

One of the main strengths of this study is its identification of key elements that support adherence, including: (1) the value of healthcare professionals’ communication skills, such as active listening and verbal/non-verbal communication; (2) the appreciation for individualised programmes, especially given the limited availability of tailored options in the community; and (3) the perceived need for external support to help sustain newly acquired health habits over time.

A key limitation of the study is that the programme includes more exercise sessions than workshops, which may have led focus group discussions to emphasise physical activity over other components of the comprehensive design. Additionally, as this study was conducted in three primary healthcare centres in Barcelona (Spain), findings may not be fully transferable to regions with different sociodemographic or cultural profiles or to populations with more advanced frailty, as our sample primarily reflects older adults in early stages with relatively preserved functional status. Future research should explore diverse contexts to examine how local factors influence the sustainability of multidomain community interventions. Furthermore, whilst this study focussed on Adoption and Implementation experiences, leaving initial non-participation (Reach) unexplored directly, this work is part of a broader evaluation of the + AGIL programme (13). Barriers to engagement were captured indirectly through healthcare professionals acting as key informants; nevertheless, future research should include non-participants to provide a comprehensive understanding of all barriers.

Conclusion

This qualitative research explored the factors influencing the adherence to FOA in the + AGIL programme, identifying fundamental elements in PCP, the community, and the personal characteristics of the participants. These findings highlight the relevance of the personal attributes and technical skills of professionals, as well as the link between community resources and the PHCC, to maintain healthy habits. Likewise, it is evident how family responsibilities and economic barriers limit adherence to adopting and maintaining new healthy behaviours.

These results provide practical implications related to the design and implementation of future programmes, suggesting the integration of BCT, such as those classified in Michie’s taxonomy, which can optimise the expected results. Furthermore, it is highlighted that the contextual elements of a programme are as relevant as the strict characteristics of the interventions. Future research should address both intervention content and delivery, considering the professional role and contextual factors that facilitate behavioural change.
