Homotopy types of fine curve and fine arc complexes
Ryan Dickmann, Zachary Himes, Alexander Nolte, and Roberta Shapiro

Abstract
. The fine curve complex of a surface is a simplicial complex whose vertices are essential simple closed curves and whose k k -simplices are collections of k + 1 k+1 disjoint curves. We prove that the fine curve complex is homotopy equivalent to the curve complex. We also prove that the fine arc complex is contractible.



1.
Introduction

The
fine curve complex
of the compact surface
S_{g,b}
with genus
g
and
b
boundary components, denoted
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b}),
is the simplicial complex whose vertices are essential nonperipheral simple closed curves in
S_{g,b}
and
k
-simplices are sets of
k+1
disjoint curves. The
fine curve graph
, first introduced in the literature by Bowden–Hensel–Webb
[
2
]
, is the 1-skeleton of the fine curve complex. Bowden–Hensel–Webb studied the fine curve complex and used the full subgraph of the fine curve graph generated by smooth curves to show that
\Diffeo_{0}(S_{g,b})
admits unbounded quasimorphisms. This result is in contrast to results on
\Diffeo_{0}(M)
for closed smooth manifolds
M
with
\mathrm{dim}(M)\neq 2,4
(see
[
3
,
19
,
20
]
). A major goal of the study of fine curve graphs is to study classical groups of homeomorphisms with geometric group theoretic tools, as in this result of Bowden–Hensel–Webb.

The fine curve complex differs from the better-studied simplicial complex called the
curve complex
, denoted
\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})
(e.g.
[
12
,
13
]
), in that the latter takes as its vertices
isotopy classes
of essential simple closed curves and
k
-simplices are collections of
k+1
isotopy classes that admit pairwise disjoint representatives. The curve complex of
S_{g,b}
is a simplicial complex of dimension
3g+b-3
, whereas the fine curve complex is an infinite dimensional simplicial complex.

[
7
, Theorem 3.5]
and
[
11
, Lemma 6.5 and Theorem 6.6]
showed that the geometric realization of
\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of
(2g+b-3)
-spheres if
b\neq 0
or a wedge of
(2g-2)
-spheres if
b=0
.
Homotopy types of curve complexes play a central role in the use of curve complexes to study mapping class groups of surfaces (see, for example,
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
).

In Theorem
1.1
, we relate the homotopy type of the fine curve complex to that of the curve complex.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S_{g,b}
be an orientable surface with
g\geq 1
or
b\geq 4
. The map
f:\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})\to\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})
that sends a curve to its isotopy class is a homotopy equivalence.

In particular, when
(g,b)\neq(0,b)
with
b\leq 3
,
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of
(2g+b-3)
-spheres if
b\neq 0
and it is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of
(2g-2)
-spheres if
b=0.

We require
g\geq 1
or
b\geq 4
because when
g=0
and
b\leq 3
, both
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
and
\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})
are empty in these cases, so the theorem is vacuously true.
We note that
(g,b)=(1,0),(1,1),(0,4)
are odd cases since some versions of (fine) curve complexes allow for curves that intersect at one point (the first two cases) or two points (the last case) to form a simplex.
In this paper, we will only consider simplices defined by disjointness. For an analogous result in the setting of 3-manifolds, see
[
16
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Alongside the fine curve complex, we have the
fine arc complex.
An
essential arc
is (the image of) a proper embedding
[0,1]\hookrightarrow S_{g,b}
such that the image does not cobound a disk with
\partial S_{g,b}.
The fine arc complex of a surface
S_{g,b}
, denoted
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b}),
is the simplicial complex whose
k
-simplices are sets of
k+1
disjoint essential arcs. The arc complex is the simplicial complex whose
k
-simplices are sets of
k+1
isotopy classes of properly embedded isotopy classes of essential arcs.
[
6
]
and
[
9
]
previously showed that the arc complex is contractible for most orientable surfaces without punctures. In this paper, we prove an analogous result for the fine arc complex.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
S_{g,b}
be an orientable surface with
b\geq 1
and
(g,b)\neq(0,1),(0,2),(0,3),(0,4),(1,1).
Then the fine arc complex
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
is contractible.

Homotopy types of curve, arc, and finitary curve complexes.

[
6
]
uses the high connectivity of the curve complex to show that the homology of mapping class groups of surfaces stabilizes. Hatcher’s more general theorem in
[
9
]
allows for Harer’s results to be extended to more types of vertex sets and larger complexes.

More recently, the fourth named author showed that the
finitary curve complex
, the simplicial complex whose vertices are the same as that of the fine curve complex but
k
-simplices are collections of
k+1
curves that pairwise intersect in finitely many points, is contractible (see
[
18
]
).

Outline of paper.
In Section
2
, we introduce some nomenclature surrounding fine curve graphs and prove a key proposition. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.1
. Along the way, we prove Theorem
3.2
, which states that the fibers over simplices of the map
f
from Theorem
1.1
are contractible. Finally, we prove that the fine arc complex is contractible (Theorem
1.2
) in Section
4
.

2.
Key definitions and a critical proposition

The main goal of this section is to prove Proposition
2.1
, which states that given any finite collection of curves or arcs, there is a curve (resp. an arc) arbitrarily close to a given curve (resp. arc) and that intersects every element of the collection only finitely many times at crossing intersections (defined below).
Classical but major results in surface topology play a major role in working with fine curve complexes, and this is the place where such results enter this paper.
Proposition
2.1
consolidates the results of Lemma 4.1 and Corollary 4.2 in
[
18
]
. We include a bare-bones proof with pointers to corresponding results and lemmas in
[
18
]
as well for completeness.

Throughout the paper, we adopt the convention that all curves and arcs are simple, i.e. have no self-intersections. Whenever we refer to an arc, we will implicitly mean an essential arc. Similarly, whenever we refer to a curve in a surface, we will implicitly assume that the curve is essential (meaning that it is not nullhomotopic) and nonperipheral (meaning that it is not homotopic to a boundary component of the surface).

We first introduce some nomenclature for points of
u\cap v
that are not accumulation points for the set
u\cap v
.

Two curves
u
and
v
are
touching
at a point
c\in u\cap v
if, in an arbitrarily small neighborhood
N
of
c,

u
and
v
can be isotoped to be disjoint. Otherwise,
u
and
v
are
crossing
at
c.

Examples of such intersections can be found on the right and left, respectively, of Figure
1
.

.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
S=S_{g,b}
be an orientable surface and let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
be a collection of curves or arcs in
S.
Let
y
be a curve (resp. an arc) in
S.
Then there exists a curve (resp. an arc)
\gamma
in
S
such that:

(1)

|\gamma\cap\gamma_{i}|<\infty
for all
i,

(2)

all intersections
\gamma\cap\gamma_{i}
for all
i
are crossing, and

(3)

\gamma
is contained in some arbitrary pre-selected open neighborhood of
y
.

The idea of the proof is that we will construct
\gamma
by removing a certain compact and totally disconnected set
\mathcal{E}\subset S_{g,b}
of “problematic” intersections of
\gamma_{i}\cap\gamma_{j}
from
S_{g,b}
.
We will show that
\mathcal{E}
is totally disconnected by using a result from dimension theory, which we now provide some background information on (for more details on dimension theory, see, for example,
[
10
]
or
[
4
]
).

A space
X
is
zero-dimensional
if it has a basis consisting of sets which are both closed and open in
X
. If
X
is zero-dimensional, then it is totally disconnected and the converse holds if
X
is locally compact and Hausdorff (see, for example,
[
1
, Proposition 3.1.7]
).
An example of a zero-dimensional space is any non-empty subspace of
\mathbb{R}
which does not contain an interval (for more details, see
[
4
, 1.2.5 Examples]
).
We will need the following theorem about unions of zero-dimensional spaces in the proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Lemma 2.2

(e.g.
[
10
, Theorem II 2]
)
.

A space which is the countable union of closed zero-dimensional subspaces is itself zero-dimensional.

We now define the set of “problematic” intersections between a finite set of curves or arcs.
Given a collection of distinct curves or arcs
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
in a surface
S_{g,b},
we define
\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})\subset S_{g,b}
in the following way. We will abuse notation by treating the
\gamma_{i}
both as injective maps from
S^{1}=I/(\{0\}\sim\{1\})
or
I
into
\Sigma
and as the images of such maps; the use should be clear from context.

Let
E=\{p\in S_{g,b}\mid p\in\gamma_{i}\cap\gamma_{j}\text{ for some }i,j\};
these are all the points of intersection between the arcs and curves. Intersections where two curves or arcs coincide for an open interval pose no problem to us, so we wish to exclude them. To this end, let
P_{i}=\partial(\gamma_{i}^{-1}(E))\subset I;
we note that
P_{i}
is empty if
\gamma_{i}
is disjoint from all other
\gamma_{j}
. To get these points back on the surface, let
\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})=\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i}(P_{i}).
We will often simplify notation and write
\mathcal{E}
for
\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The space
\mathcal{E}
is compact and totally disconnected.

Proof.

The space
\mathcal{E}
is compact because it is a finite union of compact spaces.

To show that
\mathcal{E}
is totally disconnected, it suffices to show that it is zero-dimensional. First, we show that
\gamma_{i}(P_{i})
is zero-dimensional. Since
P_{i}
is the boundary of a subspace of
I
(and therefore
\mathbb{R}
), it cannot contain an interval and so it
is zero-dimensional. Moreover, since
\gamma_{i}
is an embedding,
P_{i}
is homeomorphic to
\gamma_{i}(P_{i}).
Homeomorphisms preserve dimension, so
\gamma_{i}(P_{i})
is zero-dimensional. Since
\mathcal{E}=\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i}(P_{i})
is a finite union of closed zero-dimensional spaces, by Lemma
2.2
,

\mathcal{E}
itself is zero-dimensional. We conclude that
\mathcal{E}
is totally disconnected.
∎

Part of the proof of Proposition
2.1
involves working with a regular neighborhood of a given curve or arc
y
in
S_{g,b}
. If

is a curve, a regular neighborhood is homeomorphic to an annulus. If

is an arc, such a neighborhood is a strip in

.
More formally, a

in

is an embedded copy of

where

We will assume that

does not bound a disk with

.

In the proof of Proposition

, we will also need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

and
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
be a collection of curves or arcs in

Let

be a closed annulus (resp. a strip) in

such that, for all

the intersection
\mathrm{Int}(A)\cap\gamma_{i}\cap\gamma_{j}
is a union of connected components of
\gamma_{i}\cap\mathrm{Int}(A)
. Then, there exists a curve (resp. an arc)

such that
|\gamma_{i}\cap\gamma|<\infty
or
|\gamma_{i}\cap\alpha|<\infty
for all

and all such intersections are crossing.

Proof.

This lemma is proven identically to Lemma 3.3 of Shapiro
[

]
despite having more curves in the collection.
The key input is the tameness of arcs in

(see, for example,
[

, Ch. 10]
).
∎

We will also need the following result to prove Proposition
2.1
.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
17
, Chapter V, Theorem 14.3]
)
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
or the closed disk
\bar{D}^{2}
and let
V
be a closed subspace of
X
. Suppose that two points
x,y
in
X
are in different connected components of

. Then there is a connected component
C
of

such that

are in different components of

.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let
N
be a regular neighborhood of
y
that is closed in
S_{g,b}
. We will construct
\gamma
in such a way that it is contained in
N
.
The space
N
is homeomorphic to an annulus or a strip, depending on whether

is a curve or an arc. Let
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n})
and let
N^{*}=N\setminus\mathcal{E}.
Since
\mathcal{E}
is totally disconnected by Lemma

, each connected component of
\mathcal{E}
is a point. Lemma
2.5
then implies that
N^{*}
is connected. Since
N
is locally path-connected and
N^{*}
is a connected open subspace of

, the space

is path-connected.

Suppose

is a strip. Then there exists an arc

in

connecting the components of

in

. We may take a regular neighborhood of

to obtain a strip

in

. We can now apply Lemma

to find an arc
\gamma
in
N
that intersects all
\gamma_{i}
finitely many times.

Suppose

is an annulus. There exists a point in each component of
\partial N
that is not in

Consider these two points as points in
N^{*}
and connect them with an arc
\alpha
in
N^{*}
. Let

and then cut

Reglue the strip into

; the endpoints of

.
Let

be a regular neighborhood of
\gamma^{\prime}
inside

. Since

in

3.
Homotopy type of the fine curve complex

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.1
. To this end, we first prove that
the fiber over a simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})

under the map
f:\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})\to\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})

is contractible (Section
3.2
) and then complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
(Section
3.3
). We begin in Section
3.1
by recalling some background information and fixing some conventions involving simplicial complexes. For more on simplicial complexes, see, for example,
[
15
, Sections 1.1–1.3]
.

3.1.
Simplicial complexes

Given a simplicial complex
X
and a simplex
\sigma\in X
, we denote the dimension of
\sigma
by
\dim(\sigma)
. We denote the link and (closed) star of
\sigma
by
\link(\sigma)
and
\Star(\sigma)
respectively. We denote the
k
-skeleton of
X
by
X^{(k)}
.

We denote the geometric realization of
X
by
|X|
. When we say that a simplicial complex
X
has a topological property, we will mean that
|X|
has that property.

A point
x\in|X|
is represented in barycentric coordinates by a formal sum
\sum_{v\in X^{(0)}}t_{v}v
,with
t_{v}\in[0,1]
, all but finitely of the
t_{v}
s equal to zero (with the set of such
v
with
t_{v}
non-zero forming a simplex in
X
), and
\sum_{v\in X^{(0)}}t_{v}=1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be simplicial complexes. A map of spaces
g\colon|X|\to|Y|
is
simplicial
if it is induced from a simplicial map of simplicial complexes
X\to Y
.
In this case, we will abuse notation and also use
g
to denote the underlying simplicial map
X\to Y
inducing
g\colon|X|\to|Y|
.

For
k\geq 0
, a
combinatorial k-manifold

M
is a nonempty simplicial complex that satisfies the following inductive property. If
\sigma\in M
, then
\dim(\sigma)\leq k
. In addition, if
k-\dim(\sigma)-1\geq 0
, then
\link(\sigma)
is a combinatorial
(k-\dim(\sigma)-1)
-manifold homeomorphic to either a
(k-\dim(\sigma)-1)
-sphere or a closed
(k-\dim(\sigma)-1)
-ball. A combinatorial
k
-manifold homeomorphic to a
k
-sphere will be called a
combinatorial k-sphere
. We will often denote a combinatorial
k-
sphere by
\mathbb{S}^{k}
.

By the simplicial approximation theorem (see, for example,
[
15
, Theorem 16.1]
), every element of
\pi_{k}(|X|)
is represented by a simplicial map

|\mathbb{S}^{k}|\to|X|
.
For this reason, for the rest of the paper, whenever we work with a map
g\colon S^{k}\to|X|
, we will implicitly treat
S^{k}
as a combinatorial
k
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{k}
and assume that
g\colon|\mathbb{S}^{k}|\to|X|
is a simplicial map.

In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we will need the following proposition, which gives a condition for when a simplicial map of simplicial complexes is a homotopy equivalence.

Proposition 3.1

(e.g.
[
8
, Corollary 2.7]
)
.

Let
g\colon X\to Y
be a simplicial map of simplicial complexes. If the subcomplex
g^{-1}(\sigma)
is contractible for all simplices
\sigma\in Y
, then
g
is a homotopy equivalence.

3.2.
Preimages of simplices are contractible

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
3.2
, which states that the preimage of a simplex in
\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})
is contractible.

Given a simplex
A
in
\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})
, let
\mathcal{C}_{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
denote the subcomplex
f^{-1}(A)
in
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
. A simplex
\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{q}\}\in\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
is in
\mathcal{C}_{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
if
f(v_{i})
is a vertex in
A
for all
i
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
S_{g,b}
be an orientable surface with
g\geq 1
or
b\geq 4
and let
A
be a simplex in
\mathcal{C}(S_{g,b})
.
Then
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{A}(S_{g,b})
is contractible.

Our main tools are Proposition
2.1
and Whitehead’s theorem. To this end, we first show that any map
S^{k}\to|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{A}(S_{g,b})|
is homotopic to a map whose image is contained in simplices spanned by curves with desirable intersection properties (Lemma
3.4
). Then, we show that any map
S^{k}\to|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{A}(S_{g,b})|
can be further homotoped to a map whose image is contained in the star of a single vertex (Lemma
3.5
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S_{g,b}
be an orientable surface and
\Gamma=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be a collection of curves in
S_{g,b}.
Then there exists a collection of curves
v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{n}^{\prime}
such that, for all
i
and
j,

(1)

v_{i}\cap v_{i}^{\prime}=\emptyset
or
v_{i}=v^{\prime}_{i}
,

(2)

if
v_{i}\cap v_{j}=\emptyset,
then:

(a)

v_{i}^{\prime}\cap v_{j}^{\prime}=\emptyset
and

(b)

v_{i}^{\prime}\cap v_{j}=\emptyset
, and

(3)

|v^{\prime}_{i}\cap v^{\prime}_{j}|<\infty
and all intersections are crossing.

Proof.

Let
v^{\prime}_{1}=v_{1}
and let
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma=\Gamma\cup\{v_{1}^{\prime}\}.
Consider
v_{2}.

We may pick an open regular neighborhood
T
of
v_{2}
such that if
v_{j}
does not intersect
v_{2}
, then
v_{j}
also does not intersect
T
. Pick a curve
y
in
T\setminus v_{2}
.
By Proposition
2.1
,
there exists a curve
v^{\prime}_{2}
contained in the neighborhood
T\setminus v_{2}
of
y
such that:

(1)

v^{\prime}_{2}\cap v_{2}=\emptyset,

(2)

if
v_{j}\cap v_{2}=\emptyset,
then

v_{j}\cap v^{\prime}_{2}=\emptyset
and if
v^{\prime}_{j}
has already been defined,
v^{\prime}_{j}\cap v^{\prime}_{2}=\emptyset
, and

(3)

for
i<2
, we have
|v_{2}^{\prime}\cap v_{i}^{\prime}|<\infty
and all intersections of
v_{2}^{\prime}
and
v_{i}^{\prime}
are crossing.

Condition
(1)
follows from the fact that
v^{\prime}_{2}
is in
T\setminus v_{2}
, condition
(2)
follows from our choice of
T
, and condition
(3)
follows from Proposition
2.1
.

We now consider the set of curves
\Gamma_{2}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}\cup\{v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime}\}=\Gamma_{1}\cup\{v_{2}^{\prime}\}
and perform the above algorithm once again, this time constructing
v^{\prime}_{3}
using
v_{3}
and letting
\Gamma_{3}=\Gamma_{2}\cup\{v_{3}^{\prime}\}.

We continue similarly, constructing
v^{\prime}_{i}
using
v_{i}\in\Gamma_{i-1}
and letting
\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma_{i-1}\cup\{v^{\prime}_{i}\}.

In this manner, we have constructed a collection of curves
\Gamma^{\prime}=\{v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{n}^{\prime}\}
that satisfy the conditions in the lemma statement.
∎

We have the following consequence of Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Any map
\phi\colon S^{k}\to|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{A}(S_{g,b})|
is homotopic to a simplicial map
\phi^{\prime}\colon S^{k}\to|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{A}(S_{g,b})|
whose image lies in the full subcomplex spanned by a set of curves
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
such that
|c_{i}\cap c_{j}|<\infty
for all
i,j
and all intersections between pairs of curves are crossing.

Proof.

The proof idea is to flow
\phi(S^{k})
into simplices spanned by the curves
c_{i}
.
Recall that, without loss of generality, we can assume
\phi\colon S^{k}\to|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{A}(S_{g,b})|
is induced by a simplicial map
\mathbb{S}^{k}\to\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{A}(S_{g,b})
.
Since
S^{k}
is compact and
\phi
is continuous, the image of
\phi
is contained in the interior of simplices spanned by a finite number of vertices
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
.
By Lemma
3.3
, there are curves
c_{1}\ldots,c_{n}
such that
for all
i
and
j
, either
c_{i}\cap b_{i}=\emptyset
or
c_{i}=b_{i}
; if
b_{i}\cap b_{j}=\emptyset
then
c_{i}\cap c_{j}=\emptyset
and
c_{i}\cap b_{j}=\emptyset
; and
|c_{i}\cap c_{j}|<\infty
and all intersections are crossing. For each vertex
v\in(\mathbb{S}^{k})^{(0)}
, let
b_{v}
denote
\phi(v)
and let
c_{v}
be the corresponding curve in
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
(i.e. if
\phi(v)=b_{i}
, then
c_{v}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}c_{i}
). The map
\phi^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{S}^{k}\to\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{A}(S_{g,b})
that sends

to

A
bigon

Let

, we can assume that all vertices that span

Homotopy type of the fine arc complex

In this section, we prove that the fine arc complex is contractible (Theorem

To do this, we use Whitehead’s theorem. First we show that any map
S^{k}\to|\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})|
is homotopic to a simplicial map whose image is contained in the star of a vertex.
We follow Hatcher’s proof idea in

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We will show that any map
\phi\colon S^{k}\to|\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})|

\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
is contractible.

Since
S^{k}
is compact, the image of
\phi
is contained in the geometric realization of the full subcomplex of
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
spanned by a finite set of arcs
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}\}
.
Let
\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})^{(0)}
be an arc that intersects each
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma
at finitely many points such that 1) each intersection is crossing
and 2)
\partial\beta\cap\gamma_{i}=\emptyset
for all
i
. Such an arc is guaranteed to exist by Proposition
2.1
.

Recall that the star of
\beta
consists of simplices
\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{p}\}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(S_{g,b})
such that each
\alpha_{i}
is disjoint from
\beta
or equals
\beta
.
The following exposition is directly inspired by that of Hatcher’s proof in

]
. Throughout, we point out the differences between our situation and that of Hatcher.

Distinctions from Hatcher’s proof.
Our difference in setting requires a few changes to Hatcher’s argument in
[
9
]
, which we briefly mention here for the convenience of experts.

One distinction between our proof and that of Hatcher is that we have an uncountable number of choices whenever a surgery is performed. This is taken care of by the “Well-definedness in replacing arcs” section of the proof.

Another distinction is that Hatcher asks for there to be no bigons between any pair of arcs. Hatcher needs this since his proof is up to isotopy, but we remove this requirement by ensuring that all intersections are crossing and that all arcs are still surgered to create at least one essential arc.

We also explicitly mention here that although there were simultaneous surgeries in the “Well-definedness when replacing arcs” section, such a phenomenon does not actually occur during the flow.

One of the clearest distinctions between our proof and that of Hatcher is that Hatcher prescribes a specific set of endpoints
V\subset\partial S_{g,b}
for his arcs and asks for no two elements of
V
to be in the same connected component of the boundary. Hatcher treats the points in
V
as punctures that cannot be isotoped across, so an essential arc could bound a disk with a marked boundary in
S_{g,b}.
If this is the case, then surgery via the flow argument could result in no essential arcs. However, such arcs are not permitted in our case, as we do not treat
\partial S_{g,b}
as having marked points. As such, we impose no further restrictions on the arcs that comprise the vertices of the fine arc graph.
