Methyl jasmonate seed priming enhances pest resistance in Arabidopsis and Brassica through species-specific molecular mechanisms
Authors: Lucia Talavera-Mateo, Adrian Sabater-Gabriel, Alejandro Garcia, Rayko Halitschke, M Estrella Santamaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable and cost-efficient alternatives to conventional pest management are needed. Seed priming, which enhances plants response to stress, offers a promising solution. We previously demonstrated that seed priming with methyl jasmonate (MeJA) or salicylic acid (SA) increases resistance to pests without growth penalties in Arabidopsis thaliana. Here, we investigate whether these benefits extend to the crop Brassica rapa and whether underlying mechanisms are conserved. In both plant species, MeJA and SA enhanced resistance to the generalist mite Tetranychus urticae and the specialist caterpillar Pieris brassicae, without compromising growth. Based on its comparatively greater effects, MeJA was selected for molecular analyses. Transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling revealed conserved co-expression networks but limited overlap in specific genes and metabolites. Additionally, the magnitude and direction of MeJA priming varied with both plant and herbivore species, revealing strong plant–pest interaction effects. In B. rapa, seed priming promoted SA accumulation under mite infestation and glucosinolate pathway activation under caterpillar feeding. Overall, we observed that phenotypic benefits were broadly conserved across Brassicaceae, but its molecular bases were shaped by plant species and pest identity, as well as context-specific interactions. This comparative study underscores the potential of tailoring priming strategies to crop species for sustainable pest management.

Introduction

The food demands of an ever-growing global population are increasingly threatened by the attack of phytophagous pests, which are estimated to cause a 20% loss in global crop yield (Sharma et al., 2017). This might be exacerbated in a climate change context, due to acceleration of arthropod development and shortened generation times that will enable the expansion of geographic range and colonization of new regions (Deutsch et al., 2018). Brassica crops, which rank among the most significant vegetables and oilseed crops worldwide, suffer yearly losses of 50–60% due to herbivory (Poveda et al., 2020).

Over millennia, plants have evolved complex defence strategies to ward off herbivory, balancing the activation of resistance pathways with the costs incurred by growth and reproduction (He et al., 2022). In agriculture, the induction of natural plant defences still results in crop losses, due to both the physiological costs of metabolite biosynthesis to the detriment of growth and the delay between pest infestation and plant defence deployment (Frost et al., 2008). The main strategy for pest management is the use of pesticides, which may have a negative impact on the environment, including contamination of soil, water, and non-target organisms such as pollinators and natural enemies. Additionally, the intensive use of such chemicals imposes a strong selection pressure on phytophagous populations, which has led to the development of resistance (van Leeuwen and Dermauw, 2016).

Defence priming is a physiological state in which prior exposure to a stimulus enables a faster or stronger response upon future stress. It is considered an adaptive and cost-effective measure compared with induced defence, since it shortens the window of vulnerability and decreases the delay in activating plant defences (Conrath et al., 2006). This phenomenon, which can be triggered exogenously by a vast array of stimuli—such as phytohormones, vitamins, mechanical wounding, and chemical compounds—has been primarily studied in the context of tolerance to abiotic stresses, but is gaining increasing attention as a potential tool in sustainable crop protection (Martinez-Medina et al., 2016). In Brassica crops, seed treatments have been employed to improve tolerance to abiotic stresses (Rao et al., 1987). However, seed priming remains largely unexplored as a pest management strategy. In our previous work we showed that seed priming with methyl jasmonate (MeJA) and salicylic acid (SA) enhances resistance to phytophagous pests without the fitness penalties typically associated with defence activation in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana (Talavera-Mateo et al., 2025). However, whether similar priming benefits and underlying molecular mechanisms apply to economically important crops such as Brassica is yet to be elucidated.

Although A. thaliana and Brassica rapa diverged ∼20 million years ago, they both belong to the Brassicaceae family and retain a substantial degree of genomic and functional conservation (Cheng et al., 2013). Indeed, previous comparative transcriptomic analyses have shown that a significant proportion of stress-responsive genes are co-expressed across these species (Lou et al., 2012), suggesting that the core regulatory molecular machinery is preserved. On this basis, A. thaliana has been used as a proxy for Brassicaceae research, whether to investigate the diversification of metabolites such as glucosinolates (Windsor et al., 2005) or to provide genomic resources (Tang and Lyons, 2012). However, most of these efforts rely on the assumption that mechanisms uncovered in A. thaliana are conserved in Brassica crops, rather than on direct experimental validation. As a matter of fact, notable evolutionary differences exist. B. rapa has undergone a whole-genome triplication (WGT) event that contributed to the expansion and diversification of gene families involved in stress perception, hormone signalling, and secondary metabolism (Wang et al., 2011). As a result, B. rapa may exhibit a broader repertoire of regulatory responses to environmental stimuli, including priming agents, potentially leading to species-specific outcomes. This raises the question of whether priming molecular mechanisms characterized in A. thaliana can be extrapolated to B. rapa.

In this study, we set out to test the hypothesis that the beneficial effects of seed priming observed in A. thaliana—namely enhanced resistance to herbivory and boosted plant growth—would extend to B. rapa. We show that primed B. rapa plants exhibit enhanced resistance to Tetranychus urticae and Pieris brassicae, and even improved growth in the latter scenario. However, the underlying molecular responses of both plant species diverged: while gene co-expression network structures were largely conserved, the overlap in transcriptomic reprogramming was minimal. We suggest that this divergence reflects differences in epigenetic regulation and the complex genome architecture of B. rapa. By combining phenotypic, transcriptomic, and metabolic analyses, our study provides insights into the mechanistic basis of defence priming in Brassica crops, and extends the knowledge towards the development of sustainable and species-tailored pest management strategies.

Materials and methods

Plant material and seed treatment

In this work B. rapa spp. trilocularis inbred line R-o-18 was used for all experiments. Seeds were incubated in the dark for 3 d at 4 °C in an aqueous solution containing the priming agent. Mock treatments with water were carried along as non-primed controls (NP). Following a preliminary analysis in which six distinct priming agents were tested at different concentrations, the phytohormones MeJA and SA at a concentration of 1 mM were selected for subsequent experiments, since these two molecules allowed for a comparative analysis with our previous work (Talavera-Mateo et al., 2025) and none of the other tested treatments performed better (Supplementary Fig. S1). Plants were then germinated and grown in a mixture of peat moss and vermiculite (3:2) and cultivated in a growth chamber (Aralab D1200PLH) under controlled conditions (23±1 °C, >60% relative humidity, and a 16/8 h day/night photoperiod). Brassica rapa 21-day-old seedlings were used for all experiments.

Arthropod maintenance

A colony of T. urticae, London strain (Acari: Tetranychidae), provided by Dr Miodrag Grbic (UWO, Canada), was reared on Phaseolus vulgaris and maintained in growth chambers (Sanyo MLR-350-H, Sanyo, Japan) at 25±1 °C, >70% relative humidity, and a 16/8 h day/night photoperiod.

A colony of P. brassicae reared on Brassica oleracea L. var. gemmifera and maintained in growth chambers (Sanyo MLR-350-H, Sanyo, Japan) at 21±1 °C, 50% relative humidity, and a 16/8 h day/night photoperiod was supplied by Professor Dr Marcel Dicke and Dr Pieter Rouweler (Laboratory of Entomology, Wageningen University, the Netherlands).

Phenotypic assays

To assess plant resistance, we quantified plant defence and pest performance. Plant defence against T. urticae and P. brassicae was evaluated on whole plants—18 biological replicates—and leaf discs—32 biological replicates—respectively. Plants infested with 50 adult female mites for 4 d were scanned (HP Scanjet 5590 Digital Flatbed Scanner series) and the damaged area was analysed following Ojeda-Martinez et al. (2020). Leaf discs (1 cm diameter) were infested with two freshly hatched neonate larvae for 24 h and the consumed area was quantified with Fiji (Santamaría et al., 2017). The performance of T. urticae was evaluated by placing 12 synchronized females (Santamaría et al., 2017) on detached leaves from 12 individual plants and counting the number of eggs laid after 36 h. The performance of P. brassicae was evaluated by placing three neonate larvae on whole plants and quantifying the larval weight gained after 30 h of 27 biological replicates.

Additionally, growth and development parameters were evaluated after infestation with mites or caterpillars. Foliar area, fresh weight, and number of leaves were quantified in six biological replicates of B. rapa plants infested with 50 adult female mites at 7 days post-infestation (dpi). Development of new leaves and increase in shoot length were quantified in nine biological replicates of B. rapa plants infested with one neonate larva for 7 d.

Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses

Brassica rapa plants were infested with either 50 adult female mites or two freshly hatched neonate larvae for 24 h, then plant material was collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed as previously described in Talavera-Mateo et al. (2025). Briefly, for transcriptome profiling, total RNA of four biological replicates was extracted, quality-checked, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Novogene Europe. Clean reads were mapped to the Brapa_1.0 Ensembl genome using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2015), and gene expression levels were quantified and analysed using FeatureCounts (Liao et al., 2014) and Novogene’s Novomagic pipeline.

Targeted metabolite profiling (including phytohormones, amino acids, sugars, and glucosinolates) of six biological replicates was carried out on methanol-extracted plant tissues using LC-MS and HPLC-UV, following validated protocols from Schäfer et al. (2016) and Graser et al. (2000) as implemented in Talavera-Mateo et al. (2025). Similarly, spectrophotometric assays were employed to quantify anthocyanins and lignin following Nakata et al. (2013) and Foster et al. (2010), respectively, as implemented in Talavera-Mateo et al. (2025). For untargeted metabolomics, UHPLC-QTOF-MS was used to acquire both full-scan and MS/MS data, followed by feature extraction, annotation, and structural classification using the Bruker MetaboScape2024 software (Bruker) and SIRIUS software (Dührkop et al., 2019) tools. Differential accumulation of features was analysed with MetaboScape.

Consensus weighted gene co-expression network analysis

To identify gene co-expression patterns conserved between A. thaliana and B. rapa, a consensus weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA R package (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). Raw count data were separately processed for each species. First, counts were variance-stabilized using the vst function from DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014). For B. rapa, gene expression values corresponding to duplicated genes were collapsed by averaging across orthologous groups defined with A. thaliana, using a curated ortholog map. Only orthologous genes common to both species were retained for further analysis, resulting in matched expression matrices with common gene sets across both species.

Gene quality control was performed using the goodSamplesGenes function. Genes failing this quality check were removed; no samples were identified as outliers. To approximate scale-free topology, a soft-thresholding power of 4 was selected using the pickSoftThreshold function, based on the scale-free topology criterion (R2 > 0.8) and mean connectivity profiles (Supplementary Fig. S2).

The consensus network was built using the blockwiseConsensusModules function with the following parameters: signed network topology, a minimum module size of 30, deepSplit of 2, and a merge cut height of 0.25. This method computes species-specific adjacency matrices, transforms them into topological overlap matrices (TOMs), and integrates them into a consensus TOM to identify modules with conserved co-expression structure across both species.

Module eigengenes, defined as the first principal component of each module (Zhang and Horvath, 2005), were computed separately for each species and used to explore higher-level relationships among modules using eigengene network analysis with the plotEigengeneNetworks function, visualizing similarities in module behaviour across conditions.

To identify biologically meaningful modules, Pearson correlations were computed between module eigengene and dummy-coded trait variables for each plant species. Trait variables represented priming treatment under specific infestation conditions: (i) primed control plants; (ii) primed T. urticae-infested plants; and (iii) primed P. brassicae-infested plants. The significance of correlations was assessed using Student’s t-distribution, as implemented in the corPvalueStudent function from the WGCNA package. Trait–module relationships were visualized as heatmaps.

Histone mark enrichment analysis

To investigate whether differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were preferentially associated with specific histone modifications, we performed an enrichment analysis using publicly available ChIP-seq datasets. For A. thaliana, genome-wide histone modification peak sets were obtained from the ReMap 2022 database (Hammal et al., 2022), including four representative marks with distinct regulatory roles: H3K4me3 (active promoters), H3K27ac (active enhancers), H3K27me3 (Polycomb-mediated repression), and H3K9me2 (heterochromatic silencing). Gene promoter coordinates (±2 kb around transcription start sites) were extracted from Ensembl gene annotations, and genes overlapping histone mark peaks were identified using bedtools intersect (Quinlan and Hall, 2010).

For B. rapa, processed H3K27me3 peak annotations were obtained directly from the supplementary materials of Payá-Milans et al. (2019), where peaks had already been mapped to genes. Thus, no additional promoter extraction was required, and the provided gene lists were used.

Enrichment was assessed by testing whether DEGs contained histone marks more frequently than expected by chance compared with the genomic background of expressed genes. For each mark, a 2×2 contingency table was constructed contrasting the number of DEGs and non-DEGs with or without the mark. Fisher’s exact test was applied using the fisher.test function in R to estimate odds ratios and P-values. Odds ratios >1 indicated enrichment of DEGs among genes marked by the histone modification.

Multivariate analysis

To assess the molecular responses of plants to seed priming and pest infestation, a multivariate analysis was conducted using the vegan R package (Oksanen et al., 2025). We integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic, and biochemical datasets, including transcriptomics, untargeted metabolomic features, and quantified metabolites (e.g. anthocyanins, lignin, glucosinolates, phytohormones, sugars, and amino acids). Log2 fold change values were computed for each treatment comparison (MeJA-primed versus NP) to standardize effects. Missing values were replaced with 0 to maintain consistency across datasets.

All response variables were shifted to remove negative values and log-transformed. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion across plant groups was assessed using the betadisper function, and no significant differences were found, justifying the use of Euclidean distances.

Redundancy analysis (RDA) was then performed using the rda function with Euclidean distances, modelling molecular responses as a function of plant species, pest identity, and their interaction. The model’s overall significance and individual terms were tested using permutation tests.

For visualization, constrained ordination plots were generated using base R plotting functions. Group dispersion was visualized with SD ellipses using the function ordiellipse.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software v4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024) within the RStudio IDE v2025.5.1.513 (Posit Team, 2025). The normality of the data was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests and visualized with QQ plots. Homoscedasticity was evaluated using Levene’s test. When data deviated from normality, transformations were applied where possible to fit normal distributions.

For phenotypic assays, post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s HSD test for normally distributed data and the non-parametric Dunn’s test for non-normal data. For targeted metabolite quantification, a two-way ANOVA test was employed to evaluate the effects of Priming (PR), Pest, and their interaction. When a significant interaction was detected, Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests were performed to assess pairwise differences between treatment combinations. Additionally, when a significant effect of Pest was observed, a Dunnett test was applied to determine whether T. urticae or P. brassicae infestation had significant effects.

Results

Seed priming effect on plant and pest performance

To determine whether seed priming confers similar resistance in an agronomically relevant crop, we treated B. rapa seeds with 1 mM MeJA and SA or water (NP). Although SA treatment did not seem to influence mite feeding, MeJA-treated plants showed a reduction of 13% in damaged foliar area compared with NP (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, seed priming exerted a stronger effect on mite oviposition, with both MeJA and SA treatments decreasing the number of eggs laid 36 hours post-infestation (hpi) by 59% and 25%, respectively (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, both priming agents effectively enhanced B. rapa resistance against P. brassicae. Leaf consumption 24 hpi was reduced to 55% and 62% in MeJA- and SA-treated plants, respectively, while larvae feeding on these primed plants for 30 h gained 21% and 18% less weight compared with those feeding on NP B. rapa plants (Fig. 1A).

Due to the importance of high yield and adequate growth rates in an agricultural landscape, we also assessed how seed priming influenced B. rapa development. Evaluations were limited to the early vegetative stage, up to 30 d, to comply with containment requirements for infested plants. Primed B. rapa plants 7 dpi with T. urticae maintained usual growth rates, displaying similar foliar area, fresh weight, and total number of leaves to their respective NP control plants (Fig. 1B). To study the interactive effect of seed priming and P. brassicae feeding on B. rapa development, we quantified leaf production and shoot elongation over a 7 d infestation period. There was no difference in the number of newly developed leaves, but shoot length was reduced in infested NP and SA-primed plants compared with their non-infested counterparts (Fig. 1B). Infested MeJA-treated plants also followed this trend towards reduced shoot length upon caterpillar attack but they did not statistically differ from their non-infested control (Fig. 1B).

From these phenotypic results, we concluded that 1 mM MeJA seed treatment had the most potential as a priming agent for enhancing resistance while maintaining the growth rates of B. rapa crops. Therefore, this priming agent was selected for subsequent molecular analyses.

Molecular basis of MeJA seed priming on B. rapa

The molecular mechanisms underlying seed priming with 1 mM MeJA, both individually and in interaction with pest infestation, were assessed 24 hpi. To obtain a comprehensive overview of the primary biological processes involved, two complementary -omic approaches—transcriptomics and untargeted metabolomics—were employed. Notably, the resulting -omic profiles exhibited substantial variation.

At the transcriptome level, primed and NP B. rapa plants showed minimal differences in control and P. brassicae-infested conditions, with only 28 and 39 DEGs identified, respectively. In contrast, the interaction between MeJA-mediated priming and T. urticae infestation resulted in a markedly higher number of 376 DEGs (Fig. 2A). Conversely, metabolomic profiling revealed that the most pronounced effects of seed priming were observed in control plants, with a total of 231 differentially accumulated features (DAFs). The interaction between priming and pest infestation led to a reduction in DAFs, with 208 features identified between primed and NP mite-infested plants, and 134 distinct features following P. brassicae infestation (Fig. 2A). Notably, a similarity between the transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets was the exceptionally low number of shared DEGs and DAFs across the three infestation conditions (Fig. 2B), highlighting the highly specific nature of primed B. rapa responses to distinct biotic challenges. A comprehensive list of DEGs and enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms derived from priming is provided in Supplementary Dataset S1.

In addition, a detailed analysis of the RNA-seq data comparing infested plants with their respective non-infested controls (e.g. primed mite-infested versus primed control) revealed that the number of DEGs upon pest infestation was consistently lower in primed plants, regardless of the herbivore identity, suggesting a more constrained transcriptional response in primed plants upon phytophagous attack (Fig. 2C). A closer examination of shared DEGs in response to P. brassicae infestation showed that primed and NP plants displayed similar regulation patterns, whereas the transcriptional response to T. urticae infestation differed markedly between primed and NP plants (Fig. 2C). In the latter case, among the DEGs common to both priming treatments, only nine genes were consistently up-regulated upon mite attack. In contrast, 62 genes that were up-regulated in NP plants were down-regulated in primed plants following infestation. Additionally, while only one gene was commonly down-regulated, 77 genes down-regulated in NP plants upon T. urticae attack were up-regulated in primed plants. Notably, these 77 genes were primarily associated with cell wall biogenesis and nucleotide metabolism. The complete set of shared DEGs and enriched GO terms identified from T. urticae and P. brassicae infestation is provided in Supplementary Dataset S2. This analysis was also performed for the DAFs between infested and control plants for each priming treatment. In this case, MeJA-primed plants showed opposite behaviours depending on the biotic challenge. They accumulated a higher number of features upon mite infestation than NP plants, whereas in response to caterpillar infestation the number of detected features was lower than in NP plants (Fig. 2C). Nevertheless, when studying the common DAFs upon pest infestation between primed and NP B. rapa, all behaved similarly, except for three features and one feature detected after mite and caterpillar feeding, respectively.

We also performed a set of targeted metabolic analyses in B. rapa, focusing on key compounds from primary (sugars, amino acids) and secondary (phenylpropanoids, glucosinolates) metabolic pathways, as well as defence-related phytohormones (Fig. 3). These analyses revealed a general trend in primed plants towards reduced levels of glucose (Fig. 3A) but increased content of aliphatic glucosinolates (Fig. 3B). MeJA seed priming also resulted in lower free amino acid levels, specifically of threonine, serine, asparagine, glutamine, lysine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, proline, and valine, and in the case of cysteine a significant drop was observed in primed plants after caterpillar feeding compared with NP larvae-infested plants (Fig. 3C). We also observed lower content of the phenylpropanoid intermediate tyramine in primed plants, although no significant differences were found in others such as the polyamines putrescine and spermidine. When measuring the final products of this pathway we found no differences in anthocyanin content, but lignin deposition increased in MeJA-primed plants (Fig. 3D).

Overview of molecular similarities and divergences across plant species

The previous study on A. thaliana responses to 0.1 mM MeJA seed priming under similar experimental conditions provided a basis for comparing the molecular changes in B. rapa. We evaluated the commonalities and differences between the transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles of A. thaliana and B. rapa responses to seed priming with MeJA. Transcriptomes showed very few overlaps in the number of DEGs between plant species, regardless of the infestation condition. We observed a total of 0.09, 2.24, and 1.17% shared DEGs under control, mite, and larval infestation conditions, respectively (Fig. 5A–C). Furthermore, these few common DEGs between A. thaliana and B. rapa did not always show identical regulatory patterns. For example, in the case of mite-infested plants, 75% of shared DEGs were up-regulated in one plant species while they were down-regulated in the other, and vice versa (Fig. 5B). When analysing divergences in feature abundance, we found similar results, where only 3.36, 3.19, and 2.17% of DAFs in control, mite, and larval infestation conditions were shared between B. rapa and A. thaliana plants (Fig. 5D–F). In this case, all shared DAFs under control conditions displayed the same accumulation pattern (Fig. 5D), whereas with herbivore feeding this behaviour varied (Fig. 5E, F).

Co-expression networks and module–trait relationships

To assess the conservation of MeJA priming-associated transcriptional networks, we performed a consensus WGCNA with A. thaliana and B. rapa transcriptomic datasets. A soft power of 4 was selected to construct a scale-free network (Supplementary Fig. S2). We detected 25 consensus modules (C1–C25) of co-expressed genes. Examination of the cluster dendrograms showed that while A. thaliana displayed well-defined module boundaries, the B. rapa dendrogram appeared less clearly partitioned, although some similarities in the major divisions remained (Fig. 6A, B). The eigengene adjacency heatmaps highlighted species-specific differences in inter-module relationships (Fig. 6C, D). In A. thaliana, correlations among modules were mostly moderate, consistent with a network of relatively independent units (Fig. 6C). In contrast, B. rapa displayed a mixture of strongly correlated modules and others with little or no correlation, suggesting a higher degree of variability in how modules interact (Fig. 6D). Pairwise preservation analysis of consensus modules revealed generally high adjacency values, indicating that modules largely retained consistent co-expression relationships within the consensus network (Fig. 6E). The overall density score of 0.72 further supports a moderate-to-high level of structural conservation across species (Fig. 6F).

(C, D) Heatmap of eigengene adjacencies for (C) A. thaliana and (D) B. rapa. Values close to 1 indicate high adjacency (positive correlation), and values close to 0 indicate low adjacency. (E) Adjacency heatmap for the pairwise preservation between networks. (F) Barplot of the preservation between networks. The observed density score (D = 0.72) reflects a moderate-to-high topological preservation between networks.

However, this observed conservation of transcriptional networks contrasts with the limited DEG overlap between species. To explore whether epigenetic regulation could contribute to this paradigm, we investigated the association of DEGs with histone modifications. In A. thaliana, enrichment analysis using publicly available ChIP-seq datasets revealed that DEGs were significantly enriched in all four examined marks (H3K9me2, H3K4me3, H3K27me3, and H3K27ac), with odds ratios consistently > 2 across infestation conditions (Supplementary Fig. S3). The strongest enrichments were observed for the activating marks H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, although repressive marks also showed significant associations, suggesting that both activating and silencing pathways may contribute to DEG regulation. In B. rapa, where processed ChIP-seq data were available only for H3K27me3, DEGs likewise showed a significant enrichment for this repressive mark (odds ratio > 5), across all infestation conditions (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Subsequently, we evaluated the correlation of the 25 consensus modules with MeJA seed priming. The correlation between each module eigengene and priming traits was calculated using Spearman correlation. A total of 20 modules were significantly correlated with at least one priming condition (Fig. 7). The priming responses under different infestation conditions (control, T. urticae, and P. brassicae) significantly correlated with more than one module, regardless of the plant species, except for primed B. rapa control plants, which did not show any significant correlation. Primed A. thaliana control correlated with C4, C8, C9, C12, C14, and C18; primed mite-infested A. thaliana correlated with C2, C9, C13, C17, C18, and C25; primed caterpillar-infested A. thaliana correlated with C3, C6, and C18; primed mite-infested B. rapa correlated with C2, C3, C7, C11, C12, C19, and C24; and primed caterpillar-infested B. rapa correlated with C2, C3, C12, C14, C16, C18, C21, C22, and C23 (Fig. 7).

Multivariate analysis of plant- and pest-specific patterns

A multivariate analysis was conducted by integrating transcriptomic data, and untargeted and targeted metabolite measurements. We visualized multivariate variation using an RDA ordination plot. The first and second principal RDA axes explained 33.9% and 22.8% of the constrained variance, respectively (Fig. 8A). The B. rapa samples displayed more intra- and inter-cluster tightness, indicating more consistent biochemical responses across conditions. In contrast, A. thaliana samples were more widely dispersed. Permutation tests confirmed that both plant species and pest infestation exerted significant effects on the multivariate response to priming, and that there was a significant interaction between these two factors (Fig. 8B), supporting that the impact of seed priming is highly context dependent.

Discussion

Seed priming is emerging as a promising strategy to enhance plant resistance without compromising growth. Previous work in our lab demonstrated that seed priming with MeJA or SA conferred effective resistance against T. urticae and P. brassicae in the model plant A. thaliana, without incurring growth penalties (Talavera-Mateo et al., 2025). Here, we show that similar priming benefits apply to the crop B. rapa, where both SA and MeJA treatments improved plant resistance with no observable cost to early vegetative growth (Fig. 1). This is especially relevant in light of prior reports showing a persistent growth–defence trade-off in B. rapa under herbivore pressure, whether from the specialist P. brassicae or from the generalist Mamestra brassicae (Kellenberger et al., 2018). Interestingly, seed treatment with MeJA proved to be more effective in reducing pest performance and boosting plant growth than SA (Fig. 1), which also happened in A. thaliana (Talavera-Mateo et al., 2025), and could indicate a conserved response to this priming agent across both plant species.

Interestingly, despite the limited transcriptional differences detected between MeJA-primed and NP plants, the metabolomic analysis revealed a substantially larger number of DAFs (Fig. 2). This apparent discrepancy is consistent with current models of defence priming, as proposed by Harris et al. (2023). The authors describe Type I, which is characterized by persistent transcriptional variation prior to challenge, and Type II, in which the primed state shows little constitutive transcriptional change but remains sensitized for faster or stronger responses upon stress. Within this framework, the relatively small number of DEGs but clear metabolic shifts observed in B. rapa suggests a Type II-like priming mode, where molecular readiness may be established without persistent extensive basal transcriptional reprogramming. This may also explain why some metabolic responses, such as increased SA observed in primed plants upon T. urticae infestation (Fig. 4), are not directly mirrored by transcriptional changes. When examining the transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles of MeJA-primed B. rapa under different herbivore pressures, distinct responses were identified. As previously mentioned, primed mite-infested plants displayed a sharp peak in SA accumulation compared with their primed control. Notably, SA has been described to play a significant role in plant defence against T. urticae (Garcia et al., 2024). Furthermore, GO enrichment showed a potential link between SA accumulation and enhanced cell wall biogenesis or remodelling (Supplementary Dataset S2), which has been previously suggested (Jia et al., 2021). Supporting this view, recent work has emphasized the role of the plant cell wall as a key component of herbivore deterrence (Perez-Alonso et al., 2025). In contrast, under P. brassicae infestation, primed seedlings exhibited reduced cysteine levels (Fig. 3C), suggesting a switch in sulfur metabolism towards aliphatic glucosinolate biosynthesis, which is a known defence mechanism in Brassica crops against lepidopteran herbivores (Agrawal and Kurashige, 2003). This interpretation is supported by the significant up-regulation of genes encoding myrosinase enzymes (Supplementary Dataset S1), which catalyse glucosinolate hydrolysis into toxic defence compounds. Although specialist herbivores such as Pieris spp. have evolved counteradaptations to divert glucosinolate hydrolysis away from highly toxic isothiocyanates toward less harmful nitriles (Wittstock et al., 2004), the induction of the glucosinolate–myrosinase system by pest feeding can still provide effective protection by changing glucosinolate profiles and associated proteins that restore the toxicity of hydrolysis products (Textor and Gershenzon, 2009). Moreover, the energetic cost of processing or sequestering glucosinolates may reduce larval growth and performance.

A similarity found across both plant species was the involvement of the phenylpropanoid pathway in the response of MeJA-primed plants to herbivory, although in A. thaliana these changes led towards shifts in anthocyanin content (Talavera-Mateo et al., 2025) and in B. rapa we observed an increase in lignin deposition (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, even within B. oleracea, differential outcomes in phenolic compounds have been reported when MeJA was applied as a foliar spray. For example, anthocyanin levels decreased in a red cultivar but remained unaffected in a white one (Hassini et al., 2017). Other studies further underscore the influence of exogenous MeJA on the phenylpropanoid pathway in Brassicaceae, with several reports showing altered accumulation of phenolics depending on species, cultivar, and treatment strategy (Guan et al., 2019). These findings point to MeJA as a consistent regulator of phenylpropanoid metabolism, but the direction of its modulation within the pathway remains variable and context dependent.

Therefore, in contrast to phenotypic results, metabolic shifts suggested species-specific defence strategies activated by MeJA seed priming, which were confirmed by our integrative multivariate analysis (Fig. 8). We found that priming effects were highly context dependent, with B. rapa responding in a conservative and more consistent manner, whereas A. thaliana responses were more variable. Such species-specific plasticity is consistent with ecological strategies. For example, Kliebenstein (2014) proposed that domesticated plants such as Brassica may evolve more robust defence mechanisms, while wild species such as A. thaliana retain greater transcriptional flexibility. The more stable response of B. rapa probably reflects its ecological role and breeding history as a crop, favouring tightly coordinated defences (Sultan, 1995).

These observations prompted us to investigate how such divergent outcomes arise from a shared seed priming treatment. In our comparative study, seeds of A. thaliana and B. rapa were treated with 0.1 mM and 1 mM MeJA, respectively, which might raise concerns about the robustness of our conclusions. However, the level of the stress experienced by each plant species seems determinant for the acquisition of the primed status (Costantini et al., 2010), which directly supports the need for species-specific dosing, since Brassica crops have demonstrated higher resilience to exogenous stimuli. Indeed, when identical concentration treatments have been applied to B. napus and A. thaliana, the latter has showed faster and stronger transcriptional responses (Kagale et al., 2007). This is also consistent with the low transcriptomic reprogramming we observed in non-infested primed B. rapa seedlings.

Interestingly, we found a high degree of preservation and comparable mean density in gene co-expression networks between the two primed species (Fig. 6E, F), suggesting that the modular organization of transcriptional regulation is conserved. This observation is consistent with the topological framework for co-expression networks proposed by Horvath and Dong (2008), who showed that conserved module density and connectivity reflect stable regulatory coordination. It also aligns with findings that co-expression network topology is often more evolutionarily conserved than individual gene expression patterns (Mähler et al., 2017), probably reflecting shared core biological processes such as primary metabolism, cell cycle, or basic signalling pathways (Stuart et al., 2003).

However, this conserved co-expression backbone contrasts with the low DEG overlap and distinct species-specific transcriptomic responses observed (Fig. 5A–C). This apparent disconnection suggests that while the regulatory scaffold remains conserved, the activation of specific stress-responsive genes is differentially modulated in each species and under each condition. One plausible mechanism underlying this specificity is epigenetic regulation. Several studies propose that chromatin modifications, such as histone methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, and SUMOylation, changes in nucleosome positioning, and DNA methylation act as molecular memory marks, modulating transcriptional plasticity without altering overall co-expression architecture (Lämke and Bäurle, 2017; Hilker and Schmülling, 2019). They argue that these mechanisms enable plants to fine-tune their responses based on prior exposure, often in a species- and context-dependent manner. Our enrichment analysis further supports this hypothesis, showing that DEGs were not randomly distributed among genes but instead were significantly enriched in genes associated with specific histone modifications (Supplementary Fig. S3). While these results provide only indirect evidence, they align with recent reports that have begun to demonstrate the role of epigenetic regulation in MeJA-induced priming. For example, in Oryza sativa, histone modifications in the promoter regions of defence-related genes and genome-wide DNA methylation changes were associated with enhanced gene responsiveness upon mechanical wounding in MeJA-primed plants (Bertini et al., 2019). Similarly, in Picea abies, Wilkinson et al. (2022) showed that MeJA-induced long-term resistance was accompanied by differential expression of key epigenetic regulators, inducing repression of RNA-directed DNA methylation components and up-regulation of histone modifiers. These findings strengthen the notion that epigenetic modulation may fine-tune priming responses in a species- and context-dependent manner. Nevertheless, research on memory marks and epigenetic changes has been conducted when the priming treatment was applied to seedlings or adult plants. In the study by Huynh et al. (2025), MeJA was applied to seeds of P. abies but no DEGs related to epigenetic regulation were reported in their transcriptomic analysis. Therefore, whether this mechanism is preserved in seed treatment remains unknown.

Beyond epigenetic regulation, the divergent transcriptional responses to MeJA priming between A. thaliana and B. rapa may also stem from their contrasting genome structures. Although the B. rapa R-o-18 line is diploid, its genome still retains the imprint of an ancestral WGT event that occurred after its divergence from A. thaliana (Wang et al., 2011). Over evolutionary time, this polyploid genome has undergone diploidization through fractionation and reorganization, resulting in widespread gene loss and silencing, and retention of duplicates with divergent functions (Cheng et al., 2013). Importantly, genes retained from ancient polyploidy events tend to show higher genetic diversity and often encode stress-related functions (Qi et al., 2021). These duplicates frequently undergo subfunctionalization, neofunctionalization, or differential regulation (Cheng et al., 2013), resulting in asymmetric expression across tissues and treatments. Therefore, even within conserved co-expression modules, different B. rapa paralogues may be preferentially expressed depending on context, contributing to the divergence from A. thaliana.

Taken together, the divergence in transcriptional and metabolic outcomes despite preserved regulatory architecture is likely to reflect the interplay of two major factors: (i) epigenetic regulation, which tunes gene responsiveness in a species- and context-specific manner; and (ii) genomic complexity in B. rapa, which enables functional diversification of retained duplicates within conserved co-expression modules.

From an applied perspective, this work emphasizes the phenotypic benefits of MeJA seed priming across plant species under herbivore pressures, even if the molecular regulatory mechanisms are context specific.
