Comment on “Chiral symmetry restoration, the eigenvalue density of the Dirac operator, and the axial U(1) anomaly at finite temperature”
Matteo Giordano

Abstract
Aoki, Fukaya, and Taniguchi claim that both the spectral density of the Dirac operator at the origin and the topological susceptibility must vanish identically for sufficiently small but nonzero mass m m in the chirally symmetric phase of QCD with two light quark flavors, under certain technical assumptions on the spectrum and on the dependence of observables on m m . Independently of these assumptions, I argue that a crucial step of their proof is not justified, and the validity of these conclusions should be reassessed.



In Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
the authors study the constraints imposed
by the restoration of
N_{f}=2
chiral symmetry on the spectrum of the
Dirac operator in finite-temperature QCD on the lattice. Their
conclusions, based on certain technical assumptions on the spectral
density and on the mass dependence of certain observables, include the
claims that (i.) the spectral density of Dirac eigenvalues,

\rho(\lambda;m)
, vanishes identically at
\lambda=0
for
sufficiently small
m
, i.e.,
\rho(0;m)=0

\forall|m|<m_{0}
, for
some
m_{0}>0
; and (ii.) the topological susceptibility,
\chi_{t}
,
vanishes identically for sufficiently small
m
. These conclusions
have important consequences for the fate of the anomalous

\mathrm{U}(1)_{A}
symmetry in the chiral limit, and are used in
Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
to argue that
\mathrm{U}(1)_{A}
is effectively
restored in the chiral limit in the scalar and pseudoscalar sector as
a necessary consequence of chiral symmetry restoration. Notably,
(ii.) would indicate the presence of a phase transition at small but
nonzero
m
as one goes toward the chiral limit in the
high-temperature phase.

The predictions (i.) and (ii.) have been extensively tested with
numerical lattice simulations toward the chiral
limit
Cossu et al. 2013
;
Tomiya et al. 2017
;
Aoki et al. 2021
;
Aoki et al. 2024
;
Chandrasekharan et al. 1999
;
Dick et al. 2015
;
Ding et al. 2019
;
Ding et al. 2021
;
Kaczmarek et al. 2021
. However, while Refs.
Cossu et al. 2013
;
Tomiya et al. 2017
;
Aoki et al. 2021
;
Aoki et al. 2024
support these
predictions, Refs.
Chandrasekharan et al. 1999
;
Dick et al. 2015
;
Ding et al. 2019
;
Ding et al. 2021
;
Kaczmarek et al. 2021
reach different
conclusions. On the one hand, Refs.
Cossu et al. 2013
;
Tomiya et al. 2017
;
Aoki et al. 2021
;
Aoki et al. 2024
secure a better control
of theoretical uncertainties by using Möbius domain-wall fermions,
at the price of computational limitations on the available volumes and
number of configurations. On the other hand,
Refs.
Chandrasekharan et al. 1999
;
Dick et al. 2015
;
Ding et al. 2019
;
Ding et al. 2021
;
Kaczmarek et al. 2021
gain access to larger volumes and
statistics by using staggered fermions, but introduce theoretical
uncertainties on the chiral and topological properties of the system
by doing so. Due to these difficulties no consensus has been reached
yet, and the status of these predictions remains unsettled.

The proof of (i.) and (ii.) given in Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
makes
crucial use of the following argument. In Sec. II C of
Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
[see their Eqs. (23) and (24)], the authors
state that if for some mass-independent positive-definite observable

\mathcal{O}(A)
, involving only gauge fields,
A
, one has for some
non-negative integers
l_{0},k
that

\lim_{m\to 0}\frac{1}{m^{k}}\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle=0\,,

(1)

where
\langle\ldots\rangle
denotes the expectation value, then one can write

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle=m^{k_{0}}\int D\!A\,P(m,A)\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\,,

(2)

with
k_{0}>k
, where
D\!A
is the gauge-field integration measure and

P(m,A)
a non-negative quantity that does not vanish identically in
the chiral limit,
P(0,A)\not\equiv 0
; it is assumed that the
integral in Eq. (
2
) is finite in the large-volume
limit. According to the authors, this means that the leading
m

dependence of
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle
arises from those contributions
for which
P(0,A)\neq 0
. From Eq. (
2
) they then
infer that

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=m^{k_{0}}\int D\!A\,P(m,A)\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}=O(m^{k_{0}})\,,

(3)

for arbitrary non-negative integer
l
[see their Eq. (25)], as long
as the integral is finite (in the infinite volume limit, presumably).
According to the authors, this is because
\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}
and

\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}
are both positive and share the same support in
configuration space.

It is inherent in the nature of the problem that the expectation value

\langle\ldots\rangle
in Eqs. (
1
)–(
3
)
must be understood as the thermodynamic limit of the expectation value
computed in a finite volume. In fact, for Eq. (
1
)
to be a meaningful statement about the chiral limit, this should be
taken only after the thermodynamic limit. Similarly, the measure

D\!A\,P(m,A)
appearing on the right-hand side of
Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) should be
understood as a measure formally defined on the space of
configurations of infinite volume.

By referring to Eq. (
2
) as providing the leading
m

dependence of
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle
[see after their Eq. (24)], the
authors of Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
are implicitly assuming that the
integral does not vanish in the chiral limit, and so not only that

P(0,A)
is not identically zero, but also that the measure of the
support of
\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}
according to
D\!A\,P(0,A)
is
nonzero. Nonetheless, if this were not the case one would simply find
that
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle
vanishes faster than
m^{k_{0}}
, and their
argument would not change substantially.

Finally, notice that generally Eq. (
1
) does not
imply Eq. (
2
), as
m^{-k}\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle
may be
vanishing more slowly than a power. However, in
Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
this is justified by the assumption that in
the symmetric phase the expectation values of mass-independent
observables involving only gauge fields are analytic function of
m^{2}

at
m=0
, which also allows the authors to write

k_{0}=2\left(\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1\right)
and

P(m,A)=\hat{P}(m^{2},A)
. In any case, this is immaterial for what
follows, and one could simply replace the factor
m^{k_{0}}
with the
leading,
o(m^{k})
dependence of
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle
without
changing the arguments.

The authors of Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
do not provide details on how
they obtain Eq. (
2
) from Eq. (
1
),
and Eq. (
3
) from Eq. (
2
). As one can
always write an
O(m^{k_{0}})
expectation value in the form of
Eq. (
2
) simply by factoring
m^{k_{0}}
out from the
usual path integral, this equation is empty unless one characterizes

P(m,A)
in more detail. For example, just factoring out
m^{k_{0}}

from the usual path-integral measure would generally lead to a
divergent
P(m,A)
in the chiral limit. Moreover, for
\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}

and
\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}
to share the same support in configuration space is
by itself not sufficient to obtain Eq. (
3
) from
Eq. (
2
), as in general the configurations in this
support that are relevant respectively to
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle
and

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle
for
l\neq l_{0}
have no reason to provide
contributions of the same order in
m
. The
l
-independence of how
fast
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle
vanishes, if it does so for some
l=l_{0}
, is
an interesting technical claim on its own, so it is worth discussing
this point in detail.

A characterization of
P(m,A)
that I believe is in the spirit of
Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
, and that allows one to derive
Eq. (
3
) from Eq. (
2
), is the
following. The arguments are heuristic, and any issue of rigor in the
construction of the various measures involved is ignored. One starts
by writing the thermodynamic limit of the expectation value of a
generic observable
\mathcal{G}(A)
depending only on gauge fields as

\langle\mathcal{G}(A)\rangle=\int D\!A\,\mathcal{P}(m,A)\mathcal{G}(A)
,
for a suitable measure
D\!A\,\mathcal{P}(m,A)
on the space of
configurations of infinite volume, assumed to be well-behaved in the
chiral limit. This measure typically vanishes in most of configuration
space, and has support only on the configurations that remain relevant
in the thermodynamic limit. If one now assumes that the
(mass-dependent) support
S_{m}(\mathcal{P})
of
\mathcal{P}(m,A)
and
the (mass-independent) support
S(\mathcal{O})
of
\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}
, which is
the same as the support of
\mathcal{O}(A)
, overlap in a region of measure

O(m^{k_{0}})
according to
D\!A\,\mathcal{P}(m,A)
, and that in the
chiral limit
\mathcal{O}(A)
remains bounded in this region, it is possible
to formally define the measure
D\!A\,P(m,A)
appearing in
Eq. (
2
) so that it leads to Eq. (
3
).
Indeed, denoting with
\chi_{R}(A)
the indicator function of the set of
configurations
R
, one has for any
l

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=m^{k_{0}}\int D\!A\,P(m,A)\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}=O(m^{k_{0}})\,,

(4)

where
P(m,A)\equiv m^{-k_{0}}\mathcal{P}(m,A)\chi_{S(\mathcal{O})}(A)
. [One
should more accurately write
P_{\mathcal{O}}(m,A)
, since
P(m,A)
depends on

\mathcal{O}
.] Since the effective support
S_{m}(\mathcal{P})\cap S(\mathcal{O})
of

\mathcal{O}(A)
, i.e., the support of

\chi_{S_{m}(\mathcal{P})}(A)\chi_{S(\mathcal{O})}(A)
, is assumed to be of
measure
O(m^{k_{0}})
according to
D\!A\,\mathcal{P}(m,A)
, after
rescaling by
m^{k_{0}}
one obtains for the shrinking region of overlap
at most a finite measure according to
D\!A\,P(m,A)
; since
\mathcal{O}(A)

is assumed to be bounded there, the integral is finite.

Summarizing, if the effective support
S_{m}(\mathcal{P})\cap S(\mathcal{O})
of

\mathcal{O}(A)
is of measure
O(m^{k_{0}})
according to

D\!A\,\mathcal{P}(m,A)
, then Eqs. (
2
) and
(
3
) hold with the same meaningful, non-divergent

P(m,A)
in the chiral limit (the one defined above). Since
\mathcal{O}(A)

is by assumption bounded in its effective support, this region gives
at most
O(1)
contributions to the integrals in
Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), that cannot weaken
the
O(m^{k_{0}})
suppression given by the prefactor, independently of

l
, and so
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=O(m^{k_{0}})
follows
\forall l\geq 1
.

Even though Eq. (
3
) is a necessary consequence of
Eq. (
2
) if one accepts the heuristic argument given
above, Eq. (
2
) is not a necessary consequence of
Eq. (
1
). In fact, the relation

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle=O(m^{k_{0}})
does not necessarily require that
the expectation value receives contributions only from a region of
configuration space of vanishing measure
O(m^{k_{0}})
[according to

D\!A\,\mathcal{P}(m,A)
] in the chiral limit, as assumed above, not
even for positive observables; but could instead be due to

\mathcal{P}(m,A)
being supported where
\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}=O(m^{k_{0}})
.
This is the case, for example, for self-averaging observables, that in
the thermodynamic limit equal their expectation value on the support
of
\mathcal{P}(m,A)
. Under this assumption one would then rather
expect
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=O(m^{(k_{0}/l_{0})l})
if

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle=O(m^{k_{0}})
.

Notice that positivity of
\mathcal{O}(A)
plays no role in the argument
leading to Eq. (
4
), and also does not force one to
make the relevant assumption used there, namely that a vanishing
expectation value of
\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}
for some
l_{0}
in the chiral limit
requires it receiving contributions only from a region of
configuration space of vanishingly small size [according to

D\!A\,\mathcal{P}(m,A)
]. The possibility just discussed is equally
valid for a positive observable.

The discussion above can be made rigorous in the language of
density-of-states functions, rewriting the expectation values

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle

as

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=\int dx\,x^{l}p_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)\,,

(5)

where
p_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)\equiv\left\langle\delta(x-\mathcal{O}(A))\right\rangle
, with

\int dx\,p_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)=1
, is the density-of-states function
associated with
\mathcal{O}(A)
. Positivity of
\mathcal{O}(A)
restricts the
support of
p_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)
to
x\geq 0
. In this language, by claiming
that Eq. (
1
) implies Eqs. (
2
) and
(
3
) [with
P(m,A)
understood as in the discussion
above], the authors of Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
are probably implicitly
assuming that the density-of-states function is of the form

p_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)=[1-\alpha(m)]\delta(x)+\alpha(m)\tilde{p}(x;m)\,,

(6)

where
\tilde{p}
is an ordinary positive function supported on

x\geq 0
, normalized for convenience as a probability distribution,

\int dx\,\tilde{p}(x,m)=1
, and with moments

\mu_{l}(m)\equiv\int dx\,x^{l}\tilde{p}(x,m)
bounded in the chiral
limit; and
\alpha(m)=O(m^{k_{0}})
. In this case

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle/\alpha(m)\to\mu_{l}(0)
as
m\to 0
, so

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=\alpha(m)=O(m^{k_{0}})
,
\forall l\geq 1
. Note that
finding a positive integer
l_{0}
for which

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle=O(m^{k_{0}})
for some
k_{0}
is not sufficient to
show that
\alpha(m)=O(m^{k_{0}})
, as it is possible that

\mu_{l_{0}}(0)=0
and so
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle
vanishes faster than

\alpha(m)
in the chiral limit.

However, if
p_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)
is a Gaussian restricted to
x\geq 0
, i.e.,

p_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma(m)}e^{-\frac{(x-x_{0}(m))^{2}}{2\sigma(m)^{2}}}\theta(x)\,,

(7)

with
\theta(x)
the Heaviside function and
x_{0}(m),\sigma(m)>0
, one
finds instead

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle\mathop{\sim}_{m\to 0}\left\{\begin{aligned} &C_{1}^{(l)}x_{0}(m)^{l}&&\text{if }\varsigma(0)\neq 0,\infty\,,\\
&C_{2}^{(l)}x_{0}(m)^{l}\varsigma(m)e^{-\frac{1}{2\varsigma(m)^{2}}}&&\text{if }\varsigma(0)=0\,,\\
&C_{3}^{(l)}\sigma(m)^{l}&&\text{if }\varsigma(0)=\infty\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

where
\varsigma(m)\equiv\frac{\sigma(m)}{x_{0}(m)}
, for nonzero
constants
C_{1,2,3}^{(l)}
. Since

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle/\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle\propto x_{0}(m)^{l-l_{0}}
or

\propto\sigma(m)^{l-l_{0}}
, for
l<l_{0}
it blows up as
m\to 0
if
the relevant scale
x_{0}(m)
or
\sigma(m)
vanishes in the chiral
limit. In this case if
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle
vanishes like
m^{k_{0}}

in the chiral limit, then
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle
for
l<l_{0}
vanishes
more slowly (or not at all), and cannot be
O(m^{k_{0}})
.

An even simpler possibility is of course

p_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)=\delta(x-x_{0}(m))
with
x_{0}(m)\geq 0
vanishing in the
chiral limit. In this case
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=x_{0}(m)^{l}
, and one
reaches the same conclusions as with the Gaussian functional form.

More generally, if there is some
x_{0}(m)
for which the function

\pi_{\mathcal{O}}(x,m)\equiv p_{\mathcal{O}}(x/x_{0}(m),m)
has a well-defined chiral
limit
\pi_{\mathcal{O}}(x,0)
with finite moments, one finds

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=x_{0}(m)^{l}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{l}\pi_{\mathcal{O}}(x,0)+o\left(x_{0}(m)\right)\,,

(9)

and one reaches the same conclusions as above.

Independently of whether the discussion leading to
Eq. (
4
) correctly captures how the authors of
Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
derived Eqs. (
2
) and
(
3
), the counterexamples provided above invalidate
their claim that Eq. (
3
) necessarily follows from
Eq. (
1
), and more generally that

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle=O(m^{k_{0}})
for some
l_{0},k_{0}
implies

\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=O(m^{k_{0}})
for any
l>0
for positive observables
that depend only on gauge fields. At best, one may assume for the
support of the relevant observables the behavior discussed above that
leads to Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
); however,
there is no compelling reason to do so, and the applicability of such
an assumption in realistic cases is called into question by the
simplicity of the counterexamples.

In Sec. III G of Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
[see after their Eq. (92)],
the implication that if
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l_{0}}\rangle=O(m^{k_{0}})
for some

l_{0}
then
\langle\mathcal{O}(A)^{l}\rangle=O(m^{k_{0}})
for any
l
is crucially
used, together with technical assumptions on the spectral density and
the
m^{2}
-analyticity assumption mentioned above, to prove that

\chi_{t}
and
\rho(0;m)
vanish faster than any power of
m
, and
therefore identically below some small but nonzero
m
due to
analyticity in
m^{2}
at
m=0
. Given the problematic status of
Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), and independently
of any technical assumption on the spectrum and on the mass dependence
of observables, these conclusions of Ref.
Aoki et al. 2012
should be
critically reconsidered.
