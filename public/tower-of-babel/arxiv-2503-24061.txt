Simple general magnification of circuit lower bounds
Albert Atserias, Moritz Müller

Abstract
We introduce a technically and conceptually simple approach to magnification of circuit and formula lower bounds. Central to the method are so-called distinguishers , sparse matrices that retain some of the key properties of error-correcting codes. As applications, we generalize and strengthen known general (not problem specific) magnification results and in particular achieve magnification thresholds below known lower bounds. For example, we show that fixed-polynomial formula-size lower bounds for 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} are implied by slightly superlinear formula-size lower bounds for approximating any sufficiently sparse problem in 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} . We also show that the thresholds achieved are sharp. Additionally, our approach yields a uniform magnification result for the Minimum Circuit Size Problem (MCSP). This seems to sidestep the localization barrier.

Introduction

We use standard terminology and notation: by a
circuit
(resp.
formula
) we mean one with inner gates labeled
\neg,\wedge,\vee
of fan-in at most 2 (and, resp., of fan-out at most 1); its
size
is the number of gates. A
promise problem
is given by disjoint sets YES and NO of binary strings. It is contained in
\mathsf{SIZE}[s]
(resp.
\mathsf{FML}[s]
), where
s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, if for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is a circuit (resp. formula)
C_{n}
of size
\leqslant s(n)

that accepts all
n
-bit strings in YES and rejects all
n
-bit strings in NO. The class
\mathsf{P}\textit{-uniform-}\mathsf{SIZE}[s]
additionally requires the map
n\mapsto C_{n}
to be computable in time polynomial in
n
.

Note

\mathsf{P/poly}=\mathsf{SIZE}[n^{O(1)}]
and
\mathsf{NC}_{1}=\mathsf{FML}[n^{O(1)}]
.

1.1
Why are circuit lower bounds difficult?

The non-uniform version of
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}
asks for a problem in
\mathsf{NP}
that is not decided by circuits of polynomial size, i.e., to show
\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{P/poly}
. This seems to be out of reach of current techniques.
Today not even
fixed-polynomial size lower bounds are known, not even for
\mathsf{NE}
and formulas, i.e., whether
\mathsf{NE}\not\subseteq\mathsf{FML}[n^{c}]
for all
c\in\mathbb{N}
, equivalently
\mathsf{NEXP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{NC}_{1}
.

Why are circuit lower bounds so difficult? The natural proof barrier
[
33
]
points out some limitation of current techniques – the notion of a natural proof is, however, informal. Razborov’s program
[
31
]
asks for a formally precise barrier in form of a natural theory that proves known weak lower bounds (cf.
[
31
,
25
]
) and does not prove strong conjectured ones. The unprovability is, ironically, again dubbed to be out of reach of current techniques – of proof complexity (cf.
[
32
]
,
[
20
, Chapter 27-30]
).

A more direct approach to somehow explain the apparent hardness of circuit lower bounds is to try to establish that the
Minimum Circuit Size Problem
is computationally hard in some sense. Namely, for
\sigma:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
,

\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]

Input:

x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with
n=2^{\ell}
for some
n,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

Problem:

is
x
computable by a circuit of size
\leqslant\sigma(\ell)
?

Here, that
x
is computed by a circuit
C
means that
C
has
\ell
inputs and outputs the
i
-th bit of
x
when given the lexicographically
i
-th
\ell
-bit string as input.

Research into the complexity of
\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]
dates back to the 1950s
[
36
]
and intensified in recent years,
starting with
[
19
]
. By the idea that its computational hardness reflects the difficulty of proving circuit lower bounds,
\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]
is expected to become harder for larger

but this is not known.
Concerning its envisioned hardness we refer to
[
1
]
for a survey and mention here only an obstacle:
\mathsf{MCSP}[2^{o(\ell)}]
is
2^{n^{o(1)}}
-sparse and
Buhrmann and Hitchcock
[
4
]
showed that
such problems are not
\mathsf{NP}
-hard unless
\mathsf{PH}
collapses.
Recall, for
q:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, a problem

is

-sparse
if
|Q\cap\{0,1\}^{n}|\leqslant q(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

1.2
Magnification

Thus, not only seem strong circuit lower bounds to be out of reach of current techniques, there is also a lack of understanding of what this very statement means. This is strikingly emphasized by what
Oliveira and Santhanam
[
27
]
called
magnification
results:
a strong lower bound that appear(ed) out of reach of current techniques is implied by a lower bound that is “almost known” – meaning that there are known lower bounds that are only marginally weaker or concern slight variations of the problem or the computational model.

Such results are interesting, first, because they unveil inconsistencies in our intuitions for what is or not within reach of current techniques.
Second, they provide an approach to strong lower bounds that sidesteps the natural proof barrier – see
[
9
]
for an insightful discussion. Both perspectives focus attention on the
magnification threshold
– the “almost known” lower bound.

The initial magnification result from
[
27
]
concerns a promise problem relaxing
\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]
, namely its approximation version: for
\epsilon:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}

\epsilon
-
\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]

Input:

x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with
n=2^{\ell}
for some
n,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

YES:

x
is computable by a circuit of size
\leqslant\sigma(\ell)
.

NO:

d_{H}(x,y)\geqslant\epsilon(n)\cdot n
for all

computable by a circuit of size
\leqslant\sigma(\ell)
.

Here,

is the Hamming distance of
x,y
. In other words, NO instances are truth tables of functions that are not
(1-\epsilon(n))
-approximable by circuits of size
\leqslant\sigma(\ell)
.

Observe, the smaller
\epsilon
, the more NO instances, the harder the problem, and
\epsilon\text{-}\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]
is the same as
\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]
if
\epsilon(n)\leqslant 1/n
for all

.
Under cryptographic assumptions, it is known that
1/3
-
\mathsf{MCSP}[2^{\sqrt{\ell}}]\notin\mathsf{P/poly}
(cf.
[
27
]
).

Theorem 1

(
[

]
)
.

\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{NC}_{1}
if

This magnification threshold can be considered to be “almost known”:

(
[

]
)
.

\mathsf{MCSP}[2^{\sqrt{\ell}}]\notin\mathsf{FML}[n^{2-\delta}]

.

Following

]
a wide variety of magnification results have been discovered, with magnification thresholds concerning almost formulas

]
, probabilistic formulas

]
, zero-error heuristics

,

]
, sublinear randomized algorithms

We do not attempt a survey and refer to

A central role is

and its variants. Besides the mentioned approximation relaxation, prominently its gap relaxation

1.3
General magnification

As for a more principled approach, a significant step forward was taken by
Chen et al.
[
6
]
. Only the sparsity matters: “analogous weak circuit lower bounds […] for
any
equally-sparse
\mathsf{NP}
language also imply major separations in circuit complexity.”

We refer to such results as
general
magnification. An example predating
[
6
]
is

Theorem 3

(
[
10
]
)
.

\mathsf{NEXP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{P/poly}
if there exists an
2^{n^{o(1)}}
-sparse problem
Q\in\mathsf{NP}
such that

Q\not\in\mathsf{SIZE}[n^{1+o(1)}].

In
[
6
]
the conclusion is strengthened to

\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{SIZE}[n^{c}]
for all
c\in\mathbb{N}
and a version for
formulas is proved. A version for probabilistic formulas appears in

[
7
]
– we use the following ad hoc notation: a promise
problem belongs to
\mathsf{PFML}[s]
, where
s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, if for all large
enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a probabilistic formula
\boldsymbol{F}
of size

\leqslant s(n)
(i.e., a random variable whose values are formulas of size

\leqslant s(n)
) such that
\Pr[\boldsymbol{F}(x)=1]=1
for all YES instances
x
of
length
n
and
\Pr[\boldsymbol{F}(x)=1]\leqslant 1/4
for all NO instances
x
of
length
n
.

Theorem 4

(
[
6
,
7
]
)
.

\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{FML}[n^{c}]
for all
c\in\mathbb{N}

if there exists an
2^{n^{o(1)}}
-sparse problem
Q\in\mathsf{NP}
such that (a) or (b):

(a)

Q\not\in\mathsf{FML}[n^{3+o(1)}].

(b)

Q\not\in\mathsf{PFML}[n^{2+o(1)}].

The proofs of these results are based on deep results in complexity theory: Theorem
3

relies
on the hardness versus pseudorandomness trade-off
[
37
]
and the Easy Witness Lemma
[
16
]
. The technical core of the proof of Theorem

is the construction
[
6
]
of a highly efficient hash family based on good error correcting codes and expander-walk sampling.

The main strength of these results is their generality, as it enhances the prospects of magnification to eventually trigger breakthrough lower bounds.
Their main weakness is that it is not clear whether their magnification thresholds should be considered “almost known”. No explicit problem is known outside
\mathsf{SIZE}[5n]
or
\mathsf{FML}[n^{3}]
, and no explicit
2^{n^{o(1)}}
-sparse problem is known outside
\mathsf{FML}[n^{2}]
.
For non-sparse problems, however,
even subcubic lower bounds are known for probabilistic formulas, in particular for
\mathsf{MCSP}[2^{\ell}/\ell^{4}]
as shown in
[
9
, Theorem 47]
(based on
[
11
]
).

For sparse variants, known lower bounds sit sharply below the threshold:

Theorem 5

(
[
7
]
)
.

\mathsf{MCSP}[2^{\sqrt{\ell}}]\notin\mathsf{PFML}[n^{2-\delta}]
for all reals
\delta>0
.

The proof of this result in
[
7
]
is based on a sophisticated construction of a new pseudorandom restriction generator that deserves independent interest.

Remark 6
.

Both Theorems
4
(b) and
5
are proved for probabilistic formulas with

. For the magnification result the variant cited here is slightly stronger and implied by Theorem
9
below. We only consider one-sided error in this work.

1.4
Distinguishers

The proof idea for a magnification result is as follows. Assume
\mathsf{NP}
has small circuits and construct a tiny circuit for a given problem
Q\in\mathsf{NP}
. The crucial step is an efficient hash of YES instances of
Q
to very short fingerprints, a set in
\mathsf{NP}
.
The tiny circuit is then obtained by computing the hash and running a small circuit on the fingerprint. Typically the hash is randomized, so the tiny circuit is probabilistic. Finally, “derandomize this construction in an elementary but careful way”
[
27
]
.

Thus, the core of magnification is compression.
It is parameterized complexity theory that developed a deep theory of compression, namely kernelization theory (cf.
[
13
]
).

1

We use the informal “compression” instead of the formal “kernelization” because our problems are not parameterized. A suitable parameterization would be the logarithm of the sparsity, so only depend on the input length; this is, however, against the spirit of parameterized complexity theory.

In this context the second authors’ PhD Thesis
[
24
]
gave a non-uniform hash family for arbitrary sparse problems in order to derive, via some kernelization theory, Burhman and Hitchcock’s result
[
4
]
mentioned earlier. For this, non-uniformity did not harm but magnification does require uniformity. This leads to our main conceptual contribution:

Definition 7
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\epsilon<\delta\leqslant 1
be real.
View
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
as row vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{2}
. An
(n,m,\epsilon,\delta)
-distinguisher
is a binary
n\times m
matrix
D
such that

d_{H}(x,y)\geqslant\epsilon\cdot n\ \Longrightarrow\ d_{H}(xD,yD)\geqslant\delta\cdot m

for all
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
The
weight
of
D
is the maximum Hamming weight of a column of
D
.

Observe that a
(n,m,\delta,1/n)
-distinguisher
D
is the same as the generator matrix of a linear code with relative distance
\delta
. We trade low weight for larger
\epsilon>1/n
:

Theorem 8
.

Let
0<\epsilon\leqslant 1
.
There is an algorithm that given
n\in\mathbb{N}
computes in time polynomial in
n
for some
m\leqslant n^{7}
an
(n,m,n^{-\epsilon},1/8)
-distinguisher
of weight
\leqslant\left\lceil 2n^{\epsilon}\right\rceil
.

Given an
2^{n^{o(1)}}
-sparse
Q
, roughly, the fingerprint of an
n
-bit string

are
r
random positions in
yD
. Low weight ensures efficiency of the hash: each bit of the fingerprint is the
\mathsf{XOR}
of
\leqslant\lceil 2n^{\epsilon}\rceil
many input bits, so computed by a formula of size
O(n^{2\epsilon})
.
Assume

x\in Q
. Then the fingerprint equals that of some
x\in Q
with probability
\leqslant 2^{n^{o(1)}}\cdot(7/8)^{r}
– this is small already for
r\leqslant n^{o(1)}
. This way, a small formula for the set of fingerprints of
x\in Q
gives a tiny formula distinguishing such

from all
x\in Q
, i.e., decide
n^{-\epsilon}
-
Q
.
This notation generalizes
\epsilon\text{-}\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]
to arbitrary
Q
:
for
\epsilon:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

\epsilon
-
Q

Input:

x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

YES:

x\in Q
.

NO:

d_{H}(x,y)\geqslant\epsilon(n)\cdot n
for all
y\in Q
.

1.5
This work

The contribution of the present work is a conceptually modular and technically simple approach to general magnification. It is based on distinguishers and developed from scratch.

To illustrate how magnification is derived from compression, Section
2
gives a highly simplified proof of Theorem
3
.
Section

-sparse

-

.

This generalizes Theorem

,

.
For small

]
;

.

\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}^{\oplus\mathsf{P}}
if there exist a real

This magnification threshold can be judged “almost known” because Santhanam and Williams

This motivates two questions: the first is

An easy example

We showcase how magnification is derived from compression by giving a very simple proof of a strengthening of Theorem
3
.

Theorem 12

(
[
10
]
)
.

For all
c\in\mathbb{N}
, all reals
\delta>0
and

\gamma<\delta/(c+1)
, and all

-sparse problems

, if

, then

.

Proof.

Let
\delta,c,\gamma
accord the statement and assume
\mathsf{NE}\subseteq\mathsf{SIZE}[n^{c}]
.
Let
Q\in\mathsf{NTIME}[2^{n^{\gamma}}]
be
2^{n^{\gamma}}
-sparse. We show
Q\in\mathsf{SIZE}[n^{1+\delta}]
.

Let
K
contain the tuples
\langle t,n,1^{\left\lceil n^{\gamma}\right\rceil},i\rangle

such that
t,n,i
are natural numbers in binary,
1\leqslant i\leqslant tn
, and there are
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}\in Q_{n}
with
x_{1}<_{\mathit{lex}}\cdots<_{\mathit{lex}}x_{t}
such that
the
i
-th bit of the concatenation
x_{1}\cdots x_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{tn}
is 1. Here,

denotes (strict) lexicographic order, and we write

with exactly
\left\lceil\log(tn+1)\right\rceil
many bits.
Clearly,
K\in\mathsf{NE}.

We describe small circuits for
Q
. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We consider
interesting
inputs to
K
, namely,
\langle t,n,1^{\left\lceil n^{\gamma}\right\rceil},i\rangle
where
t:=|Q\cap\{0,1\}^{n}|

and
1\leqslant i\leqslant tn
. Their length
m=m(n)\leqslant O(n^{\gamma})
depends only on
n
.
By assumption there is a size
\leqslant m^{c}
circuit
C(i)
deciding whether
\langle t,n,1^{\left\lceil n^{\gamma}\right\rceil},i\rangle\in Q
.
Let
x^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{tn}
be the concatenated list of the
t
strings in

in

,

. Thus,

-th bit of

.

Recall, a

3.2
Existence

Recall, an
(n,m,\delta,\epsilon)
-distinguisher for
\epsilon\leqslant 1/n
is an
(m,n,\delta)
-code and we intend to trade larger
\epsilon
for small weight. We start observing that such a trade-off is possible:

Proposition 15
.

There exist

such that for every sufficiently large

and for every real
\epsilon\leqslant 1/(c\log n)

(n,dn,1/5,\epsilon)
-distinguisher of weight at most

.

Proof.

Let
\mathit{Hw}(y)
denote the Hamming weight of

.
The following is a biased random subset principle:

Claim.
Let
0\leqslant p\leqslant 1
and
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{x}
be the random string in
\{0,1\}^{n}
obtained by
independently, for each

i
-th bit to 1 with probability

and to 0 with probability

. Then

\textstyle\Pr[y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}=1]=1/2-(1-2p)^{\mathit{Hw}(y)}/2.

(1)

Write

and
p_{w}
for the r.h.s. of (
1
). The claim is trivial for

.
For

\displaystyle\Pr[y\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}=1]=p\cdot(1-p_{w-1})+(1-p)\cdot p_{w-1}=(1-2p)p_{w-1}+p=p_{w}.

We choose the constants

in the course of proof. Fix
\epsilon\leqslant 1/(c\log n)
. Set
p:=1/(2\epsilon n)

and let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{dn}
be independent and distributed as
\boldsymbol{x}
above.
Then
\mathbb{E}[\mathit{Hw}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})]=1/(2\epsilon)
for every
j\in[dn]
. By Chernoff,
\mathit{Hw}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})>1/\epsilon
with probability
<2^{-1/(d_{1}\epsilon)}
for some constant

(independent of

). Then, with probability
\geqslant 1-dn2^{-1/(d_{1}\epsilon)}
, all
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
s have Hamming weight
\leqslant 1/\epsilon
. Call this event

. If we choose

,
then
\epsilon\leqslant 1/(c\log n)

implies

for large enough

(and all

).

For every
j\in[dn]
and

of Hamming weight

, we have by (

\Pr[y\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{\top}=1]\geqslant 1/2-e^{-w/(\epsilon n)}/2>0.3.

Hence,
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{j\in[dn]}y\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{\top}]>0.3\cdot dn
. By the Chernoff Bound,
\sum_{j\in[dn]}y^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}^{j}\geqslant 0.2\cdot dn
with probability at least
1-2^{-dn/d_{2}}
for some constant

(independent of

). Choosing

, this is
\geqslant 1-1/2\cdot 2^{-n}
. By the union bound, this holds for all

as above simultaneously with probability
\geqslant 1/2
. Call this event

.
For large enough

, the event

has positive probability. Let the

columns of

consist of corresponding realizations of the
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
s.
∎

\max\{cp^{2}L(f),c\}\leqslant cp^{2}L(f)+c/(1-cp^{2})
.
For the inductive step, let
t>0
and recall

\boldsymbol{\rho}=\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}
for
\boldsymbol{\rho^{\prime}}:=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}\cdots\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t-1}
where the
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{i}
are independent and
(p,k)
-regular.
Letting

range over realizations of
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\mathbb{E}[L(f{\upharpoonleft}\boldsymbol{\rho})]&=&\textstyle\sum_{\rho^{\prime}}\Pr[\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}=\rho^{\prime}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[L((f{\upharpoonleft}\rho^{\prime}){\upharpoonleft}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t})]&\;\text{as $\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}$ are independent}\\
&\leqslant&\textstyle\sum_{\rho^{\prime}}\Pr[\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}=\rho^{\prime}]\cdot\big(cp^{2}L(f{\upharpoonleft}\rho^{\prime})+c\big)&\;\text{by Lemma~\ref{lem:hs1}}\\
&=&cp^{2}\mathbb{E}[L(f{\upharpoonleft}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})]+c&\\
&\leqslant&cp^{2}\big(c^{t-1}p^{2(t-1)}L(f)+c/(1-cp^{2})\big)+c&\;\text{by induction}\\
&=&c^{t}p^{2t}L(f)+c/(1-cp^{2}).&\end{array}\qed

6.2
The lower bound

The following implies Theorem
10
.

Theorem 32
.

There exists
d\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that for all reals
0<\epsilon,\delta\leqslant 1
and all

\sigma:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
with
\ell^{d}\leqslant\sigma(\ell)\leqslant 2^{o(\ell)}
,

n^{-\epsilon}\text{-}\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]\not\in\mathsf{PFML}[n^{2\epsilon-\delta}].

Proof.

We choose
d>0
in the end of the proof. Let
\delta,\epsilon,\sigma
be as stated.
Write
2^{\ell}=n
and
\sigma=\sigma(\ell)
.
It suffices to rule out probabilistic formulas of size at most

s:=n^{2\epsilon-6\delta}.

We want to choose
p,t,k
in Lemma
31
to ensure that
L(F{\upharpoonleft}\boldsymbol{\rho})
is probably constant. Let
c
be the lemma’s constant, and set

k:=\left\lceil\sigma^{1/d}\right\rceil,\ t:=\left\lceil\frac{2d\log s}{\log\sigma}\right\rceil.

and
for
p
choose a negative power of 2 such that

(c^{1/2}s^{1/(2t)})^{-1}/2\leqslant p\leqslant(c^{1/2}s^{1/(2t)})^{-1}.

This ensures
c^{t}p^{2t}\leqslant 1/s
. As
p\leqslant 1/(2c)^{1/2}
and
1/p^{2}\leqslant 4cs^{1/t}\leqslant 4c\sigma^{1/(2d)}\leqslant k
for large enough
n
, the lemma applies and gives
\mathbb{E}[L(F{\upharpoonleft}\boldsymbol{\rho})]\leqslant 1+2c
.
Thus, for a suitable constant
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\Pr[L(F{\upharpoonleft}\boldsymbol{\rho})\geqslant s_{0}]<1/2.

(9)

Given
\boldsymbol{\rho}
and an independent
\boldsymbol{z}
uniform in
\{0,1\}^{n}
define the random variable
\boldsymbol{x}
in
\{0,1\}^{n}
to have
i
-th bit equal to
\boldsymbol{\rho}(i)
if
\boldsymbol{\rho}(i)\neq*
, and otherwise equal to the
i
-th bit of
\boldsymbol{z}
.

Let
E
be the event that
\boldsymbol{x}

is
not
a NO instance of
n^{-\epsilon}
-
\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]
, and
\bar{E}
its complement.

Claim:

\Pr[E]\leqslant o(1)
.

Proof of the claim:
Set
\boldsymbol{S}:=\boldsymbol{\rho}^{-1}(*)
. Since
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is
(p^{t},k)
-regular,
|\boldsymbol{S}|
has expectation
p^{t}n
. Using
(4c)^{t/2}\leqslant s^{o(1)}
,

p^{t}n\geqslant n/((4c)^{t/2}s^{1/2})\geqslant n\cdot s^{-1/2-o(1)}\geqslant n^{1-\epsilon+3\delta-o(1)}\geqslant 4n^{1-\epsilon+2\delta}.

(10)

The variance of
|\boldsymbol{S}|
is
\leqslant\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{S}|]
by
k\geqslant 2
-wise independence.
Let
E_{0}
be the event that
|\boldsymbol{S}|\geqslant 2n^{1-\epsilon+2\delta}
, and
\bar{E}_{0}
be its complement. By Chebychev,

\Pr[\bar{E}_{0}]\leqslant 4/\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{S}|]\leqslant o(1).

Thus, it suffices to show
\Pr[E\mid E_{0}]\leqslant o(1)
.

Let
S
range over realizations of
\boldsymbol{S}

with
|S|\geqslant 2n^{1-\epsilon+2\delta}
, and let
y
range over YES instances. Then

E\subseteq\bigcup_{y}E_{y}
where
E_{y}
is the event that

d_{H}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{S}},y_{\boldsymbol{S}})<n^{1-\epsilon}
. Since there are at most
2^{n^{o(1)}}
many YES instances,

\Pr[E\mid E_{0}]\leqslant 2^{n^{o(1)}}\cdot\max_{y}\Pr[E_{y}\mid E_{0}]
.
Hence, it suffices to show
\Pr[E_{y}\mid E_{0}]\leqslant 2^{-n^{1-\epsilon+\delta}}
for all
y
.
Since
\Pr[E_{y}\mid E_{0}]\leqslant\max_{S}\Pr[E_{y}\mid\boldsymbol{S}=S]
it suffices to show

\Pr[E_{y}\mid\boldsymbol{S}=S]\leqslant 2^{-n^{1-\epsilon+\delta}},

for all
y,S
. To see this, note
\Pr[E_{y}\mid\boldsymbol{S}=S]=\Pr[d_{H}(\boldsymbol{z}_{S},y_{S})<n^{1-\epsilon}]
(by independence of
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{S}
) and

d_{H}(\boldsymbol{z}_{S},y_{S})
is the sum of
|S|
many independent indicators each with expectation
1/2
.
By Chernoff,
\Pr[d_{H}(\boldsymbol{z}_{S},y_{S})<n^{1-\epsilon}]\leqslant 2^{-\Omega(|S|)}\leqslant 2^{-n^{1-\epsilon+\delta}}
.
\dashv

For contradiction, assume
\boldsymbol{F}
is a size
\leqslant s
probabilistic formula for
n^{-\epsilon}
-
\mathsf{MCSP}[\sigma]
. Then, letting
x
range over NO instances,

\textstyle\Pr[\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{x})=1]\leqslant\Pr[E]+\sum_{x}\Pr[\boldsymbol{F}(x)=1,\boldsymbol{x}=x]\leqslant o(1)+1/4\Pr[\bar{E}]<1/2.

We thus find realizations
F,\rho,z,x
of
\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}
such that
F(x)=0
and
and
L(F{\upharpoonleft}\rho)<s_{0}
(by (
9
)).
We get a contradiction by finding a YES instance
x^{\prime}
rejected by
F
.

Let
x^{\prime\prime}
be
\rho
with
*
replaced by
0
. Then define
x^{\prime}
from
x^{\prime\prime}
by flipping
0
s to 1s in positions corresponding to variables in
F{\upharpoonleft}\rho
that are 1 in
z
. Then
F(x^{\prime})=(F{\upharpoonleft}\rho)(x)=F(x)=0
.
Note that
<s_{0}
many positions are flipped.
We are left to show that
x^{\prime}
is computed by a circuit of size
\leqslant\sigma
.

First consider
x^{\prime\prime}
. We ask for a small circuit with 2 output bits that computes
\rho
in the encoding
01,11,00
of
0,1,*
. Then, taking the conjunction of the output
bits gives a small circuit for
x^{\prime\prime}
.
To get such a circuit we choose
\boldsymbol{\rho}
concretely as the composition of
t
many
(p,k)
-regular
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
according to Example
28
– this can be done because
p=2^{-r}
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
with
2^{r+1}\leqslant n
; indeed,

2^{r+1}=2/p\leqslant 4c^{1/2}\sigma^{1/(4d)}\leqslant k\leqslant n
for large enough

.

We claim there is a circuit of size polynomial in
k\ell
. Since
t<\ell
, it suffices to show each of the corresponding realizations
\tilde{\rho}
is computed by a small circuit.
Each
\tilde{\rho}
is determined by some
j\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{r+1}}^{k}
,
and
i\mapsto\tilde{\rho}(i)
is computed
(given
k,2^{r+1}\leqslant k,n=2^{\ell},j
)

. Choose
e\in\mathbb{N}
such that

To get a circuit
C^{\prime}
for

<s_{0}

, we can choose

\leqslant\sigma
.
∎

Remark 33
.

Given a real
c>0
we can choose
d
sufficiently large, so that the YES instance
x^{\prime}
constructed has circuit complexity
\sigma^{1/c}
. Hence, the above holds for the gap / approximation version of
\mathsf{MCSP}
whose YES instances are strings computed by circuits of size
\leqslant\sigma^{1/c}
and whose NO instances are strings that cannot be
(1-n^{-\epsilon})
-approximated by circuits of size
\leqslant\sigma
.

Discussion

[
7
, Theorem 1.5]
proves a slight strengthening of the special case

.
As mentioned in the introduction, the proof method in
[
7
]
is much more complicated and deserves independent interest. It can handle two-sided error and for

achieves even slightly superquadratic lower bounds

[
7

(based on
[
11
]

Concerning lower bounds for the approximation version,
[

extended Theorem

\epsilon(n)
-
\mathsf{MCSP}[2^{\sqrt{\ell}}]
but only for tiny

[
28
]
states that “existing lower bound methods
are more suitable for proving lower bounds”
[

for the gap version as opposed to the approximation version of

and its proof. Maybe this is an interesting insight.
