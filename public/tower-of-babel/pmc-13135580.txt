Hematologic and molecular response to ropeginterferon alfa-2b in patients with polycythemia vera: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ammar Elgadi, Mohammed Wagealla, Tibyan Noorallah, Rayan Esmail, Shafee S. Almahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ropeginterferon alfa-2b is an interferon used in the treatment of myeloproliferative neoplasms, particularly polycythemia vera. Its efficacy in achieving hematologic and molecular responses has been demonstrated in clinical trials, but pooled data on long-term outcomes and sustained response remain limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the hematologic and molecular response over 36 months. PubMed, Scopus, Science Direct, and Google Scholar databases were searched to identify studies reporting hematologic and molecular responses to ropeginterferon alfa-2b. Studies were included if they provided data on complete hematologic response (CHR) and JAK2V617F variant allele frequency (VAF) reduction. Pooled proportions and mean reductions were calculated using random-effects models. The pooled proportion of CHR increased progressively from 0.19 (95% CI: 0.04–0.57) at 3 months to 0.73 (95% CI: 0.17–0.97) at 36 months. Molecular response, measured by VAF reduction, deepened over time from − 7.33 (95% CI: -9.85 to -4.81) at 3 months to -54.90 (95% CI: -65.61 to -43.99) at 36 months. Subgroup analyses revealed significant variability in response rates, particularly in early follow-up periods. Ropeginterferon alfa-2b achieves significant and sustained hematologic and molecular responses over 36 months. This makes it a promising treatment for polycythemia vera. While variability in early responses needs further investigation, the sustained long-term efficacy compared to hydroxyurea supports its use in clinical practice. Future studies should focus on identifying predictors of response and optimizing treatment protocols to maximize patient outcomes.

Background

Polycythemia vera (PV) is a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by excessive erythrocytosis due to mutations in the JAK2 gene, primarily JAK2V617F, which is present in more than 95% of cases [1, 2]. This clonal hematopoietic disorder results in elevated hematocrit levels, increased blood viscosity, and a heightened risk of thrombotic events, myocardial infarction, and stroke [3–5]. Traditional treatment focuses on hematocrit control through phlebotomy, cytoreductive therapy with hydroxyurea, and low-dose aspirin to reduce thrombosis risk [6]. However, these strategies have limitations, particularly concerning hydroxyurea resistance, intolerance, and a possible risk of developing leukemia when treatment failure [7, 8]. For patients who become resistant or intolerant to hydroxyurea, ruxolitinib, a targeted JAK1/2 inhibitor, is an approved and effective second-line therapy. This drug effectively reduces the JAK2V617F allele burden, a reduction that was associated with improved progression-free and event-free survival [9, 10]. As a result of the ongoing need to optimize long-term outcomes, alternative therapeutic options such as ropeginterferon alfa-2b, have gained interest due to their potential for sustained disease control and modification of the underlying disease biology [7].

Ropeginterferon alfa-2b is a long-acting, pegylated interferon targeting abnormal blood stem cells carrying JAK2 mutations. Reducing these malignant cells can help control the disease and even achieve molecular remission in some patients [11]. Unlike hydroxyurea, which primarily suppresses hematopoiesis without directly altering the malignant clone, ropeginterferon alfa-2b has demonstrated the ability to reduce JAK2V617F allele burden [11]. In the PROUD-PV and CONTINUATION-PV trials, ropeginterferon alfa-2b showed superior molecular responses compared to hydroxyurea while maintaining hematocrit control and reducing the need for phlebotomy [12]. Long-term follow-up from these trials has further indicated that ropeginterferon alfa-2b may delay disease progression, potentially reducing the risk of transformation to myelofibrosis or acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [12].

The safety and tolerability of ropeginterferon alfa-2b have been key considerations in clinical decision-making. At the same time, interferon therapy has historically been associated with significant adverse effects. Ropeginterferon alfa-2b improved pharmacokinetic profile allows for less frequent dosing, potentially enhancing adherence and reducing toxicity [5, 7, 9, 13]. Furthermore, emerging real-world data suggest that ropeginterferon alfa-2b may be particularly beneficial in younger patients and those with high-risk disease features, reinforcing its role as a viable alternative to conventional cytoreductive therapies [13].

Given the evolving landscape of PV management, a comprehensive evaluation of ropeginterferon alfa-2b efficacy and safety is essential for optimizing treatment strategies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess its impact on hematocrit control and molecular responses.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhere to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [14]. Our analysis included papers on patients diagnosed with polycythemia vera based on WHO diagnostic criteria. The targeted intervention was Peginterferon/Ruxolitinib. The primary outcomes were hematologic control, defined simply as hematocrit < 45% without phlebotomy, and molecular response rate measured through JAK2V617F allele burden reduction. We included Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, and case-control studies. Only studies in English were included. Conference proceedings, dissertations, and preprints were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A database search was conducted in January 2025. PubMed, Scopus, Science Direct, and Google Scholar databases were searched for relevant articles. Our full search strategy was “(Ropeginterferon) OR (BESREMi) OR (ropeginterferon alfa-2b-njft) OR (ropeg-IFN) OR (ropegIFN) OR (ROPEG)”.

Study selection process

The records from the databases were downloaded to reference manager. Duplicates were removed then the records were uploaded to a Google spreadsheet for screening. Two independent authors screened titles and abstracts for relevance. Discrepancies between these authors were solved through discussion with a third author. Studies meeting inclusion criteria underwent full-text screening by the same authors (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

A data extraction sheet was developed. Data fields include: study characteristics as Author, year, country, and study design; Population details: Sample size, demographics, disease duration; Intervention details: Dosage, duration, and treatment protocols; and outcomes, namely hematologic control, and JAK2V617F allele burden reduction. Information for risk of bias assessment was also extracted. We used Cochrane ROBINS-I V2 for Risk of bias assessment [15]. The standard deviation in Sou 2024 was not found, so we used the Hozo et al. (2005) formula to calculate the mean and SD from the median, assuming the min was 0 and max was 100, and the sample size is known to be 49 patients [16].

Data synthesis

Single-arm meta-analysis was conducted for the two primary outcomes using R version 4.4. The results were reported as the proportion of patients with hematologic control and the mean difference in JAK2V617F variant allele frequency (VAF), both with their 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics.

Results

Characteristics of the included studies

We included ten studies with their extension phases including: Suo 2024 [17, 18], Koschmieder 2023 [19], Kirito 2024 [10, 20], Jin 2023 [21], Yacoub 2019 [22], Palandri 2024 [13], Gisslinger 2019 [12], Gisslinger 2015 [23], Barbui 2023 [24], and Yoon and Lee 2024 [25, 26]. Only the ropeginterferon alfa-2b arms were included in the analysis. A total of 602 patients with polycythemia vera (PV) were analyzed. Most patients had received prior hydroxyurea (HU) treatment before initiating ropeginterferon alfa-2b therapy. The majority of the studies were clinical trials. They were conducted across multiple countries including China, Germany, Japan, North America, Europe, Italy, and Korea. Ropeginterferon alfa-2b was administered subcutaneously in different dosing regimens, commonly starting at 250–350 mcg and increasing to 500 mcg. Some studies used fixed or weight-adjusted doses. The follow-up duration ranged from 6 to 36 months. Some studies had longer extension phases (Table 1).

Risk of bias assessment

The ROBINS-I V2 assessment revealed that single-arm studies had a high risk of bias due to confounding (Domain 1) from lack of controls. Most of these studies had low bias in intervention classification (Domain 2), outcome measurement (Domain 6), and reporting (Domain 7). Randomized trials showed low overall bias. Retrospective study (Palandri 2024) had moderate risks in confounding and selection. Dose adjustments and tolerability-driven modifications introduced moderate bias in Domains 3 and 4 for several studies. Missing data bias (Domain 5) was consistently low across all studies (Fig. 2).

Hematologic response

The pooled proportion of complete hematologic response (CHR) to ropeginterferon alfa-2b demonstrated progressive improvement over 36 months (Fig. 1). At 3 months, the pooled CHR was 0.19 (95% CI: 0.04–0.57), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 74%). By 6 months, the proportion increased to 0.36 (95% CI: 0.16–0.63), though heterogeneity remained substantial (I2 = 80.9%). At 9 months, the pooled estimate rose to 0.51 (95% CI: 0.22–0.79) (I2 = 80.9%I2 = 80.9%, P = 0.0013P = 0.0013). By the end of the first year (12 months), the pooled CHR reached 0.54 (95% CI: 0.36–0.72) (I2 = 85.1%) (Fig. 3).

Proportion of complete hematologic response (CHR) with ropeginterferon alfa-2b treatment at different assessment visits over 36 months

In the second year sustained improvement was observed at 15 months (0.60 [0.31–0.58]; I2 = 80.5%) and 18 months (0.69 [0.26–0.93]; I2 = 79.6%). At 21 months, the pooled CHR remained at 0.69 (95% CI: 0.29–0.92) (I2 = 75.1%), and by 24 months, it reached 0.71 (95% CI: 0.65–0.83) (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3).

Responses continued to improve during the third year, with a pooled CHR of 0.55 (95% CI: 0.00–1.00) at 27 months (I2 = 85.9%) and 0.66 (95% CI: 0.00–1.00) at 30 months (I2 = 75.3%). By 33 months, the pooled CHR was 0.58 (95% CI: 0.00–1.00) (I2 = 76.7%), and at 36 months, it peaked at 0.73 (95% CI: 0.17–0.97) (I2 = 18.6%). Subgroup differences were significant across all time points (χ2 = 30.28, P = 0.0044)(Fig. 3).

Molecular response

Pooled mean reductions in JAK2V617F variant allele frequency (VAF) deepened progressively over time (Fig. 2). At 3 months, the pooled reduction was − 7.33 (95% CI: -9.85 to -4.81) with minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). By 6 months, the reduction increased to -17.80 (95% CI: -22.84 to -12.70) in the largest subgroup (Jin 2022), though heterogeneity rose (I2 = 52.4%). At 9 months, the pooled estimate was − 12.67 (95% CI: -15.55 to -6.79) (I2 = 0%). By the end of the first year (12 months) the pooled reduction was − 16.33 (95% CI: -24.40 to -8.26) (I2 = 47.1%I2 = 47.1%, P = 0.1001P = 0.1001), driven by Suo 2024 (-21.20 [− 25.87 to − 16.53]) (Fig. 4).

In the second year, at 15 months, the pooled reduction was − 14.00 (95% CI: -27.05 to -0.04) (I2 = 0%), and by 18 months, it reached − 25.67 (95% CI: -39.98 to -11.36) (I2 = 0%). At 21 months, the pooled reduction was − 30.83 (95% CI: -46.08 to -15.58) (I2 = 0%), and by 24 months, it deepened to -40.27 (95% CI: -52.46 to -28.07) (I2 = 75.9%) (Fig. 4).

During the third year, JAK2V617F VAF continued to decline. At 27 months, the pooled reduction was − 44.27 (95% CI: -52.46 to -32.07) (I2 = 0%), and by 30 months, it reached − 49.60 (95% CI: -51.11 to -38.09) (I2 = 46.7%). At 33 months, the pooled reduction was − 52.00 (95% CI: -63.20 to -40.80) (I2 = 62.1%). By 36 months, Kirilo 2024 reported the maximal reduction (-54.90 [− 65.61 to − 43.99]). Subgroup differences were highly significant across all time points (χ2 = 262.15, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis that investigated the hematologic and molecular response of ropeginterferon alfa-2b in PV patients. We included 10 studies and their extension phases with a total of 602 patients, most of whom had received prior hydroxyurea (HU) treatment before ropeginterferon alfa-2b therapy. Over a 36-month period, our findings indicate that ropeginterferon alfa-2b therapy results in an overall improvement in hematologic and molecular response.

We observe a consistent reduction in hematocrit levels, as the pooled proportion of hematologic response increases from 0.19 at 3 months to 0.73 at 36 months, indicating that ropeginterferon alfa-2b is an effective long-term therapy for Polycythemia Vera (PV) patients, providing progressive and sustained improvement in hematocrit levels over time. While standard treatment such as hydroxyurea also reduces hematocrit levels, its efficacy appears to be less sustained over time compared to ropeginterferon alfa-2b [12]. An additional advantage of ropeginterferon alfa-2b over hydroxyurea is that some patients on hydroxyurea may require additional phlebotomies to maintain target hematocrit levels, indicating variability in response [5]. In contrast, ropeginterferon alfa-2b has been shown to significantly reduce the need for phlebotomies in low-risk PV patients [27, 28].

Our results also indicate a progressive decrease in JAK2V617F variant allele frequency (VAF) over time, with the mean decline escalating from − 7.33 at three months to -54.90 at 36 months. This outcome suggests that ropeginterferon alfa-2b effectively reduces the allelic burden in patients with polycythemia vera (PV). The JAK2V617F allele burden plays a crucial role in disease severity and progression, as an allele burden greater than 50% significantly increases the risk of developing myelofibrosis (MF). Moreover, a higher JAK2V617Fallele burden is associated with more severe clinical manifestations, including elevated hemoglobin concentration and spleen enlargement [29–31]. Additionally, the JAK2V617F allele burden has a significant impact on thrombotic risk in PV patients, with greater allele burdens being related to an increased incidence of thrombotic events [32]. Also treatment response can be predicted by the degree of JAK2V617F allele burden, as patients with a lower allele burden are likely to have a complete hematological response, whereas those with greater burdens often show either a partial response or no response [33]. In contrast to ropeginterferon alfa-2b, hydroxyurea does not effectively lower allele burden [34]. Although several studies have reported that Hydroxyurea lowers the JAK2V617F allele burden in patients with polycythemia vera [35], other studies suggest that Hydroxyurea does not significantly lower the allele burden in the majority of individuals, indicating variability in response [36, 37]. Similar to the ropeginterferon alfa-2b is PegINFα, which has proven to be a successful therapy for reducing the JAK2V617F allelic load in PV patients. However, the degree of reduction varies, with some patients experiencing a decrease of less than 1% [38–40]. Thus, ropeginterferon alfa-2b is superior in reducing the allelic burden in patients with Polycythemia Vera compared to HU and PegINFα.

When comparing the side effects, ropeginterferon alfa-2b have a distinct safety profile compared to HU, largely because it functions via selective inhibition of the malignant clone rather than inducing the non-specific DNA damage characteristic of HU [17]. While the overall frequency of adverse events between the two treatments is often comparable [12], the specific nature of their toxicities differs significantly. Ropeginterferon alfa-2b is primarily associated with known interferon-related side effects, which include flu-like symptoms, elevated liver enzymes, autoimmune reactions such as thyroiditis, and psychiatric events like mood depression [12, 13, 21, 23]. In contrast, HU is frequently linked to cytopenias, aphthous ulcers, and cutaneous vasculitic toxicities, which can manifest as severe leg ulcers or gangrene [12]. A critical distinction in their long-term safety profiles is the risk of secondary malignancies; long-term HU therapy has been associated with the development of non-melanoma skin cancers (including basal cell carcinoma and melanoma) and disease transformation to acute myeloid leukemia, events that were observed exclusively in HU-treated cohorts rather than in those receiving ropeginterferon alfa-2b [12, 13, 26]. Furthermore, HU is a pregnancy category D drug linked to human fetal malformations and altered spermatogenesis, making it contraindicated for female and male patients desiring parenthood, whereas ropeginterferon alfa-2b does not share these specific reproductive contraindications [12, 13].

Nevertheless, Symptomatic improvements have also been shown in patients switching from hydroxyurea to ropeginterferon alfa-2b, including relief from abdominal pain, shortness of breath, and bone pain, which contributes to an improved quality of life. However, itching became worse in some cases [41]. In contrast, hydroxyurea has a negative influence on quality of life due to its association with a higher burden of symptoms related to inflammatory dysregulation [41].

This systematic review had important limitations to be considered. First, the authors needed to impute missing standard deviation data using a mathematical formula. This can reduce meta-analysis precision. Second, heterogeneity in study designs due to varying dosing regimens (fixed, weight-adjusted, or escalating doses) and follow-up durations (6–36 months), which complicates comparisons. Although all the doses found to be beneficial, determining the lowest dose with the highest efficacy and lowest adverse event is important in future research. Third, as we included only one arm, we could include results from the extension phase studies. However, to avoid the risk of overlapping population data from only one paper (i.e., the extension phase article) was included. Fourth, selection bias arises from analyzing only ropeginterferon alfa-2b arms and predominantly hydroxyurea-pretreated patients, limiting generalizability. Further studies should include naive patients with PV. Lastly, the side effects and adverse events were not discussed in our results due to the variability in reporting these outcomes throughout the studies.

Conclusion

Ropeginterferon alfa-2b achieves significant and sustained hematologic and molecular responses over 36 months. This makes it a promising treatment for polycythemia vera. While variability in early responses needs further investigation, the sustained long-term efficacy compared to hydroxyurea supports its use in clinical practice. Future studies should focus on identifying predictors of response and optimizing treatment protocols to maximize patient outcomes.
