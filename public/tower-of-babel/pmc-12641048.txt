Closing the data gap: leveraging pretrained neural networks for robotic surgical assessment on limited clinical data
Authors: Nasseh Hashemi, Matias Mose, Lasse R. Østergaard, Flemming Bjerrum, Erik Søgaard-Andersen, Knud Fabrin, Grazvydas Tuckus, Mikkel L. Friis, Sten Rasmussen, Martin G. Tolsgaard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn robot-assisted surgery (RAS), surgical assessment is critical for ensuring competence and achieving optimal surgical outcomes. Artificial intelligence (AI)-based assessment offers an alternative to expert-based assessment but often requires large datasets, which are challenging to obtain. Transfer learning with pretrained algorithms may offer a potential solution and could reduce the need for clinical data. This study explores the use of transfer learning with preclinical porcine data to reduce the clinical data needed for action recognition (AC) and skills assessment (SA) in RAS.MethodsAbdominal, thoracic and urologic RAS procedures were video recorded. A convolutional neural network (CNN) with a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layer, initially trained using preclinical data, was applied to the clinical dataset through three strategies; (1) direct application on the clinical dataset, (2) only training the LSTM and dense layers, and (3) retraining the entire network. For comparison, a baseline model was trained from scratch on clinical data.ResultsRecordings from 15 procedures were included. The baseline clinical model achieved accuracies of 82.7% (AC) and 40.8% (SA). Direct application of the pretrained network resulted in accuracies of 84.8% (AC) and 51.6% (SA). Fine-tuning the LSTM and dense layers of the pretrained network yielded accuracies of 90.1% (AC) and 60.4 (SA), while retraining all layers achieved 90.5% (AC) and 57.6% (SA). Ablation analysis demonstrated higher accuracies with less data using transfer learning, 87.9% vs. 81.6%.ConclusionsUsing pretrained preclinical AI models increases the accuracy of models trained on limited clinical data and reduces the need for clinical data.Public trial registrywww.clinicaltrials.gov (ID: NCT06612606).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-025-02994-y.

Introduction

The technical skills of the surgeon is an important factor in optimal surgical outcome, and objective assessment of surgical performance remains central to surgical education and quality assurance [1]. In robot-assisted surgery (RAS), skill assessment has traditionally relied on expert observation, which is resource-demanding and subject to inter-observer variability [1, 2]. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled automated, video-based assessments that provide consistent and scalable alternatives [1, 2].

Yet, the promise of AI is limited by the availability of large, high-quality labelled datasets [1–5]. Surgical video datasets are difficult to obtain due to patient privacy concerns, ethical constraints, and the rarity of adverse events [1–5]. As a result, most AI studies in surgery rely on small, pre-clinical datasets [1–5]. These limitations restrict generalizability, reduce clinician trust, and have confined most efforts to proof-of-concept rather than clinical application [1–6].

Transfer learning, in which information from pretrained models is applied to related tasks, has shown promising results in other fields by reducing data requirements and enhancing model generalizability [1, 2, 7–9]. Yet, its use in surgical AI remains limited [10–12]. The scarcity of diverse and clinically annotated surgical data underscores the urgent need for innovative strategies that enable robust AI model development without reliance on prohibitively large patient datasets.

To address this challenge, we previously developed AI models for action recognition and skills assessment in RAS using diverse porcine wet-lab data [13, 14]. Our method has also proven applicable in the preclinical setting of other fields of RAS [15]. Recording videos in the wet-lab setting also allows easier recording of novice participant, adverse events, technical errors, and poor performance without patient risk, thereby enabling capture of data that are rarely available in clinical practice. In this study, we investigate whether transfer learning from porcine wet-lab datasets improves AI-based evaluation of surgical actions and technical skills in clinical environments. Through ablation analysis, we will also determine how pretraining on wet-lab data reduces the amount of clinical data required for model development, thereby lowering barriers to implementing AI-driven skill assessment in surgery.

Methods

Study setting, participants, and patients

This study was approved by The North Denmark Region Committee on Health Research Ethics (ID: 2021 − 000438), the Danish Medicines Agency (ID: 2022022711), the Danish Research Ethics Committees (ID: 2206004), and the Regional Research Review Board under The Danish Data Protection Agency (ID: 2021 − 247). The study protocol was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (ID: NCT06612606).

The study was conducted at Departments of Urology, Gastro-intestinal surgery, and Thoracic surgery at Aalborg University Hospital, Aalborg, Denmark. We collected data from patients undergoing abdominal or thoracic RAS procedures as part of their treatment. Informed consent for participation and publication was obtained from all individual patients and participants included in the study. Participating surgeons were either experienced with RAS or in training to become RAS surgeons, see Table 1. Based on existing literature, we defined experienced surgeons as those with >100 RAS procedures and novices as < 100 RAS procedures [2, 4, 5, 16–18].

All data used in this study, including anonymized labels, video footage, and all associated coding scripts are made open-source in our GitHub repository www.Github.com/NasHas.

Data collection and preprocessing

We recorded video footage of RAS procedures based on our method on collection and preparation of RAS data for machine learning from our previous study [14]. Preprocessing and labelling also followed the same method as described in another previously published study [13].

All experiments were conducted on a laptop equipped with an NVIDIA RTX A5000 Laptop GPU (16 GB VRAM), 11th Gen Intel Core (6 cores, 2.92 GHz) and 64 GB RAM, running Windows 10 Pro, Python 3.9, TensorFlow 2.10.0, CUDA 11.2, and cuDNN 8.1.0.

Using the da Vinci Surgical System (dVSS), the surgical console was connected to a computer through two HDMI to USB Video Capture Cards (VCCS) from Ozvavzk. The videos, set at 15 frames per second (FPS) and 2560 × 720 pixels, were recorded using OBS Studio (Open Broadcaster Software Studio, Wizards of OBS, OBS Studio v. 27.2.4, 64-bit).

Video footage was sampled at 1 FPS and sequences of five consecutive frames were stacked together for the action recognition task, and stacks of ten consecutive frames were used for the skills assessment task. Frames were cropped to only show one optic recording and to remove information bars, so that training only happened on actual surgical actions and movements, and resized to 256 × 256 pixels, using Free Video Crop (RZSoft Technology Co. Ltd, v. 1.08), the command-line software FFmpeg, and Python 3.9.

Using the Behavioral Observation Research Interactive Software (BORIS, v. 7.13.8) all videos were labelled temporally according to whether suturing or dissection sequences occurred for the action recognition task [13]. In short, suturing was labelled from needle positioning to knot tying, with additional labels for suture type (single or running) and action (needle driving or handling). General dissection was labelled and analyzed [13]. All actions were labeled from start to completion. For skills assessment, complete videos were labelled as either ‘experienced’ or ‘novice’ depending on whether the procedure was done by an experienced or a novice RAS surgeon [13]. Sequences with camera cleaning and changing of instruments were removed, and all annotations were done by the principal investigator.

The neural network

We have previously developed and trained a convolutional neural network (CNN) with a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layer based on preclinical wet-lab data from porcine models [13]. The data was collected, prepared, and applied in our trained and tested CNN-LSTM network according to our previously published method [14].

The above-mentioned CNN-LSTM networks were used in this study as pretrained preclinical networks to be deployed on the collected clinical data from the human RAS procedures, and the same configurations were also used for newly trained baseline models.

The pretrained network consists of four convolutional layers (64-128-256-512 filters; all 3 × 3 filters with 2 × 2 strides and ‘Same’ padding) employing rectified linear unit (ReLU) as activation function, with a max pooling layer (3 × 3 size, 2 × 2 strides) after each convolutional layer, lastly followed by a flattening layer preceding the LSTM layer. The final layer is a fully connected layer employing the Softmax activation function, which outputs two categories: suture vs. dissection or experienced vs. novice, depending on the task of action recognition or skills assessment. The Cross Entropy Loss Function (CELF) and Adam Optimizer (learning rate: 0.0001) were used for the network, see Fig. 1.

The LSTM component of the network temporally aggregates the per-frame spatial features using a single LSTM layer with 64 units. The temporal window spans 5–10 s, depending on the specific task. Video clips were extracted using a sliding window approach with non-overlapping strides of 5 or 10 frames. The final hidden state of the LSTM was used as the clip-level representation, which was then passed to a softmax classifier for prediction.

For the skills assessment task the same architecture was used, but an extra dense layer of 64 neurons was added before the final layer, and to prevent overfitting, we added dropout (rate 0.02), batch normalization and L2 regularization (rate 0.01).

Training was performed for up to 50 epochs with early stopping of based on validation loss that was activated if no improvement was seen in five consecutive epochs. We used a batch size of 8 sequences to make training feasible.

Model capacity was 31,059,202 trainable parameters for the action recognition model and 31,065,282 trainable parameters for the skills assessment model.

Model evaluation

After collecting and preprocessing the clinical data, we balanced and split the clinical dataset into a training set, a validation set and a test set. Aiming for a split of 60% training data, 20% validation data and 20% test data. No participants or sequences were used in two different sets, meaning that all data were exclusively used for one purpose to prevent leakage of information from one stage to another. We trained a new baseline model to compare the transfer learning models with a model trained from scratch using our clinical videos.

For transfer learning we experimented with different strategies for both action recognition and skills assessment.

Firstly, we directly applied the pretrained preclinical CNN-LSTM networks to our target domain (the full set of balanced data) without any use of fine-tuning or training [12].Secondly, we tried to gain advantage of the weights and biases of the pretrained preclinical networks and kept all the weights of the convolutional layers and only trained the LSTM and dense layers of the network [8, 11, 12].Thirdly, we fully fine-tuned all layers of the pretrained networks using the clinical data. This was a complete finetuning of all weights and layers [19].

We evaluated the second and third strategy by using K-fold cross validation. We used a split of five folds and for each fold, the performance accuracy was saved, and an overall mean and standard deviation was calculated. Mean ± SD and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed across folds, and differences between strategies were evaluated using paired two-tailed t-tests across folds (α = 0.05).

For the baseline model and the first step of the transfer learning strategy, we evaluated the models using overall accuracies and weighted recall/sensitivity, precision and F1 score, and true- and false-positive rates which were calculated and depicted. These results were reported as point values only, since variability and significance cannot be estimated from a single test set.

To further assess the relation between preclinical and clinical data during transfer learning we conducted an ablation analysis to estimate how much preclinical data may substitute for clinical data during training. We used a data-centric approach where the preclinical and clinical training sets were divided by frame-count and task type into ten incremental sets ranging from ⁓10%-100% of the complete training set. We also used a participant-based approach, increasing each subset with the data of one participant until the full training set was reached.

For extrapolation we tested multiple scaling models (linear regression, logarithmic, exponential, logistic (sigmoid), power law, and shifted power law) on the accuracy curves, modeling accuracy as a function of training set size [20–23]. This allowed us to interpolate performance and estimate the amount of clinical data required to match transfer learning benchmarks.

Total processing times were reported for all steps, and inference times per sequence were provided for all applicable stages, excluding cross-validation results. All descriptive statistics and statistical analysis were conducted using Python 3.9.

The training, validation and test sets with procedures overview and frame-counts for the clinical data, the clinical ablation splits and the preclinical ablation splits can be seen in Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and 3.

Results

The preclinical data

The pretrained preclinical CNN-LSTM network was trained on 130 porcine RAS (gynecological, urological and gastrointestinal procedures (full and part-procedures) [13]. The recordings were made of procedures on anesthetized pigs at the Biomedical Laboratory, Aalborg University Hospital, and consisted of a total of 38 h and 42 min of footage. The procedures were made by 21 surgeons (16 novices and five experienced surgeons), see Table 1. The distribution of procedures and frames per participant can be seen in Supplementary Table 4.

The preclinical CNN-LSTM network reached an accuracy of 91.5% for the action recognition and an accuracy of 81.3% for the skills assessment task [13].

The clinical data

The clinical data consisted of 15 full surgical procedure recordings made on urological, general surgical and thoracic procedures, as seen in Table 1. The total recordings length was 25 h, 5 min, and 49s. The total amount of labelled sequences was 11,330 for action recognition and 9,021 for skills assessment. We included 12 surgeons, eight experienced RAS surgeons and four novice RAS surgeons, one of which was a surgical fellow, who was undergoing training to become a RAS surgeon, see Table 1.

Baseline model trained on clinical data

As a baseline, we trained a model exclusively on the clinical dataset. For the action recognition task, 58.9% of the available data was usable after class balancing. This subset was divided into 49.1% for training, 25.9% for validation, and 25.0% for testing. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 82.7%, with a weighted precision of 86.0%, recall of 82.7%, and an F1-score of 82.3%. The corresponding confusion matrix and per-class plots are presented in Fig. 2A.

For the skills assessment task, 58.3% of the dataset was usable after class balancing. This subset was divided into 61.9% for training, 19.0% for validation, and 19.2% for testing. The model achieved an accuracy of 40.8%, with a weighted precision of 35.4%, recall of 40.8%, and an F1-score of 37.2%. The confusion matrix and per class plot are shown in Fig. 2B.

The total processing time was 6 h 31 min 32s, and the inference time ranged from 16-34ms per sequence for action recognition and from 18ms-56ms per sequence for skills assessment.

Direct application of the pretrained preclinical neural network

When the pretrained preclinical CNN-LSTM network was directly tested on the complete balanced clinical dataset, the action recognition accuracy was 84.8%, with weighted precision of 85.7%, recall 84.8, and F1-score at 85.1%.

For the skills assessment task, direct application of the pretrained network to the full clinical dataset achieved an accuracy of 49.4%, with a weighted precision of 49.7%, recall of 49.4%, and an F1-score of 49.6%. Confusion matrices and per class plots are presented in Fig. 2C and D.

Total processing time was 1 h 26 min 47s and inference times ranged from 16-35ms per sequence for action recognition and from 17ms-54ms per sequence for skills assessment.

Ablation analysis

Both the preclinical and clinical datasets were divided into approximately 10% increments, yielding segments with an average of 1,637 frames for the clinical dataset and 4,903 frames for the preclinical dataset. As seen in Fig. 3A and B, increasing the training set size incrementally improved the accuracy of both the clinical model and the transfer learning model in the action recognition task, achieving final accuracies of 81.6% and 87.9%, respectively. The total processing time for clinical data-based ablation was 5 h 5 min 30s and inference time ranged from 15-61ms. For preclinical data-based ablation processing time totaled 16 h 36 min 43s with inference time ranging 15-68ms. A similar trend was observed in the participant-based ablation analysis, with final accuracies of 82.2% for the clinical model and 87.3% for the transfer learning model. Notably, in both analyses, the accuracy of the transfer learning model declined in the final step of the ablation. The total processing time for the clinical participant-based ablation was 4 h 18 min 1s with inference time ranging from 15-65ms. For preclinical participant-based ablation processing time totaled 17 h 46 min 13s with inference time ranging from 15-60ms.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized our pretrained CNN-LSTM network, based on preclinical intraabdominal procedures on porcine [13]. Directly applying the pretrained model on clinical data, yielded better accuracies for action recognition than the baseline model. Also, transfer learning, either by keeping the weights of the convolutional layers or by retraining all layers of the pretrained preclinical CNN-LSTM, achieved the highest accuracies. For skills assessment, models trained from scratch with limited clinical data and direct application of the pretrained network without fine-tuning produced the lowest accuracies. The best results for skills assessment were achieved by keeping the weights of the convolutional layers and training only the LSTM and dense layers of the pretrained network, however all accuracies remained low regardless of method. Furthermore, our ablation analysis demonstrated that transfer learning can achieve performance comparable to a larger, extrapolated model of clinical data.

The raw clinical data collected in this study contained a higher proportion of dissection sequences and experienced surgeons in the skills assessment task. This was mainly due to the surgical procedures included and the requirement for experienced surgeons to perform complex RAS procedures [13]. The skewed data also prevented the training, validation, and test sets from reaching the desired splits and may explain some of the misclassifications seen in Fig. 2. For skills assessment, all strategies produced low accuracies, and the K-fold cross-validation showed poor generalization with high variability, indicating overfitting in some folds or class imbalance in the dataset, regardless of the use of transfer learning [24, 25]. As seen in Fig. 2, the CNN–LSTM model correctly classified most suturing and dissection segments, however, the skills assessment task showed greater confusion between novice and experienced classes, indicating overlapping performance levels in intermediate surgeons. These results suggest that further refinement in skill-level annotation may be required to reduce overlap between classes and reflect the inherent subjectivity of binary skill labeling rather than systematic model bias. The binary categorization of skills assessment and the definition of experience by case numbers is a limitation and may not accurately reflect individual skill levels [2, 4, 5, 16–18]. This method creates gray zones where skill levels overlap [26]. For example, one participant was categorized as a novice surgeon based on a total of 35 RAS procedures, but 33 of these were performed unsupervised, and in clinical practice the participant operated without supervision. Such cases may confuse the deep learning model and contribute to misclassification. While newer studies experiment with different categorizations of skill assessment, this simplified labeling was intentionally retained to ensure comparability between preclinical and clinical datasets and to isolate the effects of transfer learning, rather than to develop a new skill assessment framework, as extensively discussed in our previous paper Hashemi et al. [13] and previous studies regarding skills assessment [1, 2, 4, 5, 27, 28]. Using the same label standard and model configuration across domains allowed for a controlled evaluation of domain adaptation performance. Additionally, the available sample size and the limited number of clearly annotated intermediate surgeons made reliable multi-class or continuous labeling infeasible. In addition, dataset balancing excluded a considerable amount of data, further reducing the size of an already limited dataset. Skills assessment is a complex task which involves context-dependent judgments, which can be more difficult to capture reliably than clearly defined actions [1, 5, 13, 15]. It may also be that the sequence lengths were too short for skills assessment in the clinical setting, or that the model architecture did not suffice. Factors such as the above mentioned may be the reason why the accuracies remained low despite the use of transfer learning. However, the improvement seen with the use of transfer learning might indicate the potential of this application. Beyond collecting more data, skill classification could be improved by using procedure-specific thresholds for skill evaluation and established scoring systems, which may better capture variations in skill levels [2]. Another limitation is the use of a single annotator for annotations and labelling. Although it ensures consistency and is resource efficient, it increases risk of bias and limits the reproducibility of annotations.

Our clinical dataset was relatively small due to the costs and time required for data acquisition and annotation similar to most existing surgical datasets [2, 4, 5, 27]. In other fields, large public datasets such as ImageNet, with 14 million labeled images, are available, but surgical datasets, both image- and time-series-based, remain scarce [5, 10, 12, 29]. As previously discussed in Hashemi et al. 2025 [13] prior studies in action recognition with machine learning, including deep learning solutions such as 3D CNNs and vision transformers (ViT), have reported accuracies between 68% and 90% [1, 2, 4, 5, 27]. For skill assessment, performance ranges from 76% to 100% when relying solely on video data [1, 2, 4, 5, 27]. Most of these models have been evaluated using the JIGSAWS dataset, a publicly available collection of video and kinematic recordings acquired in a highly standardized dry-lab setting [30, 31]. However, the limited size and controlled nature of this dataset increase the risk of overfitting, reducing generalizability to data from different environments or surgical procedures [30, 31]. Recent efforts have led to datasets such as SAR-RARP50, ESAD, and PSI-AVA, as well as non-public datasets like the SAIS dataset [5, 18, 32–34]. The current state-of-the-art, the SAIS vision transformer, is trained on multicenter data and is capable of identifying surgical steps and actions, as well as evaluating the quality of surgical performance in urologic procedures [18]. However, SAIS dataset, as with the publicly available datasets are often procedure-specific, vary in annotation quality, and typically only include experienced surgeons, thereby limiting variability [2, 4, 5, 27]. By contrast, collecting preclinical data from animal or ex vivo models allow systematic capture of varied scenarios, and enables realistic surgical environments [35, 36]. Transfer learning offers a potential solution to the problem of small and skewed clinical datasets by leveraging these larger, more varied preclinical sources [37, 38]. Base models trained on such data can learn complex feature representations, which improves performance when transferred to smaller clinical datasets [12, 38, 39]. This may explain why keeping the convolutional layers fixed or retraining the entire pretrained CNN-LSTM network yielded the highest accuracies for both tasks, consistent with prior studies using similar approaches [10–12, 29]. The advantage of transfer learning is that it avoids training a network entirely from scratch when data are limited [12].

Our ablation analysis showed that transfer learning substantially reduces the amount of clinical data required to achieve high model accuracy. The observed performance improvements in our ablation results (see Fig. 3) demonstrate that pretraining on preclinical data enhances clinical accuracy, suggesting that the model effectively transfers generalizable surgical features rather than memorizing domain-specific appearance cues. This finding supports prior evidence that cross-domain transfer can accelerate adaptation to real environments [12, 37–39]. This approach allows training to be initiated in preclinical environments while still maintaining or improving performance when applied to clinical data. While most ablation studies focus on altering model architecture, relatively few have examined data ablation as a way to evaluate transfer learning [40–47]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply data ablation as a method for evaluating transfer learning in surgical data. Since the main focus was on transfer learning between preclinical and clinical data, all available clinical procedures were combined to increase sample size and statistical power. As a result, cross-surgery generalization was not evaluated, representing another study limitation. The limited number of cases per surgery type may also underpower cross-surgery ablations. However, differences in workflow and visual features across surgeries may introduce domain shifts affecting model performance [40–47]. Future work should assess this through leave-one-surgery-type-out ablation studies to evaluate model robustness across surgical procedures. As seen in Fig. 3, both ablation analyses revealed a drop in accuracy for the transfer learning model at later stages. This decline may be because of a distributional shift as the proportion of preclinical data in the training set increased during ablation, and the proportion of clinical data decreased [48]. Because the validation and test sets consisted entirely of clinical data, this imbalance may have led to overfitting on preclinical data and underfitting on clinical data [48]. Also, due to the large standard deviations and instability in results, we restricted the ablation study to action recognition, to ensure more consistent outcomes. These results suggest that transfer learning should not be viewed as a complete solution to the problem of small datasets; rather, the best results may be achieved by combining robust pretrained models with gradually expanding high-quality clinical datasets [10]. Extrapolation was performed using only the data-based ablation, as participant-based data showed substantial variability in both frame counts and procedure counts, as presented in Supplementary Table 1. Due to the limited size of the dataset, the extrapolation should be interpreted as indicative, reflecting a trend rather than an absolute measure of performance. Moreover, extrapolation depends not only on data quality but also on network architecture, as different architectures may scale differently with increasing data [20–23]. The CNN–LSTM architecture used in this study provides an efficient and interpretable framework for spatiotemporal modeling while maintaining manageable capacity under limited clinical data [13, 49]. More recent architectures such as 3D CNNs and transformer-based models typically require larger datasets and computational resources to generalize effectively and prevent overfitting [18, 50–52]. In contrast, our trainable parameters represented an appropriate moderate capacity for the available clinical data consisting of approximately 11,330 and 9,021 sequences for action recognition and skills assessment, respectively.

Also, we aggregated per-frame CNN features because it provides parameter-efficient modeling of temporal dependencies and handles variable-length or streaming inputs naturally and LSTMs capture long-range temporal dependencies reducing the frame-to-frame instability often seen in frame-based temporal models [18, 49–53]. In contrast, 3D CNNs couple spatial and temporal convolutions and require more data [50]. Also, the temporal convolutional networks (TCN) are competitive but need careful receptive-field tuning and usually more channels to match LSTM performance on short clips [51, 54]. Given our limited clinical data and the improvement seen when freezing or reusing the CNN while adapting the LSTM and dense layer to clinical data improved performance, indicating that temporal dynamics played an important role in the domain shift, rather than pure spatial appearance.

During transfer learning, we also observed discrepancies between the accuracies of the transfer learning experiments and ablation analysis. Accuracies varied across the baseline, transfer learning, and ablation models, indicating that reproducibility was influenced by random initialization, model complexity, hyperparameter settings, and dataset composition. These findings highlight the importance that single performance values can vary, as model variability and random initialization may mask true performance differences between methods [55]. A way may be to use cross validation to ensure that results are averaged across multiple splits and training runs [24]. Another limitation is the clip-based splits of our K-fold cross validation. Strict surgeon- or procedure-level exclusivity was not enforced during cross validation because the dataset included only a small cohort, and enforcing such grouping would have led to highly unbalanced folds and reduced training stability. Clips were sampled independently to minimize overlap, and no identical samples appeared in more than one fold.

Recent surgical foundation models leverage large-scale video, language, or image data and adaptation strategies with promising generalization across datasets and modalities [56–59]. In contrast, our current work emphasizes preclinical-to-clinical transfer using a compact CNN–LSTM suitable for small datasets and real-time inference [18, 49–53]. Given the small clinical dataset, high-capacity video transformers risk overfitting and increased latency. Nonetheless, our pipeline is compatible with foundation backbones and will be grounds for future work.

Currently, surgical assessments of trainees are most often performed retrospectively using video recordings, as in this study [3, 6, 13]. Video-based AI approaches expand opportunities for building large and diverse datasets, since video can be captured across surgical platforms [3]. However, publicly available datasets remain limited in size or diversity, and are often used only in conceptual studies rather than implemented in practice [1–5]. A major step toward automatic, reliable, and generalizable surgical assessment will be the development of large, diverse, and publicly available datasets [2, 14]. A recent wave of new studies is beginning to address this problem and have published larger and more heterogenous RAS and laparoscopic datasets [60–62]. Our study contributes to this expanding body of open-source surgical video data by publishing all labels, code-scripts and models for future research. Furthermore, our findings indicate that integrating preclinical and clinical data enables the training of models that are more flexible and better adapted to clinical settings. Future research should focus on developing networks that integrate action recognition and skills assessment into feedback systems to support personalized training. More accurate and interpretable model predictions may also increase the likelihood of clinical adoption [2].

Conclusions

Using pretrained preclinical AI models increases the accuracy of models trained on limited clinical data and reduces the need for clinical data significantly. Future research should focus on collecting diverse datasets and making them publicly available to accelerate the development of more robust AI models. Developing large, diverse datasets will be essential for advancing AI reliability and implementation.
