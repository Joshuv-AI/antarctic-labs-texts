Trends in smoking tobacco in Africa, 1990–2019: analysis from the global burden of disease study 2019
Authors: Olayinka Stephen Ilesanmi, Aanuoluwapo Adeyimika Afolabi, Oluwatobi Joshua Afolabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTobacco is one of the leading causes of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and premature deaths. The World Health Organization’s global NCD monitoring framework emphasizes a goal of reducing premature mortality from NCDs. This study described the population of smokers in Africa, alongside the trends in smoking by subregion, country, age, and sex between 1990 and 2019.MethodsWe utilized tobacco smoking data from the Global Burden of Disease, produced by the Institute of Health Metrics and Evaluation and population reports from the World Bank database which was used to normalize the number of smokers and present the percentage of the population (≥ 15 years) that are smokers. We used the comparative risk assessment framework for 55 countries across the five subregions in Africa. The results of the analysis included the actual values as well as the lower and upper boundaries within 95% uncertainty interval (UI). The ranking of the countries (i.e., top 10 or bottom 10) was done using the year 2019 data sets.ResultsIn Africa, the number of smokers increased from about 50 million in 1990 to 90 million in 2019. From 1990 to 2019, the proportion of smokers declined across all African subregions, with Southern Africa showing the highest initial rates. Countries like Seychelles, Djibouti, and Tunisia consistently had the highest smoking prevalence, exceeding 20%. In contrast, nations such as Nigeria, Ethiopia, and Sao Tome and Principe maintained the lowest proportions, mostly under 5%. Despite reductions in the proportion of smokers in some countries, others experienced rising trends, particularly in North and East Africa.ConclusionsThe persistent regional and national disparities in smoking proportions suggest uneven implementation and effectiveness of tobacco control policies across the continent. These trends have significant implications for the growing burden of NCDs in the region, as tobacco use remains a key risk factor for cardiovascular disease, cancer, and respiratory illness. High smoking proportions signal potential future increase in NCD-related morbidity and mortality, emphasizing the urgency for comprehensive tobacco control measures. Strengthening policy enforcement, public education, and cessation support will be critical to mitigating tobacco-related NCDs in Africa.Keywords: Smoking, Health education, Non-communicable diseases, Tobacco control, Africa

Introduction

How does the evolving landscape of tobacco use in Africa shape the region’s growing non-communicable disease (NCD) crisis, and what does this mean for health systems already under pressure? Tobacco is one of the leading preventable causes of premature deaths worldwide, responsible for over 200 million deaths in the past 30 years [1, 2]. Globally, more than one in four adult men and one in 15 women currently use tobacco, with projections suggesting these figures will rise in some low- and middle-income countries [1]. Tobacco use is a key modifiable risk factor for several NCDs, including cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory illnesses, and diabetes [3, 4], diseases that now account for over a third of all deaths in sub-Saharan Africa [4, 5].

Some high-income regions have achieved sustained reductions in smoking due to decades of stringent tobacco control policies [6]; however, Africa has seen slower progress and, in some areas, an emerging tobacco epidemic [7, 8]. Despite near-universal ratification of the World Health Organization (WHO) Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) [9], many African countries face implementation gaps due to limited resources, competing health priorities, and weak regulatory enforcement [10]. At the same time, the continent’s youthful population and expanding tobacco industry presence threaten to increase smoking uptake, placing additional strain on health systems already grappling with infectious diseases and underinvestment in chronic care [11].

To address this, timely, high-quality data on smoking patterns is essential. This study draws on data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 to assess trends in smoking prevalence across African countries between 1990 and 2019. We present changes in the proportion of smokers by country, subregion, age, and sex, highlighting where the burden is rising or declining. This evidence can help inform targeted interventions, policy implementation, and national NCD strategies as Africa enters a critical decade for public health planning.

Methods

Data sources

This study used data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 study, produced by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), and population estimates from the World Bank database [12]. These sources were selected due to their global coverage, methodological rigor, and widespread use in health and development research.

We extracted estimates of tobacco smoking prevalence from the GBD 2019 dataset for 55 African countries across five subregions. The analysis focused on individuals aged 15 years and above, and covered the period from 1990 to 2019, disaggregated by sex and age. GBD estimates are based on a comprehensive modeling framework using data from over 3,600 nationally representative surveys. Country-level population data were retrieved from the World Bank’s open-access database, which compiles demographic estimates primarily from the United Nations Population Division. We used these datasets to estimate the proportion of smokers relative to the population in each country and sub-region, enabling consistent comparisons over time and across demographic groups.

Statistical analysis

For the analysis, the population data for African countries for both males and females were extracted from the World Bank's population database, while the number of smokers for each African country was extracted from the IHME GBD tobacco data. We conducted analysis on the extracted data to depict countries with the highest and lowest prevalence in the continent. The analysis was done by region and sex. Population data was used to normalize the number of smokers and hence present the percentage of the population (≥ 15 years) that are smokers. The results of the analysis included the actual values as well as the lower and upper values at 95% uncertainty interval (UI). We used the year 2019 data sets since it is the latest year in the available GBD datasets.

Ethics

This study utilized publicly available, de-identified secondary data from the GBD project, which was collected under prior ethical approvals. No additional ethical clearance was required for this analysis.

Results

In Africa, the number of smokers increased from about 50 million in 1990 to 90 million in 2019 (Fig. 1).

Trends in the population of smokers in Africa between 1990 and 2019

In 2019, North African [29.89 million (UI = 28.508–31.325)] and East African [20.409 million (UI = 18.758–21.160)] subregions had the highest average number of smokers, while the Central African subregion [8.41 million (UI = 7.704–9.186)] had the lowest average number of smokers (Table 1). The number of smokers in North Africa increased twofold from 15 million in 1990 to 30 million in 2019 and increased from 12 million to 21 million in East Africa between 1990 and 2019. Southern Africa had the least (50%) gain in the number of smokers, rising from 10 million in 1990 to 15 million in 2019 (Fig. 2).

After normalizing the average number of smokers with the population in the region, we computed the proportion of smokers per subregion for each of the periods considered (Table 2). Between 1990 and 2019, the proportion of smokers declined across all African sub-regions, with the most significant reductions observed in Southern and East Africa. Southern Africa dropped from 23.1% to 17.8%, while East Africa declined from 12.2% to 8.8%, though the latter plateaued after 2010. Central and West Africa also saw gradual decreases, with West Africa maintaining the lowest smoking rates throughout. North Africa experienced only slight changes, with a small dip between 2000 and 2010 followed by a modest rise by 2019, indicating more persistent tobacco use in that region in recent times (Table 2).

Figure 3 shows the trend in the number of smokers (in millions) across African subregions from 1990 to 2019, disaggregated by gender. Male smoking populations increased steadily in all regions, with North Africa showing the highest growth, rising from 14 million to over 27 million. East and West Africa also saw notable increases among males. In contrast, female smoking rates remained relatively low and stable across all regions, with Southern and West Africa having the highest numbers of female smokers. The consistent rise in male smoking contrasts with the minimal changes in female smoking, highlighting a persistent gender disparity in tobacco use across the continent.

Trends in the population of male and female smokers in African subregions (1990 to 2019)

Tables 3 and 4 show the 10 African countries with the highest and lowest number of smokers from 1990 to 2019. From 1990 to 2019, Egypt and South Africa consistently had the highest average number of smokers, with Egypt experiencing a sharp increase from 6.5 million to over 15 million, surpassing South Africa by 2010. The Democratic Republic of the Congo and Algeria also showed significant upward trends, particularly after 2000, while Nigeria maintained a relatively steady growth in smokers' population, reaching about 4.7 million by 2019. Between 1990 and 2019, the 10 African countries with the lowest average number of smokers showed gradual increases in smoking prevalence, though their absolute numbers remained low relative to countries with larger populations. Sao Tome and Principe, Seychelles, and Cabo Verde maintained the lowest smoking populations, each with fewer than 0.03 million smokers by 2019. Countries such as Djibouti, Equatorial Guinea, and Gabon experienced more notable increases, with Djibouti rising from 0.05 million in 1990 to approximately 0.19 million in 2019. Equatorial Guinea and Gabon also saw more than threefold increases, suggesting emerging public health concerns.

Tables 5 and 6 presents the 10 countries with the highest and lowest proportion of smokers per population from 1990 to 2019 in Africa. Seychelles consistently recorded the highest smoking prevalence, maintaining a rate above 25% throughout the period. Other countries with high and sustained smoking rates include Tunisia, South Africa, Mauritius, and Libya. Notably, Djibouti and Libya saw significant increases in smoking prevalence over time, with Djibouti rising from 16.3% in 1990 to 26.2% in 2019. Although some countries like Tunisia and South Africa experienced slight declines after 1990, their rates remained among the highest on the continent. From 1990 to 2019, Nigeria consistently had one of the lowest proportions, decreasing from 7.1% in 1990 to 4.1% in 2019, reflecting improved public health interventions or shifting behaviors. Similarly, countries like Benin, Ethiopia, and Cabo Verde saw notable declines over time; however, a few nations such as Guinea-Bissau, Sao Tome and Principe, and Uganda showed slight increases or fluctuating trends, indicating localized changes in tobacco use.

Discussion

This study provides one of the most extensive subregional analyses of tobacco smoking trends in Africa from 1990 to 2019. The findings reveal a near doubling in the number of smokers from 49 million to 89 million during this period, highlighting a concerning trajectory with significant implications for the continent. Tobacco use remains a leading modifiable risk factor for NCDs, contributing to cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory conditions, cancer, and diabetes, all of which are rising in Africa [13, 14]. As the population grows and urbanizes, the burden of tobacco-related morbidity, mortality, and economic cost is expected to increase significantly unless robust interventions are implemented.

Our findings align with previous research showing increasing rate of smoking in low- and middle-income countries, especially in Africa, where market penetration by transnational tobacco companies has intensified [15, 16]. North Africa consistently reported the highest smoking prevalence, driven partly by rapid urbanization, shifts in social norms, and greater tobacco product availability [17]. Notably, Egypt and Libya, among the most urbanized countries in the region, recorded substantial increases, consistent with reports suggesting a strong association between urbanization and tobacco uptake [18].

Although several countries, including South Africa, Mauritius, and Seychelles, are parties to the WHO FCTC, their high smoking prevalence indicates that ratification alone is insufficient [10]. Effective implementation of FCTC provisions such as higher excise taxes, graphic warnings, advertising bans, and cessation support remains uneven and underfunded in many settings [19]. Conversely, non-signatory countries such as Djibouti and Libya also recorded high prevalence, suggesting an urgent need for broader engagement with global tobacco control instruments.

The sharpest increases in smoking prevalence post-2010 in North, Central, and Southern Africa may reflect weakening donor and government commitment to tobacco control efforts, as previously observed in health policy reversals in low-resource settings [20]. Tobacco industry interference, coupled with inadequate domestic financing, continues to undermine progress in implementing smoke-free policies and regulating tobacco advertising [21, 22]. Moreover, our results showed that men remain disproportionately affected, although female smoking, still relatively low, may increase as gender-targeted marketing expands, following historical patterns seen in Southeast Asia and Latin America [23, 24].

Findings from countries like Guinea-Bissau and São Tomé and Príncipe, where poverty, youth unemployment, and urbanization have coincided with rising smoking rates, mirror global evidence linking socioeconomic deprivation and smoking [5]. This underscores the need for integrated, equity-focused smoking control efforts that address both behavioral risk factors and their underlying social determinants. Given that sub-Saharan Africa is projected to account for a growing share of the global NCD burden by 2030 (WHO, 2022), reducing tobacco use must become a foundational element of national NCD strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights a significant and growing burden of smoking in Africa, with the number of smokers nearly doubling from 1990 to 2019. The increase was most pronounced in North Africa and among males, older adults, and urban populations. This trajectory suggests that Africa is in the early to mid-stages of the tobacco epidemic and may face a substantial rise in NCD-related morbidity, mortality, and economic loss in the coming decades.

Despite the adoption of the WHO FCTC by many African countries, gaps in implementation, enforcement, and funding persist. Our findings emphasize the urgent need for evidence-based, context-specific smoking control strategies that are not only policy-driven but also culturally and economically responsive. These strategies should be integrated into broader NCD prevention frameworks and supported by sustained political will, domestic financing, and robust health system capacity. Without timely intervention, the continent risks replicating the devastating health and economic consequences of tobacco witnessed elsewhere in the world. Strengthening smoking control in Africa is no longer optional; it is essential for securing long-term health and development.
