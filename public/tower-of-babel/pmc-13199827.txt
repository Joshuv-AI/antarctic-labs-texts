A Preschool-Aged Child in Tenwek, Kenya, With Limb Pain
Authors: Victor M Karomo, Eric K Langat, Samuel Burleson, Luke Bishop, Ariane Kubena
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A previously healthy 3-year-old girl presented with 4 days of progressive respiratory distress, nonproductive cough, pleuritic chest pain, and fever. On initial examination, she had marked intercostal retractions, nasal flaring, and a left lower extremity splint in place from a reported previous trauma.

Initial laboratory studies demonstrated significant leukocytosis (white blood cell count 27.99 × 109/L) with neutrophilia (absolute neutrophil count 19.11 × 109/L) and a microcytic anemia (hemoglobin concentration 9.42 g/dL). A computed tomography scan of the chest revealed extensive, bilateral, cavitating lung lesions, consistent with severe necrotizing pneumonia.

An initial Doppler ultrasound from an outside facility reportedly revealed an occlusive popliteal deep vein thrombosis, prompting initiation of therapeutic rivaroxaban. We performed a point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), which instead identified a large abscess in the anterior compartment of the leg with tibial discontinuities, subsequently confirmed intraoperatively as a case of tibial osteomyelitis (Fig 1).

Axial point-of-care ultrasound image of the left lower extremity revealing the tibia (T), with cortical discontinuity (white arrows), as well as complex heterogenous periosteal fluid, concerning for abscess (∗).

Diagnosis: Osteomyelitis

Tsung et al1 (2024) demonstrated key sonographic signs of osteomyelitis, which included cortical disruption and periosteal elevation, often with an associated subperiosteal or soft-tissue abscess. In their cases, POCUS reliably identified these abnormalities even when initial radiographs were inconclusive (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4).

Initial anteroposterior and lateral left tibia and fibula x-ray views, without identifiable acute abnormality.

Anteroposterior and lateral left tibia/fibula x-ray views of x-ray indicating focal bony lysis and cortical loss (white arrows), taken 11 days after normal initial x-rays.

Axial point-of-care ultrasound image of the left lower extremity revealing normal bone (B), with entirely collapsible popliteal vein (PV) and corresponding popliteal artery (PA).

In our case, bedside ultrasound definitively changed the management course and patient outcome. This aligns with the conclusion that POCUS can reliably guide critical decision making in the setting of pediatric soft-tissue infections.1,2
