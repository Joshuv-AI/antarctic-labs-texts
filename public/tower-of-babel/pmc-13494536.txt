Unsupervised deep learning enables blur-free resolution enhancement in two-photon microscopy
Authors: Haruhiko Morita, Shuto Hayashi, Takahiro Tsuji, Daisuke Kato, Hiroaki Wake, Teppei Shimamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTwo-photon microscopy enables the non-invasive imaging of deep living tissue. Quantitative three-dimensional analysis is hampered by axial blur and anisotropic resolution in two-photon microscopy. We introduce the two-photon microscopy image enhancement network (TENET), a fully unsupervised framework that simultaneously performs deblurring, up to 12× resolution enhancement, and semantic segmentation on two-photon microscopy volumes in a single pass. TENET embeds a physics-informed blur-generation module with a trainable neural implicit point spread function (PSF), requiring only approximate PSF initialization rather than rigorous experimental measurement, paired “unblurred” images, or isotropy assumptions. On synthetic images, fluorescent beads, and in vivo microglia datasets, TENET surpasses the total-variation-regularized Richardson-Lucy (RLTV) algorithm, CARE, and Neuroclear in image fidelity and segmentation accuracy. Using time-lapse images of microglia surrounding metastatic brain tumors, TENET enables automated 3D morphometry that reveals dynamic microglia-tumor interactions. By converting blur-limited two-photon microscopy data into high-fidelity volumetric reconstructions with ready-to-use masks, TENET streamlines downstream analysis and expands the reach of deep-tissue live imaging.Keywords: multiphoton excitation microscopy, unsupervised machine learning, autoencoder, microglia

Introduction

In biological and neuroscientific research, three-dimensional (3D) visualization provides microstructural information that cannot be obtained from two-dimensional observations. In particular, confocal microscopy1 and light-sheet microscopy2 have enabled the 3D visualization of neurons and glia, elucidating their morphology and dynamics.3,4 For instance, microglia change their morphology depending on the presence or absence of stimuli.5 Two-photon microscopy6,7,8,9(TPM), which allows noninvasive imaging of deep tissue, has proven extremely valuable for in vivo observations. However, deep imaging with TPM shows strong axial (Z-direction) blurring, making it difficult to visualize the 3D morphology in subsequent analyses.10

To address this issue, deconvolution methods, such as the Richardson-Lucy (RL) algorithm and total-variation-regularized RL (RLTV) algorithm,11,12,13 have been proposed. However, these approaches rely on the accurate measurement or setting of the point spread function (PSF), which can be difficult to obtain with high precision in living tissues.14,15 While deep-learning-based image restoration shows promise for high accuracy, supervised learning models require numerous paired datasets (blurred images and corresponding “sharp” ground truths), which are scarce for TPM. In contrast, unsupervised learning methods16,17,18 do not require paired data but often assume structural isotropy (i.e., similar tissue architecture in axial and lateral directions) and train on images taken from multiple angles. Consequently, these methods can exhibit significant inaccuracies for in vivo TPM data acquired from only one angle and characterized by pronounced anisotropy in the axial direction (e.g., laminar organization in the cerebral cortex, where cellular arrangements differ systematically between depth and lateral dimensions).

Additionally, segmentation (delineating cells from the background), which is crucial for many downstream analyses, has conventionally been performed by deblurring or denoising, resulting in complex multi-step pipelines and an increased risk of error propagation. This complication is especially problematic for quantitative 3D analyses of microglia because deeper imaging depths lead to more pronounced blurring.19,20,21,22 Thus, capturing subtle morphological changes in microglia in 3D over extended periods in vivo, such as during disease or stimulation, remains extremely challenging.23

In this study, we propose TENET (TPM image enhancement network), an unsupervised deep learning framework that addresses axial blur, noise, and resolution limitations in TPM images through a unified approach. TENET inverts the image formation process: an encoder predicts the underlying biological structure (segmentation) and brightness distribution, while a differentiable physics-based decoder regenerates the observed blurred image from these predictions. This self-supervised reconstruction loop enables simultaneous deblurring, up to 12× axial super-resolution, and semantic segmentation in a single pass. TENET incorporates a trainable neural implicit PSF within its physics-based decoder, allowing it to adapt to imaging conditions without requiring rigorous experimental PSF measurement or assumptions of structural isotropy. By integrating segmentation directly into the restoration process, TENET streamlines downstream analyses and may facilitate more detailed quantification of microglial morphology in deep-tissue imaging. We validated TENET using synthetic phantoms, fluorescent beads, and in vivo microglial datasets, showing improved performance in image fidelity and segmentation accuracy compared to existing methods, including RLTV, CARE (content-aware image restoration16), and Neuroclear.

Results

Schema of the method: The physics-informed reconstruction loop

TPM has emerged as a powerful tool for deep-tissue imaging of biological structures, yet remains fundamentally limited by optical aberrations, anisotropic resolution, and photon noise. In this study, we developed TENET to address the fundamental challenge of restoring TPM images without paired training data, requiring only raw TPM volumes for training.

TENET formulates image restoration as an inverse problem; given a blurred observation X, we seek to recover the underlying sharp structure P and brightness distribution B that would produce X under known imaging physics. Rather than relying on paired training data, we exploit an encoder-decoder architecture where the decoder implements a differentiable forward model of the image formation process.

The encoder f, a 3D U-Net-based architecture, maps the blurred input volume X to predicted deblurred, super-resolved, and segmented structure and brightness representations (Pˆ,Bˆ). The decoder g implements a differentiable forward model of the image formation process, taking (Pˆ,Bˆ) and generating a reconstructed blurred image Xˆ through PSF convolution, downsampling, and noise addition. Training minimizes the discrepancy between Xˆ and X, providing a self-supervised signal without ground-truth labels. This approach is predicated on accurate modeling of the forward imaging physics, which enables learning of the inverse transformation through reconstruction consistency.

A potential issue with this reconstruction-based approach is that minimizing reconstruction loss alone could drive both models toward trivial identity mappings, where the encoder simply outputs the blurred input and the decoder acts as an identity function. To prevent this solution while maintaining unsupervised learning, we incorporate auxiliary regularization terms: vector quantization (VQ) to encourage discrete structural representations, Markov random field (MRF) regularization to promote spatial smoothness in segmentation, PSF regularization to constrain the learned PSF to physically plausible shapes, and elastic weight consolidation (EWC) to retain knowledge from pre-training (see STAR Methods for details). The combination of these terms guides the model toward meaningful deblurring and segmentation rather than identity mappings.

Training proceeds in two stages. Firstly, we generate synthetic training pairs by creating random geometric structures with varying brightness, applying the blur generation model (decoder g) initialized with an initial PSF, to produce blurred images, and training the encoder to recover the original sharp structures. Secondly, we pass actual TPM images through the encoder and decoder in sequence to reconstruct blurred images and then minimize the difference between reconstructed and original images (reconstruction loss) together with the auxiliary regularization terms. The neural implicit PSF is simultaneously refined during this stage, adapting to the imaging characteristics of the dataset. This adaptation occurs without explicit PSF measurement, though performance does depend on reasonable PSF initialization (Figure 1).

TENET reconstructs both isotropic and anisotropic structures in simulations

For quantitative evaluation, we first applied the model to the simulation images (Figures 2A–2D). Experiments were conducted on images with and without Poisson noise.

The model can infer the shapes of objects (Figure 2A). Figure 2D shows the intersection over union between the inference results and ground-truth images. Comparing the results of previous studies12,16,17 with our model’s predictions shows that our model reconstructs the shape of objects more accurately than the other methods. The RLTV deconvolution method shows reduced accuracy depending on the presence or absence of noise. However, our model has a small difference in accuracy and can reconstruct images independently of noise. It is possible that CARE and Neuroclear were unable to properly reconstruct the images because the blur was larger than the size of the input images used in the experiments. In addition, Neuroclear showed artifacts in regions where nothing was captured in the image, which was thought to be the reason for the low intersection over union scores.

In the next experiment, we verified whether the model could accurately reconstruct structurally anisotropic features. Previous studies on unsupervised learning, such as those on CARE and Neuroclear, assumed biological structural isotropy. However, this assumption does not necessarily hold for live TPM microscopy imaging, in which imaging can only be performed in one direction. Our method does not make this assumption; therefore, it is expected to learn from the images of biologically anisotropic structures. To verify this, we randomly generated linear structures on a plane, applied elastic deformation, and created simulation images by rotating them randomly or by 90° from the center of the stack image along the z axis (Figure 2C). The randomly rotated simulation images had an isotropic structure, whereas the images rotated 90° had an anisotropic structure.

Our model and RLTV could predict the shape of the objects regardless of isotropy or anisotropy; however, CARE and Neuroclear, which rely on structural isotropy, were unable to predict the linear shape in the images rotated 90°, and round artifacts were generated.

TENET precisely estimates bead volume

When conducting a quantitative evaluation of segmentation accuracy using real images, labeled images are typically required; however, obtaining such microscopic images is difficult. In this study, we conducted a quantitative evaluation using images of spherical beads, whose radii and volumes were measured with high accuracy. If the deblurring results are accurate, the volume of the beads in the deblurring results should be close to the correct volume.

Our model could restore the shape of the beads and accurately estimate their volumes (Figures 3A, 3B, and 3C; Table 1). Since the ground-truth volume and shape parameters of the beads were known in this experiment, we determined the thresholds for prior methods by selecting values that optimized these parameters (see Figures S1 and S2 for details). In contrast, our method estimated bead volumes without utilizing any such ground-truth information. Nevertheless, our method achieved estimation accuracy comparable to or better than the prior methods, demonstrating its effectiveness under more stringent conditions. Neuroclear’s estimation result images varied considerably depending on the number of epochs, and we chose images with an epoch number that seemed to be the closest to the bead shape for analysis. We believe that this learning instability adversely affects volume estimation.

TENET reveals the relationship between tumors and microglia using volume-based analysis

Finally, we present the results of the simultaneous measurement of metastatic brain tumors and microglia using this method. For this analysis, we used time-series images of metastatic brain tumors and microglia in mouse brains captured over a period of 10–13 days. Our model removed the blur from microglia and tumors and generated detailed segmented images. Based on these results, instance segmentation of tumors was performed using Roi Manager26 on 59 images from five individuals, and we measured the tumor volume and microglial density in the vicinity of the tumors (within 10 μm from the tumor surface).

The microglial density was significantly higher in the vicinity of the tumors than in other regions (Figure 5B, violin plot). Therefore, further analyses were conducted to investigate the function of microglia in tumors.

Discussion

We developed TENET, a fully unsupervised deep learning framework that performs deblurring, super-resolution, and segmentation of TPM volumes in a single pass. By embedding a physically informed blur-generation model with a neural implicit PSF into the training loop, TENET reduces reliance on the following long-standing requirements: measured PSFs, paired sharp ground-truth images, and the isotropy assumptions that earlier unsupervised methods rely on.

Through the simultaneous optimization of deep learning and generative models, TENET accurately removed the axial blur and noise that remained in conventional methods across simulation, bead, and in vivo microglia images. Although the RLTV, CARE, and TENET methods used the same PSF in the experiment with bead images, the incomplete blur removal by RLTV suggests that the actual blur might have been larger than the set PSF. Even in such situations, TENET can infer and update the PSF from the images to predict the shape. TENET does not use isotropic assumptions, enabling it to generate structures with anisotropy between the depth and lateral directions without artifacts.

We further applied TENET to dual-color TPM data from metastatic brain tumors and resident microglia. Our downstream analysis results provide quantitative evidence for these two previously reported findings. We quantitatively demonstrated that microglia exist in large numbers around tumors. This is consistent with previous reports that showed that microglia gather around tumors.27 We found that when the microglial density was high after day 5, the tumor volume was small. This suggests that a low microglial density is essential for tumor growth, which is consistent with previous research indicating that microglia have antitumor effects.28

Despite several limitations, TENET offers a practical approach to improving spatial resolution in deep TPM without requiring paired training data or rigorous PSF measurement. By delivering non-invasive, high-fidelity 3D reconstructions together with segmentation masks, downstream morphometric analysis can be streamlined and new avenues for studying microglial dynamics and other deep tissue processes in situ can be opened.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. It was difficult to perform cell-by-cell instance segmentation from the microglia semantic segmentation obtained using this method. This might be because of the complex morphology of microglia, which have several protrusions. Even with our method, the imaging resolution used in this study was insufficient for accurate shape reconstruction, potentially resulting in the downstream analysis software failing to segment individual cells. As a future direction, we plan to perform shape analysis of individual microglia by combining microglia analysis technologies, such as 3DMorph.29

Similar to any deconvolution-based approach, TENET must be initialized with a PSF. Additionally, the restoration quality shows some dependence on the starting estimate, although the PSF is automatically adjusted within the model. TENET also has numerous hyperparameters, namely the weights of the VQ, EWC, PSF, and MRF loss terms for optimization, which can be difficult to adjust. We provided the best combination of each parameter along with a robustness analysis in the supplementary materials to serve as a reference for tuning each parameter (see Figures S2, S3, S4, and S5; Tables S1 and S2). In addition, we are considering developing methods to automatically adjust these parameters. Finally, the current implementation assumes a depth-invariant PSF and a fixed voxel size, whereas real TPM blur typically increases with imaging depth, and voxel sizes are often adjusted to suit the specimen. Therefore, extending the framework to handle depth-dependent blurring and multiresolution acquisition is an important next step.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagoya University. Heterozygous B6.129P2(Cg)-Cx3cr1tm1Litt/J (Cx3cr1GFP/+) mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were used for microglia visualization. For microglia imaging, 9-week-old female mice were used. For the metastatic brain tumor time-lapse experiments, 7-week-old female Cx3cr1GFP/+ mice were inoculated with mCherry-expressing tumor cells (CMT167mCh) as previously described30 and imaging was performed 12–18 days after inoculation, when animals were approximately 9–10 weeks of age. All efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and their suffering.

Methods details

Simulation setup

For the simulation images used in pre-training, we prepared 20 volumes of 1200 × 500 × 500 voxels and, by randomly placing 1500 to 2500 objects (randomly selected from spheres, regular octahedrons, cubes, and lines), and deforming them (with elastic deformation). These simulation images represent objects such as cells randomly distributed in the tissue. Each object was assigned a brightness sampled from a log-normal distribution logNμB,σB. In the finetuning experiments using simulations, we used images generated by randomly placing 200–300 objects in an image of the same size. For the blur model, we used a neural implicit function fitted to the PSF created using the parameters of the blur function intended for use in finetuning.

Bead image preparation and image acquisition

We used beads with a diameter of 2 μm. All images were captured using a Nikon A1Plus microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with a 20× objective lens, 20× digital zoom, and a z-step of 0.5 μm.

Mouse brain tissue sample preparation and image acquisition

Microglia were visualized in vivo in Cx3cr1GFP/+ mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA). All images in this experiment were captured using a Nikon A1Plus microscope with a 20× objective lens, 2× digital zoom, a 0.17 mm correction lens, and a z-step of 1 μm. Thirty images of different beads were captured using a two-photon microscope. Each image had a resolution of 0.05 × 0.05 × 0.5 μm, with a field of view of 31 × 512 × 512 pixels. As validation data, cropped images of size [24 × 128 × 128] were created using approximately 30 manually selected beads.

The segmentation results were binarized using the thresholds: 0.5 for our model, CARE, Neuroclear, and 0.1 for RLTV, converting them into {0, 1}. The regions identified as containing objects in the resulting segmentation images were counted, and the voxel volume was multiplied to estimate the bead volume.

Mouse brain tissue sample preparation and image acquisition for downstream analysis

Time-series images of the tumors and microglia simultaneously captured in the mouse brain over a period of 9–15 days were used for the experiment. Each image had a resolution of 1 μm × 1 μm, with a z stack of 12 μm. The field of view was 1972 × 1972 μm, with a depth of 324 μm.

The images were processed using our model for 12x super-resolution, deblurring, and segmentation. Semantic segmentation of the tumors was performed on the processed images using the 3D Roi Manager. Tumor volume was calculated as the sum of the brightness values. Objects with tumor volume less than 1000 μm3 were excluded as noise.

The area within 10 μm from the tumor surface was defined as the tumor perimeter. The density of microglia around the tumors was defined as the ratio of the total volume of microglia in the tumor perimeter to the volume of the tumor perimeter.

A paired two-sample t test was used to assess the difference in density between the tumor perimeter and other regions.

Image preprocessing steps

The images were normalized to a minimum value of 0 and a maximum value of 1.

Network architecture

A deblurring model was created based on the 3D U-Net structure.32 The model consisted of an initial layer, U-Net layers, and a final layer. The initial layer consisted of a brightness adjustment hill function, upsampling by trilinear interpolation, and a 7 × 7 × 7 convolutional layer. The U-Net layers comprised contraction and expanding paths, each consisting of five stacked blocks. Each layer of the contraction path consisted of residual blocks and downsampling (MaxPooling and 1 × 1 × 1 convolution, an activation function). Each layer of the expanding path consisted of residual blocks and upsampling (trilinear interpolation and 1 × 1 × 1 convolution, activation function). For each residual block, with reference to the fully pre-activated ResNet block,33 the following structure was adopted: batch normalization, activation function, convolutional layer, batch normalization, activation function, dropout, convolutional layer.

The final layer was divided into a shape estimation layer and a brightness estimation layer. Both layers were inputs from the final output of the U-Net layer. The common structure comprises residual blocks, a final convolutional layer (3 × 3 × 3) that outputs one channel, and an activation function (sigmoid). The number of blocks in the residual block was set to two. During reconstruction learning with actual images, the quantization layer follows the shape estimation layer, and a process is performed to set pixels with brightness values above a threshold of 1 and others to 0. To make this process differentiable, the stop-gradient estimator proposed in the VQVAE was used.34

The blur generation model consists of 1) a model of the process of fluorescence emission from objects, 2) a model of the process of fluorescence blurring, and 3) a process of noise addition during imaging.

Data preparation

In all training sessions, the model was provided with randomly cropped regions of (240, 112, and 112).

The microglial data used for learning and inference contained regions without objects. With such sparse data, the optimal model output may always be zero regardless of the input. To prevent this, the model was supplied with data using the following procedure to provide as many objects as possible to the small regions:

A random small region was obtained, its mean brightness value was taken, and when inputting data to the model, r was sampled from a uniform distribution. The data were input to the model only if the mean value exceeded r; otherwise, the small region and r were resampled.

For data augmentation, 90° rotation, left-right flipping, and masking (randomly selecting a (1, 10, 10) region of the input and setting it to zero) were used.

Deblurring network architecture details

The number of channels in the U-Net layers was set to [16, 32, 64, 128, and 256]. Downsampling and upsampling were performed using a factor of 2. The number of blocks in each layer was set to two. ReLU was used as the activation function. The dropout rate was set to 0.5.

Blur generation model architecture

In all the experiments, the Gibson–Lanni model was used as the label data for the PSF. The number of channels in the middle layer of the neural implicit function PSF was set to 40, and a sigmoid function was used as the activation function. Fast Fourier transformation convolution was used to convolute the PSF and images.

Hyperparameters for training

The batch size was set to 1, and the number of small regions loaded per epoch was set to 200. In all experiments, wS = 1andwL = 1 were set during pretraining. For fine-tuning on the simulation images in Figure 2, wR = 1,wQ = 1,wM = 1,w00 = −0.5,w01 = 0.5,w10 = 1,w11 = −1,wLf = 1,wE = 0.1,andwP = 10 were set. For the fine-tuning shown in Figure 3, w00 = 0,w01 = 0,w10 = 0,w11 = 0,wM = 2 were set.

The misalignment parametersαb,αA,andαω were linearly increased from 0 to 0.2, 2, and 2, respectively. αλ was linearly decreased from 10 to 2. This process was performed over 60 steps.

The noise parameters α = 0.001,σ = 0.001 were set for initial values for all experiments.

The Gibson-Lanni model parameters were set to NA = 0.3, λwavelength = 0.5 nm, M = 25, RIspecimen = 1.4, RIcoverslip = 1.5, RIimmersion = 1.33, ti = 150 μm, tg = 170 μm, pZ = 0 in all experiments. For a detailed description of each parameter, the reader is referred to the original paper.24

In all the experiments, the deblurring model before pre-training and the neural implicit PSF were initialized using Gaussian initialization.

Optimization

The deblurring model was trained using the Adam optimizer35 with a learning rate of 10−3 during pre-training and 10−3 during fine-tuning. The warm-up increased linearly from 0 to the learning rate over 10 epochs. A reduction on the plateau was used with a decay of 0.1 and patience of 20 epochs. In all the training sessions, the total number of epochs was 200, and the weights with the smallest validation loss were used.

The neural implicit PSF was trained by full-batch learning using the RProp optimizer36 with a learning rate of 10−3 and optimized until the error reached 10−5.

Machine used for experiments

The experiments were performed on a computer equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 graphics card and an AMD Ryzen 9 5900X 12-Core Processor CPU.

Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis

To provide users with guidance when setting hyperparameters for our method, we conducted the experiment from Figure 3 with modified hyperparameter values. The results are shown in Figure S3 and Table S2.

We doubled the weights of EWC loss, PSF loss, and VQ loss, and added MRF loss.

Through the ablation study and sensitivity analysis, we found that while each loss term affects shape reconstruction, their impact on volume estimation results is limited, suggesting that TENET is a robust method for volume estimation.

Dependence on PSF initialization

The results of conducting the experiment from Figure 3 using a Gaussian distribution as the PSF are shown in Figures S3 and S4. The impact on the model’s volume estimates was limited. Comparing the PSF before and after training, the trained PSF approached that of the original experiment, suggesting that the model can adapt to the data and improve the PSF regardless of the initial values.

Guidelines for adjusting each hyperparameter

EWC loss is applied to the encoder and serves to preserve knowledge from pretraining. Since it plays the role of maintaining the image reconstruction capabilities of the pretrained encoder while adapting to microscopy images, increasing the weight of EWC loss strongly preserves pretrained knowledge but limits adaptation to new data. Conversely, decreasing the weight leads to loss of pretrained knowledge (catastrophic forgetting) and increases the risk of overfitting, especially when the amount of data is limited. Users are recommended to set the weight lower when the domain of the pretraining data and target images differs significantly, and higher when they are similar.

PSF loss serves to prevent the learned PSF shape from deviating from the initial PSF. Since the PSF derived from the microscope’s optical system has physical constraints, it plays the role of preventing divergence to unrealistic shapes during training. Increasing the weight of PSF loss strengthens the constraint to the initial PSF and limits the PSF’s adaptation to the data. Decreasing the weight promotes adaptation to the data but carries the risk of converging to a physically unreasonable PSF. Users are recommended to adjust this weight according to the quality of the initial PSF (e.g., agreement with design values or pre-measured values), and to set the weight higher when a high-quality PSF is known.

VQ loss serves to binarize the reconstruction results. Decreasing the weight tends to leave the reconstruction blurry without improvement, but setting it too high can destabilize gradients due to binarization, potentially making training unstable. Users are recommended to either not adjust the weight of this term from the default value, or to decrease it if training becomes unstable.

MRF loss serves as a regularization term that considers spatial smoothness. Increasing the weight makes the reconstruction results smoother, but may over-smooth fine structures. Decreasing the weight helps preserve local details but increases susceptibility to noise. In the bead experiments, the limited impact on performance is likely because the ground truth structures are smooth. Users are recommended to adjust this weight according to the noise level of the images and the scale of the target structures (i.e., set the MRF loss weight higher when image noise is large, and lower when the target structures are complex).

The ablation study and sensitivity analysis have shown that all of these hyperparameters have limited impact on volume estimation (Tables S1 and S2), and primarily affect shape estimation. Therefore, users can adjust these parameters according to the characteristics of their target images and analysis objectives, and the default values used in this paper serve as a starting point with sufficient robustness across a wide range of conditions.
