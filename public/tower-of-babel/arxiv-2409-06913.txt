Wigner rotations for cascade reactions
Kai Habermann, Mikhail Mikhasenko

Abstract
Parametrization of cascading hadronic reactions is a central tool in hadron spectroscopy for modeling matrix elements and extracting parameters of hadronic states. Implementing the helicity formalism consistently presents challenges, particularly for particles with spin, due to the need to match spin states of final-state particles, an operation known as the Wigner rotation. This paper discusses these challenges in detail and offers solutions, including a practical method for implementation. Equipped with a general algorithm for computing Wigner rotations, we extend the studies to alternative amplitude formulations, the minus-phi and canonical conventions.



I
Introduction

The analysis of multibody decays is an essential method for studying hadronic resonances,
leading to a significant increase in the number of observed hadronic states,
some of which challenge the constituent quark model
Karliner et al. 2018
;
Klempt and Richard 2010
;
Richard 2016
;
Guo et al. 2018
;
Esposito et al. 2017
.
Among these are pentaquarks observed in the

\Lambda_{b}^{0}\rightarrow J/\psi pK^{-}
decays
Aaij et al. 2015
;
Aaij et al. 2019

and hidden-charm strange states in
B^{+}\rightarrow J/\psi\phi K^{+}
decays
Aaij et al. 2017a
;
Aaij et al. 2017b
;
Aaij et al. 2021
,
as well as charged tetraquarks in
e^{+}e^{-}\to\pi^{-}\pi^{+}J/\psi
processes
Ablikim et al. 2020

and
B^{0}\rightarrow\psi K^{+}\pi^{-}
decays
Chilikin et al. 2013
.
With more data of increasingly better quality,
many observations of hadronic states are driven by amplitude analysis,
a technique that utilizes angular correlations and Lorentz group properties to constrain the spin and parity of particles.
Insights into the dynamics within individual subsystems are obtained by modeling the matrix element of the reaction.
However, examining the mass spectrum of the subsystem is not sufficient since the cross-channel contributions – referring to competing coherent processes in different combinations of particles where an intermediate resonance occurs – create a background. Amplitude analysis is the only known systematic method of distinguishing these contributions.

Modeling dynamics of a multibody final state is a complex process.
One common approach is the
isobar model

Herndon et al. 1975
;
Hansen et al. 1974
, where the multibody decay is represented as a series of two-body decays assuming intermediate resonances.
The notion of the isobar model is widely used in the literature as a vague term, sometimes implying that Breit-Wigner parametrization is used to model resonances. More advanced approaches such as the K-matrix
Aitchison 1972
;
Workman et al. 2022
are also commonly used
Link et al. 2004
;
Aaij et al. 2024a
, with the Khuri-Treiman framework
Khuri and Treiman 1960
;
Aitchison and Brehm 1979
;
Pasquier and Pasquier 1968
being one of the most sophisticated ways of describing resonance lineshapes in three-body decays.
It accounts for final-state interactions by replacing the Breit-Wigner parametrization with functions derived from solving integral equations specific to each process. Phenomenological modeling using effective-field-theory methods is also used to derive a matrix element for sequential decays,
e.g.
see
Altmannshofer et al. 2009
;
Aaij et al. 2024b
.
All such models, simple or advanced, can be mapped into the angular-function basis described here, with differences arising only from the choice of resonance lineshapes.

In this paper, we refer to the modeling method as the
cascade-decay
parametrization,
meaning a general framework that covers all categories above
by focusing on angular variables while leaving the energy dependence out of scope.
One finds an extended discussion on the impact of different formalisms for energy dependence in Refs.
Filippini et al. 1995
;
Chung and Friedrich 2008
;
Mikhasenko et al. 2018
;
Pilloni et al. 2018
;
Chen and Ping 2017
.
We assume that the amplitude is written as a sum of terms corresponding to distinct topologies, with each part seen as a truncated series of partial waves.
In this paper we focus on decays for simplicity,
although the methods and arguments presented here are equally applicable to scattering amplitudes
modeled using the partial-wave expansion.

Lorentz group properties play a central role in describing cascade reactions.
The particle spin states necessitate basis changes, which are performed using Wigner D-functions, a representation of the rotation group.
It is widely understood that in cascade decays, a set of Wigner matrices appears in the description of the sequential decays.
Less straightforwardly, another set of Wigner matrices is required to ensure consistent treatment of spin quantization axes across various coherent processes contributing to the amplitude.
These alignment rotations are known as the
Wigner rotations
.
This paper aims for an extended discussion on the computation and application of the Wigner rotations within the parametrization of cascade reactions.
Although several papers
Chen and Ping 2017
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Marangotto 2020
have discussed how decay amplitudes are constructed using different formalisms, the computation of Wigner rotations, which are essential in any formalism, has often been set aside.
Analytic expressions for Wigner rotations in simple aligned three-body kinematics are derived in Ref.
Mikhasenko et al. 2020
. Reference
Wang et al. 2021
uses
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
group elements and clever particle ordering to circumvent complications in Wigner rotations.
To our knowledge, no existing reference details the computation of Wigner rotations in the context of general cascade amplitudes.
These alignment factors are integral to every amplitude analysis, including those that discover and study exotic hadronic configurations in experiments worldwide.

A comment is due that tensor formulations
Anisovich and Sarantsev 2006
;
Zemach 1965
;
Chung 1998
do not use spin states explicitly, and therefore, are exempt from the need of Wigner rotations.
A consistency of quantization axes is built in automatically by using the same tensor structure for decay chains.

The structure of this paper is as follows: we begin with a discussion of kinematics in Sec.
II
.
This is followed by Sec.
III
with an examination of the Lorentz group, its representations, and our approach to implement the group operations.
Next, we review the helicity parametrization of cascade reactions and introduce the Wigner rotations in Sec.
IV
.
Section
V
is dedicated to
a discussion of subtleties and possible caveats involved in amplitude construction.
Finally, in Sec.
VI
we discuss alternative conventions and investigate the impact of the choice of convention on the Wigner rotations. We then conclude with a summary of our findings.

II
Kinematics

The phase space for
n
particles can be parametrized using variables of
n-1
two-particle systems in a recursive phase-space representation
Byckling and Kajantie 1971
. Each two-particle node in the splitting graph is described by three variables (
m,\theta,\phi
). With the mass of the first node fixed to the total mass, the phase-space domain is defined by
3n-4
kinematic variables.

The choice of kinematic variables is intrinsically linked to the form of the decay matrix element. Specific decay models often necessitate particular sets of variables. It is common practice to use distinct sets of variables when calculating different coherent parts of the amplitude. However, employing different sets of variables within the same amplitude, while being necessary, leads to severe complications, which will be addressed in detail below.

To facilitate notation, we use
\kappa
to label nodes in the decay graph. Internal nodes correspond to a decay
{{\kappa}}\to{\kappa{[1]}},{\kappa{[2]}}
, where
{\kappa}
represents the decaying system, and
\kappa{[1]}
and
\kappa{[2]}
are the ordered node labels for the decay products. Also, each node,
{\kappa}
,
\kappa{[1]}
, and
\kappa{[2]}
, is associated with either intermediate particle subsystems or a final-state particle. An example using bracket notation (see also Appendix
C
) further illustrates the node labeling:

In the rest frame of
{{\kappa}}
, the solid angle
\varOmega_{\kappa}
is represented by the pair
(\theta_{\kappa},\phi_{\kappa})
, which are the spherical angles of the first particle in the daughter pair. These angles can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{\kappa}=

\displaystyle\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{p_{z}}{|\vec{p}\,|}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad p=p_{\kappa{[1]}}\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\phi_{\kappa}=

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}\left(p_{y},p_{x}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad p=p_{\kappa{[1]}}\,.

(2)

It is to note, that both particle
{\kappa{[1]}}
and particle
{\kappa{[2]}}
may themselves decay.
In the
helicity
convention, the rest frame of
{\kappa{[1]}}
or
{\kappa{[2]}}
is approached by
a set of active transformations,
\Lambda_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[1]}}}^{-1}
or
\Lambda_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[2]}}}^{-1}
, respectively.

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(\text{hel.})}_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[1]}}}

\displaystyle=R(\phi_{\kappa},\theta_{\kappa},0)B_{z}(\gamma_{\kappa}^{\kappa{[1]}})\,,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(\text{hel.})}_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[2]}}}

\displaystyle=R(\phi_{\kappa},\theta_{\kappa},0)R_{y}(\pi)B_{z}(\gamma_{\kappa}^{\kappa{[2]}})\,,

(3)

where
R(\phi_{\kappa},\theta_{\kappa},0)=R_{z}(\phi_{\kappa})R_{y}(\theta_{\kappa})
, and
B_{z}
,

R_{y}
, and
R_{z}
are operators from the Lorentz group acting on the particle states,
\gamma_{\kappa}^{\kappa{[1]}}
and
\gamma_{\kappa}^{\kappa{[2]}}
denote the Lorentz factors of particles
\kappa{[1]}
and
\kappa{[2]}
, respectively, in the
{{\kappa}}
frame.

For a particle with a four-momentum
p
, and spin
j
, a helicity
\lambda
is defined as a projection of its spin onto the direction of flight.
A helicity state can be constructed by applying active boost and rotation transformations to the rest-frame state quantized along the
z
axis.

\displaystyle\ket{p;j,\lambda}=RB_{z}\ket{0;j,\lambda}\,.

(4)

The helicity state is not invariant under boosts, so it is important to know in which frame it is defined.
It also receives a phase factor under rotations, despite the fact that the helicity value stays the same.

The helicity states of the particles
{\kappa{[1]}}
and
{\kappa{[2]}}
in the rest frame of the mother particle
{{\kappa}}
,
are given by
\Lambda^{(\text{hel.})}_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[1]}}}\ket{0;j_{{\kappa{[1]}}},\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}}}
, and
\Lambda^{(\text{hel.})}_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[2]}}}\ket{0;j_{{\kappa{[2]}}},\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}
, respectively.
The transformation for the second particle
{\kappa{[2]}}
in Eq. (
3
) is different from the naive expression,
R(\pi+\phi_{\kappa},\pi-\theta_{\kappa},0)
.
A critical need to use the same rotation
R(\phi_{\kappa},\theta_{\kappa},0)
for both daughter particles is further discussed in Sec.
IV
, when introducing helicity couplings.

III
Parameters of Lorentz transformations

An arbitrary Lorentz transformation can be represented in terms of a combination of rotations and a single boost:

\displaystyle\Lambda=R(\phi,\theta,0)B_{z}(\gamma)R(\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}},\theta_{\mathrm{r.f.}},\psi_{\mathrm{r.f.}})\,,

(5)

where the subscript
\mathrm{r.f.}
, for rest frame, indicates that the rotation is applied before the boost, and

R(\phi,\theta,\psi)=R_{z}(\phi)R_{y}(\theta)R_{z}(\psi)
.
In this parametrization, the Lorentz transformation is fully described by the six real numbers
\phi
,
\theta
,
\gamma
,
\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
,
\theta_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
, and
\psi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
.
The ranges of the angles are as follows:
\phi
and
\psi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
in
[-\pi,\pi]
,
\theta
and
\theta_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
in
[0,\pi]
, and
\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
in
[-\pi,3\pi]
.
The necessity of the extended range of the
\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
angle becomes clear when considering odd-dimensional representations of the Lorentz group corresponding to
half-integer spin.
For these matrices, a
2\pi
shift in the
\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
angle results in
a sign flip for the matrix
\Lambda
.

The process of decoding an arbitrary sequence of boosts and rotations into the form of Eq. (
5
) requires determining
the set of six parameters
\phi
,
\theta
,
\gamma
,
\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
,
\theta_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
, and
\psi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
.
We use a trick to do that: a combination of two representations of the Lorentz group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, acting on Dirac spinors, and
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(3,1)
, acting on four-momenta, is utilized.
It is easy to see from Eq. (
5
) that the parameters
\theta
,
\phi
, and
\gamma
can be obtained from the effect of the transformation on a four-vector with zero spatial components.
With these values determined, we compute the rest-frame rotation part of the matrix as

R_{\mathrm{r.f.}}=B_{z}^{-1}(\gamma)R_{y}^{-1}(\theta)R_{z}^{-1}(\phi)\,\Lambda

(6)

The angles
\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
,
\theta_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
, and
\psi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
are obtained from the
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
representation in the case of a pure rotation.
The procedure is detailed in Appendix
B
.

IV
Parametrization of Cascade Reactions

We formally define the matrix element describing an
n
-body decay
from the initial state labeled
0
to the final state with particles labeled
1,\ldots,n
by the expression,

\displaystyle\bra{\text{final}}T\ket{\text{initial}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}(\tau_{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}})

\displaystyle\qquad\,\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p_{0}-\sum_{i}^{n}p_{i}),

(7)

where the initial state is a canonical state in the center-of-momentum frame,
\ket{\text{initial}}=\ket{0;j_{0},\lambda_{0}}
, and
the final state is a direct product of helicity states for all final-state particles,
\ket{\text{final}}=\prod_{i}\ket{p_{i},\lambda_{i}}
.
The transition amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}(\tau_{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}})
is a complex function, which is continuous in kinematic variables, collectively named
\tau_{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}}
, and is dependent on discrete indices of particle-spin projections
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
. We use a lower Fraktur index
\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}
for the reference set of kinematic variable.
The cascade parametrization models the dynamics of the decay as a sum of coherent processes, each representing sequential decays with intermediate resonances:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}(\tau_{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{c}}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}}\mathcal{A}^{\mathfrak{c}}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}}(\tau_{\mathfrak{c}})

\displaystyle\qquad\times W_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n};\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}^{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}(\tau_{\mathfrak{c}}|\tau_{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}})\,,

(8)

where the Fraktur index
\mathfrak{c}
numbers the decay chains,
and
\tau_{\mathfrak{c}}
is a set of kinematic variables specific to the chain
\mathfrak{c}
.
The primed helicity indices refer to spin projections with the quantization axes specific to the chain
\mathfrak{c}
.
The term
W_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n};\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}^{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}(\tau_{\mathfrak{c}}|\tau_{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}})
implements Wigner rotations to ensure a consistent treatment of the quantization axes across different chains. This term is the central object of discussion for this paper and will be further elaborated on in the following paragraphs.

.

The chain amplitude
\mathcal{A}^{\mathfrak{c}}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}}(\tau_{\mathfrak{c}})
represents the amplitude
of a single decay chain connecting the initial state with the final state.
A
cascade topology
or
decay graph
is a tree of two-body transitions.
Within a decay graph
nodes
can be labeled in various ways; we index them with
\kappa
.
Each node corresponds to the center-of-momentum frame of the decay pair as approached from the parent node.
A decay topology, accompanied by quantum numbers for all internal nodes, is referred to as a
decay chain
.
The chain amplitude is obtained by multiplying the two-body amplitudes associated with each node of the decay chain.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\mathfrak{c}}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}}(\tau_{\mathfrak{c}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{\text{internal}}^{\prime}\dots}\prod_{{\kappa}}D^{j_{{{\kappa}}}*}_{\lambda_{{{\kappa}}}^{\prime},\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}}^{\prime}-\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}^{\prime}}(\varOmega_{\kappa}^{\mathfrak{c}})X_{\kappa}(\mu_{{{\kappa}}})

\displaystyle\quad\times H_{\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}}^{\prime},\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}^{\prime}}^{{\kappa}}(\mu_{{{\kappa}}},\mu_{{\kappa{[1]}}},\mu_{{\kappa{[2]}}})\,,

(9)

where,
j_{{{\kappa}}}
is the spin of
{{\kappa}}
,
D
is a Wigner D-function.
In the
helicity
conventions,
D^{j}_{\rho,\eta}(\varOmega)=D^{j}_{\rho,\eta}(\phi,\theta,0)
.
The product index
\kappa
runs over all
nodes
of the decay graph, where each node
\kappa
describes a decay
{{\kappa}}\to{\kappa{[1]}},{\kappa{[2]}}
.
States in the intermediate propagators (all internal nodes except the root node) have their helicity values appearing twice: as
\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}}^{\prime}
or
\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}^{\prime}
for the production node and as
\lambda_{{{\kappa}}}^{\prime}
if the state decays further. These values must be the same, and their equality is ensured by transformations between frames. Additionally, these values are summed over as indicated by
\lambda_{\text{internal}}^{\prime}
, as these internal helicities are not observable quantities.
The
X_{\kappa}(\mu_{{\kappa}})
parametrizes the lineshape function and depends on the mass of the system
{\kappa}
,
\mu_{{\kappa}}=\sqrt{p_{{\kappa}}^{2}}
,
and
H_{\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}}^{\prime},\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}^{\prime}}^{{\kappa}}
is the helicity vertex function, that might depend on all masses related to the node
\kappa
.
In practice, it’s more convenient to deal with the helicity couplings in the
particle-two
convention, denoted by
h_{\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}},\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}^{{\kappa}}
. The couplings
H
and
h
differ by a phase,

\displaystyle H_{\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}},\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}^{{\kappa}}=(-1)^{j_{{\kappa{[2]}}}-\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}h_{\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}},\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}^{{\kappa}}\,,

(10)

that arises from the definition of the particle-two state
Martin and Spearman 1970
;
Richman 1984
.
The couplings
h
have convenient transformation properties under parity,

h_{-\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}},-\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}^{{\kappa}}=(-1)^{j_{{{\kappa}}}-j_{{\kappa{[1]}}}-j_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}P_{{{\kappa}}}P_{{\kappa{[1]}}}P_{{\kappa{[2]}}}\,h_{\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}},\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}^{{\kappa}}\,.

(11)

where
P_{{\kappa{[1]}}}
,
P_{{\kappa{[2]}}}
, and
P_{{{\kappa}}}
are parities of the daughter particles and the mother particle for the node
\kappa
.
It is mapped to spin-orbit vertex
h_{l_{\kappa}s_{\kappa}}^{{\kappa}}
couplings using Clebsch-Gordan coefficients,

\displaystyle h_{\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}},\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}^{{\kappa}}=\sum_{l_{\kappa}s_{\kappa}}h_{l_{\kappa}s_{\kappa}}^{{\kappa}}\,C_{\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}},-\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}^{j_{{\kappa{[1]}}},j_{{\kappa{[2]}}}|s_{\kappa}}\,C_{0,\Delta\lambda_{{\kappa}}}^{l_{\kappa},s_{\kappa}|j_{{{\kappa}}}}\,,

(12)

with
\Delta\lambda_{{\kappa}}=\lambda_{{\kappa{[1]}}}-\lambda_{{\kappa{[2]}}}
and the indices
l_{\kappa}
and
s_{\kappa}
going over all possible combinations of ordinal angular momentum and spin of the
({\kappa{[1]}},{\kappa{[2]}})
pair.
Indices of the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients map into the standard notations
Workman et al. 2022
as follows,

C_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}^{j_{1},j_{2}|j_{3}}=\left\langle j_{1},\lambda_{1};j_{2},\lambda_{2}|j_{3},\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\right\rangle\,.

Note the minus sign for particle-two helicity in Eq.(
12
).
It appears because particle one is used as the reference, as shown in Fig.
1
.

Wigner rotations arise in Eq. (
8
) due to the mismatch of quantization axes between different decay chains.
One finds the primary cause of the Wigner rotations when examining what helicities are listed as arguments of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathfrak{c}}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}}
in Eq. (
9
). Building the decay amplitude implies moving with Lorentz transformations through the decay graph from the initial node toward decay products.
In this procedure, the helicity value of every particle appears once, and from a single node, which corresponds to a particular rest frame of the reaction subsystem. This frame defines the particle helicity based on transformations from Eq. (
3
). Hence, the particle helicity values
\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}
in argument of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathfrak{c}}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}}
are defined in various frames within the same decay chain. Moreover, which frames define the helicities varies between different decay chains.

At this point, it comes with no surprise that aligning the quantization axes between different chains is crucial.
It is practical to select one of the decay chains as a
reference chain
and apply the Wigner rotations to all other chains to align the quantization axes with the reference.
Let us compare a helicity state in a particular moving frame, defined by Eq. (
4
)
with a reference transformation where the path from the rest frame to the moving frame
is given by a general transformation
\Lambda
.
Using Eq. (
5
), one finds,

\displaystyle\Lambda\ket{0;j,\lambda}

\displaystyle=RB_{z}R_{\mathrm{r.f.}}\ket{0;j,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}}D^{j}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}(R_{\mathrm{r.f.}})\ket{p;j,\lambda^{\prime}}\,.

(13)

The state vectors in Eq. (
4
) and Eq. (
13
) share the same momentum value. The rotation
R_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
in the particle’s rest frame affects only the helicity quantum number. This rest-frame rotation accounts for the difference between two Lorentz transformations that connect the moving frame to the rest frame of a particle.
In the context of decay amplitude construction, the general transformation
\Lambda
is associated with the reference topology. We denote the rest-frame mismatch rotation between the reference chain
\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}
and the chain
\mathfrak{c}

for the final-state particle
i
as
R_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i}
.
The coefficients
D_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}^{j}
for transforming the final state appear conjugated in Eq. (
8
) according to Eq. (
7
).
Namely,

W_{\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n};\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}^{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}D_{\lambda_{i}^{\prime},\lambda_{i}}^{j_{i}*}(R_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i})\,.

(14)

where

D^{j}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}(R_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i})
is a short notation for
D^{j}_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}(\phi_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i},\theta_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i},\psi_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i})
referring to the angles of the rotation
R_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i}
;

R_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i}
is the
Wigner rotation
that relates the chain
\mathfrak{c}
to a reference chain, and the index
i
numbers the final-state particles.

To calculate the relative rotation between the frames reached by traversing two different topologies,
we compare a series of transformations between different chains.

\displaystyle R_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i}=

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{a}^{0\rightarrow i}\Lambda_{a\leftarrow a_{\text{next}}}^{\mathfrak{c}}\right)^{-1}\times\left(\prod_{b}^{0\rightarrow i}\Lambda_{b\leftarrow b_{\text{next}}}^{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}}\right)\,,

(15)

where the transformations
\Lambda_{a\leftarrow a_{\text{next}}}^{\mathfrak{c}}
are defined in Eq. (
3
).
The first matrix product is built from left to right while traversing all internal nodes from the initial state
0
to the final state
i
,
following the path set by chain
\mathfrak{c}
. The second product follows the same procedure for the reference topology.
Figure
2
demonstrates the construction of these matrix products.
The resulting transformation
R_{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}^{i}
rotates from the rest frame of particle
i
reached by traversing the reference topology to the rest frame obtained by following topology
\mathfrak{c}
.
A sketch of this construction for a four-body decay is shown in Fig.
2
, and a detailed expression of the example amplitude is provided in Appendix
C
.

V
Discussion on Application

We begin by discussing the general principle of rotation factorization in
n
-body final states.
As pointed in Ref.
Mikhasenko et al. 2020
, for any system with a fixed total spin
j_{0}
, the overall rotation of the final-state momenta can be factored out,
simplifying the analysis of the remaining dynamics.
It reads,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\lambda_{0};\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}D^{j_{0}*}_{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\mathcal{A}^{\text{aligned}}_{\lambda_{0}^{\prime};\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}(\tau_{\text{align}}),

(16)

where
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
are the Euler angles of the overall rotation and
\tau_{\text{align}}
encapsulates
3n-7
kinematic variables of the aligned reaction.
These angles can be chosen from a reference decay chain as

\phi_{A}^{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}}
,
\theta_{A}^{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}}
, and
\phi_{B}^{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}}
for
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
, respectively,
where
A
is a root node, and
B
is an internal node connected to
A
.
The factorization is not used explicitly in Eq. (
8
),
but it comes as a consequence of the construction if implemented correctly.

The general case of a three-body decay is complex enough to manifest a subtle feature of an additional
2\pi
phase that cannot be captured with the rotation group of particle four-momenta,
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(3,1)

V.2
Particle ordering

A topology defines its ordering scheme
i.e.
, how particle one and particle two are chosen.
Upon modifying the ordering scheme, three changes are in place:
opposite helicity angles, new Wigner rotation, and order of particles in the vertex spin-orbit coupling.
Let us start with the latter update.
The spin-orbit couplings, introduced in Eq. (
12
),
have a convenient transformation property [see Eq. (5.57) in Ref.
Martin and Spearman 1970
]:

\displaystyle h_{l_{\kappa}s_{\kappa}}^{{{\kappa{[1]}}{\kappa{[2]}}}}=(-1)^{l_{\kappa}+s_{\kappa}-j_{\kappa{[1]}}-j_{\kappa{[2]}}}h_{l_{\kappa}s_{\kappa}}^{{{\kappa{[2]}}{\kappa{[1]}}}}\,,

(17)

The helicity angles are affected as follows,

\displaystyle\theta_{j}=\pi-\theta_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\phi_{j}=\phi_{i}\pm\pi\,,

where the sign of the
\phi
shift depends on the ranges of the azimuthal angle,
the physical
\phi
always stays within
[-\pi,\pi]
, as also discussed in Ref.
Wang et al. 2021
.
It impacts the reaction amplitude via the Wigner D-function in Eq. (
9
).
When comparing a decay-chain amplitude for non-integer spin state with its order-swapped version, one finds a bizarre situation: due to the
2\pi
difference of the
\phi
shift, there is a relative sign factor that depends on
\phi
.
Such an unaccounted sign would show up in an interference between decay chains moving it from positive to negative values depending on the definition range of the azimuthal.
Fortunately, the Wigner rotations come with a complementary phase making things consistent again.
In fact, the
\phi
-dependent
2\pi
shift appears in even numbers of Wigner rotations. This
2\pi
shift does not translate into a phase for integer spin.
For both possible transitions with bosons
b
and fermions
f
,
b\to ff
, and
f\to bf
,
a full cancellation of the
\phi
-dependent phase is achieved.

VI
Other formulations

Alternative amplitude formulations known as
minus_phi
and the
canonical
convention,
prescribe different ways of traversing the decay graph.
Both solid angles of decay products and Wigner rotations appear different in these approaches.
Nonetheless, once implemented correctly, these formulations are mathematically equivalent to each other.

The
minus_phi
convention was originally used by Jacob and Wick in their foundational paper
Jacob and Wick 1959
.
It is adopted in many books and reviews, including
Martin and Spearman 1970
;
Richman 1984
;
Chung 1971
.
It adds an additional
R_{z}^{-1}(\phi_{\kappa})
transformation to the rotation sequence in Eq. (
3
). Namely,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(-\phi)}_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[1]}}}

\displaystyle=R(\phi_{k},\theta_{\kappa},-\phi_{\kappa})\,B_{z}(\gamma_{\kappa}^{{\kappa{[1]}}})\,,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(-\phi)}_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[2]}}}

\displaystyle=R(\phi_{k},\theta_{\kappa},-\phi_{\kappa})R_{y}(\pi)\,B_{z}(\gamma_{\kappa}^{{\kappa{[2]}}})\,.

(18)

The extra rotation impacts azimuthal angles in the daughter frames.
The full amplitude is computed using the same formulae as in the helicity formulation, Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
),
given a new set of angles and the rotation function
D^{j}_{\rho,\eta}(\phi,\theta,-\phi)
.

The
canonical
formulation deals with the spin projection to a fixed axis rather than the momentum direction.

\displaystyle\ket{p;j,m}_{\text{can}}=RBR^{-1}\ket{0;j,\lambda}

(19)

A pure boost is used to transit between frames, namely,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(\text{can.})}_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[1]}}}

\displaystyle=R(\phi_{\kappa},\theta_{\kappa})B_{z}(\gamma_{\kappa}^{{\kappa{[1]}}})R^{-1}(\phi_{\kappa},\theta_{\kappa})\,,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(\text{can.})}_{{{\kappa}}\leftarrow{\kappa{[2]}}}

\displaystyle=R(\tilde{\phi}_{\kappa},\tilde{\theta}_{\kappa})B_{z}(\gamma_{\kappa}^{{\kappa{[2]}}})R^{-1}(\tilde{\phi}_{\kappa},\tilde{\theta}_{\kappa})\,,

where
(\tilde{\phi}_{\kappa},\tilde{\theta}_{\kappa})
is the solid angle of particle
{\kappa{[2]}}
.

Using the cascade parametrization,
the full amplitude reads as a sum of the decay chains analogously to Eq. (
8
).

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m_{0};m_{1},\dots,m_{n}}(\tau_{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}_{1},\dots,m^{\prime}_{n}}\sum_{\mathfrak{c}}\mathcal{A}^{\mathfrak{c}}_{m_{0};m^{\prime}_{1},\dots,m^{\prime}_{n}}(\tau_{\mathfrak{c}})

\displaystyle\qquad\times W_{m^{\prime}_{1},\dots,m^{\prime}_{n};m_{1},\dots,m_{n}}^{\mathfrak{c}(\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}})}(\tau_{\mathfrak{c}}|\tau_{\mathrm{\mathfrak{o}}})\,,

(20)

where
m_{0}
is a spin projection of the decaying particle,
and
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}
collectively denotes the spin projections of the final-state particles.
The canonical formulation of decay chain amplitudes differs from Eq. (
8
).
The parametrization, as given by Refs.
Martin and Spearman 1970
;
Li et al. 2023
, is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\mathfrak{c}}_{m_{0};m_{1},\dots,m_{n}}(\tau_{\mathfrak{c}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{\text{int.}}\dots}\prod_{k}Y_{{l_{\kappa}}}^{m_{l_{\kappa}}}(\varOmega_{\kappa}^{\mathfrak{c}})X_{\kappa}(\mu_{\kappa})

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{l_{\kappa}s_{\kappa}}h_{l_{\kappa}s_{\kappa}}^{{\kappa}}(\mu_{{{\kappa}}},\mu_{{\kappa{[1]}}},\mu_{{\kappa{[2]}}})

\displaystyle\quad\times C_{m_{{\kappa{[1]}}},m_{{\kappa{[2]}}}}^{j_{{\kappa{[1]}}},j_{{\kappa{[2]}}}|s_{\kappa}}\,C_{m_{lk},m_{s{\kappa}}}^{l_{\kappa},s_{\kappa}|j_{{{\kappa}}}}\,,

where
m_{s{\kappa}}=m_{{\kappa{[1]}}}+m_{{\kappa{[2]}}}
, and
m_{l{\kappa}}=m_{{{\kappa}}}-m_{s{\kappa}}

Angular dependence is expressed using spherical harmonics,
related to the Wigner D-functions as

(see derivation on p. 215 of Ref.
Gourgoulhon 2013

The approach devised in this paper provides a general method for computing Wigner rotations in cascade decays. We established the applicability of our method across three distinct formulations, demonstrating their equivalence. With this work, we aim to lower the barrier to entry for amplitude analysis and offer a comprehensive, concise framework for modeling cascade reactions.

Appendix A
Matrices for Lorentz Transformations

In this appendix, we provide the explicit representation of the active transformation matrices for the
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(3,1)
and
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
groups.

The
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
matrices are used to represent Lorentz transformations in spinor space.
The boost along the z axis is given by:

\displaystyle B_{z}^{2\times 2}(\gamma)=\cosh(\xi/2)\cdot\mathds{1}+\sinh(\xi/2)\cdot\sigma_{z}

(21)

where
\xi
is the rapidity, related to the Lorentz factor
\gamma
by
\xi=\cosh^{-1}(\gamma)
.

The rotation matrices for rotations around the y axis and z axis can be written as:

\displaystyle R_{y}^{2\times 2}(\theta)=\cos(\theta/2)\cdot\mathds{1}-i\sin(\theta/2)\cdot\sigma_{y}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle R_{z}^{2\times 2}(\phi)=\cos(\phi/2)\cdot\mathds{1}-i\sin(\phi/2)\cdot\sigma_{z}\,.

(23)

The Pauli matrices
\sigma_{y}
and
\sigma_{z}
are defined as:

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma_{z}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}

(24)

The
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(3,1)
matrices represent Lorentz transformations in four-dimensional spacetime.
The matrices are used to transform the four-vectors of particles.
The boost along the z axis can be written as

\displaystyle B_{z}^{4\times 4}(\gamma)=\begin{bmatrix}\cosh(\xi)&0&0&\sinh(\xi)\\
0&1&0&0\\
0&0&1&0\\
\sinh(\xi)&0&0&\cosh(\xi)\end{bmatrix}\,,

(25)

where the elements of the matrix are indexed according to
p=(E,p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})
representation.

The rotation matrices around the y axis and z axis are defined as

\displaystyle R_{y}^{4\times 4}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&\cos(\theta)&0&\sin(\theta)\\
0&0&1&0\\
0&-\sin(\theta)&0&\cos(\theta)\end{bmatrix}

(26)

\displaystyle R_{z}^{4\times 4}(\phi)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&\cos(\phi)&-\sin(\phi)&0\\
0&\sin(\phi)&\cos(\phi)&0\\
0&0&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

(27)

Appendix B
Decoding parameters of Lorentz transformation

This appendix provides the exact equations to obtain the parameters of a Lorentz transformation composed of an arbitrary set of boosts and rotations,

\Lambda=OO\ldots O\,,

(28)

where
O
represents a boost or rotation operator. The objective is to convert this expression into a set of six parameters as defined in Eq. (
5
).
We use two representations of the Lorentz group:
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(3,1)
for intuitive decoding of angles and
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
for sensitivity to the
2\pi
azimuthal phase.

First, apply the transformation
\Lambda
to a vector at rest,
p_{\mathrm{r.f.}}=(\vec{0},m)
.

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\Lambda\,p_{\mathrm{r.f.}}

\displaystyle=R_{z}(\phi)R_{y}(\theta)B_{z}(\gamma)R_{z}(\psi_{\mathrm{r.f.}})R_{y}(\theta_{\mathrm{r.f.}})R_{z}(\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}})\,p_{\mathrm{r.f.}}

\displaystyle=R_{z}(\phi)R_{y}(\theta)B_{z}(\gamma)\,p_{\mathrm{r.f.}}

The first three rotations do not affect a vector with a zero momentum component. Thus, only the two rotations after the boost and the boost affect the vector. One can find the rapidity
\gamma
and the angles
\theta
and
\phi
via:

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{E}{m}\,,

(29)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}(p_{y},p_{x})\,,

(30)

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{p_{z}}{|\vec{p}\,|}\right)\,,

(31)

where
|\vec{p}\,|
is the magnitude of the three-momentum vector,
E
is the energy and
m
is the mass of the particle.

With the values of
\gamma
,
\theta
, and
\phi
determined, one can extract the pure rotation part of
\Lambda
, denoted
R_{\mathrm{r.f.}}
, by inverting the boost and post-boost rotations.

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{r.f.}}

\displaystyle=B_{z}^{-1}(\gamma)R_{y}^{-1}(\theta)R_{z}^{-1}(\phi)\,\Lambda

(32)

The rest-frame rotation angles can be calculated from the matrix elements
r_{ij}
of
R_{\mathrm{r.f.}}^{4\times 4}
.

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}\left(r_{y,z},r_{x,z}\right)\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}\left(r_{z,y},-r_{z,x}\right)\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{r.f.}}

\displaystyle=\cos^{-1}\left(r_{z,z}\right)\,.

(35)

With these angles one constructs the matrix
\Lambda_{\mathrm{2\times 2}}^{\mathrm{r.f.}}=R^{2\times 2}(\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}},\theta_{\mathrm{r.f.}},\psi_{\mathrm{r.f.}})
as the
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
matrix. To acquire sensitivity to
2\pi
jumps one can use the properties of the
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
representation and compare the reconstructed matrix to the original matrix.

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}=\begin{cases}\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}^{\prime}&\text{if }\Lambda_{\mathrm{2\times 2}}=\Lambda_{\mathrm{2\times 2}}^{\mathrm{r.f.}}\\
\phi_{\mathrm{r.f.}}^{\prime}+2\pi&\text{if }\Lambda_{\mathrm{2\times 2}}=-\Lambda_{\mathrm{2\times 2}}^{\mathrm{r.f.}}\end{cases}

(36)
