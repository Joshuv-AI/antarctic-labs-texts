Elementary properties of free lattices II: Decidability of the universal theory Thanks: Research of the second named author was supported by project PRIN 2022 “Models, sets and classifications”, prot. 2022TECZJA, and by INdAM Project 2024 (Consolidator grant) “Groups, Crystals and Classifications”.
J. B. Nation, Gianluca Paolini

Abstract
. We continue our work on the model theory of free lattices, solving two of the main open problems from our first paper on the subject. Our main result is that the universal (existential) theory of infinite free lattices is decidable. Our second main result is a proof that finitely generated free lattices are positively distinguishable, as for each n ⩾ 1 n\geqslant 1 there is a positive ∃ ∀ \exists\forall -sentence true in 𝐅 n \mathbf{F}_{n} and false in 𝐅 n + 1 \mathbf{F}_{n+1} . Finally, we show that free lattices are first-order rigid in the class of finitely generated projective lattices, and that a projective lattice has the same existential (universal) theory of an infinite free lattice if and only if it has breadth > 4 >4 (i.e., a single existential sentence is sufficient).



1.
Introduction

The model theory of free objects, particularly free groups, has produced many deep and beautiful results. Building on this tradition, in
[
14
]
we began investigating the model theory of free lattices, focusing on questions of decidability and elementary equivalence. Three key questions from
[
14
]
have remained open:

•

Is the
\forall
-theory of an infinite free lattice decidable?

•

Can finitely generated free lattices be distinguished by positive first-order logic (i.e., first-order logic without negations)?

•

Is each finitely generated free lattice the unique finitely generated model of its first-order theory (i.e., is it first-order rigid)?

In this paper, we resolve the first two questions affirmatively and provide new insights on the third. The decidability of the
\forall
-theory of free lattices represents our most significant contribution and will be our primary focus. We start by introducing this problem in more detail.
Let
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
denote the free lattice on
\lambda
-many generators.
The free lattice
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
is infinite whenever
\lambda\geqslant 3
.
It is well known that all countable infinite free lattices are bi-embeddable,
that is,
\mathbf{F}_{m}
and
\mathbf{F}_{n}
are bi-embeddable whenever
3\leqslant m,n\leqslant\aleph_{0}
.
Moreover, easily, for any uncountable cardinal
\kappa
,
\mathbf{F}_{\aleph_{0}}
is an elementary substructure of
\mathbf{F}_{\kappa}
.
Consequently, all infinite free lattices
share the same universal (and thus also the same existential) theory. This uniformity justifies our discussion of “the
\forall
-theory of an infinite free lattice” as a well-defined concept. To the best of our knowledge, nothing is known about this problem, apart from the decidability of the word problem for free lattices, which dates back to Whitman’s fundamental paper
[
16
]
from 1941. Actually, at this point, a historical remark seems in order, as this will be relevant for our proof strategy toward decidability. Skolem, as part of his 1920 paper which proves the Löwenheim-Skolem Theorem
[
15
]
, solved the word problem not only for free lattices, but for finitely
presented lattices as well. However, by the time the great awakening of lattice theory occurred in the 1930’s, his solution had been forgotten. Thus, Whitman’s 1941 construction of free lattices became the standard reference on the subject. It was not until 1992 that Stan Burris rediscovered Skolem’s solution. In fact, to be precise, Skolem also proved another result, i.e., that the universal Horn theory of lattices is decidable (see e.g.
[
10
, Chapter 9]
on Horn sentences). This is the starting point of our algorithm for deciding the truth value of a universal (equiv. existential) sentence in free lattices, which we now outline.

Recall that every first-order sentence can be put into prenex form, moving the quantifiers to the front.
Then the statement can be written in a disjunctive normal form.
We consider sentences
\psi(\mathbf{x})
whose prenex disjunctive normal form is:

\exists x_{1}\dots\exists x_{m}\ \psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,\operatorname{OR}\,\dots\,\operatorname{OR}\,\psi_{p}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
and each
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is a (finite) conjunction of lattice literals, i.e.,
s(\mathbf{x})\leqslant t(\mathbf{x})
or
u(\mathbf{x})\not\leqslant v(\mathbf{x})

with
s
,
t
,
u
,
v
lattice terms in the variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
.
The sentence
\psi(\mathbf{x})
holds in a free lattice
\mathbf{F}(Y)
if and only if
\exists\mathbf{x}\ \psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
holds in
\mathbf{F}(Y)
for some
1\leqslant j\leqslant p
.
Now,
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is like a lattice presentation, except that negations can be included.
Modifying Skolem’s algorithm slightly, we will convert each
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
into a
relational quasilattice with negations
, and use that to produce a
partially defined lattice
\mathbf{S}
with negations
. From there we will construct the
partial completion

\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})
(a partially defined lattice in the sense of
2.4
), and from it a
finitely presented lattice

\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
. The crucial observation is then that the partially defined lattice
\mathbf{S}
occurs in a free lattice
\mathbf{F}(Y)
(equivalently,
\mathbf{F}(Y)
satisfies
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
) if and only if there is a retraction of
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
onto a projective lattice
\mathbf{G}
that also contains
\mathbf{S}
(Lemma
2.21
). Whether or not this retraction of
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
exists can be tested in the
congruence lattice of
\mathbf{S}
, which is finite.
There are various properties to be tested for this, making the criteria for the retraction rather technical, but finite, and that makes our problem decidable, thus establishing the main theorem of our paper:

Theorem 1.1
.

The universal theory of infinite free lattices is decidable.

We recall that by
projective lattice
we mean a projective object in the category of lattices. In analogy with the theory of hyperbolic groups, we say that a projective lattice is non-elementary if it embeds
\mathbf{F}_{3}
. Now, every non-elementary projective lattice is bi-embeddable with
\mathbf{F}_{3}
and so it has the same
\forall
-theory of
\mathbf{F}_{3}
. So naturally we ask if we can separate the
\forall
-theories of elementary and non-elementary projective lattices. We answer this question with a result of independent interest:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
L
be a lattice satisfying Whitman’s Axiom
(W)
, then the following are equivalent:

(1)

L
embeds
\mathbf{F}_{3}
;

(2)

L
has breadth
>4
.

Corollary 1.3
.

A projective lattice has the same
\forall
-theory (equiv.
\exists
-theory) of an infinite free lattice if and only if it has breadth
>4
.

Notice that many infinite projective lattices which do not embed
\mathbf{F}_{3}
are known in the literature, the most famous example is probably the lattice
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{2+2})
(cf.
[
7
, pg. 105]
), i.e., the free lattice over a partial order set consisting of two independent
2
-element chains. This lattice is diagrammed in Figure
1
; using the diagram one can easily verify that this lattice has breadth
\leqslant 4
(and so it does not embed
\mathbf{F}_{3}
).

We now move to the second main theorem of our paper, which is concerned with positive logic. In
[
14
]
we showed that
\mathbf{F}_{3}
and
\mathbf{F}_{4}
were positively distinguishable, but a general solution for arbitrary
n
has eluded us. This made us naturally wonder if this was a limitation of our methods or if there was an intrinsic reason for this. In the subsequent theorem we solve this problem, showing that the situation is uniform in
n
. Interestingly, we will show in Section
5.5
that our proof actually applies to many other varieties of lattices (see there for details on which varieties).

Theorem 1.4
.

Finitely generated free lattices are positively distinguishable, as for each
n\geqslant 1
there is a positive
\exists\forall
-sentence true in
\mathbf{F}_{n}
and false in
\mathbf{F}_{n+1}
.

Having solved two of the main open problems from
[
14
]
we now move to the third problem mentioned at the beginning of the introduction. In this respect, in
[
14
]
we showed that every model of
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbf{F}_{n})
admits a canonical projection on the profinite-bounded completion
\mathbf{H}_{n}
of
\mathbf{F}_{n}
, and that this lattice is isomorphic to the Dedekind-MacNeille completion of
\mathbf{F}_{n}
. From this we deduced that
\mathbf{F}_{n}
and
\mathbf{H}_{n}
are not elementary equivalent. This gave us some information on models of
\mathbf{F}_{n}
(and so also on finitely generated models of
\mathbf{F}_{n}
), but it did not allow us for a full solution to the problem of first-order rigidity of finitely generated free lattices. This problem appears to us to be untractable at the moment in its general form. On the other hand, we will show that if we restrict to projective lattices, then we can obtain a strong form of first-order rigidity. Recall from above that every non-elementary projective lattice share the same
\forall
-theory of an infinite free lattice. In contrast:

Theorem 1.5
.

Free lattices are first-order rigid in the class of finitely generated projective lattices, i.e., if
\mathbf{L}
is a finitely generated projective lattice and there is
3\leqslant n<\omega
such that
\mathbf{L}
is elementary equivalent to
\mathbf{F}_{n}
then
\mathbf{L}\cong\mathbf{F}_{n}
.

Thus, the question of first-order rigidity of finitely generated free lattice reduces to the following fundamental question which we shall leave open:

Problem 1.6
.

Is there a non-projective (equivalently, non-bounded) finitely generated lattice
\mathbf{L}
elementary equivalent to a finitely generated free lattice?

The other major open problem on the model theory of free lattices is:

Problem 1.7
.

Is the full first-order theory of an infinite free lattice decidable?

2.
The strategy for decidability of the universal theory

In this section, we lay out our strategy for a proof of decidability of the universal theory of free lattices. For the benefit of the reader, we repeat the outline of our strategy from the introduction. Every first-order sentence can be put in prenex form, moving the quantifiers to the front.
Then the statement can be written in a disjunctive normal form.
We consider sentences
\psi(\mathbf{x})
whose prenex disjunctive normal form is:

\exists x_{1}\dots\exists x_{m}\ \psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,\operatorname{OR}\,\dots\,\operatorname{OR}\,\psi_{p}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
and each
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is a (finite) conjunction of lattice literals, i.e.,
s(\mathbf{x})\leqslant t(\mathbf{x})
or
u(\mathbf{x})\not\leqslant v(\mathbf{x})

with
s
,
t
,
u
,
v
lattice terms in the variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
.
The sentence
\psi(\mathbf{x})
holds in a free lattice
\mathbf{F}(Y)
if and only if
\exists\mathbf{x}\ \psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
holds in
\mathbf{F}(Y)
for some
1\leqslant j\leqslant p
.

Now
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is like a lattice presentation, except that negations can be included.
So we follow Section 2-3.1 of Freese and Nation
[
9
]
on finitely presented lattices, which in turn are based on Skolem
[
15
]
.
Modifying Skolem only slightly, we will convert each
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
to a
relational quasilattice with negations
, and use that to produce a
partially defined lattice
\mathbf{S}
with negations
. From there we will construct the
partial completion

\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})
(a partially defined lattice in the sense of
2.4
), and from it the
finitely presented lattice

\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
. We introduce a useful definition.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
\mathbf{S}
occurs in
\mathbf{F}(Y)
if there is a (partial lattice) homomorphism

\eta:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{F}(Y)
preserving negations.

Notice that
\mathbf{S}
occurs in the free lattice
\mathbf{F}(Y)
if and only if
\mathbf{F}(Y)
satisfies
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
, where
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is the formula used to construct
\mathbf{S}
.

The crucial observation is then that the partially defined lattice
\mathbf{S}
occurs in a free lattice
\mathbf{F}(Y)
(equivalently,
\mathbf{F}(Y)
satisfies
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
) if and only if there is a retraction of
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
onto a projective lattice
\mathbf{G}
that also contains
\mathbf{S}
(Lemma
2.21
). Whether or not this retraction of
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
exists can be tested in the

\mathbf{S}
.
he criteria for the retraction to exist are rather technical, but finite, and that makes our problem decidable, thus establishing the main theorem of our paper, i.e., Theorem
1.1
.

This section is devoted to proving all the necessary lemmas on which our strategy relies, while in the next one we will prove that the existence of the retraction of
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
can be tested in the finite lattice
\operatorname{Con}\,\mathbf{S}
. This section is divided into four subsection. In the first one we review Skolem’s algorithm to decide whether an existential sentence is true in all lattices, and use it to construct the lattice
\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})
. The second subsection is devoted to the construction of the lattice
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
. The third section presents the necessary background on bounded and projective lattices, toward a proof in the fourth subsection of our “Basic Lemma” (i.e., Lemma
2.21
) connecting satisfiability of the formula
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbf{F}(Y)
and the existence of a retraction of
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
onto a projective lattice
\mathbf{G}
.

2.1.
Skolem’s algorithm and the lattice
\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})

So let us begin. Recall that
lattice terms
over an alphabet
X
are defined recursively:

•

each
x\in X
is a term;

•

if
r
and
s
are terms, then
(r\vee s)
and
(r\wedge s)
are terms;

•

only expressions obtained by these rules are terms.

In particular, we regard
\vee
and
\wedge
as binary operations; with minimal adjustment one can treat them as finitary operations.

The set of subterms of a term is defined as usual:
if
t
is a variable then
\{t\}
is its set of subterms, and if
t=t_{1}\vee t_{2}
or
t=t_{1}\wedge t_{2}
then the set of subterms is the union of
\{t\}
and the subterms of
t_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

Dropping the subscript “
j
” on
\psi
, we are given a formula
\psi(\mathbf{x})
which is a conjunction of lattice literals, and thus can be written in the form

\psi(\mathbf{x})\qquad s_{1}\leqslant t_{1}\ \&\ \dots\ \&\ s_{k}\leqslant t_{k}\ \&\ u_{1}\nleqslant v_{1}\ \&\ \dots u_{\ell}\nleqslant v_{\ell}

where
s_{i}
,
t_{i}
,
u_{i}
,
v_{i}
are all terms in
X=\{x_{1},...,x_{m}\}
, where we recall that
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
.
Let us record such a
lattice presentation with negations
as a triple
\langle X,R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
where
X
is the set of variables and we let:

\displaystyle R_{+}

\displaystyle=\{s_{1}\leqslant t_{1},\dots,s_{k}\leqslant t_{k}\},

\displaystyle R_{-}

\displaystyle=\{u_{1}\nleqslant v_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}\nleqslant v_{\ell}\}.

Definition 2.2
.

A set
U
with three relations
\leqslant
,
\vee
, and
\wedge
of arities 2, 3 and 3, respectively,
is a
relational quasilattice
if it satisfies
the following axioms (i)–(v) and the duals of (iii)–(v):

(i)

\leqslant
is reflexive.

(ii)

\leqslant
is transitive.

(iii)

If
(x,y,z)\in\wedge
, then
(z,x)
and
(z,y)
are in
\leqslant
.

(iv)

If
(x,y,z)\in\wedge
and both
(u,x)
and
(u,y)
are in
\leqslant
then
(u,z)
is in
\leqslant
.

(v)

If
(x,y,z)\in\wedge
and

(x,x^{\prime})
,
(x^{\prime},x),

(y,y^{\prime})
,
(y^{\prime},y),

(z,z^{\prime})
,
(z^{\prime},z)
are all in
\leqslant
, then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\wedge
.

Skolem has a part (vi), which was copied into
[
9
]
, but is never used and best not included.
It says
(\forall x)(\forall y)(\exists z)
such that
(x,y,z)\in\wedge
.

Now it is possible that a lattice presentation with negations is inconsistent within lattice theory.
This would happen when:

(\dagger)\qquad\quad\&_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}\;s_{i}\leqslant t_{i}\implies u_{j}\leqslant v_{j}

for some pair with
u_{j}\nleqslant v_{j}
in
R_{-}
.
An example is the presentation

a\leqslant b\vee c\quad\text{AND}\quad d\leqslant a\vee b\quad\text{AND}\quad d\nleqslant b\vee c

This is inconsistent with lattice theory
itself
, and not only with the theory of free lattices.

Skolem’s algorithm, Theorem
2.3
below, tests for inconsistency in the presentation.
The algorithm creates a relational structure
(U,\leqslant,\vee,\wedge)
,
where
U
is the set of all subterms of the terms occurring in
R_{+}\cup R_{-}
. All three relations
\leqslant,\wedge,\vee
are initially empty.

Theorem 2.3

(Skolem’s Algorithm)
.

The following polynomial time algorithm
decides if the implication
(\dagger)
is valid. Let

U
be the set of all subterms of the terms
s_{i}
,
t_{i}
,
u_{j}
,
v_{j}
in
R_{+}\cup R_{-}
.

(a)

For
i=1,\ldots,k
, add
(s_{i},t_{i})
to
\leqslant
.

(b)

If
r=r_{1}\vee r_{2}
is a subterm, then add
(r_{1},r_{2},r)
to
\vee
.

(c)

If
r=r_{1}\wedge r_{2}
is a subterm, then add
(r_{1},r_{2},r)
to
\wedge
.

(d)

Close
(U,\leqslant,\vee,\wedge)
under
(i)–(iv)
and the duals of
(iii)
and
(iv)
.

Once we are done constructing
U
we check if in
U
we have that
(u_{j},v_{j})
is in
\leqslant
; if so, then
(\dagger)
is
true; otherwise not.

Of course, when the implication is true for any
j
, the presentation is inconsistent.
The proof is given as Theorem 2-3.1 in
[
9
]
.
We need to extract some information from that proof, as it contains the construction of the partially defined lattice from which we get the partial completion of
\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})
, which is the objet of interest of the present section.

After completing part (d), the relation
\leqslant
becomes a
quasiorder by axioms (i) and (ii) from
2.2
.
Moreover, axiom (v) and its dual hold for
(U,\leqslant,\vee,\wedge)
.
Let
\equiv
be the equivalence
relation associated with this quasiorder, where
u\equiv v
if
(u,v)

and
(v,u)
are in
\leqslant
. Let
\overline{u}
be the equivalence
class of
u
and
\overline{U}=\{\overline{u}:u\in U\}
. Of
course
\overline{U}
is an ordered set. Also note that if

(a,b,c)\in\vee
then by axioms (iii) and (iv),
\overline{c}
is the least upper bound of
\overline{a}
and
\overline{b}
under the order of
\overline{U}
.

Definition 2.4
.

A
partially defined lattice
(
\mathrm{PDL}
, for short)
is a partially ordered set
(P,\leqslant)
together with two partial
functions,
\bigvee
and
\bigwedge
, from subsets of
P
into
P
such that
if
p=\bigvee S
then
p
is the least upper bound of
S
in

(P,\leqslant)
, and dually. We use
(P,\leqslant,\bigvee,\bigwedge)
to denote this structure.

By the above remarks,
(\overline{U},\leqslant,\bigvee,\bigwedge)
is a partially defined lattice where
\bigvee
is given by the rule:
for each
(a,b,c)\in\vee
we have

\bigvee\{\overline{a},\overline{b}\}=\overline{c}
, and dually for
\bigwedge
.

Lemma
2.6
below shows that a partially defined lattice is embedded into its ideal lattice, in this case
\operatorname{Idl}(\overline{U},\leq,\bigvee,\bigwedge)
.
Since
\overline{s}_{i}\leqslant\overline{t}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
, if
\overline{u}_{j}\nleqslant\overline{v}_{j}
then this substitution into
\operatorname{Idl}(\overline{U},\leqslant,\bigvee,\bigwedge)
witnesses the failure of
(\dagger)
.

Let us now rename
\mathbf{S}=(\overline{U},\leqslant,\bigvee,\bigwedge)
, and since we still need to keep track of the non-inclusions in
R_{-}
, call the pair
\langle\mathbf{S},R_{-}\rangle
the
partially defined lattice with negations
determined by the presentation
\langle X,R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
(assuming it is consistent, i.e., that it has passed Skolem’s test). Note that the construction of
\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})
which we will introduce below contains terms and subterms from both
R_{+}
and
R_{-}
, and so in particular it depends on both
R_{+}
and
R_{-}
. This point is slightly obscured by the notation
\mathbf{S}
; thus we invite the reader to keep this in mind despite the notation.

Definition 2.5
.

Given
\langle\mathbf{S},R_{-}\rangle
and a partially defined lattice
\mathbf{K}
, a map
h:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{K}
is an
\mathbf{S}
-homomorphism
if it is a partially defined lattice (PDL) homomorphism such that
h(u)\nleqslant h(v)
for all
u\nleqslant v
in
R_{-}
.
We say that
\mathbf{S}
occurs in
\mathbf{K}
if there is an
\mathbf{S}
-homomorphism
h:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{K}
.

An
ideal

I
in a partially defined lattice

\mathbf{S}
is a subset of
\mathbf{S}
such that if
a\in I

and
b\leqslant a
then
b\in I
, and if
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
are in
I
and
a=\bigvee a_{i}
is defined then
a\in I
.
It is worth pointing out that these two rules may have to be applied repeatedly to find the ideal generated by a set.
The set of all ideals of
\mathbf{S}
including the empty ideal, ordered by set inclusion, forms a lattice denoted
\operatorname{Idl}(\mathbf{S})
.

Lemma 2.6
.

The map
s\mapsto\,\downarrow\!s
embeds
\mathbf{S}
into
\operatorname{Idl}(\mathbf{S})
, preserving the order (and its negation) and all the defined joins and meets.

Proof.

If
x
,
y\leqslant s
then
x\vee y\leqslant s
by axiom (iii) of relational quasilattices. If
s\wedge t
is defined, say
s\wedge t=w
, then
\downarrow\!s\,\cap\downarrow\!t=\,\downarrow\!w
by axiom (iv).
∎

Dually, a
filter

F
in
\mathbf{S}
is a subset of
\mathbf{S}
such that if
a\in F

and
b\geqslant a
then
b\in F
, and if
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
are in
F
and
a=\bigwedge a_{i}
is defined then
a\in F
.
The set of all filters of
\mathbf{S}
including the empty filter,
ordered by reverse set inclusion,
forms a lattice denoted
\operatorname{Fil}(\mathbf{S})
.
Likewise, the map
s\mapsto\,\uparrow\!s
embeds
\mathbf{S}
into
\operatorname{Fil}(\mathbf{S})
.

We are now finally in the position to define our lattice
\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})
: it is the sublattice of
(\operatorname{Id}\,\mathbf{S})\times(\operatorname{Fil}\,\mathbf{S})
generated by the diagonal (this idea is due to Day, cf.
[
4
]
).

2.2.
Constructing the finitely presented lattice
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})

Recall that the construction of
\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})
which we introduced above contains terms and subterms from both

and
R_{-}
, and so in particular it depends on both
R_{+}
and
R_{-}
. We will now introduce a finitely presented lattice
\operatorname{FP}(\mathbf{S})
. This lattice is constructed with
\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{S})
as a starting point (and so it has the information from

Notation 2.10

2.3.
Standardization

For technical reasons, before implementing the strategy, we need to
standardize
the
\mathrm{PDL}

\mathbf{S}
and the negations in
R_{-}
.
Remember that each element of
\mathbf{S}
is either a formal meet, or a formal join, or both, or neither (a formal generator).

Recall that we start with a presentation
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
and by Skolem’s algorithm produce a
\mathrm{PDL}

\mathbf{S}
. Let
S_{+}
denote the positive relations
s(\mathbf{x})\leqslant t(\mathbf{x})
that hold in
\mathbf{S}
. Of course
R_{+}\subseteq S_{+}
, but the latter also contains relations that are a consequence of those in

.
For example, if

then
S_{+}
contains
t_{j}\leqslant t
for all

. But others could be less trivial; that is why we have Skolem’s algorithm. Observe that:

\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in a lattice

if and only if
\langle\mathbf{x},S_{+},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in
\mathbf{L}
.

Definition 2.12
.

We say that a presentation
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
with associated
\mathrm{PDL}

\mathbf{S}
is standardized if it satisfies:

(1)

\mathbf{S}
satisfies
(\operatorname{W})
, that is, there is no relation of the form
\bigwedge s_{i}\leqslant\bigvee t_{j}
in
S_{+}

unless one of the inclusions
s_{i}\leqslant t
or
s\leqslant t_{j}
is also in
S_{+}
;

(2)

there is no relation of the form
\bigvee u_{i}\nleqslant v
in
R_{-}
;

(3)

there is no relation of the form
u\nleqslant\bigwedge v_{j}
in
R_{-}
.

Lemma 2.13
.

For each presentation
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
there is a finite collection of standardized presentations
\langle\mathbf{x},T_{1},U_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle\mathbf{x}T_{m},U_{m}\rangle
such that
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in a lattice
\mathbf{L}
satisfying
(\operatorname{W})

if and only if some
\langle\mathbf{x},T_{j},U_{j}\rangle
occurs in
\mathbf{L}
.

Proof.

We describe a process of replacing a given presentation
\mathbf{S}=\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
by a collection of standardized ones. The process shows how to reduce the number of failures of (1)–(3) (cf.
2.12
) in
\langle\mathbf{x},S_{+},R_{-}\rangle
; at each step we reduce the number of instances of failures of (1)–(3), and this will eventually lead to a finite collection of standardize presentations, as
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
is a finite object.

(1) Assume there is a relation
\bigwedge s_{i}\leqslant\bigvee t_{j}
in
R_{+}
.
Set
S_{i}=R_{+}\cup\{(s_{i},t)\}
and
T_{j}=R_{+}\cup\{(s,t_{j})\}
.
Then
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in a lattice satisfying (W) if and only if some
\langle\mathbf{x},S_{i},R_{-}\rangle
occurs or some
\langle\mathbf{x},T_{j},R_{-}\rangle
occurs.

(2) If some pair
(u,v)
is in
R_{-}
with
u=\bigvee u_{i}
a formal join, let
U_{i}=R_{-}\setminus\{(u,v)\}\cup\{(u_{i},v)\}
. Then
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in a lattice if and only if
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},U_{i}\rangle
occurs for some
i
.

(3) Dually, if there is a pair
(u,v)
in
R_{-}
with
v=\bigwedge v_{j}
a formal meet, let
V_{j}=R_{-}\setminus\{(u,v)\}\cup\{(u,v_{j})\}
. Again
\langle\mathbf{x},R_{+},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in a lattice if and only if
\langle\mathbf{z},R_{+},V_{i}\rangle
occurs for some
i
.
∎

After making these adjustments, thereby replacing the original problem by a disjunction of standardized problems, we can assume that the elements of
\mathbf{S}
are formally generators, or meets, or joins, but not both a meet and a join. Moreover, we can assume that the non-inclusions of
R_{-}
are of the form (gen,gen), or (meet, gen), or (gen, join).
Then apply the Skolem algorithm to each
\langle T_{j},U_{j}\rangle
to see if the presentation is consistent with lattice theory. For those that are, form the partial completion
\operatorname{PC}(\mathbf{T}_{j})
, and continue as below to see if it can be realized in a free lattice.

2.4.
Standardization and doubling

Recall that there is a natural homomorphism
\lambda:L[I]\to L
. We are interested in the following situation: given a
\mathrm{PDL}

\mathbf{S}
and a
\mathrm{PDL}
homomorphism

\mathrm{PDL}

.

.

2.5.
Bounded and projective lattices

In this section, we review the background and characterization of finitely generated projective lattices. This will be relevant for our “Basic Lemma”, i.e., Lemma
2.21
.

A
join cover
of an element
p\in\mathbf{L}
is a finite subset
A\subseteq\mathbf{L}
such that
p\leqslant\bigvee A
.
The join cover is
nontrivial
if
p\nleqslant a
for all
a\in A
.
An element
p
in a lattice
\bf L
is
join prime
if it has no nontrivial join cover, i.e.,
p\leqslant\bigvee A
implies
p\leqslant a
for some
a\in A
.
For finite subsets
A
,
B\subseteq\mathbf{L}
, we say that
A

refines

B
, written
A\ll B
, if for every
a\in A
there exists
b\in B
such that
a\leqslant b
.
The join cover
p\leqslant\bigvee A
is
minimal
if whenever
p\leqslant\bigvee B
and
B\ll A
, then
A\subseteq B
.
The dual notions, including
meet cover
and
meet prime
, are defined analogously.

Let
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{L}
be lattices.
A homomorphism
f:\mathbf{K}\rightarrow\mathbf{L}
is said to be
lower bounded
if for every
a\in\mathbf{L}
, the set
\{u\in\mathbf{K}:f(u)\geqslant a\}
is either empty or has a least element.
A finitely generated lattice
\mathbf{L}
is called
lower bounded
if every homomorphism
f:\mathbf{K}\rightarrow\mathbf{L}
, where
\mathbf{K}
is finitely generated, is lower bounded.
Let
D_{0}(\mathbf{L})
denote the set of join prime elements of
\mathbf{L}
. For
k>0
, let
a\in D_{k}(\mathbf{L})
if every nontrivial join-cover
V
of
a
has a refinement
U\subseteq D_{k-1}(\mathbf{L})
which is also a join-cover of
a
.
Observe that, from the definition,
D_{0}(\mathbf{L})\subseteq D_{1}(\mathbf{L})\subseteq D_{2}(\mathbf{L})\subseteq\cdots
.
Let
D(\mathbf{L})=\bigcup_{k<\omega}D_{k}(\mathbf{L})
.

The characterization of (finitely generated) lower bounded lattices is due to Kostinsky
[
12
]
, building on Jónsson
[
11
]
and McKenzie
[
13
]
.
See
[
7

.

Theorem 2.15
.

For a finitely generated lattice
\mathbf{L}
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

There exists a finite set
X
and a lower bounded epimorphism
f:\mathbf{F}(X)\twoheadrightarrow\mathbf{L}
.

(2)

For every finitely generated lattice
\mathbf{K}
, every homomorphism
h:\mathbf{K}\to\mathbf{L}
is lower bounded.

(3)

D(\mathbf{L})=\mathbf{L}
.

The notions of upper bounded homomorphism, upper bounded lattice, etc. are defined dually. A lattice is
bounded
if it is both lower and upper bounded. Day characterized finite bounded lattices in terms if doubling intervals
[
1
]
.

Theorem 2.16
.

A finite lattice is bounded if and only if it can be obtained from a 1-element lattice by a sequence of doubling intervals.

Since every finite distributive lattice can be obtained from a 1-element lattice by a sequence of doublings, we could also start with a finite distributive lattice.
There are analogues for finite lower and upper bounded lattices in terms of doubling other types of convex sets
[
2
]
.

A lattice
\mathbf{L}
is said to be
projective
if for any epimorphism
f:\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{N}
and any homomorphism
h:\mathbf{L}\to\mathbf{N}
there exists a homomorphism
g:\mathbf{L}\to\mathbf{N}
such that
h=fg
.

A lattice
\mathbf{L}
is a
retract

3.2.
Testing whether
\langle\mathbf{S},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in a finite bounded lattice

Theorem
3.1
tells us that
\langle\mathbf{S},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in a free lattice if and only if it occurs
in a finite bounded lattice
\mathbf{K}
, but it does not tell us enough about
\mathbf{K}
to be turned into
an effective test. This section provides an algorithm to decide that.

A
pseudovariety
is a class of finite algebras closed under homomorphic images, subalgebras, and finite direct products.
Lattice pseudovarieties include all finite lattices in the following list:

•

\mathcal{D}
, distributive lattices;

•

\mathcal{V}
, any lattice variety;

•

\mathcal{SD}
, semidistributive lattices;

•

\mathcal{B}
, bounded lattices.

For any pseudovariety
\mathcal{K}
and any finite lattice
\mathbf{L}
, there is a least congruence
\zeta
such that
\mathbf{L}/\zeta\in\mathcal{K}
.
We call this the
reflection
of
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathbf{L}
, and denote the congruence by
\zeta(\mathcal{K})
. Thus
\mathbf{L}/\theta\in\mathcal{K}
if and only if
\theta\geqslant\zeta(\mathcal{K})
.

On the other hand, a partially defined lattice
\mathbf{S}
has its own congruence lattice
\operatorname{Con}\,\mathbf{S}
,
consisting of those equivalence relations on
\mathbf{S}
that respect the operations (joins and meets) defined in
\mathbf{S}
.
To say that
\mathbf{S}
occurs in
\mathbf{L}
(ignoring
R_{-}
for the time being) means that there is a PDL homomorphism

r:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{L}
, in which case
\ker r
is in
\operatorname{Con}\,\mathbf{S}
.
We say that
\varphi\in\operatorname{Con}\,\mathbf{S}
is a
\mathcal{K}
-congruence
if there exist
\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{K}

and
r:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{L}
such that
\varphi=\ker r
.

Observe that if
r_{1}:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{L}_{1}\in\mathcal{K}
and
r_{2}:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{L}_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
,
then
r_{1}\times r_{2}:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{L}_{1}\times\mathbf{L}_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
with
\ker(r_{1}\times r_{2})=\ker r_{1}\ \cap\ \ker r_{2}
.
Thus there is also a least
\mathcal{K}
-congruence on
\mathbf{S}
, which we denote by
\widehat{\zeta}(\mathcal{K})
, to distinguish it
from the lattice congruence on
\mathbf{L}
.
It also holds that if
r:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{K}
and
g:\mathbf{L}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbf{M}
is a surjective lattice homomorphism,
then
gr:\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{K}
is a PDL homomorphism.
That does not quite prove that
\varphi
is a
\mathcal{K}
-congruence on
\mathbf{S}
if and only if
\varphi\geqslant\widehat{\zeta}(\mathcal{K})
,
but that will hold when
\mathcal{K}
is the pseudovariety of distributive or bounded lattices, as we will see in the course of the proofs below.

To build any finite bounded lattice, you start with its distributive reflection, and then perform a series of doublings (cf. Theorem
2.16
above).
So our first task will be to find the distributive congruence
\delta:=\widehat{\zeta}(\mathcal{D})
on
\mathbf{S}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
be a finite bounded lattice. The following are equivalent for a congruence
\gamma\in\operatorname{Con}\,\mathbf{L}
.

(1)

\gamma
is a coatom of
\operatorname{Con}\,\mathbf{L}
.

(2)

\mathbf{L}/\gamma\cong\mathbf{2}
, whence
\gamma
is the kernel of a lattice homomorphism
g:\mathbf{L}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbf{2}
.

(3)

\gamma
partitions
\mathbf{L}
into a prime filter,

and a prime ideal,

(1) implies (2) because
\mathbf{2}

, the only simple finite bounded lattice.
Clearly (2) implies (3) implies (1). The last statement is because
\mathbf{2}
is the only subdirectly irreducible distributive lattice.
∎

\mathbf{L}

Theorem 3.5
.

The existential theory of infinite free lattices is decidable.

This is of course equivalent to the fact that the universal theory of free lattices is decidable, thus proving our Main Theorem

Proof.

A sentence
\exists\mathbf{x}\ \psi(\mathbf{x})
, where
\psi
is quantifier-free, holds in a lattice
\mathbf{L}
if there exists an assignment of the variables

\varepsilon:\mathbf{x}\to L
such that
\psi(\varepsilon\mathbf{x})
is true.
We want a procedure to decide whether such a sentence holds in some/every infinite free lattice
\mathbf{F}_{n}
with
n\geqslant 3
.

The first step is to put
\psi(\mathbf{x})
into disjunctive normal form. This yields a sentence:

(\dagger)\quad\exists\mathbf{x}\ \psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,\operatorname{OR}\,\dots\,\operatorname{OR}\,\psi_{p}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
and each
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is a (finite) conjunction of lattice literals, i.e.,
s(\mathbf{x})\leqslant t(\mathbf{x})
or
u(\mathbf{x})\not\leqslant v(\mathbf{x})
.

For each
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
, we use Skolem’s Algorithm (Theorem
2.3
) to test whether the sentence is
consistent with lattice theory, i.e., whether it holds in any lattice. If every
\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
is inconsistent, then
the answer is NO:
\exists\mathbf{x}\ \psi(\mathbf{x})
does not occur in any lattice, much less a free one.
If some of the
\psi_{j}
are consistent, then remove the inconsistent ones, and for the ones that are consistent
form a pair
\langle\mathbf{S}_{j},R_{-j}\rangle
consisting of a partially defined lattice and negations.

These will be tested separately, to see if any one of them occurs in a free lattice.
Bur first we standardize each presentation, using Lemma
2.13
. This replaces each presentation

\langle\mathbf{S}_{j},R_{-j}\rangle
with a disjunction of standardized presentations of the same form.
The problem still has the form of
(\dagger)
, but with more options to be tested.

By Theorem
3.1
, a standardized configuration
\langle\mathbf{S},R_{-}\rangle
occurs in a free lattice
if and only if it occurs in a finite bounded lattice. That, in turn, in tested by the algorithm of this Section
3.2
.
If the answer is YES for any of the configurations in the disjunction, then
\exists\mathbf{x}\ \psi(\mathbf{x})
occurs in a free lattice.
If the answer is NO for every option, then the overall answer is NO, and it does not occur in a free lattice.
∎

4.
Non-elementary projective lattices

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a lattice
\mathbf{L}
is said to have
breadth at most
n
if whenever
a\in L
and
S
is a finite subset of
L
such that
a=\bigvee S
, there is a subset
T
of
S
, with
a=\bigvee T
and
|T|\leqslant n
. The breadth of a lattice is the least
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that it has breadth at most
n
(it such an
n
exists and infinite otherwise). The reader can verify that this concept is self dual. It is easy to see that having breadth
\leqslant n
is expressible by a universal sentence, and so having breadth
>n
is expressible by an existential one.

Fact 4.1

(
[
7
, Theorem 1.30]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
be a lattice satisfying (W). Suppose elements
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
a_{3}
, and
v\in L
satisfy:

(1)

a_{i}\not\leqslant a_{j}\vee a_{k}\vee v
whenever
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
;

(2)

v\not\leqslant a_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
;

(3)

v
is meet irreducible.

Then
\mathbf{L}
contains a sublattice isomorphic to
\mathbf{FL}_{3}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Suppose
\mathbf{L}
satisfies (W). Suppose now that
\mathbf{L}
has breadth
\geqslant 5
, then we can find
a,a_{1},...,a_{5}\in\mathbf{L}
such that
a=a_{1}\vee\cdots\vee a_{5}
holds and that this join is irredundant. Then, since every element of a lattice which satisfies (W) must be either join or meet irreducible,

is meet irreducible. Then by Fact
4.1

\mathbf{L}
has a sublattice isomorphic to
\mathbf{FL}_{3}
.
∎

Proof of Corollary

.

If
\mathbf{L}
is infinite and projective, then it is a sublattice of a free lattice and it satisfies
(W)
. Hence by

we are done, as

is bi-embeddable with an infinite free lattice.

5.
The positive theory of free lattices

In the paper
[
14
]
we showed that the positive theory of the free lattice
\mathbf{F}_{3}
properly contains the positive theory of
\mathbf{F}_{4}
.
This note intends to do the same for
\mathbf{F}_{n}
vs.
\mathbf{F}_{n+1}
for all finite
n\geqslant 3
.
Let
\operatorname{PTh}(\mathbf{L})
denote the positive first-order theory of
\mathbf{L}
.
Since
\mathbf{F}_{n}
is a homomorphic image of
\mathbf{F}_{n+1}
, we have
\operatorname{PTh}(\mathbf{F}_{n})\supseteq\operatorname{PTh}(\mathbf{F}_{n+1})
.
To distinguish them, we seek a positive sentence
\pi^{n}(\mathbf{x})
that holds in
\mathbf{F}_{n}
but not in
\mathbf{F}_{n+1}
, thus:

\operatorname{PTh}(\mathbf{F}_{1})\supsetneq\operatorname{PTh}(\mathbf{F}_{2})\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq\operatorname{PTh}(\mathbf{F}_{n})\supsetneq\operatorname{PTh}(\mathbf{F}_{n+1})\supsetneq\cdots

In this section and this section only we use the
\{+,\cdot\}
notation rather than the
\{\vee,\wedge\}
notation as this notation is preferable for some longer expressions of lattice terms appearing below.

5.1.
Preliminaries

The cases
n=1
and
n=2
are easy:

\displaystyle\pi^{1}=\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\forall y\ x\approx y

\displaystyle\pi^{2}=\qquad

\displaystyle\forall x\forall y\ [x\approx y\ \text{OR}\ \forall z\ (z\leqslant x+y)\ \text{OR}\ \forall z\ (z\geqslant x\cdot y)]

are sentences with the property that
\pi^{n}
holds in
\mathbf{F}_{n}
but not
\mathbf{F}_{n+1}
for
n=1,2
. So henceforth we assume
n\geqslant 3
.

As part of his solution of the word problem
[
16
]
, Whitman showed that free lattices satisfy the following condition:

\mathrm{(W)}\quad s\wedge t\leqslant u\vee v\text{ implies }s\leqslant u\vee v\text{ or }t\leqslant u\vee v\text{ or }s\wedge t\leqslant u\text{ or }s\wedge t\leqslant v.

A free lattice
\mathbf{F}_{n}
has a unique set of generators, say
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
.
These generators are both join and meet prime, which means:

•

if
x_{j}\leqslant\sum U
for some finite
U\subseteq F_{n}
, then
x_{j}\leqslant u
for some
u\in U
;

•

if
x_{j}\geqslant\prod V
for some finite
V\subseteq F_{n}
, then
x_{j}\geqslant v
for some
v\in V
.

In particular, the set
X
of free lattice generators is
independent
:
x_{i}\nleqslant\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}
and

x_{i}\not\geqslant\prod_{j\neq i}x_{j}
.
In a lattice satisfying Whitman’s condition
\mathrm{(W)}
, any independent set
Y
generates
a sublattice isomorphic to
\mathbf{F}(Y)
, see Section 1.2 of
[
7
]

Every element in a finite distributive lattice
\mathbf{D}
can be written uniquely
as a join of join irreducible elements, or as a meet of meet irreducible
elements. The former is often known as the
join normal form
and
the latter as the
meet normal form
. We are particular interested
in the case when
\mathbf{D}
is the free distributive lattice
\mathbf{FD}_{n}
.

Accordingly, let
Z=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}=\mathbf{z}
and let

f_{n}:\mathbf{F}(Z)\to\mathbf{FD}(Z)

be the standard homomorphism with
f_{n}(z_{i})=z_{i}
for
i\in[1,n]
.
Now
\mathbf{FD}_{n}
is a (finite) bounded lattice. Let
\mathbf{F}(Z)=\mathbf{F}_{n}
and
\mathbf{FD}_{n}=\mathbf{FD}(Z)
.
Thus
f_{n}
is a bounded homomorphism, i.e., the
\ker f_{n}
-class
that is the preimage of an element
d\in\mathbf{FD}_{n}

is a bounded interval
[\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z}),\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z})]
in
\mathbf{F}_{n}
. That is, for
w=w(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbf{F}_{n}
we have:

f_{n}(w)=d\quad\text{iff}\quad\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})\leqslant w\leqslant\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z}).

Moreover,
\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
is a join of meets of variables, and dually
\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
is a meet of joins of variables.
Not surprisingly,
\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
and
\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
are the lattice terms in
\mathbf{F}(Z)
corresponding to the join normal form and meet normal form of
d
in
\mathbf{FD}(Z)
, respectively. Notice that
\mathbf{FD}(Z)\models\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})=\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
but in general
\mathbf{F}(Z)\models\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})\neq\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
, unless
d
is either
0
,
1
, an atom, a coatom, or a generator; thus, in these cases
d
has a unique preimage under
f_{n}
. For example, let
d
be the upper cover of
z_{1}
in
\mathbf{FD}_{3}
.
Then
\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})=z_{1}+z_{2}z_{3}
and
\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z})=(z_{1}+z_{2})(z_{1}+z_{3})
, which are the join and meet normal forms (respectively) of the upper cover of
z_{1}
in
\mathbf{FD}_{3}
, and in fact in this case we have that
\mathbf{FD}(Z)\models\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})=\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
but
\mathbf{F}(Z)\models\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})\neq\mu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
.
That the bounds of the
f_{n}^{-1}
-classes are join and meet normal forms reflects the fact that join irreducible elements are join prime in distributive lattices, and, dually, meet irreducibles are meet prime.
When
\mathbf{L}
is a non-distributive lattice and

is a bounded homomorphism, the algorithm for finding the least element of
\ker g
-classes (Theorem 2.4 of
[
7

) includes terms for nontrivial join covers of join irreducible elements,
a\leqslant\sum B
in
\mathbf{L}
with
a\nleqslant b
for all

\mathbf{FD}(Z)
, and consequently no such terms in
\nu_{d}(\mathbf{z})
, the least element of
f_{n}^{-1}(d)
when

is join irreducible. Moreover, general considerations show that the least elements of congruence classes satisfy
\nu_{\sum a_{i}}(\mathbf{z})=\sum\nu_{a_{i}}(\mathbf{z})
, so when
\mathbf{L}
is finite it suffices to compute
\nu_{a}(\mathbf{z})
for
a
join irreducible. The dual statements hold for the largest elements

of congruence classes.

5.2.
The sentence
\pi^{n}(\mathbf{x})

The negation of independence is a positive property.
Likewise, being the greatest or least element of a lattice are positive properties.
Thus we have the following positive first-order formulas in the language of lattice theory:

\operatorname{NI}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):\quad(\operatorname{OR}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}x_{i}\leqslant\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j})\quad\operatorname{OR}\quad(\operatorname{OR}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}x_{i}\geqslant\prod_{j\neq i}x_{j});

t(u):\quad\forall w\ w\leqslant u;

b(u):\quad\forall w\ w\geqslant u.

We need one more type of formula.
Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1})
and
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
.
Let
E=(I_{1},\dots,I_{n+1})
be a vector of bounded intervals, say
I_{j}=[b_{j},a_{j}]
, where each
b_{j}(\mathbf{z})
and
a_{j}(\mathbf{z})
is given by a lattice term in the variables
\mathbf{z}
.
Because the intervals are bounded, the condition

x\in I=[b,a]
can be written out as
b\leqslant x\ \&\ x\leqslant a
. The condition that
x_{j}\in I_{j}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n+1
can thus be written as a positive first-order predicate:

\operatorname{CJ}(\mathbf{x},E,\mathbf{z}):\quad\&_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n+1}\ [b_{j}(\mathbf{z})\leqslant x_{j}\leqslant a_{j}(\mathbf{z})].

In the presence of Whitman’s condition
\mathrm{(W)}

says exactly that those elements
do not generate a copy of
\mathbf{F}_{k}
by
[

On the other hand,

\mathbf{F}_{3}
contains

[

]
, see

[
7
, Theorems 1.28 and 9.10]
.
Indeed, by Tschantz’s Theorem (
[
7

), every infinite interval in
\mathbf{F}_{n}
contains a copy of
\mathbf{F}_{\omega}
.
Thus
\mathbf{F}_{n}
contains many copies of
\mathbf{F}_{n+1}
, and the sentence
\pi^{n}
restricts their location.
By way of comparison,
\mathbf{F}_{n}
contains only finitely many finite intervals except for 2- and 3-element chains associated with completely join irreducible elements; see Theorem 7.5
of

5.5.
The positive theory of (some) relatively free lattices

A direct adaptation of the proof shows that for any lattice
variety
\mathcal{V}
with
\mathbf{A}_{n}\in\mathcal{V}
, and
n\geqslant 3
, the sentence
\pi^{n}
holds in the relatively free lattice
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}_{n}
and fails in
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}_{n+1}
.

The following facts are relevant.

(i)

The generators of
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}_{n}
are join and meet prime; indeed, this property is inherited from free distributive lattices, as
\mathcal{D}
is contained in
\mathcal{V}
.

(ii)

The split of
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}(Z)
into
\uparrow\!(\prod Z_{1})\ \dot{\cup}\,\downarrow\!(\sum Z_{2})

whenever
Z=Z_{1}\dot{\cup}Z_{2}
is a consequence of (i).

(iii)

Whitman’s condition
(\mathrm{W})
no longer holds, so independent sets need not generate a copy of a (relatively) free lattice. Instead, we just work with independence
per se
.

(iv)

The condition
\mathcal{E}
used in
\pi^{n}
assumes that we know, at least in principle, where the independent subsets of
\mathbf{F}_{n}
of size
n+1
are located. We no longer have Whitman’s or Tschantz’s theorem to guarantee their existence in certain intervals; for all we know, there could be none. Having fewer (or no) independent sets in
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}_{n}
just shortens the sentence
\pi^{n}
; the argument remains valid.

(v)

No independent set
X
of
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}(n)
of size
k>n
contains the join two atoms. To see this, first recall that any nontrivial lattice variety satisfies
\mathcal{D}\leqslant\mathcal{V}\leqslant\mathcal{L}
, where
\mathcal{D}
denotes distributive lattices and
\mathcal{L}
denotes all lattices. Hence there are natural homomorphisms
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{L}(Z)\to\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}(Z)\to\mathbf{F}\mathcal{D}(Z)
. But the atoms and joins of pairs of atoms are the same in
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{L}(Z)
and
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{D}(Z)
,
viz.
,
0\prec\underline{z}_{i}\prec\underline{z}_{i}+\underline{z}_{j}
.
So the same holds in
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}(Z)
. Thus if
u
is the join of two atoms in
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}(Z)
, i.e.
u=\underline{z}_{i}+\underline{z}_{j}
with
i\neq j
, then
|\!\downarrow\!u\,|=4
.
Meanwhile, if
X
is an independent set, then meets of distinct subsets of
X
are distinct. If
|X|=k>n\geq 3
and
x\in X
, then
|\!\downarrow\!x\,|\geqslant 2^{k-1}\geq 8
.
Consequently, an independent subset of
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}_{n}
with more than
n
elements cannot contain an atom or the join of two atoms.

(vi)

Since
\mathbf{A}_{n}\in\mathcal{V}
, there is a surjective homomorphism
h_{n}:\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}_{n}\to\mathbf{A}_{n}
.
This ensures that a join of two atoms
\underline{z}_{i}+\underline{z}_{j}

and its upper cover
\prod_{k\neq i,j}(\underline{z}_{i}+\underline{z}_{j}+\underline{z}_{k})
are distinct in
\mathbf{F}\mathcal{V}_{n}
, because they evaluate distinctly under
h_{n}
in
\mathbf{A}_{n}
.

(vii)

Because
\mathbf{A}_{n}
is a lower bounded lattice, any homomorphism from a finitely generated lattice to
\mathbf{A}_{n}
is a bounded homomorphism; see
[
7
, Theorem 2.13]
.
