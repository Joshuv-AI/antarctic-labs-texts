The Graph Geometric Control Condition
Kaïs Ammari, Alessandro Duca, Romain Joly & Kévin Le Balc’h

Abstract
Résumé Dans cet article, nous introduisons un nouveau concept appelé la Condition Géométrique de Contrôle sur les Graphes (GGCC). Il s’avère que cette condition constitue une reformulation géométrique simple de nombreux cadres dans lesquels la contrôlabilité des équations aux dérivées partielles sur les graphes a été étudiée. Nous démontrons que la condition (GGCC) est à la fois nécessaire et suffisante pour la contrôlabilité exacte de l’équation des ondes sur les graphes métriques avec des contrôles internes et des conditions aux limites de Dirichlet. Nous examinons ensuite la contrôlabilité exacte interne de l’équation des ondes avec des conditions aux limites mixtes, ainsi que celle de l’équation de Schrödinger, et nous étudions également la contrôlabilité à zéro interne de l’équation de la chaleur. Nous montrons que (GGCC) fournit une condition suffisante pour la contrôlabilité de ces équations et nous présentons des exemples explicites prouvant que (GGCC) n’est pas une condition nécessaire dans ces cas.



Keywords and phrases:
Graphs, Geometric control condition, Controllability, Observability, Wave equation, Schrödinger equation, Heat equation.

Introduction and main results

Network models have been extensively studied in the literature over the last few decades for modeling phenomena in science, engineering and social sciences. Notable applications include the dynamics of free electrons in organic molecules (see the seminal work
[
46
]
), superconductivity in granular and artificial materials (see
[
1
]
) and acoustic and electromagnetic wave-guide networks (see
[
29
,
44
]
). Control problems on metric graphs, particularly those involving tree-like structures, have been a focal point of extensive research due to their wide-ranging applications in science and engineering. These applications span various fields, including network theory, vibration analysis and structural dynamics (see
[
4
,
13
,
26
,
34
,
47
,
52
]
and the references therein).

Although network-type structures have garnered significant interest in control theory, there is currently no explicit characterization in the literature analogous to the well-known Geometric Control Condition (GCC) of Bardos, Lebeau, Rauch and Taylor

[
16
,
17
,
50
]
for Euclidean domains. The condition (GCC) provides necessary and sufficient condition for the controllability of the wave equation: it encodes the geometrical properties of the domain
\Omega
, and the controlled subdomain
\omega\subset\Omega
to ensure controllability. We recall that
\Omega
and
\omega
satisfy the Geometric Control Condition (GCC) if there exists
L>0
such that any generalized geodesic of
\Omega
of length greater than
L
meets the set
\omega
. The Geometric Control Condition for metric graphs has only been hinted at in a few existing works, see for instance
[
10
,
11
]
, but no complete study has been provided yet. The absence of such a characterization has left a significant gap in the understanding of controllability for wave equations in these non-Euclidean settings.

The aim of this work is to address this issue by introducing a novel Graph Geometric Control Condition (GGCC) and comparing it with the hypotheses commonly used in the literature to control PDEs on metric graphs. The condition (GGCC) provides a rigorous and precise framework for characterizing the exact controllability of the wave equation on networks with Dirichlet boundary conditions in the presence of internal controls. Moreover, it serves as a sufficient—though not necessary—condition for establishing the internal exact controllability of the wave equation with mixed boundary conditions, the Schrödinger equation and the internal null-controllability of the heat equation.

1.1
The Graph Geometric Control Condition

Throughout the rest of this article, a graph
\mathcal{G}
is defined as a structure composed by some edges (segments) connecting some vertices (points); see Figure
1
.
In this paper, we focus on studying partial differential equations (PDEs) defined on the edges of a graph. In this context, the primary structure of interest lies in the edges, while the vertices play a secondary role, being significant only through the connections they establish rather than as standalone elements.
We refer to vertices that are attached to the structure by only one edge as exterior vertices. The remaining ones are referred to as interior vertices. We consider very general graphs: loops (an edge connecting a vertex to itself) and parallel edges (multiple edges with the same endpoints) are allowed. We impose only the finiteness of the graph: there are a finite number of edges, all having a finite length.
Notice that relaxing the finiteness assumption would, of course, alter the analysis of the problem. However, most of the ideas presented in this paper could still be adapted to such cases.

We aim to define the Graph Geometric Control Condition adopted in this work and present some equivalent formulations. To this purpose, we need to provide few abstract definitions (their mathematical description is presented in Section

path

is defined as follows (see Figure

—

We start at any point of any edge of the graph and move in one of the two possible directions until we reach a vertex.

—

If we reach an exterior vertex, we turn back and continue in the other direction. Otherwise, we move along any other edge starting from the vertex.

—

We continue the route through the graph by repeating the previous point and stop at some point.

The total
length
of a path is the distance covered in
\mathcal{G}
. In all the article,
\omega
is an open subset of the graph
\mathcal{G}
, that is an open subset of the disjoint union of the edges. A path is said to
meet

\omega

\omega

We are finally ready to state our Graph Geometric Control Condition.

Definition 1.1

(GGCC)
.

Consider a graph
\mathcal{G}
and an open set
\omega\subset\mathcal{G}
. We say that
\mathcal{G}
and
\omega
satisfy the Graph Geometric Control Condition (GGCC) when there exists a length
L>0
such that any path longer than
L
meets
\omega
.

The setting of this condition is written as an echo of the famous Geometric Control Condition of Bardos, Lebeau, Rauch and Taylor, recalled above.
The main purpose of this article is to show that (GGCC) is, like its inspiration (GCC), an important property for the control and observation of PDE on graphs.

The condition (GGCC) is somehow a natural condition but, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that it is introduced in this geometrical way.
For example, from the perspective of boundary control, it is not difficult to see that (GGCC) means that
\mathcal{G}
is a tree and that the control is efficient at all the exterior vertices except maybe one. This geometric condition appear in many works. For example, Schmidt proves in
[
52
]
that such boundary control is sufficient for the exact controllability of the wave equation on tree graphs, see
[
26
, Theorem 2.7 in Section 2.4]
. The necessity of such a configuration for the exact controllability of the wave equation on tree graphs was latter obtained in
[
26
, Theorem 2.8 in Section 2.4]
, see also
[
12
, Chapter VII]
. These results were revisited by Avdonin and Zhao in
[
14
]
. Actually, to study the optimal time of control, these last authors introduce a geometric description that provides a criterion very close, and actually equivalent, to (GGCC). In the present paper, we choose to call it
watershed condition
, see Definition
3.2
in the next section.

In the case of internal control, one has more freedom concerning the geometry. In
[
9
]
, Apraiz and Bárcena-Petisco prove the internal null-controllability of a parabolic equation on a graph, assuming some technical hypotheses on
\mathcal{G}
and
\omega
which we denote here
ABP Condition
, see the precise definition in Definition
3.1
in Section
3
. As we will show here, the ABP Condition is equivalent to (GGCC), which provides a much more geometric description.

As a first contribution, we show in this article that all the previous conditions introduced to study the controllability of PDE on graphs are actually equivalent to the geometric criterion (GGCC).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a connected graph and let

5.

\mathcal{G}
and

satisfy the ABP Condition of Definition
3.1
.

6.

\mathcal{G}
and

satisfy the watershed Condition of Definition

1.2
Exact controllability of the wave equation

We now present one of the main results of this work: we show that (GGCC) precisely encodes the geometrical assumptions required for controllability of the wave equation. In detail, we prove that (GGCC) is both a necessary and sufficient condition for the internal exact controllability of the wave equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions. In what follows, we present our theory but omit to define some standard elements to keep the presentation as simple as possible. We refer to Section
2
for the formal definitions of all considered mathematical objects.

We denote by
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
the Laplacian defined on the graph
\mathcal{G}
equipped with either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions on the exterior vertices and with the standard Kirchhoff’s transmission conditions on the interior ones. We set
H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})
the set of functions in
H^{1}(\mathcal{G})
satisfying the Dirichlet boundary conditions at the suitable exterior vertices. We consider the following controlled wave equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}u=\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u+q(x)u+h1_{\omega},&t>0,\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)(t=0)=(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

(1)

with
q\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
, and where, at time
t\geq 0
,
(u,\partial_{t}u)(t,\cdot):\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the state and
h(t,\cdot):\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is the control.
The definition of the exact controllability of the wave equation (
1
) is as follows.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
T>0
. The wave equation (
1
) is exactly controllable at time
T>0
when, for every
(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and for every
(v_{0},v_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists
h\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\omega)
such that the solution
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T],H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G}))
of (
1
) with initial state
(u,\partial_{t}u)(t=0)=(u_{0},u_{1})
satisfies

(u,\partial_{t}u)(t=T)=(v_{0},v_{1}).

Our second main contribution of this article is as follows.

Theorem 1.4

(Control of the wave equation with Dirichlet b.c.)
.

Assume that
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
is the Laplacian operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions at all the exterior vertices and assume that
0
is not in the spectrum of
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+q
. There is a time
T>0
such that the wave equation (
1
) is exactly controllable at time
T
if and only if (GGCC) is satisfied. Moreover, when (GGCC) holds, the exact controllability is ensured for any time
T>L
where
L
is the length appearing in (GGCC).

Theorem 1.5

(Control of the wave equation with general b.c.)
.

Assume that
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
is the Laplacian operator with either Neumann or Dirichlet boundary conditions at the exterior vertices and assume that
0
is not in the spectrum of
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+q
.
If (GGCC) holds for the length
L
, then, for any time
T>L
, the wave equation (
1
) is exactly controllable at time
T
.

Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
show that (GGCC) characterizes the main geometrical properties required for the exact controllability of (
1
).
These results are proved in Section
4
by rewriting the control problem in terms of the observability for the dual system, see Theorem
4.1
. If (GGCC) is not satisfied, we can construct quasimodes that are mainly concentrated in the unobserved part of the graph and vanish at the exterior vertices. This strategy is common and very similar proofs can be found in
[
26
]
for example. It shows the necessity of (GGCC) when Dirichlet boundary conditions are assumed at the exterior vertices of the uncontrolled edges, whatever are the boundary conditions at the ends of the controlled edges, even Neumann ones. Notice however that (GGCC) may not be necessary when Neumann boundary conditions are imposed at uncontrolled edges, see Theorem
1.7
below.

To prove that (GGCC) is sufficient in any cases, it is possible to use explicit computations based on d’Alembert’s formula, see
[
14
,
26
]
. However, we choose here another strategy:

—

Fix a classical method to extend the observation on intervals. Here, we use the method of multipliers, which is more general than d’Alembert’s formula.

—

Use the previous tool to propagate the observation on edges up to the interior vertices.

—

When the PDE is controlled on all but one edge connected to an interior vertex, use Kirchhoff’s transmission condition to propagate the known observations through the vertex to the remaining edge.

—

Use Description 3 of Theorem
1.2
to show that (GGCC) ensures that the above steps are sufficient to extend the observation everywhere.

This global scheme of proof is robust in the sense that it can be applied to other PDEs, see
[
9
]
for a similar strategy applied to the heat equation.

Let us recall that the observation is also connected to the exponential stability of the damped wave equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}u+a(x)\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u+q(x)u,&t>0,\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)(t=0)=(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\\
\end{array}\right.

(2)

where
a\geq 0
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
and
\omega\subset\mathrm{supp}(a)
.
By leveraging the characterization of exponential stability in terms of the exact observability of the associated undamped problem (where
a=0
), we gain valuable insights into the dynamics of the system. This result, initially established by Haraux in
[
30
, Proposition 1]
and generalized by
[
5
]
, provides a direct link between the ability to observe the full state of the system over time and the exponential decay of its energy. Thus, the following corollary is a direct consequence of Theorem
1.4
and represents a graph version of a result established by Lebeau in
[
37
]
where the damped wave equation is addressed within the context of an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Assume that
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
is the Laplacian operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions at all the exterior vertices and assume that
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+q
is a negative operator. The exponential stability of (
2
) holds if and only if
\mathcal{G}
and
\omega
satisfy the (GGCC).

The assumption that
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+q
is a negative operator is important to ensure that the energy related to (
2
) is positive. In the same way, we need
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+q
to be invertible to obtain the observation inequality and thus the control. For example, if we set
q=0
and if there is no exterior vertex, then the constant solutions
u\equiv c\in\mathbb{R}
are neither observed nor damped and Corollary
1.6
obviously fails. In such cases, we should be able to generalize our results by restricting our problems in a suitable subspace. For instance, if there is no exterior vertex and
q=0
, Corollary
1.6
holds in the space orthogonal to the constant functions, that is the space of zero-average functions.

We would also like to emphasize that the time stated in Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
is not optimal for the exact controllability of (
1
). Actually, we provide in appendix an example where the optimal time is explicit, see Proposition
C.2
. The interested reader can find in Section
7.1
a discussion concerning the optimal time for observation and control of the wave equation.

We finally emphasize that the presence of Neumann boundary conditions strongly affects the result, which might be surprising given the classical results on open domains of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The condition (GGCC) is still sufficient but is not necessary as shown by the following example.
We consider the X graph of Figure
2
: a star-graph with four edges, here assumed to have the same length
\ell_{\text{b}}=\ell_{\text{t}}=1
, two (say
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
) have external vertices endowed with Neumann boundary condition and two (
e_{3}
and
e_{4}
) have external vertices endowed with Dirichlet boundary condition. The set
\omega
is the union of the left edges
e_{1}\cup e_{3}
. Obviously, this geometry does not satisfy (GGCC), since there exists a periodic path living in
e_{2}\cup e_{4}
, see Figure
2
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Consider the above geometry for the X graph and assume that
q=0
. For any time
T>4
, the wave equation (
1
) is exactly controllable at time
T
.

The proof of this result in mainly based on the result of pointwise observation of the wave equation on an interval with Dirichlet boundary condition on the left side and Neumann condition on the right side from
[
3
]
and is provided in Section

.

1.3
Exact controllability of the Schrödinger equation

Keeping in mind the results obtained for the wave equation, we now discuss the exact controllability issue for the Schrödinger equation. As we will see, (GGCC) is a sufficient but not necessary condition for the control of the Schrödinger equation.
More precisely, using the previous notations (see also Section
2
), we consider the Schrödinger equation in a graph
\mathcal{G}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u-q(x)u+h1_{\omega},&\ \ \ \ t>0,\\
u(t=0)=u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

(3)

with
q\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
the Laplacian defined on the graph
\mathcal{G}
, with either Dirichlet or Neumann conditions at the exterior vertices. In (
3
), at time
t\geq 0
,
u(t,\cdot):\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}
denotes the state and
h(t,\cdot):\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}
is the control.
The definition of the exact controllability of the Schrödinger equation (
3
) is as follows.

Definition 1.8
.

Let
T>0
. The Schrödinger equation (
3
) is exactly controllable at time
T>0
if for every
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G};\mathbb{C})
and for every
u_{1}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G};\mathbb{C})
, there exists
h\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\omega;\mathbb{C})
such that the solution
u\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
of (
3
) with initial state
u(t=0)=u_{0}
satisfies

u(t=T)=u_{1}.

When the Schrödinger equation (
3
) is exactly controllable for every time
T>0
, we say that it is small-time exactly controllable.

It is well-known that the exact controllability of the wave equation implies the controllability in small-time of the Schrödinger equation, see for instance
[
42
, Theorem 3.1]
. Then, from Theorem
1.5
, we obtain the following exact controllability result.

Theorem 1.9
.

Assume that the graph
\mathcal{G}
and the control set
\omega
satisfy (GGCC). Then, the Schrödinger equation (
3
) is small-time exactly controllable.

Theorem
1.9
is proved in Section
5
and yields that (GGCC) is a sufficient condition for the exact controllability of the Schrödinger equation. It is then natural to wonder if (GGCC) is a necessary condition. The graph-type domains being an intermediate geometry between the one-dimensional segments and the higher-dimensional domains, guessing the answer is not immediate. Indeed, in the standard one-dimensional cases of intervals, the controllability of the Schrödinger equation is equivalent to the one of the wave equation,
\omega
could be any arbitrary open subset, see for instance
[
36
]
.
On the other hand, in higher dimensional domains, the Schrödinger equation may be controllable even if the classical geometric control condition of Bardos, Lebeau, Rauch and Taylor is not satisfied, see for instance
[
8
,
21
,
32
]
for the control of Schrödinger equation in rectangles where
\omega
could be any arbitrary open subset, and
[
7
]
for the control of Schrödinger equation in the unit disk where
\omega
has to be an open subset touching the boundary of the disk.

In this article, we show that the graphs are closer to the higher dimensional domains. Indeed, we exhibit a example of graph where the Schrödinger equation is controllable even if (GGCC) is not satisfied.
It is also interesting to note how much our example depends on the diophantine properties of the lengths of its edges. Let us recall the following definition.

Definition 1.10
.

A real number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is said to be
badly approximable
if there exists
C>0
such that, for all
(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\geq\frac{C}{q^{2}}.

Examples of badly approximable numbers are
\sqrt{2}
,
\sqrt{3}
, or, in general, all algebraic irrational numbers of order
2
, see Liouville’s Theorem
A.5
recalled in Appendix
A
. Also notice that almost all numbers are not badly approximable and that there are explicit examples of non-badly approximable numbers as
e=\exp(1)
. We refer to Appendix
A
for further discussions on the subject.

Now, we consider the X graph of Figure
2
: a star-graph with four edges, two (say
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
) of length
\ell_{\text{b}}>0
and two (
e_{3}
and
e_{4}
), of length
\ell_{\text{t}}>0
. The set
\omega
has exactly two non-empty parts, one in
e_{1}
and one in
e_{3}
. Obviously, this geometry does not satisfy (GGCC), since there exists a periodic path living in
e_{2}\cup e_{4}
, see Figure
2
. To simplify, we set here
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
to be the operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions and
q
to be
0
.

Theorem 1.11
.

Consider the above geometric setting of the X graph
\mathcal{G}
. The Schrödinger equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u+h1_{\omega},&\ \ \ \ t>0,\\
u(t=0)=u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

is exactly controllable in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
for some time
T>0
if and only if the ratio
\ell_{\text{t}}/\ell_{\text{b}}
is a badly approximable number.

As noticed above, Theorem
1.11
provides both examples and counter-examples: Schrödinger equation on the X graph is controllable for the lengths
(\ell_{\text{t}},\ell_{\text{b}})=(\sqrt{2},1)
but not controllable for the lengths

. In particular, the case
(\ell_{\text{t}},\ell_{\text{b}})=(\sqrt{2},1)
provides an example where (
3
) is controllable despite (GGCC) not being satisfied and Dirichlet boundary conditions being imposed. It is worth mentioning that when
\ell_{\text{t}}/\ell_{\text{b}}
is a badly approximable number, the Schrödinger equation on the X graph holds is controllable for some time
T>0
but we actually do not know if the small-time controllability is satisfied or not, see Section

for a discussion on this topic. Last but not least, recalling that the set of badly approximable numbers if of measure zero, it appears that the counterexample of Theorem

Theorem
1.11
is proved in Section
5
. It is based on the well-known equivalence between the exact controllability of the Schrödinger equation and a so-called resolvent estimate, see
[
22
,
42

. We then strongly exploit the presence of a symmetry in the structure of the graph. Such symmetry allows us to rewrite (
3

, the small-time exact controllability is an open problem that we discuss in Section
7.2
.

1.4
Null-controllability of the heat equation

Let us now consider the case of the heat equation. We study the relation of the condition (GGCC) and the controllability of the following heat equation in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u+q(x)u+h1_{\omega},&\ \ \ \ \ t>0,\\
u(t=0)=u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

(4)

with
q\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
the Laplacian defined on the graph
\mathcal{G}
, with either Dirichlet or Neumann conditions at the exterior vertices. In (
4
), at time
t\geq 0
, the function
u(t,\cdot):\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is the state and
h(t,\cdot):\omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the control.

Due to the well-known regularizing effects of the heat equation, the standard notion of the controllability of (
4
) is the so-called null-controllability.

Definition 1.12
.

Let
T>0
. The heat equation (
4
) is null-controllable at time
T
if for every
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists
h\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\omega)
such that the solution
u\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
of (
4
) with initial state
u(t=0)=u_{0}
satisfies

u(t=T)=0.

When the heat equation (
4
) is null-controllable for every time
T>0
, we say that it is small-time null-controllable.

As for the Schrödinger equation, it turns out that (GGCC) is a sufficient but not necessary condition for the null-controllability of the equation (
4
).
Actually, the sufficiency can be seen as a direct corollary of the work of Apraiz and Bárcena-Petisco
[
9
]
. Indeed, from Proposition
1.2
, we know that (GGCC) is equivalent to Apraiz, Barcena-Petisco’s condition recalled in Definition
3.1
. Thus,
[
9
]
implies that (
4
) is small-time null-controllable whenever as (GGCC) holds.

Theorem 1.13

(
[
9
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\omega

We consider a star-graph

2.2
Sobolev spaces on graphs

In this paper, we are considering PDEs defined on the edges of a graph
\mathcal{G}
. It is natural to introduce the following space of functions. First, we define

L^{2}(\mathcal{G}):=\prod_{j\in J}L^{2}(e_{j}):=L^{2}\big(\sqcup_{j\in J}e_{j}\big),

to be the Hilbert space endowed with the inner product

\Big((u_{j}),(w_{j})\Big)_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}=\sum_{j\in J}\int_{0}^{\ell_{j}}u_{j}(x)\,\overline{w_{j}(x)}\,{\text{\rm d}}x.

The above sum is a sum of the
L^{2}
-products on all the edges. In this paper, we will write this kind of terms more shortly as the integral on
\mathcal{G}
. In other words, for any integrable function
f
of
L^{1}\big(\sqcup_{j\in J}e_{j}\big)
, we set

\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(x)\,{\text{\rm d}}x\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum_{j\in J}\penalty\ \int_{0}^{\ell_{j}}f(x)\,{\text{\rm d}}x.

We also use here and everywhere the abuse of notation of assimilating
e_{j}
to
(0,\ell_{j})
and we denote by

u_{j}(v)
the value
u_{j}(0^{+})
if
v
is the incident vertex of
e_{j}
assimilated to
0
(resp. the value
u_{j}(\ell_{j}^{-})
if
v
is the incident vertex of
e_{j}
assimilated to
\ell_{j}
) and we set
\frac{\,{\text{\rm d}}u_{j}}{\,{\text{\rm d}}n_{j}}(v)=-\partial_{x}u_{j}(0^{+})
(resp.
=\partial_{x}u_{j}(\ell_{j}^{-})
) the exterior derivative of
u
at the edge
v
.
We introduce the following Sobolev spaces. Let

H^{1}(\mathcal{G}):=\Big\{u=(u_{j})_{j\in J}\in\prod_{j\in J}H^{1}(e_{j})\penalty\ :\penalty\ u_{j}(v_{k})=u_{j^{\prime}}(v_{k}),\ k\in K_{\rm int},\ j,j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\Big\},

be the Hilbert space endowed with the inner product

\Big((u_{j}),(w_{j})\Big)_{H^{1}(\mathcal{G})}=\int_{\mathcal{G}}u_{j}(x)\,\overline{w_{j}(x)}\,{\text{\rm d}}x+\int_{\mathcal{G}}\partial_{x}u_{j}(x)\,\overline{\partial_{x}w_{j}(x)}\,{\text{\rm d}}x.

Notice that the condition in the definition of
H^{1}(\mathcal{G})
means that the function
u
is continuous at the interior vertices. We also define the subspace

H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{G}):=\big\{u=(u_{j})_{j\in J}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{G})\penalty\ :\penalty\ u_{j}(v_{k})=0,\ k\in K_{\rm ext},\ j\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\big\}

of
H^{1}-
functions satisfying the Dirichlet boundary conditions at the exterior vertices.
We finally introduce the Hilbert space

\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathcal{G}):=\Big\{u=(u_{j})_{j\in J}\in\prod_{j\in J}H^{2}(e_{j})\penalty\ :

\displaystyle\ u_{j}(v_{k})=u_{j^{\prime}}(v_{k}),\ k\in K_{\rm int},\ j,j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{k};

\displaystyle\ \sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}\frac{\,{\text{\rm d}}u_{j}}{\,{\text{\rm d}}n_{j}}(v_{k})=0,\ k\in K_{\rm int}\Big\},

endowed with the inner product

\Big((u_{j}),(w_{j})\Big)_{H^{2}(\mathcal{G})}\\
=\int_{\mathcal{G}}u_{j}(x)\,\overline{w_{j}(x)}\,{\text{\rm d}}x+\int_{\mathcal{G}}\partial_{x}u_{j}(x)\,\overline{\partial_{x}w_{j}(x)}\,{\text{\rm d}}x+\int_{\mathcal{G}}\partial_{x}^{2}u_{j}(x)\,\overline{\partial_{x}^{2}w_{j}(x)}\,{\text{\rm d}}x.

In the definition of
H^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, notice the presence of the classical Kirchhoff boundary condition saying that the flux through any interior vertex is conserved.

2.3
The Laplace operator and well-posedness of PDEs on graphs

We now define the Laplacian operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
equipped with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions on the exterior vertices. We denote by
K_{\rm ext}^{D}\subseteq K_{\rm ext}
the indices of the exterior vertices with Dirichlet boundary conditions and
K_{\rm ext}^{N}\subseteq K_{\rm ext}
the indices of the exterior vertices with Neumann boundary conditions.
We can then define the Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
as the operator with domain

\displaystyle D(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}})=\Big\{u=(u_{j})_{j\in J}\in H^{2}(\mathcal{G})\penalty\ :

\displaystyle\ \partial_{x}u_{j}(v_{k})=0,\ k\in K_{\rm ext}^{N},\ j\in\mathcal{E}_{k};

\displaystyle\ u_{j}(v_{k})=0,\ k\in K_{\rm ext}^{D},\ j\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\Big\}.

and such that, for every
u\in D(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}})
, its action is
\big(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u\big)_{j}=\partial_{x}^{2}u_{j}
for every
j\in J
.
The Laplacian operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
is a non-positive self-adjoint operator with a compact resolvent, hence its spectrum
\sigma(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}})
is a discrete set, see
[
19
, Theorem 3.1.1]
. We finally notice that

D(|\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}|^{1/2})=\big\{u=(u_{j})_{j\in J}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{G})\penalty\ :\penalty\ u_{j}(v_{k})=0,\ k\in K_{\rm ext}^{D},\ j\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\big\},

and we set in short

H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G}):=D(|\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}|^{1/2}).

In the specific case of the Dirichlet Laplacian operator, we have of course
H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})=H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{G})
. Its dual space, with respect to the pivot space
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, will be denoted by

H^{-1}(\mathcal{G}):=H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{G})^{\prime}.

By using the properties of the operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
, in particular the fact that it is sectorial, and standard semigroup theory (see for instance
[
25
, Appendix A and Chapter 2]
), we obtain the following classical well-posedness results for PDEs on graphs. Let
T>0
,
q\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
and
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
.

—

Let
a\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
,
a\geq 0
. For all
(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, the wave equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}u+a(x)\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u+q(x)u+f,&t\in(0,T),\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)(t=0)=(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

(5)

admits a unique solution
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\big([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\big)\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}\big([0,T],H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\big)
.

—

For all
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and for all
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, the Schrödinger equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u-q(x)u+f,&t\in(0,T),\\
u(t=0)=u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

(6)

admits a unique solution
u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}\big([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\big)
.

—

For all
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and for all
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))
, the heat equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u+q(x)u+f,&t\in(0,T),\\
u(t=0)=u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

(7)

admits a unique solution
u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}\big([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\big)
.

In particular, these well-posedness results ensure that the wave equation (
1
), the Schrödinger equation (
3
) and the heat equation (
4
) are well-posed linear controlled systems in the sense of
[
25
, Section 2.3]
. Finally, the Cauchy problem associated to the damped wave equation (
2

3
Study of the Graph Geometrical Control Condition

The aim of this section is to study the Graph Geometric Control Condition and provide some equivalent formulations.

3.1
Definitions of the APB Condition and the watershed condition

Recall that a
cycle
is a sequence of edges
e_{0}
,
e_{1}
,
\dots
,
e_{n}
in
\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{k}\cap\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\neq\emptyset
for every
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
(meaning that the edges are adjacent) and
e_{0}=e_{n}
. We are ready to formally introduce the
Apraiz-Bárcena-Petisco Condition
(ABP condition in short) mentioned in the introduction and adopted in the work
[
9
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a graph
\mathcal{G}
and an open set
\omega\subset\mathcal{G}
satisfy the
ABP Condition
if:

(i)

the set
\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
of uncontrolled edges does not contain any cycle of edges,

(ii)

there exists an injective function
u
from
\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
into the set
\mathcal{V}_{\rm int}
of interior vertices such that, for all
e_{j}\in\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
,
u(e_{j})
is one of the incident vertices
\mathcal{V}_{j}
of
e_{j}
.

We also introduce a geometric description following the work of Avdonin and Zhao. They show in
[
14
]
that this description is related to the optimal time of control.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a graph
\mathcal{G}
and an open set
\omega\subset\mathcal{G}
satisfy the
watershed Condition
if there exists a finite number of open paths
(p_{i})_{i=1\dots N}
on the graph such that:

1.

each path
p_{i}
has at least one end in
\omega
,

2.

the union
\cup_{i}p_{i}
of the paths covers
\mathcal{G}\setminus\omega
,

3.

all the paths are disjoints,

4.

if a vertex belongs to the closure of several paths, only two situations are allowed: each incoming edge belongs to a different path or there are exactly two edges belonging to the same path and all the others edges belong to different paths.

The above condition can be seen as the fact that the uncontrolled part
\mathcal{G}\setminus\omega
of the graph can be covered by watersheds consisting of rivers starting from
\omega
and flowing down, up to reach an exterior vertex or
\omega
again. The last condition states that no river can cross another one and if several rivers meet at some vertex, only one can continue after this point.

3.2
Equivalent formulations of (GGCC)

As explained in the introduction, the above criteria are equivalent to the Graph Geometric Control Condition. The purpose of the present section is to provide a proof of this fact, i.e. prove Proposition
1.2
.

Proof of Proposition
1.2
.

We prove 1
\Leftrightarrow
2, 2
\Leftrightarrow
3, 1
\Rightarrow
4
\Rightarrow
2, 3
\Rightarrow
5
\Rightarrow
2 and 3
\Rightarrow
6
\Rightarrow
5.

1
\Rightarrow
2. If the graph contains a cycle or a path connecting two exterior vertices that does not intersect
\omega
, then this cycle or path induces a periodic path of infinite length that never intersects
\omega
.

2
\Rightarrow
1. Suppose that every cycle of the graph and every path connecting two exterior vertices contains a part of
\omega
. Let
\ell
be the sum of the lengths of all edges in the graph. Consider a path of length at least
\ell
; it must necessarily pass through the same point twice. This can occur in only two ways: either the path contains a cycle and thus part of
\omega
, or the path has made a U-turn at an exterior vertex. In the latter case, we wait another time interval of
\ell
. Once again, the path must pass through the same point twice. At this point, either it contains a cycle (and thus part of
\omega
), or it connects to another exterior vertex. In this case, the path has followed a path connecting two exterior vertices and therefore intersects
\omega
.

2
\Leftrightarrow
3. This equivalence is immediate: (GGCC) means that each connected uncontrolled subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
cannot contain a cycle or two exterior vertices of
\mathcal{G}
, which is exactly saying that each such subgraph is indeed a tree containing at most one exterior vertices of the original graph.

1
\Rightarrow
4. Following repeatedly any periodic path leads to a path of infinite length, which must necessarily intersect
\omega
.

4
\Rightarrow
2. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that the point 4 is satisfied but the point 2 is not. If
\omega
does not intersect a cycle or a path connecting two exterior vertices, then it is possible to construct a periodic path that does not intersect
\omega
, which leads to a contradiction. Therefore, Property 2 holds.

3
\Rightarrow
5. Because we have already proved that 2
\Leftrightarrow
3, the condition on the cycles is automatically satisfied. We construct as follows the injective function described in the point (ii) of ABP Condition (see Definition
3.1
). We remove all controlled edges from the graph and we obtain a forest where each tree contains at most one exterior vertex from the original graph. If such a vertex exists, then we designate it as the root of the tree; otherwise, we choose any vertex of the tree as the root. For each of the trees, we place the root at the bottom and construct the injective function by associating each edge with the vertex “above”. Since the only potentially restricted vertex is at the root, this procedure successfully constructs the injective function required by ABP Condition.

5
\Rightarrow
2. First, notice that the condition on cycles is the same in both items.
Assume now, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists a path connecting two exterior vertices without intersecting
\omega
. This path consists of
N
edges and
N-1
interior vertices. In such a case, it would be impossible to construct an injective function as described in the point (ii) of ABP Condition. Therefore, Property 2 holds.

3
\Rightarrow
6. For each uncontrolled tree, we can construct a watershed as follows. If the tree contains an (unique) exterior vertex of
\mathcal{G}
, put it as the root, else choose any vertex as root. Start all the paths for the upper leaves of the tree: all these leaves are at the boundary of the controlled part
\omega
and we can start the “rivers” from there. Then simply extend the “river” paths downwards. When several rivers meet at a vertex, stop all of them except one. Obviously, we can cover all the tree up from its leaves to its root.

6
\Rightarrow
5. Assume that a “watershed” covers the uncontrolled part of
\mathcal{G}
that is that we have a finite number of disjoint “river” paths
(p_{i})
covering
\mathcal{G}\setminus\omega
. Each
(p_{i})
has at least one end in
\omega
and we use it to define a sense: the “upstream” of
p_{i}
must arrive at such a point, called the “source”, and the “downstream” sense is define the other way. If we start from an uncontrolled edge, it must belong to one of the rivers and we can start to move upstream. If we arrive at a “confluence” vertex, we can choose still follow the same river or choose any tributary river. In any case, we must still travel upstream because this vertex cannot be the source of the tributary rivers. By finiteness of the paths (they are disjoints in a finite graph), we must arrive at a source. This excludes the possibility of having a cycle in
\mathcal{G}\setminus\omega
and shows (i) of Definition
3.1
. To obtain (ii), we simply associate to any uncontrolled edge the first vertex reached when moving upstream. This mapping is injective because it is impossible to have two rivers flowing downstream from the same vertex (one must be stopping at it and, by assumption, it cannot be the source). Also notice that the image vertex cannot be an exterior vertex of
\mathcal{G}
because the followed river cannot start from here and so there must exist another edge to travel up.
∎

Theorem 4.1

Unobserved quasimodes with Dirichlet boundary conditions

The use of multiplier techniques developed to control the wave equation on a segment. This kind of methods has been introduced by Morawetz in

We underline that

is differentiable with respect to time in

, we have that

converges to

{H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}
when
\tau\rightarrow 0
. Up to extracting a subsequence, it must have a weak limit in this space. To identify this weak limit, we notice that, in any compact subinterval of any edge,

converges to
(\partial_{t}\varphi,\partial^{2}_{t}\varphi)(t=0)
at least in the sense of distributions. Thus, the previous weak limit must be

H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
. In other words,
\partial_{t}\varphi
is a solution of the free wave equation with the regularity
\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T],H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G}))
. Since the “invisibility" is obviously a conserved through the time derivative, we have that
\partial_{t}\varphi
also belongs to
\mathcal{N}
.

We just prove that
\partial_{t}
is a linear operator on
\mathcal{N}
. If
\mathcal{N}\neq\{0\}
, since
\mathcal{N}
is a finite-dimensional space, this operator must have at least one eigenvalue
\lambda
with a non-zero eigenfunction
\varphi
. It satisfies
\partial_{t}\varphi(x,t)=\lambda\varphi(x,t)
, implying
\varphi(x,t)=e^{\lambda t}\varphi(x)
with
\varphi(x):=\varphi(x,0)
. Assume first that
\lambda\neq 0
. Coming back to the wave equation and the invisibility, it comes that

(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+q(x))\varphi(x)=\lambda^{2}\varphi(x)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \varphi_{|\omega}\equiv 0.

(27)

We can argue as in Step 4: due to the structure of forest of
\mathcal{G}\setminus\omega
, the information that
\varphi\equiv 0
propagates everywhere. Indeed, if it holds on all the edges attached to a vertex, except one, then it implies
\varphi(0)=\partial_{x}\varphi(0)=0
at the end of this remaining edge. Then, the first equation of (

\varphi\equiv 0

The only remaining case is
\lambda=0
that is that
\varphi
is actually a stationary solution of the free wave equation and the observation of
\partial_{t}\varphi
is useless. It remains to remember that the suitable property has been assumed in the statement:

implies
\varphi\equiv 0
. In other words, there is no such non-zero stationary solution and we meet a final contradiction. This shows that
\mathcal{N}=\{0\}
.
∎

5
Control of the Schrödinger equation

In this section, we discuss the exact controllability of the Schrödinger equation in a graph
\mathcal{G}
, that is the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u-qu+h1_{\omega},&t>0,\\
u(t=0)=u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

(31)

with

and

5.1
The exact controllability under (GGCC)

Note that Theorem
1.9
in the introduction states that (GGCC) is sufficient to ensure the exact controllability for the Schrödinger equation. This fact is a direct consequence of the general principle that the controllability of the wave equation implies the controllability of the Schrödinger equation.

Proof Theorem
1.9
.

Assume that
\mathcal{G}
and
\omega
satisfy (GGCC). We set

where
u
is solution of (
31

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\partial_{t}w=-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}w-(q(x)-\|q\|_{\infty}-1)w+e^{-it(\|q\|_{\infty}+1)}h1_{\omega},&\ \ \ \ \ t>0,\\
w(t=0)=u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}).&\end{array}\right.

(32)

Observe that
0
does not belong to the spectrum of
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+q(x)-\|q\|_{\infty}-1
. So one can apply Theorem
1.5
to deduce that the associated controlled wave equation is exactly controllable for
T\geq L
, where

is the length appearing in Definition
1.1
. From

, Theorem 3.1]
, we then deduce that (
32

∎

5.2

Without loss of generality, we can assume that

The null-controllability under (GGCC)

Proof of Theorem

6.2
Study of a graph with at most polynomially approximable lengths

We just show that (GGCC) is a sufficient condition for the null-controllability of (

[

. However, in the remaining part of this section, we show that, in general, (GGCC) is not a necessary condition for the null-controllability of (

Theorem 6.1

(
[

)
.

Let
N\geq 3

\mathcal{G}

are at most polynomially approximable numbers. Then, the boundary control heat equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u,&t>0,\\

(51)

is small-time null-controllable, i.e. for every

.

Theorem
1.15
follows from the above result by using a general strategy passing from the boundary control result to an interior control result. Such a procedure has been widely used, see for instance

Proof of Theorem
1.15
.

Let us take
\hat{\omega}\subset\subset\omega
, and we consider
\hat{\mathcal{G}}=\mathcal{G}\setminus\overline{\hat{\omega}}

\hat{\mathcal{G}}
is composed of two connected graphs,
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
is a graph with the same structure as
\mathcal{G}
and
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
is a graph composed with only one edge. We also take
\theta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
and
\eta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,T])
such that

\theta\equiv 1\ \text{in}\ \mathcal{G}\setminus\omega,\ \theta=0\ \text{in}\ \overline{\hat{\omega}},\ \eta=1\ \text{in}\ [0,T/4]\ \text{and}\ \eta\equiv 0\ \text{in}\ [3T/4,T].

(52)

Let
U
be the solution to (
47

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\hat{u}_{1}=\Delta_{\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}}\hat{u}_{1},&t>0,\\

\hat{u}_{1}(t,v)=0,&t>0,\ v\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{ext}}\setminus\{\hat{v_{1}}\},\\
\hat{u}_{1}(t=0)=u_{0}1_{\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}},\ \hat{u}_{1}(t=T)=0.&\end{array}\right.

(53)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\hat{u}_{2}=\Delta_{\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{2}}\hat{u}_{2},&t>0,\\
\hat{u}_{2}(t,\hat{v_{2}})=h_{2}(t),&t>0,\\
\hat{u}_{2}(t,v)=0,&t>0,\ v\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{ext}}\setminus\{\hat{v_{2}}\},\\
\hat{u}_{2}(t=0)=u_{0}1_{\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{2}},\ \hat{u}_{2}(t=T)=0.&\end{array}\right.

(54)

To solve (

6.1

[

[

. Let us set

\hat{u}=\hat{u}_{1}+\hat{u}_{2},\ u=\theta(x)\hat{u}+(1-\theta(x))\eta(t)U.

(55)

Then

is a solution to (

h=(1-\theta)\eta^{\prime}(t)U+2\theta^{\prime}(x)(\hat{u}_{x}-\eta(t)U_{x})+\theta^{\prime\prime}(x)(\hat{u}-\eta(t)U),

By using the properties of

[0,T]\times\omega

Let us check now that

The first term in (

because the Laplacian

and

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
y\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],H^{-1}(0,\ell))\cap L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(0,\ell))
and
f\in L^{2}((0,T)\times(0,\ell))
be such that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}y-\partial_{x}^{2}y=f\ \text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times(0,\ell)),\qquad y(0,\cdot)=0\qquad\text{in}\ (0,\ell).

(57)

Then for any nonempty
\mathcal{O}\subset\subset(0,\ell)
, we have
y\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathcal{O}))
.

Proof.

The proof is rather standard and consists in considering
\widetilde{y}=y\chi
defined by
\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,\ell],[0,1])
such that
\chi(x)=1
for
x\in\mathcal{O}
and
\mathrm{supp}(\chi)\subset\subset(0,\ell)
. The function
\widetilde{y}
solves a parabolic equation as in (

. The result then follows from

which infers

\widetilde{y}\in\mathcal{C}\big([0,T],L^{2}(0,\ell)\big)\cap L^{2}\big(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(0,\ell)\big)\cap H^{1}\big(0,T;H^{-1}(0,\ell)\big).

This ends the proof.
∎

We come back to the proof of Theorem

and

\partial_{t}z_{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}z_{2}=-\eta^{\prime}(t)U\ \text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times(0,\ell_{2}^{\prime})),\qquad z_{2}(0,\cdot)=0\qquad\text{in}\ (0,\ell_{2}^{\prime}),

where
(0,\ell_{1}^{\prime})
denotes the controlled edge of
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
and
(0,\ell_{2}^{\prime})
denotes the (unique) edge of
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
. By using the property of the support of

7
Discussions

In this final section, we provide a detailed discussion on several key topics related to the control and observation of partial differential equations (PDEs) on graphs. Specifically, we explore the optimality of the observation and control time for the wave equation, examining the conditions under which minimal time control can be achieved. Additionally, we address the question of the (potential) minimal control time for the Schrödinger equation and investigate the necessity of the Graph Geometric Control Condition (GGCC) for ensuring controllability on a generic graph. Furthermore, we analyze the polynomial stability of the wave equation, providing insights into its long-term behavior under various conditions. Finally, we compare and contrast boundary control and internal control of PDEs on graphs, highlighting their respective advantages, limitations, and implications for different types of networked structures.

7.1
Optimality of the observation/control time for the wave equation

In this paper, we obtain the controllability of the wave equation as soon as (GGCC) holds and with any control time (strictly) larger than the length
L
appearing in Definition
1.1
. It is therefore natural to wonder if
L
is actually the
optimal
control time, that is the infimum of all the times
T
for which the control holds. We can answer shortly “no”. Indeed, let us consider a three edges star-graph equipped with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Assume we observe every edge only on the exterior side so that the unobserved part of the graph consists again in a three edges star-graph of lengths
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}>\ell_{3}
meeting at one vertex. This
\bot
-shaped graph is represented in Figure
5
.
For this graph, the optimal time for the observation of the free wave equation is

T^{*}=\max(2\ell_{2},\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}).

(58)

[formula]
[formula]
[formula]

Figure 5:
The unobserved part of the studied star-graph consists in three edges of lengths
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}>\ell_{3}
meeting at
x=y=0
.

To enlight the discussion, the proof of this fact is postpone in Appendix
C
.
Here, we simply notice that the optimal time for observation is not always the length
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
of the longest unobserved path, the sufficient time of Proposition
4.5
, neither the length
\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}
of the shortest unobserved path, or the length
\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
, the longest time needed for a wave to travel from one end of the unobserved part to another end.

Actually, this time
T^{*}
of (
58
) is the exactly the one introduced by Avdonin and Zhao in
[
13
]
: cover the uncontrolled part
\mathcal{G}\setminus\omega
of the graph by a watershed and minimize the length of the longest river (see the geometric description of Definition
3.2
), the optimal time is twice this length. The optimal watershed for the graph of Figure
5
is reached by a river covering the unobserved part of
e_{2}
and two rivers covering the unobserved part of
e_{1}\cup e_{3}
starting at each end this segment and meeting at the middle. This watershed provides a time
T^{*}
which is exactly the optimal time (
58
). In this sense, we can say that the method of Avdonin and Zhao in
[
13
]
is better than ours since it provides a better time. However, we notice that their method is purely based on d’Alembert’s formula and is less robust with respect to changes in the PDE. It is also noteworthy that Avdonin and Zhao themselves argue in
[
13
]
that their method is not optimal either. First notice that they state that there exist graphs for which their time is optimal, but they only provide examples, not a general fact, see Part 3 of Theorem 1 of
[
13
]
and its proof. They are also able to give an example where their method does not provide the optimal time for the “shape control”, that is the problem of controlling the shape function
u
only, no target being specified for
\partial_{t}u
, see Remark 1 of
[
13
]
.

To conclude this discussion, as far as we know, computing the optimal time for the observation/control of the wave equation on a general graph is still an open question. We know that our time coming from (GGCC) is not optimal in general and that the time provided by the watershed description of Avdonin and Zhao is always shorter. We also know that their description do not provide the optimal time for “shape control”. However, our experience on this topics leads us to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 7.1
.

The watershed description of Avdonin and Zhao in
[
13
]
provides the optimal time for the observation and control of the free wave equation on a graph.

7.2
On the (possible) minimal time for the Schrödinger equation

In Theorem
1.11
, we exhibit a specific configuration where the Schrödinger equation turns out to be exactly controllable for some

despite (GGCC) is not satisfied. A natural question would consist in investigating if there is indeed a minimal time of control or not. Actually, based on our experience on the subject of the internal control of linear Schrödinger equation where as far as we know, minimal time never appears, we conjecture the following.

Conjecture 7.2
.

Let us consider the X graph with four edges, two (say

and

) of length

(59)

is small-time exactly controllable in

.

One could hope to prove the above statement by exploiting the following general result, see
[

Theorem 7.3
.

Let

. If there exists

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}\leq C_{1}(|\lambda|^{-\varepsilon}\|\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u+\lambda u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\omega)})\qquad\forall\lambda\geq C_{2},\ \forall u\in D(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}).

7.3
On the necessity of (GGCC) for a generic graph

In this article, we have seen that (GGCC) is not always necessary to the control of PDEs, except for the wave equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Our examples are very specific and we may wonder whether they are representative of the general situation. In the following discussion, we consider that picking a “generic” graph consists of having a fixed graph structure and randomly choosing the lengths of the edges with respect to the Lebesgue measure, which is certainly equivalent to choosing both the lengths and the graph structure by any reasonable random process.

First, we consider the case of the wave equation with mixed boundary conditions. We know that (GGCC) is not necessary in general due to the specific example of Theorem
1.7
. This example is based on the pointwise observation result stated in
[
3
]
. Here, we can point a general intuition that controlling a graph without (GGCC) is very related to controlling at a single point the same PDE on a segment: the pointwise control of the wave equation in
(0,1)
fails for Dirichlet boundary condition (
[
33
]
) but may be possible for mixed boundary conditions (
[
3
]
). This is due to the fact that the modes
\cos\big(\frac{2n+1}{2}\pi\cdot\big)
of the later case always have value
1
at
x=p/q
with odd
p
. In
[
3
]
, it is shown that, if the pointwise control is acting at any other place, then the exact control fails. So, we expect that the situation of Theorem
1.7
can only occur for specific length ratios.

Conjecture 7.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph with
N
edges
(e_{i})_{i=1,\dots,N}
and
\omega
be an open subset of
\mathcal{G}
. Then, generically with respect to the lengths
(\ell_{i})_{i=1,\dots,N}
, (GGCC) is a necessary and sufficient condition for the internal control of the wave equation, whatever the boundary conditions are.

As with all the conjectures in this section, we leave the study to future works.

Concerning the Schrödinger equation on graphs, Theorem
1.11
shows that (GGCC) is not necessary for the internal control, the X graph with (very) specific lengths providing indeed a counterexample. But this situation also seems to be very particular due to the following result.

Theorem 7.5
.

\mathcal{G}
be a star-graph with
N

(

Let
\mathcal{G}

a\geq 0
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G})
and
\omega\subset\mathrm{supp}(a)
. More precisely, we obtain the existence of
C>0
such that, for all
(u_{0},u_{1})\in D(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}})\times H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})
,

\forall t\geq 0,\ \left\|(u(t),\partial_{t}u(t))\right\|_{H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}\left\|(u_{0},u_{1})\right\|_{D(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}})\times H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})}.

(63)

In the general case where (GGCC) fails, the damped wave equation with Dirichlet boundary condition is not uniformly stable but we can still expect to obtain a weak polynomial decay of the type (
63
) as soon as the lengths of the undamped edges have irrational ratio. As a guiding example, we recall the work
[
33
]
where the wave equation on
(0,1)
is damped at a single place with a Dirac damping
a(x)=\delta_{x=x_{0}}
. It is shown that the rate of decay depends on the Diophantine properties of
x_{0}
. Following the above discussion, we claim that the following results hold.

Proposition 7.10
.

Consider the damped wave equation on the X graph of Figure
2
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. If
\ell_{\text{t}}/\ell_{\text{b}}
is rational, then some solutions of (
62
) do not converge to zero. If
\ell_{\text{t}}/\ell_{\text{b}}
is a badly approximable number, then every solutions goes to zero and we have the estimate (
63
).

Conjecture 7.11
.

Consider the damped wave equation on the X graph of Figure
2
. If
\ell_{\text{t}}/\ell_{\text{b}}
is a at most
\sigma-
approximable number, then every solutions of (
62
) goes to zero and we have the estimate

\forall t\geq 0,\ \left\|(u(t),\partial_{t}u(t))\right\|_{H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}\leq\frac{C}{t^{\frac{1}{2}(\sigma-1)}}\left\|(u_{0},u_{1})\right\|_{D(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}})\times H^{1}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})}.

7.5
Comparison between boundary/internal control of PDEs on graphs

The goal of this part consists in comparing internal control results to boundary control results for the wave equation, the Schrödinger equation and the heat equation. In the context of boundary control, let us recall that (GGCC) means that
\mathcal{G}
is a tree, i.e.
\mathcal{G}
does not contain cycles, and that the control is efficient at all the exterior vertices except maybe one. In what follows, we use the notation
\mathcal{C}
for the controlled exterior vertices.

We begin by the wave equation. Let us recall the following result, due to Schmidt
[
52
]
, see also for instance
[
26
, Theorem 2.7]
.

Theorem 7.12

(Schmidt, 1992)
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a tree and the set
\mathcal{C}
contains all the exterior vertices, except at most one, then the wave equation is exactly controllable in the state space
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\times H^{-1}(\mathcal{G})
in any time
T\geq T^{*}
with boundary controls in
L^{2}(0,T)
, where
T^{*}
is twice the length of the largest simplest past connecting the uncontrolled vertices with the controlled ones.

The previous result, i.e. Theorem
7.12
is optimal in the following sense, see
[
26
, Section 6.3]
.

Theorem 7.13
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a tree and there exist at least two uncontrolled vertices, then the wave equation is not exactly controllable in the state space
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})\times H^{-1}(\mathcal{G})
whatever the time
T>0
is, with boundary controls in
L^{2}(0,T)
.

As a consequence of the two above results, we observe that as in the case of internal controls, (GGCC) is a necessary and sufficient condition for the exact controllability of the wave equation with boundary controls.

Let us now consider the Schrödinger equation. By using standard results that allow to pass from an exact controllability result for the wave equation to a small-time exact controllability result for the Schrödinger equation (see
[
42
]
), we have the following theorem.

Theorem 7.14
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a tree and the set
\mathcal{C}
contains all the exterior vertices, except at most one, then for every
T>0
the Schrödinger equation is exactly controllable at time
T
in the state space
H^{-1}(\mathcal{G})
with boundary controls in
L^{2}(0,T)
.

However, as in the context of internal controls, (GGCC) is not a necessary condition for the exact controllability of the Schrödinger equation with boundary controls. Indeed, we consider again the X graph: a star-graph with four edges, two (say
e_{1}=(0,v_{1})
and
e_{2}=(0,v_{2})
) of length
\ell_{\text{b}}>0
and two (
e_{3}=(0,v_{3})
and
e_{4}=(0,v_{4})
), of length
\ell_{\text{t}}>0
. The boundary controls act on the exterior vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{3}
. This geometry does not satisfy (GGCC) because the control is efficient at
2
exterior vertices while there are
4
exterior vertices.

Theorem 7.15
.

Consider the above geometric setting of the X graph
\mathcal{G}
. The Schrödinger equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}u,&\ \ \ \ t>0,\\
u(t,v_{1})=h_{1}(t),\ u(t,v_{3})=h_{3}(t),\ u(t,v_{2})=u(t,v_{4})=0,\\
u(t=0)=u_{0}\in H^{-1}(\mathcal{G}),&\end{array}\right.

is exactly controllable in
H^{-1}(\mathcal{G})
for some time
T>0
if and only if the ratio
\ell_{\text{t}}/\ell_{\text{b}}
is a badly approximable number.

The proof of Theorem
7.15
is an adaptation of the one of Theorem
1.11
, but for the sake of completeness we provide it in Appendix
D
.

We finally go back to the heat equation where the following result holds, as a consequence of Theorem
7.12
and
[
43
]
, that allows to pass from an exact controllability result for the wave equation to a small-time null-controllability result for the heat equation.

Theorem 7.16
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a tree and the set
\mathcal{C}
contains all the exterior vertices, except at most one, then for every
T>0
the heat equation is null-controllable at time
T
in the state space
H^{-1}(\mathcal{G})
with boundary controls in
L^{2}(0,T)
.

Note also that one can deduce Theorem
7.16
by using Theorem
1.13
and the general strategy passing from the internal control result to a boundary control result, see for instance
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
. By recalling Theorem
6.1
, i.e.
[
26
, Corollary 8.6]
that establishes the possibility of controlling the heat equation in arbitrary time in some specific star-shaped graph with only one boundary control, we have that (GGCC) is a sufficient but not necessary condition for the null-controllability of the heat equation with boundary controls. Therefore, the situation is analogue to the case of internal controls, see Theorem
1.13
and Theorem
1.15
.

Appendix A
Diophantine approximation

As we can see in this article, the speed with which a real number can be approximated by rational numbers can have a great influence in mathematical studies, particularly in control theory. Studying the speed of convergence of the rational approximation is the purpose of the theory of Diophantine approximations.
Based on the pigeonhole principle, Dirichlet proves in 1842 the following result.

Theorem A.1

(Dirichlet’s approximation theorem)
.

For any real number
\alpha
and any
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exist integers
p
and
q
with
1\leq q\leq N
, such that

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|<\frac{1}{qN}.

In particular, there exist sequences
(p_{n})
and
(q_{n})
of integers such that

q_{n}\xrightarrow[\penalty\ n\longrightarrow+\infty\penalty\ ]{}+\infty\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \left|\alpha-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|<\frac{1}{q_{n}^{2}}.

(64)

Indeed, if
\alpha=p/q
, we can choose
p_{n}=np
and
q_{n}=nq
and if
\alpha\not\in\mathbb{Q}
, we apply Theorem
A.1
with increasing
N
and notice that
1/N\leq 1/q
.
We recall that, if
\alpha
is irrational, the “best approximations"
p_{n}/q_{n}
are provided by the expansion in
continuous fraction

\alpha=a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{1}{a_{2}+\frac{1}{a_{3}+\frac{1}{a_{4}+\dots}}}}=:[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},\dots],

(65)

see for example
[
31
]
. The coefficients
(a_{n})
are called the
partial quotients
.

In the present article, we need the “simultaneous version” of Theorem
A.1
. This very classical result can be found in many textbooks, see for example
[
20
]
or
[
23
, Theorem VII]
.

Theorem A.2
.

For any real numbers
\alpha_{1}
,…,
\alpha_{d}
and any
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exist integers
p_{j}
and
q
with
1\leq q\leq N^{d}
, such that

\forall j=1,\ldots,d\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \left|\alpha_{j}-\frac{p_{j}}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{qN}.

(66)

Notice that (
66
) provides a simultaneous approximation of
d
numbers with an error of order
1/q_{n}^{1+1/d}
, generalizing the bound
1/q_{n}^{2}
of (
64
). We use in this article the slightly different version stated in Proposition
4.3
. This last result is an immediate corollary of the classical theorem.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

We apply Theorem
A.2
with the numbers
(\alpha_{i})
. If the resulting
q
is larger than
\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor
, Proposition
4.3
is proved. If not, we set
k=\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor
,
q^{\prime}=kq
and
p^{\prime}_{j}=kp_{j}
. Obviously,
\frac{p^{\prime}_{j}}{q^{\prime}}=\frac{p_{j}}{q}
,
q^{\prime}\geq\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor
and
q^{\prime}=\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor q\leq\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor^{2}\leq N^{d}
. It remains to notice that the bound in (
66
) becomes
\frac{1}{qN}=\frac{\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor}{q^{\prime}N}\leq\frac{1}{q^{\prime}\sqrt{N}}
.
∎

In the view of the bound (
64
) provided by Dirichlet’s approximation theorem, it is natural to study the speed of convergence of the rational approximations. Actually, in the present paper, we are rather interested in numbers with a bad approximation rate, which leads to the following definition.

Definition A.3
.

Let
\sigma>0
. We say that a number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is
at most
\sigma-
approximable
if there exists
C=C(\sigma)>0
such that, for all
(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\geq\frac{C}{q^{\sigma}}.

If
\alpha
is at most
\sigma-
approximable for some
\sigma>0
, we say that it is
at most polynomially approximable
.

The above definitions are not commonly accepted ones but they are convenient in our context. However, the special case of numbers that are “as far from rationals as possible" has a well-accepted denomination.

Definition A.4
.

A real number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is said to be
badly approximable
if there exists
C>0
such that, for all
(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\geq\frac{C}{q^{2}}.

In other words, badly approximable numbers are numbers that are at most
2
-approximable and due to the bound (
64
), this is the worst possible rate. In 1844, Liouville proves one of his most famous theorems in
[
40
]
.

Theorem A.5

(Liouville’s theorem on diophantine approximation)
.

If
\alpha
is an algebraic number of degree
d\geq 2
, that is that
\alpha
is an irrationnal number which is a root of a polynomial of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
of degree
d
, then there exists
C(\alpha)>0
such that

\forall p\in\mathbb{Z}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall q\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\geq\frac{C(\alpha)}{q^{d}}.

(67)

In particular, for
d=2
, this result implies that any algebraic number of degree two, as
\sqrt{2}
, is a badly approximable number. It also implies that any irrational algebraic number is at most polynomially approximable. Liouville uses this fact to construct the first example of transcendental number, the famous Liouville’s constant
\sum_{n\geq 1}{10^{-n!}}
. Later, the works of Thue, Siegel, Dyson and Roth show that the lower bound
C(\alpha)/q^{d}
of (
67
) can be replaced by
C(\alpha,\varepsilon)/q^{2+\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
, see
[
51
]
. However, it is still unknown in general if algebraic numbers of degrees other than
d=2
may be badly approximable numbers or not, even if numerical experiments suggest that this is not the case.
At least, it is known that the class of badly approximable numbers is larger than the set of algebraic numbers of degree
2
: it is actually the set of irrational numbers
\alpha
whose sequence of partial quotients
(a_{n})
, coming from the expansion in continued fraction (
65
), is bounded, see
[
35
, Theorem 6, p.24]
or
[
31
]
for example.

To conclude this brief and incomplete review, let us summarise some interesting facts to bear in mind when considering our results.

—

The algebraic numbers of degree
2
are examples of badly approximable numbers but there exist many other badly approximable numbers
. For example, it is sufficient to consider a sequence
(a_{n})
which is quasi-periodic but not periodic. Indeed, Lagrange has shown that algebraic numbers of degree
2
are exactly the irrational numbers such that the sequence
(a_{n})
is ultimately periodic, see for example
[
31
, Chapter 10]
. Surprisingly, there even exist numbers that are both transcendental and badly approximable, as
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}3^{-2^{n}}
, see
[
53
]
.

—

There exist examples of non-badly approximable numbers that are less artificial than Liouville’s constant
. Indeed, as shown by Euler himself in 1737, the expansion of “his" number in continued fractions is

e=[2;1,2,1,1,4,1,1,6,1,1,8,1,1,10,1,1,12,\dots].

Actually, the best rate of approximation of
e
is of order
\frac{\ln(\ln q)}{q^{2}\ln q}
as shown in
[
27
]
. In particular, notice that
e
is at most
\sigma-
approximable for any
\sigma>2
.
The case of
\pi
, on the other hand, seems still open.

—

Almost all numbers are not badly approximable but almost all are at most polynomially approximable
. Indeed, Borel and Bernstein have shown that the set of badly approximable numbers is a set of Lebesgue measure zero, see
[
31
, Chapter 11]
. On the contrary, Khinchin proves in 1926 that, as soon as
\sigma>2
, the set of numbers that are at most
\sigma-
approximable is of full Lebesgue measure, see
[
23
, p. 120]
.

Appendix C
Example of optimal time for the observation of the free waves

We consider here the particular case of Figure
5
. The start-graph
\mathcal{G}
has a
\bot
-shape, with an horizontal basis being the segment
(-1,1)
and a vertical edge of length
1
being attached at
x=0
. A solution of the wave equation on
\mathcal{G}
is decomposed in a function
x\mapsto u(x)
defined on the horizontal basis
(-1,1)
and a function
y\mapsto v(y)
in the vertical edge. The free wave equation is then given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}u(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}u(x,t)&x\in(-1,0)\cup(0,1),\,t>0,\\
\partial_{t}^{2}v(y,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}v(y,t)&y\in(0,1),\,t>,0\\
u(-1,t)=u(1,t)=v(1,t)=0&t>0,\\
u(0,t)=v(0,t)&t>0,\\
\partial_{x}u(0^{+},t)-\partial_{x}u(0^{-},t)+\partial_{y}v(0^{+},t)=0&t>0.\end{array}\right.

(68)

The observation of the solution is made in the set

\omega:=\{x\in(-1,-\ell_{1})\cup(\ell_{2},1)\}\,\cup\,\{y\in(\ell_{3},1)\}

so that the unobserved part consists in three edges of lengths
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{3}
meeting at one vertex. To fix the notation, we also assume that
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}>\ell_{3}
.
We underline that the specific shape of
\mathcal{G}
, three edges of the same length, will not be important in the analysis. The important shape is the unobserved part of the star-graph, so our discussion can be adapted to any graph having for unobserved part a forest of trees with three branches.

In this setting, we have a first result showing that the time

T^{*}:=\max(2\ell_{2},\ell_{1}+\ell_{3})

is necessary for the observation.

Proposition C.1
.

Let

be a time strictly less than

. Then, there exists a non-zero solution
\varphi:=(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T],H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{G}))
of (
68
) with

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\omega}|\partial_{t}\varphi(x,t)|^{2}\,{\text{\rm d}}x\,{\text{\rm d}}t=0.

Proof.

It is sufficient to use the laws of transmission of Appendix
B
and
the solutions of Figure
7
.

If
T
is strictly less than
2\ell_{2}
, we can consider a function
v\equiv 0
and
u(x,t)=h(x+t)-h(-x+t)
with
h
a small bump localised in
(\ell_{2}-\varepsilon,\ell_{2})
with small
\varepsilon>0
. This solution
\varphi
presents two symmetric bumps that travel horizontally. They will not enter the part
(-1,-\ell_{1})
nor
(\ell_{2},1)
before the time
2\ell_{2}-\varepsilon
.

Now, assume that
T
is strictly less than
\ell_{1}+\ell_{3}
. For small times

, we can consider a function
v\equiv 0
and
u(x,t)=h(x-t)
with

a small bump localised in
(-\ell_{1},-\ell_{1}+\varepsilon)
with small

. This solution

presents a bump travelling to the right until it reaches the vertex, at a time as close to

∎

The second result shows the optimality of

Proposition C.2
.

Let
T
be a time (strictly) larger than
T^{*}
. There exists a constant

such that, for all solution
\varphi=:(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,T],H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{G}))
of (

\|(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{G})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\omega}|\partial_{t}\varphi(x,t)|^{2}\,{\text{\rm d}}x\,{\text{\rm d}}t.

(69)

Proof.

The main trick is to split
u

u=u_{\text{e}}+u_{\text{o}}
of its even and odd part by setting

\forall x\in[-1,1]\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ u_{\text{e}}(x):=\frac{u(x)+u(-x)}{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ u_{\text{o}}(x):=\frac{u(x)-u(-x)}{2}.

We notice that
u_{\text{o}}
does not interact with

and that both
u_{\text{e}}
and
u_{\text{o}}
are determined by their values on
[-1,0]
or
[0,1]
only. We can split the problem in two equations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}u_{\text{o}}=\partial_{x}^{2}u_{\text{o}},&\text{ in }(0,1),\\

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}w=\partial_{x}^{2}w,&\text{ in }(-1,0)\cup(0,1),\\
w(-1,t)=w(1,t)=0,&t>0,\\
w(0^{-},t)=w(0^{+},t),&t>0,\\
2\partial_{x}w(0^{-},t)=\partial_{x}w(0^{+},t),&t>0,\end{array}\right.

with

w(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{\text{e}}(x),&\text{ if }x\in(-1,0),\\
v(x),&\text{ if }x\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

Next, we reproduce the strategy of the proof of Proposition
4.5
on each equation.
The only point where we have to be careful is the coefficient

in the transmission condition at

for

. We will explain why it is harmless. The other details of the proof will be skipped since they are the same as the ones of the proof of Proposition

.

First notice that, obviously, the energies

\|(\varphi,\partial_{t}\varphi)(t)\|_{H^{1}_{0}\times L^{2}}^{2}
and
