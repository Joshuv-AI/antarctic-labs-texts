Disorder-induced broadening of quantum momentum distribution
Vili Heinonen, Jani Lukkarinen

Abstract
We study the long-time behavior of a non-interacting two-dimensional quantum gas in a weak random potential with long-range correlations. Any peaked initial momentum distribution will eventually become isotropic and broaden due to scattering events with the random potential. We derive an expression for the long-time average of the momentum distribution and test it against computer simulations. We also discuss momentum isotropization and spatial diffusion.



I
Introduction

Whether studying the transport of charged particles
5
or light
23
, the presence of disorder plays a fundamental role in the physics of the system. Some of the hallmark disorder-induced phenomena include, e.g., Anderson
4
;
1
and weak localization
11
;
3
manifesting as inhibition (Anderson) or reduction (weak) of diffusion due to interference of multiply scattered waves. Similar mechanisms underlie the appearance of backward
12
and forward scattering peaks
13
in the momentum spectrum observed as sharply increased wave intensity in the opposite (backward) or same (forward) direction with the incident wave. Studying quantum transport in disordered environments has become experimentally accessible with the advent of cold atom physics revolutionizing experimental study of quantum transport.

One of the major problems in previously studied solid-state systems is that the particles are interacting
5
leading to significantly more complicated physics. Conversely, in cold atom clouds these interactions can be readily suppressed e.g. by using spin-polarized fermions
14
or by tuning the Feshback resonances via external magnetic fields
6
. In these systems the spatially disordered background can be realized via interactions with light obtained by reflecting a laser source with a rough plate
10
;
15
. Using additional confining optical potentials also allows studying systems in effective lower dimensions
19
. Furthermore, time series of momentum data can be accurately recorded in experiments by using e.g. momentum space through time-of-flight absorption imaging
17
.

In the non-interacting regime the momentum of a collection of particles is conserved in the absence of an external potential. Introducing a weak random potential at the energy scale
\epsilon
will induce momentum isotropization through scattering events
12
;
17
;
20
.
The linear Boltzmann equation governing this isotropization has been previously derived in the kinetic scaling limit
\epsilon\to 0

21
;
9
.
At this limit these scattering events are elastic preserving the energy of the incident wave. However, keeping
\epsilon
finite will induce a broadening of the momentum (kinetic energy) spectrum of the particle. In this Article we derive an expression for the momentum (kinetic energy) distribution assuming that the correlations in the random environment are long-range compared to the wavelength of the incident wave.

The Article is organized as follows: In Sec.
II
we introduce the underlying dynamics and describe the random environment, while Sec.
III
discusses ensemble averaged dynamics. The main results are presented in Sec.
IV
, where we derive the expression for momentum broadening as well as discuss isotropization. In Secs.
V
and
VI
we calculate the transport coefficients using methods described in
8
. Sec.
VII
concludes the Article and discusses some ramifications for thermalization in quantum mechanical systems.

II
The model

Consider the Schrödinger equation for non-interacting particles with mass
m

in a two-dimensional periodic domain of size
L\times L
. The system is initialized with a single wave packet
\psi(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp(i \vkcrit\cdot\vx)
with
|\mathbf{k}_{\text{p}}|=:k_{\text{p}}
with
1/k_{\text{p}}\ll L
. We choose the units s.t. the length scale is given by
x_{0}=1/k_{0}
, mass by
m_{0}=m
and time by
t_{0}^{-1}=\hbar k_{0}^{2}/m
, where
k_{0}
is a length scale comparable to
k_{\text{p}}
.
The dynamics for
\psi(t,\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbf{x}
-space are given by

i\partial_{t}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})+\epsilon V(\mathbf{x})\psi(t,\mathbf{x}),

(1)

where we take
V(\mathbf{x})
to be an isotropic homogeneous random field

V(\mathbf{x})=\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}

(2)

with zero mean (
\langle V(\mathbf{x})\rangle=0
). Throughout this Article, the angle brackets
\langle\cdot\rangle
denote the average over
realizations
of the random field
V
.

The potential strength is set by the parameter
\epsilon
, which is assumed to be small compared to the kinetic energy of the initial wave packet.
We define the 2-point covariance function

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})=\langle V(\mathbf{x})V(0)\rangle=\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}},

(3)

where

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}}=\left\langle|V_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\right\rangle

(4)

due to the homogeneity of
V
(See App.
A.2
).
We assume that
\mathcal{C}
describes long-range correlations peaking at
\mathbf{k}=0
and having a natural length scale
\zeta
for which
\zeta k_{\text{p}}\ll 1
. This corresponds to the assumption that the random potential
V
varies at large length scales compared to the wavelength of the initial wave packet. Furthermore, we assume that the potential is peaked at
\mathbf{k}=0
so that it is effectively described by a Gaussian

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}}\approx C_{\mathcal{C}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{2}k^{2}},

(5)

where the normalization constant
C_{\mathcal{C}}
is set by

\langle V(\mathbf{x})^{2}\rangle=\langle V(0)^{2}\rangle=\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}}=1.

(6)

In
\mathbf{k}
-space the dynamics Eq. (
1
) can be written as

\partial_{t}\psi_{\mathbf{k}}=-\frac{i}{2}k^{2}\psi_{\mathbf{k}}-i\epsilon\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\psi_{\mathbf{q}}.

(7)

We write the dynamics in the interaction picture by defining
\phi_{\mathbf{k}}={\psi}_{\mathbf{k}}\exp(i k^2 t/2)
. The time evolution for
\phi_{\mathbf{k}}
becomes

\partial_{t}\phi_{\mathbf{k}}=\epsilon\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}K_{kq}V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}},

(8)

where

\displaystyle K_{kq}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\exp(i t T_{kq}),

(9a)

\displaystyle T_{kq}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}-q^{2}}{2}.

(9b)

The interaction kernel
K
has the symmetries

K_{kq}(t)^{*}=-K_{kq}(-t)=-K_{qk}(t)

(10)

and the multiplication property

K_{kq}(t_{1})K_{kq}(t_{2})^{*}=iK_{kq}(t_{1}-t_{2}).

(11)

Note that
e^{-ik^{2}t/2}
is the
k
space representation
\bra{k}U_{\text{f}}(t)\ket{k}
of the time evolution operator
U_{\text{f}}
of the free particle.

We initialize the fields
\phi
with a random global phase factor
e^{iS}
, where
S
is uniformly distributed and independent of the potential
V
. The addition of this phase factor leaves the dynamics invariant and
implies that terms of the form

\left\langle V_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\ldots V_{\mathbf{k}_{n}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}_{1}}\ldots\phi_{\mathbf{q}_{m}}\right\rangle=0,

(12)

if the list
(\phi_{\mathbf{q}_{1}},\ldots,\phi_{\mathbf{q}_{m}})
does not contain an equal number of fields and their complex conjugates. In particular, we have
\langle\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=0
.

Dynamics of Eq. (
8
) leads to momentum isotropization as
t\to\infty
. Fig.
1
shows snapshots from simulations illustrating this process.

Averaged dynamics

n=\langle|\phi|^{2}\rangle

) showing momentum isotropization. In the first panel the brightest spot corresponds to the initial peak. Dynamics are averaged over 50 realizations and the parameters are
\epsilon=\frac{1}{32}k_{\text{p}}^{2}

In this Section we will look at the time evolution of the average particle number at
\mathbf{k}

n_{\mathbf{k}}:=\left\langle\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle,

(13)

which is given by

\partial_{t}n_{\mathbf{k}}=\langle\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\partial_{t}\phi_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle+\text{c.c.}=\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}2\epsilon\real\left[K_{kq}\left\langle V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}}\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle\right],

(14)

where
c.c.
stands for
complex conjugate
.
The time evolution of the triad on the RHS can be written as

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\left\langle V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}}\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle&=\epsilon\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{p}}\left[K_{qp}\left\langle V_{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}}\phi_{\mathbf{p}}V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle\right.\\
&\left.+K_{kp}^{*}\left\langle V^{*}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p}}\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{p}}V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}}\right\rangle\right].\end{split}

(15)

We expand the moments to cumulants on the RHS generating 4th and 2nd order cumulants (See App.
A.1
and Eq. (
107
) therein). There are no 3rd order cumulants in this expansion because the trailing 1st order cumulant (moment) is zero both for the coefficients of the field
\phi
and the potential
V
. This is a consequence of the homogeneity and zero average of
V
and the added phase degrees of freedom for
\phi
discussed earlier.
For the same reason 2nd order cumulants can be written as second order moments.

Assuming that the effect of the 4th order cumulants is negligible (small
\epsilon
expansion) gives a sum of products of 2nd order cumulants.
Eq. (
12
) implies that the 2nd order cumulants between the field and the potential are zero
i.e. the only terms that contribute are terms with two coefficients of the background potential and two coefficients of the field
\phi
. Now

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\left\langle V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}}\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle&=\epsilon\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{p}}\left[K_{qp}\left\langle V_{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}}V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi_{\mathbf{p}}\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle\right.\\
&\left.+K_{kp}^{*}\left\langle V_{\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}}V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{p}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}}\right\rangle\right]\end{split}

(16)

Here we have used the fact that
V
is a real field i.e.
{V}^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}={V}_{-\mathbf{k}}
. Using the homogeneity of the field
V
gives

\partial_{t}\left\langle V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}}\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle=\epsilon\left(n_{\mathbf{q}}-n_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}K_{kq}^{*},

(17)

where we have used
K_{qk}=-K_{kq}^{*}
. For more details, see App.
B
.

Integrating Eq. (
17
) and substituting into Eq. (
14
) gives

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}n_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=2\epsilon\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}\real\left[\left\langle V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\phi_{\mathbf{q}}\phi^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle_{t=0}K_{kq}(0)\right]\\
&+2\epsilon^{2}\real\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{t'}iK_{kq}(t-t^{\prime})\Delta n_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}}(t^{\prime}),\end{split}

(18)

where we used the addition property Eq. (
11
) of
K
and
\Delta n_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}}=n_{\mathbf{q}}-n_{\mathbf{k}}
.
We notice that at time
t=0
the coefficients
\phi_{\mathbf{k}}
are deterministic and
\langle V_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=0
implying that the first term is zero. Therefore, the truncated hierarchy leads to the the evolution equation

\partial_{t}n_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}2\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\cos(T_{kq} (t -t'))\Delta n_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}}(t^{\prime}).

(19)

IV
Long-time properties

In this section, we will study how the density
n
spreads on a ring in momentum-space around the initial momentum
k_{\text{p}}
. To this end, we will consider the continuum limit (
L\to\infty
) of the previous periodic system. The corresponding continuum fields
n(\mathbf{k})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k})
are subject to normalization

\displaystyle\int\differential{\vk}n(t,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=1,

(20a)

\displaystyle\int\differential{\vk}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=1.

(20b)

For the approximate covariance function this implies that

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2\pi}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{2}k^{2}}.

(21)

Explicitly, the above continuum limits are obtained from the corresponding lattice quantities via
n(t,\mathbf{k})=\lim_{L\to\infty}n_{\mathbf{k}}(t)/\Delta k^{2}
and

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k})=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}}/\Delta k^{2}
, where
\Delta k
is the discrete volume element
2\pi/L
. Per previous discussion, we assume an initial condition
n(0,\mathbf{k})=\delta(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}_{\text{p}})
, where
\delta
is the Dirac delta.

In the continuum description, Eq. (
19
) reads

\begin{split}\partial_{t}n(t,\mathbf{k})&=\int\differential{\vq}2\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\\
&\times\int_{0}^{t}\differential{t'}\cos(T(k,q) (t-t'))\Delta n(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k},t^{\prime}).\end{split}

(22)

Next, we take the Laplace transform

\mathcal{L}[n(t,\mathbf{k})](s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}te^{-st}n(t,\mathbf{k})

(23)

of Eq. (
22
).
On the LHS we use the property

\mathcal{L}[\partial_{t}n(t,\mathbf{k})](s)=\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{k})-n(t=0,\mathbf{k}),

(24)

where we define

\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{k}):=s\mathcal{L}[n(t,\mathbf{k})](s),

(25)

whereas on the RHS of Eq. (
22
) we use the convolution property

\mathcal{L}[f*g](s)=\mathcal{L}[f](s)\mathcal{L}[g](s),

(26)

where

(f*g)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\differential{t'}f(t-t^{\prime})g(t^{\prime})

is the convolution.
Since

\mathcal{L}\left[\cos(t T(k,q))\right](s)=\frac{s}{T(k,q)^{2}+s^{2}}\,,

we finally have

\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{k})-n(0,\mathbf{k})=\int\differential{\vq}\frac{2\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})}{T(k,q)^{2}+s^{2}}\Delta\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}),

(27)

where, again,
\Delta\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k})=\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{q})-\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{k})
.

The definition of
\bar{n}
is motivated by the property

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{k})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\differential{t}n(t,\mathbf{k}),

(28)

which holds in a distributional sense if
\int\differential{\vk}f(\mathbf{k})n(t,\mathbf{k})
is bounded for suitably nice test functions
f
.
Ultimately, this identity will be used to extract time averaged behavior of
n
.
Multiplying Eq. (
27
) by
s
and sending
s\to 0
gives 0 on the LHS and on the RHS we use the identity

\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{s}{s^{2}+x^{2}}=\pi\delta(x).

(29)

This gives the equation

\int\differential{\vq}\delta(T(k,q))\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\left(\bar{n}(0,\mathbf{q})-\bar{n}(0,\mathbf{k})\right)=0,

(30)

which, since
T(k,q)=\frac{k^{2}}{2}-\frac{q^{2}}{2}
and
\mathcal{C}
is positive and isotropic, is exactly the isotropy condition for
\bar{n}(0,\mathbf{k})
. This implies that the long-time dynamics Eq. (
22
) will eventually isotropize the field
n
.

IV.1
Momentum broadening

In order to capture the radial behavior of
\lim_{s\to 0}\bar{n}
,
we integrate Eq. (
27
) over the circle fixed by the radius
k
. We introduce radial variables

\bar{n}(s,k)=\int\differential{\vk'}\frac{\delta(k-k^{\prime})}{k}n(s,\mathbf{k}^{\prime})

(31)

and

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}(k,q)&=\int\differential{\vk'}\frac{\delta(k-k^{\prime})}{k}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q})\\
&=\int\differential{\vq'}\frac{\delta(q-q^{\prime})}{q}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}).\end{split}

(32)

The normalization conditions read
\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{k}k\bar{n}(s,k)=1
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{k}k\mathcal{C}(k,q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\mathcal{C}(k,q)=1
.
This form of
\mathcal{C}
is possible because
\mathcal{C}
is isotropic and is therefore a function of the squared modulus
|\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}|^{2}=k^{2}+q^{2}-2kq\cos\theta
, where
\theta
is the angle between
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{q}
. This implies also the symmetry
\mathcal{C}(k,q)=\mathcal{C}(q,k)
.
Upon integration, the LHS gives

\int\differential{\vk'}\frac{\delta(k-k^{\prime})}{k}(\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{k}^{\prime})-n(0,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}))=\bar{n}(s,k)-n_{0}(k),

(33)

where

n_{0}(k)=\frac{\delta(k-k_{\text{p}})}{k}.

(34)

On the RHS we have

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\int\differential{\vq'}\int\differential{\vk'}\frac{\delta(k-k^{\prime})}{k}\frac{\delta(q-q^{\prime})}{q}\frac{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}{s^{2}+T(k,q)^{2}}\\
&\times\left(\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{q}^{\prime})-\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right),\end{split}

where we added the identity
\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\delta(q-q^{\prime})/q=1
.
Calculating the integrals gives

\bar{n}(s,k)-n_{0}(k)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\frac{2\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}(k,q)}{s^{2}+T(k,q)^{2}}\Delta\bar{n}(s,q,k),

where
\Delta\bar{n}(s,q,k)=\bar{n}(s,q)-\bar{n}(s,k)
.

Sending
s\to 0
gives

\bar{n}(k)-n_{0}(k)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\frac{2\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}(k,q)}{T(k,q)^{2}}\left(\bar{n}(q)-\bar{n}(k)\right),

(35)

where we have adapted the notation
\bar{n}(s=0,k)=\bar{n}(k)
.

Changing into scaled energy variables
E=\frac{1}{2}k^{2}/\epsilon
,
E^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}q^{2}/\epsilon
we can write
T(k,q)=\epsilon(E-E^{\prime})
and expand the function
\bar{n}(q)=\bar{n}(E^{\prime})
around
E
giving

\bar{n}(E^{\prime})-\bar{n}(E)=(E^{\prime}-E)\partial_{E}\bar{n}(E)+\frac{1}{2}(E^{\prime}-E)^{2}\partial^{2}_{E}\bar{n}(E)+\ldots

Dividing by
T^{2}=\epsilon^{2}(E-E^{\prime})^{2}
leaves us with terms of the form

\frac{2}{(n+2)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\,\mathcal{C}(k,q)(E^{\prime}(q)-E)^{n}\partial^{n+2}_{E}\bar{n}(E),

(36)

with
n\geq-1

on the RHS of Eq. (
35
). Writing the terms with
E
and
E^{\prime}
in terms of
k
and
q
we have to evaluate

\frac{2\partial_{E}^{n+2}\bar{n}}{2^{n}(n+2)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\,\mathcal{C}(k,q)(q^{2}/\epsilon-k^{2}/\epsilon)^{n}.

Because the covariance function
\mathcal{C}
is scaled by
\zeta
we can write this in terms of scaled variables
Q=\zeta q
,
K=\zeta k
giving

\frac{1}{(n+2)!}\frac{2\partial_{E}^{n+2}\bar{n}}{(2\epsilon\zeta^{2})^{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{Q}Q\,\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,Q)(Q^{2}-K^{2})^{n},

(37)

where
\mathcal{C}_{\zeta}(k,q)=\mathcal{C}(k,q)
is the covariance function with the scaling written down explicitly i.e.
\mathcal{C}_{1}
does not depend on
\zeta
explicitly.

We define
P(K,Q)=(K+Q)\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,K+Q)
. We have to evaluate the magnitude of terms

\int_{-K}^{\infty}\differential{Q}P(K,Q)Q^{n}(2K+Q)^{n},

(38)

where, again,
n\geq-1
.
Notice that
\int_{-K}^{\infty}\differential{Q}P(K,Q)=1
since we had already integrated out the angle dependence. For this reason, this term will give a unity for
n=0
contributing a term
\partial_{E}^{2}\bar{n}
. Since
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})
is peaked at
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{q}
, it is safe to assume that the angle averaged version
\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,Q)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt'}\mathcal{C}_{1}(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{Q}(\theta^{\prime}))
is also peaked around
Q=K
and equivalently
P(K,Q)
is peaked around
Q=0
. For this reason, the function
P(K,Q)
gives a noticeable contribution only when
Q\ll K
. Therefore, we expand the term
(2K+Q)^{n}
. Since
P(K,Q)
is nearly an even function with respect to
Q
, we maintain the zeroth order term if
n
is even giving

(2K)^{n}\int_{-K}^{\infty}\differential{Q}P(K,Q)Q^{n}.

In case
n>0
is odd, the integral is approximated by the first term

n(2K)^{n-1}\int_{-K}^{\infty}\differential{Q}P(K,Q)Q^{n+1}.

The contribution of odd
n
terms is minimal compared to the even terms. This is seen by plugging in
K=\zeta k
in the prefactor, which shows that the prefactor has a smaller power of
\zeta
than the even
n
terms.

Considering only the even
n
terms, the truncation of the expansion of
\bar{n}
depends on the magnitude of

\frac{1}{(n+2)!}\frac{2k^{n}}{(\epsilon\zeta)^{n}}\int_{-K}^{\infty}\differential{Q}P(K,Q)Q^{n},

(39)

where we replaced
K=\zeta k
in the prefactor. Since
P(K,Q)
does not have explicit
\zeta
dependence, the scaling is fully contained in the prefactor.
It turns out that it suffices that
\epsilon\zeta\approx 1
: even for
n=2
the integral gives the variance that is close to unity but the prefactor has the factorial
1/4!=1/24
. For sufficiently peaked
P(K,Q)
the moments should not grow faster than the factorial. However, it should be kept in mind that the large value of
\zeta k
was needed earlier to ensure that the angle averaged
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is indeed peaked at
Q=K
. Since
n=0
contributed a unity, the only remaining term is
n=-1
corresponding to the singular integral

4\epsilon\zeta^{2}\int_{-K}^{\infty}\differential{Q}\frac{P(K,Q)}{Q(2K+Q)},

(40)

where we have retained the term
2K+Q
.

In order to evaluate the magnitude of the integral Eq. (
40
) we have to go back to the definition of

\begin{split}&\frac{P(K,Q)}{K+Q}=\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,K+Q)\\
&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}(Q^{2}+2K(K+Q)(1-\cos\theta)),\end{split}

(41)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is the scaled covariance function with the argument written in terms of the squared modulus i.e.
\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,Q)=\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}(|\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{Q}|^{2})
and
\theta
is the angle between
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{K}
. In order to correctly capture the scaling behavior we write this integral in terms of the circle line element
\mathrm{d}s=K\mathrm{d}\theta
having

\frac{P(K,Q)}{K+Q}=\frac{1}{K}\int_{-\pi K}^{\pi K}\differential{s}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}(Q^{2}+2K(K+Q)(1-\cos(s/K))),

Since
K
is assumed to be large, we expand the argument of the
\cos
and discard the small terms
Q/K
. We have

P(K,Q)=\frac{K+Q}{K}\tilde{P}(K,Q),

where

\tilde{P}(K,Q)\approx\int_{-\pi K}^{\pi K}\differential{s}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1}(Q^{2}+s^{2}).

\tilde{P}(K,Q)
is clearly minimized when
Q=0
and is an even function of
Q
. Plugging this back in Eq. (
40
) gives

4\epsilon\zeta^{2}\int_{-K}^{\infty}\differential{Q}\frac{\tilde{P}(K,Q)}{Q}\frac{K+Q}{K(2K+Q)}.

We expand the latter rational expression in
Q
giving

4\epsilon\zeta^{2}\int_{-K}^{\infty}\differential{Q}\frac{\tilde{P}(K,Q)}{Q}\frac{1}{2K}\left(1+\frac{Q}{2K}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{2}K^{-3}).

Since
\tilde{P}
is even function of
Q
, the first term gives no contribution.
\tilde{P}
still integrates approximately to unity resulting in a contribution

\frac{1}{2E}\partial_{E}\bar{n}(E).

(42)

The exact value of this term depends on the approximations we made but we will show that it does not matter for the solution to the lowest order in
\epsilon
as the assumption
E\gg 1
continues to apply. We have included the calculation for the approximate covariance function Eq. (
21
) in App.
E.1
and it turns out that in that case this term scales as
1/\zeta^{2}
.

We plug in our results in Eq. (
35
) giving

\bar{n}(\omega)-\epsilon^{2}\left(\bar{n}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)+\frac{1}{2\omega}\bar{n}^{\prime}(\omega)\right)=n_{0}(\omega),

(43)

where we have introduced the unscaled energy variable
\omega=\epsilon E=\frac{1}{2}k^{2}
. Here
n_{0}(\omega)=\delta(\omega-\omega_{\text{p}})
with
\omega_{\text{p}}=\frac{1}{2}k_{\text{p}}^{2}
and
\bar{n}
is subject to the normalization condition
\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\go}\bar{n}(\omega)=1
. Eq. (
43
) can be solved exactly by using solutions
\omega^{1/4}I_{1/4}(\omega/\epsilon)
and
\omega^{1/4}K_{1/4}(\omega/\epsilon)
(modified Bessel functions) to the homogeneous problem
n_{0}(\omega)\to 0
. However, since we have already assumed that
\omega\gg\epsilon
we will solve the homogeneous problem by plugging in an ansatz

\bar{n}(\omega)=e^{f(\omega)/\epsilon},

(44)

where

f(\omega)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{j}f_{j}(\omega)

(45)

is expanded in
\epsilon
. This gives an equation

f^{\prime}(\omega)^{2}+\epsilon f^{\prime\prime}(\omega)+\epsilon f^{\prime}(\omega)/(2\omega)=1.

(46)

To the zeroth order in
\epsilon
this reads

f_{0}^{\prime}(\omega)^{2}=1

(47)

solved by
f_{0}(\omega)=\pm\omega+c
(
c
is a constant of integration). Then the homogeneous solutions are
\exp(\pm\go/\eps)
, which actually solve the differential equation

\bar{n}(\omega)-\epsilon^{2}\bar{n}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\delta(\omega-\omega_{\text{p}}),

(48)

when
\omega\neq\omega_{\text{p}}
. This is the reason for dropping the term with the single derivative – it contributes to a correction in the exponential decay of the order
\mathcal{O}(1)
. The first order correction gives the homogeneous solution
\exp(\pm\go/ \eps)/\omega^{1/4}
, which corresponds to the asymptotic large argument approximation of the earlier solutions expressed in terms of the modified Bessel functions.

We solve the inhomogeneous problem Eq. (
48
) corresponding to the first term in the approximation discussed earlier. We take the Fourier transform giving

\hat{n}(\xi)+\epsilon^{2}\xi^{2}\hat{n}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\differential{\go}e^{-i\omega\xi}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{\text{p}}\right)=e^{-i\xi\omega_{\text{p}}},

(49)

which is solved by

\bar{n}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\differential{\eta}\frac{e^{i\frac{\eta}{\epsilon}(\omega-\omega_{\text{p}})}}{1+\eta^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\exp(-\frac{\left|\go- \ocrit\right|}{\eps}).

(50)

We can add any solution to the homogeneous equation
\bar{n}(\omega)-\epsilon^{2}\bar{n}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=0
to fix the normalization. The final solution is

\bar{n}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\exp(-\frac{\left|\go- \ocrit\right|}{\eps})+\exp(-\frac{\go+ \ocrit}{\eps})\right).

(51)

In terms of
k
this becomes

\bar{n}(k)=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\exp(-\frac{\left|k^2 - \kcrit^2 \right|}{2 \eps})+\exp(-\frac{k^2 + \kcrit^2 }{2\eps})\right).

(52)

However, since the integrated error in the normalization without the correction is of the order
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\f 1 2 \kcrit^2 \eps^{-1}))
, we have the approximate solution

\bar{n}(k;k_{\text{p}})=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\exp(-\frac{\left|k^2 - \kcrit^2\right|}{2\eps}).

(53)

IV.2
Angle dependence

In this Section we calculate the first order correction to the
s\to 0
results discussed in the previous Section. The main goal is to give a long-time description of how the field
n(\mathbf{k})
becomes isotropic.

We start by calculating the Fourier coefficients of Eq. (
27
) in the
\theta
variable of the polar coordinates
\mathbf{k}=k(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
,
\mathbf{q}=q(\cos\theta^{\prime},\sin\theta^{\prime})
. We write

\bar{n}_{m}(s,k)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{k}(\theta)).

(56)

On the LHS of Eq. (
27
) we have
\bar{n}_{m}(s,k)-n_{0,m}(k)
and the RHS gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt'}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}\frac{2\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}(k,q,\theta-\theta^{\prime})}{s^{2}+T(k,q)^{2}}\Delta\bar{n}(s,\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}).

Calculating the integrals and multiplying by
s
on both sides gives

\begin{split}s\bar{n}_{m}&(s,k)-sn_{0,m}(k)=2\pi\epsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}q\,\delta_{s}\left(T(k,q)\right)\\
&\times\left(\mathcal{C}_{m}(k,q)\bar{n}_{m}(s,q)-\mathcal{C}_{0}(k,q)\bar{n}_{m}(s,k)\right),\end{split}

(57)

where

\delta_{s}\left(T(k,q)\right)=\frac{s/\pi}{s^{2}+T(k,q)^{2}},

(58)

\mathcal{C}_{m}(k,q)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}\mathcal{C}(k,q,\theta),

(59)

and

n_{0,m}(k)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}n_{0}(\mathbf{k}(\theta)).

(60)

Next we assume that
s
is small and expand this equation in
s
writing

\bar{n}_{m}(s,k)=\bar{n}_{m}^{(0)}(k)+s\bar{n}_{m}^{(1)}(k)+\mathcal{O}(s^{2}).

(61)

In order to expand
\delta_{s}
, we write Eq. (
57
) in energy coordinates
\omega=\frac{1}{2}k^{2}
,
\omega^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}q^{2}
. We examine the functional

\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\go'}\delta_{s}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})f(\omega^{\prime},\omega),

(62)

where we have suppressed the
s
dependence of
f
.
The zeroth order term gives the Dirac delta
\delta_{s}(\omega)\to\delta(\omega)
,
while the first order term gives

\begin{split}\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\go'}&\left(\partial_{s}\frac{s}{s^{2}+(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}}\right)f(\omega^{\prime},\omega)\\
&=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\go'}\frac{f(\omega^{\prime},\omega)}{(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}}.\end{split}

(63)

A necessary condition for this integral to be finite (as a Cauchy principal value) is that
\lim_{\omega^{\prime}\to\omega}f(\omega^{\prime},\omega)=0
. To summarize, we have

\delta_{s}(\omega)=\delta(\omega)+\frac{s}{\pi\omega^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(s^{2}).

(64)

Plugging in the expansion Eq. (
61
) and the identity Eq. (
64
) above in Eq. (
57
) gives to zeroth order

\int\differential{q}q\delta\left(T(k,q)\right)\left(\mathcal{C}_{m}(k,q)\bar{n}_{m}^{(0)}(q)-\mathcal{C}_{0}(k,q)\bar{n}_{m}^{(0)}(k)\right)=0,

which evaluates to

\bar{n}_{m}^{(0)}(k)\left(\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)-\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)\right)=0,

(65)

where we use the notation
\mathcal{C}_{m}(k,k)\to\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)
.
Since
\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)<\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)
(see App.
D
) when
m>0
, this is true only if
\bar{n}_{m}^{(0)}(k)=0
when
m\neq 0
i.e. the field
\bar{n}
becomes isotropic as
s\to\infty
.

The first order equation reads

\begin{split}&\bar{n}_{m}^{(0)}(k)-n_{0,m}(k)=2\pi\epsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)-\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)\right)\bar{n}_{m}^{(1)}(k)\\
&+\int\differential{q}\frac{2\epsilon^{2}q}{T(k,q)^{2}}\left(\mathcal{C}_{m}(k,q)\bar{n}_{m}^{(0)}(q)-\mathcal{C}_{0}(k,q)\bar{n}_{m}^{(0)}(k)\right).\end{split}

(66)

Now if
m=0
, this gives the equation for the
s\to 0
limit discussed in Sec.
IV.1
, namely

\bar{n}_{0}^{(0)}(k)-n_{0,0}(k)=\int\differential{q}q\frac{2\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}_{0}(k,q)}{T(k,q)^{2}}\left(\bar{n}_{0}^{(0)}(q)-\bar{n}_{0}^{(0)}(k)\right).

If
m\neq 0
the first order correction is

\bar{n}_{m}^{(1)}(k)=\frac{n_{0,m}(k)}{2\pi\epsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)-\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)\right)}.

(67)

Next, we calculate
\mathcal{C}_{m}
for the approximate covariance Eq. (
21
):

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}\mathcal{C}(k,\theta)\\
&=\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}e^{-\zeta^{2}k^{2}\left(1-\cos\theta\right)}.\end{split}

(68)

Since
\zeta k\gg 1
, we can use Laplace’s method expanding the argument of the
\cos
giving

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)&\approx\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}e^{-\frac{\zeta^{2}k^{2}}{2}\theta^{2}}\\
&\approx\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}e^{-\frac{\zeta^{2}k^{2}}{2}\theta^{2}}.\end{split}

(69)

Replacing the limits
(-\pi,\pi)
with
(-\infty,\infty)
is justified if
\zeta k\gg 1
.
Calculating the Fourier transform results in

\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)\approx\frac{\zeta}{k\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{2\zeta^{2}k^{2}}}.

(70)

Plugging Eq. (
70
) in Eq. (
67
) gives

\bar{n}_{m}^{(1)}(k)=\frac{kn_{0,m}(k)}{\sqrt{2\pi}\zeta\epsilon^{2}\left(1-\exp(-\f 1 2 \frac{m^2}{\zeta^2 k^2})\right)}.

(71)

For small
m
we have

\bar{n}_{m}^{(1)}(k)=\frac{2k^{3}\zeta n_{0,m}(k)}{\sqrt{2\pi}\epsilon^{2}m^{2}}

while for large
m

\bar{n}_{m}^{(1)}(k)=\frac{kn_{0,m}(k)}{\sqrt{2\pi}\zeta\epsilon^{2}}.

We find that for the slowest modes to relax we must have

s\ll\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\zeta k^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}.

(72)

V
Boltzmann equation

The linear Boltzmann equation on the
k
-space ring has been derived rigorously
21
;
9
in the kinetic scaling limit
T=\epsilon^{2}t
,
X=\epsilon^{2}x
as
\epsilon\to 0
when the dimension
d\geq 2
. We shall rewrite the result here assuming the truncation of the cumulant hierarchy described earlier.
We start with Eq. (
27
) denoting the Laplace transform as
\tilde{n}
:

s\tilde{n}(s,\mathbf{k})-n_{0}(\mathbf{k})=\int\differential{\vq}\frac{2\epsilon^{2}s\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})}{s^{2}+T(k,q)^{2}}\Delta\tilde{n}(s,\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}).

(73)

We define the rescaled field in variables
S=s/\epsilon^{2}
,
T=\epsilon^{2}t
writing
N_{\epsilon}(T,\mathbf{k})=n(t,\mathbf{k})
. The Laplace transform becomes
\tilde{n}(s,\mathbf{k})=\tilde{N}_{\epsilon}(S,\mathbf{k})/\epsilon^{2}
. Since
s\tilde{n}=S\tilde{N}_{\epsilon}
, the rescaled equation reads

S\tilde{N}_{\epsilon}(S,\mathbf{k})-N_{0}(\mathbf{k})=\int\differential{\vq}\frac{2\epsilon^{2}S\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})}{\epsilon^{4}S^{2}+T(k,q)^{2}}\Delta\tilde{N}_{\epsilon}(S,\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}).

(74)

Letting
\epsilon\to 0
gives the previously discussed limit

\frac{\epsilon^{2}S}{\epsilon^{4}S^{2}+T(k,q)^{2}}\to\pi\delta\left(T(k,q)\right).

(75)

Calculating the inverse transformation gives

\partial_{t}n(t,\mathbf{k})=2\pi\epsilon^{2}\int\differential{\vq}\delta\left(T(k,q)\right)\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\Delta n(t,\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}).

(76)

in terms of the original field corresponding to the lowest order expansion in
\epsilon
.

Next we write Eq. (
76
) in polar coordinates
\mathbf{k}=k(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
,
\mathbf{q}=k(\cos\theta^{\prime},\sin\theta^{\prime})
on the ring
q=k
.
Taking the Fourier transform
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}
of Eq. (
76
) gives

\partial_{t}n_{m}(t,k)=2\pi\epsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)-\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)\right)n_{m}(t,k),

(77)

where we used the convolution theorem

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt'}f(\theta-\theta^{\prime})g(\theta^{\prime})=f_{m}g_{m}.

(78)

Plugging in the approximation Eq. (
70
) in Eq. (
77
) gives

\partial_{t}n_{m}(t,k)=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\zeta\epsilon^{2}}{k}\left(e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{2\zeta^{2}k^{2}}}-1\right)n_{m}(t,k).

(79)

The modes with small
m
decay according to

\partial_{t}n_{m}(t,k)=-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{k^{3}\zeta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}m^{2}n_{m}(t,k)

(80)

equivalent to the diffusion equation

\partial_{t}n(t,k,\theta)=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{k^{3}\zeta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}n(t,k,\theta).

(81)

The dissipation of large
m
modes defines a collision time-scale

\frac{1}{t_{\text{c}}}=2\pi\epsilon^{2}\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)\approx\sqrt{2\pi}\zeta\epsilon^{2}/k,

(82)

results that we already saw in the end of Sec.
IV.2
. Fig.
3
shows the latter decay rate obtained from simulating Eq. (
1
).

The slowest mode corresponding to
m=1
defines another, diffusive, time scale

\frac{1}{t_{\text{d}}}=2\pi\epsilon^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)-\mathcal{C}_{1}(k))\approx\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\zeta k^{3}}.

(83)

The time
t_{\text{d}}
is the time for a wave packet to forget its initial direction and is an important characteristic of the diffusive regime. Authors in Ref.
16
;

find the same relations for
t_{\text{c}}
and

expressed in terms of Fourier coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)
, albeit using notedly different methods.

VI
Diffusion equation

A Boltzmann equation can be derived in a similar way to Eq. (
76
) for the Wigner quasiprobability distribution function (Wigner function)
8

W(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\differential{\xi}e^{i\xi\cdot\mathbf{x}}\psi^{*}\left(\mathbf{k}-\frac{\xi}{2}\right)\psi\left(\mathbf{k}+\frac{\xi}{2}\right)

(84)

yielding

D_{t}F=LF,

(85)

where

\begin{split}LF(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})&=2\pi\epsilon^{2}\int\differential{\vq}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\delta(T(k,q))\\
&\times\left(F(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{q})-F(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})\right).\end{split}

(86)

Here,
F(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})=\langle W(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})\rangle
and
D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\mathbf{k}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}
is the advective derivative.
F
can be seen as a quasiprobability distribution for finding the particle at position
\mathbf{x}
with momentum
\mathbf{k}
. Importantly, it has the marginal
\int\differential{\vx}F(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})=n(t,\mathbf{k})
and we see that the linear Boltzmann equation Eq. (
76
) can be obtained by integrating Eq. (
85
) over
\mathbf{x}
. Let
\rho(t,\mathbf{x})=\int\differential{\vk}F(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})
be the probability (or number density) of finding a particle at
\mathbf{x}
. Authors of Ref.
8
showed that
\rho
obeys the diffusion equation

\partial_{t}\rho(t,\mathbf{x})=D\nabla^{2}\rho(t,\mathbf{x})

(87)

in the scaling limit
t\to\epsilon^{2+2\kappa}t
,
x\to\epsilon^{2+\kappa}x
with some small
\kappa>0
as
\epsilon\to 0
for dimensions larger than 2.

The diffusion constant
D
can be obtained using a probabilistic interpretation of Eq. (
85
), where

F(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})=\langle\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}_{t})\delta(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{K}_{t})\rangle_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{K}}

(88)

is the probability density function of stochastic variables
\mathbf{X}_{t}
and
\mathbf{K}_{t}
obeying the dynamics

\partial_{t}\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{K}_{t}

(89)

and the time evolution of the probability density
n(t,\mathbf{k})
of
\mathbf{K}_{t}
is determined by Eq. (
76
) i.e.

\partial_{t}n=Ln.

(90)

The dynamics of
\mathbf{K}
can be seen as a Poisson process on the circle with radius
k
and jump rate

\lambda(k)=2\pi\epsilon^{2}\bar{\mathcal{C}}(k)

(91)

and jump size probability distribution function

\bar{\mathcal{C}}(k)^{-1}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}),

(92)

where we have

\bar{\mathcal{C}}(k)=\int\differential{\vq}\delta(T(k,q))\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}).

(93)

As already pointed out in Ref.
8
, the diffusion constant
D
can be obtained using a Green-Kubo formula

D=\frac{1}{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{t}\langle\mathbf{K}(t)\cdot\mathbf{K}(0)\rangle,

(94)

where
d
is the dimension of the system and
\mathbf{K}
is a Markov process defined by Eq. (
90
), whose initial condition
\mathbf{K}(0)
is sampled from the equilibrium distribution of the said Markov process. Here, the diffusion tensor is isotropic due to the isotropy of
L
, which is ultimately implied by the isotropic properties of
V
.

We can calculate the diffusion constant in 2 dimensions using the polar coordinates
\mathbf{k}=k(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
. Since the equilibrium distribution of the momentum isotropization process is the uniform distribution on the circle with radius
k
, we get a particularly nice expression. In polar coordinates we have

\begin{split}Ln(t,k,\theta)&=2\pi\epsilon^{2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt'}\mathcal{C}(k,\theta-\theta^{\prime})\\
&\times\left(n(t,k,\theta^{\prime})-n(t,k,\theta)\right)\end{split}

(95)

and

D=\frac{k^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{t}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\differential{\gt_0}\int\differential{\gt}\cos(\gt- \gt_0)n(t,\theta),

(96)

where
n(t,\theta)
is the probability distribution with the initial condition
n_{0}(\theta)=\delta(\theta-\theta_{0})
. This can be expressed as
n(t)=e^{tL}n_{0}
, allowing the calculation of the time integral. We notice that
L
commutes with the shift operator
S(-\theta_{0})f(\theta)=f(\theta-\theta_{0})
giving

D=\frac{k^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{t}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}\cos(\gt)e^{tL}\delta(\theta).

(97)

Calculating the time integral results in

D=-\frac{k^{2}}{2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}\cos(\gt)L^{-1}\delta(\theta).

(98)

The operator
L
is diagonalized by the Fourier transform. We have the Fourier transform of
Ln

Ln_{m}(k)=\hat{L}_{m}(k)n_{m}(k),

(99)

where

\hat{L}_{m}(k)=2\pi\epsilon^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)-\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)).

(100)

Since
\mathcal{C}(k,\theta)
is an even function of
\theta
, Eq. (
98
) gives the Fourier transform

D=-\frac{k^{2}}{2\hat{L}_{1}(k)}=\frac{k^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon^{2}(\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)-\mathcal{C}_{1}(k))}.

(101)

Comparing with Eq. (
83
) we write

D=\frac{t_{\text{d}}k^{2}}{2},

(102)

which gives

D\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\zeta k^{5}}{\epsilon^{2}}

(103)

for the approximation Eq. (
5
). Although derived using Green’s function methods, the relationship Eq. (
102
) is also reported in Refs.
16
;
15
.

VII
Conclusion and discussion

We have derived an expression for the momentum broadening Eq. (
53
) in the long-time limit assuming that the potential energy scale
\epsilon
is small compared to the kinetic energy of the initial wave. Furthermore, we calculated the general expressions for the diffusion time scale and the diffusion constant by using a Boltzmann equation applicable for small
\epsilon
. The diffusive time scale was also obtained from a direct expansion in the long-time limit.

The results depend on the low-order truncation of the cumulant hierarchy. This truncation is expected to yield better results if
V
is a Gaussian free field.
However, no such assumption is needed for the results presented here as long as
\epsilon
is sufficiently small.

It is known that the two-dimensional system is localized for any finite
\epsilon
as the system size tends to infinity
1
. Moreover, interference effects are also expected to hinder spatial diffusion due to wave-interference effects. Authors in Refs.
16
;
15
introduce a localization length

\xi_{\text{loc}}(k)=kt_{\text{d}}e^{\pi D}\approx\frac{\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}k^{4}\zeta}{\epsilon^{2}}\exp(\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{\zeta k^5 }{\eps^2})

(104)

that should be comparable to the system size
L
in order to observe Anderson localization. We see immediately that in our case
\xi_{\text{loc}}
is extremely large implying that even weak localization effects are negligible.

The momentum broadening can be interpreted as a signature of the mixing mechanism that thermalizes the non-interacting gas. This interpretation is in the spirit of Eigenstate thermalization hypothesis
7
. Introducing the random potential
V
breaks the linear and angular momentum conservation of the free particles. However, since the gas is non-interacting we still expect to observe a kinetic energy close to that of the initial state. Calculating the expectation value

\Tr(O \hat\rho)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\mathbf{k}}\bra{k}O\ket{k},

(105)

for an operator
O
we see that it is in fact a microcanonical trace operation, where
n_{\mathbf{k}}
picks up exactly the energies within
\epsilon
from the initial kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}k_{\text{p}}^{2}
. Here
\hat{\rho}
is the density matrix and we have included
\mathcal{N}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\mathbf{k}}
in case
n
is not normalized to unity. Another way to see this result is that the
t\to\infty
limit gives exactly the thermalized state of one particle. The finite width
\epsilon
gives now information about the mixing mechanism needed to thermalize the system.

Appendix A
Mathematical preliminaries

A.1
Cumulants

Let
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n})
be a list
n
indices. We define the moment of a collection of stochastic variables as

\langle X^{I}\rangle=\left\langle\prod_{j\in I}X_{j}\right\rangle,

(106)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the average over the joint probability measure
\mu(X_{I})=\mu(X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},\ldots,X_{i_{n}})
. We will use the shorthand
X_{k^{*}}=X_{k}^{*}
, where the last term means the complex conjugate of
X_{k}
.

Cumulants of a list of stochastic variables
X_{I}
can be defined using the moments to cumulants formula

\langle X^{I}\rangle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(I)}\prod_{A\in\pi}\kappa(X_{A}).

(107)

This is the sum over all partitions
\pi
of the index set
I
, where the product is taken over different
clusters

A
in the partition
\pi
.

Example:

Let
I=(1,2,3)
. The partitions in this case would be
\{(1,2,3)\}
,
\{(1,2),(3)\}
,
\{(1,3),(2)\}
,
\{(2,3),(1)\}
, and
\{(1),(2),(3)\}
. This means that the sum in Eq. (
107
) would have 5 terms altogether.

The time evolution of cumulants can be written using the following formula:

\partial_{t}\kappa(X_{I})=\sum_{j\in I}\langle\partial_{t}X_{j}:X^{I\setminus j}:\rangle,

(108)

where
:X^{I\setminus j}:
is the Wick polynomial of the stochastic variables with the index
j
excluded. It suffices to know that Wick polynomials have the following property:

\langle X^{K}:X^{I}:\rangle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(I+K)}\prod_{J\in\pi}\chi(J\cap K\neq\emptyset)\kappa(X_{J}),

(109)

where
\chi
evaluates to 1 when its argument is true and to 0 otherwise. Here we use a notation in which
I+K
gives the concatenation of lists
I
and
K
. The meaning of Eq. (
109
) is that any partitions with clusters internal to
X^{I}
in the cluster expansion of the expectation value
\langle X^{K}:X^{I}:\rangle
are excluded.

Example:

For monomials
X^{K}=X_{0}
we have

\langle X_{0}:X^{I}:\rangle=\kappa(X_{0},X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},\ldots,X_{i_{N}})=\kappa(X_{0},X_{I}),

(110)

whereas
X^{K}=X_{1}X_{2}
gives

\begin{split}\langle X_{1}X_{2}:X^{I}:\rangle&=\kappa(X_{1},X_{2},X_{I})\\
&+\sum_{A\subset I}\kappa(X_{1},X_{A})\kappa(X_{2},X_{I\setminus A}),\end{split}

(111)

where the sum goes through all the subsets
A
of
I
including the empty set
\emptyset
.
We refer the reader to Ref.
18
for more details on Wick polynomials and their relation to cumulants.

A.2
Homogeneous isotropic random fields

We will be looking at properties of random field
\psi(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}(L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{d})
i.e. a box with sizes
L_{i}
with periodic boundaries. We write the Fourier coefficients by defining

\psi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\Lambda_{d}}\psi_{\mathbf{k}}\exp(i \vk\cdot\vx),

(112)

where
\Lambda_{d}
denotes the set of reciprocal lattice points

\Lambda_{d}=\left\{\left(n_{1}\Delta k_{1},\ldots,n_{d}\Delta k_{d}\right):\left(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d})\right\}

(113)

and
\Delta k_{i}=2\pi/L_{i}
.

We say that the field is
homogeneous
if

\langle\psi(\mathbf{x})\psi(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d}_{2})\ldots\psi(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d}_{n})\rangle=\mathcal{C}_{n}(\mathbf{d}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{n}),

(114)

i.e. the
n
-point covariance function
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is independent of
\mathbf{x}
.
This means that the spatial correlations do not depend on the base point
\mathbf{x}
i.e. the field is statistically translation invariant. Writing this in terms of the Fourier coefficients gives

\begin{split}&C_{n}(\mathbf{d}_{2},\mathbf{d}_{3},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{n})\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{j}\in\Lambda_{d}}\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\ldots\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{n}}\rangle e^{i(\mathbf{k}_{1}+\ldots+\mathbf{k}_{n})\cdot\mathbf{x}}e^{i(\mathbf{k}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{2}+\ldots+\mathbf{k}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{n})}.\end{split}

(115)

This is true for all
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{d}_{j}
if and only if the resonance condition

\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\ldots\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{n}}\rangle=0,\;\text{if}\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{k}_{j}\neq 0

(116)

holds. In case of complex fields we can let any of the fields in Eq. (
114
) be complex conjugated and the resulting resonance condition reads

\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}^{(s_{1})}\ldots\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{n}}^{(s_{n})}\rangle=0,\;\text{if}\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}\mathbf{k}_{j}\neq 0,

(117)

where

\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{j}}^{(s_{j})}=\begin{cases}\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{j}}&\text{if $s_{j}=1$};\\
\psi_{\mathbf{k}_{j}}^{*}&\text{if $s_{j}=-1$},\end{cases}

(118)

i.e. the sign in the resonance condition is flipped for the fields that have been complex conjugated.

Two very useful corollaries are

\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=0,

(119)

when
\mathbf{k}\neq 0
and

\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\psi_{\mathbf{q}}\rangle=0,

(120)

if
\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{q}
. The same resonance condition holds for cumulants. This can be seen by using the cumulants to moments formula

\kappa(\psi_{I})=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(I)}(|\pi|-1)!(-1)^{|\pi|-1}\prod_{A\in\pi}\langle\psi^{A}\rangle,

(121)

where the sum goes over all partitions
\mathcal{P}(I)
of
I
and
|\pi|
is the number of clusters in the partition
\pi
. If
\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in I}\mathbf{k}\neq 0
, it follows that for each term in the sum in Eq. (
121
), at least one
A\in\pi
has the property
\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in A}\mathbf{k}\neq 0
.

The homogeneous field
\psi
is isotropic if the
n
-point correlation function
C_{n}(\mathbf{d}_{2},\mathbf{d}_{3},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{n})
is independent of simultaneous rotations of the vectors
\mathbf{d}_{j}
. In particular it means that the two-point correlation function
C_{2}(\mathbf{d})
only depends on the modulus
|\mathbf{d}|
.

Appendix B
Cumulant expansion of the time evolution

The time evolution fields
\phi_{\mathbf{k}}
follow Eq. (
8
). However, in this section we will include the parameter
\epsilon
in the potential
V
as
\epsilon V\to V
. We will use the multi-index notation defined in Sec.
A.1
, where multi-indices are comprised of vectors in
\mathbf{k}
-space.
We allow vectors of the form
\mathbf{k}^{*}
, which is a shorthand defined by

\phi_{\mathbf{k}^{*}}:=\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}.

(122)

We use Eq. (
108
) to write down the time evolution for a generic cumulant with
I,J\neq\emptyset
as

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\kappa(\phi_{I},V_{J})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in I}\langle\partial_{t}\phi_{\mathbf{k}}:V^{J}\phi^{I\setminus\mathbf{k}}:\rangle\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in I}\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}K_{kq}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})}(t)\langle\phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})}V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})}:V^{J}\phi^{I\setminus\mathbf{k}}:\rangle,\end{split}

(123)

where, as before,
f^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})}
is complex conjugated only if
\phi_{\mathbf{k}}
is complex conjugated.
Using Eq. (
109
) gives

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\kappa(\phi_{I},V_{J})\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in I}\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}K^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})}_{kq}(t)\left[\kappa(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})},V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})},\phi_{I\setminus\mathbf{k}},V_{J})\right.\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K_{1}\subset I\setminus\mathbf{k},\\
K_{2}\subset J\end{subarray}}\left.\kappa(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})},\phi_{K_{1}},V_{K_{2}})\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})},\phi_{K_{1}^{c}},V_{K_{2}^{c}})\right],\end{split}

(124)

where
K_{i}^{c}
is the complement of the list
K_{i}
.
Next, we integrate Eq. (
124
) from
0
to
t
. Note that there are no correlations of
(\phi_{I},V_{J})
at time
t=0
. This gives

\begin{split}&\kappa(\phi_{I},V_{J})\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in I}\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{q}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t_{1}K_{kq}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})}(t_{1})\left[\kappa(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})},V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})},\phi_{I\setminus\mathbf{k}},V_{J})\right.\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K_{1}\subset I\setminus\mathbf{k},\\
K_{2}\subset J\end{subarray}}\left.\kappa(\phi_{\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})},\phi_{K_{1}},V_{K_{2}})\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}^{(s_{\mathbf{k}})},\phi_{K_{1}^{c}},V_{K_{2}^{c}})\right].\end{split}

(125)

We can use this equation to hierarchically expand any further cumulants. High order cumulants are generally small because
with every step of the expansion, a term
V
is added introducing another small factor
\epsilon
. The resonance condition will pick up exactly one term in the downward expansion. The upward cumulant hierarchy can be truncated due to the small coupling parameter
\epsilon
. For details we refer the reader to Ref.
8
and references therein.

In the following we will repeat the calculation to obtain the time evolution of
n_{\mathbf{k}}=\kappa(\phi_{\mathbf{k}},\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*})
. We should note that the truncation of the cumulant hierarchy works better for random potentials that have (near) Gaussian statistics. The reason for this is that if
V
is Gaussian random field,
\kappa(V_{I})=0
if the list
I
contains more than 2 elements. Consequently Gaussian random fields are fully characterized by the first two cumulants
\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}})
and
\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}},V_{\mathbf{q}})
. However, for a sufficiently small
\epsilon
the truncation of the hierarchy should work for random fields
V
that have reasonably small fluctuations
8
.

In order to calculate
\partial_{t}n_{\mathbf{k}}
we had to expand the cumulant
\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}},\phi_{\mathbf{q}},\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*})
(see Sec.
III
). Using Eq. (
125
) we have now
I=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}^{*})
and
J=(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})
. Discarding the upward expansion (higher order cumulants), leaves the downward expansion, where both lists
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are empty or have a single term. We see that
K_{1}
cannot be empty because of the added phase degree of freedom. Also, since
\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}})=0
,
K_{2}
has to be empty. Summing over the terms in
I
gives

\begin{split}&\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}},\phi_{\mathbf{q}},\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*})\\
&=\int_{0}^{t}\differential{t'}\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{p}}\left[K_{qp}\kappa(\phi_{\mathbf{p}},\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*})\right.\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}},V_{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}})\\
&\left.+K_{kp}^{*}\kappa(\phi_{\mathbf{p}}^{*},\phi_{\mathbf{q}})\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}},V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p}}^{*})\right]+\text{h.o.},\end{split}

(126)

where h.o. denotes the higher order cumulants that we will discard. We use the resonance condition Eq. (
116
) for the potential terms giving

\kappa(V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}},\phi_{\mathbf{q}},\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{*})\approx\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{t'}K_{kq}^{*}(t^{\prime})(n_{\mathbf{q}}-n_{\mathbf{k}}),

(127)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
17
) (we defined
V
in this section s.t. it includes
\epsilon
).

In this section we repeat the calculations in Sec.
IV.1
for the 2-dimensional Gaussian covariance function given by Eq. (
21
). We start by calculating the rescaled covariance function
\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,Q)
discussed in Sec.
IV.1
.

We can write Eq. (
32

\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,Q)=\frac{e^{-K^{2}/2-Q^{2}/2}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{KQ\cos\theta},

(128)

where
\theta
is the angle difference between
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. Calculating the integral gives

\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,Q)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}(K^{2}+Q^{2})}I_{0}(KQ),

(129)

where
I
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind. Since
Q\approx K
and
K=\zeta k
is assumed to be large, we can use an asymptotic approximation
2

I_{0}(KQ)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi KQ}}e^{KQ}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{KQ}\right)\right).

(130)

Plugging in the lowest order expansion in Eq. (
129
) gives

C_{1}(K,Q)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi KQ}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(Q-K)^{2}}.

(131)

In Sec.
IV.1
we defined the function

\begin{split}P(K,Q)&=(K+Q)\mathcal{C}_{1}(K,K+Q)\\
&=\sqrt{\frac{K+Q}{K}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}}.\end{split}

(132)

We will first evaluate the singular term in the series expansion (
37
) given by

4\epsilon\zeta^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{Q}\frac{P(K,Q)}{Q(2K+Q)},

(133)

where we have taken the lower limit to
-\infty
(
K\gg 1
).
We expand the function
appearing in front of the normalized Gaussian giving

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Q(2K+Q)}\sqrt{\frac{K+Q}{K}}&=\frac{1}{2KQ}-\frac{Q}{16K^{3}}\\
&+\frac{Q^{2}}{16K^{4}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{Q^{3}}{K^{5}}\right).\end{split}

(134)

Notice that the constant
Q
term is exactly zero in this expansion. Since the Gaussian

is an even function, only even powers of
Q
give a contribution. The lowest order contribution is

4\epsilon\zeta^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{Q}\frac{Q^{2}}{16K^{4}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}}=\frac{\epsilon}{4\zeta^{2}k^{4}}

(135)

showing that it scales with

and is therefore small.

Next we will show for the Gaussian covariance function that the expansion terms

are indeed small. To the lowest order we have

. We evaluate Eq. (
39
) giving

\frac{(n-1)!!}{(n+2)!}\frac{2k^{n}}{(\epsilon\zeta)^{n}}=\frac{1}{2^{n/2}\frac{n}{2}!(n+1)(n+2)}\frac{2k^{n}}{(\epsilon\zeta)^{n}}

(136)

for even

Appendix D
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is the largest Fourier coefficient

The function
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})=\mathcal{C}(k,q,\theta-\theta^{\prime})
is a function of the squared modulus
|\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}|^{2}=k^{2}+q^{2}+2\cos(\gt- \gt')
.
The Fourier coefficient
\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)
is defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}e^{-im\theta}\mathcal{C}(k,\theta)\\
&=2\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}\cos(m \gt)\mathcal{C}(k,\theta).\end{split}

(137)

The latter equality follows from the fact that
\mathcal{C}(k,\theta)
is an even function of
\theta
. Since
\mathcal{C}(k,\theta)\geq 0
, Hölder’s inequality gives

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{m}(k)&=2\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}\cos(m \gt)\mathcal{C}(k,\theta)\\
&<2\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential{\gt}|\mathcal{C}(k,\theta)|\sup_{x\in(0,\pi)}|\cos(m x)|\\
&=\mathcal{C}_{0}(k,q)\end{split}

(138)

for all
m>0
. The strict inequality follows from the fact that

Appendix E
Numerical methods

We solved the time and space discretized version Eq. (
1
) by using an operator splitting method introduced in
22
. We will use space and time units
x_{0}=2\pi/L
,
t_{0}=mx_{0}^{2}/\hbar
, where
L\times L
is the size of the periodic domain. We will use the same symbols for the coordinates throughout this Appendix with the understanding that they describe the system in these new units. In the new units the size of the periodic domain is
2\pi\times 2\pi
.

We used
N\times N
spatial points to describe the system and set the initial wave packet
\mathbf{k}_{\text{p}}=(k_{\text{p}},0)
. The spatial grid is then
\mathbf{x}=(j_{x}\Delta x,j_{y}\Delta x)
, where
\Delta x=2\pi/N
and
j_{x},j_{y}\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
. The Fourier domain is
(k_{x},k_{y})
, where
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
are integers between
\lfloor-\frac{N-1}{2}\rfloor
and
\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\rfloor
.
The time step was set to
\tau=1/k_{\text{p}}^{2}
, which is one half of the kinetic energy of the initial wave packet. We write the Hamiltonian of the system
H=T+\epsilon V
, where
T
is the kinetic energy operator with the
k
-space representation
T(k)=\frac{1}{2}k^{2}
. The time step is calculated as

\psi_{t+\tau}=e^{-i\tau K/2}e^{-i\tau\epsilon V}e^{-i\tau K/2}\psi_{t}.

(139)

The discrete Fourier transforms

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{F}f)(\mathbf{k})=\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}};

(140a)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{F}^{-1}f)(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}},

(140b)

are calculated using the Fast Fourier Transform.
The discretized version of Eq. (
139
) becomes

\hat{\psi}_{t+\tau}(\mathbf{k})=e^{-i\tau K/2}\mathcal{F}e^{-i\tau\epsilon V}\mathcal{F}^{-1}e^{-i\tau K/2}\hat{\psi}_{t}(\mathbf{k}).

(141)

The random field
V(\mathbf{x})
is generated in
k
-space with the help of the covariance function Eq. (
5
).
We then define the potential as

V(\mathbf{x})=d^{2}\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{k})}(X(\mathbf{k})+iY(\mathbf{k})),

(142)

where
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are discrete fields, whose elements are independently sampled from a normal distribution with zero mean and unit variance. The factor
N^{2}
is needed for proper normalization of
\langle V(x)^{2}\rangle
when using the transforms defined by Eq. (
140
) (remember that
\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{k})=1
).
