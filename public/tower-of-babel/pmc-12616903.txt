Development and validation of the third-party disability assessment scale for family caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly
Authors: Na Li, Yang Liao, Yongqing Zhang, Jiawen Qin, Yili Gao, Xiaoshan Rong, Hui Shi, Yanfen Gu, Haiping Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong-term caregiving burden can lead to multiple health issues among caregivers of disabled elderly patients, including impaired physical functioning, psychological distress, and restricted social roles. The World Health Organization (WHO) defined it as “third-party disability”, referring to the sustained negative impacts on caregivers who provide long-term care for patients. These negative impacts include impairments in physiological functions, social participation, and environmental adaptation. Based on that, this study developed and validated a specialized assessment tool to evaluate third-party disability among caregivers of elderly patients with post-stroke disability, aiming to provide scientific and precise measurement criteria for clinical practice.MethodsThis study is comprised of two phases. In the first phase, scale items were identified through literature review and semi-structured interviews. The content of items was revised by expert review and pre-surveys to generate a preliminary scale. In the second phase, data from 251 family caregivers of elderly patients with post-stroke disability were used. We conducted item analysis and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to refine scale items, k-means cluster analysis and discriminant analysis to refine scale score levels. Furthermore, we completed the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and reliability analysis of the refined scale with data from another 398 family caregivers.ResultsThe final developed scale consists of four dimensions (totally 26 items), which are mental functioning, physical functioning, daily living, and environmental adaptation. The scale demonstrates strong content validity, with item-level content validity indices (I-CVIs) ranging from 0.79 to 1.00 and a scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) of 0.93. The CFA demonstrates that the scale model fits well and has good factorial validity. The average variance extracted (AVE) of the scale model ranges from 0.57 to 0.70. Meanwhile, the correlation coefficients between different dimensions are all less than the square roots of their respective AVEs, indicating the scale model has good convergent validity and discriminant validity. The scale also demonstrates robust reliability, with Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.91, split-half reliability of 0.80, and test-retest reliability (assessed with a two-week interval) of 0.96.ConclusionsWhile the final developed scale demonstrates strong reliability and validity, further refinement is still necessary for the scale to perfectly serve as a reference for constructing targeted intervention measures.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04019-3.

Background

Stroke ranked as the second leading cause of death (after ischaemic heart disease) and the third leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), after ischaemic heart disease and neonatal disorders, among global non-communicable disorders (NCDs) [1]. The most recent global burden of disease study has estimated an almost doubling of disability-adjusted life-years, deaths, and cost due to stroke from 2020 to 2050 [1]. Among these cases, approximately 80% to 90% of strokes occur in adults aged ≥ 55 years [2]. China is a country with a high stroke burden, with approximately 2 million new stroke cases reported annually, among which elderly stroke patients account for about two-thirds of the total [3]. The number of elderly individuals with stroke is increasingly prevalent, posing a significant burden on both families and society [4].

Currently, 75% of stroke survivors experience residual functional impairments of varying severity, requiring complete or partial dependence on caregivers for activities of daily living [5]. Therefore, the functional impairments of disabled elderly individuals not only affect their own quality of life but also have multiple impacts on the mental health, physical functioning, and social life of their primary caregivers [4]. Specifically, these impacts manifest as concerns about the patient’s physical health, fear of stroke recurrence, helplessness in dealing with heavy caregiving tasks, and psychological distress caused by role transitions [4, 6]. Accurate and comprehensive assessment of the extent to which caregivers’ mental and physical well-being, activities, and participation are impaired due to caregiving for disabled elderly enables healthcare professionals to intervene early, thereby preventing further deterioration of their health status [7, 8].

Third-party disability, a term similar to caregiver burden, was first introduced by the WHO in 2001. It is defined as “the disability and functional status of family caregivers (e.g., third-party disability caused by the health condition of significant others)“(p. 251) [9]. This concept is grounded in the biopsychosocial model of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), and its primary practical value lies in its complementary role within the existing healthcare system: it offers clinicians a standardized framework to identify and evaluate role-induced functional limitations among caregivers, thereby enabling tailored extension of medical interventions from individual patients to the family system and facilitating effective connections to social support services—rather than establishing a novel diagnostic category [10, 11]. In the same year, to assess and describe an individual’s functional status across physical, psychological, and social dimensions, WHO issued the ICF [12]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that caregivers of patients with aphasia, tinnitus, and dysphagia all experience varying degrees of third-party disability [7, 8, 13]. This framework, based on the “biopsychosocial” model, serves as a theoretical framework and classification system for describing an individual’s health and related conditions. Considering the interactive influence mechanism between the ICF framework and third-party disability, for example, the “environmental factors” category within the ICF framework (e.g., family support, societal attitudes) can directly analyze how patients’ diseases alter family environments, leading to caregivers’ activity limitations (e.g., quitting work to care for patients) or reduced participation (e.g., social isolation). Therefore, researchers in this field have proposed that scholars can utilize the ICF framework to study third-party disability among caregivers of patients [11].

Currently, there is relatively limited research on third-party disability among family caregivers. Scholars often use the term “caregiver burden” to represent the caregiving stress experienced by family caregivers [4, 14]. However, the concept of third-party disability differs from caregiving stress. Caregiver burden arises from the mismatch between a caregiver’s responsibilities and their actual capabilities, manifesting in strained family relationships and stress [12]. In contrast, third-party disability refers to the disability and functional status of caregivers caused by the health conditions of significant others [9]. “caregiver burden” and “third-party disability” are interrelated concepts that capture distinct aspects of the caregiving experience, with the former referring to the subjective perception of caregiving stress and the latter, in contrast, describing the objective limitations in a caregiver’s functioning and participation resulting directly from their caregiving responsibilities (with reference to the ICF framework).

Currently, scholars frequently utilize the Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview (ZBI) to assess the caregiving stress experienced by family caregivers when caring for individuals with dementia, stroke, cancer, or other chronic conditions. The ZBI comprises two dimensions and 22 items, characterized by high reliability and validity, ease of use, and cultural sensitivity, making it one of the most widely used caregiver burden assessment scales [15]. Upon a detailed review of the ZBI items, it becomes evident that the scale focuses solely on two dimensions, primarily addressing the mental health status and daily lives of caregivers, while neglecting their physical health changes and environmental adaptability [11]. Compared to the ZBI, the third-party disability assessment scale, grounded in the ICF framework, constructs a theoretical model that comprehensively evaluates changes in caregivers’ physical functions, mental functions, activities of daily living, and environmental adaptability. This allows for the early identification of caregivers with third-party disability and timely provision of targeted support and interventions [16]. Given that the ZBI is currently the most widely applied and universally applicable scale for assessing caregiver burden among patient caregivers, this study selects the ZBI as a reference tool for the development of a new scale.

Based on the ICF framework, scholars from various countries have developed multiple third-party disability assessment tools with good measurement properties. For example, Australian scholars such as Scarinci et al. developed the Significant Other Scale for Hearing Disability based on the ICF framework, American scholars like Preminger et al. created the Hearing Impairment Impact-Significant Other Profile, New Zealand scholars including Grawburg et al. developed the Family Aphasia Measure of Life Impact, and Swiss scholars such as Beukes et al. created the Consequences of Tinnitus on Significant Others Questionnaire [17–20]. These scales not only accurately identify caregivers with severe third-party disability but also assist healthcare professionals in targeting populations to ensure priority support for individuals with higher levels of disability, thereby helping alleviate caregivers’ stress, improve their health status, and enhance their quality of life [7, 8, 21, 22]. Additionally, these scales can serve as evaluation tools for interventions such as online support programs, group rehabilitation plans, and online cognitive-behavioral therapy, enabling the optimization of intervention strategies and advancing practical research in the field of third-party disability [7, 23, 24]. However, the existing third-party disability assessment scales are designed for specific impairments such as aphasia, tinnitus, and hearing impairments, focusing on the specific impacts these impairments have on caregivers, such as communication difficulties and emotional stress. However, disabled elderly individuals with post-stroke disabilities may experience multidimensional functional impairments, making it difficult for a single assessment scale to comprehensively evaluate the overall burden of third-party disability on their family caregivers. Therefore, there is a need to develop an assessment tool specifically for third-party disability among family caregivers of disabled elderly individuals following stroke.

This study aims to develop and validate a standardized scale to assess third-party disability among family caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly. The resulting instrument is intended to serve as a foundational tool for identifying key domains of caregiver disability, with potential future applications in exploring associated factors and evaluating targeted interventions.

Design

The methodology of this study was conducted in two phases, aiming to develop the scale, based on the ICF framework (Fig. 1). In the first phase, based on the three dimensions of the ICF framework, items reflecting the third-party disability of caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly were identified through a literature review, semi-structured interviews, expert review, and a pilot survey, and a preliminary version of the scale was developed. Phases in the study

Phase 1: scale development

Conceptualization

Based on the ICF framework, relevant literature and assessment tools related to third-party disability among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly were systematically retrieved both domestically and internationally. This study aimed to understand the current research status of third-party disability among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly and provide a reference for developing an assessment scale for third-party disability in this population. This study adopted a combined search strategy using subject terms and free-text terms to systematically retrieve 11 databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang, etc., with a search time frame from database inception to January 5, 2024. For this study, the following types of literature were excluded: (1) duplicated publications; (2) conference papers; (3) studies lacking sufficient data for extraction; and (4) studies where access to the full text was unavailable. All literature was independently screened by two authors. If discrepancies arose, a third party with a senior academic title in nursing (nursing PhD) was invited to discuss and resolve the disagreements until consensus was reached. Data related to third-party disabilities among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly were extracted from the 18 included studies and categorized into the three components of the ICF framework (i.e., body functions and structures, activities and participation, environmental factors). Data extraction was conducted by the first author and reviewed by the second author.

Using purposive and maximum variation sampling methods, semi-structured interviews were conducted with caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly from the Neurological Rehabilitation Department of Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital and the Stroke Center of Dongfang Hospital affiliated with Tongji University from May to June 2024 until data saturation was achieved. Inclusion criteria for caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) caring for elderly ≥ 60 years diagnosed with stroke via cranial CT or MRI, with Barthel Index score < 100; (3) providing care for post-stroke disabled elderly for ≥ 6 months, with daily care duration ≥ 4 hours; (4) longest-term family caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly; (5) caregivers must have basic communication skills, be willing to participate in the study, and sign the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria: (1) caregivers with an employment relationship and remuneration for caregiving; (2) caregivers with visual, auditory, or language impairments; (3) caregivers with severe illnesses (e.g., cancer). All interviewers received training in qualitative research and phenomenological interviews. The caregivers were requested to sign the informed consent form prior to the interview. After the interviews, the first author was responsible for transcribing the audio recordings within 24 hours and categorizing and archiving the audio files, transcribed texts, background information forms, interview notes, and interview record sheets. During the data analysis phase, the researchers employed Nvivo 14.0 software in combination with Colaizzi’s 7-step analysis method to repeatedly review and code the interview data, ultimately extracting the research themes [25]. The transcription of the interview transcripts was conducted by the first author, with the second author, who possesses dialect proficiency, verifying the accuracy based on the audio recordings. Subsequently, the first and second authors independently performed the coding of the transcribed documents. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the third author. Ultimately, all team members collaboratively extracted the research themes and invited a nursing expert with a doctoral degree to evaluate and guide the research outcomes. To ensure the authenticity of the study, the authors returned the research results along with the transcribed documents to the participants for verification.

Finally, through literature review and qualitative research, items were extracted and subjected to extensive discussions within the research team (comprising 3 nursing managers with over 20 years of clinical experience, 2 specialist nurses with more than 8 years of rehabilitation nursing experience, and 2 nursing master’s degree holders with experience in scale development or Chinese adaptation) regarding the dimensions of the scale, the items, and the phrasing and wording of the questions.

Item generation

Content validity was assessed using the I-CVI and S-CVI to determine the scale’s consistency with its predefined measurement goals. The CVI is typically calculated based on evaluations by 3–5 experts. Generally, a higher CVI value indicates better content validity of the scale. It is commonly recommended that the CVI should be ≥ 0.75 [26].

Open-ended items were included to collect feedback on caregivers’ understanding of the scale items, their response experience, and the total time taken to complete the survey. Studies have shown that the sample size for a pilot survey should be 10 to 40 cases [27]. To investigate caregivers’ understanding of the scale items and their response experience, in order to identify potential issues and make necessary adjustments.

Phase 2: psychometric evaluation

Sample and data collection

In the second phase, researchers conducted a systematic evaluation of the psychometric properties (e.g., reliability and validity) of the scale based on data from caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly in two teaching hospitals with a total of 2,780 beds (Tongji University Affiliated Dongfang Hospital and Tongji University Affiliated Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital). The inclusion and exclusion criteria for study participants were consistent with those in the previous semi-structured interviews phase. The researchers recruited sample data from the two hospitals mentioned above through a convenience sampling method at two different stages: from July to September 2024 (subsample 1 was used for item analysis, EFA, k-means cluster analysis, and discriminant analysis of the scale) and from September to November 2024 (subsample 2 was used for CFA, convergent and reliability analysis of the scale). Eligible participants were caregivers of elderly people with post-stroke disability who met the inclusion criteria. It should be specifically noted that the sample data for EFA and CFA are independent of each other, with no overlap in terms of both the temporal dimension and the source of the samples.

For the stages of factor analysis and reliability and validity analysis, it is recommended that the ratio of sample size to the number of scale items be within the range of 5:1 to 10:1. Considering a 10% inefficiency rate, it is necessary to expand the sample size accordingly [28]. The initial version of the scale contained 34 items, and therefore, the minimum sample size required for the factor analysis stage was 187 cases. After factor analysis, the scale formed in this study included 26 items, and thus, the minimum sample size required for the reliability and validity testing stage was 143 cases. If the proportion of unanswered items in the scale exceeds 30%, the scale will be excluded. If it is below 30%, the average score of the other items in the same dimension will be used to replace the missing items when calculating the dimension scores.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tongji University Affiliated Dongfang Hospital, approval number: [2024] Res. Review No. (127). All participants voluntarily participated in this study and signed informed consent forms prior to its commencement. They were informed that the experimental data were de-identified using unique identifiers and stored in encrypted servers. Additionally, participants were aware that they could withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their rights.

Data analysis

Item analysis

Item analysis employed both the critical ratio method and the correlation coefficient method for item selection to enhance selection accuracy [29]. Any entry that does not meet the criteria in at least one screening method will be removed.

Exploratory factor analysis

Both EFA and CFA were performed to evaluate validity of the scale using IBM SPSS Amos 25.0 [30]. Factor extraction was performed via principal component analysis employing oblique rotation. When the KMO value exceeds 0.6, Bartlett’s test of sphericity is statistically significant (p < 0.05), and the measures of sampling adequacy (MSA) in the anti-image correlation matrix exceed 0.5, the data can be considered suitable for EFA [31]. To ensure the high stability of the factors, it is recommended to extract factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.00 (with CFA subsequently employed to verify the factor extraction appropriateness and prevent over-extraction), and the minimum factor loadings should be above 0.50, and there should be no cross-loadings [32, 33].

Confirmatory factor analysis

This study evaluated the fit of the CFA model to the data using subsample 2, employing multiple fit indices, including chi-square degrees of freedom (χ2/df ≤ 3), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR < 0.08), normed fit index (NFI > 0.90), incremental fit index (IFI > 0.90), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI > 0.90), and comparative fit index (CFI > 0.90) [34–36].

Convergent validity and discriminant validity

The convergent validity of the model was evaluated through the calculation of the AVE. Convergent validity is considered satisfactory when the AVE value for each factor exceeds 0.5. The square root of the AVE value represents the aggregation of the factor, whereas the correlation coefficient reflects the relationship between factors. Discriminant validity is deemed appropriate when the aggregation of a factor is substantially greater than the correlation coefficient between that factor and other factors, thereby demonstrating the scale’s discriminant validity [37].

Reliability

Given that internal consistency reliability is essentially the expected value of split-half reliability, split-half reliability offers unique complementary value to internal consistency reliability. Test-retest reliability reflects the long-term stability of the measurement tool, whereas split-half reliability captures the “cross-item consistency” within the test. These two types of reliability are complementary. In this study, the reliability of the scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficient, split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability. The Cronbach’s α coefficients greater than 0.70 indicate acceptable internal consistency [38]. Two weeks later, 55 caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly who had been previously marked were selected for re-measurement to evaluate the test-retest reliability of the scale. This study employed Pearson correlation coefficients to assess test-retest reliability, although the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) represents a more robust alternative, which is one of the limitations of this research. We considered that the split-half reliability and test-retest reliability should be at least 0.7 [39, 40].

K-means cluster analysis and discriminant analysis

Based on the strong inter-item correlations established during the scale development phase, this study employed total scores for K-means cluster analysis to achieve grade classification of the scale [41]. The research team referenced existing disability-related scales and divided the scale into three levels (low, moderate, and high). If the score differences between levels are statistically significant (p < 0.05), it indicates appropriate grading. Based on the cluster analysis results, discriminant analysis was conducted to calculate the correct classification rate, thereby validating the grading outcomes.

Results

Conceptualization

A total of 18 studies were included after a comprehensive review of the literature, including 13 English and 5 Chinese studies. A total of 27 caregivers were interviewed, with an average age of (62.48 ± 10.10) years, and the interviews averaged 30 minutes in duration.

Previous studies have shown that caregiving generally does not significantly affect the physical structures of caregivers, but has a greater impact on their mental functions. Additionally, the personal factors within the ICF framework have not been thoroughly categorized. Therefore, the authors further categorized the 56 extracted items into the four components of the ICF framework for third-party disability among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly (i.e., mental functions, physical functions, daily living, and environmental adaptation). After organizing and analyzing the interview data, four main themes were identified: (1) compromised physical and mental health of caregivers; (2) impact on caregivers’ own lives and family life; (3) lack of a support system for caregivers; and (4) caregivers’ inability to cope with the pressures of caregiving. Additionally, 25 relevant items related to third-party disability among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly individuals were extracted. After multiple revisions by the research team, a preliminary version of the third-party disability scale for caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly was developed, consisting of 4 dimensions (mental functions, physical functions, activities of daily living, and environmental adaptation) and a total of 37 items.

Item generation

This study invited 19 experts with 10 to 46 years of professional experience via email from June to July 2024, among whom 8 held doctoral degrees and 11 held master’s degrees. Among them, 10 hold associate senior titles and 9 hold full senior titles. Their professional expertise spans across various fields: 9 in stroke nursing, 3 in rehabilitation medicine, 3 in geriatric nursing, 2 in stroke medicine, 1 in psychology, and 1 in home healthcare environment design. The 34 items of the scale were rated for importance using a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 = “Very Unimportant” to 5 = “Very Important”). The items of the scale were developed after expert review, retained the original semantics and were developed by taking into account the caregivers’ cognitive abilities, thinking patterns, and language habits. Participants rate 26 items on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (Never) to 4 (Always), with higher scores indicating greater disability. The I-CVI was calculated by averaging the scores assigned by experts to individual items, with results ranging from 0.79 to 1.00. The S-CVI was then obtained by averaging the I-CVI values of all items, yielding a result of 0.93. In addition, following expert review, 3 items were deleted and 13 items were modified. Based on feedback from caregivers, several items were revised to ensure clarity and simplicity of expression. Testing revealed a completion time of (6.21 ± 1.23) minutes, with no modifications made to the scale items.

This study was conducted in July 2024 at the Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital affiliated with Tongji University and Dongfang Hospital, employing a convenience sampling method. Respondents were first informed about the purpose and significance of the survey. After obtaining their consent, a pilot survey was conducted. If consent was not obtained, the next potential respondent was approached. Respondents who participated in the pilot survey did not participate in the subsequent formal study.

Characteristics of the sample

Item analysis

(1) Critical Ratio Method: Items were deleted if there was no statistically significant difference between the high-performing group (top 27%) and the low-performing group (bottom 27%). (2) Correlation Coefficient Method: This method was used to assess the homogeneity among the items of the scale. Deletion criteria include: correlation coefficients less than 0.3, correlation coefficients greater than 0.85, or non-significant correlation coefficients (i.e., p > 0.05) [42]. The results of the Critical Ratio Method indicated that the decision values for 2 items were < 3, which did not meet the retention criteria and were therefore deleted. The results of the Correlation Coefficient Method showed that the correlation coefficients for 3 items were < 0.3, which also did not meet the retention criteria and were deleted. In summary, after item analysis, a total of 3 items were deleted, leaving 31 items remaining.

Exploratory factor analysis

The results of the first EFA indicated a KMO value of 0.84, a Bartlett’s sphericity test showed significant sphericity (χ2 = 5,543.03, p < 0.001), and MSA values ranging from 0.70 to 0.92 for the items, indicating suitability for factor analysis. The analysis revealed 6 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, accounting for 68.32% of the cumulative variance. Items with factor loadings < 0.5 on Factor 1 and Factor 6 were deleted, and Factor 5, which contained only 2 items, was also deleted. A total of 4 factors were extracted, and a second factor analysis was conducted on the remaining 26 items.

The results of the secondary analysis indicated: KMO measure of sampling adequacy was 0.81, Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2 = 3,889.24, p < 0.001), and the MSA values ranged from 0.68 to 0.93. The cumulative explained variance was 60.17%. All items had factor loadings > 0.50 on their respective factors. Specifically, factor 1 had loadings ranging from 0.53 to 0.84, factor 2 from 0.64 to 0.86, factor 3 from 0.63 to 0.83, and factor 4 from 0.60 to 0.88. Detailed information is provided in Appendix A.

Discussion

Given the high prevalence and severity of third-party disability among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly, the development of relevant assessment tools is essential for accurately evaluating the level of third-party disability and providing a scientific measurement tool for validating the effects of subsequent interventions [7, 8]. In the context of nursing and rehabilitation, this study developed the scale based on the ICF framework. The final scale (Appendix B) consists of four dimensions and a total of 26 items, including 6 items for mental functions, 6 items for physical functions, 7 items for daily living, and 7 items for environmental adaptability.

The seamless integration of theoretical frameworks with scale dimensions is critical for scale development [43]. This study conducted a comprehensive literature review to understand the current status of research on disability from a third-party perspective and the methodologies used in the development of existing assessment tools. Building upon similar approaches to scale development, specifically linking the ICF framework to scale items and categorizing them, the research team formed an item pool through discussions, integrations, and classifications, ensuring the scientific and reliable nature of the scale items [7, 18]. Among post-stroke disabled elderly individuals, functional impairments such as motor, sensory, speech, cognitive, and swallowing disabilities are often observed to varying degrees. For most families of moderately to severely disabled elderly individuals, constant and close care for the patient is required. Compared to families of patients with single function impairments (e.g., aphasia, tinnitus, or swallowing disorders), the significant caregiving burden associated with post-stroke disability has a severe impact on the mental and physical well-being and daily lives of the caregivers [8, 13, 19, 44]. Therefore, this study comprehensively analyzed the detailed categorizations at various levels of physical functions, activities and participation, and environmental factors among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly, based on the ICF framework. The aim was to comprehensively assess the adverse effects of stroke-related disabilities on various aspects of caregivers’ functions. Consequently, the scale developed in this study addresses the limitations of existing scales, which only assess caregivers’ mental health and daily living, by enabling a comprehensive evaluation of changes in various aspects of caregivers’ functions.

Through semi-structured interviews with caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly, we gained an in-depth understanding of their third-party disability and further refined the item pool, ultimately developing the initial scale. Subsequently, 19 experts were invited to revise and improve the scale items. Through a pre-survey of 37 caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly, we gained insights into the respondents’ experiences and perceptions of completing the scale. Notably, the development of this scale incorporated semi-structured interviews and pre-survey feedback, ensuring that it reflects the genuine experiences and perceptions of caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly.

This study employed item analysis, and EFA established a four-factor structure for the scale. CFA was used to test the multidimensional and interrelated construct of “third-party disability,” and the model fit indices indicated that the scale model demonstrated good fit with the data, suggesting that the scale has ideal factorial validity. Currently, most caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly face severe third-party disabilities, including psychological, physical, and social participation issues; however, there is a lack of corresponding assessment tools. The scale developed in this study can effectively assess the level of third-party disabilities among family caregivers of disabled elderly, thereby timely understanding their health status, promoting the rational allocation of social resources, and providing data support for policy-making [4, 6]. Additionally, this study utilized K-means clustering analysis and discriminant analysis to classify scale scores, which helps medical staff rapidly identify the degree of third-party disability among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly individuals and implement corresponding intervention measures. Additionally, this study employed K-means cluster analysis and discriminant analysis for grading scale scores, enabling healthcare professionals to quickly identify third-party disability levels among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly individuals and implement interventions such as web-based support program, group audiological rehabilitation, and Internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy [7, 23, 24].

However, this study has certain limitations. This study utilized a convenience sampling method to collect data for the psychometric properties of the scale, resulting in a lack of diversity in the sample and overlooking other potential respondents, which limits the generalizability of the research. Therefore, although the four-factor structure of this scale demonstrates good stability and reproducibility, the generalization of the research results should be approached with caution. While this study determined the scale items by understanding the true caregiving experiences of caregivers through interviews, some items may be related to multiple dimensions, making it challenging to accurately categorize them into specific dimensions. For example, items in the physical functioning dimension, such as caregivers’ perception of fatigue, posed challenges for researchers when attempting to classify them under either the mental functioning or physical functioning dimension. This is because, during interviews, it was found that caregivers’ fatigue includes both mental and physical fatigue, leading researchers to have reservations during the categorization process. Finally, the researchers linked the classification decisions to ICF definitions and expert consensus to provide a clearer basis for item categorization, such as classifying fatigue under physical functions. Similar considerations apply to items related to changes in caregivers’ weight [13, 45]. Therefore, it is recommended that future research design measurement items to distinguish between mental fatigue and physical fatigue, aiming to address the classification challenges. Regarding long-term care insurance, medical assistance policies, and caregiver leave, these were not included in the scale because caregiving policies for caregivers vary across different countries and regions due to differences in local economic levels, social systems, and cultural backgrounds. Therefore, when applying this scale, the environmental adaptation dimension should be revised according to local caregiver policies. Furthermore, this study did not assess the criterion validity of the scale. Future studies could consider conducting correlation analysis, factor analysis, and the construction of structural equation models to evaluate the consistency and complementarity between the newly developed scale and existing tools, such as the ZBI and Caregiver Quality of Life Scale. This would provide stronger evidence for the reliability and validity of the scale. Furthermore, although the scale developed in this study aims to provide a more comprehensive assessment of third-party disability among family caregivers of older adults with post-stroke disabilities, it should be noted that its validity specifically among caregivers of patients with comorbid severe impairments, such as concurrent aphasia and dysphagia, requires further verification in future studies. The sample of this study was limited to two hospitals in Shanghai, China, and cultural factors such as caregiving norms, policy differences, and the accessibility of barrier-free facilities may vary significantly due to cultural differences, potentially affecting the reliability, validity, applicability, and cross-cultural comparability of the scale. Given the anticipated wide applicability of the scale, it is recommended that future research conduct adjustments or validation studies in diverse cultural contexts.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, this study is the first to develop an assessment scale for evaluating third-party disability among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly based on the ICF framework. The newly developed scale consists of 26 items specifically designed for caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly, assessing changes in their mental functions, physical functions, daily living, and environmental adaptability, from internal to external aspects. The study demonstrates that the newly developed scale has satisfactory reliability and validity. However, further refinement and development of the scale are needed to make it a precise tool for assessing the level of third-party disability among caregivers of post-stroke disabled elderly, and to provide references for developing targeted intervention measures and evaluating the effectiveness of such measures.
