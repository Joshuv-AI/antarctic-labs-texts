FXR-mediated antigen-specific CD8+ T cell enhances antitumor immunity in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Shanshan Liu, Zhixing Liang, Lisi Zhu, Yuhao Zheng, Haoyuan Yu, Shusen Ye, Texi Liang, Linzi Huang, Jiayi Ouyang, Yingjiao Cao, Lilin Ye, Linsen Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) offers limited opportunities for surgical treatment, and advanced-stage patients exhibit poor responses to immunotherapy. Therefore, the exploration of new therapeutic strategies is of paramount importance. The farnesoid X receptor (FXR) is a nuclear receptor that has been reported to regulate immune cells in recent years. However, whether FXR can regulate CD8+ T cells to affect tumor development remains unknown.MethodsThe function of FXR in CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity was assessed using spontaneous murine ICC models in systemic Nr1h4-knockout, and T cell-specific conditional knockout mice, with immune phenotyping performed by multicolor flow cytometry. The tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T response was tracked by tetramer staining and adoptive transfer of OT-1 T cells. The regulatory mechanism of FXR was confirmed using RNA sequencing, Chromatin Immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), Chromatin Immunoprecipitation polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-PCR), and luciferase reporter assays. The translational therapeutic potential was evaluated by administration of the FXR inhibitor ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), both as monotherapy and in combination with anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) blockade, in murine models and ex vivo using patient-derived tumor fragments.ResultsFXR was specifically overexpressed in exhausted CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in both human and murine ICC. FXR ablation enhanced CD8+ T-cell effector function, proliferation, and stem-like progenitor capacity, leading to potent tumor control. Mechanistically, FXR transcriptionally upregulated the exhaustion marker LAG3 by directly binding to its promoter. Pharmacological inhibition of FXR with UDCA reversed T-cell exhaustion, and synergized with anti-PD-L1 therapy to significantly suppress tumor growth and enhance tumor cell apoptosis in murine models and human ICC ex vivo cultures.ConclusionsTogether, these data identify FXR as an immune checkpoint and support repurposing UDCA for ICC immunotherapy.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is the second most common primary liver malignancy,1 which arises from cholangiocytes or epithelial cells lining the biliary tree. ICC is characterized by a poor prognosis, aggressive behavior, and a low survival rate. Although the incidence of ICC is relatively low, a sharp increase has been observed in recent years.2 Currently, curative resection remains the cornerstone of ICC treatment. However, 60% of patients who undergo surgery develop recurrent or metastatic disease.3 Gemcitabine/oxaliplatin-based regimens are used as a standard chemotherapy for advanced ICC, and lately,4 durvalumab plus gemcitabine plus cisplatin has also been recommended as one of the preferred first-line therapies for advanced ICC, but its efficacy is rather limited.5 Thus, enhancing the antitumor immune response rate and reducing side effects are worthy of in-depth study.

Cancer immunotherapy relies on the priming of T cells, generation and stimulation of tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME), and the establishment of an efficient and durable antitumor immune response.6 Signals and factors that determine the fate of tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells in the TME have been the focus of intensive research.

Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) is a ligand-activated transcription factor encoded by the NR1H4 gene, which belongs to the bile acid receptor of the nuclear receptor superfamily.7 FXR is most abundant in the liver, the lower small intestine, the proximal and distal tubules of the kidney, and it is involved in many diseases.8 9 Although the expression of FXR is generally low in immune cells, accumulating evidence demonstrates that it exerts profound immunomodulatory effects through various leukocyte subsets, thereby influencing disease progression. FXR regulates T follicular helper cell differentiation and autoantibody production through cholesterol homeostasis, thereby ameliorating systemic lupus erythematosus10; in macrophages, it restricts M1 polarization and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, suppressing colitis-associated colon cancer11; it also disrupts group 3 innate lymphoid cell-derived interleukin (IL)-22 production via intestinal epithelial ferroptosis, exacerbating neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis12; furthermore, in non-small cell lung cancer, FXR promotes tumor immune evasion by downregulating programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and inhibiting CD8+ T-cell function.13 Nevertheless, the precise effect of FXR on tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells within the ICC remains largely elusive.

In this study, it was discovered that the transcription factor FXR is prominently induced in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells from ICC. FXR induction was found to be necessary and sufficient to program key features of tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell effector function and migration within the TME. This study therefore demonstrates that FXR exacerbates exhaustion in tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells within the TME. Furthermore, it suggests that pharmacological inhibition of FXR signaling may enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy against ICC.

Results

FXR was highly expressed on CD8+ TILs in ICC

To elucidate the localization and role of FXR in tumor immune microenvironment, we investigated the expression pattern and function of FXR (figure 1). Multicolor immunofluorescence staining demonstrated the predominantly nuclear location of FXR in CD8+ T tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and colocalization of FXR and CD8+T cells (figure 1A). Survival analysis of patients who received immunotherapy stratified on the basis of the levels of NR1H4/CD8A ratio revealed that lower NR1H4/CD8A ratio is associated with increased survival of patients after immunotherapy (figure 1B). We established an NICD/AKT ICC mouse model by hydrodynamic plasmid injection through the tail vein. At the endpoint, Nr1h4 messenger RNA levels in sorted CD8+ T cells from the tumor, Tumor-draining lymph node (TdLN), and Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cell (PBMC) were measured by quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays. We found that Nr1h4 expression levels were significantly upregulated in CD8+ TILs compared with CD8+ T cells localized to the PBMC and TdLN, indicating that tumor-specific factors contribute to Nr1h4 expression in CD8+ TILs (online supplemental figure 1A). The expression levels of FXR proteins were validated by flow cytometry. CD8+ TILs exhibited higher expression of FXR than CD4+ TILs and CD8+ T cells of the TdLN and spleen (figure 1C, online supplemental figure 1B). Since exhausted CD8+ T cells are considered a primary target for therapeutic interventions involving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs),1416 we examined the relationship between FXR expression and different exhaustion states of CD8+ TILs. We observed high expression of FXR in the programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) +T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3)+ and PD-1+TIM-3− subsets (figure 1D), which suggests that the presence of FXR is correlated with exhausted CD8+ T cells and a suppressive TME.

Collectively, our integrated analysis delineates that FXR is highly expressed in exhausted CD8+ TILs and critically regulates CD8+ T cells exhaustion in ICC, prompting us to investigate its role in tumor growth online supplemental figures 2; 8.

FXR deficiency can enhance the antitumor effects

To further examine whether the loss of FXR influences ICC progression, we began by studying FXR-deficient mice carrying a hypomorphic Nr1h4 mutant. FXR knockdown efficiency was validated by Western Blot (WB) assays (online supplemental figure 3A). Nr1h4-/- mice showed normal T-cell development in the thymus, Lymph Node (LN), and spleen compared with Wild Type (WT) control mice (online supplemental figure 2A-C), while the CD8/CD4 ratios in the liver of the Knockout (KO) group were higher than those in the WT group (online supplemental figure 2D). Before the tumor challenge, T cells displayed a similar activation state in lymph nodes between the two groups, and there were fewer activated T cells in the spleen but more in the livers of KO versus WT groups, suggesting that FXR affects T-cell development and function (online supplemental figure 2E-J).

Using an NICD/AKT ICC mouse model, we observed that tumor growth in KO mice was significantly suppressed compared with that in WT mice at the end of the experiment (figure 2A–G). KO mice had a lower liver/body weight ratio (figure 2B), smaller tumor region area (figure 2C and D) and a significantly prolonged survival time compared with WT mice (figure 2E). Additionally, no significant differences were observed in serum levels of interferon (IFN)-γ and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (onlinesupplemental figure 3BC), while Ki67 immunohistochemical staining in tumors demonstrated that the KO group harbored lower Ki67 expression (figure 2F and G). Subsequently, we established ICC subcutaneous tumor models in WT and KO mice using LD-1 cells. The KO mice exhibited slower tumor growth (online supplemental figure 3D), smaller tumor weight (onlinesupplemental figure 3EF), and significantly longer survival time than the WT mice (online supplemental figure 3G). To delineate the T cell-intrinsic role of FXR, we generated Nr1h4 conditional knockout mice and established another ICC model induced by Fbxw7ΔF/AKT. FXR knockdown efficiencies were validated by WB assays (online supplemental figure 3H). Mirroring the phenotype in systemic KO mice, cKO mice exhibited significantly suppressed tumor progression compared with Nr1h4fl/fl controls (figure 2H–L). Notably, serum IFN-γ and TNF-α levels remained unaltered (onlinesupplemental figure 3IJ), consistent with systemic KO data. These results formally demonstrate that FXR in T cells is both necessary and sufficient for promoting ICC progression, independently of cytokine-mediated systemic effects. We were especially interested in studying whether FXR regulates CD8+ effector T cells, which play a critical role in the immune response against cancer.

Deletion of FXR enhances antitumor immunity

To formally test CD8+ T-cell dependency, WT and Nr1h4−/− mice were pretreated with isotype control antibody, anti-CD4, or anti-CD8 depletion antibodies (figure 3A, online supplemental figure 4A), and the tumor burden of WT or KO ICC was monitored at the indicated time points (figure 3B, online supplemental figure 4B). We found that neutralization of CD8+ T cells minimized the differences in tumor burden between WT and KO groups (figure 3B–C), while the KO group significantly slowed tumor progression using an anti-CD4 depletion antibody (onlinesupplemental figure 4CD), thus revealing that improved antitumor responses in KO mice were CD8+ T cell-dependent but CD4+ T cell-independent. To further explore the role of FXR in the regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, immune cell populations in the TME were analyzed. Flow Cytometry (FC) analyses revealed that the infiltration of tumors with regulatory T cells (Tregs), macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells and B cells were similar in both groups (online supplemental figure 4D-J). Although an increase in natural killer (NK) cell infiltration was observed in the KO group (online supplemental figure 4K), functional validation using T cell-specific conditional knockout mice confirmed that the antitumor phenotype was primarily mediated by T cells, rather than NK cells (figure 2H–L). Consistent with this, CD8+ T-cell activation was significantly enhanced in the KO group (figure 3D).

We next sought to investigate the mechanism underlying the increased frequency of activated CD8+ TILs in the KO group. On the one hand, such an increase could be a result of either increased proliferation or decreased death of CD8+T cells in the TME. However, it relies, in part, on the recruitment of CD8+ T cells from the TdLN. We found that activated CD8+ TILs exhibited higher proliferative capacity (Ki67+) in the KO group than in the WT group, while no differences were observed in CD8+ T-cell death between the two groups of mice (online supplemental figure 4L-M). We observed a corresponding increase in the proliferation (Ki67+) and migration (CXCR3+) of CD8+ T cells in the TdLN, suggesting increased expansion and recruitment of CD8+ T cells to the tumor (online supplemental figure 4N-O). Next, we focused on the expression of effector molecules related to CD8+ cytotoxic T cell (CTL) function (figure 3G–L). FXR deficiency significantly enhanced effector functions in activated CD8+ T cells, evidenced by elevated production of IFN-γ (figure 3G–I, online supplemental figure 4P-Q), TNF-α (figure 3J–L, onlinesupplemental figure 4RS), and granzyme B (GzmB) (online supplemental figure 4T-X) compared with WT groups. While elevated IFN-γ/TNF-α/GzmB demonstrates functional reinvigoration, sustained antitumor immunity requires the maintenance of progenitor exhausted T cells (Tpex) populations (characterized by T-cell factor (TCF1)+ Thymocyte selection-associated high mobility group box protein (TOX)+) that resist terminal exhaustion and generate effector progeny. We found that the Tpex were significantly increased in KO mice (figure 3M and N, online supplemental figure 4Y), suggesting that FXR ablation may potentiate immunotherapy efficacy. The percentages of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells (PD-1+ CD39+) from the KO group were higher than those from the WT group (figure 3O and P, online supplemental figure 4Z), which implies expansion of tumor-reactive clones, likely encompassing tumor antigen-specific populations given CD39’s role as a surrogate for strong T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling. We further verified the CD8+ T-cell phenotype in cKO mice. Crucially, activated CD8+ T-cell proportions remained unchanged in cKO (figure 3Q–R), indicating FXR specifically regulates functional differentiation rather than initial activation. The cKO mice fully recapitulated the enhanced effector functions (figure 3S-T), tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells (figure 3U) and stem-like phenotypes (figure 3V) observed in systemic KO mice. Collectively, FXR ablation in CD8+ T cells orchestrates antitumor mechanisms: enhanced cytotoxic effector function, preservation of stem-like Tpex, expansion of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells, enhanced proliferation and migratory capacity of CD8+ T cells in TdLN, synergistically driving CD8+ T cell-dependent tumor control.

Next, we investigated whether FXR could enhance tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell function. In NICD/AKT-ovalbumin (OVA) tumors, there was no difference in activated CD8+ T cells specific for SIINFEKL (OVA) between the two groups (figure 4A–C). The activation status of tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells was assessed using intracellular cytokine staining. KO mice had higher tumor antigen-specific cells producing IFN-γ and TNF-α and significantly increased proliferation ability compared with WT mice (figure 4D–I). In addition, we observed an increase in tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells, and as expected, cell proliferation and migratory abilities were significantly elevated in TdLN of the KO group as compared with the WT group (figure 4J–M). Collectively, FXR deficiency potently modulated the immunosuppressive TME, leading to enhanced infiltration, migration, and functional activation of CD8+ T cells and tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells to potentially inhibit tumors.

FXR directly regulates CD8+ T-cell antitumor immunity

To test whether CD8+ T cells can be directly modulated by FXR, we sorted naïve CD8+ T cells from WT and KO mice and cultured them in anti-CD3/CD28 and IL-2 containing medium for 48 hours. KO CD8+ T cells showed stronger cytotoxicity (IFN-γ+ TNF-α+) and proliferation ability than WT CD8+ T cells (figure 5A–F). In addition, as shown in figure 5G, KO effector CD8+T cells showed significantly higher killing activity than WT CD8+ T cells in vitro.

To further study whether FXR directly regulates the antitumor reactivity of CD8+ T cells, we bred Nr1h4−/− mice with OT1 transgenic (OT1-Tg) mice and generated OT1-Tg/Nr1h4−/− mice producing OVA-specific CD8+ T cells deficient in FXR. We isolated OT1 T cells (online supplemental figure 5A) from either OT1-Tg or OT1-Tg/Nr1h4−/− mice (denoted as OT1-WT or OT1-KO T cells, respectively) and separately transferred these cells into Cd8−/− mice bearing pre-established NICD/AKT-OVA ICC tumors (figure 5H). OT1-KO T cells were more effective in controlling tumor growth (figure 5I–J), corresponding to their enhanced effector function (figure 5L–Q), although the frequency of OT1 cells did not differ between the two groups (figure 5K).

We performed another adoptive transfer experiment wherein CD45.2 OT1-WT and CD45.1 OT1-KO T cells were mixed in a ratio of 1:1 and then adoptively transferred into Cd8−/− recipient mice with established subcutaneous B16-OVA tumors (online supplemental figure 5B). Flow cytometry analysis showed that the frequency of OT1-KO and OT1-WT T cells was not different in the TME, but the proportion of Tpex (PD-1+TCF-1+), GzmB-producing cells, and GzmB mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) were significantly increased in OT1-KO T cells (online supplemental figure 5C-H).

Collectively, these in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that FXR functions as an autonomous factor that directly regulates CD8+ T-cell antitumor immunity.

Epigenetic programming of CD8+ T-cell effector phenotype by the FXR-LAG3 axis.

We next investigated the mechanism by which FXR acts during effector formation in tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells. After tetramer staining, KO/WT tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells in the TME and TdLN were harvested from 10 NICD/AKT-OVA mice per group, and total RNA was extracted for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) library preparation and sequencing. Principal component analysis demonstrated that KO was significantly different from WT tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells (online supplemental figure 6A). The heatmap of the global phenotypic characteristics of TME tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells showed a clear distinction between the two groups (figure 6A). Of these, the transcription factor (eg, If4 and Prdml) genes, genes involved in effector responses (eg, Ifng and Fasl), and cell cycle regulation (eg, Ccnb1 and Ccnb2) genes were consistently upregulated in the TME KO group. Differential gene expression analyses were performed, and volcano plots showed 446 downregulated and 25 upregulated genes in the TME KO group (online supplemental figure 6B, online supplemental table 1). We found that these genes were mainly enriched in metabolism, T-cell differentiation, T-cell activation, and T-cell homeostasis (online supplemental figure 6C). In addition, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) pathway analysis showed that the KO TdLN group’s genes were mainly enriched in lymphocyte proliferation and lymphocyte migration pathways (online supplemental figure 6D). Above, KO tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells are more prone to effector T-cell differentiation in the TME and have a higher ability to migrate from the TdLN to the TME, which is in agreement with the experimental results.

After overlapping the Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) of TME tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells with the FXR target genes in the mouse liver using the ChIPBASE V.3.0 database (https://rnasysu.com/chipbase3/index.php) (figure 6B), it identified Lag3 (a T-cell exhaustion marker, p<0.0001, log2FC=−5.86) as the 11th most significantly downregulated gene in overlapping targets. Subsequent interrogation of immunotherapy cohorts showed that elevated LAG3/CD8A ratios correlated with poor patient survival (HR=1.19, p=0.068), although statistical significance was not achieved (online supplemental figure 6E). Strikingly, a weighted co-expression index combining LAG3 and NR1H4 expression normalized to CD8A further predicted worsened clinical outcomes (online supplemental figure 6F, HR=1.73, p<0.0001). These clinical correlations strongly suggested functional synergy between FXR and LAG3 in promoting CD8+ T-cell dysfunction.

Based on these converging lines of evidence, we hypothesized direct transcriptional regulation of Lag3 by FXR. We performed bulk RNA-seq on sorted CD8+ T cells from obeticholic acid (OCA)-treated cells. This pharmacological FXR activation robustly inhibited T-cell response pathways (online supplemental figure 6G), while concomitantly elevating Lag3 expression compared with vehicle controls (online supplemental figure 6H).

To experimentally validate this mechanism, we performed Quantitative Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (qChIP) for FXR binding to several gene promoters predicted to share FXR and LAG3 sites, and the results showed that FXR can bind to site 3 to activate the expression of the downstream LAG3 gene (figure 6C). An additional promoter assay was performed using a deletion-mutant reporter plasmid in which the FXR-binding site 3 had been deleted. Compared with the control group, the WT LAG3 promoter sequence significantly increased the relative luciferase activity and had no significant effect on luciferase activity when co-transfected with mutated plasmids (figure 6D). The dual-luciferase reporter assay verified that FXR can bind to the LAG3 promoter site 3.

To prove that FXR elicits the effector function of CD8+ T cells by interacting with LAG3, we sorted OT-1 cells in the NICD/AKT-OVA tumor model and overexpressed FXR (OE-FXR) in OT-1 cells. FXR overexpression efficiencies were validated by WB assays (online supplemental figure 6I). FXR-mediated reduction of TNF-α and IFN-γ in OE-FXR OT-1 T cells from NICD/AKT-OVA-bearing mice was attenuated by the anti-LAG3 HY-P99141 in vitro (figure 6E–F). Subsequent Lag3 knockdown by siRNA (online supplemental figure 6J) in OCA-treated OT-1 cells demonstrated unaltered IFN-γ secretion but increased TNF-α production (figure 6G–H), indicating FXR specifically requires Lag3 for suppressing effector function. For in vivo validation, OT1-WT or OT1-KO cells transduced with Lag3-overexpressing lentivirus (online supplemental figure 6K) were adoptively transferred into NICD/AKT-OVA tumor-bearing mice, confirming that Lag3 overexpression abrogates the antitumor advantage of FXR deficiency (figure 6I–K), establishing Lag3 as the critical effector through which FXR promotes immune evasion. These results collectively indicate that FXR functions as a negative regulator of the cytotoxicity program of CD8+ T cells by directly binding to the Lag3 promoter and upregulating its expression in the TME.

To identify the endogenous ligand responsible for FXR activation in the immunosuppressive microenvironment of ICC, we focused on chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), a potent endogenous FXR agonist. Functional validation was performed in vitro CDCA treatment (100 µM) significantly activated FXR expression in CD8+ TILs (figure 6L), confirming its role as a physiological regulator.

FXR blockade for cancer immunotherapy

The identification of FXR as a new immune checkpoint that negatively regulates CD8+ T-cell antitumor immunity is a promising drug target for developing new forms of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Small-molecule FXRIs ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) have been developed and clinically used for treating primary biliary cholangitis,17 18 making it a highly feasible and attractive approach to repurposing UDCA for cancer immunotherapy. When tested in vivo in an NICD/AKT-OVA model (figure 7A), the expression of Nr1h4 in liver CD8+ TILs was lower in UDCA-gavaged mice than in saline-gavaged mice (figure 7B). UDCA markedly suppressed the tumor burden and prolonged survival (figure 7C–E).

In addition, a significant increase was observed in the proportion of activated CD8+ T cells (figure 7F), tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells (figure 7G), effector molecules (IFN-γ and TNF-α) (figure 7H–K), and Tpex (figure 7L). Therefore, UDCA can alleviate T-cell exhaustion and improve the overall effector function of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. Critically, Glycoursodeoxycholic Acid (GUDCA), as another direct FXR antagonist,19 recapitulated the therapeutic benefits observed with UDCA, significantly reducing tumor burden (online supplemental figure 7A-C) and enhancing CD8+ T-cell phenotypes (online supplemental figure 7D-I) associated with improved antitumor immunity, thereby further validating FXR inhibition as a potent approach across ICC contexts. Despite the comparable efficacy of GUDCA in validating the FXR inhibition mechanism, we selected UDCA for subsequent translational studies based on its established clinical profile as a first-line therapeutic for cholestatic liver diseases and its well-documented safety and dosing paradigms in humans. To confirm the T-cell dependence of UDCA’s antitumor effect, we administered it to tumor-bearing RAG1 knockout (Rag1−/−) mice, which lack adaptive immune cells. Consistent with this dependence, UDCA showed no significant efficacy in these mice, indicating that UDCA’s therapeutic effect is primarily mediated by T cells (online supplemental figure 7J-L). Given that UDCA does not directly kill tumor cells, we further investigated whether it might exert other beneficial effects on tumor cells that could contribute to its antitumor efficacy. To this end, in vitro co-culture experiments combined with qPCR and ELISA analyses revealed that UDCA enhanced the migratory capacity of CD8+ T cells and, importantly, upregulated the expression and secretion of key T cell-attracting chemokines (eg, CXCL9, CXCL10) by tumor cells (online supplemental figure 7M-P).

Next, we investigated the therapeutic potential of the UDCA/anti-PD-L1 combination in vivo (figure 7M). Significant tumor growth suppression and prolonged survival were observed in the UDCA monotherapy and combination treatment groups, but not in the anti-PD-L1 monotherapy group (figure 7N–P). To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the limited efficacy of anti-PD-L1 monotherapy and the pronounced effect of UDCA, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of resected tumor tissues. As expected, tumors from UDCA-treated and combination-treated mice showed significantly reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis compared with those from control or anti-PD-L1 monotherapy groups (onlinesupplemental figure 7QR). However, no significant differences in PD-L1 expression were detected among the four groups (online supplemental figure 7S). These results suggest that the antitumor effect of UDCA is primarily mediated through direct suppression of tumor cell proliferation and induction of apoptosis rather than modulation of PD-L1 expression. Furthermore, the absence of PD-L1 upregulation may explain, at least in part, the lack of response to anti-PD-L1 monotherapy in this model.

Critically, to directly evaluate the potential of UDCA to enhance ICB therapy in a clinically relevant setting, we employed patient-derived tumor fragment (PDTF) cultures from human patients with ICC. In this ex vivo model, combining UDCA with anti-PD-L1 antibody resulted in a significantly higher proportion of apoptotic tumor cells compared with either monotherapy, as quantified by both Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) staining and flow cytometry-based apoptosis assays (annexin V+) (online supplemental figure 7Q-S). This synergistic enhancement of tumor cell death unequivocally demonstrates that UDCA can potentiate the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 ICB therapy. The synergistic effect observed in PDTF models with anti-PD-L1 provides compelling translational evidence that UDCA is a promising candidate for combination strategies to overcome resistance and improve outcomes in cancer immunotherapy, particularly for ICC.

Discussion

Here, we establish the FXR-LAG3 axis as a central mechanism governing CD8+ T-cell exhaustion in ICC. Contrasting prior reports of FXR’s tumor-suppressive role in certain solid tumors,2022 our integrated epigenomic approach reveals its immune checkpoint function specifically within the TME. Mechanistically, FXR directly binds the LAG3 promoter to transcriptionally upregulate this exhaustion marker—a regulatory relationship undetected in bulk tissue studies due to cell type-restricted chromatin accessibility at the Nr1h4 locus exclusively in CD8+ TILs. This FXR-driven immunosuppression manifests clinically as elevated FXR/LAG3 co-expression correlating with poor immunotherapy response, while its pharmacological inhibition via UDCA revitalizes CD8+ T-cell effector function and synergizes with PD-L1 blockade in human ICC models.

Our study showed that NR1H4 expression was induced in CD8+ TILs, which in turn restrained T-cell activation. This direct inhibitory pathway qualifies FXR as an ICC immune checkpoint. However, FXR is unique because it is already a well-established drug target owing to its known function in the liver and colon. Small-molecule FXRIs have been developed to block FXR activity, thereby regulating cholesterol, bile acid, and fatty acid synthesis metabolism, and were the first drugs approved for treating chronic cholestatic liver disease.17 18 In our study, we tested the clinically approved FXRIs UDCA and demonstrated its T cell-enhancing and tumor-suppressive effects in preclinical animal models of ICC, pointing to the possibility of repurposing these drugs for cancer immunotherapy. Developing new cancer drugs is extremely costly and time-consuming, and drug repurposing offers an economic and speedy pathway to additional cancer therapies because approved drugs have known safety profiles and modes of action and thus can enter the clinic quickly. Although UDCA has several hepatoprotective activities, it is currently only approved for the resolution of cholesterol gallstones, primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC), and primary biliary cholangitis.23 More recently, it was shown that supplementing a diet with UDCA significantly reduced the hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tumor burden and enhanced CTL responses.24 Therefore, repurposing UDCA for cancer immunotherapy is an attractive new application of potent drugs.

Several important translational considerations merit discussion. First, while systemic FXR inhibition may theoretically promote hepatic steatosis and cholestasis as observed in Fxr-null mice,2527 these models reflect lifelong FXR absence in parenchymal cells. By contrast, our T cell-specific Nr1h4 cKO and acute UDCA treatment did not induce tumorigenesis, suggesting cell type-specific and temporal contexts critically determine FXR’s oncogenic role. We acknowledge the study indicating that prolonged, high-dose UDCA can exhibit hepatotoxicity and suppress immune responses in certain models.28 In our study, we employed a defined, well-tolerated dose regimen and observed a clear enhancement of CD8+ T-cell effector functions rather than generalized immunosuppression. For cancer immunotherapy, short-term FXR antagonist regimens could offer a favorable risk-benefit ratio given their potent antitumor efficacy. Nonetheless, this dichotomy emphasizes the necessity for careful dose-finding and safety studies in any future translational development of UDCA or other FXR antagonists for oncology indications. Second, UDCA can have pleiotropic effects beyond FXR signaling.29 Our results do not exclude the contribution of such alternative pathways. However, the critical finding of our study is that the specific inhibition of FXR is sufficient to recapitulate the key therapeutic benefits, as demonstrated by the congruent antitumor effects achieved with both UDCA and GUDCA. Therefore, while we recognize that UDCA may engage multiple mechanisms, our functional and genetic data strongly argue that FXR antagonism is a major and therapeutically relevant pathway underlying the improved antitumor immunity we observe in ICC. Third, the potential oncogenic risk of long-term FXR agonism (eg, OCA for PBC) is a critically important consideration. Our data demonstrating that FXR antagonism enhances antitumor immunity, logically suggest that constitutive FXR activation could exert the opposite effect, potentially fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment permissive for tumor growth. Indeed, this raises a clinically relevant hypothesis that patients with chronic liver diseases undergoing treatment with potent FXR agonists might warrant enhanced oncologic surveillance. However, it is crucial to note that the clinical evidence directly linking OCA to an increased incidence of cholangiocarcinoma in patients with PBC remains limited and inconclusive. Long-term studies and large-scale registries are needed to definitively address this potential risk.

Here, we identified FXR as an immune checkpoint and demonstrated the potential of repurposing UDCA for cancer immunotherapy. The notion that FXR not only acts in the liver and colon to regulate metabolic homeostasis but also in tumors to control the aggressiveness of antitumor immunity. Future clinical studies are encouraged to investigate the clinical correlations between UDCA treatment and clinical outcomes in patients with ICC and to explore the possibility of repurposing UDCA for combination cancer immunotherapy. The immune regulatory function of FXR goes beyond the regulation of CD8+ T cells. The expression of FXR has been observed in various immune cells (eg, Dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and Tregs),30 and in Nr1h4−/− mice, previous studies have reported the hyper-responsiveness of multiple immune cells in various mouse models (eg, inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer).31 32 It is also likely that UDCA regulates immune reactions to multiple diseases other than cancer, such as infections and autoimmune diseases. Studying the role of FXR in regulating various immune cells under different health and disease conditions will be an interesting topic for future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study identifies the FXR-LAG3 axis as a critical mechanism of tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell exhaustion in ICC and demonstrates that pharmacological inhibition of FXR with UDCA revitalizes antitumor immunity and synergizes with PD-L1 blockade, presenting a readily translatable therapeutic strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance.
