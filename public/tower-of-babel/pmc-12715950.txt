Risk factors of ventilator-associated pneumonia in patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a meta-analysis and systematic review
Authors: Yang Hongwei, Wang Juan, Ji Hui, Li Zhongjian, Li Xueying, Liu Rui, Li Min, Wang Xiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesThis meta-analysis aimed to identify risk factors for ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) in patients with Acute exacerbations of Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD).MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Embase, CNKI, and other databases for studies investigating risk factors for VAP in patients experiencing AECOPD. The search encompassed records from database inception up to July 2, 2025. The quality of the studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Meta-analysis was performed using Stata 18.0.ResultsA total of 16 articles were included, encompassing 3,664 subjects and 16 risk factors. Meta-analysis results showed that, Age (OR: 2.49, 95%CI : 1.49, 4.17; P < 0.001), Smoking history (OR: 2.70, 95%CI : 1.65, 4.44; P < 0.001), Acute physiology and chronic health evaluation composite score (APACHE Ⅱ) score (OR: 3.03, 95%CI : 1.98, 4.65; P < 0.001), Sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score (OR: 2.75, 95%CI : 1.90, 3.99; P < 0.001), Diabetes (OR: 2.11, 95%CI : 1.38, 3.24; P = 0.001), Underlying Diseases (OR: 3.42, 95%CI : 1.85, 6.32; P < 0.001), Duration of mechanical ventilation (OR: 4.53, 95%CI : 2.68, 7.65; P < 0.001), Tracheal intubation (OR: 4.21, 95%CI : 1.85, 9.57; P = 0.001), Indwelling gastric tube (OR: 3.31, 95%CI : 1.38, 7.95; P = 0.008), Total parenteral nutrition (OR: 1.86, 95%CI : 1.29, 2.70; P = 0.001), Combined antibiotics (OR: 2.79, 95%CI : 1.32, 5.93; P = 0.007), Tracheotomy (OR: 2.92, 95%CI : 2.04, 4.17; P < 0.001), History of mechanical ventilation within one year (OR: 2.92, 95%CI : 2.04, 4.17; P = 0.005), Use acid suppressants (OR: 2.10, 95%CI : 1.49, 2.97; P < 0.001) were associated with the development of VAP in AECOPD patients.ConclusionsThis study identified 14 risk factors associated with the risk of VAP in AECOPD patients. This finding is helpful for early identification of high-risk patients, which is of great value for reducing mortality and improving the clinical prognosis of patients with mechanical ventilation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-04003-2.Keywords: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Acute exacerbations, Ventilator-associated pneumonia, Risk factors, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a prevalent and life-threatening chronic non-communicable disease, posing a substantial burden on global public health. According to data from 2021, COPD represents the fourth leading cause of mortality worldwide, responsible for approximately 3.5 million deaths, accounting for 5% of all global deaths [1]. recent studies project that the number of COPD patients may rise to 600 million by 2050 [2].

COPD is categorized into the stable phase and the acute exacerbation phase. An acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD) is defined as an acute and sustained worsening of respiratory symptoms that necessitates additional therapy, with severe cases often requiring hospitalization [3, 4]. As reported by Foda et al. [5], based on Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) staging criteria, the annual incidence of acute exacerbations varies significantly according to disease severity: mild (0.15 events), moderate (0.27 events), severe (0.38 events), and very severe (0.72 events). Patients experiencing AECOPD frequently require mechanical ventilation. Prolonged ventilation, however, predisposes them to colonization and infection by pathogens in the lower respiratory tract, markedly increasing the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). VAP is defined as pneumonia that arises more than 48 h after tracheal intubation or tracheostomy [6]. As one of the most common hospital-acquired infections, VAP is associated with prolonged hospitalization, elevated healthcare costs, and higher mortality rates, significantly impairing patient prognosis [6]. Studies indicate that the incidence of VAP ranges from 1 to 18.5 per 1000 ventilator-days [7, 8]. Wicky et al. reported that VAP increased the risk of death by 36% [9].

Current evidence suggests that advanced age, smoking, tracheotomy, duration of mechanical ventilation, and concomitant antibiotic use are associated with VAP in AECOPD patients. However, consensus is lacking regarding certain risk factors—such as smoking history, tracheotomy, and hypoproteinemia—likely due to heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria and study designs across investigations. For instance, Lv et al. [10] reported no significant correlation between smoking history and VAP in AECOPD patients. Although previous meta-analysis explored the risk factors of VAP, such as Li et al. [11] reported that male, smoking and other factors were related to the occurrence of VAP in ICU patients; however, these studies were not specifically focused on AECOPD patients, limiting the generalizability of their findings to this high-risk population. To date, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized evidence on VAP risk factors specific to patients with AECOPD. Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate the risk factors associated with VAP in AECOPD patients, thereby providing an evidence-based foundation for early clinical intervention and preventive strategies.

Methods

The study was written in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was displayed in keeping with the PRISMA 2020 checklist (Additional file 1). The protocol was published in the Prospective Registry Systematic Reviews Database (PROSPERO) under the number CRD420251084714.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients aged ≥ 18 years with AECOPD; (2) Studies providing clear VAP diagnostic criteria; (3) Cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional designs; (4) Investigation of ≥ 1 VAP risk factor or predictor in AECOPD patients with extractable outcome data.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Duplicate publications; (2) Unavailable full texts, incomplete data, or non-extractable outcomes; (3) Non-English/non-Chinese publications; (4) Reviews, conference abstracts, guidelines, or animal studies.

Literature search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, Embase, CNKI, CBM, Wanfang, and VIP databases from their inception through July 2, 2025. This search employed a comprehensive strategy combining standardized subject terms with relevant free-text keywords.The Chinese search terms were “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,” “chronic obstructive pneumonia,” “chronic airflow obstruction,” “ventilator-associated pneumonia,” “ventilator-associated pneumonia,” “acute exacerbation,” “risk factors,” “related factors,” and so on. The English search terms were “Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,” “AECOPD,” “ventilator-associated pneumonia,” “acute exacerbation period,” “risk factors” “related factors”. Literature screening and data extraction

Two investigators independently screened titles and abstracts against predefined inclusion criteria using NoteExpress documentation management software. Articles meeting initial eligibility thresholds underwent duplicate full-text review and cross-verification. Following final selection, both investigators independently extracted data using standardized spreadsheet templates, including: first author, publication year, country of origin, study design, sample size, diagnostic criteria for VAP, evaluated risk factors, and clinical outcome measures.

Evaluation of literature quality

Two researchers independently screened studies, extracted data, and evaluated study quality based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Discordant evaluations underwent resolution through third-party adjudication. The NOS evaluated three domains: (1) Study population selection; (2) Intergroup comparability; (3) Outcome ascertainment. Total scores (range: 0 ~ 9) were categorized as: high quality (≥ 7), moderate quality (4 ~ 6), or low quality (< 4) [12].

Quality of evidence

The Grades of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) [13] system was used to grade the quality of evidence for outcome indicators. The quality of evidence was evaluated from five aspects including risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias, and the quality of evidence was divided into four levels: high, medium, low and very low. The results are presented in a summary of findings table.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using Stata 18.0 software, with pooled effects expressed as odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was quantitatively assessed via Q-test and I2 statistics; when P > 0.1 and I2 ≤ 50%, heterogeneity was deemed acceptable and fixed-effect models were applied, whereas P < 0.1 or I2 ≥ 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity requiring random-effects models. risk factor sensitivity was additionally evaluated under both effect models. For risk factors with substantial heterogeneity (I² >70%), we planned to conduct univariate meta-regression analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test for risk factors with ≥ 3 included studies.

Results

Results of literature search

From an initial pool of 1,206 identified records, 1,107 studies remained after duplicate removal. Following title and abstract screening, 1,082 records were excluded. Full-text assessment excluded 9 additional articles due to unmet eligibility criteria, resulting in 16 studies qualifying for final inclusion [10, 14–28]. The reasons for excluding literature were as follows: inconsistent research topic (n = 1078), book (n = 2), study protocol (n = 2) not available in full text (n = 1), repeated publication (n = 1), incomplete data (n = 4), and unavailable data (n = 3).The complete selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

General characteristics and quality evaluation of the included studies

Sixteen studies met final inclusion criteria. Among these, 14 were Chinese-language publications and 2 were English-language publications, spanning publication years from 2014 to 2025. The collection comprised 7 cohort studies [10, 16, 17, 22, 26–28] and 9 case-control studies [14, 15, 18–21, 23–25]. The sample size ranged from 83 to 643 cases, with a total of 3,664 cases, including 1,070 patients (29.20%) with VAP. For certain factors, including “Duration of mechanical ventilation” and “Indwelling gastric tube”, the evidence was further downgraded to very low certainty because of serious imprecision (reflected by wide confidence intervals). Results of meta-analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted on 16 risk factors identified for inclusion in this study, the findings of which are as follows:

General factors

Age and its effect on VAP

Four studies assessed age as a risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD. Random effects model analysis showed that AECOPD patients aged ≥ 60 years had an increased risk of VAP (OR: 2.49, 95%CI : 1.49, 4.17; P < 0.001; I2 = 67.1%). Figure 2 shows the forest plot for age.

Forest plot of the relationship between age and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Smoking history and its effect on VAP

Seven studies evaluated smoking history as a risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD. The results of subgroup analysis according to study type showed that smoking also increased the risk of VAP in AECOPD patients (OR: 2.70, 95%CI : 1.65, 4.44; P < 0.001; I2 = 81.8%). Figure 3 shows the forest plot for History of smoking.

Forest plot of the relationship between smoking history and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Objective index factors

APACHE Ⅱ score and its effect on VAP

Six studies evaluated the association between APACHE II score and VAP risk in patients with AECOPD. Our pooled results showed that APACHEⅡ score was associated with VAP in AECOPD patients (OR: 3.03, 95%CI : 1.98, 4.65; P < 0.001; I2 = 73.3%). Figure 4 shows the forest plot for APACHE Ⅱ score.

Forest plot of the relationship between APACHE Ⅱ score and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

SOFA score and its effect on VAP

Three studies evaluated the association between SOFA scores and VAP risk in patients with AECOPD. Evidence from the fixed-effect model analysis indicated an association between a higher SOFA score and VAP in AECOPD patients (OR: 2.75, 95%CI : 1.90, 3.99; P < 0.001; I2 = 0.0%). Figure 5 shows the forest plot for SOFA score.

Forest plot of the relationship between SOFA score and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Disease factors

Diabetes and its effect on VAP

Six studies evaluated the potential risk role of diabetes on VAP in patients with AECOPD. The pooled results showed that diabetes could increase the risk of VAP in AECOPD patients (OR: 2.11, 95%CI : 1.38, 3.24; P = 0.001; I2 = 63.4%). Figure 6 shows the forest plot for Diabetes.

Forest plot of the relationship between Diabetes and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Hypoproteinemia and its effect on VAP

Three studies evaluated hypoproteinemia as a potential risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD. The results of Meta-analysis showed that hypoproteinemia was not statistically associated with the risk of VAP (OR: 1.21, 95%CI : 0.22, 6.54; P = 0.826; I2 = 92.7%). Figure 7 shows the forest plot for Hypoproteinemia.

Forest plot of the relationship between Hypoproteinemia and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Underlying Diseases and its effect on VAP

Two studies evaluated comorbid conditions as risk factors for VAP in patients with AECOPD. Fixed effect model analysis showed that combined underlying may increased the risk of VAP in AECOPD patients (OR: 3.42, 95%CI : 1.85, 6.32; P < 0.001; I2 = 0.0%). Figure 8 shows the forest plot for Underlying Diseases.

Forest plot of the relationship between Underlying Diseases and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Treatment factors

Duration of mechanical ventilation and its effect on VAP

Twelve studies evaluated the association between prolonged mechanical ventilation and the risk of VAP in patients with AECOPD. Our pooled results showed that the occurrence of VAP in AECOPD patients was related to the duration of mechanical ventilation (OR: 4.53, 95%CI : 2.68, 7.65; P < 0.001; I2 = 86.0%). Figure 9 shows a forest plot of the duration of mechanical ventilation.

Forest plot of the relationship between Duration of mechanical ventilation and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Tracheal intubation and its effect on VAP

Seven studies evaluated the association between tracheal intubation and the risk of VAP in patients with AECOPD. Subgroup analysis according to different diagnostic criteria showed that endotracheal intubation was a risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD (OR: 4.21, 95%CI : 1.85, 9.57; P = 0.001; I2 = 93.0%). Figure 10 shows the forest plot for Tracheal intubation.

Forest plot of the relationship between Tracheal intubation and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Indwelling gastric tube and its effect on VAP

Five studies evaluated the association between gastric tube placement and VAP risk in AECOPD patients. The pooled results showed that placement of gastric tube may increase the risk of VAP (OR: 3.31, 95%CI : 1.38, 7.95; P = 0.008; I2 = 94.0%). Figure 11 shows the forest plot for Indwelling gastric tube.

Forest plot of the relationship between Indwelling gastric tube and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Total parenteral nutrition and its effect on VAP

Four studies evaluated total parenteral nutrition as a risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD. Our pooled results showed that total parenteral nutrition support was associated with the occurrence of VAP in AECOPD patients (OR: 1.86, 95%CI : 1.29, 2.70; P = 0.001; I2 = 76.7%). Figure 12 shows the forest plot for Total parenteral nutrition.

Forest plot of the relationship between Total parenteral nutrition and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Combined antibiotics and its effect on VAP

Five studies evaluated combined antibiotic therapy as a risk factor for VAP in AECOPD patients. The pooled results suggest that combined antibiotic use may be associated with an increased risk of VAP in AECOPD patients (OR: 2.79, 95%CI : 1.32, 5.93; P = 0.007; I2 = 85.5%). Figure 13 shows the forest plot for Combined antibiotics.

Forest plot of the relationship between Combined antibiotics and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Tracheotomy and its effect on VAP

Three studies evaluated tracheotomy as a risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD. The pooled results suggest that tracheotomy may be a risk factor for VAP in AECOPD patients (OR: 2.92, 95%CI : 2.04, 4.17; P < 0.001; I2 = 0.0%). Figure 14 shows the forest plot for Tracheotomy.

Forest plot of the relationship between Tracheotomy and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

History of mechanical ventilation within one year and its effect on VAP

Three studies evaluated prior mechanical ventilation within the preceding year as a risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD. Our meta-analysis showed that AECOPD patients with a history of mechanical ventilation within the previous year may be associated with an increased risk of VAP (OR: 1.82, 95%CI : 1.20, 2.74; P = 0.005; I2 = 14.0%). Figure 15 shows the forest plot for History of mechanical ventilation within one year.

Forest plot of the relationship between History of mechanical ventilation within one year and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Use acid suppressants and its effect on VAP

Two studies evaluated acid-suppressing therapy as a risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD. The pooled analysis showed that the use of acid suppressors may be associated with an increased risk of VAP in patients with AECOPD (OR: 2.10, 95%CI : 1.49, 2.97; P < 0.001; I2 = 0.0%). Figure 16 shows the forest plot for Use acid suppressants.

Forest plot of the relationship between Use acid suppressants and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Other factors

Hospital stay and its effect on VAP

Two studies evaluated prolonged hospitalization as a potential risk factor for VAP in patients with AECOPD. The results of the analysis in this study indicated that no significant association was observed between length of hospital stay and the risk of VAP in patients with AECOPD (OR: 1.94, 95%CI : 0.73, 5.21; P = 0.186; I2 = 80.5%). Figure 17 shows the forest plot for Hospital stay.

Forest plot of the relationship between Hospital stay and concurrent VAP in AECOPD patients

Sensitivity analysis results

Fixed-effect and random-effect models were used to calculate the combined odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for the 16 risk factors. The results indicated that the combined estimates for hypoproteinemia and hospital length of stay differed significantly between the two models and exhibited low stability due to high sensitivity. In contrast, the combined results for the remaining 14 risk factors showed no significant heterogeneity across models, indicating robustness. These findings are presented in Table 4.

Meta-regression analysis results

To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity among risk factors exhibiting significant inconsistency (I2 > 70%), univariate meta-regression analyses were performed using the following study-level covariates: publication year, study design, diagnostic criteria for VAP, and NOS score. The results, summarized in Table 5 (Additional file 2), indicated that the diagnostic criteria for VAP significantly influenced heterogeneity in the effect estimate for tracheal intubation(P < 0.001). In addition, publication year accounted for a portion of the heterogeneity associated with combined antibiotics(P = 0.002). No other covariates showed significant associations with heterogeneity.

Results of publication bias analysis

Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test for 13 risk factors. Significant bias was detected for duration of mechanical ventilation (P = 0.012) and diabetes (P = 0.012). This bias likely led to an overestimation of the true effect sizes, as studies with positive results are more frequently published. Specifically, the significant but moderate association we found for diabetes may be weaker in reality. These findings necessitate cautious interpretation and highlight the need for validation by future large-scale prospective studies. No significant publication bias was found for other factors (e.g., age, smoking, SOFA score; all P > 0.05). Assessment was not performed for underlying diseases, hospital stay, or use acid suppressants due to insufficient data (n < 3). Take duration of mechanical ventilation as an example, see (Fig. 18). Discussion

Existing studies indicate that VAP poses a significant burden on patients and healthcare systems. A multicenter study in Portugal found that a single episode of VAP can incur excess costs of up to $50,000, substantially increasing financial strain on families and medical resource consumption [29]. Therefore, identifying risk factors for VAP in AECOPD patients is essential for implementing targeted interventions to reduce its incidence.

This study identified multiple factors associated with VAP, which we categorized into: general factors (e.g., age, smoking history), clinical scores (APACHE Ⅱ, SOFA), underlying conditions (e.g., diabetes), and treatment-related factors (duration of mechanical ventilation, tracheal intubation, indwelling gastric tube, total parenteral nutrition, combined antibiotics, tracheotomy, history of mechanical ventilation within one year, and use acid suppressant). In contrast, hypoproteinemia and hospital stay showed no significant association with VAP. However, the overall certainty of evidence remains low, highlighting the need for further rigorously designed studies.

Our results indicated that age ≥ 60 years is associated with a higher risk of VAP, consistent with Thakur et al. [30], possibly due to age-related immune senescence and impaired clearance of respiratory secretions. However, these findings contradict a single-center study by Hassan et al. [31]. Additionally, a cohort study by Cabrera-Tejada et al. [32] demonstrated an association between a smoking history and VAP occurrence, which aligns with the results of our investigation. One potential explanation is that smoking can impair pulmonary macrophage function, diminish bacterial clearance, and thereby increase susceptibility to pathogen colonization and infection.

Haliloglu et al. [33] found no correlation between APACHE II scores or SOFA scores and VAP, whereas Güner et al. [34] identified high APACHE II scores as a risk factor. We speculate that higher illness severity in these patients, often accompanied by respiratory muscle weakness, immune dysfunction, and impaired sputum clearance, may contribute to VAP risk. Morimoto et al. [35] and Tsakiridou et al. [36] reported an association between SOFA scores and VAP, consistent with our findings. Since the SOFA score objectively assesses organ dysfunction [37], higher values may indicate greater vulnerability to VAP.

Kozka et al. [38] demonstrated that prolonged mechanical ventilation duration was significantly associated with an increased risk of VAP. The results of our study are in agreement with theirs. This finding is supported by studies from Robba et al. [39] and Li et al. [40], which similarly indicated that extended mechanical ventilation time elevates the risk of VAP. Uvizl et al. [41] Further quantified this risk, reporting a 1%–3% increase in VAP likelihood for each additional day of mechanical ventilation. Previous research has shown that both tracheal intubation and tracheotomy contribute to VAP risk, with tracheal intubation posing a higher risk compared to tracheotomy [38]. In our study, tracheal intubation increased the risk of VAP by 4.21-fold, while tracheotomy was associated with a 2.92-fold increase. Karaoglan et al. [42] also obtained similar results, Stoian et al. [43] also identified tracheal intubation as a major risk factor for VAP, likely due to its disruption of the upper airway’s natural defense mechanisms. Furthermore, Xie et al. [44] reported a significant correlation between indwelling gastric tubes and VAP, a result corroborated by our study. The presence of a gastric tube may compromise esophageal sphincter function, promote gastroesophageal reflux, and facilitate the translocation of gastric pathogens into the respiratory tract, thereby predisposing patients to VAP. Pan et al. [45] suggested that total parenteral nutrition increases VAP risk, potentially through impaired intestinal barrier function. Our analysis also indicated that multiple antibiotic use elevates VAP risk, a conclusion aligned with the work of Xu et al. [46] Specifically, patients receiving more than three antibiotics exhibited a 4.947-fold higher risk of VAP compared to those receiving fewer than three. Additionally, Jiang et al. [47] proposed that a history of mechanical ventilation within one year was associated with VAP development, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Finally, our findings are consistent with those of Ji et al. [48] In a meta-analysis of patients after cardiac surgery, an association was found between the use of acid suppressants and the development of VAP.

In this study, neither hypoproteinemia nor hospital stay showed a significant association with VAP. However, Pawlik et al. [49] reported increased VAP incidence with ICU stays exceeding five days. Thus, although not significant in our cohort, prolonged hospitalization should still be considered a potential risk factor in clinical practice.

Strength and limitations of the review

The main strength of this study is the application of a meta-analytic methodology to objectively and systematically synthesize risk factors for VAP in patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD). To ensure comprehensive literature coverage, multiple databases were searched to maximize the identification of relevant publications. Several limitations, however, should be acknowledged. First, despite employing a comprehensive search strategy, it remains possible that some relevant studies were inadvertently omitted. Second, the inclusion of only English and Chinese publications may have introduced language bias. Moreover, as the majority of included studies involved Asian populations, the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups may be limited. Given the limited number of studies reporting on each risk factor, we only performed univariate meta-regression analyses. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across the studies included in the meta-analysis. Meta-regression indicated that this heterogeneity may be partially attributable to variations in VAP diagnostic criteria across the original studies (e.g., CDC versus CPIS criteria), which could affect the broader applicability of the results. Finally, the overall quality of the available evidence was relatively low, which constrains the robustness of the conclusions and highlights the need for further validation through higher-quality research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified that advanced age (≥ 60 years), smoking history, elevated APACHE II scores and SOFA scores, diabetes, underlying diseases, duration of mechanical ventilation, tracheal intubation, indwelling gastric tubes, total parenteral nutrition, tracheotomy, mechanical ventilation within the past year, and use acid suppressants were associated with an increased risk of VAP in patients with AECOPD. These findings suggest that early identification of high-risk patients and implementing targeted preventive strategies may help reduce the incidence of VAP.
