Persistent collaboration as a structural signature of scientific resilience
Authors: Hong Chen, Yi Bu, Lu Zhong, Caifan Du, Eric T Meyer, Ying Ding, Jianxi Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScientific progress today is increasingly realized through collaboration. Recent global crises and shifting geopolitical and funding landscapes have shown that collaboration is not immune to shocks. These disturbances call for a deeper examination of the resilience of science, specifically how the scientific community sustains its functionality and collaborative output amid potential shocks and failures. In this study, we conceptualize resilience as a structural property of scientific collaboration and quantify it using an interpretable, network-theoretic measure. Using a large-scale bibliographic dataset, we construct coauthorship networks across multiple disciplines from 1985 to 2014 and perform stress-test experiments that simulate stylized perturbations to identify the structural elements that support system resilience. Our findings show that discipline-level resilience is positively associated with higher collaboration intensity and, more strongly, with greater variance in scientists’ collaboration outcomes. Moreover, we find that persistent collaborations—stable and strong collaborations between scientists—play a central role in fostering resilient network structures across fields; they comprise only a small share of links yet are disproportionately held by scientists in the top decile of productivity. Overall, this study offers a network-based framework for understanding the structural resilience of scientific collaboration and implies the important role of persistent collaboration in supporting that resilience.

Scientific collaboration is essential for the production of knowledge, yet it is increasingly exposed to disruptions ranging from global crises to shifts in funding and geopolitics. This study introduces a network-based framework to quantify structural resilience of scientific collaboration by analyzing the ability of coauthorship networks to maintain resilience under different hypothetical shocks. Using longitudinal bibliographic data across multiple disciplines, we find that scientific fields exhibit higher baseline structural resilience primarily through increased structural heterogeneity, i.e. wider variance in collaboration outputs. Persistent collaboration, long-standing and high-strength ties, emerges as the structural signature of resilience. Stress tests show that these ties, though few, are foundational to resilient collaboration networks and are often concentrated among top-decile scientists. These findings shed light on how collaboration structures influence the resilience of scientific fields and provide a foundation for future research on the dynamics of science systems in the face of real-world shocks.

Introduction

Scientific progress is increasingly driven by greater collaboration among scientists. More scientific advances depend on the joint contributions of scientists in larger teams from different disciplines, regions, and institutions (1, 2). This increasing number of connections among scientists signifies a global, complex, and dynamic interaction within the science system and a constant flow of knowledge within the scientific community (3, 4). While this interconnected nature holds the promise of higher prosperity in scientific outcomes, it remains vulnerable to disruptions that could risk system failures or major changes in dynamics (5). For example, the COVID-19 pandemic has significantly influenced how scientists connect, communicate, and collaborate (6). Specifically, it has resulted in a discontinuity in the propensity to collaborate and has prevented the formation of new collaborations (7). In addition, restrictive policies on international collaboration (8–11), shifts in funding allocation for ongoing projects (12–14), and limitations in physical interaction (7, 15–17) may temporarily suspend or slow down effective collaborations among researchers. These disturbances highlight the need to assess the resilience of the science system, more specifically, how the capacity of coauthorship networks sustains basic connectivity and collaborative output under plausible shocks.

Previous research has focused chiefly on the resilience of natural and engineered complex systems (18–20), while limited effort has been made to explore resilience in the context of complex human dynamics. In the scientific collaboration setting, resilience involves contextualized factors such as adaptability, sustainability of research output, and the ability to maintain collaboration activities under stress (e.g. funding cuts and global crises), which are inherently complex, multifaceted, and challenging to measure in a standardized manner. Here, we employ a recent metric developed by Gao et al (21) to quantify resilience, which allows us to capture these multidimensional characteristics through a universal function with three interpretable network structural characteristics: network density, heterogeneity, and symmetry. Within this network framework, resilience is operationalized as a structural property (baseline structural resilience) reflecting the system's potential buffer against perturbations, distinct from broader sociological definitions of organizational or institutional resilience (disruption-specific resilience outcomes).

To understand resilience in science, and more particularly, in scientific collaboration, and its dynamics due to potential disturbances, we use a large-scale bibliographic database, which covers 19 major scientific fields spanning 30 years, to construct discipline-level coauthorship networks where nodes are scientists and edges are collaboration ties established by coauthored publications (see Materials and methods and Fig. 1A). As a theoretical and conceptual exploration, we specify three classes of stylized perturbations, including author loss, collaboration loss, and publication loss, intended to represent broad categories of possible shocks. We then simulate the effect of these disruptions by stress-test experiments on the coauthorship network. In our coauthorship network setting, a resilient coauthorship network has greater ability to preserve basic connectivity and collaboration intensity under some external perturbations.

To build intuition, an illustrative coauthorship network is constructed to show the scientific collaboration between two prominent scientists in computer science from 2005 to 2014 (Fig. 1A and B). We then apply three hypothetical perturbations to this example: removing the two highly connected scientists, which produces nontrivial structural loss and reconfigures topology (Fig. 1C); severing selected collaborations, which changes the pattern of links (Fig. 1D); and broadly reducing publication volume, which lowers edge weights and overall density while preserving the structure (Fig. 1E).

Our analyses across most disciplines show a consistent pattern in the evolution of resilience over the years, with heterogeneity as the primary structural source of resilience. The result suggests that increasing resilience is not only associated with the higher collaboration intensity of a discipline but also with growing variances among scientists’ collaboration activities. We then perform stress-test experiments by systematically removing nodes or edges under three stylized types of perturbations, which map to broad scenario classes and helps us to identify which structural elements matter most to resilience. Simulated experiments reveal that disciplines with higher baseline resilience exhibit greater overall robustness and that, for any single discipline, targeted removal degrades resilience more severely than random removal as it attacks the components that have the most influence on structural heterogeneity. Specifically, effective shocks to network resilience typically include disruptions to scientists with the highest productivity from collaboration and collaborative links with the highest strength or stability.

To investigate the contributions of individual scientists and collaboration ties to overall structural resilience, we also examine the source of resilience at the microlevel. Accordingly, we evaluate edge-level characteristics, strength and stability, of collaboration ties (22). We define edges with both high strength and stability as “persistent collaboration” (see Materials and methods). Although these ties constitute a small share of all links, targeting such ties produces disproportionately large declines in resilience relative to random perturbation, which indicates such ties are a major structural contributor to resilience across disciplines. Elite scientists (top decile by cumulative publications within field-year) are more likely to maintain such persistent collaborations than nonelites, and the changes in elite status are closely associated with corresponding changes in their persistent collaborations.

Overall, we introduce a network-based framework for assessing the structural resilience of scientific collaboration. Across fields and years, resilience is associated with higher collaboration intensity and, more strongly, with greater variance in collaboration outcomes. At the microscale, we identify persistent collaboration as a structural signature that disproportionately supports resilience despite comprising a minority of ties. This suggests that the current resilient science system tends to rely on greater heterogeneity in collaboration structures, in other words, on a small layer of hubs and persistent ties. Our framework indicates where resilience resides, provides a basis for monitoring and stress-testing collaboration structures, and may inform policy discussions on sustaining the scientific system amid shocks.

Results

Science resilience in different disciplines

We first measured the resilience of coauthorship networks across 19 academic fields from 1985 to 2014 using the Microsoft Academic Graph (MAG) (23, 24) (Materials and methods). The coauthorship networks are constructed with scientists as nodes and coauthorship relationships as edges. The edge weight accounts for the team size to measure collaboration intensity (i.e. the number of coauthors in a publication). For example, in a paper with three authors, the contribution to the edge weight from this coauthored paper is (1/(3−1))=0.5 (Fig. 1A; Materials and methods) (25).

While there are multiple strategies for quantifying resilience, extant theoretical studies have shown that these networked systems, regardless of their underlying dynamics, can be reduced to a one-dimensional equation by a mean-field approach to collapse the high-dimensional interactions within the system, with only the control parameter indicating the system's resilience with various applications (21, 26, 27). This analytical tool allows us to systematically separate the roles of the system's dynamics and topology. This established measurement operationalizes resilience as a universal function of three key structural properties: network density, heterogeneity, and symmetry (Materials and methods). Network density reflects the system's overall connectivity; heterogeneity measures the variance in nodes (i.e. the inequality of connections); and symmetry defines the nature of the interactions (e.g. mutualistic vs. competitive).

In the discipline-level coauthorship networks we construct, each node represents a scientist, and the weighted degree of a node equals the number of collaborative publications authored by the scientist. Here, symmetry is operationalized as the in-out weighted-degree correlation coefficient. Because coauthorship is a mutualistic interaction—coauthors jointly produce a paper—the adjacency matrix of the network is symmetric; accordingly, the symmetry component equals one in our setting.

Density is defined by the average weighted degree of the network. Thus, a “denser” coauthorship network has greater average productivity, suggesting more intensive collaborations within the disciplines (Fig. 2B and C). Heterogeneity is measured by the normalized SDs of the edge weights. The heterogeneity metric H is adopted to measure how skewed the distribution of collaborative research output is toward only a small set of nodes in the network (Materials and methods). If the collaboration outputs are homogeneously distributed among all the scientists, H=0; otherwise, H>0. The more heterogeneous a coauthorship network is, the more extensive gaps exist between the scientists with the most collaborative output and those with the least in terms of the volume of collaborative work each scientist has published. Considering that opportunities and resources for establishing scientific collaboration are necessary for producing research output, such a gap in collaborative output between scientists is a sign of the unequal distribution of productivity among scientists within a discipline (28).

Examining resilience and its underlying topological change from 1985 to 2014 reveals that in our empirical networks, density and heterogeneity turns out to be highly correlated in almost all disciplines and that there is a consistent increase in collaboration strength (density) across all disciplines (Fig. 2A), suggesting heightened scientific collaboration and productivity. Notably, the increase in heterogeneity surpasses that of density growth significantly and predominantly determines levels of resilience, which means that resilient coauthorship networks are accompanied by higher variance of productivity among scientists. A highly heterogeneous coauthorship network is characterized by persistent collaborations among a small set of scientists with intensified collaborative bonds and much looser connections across the broader scientific community (Fig. 2C). Statistically, this indicates that the edge weight distribution is positively skewed. Disciplines like Materials Science are characterized by intensive collaborations around influential “hub” scientists (Fig. 2A) and tend to exhibit a higher level of heterogeneity, while disciplines like Economics tend to form community-based collaboration reflecting lower heterogeneity (Fig. 2D).

Cross-discipline differences in baseline structural resilience reflect differences in structural components—primarily collaboration density and heterogeneity—rather than inherent attributes of the disciplines themselves. In fields with more team-based coauthorship patterns (e.g. larger teams and recurring collaborations), both density and heterogeneity tend to be higher, which under our metric translates into higher baseline structural resilience scores.

Longitudinally, density and heterogeneity show different evolutionary patterns (Figs. 2A, S2, S3, and S5). For example, the increase of heterogeneity is greater than that of density in Psychology and Mathematics, demonstrating that variance in collaborative output grows faster than the average collaboration intensity, while Economics demonstrates the opposite pattern. Moreover, while all disciplines’ networks grow in size through the three decades, given that more new researchers become active in those disciplines, some even have a >10-fold growth (e.g. Political Science, Sociology, and Psychology; Fig. S1). We find that such network growth in size is accompanied by increases in network density and heterogeneity. These cross-field differences suggest that average collaborative productivity scales at different rates by discipline. Moreover, while the distribution of collaborative output generally tends to skew toward scientists with higher collaborative productivity, those coauthorship networks with more intensive growth in size feature more substantial inequality in collaborative output (Figs. 2A and S4) (29).

Response under simulated real-world shocks

Since resilience demonstrates a system's ability to maintain its basic functionality when internal failures or external disturbances occur, we stress-test the resilience of coauthorship networks using a set of stylized perturbations designed to approximate different classes of shocks (Materials and methods). We summarize three forms of perturbations that map onto broad real-world scenarios: author perturbations (loss of scientists) which approximate sudden exits or reassignments of certain scientists, publication perturbations (alterations in publications) which approximate resource contractions that lower output while preserving ties, and collaboration shuffle (changes in the arrangement of ties) which approximate constraints that reconfigure who is linked to whom. In each case, we simulate the perturbation, recalculate the resilience of the perturbed network, and compare it with the baseline. We first implement author perturbations in accordance with exogenous, unexpected events that lead to a loss of scientists in a discipline; specifically, we either randomly delete nodes from the original network (random author loss) or delete them based on their weighted degree (targeted author loss; Fig. 3A). We find that removing a small number of top-ranked nodes based on their weighted degree significantly decreases resilience, and this is much more pronounced than that triggered by random node loss (Fig. 3E and i). This indicates that “super nodes” disproportionately support structural resilience in terms of quantity and connectivity in a coauthorship network. Although certain targeted attacks are unlikely in real life, e.g. prominent scientists often persist through crises, they delineate upper bounds on vulnerability. Random edge removal, which more closely approximates widespread disruptions (e.g. travel restrictions or broad funding cuts), has a smaller effect on resilience.

Just as the resilience of an ecological system often depends on individual efforts in sustaining lasting linkages, resilience in a coauthorship network might be triggered by long-term scientific collaborations that generate many joint publications (30–32). This inspires us to ask: Is the stability or the strength of collaboration more critical in shaping resilience in science (22)? Therefore, we implement various publication perturbations based on the stability and strength of scientific collaboration. Within a selected period (e.g. 10 years), collaboration stability indicates the number of years two authors have published at least one coauthored publication together; an author pair with stability ranking in the top five percent is defined as a stable link (Fig. 3B); collaboration strength of an author pair refers to the weighted sum of their number of coauthored publications each year; an author pair ranking in the top five percent is defined as a strong link (Fig. 3C). By removing random publications or publications generated by stable/strong collaborations, we observe that the difference between random and targeted publication loss, no matter whether they pertain to stable or strong publication loss, is less pronounced compared with author loss in a coauthorship network (Fig. 3F, G, J, and K).

To probe sensitivity to changes in relational patterns, we stress-test the network using collaboration-shuffle perturbations, which model generic system-level disturbances (e.g. global environmental perturbations and science inequity) that reconfigure collaboration structures. More specifically, we reshuffle edge weights (randomization of the collaboration strength between the scientists), randomize the topology (randomization of edges themselves), or remix elite and nonelite ties (reshuffle an elite–elite tie with a nonelite–nonelite tie; Fig. 3D) and find quite different patterns in implementing the three distinct simulation strategies (Fig. 3H and L). While weight shuffle would slightly increase resilience, topology shuffle and elite and nonelite remixing notably decrease resilience because two unconnected scientists in a network may link with each other after reshuffling. In other words, topology shuffles may further make the ecosystem more “fragile.” Moreover, topology shuffle would diminish resilience more substantially than elite–nonelite remixing, which might be attributable to the fact that the former shuffle changes a scale-free network to an Erdős–Rényi network.

In addition to aggregate resilience under simulated perturbations, we also demonstrate how density and heterogeneity are affected by different perturbations across four example disciplines: Computer Science, Economics, Materials Science, and Geology. We find that targeted loss reduces heterogeneity more significantly than random loss in all four disciplines (Fig. 3I–L). This reveals a dual character of resilience: disciplines with higher baseline resilience better withstand random disruptions, yet the same structural features (e.g. heavy-tailed degree/weight distributions or scale-free topology) make them disproportionately sensitive to targeted removal of hub scientists or stable, strong ties. Moreover, in Fig. 3J, as topology shuffles can introduce potential new pairings between scientists, we observe that this exogenous perturbation may diminish gaps and flatten heterogeneity in existing environments; similar patterns are shown in other disciplines as well (Figs. S7–S10). We interpret these results as associations emerging from our simulations rather than as causal effects, and as upper-bound stress tests that identify which structural elements matter most for resilience and which parts of the collaboration system are most load-bearing under stress.

Persistent scientific collaborations as a signature of structural resilience

Consistent with viewing resilience as a structural property that reflects a network's capacity to withstand perturbations, we examine edge-level strength and stability—two properties that capture persistence in collaboration ties. Here, we define strength of collaboration as the fractional sum of coauthored publications between any two scientists and stability of collaboration as the number of years that any pair of scientists have coauthored publications, given a fixed time window of 10 years.

Intuitively, stability and strength yield four types of collaboration links in practice, namely not stable or strong, stable but not strong, strong but not stable, and strong and stable (i.e. persistent) links (Figs. 4A, B and S11; Materials and methods). This taxonomy inspires us to explore how the four types of collaboration links relate to patterns of resilience. We then separately investigate the patterns of the four types of links—most researchers maintain nor stable or strong collaborations that are dominant in all disciplines, but the proportions of other types of links may vary (Fig. S14). We also find a high correlation between having high productivity and maintaining persistent collaborations (Fig. S12). While not strong or stable links dominate all types of edges, elite scientists with a large number of collaborative publications tend to have a greater proportion of persistent links than nonelites (Fig. 4C). Specifically, >15% of collaboration links maintained by highly productive researchers are persistent links, while this is <5% for those with productivity in the bottom 20%.

Particularly, the world's top 1% scientists in productivity, on average, have ∼17% persistent links in their ego-centered coauthorship network, but the percentage decreases to <5% for those ranking in the bottom 20%. Figure 4E shows to what extent resilience would change, compared with that of the original network, when only specific combinations of link types are retained and all others are removed. We clearly observe that, although persistent links account for only a small proportion of all links, they contribute to a notable change in network resilience (>80%). In contrast, the majority of not strong or stable links contribute <20% of original resilience. In particular, Fig. 4D shows that despite the low prevalence of persistent links in most disciplines, networks composed solely of these links can retain a substantial proportion of the original resilience, or even exceed it. For example, in Engineering and Economics, a network with only persistent links retains about 95 and 80% of the original resilience, respectively, while in Materials Science the resilience even exceeds the original, reaching ∼110%.

Do persistent links increase resilience solely through boosting density, heterogeneity, or both? To explore this, the changes in density and heterogeneity are also examined when 1% of each type of edge is removed from the original network, respectively. We see that heterogeneity tends to have a far greater change than density, especially when the network loses 1% of its persistent links, indicating the persistent collaboration is the primary contributor to variances within a discipline (Fig. 4F). These results are robust with different numbers of links removed (Fig. S15).

The mutual growth of persistent collaboration and elite scientists

The highly skewed distribution of weighted degree in the coauthorship network (Fig. S4) indicates its high heterogeneity as well as high variances among scientists in terms of their productivity from collaboration. Our results reveal that resilience is heavily determined by heterogeneity around super nodes (Fig. 2A) and by persistent links (Fig. 4D). Meanwhile, extant studies have found abundant evidence on the positive relationship between persistence and impact (33). To further study the association between super nodes and persistent links, we investigate the collaboration patterns among scientists with different productivities and how changes in productivity relate to maintaining persistent links. This analysis is performed at the individual level. As shown in Fig. 4C, we define elite scientists as those in the top 20% of cumulative productivity within a cohort (Materials and methods) and categorize links according to whether they connect elite–elite, elite–nonelite, or nonelite–nonelite pairs (Fig. 5A).

We observe that collaborations between elite members dominate all persistent links, followed by those between elite and nonelite members, and, lastly, between nonelite members. The dominant proportion of elite–elite ties is also notable for stable-but-not-strong links rather than strong-but-not-stable or not-strong-or-stable links (Fig. 5A and S16), demonstrating the preeminent role of persistent ties in powerful combinations between super nodes (elites)—a symbiosis between super nodes that may generate more variance in the system. The distinction of persistent collaborations is consistent across disciplines and in various cohorts over time (Figs. 5B, C and S17). Accordingly, strong but not stable and not strong or stable ties link more nonelite scientists across disciplines.

We next examine how changes in elite status over time relate to maintaining persistent collaboration. Scientists are grouped into four categories based on their elite status in 1995–2004 and 2005–2014: those who remain elite, those who transition from elite to nonelite, those who rise from nonelite to elite, and those who remain nonelite (Fig. 5D). We see that scientists who remain elite or become elite accumulate and continue to increase their persistent links whereas those who remain or become nonelite maintain fewer or minimal persistent links (Figs. 5E–I and S18). These results show that elite scientists play a major role in sustaining persistent scientific collaboration, and that the growth of elite scientists is accompanied by the growth of persistent collaboration.

Discussion

Science advances through growing collaboration, but this interconnectedness within the scientific community still remains vulnerable to potential disruptions (34). Recent disturbances, such as geopolitical tensions and global health crises (35, 36), have led to changes in collaboration patterns, funding distributions, and research priorities. However, researchers are still unsure as to how these disturbances might affect the science system and to what extent a science community can maintain its function under possible shocks. In this work, we explore science resilience in the context of scientific collaboration by applying a network-theoretic measure of resilience to coauthorship networks and dissecting resilience as structural components: density and heterogeneity. We find that resilience is positively associated with collaboration intensity and more strongly with variance in collaboration patterns. Building on work showing that repeated, high-strength collaborations (“super ties”) correlate with productivity (37) and impact at microscales (38), we extend the lens to system structure: persistent collaborations—long-standing, high-strength ties—form a small but consequential layer that underpins measured resilience. These ties are disproportionately concentrated among top-decile scientists, helping to explain why higher resilience often coincides with greater variance.

In this paper, we extend resilience theory from physics to scholarly networks. Our work does not conflate resilience with general scientific expansion or epistemological variations. Instead, we explicitly dissect resilience as a structural property of collaboration networks, quantified through heterogeneity, density, and symmetry (Eq. 4), which was an approach originally applied to ecological, biological, and infrastructural systems. While productivity disparities may reflect field-specific norms (e.g. lab hierarchies in Materials Science vs. dispersed collaboration in Economics), our key contribution lies in demonstrating that such heterogeneity, measured via network topology, is the primary driver of resilience against perturbations. For instance, we observe that disparities alone do not guarantee resilience; our perturbation experiments reveal that resilience emerges when heterogeneity is anchored by persistent elite collaborations, which buffer targeted shocks. We also notice that epistemological differences contextualize baseline network structures, but our framework shows how these structures differentially respond to disruptions (e.g. topology shuffles harm resilience more in hub-dependent fields like Materials Science than in Economics). Thus, while growth and disciplinary traits shape collaboration patterns, resilience, as operationalized here, arises from how these structures absorb shocks, a mechanism we uniquely quantify and link to persistent ties.

In addition to comparing resilience across different domains, we also examine its temporal dynamics. We show that as a domain matures, growth in size does not automatically lead to increases in heterogeneity. Rather, heterogeneity can evolve at different levels and rates. Our longitudinal analysis demonstrates how domains grow, how heterogeneity changes over time, and how resilience develops in parallel.

Using distinct perturbation strategies, we distinguish collaboration loss (motivated by policy-based constraints) from publication loss (motivated by general funding shortfalls) because they affect network structure versus output in different ways. First, collaboration loss denotes a stylized shock that alters relational patterns by severing existing ties or impeding the formation of new ones. In our simulations, rewiring ties (topology shuffles) produces larger declines in measured resilience than weight-only reductions, consistent with the idea that breaking persistent links—often among top-decile (“elite”) authors—more strongly undermines the network's structural buffer.

Second, our analysis indicates that broad resource contractions are best represented by publication-loss perturbations: uniform reductions in edge weights that lower output without directly altering who collaborates with whom. In simulations, such weight reductions primarily depress network density (average productivity), with comparatively smaller effects on heterogeneity or tie stability. Third, author-loss perturbations (e.g. top-decile scientists exiting) disproportionately erode hubs, reducing both productivity and measured structural resilience. Findings on persistence, elite concentration, variance, and resilience delineate where structural vulnerability is likely to reside; they do not prescribe interventions. For example, the frequent co-movement of higher resilience with greater variance suggests a potential tension between robustness and concentration. Assessing whether—and how—to address that tension (e.g. by broadening access to persistent ties) requires additional data and causal designs beyond the scope of this study. Our goal is not to propose a new theory of inequality but to quantify how unequal collaboration structures are reflected in the resilience and heterogeneity of coauthorship networks.

While this work is a very early quantitative attempt to apply resilience in the context of science of science, we must note that resilience is “not” absolute; it depends on the types and scale of disruptions. What we analyze in this work is “network resilience,” which is more concentrated on the connections “within a network.” The initial resilience functions as a baseline under normal conditions. The disruption-specific resilience differs from baselines, depending on the nature of disruptions. Our operationalization of resilience, quantified in Eq. 4, captures a structural property of collaboration networks under baseline conditions. This metric reflects the system's potential to absorb disturbances but does not predetermine its response to specific shock types.

Dataset issues represent an important limitation of the current study. First, the generalizability of our findings might be limited to English-language scientific publications due to the limited coverage of MAG on non-English works. Second, scientific collaboration patterns may have evolved over the past decade beyond what our empirical data cover, particularly in light of rising team sizes and the emergence of remote and cross-national collaborations (2, 29, 39). While these changes may impact structural features of collaboration, our framework remains applicable as a tool for analyzing resilience in any networked scientific environment. Future work could apply this method to more recent datasets to assess such changes.

There are also many opportunities for further investigation as the exploration of resilience may involve many important, yet intercorrelated, properties in the science system, e.g. interdisciplinarity (40, 41), gender bias (42), impact on scientific careers (43), team assembly (44), and citation dynamics (45). Considering these may unveil the mysteries of the hidden rules and patterns in scientific research through the process of building the linkage between the different scales and dimensions of a system. In our framework, heterogeneity refers to variance in degree distribution and is distinct from inclusivity or diversity. Rising variance in collaboration activity could be consistent with widening gaps among scientists; evaluating any equity or inclusivity implications would require additional demographic and institutional data.

Real-world disruptions, such as funding cuts and geopolitical crises, are complex and often have long-term impacts. Our study adopts a conceptual approach by simplifying these disruptions into three types of perturbations applied to collaboration networks and does not account for the fact that real-world disruptions can be more intertwined, multifaceted, and variable in magnitude. As a theoretical, structural investigation, our stress tests are best viewed as upper-bound experiments that probe how resilience responds to well-defined shocks in collaboration networks. Our framework provides valuable insights for future work on how these conceptual perturbations can be applied and tested in real-world contexts. In particular, follow-up studies could implement empirically grounded perturbations with empirical data (e.g. shrinkage of a discipline (46), deaths of important researchers in a discipline (47), and wars/conflicts between countries/regions (11) that lead to collaboration interruption) that may bridge the gaps between theoretical models and real networks and apply resilience theories to various real-world scenarios. This may serve as important empirical evidence to establish causation on science resilience and to inspire real-world policy changes as well.

Materials and methods

Data

Descriptive metadata of publications used in the current study were obtained from the MAG, a large-scale bibliographic dataset (which is no longer active) that covers a wide range of disciplines (23, 24). This dataset has been widely adopted in previous science of science studies on scientist-level analyses (48). It contains metadata from ∼210 million academic articles (e.g. author, affiliation, venues, and publication fields) and ∼1.7 billion citation relationships from 1800 to 2021.

MAG presents disciplinary relations of publications with a hierarchical structure. Specifically, with some bottom-up clustering algorithms, MAG clusters all disciplines into six distinct levels, namely L0, L1, L2, L3, L4, and L5. L0 is the top level, covering 19 major disciplines: Art, Biology, Business, Chemistry, Computer Science, Economics, Engineering, Environmental Science, Geography, Geology, History, Materials Science, Mathematics, Medicine, Philosophy, Physics, Political Science, Psychology, and Sociology. L0 is the most frequently adopted level in disciplinary analyses on MAG in prior studies (48) due to its representativeness. Nonetheless, this six-level hierarchy does not show a typical tree structure; thus, a specific discipline may have multiple ancestors in this taxonomy. Despite this, we still establish a discipline network where each node represents a discipline (regardless of its level) and an edge indicates that the “ingoing” node is an ancestor of the “outgoing” node in the taxonomy. Based upon the disciplinary network, we define a publication belonging to an L0 discipline if and only if it is also marked as a descendant L1 discipline in MAG. This ensures that the incorporated oeuvres are semantically “core” publications in a discipline and are thus more representative. Note that a publication might be marked as belonging to multiple disciplines at the same level. For example, the publications in “Biomedical Science” are categorized into both Biology and Medicine. In such cases, we assign the publication to each of the matching L0 disciplines, a typical full-counting strategy (49).

With the aforementioned strategy, we are able to identify a set of publications that belong to a specific discipline. For each cohort in a 10-year time window (i.e. 1985–1994, 1995–2004, and 2005–2014), we first select all publications in this cohort in a certain discipline. All coauthors of these publications are defined as nodes and their coauthorship strength within this cohort as edges of the network.

Perturbations

To investigate how coauthorship networks respond to plausible shocks, we initiate our study with preexisting coauthorship networks and examine the changes in measured resilience under simulated disruptions. We first use a recent Computer Science network spanning from 2005 to 2014 as a testbed. In our stress tests, hypothesized researcher attrition is represented by node deletions (author loss), declines in papers by reductions in edge weights (publication loss), and changes in partnership patterns by rewiring ties (collaboration shuffle).

Specifically, for node loss, we remove the top 20% of nodes with the greatest weighted degree for targeted loss and randomly sample 10% of nodes for random loss, along with their associated edges. We have also explored a broad range of removal percentages, from the top 1 to 99%, observing the ensuing resilience of the network's remaining largest component (Fig. S7). We also present the alterations of density and heterogeneity under both random loss and targeted losses across four disciplines (Computer Science, Economics, Materials Science, and Geology).

The publication loss is simulated by removing papers and their associated collaborative links. To account for disruptions that may affect collaboration around specific papers, we assess each paper based on the average level of collaboration strength or stability between each member within the authorship team. The top 20% of papers from teams with the strongest or most stable collaborative links are selectively removed to simulate targeted loss, while a random selection of 20% of papers serves as a baseline comparison. Additionally, we explore resilience by varying the percentage of paper removal (from the top 1 to 99%) and examining differences in density and heterogeneity across various disciplines.

Furthermore, we address network inequality through network rewiring methods. The topology shuffle entails shuffling all the collaborative links among all nodes. This preserves the link weight distribution while randomizing the connections between nodes. In contrast, the weight shuffle method only shuffles the collaboration link weights, keeping the link connections unchanged. We also explore other targeted rewiring methods to remix elite and nonelite collaborations. Specifically, we select pairs of links, established between either two elite nodes or two nonelite nodes. We disconnect these link pairs and establish new links between elite and nonelite nodes. In this context, adjusting the percentages of links to be shuffled means preserving a portion of the existing links and shuffling the remainder using certain techniques.
