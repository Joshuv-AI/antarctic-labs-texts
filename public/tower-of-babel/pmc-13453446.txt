High degree of remission in pediatric severe asthma patients treated with biologics in a real-world setting☆
Authors: Saraid Cerda Reyes, Désirée Larenas-Linnemann, Nidia K. Castillón Benavides
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthma is the most common chronic disease in childhood and although many children with asthma achieve disease control with low- to medium-dose inhaled corticosteroids, 2-10% have severe asthma (SA). Biologics have been available for treatment in adults and adolescents for almost twenty years, and currently 5 biologic therapies have been approved by regulatory agencies for SA in children and/or adolescents.ObjectiveTo describe the evolution and remission rate of pediatric SA patients on biologics, including after switch of biologic.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted January 2023 to January 2024 in SA children (6-18 years) in a tertiary care hospital. American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) criteria classified asthma as severe. Patients who started biologic therapy, or who switched from a previous biologic, were included. Index date is the timepoint at which biologic therapy was started or switched. We compare clinical and biomarker (fractional exhaled nitric oxide, blood eosinophil count [BEC], IgE) data from 12 months pre- with 12 months postindex- date.ResultsSixty children were included (mean age 14.1 ± 3.2 years); 34/60 (56%) female. Our 46 biologic-naïve patients received omalizumab in 21/46 (46%), dupilumab in 23/46 (50%), and mepolizumab in 2 (4%). Fourteen switchers (23%), who presented incomplete improvement on their first biologic, were changed to a second/third biologic including benralizumab and tezepelumab. Our children experienced an average of 2.4 severe exacerbations 12 months pre-index date. Twelve months post-index-date, no child experienced severe exacerbations and 98% (59/60) reached complete, 4- parameter, clinical remission plus a significant reduction of all biomarkers.ConclusionThe use of biologic therapies in pediatric patients with SA allowed for adequate disease control. With correct biologic selection or timely switch when incomplete response, clinical remission, normalization of lung function and a reduction in ICS daily dose was achieved in a very high percentage. Moreover, controlling asthma enables allergen-specific immunotherapy for children with allergic SA.

Introduction

Asthma is the most common chronic disease in children, and although many children with asthma achieve disease control with low-to medium-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), approximately 2%–10% have severe asthma. Severe asthma in childhood places a significant burden on patients and society1,2 with morbidity, mortality, and high costs, making personalized treatments necessary.

ICS, with or without long-acting beta2-agonists (LABAs), are currently the cornerstone of asthma treatment. However, specific biologics have become available that promise to reduce severe exacerbations, limit corticosteroid-associated systemic adverse effects, and improve quality of life.3,4

Biologic therapies have been available for treatment in adults and adolescents for almost 20 years, and currently 5 biologic therapies have been approved by regulatory agencies for severe asthma (SA) in children and/or adolescents: omalizumab, mepolizumab, benralizumab, dupilumab, and tezepelumab. However, despite their positive results in pivotal clinical trials with well-selected patient groups,5 real-world effectiveness data in children are limited to case-reports.6 Consequently, there are still relevant points of debate that should lead to caution when selecting the most appropriate biologic for a child with SA. Apart from effectiveness, safety is essential, and for most existing treatments medium- and long-term data in children are scarce.5,6

Three biologic therapies—omalizumab, mepolizumab, and dupilumab—are approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for children 6 years and older, while benralizumab and tezepelumab are approved for adolescents 12 years and older. All of these agents reduce the rates of SA exacerbations, while their effects on lung function vary per compound. The safety profiles are reassuring, although more long-term data in children are still needed. According to international7 and national8 guidelines, the choice of the most appropriate biologic agent for each individual child is ideally made after: 1) endotyping with biomarkers such as blood eosinophil count (BEC), fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), allergic sensitization, and total IgE levels, 2) a careful evaluation of comorbidities, and 3) other factors contributing to uncontrolled asthma.5 After this comprehensive evaluation, a patient could be eligible for more than 1 biologic therapy.7,8 Other factors such as frequency of administration, possible administration at home, costs and reimbursement issues can then be taken into account to select the biologic to start with. However, in many regions of the world, including Mexico, the choice of which biologic agent to start with is primarily based on availability.

Once a biologic therapy has been initiated, follow-up with clinical parameters, lung function and biomarkers, particularly BEC and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) indicate response. The first effectiveness evaluation is generally done around 4–6 months,7,8 and patients should be switched if there is no response at all. Also, when the response after twelve months is insufficient switching to another option might be more appropriate.

We here present the baseline data and evolution of a cohort of SA children and adolescents on biologic therapy. Among these children some received biologic therapy without adequate response before entering in the study, and we present their data after 1 year of the second (or third) biologic.

Methodology

A retrospective observational study was conducted in patients aged 6–18 years, diagnosed with SA and treated with biologic therapy from January 2023 to January 2024 in in a tertiary care military hospital in Mexico City. Asthma was classified as severe according to the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) criteria (ACT <20, high-dose inhaled corticosteroid plus a second controller plus LAMA, ≥2 exacerbations in the previous year or FEV1 <80% of predicted).9,10

These patients underwent physical examination, pulmonary function testing, FeNO, and a complete atopy assessment including skin prick testing with aeroallergens. Symptom control was assessed using the Asthma Control Test (ACT) or the Childhood Asthma Control Test (C-ACT) depending on the participant's age. Standardized equipment was used to assess variable airflow obstruction by spirometry, including response to bronchodilator (post 200-400mcg albuterol by metered dose inhaler plus spacer). The standard reference equations of Perez Padilla JR were used to determine the % predicted values per age, sex and stature, as such correcting for pediatric and pubertal lung-growth. FeNO was measured using the standardized approach, with 10-s or 6-s testing depending on age, with cut-offs for high eosinophilic inflammation >35 and > 50 ppb and low-medium inflammation >20 and > 25 ppb, respectively. Total IgE and allergen-specific IgE levels were measured against Alternaria tenuis, Aspergillus fumigatus, cat dander, Cladosporium herbarum, cockroach, common ragweed, Helianthus annuus, taraxacum, Dermatophagoides farinae and pteronyssinus, dog, cat, mixed grass, ash, cypress, oak, privet, Phleum pratense, Cynodon dactylon and Ligustrum vulgare using ImmunoCAP and skin prick tests. Aeroallergen sensitization was considered positive with a positive skin prick test or a specific IgE level ≥0.35 kU/L. Blood eosinophil counts (BEC) were also quantified.

Biomarkers, except for IgE related tests, were not only determined at the start of the biologic but also at 12 months, as well as pre- and post-bronchodilator spirometry, and (C-)ACT.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses shall be presented for age-groups (children 6–11 years versus adolescents 12–18 years) and for the patients who switched biologic.

Primary and secondary outcome variables

The primary outcome measure was clinical remission of SA in pediatric patients aged 6–18 years after 12 months of biologic use or for switchers of the last biologic use, for the 1, 2, 3, and 4 domains of remission, according to the criteria used by the International Severe Asthma Registry (ISAR).11 The 4 domains of clinical remission are absence of severe exacerbations, no long-term oral corticosteroid (LTOCS) use, well or partly controlled symptoms (in our study for both ACT and C-ACT ≥20, as reported12) and pre-bronchodilator FEV1 ≥80%.

Secondary outcome measures included biomarker behavior (eosinophils, FeNO), and lung function before and after biologic use.

Statistical analysis

Results shall be presented in part descriptively. For exacerbation rates, ACT values, FeNO and blood eosinophil counts (BEC) Wilcoxon signed-rank tests shall be used to compare the patient values pre- and post-biologic or biologic switch, considering a p < 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

Sixty children aged 7–18 years were included (mean age 14.1 ± 3.2 years), with a female predominance (34/60; 56%). The mean age at onset of asthma was 5.8 ± 2.4 years). These children experienced an average of 2.4 SA exacerbations during the 12 months prior to starting treatment with a biologic and/or before switching to the new biologic. One-third was on triple therapy, and one-third on Maintenance and Reliever Therapy with budesonide-formoterol, but none of our children were on LTOCS. Our population presented a high degree of aeroallergen sensitization, with positivity to ≥4 aeroallergens in 48/60 (80%). All but 1 patient, were diagnosed as allergic asthma (positive specific IgE plus symptoms on exposure) and 56/60 (93%) received simultaneous subcutaneous allergen immunotherapy with allergoids (Inmunotek®). Evolution of asthma at 12 months of the last biologic treatment for the whole cohort

The annual number of exacerbations decreased from a mean of 2.43 to 0.00 events per patient. ACT/C-ACT scores improved by an average of 7.87, which is far above the minimal clinical importan difference.3 Both improvements were highly statistically significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.0001). These changes were consistent between the pediatric and adolescent age-groups. The reduction in FeNO and BEC, supporting a substantial decrease in type 2 airway inflammation, are consistent with the observed clinical improvements in exacerbations and ACT scores (Fig. 2 and Table 2). It is important to note that this improvement in lung function was accompanied by a reduction in daily ICS dose: before the introduction of the biologic and/or the biologic switch, the daily dose of inhaled budesonide in these patients was 864 ± 171 mcg, and at 12 months of biologic treatment the dose of budesonide decreased by 53%–461 ± 117 mcg daily.

Subgroup analysis: baseline and evolution of biologics switchers

Fourteen patients (23%) had already received a biologic but presented incomplete improvement as freely judged by the investigator taking into account exacerbations, asthma control and lung-function testing, and in some patients biomarkers. They were switched from 1 biologic to another. Patients switched from omalizumab to another biologic after an average of 4.5 years, with the exception of 1 patient who switched after 7 years; from mepolizumab to tezepelumab after 3 and from mepolizumab to dupilumab or dupilumab to tezepelumab after 2 years. Their mean age was 13.42 ± 4 years; eleven were adolescents and 3 were between 7 and 11 years old. The switches were from omalizumab to dupilumab in 7/14 patients, from omalizumab to mepolizumab in 2/14 patients; switches from omalizumab to benralizumab, from omalizumab to tezepelumab, from mepolizumab to dupilumab, or from mepolizumab to tezepelumab or dupilumab to tezepelumab were each in 1 patient. One patient was switched twice, from omalizumab to dupilumab to tezepelumab. Their most important pre-post last biologic parameters are depicted in Fig. 4. Safety of biologic herapies

Among the adverse events reported in our cohort, the most frequent ones were pain at the injection site (8.3% of patients) with dupilumab (n = 5); headache (3.3%) with omalizumab;2 and headache1 with mepolizumab.1 No reports of conjunctivitis were documented with dupilumab.

Discussion

We demonstrated that the use of biologic therapies in a cohort of 60 pediatric patients with SA allowed for adequate disease control. Moreover, with timely switch of biologics in case of incomplete response, complete, 4-domain clinical remission was achieved in all but 1 patient, with normalization of lung function even though the daily ICS dose was reduced. This is the first report on clinical remission in a large pediatric SA cohort. As we decided to apply the clinical remission criteria to this pediatric age-group, we included the relative lung-function parameter ≥80% predicted, that accounts for lung-growth, and not the absolute 100 mL cut-off. Moreover, we used the childhood C-ACT in our younger ones, but cut-off for well control is the same as in the adult ACT, ≥20.12 These clinical achievements were accompanied by clear reductions in the both T2 biomarkers: BEC and FeNO; here again in FeNO cut-offs were adjusted for the 6- or 10 s test. We did document total IgE in some patients and observed equally a reduction, but as the measurement of total IgE -that not only measures free but also (omalizumab) bound IgE-is affected by omalizumab, we refrained from further reporting. The male-female distribution in our 2 age-groups is in line with cross-sectional studies, that have shown that SA is more common in boys than in girls, but more common in women than in men.13

Among the comorbidities, allergic rhinitis was found to be the most common,14 followed by atopic dermatitis. This is consistent with the PANDA study,15 a group of 72 children with SA who presented up to 70% comorbidity of allergic rhinitis, and atopic dermatitis in 50%. Thirteen percent of our patients presented only asthma, but solely 1 of those had no specific IgE.

Severe exacerbations are a factor that influences the initiation of biologics in SA. Our patients presented an average of 2.4 severe exacerbations the year prior to the use of biologics and/or switches. After 12 months of biologics, the severe exacerbations decreased to zero. This is an observation of high clinical relevance as the frequency and magnitude of SA exacerbations profoundly impact the progression of the disease, as measured by the loss of lung function in adults16, 17, 18 and children,19 patient-reported outcomes and quality of life. Exacerbations represent episodes of severe airflow limitation that do not respond to standard rescue therapy and require additional treatment, including oral corticosteroid therapy, unscheduled visits to an urgent care provider or even hospitalizations, and are associated with an accelerated decline in lung function.18,20 Asthma exacerbations can occur across the spectrum of asthma severity but are particularly common in patients with a history of prior exacerbations, those receiving high-intensity asthma therapy, and those with active type 2 airway inflammation, as reflected by elevated blood eosinophil counts and FeNO.21 Recent nasal transcriptomic modular analysis during exacerbations in children with SA showed an upregulation of eosinophilic pathways linked to exacerbations, that were downregulated after treatment with mepolizumab. However, upregulation of certain epithelial pathways was also linked to an augmented exacerbation frequency. These epithelial pathways were previously identified to underlie aspects of both viral and pollution triggered exacerbations in urban children; in the MUPPITS-2 trial these were not improved in the mepolizumab group.22

Among our pediatric patients, most received dupilumab, which targets IL-13 and exerts deeper effects on airway epithelium; omalizumab was next, having the longest market availability worldwide. Consequently, it was initially our center's only used biologic, though not optimal for all cases. Also, tezepelumab blocking more upstream the effects of alarmins on inflammation just became available. Consequently, we were able to strictly apply our treatment-selection-flow-diagram (Fig. 1) only very recently. None of the patients most recently started on biologics has needed a switch till now, but patients previously started on omalizumab (or some on mepolizumab), did, with very good post-switch results. Even so, given asthma's heterogeneity, a given biologic may be suboptimal for some patients and close, multidomain follow-up of remission is essential, since patients receiving biologics may need switching to optimize outcomes.7,23 Given the low numbers per biologic, our study does not allow us to make inter-biologic comparisons on outcomes.

A high proportion of pediatric SA is allergic; in our cohort, up to 80% were sensitized to ≥4 aeroallergens. Although guidelines in the past listed SA as a contraindication to allergen immunotherapy (AIT), newer evidence supports AIT as an add-on after disease control. Because AIT is the only therapy with proven long-term, disease-modifying effects, the treating physician can consider initiating it once control is achieved.8 AIT may reduce symptoms and medication use and improve quality of life, with benefits persisting after discontinuation.24, 25, 26 In our program, AIT was begun 6 months after attaining control with biologics in allergic asthmatics (almost all children, as presented above), aiming for tolerance to aeroallergens, since allergens commonly trigger exacerbations.7

In pediatrics, persistently low lung function may predict poor adult lung function and an early COPD-like phenotype, yet it does not predict that SA will fail to resolve.27 In our cohort, biologic therapy produced striking gains at 12 months: pre-bronchodilator FEV1 increased 997 mL and post-bronchodilator FEV1 714 mL—substantially exceeding typical adult biologic responses, likely because children add the effect of lung growth. Adult pivotal trials report much smaller mean improvements versus placebo: benralizumab 125 ± 159 mL,28,29 mepolizumab 100–183 mL,30,31 dupilumab 200 ± 320 mL(32,33) and in those with asthma onset <20 years, tezepelumab 290 mL at 52 weeks.34

The Severe Asthma Research Program 3, pediatric cohort identified time-varying factors (age, obesity, exacerbation frequency) that predict FEV1 trajectories. A significant age–sex interaction emerged: in girls, exacerbations did not affect FEV1, whereas in boys, 1–2 exacerbations in the prior 12 months were associated with −20 mL/year (95% CI, −39 to 2) and ≥3 exacerbations 12–24 months earlier with −34 mL/year (95% CI, −61 to −7). The authors concluded that, across severe and non-severe pediatric asthma, several clinical factors predict FEV1 over time, and recurrent exacerbations confer higher risk for lower FEV135— findings echoed by other groups linking frequent exacerbations to low-function trajectories.27,36

At baseline, our children used high-dose ICS; with biologics, they achieved a 50% reduction in daily ICS dose at 12 months and required no oral steroid bursts over 12 months. Although ICS are generally safe in children, higher doses raise concerns —adrenal insufficiency, growth retardation, behavioral changes, weight gain— supporting earlier biologic use to limit long-term toxicity.37 Consistently, in the PANDA cohort, steroid toxicity was a key reason pediatric pulmonologists initiated biologics, despite this not being included in current GINA prescribing criteria.15

Baseline BEC ≥300 cells/μL, associated with higher exacerbation rates, were common in our cohort and fell markedly after 12 months of biologics. Adult trials (SIROCCO, CALIMA) similarly show higher exacerbation rates when BEC >300 cells/μL versus placebo.38 Eosinophils are central to poor asthma control; another T2-high marker, FeNO ≥25 ppb, also correlates with more frequent exacerbations.39 Again, most of our children had high FeNO at baseline, returning to normal levels after biologic treatment, aligning biomarker improvement with clinical gains.

Complete clinical remission requires satisfying all 4 core criteria for ≥12 months.11 Harm in asthma includes morbidity from comorbidities and oral corticosteroids, and airway remodeling leading to fixed obstruction; once obstruction is irreversible, complete remission might become unattainable. In our cohort, up to 100% achieved remission in up to 3 domains, and 98% attained complete four-domain remission (n = 59). These findings support early biologic intervention to maximize remission and prevent remodeling; biologic treatment should not be delayed when remission is the goal.11,21

Adolescence may further favor resolution: rising androgens in both sexes, together with growth-related increases in airway caliber and chest-wall strength, improve physiology.11,40 In a longitudinal cohort, about half of the children with SA no longer met severe criteria after 3 years; from 2012 to 2015, among patients aged 6–17 years, the proportion classified as severe declined steadily. Severity decreased equally in males and females, and peripheral eosinophilia predicted resolution.14 Thus, part of our favorable outcomes likely reflects the natural history of adolescent asthma in addition to biologic effects.

Conclusions

Here, the use of well-selected biologic therapies (in a quarter after switching from a previous biologic) in pediatric patients with SA allowed for adequate disease control, with a decrease in the rate of severe exacerbations and thus prevented the deterioration of lung function and the possible development of corticosteroid-related comorbidities. Clinical remission was achieved in a very considerable percentage, with normalization of lung function and a reduction in ICS daily dose. The possibility to administer AIT in children with allergic asthma, as soon as their asthma was under control, is another positive finding. Based on the rate of complete clinical remission, children and adolescents seem to obtain a higher benefit from biologics for SA than adults.
