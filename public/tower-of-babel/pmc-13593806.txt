A Neutral Comparison of Multivariate Hypothesis Tests for Parallel Group Designs in Rare Disease Settings
Authors: Martin Geroldinger, Martin Laimer, Verena Wally, Johann W Bauer, Georg Zimmermann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMultivariate endpoints are increasingly used in clinical studies on rare diseases, where sample sizes tend to be small and distributional assumptions are often violated. In such settings, commonly applied parametric multivariate methods, such as classical MANOVA approaches, may suffer from inflated type‐I error or reduced statistical power. Therefore, a neutral simulation‐based comparison of four methodologically different approaches for the analysis of multivariate outcomes in parallel group designs was conducted. The four approaches included the nonparametric R package npmv (using permutation and randomization resampling for small samples), a semiparametric MANOVA approach from the R package MANOVA.RM with both a Wald‐type statistics (WTS) and a modified ANOVA‐type statistics (MATS) using all available variants including also bootstrap approaches, a classical parametric MANOVA, and multiple univariate Mann–Whitney tests with Holm correction. The simulation framework was informed by empirical distributions derived from anonymized clinical data and clinical expert guidance. Two clinically realistic effect types were mainly evaluated: (i) percent‐responder effects for wound size reduction and (ii) additional location shifts in pain and pruritus visual analog scales (VAS) scores. In addition, scenarios are examined in which classical assumptions such as multivariate normality and homogeneity of covariance matrices are violated. The sample sizes considered ranged from 12 to 360 subjects. Both balanced and unbalanced designs were evaluated, with the objective of illustrating potential limitations of methods when applied to small samples. Outcomes were assessed in terms of type‐I error control and statistical power. Overall, npmv, the MATS using especially parametric bootstrap within the semiparametric MANOVA.RM package maintained reliable type‐I error control, whereas the semiparametric MANOVA.RM approach with the WTS using its asymptotic approximation showed very liberal type‐I error behavior to almost no type‐I error control in small sample scenarios, and especially in unbalanced settings. Bootstrap approaches especially of the WTS substantially improved type‐I error control compared with the asymptotic WTS. The nonparametric npmv approach achieved good power values, especially in small samples, as did the modified ATS approach using parametric bootstrap from the MANOVA.RM package, which achieved very similar results. The univariate Mann–Whitney U test with Holm correction tended to yield conservative results for small samples; however, despite the univariate nature of this approach, it achieved a high and comparable level of statistical power in many scenarios. Classical MANOVA also yielded good results in certain scenarios; however, it exhibited rather liberal type‐I error rates, primarily in heteroscedastic scenarios and unbalanced designs, which can be explained by a violation of the assumption of homoscedastic covariance matrices. These results highlight the strengths and limitations of a multi‐aspect analysis in parallel group designs especially for small sample settings, as common in rare diseases, and provide guidance for the analysis in rare disease research.Keywords: MANOVA.RM, multi‐aspect modeling, multivariate statistics, neutral comparison, npmv, parallel‐group design, rare diseases

Introduction

In clinical research on rare diseases, multivariate endpoints are common. These include multiple correlated outcomes, such as metric, ordinal and binary data (e.g., wound surface area, pain intensity and pruritus), which are clinically relevant and assessed in each patient. Analyzing such endpoints jointly is advantageous as it preserves the correlation structure, avoids multiplicity inflation and may increase efficiency compared with multiple univariate tests. Classical parametric multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) are widely used, but performance relies on strong assumptions, including multivariate normality, equality of covariance matrices, and sphericity [1]. These requirements are frequently violated in small samples or in data combining metric and ordinal‐like endpoints, overall restrictive and often hard to verify [2], especially in rare disease research, where heteroscedasticity, skewed distributions, and mixed‐scale outcome types are prevalent. As a result, parametric multivariate procedures may produce misleading or unstable results. Nonparametric and semiparametric multivariate procedures offer attractive robust alternatives, particularly when parametric modeling assumptions are not met. Among such approaches, the npmv package implements rank‐based multivariate tests with permutation or randomization inference especially for small samples [2], while MANOVA.RM provides semiparametric Wald‐type (WTS) and modified ANOVA‐type (MATS) statistics using resampling techniques (including wild and parametric bootstrap) [3, 4]. It is noteworthy that both approaches have been proposed as robust options for small samples. However, comparative performance in realistic clinical scenarios remains insufficiently studied. Furthermore, it is becoming increasingly important not only to publish new statistical methods, but also to compare existing ones. This is also supported by the work of Heinze et al. [5], proposing four phases of methodological research in order to establish a robust evidence base for biostatistical methods. The present manuscript is congruent with the proposed phase III, in which, as stated by Heinze et al. [5], the investigation of the performance of methods in a wide range of settings is undertaken, and, if applicable, for different types of outcomes.

Therefore, the present work provides a neutral simulation‐based framework of four different statistical approaches for multivariate data in a parallel group design: (1) the nonparametric npmv package (with permutation tests for small samples), (2) the semiparametric MANOVA.RM package (with both the Wald‐type statistics (WTS) and modified ANOVA‐type statistics (MATS) with bootstrap approaches), (3) classical parametric MANOVA, and (4) multiple univariate Mann–Whitney U‐tests with Holm correction. In the simulation setting and in the general framework, we follow neutral comparison key principles proposed, for example, in Boulesteix, Lauer & Eugster [6] and [7] as closely as possible. The authors of this manuscript are statistical and clinical experts who are part of the servEB project consortium (funded by Land Salzburg, WISS 2025 project ‘ServEB’ (20102/F2300645‐FPR) and DEBRA Austria), aimed at developing statistical guidance regarding appropriate statistical methods for the analysis of multi‐aspect outcomes in Epidermolysis Bullosa (EB) research and overall in rare diseases. Simulations were informed by a clinical data basis from 2024 and 2025, including anonymized data for pain and pruritus and clinical expertise for estimating appropriate wound sizes. We considered percent‐responder effects for wound healing (wound size and wound closure as outcome variable), as used in many clinical trials for EB, and clinically meaningful shifts in pain and pruritus VAS scores. Our goal was to evaluate type‐I error control and statistical power under different distributions and effect patterns typical for rare‐disease trials. In Section 2, we present the motivation and insights in this project and examples from current rare disease research. This part is followed by an outline of key concepts of the aforementioned statistical methods (Section 3). The simulation settings and the corresponding results are reported in Section 4. To demonstrate the practical application of the methods and findings, Section 5 presents a simulation‐based illustration. Finally, Section 6 contains a discussion of key results and summarizes main conclusions of this manuscript.

Motivation

In previous studies, also motivated by EB research, [8] derived univariate statistical recommendations regarding which statistical method should be used for ordinal [9], binary, and metric [10] target variables in univariate crossover designs. Particularly in rare disease research, the focus often shifts from a single outcome to a composite of multiple clinical endpoints, which are analyzed in conjunction with an explanatory variable such as group assignment (treatment versus placebo) [11].

Therefore, the methodological motivation for this work arises from the specific characteristics of rare disease studies, especially from EB research namely:

i.the presence of multivariate outcomes with a possible dependency structure between variables (e.g., correlation structure between pain and pruritus, pain and wound size, etc.), and

the presence of multivariate outcomes with a possible dependency structure between variables (e.g., correlation structure between pain and pruritus, pain and wound size, etc.), and

ii.the small sample sizes typically emerging in rare disease contexts.

Under these conditions, it would be beneficial to compare existing statistical approaches in terms of their strengths and weaknesses, not only to recommend promising methods, but also to assess which methods work best in which scenarios, and whether classical parametric methods with strict assumptions are robust enough for small sample sizes.

No real clinical trial data were used for the underlying project. The data were derived from routinely collected, fully anonymized clinical data and expert‐based assessments of EB lesions, including typical wound sizes and associated patient‐reported outcomes such as pain and pruritus. Based on these data, realistic simulations of wound size, pain, and pruritus as a trivariate outcome were performed to generate artificial study data focusing on the predefined key characteristics.

Methods

In this manuscript, we considered the essential criteria for “neutral comparisons” as defined in [6, 12] and [7] wherever appropriate. The methods that are subsequently employed in the simulation studies are first introduced theoretically, differences and similarities are examined neutrally, and a common simulation framework is defined. The emphasis is on already existing and rationally well justifiable methods, without having a preferred analysis method specified in advance [6, 9].

In order to compare the statistical performance of different approaches suitable for analyzing multivariate data, we include four methods in our neutral comparison simulation study:

We focus on the analysis of data from a parallel group design (e.g., one wound per patient in one respective treatment phase j∈{treatment,placebo}) and with multivariate outcomes (e.g., trivariate outcome comprising wound size, pain, and pruritus).

Although all four procedures address suitable treatment comparisons for multi‐aspect settings, it is important to note that they do not test identical null hypotheses. Classical MANOVA and the semiparametric procedures implemented in MANOVA.RM (WTS and MATS, including wild and parametric bootstrap) assess hypotheses on multivariate mean vectors, that is, contrasts of expected values. In contrast, the rank‐based procedure implemented in npmv is based on multivariate relative effects and tests equality of the multivariate outcome distributions among groups. In contrast, the multiple Mann–Whitney approach tests separate marginal hypotheses for each outcome variable with multiplicity adjustment using Holm correction. Consequently, differences in type‐I error control and statistical power should always be interpreted with regard to the respective null hypothesis addressed by each method.

Nonparametric Multivariate Method: npmv

The R package npmv described in more detail in Burchett, Ellis, Harrar & Bathke [2] implements an easy‐to‐use nonparametric rank‐based framework for multivariate data without requiring multivariate normality in the data, unlike in classical multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) settings. Different response variables may even be measured on different scales, p values are calculated for overall tests, and multiple testing algorithms are used to control the family‐wise error rate [2]. The package provides four rank‐based multivariate test statistics: the ANOVA‐type statistic, Wilks' Lambda‐type statistic, Lawley–Hotelling‐type statistic, and Bartlett–Nanda–Pillai‐type statistic. In this work, the ANOVA‐type statistic is considered in conjunction with permutation and randomization tests, for sample sizes below N=30, which have been shown to perform particularly well in limited sample settings. In particular, permutation tests are recommended when the total sample size satisfies N<10 [2]. In the simulation study, 1000 and 10 000 permutation runs were used for evaluation. Due to the limited benefits observed in the results and significantly greater computing power, only 1000 permutation runs were included in the results of this study.

Semiparametric MANOVA: MANOVA.RM

The R package MANOVA.RM [3] provides a general and flexible semiparametric framework for the analysis of multivariate factorial and repeated‐measures designs; see Bathke et al. [14] for an overview of applications. The methodology is particularly well suited for situations in which standard MANOVA assumptions, such as multivariate normality and homoscedastic covariance structures, are violated. In this manuscript, we focus on parallel‐group designs, therefore using only implemented MANOVA functions.

However, for small to moderate sample sizes, this asymptotic approximation may be inaccurate, as the WTS requires inversion of the matrix TV^T⊤ and thus implicitly assumes that this matrix is of full rank. This assumption may be violated in high‐dimensional settings, under linear dependencies among outcome components, or in limited sample sizes. In such cases, the WTS may become unstable or undefined [3]. Under the null hypothesis H0:Cμ=0 and suitable regularity conditions, the MATS converges asymptotically to a weighted sum of independent χ12 random variables [4]. Since the limiting distribution depends on unknown nuisance parameters, direct use of this asymptotic distribution is typically infeasible in practice. Therefore, MANOVA.RM employs resampling‐based procedures, most notably a parametric bootstrap, to approximate the null distribution of the MATS or the WTS. These approaches have been shown to substantially improve small‐sample performance in both MANOVA and repeated‐measures designs [4, 15].

Simulation studies indicate that the MATS exhibits competitive or superior finite‐sample performance compared to the WTS, particularly in scenarios with heterogeneous covariance matrices, high‐dimensional outcomes, or nearly singular covariance structures. When the sample sizes are sufficiently large, both procedures tend to yield comparable results [3, 4].

Consequently, employing both WTS and MATS within the same analytical comparison framework allows for a systematic comparison of their empirical behavior across different design settings and covariance structures, especially in finite‐sample contexts where asymptotic approximations may be unreliable. For all simulation scenarios, the WTS was primarily evaluated using its asymptotic approximation, while the assessment of the MATS was based on the parametric bootstrap method, in accordance with the recommendations of Friedrich & Pauly [4], Konietschke, Bathke, Harrar & Pauly [15]. To provide a more comprehensive comparison, additional simulations using the implemented wild and parametric bootstrap approaches for the WTS and the wild bootstrap approach for the MATS were conducted and the results are briefly discussed in this manuscript and fully presented in the Appendix.

Classical MANOVA

As a fully parametric method, classical multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) is applied. Classical MANOVA tests for equality of mean vectors across groups and relies on several assumptions, including multivariate normality of the residuals and homogeneity of covariance matrices across groups.

Univariate Multiple Mann–Whitney U‐Test With Holm Correction

As a univariate nonparametric approach, Mann–Whitney U‐tests for independent samples are applied separately to each outcome variable. This procedure does not rely on distributional assumptions and is therefore robust to deviations from multivariate normality and heteroskedasticity. Let Yij(k) denote the k‐th component of the multivariate response vector Yij for subject j in group i∈{1,2}, where 1 and 2 denote the treatment and placebo groups, respectively. where Fk(1) and Fk(2) denote the marginal distribution functions of Yij(k) in the treatment and placebo groups, respectively [20, 21]. Since multiple outcome‐specific hypotheses are tested simultaneously, control of the family‐wise error rate is ensured by applying the Holm correction procedure to adjust the resulting p‐values. The global null hypothesis is rejected if at least one of the adjusted p‐values falls below the pre‐specified significance level α [22]. While this approach is robust and straightforward to implement, it ignores the potential dependence structure among the outcome variables and may suffer from a loss of power compared to multivariate procedures that explicitly account for the joint distribution of the outcomes.

Results

In what follows, the results of the above‐mentioned simulation study are reported for the respective four statistical approaches. In particular, the npmv package using the ANOVA‐type statistics, the semiparametric MANOVA.RM package with both the MATS using wild and parametric bootstrap and the WTS using its asymptotic approximation and wild and parametric bootstrap, the classical parametric MANOVA, and the univariate Mann–Whitney U‐Test with Holm correction were evaluated.

The type‐I error results are reported in Table A1. Table A2 shows the supplementary results for the respective bootstrap variants of WTS and MATS from the MANOVA.RM package. Note that the additional analyses were carried out to illustrate the variety of option of the MANOVA.RM approach and to briefly review the overall results. Across all homoscedastic scenarios and sample size conditions, the nonparametric npmv method, the semiparametric MATS using its bootstrap approaches of the MANOVA.RM package, and the classical parametric MANOVA exhibited error rates very close to the nominal level of 5%, with only small deviations in some scenarios. In particular, the permutation test possibility within npmv showed even higher stability for small sample sizes (N=12). In addition, it was possible to clearly demonstrate that the developed semiparametric MATS in MANOVA.RM exhibited very good type‐I error control compared to the very liberal behavior of the WTS using its asymptotic approximation within the MANOVA.RM package. In the smallest sample sizes (N=12), especially in unbalanced designs, the type‐I error was inflated to levels that substantially exceeded the nominal rate, reflecting the liberal nature of the WTS‐based statistic under very limited and unbalanced sample sizes. However, this inflation diminished with increasing sample size, and for moderate to large N the error rate approached the intended 5% level. Furthermore, the use of the respective wild or parametric bootstrap approach of the WTS within MANOVA.RM also improved the type‐I error rate and provided better control, as shown in Table A2. The univariate Mann–Whitney U‐Test with Holm correction was found to be conservative when sample size is limited. For larger N, the method stabilized and produced error rates closer to the nominal level.

The power results for the responder‐based effect scenario (Scenario 1) and the shift scenario (Scenario 2) are reported in Tables A3 and A5, respectively. In Scenario 1, where treatment differences were induced solely through differing responder probabilities of wound closure, the nonparametric npmv procedure showed uniformly stable and competitive power across all designs and, in many of the smaller sample settings, exceeded the power of the classical MANOVA and the semiparametric MATS bootstrap approaches within MANOVA.RM. The WTS approach using its asymptotic approximation within MANOVA.RM showed very high power values even in small sample sizes; however, this is explained by the inflated type‐I error in those cases. More stable results were obtained with the WTS bootstrap variants, as better type‐I error control was observed with adequate power (see also Table A4 and Table A6). The univariate nonparametric Mann–Whitney U‐test with Holm correction, although not explicitly multivariate, achieved surprisingly high power values in many settings even for small samples.

For Scenario 2, which introduced, in addition to the percentage responder effects described in scenario 1, systematic location shifts in pain and pruritus, similar results could be found. For the smallest samples, the WTS approach using its asymptotic approximation within MANOVA.RM again showed high empirical power, while having a very liberal to uncontrolled type‐I error behavior. As in Scenario 1, the nonparametric npmv ANOVA‐type statistics and the MATS bootstrap approach in MANOVA.RM provided already robust power performance in small to medium sample settings. By contrast, classical parametric MANOVA and the univariate Mann–Whitney U‐test lagged noticeably in the smallest settings and even in balanced designs. For N≥150, all multivariate procedures achieved power values of (almost) one across all effect sizes, demonstrating that the differences between methods are practically negligible in sufficiently large parallel‐group trials.

To further emphasize this performance, Figure A1 and Figure A2 show the respective power curves for all tested sample sizes and power scenarios 1 and 2, in balanced and unbalanced design, respectively. In power scenario 2, only the shift effect A (𝒟∼𝒩(2,1)) is visualized as an illustration.

In addition, a third scenario was carried out, assuming a higher correlation (ρ=0.7) between pain and pruritus than in the previous scenarios. The simulation results (see Table A7) were consistent with the original analysis, and can be found in the appendix. With larger sample sizes or stronger effects, the differences between the simulated methods become marginal.

Under Scenario 4, which assesses deviations from multivariate normality, the overall ranking of the investigated methods remained largely unchanged (see Tables A10, A11, A12, A13, A14, A15). The npmv procedure consistently maintained appropriate type‐I error control and achieved the highest power across most scenarios. The asymptotic WTS approach continued to exhibit even higher inflated type‐I error rates in small samples despite its comparatively high power, whereas the bootstrap‐based WTS approaches and the bootstrap‐based MATS variants of the MANOVA.RM package provided a better balance between type‐I error control and statistical power. The MATS parametric bootstrap approach showed superior results in most settings compared to WTS bootstrap variants and similar results compared to npmv.

The results from Scenario 5 (see Tables A16, A17, A18, A19), which examines violations of the homogeneity of the covariance matrices, provide evidence that both the classical parametric MANOVA and the asymptotic WTS in MANOVA.RM can lead to a considerable inflation of type‐I error rates when considering heteroscedastic scenarios. This liberal behavior is particularly evident with small sample sizes and in unbalanced designs. The other methods yielded results similar to those described above. Once again, npmv in particular, as well as MATS using parametric bootstrap in MANOVA.RM, demonstrated particularly good power results while maintaining the nominal type‐I error level. These findings indicate that the main conclusions are robust to substantial deviations from multivariate normality or heterogeneous covariance matrices.

Illustration

This section illustrates the application of the considered statistical methods to a synthetic dataset reflecting a realistic clinical scenario, based on the simulation setup outlined above, with a total of N=36 observations. This corresponds to a moderate sample size setting of the simulation study. The sample consists of two balanced groups: a treatment group (n=18) and a placebo group (n=18). The multivariate outcome analyzed by all statistical procedures consisted of the continuous post‐treatment wound size, pain score, and pruritus score. Although a binary responder status was generated during data simulation to define treatment effects on wound healing, this variable was not included in any of the statistical models. The wound size post‐treatment was simulated using the first “percent responder” setting used for the power simulations in scenario 1: in the treatment group, 80% of patients were considered healed, whereas in the placebo group only 20% were healed. Furthermore, the scaled χ2‐distribution was used for wound size generation as outlined above. For healed patients, the wound size was reduced to approximately 10% of the baseline value plus a small normally distributed noise (𝒩(0,2)). This corresponds to the clinically relevant endpoint of wound closure (see Ikeda et al. [23] for more clinical details). Pain and pruritus scores were generated from a bivariate normal distribution with mean 5, standard deviation 3, and correlation 0.4. In this example, no additional shifts were applied. From a practical perspective, the differing conclusions obtained by the multivariate procedures should not be interpreted as contradictory, but rather as a consequence of the different null hypotheses being tested. In this example, treatment primarily affects the probability of wound healing, resulting in a responder‐driven change in the distribution of post‐treatment wound size rather than a simple multivariate location shift. Consequently, the rank‐based npmv procedure, which targets global distributional differences, detects a significant treatment effect. In contrast, the semiparametric MANOVA.RM package and classical MANOVA evaluate hypotheses on multivariate mean vectors and therefore provide weaker evidence in this setting. This finding aligns with the underlying simulation mechanism, in which the treatment predominantly affects the probability of healing and thus induces a large reduction in wound size (percent responder setting of 80% in the treatment and 20% in the placebo group, respectively). In contrast, pain and pruritus scores were assessed without any systematic group‐specific shifts, and the absence of statistically significant differences in these endpoints can hence be explained by this.

Overall, the multivariate analysis results reflects the global treatment effect, whilst the univariate analyses demonstrate that this effect is primarily related to the reduction in the outcome variable wound size. This example highlights how different statistical methods can differ in their sensitivity and illustrates the simulated methods using a concrete data set. Furthermore, this illustration shows a scenario of slightly skewed data. In this context, interpretation must be approached with caution, as results using scenarios with deviations from multivariate normality and violations of homogeneity of covariance matrices have shown that, in particular, the Wald‐type statistic using its asymptotic approximation and the parametric MANOVA tend to produce rather liberal type‐I errors in these situations.

Discussion and Conclusions

The objective of this study was to conduct a systematic neutral comparison of several statistical procedures for multivariate analysis in parallel‐group designs, with a particular focus on small to moderate sample sizes as typically encountered in rare disease research. Given the limited availability of comprehensive simulation studies addressing the simultaneous inclusion of multiple outcome measures under limited sample conditions, this work aimed to provide methodological guidance grounded in an extensive simulation framework.

The simulation design was constructed to resemble realistic clinical study design settings. Clinical expertise informed both the selection of outcome variables and the data‐generating mechanisms, and elements of a real data basis were incorporated where possible. Furthermore, the guiding principles for neutral comparison studies derived from Boulesteix, Wilson & Hapfelmeier [12], Boulesteix et al. [7] were taken into account during the design and implementation of the simulation scenarios. Furthermore, no method was predominantly favored in advance; instead, all procedures were selected based on their methodological relevance and practical applicability in rare disease trials. This can be seen as a strength of this work, as different methods reveal different facets through their respective methodological approaches. However, it should be noted at this point that the selected methods of the neutral comparative study tested different hypotheses, as described above. Since both mean‐based parametric and nonparametric ranking methods were used, among others, this can be considered a limitation. The conceptual difference must be kept in mind when interpreting the results. Overall, the results of the comparative simulation study highlight the importance of such neutral simulation studies:

The asymptotic WTS procedure in MANOVA.RM yielded high empirical power in small samples; however, this was directly linked to a highly inflated type‐I error and therefore does not reflect superior inferential performance. Although this inflation diminished as the sample size increased, the small sample performance of WTS limits its suitability in rare disease contexts. In contrast, both npmv and the parametric bootstrap MATS approach within MANOVA.RM combined appropriate type‐I error control with competitive power across the investigated effect scenarios even in small sample size settings. The additional sensitivity analyses of further MANOVA.RM variants strengthen the robustness of the findings. Alternative implementations available in the MANOVA.RM package, particularly bootstrap‐based WTS and MATS procedures, largely confirmed the conclusions from the primary simulations. While bootstrap approaches of the WTS substantially improved type‐I error control compared with the asymptotic WTS, this improvement was accompanied by a slight reduction in statistical power in those settings. In scenarios in which the homogeneity of covariance matrices was violated and especially in unbalanced designs, the parametric MANOVA tended to result in a liberal type‐I error, whereas the univariate Mann–Whitney U‐test with Holm correction demonstrated slightly conservative type‐I error behavior in small samples but provided adequate control and reasonable power in many other settings. Despite its conceptual simplicity and the fact that it does not constitute a genuinely multivariate procedure, it may represent a pragmatic alternative when interpretability at the level of individual endpoints is of primary interest. Nevertheless, its methodological limitations must be acknowledged. For sufficiently large samples (e.g., N≥150), all procedures achieved power values close to one, meaning that methodological differences in large clinical trials are practically negligible. The additional non‐normal and heteroscedastic simulation scenarios further demonstrate that the superiority of the rank‐based npmv procedure and the robust performance of MATS within MANOVA.RM are not merely consequences of the original simulation assumptions but persist under distributional misspecification.

An important practical implication of these findings is that the choice of the statistical method should primarily be driven by the underlying scientific question and the expected treatment effect mechanism rather than by statistical power alone. If the primary objective is to detect any clinically relevant difference in the joint outcome distribution, including responder‐driven treatment effects or deviations from distributional differences, the rank‐based npmv procedure appears particularly suitable. If, however, the research question concerns differences in multivariate mean responses, the semiparametric MATS using parametric bootstrap within MANOVA.RM provides a robust alternative while maintaining appropriate type‐I error control. In contrast, the asymptotic WTS within MANOVA.RM and the parametric MANOVA should be interpreted with caution in small and unbalanced samples and especially in heteroscedastic scenarios because of their consistently liberal type‐I error behavior observed throughout our simulations. Consequently, practitioners should first clarify the scientific question of interest before selecting a corresponding statistical procedure, as different methods may address different hypotheses and thus lead to different inferential conclusions.

In conclusion, the choice of an appropriate statistical method in multi‐aspect rare disease settings should prioritize reliable type‐I error control while maintaining adequate power. Based on the present simulations, the nonparametric npmv procedure and the semiparametric MATS approach using parametric bootstrap within MANOVA.RM emerge as particularly suitable approaches for rare disease trials with multiple endpoints. For larger samples, however, the differences between procedures become negligible, and practical considerations such as computational complexity, interpretability, and familiarity may guide the final methodological decision.
