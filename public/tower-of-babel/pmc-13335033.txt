Beauveria bassiana: an emerging pathogen of fungal keratitis
Authors: Xiaoting Guo, Li Zhang, Jinhui Liu, Xiaofeng Li, Man Du, Yang Yang, Peng Li, Hongmei Qi, Ying Shi, Xiaolin Qi, Xiuhai Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes of fungal keratitis caused by Beauveria bassiana.MethodsThe clinical data of 11 consecutive patients with fungal keratitis caused by Beauveria bassiana in the Eye Hospital of Shandong First Medical University from January 2019 to May 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. The clinical features, etiological characteristics, treatments and outcomes were analyzed.ResultsMost patients were farmers (10/11), with three cases caused by plant trauma and five cases having a history of corneal transplantation. Slit-lamp examination revealed that the keratopathy was characterized by dry ulcerative lesions, predominantly circular with relatively irregular borders. Several cases exhibited typical keratopathy signs of fungal keratitis, such as mycelial mat, pseudopodia and hypopyon. No significant immune ring structure was observed in any patients. Both the corneal scraping and in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) revealed branched and septate hyphae structures. Morphological and ITS sequencing identification was performed after isolation and cultivation. B. bassiana exhibited typical morphological characteristics: hyphae were branched, septate, transparent and slender; sympodial development of conidia was on a geniculate or zig-zag pattern; conidiogenous cells were flask-shaped, rachiform and sympodially proliferating, often aggregating into sporodochia or synnemata; conidia were hyaline and globose or ovoid in shape. B. bassiana had a relatively high susceptibility to the majority of antifungal agents in vitro. One patient was treated with medical therapy alone. The five patients with a history of corneal transplantation underwent penetrating keratoplasty (PKP). The two patients with ulcer depth < 1/2 corneal thickness and ulcer diameter ≤ 3 mm underwent corneal ulcer debridement combined with medical treatment. The three patients with ulcer depth exceeding 1/2 corneal thickness underwent PKP. Following treatment, no patient experienced recurrence during the follow-up period.ConclusionsPlant trauma, immunosuppression and involvement in agricultural production have been identified as significant risk factors for B. bassiana keratitis. Although the strains exhibited low minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) to most antifungal agents in vitro, early diagnosis and treatment remain crucial.

Introduction

Fungal keratitis is a global ocular disease that can result in serious visual impairment and potentially lead to blindness. It has been reported that over 100 fungal pathogens, of which more than 95% are filamentous fungi (Fusarium spp. and Aspergillus spp.) and Candida spp. could lead to fungal keratitis [1]. In recent years, a variety of rare pathogens of fungal keratitis have been gradually reported, including Pythium spp., Alternaria spp., Acremonium spp., Cladosporium spp., Curvularia spp., Bipolaris spp., Microsporidia spp., Pseudallescheria spp., Colletotrichum spp. and others [2]. Beauveria bassiana belongs to the Fungi, Ascomycota, Sordariomycetes, Hypocreales, Cordycipitaceae, Beauveria. It is an opportunistic pathogenic filamentous fungus that is widely found in soil and other substrates. It is also the pathogen of many insects and can cause muscardine disease of silkworms [3]. As an entomopathogenic fungus, it is used as an insecticide and rarely causes human infection. At present, only a few clinical cases of keratitis, deep tissue infection, and pulmonary infection caused by B. bassiana have been reported [4–11]. In clinical practice, we have found that plant trauma and other factors can cause B. bassiana to infect the human cornea and result in fungal keratitis. The infection is characterized by slow disease progression, superficial corneal involvement and small grey-white punctate ulcers in the early stages of infection. In the late stage, the ulcers infiltrate the deep stroma and are characterised by irregular borders. This retrospective study evaluates the clinical features and treatment outcomes of fungal keratitis caused by B. bassiana, aiming to guide clinicians in the early diagnosis and management.

Methods

Retrospective case series study. We retrospectively reviewed all consecutive patients diagnosed with fungal keratitis caused by B. bassiana in our hospital between January 2019 and May 2024. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Eye Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (SDSYKYY201901-1). The diagnostic criteria for fungal keratitis caused by B. bassiana were as follows [12, 13]: (1) corneal scrapings revealed branched and septate hyphae on smears; (2) in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) (Heidelberg HRT3, RCM) showed typical irregular, linear, highly reflective structures suggestive of fungal hyphae; (3) white, powdery or fluffy colonies were observed upon cultivation; (4) the fungus was identified as B. bassiana through DNA sequencing.

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the medical records of all patients, including medical history, clinical symptoms, etiology, onset time, duration of symptoms, medications and changes in their condition. The patients’ visual acuity, intraocular pressure, treatment options and therapeutic effect were recorded and summarized. Corneal examinations were performed using a slit-lamp microscope, and the fungal hyphae were detected with IVCM. The depth of corneal infiltration was evaluated using RTVue optical coherence tomography (OCT; Optovue, Fremont, California, USA). A portion of the corneal scrapings was examined microscopically after 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) smears and Calcofluor white (CFW) staining to identify fungal hyphae or spores. The remaining tissue was inoculated onto Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) medium and cultured at 28 °C. After 7 days of incubation, hyphae and spore morphology were examined following lactophenol cotton blue staining. For histopathological examination, corneal tissue sections were routinely fixed, dehydrated, cleared and embedded in paraffin. 4 μm sections were then cut and fully deparaffinized in xylene. Fungal fluorescence staining was performed on the corneal specimens. The stained sections were examined under the fluorescence microscope to observe fungal hyphae or spores.

The primers used for DNA sequencing were ITS1 (TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG) and ITS4 (TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC). The resulting sequences were deposited in the GenBank database. Species identification was performed by searching databases using the BLAST sequence analysis tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/). All 11 isolates were identified as B. bassiana by ITS sequencing, showing 99%–100% sequence similarity to reference sequences in GenBank. The accession numbers were PP776759.1, PP737845.1, PP776736.1, OL684590.1, KU158435.1, MT834953.1, MT528713.1, OL684595.1 and KF359944.1. Three isolates shared the same accession number, PP737845.1.

The in vitro antifungal susceptibility of the B. bassiana isolates was evaluated using Sensititre Yeast One™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Antifungal agents included amphotericin B, anidulafungin, caspofungin, fluconazole, 5-fluorocytosine, isavuconazole, itraconazole, micafungin, posaconazole and voriconazole. Experiments were conducted after seven days of incubation at 28 °C on PDA medium, with the suspension adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity. The incubator temperature was maintained at 28 °C, and results were read after 48 h. MIC50 is defined as the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) at which 50% of the isolates were inhibited; whereas MIC90 is the MIC at which 90% of the isolates were inhibited (Table 1).

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

The study included 9 males and 2 females, aged from 50 to 76 years (mean age 63 years). Among the 11 patients, 1 was a worker and the remaining 10 were farmers; 9 of these farmers were from Shandong Province, China. The causes of the infections were as follows: three cases were due to plant scratches, five cases occurred after corneal transplantation and three cases had no obvious precipitating factors. All patients who had undergone corneal transplantation (5/11) were treated with topical tobramycin and dexamethasone ophthalmic ointment. Symptoms in the affected eyes included foreign body sensation, eye redness, photophobia, tearing, and vision loss. The onset time before treatment and the hospitalization time ranged from 4 to 180 days (mean 38.1 days) and 4 to 10 days (mean 6.5 days), respectively.

Figure 1 presents the initial findings, treatment, and clinical course of three representative cases, whereas Fig. 2 summarizes the initial slit-lamp findings of the entire cohort. The surface of the corneal ulcers was dry, and most ulcers resembled circles with irregular borders. Some patients’ corneas displayed typical manifestations of fungal keratitis, including mycelial mat in 1 patient (Fig. 1A), pseudopodia in 1 patient (Fig. 1C), and hypopyon in 4 patients (Figs. 1E and 2). No obvious immune ring structure was observed in any patients. The diameter of the ulcers ranged from approximately 2 to 5 mm, with 3 ulcers measuring ≤ 3 mm and 8 ulcers ≥ 4 mm (Table 2). OCT indicated that the depth of the ulcers was less than 1/3 of the corneal thickness in 1 patient, about 1/3 to 1/2 in 9 patients, and about 2/3 in 1 patient (Table 2).

Mycology

Filamentous structures were observed in corneal scrapings from all patients and eleven strains of filamentous fungi were isolated from the samples. The positive rates for both smear and culture of corneal smears were 100%. The hyphae had a thin diameter of approximately 1.5–2.0 μm, which is narrower compared to common pathogenic filamentous fungi associated with fungal keratitis, such as Fusarium and Aspergillus. Fluorescent staining of pathological sections of the corneal tissue revealed that filaments primarily grew horizontally (Fig. 4). The isolates demonstrated moderate growth on PDA medium, initially appearing with a fluffy or cottony, white or creamy surface and a white underside. With prolonged incubation, the underside of the colonies turned yellow or pink, and the hyphae in the centre of the colonies formed hairball-like structures, causing the colony to become powdery (Fig. 5).

Microscopic examination showed that the hyphae were transparent, branched and septate. Racquet-shaped hyphae were observed. The conidiophores were bottle-shaped or elongated cylindrical, with a swollen base and a pointed tip. The conidiogenous cells had the shape of flask or axon, showing sympodial ontogeny, and often aggregated to form sporodochia and synnemata. The tips of the conidiogenous cells were zigzag-shaped and produced single-celled, transparent, spherical or oval conidia with a diameter of 2.0–3.5 μm (Fig. 5).

The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for different antifungal medications were as follows: amphotericin B (1–4 µg/mL), fluconazole (16–128 µg/mL), itraconazole (0.25–1 µg/mL), voriconazole (0.5–1 µg/mL), posaconazole (0.12–0.5 µg/mL), anidulafungin (0.06–0.25 µg/mL), caspofungin (0.06–8 µg/mL), micafungin (0.03–8 µg/mL), 5-fluorocytosine (> 64 µg/mL) and isavuconazole (0.25–1 µg/mL). The geometric means (GMs) were 2.27, 46.70, 0.54, 0.78, 0.22, 0.21, 0.26, 0.26, 64 and 0.74 µg/mL, respectively.

Treatment and outcomes

Case 1 was clinically diagnosed with fungal and viral keratitis and was treated with topical fluconazole, natamycin, and ganciclovir eye drops. Following treatment, the patient’s corneal ulcer healed, though corneal macular opacity changes were still present. Visual acuity improved from 0.02 to 0.6 (Table 2). Case 2 presented with a small grey-white round ulcer (2 mm) in the early stage of the disease (7 days). After undergoing corneal ulcer debridement combined with medical therapy for three days, the ulcer healed and the patient was discharged (Table 2; Fig. 1). The remaining nine patients showed significant improvement in visual acuity after surgery (Table 2). The duration of topical antifungal therapy depended on the clinical condition and was approximately 30 days. The minimum, mean, and median follow-up durations were 10, 147, and 100 days, respectively. No recurrence, reinfection, graft-related complications, or other long-term complications were observed during the follow-up period.

One patient with mild disease was treated with medication alone. Ten hospitalized patients received intravenous fluconazole once daily, together with topical treatment using polyenes (5% natamycin eye drops or 0.25% amphotericin B eye drops) and imidazoles (0.5% fluconazole eye drops or 10 mg/mL voriconazole eye drops). Among these, five patients who had previously undergone corneal transplantation underwent penetrating keratoplasty (PKP). Two patients, with ulcer depths less than 1/2 corneal thickness and ulcer diameters of ≤ 3 mm, underwent corneal ulcer debridement combined with medical therapy. The remaining three patients, with ulcer depths exceeding 1/2 corneal thickness and ulcer diameters of ≥ 4 mm, also underwent PKP (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Discussion

Fungal keratitis is a visually threatening disease caused by fungal infection and has become one of the leading causes of blindness in developing countries, particularly in agricultural regions [14, 15]. Currently, more than 100 kinds of fungal pathogens have been reported to cause fungal keratitis, with Fusarium spp. being the predominant pathogen in Shandong, China [16, 17]. In recent years, there has been an increase in reported cases of fungal keratitis caused by rare fungi [2, 18–22]. B. bassiana should be viewed within the broader spectrum of emerging rare fungal pathogens causing ocular infection. Recent reports of keratitis caused by Macrophomina phaseolina and Metarhizium anisopliae further suggest that fungi associated with plants or insects may represent an emerging and clinically relevant source of ocular infection [23, 24]. B. bassiana is a rare fungal pathogen that can infect humans. To date, only a few cases of B. bassiana-induced keratitis have been reported [5–10], and thus, the clinical features of B. bassiana keratitis have not yet been fully determined. This article reports the detailed clinical symptoms and treatment outcomes of a series of fungal keratitis cases caused by B. bassiana.

Delayed diagnosis remains one of the major unresolved challenges in fungal keratitis. Because its clinical manifestations are often non-specific and may overlap with other forms of infectious keratitis, early recognition is difficult, which can delay appropriate antifungal treatment. Recent reviews have emphasized that while culture remains the gold standard, its turnaround time and imperfect sensitivity limit its value for early decision-making. Adjunctive tools such as IVCM may support earlier diagnosis through real-time visualization of fungal structures, whereas emerging technologies such as PCR and MALDI-TOF MS show promise for rapid identification, particularly in rare fungal infections [25]. Beyond slit-lamp examination and IVCM, OCT may serve as a useful adjunctive tool in the follow-up of fungal keratitis. Recent evidence suggests that OCT can dynamically demonstrate infiltrate depth, stromal hyperreflectivity, epithelial defects, cystic spaces and corneal scarring, thereby providing additional structural information for monitoring progression and treatment response [26]. In addition, newer molecular diagnostic approaches have further expanded the diagnostic landscape of infectious keratitis. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing may be particularly useful in culture-negative cases, while metagenomic profiling has further demonstrated the microbial complexity of keratitis, supporting sequencing-based methods as promising adjuncts for more precise diagnosis in selected cases [27, 28].

B. bassiana is a widely distributed ascomycete entomopathogenic fungus in nature, which can be spread with plant growth [7]. It is a common entomopathogenic fungus used as a biopesticide to control pine caterpillars and corn borers and is widely applied in the biological control of agricultural pests [10]. Therefore, agricultural environments and plant trauma are common risk factors for B. bassiana keratitis. In this study, we reviewed the medical history of B. bassiana keratitis and found that besides plant trauma (27.3%), previous ocular surgery (45.5%) was also a common risk factor. This included 4 cases of PKP and 1 case of lamellar keratoplasty (LKP). Five patients with a history of ocular surgery had used glucocorticoid drugs to prevent immune rejection after corneal transplantation. Persistent non-healing or recurrent corneal epithelial defects after PKP and LKP surgery, combined with local steroid use, may increase the risk of secondary B. bassiana infection.

Typical ocular infection features included ulcers with blurred borders and dry surface texture, occasionally accompanied by typical clinical signs of fungal keratitis such as mycelial mat, pseudopodia and hypopyon. Ulcers ranged from 2 to 5 mm in diameter. Typical morphological characteristics included flat colonies with a white or creamy-white surface and a light yellow reverse; furry aerial hyphae; paddle-shaped hyphae; flask-shaped sporulation cells that grew vertically on hyphal branches or short side branches; and sporulation cells that often aggregated to form sporodochia and synnemata; the ends of the sporulation cells were curved in a zig-zag pattern and unicellular, transparent, spherical or oval conidia were produced at the top with a diameter of 2.0–3.5 μm. In addition, IVCM showed that the hyphae of B. bassiana were highly reflective, with uniform thickness and diameters of 1.5–2.0 μm, which could be distinguished from Fusarium and Aspergillus [29, 30].

B. bassiana is a rare pathogen of fungal keratitis, and there is still a lack of drug sensitivity data from large samples. This study found that B. bassiana has low MICs for itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, anidulafungin, caspofungin, isavuconazole and micafungin. Some reports indicated that B. bassiana has high MICs for fluconazole and 5-fluorocytosine, and moderate MICs for amphotericin B, which is consistent with our drug sensitivity results [31]. Clinical cures of B. bassiana keratitis with voriconazole alone or in combination with natamycin have been reported [3, 5, 32]. D. L. Tucker et al. reported that amphotericin B combined with itraconazole had a positive effect in treating leukemia patients with disseminated infections induced by B. bassiana [33]. Because no established clinical breakpoints are currently available for B. bassiana, the MIC data reported in this study should be interpreted as descriptive in vitro findings only and should not be used to directly predict clinical outcomes or treatment efficacy.

Fungal keratitis requires diversified treatments. Although B. bassiana shows low MICs to several antifungal drugs in vitro, early diagnosis and prompt treatment are crucial. If the ulcer involves the superficial stroma, corneal ulcer debridement combined with medical treatment can be adopted. When the ulcer extends into the deep stroma and medical treatment is inadequate, timely corneal transplantation is necessary. In this study, 5 patients who had previously undergone corneal transplantation were treated with PKP; 2 patients had small corneal ulcers (2–3 mm) with shallow ulcer infiltration depth (< 1/2 corneal thickness), which were appropriate for corneal ulcer debridement; one patient was treated only with medication and a follow-up examination revealed corneal macular opacity. Cases 3 and 6 required PKP because of prolonged disease course, deep stromal involvement, and poor response to medical therapy. Case 7 also showed limited response to initial antifungal treatment, possibly related to the low susceptibility to fluconazole. Therefore, in the early stages of B. bassiana keratitis, appropriate antifungal drugs should be administered to control the infection. However, in cases of prolonged illness, deep ulcer depth (ulcer depth exceeding 1/2 corneal thickness), large ulcers and poor effect of medical therapy alone, timely corneal transplantation is crucial to prevent permanent visual damage.

The susceptibility of B. bassiana to corneal infection can be explained by its ability to produce chitinase, a hydrolytic enzyme that penetrates insect bodies. Human corneal keratin and collagen share a similar structure with the chitin of insects, allowing this enzyme to attach to corneal tissue and then penetrate the stroma [7, 34]. It has been reported that B. bassiana cannot grow above 35 °C. Since the corneal temperature is about 35 °C or even lower, and the ability of pathogenic microorganisms to grow at 35–37 °C is considered essential for human infection, B. bassiana infection in humans is extremely rare. Even if it occurs, it more often infects surface tissues such as the cornea [31]. Consequently, B. bassiana is usually regarded as a contaminant in the early stages. However, recent studies have shown that B. bassiana can result in keratitis, allergic diseases and even disseminated infections in leukemia patients [33, 35].

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective case series with a small sample size, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, because treatment decisions were based on lesion severity, prior ocular history, and clinician judgment rather than a standardized protocol, comparisons between medical and surgical treatment strategies should be interpreted cautiously. Third, the reported clinical outcomes are descriptive and observational, precluding any firm conclusions regarding treatment efficacy or prognosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, B. bassiana keratitis is characterized by early involvement of the corneal superficial layers, small greyish-white round ulcers, and later invasion into the deep stroma layers, typically presenting with blurred-boundary round ulcers. It is a rare but serious ocular infection that threatens vision. This study may improve clinical practice by increasing awareness of B. bassiana as a rare but clinically relevant corneal pathogen, particularly in patients with agricultural exposure, plant trauma, prior corneal transplantation, or topical corticosteroid use. Early identification and prompt microbiological confirmation may facilitate more appropriate treatment and reduce the risk of irreversible visual impairment. Although the strains exhibited low MICs to most antifungal agents in vitro, early identification and timely treatment remain essential to prevent permanent visual damage.
