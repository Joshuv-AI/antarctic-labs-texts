Transcriptome reanalysis and gene expression of 13 detoxification genes for avermectin and pyridaben resistance in Panonychus citri
Authors: Xiaoqing Han, Mengyuan Peng, Yunlong Zhang, Pan Wu, Xiaoyu Zheng, Xinze Zhang, Shuchen Guo, Yanhong Ding, Na Yang, Meng Li, Yidong Lv, Yi Zhang, Shuzhen Liu, Guohua Yu, Bin Liu, Liujun Tian, Chongbin Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Citrus red mites (P. citri) are key pests affecting citrus production worldwide due to pesticide resistance. The resistance mechanisms of ten pesticides are known, but a comprehensive study using transcriptome data is missing. This study employed deeptools, cuffdiff, rmats, bcftools and other software to examine gene expression variation, alternative splicing (AS), and mutations in mite resistance. The research highlighted that pesticides can regulate gene transcription, and red mites with resistance increase cytochrome P450, glutathione S-transferases, carboxylesterase, and acetylcholinesterase expression. Pyridaben also induces new AS events. Fluazinam-induced mites show mRNA splicing peaking earlier than transcription, both peaking at one day and returning to baseline after two days. AS profiles are similar in different mite populations with overlapping pesticide resistances. Lastly, specific mitochondrial SNPs in mites might mediate resistance against select pesticides.

Introduction

P. citri is one of the important citrus pests worldwide. Farmers usually apply chemical agents to control the mite, which leads to the resistance of the mite and increases the difficulty to control the mite and the cost of citrus cultivation. The first transcriptome of P. citri was sequenced with single-ended reads in 2011 using the Illumina HiSeq™ 2000 sequencing platform, which identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the hexythiazox resistant strain of P. citri1. Later, a different sequencing technology was used to analyze the DEGs of field mites with varying resistance levels. Specifically, there are three populations: one that is resistant to a single pesticide (fenpyroximate)2, another that is resistant to two pesticides (fenpropathrin and bifenazate)3 and a third that is resistant to four pesticides (fenpropathrin, abamectin, cyflumetofen, and bifenazate)3. At the same time, two pesticides, fluazinam4 and pyridaben5, induced a large number of DEGs. In 2021, Yu et al. published the first genome of P. citri and tested DEGs in Amitraz6 and Spirodiclofen7 resistant strains. In addition, the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) annotation of this mite genome was completed in January 2023. Therefore, the resistant studies by transcriptome to the above 10 pesticides either lacked genomic sequences or complete NCBI gene annotations.

Furthermore, a macroscopic understanding of how these pesticides affect gene expression of P. citri and integrating all transcriptomes to discover interesting genes has not yet been discovered. Classical DEG analysis involves software like htseq-count to calculate the reads number by using the bam files generated by bowtie2 and samtools, and then use DESeq software to determine DEGs7. With genome and gene annotation files, cuffdiff8 and rmats9 software were used to calculate DEGs and alternative splicing (AS). We integrate DEGs of detoxification genes in different resistance samples and all transcriptome data to macroscopically understand how pesticides regulate gene expression.

AS types include alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS), skipped exon (SE), retained intron (RI) and mutually exclusive exons (MXE). In insects, transmembrane proteins, such as ion channels10, receptors11, can be altered by AS to produce phenotypes. AS is involved in pest neurodevelopment, immune specificity, acclimatization and pest resistance10,12. However, transcriptome studies of resistance in P. citri have focused on the study of DEGs. Therefore, the rMATS software was used to capture the complexity of the mRNA affected by the above 10 pesticides through AS, and to better explain the resistance and environmental adaptation mechanism of mites by integrating both AS and DEGs.

The control of the pesticide-resistant transcriptome of P. citri contains data on their different developmental stages and geographical distribution. This study will integrate these data to reveal complex mRNA changes, which, together with pesticide resistance data, will provide scientific guidelines for the control of P. citri.

Currently, a total of 16 mite genomes have been published in NCBI, and we calculated the GC content of these genomes, of which the GC content of P. citri was 31.33%. The lowest and highest genomic GC levels of Acarapis woodi and Galendromus occidentalis were 25.64% and 51.58%, respectively (Fig. 2A). We selected 57 resistance-related transcriptome data for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1B). The correlation analysis showed high correlation between samples with the same background and treatment, indicating the reproducibility and reliability of transcriptome data (Fig. 3). Gene expression from deutonymph to adult

The study of the growth and development of P. citri can be scientifically used to control the mite population. Since the second nymph transcriptome lacks a corresponding pesticide-free adult transcriptome, we used previously published adult control transcriptome data from two laboratories as a control to study the DEGs of P. citri from the second nymph stage to the adult (Fig. 1B). In this study, we screened out the DEGs that were inconsistent in the two sets of comparisons, and finally obtained 845 significantly upregulated genes and 687 significantly down-regulated genes (Fig. 4A). We selected 9 genes with very different expression among the above differential genes (Fig. 4B). The nine genes are LOC128385650 (cholinesterase), LOC128389088 (Kielin/chordin-like protein), LOC128392157 (Putative glucosylceramidase 4), LOC128393194 (Estradiol 17-beta-dehydrogenase 2), LOC128394507 (Protein D3), LOC128394636 (Retinol dehydrogenase 11), LOC128394849, LOC128397621 (Estradiol 17-beta-dehydrogenase), LOC128395179 (Histone-lysine N-methyltransferase SETMAR). The read coverage data is the same as our calculations (Fig. 4C).

The integrated data from pesticide resistance studies

In this section, we integrate data on DEGs, AS, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and insertion-deletion (Indel) mutation to reveal the complex genetic changes in pesticide-resistant mites. By using cuffdiff software, we obtained 1899 DEGs (q value < 0.05, |log2(fold_change)|> 1) which associate with resistance to the 10 pesticides. Among these genes, we found 18 P450 genes, 1 mitochondrial cytochrome b (CYTB), 8 GSTs, 1 CES, and 10 AChEs (Fig. 5A). These genes of detoxifying enzymes were expressed significantly differently in multiple pesticide-resistant strains of the mite, including LOC128387437 (P450 2J2), LOC128396054 (P450 3A24), LOC128390480 (CES 5A), LOC128386140 (AChE), etc. AS alters mRNA composition, which in turn affects protein biological function. According to rmats software, 875 genes produced AS events due to pesticides (p < 0.05). In this study, DEGs and AS genes were compared and found that most genes had AS but no significant difference in gene expression (Fig. 5L). Fluazinam induced P. citri to produce a large number of AS events at 6 h and 24 h, but at 48 h, the induced majority of AS events returned to the control sample level (Fig. 5H,I). Citrus red mites from Yunnan and Guangxi provinces of China are highly resistant to two (Fenpropathrin, Bifenazate) and four (Fenpropathrin, Bifenazate, Abamectin, Cyflumetofen) pesticides, respectively, with few identical DEGs (Fig. 5K). However, AS profile is similar in both resistant mites (Fig. 5J). This study also found that transcription factors, transcription-related proteins, translational initiation factors, and related proteins had the same type of AS in different pesticide resistance studies (Table S2). These transcription factors include Tbx2, Sp9, TFEB, Rfx4, Elf-2; Translation initiation factors include eIF-2Bα, eIF-2, eIF-4E, eIF-3J (Table S2). We analyzed the SNPs of mitochondrial DNA of mites from Yunnan and Guangxi, China, which are resistant to two pesticides and four pesticides respectively, as well as field citrus red mites from Iran, which are resistant to fenpyroximate, and hexyzhiazox-, amitraz- and spirodiclofen-resistant mites from Ran's laboratory, and we found 404, 500, 50, 65, 315 and 249 SNPs, respectively (Fig. 5B-G). The results showed that these pesticides prompted the accumulation of SNP sites that associated with the resistance in P. citri (Fig. 5B-G). Discussion

P. citri is one of the important citrus pests and an important source of allergies13. This article integrates transcriptome data from published study of resistance of P. citri to 10 pesticides. The analysis results can provide reliable and comprehensive data for the study of the mechanism and application of P. citri’s resistance. Over the course of the past few decades, plant and animal extracts and chemically synthesized pesticides have been widely used to control P. citri populations. In terms of commercial pesticides, there are also etoxazole14, emamectin benzoate15, bifenazate16, abamectin3, diflubenzuron17, avermectin18 used to control P. citri. Venom19, alcohol extract20 and ethanolic extract21 from Jellyfish Nemopilema nomurai, Mikania micrantha and Annona mucosa respectively have been used to control P. citri. The rapid reproduction and migration of mites makes it difficult to control, and up to 50 types of pesticides are used to control them22. Therefore, the study of pesticide resistance in P. citri is very important. Of course, the predatory mite of the P. citri is also one of the options to control this mite.

AS of the CYP4C62 reduces the enzyme's metabolism of chlorpyrifos to an active state, thereby increasing the resistance of insects to chlorpyrifos23. The AS of type A3SS in LOC128390538 (P450 307a1) in both field resistant and pyridaben-induced citrus red mites was found, while LOC128393706 (GST 2) also produced AS of A3SS type in fenpyroximate-resistant mites (from RAMSAR) and amitraz-resistant mites. However, when we looked at the DEGs of the detoxification gene, we did not find any significant AS events due to pesticides. This suggests that the up-regulation of detoxification genes in P. citri rather than AS of these genes plays a leading role in pesticide resistance. However, we found that their AS profiles were highly similar in Yuxi (YX) and Nanning (NN) populations of citrus red mites with partial pesticide resistance overlap (Fig. 5). Previous reports have shown that AS profiles are species-conserved12. Pesticide induction can also cause citrus red mites to temporarily produce a large number of AS and a small number of AS that may be inherited. This indicates that the pesticide changes the AS events of P. citri, and the AS generated in the above 10 pesticide-resistant mites may disable the interaction between the pesticides and their targets and the metabolic enzymes. The complexity of these AS events is regulated by the sequence of splicing site and some specific splicing regulators12.

SNPs and Indels, a type of genetic variation, are classical mechanisms of pesticide resistance. In textbooks, a eukaryotic cell contains a single copy of a circular DNA molecule. However, mitochondria fragment into multiple copies during important biological processes such as agent stress and differentiation24–26. Therefore, we focused on examining the SNPs of the mitochondrial genome corresponding to each pesticide and plotting LDheatmap. Since the mitochondrial genome of P. citri annotated by NCBI do not come from China, we found that the mitochondrial genome of P. citri in different regions of the world vary greatly (Fig. 5). SNPs (R2 < 0.35) associated with resistance to pesticides such as fenpyroximate, hexyzhiazox and spirodiclofen.

As far as I know, this is the first study to integrate all pesticide-resistant transcriptome data since the publication of the genome and gene annotation of P. citri. From the results of resistant studies for 10 pesticides in P. citri, the pesticide regulates the transcriptional initiation of genes and makes the mites acquire resistance. We selected 16 genes from the integrated significantly differentially expressed detoxification genes for qPCR verification, and the results were in good agreement with transcriptome data analysis. This demonstrates the reliability of our analysis results, prompting mite resistance research to shift to the transcriptional regulation. Pharmacological induction not only regulates the expression of gene levels, but also affects splicesome that produce significant differences in AS without affecting mRNA amounts. These AS events affect the function of the protein through structure, i.e., resistance or other phenotypes. SNP analysis results from the mitochondrial genome of P. citri suggest that CYTB, ND3, ND4 and ND6 may mediate pesticide resistance. Complex environmental factors result in AS patterns for P. citri that are not identical.

Methods

Data collection and overview

Before calculating the GC content the genome sequences of a total of 16 species of mites was downloaded, including Acarapis woodi (GCA_023170135.1), Psoroptes ovis (GCA_002943765.1), Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (GCA_003076615.1), Dermatophagoides farinae (GCA_024713945.1), P. citri (GCA_014898815.1), Tetranychus urticae (GCA_000239435.1), Dinothrombium tinctorium (GCA_003675995.1), Tetranychus cinnabarinus (GCA_022266195.1), Leptotrombidium deliense (GCA_003675905.2), Leptotrombidium pallidum (GCA_013372475.1), Brevipalpus yothersi (GCA_003956705.1), Varroa destructor (GCA_002443255.1), Fragariocoptes setiger (GCA_019968535.1), Halotydeus destructor (GCA_022750525.1), Aculops lycopersici (GCA_015350385.1) and Galendromus occidentalis (GCA_000255335.2). In this study, the number of four bases in each genome was calculated by using a python script to obtain the GC content of each mite.

We downloaded the reads included in Fig. 1B with fastq-dump software and blasted the single-ended or double-ended reads against the genome of P. citri (GCA_014898815.1) using bowtie2 software. The Sam files were converted to Bam files that were further sorted by using samtools software. We performed pearson correlation calculation and PCA between samples with deeptools software27 for bam files of all samples. Based on the correlation values of replicate samples, one sample from the control group and the experimental group of each pesticide resistance study were selected for matrix calculation and profile plotting. A BED file required to calculate the matrix is prepared by extracting the start and end sites of the translation by using Office software.

Differential gene expression

We obtained the annotation file in gtf format for P. citri of genome from the NCBI website. According to the properties of the sample, the sample is divided into three directions: development, pesticide resistance, and geographical distribution. We performed differential gene expression calculations for the transcriptome data of the control (bold) and experimental groups in Fig. 1B with the default settings of cuffdiff software. We set a value of 0.01 to plot heat map for a particular gene when there was no significant difference between treatment groups.

Alternative splicing

The genome index of P. citri was established using the genome Generate mode of STAR software and the transcriptome data was blasted against the mite genome to obtain the sorted bam files. rmats was used to calculate AS events for transcriptome data across experimental and control groups. AS with a q-value of less than 0.05 is considered to be a AS event caused by pesticide and geographical distribution. To facilitate plotting, we convert the q value with the formula -log10 (q value) to a positive value between 1.3–16. Based on observations, when the q value is zero, we set it to 1e-16 according to our observation. If there are multiple AS, but the same type in the same gene, we take the smallest Q value.

SNP and Indels

We use bcftools software to obtain reliable transcriptome SNP and Indels data through identification and filtering. In this paper, the SNPs of the control and experimental groups of pesticide for mitochondria were extracted and Graphical Display of Pairwise Linkage Disequilibria Between SNPs (LDheatmap) was plotted. The SNPs studied by amitraz were derived from genome sequencing data from the study. At the same time, we extracted mitochondrial SNPs from the transcriptome of SWU, GU/IUT/UG, ZAFU and SWU/CRI and plotted LDheatmap.

Technical validation

The transcriptome and genomic data used in this article are of published. We further verify the reliability of replicate samples through sample correlation calculations. Upon verification, we were unable to find data for the same batch of the fenpropathrin study, so we only covered the correlation and PCA for the fenpropathrin study. The DEGs, AS and SNPs were checked by IGV software28.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

The pesticide-sensitive and avermectin- (4700 fold), pyridaben-resistant (559 fold) mites required for qPCR came from SWU Lab. Primer design (Table S1), RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR are consistent with the methods described in the previous publication29,30. In this paper, we selected two housekeeping genes for qPCR, which were GAPDH and ELF1A.

Conclusion

When citrus red mites become resistant to pesticides, there are a variety of strategies we can employ to reduce the resistance of the mites. First, we can introduce new pesticides for animals or other insects. Second, we can use RNAi to interfere the expression of resistant and developmentally relevant genes reducing the number of resistant mites. At the same time, we can also use predator mites to control the population of citrus red mites. Breeding citrus varieties resistant to P. citri is also an effective way to deal with the resistance of P. citri. Therefore, summarizing the existing resistance to miticides in P. citri and analyzing its genomic and transcriptome data is beneficial to our comprehensive management of the mite.
