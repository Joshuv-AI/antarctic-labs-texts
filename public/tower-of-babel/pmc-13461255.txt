Establishment of a human osteocyte model to reveal mechanisms of chronic Staphylococcus epidermidis bone and joint infections
Authors: Claudia Siverino, Qi Sun, Lucian Bogdan Solomon, Thomas Fintan Moriarty, Gerald J Atkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsChronic bone and joint infections caused by Staphylococcus epidermidis can be difficult to treat and are prone to recurrence. In this study, established human osteoblast- and osteocyte-like cell line models were used in vitro to investigate the intracellular persistence of S. epidermidis as a potential mechanism for infection persistence.MethodsThe SaOS2 human osteosarcoma cell line was differentiated into osteoblast-like (SaOS2-OB) and osteocyte-like (SaOS2-OY) stages and infected with four S. epidermidis strains at varying multiplicities of infection. Susceptibilities to lysostaphin, a known bacteriolytic enzyme, and a panel of antibiotics were determined to identify an antimicrobial capable of clearing extracellular bacteria while preserving intracellular bacterial viability. Bacterial survival and number were quantified by colony-forming unit and droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (PCR), while host gene expression was analyzed by real-time PCR.ResultsLysostaphin was ineffective against all four S. epidermidis strains, while 10 × minimal bactericidal concentration levofloxacin consistently eliminated extracellular but not intracellular bacteria. Intracellular S. epidermidis at multiplicities of infection of 1,000 persisted in SaOS2-OY for 14 days at 10 × minimal bactericidal concentration levofloxacin, while it was cleared within five days in SaOS2-OB cells. Droplet digital PCR genome copy analysis identified a higher number of bacteria than conventional culture, indicating more accurate counting and a transition of the intracellular bacteria to a non-culturable state. Proinflammatory and bone remodelling responses by both bone cell types were evident upon infection, with increased messenger RNA levels of CCL5, CXCL6, CXCL10, SOST, receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL), and matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13).ConclusionWe report the first model of intracellular S. epidermidis persistence in osteocyte-like cells, which provides a robust platform for mechanistic studies of chronic bone and joint infections and the preclinical screening of novel therapeutics targeting intracellular bacteria.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(8):987–997.Keywords: Osteocytes, S. epidermidis, In vitro model, Bone and joint infection, Intracellular infection, Staphylococcus epidermidis, joint infections, osteocytes, infections, bacteria, strains, antibiotics, osteoblasts, Gene expression, matrix metalloproteinase 13

Introduction

Bone and joint infections (BJIs) are characterized by progressive bone destruction and relatively high recurrence rates.1 Although not completely understood, the factors typically believed to be responsible for failure of antibiotic therapy are poor diffusion and concentration of the antimicrobial agents within the bone tissue, and the formation of bacterial biofilms on implant materials.

Staphylococci are the principal agents responsible for BJIs.2-4 Although coagulase-negative staphylococci are generally less virulent than Staphylococcus aureus, they account for a substantial percentage (30% to 60%) of late-diagnosed, chronic BJIs.5 Internalization of bacteria by bone cells has only been recognized as a phenomenon in BJIs within the last two decades.6-8 Importantly, osteocytes make up approximately 90% to 95% of all cells in bone tissue and are long-lived. These characteristics make them potentially critical hosts for bacterial pathogen persistence, allowing bacteria to survive for extended periods in patients with BJI and contributing to recurrence of infection. Several studies have reported the effect of intracellular S. aureus on osteoblast differentiation, showing decreased expression of bone anabolic markers in vitro,9 and reduced calcium deposition. Soluble biofilm factors produced by Staphylococcus epidermidis have also been shown to alter osteoblastic gene expression and inhibit mineral deposition.10 Additionally, intracellular bacteria can trigger the release of proinflammatory mediators, leading to increased osteoclast activity, as well as osteocyte-mediated bone matrix destruction.11-13

Osteoblast localization at bone surfaces potentially exposes them to greater numbers of bacteria during an infection, which would be expected to cause cell death. However, as a less-differentiated cell type with greater proliferative potential than osteocytes, osteoblasts are less of a metabolic burden to replace. Localization at the bone surface also increases osteoblast accessibility to immune cells.14 In contrast, bone-encased osteocytes represent a higher metabolic burden to replace via bone remodelling and are relatively inaccessible to immune cells. Thus, osteocyte infection may be more likely to result in the inaccessible persistence of bacteria deep within the bone.15 Long-term intracellular dormancy within viable osteocytes is also potentially more favourable for the pathogen due to the inherently long lifespan of osteocytes.12,16

Recent studies have shown that bacteria, including S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and Cutibacterium acnes can all reside within the extensive osteocyte lacunocanalicular network.12,15,17,18 As a result, these bacteria can potentially infect and persist within osteocytes. The combination of bone tissue localization and intracellular residence appears to render the majority of available antimicrobial treatments for S. aureus infection ineffective.19,20

Despite its obvious importance and clinical relevance, our understanding of osteocyte infection remains limited. In vitro models of this process are important for understanding the mechanisms of bacterial persistence and developing therapeutic strategies accordingly. Recent models have described the use of either human primary osteocytes12 or a human osteocyte-like cell line, consisting of osteogenically differentiated SaOS2 cells.16,21 This model has previously demonstrated that S. aureus can establish a stable intracellular infection lasting at least 28 days, associated with altered expression of key host immunomodulatory factors.12,16 While S. epidermidis has been shown to persist in human cultured osteoblasts for up to ten days,22 there are no reported studies or available models of its intracellular persistence in osteocytes. Herein, we describe a new in vitro model of intracellular S. epidermidis persistence in human osteocyte-like cells.

Methods

Cell culture

SaOS2 cells (HTB-85, American Type Culture Collection; approx. passage 65) were maintained in growth media consisting of alpha-minimum essential medium (αMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% v/v foetal calf serum, 50 mg/ml ascorbate 2-phosphate, and standard tissue culture additives (10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 2 mM L-glutamine, and penicillin/streptomycin each 1 unit/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C/5% CO2.23 For experimentation, SaOS2 cells were grown to either an osteoblast-like (OB) state (SaOS2-OB) or differentiated into SaOS2 osteocyte-like cells (SaOS2-OY). For SaOS2-OB, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and grown to confluence without differentiation. To achieve an osteocyte-like phenotype, we used a recently described protocol that generates a highly mineralized, osteocyte-like state within 14 days.21 For this, SaOS2 cells seeded as above were switched to differentiation media at confluence, consisting of αMEM supplemented with 5% v/v foetal calf serum, 50 mg/ml ascorbate 2-phosphate, and 1.8 mM potassium dihydrogen phosphate (Sigma Aldrich, USA) and then cultured at 37°C/5% CO2/1% O2 for 14 days.21

Bacterial culture and antimicrobial testing

S. epidermidis strains ATCC12228, ATCC14990, ATCC35984, and a clinical osteomyelitis strain, RAH-SE1, along with two methicillin-resistant S. aureus strains WCH-SK2 and WCH-SK3 were used in this study.24 All strains were cultured in nutrient broth (NB) (Chem-Supply, Australia), on a rocking platform at 100 rpm/37°C.

For the purposes of identifying an antimicrobial capable of clearing extracellular bacteria while preserving intracellular bacterial viability in a coculture setting, susceptibilities to lysostaphin, a known S. aureus bacteriolytic enzyme, were tested. Each strain was cultured from a single colony on a rocking platform at 100 rpm/37°C overnight. Cultures were then diluted 1:20 in fresh NB for two hours, adjusted to an optical density (OD) at 600 nm of 0.1 (2.5 × 106 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml) or 0.9 (1.8 × 107 CFU/ml) in NB and then exposed for two hours or 24 hrs to 10 µg/ml lysostaphin (AMBI Products, USA). At experimental timepoints, serial dilutions of the cultures were plated on NB 1.5% w/v bacteriological agar (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and colony growth at 37°C monitored over 72 hours. Sensitivities of the S. epidermidis strains were also tested to gentamicin, levofloxacin, linezolid, doxycycline, and oxacillin. Antibiotic effectivities were assessed at a concentration range of 0.046 to 1,000 µg/ml. For this, overnight cultures were adjusted to an OD600 = 0.01 and incubated at 100 rpm/37°C for 24 hours with the appropriate antibiotic dilution and the respective minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) determined following agar plating, as above. All concentrations of antibiotics (1 × MBC) used for the coculture experiments are summarized in the relevant figures.

Host cell infection and measurements of intracellular bacterial number

For infections, overnight bacteria cultures were diluted 1:20 in fresh media, pelleted at 3,000× g for ten minutes, then resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to the required target density for the various multiplicities of infection (MOIs), defined as the ratio of infectious bacterial cells to host cells at the time of infection. Host cell culture media were removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS before bacterial suspensions or PBS control were added. MOIs of 10, 100, and 1,000 were used. SaOS2-OB were measured to be at 104 cells/well on the day of infection, while SaOS2-OY were at 105 cells/well. Accordingly, S. epidermidis was applied at 1 × 105, 1 × 106, 1 × 107 or 1 × 106, 1 × 107, 1 × 108 CFU/mL for MOIs of 10, 100, and 1,000, for SaOS2-OB or SaOS2-OY, respectively. After incubation at 37°C for two hours, cultures were washed three times with sterile PBS and treated with defined concentrations of antibiotics at ten times their respective MBC in media overnight at 37°C. The following day, media were replaced with fresh differentiation media containing maintenance concentrations (1 × MBC). The presence of extracellular bacteria was assessed by plating the entire culture supernatant and culturing for 72 hours, with growth recorded as positive or negative. Intracellular CFU were measured from replicate cultures lysed in sterile Milli-Q water (Merck, Germany) for ten minutes at room temperature and plated undiluted on NB agar at one, three, five, or 14 days post-infection (DPI). Extracellular CFU were measured at the same timepoints. For each timepoint, CFU were recorded after 24 hours and 120 hours of incubation at 37°C. Cell culture media were changed every four days.

Measurement of host gene expression

For quantification of gene expression of CCL5, CXCL6, CXCL10, SOST, RANKL, and MMP13, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) and complementary DNA templates were prepared using the iScript gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, USA), as per manufacturers’ instructions. Real-time PCR reactions were then performed in duplicate to determine the mRNA levels of target genes using RT2 Forget-Me-Not EvaGreen qPCR Mastermix (Biotium Inc., USA) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences targeting each gene are detailed in Table I. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, normalizing target genes to the expression of ACTB, a housekeeping gene shown to be stable for this cell model,16,21 and calibrating to uninfected control samples.

Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction

For the quantification of host and bacterial genome copy number, total DNA was isolated using Direct PCR lysis reagent (Direct buffer, Viagen Biotech Inc, USA), as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 500 μl Direct buffer together with 200 μg/ml proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for the lysis of either SaOS2-OB or SaOS2-OY per single well of a 48-well tissue culture plate. Individual cell lysates were transferred to 1.5 ml reaction tubes, digested at 55°C for 35 minutes and then heat-inactivated at 85°C for 15 minutes to terminate enzymic digestion. Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) was then performed using QX200 Digital Droplet PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories), as per the manufacturer’s instructions and as previously described.24 Primer sets targeting a human genome–specific sequence within the single-copy type X collagen (COL10A1) gene and a homologous region of the elongation factor Tu (tuf) gene common to S. aureus and S. epidermidis were used (Table I).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v. 10.1.2; GraphPad Software, USA). Data are presented as median values unless otherwise indicated. The normality of each dataset was assessed using the Anderson–Darling, D’Agostino–Pearson, Shapiro–Wilk, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. If data passed normality testing, comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance, as appropriate, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. If data did not meet normality assumptions, non-parametric analysis was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Specific statistical tests applied to individual datasets are detailed in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Lysostaphin is ineffective towards S. epidermidis strains

As expected, lysostaphin (10 µg/ml) was effective in killing S. aureus WCH-SK2 and WCH-SK3. However, it was ineffective for four different strains of S. epidermidis after two hours of incubation (Figure 1a) with cultures at optical densities of 0.1 or 0.9, which correspond to 106 CFU/ml and 107 CFU/ml, respectively. After 24 hours, S. epidermidis partial elimination was achieved only with the lower bacteria number (OD600 = 0.1) (Figure 1b).

Four S. epidermidis and two methicillin-resistant S. aureus strains were exposed to 10 µg/ml lysostaphin in differentiation media (minimum essential medium (α-MEM); 5% foetal calf serum) for two hours or for 24 hours at initial bacterial densities of optical density (OD)600 = 0.1 or 0.9. a) Quantitative assessment of bacterial survival after two hours’ exposure, presented as mean with SD. b) Qualitative assessment of bacterial survival after 24 hours’ exposure, based on the presence or absence of colony growth following plating. OD600 of 0.1 and 0.9 corresponds to 106 and 107 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml, respectively.

Levofloxacin used for the eradication of extracellular bacteria

To test for more effective extracellular bacterial clearance, various antibiotics were tested, including those with lower (doxycycline, oxacillin, and gentamicin) and higher (levofloxacin and linezolid) intracellular activity.19 The MBC for each bacterial strain and antibiotic combination was measured by plating each concentration and observing the absence of bacterial growth (Figure 2). These values were then applied in coculture experiments, using a 10 × MBC for overnight incubation to determine their ability to eliminate extracellular bacteria, followed by using 1 × MBC for culture maintenance until the predefined timepoints (Figure 2).

Bacteria cultures were diluted to an optical density (OD)600 of 0.1 and exposed to log2 diluted antibiotic concentrations, starting from 1,000 µg/ml. Representative results for individual S. epidermidis strains are shown. a) RAH-SE1. b) ATCC12228. c) ATCC14990. d) ATCC35984. After 24 hours’ incubation, aliquots from each condition were plated and assessed for bacterial growth. Growth at each antibiotic concentration is indicated by green boxes. The table summarizes the corresponding 1 × MBC values for each antibiotic–strain combination.

In a pilot experiment, SaOS2-OB were infected with each of four S. epidermidis strains (RAH-SE1, ATCC12228, ATCC14990, ATCC35984) with a MOI of 1, 10, and 100 and treated overnight with the respective 10 × MBCs of levofloxacin, oxacillin, or doxycycline and then at 1 × MBC for maintenance. Cultures treated with a MOI of 1 resulted in no detectable intracellular or extracellular bacteria, while MOIs of 10 and 100 showed the presence of extracellular bacteria when using oxacillin and doxycycline (data not shown). In contrast, levofloxacin showed no survival of extracellular bacteria but culturable intracellular bacteria. Consequently, levofloxacin was chosen for eliminating extracellular bacteria in the subsequent experiments.

Intracellular model of S. epidermidis in SaOS2-OB with different MOIs

The clinical isolate RAH-SE1, along with strains ATCC12228, ATCC14990, and ATCC35984 exhibited comparable low levels of intracellular infection in SaOS2-OB of approximately 102 CFU after 24 hours using the MOI 10 condition. However, the number of intracellular bacteria decreased over time, becoming non-culturable by day 5 (Figure 3). Using MOIs of 100 and 1,000, higher intracellular bacterial numbers were observed at early timepoints (day 1 and day 3), reaching approximately 103 to 104 CFU across all strains (Figure 3). However, intracellular persistence until day 5 was observed only with a higher MOI of 1,000 for the ATCC strains (Figure 3).

Intracellular model of S. epidermidis in SaOS2-OY with different MOIs

When testing SaOS2-OY infection with S. epidermidis, we observed more-challenging extracellular killing conditions. On day 1, after the overnight culture with 10 × MBC levofloxacin, no culturable bacteria were detected. However, by day 3, having switched to a 1 × MBC of levofloxacin, culturable extracellular bacteria were present, even at the lowest MOI (Figure 4). Importantly, the persistence of intracellular infection was dependent on the strain used. A MOI of 10 was insufficient to establish an intracellular infection in SaOS2-OY (Figure 4a). In contrast, intracellular infection was successfully achieved using MOIs of 100 and 1,000, but with low levels of extracellular bacteria remaining. ATCC35984 showed the best combination of intracellular persistence with the lowest incidence of extracellular escape at day 3 (Figure 4b).

Generally, intracellular infections using a MOI of 1,000 could not be reproducibly maintained for more than five days with levofloxacin at 1 × MBC because of extracellular overgrowth (Figure 5a). This model therefore represents an uncontrolled intracellular infection. To achieve and maintain a controlled, persistent intracellular infection, the maintenance antibiotic concentration was increased to 10 × MBC. In the presence of this increased levofloxacin concentration, low levels of intracellular S. epidermidis were detectable up to 14 days in a subset of experiments, suggesting that prolonged intracellular survival can occur under controlled conditions (Figure 5b).

SaOS2-OY intracellular bacterial quantification using CFU and ddPCR

Strains RAH-SE1 and ATCC35984 were selected for comparison and quantification of intracellular bacteria using either CFU plating or ddPCR quantification in SaOS2-OB and SaOS2-OY cultures (Figure 6). Following infection at MOI 100 for one, three, six, or nine days, using a 1 × MBC levofloxacin for maintenance, intracellular CFU levels in SaOS2-OB decreased until day 6 but reappeared on day 9 (Figure 6a), while for SaOS2-OY CFU levels were constant over time. However, ddPCR genome quantification indicated the constant presence of intracellular bacteria for SaOS2-OB, unlike the CFU count (Figure 6b), and moreover bacterial numbers assessed by ddPCR were generally greater than in CFU assessments by three-fold and four-fold at 24 hours post-infection, respectively, for SaOS2-OB and SaOS2-OY (Figure 6b).

Normalization of bacterial to host genome copy numbers (Figure 6c) revealed statistically significant differences in intracellular persistence between the two cell types (Figure 6d). The bacterial:host genome ratio was markedly elevated in SaOS2-OY compared with SaOS2-OB as early as day 1 and remained significantly higher through day 6 for both bacterial strains. By day 9, the ratio declined, particularly for ATCC35984 (Figure 6d).

Host gene expression responses

A panel of genes representing the innate immune response and bone remodelling markers was chosen to characterize the SaOS2-OB and SaOS2-OY infection models. The immunomodulatory genes CCL5, CXCL6, and CXCL10 showed a response in SaOS2-OB cells within the first 24 hours post-infection, with ATCC35984 inducing a significant increase in CXCL6 (p = 0.027) compared with uninfected controls (Figure 7a). The expression of the osteocyte marker gene SOST was upregulated in SaOS2-OB cells at all DPI with RAH-SE1, even though not significant, compared with both S. epidermidis ATCC35984 infection and uninfected controls (Figure 7a). Bone remodelling genes RANKL (p = 0.034) and MMP13 (not significant) were both upregulated at nine DPI with both bacterial strains. In SaOS2-OY cells, the immunomodulatory genes were upregulated at three DPI for CCL5 (p = 0.201) and CXCL10 (p = 0.034), while CXCL6 was upregulated at three and six DPI, even though not significant (Figure 7b). In addition, SOST was significantly upregulated at three DPI for RAH-SE1 in SaOS2-OY cells (p = 0.028). RANKL (p = 0.023) and MMP13 (p = 0.123) were also upregulated in the infected SaOS2-OY cells compared with uninfected controls (Figure 7b).

Discussion

This study showed for the first time that S. epidermidis can infect human osteocytes and persist intracellularly. Additionally, various antibiotics at their respective MBCs or higher failed to eradicate intracellular bacteria, suggesting that persistence within osteocytes could contribute to treatment failure and establishment of chronic S. epidermidis BJI.

In response to lysostaphin, S. epidermidis strain elimination was observed only at lower bacterial densities (OD600 = 0.1) after 24 hours, whereas S. aureus elimination was achieved at both low and high (OD600 = 0.9) bacterial densities and within 2 hours of exposure. These observations indicate, however, that the resistance by S. epidermidis is not necessarily absolute but rather reflects reduced susceptibility that can be overcome under specific conditions of extended exposure time and lower bacterial burden. The differential susceptibility of lysostaphin between S. aureus and S. epidermidis likely originates from the unique peptidoglycan cross-linking pattern in S. aureus, which can be cleaved by the glycyl-glycine endopeptidase function of lysostaphin, while peptidoglycans in S. epidermidis are less accessible with fewer cross-links.29,30 In addition, the overall cell wall composition and structure are different between the species.31,32 Previous studies reported that certain S. epidermidis strains were sensitive to high lysostaphin concentrations;33,34 however, our findings suggest limited effectivity in this regard.

Osteoblast- and osteocyte-stage cultures exhibited marked differences in intracellular bacterial load, culturability, and host responsiveness. ddPCR analysis revealed substantially higher bacterial genome copy numbers in SaOS2-OY than in SaOS2-OB cells, indicating enhanced bacterial uptake and/or persistence in osteocyte-like cells. Bacterial culturability in SaOS2-OB cultures declined over five days of infection, while ddPCR analysis indicated stable intracellular bacteria numbers, indicating transition to a non-culturable state. The relationship between culturability and overall bacterial (genome) yield was largely maintained in SaOS2-OY cultures, despite exposure to high levofloxacin concentrations, an antibiotic considered to have good intracellular effectivity.19 Our study also indicated a difference in host response to S. epidermidis between osteoblast- and osteocyte-like stages. SaOS2-OB demonstrated significant upregulation of CCL5 and CXCL6 chemokine gene expression as early as 24 hours post-infection, whereas SaOS2-OY cultures did not exhibit notable upregulation until three DPI. SaOS2-OY also showed more robust upregulation of SOST mRNA expression, a bone formation inhibitor and pro-catabolic factor,35 than SaOS2-OB cells in response to infection.

Of note, this study reaffirms the general insensitivity and inaccuracy of traditional CFU analysis in assessing bacterial load in the context of an intracellular infection. For example, Sun et al24 demonstrated using a similar host cell model that while CFU and ddPCR measurements were strongly correlated and of similar magnitudes under planktonic growth conditions, CFU failed to recapitulate true bacterial numbers in an S. aureus infection setting. In the current study, ddPCR estimates of intracellular S. epidermidis levels were consistently 102-fold higher than in CFU analysis. This discrepancy between ddPCR and CFU likely reflects the persistence of bacterial DNA from non-culturable or dormant bacteria that remain detectable by ddPCR but fail to form colonies under standard culture conditions. ddPCR of DNA levels potentially detects dead bacterial and host cell genome numbers, which might have contributed to some of the observations here, and this is therefore a study limitation. However, a recent study tested the contribution to the ddPCR signal of residual DNA in heat-killed suspensions and post-viable bacteria culture supernatant of S. aureus strains, and showed that only 7% and 2% of the signal was obtained, suggesting that the method favours detection of viable cells, possibly due to DNA instability following bacterial death and/or loss during processing.28

This study has several other limitations. The susceptibility of other S. epidermidis and coagulase-negative staphylococci strains to lysostaphin, and the antibiotics trialled, may differ, and antimicrobial effectivity would therefore need to be tested and optimized if alternative strains were used. Further, the cell line used, SaOS2, is an immortalized osteosarcoma, and human primary osteoblasts and osteocytes may yield different results. For example, human primary osteocyte-like cells were reported to elicit far greater proinflammatory responses to S. aureus strain WCH-SK2 than SaOS2 osteocyte-like cultures.12,16 However, the use of SaOS2 allows human osteoblast- and osteocyte-like stages to be tested on the same genetic background, in a scalable way that is easily reproducible between laboratories.23

In conclusion, this study established a model for studying S. epidermidis infection of osteocytes, as well as a foundation for understanding the differential susceptibility of human osteoblasts and osteocytes to S. epidermidis infection, and highlights the vulnerability of osteocytes to persistent intracellular bacterial colonization. This in vitro model provides a platform to explore innovative antimicrobial techniques before proceeding to costly and time-consuming preclinical models. Understanding the mechanistic basis of osteocyte-mediated bacterial persistence, which potentially contributes to the chronic, difficult-to-treat, and reinfective nature of osteomyelitis, will be essential for developing more effective treatment strategies for chronic BJIs.
