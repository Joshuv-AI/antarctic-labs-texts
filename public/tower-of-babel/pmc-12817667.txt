Small fiber neuropathy in pediatric female heterozygotes of Fabry disease: a twin case report

Introduction

Fabry disease is an X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the GLA gene. These variants result in deficient activity of the enzyme α-galactosidase A (AGA) and the accumulation of globotriaosylceramide (Gb3), globotriaosylsphingosine (lyso-Gb3), and other α-galactosyl derivatives in vascular endothelial cells. The multisystem effects of these accumulations lead to a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, including neuropathic, ocular, cardiovascular, renal, gastrointestinal, and dermatologic involvement [5]. The systemic impact of Fabry disease necessitates a comprehensive understanding to reduce the significant morbidity and mortality associated with delayed diagnosis and treatment. While Fabry disease follows an X-linked inheritance pattern and has traditionally been considered more severe in males, female heterozygotes can also exhibit a wide range of symptoms due to X-chromosome inactivation, often presenting with variable disease severity [3].

Small fiber neuropathy is a recognized complication of Fabry disease and significantly contributes to disease-related morbidity. It is characterized by symptoms such as limb paresthesias, hypohidrosis, abdominal pain, cold hypoesthesia, and diarrhea. These symptoms typically emerge in childhood and progressively worsen over time, adversely affecting quality of life and long-term prognosis [2, 8]. Importantly, small fiber neuropathy and neuropathic pain are primarily associated with the classic phenotype of Fabry disease and do not occur in late-onset forms.

The diagnosis of Fabry disease is often delayed due to its rarity and the nonspecific nature of its early clinical signs. While the definitive diagnosis is established through genetic testing of the GLA gene, small fiber neuropathy can be confirmed using diagnostic techniques such as quantitative sensory testing (QST), skin biopsy, or pain-related evoked potentials (PREP). These advanced diagnostic tools require specialized equipment and expertise, which are often limited to specialized reference centers. Early recognition of this underdiagnosed condition is crucial, particularly given the availability of targeted therapies such as enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) and chaperone therapy, which can alter disease progression when initiated promptly.

In this report, we describe the case of twin sisters diagnosed with Fabry disease, genetically confirmed by the presence of a pathogenic GLA variant. Despite being female heterozygotes, they exhibit symptoms indicative of small fiber neuropathy, reinforcing the importance of considering Fabry disease in the differential diagnosis of pediatric neuropathic pain. We focus on their clinical presentation, the diagnostic challenges encountered, and the utility of QST and functional assessments in identifying small fiber neuropathy in affected individuals.

Case reports

The first twin exhibited initial symptoms at the age of four, including acroparesthesia, neuropathic pain in the feet, and abdominal discomfort. These episodes occurred 2 to 4 times per week with severe intensity, frequently associated with constipation. Physical examination revealed distal coldness but no other significant abnormalities. Despite normal nerve conduction studies and needle electromyography, quantitative sensory testing (QST) using CASE IV® software (4,2,1 algorithm with null stimuli) demonstrated a significant elevation in Vibratory Detection Thresholds (VDT) in both upper and lower extremities, with percentiles exceeding 97.5, indicating hyposensitivity. Additionally, Heat Pain Thresholds (HPT) were significantly reduced, with percentiles below 2.5 in response to moderate pain stimuli (HPT 5.0), alongside a leftward shift in the thermoalgesic curve, suggesting hyperalgesia. These findings supported the diagnosis of small fiber neuropathy (Figs. 1 and 2).

Further cardiac evaluation with 24-hour Holter monitoring indicated sinus tachycardia and reduced heart rate variability. However, additional assessments, including transthoracic echocardiography, pulmonary function studies, blood chemistries, and abdominal imaging, did not reveal any abnormalities.

The second twin developed symptoms at the age of five, initially presenting with abdominal pain localized in the hypogastrium, later becoming more diffuse. She experienced moderate to severe pain episodes 1–2 times per week, accompanied by diarrhea and acroparesthesia. Her physical examination was unremarkable, and nerve conduction studies along with needle electromyography yielded normal results. However, QST revealed significantly elevated VDT in both upper and lower extremities (Fig. 3), with calculated percentiles exceeding 97.5, indicating hyposensitivity. All other sensory thresholds remained within normal limits. Similar to her sister, Holter monitoring demonstrated decreased heart rate variability, though echocardiography, blood chemistry, and abdominal imaging were unremarkable.

Both patients were confirmed to have a pathogenic GLA variant (c.298 A > T), which results in a premature stop codon and leads to a truncated α-galactosidase A enzyme. This pathogenic variant is associated with the classic phenotype of Fabry disease, characterized by early-onset symptoms and progressive multisystemic involvement. The clinical presentation and QST findings were consistent with small fiber neuropathy, leading to a definitive diagnosis of Fabry disease.

In accordance with international Fabry disease guidelines, enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with recombinant agalsidase beta (1 mg/kg every two weeks) was initiated in both patients due to the presence of small fiber neuropathy. The diagnosis was supported by clinical symptoms, objective quantitative sensory testing (QST) findings, and a pathogenic GLA variant (c.298 A > T), which is known to be associated with the classic phenotype of Fabry disease. Given the progressive nature of Fabry neuropathy, early therapeutic intervention was deemed necessary to mitigate disease progression and improve long-term outcomes. Additionally, neuropathic pain management was commenced with pregabalin at 5 mg/kg/day, divided into two doses. Following the initiation of enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) in combination with multimodal symptomatic pain management, both patients reported a subjective reduction in neuropathic and autonomic symptoms. Although this perceived improvement aligns with previously reported clinical trajectories in Fabry-associated small fiber neuropathy, these observations must be interpreted cautiously, as no validated pain or autonomic assessment scales were applied to objectively quantify change.

Discussion

We present two sisters diagnosed with Fabry disease who exhibited clinical signs of small fiber neuropathy, a condition increasingly recognized in pediatric female heterozygotes. Both experienced acroparesthesia and gastrointestinal symptoms, with one displaying altered thermoalgesic and vibratory sensitivity, while the other demonstrated decreased vibratory sensitivity.

The identified pathogenic GLA variant (c.298 A > T), resulting in a truncated protein, underscores the genetic heterogeneity of Fabry disease and its implications for phenotype severity. These cases highlight the diagnostic challenges posed by Fabry disease, particularly given its nonspecific early symptoms. The presence of acroparesthesia and abdominal pain aligns with prior reports and reinforces the necessity for heightened clinical suspicion. Neuropathic pain is a common manifestation in pediatric females with Fabry disease, affecting up to 40% of individuals under the age of 18 [2]. Additionally, severe and disabling neuropathic pain in female heterozygotes has been well documented [4], with pain attacks occurring in 58% of affected girls [8].

“Fabry neuropathy has been extensively studied using specific questionnaires, qualitative and quantitative peripheral nerve studies, functional sensory assessments, and tissue biopsies. While some pain questionnaires might be an alternative to measure the neuropathic pain [9], QST has been widely used to assess pediatric small fiber function [12], and abnormal QST findings in female heterozygotes with Fabry disease have been correlated with sensory symptom severity [1]. Although sensory dysfunction in heterozygous females has been previously described, quantitative small fiber assessments in pediatric populations remain limited. In these cases, QST performed at age seven revealed significant abnormalities, reinforcing the potential role of QST in early diagnosis and monitoring of Fabry neuropathy.”

Gastrointestinal symptoms are frequently reported in Fabry disease; however, their evaluation and monitoring remain challenging. In this study, gastrointestinal symptoms were assessed through clinical history and patient-reported complaints rather than a standardized evaluation scale. While this approach provided insight into symptom burden, the lack of a structured assessment tool represents a limitation. Recent literature suggests that validated scales such as the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) or Fabry-specific questionnaires can provide objective measures of gastrointestinal dysfunction [7]. Future studies should incorporate these tools to improve symptom quantification and assess treatment response more effectively.

In Fabry disease, early sensory dysfunction typically affects Aδ fibers, with cold detection thresholds being among the most consistently altered parameters [11]. However, in these cases, both twins exhibited significantly elevated vibratory detection thresholds (VDT), suggesting hyposensitivity. While VDT abnormalities are not typically reported in early Fabry disease, potential explanations include broader small fiber impairment extending to Aβ fibers or secondary neuronal involvement due to progressive disease. Another possibility is the influence of central sensitization mechanisms, which have been reported in Fabry neuropathy and may contribute to altered sensory processing [6]. Additionally, vibratory threshold testing can be subject to variability in pediatric patients, influenced by factors such as attention span, cognitive engagement, and response variability. Unlike cold detection thresholds, which rely on an immediate stimulus perception, vibrotactile assessments require sustained focus, making results potentially less reliable in young children. Future studies should incorporate repeated measures or complementary diagnostic tools to enhance the accuracy of sensory evaluations in pediatric Fabry disease patients.

The presence of small fiber neuropathy in these cases is particularly informative. The paradoxical findings of vibratory hyposensitivity and thermal hyperalgesia illustrate the complex alterations in sensory processing characteristic of Fabry disease. These results emphasize the necessity of incorporating objective measures such as QST to evaluate neuropathic pain and sensory dysfunction, particularly in young patients. Additionally, treatment selection in pediatric Fabry disease should consider both guideline recommendations and patient-specific factors.

In accordance with international guidelines, sodium channel blockers (such as carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine) are generally recommended as first-line agents for the symptomatic treatment of Fabry neuropathy. However, in these cases, pregabalin was selected due to its established efficacy in treating small fiber neuropathy, its better safety profile in pediatric patients, and its ease of titration. Pregabalin has demonstrated benefits in Fabry-related neuropathic pain and has been widely used as a second-line option when sodium channel blockers are contraindicated or poorly tolerated [10]. In our patients, pregabalin was chosen due to concerns regarding the potential side effects of sodium channel blockers, particularly in pediatric populations, including sedation, dizziness, and cognitive impairment. Additionally, the dosing flexibility of pregabalin allowed for better symptom control with fewer side effects, making it a more suitable choice for long-term management in young children.

In the present cases, both individuals reported a subjective improvement in neuropathic pain after the initiation of treatment. This clinical perception is consistent with prior literature suggesting that disease-specific therapy together with adjunctive pharmacologic interventions may attenuate neuropathic pain in Fabry disease. However, because no standardized instruments—such as the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4), Small fiber neuropathy–symptoms inventory questionnaire (SFN-SIQ) (SFN-SIQ), or Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31 (COMPASS-31)—were used to document the course of symptoms over time, it is not possible to determine whether this reported change represents a true therapeutic effect or reflects spontaneous variability, natural disease course, or psychological adaptation. The clinical response observed here is therefore likely multifactorial, potentially involving effects of ERT, pharmacologic modulation of nociceptive pathways, nonpharmacological strategies, and patient-level coping mechanisms. Future studies would benefit from incorporating validated, quantitative measures of pain and autonomic dysfunction to better characterize treatment response and delineate the relative contribution of each therapeutic modality.

These findings reinforce the need for individualized treatment strategies that consider both symptom severity and the risk-benefit profile of available therapeutic options. Future studies should further evaluate the long-term efficacy of different pharmacological approaches in pediatric Fabry disease patients. Furthermore, since ERT response in Fabry neuropathy remains a controversial area, future studies should integrate both subjective measures (e.g., pain questionnaires) and objective evaluations such as post-treatment QST assessments to quantify the effects of therapy on small fiber function. This would help establish a clearer relationship between treatment intervention and sensory function improvements in pediatric Fabry patients.

Finally, practical considerations for QST in pediatric patients are crucial. The use of QST in children presents unique challenges, including cognitive engagement, attention span, and response accuracy. To improve test reliability, it is recommended that QST assessments in children incorporate repeated measures and standardized protocols that account for developmental differences in sensory processing. Additionally, the involvement of trained personnel experienced in pediatric neurophysiology can help optimize test accuracy. Despite these challenges, QST remains a valuable tool for identifying small fiber dysfunction in Fabry disease and should be considered in the evaluation of young patients presenting with neuropathic pain.

Conclusions

Small fiber neuropathy may be an early and prominent feature of Fabry disease, underscoring the need to consider this condition in the differential diagnosis of children presenting with neuropathic pain and autonomic symptoms. Early recognition and intervention are critical to slowing disease progression and optimizing patient outcomes. Further research is necessary to delineate the natural history of Fabry disease in pediatric populations and refine therapeutic approaches.

The findings from these twin cases, in conjunction with existing literature, suggest a need to reassess diagnostic and management strategies for Fabry disease, particularly in addressing neuropathic pain and gastrointestinal manifestations in affected females. These cases also reinforce the utility of QST as a valuable tool in detecting small fiber neuropathy in children with Fabry disease.

The patients described a subjective improvement in neuropathic symptoms after starting ERT in combination with symptomatic pain management. While this finding is clinically encouraging, the absence of validated outcome measures limits the ability to attribute this improvement to any specific intervention. These observations underscore the need for structured longitudinal follow-up using standardized sensory and pain assessment tools to more accurately evaluate therapeutic benefit in Fabry-related small fiber neuropathy.
