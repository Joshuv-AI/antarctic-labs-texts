Optimizing Bidding Strategies in First-Price Auctions in Binary Feedback Setting with Predictions
Jason Tandiary Columbia University, Industrial Engineering and Operations Research

Abstract




Abstract

This paper studies Vickrey first-price auctions under binary feedback. Leveraging the enhanced performance of machine learning algorithms, the new algorithm uses past information to improve the regret bounds of the BROAD-OMD algorithm.

Motivated by the growing relevance of first-price auctions and the predictive capabilities of machine learning models, this paper proposes a new algorithm within the BROAD-OMD framework (Hu et al., 2025)
[
4
]
that leverages predictions of the highest competing bid.

This paper’s main contribution is an algorithm that achieves zero regret under accurate predictions. Additionally, a bounded regret bound of
\tilde{O}\left(T^{\frac{3}{4}}V_{t}^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)
is established under certain normality conditions.

1
Introduction

The Vickrey auction, also known as the sealed-price auction, has been increasing in popularity in various markets, from online ads to the financial markets. In this setting, auction participants submit a bid for an item to an auctioneer simultaneously, each with no knowledge of the other participant’s bid. The auctioneer will announce the highest bid, paying a certain amount of value. Depending on the mechanism, the winner would pay either the second-highest or the highest bid, which is their bid.

Historically, research on auctions has revolved around the second-price auction, the primary means implemented in the industry. This is because it has been proven, among other reasons, that second-price auctions encourage truthful bidding, where each bidder’s best strategy is to bid their accurate valuation of the object, eliminating the need to anticipate others’ bids
[
6
]
.

However, more recently, there has been a shift towards first-price auctions due to the increased alleged price manipulation in the online ad market. Since auctioneers are the only entity with access to all individuals’ bids, the auctioneers can submit a false bid that is the second highest among participants such that it is as close as possible to the highest bid, which maximizes their profit. Many antitrust lawsuits have been filed against these online bidding companies, most notably against auctioneer Google
[
3
]
. Research done on this matter also suggests that some second-price auctions have exhibited behaviors that do not match the theoretical guarantees of second-price auctions.
[
2
]
This trend leads to a shift towards first-price auctions, as the transparency issue can be mitigated due to the bidders paying exactly what they bid, with many auctioneers in the market shifting towards this method.

A natural question of interest in this setting is how one can bid optimally in a first-price auction. Since auction players would pay the amount they bid, there is more incentive in bidding less than their valuation of the item, as long as they can still win the item by learning how much other participants will bid from past auctions. Therefore, this can be modelled as a multi-armed bandit problem, where it is modeled as a sequential game where the player slowly learns the patterns of the environment and updates their bidding strategy accordingly. An interesting, realistic scenario is to consider the case when, after each round, the player only observes whether they win or lose the bid, without knowing the winning bid value. This feedback structure is called the binary-feedback setting, where players only know whether they win or lose a round. The benchmark is the regret, which measures the difference between the player’s performance and the best strategy in hindsight.
[
4
]
The goal is to then achieve a less than linear regret with respect to time, as this implies that as time goes on, the player learns and adapts to the environment. An algorithm by Hu et al. in 2025 utilizing the BROAD-OMD has achieved such result, given certain normality conditions.
[
4
]

However, current algorithms do not implement one significant resource in recent biddings: the second-highest bid prediction of other players.This is possible through the advancements of predictive technology such as AI. When implementing predictions to algorithms, the aim is to have an algorithm that achieves a lower regret bound compared to the algorithms without predictions. This is called consistency, which is the expected regret when predictions are 100% correct. The algorithm must also be robust meaning that the regret is still bounded even when the predictions given are sometimes false. Thus the primary motivation of this research is: Can leveraging predictions minimize regret in a repeated first-price auction in a binary feedback setting to guarantee low consistency while ensuring robustness is bounded by the known regret bounds?

1.1
Results

Assume a learner is participating in a
T
-round online first-price auction. At each round, the learner observes an item and makes a private valuation
v_{t}
while receiving a prediction of the highest bid of other players
\tilde{m}_{t}
. The learner then submits a bit
b_{t}
which is then compared to the actual highest bid of other players
m_{t}
. If
b_{t}\geq m_{t}
, the learner wins the bid. After each round, the player only knows whether they have won the bid or not, which characterizes the binary-feedback setting. The main results are as follows:

•

The dynamic regret under consistency is defined to be the value of
R_{T}(\pi):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}-r(b_{t};v_{t},m_{t})\right)
when
\tilde{m}_{t}=m_{t}\text{
}\forall t\in T
. Additionally, robustness is defined to be the value of
R_{T}(\pi)
when
\exists t\in T\text{ where }\tilde{m}_{t}\neq m_{t}
.

•

The showing of a trivial approach of trusting the prediction until the learner loses an auction and resorts to the BROAD-OMD algorithm. While this achieves a 0-dynamic regret under consistency, it may have unbounded regret when predictions is inaccurate and therefore is not robust.

•

\tilde{O}(T^{\frac{3}{4}}V_{t}^{\frac{1}{4}})
at worst, under the normality condition that

1.2
Related Work

There are two primary perspectives in approaching the optimal bidding problem :the game theory and online learning perspective. The aim of the game theory perspective is to find a Nash equilibrium. This is done by finding a stable set of strategies, meaning each bidder cannot improve their outcome by unilaterally changing their bid, given others’ strategies. The most notable work example of this is William Vickrey’s nobel-winning work in second-price auction. Vickrey found that in a second-price sealed-bid auction, the optimal strategy is to bid truthfully, implying the existence of a Nash equilibrium in second-price auctions
[
6
]
.

Although there have been works on the game theory approach for first price auctions, such as the works by Wang et.al in 2020, computing the Nash equilibrium is often not difficult. More assumptions on the bidding pattern or distribution of the other players are needed
[
7
]
. These assumptions may not be realistic in a more general setting. Because of this, there has been a shift in solving the problem by modeling it as an online learning problem. The bidder is modeled as a learner, trying to bid optimally through learning the bidding pattern from other bidders (the environment).

First-price auctions under binary-feedback

The work by Hu, et.al (2025) provides the online-learning approach setting when working with first-price auctions. The problem is modeled as a multi-armed bandit problem, where the arms correspond to values the learner and the other bidders can bid and value an item. The goal is then, is to win the bid with the least bid possible by predicting which arm the other bidders will ”pull”. Hu, et.al (2025) provided an approach using the BROAD-OMD algorithm to generate the bidding policy
[
8
]
. Assuming that the leaner receives binary-feedback and
V_{t}=o(T)
is known, where Vt is the temporal variation, the algorithm achieves a
\tilde{O}(T^{\frac{3}{4}}V_{t}^{\frac{1}{4}})
dynamic regret. The original BROAD-OMD algorithm is attached in the appendix.

The temporal variation for online bidding, proposed also by Hu, et. al, is defined by the variation of the other player’s bidding sequence, more specifically,
V_{T}:=\sum_{t=2}^{T}|m_{t}-m_{t-1}|
is
o(T)
. This is necessary, as it is a known result that achieving sublinear regret is impossible without further assumptions on the bidding’s normality
[
1
]
[
5
]
. This will also be the assumption used for the results in this paper.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Problem Formulation

The auction is modeled as a multi-armed bandit problem where the the learner (in this context, the bidder) and the environment interacts sequentially over
T
rounds. For simplicity and without loss of generality, assume that the range of values the learner and competing players bid is discretized as
N
arms in the range of
[0,1]
meaning the possible bids and valuation are
[\frac{1}{N},\frac{2}{N},...1]
.

At each round, the learner observes an item and generates a private valuation
v_{t}
while at the same time, is given a prediction
\tilde{m}_{t}
, which is a prediction of what the highest bid of the other players
m_{t}
is going to be. The learner then submits a bid
b_{t}
which is then compared with the actual bid
m_{t}
. The payoff received by the learner is given by

r(b_{t};v_{t},m_{t}):=(v_{t}-b_{t})\cdot\mathbb{1}(b_{t}\geq m_{t}).

The goal of the general auction algorithm is to design a policy
\pi
that minimizes the learner’s regret, which is defined as:

R_{T}(\pi):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\,\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}\;-\;r(b_{t};v_{t},m_{t})\Bigr).

After each round, the learner will receive information on the auction. This paper assumes that the learner observes a binary feedback, which means that the learner only receives feedback
\mathbb{1}(b_{t}\geq m_{t})
, indicating whether they win the auction or not. This feedback can be defined formally as filtration

H_{t}^{b}:=\sigma\left((\mathbb{I}(b_{t}\geq m_{t}))_{s=1}^{t-1},v_{t}\right)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the observations.

2.2
Goal

Due to the nature of working with predictions in this paper, the definition of regret must be defined.

definition 1
Let us define the regret under consistency of a policy
\pi
to be the value of
R_{T}(\pi)
when the prediction of the highest bid
m_{t}
is accurate, i.e

R_{T}^{c}(\pi):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}-r(b_{t};v_{t},m_{t})\right)\qquad\text{where }\tilde{m}_{t}=m_{t}\forall t\in T

definition 2
Let us define the regret under robustness of a policy
\pi
to be the value of
R_{T}(\pi)
when the prediction of the highest bid
m_{t}
is accurate, i.e

R_{T}^{r}(\pi):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}-r(b_{t};v_{t},m_{t})\right)\qquad\exists t\in T\text{ where }\tilde{m}_{t}\neq m_{t}

The goal is to achieve a regret under consistency
R_{T}^{c}(\pi)
. that is better than
\tilde{O}(T^{\frac{3}{4}}V_{t}^{\frac{1}{4}})
, which is the regret bound proposed by Hu et.al without regret, while at the same time achieving a bounded regret under robustness
R_{T}^{r}(\pi)
.

2.3
Normality conditions

It is a known result from Besbes et al. 2015 that achieving a sublinear dynamic regret is impossible without additional assumptions on the problem instance, such as the normality of bidding structure of other players
m_{t}
.
[
1
]
Because of this, this paper uses the temporal variation of the bidding sequence
V_{T}
as a normality condition, proposed by Hu et al. 2025.

V_{T}:=\sum_{t=2}^{T}|m_{t}-m_{t-1}|

Assume that
V_{T}=o(T)
holds.

3
Methodology

3.1
Trivial approach

One possible solution to the problem is to always trust the prediction by submitting bid
b_{t}=\max(\tilde{m}_{t},0)
as long as the bid is won or the private valuation
v_{t}

\tilde{m}_{t}

The above algorithm achieves the lowest regret possible of 0 under consistency, as with perfect predictions, it always bids the prediction

However, this algorithm breaks down when the prediction consistently overbids. In the case of overbidding, the learner will always win the bid while incurring a constant regret, which scales linearly with the number of rounds. Let us formalize this in the following proposition, with the proofs being in the appendix:

Proposition 1.
Assume the learner receives the binary-bid feedback and
V_{T}=o(T)
and is known. Then the trivial algorithm achieves 0 regret under consistency, i.e.,
R_{T}^{c}=0\ \forall T
, and an unbounded regret under robustness, i.e.,
R_{T}^{r}\rightarrow\infty\text{ as }T\rightarrow\infty
.

3.2
Window Algorithm

The Window Algorithm takes the same idea as the trivial approach, starting with bidding
b_{t}=\tilde{m}_{t}
until a mistake is made. The robustness, however, is bounded by the Window
W

The intuition of this algorithm is as follows: If the prediction is always correct,
\alpha=1
almost surely. This means that the algorithm will stay bidding using the prediction, yielding regret of 0.

If the prediction overbids, the window guarantees that at some point, the bid will change to BROAD-OMD samples, thereby giving the regret bound of BROAD-OMD itself. If the the prediction underbids, the algorithm switches to BROAD-OMD immediately, therefore also giving the same regret bound as BROAD-OMD.

4
Next Steps

The window algorithm proposed reduces to a dynamic regret bounded by
\tilde{O}(T^{\frac{3}{4}}V_{t}^{\frac{1}{4}})
in the case where there is the slightest inaccuracy in the prediction, even if the prediction is accurate
99\%
in all rounds. This is because the algorithm distrusts the prediction with only a single mistake. An algorithm that can increase and reduce the confidence under certain scenarios would be desirable to avoid this happening, with the hope that the regret bound can be dependent on
\Delta m:=\tilde{m}_{t}-m_{t}
. Such algorithm would be more applicable as in real life scenario, it is very unlikely for a model to achieve a
100\%
in predicting the highest bid of other participants.

The algorithm proposed works on a discreteized finite number of arms corresponding to the possible bids. In reality, the value bidder can place on auction is almost continuous, and so a natural extension is to adapt the algorithm in a setting where the bid takes a continuous value.

Appendix

5.1
Proof of Consistency of trivial approach

In the case where
\tilde{m}_{t}
predicts the highest bid
m_{t}
correctly every round, the algorithm will always bid equal to the prediction
\tilde{m_{t}}
. Note that as long as
v_{t}\geq\tilde{m}_{t}
, since
\tilde{m_{t}}
=
m_{t}
, the bid is won. And so, throughout all
T
rounds,

b_{t}\begin{cases}m_{t},&\text{if }v_{t}\geq\tilde{m}_{t}\\
0,&\text{if }v_{t}<\tilde{m}_{t}\end{cases}

The regret of the algorithm will simplify into:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}-(v_{t}-b_{t})\cdot\mathbb{1}(b_{t}\geq m_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}-(v_{t}-m_{t})\cdot\mathbb{1}(v_{t}\geq m_{t})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}-\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}

\displaystyle\quad=0

Thus, in the case where the prediction is always right, the algorithm will achieve the best possible regret of 0.

5.2
Proof of Robustness of trivial approach

Take the simple case when
m_{t}=\frac{1}{N}
and
\tilde{m}_{t}=v_{t}=\frac{2}{N}
for all rounds
t=1,...T
. This case satisfies the normality condition
V_{T}=o(T)
. However since the prediction always overbids, the algorithm will win all
T
rounds, meaning the bid
b_{t}
is always equal to the prediction
\tilde{m_{t}}
. The regret in this case is:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}-(v_{t}-b_{t})\cdot\mathbb{1}(b_{t}\geq m_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\max\{v_{t}-m_{t},0\}-(v_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})\cdot\mathbb{1}(\tilde{m}_{t}\geq m_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max\{\frac{2}{N}-\frac{1}{N},0\}-(\frac{2}{N}-\frac{2}{N})\cdot 1

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{N}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{T}{N}

Which grows unboundedly to infinity as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

5.3
Proof of Consistency of Window Algorithm

It is trivial to see that as long as
\alpha=1
, the regret is always 0, since the predictions are 100% accurate. The question then becomes: given that the predictions are accurate, what are the chances that
\alpha=0
?

Since the BROAD - OMD algorithm maintains a probability distribution over all possible arms, denote
\tilde{p_{t}}
as arms that are greater than the sample from BROAD - OMD
\theta_{t}
. As BROAD-OMD keeps updating the probability distribution, since no single arm ever reaches 0 probability, the
\sum(\tilde{p_{t}})
is greater than 0. The probability of
\alpha
being 0 then is
(1-\sum(\tilde{p_{t}}))^{W}

Since
W
grows with
T
, as
T
goes to infinity, the chances of
\alpha=0
is vanishingly small. Therefore the total regret is 0 almost surely.

Let S be the event that alpha = 0

\mathbb{E}[R_{T}^{c}]=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}^{c}\mid S\right]P(S)+\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T}^{c}\mid S^{c}\right]P(S^{c})

=\left(1-\sum_{t}\tilde{p_{t}}\right)^{W}\cdot o\left((T)^{3/4}V_{t}^{1/4}\right)+\left[1-\left(1-\sum_{t}\tilde{p_{t}}\right)^{W}\right]\cdot 0=0\quad{a.s}

5.4
Proof of Robustness of Window Algorithm

The worst-case scenario of the algorithm is exhibited if the prediction consistently over-bids. This can be proven through analyzing three realistic separate cases of wrong predictions.

Case 1: Predictions constantly underbid (
\tilde{m_{t}}<m_{t}
)

It is not uncommon for a predicting model to under-predict every bid due to faulty data. Cases for underbidding could be due to an item that has its value tied to its antiquity. In these cases, values may rise exponentially, especially if said item has limited production.

In this case, since the algorithm immediately switches to BROAD-OMD given a lost bid, it is then clear to see that the regret here is
C
, for the first round added by the error of BROAD-OMD.

\displaystyle\text{Let }C

\displaystyle:=\text{ Range of bids},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[R_{T}^{r}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq C+o\bigl((T-1)^{3/4}\,V_{t}^{1/4}\bigr).

Therefore, the regret is bounded.

Case 2: Predictions overbid (
\tilde{m_{t}}>m_{t}
)

Just like underbidding, this occurs when looking at values of items that are depreciating. An example of this could be a bid for a used car, where values fall as time goes on.

Since the algorithm switches to BROAD-OMD after
M
rounds and
W
is finite and monotonically decreasing, for the first
M
rounds, the regret can be bounded by a constant
C
where
C
is the range of possible bids.

\displaystyle\text{Let }M

\displaystyle:=\text{ \# of rounds where }\alpha=1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[R_{T}^{r}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq M\,C+o\!\bigl((T-M)^{3/4}\,V_{t}^{1/4}\bigr).

Case 3: Prediction oscillates between overbidding and underbidding

In most cases of a predictive model, it will fall under Case 3, where for some rounds the prediction overbids, and for others underbids. Let the chance of the prediction underbidding be
u
.

Then, the regret of the first
n
rounds where the prediction over- or underbids can be bounded by the same constant
C
, where
C
is the range of possible bids.

n\sim\mathrm{Geom}(u)

Note that if
u=0
, case 2 is recovered.

If
u>0
, then:

\mathbb{E}[n]=\frac{1}{u}

Giving a regret bound of:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[R_{T}^{r}\bigr]\leq\frac{C}{u}+o\!\bigl((T-\tfrac{1}{u})^{3/4}\,V_{t}^{1/4}\bigr).
