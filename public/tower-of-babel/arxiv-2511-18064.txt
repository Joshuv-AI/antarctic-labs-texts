Abstract
. An upper mean here is a subadditive functional M ¯ \overline{M} defined on bounded functions on a commutative group which has, beside some natural requirements, the property we call restricted additivity: if g ⁡ ( x ) = f ⁡ ( x ) + f ⁡ ( x + t ) g(x)=f(x)+f(x+t) , then M ¯ ​ ( g ) = 2 ​ M ¯ ​ ( f ) \overline{M}(g)=2{\overline{M}}(f) . This tries to grasp that it should not depend on local properties. This naturally induces a lower mean, and when they coincide it is the mean. Restriction to 0–1 valued functions (sets) is a density. We answer the following questions: Given a functional defined on a subset of all functions, when is it a mean? Given a functional, which is a mean, how do we find the upper mean it came from? Is it unique? Given a function f f , what are the possible values of M ¯ ​ ( f ) \overline{M}(f) , for upper means M ¯ \overline{M} ? In particular, we find the extremal means and give several expressions for it. We propose the names “lowest and uppermost mean” for them to replace the not really justified names “lower and upper Banach mean and density”. We also consider analogous questions for densities, with partial answers only.

1.
Introduction

There are many concepts of density in use. For sets of integers we have the

asymptotic upper density
, defined as

\overline{d}(A)=\limsup\frac{|A\cap[1,n]|}{n},

its symmetric variant, where the interval
[-n,n]
is used, the logarithmic density and others;
each has the corresponding lower density, and when they coincide, they are called the density.
There is one, generally called the
Banach density
defined as

d^{*}(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\max|A\cap[t+1,t+n]|}{n}.

We shall propose the alternative name “uppermost density”, since we
not find any connection with Banach. The first similarly defined concept we could locate is
from Pólya
[
10
]
(he says it “deserves less attention”, see page 561), and the first real application by Kahane
[
7
]
, see also
[
8
]
, page 54.

In the 60s the notion resurfaced in several works. Landau
[
9
]
referred to lectures of Beurling – related studies of Beurling himself was published only later in his collected works, see
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
. The notion was introduced in relation to packing in geometry by Groemer
[
6
]
. The name “Banach density” originated most probably from Fürstenberg
[
4
]
. The extension of the density to groups – in particular when the group is non-metrisable – required new constructions. This has been done in
[
5
]
and also in
[
11
]
. The extended notion has been successfully applied to questions of interpolation
[
5
]
, densities of packing
[
12
]
,
[
1
]
, and syndetic sets
[
13
]
.

Since there is no natural analog of the intervals in other groups, or there are several
possible candidates (balls, cubes etc.), the extension of this concept may proceed in several ways.
They happen to be equivalent, and equivalence is proved on an ad hoc basis. Our aim is to
develop a theory of densities in groups, and point out the special position of the generalized Banach density among them.

A set can be identified with a 0-1 valued function. We found it preferable to treat
general bounded functions instead, then specialize to sets. So first we consider the concept of a mean.

In this first part we restrict ourselves to commutative discrete groups. The starting point was sets of positive integers, which is only a semigroup; we plan to return to this and to the more general situation where the structure is a locally compact commutative group.

2.
Upper and lower means

Let
G
be an infinite commutative (discrete) group.

Definition 2.1
.

A functional
\overline{M}
, which assigns a real number to every bounded real function
defined on
G
, is an

upper mean
, if the following hold:

(a) Norming: if
f
is a constant function,
f\equiv c
, then
\overline{M}(f)=c
.

(b) Monotonicity: if
f\leq g
, then
\overline{M}(f)\leq{\overline{M}}(g)
.

(c) Homogeneity: if
f=cg
with a constant
c\geq 0
, then
\overline{M}(f)=c\overline{M}(g)

(d) Restricted additivity: if
g(x)=f(x)+f(x+t)
,
then
\overline{M}(g)=2{\overline{M}}(f)
.

(e) Subadditivity:
\overline{M}(f+g)\leq\overline{M}(f)+\overline{M}(g)
.

Translation invariance will follow from these assumptions. Property (d) is a way to express that
the men does not depend on local properties, like
x
being odd or even. On the other hand,
invariance under automorphisms of
G
(e.g.
x\hookrightarrow-x
in
{\mathbb{Z}}
) is not assumed.
Upper asymptotic mean on
{\mathbb{N}}
, that is

\limsup\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(j)

is an admissible upper mean in our sense.

Convention.

In the sequel we shall write simply “function” to mean bounded real function
defined on
G
.

Statement 2.2
.

Instead of assumption (b) above, the following special case suffices:

(b’) Negativity: if
f\leq 0
, then
\overline{M}(f)\leq 0
.

Proof.

Indeed, if
f\leq g
, by subaditivity we get

{\overline{M}}(f)\leq{\overline{M}}(g)+{\overline{M}}(f-g)\leq{\overline{M}}(g).

∎

Remark 2.3
.

Instead of (b) it is not sufficient to assume

(b”) Positivity: if
f\geq 0
, then
\overline{M}(f)\geq 0
.

Example 2.4
.

On the set of integers define

M(f)=\limsup\frac{1}{n}\left({2\sum_{i=1}^{2n}f(i)-3\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)}\right).

Properties (a), (c), (e) are obvious. Property (d) is easy and we suppress the simple proof.
For a nonnegative
f
let

\alpha=\limsup\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i),

the usual asymptotic upper mean. For all large
n
we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)<\alpha+\varepsilon,

and for infinitely many values

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}f(i)>2\alpha-\varepsilon,

hence

M(f)>2(2\alpha-\varepsilon)-3(\alpha+\varepsilon)=\alpha-5\varepsilon,

which shows the positivity of
M(f)
.

To see a negative
function with a positive
M
let

f(n)=\begin{cases}-1&\text{ if }2^{2i-1}<n\leq 2^{2i},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

For
n=2^{2i}
the sum in
(n+1,2n]
vanishes, hence we have

\frac{1}{n}\left({2\sum_{i=1}^{2n}f(i)-3\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)}\right)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)>n/2,

so
M(f)\geq 1/2
.

Statement 2.5
.

Instead of (d) we can assume

(d’)
restricted substractivity
: if
h(x)=f(x)-f(x+t)
,
then
\overline{M}(h)=0
.

Proof.

Restricted substractivity implies resricted additivity as follows. If
g(x)=f(x)+f(x+t)
,
then
g+h=2f
, so

2{\overline{M}}(f)={\overline{M}}(2f)\leq{\overline{M}}(g)+{\overline{M}}(h)={\overline{M}}(g),

and

{\overline{M}}(g)\leq{\overline{M}}(2f)+{\overline{M}}(-h)={\overline{M}}(2f);

we used that
-h(x)=f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(x-t)
with
f^{\prime}(x)=f(x+t)
.

The converse implication is a particular case of the following theorem.
∎

Theorem 2.6

(General restricted additivity)
.

Let
\overline{M}
be an upper mean,
f
a function,
c_{i}
a sequence of real numbers such that

\sum|c_{i}|<\infty
and
\sum c_{i}\geq 0
,
t_{i}\in G
. For
g(x)=\sum c_{i}f(x+t_{i})
we have

\overline{M}(g)=\sum c_{i}{\overline{M}}(f)
.

Proof.

We will now and then use the following easy consequence of the assumptions:

|{\overline{M}}(f)-{\overline{M}}(g)|\leq\sup|f(x)-g(x)|.

Consider first the case when the sequence is finite, of length
k
, and
\sum c_{i}=1
.

We can write restricted additivity in the form

{\overline{M}}(f)={\overline{M}}\left({\frac{f(x)+f(x+t)}{2}}\right)

(we use
x
as a dummy variable). Applying this equation to the right hand side with
2t,4t,\ldots,2^{n-1}t

in the place of
t
we obtain

{\overline{M}}(f)={\overline{M}}\left({2^{-n}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}f(x+jt)}\right).

By applying this successively for
t=t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
we get

{\overline{M}}(f)={\overline{M}}\left({2^{-kn}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{n}-1}\ldots\sum_{j_{k}=0}^{2^{n}-1}f(x+j_{1}t_{1}+\ldots+j_{k}t_{k})}\right)={\overline{M}}(f^{\prime}).

The same transformation applied to the function
g
yields

{\overline{M}}(g)={\overline{M}}\left({2^{-kn}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{n}-1}\ldots\sum_{j_{k}=0}^{2^{n}-1}g(x+j_{1}t_{1}+\ldots+j_{k}t_{k})}\right)=

={\overline{M}}\left({2^{-kn}\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{2^{n}-1}\ldots\sum_{j_{k}=0}^{2^{n}-1}f(x+j_{1}t_{1}+\ldots+j_{k}t_{k}+t_{i})}\right)={\overline{M}}(g^{\prime}).

The sums defining
f^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime}
are almost identical: both contain summands of the form

f(x+j_{1}t_{1}+\ldots+j_{k}t_{k})
with
0\leq j_{i}\leq 2^{n}
, and of total weight 1 if all
j_{i}
satisfy
0<j_{i}<2^{n}
.
If some
j_{i}
is 0 or
2^{n}
, then their weights may differ, the difference being at most
\sum|c_{i}|
. The number of such

k
-tuples is
\leq(2^{n}+1)^{k}-(2^{n}-1)^{k}
.
Consequently

|{\overline{M}}(f)-{\overline{M}}(g)|=|{\overline{M}}(f^{\prime})-{\overline{M}}(g^{\prime})|\leq\sup|f^{\prime}-g^{\prime}|

\leq 2^{-kn}\left({(2^{n}+1)^{k}-(2^{n}-1)^{k}}\right)\sum|c_{i}|\sup|f(x)|.

This bound tends to 0 as
n\to\infty
which concludes the proof of this subcase.

Homogenity yields the case of finitely many terms with
\sum c_{i}>0
.

To show the general case take an
\varepsilon>0
, and choose
k
so that

\sum_{i>k}|c_{i}|<\varepsilon.

Consider

g^{\prime}(x)=2\varepsilon f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}f(x+t_{i}).

This is covered by the previous case, so we have

{\overline{M}}(g^{\prime})=\left({2\varepsilon+\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}}\right){\overline{M}}(f),

and also

|{\overline{M}}(g^{\prime})-{\overline{M}}(g)|\leq\sup|g^{\prime}(x)-g(x)|\leq 3\varepsilon\sup|f(x)|.

These equations together imply the claim.
∎

To show that a given functional is an upper mean we shall check criteria (a), (b’), (c), (d’), (e).

Definition 2.7
.

A functional
\underline{M}
, which assigns a real number to every function
defined on
G
, is a

lower mean
, if it satisfies requirements (a)-(d) above, and instead of (e) it has the
opposite property:

(e’) Superadditivity:
\underline{M}(f+g)\geq\underline{M}(f)+\underline{M}(g)
.

There is a natural correspondence between upper and lower means:

\underline{M}
is a lower mean if and only if

\overline{M}(f)=-\underline{M}(-f)
is an upper mean. We shall call such a pair of
an upper and a lower mean
conjugates
. (Exercise: show that
{\underline{M}}(f)\leq{\overline{M}}(f)
.)

3.
The mean when it exists

If
\overline{M}(f)=\underline{M}(f)
, we call their common value
M(f)
the
mean
,
and the function
medial
.

In the sequel we try to aswer the following questions

Given a functional defined on a subset of all functions, when is it a mean?

Given a functional, which is a mean, how do we find the upper mean it came from? Is it unique?

Given a function
f
, what are the possible values of
\overline{M}(f)
, for upper means
\overline{M}
?

Theorem 3.1
.

Medial functions form a linear subspace
L
, and the mean is a linear functional on
L
.
It has the following further properties:

(a) Norming: constants belong to
L
, and if
f
is a constant function,
f\equiv c
, then
M(f)=c
.

(b) Monotonicity: if
f\leq g
,
f.g\in L
, then
M(f)\leq M(g)
.

(c) Closedness: if a function
f
has the property that for every
\varepsilon>0
there are
functions

\underline{g},\overline{g}\in L
such that
\underline{g}\leq f\leq\overline{g}
and

M(\overline{g})-M(\underline{g})<\varepsilon
, then
f
is also medial.

(d) For every function
f
, the functions
f(x+t)-f(x)
are medial and their mean is 0.

Proof.

If
f
is medial, then
{\underline{M}}(f)={\overline{M}}(f)=-{\underline{M}}(-f)
, hence homogeneity extends to
negative numbers:
M(cf)=cM(f)
for all real
c
.

If
f,g
are medial, then

{\overline{M}}(f+g)\leq{\overline{M}}(f)+{\overline{M}}(g)={\underline{M}}(f)+{\underline{M}}(g)\leq{\underline{M}}(f+g),

hence mediality of the sum and additivity of the mean follow.

Properties (a)-(d) immediately follow from the definition.
∎

L
is typically a small subspace (later we shall describe the smallest), but it can be the whole space.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a functional
M
, defined on a subspace
L
of bounded functions is a
mean
,
if there is an upper mean
{\overline{M}}
with conjucate lower mean
{\underline{M}}
such that
{\underline{M}}(f)={\overline{M}}(f)
holds
exactly for
f\in L
and then this value is
M(f)
.

Definition 3.3
.

We call a mean
full
, if every bounded function is medial.

Theorem 3.4
.

Given an upper mean
{\overline{M}}
with conjugate lower mean
{\underline{M}}
, there is a full mean
M_{1}
satisfying
{\underline{M}}(f)\leq M_{1}(f)\leq{\overline{M}}(f)
for all
f
.
(Consequently
M_{1}
is an extension of
M
).

Proof.

As
M\leq{\overline{M}}
and
{\overline{M}}
is subadditive, the Hahn-Banach theorem yields the existence of a linear extension
M_{1}

of
M
satisfying
M_{1}\leq{\overline{M}}
. To see that it is a mean, that is, both and upper and lower mean, note that
homogeneity, sub- and superadditivity are included in linearity. Norming and restricted substractivity are
consequences of being an extension of
M
; for the latter note that all difference functions
f(x)-f(x+t)

are medial with mean 0. To
show monotonicity, observe that if
f\leq g
, then

M_{1}(f)-M_{1}(g)=M_{1}(f-g)\leq{\overline{M}}(f-g)\leq 0.

∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
M
be a linear functional, defined on a linear subspace
L
of bounded functions.
M
is a mean
if and only if it satisfies (a)-(d) of Theorem
3.1
.

This will be proved together with the next theorem.

Given a mean, the upper mean it came from is not unique, but we can find the largest upper mean
and the smallest lower mean among the possible choices.

Definition 3.6
.

Given a mean
M
on a subspace
L
of medial functions,, its
upper and lower envelope
are

M^{e}(f)=\inf_{g\in L,g\geq f}M(g),\ M_{e}(f)=\sup_{g\in Lg\leq f}M(g),

over medial
g
.

Theorem 3.7
.

The upper and lower envelope of a mean
M
are upper and lower means, resp., which induce the mean
M
.

Proof.

We show that for any functional
M
satisfying conditions (a)-(d) of Theorem
3.5

the functional
M^{e}
defined above is an upper mean. The only nontrivial property is restricted additivity.
Let
g(x)=f(x)+f(x+t)
, and take a medial
h
such that
g\leq h
,
M(h)<M^{e}(g)+\varepsilon
. Then

2f(x)\leq h(x)+\bigl(f(x)-f(x-t)\bigr),

hence
2M^{e}(f)\leq M(h)<M^{e}(g)+\varepsilon
. As we can do this for every positive
\varepsilon
, we conclude

M^{e}(g)\geq 2M^{e}(f)
. The converse inequality is obvious.

Clearly
M^{e}
and
M_{e}
both extend
M
. Conversely, if
M^{e}(f)=M_{e}(f)
, then
f
is medial by property (c).
∎

On the other hand, there is no smallest upper and largest lower mean for a given mean
M
. Indeed, if
M_{1}
is a full
extension of
M
, then for any
c\in(0,1)
the upper mean
{\overline{M}}_{2}(f)=c{\overline{M}}(f)+(1-c)M_{1}(f)
is smaller than
{\overline{M}}
.

Remark 3.8
.

The upper envelope of the asymptotic mean is not the asymptotic upper mean but

M^{e}f(f)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\varepsilon x}\sum_{x<n<(1+\varepsilon)x}f(n).

This was also observed by Pólya
[
10
]
.

4.
Commarginality

We try to describe those classes of functions on which all means coincide.
To this end we introduce the concept of commarginality.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f,f^{\prime}
be bounded functions on
G
. We say that they are
commarginal
,
and write
f\sim f^{\prime}
, if there
exists a bounded function
F
on
G^{2}
and a finite set
B\subset G
such that
F(x,y)\neq 0
implies

x-y\in B
, and we have always

\sum_{y}F(x,y)=f(x),\ \sum_{x}F(x,y)=f^{\prime}(y).

(Note that the previous condition implies that the sums are finite. Intuitive meaning:
cut each
f(x)
into finitely many parts, move them by bounded distances and reassemble to get
f^{\prime}
.)

Theorem 4.2
.

Commarginality is an equivalence relation, and it is compatible with linear operations;
that is,

(a)
f\sim f
,

(b) if
f\sim f^{\prime}
, then
f^{\prime}\sim f
;

(c) if
f\sim f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime}\sim f^{\prime\prime}
, then
f\sim f^{\prime\prime}
;

(d) if
f\sim f^{\prime}
, then
cf\sim cf^{\prime}
for every constant
c
;

(e) if
f\sim f^{\prime}
and
g\sim g^{\prime}
, then
f+g\sim f^{\prime}+g^{\prime}
.

Proof.

All save transitivity (c) immediately follow from the definition. To prove transitivity assume first that
f^{\prime}

is bounded away from 0, say
|f^{\prime}(x)|>c>0
. The functions
f,f^{\prime}
are marginals of some
F
restricted by
a finite set
B
;
f^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime}
of
F^{\prime}
,
B^{\prime}
. Now define

F"(x,z)=\sum_{y}\frac{F(x,y)F^{\prime}(y,z)}{f^{\prime}(y)}.

This is a finite sum, it is bounded by our assumptions, and if the sum is nonempty, then we have

x-y\in B
and
y-z\in B^{\prime}
for some
y
, whence
x-z\in B^{\prime\prime}=B+B^{\prime}
.
By summing first over
z
we see that one marginal of
F"
is indeed
f
, and by summing over
x
we see that
the other one is
f"
.

To get rid of the assuption choose a number
c>\sup|f^{\prime}|
, apply the previous case for the functions

f+c,f^{\prime}+c,f"+c
and use linearity.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Commarginality is compatible with any upper or lower mean; that is, if

f,f^{\prime}
are commarginal functions and
\overline{M}
is any upper mean, then

\overline{M}(f)=\overline{M}(f^{\prime}).

Lemma 4.4
.

For every finite
B\subset G
and
\varepsilon>0
there is a finite
C\subset G
such that
|B+C|/|C|<1+\varepsilon
.

Easy and well-known. We shall show a stronger result as Lemma
10.4
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Let
F
and
B
be the function and the finite set inducing commarginality of
f
and
f^{\prime}
.
Assume first that
F>0
. Take an
\varepsilon>0
and a finite
C\subset G
such that
|B+C|/|C|<1+\varepsilon
.
Put

g(x)=\sum_{t\in C}f(x-t),

g^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{t\in B+C}f^{\prime}(x-t).

We have

g(x)=\sum_{t\in C}\sum_{y}F(x-t,y).

All nonzero terms of this sum satisfy
x-t-y\in B
, hence
y\in x-t-B\subset x-C-B
, so

g(x)\leq\sum_{y\in x-C-B}\sum_{z}F(z,y)=g^{\prime}(x).

Consequently

{\overline{M}}(g)=|C|{\overline{M}}(f)\leq{\overline{M}}(g^{\prime})=|B+C|{\overline{M}}(f^{\prime}),

that is,

{\overline{M}}(f)\leq\frac{|B+C|}{|C|}{\overline{M}}(f^{\prime})<(1+\varepsilon){\overline{M}}(f^{\prime}).

Similarly we deduce
{\overline{M}}(f^{\prime})<(1+\varepsilon){\overline{M}}(f)
,
and as this holds for every positive
\varepsilon
, we infer
{\overline{M}}(f^{\prime})={\overline{M}}(f)
.

To get rid of the positivity assumption, we apply the positive case for the functions

F_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}F(x,y)+c&\text{ if }x-y\in B,\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

with some
c>-\inf F
,
f_{1}(x)=f(x)+c|B|
,
g_{1}(x)=g(x)+c|B|
.
∎

Some weaker relations still imply coincidence of means.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
f,f^{\prime}
be bounded functions on
G
. We say that they are
l^{1}
-commarginal
,
and write
f\overset{1}{\sim}f^{\prime}
, if there
exists a bounded function
F
on
G^{2}
and a function
h\in l^{1}(G)
such that
|F(x,y)|<h(x-y)
for all
x,y\in G
and

\sum_{y}F(x,y)=f(x),\ \sum_{x}F(x,y)=f^{\prime}(y).

Definition 4.6
.

Let
f,f^{\prime}
be bounded functions on
G
. We say that they are
limit-commarginal
,
and write
f\overset{{\rm lim}}{\sim}f^{\prime}
, if there exist two sequences
g_{n},g_{n}^{\prime}
of bounded functions such that

g_{n}\sim g_{n}^{\prime}
,
g_{n}\to f
and
g_{n}^{\prime}\to f^{\prime}
uniformly.

Statement 4.7
.

f\sim f^{\prime}\to f\overset{1}{\sim}f^{\prime}\to f\overset{{\rm lim}}{\sim}f^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The first implication is obvious. For the second, given the functions
F
and
h
, take finite sets
B_{n}
such that

\sum_{x\notin B_{n}}h(x)<1/n.

Put

F_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}F(x,y)&\text{ if }x-y\in B_{n},\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

\sum_{y}F_{n}(x,y)=g_{n}(x),\ \sum_{x}F_{n}(x,y)=g_{n}^{\prime}(y).

Clearly
g_{n}\sim g_{n}^{\prime}
and

|f(x)-g_{n}(x)|<1/n,\ |f^{\prime}(x)-g_{n}^{\prime}(x)|<1/n.

∎

Statement 4.8

(Asymmetric form of limit-commarginality.)
.

If
f\overset{{\rm lim}}{\sim}f^{\prime}
, there are functions
f_{n}
such that
f_{n}\sim f
,
f_{n}\to f^{\prime}
uniformly.

Proof.

If
g_{n}\sim g_{n}^{\prime}
,
g_{n}\to f
and
g_{n}^{\prime}\to f^{\prime}
, we can put
f_{n}=g_{n}^{\prime}-g_{n}+f
.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Both
l^{1}
-commarginality and limit-commarginality are equivalence relations, compatible with linear operations
and any upper or lower mean.

Proof.

Transitivity of
l^{1}
-commarginality can be proved like that of commarginality above. Transitivity of
limit-commarginality is shown as follows. Assume
f\overset{{\rm lim}}{\sim}f^{\prime}\overset{{\rm lim}}{\sim}f"
. By Statement
4.8
there are sequences of
functions
f_{n}\to f
,
f_{n}\sim f^{\prime}
and
f_{n}"\to f"
,
f_{n}"\sim f^{\prime}
; hence
f_{n}\sim f_{n}"

and these sequences are sufficient using
the original definition. Reflexivity symmetry and compatibility of linear opertions of both relations are obvious.

To show compatibility of limit-commarginality with upper means take
sequences
g_{n},g_{n}^{\prime}
of bounded functions such that
g_{n}\sim g_{n}^{\prime}
,
g_{n}\to f
and
g_{n}^{\prime}\to f^{\prime}

uniformly. We have
{\overline{M}}(g_{n})={\overline{M}}(g_{n}^{\prime})
,

|{\overline{M}}(g_{n})-{\overline{M}}(f)|\leq\sup|g_{n}-f|\to 0,\ |{\overline{M}}(g_{n}^{\prime})-{\overline{M}}(f^{\prime})|\leq\sup|g_{n}^{\prime}-f^{\prime}|\to 0

and these relations together yield the conclusion.

The claim for

∎

5.
Uppermost and lowest means

For a given function
f
consider all convex combinations of translates, that is, all
functions of the form

g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f(x+t_{i}),\ c_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum c_{i}=1.

Put

M^{*}(f)=\inf_{g}\sup_{x}g(x),

M_{*}(f)=\sup_{g}\inf_{x}g(x).

Theorem 5.1
.

M^{*}
is an upper mean, and
M_{*}
is the conjugate lower mean. From among all possible upper
means
M^{*}
is maximal, and from among all possible lower means

M_{*}
is minimal.

Proof.

Norming, homogeneity and subadditivity of
M^{*}
is clear. For restricted additivity take

f^{\prime}(x)=\bigl(f(x)+f(x+t)\bigr)/2
. As the collection of convex combinations of translates
of
f^{\prime}
is a subset of those of
f
, we immediately see that
M^{*}(f^{\prime})\geq M^{*}(f)
, while the converse inequality
follows from subadditivity.

From Theorem
2.6
we get that
{\overline{M}}(f)={\overline{M}}(g)\leq\sup g
and hence

{\overline{M}}(f)\leq M^{*}(f)
for any upper mean.

The claims for lower means follow by obvious calculations.
∎

This motivates the following name.

Definition 5.2
.

We call
M^{*}
the
uppermost mean
, and
M_{*}
the
lowest mean
.

If the uppermost and lowest means coincide (a rare thing, a sort of quasi-periodicity), their value is
the
absolute mean
, denoted by
{M^{*}_{*}}(f)
. Such functions have the same mean under all circumstances; we call them

absolutely medial
. Functions commarginal to a constant have this property.

Theorem 5.3
.

A function is absolutely medial if and only if it is limit-commarginal to a constant.

Proof.

Sufficiency follows from Theorem
4.9
. To show necessity take a function with
{M^{*}_{*}}(f)=\alpha
and a positive integer
k
.
By definition we find nonnegative constants
c_{i},c_{i}^{\prime}
and
t_{i},t_{i}^{\prime}\in G
such that
\sum c_{i}=\sum c_{i}^{\prime}=1
and

g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f(x+t_{i})<\alpha+1/k,\ g^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}c_{i}^{\prime}f(x+t_{i}^{\prime})>\alpha-1/k

for all
x
. Put

h_{k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}c_{j}c_{i}^{\prime}f(x+t_{j}+t_{i}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}g^{\prime}(x+t_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}c_{i}^{\prime}g(x+t_{i}^{\prime}).

The first expression shows that
h_{k}\sim f
, the second that
h_{k}(x)>\alpha-1/k
and the third that
h_{k}(x)<\alpha+1/k
;
hence
h_{k}\to\alpha
uniformly and
f\overset{{\rm lim}}{\sim}\alpha
.
∎

6.
Uppermost mean as average

Convention.

Typically a “set” is a finite nonempty subset of
G
; we shall write

B\Subset A
to mean
B\subset A
,
B\neq\emptyset
,
|B|<\infty
.

Definition 6.1
.

Given a set
B\Subset G
, the
B
-average (conditional expectation)
of a function is

(f|B)(t)=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{b\in B}f(b+t).

Definition 6.2
.

The
expansion pseudomean
of a function
f
is

E(f)=\inf_{T\Subset G}\sup_{B\Subset G}\frac{1}{|B+T|}\sum_{b\in B}f(b).

In the next section we will see why it is not really a mean.

Theorem 6.3
.

If
f\geq 0
, then

M^{*}(f)=E(f)=\inf_{B\Subset G}\sup(f|B).

Proof.

Since
f|B
is a particular case of the linear combinations used in the definition
of
M^{*}
, we immediately see that
M^{*}(f)\leq\inf_{B\Subset G}\sup(f|B)
.

Next we show that

M_{1}:=\inf_{B\Subset G}\sup(f|B)\leq E(f).

To see this, given any
T\Subset G
and
\varepsilon>0
, we need to find a
B\Subset G
such that

\sum_{b\in B}f(b)>(M_{1}-\varepsilon)|B+T|.

Take
\delta
such that
M_{1}/(1+\delta)^{2}>M_{1}-\varepsilon
, then a set
C
such that
|C+T|<(1+\delta)|C|
(Lemma
4.4
).
By definition, for some
t
we haveg

(f|C)(t)\geq M_{1}/(1+\delta),

hence with
B=C+t
we have

\sum_{b\in B}f(b)=|B|(f|C)(t)\geq M_{1}|B|/(1+\delta)\geq M_{1}|C+T|/(1+\delta)^{2}\geq(M_{1}-\varepsilon)|B+T|.

Finally we show that
E(f)\leq M^{*}(f)
.

Take
t_{i}\in G
and constants
c_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum c_{i}=1

such that

g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f(x+t_{i})\leq M^{*}(f)+\varepsilon.

Write
\{t_{i}\}=T
. Take any set
B\Subset G
. Write
A=B-T
.
We have

\sum_{b\in B}f(b)=\sum_{b\in B}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f(b-t_{i}+t_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\sum_{b\in B}f\bigl((b-t_{i})+t_{i}\bigr)

\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\sum_{a\in A}f(a+t_{i})=\sum_{a\in A}g(a)\leq|A|\bigl(M^{*}(f)+\varepsilon\bigr),

consequently

E(f)\leq\sup_{B\Subset G}\frac{1}{|B-T|}\sum_{b\in B}f(b)\leq\bigl(M^{*}(f)+\varepsilon\bigr).

Nonnegativity was only used in this step, and in the next section we show that this inequality
fails for general functions. ∎

Theorem 6.4
.

For evey
f
we have

M^{*}(f)=\inf_{B\Subset G}\sup(f|B)),

M_{*}(f)=\sup_{B\Subset G}\inf(f|B).

Proof.

The case of nonnegative functions is included in the previous theorem.
For the general case observe that
for constants we have
M^{*}(f+c)=M^{*}(f)+c
and
(f+c|B)(t)=(f|B)(t)+c
,
so with suitable
c
it reduces to the nonnegative case.
∎

We do not need all finite sets for the above equalities.

Definition 6.5
.

We call a collection
{\mathcal{B}}
of nonempty subsets of the group
G
a

witness
, if for every function
f
we have

M^{*}(f)=\inf_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}\sup(f|B).

Theorem 6.6
.

A collection
{\mathcal{B}}
of nonempty subsets of the group
G
is a witness if and only if for
every finite set
T\Subset G
we have

(6.1)

\inf_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}\frac{|B+T|}{|B|}=1.

Proof.

Sufficiency is shown like in the previous proof, where the only fact we used was
that the set of all subsets satisifies (
6.1
).

To show necessity, assume that
{\mathcal{B}}
fails to satisfy (
6.1
). This means that there is a finite
set
T\Subset G
such that
|B+T|/|B|>c>1
for all
{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}
. We may suppose
0\in T
, since this property
is translation invariant.

For each
{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}
take an element

t(B)\in G
such that all sets
B+T+t(B)
are disjoint. The existence of such
t(B)
is easily shown
by a transfinite induction, since the cardinality of the collection of finite subsets of
G

is the same as that of
G
.

For every
x\in G
select a
z(x)\in x-T
so that
z(x)\in B+t(B)
if

(B+t(B))\cap(x-T)\neq\emptyset,

in other words, if
x\in B+t(B)+T

for some
{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}
. By the disjointness assumption there can be at most one such
B
. If there
is no such
B
, let
z(x)\in x-T
arbitrary.

Put

f=\sum_{x\in G}{\bf 1}_{z(x)}.

This function is commarginal to the identically 1 function, hence
M^{*}(f)=1
. On the other hand

\sum_{x\in B+t(B)}f(x)=|B+T|,

consequently
\sup(f|B)>c>1
for all
{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}
.
∎

Sometimes we can replace infimum by a limit.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
B_{i}
be a sequence of finite subsets of
G
such that for every

t\in G
we have

(6.2)

\frac{|(B_{i}+t)\setminus B_{i}|}{|B_{i}|}\to 0.

Then

(6.3)

M^{*}(f)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\sup(f|B_{i}).

Condition (
6.2
) is also necessary.

Such a sequence exists if and only if
G
is countable.

Proof.

As for every finite
T\Subset G
we have

|B_{i}+T|\leq|B_{i}|+\sum_{t\in T}|(B_{i}+t)\setminus B_{i}|,

from condition (
6.2
) we infer that
|B_{i}+T|/|B_{i}|\to 1
, hence every infinite collection of
the sets
B_{i}
is a witness. Hence

M^{*}(f)=\inf_{i\in I}\sup(f|B_{i})

for every infinite set
I
of integers, which is clearly equivalent to (
6.3
).

Conversely, if
(
6.2
) fails for some
t
, then (
6.1
) fails for
T=\{0,t\}
and the collection

\{B_{i}\}
is not a witness.

If
G
is countable, list the elements of
G
as

G=\{0,g_{1},g_{2},\ldots\}.

Take a set
B_{k}
such that

|B_{k}+\{0,g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\}|<(1+1/k)|B_{k}|.

On the other hand, if
G
is uncountable, let
H
be the subgroup generated by
\bigcup B_{i}
. This is a countable
subgroup. Take any
t\in G\setminus H
. The sets
B_{i}
and
B_{i}+t
are disjoint, so property (
6.2
) cannot hold.
∎

7.
The expansion mean for general functions

Given a function
f
we put

f^{+}(x)=\max\{f(x),0\},

the positive part of
f
.

Theorem 7.1
.

For every function we have

E(f)=\min\{\sup f,M^{*}(f^{+})\}.

Proof.

Clearly
E(f)\leq\sup f
, and
E(f)\leq E(f^{+})=M^{*}(f^{+})
by Theorem
6.3
.
To prove equality we will find for every
\varepsilon>0
and finite
T\Subset G
a set
B\Subset G
such that

\frac{1}{|B+T|}\sum_{b\in B}f(b)>\min\{\sup f,M^{*}(f^{+})\}-\varepsilon.

Case 1.

M^{*}(f^{+})>0
.

As
E(f^{+})=M^{*}(f^{+})
, there is a set
B^{\prime}
such that

\frac{1}{|B^{\prime}+T|}\sum_{b\in B^{\prime}}f(b)>M^{*}(f^{+})-\varepsilon.

If
\varepsilon<M^{*}(f^{+})
, the sum must be positive, so the set

B=\{b\in B^{\prime}:f(b)>0\}

is not empty. Clearly
|B+T|\leq|B^{\prime}+T|
and

\sum_{b\in B}f(b)\geq\sum_{b\in B^{\prime}}f(b),

so this
B
suffices.

Case 2.

M^{*}(f^{+})=0

Take a
b\in G
such that
f(b)>\sup f-\varepsilon
. Put
B=\{b\}
. Then

\frac{1}{|B+T|}\sum_{b\in B}f(b)=\frac{f(b)}{|T|}\geq\min\{f(b),0\}.

Observe the dichotomy: if
f(b)<0
, the minimum is attained for
|T|=1
,
and if
f(b)>0
, we get the infimum for
|T|\to\infty
. This explains the
strange behavior of
E(f)
.

∎

8.
Means with prescribed values

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
\mu
be a functional, defined on a subset
\Lambda
of bounded functions.
Assume that constants belong to
\Lambda
, and if
f
is a constant function,
f\equiv c
, then
\mu(f)=c
.
Suppose that

(*) for every
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\Lambda
,
t_{1},..,t_{k}\in G
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

c_{1}f_{1}(x+t_{1})+...+c_{k}f_{k}(x+t_{k})\geq 0

we have
\sum c_{i}\mu(f_{i})\geq 0
.

Then
\mu
can be extended to a full mean.

Remark 8.2
.

Condition (*) is clearly necessary for the existence of such an extension.

\Lambda
need not be a subspace. We do not need to assume that
\mu
is bounded and linear,
this will follow from condition (*).

Proof.

Consider all extensions of
\mu
that satisfy (*). By Zorn’s lemma there is a maximal among them. Let

M
be such a maximal one, defined on a set
L\supset\Lambda
. We show that
M
and
L
satisfy the conditions
of Theorem
3.5
.

Observe first that
L
is translation invariant. Indeed, if (*) holds for a collection of tunctions,
it automaticallyy holds for the collection of translations of these functions.

Let
L^{\prime}
be the subspace generated by
L
. Any
g\in L^{\prime}
is of the form

g=c_{1}f_{1}+...+c_{k}f_{k},\ f_{i}\in L.

This representation is typically not unique, but the value of
\sum c_{i}M(f_{i})
is independent of the representation.
Indeed, if
\sum c_{i}f_{i}=\sum c_{i}^{\prime}f_{i}^{\prime}
, then
\sum c_{i}f_{i}-\sum c_{i}^{\prime}f_{i}^{\prime}
and
\sum c_{i}^{\prime}f_{i}^{\prime}-\sum c_{i}f_{i}
are both nonnegatve, whence

\sum c_{i}M(f_{i})-\sum c_{i}^{\prime}M(f_{i}^{\prime})
is both nonegative and nonpositive.
This argument shows that
M^{\prime}(g)=\sum c_{i}M(f_{i})
is a well-defined linear extension of
M
to
L^{\prime}
;
by the maximality assumption
M^{\prime}=M
,
L^{\prime}=L
.

From the conditions of Theorem
3.5
, (a), norming, is among our assumptions.
(b’), negaivity, is the case
k=1
of (*).

(c) Closedness means that if a function
f
has the property that for every
\varepsilon>0
there are functions

\underline{g},\overline{g}\in L
such that
\underline{g}\leq f\leq\overline{g}
and

M(\overline{g})-M(\underline{g})<\varepsilon
, then
f\in L
. If this were not the case, we could extend
M

to
L\cup\{f\}
by putting

M^{\prime}(f)=\sup_{\underline{g}\leq f}M(\underline{g})=\inf_{\overline{g}\geq f}M(\overline{g}).

We need to check that (*) holds for this extension, that is, if

(8.1)

c_{1}f(x+t_{1})+...+c_{k}f(x+t_{k})+h(x)\geq 0

with
h\in L
, then
we have
(\sum c_{i})M^{\prime}(f)+M(h)\geq 0
. Now (
8.1
) implies that

\sum c_{i}g_{i}(x+t_{i})+h(x)\geq 0,

where
g_{i}=\overline{g}
if
c_{i}\geq 0
and
g_{i}=\underline{g}
if
c_{i}<0
. Applying (*) to this equation we obtain

(\sum c_{i})M^{\prime}(f)+M(h)\geq-\varepsilon\sum|c_{i}|,

and we are done.

Finally we show
(d), that is, for every function
g
, the function
f(x)=g(x+t)-g(x)
is in
L
and
M(f)=0
.
If this is not the case, we extend
M

to
L\cup\{f\}
by putting
M^{\prime}(f)=0
.
We need to check that (*) holds for this extension, that is, if

(8.2)

c_{1}f(x+t_{1})+...+c_{k}f(x+t_{k})+h(x)\geq 0

with
h\in L
, then
we have
M(h)\geq 0
. (In (*) we can have many wighted translations of functions from
L
, but
we can combine them into a single one by the previous arguments.)

We know that
f
is absolutely medial with mean 0, hence so is
F(x)=\sum c_{i}f(x+t_{i})
.In particular,

M^{*}(F)=0
, which means that there are constants
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\geq 0
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in G

such that
\sum a_{i}=1
and
\sum a_{i}F(x+u_{i})<\varepsilon
.

Summing the assumption
F+h\geq 0
with these weights and translations we obtain

\sum a_{i}h(x+u_{i})\geq-\varepsilon,

that is,
\sum a_{i}(h(x+u_{i})+\varepsilon)\geq 0
. By (*) this implies
M(h)\geq-\varepsilon
. As we have this for all
positive
\varepsilon
, we conclude
M(h)\geq 0
.

So by Theorem
3.5

M
is a mean, which by Theorem
3.4
can be extended to a full mean.
∎

.

.

Let

be a bounded function, and
\alpha\in[M_{*}(f),M^{*}(f)]

Theorem 8.5
.

For a pair

(b) The equality

(d)

.

9.
Upper and lower densities

Definition 9.1
.

We call a function
\overline{d}
(or
\underline{d}
), assigning a real number to every subset of
G

an
upper density
(
lower density
), if there exists an upper mean
{\overline{M}}
(a lower mean
{\underline{M}}
)
such that

{\overline{d}}(A)={\overline{M}}({\bf 1}_{A}),\ {\underline{d}}(A)={\underline{M}}({\bf 1}_{A})

for all
A\subset G
.

A conjugate pair of an upper and a lower mean induces a conjugate pair of an upper and a lower density.
These are connected by
\overline{d}(A)=1-\underline{d}(G\setminus A)
.
If
{\overline{d}}(A)={\underline{d}}(A)
, it is
the density

d(A)
.

We consider the following questions.

When is a function, defined on subsets of
G
, an upper density?

Does an upper density uniquely determine the upper mean used in the definition?

When is a function, defined on a family of subsets of
G
, a density?

Given a density, how do we find the upper density it came from? Is it unique?

Given a subset of
G
, what are the possible values of its (upper/lower) densities?

Definition 9.2
.

We say that two sets
A,A^{\prime}
are
perturbations
of each other and write
A\simeq A^{\prime}
, if there is a finite set
B

and a 1-to-1 mapping
\varphi:A\to A^{\prime}
such that
\varphi(x)-x\in B
for all
a\in A
.

Statement 9.3
.

Any upper density
\overline{d}
(with conjugate lower density
{\underline{d}}
) has the following properties:

(a) Norming:
\overline{d}(\emptyset)=0
,
\overline{d}(G)=1
.

(b) Monotonicity: if
A\subset B
, then
\overline{d}(A)\leq\overline{d}(B)
.

(c) Restricted additivities: if the sets
A+t_{i}
are disjoint, then
\overline{d}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A+t_{i})=n\overline{d}(A)
,

{\underline{d}}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A+t_{i})=n{\underline{d}}(A)
.

(d) Sub- and superadditivity:
\overline{d}(A\cup B)\leq\overline{d}(A)+\overline{d}(B)
,

{\underline{d}}(A\cup B)\geq{\underline{d}}(A)+{\underline{d}}(B)
if
A\cap B=\emptyset
.

(e) Perturbation: if
A^{\prime}
is a perturbation of
A
, then
\overline{d}(A)=\overline{d}(A^{\prime})
.

Remark 9.4
.

In (c) and (d) above, the requirements for upper and lower densities are not equivalent (at least not obviously).
Those with lower density could be reformulated using the upper one, which would seem less natural.

Proof.

Properties (a)-(d) are obvious. For (e) observe that
A\simeq A^{\prime}
implies
{\bf 1}_{A}\sim{\bf 1}_{A}^{\prime}
; we need just to define

F(x,y)=1
if
x=\varphi(y)
, 0 otherwise.
∎

Problem 9.5
.

If
{\bf 1}_{A}\sim{\bf 1}_{A^{\prime}}
, is
A^{\prime}
“almost” a perturbation of
A
?

Let
G={\mathbb{Z}}
, and consider

F(x,y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }x=y\geq 0,\\
-1&\text{ if }x=y+1\geq 1.\end{cases}

This shows that the sets

We dot not have a general exact expression for the “almost” above.

Take a tile

10.
Uppermost and lowest densities

Definition 10.1
.

We call the upper density induced by the uppermost mean the
uppermost density
,
the lower density induced by the lowest mean the
lowest density
, denoted by

d^{*}
, and
d_{*}
, resp.

Theorem 10.2
.

d^{*}(A)=\inf_{B}\max_{t}\frac{|A\cap(B+t)|}{|B|}=\inf_{X}\sup_{B}\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B+X|},

infimum, supremum are over finite nonempty
B,X\Subset G
.

This is a special case of Theorem
6.3
.

For this equality we do not need all finite sets. Recall from Section 4 that we called
a collection
{\mathcal{B}}
of nonempty subsets of the group
G
a

witness
, if for every function
f
we have

M^{*}(f)=\inf_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}\sup(f|B).

Theorem
6.6
tells that a
collection
{\mathcal{B}}
of nonempty subsets of the group
G
is a witness if and only if for
every finite set
T\Subset G
we have

(10.1)

\inf_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}\frac{|B+T|}{|B|}=1.

Theorem 10.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a family of finite sets. If
{\mathcal{B}}
is a witness, then

d^{*}(A)=\inf_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}\max_{t}\frac{|A\cap(B+t)|}{|B|}.

The above condition is necessary (witnesses for sets and functions are the same).

Lemma 10.4
.

For every finite
B\Subset G
and
\varepsilon>0
there is a tile
Y
such that

|B+Y|/|Y|<1+\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
H
be the subgroup generated by
B
. As a finitely generated commutative group,
H
is isomorphic
to
H^{\prime}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\times F
with some finite group
F
. Let
\varphi:H\to H^{\prime}
be the isomorphism. First we tile
H^{\prime}
.
If
d=0
, put
Y^{\prime}=H^{\prime}
,
Z^{\prime}=\{0\}
. In this case

\frac{|\varphi(B)+Y^{\prime}|}{|Y^{\prime}|}=1.

If
d\geq 1
, put
Y^{\prime}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}^{d}\times F
,
Z^{\prime}=(n{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}\times\{0\}

with some
n
. If
m
is the maximum of the absolute value of coordinates of
\varphi(B)
, then

\frac{|\varphi(B)+Y^{\prime}|}{|Y^{\prime}|}\leq\left({\frac{n+2m}{n}}\right)^{d}<1+\varepsilon

if
n
is large enough.

Y=\varphi^{-1}(Y^{\prime})
satisfies
|B+Y|/|Y|<1+\varepsilon
and tiles
H
with
Z^{*}=\varphi^{-1}(Z^{\prime})
.

Finally take a set
R
which contains exactly one elment from each coset
H+t
of
H
and put

Z=Z^{*}+R
to see that
Y
also tiles
G
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
10.3
.

The “if” part of Theorem
10.3
is a special case of Theorem

6.6
.

To prove necessity we shall construct, given a collection
{\mathcal{B}}
that fails to satisfy (
10.1
),
a set
A
such that

d^{*}(A)<\inf_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}\max_{t}\frac{|A\cap(B+t)|}{|B|}.

The fact
that
{\mathcal{B}}
fails to satisfy (
10.1
) means that there is a finite
set
T\Subset G
such that
|B+T|/|B|>c>1
for all
{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}
. We may suppose
0\in T
, since this property
is translation invariant.

Take a tile
Y
, tiling
G
with some set
Z
, such that

\frac{|T+Y|}{|Y|}=c^{\prime}<c.

For each
{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}
take an element

t(B)\in G
such that all sets
B+T+t(B)
are disjoint. The existence of such
t(B)
is easily shown
by a transfinite induction, since the cardinality of the collection of finite subsets of
G

is the same as that of
G
.

For every
z\in Z
select an
a(z)\in(Y+z)\cap(B+t(B))
if there is such a
{B\in{\mathcal{B}}}
.
By the disjointness assumption there can be at most one such
B
. If there is no such
B
,
let
a(z)\in Y+z
arbitrary. Put

A=\{a(z):z\in Z.\}

Clearly
d^{*}(A)=1/|Y|
.

Now we estimate
n=|A\cap(B+t(B)|
. The set
B+t(B)
is covered by
n
translations of
Y
, hence

(B+t(B))+T
is covered by
n
translations of
Y+T
. Comparing the sizes of these sets we obtain

c|B|<|B+T|\leq n|Y+T|=c^{\prime}n|Y|,

that is,

\frac{|A\cap(B+t(B)|}{|B|}=\frac{n}{|B|}>\frac{c}{c^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|Y|}=\frac{c}{c^{\prime}}d^{*}(A).

∎

Sometimes we can replace the infimum by a limit.

Theorem 10.5
.

Assume that
G
is countable, and let
B_{i}
be a sequence of finite subsets of
G
with the property that for every

x\in G
we have

\frac{|(B_{i}+x)\setminus B_{i}|}{|B_{i}|}\to 0.

Then

d^{*}(A)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\max_{t}\frac{|A\cap(B_{i}+t)|}{|B_{i}|}.

This is a special case of Theorem

.

Remark 10.6
.

This is the way upper Banach density is usually defined in
{\mathbb{Z}}
(with intervals) or in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
(with cubes or balls).

11.
Density, packing and covering

Definition 11.1
.

Let
A\subset G
. For a positive integer
k
let
p(k)
(packing) denote the largest integer with the property that
there exist
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in G
, such that every element of
G
is contained in at most in
p(k)
of the sets
A+t_{i}
.

Theorem 11.2
.

We have

\lim p(k)/k=d^{*}(A).

Proof.

Observe first that
p
is subadditive, hence the limit exists.

Write
f={\bf 1}_{A}
and
g(x)=\sum f(x-t_{j})
. We have
g(x)\leq p(k)
and

kd^{*}(A)=kM^{*}(f)=M^{*}(g)\leq p(k),

hence
d^{*}(A)\leq p(k)/k
for all
k
.

On the other hand, by Theorem
10.2
for every
\varepsilon>0
we find a finite set
B
such that

|A\cap(B+t)|\leq|B|(d^{*}(A)+\varepsilon)

for all
t
. With
k=|B|
and
-B=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}
this shows
p(k)\leq k(d^{*}(A)+\varepsilon)
, that is,

\limsup p(k)/k\leq d^{*}(A).

∎

Definition 11.3
.

Let
A\subset G
. For a positive integer
k
let
lc(k)
(covering) denote the largest integer with the property that
there exist
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in G
, such that every element of
G
is contained in at least in
c(k)
of the sets
A+t_{i}
.

Theorem 11.4
.

We have

\lim c(k)/k=d_{*}(A).

Proof.

Apply the previous theorem for the complement of
A
.
∎

The following result was obsrved in several situations.

Theorem 11.5
.

Let
A
be a set of positive uppermost density. We have

\left\lceil{\frac{1}{d_{*}(A-A)}}\right\rceil\leq\left\lfloor{\frac{1}{d^{*}(A)}}\right\rfloor,

consequently

d_{*}(A-A)\geq d^{*}(A).

Proof.

Let
k
be the largest integer with the property that
there exist
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in G
, such that the sets
A+t_{i}
are disjoint. In the notation of Theorem
11.2

this means that
p(k)=1
and hence
k\leq 1/d^{*}(A)
. For every
x\in G
the set
A+x
must intersect some
A+t_{i}
,
that is,
x+a=t_{i}+a^{\prime}
with
a,a^{\prime}\in A
which means
x\in t_{i}+(A-A)
. This shows
d_{*}(A-A)\geq 1/k

∎
