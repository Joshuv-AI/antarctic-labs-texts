Identifying cancer prognosis genes through causal learning
Authors: Siwei Wu, Chaoyi Yin, Yuezhu Wang, Huiyan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate identification of causal genes for cancer prognosis is critical for estimating disease progression and guiding treatment interventions. In this study, we propose CPCG (Cancer Prognosis’s Causal Gene), a two-stage framework identifying gene sets causally associated with patient prognosis across diverse cancer types using transcriptomic data. Initially, an ensemble approach models gene expression’s impact on survival with parametric and semiparametric hazard models. Subsequently, an iterative conditional independence test combined with graph pruning is utilized to infer the causal skeleton, thereby pinpointing prognosis-related genes. Experiments on transcriptomic data from 18 cancer types sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas Project demonstrate CPCG’s effectiveness in predicting prognosis under four evaluation metrics. Validations on 24 additional datasets covering 12 cancer types from the Gene Expression Omnibus and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas Project further demonstrate CPCG’s robustness and generalizability. CPCG identifies a concise but reliable set of genes, obviating the need for gene combination enumeration for survival time estimation. These genes are also proved closely linked to crucial biological processes in cancer. Moreover, CPCG constructs a stable causal skeleton and exhibits insensitivity to the order of data shuffling. Overall, CPCG is a powerful tool for extracting cancer prognostic biomarkers, offering interpretability, generalizability, and robustness. CPCG holds promise for facilitating targeted interventions in clinical treatment strategies.

Introduction

Identifying cancer prognosis biomarkers is crucial for guiding clinical treatment intervention. With the advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies, transcriptomic data have emerged as a rich resource for identifying genes and gene patterns closely associated with important biological processes of tumor and patient survival [1–3].

In conventional prognosis analysis, researchers typically employ accelerated failure models or proportional hazard models, with overall survival time or hazard as target variables, combined with the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) model to unveil key prognostic genes for cancer [4–6]. The most commonly used COX-LASSO is a semiparametric approach to identify cancer prognosis–related genes [7, 8], but there is no causality between these genes and cancer prognosis [9]. Besides, machine learning (ML)–based and deep learning (DL)–based computational methods are also utilized to identify biomarkers and gene patterns, sometimes serving as an end-to-end strategy to predict survival [10–14]. However, the limited interpretability, robustness, and generalizability for end-to-end learning hinder their adoption in clinical application.

In contrast to generalized correlation-based methods (ML, DL, etc.) that primarily rely on statistical metrics to screen variables highly associated with overall survival, causality-based approaches are designed to establish causal graphs or quantify causal effects to reveal causal relationships between variables, thereby identifying genes that are directly linked to survival outcomes. Unlike correlation, a causal relationship is stable and robust. Some variables may exhibit associations because they are both influenced by a third factor, or the association exists due to data distribution bias. In such scenarios, correlation may disappear or alter when the data distribution changes, often resulting in poor generalizability of the identified prognostic genes on independent test sets, limiting their clinical utility. Conversely, variables with a causal relationship typically remain stable amidst such disturbances [15].

Current methods for identifying causal genes can be roughly categorized into two types: causal structure learning–based method [16–18] and causal effect estimation–based ones [19, 20]. The former typically identifies causal genes linked to disease variables through causal feature selection algorithms, aiming to identify features causally related to the target variable [21]. These methods often rely on Bayesian networks and the theory of Markov boundaries [22–24]. Bayesian networks are probabilistic graphical models that describe causal relationships between variables using nodes (variables) and directed edges (relationships) [25]. The Markov boundary defines the local causal structure as the minimal set of variables that can best predict the target variable, encompassing the target variable’s parent nodes (direct causes), child nodes (direct effects), and spouse nodes (other parents of its children nodes) [25]. The stable impact of causal features on target variable across diverse data contexts ensures that models constructed with these features possess enhanced explanatory power [15] and robustness [26, 27], thereby substantially augmenting the predictive capacity of the models. Therefore, the primary objective in identifying the causal genes for cancer prognosis lies in determining the local causal structure of cancer prognosis. However, it challenges by the identification of the direction of edges within the Markov equivalence class [28]. On the other hand, causal effect estimation–based feature screening estimates the pure effect of one or a set of gene perturbations on core biological processes in cancers by balancing the distribution of all the confounders. Subsequently, genes exhibiting strong causal effects on the target variable are identified as causal genes [19, 20].

However, these causal feature selection methods encounter challenges when applied to prognostic gene recognition tasks, particularly due to right-censored survival data. Additionally, the presence of high-dimensional data poses significant computational hurdles in learning the causal skeleton [29, 30]. Consequently, there is a need for innovative methods that can simultaneously address these challenges.

In this study, we propose the CPCG (Cancer Prognosis’s Causal Gene), a two-stage framework for identifying prognostic causal genes. In the first stage, CPCG aims to obtain a simplified gene dataset that is significantly correlated with prognosis, to facilitate subsequent causal skeleton construction. We employ an ensemble strategy, using both parametric and semiparametric hazard models, to model the association between gene expression and patient prognosis. In the second stage, we utilize these genes with overall survival time as the target variable. Through iterative conditional independence tests for each variable pair, we roughly construct a causal skeleton, selecting genes directly linked to overall survival time as prognosis causal genes. To assess predictive performance, we use a variety of metrics and validate generalizability across diverse datasets. We also explore the biological functions of these genes. In summary, we aim to develop a simple yet effective, interpretable, and highly generalizable method for identifying prognostic genes, which is crucial for clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Method overview

In this study, we propose a framework called CPCG that integrates survival analysis techniques with censored data and causal feature selection methods to identify causal genes related to cancer prognosis from high-dimensional transcriptome data. CPCG is a two-stage identification scheme. In Stage I, to obtain a comprehensive yet relatively concise set of credible candidate genes, we employ both parametric and semiparametric strategies to obtain candidate genes while taking hazard as the target variable. Hazard encompasses survival time and survival status, effectively handling censoring in survival data. The distinction between these two strategies lies in the data types and the way that hazard is calculated so that the genes obtained by the two strategies can effectively complement each other. The union of these two gene sets forms the candidate gene pool for subsequent causal gene identification. In Stage II, we deploy a causal feature selection method that constructs a causal skeleton linking cancer prognosis–related genes and overall survival time through death cases data through iterative conditional independence tests, marginal independence tests, and graph pruning. These steps significantly eliminate confounding factors that interfere with causal inference. Genes directly linked to overall survival time are ultimately identified as the causal genes for cancer prognosis. Figure 1 illustrates the workflow of CPCG.

In the CPCG approach, the parametric model is particularly adept at precisely quantifying the impact of gene expression levels on cancer prognosis. This model operates under the assumption that a patient’s hazard conforms to an exponential distribution, which allows for a detailed and accurate assessment of gene influence within a specific framework. On the other hand, the semiparametric model excels in extrapolating candidate genes across a broader range of patient data without relying on such specific distributional assumptions. This flexibility enables the identification of genes that may not conform to the strict assumptions of the parametric model, thus broadening the scope of potential prognostic markers. By combining the candidate gene sets derived from both models, our approach yields a more comprehensive gene list. This list includes genes that are explicable by the exponential proportional hazards model, as well as those that are not bound by data distribution assumptions. Consequently, the genes identified through CPCG exhibit heightened statistical robustness and are adaptable across diverse datasets. Furthermore, this collaborative approach facilitates the identification of genes that may have been overlooked when utilizing a single method.

Stage II: Identify causal gene of cancer prognosis

Utilizing data from deceased patients, we employ conditional independence tests and graph pruning techniques to construct a causal skeleton aimed at identifying the causal genes from the candidate pool. Initially, all pairs of variables, including candidate genes and survival time, are connected to form an undirected graph. Edges between variables showing only conditional dependence are then removed. By employing conditional independence testing and edge pruning iteratively until no further edges can be removed from the graph, we construct a skeleton that represents the potential causal relationships among variables. Genes directly linked to overall survival time within this skeleton are identified as the causal genes influencing cancer prognosis. If a gene and survival time are linked through intermediary genes, their variations usually do not directly impact each other. We exclude the edge linking them as including these genes often leads to unstable and biased predictions. We do not distinguish whether the first-order neighbors of survival time are causes or results as we believe these genes can effectively reflect and predict prognosis in either scenario.

Data collection and preprocessing

We collect data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Project (TCGA), which involves 7371 patients across 18 distinct cancer types throughout the body (Fig. 2A), including a total of 2654 (36%) death cases and 4717 (64%) survival cases. Specifically, we mainly focus on cancer types with a death count exceeding 100 within the TCGA dataset. This threshold is set to ensure that the statistical analyses conducted would be robust and meaningful. In addition to these datasets, we include cancer types such as ESCA, UCEC, LAML, MESO, and SARC. These are chosen for their moderate mortality population, ranging between 50 and 100 deaths. The inclusion of these datasets allows us to assess the generalizability and effectiveness of our CPCG approach across a variety of mortality scales. To ensure the reliability and clinical relevance of the identified causal genes, we only focus on protein-coding genes and filter out genes with an average raw count below 10. The number of genes used for each cancer is detailed in Fig. 2A. In data preprocessing, we standardize the transcriptome data using transcripts per million (TPM) normalization.

To assess the generalization and robustness of our method, we collect an additional 24 independent datasets from 12 cancer types in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and 2 datasets from 1 cancer type in the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas Project (CGGA) (Fig. 2B). These datasets encompass 4625 patients, with 2103 (45%) death cases and 2522 (55%) survival cases. Here, from these two databases, we found 6 datasets with over 100 deaths, another 6 datasets containing a moderate population of death cases, and additionally, 12 sets with <50 deaths. We expect these common cancer datasets can validate the relationship between the genes identified by CPCG and cancer prognosis in populations with large, medium, and small death cases. Gene expression levels from the GEO are stored in microarray data, and we apply a log2 transformation to the data. The CGGA data, obtained through high-throughput transcriptome sequencing, are standardized using TPM normalization.

Experimental settings

In Stage I, we establish a P-value threshold of .01 in the logrank test and filter out genes with P-values exceeding .01. For the parametric and semiparametric models, we select the top 100 genes as candidate prognosis-related genes. Moving to Stage II, we utilize the Fisher Z test to evaluate the significance of conditional independence tests, with the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha$\end{document} set to 0.05.

Biological relevance of identified causal genes

The set of 68 prognosis causal genes identified by CPCG in TCGA data across 18 cancer types holds significant biological relevance. Upon reviewing the literature, we discovered that 31 out of these 68 genes are intricately linked to the respective cancers they are associated with, as detailed in Supplementary Table 5. Through gene function analysis, we observed that among these 31 causal genes: five genes (PINX1, CYP2E1, MYLIP, DUSP22, VPS13C) may participate in regulating the migration and invasion capabilities of cancer cells, affecting the metastasis of cancer to distant sites [34–38]; four genes (TSPAN13, MYLIP, DUSP22, DNASE1L3) play roles in regulating the proliferation and apoptosis of cancer cells; six genes (TSPAN13, MT-C01, ABAT, MYLIP, CYP2C18, KCNQ1) are crucial in cancer occurrence and development, potentially involved in regulating abnormal cell proliferation and transformation [36, 39–43]; six genes (CD5, RARS1, DNASE1L3, NOX4, CLIC2, CD1C) may mediate the interaction between cancer and immune responses, impacting the recognition and immune attack on cancer cells [44–49]; and the expression level of 15 genes (PINX1, MED10, RPL39L, CD5, MS4A1, RPL3, DNASE1L3, BCL3, CBR, CD33, VPS13C, CLIC2, SLC16A1, MS4A1, PILL1) are associated with cancer prognosis, and some serve as prognostic markers [34, 38, 45, 47, 49–58].

CPCG has a low sensitivity to the input order of the data

In computational methodology, the construction of a causal skeleton by CPCG is dependent on a series of conditional independence tests between variables. However, the accuracy of these tests hinges on correctly capturing the variables’ conditional independence relationships in preceding tests as altering the order of variables in the tests leads to variations in the captured relationships and yields different outcomes. Consequently, the conditional independence tests in Stage II are supposed to be sensitive to the input order of data, impacting the inference of causal structures.

To assess the sensitivity of CPCG to data input order, we conduct shuffling experiments in Stage II of our method. Genes obtained from Stage I are ranked in descending order based on their R-square values from least squares regression for overall survival time. These genes are then input into Stage II in both sorted and randomly shuffled orders (repeated over five iterations), respectively. Through the experiment results, we observe that despite variations in data input order across experiments, the performance of Cox regression, particularly under C-index and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $PS{P}_C$\end{document} evaluations, remained stable in predicting cancer prognosis (Fig. 6). Notably, CPCG consistently identified the same gene set in over half of the experiments across 12 cancer types, including BLCA, BRCA, COAD, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, LAML, LIHC, OV, SKCM, STAD, and UCEC. In some cases, such as COAD and STAD, shuffling the input order even had no discernible impact on the results. For other cancer types, the C-index and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $PS{P}_C$\end{document} values from the six experiments were similar. We suspected that this may be attributed to the accurate candidate gene sets obtained in Stage 1.

In practical application, we recommend inputting the genes obtained from Stage I into Stage II in descending order according to the correlation strength with the overall survival time. This is primarily because genes with higher correlation imply their higher impact on patients’ overall survival time, potentially serving as causal genes. Prioritizing these genes can expedite the construction of the causal skeleton.

CPCG doesn’t need to consider Markov equivalence class and V-structure

In the previous section, we take genes directly linking to survival time in the causal skeleton as causal genes. However, a question arises regarding whether including spouse genes or solely utilizing parental genes of survival time would enhance the predictive performance of Cox models. In this context, we conducted two additional experiments to validate this assumption.

Unlike condition-based causal feature selection methods, which treat variables within the Markov boundary through causal structure learning as causal features (refer to Markov equivalence class and V-structure in Supplementary Methods for detailed explanations). CPCG does not consider spouse genes as we believe that they do not directly influence cancer prognosis. To investigate whether incorporating spouse genes improves the predictive ability of Cox models, we first identify variables sharing a common child node with overall survival time but not directly linked to each other as spouse variables. Using the causal skeletons from Result 3.5 as a basis, we then compared the C-index and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $PS{P}_C$\end{document} of Cox regressions before and after including spouse genes (see Supplementary Fig. 5). Our analysis reveals that incorporating spouse genes does not significantly enhance the C-index of Cox regression models. In fact, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $PS{P}_C$\end{document} shows a decreasing trend and is lower compared to models without spouse genes. Across experiments in COAD, ESCA, HNSC, LGG, and LIHC, the Cox regression model excluding spouse genes consistently exhibits similar C-index values and higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $PS{P}_C$\end{document}. These findings suggest that including spouse genes does not notably improve and may even reduce the predictive ability of Cox models.

In our second experiment, we delved into the issue of Markov equivalence classes, specifically exploring the necessity of inferring edge directions for variable pairs and only outputting the parental genes of overall survival time. To assess whether constructing a Cox model solely based on parental genes yields superior predictive performance, we utilized various causal direction inference methods, including ANM [59], BivariateFit, CDS [60], IGCI [61], GES [62–65], GIES [62–65], LiNGAM [66], and PC [67], to determine the directionality of relationships among variables. By integrating the outcomes of these methods and identifying parental genes based on the direction of edges pointing toward survival time in at least four techniques, we conduct statistical analyses on the C-index and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $PS{P}_C$\end{document} of Cox regressions before and after incorporating child genes (Supplementary Fig. 6). Our findings reveal that among the cancers where parental genes are successfully identified, integrating child genes into the Cox model significantly improved the C-index with minimal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $PS{P}_C$\end{document} loss. This enhancement is observed in BLCA, BRCA, COAD, ESCA, LAML, LUSC, OV, SARC, and SKCM. On the other hand, despite the integration of multiple methods, the precise causality between genes and cancer prognosis remains challenging to determine conclusively. Therefore, we propose to take a balanced approach without excessive emphasis on predicting cancer prognosis solely based on parental genes of survival times in the causal skeleton.

Discussion and conclusion

We proposed a two-stage framework CPCG to identify genes associated with cancer prognosis from the perspective of causal as causation is more stable than correlation. The experiments demonstrate that the genes obtained through CPCG exhibit stability, reliability, good reusability, and enhanced interpretability in cancer prognosis prediction, aiding in more effective interventions for diseases.

Before causal inference, integrating parametric and semiparametric components to process hazard and construct a one-to-one relationship between each gene and cancer prognosis for preliminary screened causal genes helps precisely narrow down the set size of potential causal genes and avoids the influence of high-dimensional features on correlation measurement, undoubtedly facilitating the efficiency and effectiveness of subsequent causal feature selection. This is because obtaining causal structures is highly challenging in high-dimensional but relatively low-sample scenarios as it can be computationally complex and, in some cases, even intractable. Therefore, precise initial screening of candidate genes is crucial. The conditional independence test used in Stage II of CPCG can effectively solve the multicollinearity issue. We also demonstrated that within the structural learning module, CPCG is insensitive to the input order of the data, which is also attributed to the precise selection of causal genes obtained from Stage I. Furthermore, we show that our method does not need to consider Markov equivalence classes, which is a challenging issue in causal structure learning. These all make using CPCG involve fewer considerations.

Our method undoubtedly eliminates spurious relationships between genes and survival, some of which are effects manifested after multiple mediating variable transmissions and some are due to common causes. Hence, it helps tumor systems biologists and clinical practitioners address long-standing issues. Traditional biomarker identification methods based on feature selection also face certain gaps when it comes to practical clinical application, one of which is how to select a final, small set for optimal predictive performance. Typically, the best set is obtained through enumeration. Our method can acquire a small set of causal genes, without the need for further time-consuming enumeration. Through a literature review, it also demonstrates the biological association between identified causal genes and cancer progress and malignancy. Moreover, we investigated and found that 13 out of the 68 genes are related to secreted proteins, with proteins encoded by 11 genes easily detectable in blood (CYP2E1, H2AW, CD5, OAF, CBR1, CRTAC1, RARS1, KIRREL3, CYP2C18, SNAP25, CD33) [68–70]; these further underscored the potential of these causal genes as clinical prognosis biomarkers that the proteins they encode can be detected in the body fluids of cancer patients.

There is also room for further improvement in our approach. Currently, CPCG focuses only on transcriptomic data; if other multi-omics data, as well as imaging data, become a larger sample size, we will continue exploring causal associations with other features related to prognosis. Besides, In the semiparametric part of Stage I, we simply evaluate the impact of each gene on hazard using the least squares method. Future research should consider integrating more nonlinear regression methods to better quantify the gene–hazard relationship.

In conclusion, effective methods for identifying prognosis-related genes from a causality perspective are currently lacking. CPCG is anticipated to be a valuable tool for efficiently identifying a concise combination of causal genes that exhibit stability, reliability, and good reusability and provide significant assistance in predicting cancer prognosis. The methodology and design logic of CPCG can also serve as a crucial reference for robust biological feature selection, such as features used for early diagnosis and drug efficacy identification.
