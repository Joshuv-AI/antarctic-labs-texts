Increased integration and synergy in EEG- and fNIRS-based data in migraine patients after a three-month treatment with galcanezumab: relations with long-term clinical outcome
Authors: Marianna La Rocca, Antonio Lacalamita, Livio Clemente, Giulia Paparella, Elena Ammendola, Nicola Amoroso, Emmanuella Ladisa, Alfonso Monaco, Sebastiano Stramaglia, Roberto Bellotti, Marina de Tommaso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGalcanezumab is a well-tolerated monoclonal antibody developed for migraine prophylaxis. Although its action mechanisms are not fully understood, they may involve changes in cortical connectivity. This study explores galcanezumab-induced central changes by investigating metabolic-electrical brain interactions and network properties. We analyzed electroencephalography (EEG) and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) during visual stimulation through connectivity and high-order interaction analyses using Partial information decomposition (PID). Furthermore, we explored the relationship between neurophysiological measures and long-term clinical effects. Fifteen patients underwent clinical and EEG/fNIRS recordings at baseline (B), 1 hour after the first galcanezumab administration (B’), and after 3 months of therapy (3M). Clinical outcomes were assessed after one year of treatment (1Y). Ten healthy controls underwent baseline EEG/fNIRS recordings. EEG and fNIRS signals were analyzed and harmonized. We computed global network properties including strength, global efficiency, clustering coefficient, and high-order interaction properties such as synergy and redundancy. For both EEG and fNIRS data, we found lower strength and clustering coefficients in patients than in controls at all time points . Global efficiency was reduced at B, and significantly increased in patients at 3M, reaching levels comparable to controls. PID on fNIRS data showed an overall increase in redundancy and synergy after treatment . Cluster analysis on EEG and fNIRS synergy matrices over time showed expansion of high-synergy channel clusters at 3M. Finally, for both EEG and fNIRS data, synergy and global efficiency at 3M correlated with clinical indicators at 1Y, including migraine-related disability and the number of acute medications/month . The results support central effects of galcanezumab associated with increased integration in functional and electrical brain connectivity, explaining its long-term efficacy and disease-modifying action.Keywords: Functional near infrared spectroscopy, Electroencephalography, Brain connectivity, High-order interaction, Migraine, GalcanezumabSubject terms: Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Migraine is a neurovascular disorder that requires appropriate treatment to reduce disability and prevent progression to chronic and more severe forms. Recent therapeutic advances have focused on targeting the Calcitonin Gene Related Peptide (CGRP), a peptide that plays a fundamental role in triggering the inflammatory cascade responsible for the migraine pain. Both direct antagonists or monoclonal antibodies against the receptor or CGRP itself have demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy1. Since their initial application in episodic, high-frequency and chronic migraine, anti-CGRP treatments have consistently shown significant improvements in disease severity, marking a potential paradigm shift in migraine management1.

A question that arises regarding the use of anti-CGRP in migraine relates to the possible modifying properties of drugs that act in the periphery and neutralize the neuropeptide produced by meningeal C-nociceptors, according to a complex mechanism of retrograde trigeminal activation2. Migraine, however, is a functional brain disorder characterized by altered neuronal excitability, hypersensitivity and decreased habituation to multimodal stimuli, often culminating in cortical spreading depression, which is responsible for the activation of trigeminal nociceptors and the production of CGRP3. It remains unclear whether peripheral inhibition of CGRP is sufficient to modulate central brain processes involved in pain perception and neuronal excitability. Recent studies using CGRP receptor monoclonal antibodies showed central antinociceptive effects, as evidenced by changes in pain-related evoked potentials and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data4,5. Abnormal brain reactivity, particularly in response to visual stimuli, has long been documented in migraine, dating back to early electroencephalography (EEG) studies6. More recently, our group has demonstrated altered occipital cortex connectivity in migraine patients compared to healthy controls7, consistent with findings from other EEG and magnetoencephalography (MEG) studies showing disrupted brain network dynamics8. Furthermore, in two studies involving galcanezumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CGRP, we observed normalization in steady-state visual evoked responses and connectivity patterns on EEG, accompanied by reduced occipital cortex metabolism as measured by functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS)9,10.

The integration of EEG and fNIRS is gaining interest as a powerful multimodal approach, though the interplay between electrical and hemodynamic signals remains an open research question. Brain network analyses and high-order interaction models offer promising tools for investigating the complex brain dynamics captured by such multimodal techniques11,12. In our previous work9, we developed an EEG connectivity model that has highlighted the key role of the global efficiency in migraine treatment. Besides this, abnormal EEG and MEG connectivity has been found in migraine patients8. High-order interactions are particularly important for understanding how brain regions coordinate to perform specific cognitive functions. Among the methods used to study such interactions, partial information decomposition (PID) stands out as a powerful approach. PID decomposes joint information between three or more variables into Unique, Redundant, and Synergistic components and, unlike standard methods in the field of information theory, PID does not have the limit of considering redundancy and synergy mutually exclusive13–15. Specifically, redundancy refers to information shared across three or more variables. Conversely, synergy indicates information that can only be obtained by taking into account all variables and not using any subset of them16. Recent applications of PID to EEG data have provided new insights into how the brain processes information17–20. For instance, Takacs et al.17 used temporal signal decomposition to separate stimulus-related, response-related, and intermediate cognitive processes in EEG recordings. This approach aligns with PID by revealing how different types of information are processed in the brain during tasks, particularly by focusing on event file binding processes in EEG data. Similarly, Pastore et al.18 analyze EEG data while participants listened to speech. The study found that articulatory movements (such as tongue movements) provided unique and synergistic information about neural responses, suggesting that the brain integrates auditory and motor information during speech processing. This illustrates how PID can quantify different types of information captured in EEG signals, enhancing our understanding of brain electrical activity.

In the present study we intended to expand previous analyses on EEG and metabolic effects induced by galcanezumab, as possible central action has a pivotal role in long-term efficacy and modifying properties of anti-CGRP drugs. We specifically aimed to 1) test a novel approach that integrates EEG–fNIRS connectivity, to extrapolate a pattern of bioelectrical and metabolic drug-induced changes 2) apply high-order interaction analysis to EEG and fNIRS signals, to reveal subtle functional modifications of occipital cortex, and 3) to investigate whether these neural changes are predictive of long-term clinical outcomes after one year of treatment. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the effects of a targeted migraine therapy by examining the combined dynamics of electrical and hemodynamic brain activity through complex network and high-order interaction frameworks.

Discussion

This study investigated the central effects of galcanezumab, a monoclonal antibody against CGRP, in migraine patients using a multimodal neurophysiological approach combining EEG and fNIRS with high-order and connectivity analyses. Furthermore, we investigated whether galcanezumab-induced changes in brain dynamics can predict long-term clinical outcomes. Our aim was to further address the effects on EEG and metabolic changes associated with occipital brain areas activated by visual stimulation, which have shown different response patterns in migraine compared to controls21. We previously observed that anti-CGRP therapies induce changes in trigeminal pain–related responses and steady-state evoked potentials, as assessed by spectral analysis and brain-networking methods9,22. We here investigated the long-term efficacy of anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies to understand whether the efficient antinociceptive effect could alter stimulus processing at the central level and modify the basal abnormalities underlying migraine. In this sense, the occipital cortex may have distinct basal abnormalities in both migraine with and without aura, implying a possible involvement in the development of attacks in migraine23,24. To confirm previous findings9,10 and support the hypothesis that modifying occipital abnormalities could lead to long-term improvement in migraine, we adopted a novel approach of synchronization between bioelectrical and metabolic signals recorded with EEG and fNIRS to obtain a unique integrated paradigm of occipital function. Furthermore, we introduced a novel analysis to assess synergy and redundancy between different occipital brain areas. We found that early changes in global efficiency, as well as in synergy, predict response to galcanezumab after one year. Thus, changes in the occipital cortex are likely related to long-term clinical improvement due to galcanezumab. In the following section, we discuss the key points.

Novel approach that integrates EEG–fNIRS connectivity, to extrapolate a pattern of bioelectrical and metabolic drug-induced changes in the occipital cortex

Using EEG and fNIRS data from previous datasets9,10, we here focused on occipital areas and succeeded in harmonizing and synchronizing the bioelectrical and hemodynamic results obtained with the two methods. As in another study using EEG only,9, we were able here to demonstrate impaired brain connectivity in migraine patients also using fNIRS assessment, confirming the results of other studies conducted with fMRI25–27. Notably, galcanezumab administration exerted a positive effect on global efficiency in both EEG and fNIRS data, and although some inter-individual variability in the trajectories of change was observed, a consistent overall trend toward increased global efficiency emerged across patients. On the one hand, these results support the validity of our harmonization and synchronization approach, which allowed a meaningful comparison between the two multimodal signals. Most importantly, the results provide evidence that galcanezumab can facilitate long-distance neuronal integration and restore functional connectivity, as reflected by both bioelectrical and hemodynamic changes, as also suggested by recent studies28,29. The enhancement of global efficiency in occipital areas helps to clarify our previous findings, in which EEG signals from the entire scalp were analyzed9. This localized improvement may reflect a rebalancing of brain network topology that promotes more efficient communication, potentially associated with the observed reduction in migraine frequency and intensity following galcanezumab treatment. Finally, we observed a lower inter-subject variability in fNIRS compared with EEG data. This greater stability may reflect both physiological factors, such as the slower and more integrated nature of the hemodynamic response, and methodological aspects, including the fNIRS lower temporal resolution, which can attenuate fluctuations across subjects.

Applied high-order interaction analysis to EEG and fNIRS signals, to reveal subtle functional modifications of occipital cortex

Another new aspect of the present work is the high-order analysis, which is based on PID. As far as we know, this is the first application of this metric to a clinical migraine sample and extends its previous use in theoretical neuroscience and disorders of consciousness16. Using a higher-order interaction framework based on PID, we jointly analyzed EEG and fNIRS to dissociate redundant (shared) from synergistic (integrative) information, thereby revealing subtle occipital functional modifications in migraine beyond pairwise connectivity30,31. The analysis showed that redundancy and synergy were lower in patients at baseline compared to controls. Redundancy reflects shared or overlapping representations, for instance brain regions encoding the same sensory input32. Conversely, synergy reflects information integration, in which several brain areas jointly encode complex states, although no single region is sufficient on its own32. The altered redundancy and synergy in patients could be due to a loss of integration capacity and functional specialization in cortical dynamics, which is consistent with evidence of altered large-scale brain connectivity in migraine4. We observed that galcanezumab affected neuronal synergy in parallel to its effects on connectivity. Specifically, after three months of treatment, we observed a local increase in EEG synergy (an increase in channel clusters with similar synergy) as well as the addition of synergistically similar channels in fNIRS, which overall appeared to be more sensitive to the treatment. These increases indicate improved cooperative interactions between brain regions and a more integrated hemodynamic response33. The effect of galcanezumab on EEG redundancy still needs to be clarified. This parameter, which was already reduced in patients compared to controls at the start of treatment, decreased further after treatment. The biological significance of this change is still unclear and requires further investigation. It is unclear whether this reduction reflects an adaptive reorganization of the brain networks, a marker for the therapeutic effect or a compensatory mechanism for the increased synergy. However, a more granular analysis on EEG redundancy revealed that this decrease was driven only by low redundancy interactions, while patterns with above-average redundancy tend to increase after 3 months of treatment, although not significantly. Finally, fNIRS redundancy and synergy increase after the first galcanezumab injection (B’) indicates a potential neurovascular modulation in a time window that allows to uncover a possible effect of suggestion consistent with placebo-related mechanisms, not evident in the real-time EEG recording34. This possible temporal shift of the placebo response seems to be an interesting phenomenon that should be further confirmed by an integrated analysis of bioelectrical and metabolic signals.

EEG/fNIRS changes predictive of long-term clinical outcomes after one year of treatment.

Another important finding of the study is that the EEG and fNIRS measures of global efficiency and synergy at three months were significantly correlated with clinical measures at one year. Specifically, higher fNIRS global efficiency and EEG synergy at three months were associated with lower pain intensity, lower symptomatic medication use, lower migraine frequency and better MIDAS scores. Notably, the strongest correlations for both fNIRS global efficiency and EEG synergy were observed with the reported pain intensity of migraine attacks. Although some variability was observed in the longitudinal evolution of clinical parameters, these findings carry some pathophysiological implications. The early central effect of galcanezumab on functional changes in the occipital cortex observed at 3 months reflects a potential sustained action on cortical modulation that may be responsible for preventing migraine attacks. Furthermore, the neurophysiological changes in brain network organization were closely related to the subjective experience of pain, suggesting that improvements in network efficiency and synergy may underlie reductions in migraine intensity. We can confirm our previous hypothesis that a specific and efficient antinociceptive action could inhibit brain areas involved in the processing of pain stimuli, leading to a global restoration of cortical abnormalities that predispose to the onset of migraine4,9,10. The most recent fMRI studies confirmed that responder patients experienced a global restoration of connectivity at rest that extended to the occipital regions29. Interestingly, the correlation between the global efficacy and synergy and clinical improvement observed after three months was even more stable after one year of treatment, suggesting that the neurophysiological changes are not simply a sign of the brain’s regained well-being, but a possible specific action mechanisms of the anti-CGRP drugs that could be maintained and enhanced over time. Recent changes observed in aura symptoms with anti-CGRP drugs35, though still controversial36,37, further support our findings, as they may reflect central occipital effects and suggest potential disease-modifying properties of anti-CGRP treatments. Although no EEG/fNIRS measurements were collected at 1 year, the stability of clinical benefits observed at 3 months, together with the correlations between neurophysiological and clinical changes, supports the possibility of sustained cortical reorganization over the long term. Future studies are needed to confirm whether these neurophysiological changes persist at 1 year.

Study confounding and limitations

As reported in previous studies, our investigations were conducted as part of routine clinical practice. For ethical reasons, we were not allowed to use placebos in these forms of severe migraine where several treatments had failed, so we tested the effect of suggestion only a short time after injection of the drug and before any biological effect. Importantly, we used EEG and fNIRS to capture complementary aspects of brain activity: EEG reflects fast electrophysiological dynamics, whereas fNIRS measures slower hemodynamic responses. Their combination provides a more comprehensive assessment of cortical integration and allowed us to demonstrate the robustness and consistency of the longitudinal effects across modalities. While evaluating the added predictive value of this multimodal approach was not the main aim of the present study, future research with larger samples will be necessary to determine whether combining EEG and fNIRS enhances the ability to predict clinical outcomes or provides additional insights beyond those obtained with a single modality. Furthermore, PID and network metrics, while powerful, depend heavily on signal quality and pre-processing techniques. Differences in signal-to-noise ratios between EEG and fNIRS, as well as potential residual artifacts, could introduce bias. Again, due to the relatively limited sample size, we did not stratify patients into good and poor responders. Such an analysis would have allowed us to further explore the inter-individual variability in treatment response. Finally, the generalizability to broader migraine populations, including those with different subtypes or comorbidities, remains uncertain.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary evidence that galcanezumab can exert central modulatory effects in migraine patients beyond its known peripheral effects. Using multimodal advanced analytical techniques, significant improvements in global efficiency and synergy were observed. These changes reflect improved neuronal integration and also correlate with long-term clinical improvements, suggesting a possible mechanistic link. Importantly, the results support the growing notion that effective migraine therapies can also alter central neural processes. The use of EEG and fNIRS in conjunction with high-order connectivity analysis offers promise for the development of objective biomarkers of treatment response. However, the limited sample size in this study restricts the scope and generalizability of the findings, and further studies with larger cohorts, placebo controls, and longer follow-up are needed to confirm and extend these observations..

Materials and methods

Participants

The study is based on EEG/fNIRS data collected for primary analysis, reported in9,10. EEG/fNIRS data from 15 migraine patients [1 male, mean age ± standard deviation (SD): 50.26 ± 9.33, mean disease duration ± SD: 31.1 ± 11.15, 7 with chronic migraine], diagnosed according to International Headache Society Classification (ICHD), and from 10 sex and age-matched healthy controls (C) were included in the present study. Details for patients’ inclusion and exclusion criteria are reported in9,10. Of note, among the 20 migraine patients included in10, only 15 were considered in the present study, as they had undergone simultaneous EEG/fNIRS coregistration. Data from 13 of these patients had also been analyzed in9. Healthy subjects (1 male, mean age 48.8 ± 9.9 years, by Fisher’s exact test for sex distribution and by Welch’s t-test for age) were selected among university and hospital personnel, and none of them had a history of medical and neurological diseases9,10. Special attention was devoted to ensure that control subjects were not affected by any form of primary headache. The local ethics committee of the Policlinico General Hospital in Bari approved the neurophysiological recordings, which were conducted during routine clinical observation. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and international safety guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Data collection design

Migraine patients underwent clinical and EEG/fNIRS evaluation before the first galcanezumab administration (B), 1 hour after the first galcanezumab administration (B’) and after 3 months of galcanezumab therapy (3M) (Fig. 10).

Visual description of the design of the data collection phase.

The recording session at B’ served to test the effect of suggestion as a result of drug injection9,10. Patients’ clinical evaluation, performed at B and 3M, included a complete neurological examination. Headache diary was reviewed for each patient: headache intensity was assessed using a numerical rating scale from 0 to 10, headache frequency was expressed as the average number of days with headache per month, calculated in 3 months, and the use of symptomatic drugs was considered along with the Italian version of the Migraine Disability Assessment (MIDAS) questionnaire38. To understand whether metrics based on complex networks and high-order interactions could have a predictive effect on clinical outcome, we repeated the clinical evaluation after 1 year of galcanezumab treatment (1Y). Healthy controls underwent one experimental session, encompassing EEG/fNIRS recordings.

Acquisition and pre-processing of the neurophysiological data

The stimulation and EEG-fNIRS recording modality are reported in9,10. Checkerboard patterns at a frequency of 5 Hz (10 reversal/s) were shown on a 17-inch monitor subtending at a viewing distance of 90 cm during acquisition. We used a spatial frequency of 0.5 cycles per degree and a mean luminance of 14 cd/m2. EEG data were registered by 64 scalp electrodes, in accordance with the enlarged 10–10 system, referred to the nasion with the ground at Fpz. We used the EEG Micromed Brain Quick apparatus (http://www.micromed.eu). For fNIRS data, we used a continuous wave NIRS system (NIRSport 8X8, Nirx Medical Technologies LLC, Berlin, Germany). The fNIRS data acquisition software was the NIRStar 14.2 (http://www.nirx.net). The fNIRS instrument included LED sources and photosensitive detectors. Each source employs two LEDs that emit a near-infrared light at 760 nm and 850 nm. The resulting sampling rate of the fNIRS signal was 7.81 Hz. The arrangement of sources and detectors resulted in a total of 24 fNIRS measurement channels in the parieto-occipital area, 12 for each hemisphere.

The EEG data were processed with an automatic pipeline based on EEGLAB (v2022) running on MATLAB. Data were filtered between 1 and 30 Hz using an FIR filter. Next, we used the artifact subspace reconstruction method to correct continuous data and reject bad channels and data segments. Then, we interpolated the bad channels and re-referenced all the data to the average. To avoid volume conduction, we applied the Laplace filter to the EEG data39. For further details, see9. The fNIRS signal processing was performed using nirsLAB (version 2017.6) running on MATLAB (version R 2018 b). The quality of the signals was evaluated by checking that the gain factor (indicating how much photo-current is amplified) and the coefficient of variation (the ratio between 100 times the standard deviation and the mean of the signal) were respectively lower than 8 and 7.5, two thresholds chosen during the calibration phase. fNIRS signals are characterized by limited spatial overlap between neighboring channels and have slower temporal dynamics compared to EEG, therefore the risk of volume conduction or in other words spurious correlations due to spatial overlap or instantaneous signal spread is low. However, spurious correlations in fNIRS signals can be due to systemic components such as scalp blood flow. To mitigate these effects and retain task-related hemodynamic fluctuations while attenuating very slow drifts and high-frequency physiological noise such as cardiac and respiratory signals, we carried out the following steps after the quality check: removing discontinuities and artifacts, filtering in the band-pass 0.008–0.2 Hz. For further details, see10.

EEG-fNIRS harmonization

To study the EEG/fNIRS interaction, it was necessary to make the two multimodal signals comparable. It is well known that neural activity is mainly dominated by electrophysiological mechanisms and is mediated by metabolic and hemodynamic processes. As a consequence, hemodynamic responses are slower than electrophysiological signals11. In this regard, we developed a pipeline that allowed the comparison of the two signals and evaluated the time delay between them. This procedure also takes into account other differences between the two signals, such as the varying sampling rates (7.81 Hz for the fNIRS signal versus 256 Hz for the EEG signal), the distinct frequency ranges in which they are filtered (0.008 Hz to 0.2 Hz for fNIRS and 1 Hz to 30 Hz for EEG), and the different numbers of channels used to acquire the two signals (64 EEG channels versus 24 fNIRS channels).

First, to handle the disparity in signal frequency, the envelope of the EEG signal was calculated using the Hilbert transform. To manage the different sampling rates, the fNIRS signal was interpolated, and to account for the difference in the number of channels, only 26 occipital EEG channels, corresponding to the brain region where the 24 fNIRS channels were placed, were considered. In addition, both signals were normalized.

Concerning the physiological delay between EEG and fNIRS signals, several studies demonstrated that it may vary depending on the specific protocols and the pathologies under investigation. For most experiments, the observed delays are generally in the range of 1 to 6 seconds. However, it is unclear how this delay changes in different migraine patients40–42. Brain connectivity analysis

After the signal preprocessing and harmonization, for both the EEG and fNIRS signals, we built a weighted network for each subject. For EEG data, this was carried out in each of the four frequency bands that we considered: delta (0.5–3.5Hz), theta (4–7Hz), alpha (8–12Hz), beta (13–30Hz). Only occipital EEG electrodes (T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, Oz, O2, CP1, CP2, PO3, PO4, CP5, CP6, TP7, CP3, CPz, CP4, TP8, P5, P1, P2, P6, PO7, POz, PO8) were used to define network nodes, and the magnitude squared, calculated for each pair of channels, was used to define network connections that therefore represented the similarity of the neuronal oscillations of two channel signals. We focused on occipital channels for two main reasons: (i) to have a fair comparison with the fNIRS signals that cover the occipital brain area and (ii) to study the brain areas more involved in migraine9,10,43. In terms of fNIRS data, the 24 available channels were used to define network nodes, and the Pearson’s correlation, calculated for each pair of channels, was used to define network links. For each subject’s brain network, mathematically represented by a connectivity matrix, we computed three distinct weighted metrics (strength, clustering coefficient and global efficiency) for each node, as described in our previous study9. To derive global network characteristics, we averaged the values of each specific metric across all nodes in the network. Subsequently, we applied a Wilcoxon rank sum test to assess whether these metrics differed significantly (at a level of significance of ) between the different timepoints (B, B’, 3M) and from normal controls data. For each comparison, we corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). In addition, we performed a more granular analysis, computing which EEG and fNIRS channels were significantly different, in terms of the three network metrics, at 3M compared to B/B’. Finally, using the Pearson correlation coefficient, we evaluated whether the three network properties were statistically correlated with the clinical scores evaluated at 3M and 1Y of treatment. To this end, we considered both the raw clinical data collected at 3M and 1Y, and indices of clinical change, calculated as ratios between values collected at 3M or 1Y and the corresponding baseline measurements.

High-order interaction analysis

Entropy is one of the basic notions in information theory, which defines a measure of uncertainty of a random variable44–46 and, at the same time, characterizes signal complexity47. Nevertheless, Mutual information (MI) extends this to two variables as a measure of dependency between them, offering a flexible framework able to detect nonlinear and nonmonotonic relationships48,49. It can be represented using three mathematically equivalent formulations concerning entropy differences, each providing a different perspective on the information shared between the variables.

MI provides such a method with sensitivity, robustness, and additivity, enabling comparisons of different types of variables measured on comparable scales50–52. However, accurate estimation of MI from experimental data is a very challenging task53.

One important class of MI estimators is the Gaussian Copula Mutual Information (GCMI)32, which uses copulas for capturing dependencies between variables in a possibly marginal-independent way54. Sklar’s theorem implies that the copula completely captures the dependence structure between the two variables55, and hence MI is invariant to marginal transformations.

Because MI is equal to the negative entropy of the copula, estimates obtained by using a Gaussian copula are a lower bound on the true MI (https://github.com/robince/gcmi/tree/master/matlab32).

In systems where multiple source elements connect to a single target, the distribution of information among the sources can be complex.
