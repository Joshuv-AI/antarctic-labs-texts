A light-response bilayer actuator for light-controlled capsule system
Authors: Taoli Deng, Hao Luo, Jin Jiaxing, Xianzhong Wang, Chen Jun, Ren Zhenhua, Chen Yonghe, Long Deng, Weiyong Tian, Zan Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microrobotics technology shows great potential in the field of minimally invasive medicine but still faces key challenges regarding motion control, safety, and cost. Hence, a near-infrared light-response bilayer actuator is designed in this work. The light-driving mechanism and its application in the light-controlled drug release capsule system is deeply explored by taking the double-layer polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)-carbon nanotube (CNT)/polyethylene (PE)-graphene oxide (GO) (PVA-CNT/PE-GO) composite films as the core actuating material. Due to the significant mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between PVA and PE, the film achieves large bending deformation with a rapid response under NIR irradiation, demonstrating excellent actuation performance. Furthermore, the effects of CNT concentration gradients and PVA-CNT layer thickness on the performance of the flexible films are analyzed to ensure superior stability and fatigue resistance. Additionally, based on COMSOL finite element simulations, a “light-thermal-mechanical” energy conversion model is proposed to validate the thermal response and actuation mechanism of the composite films. Ultimately, based on the light-response bilayer actuator, a light-controlled capsule is designed to demonstrate the photothermal actuation and on-demand opening, providing a design strategy for light-response actuators with potential for further biomedical exploration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01822-0.

Introduction

Intelligent actuating materials have garnered significant attention due to their unique energy conversion capabilities, which transform physical or chemical stimuli, such as light, electricity, heat, and magnetism into mechanical energy for shape morphing. This capability endows them with broad application prospects in fields ranging from artificial muscles and flexible electronics to precision medicine [1–4]. Here, the innovation of flexible intelligent material systems is the direction for breakthroughs in core technologies. Currently, mainstream responsive materials include temperature-sensitive hydrogels with excellent biological affinity [5–8], liquid crystal elastomers capable of achieving high strain rates [9–11], dielectric elastomers induced by field deformation [12, 13], and magnetic-responsive polymer composites [14] as well as cellulose‑based fibers [13, 15]. These materials generate precise and controllable deformation responses under the stimulation of light, magnetism, electricity, temperature and chemistry, overcoming the motion limitations of traditional rigid structures.

In recent years, breakthroughs in medical micro-robotics have demonstrated a level of precision often unattainable by traditional medical methods. For instance, Chatzipirpiridis et al. developed implantable magnetic tubular micro-robots, verifying their feasibility for posterior segment eye surgery in live rabbits [16]. Similarly, Yang et al. fabricated an untethered millirobot with a streamlined shape integrating engine, delivery, and biopsy modules, facilitating biomedical tasks such as in vivo drug delivery and cell transportation in rabbit arteries [17].

The precise movement and control of flexible microrobots depend critically on their response to external stimuli, with the driving mechanism rooted in the efficient interaction and conversion between materials and external energy fields. Depending on the type of stimulus source, the action principle and applicable scenarios show significant differences. Each stimulation method, while achieving specific functions, also faces unique challenges and optimization space. Traditional magnetic drive technology typically requires complex and bulky field-generating equipment, whereas chemical drives often pose risks of potential biological toxicity [18–20]. In contrast, light-responsive actuation has emerged as a superior alternative due to its non-contact control, high spatial precision, and the deep tissue penetration of near-infrared (NIR) light (approximately 3–5 mm) [21–23]. These attributes allow for millimeter-level motion accuracy and ensure operational safety with controllable power densities (< 1 W/cm²). However, despite these systemic advantages, current light-response material implementations still face challenges regarding biocompatibility and fabrication cost. For instance, photochemical bubble-jet actuators typically rely on the catalysis of H₂O₂ to produce O₂ bubbles, raising safety concerns for in vivo applications [23]. Similarly, polymer “crane” systems incorporating gold nanorods often require costly magnetron sputtering techniques and utilize materials with poor biocompatibility [24]. Therefore, developing a light-response system based on biocompatible materials through a simple, cost-effective fabrication process remains a critical need.

To address this, we focused on carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and graphene oxide (GO) as functional photothermal materials, which possess excellent photothermal conversion efficiency, capable of achieving rapid temperature increases within 0.5 s under 808 nm NIR irradiation [25–27]. Meanwhile, polyethylene (PE) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) with different coefficients of thermal expansion were selected as the supporting materials, among which the coefficient of thermal expansion of PE (200 × 10− 6/K) is approximately 2.5 times that of PVA (80 × 10− 6/K) [28, 29]. This significant mismatch in thermal expansion drives the composite structure to undergo asymmetric deformation. Upon NIR irradiation, the thermal expansion of the PE layer exceeds that of the PVA layer, driving the film to bend towards the PVA side. Conversely, when the light source is removed, the film cools down and reverts to its original shape.

In this work, we fabricated a bilayer flexible light-response film composed of polyvinyl alcohol-carbon nanotube/polyethylene-graphene oxide (PVA-CNT/PE-GO). The influence of different CNT concentration gradients and PVA-CNT thicknesses on the performance of the double-layer PVA-CNT/PE-GO flexible light-response film were thoroughly studied. Furthermore, a “light-thermal-mechanical” energy conversion model was established using COMSOL finite element simulations to validate the thermal response and driving mechanism. Finally, a light-controlled drug release capsule system was designed based on this film, which is designed to demonstrate the photothermal actuation and on-demand opening, providing a design strategy for light-response actuators with potential for further biomedical exploration.

Experimental section

Preparation of light-responsive flexible intelligent materials

The light-responsive flexible intelligent material was prepared by the solution film-forming technology. Firstly, the PVA-CNT (CNTs diameter: 2–12 μm, external diameter: 13 nm, purity: 95%, Xianfeng Nanotechnology Co., Ltd., China) precursor solution (mass ratio of water : PVA: CNT = 1 : 9 : 0.01/0.1/0.2/0.4/1) was scraped onto the clean commercial PE-GO (GO thickness: 3–10 nm, particle diameter: 5–10 μm, purity: 99%, Xianfeng Nanotechnology Co., Ltd., China) film by the scraping method and stirred at 90 °C for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous pure PVA solution, and the concentration range of CNT is based on the percolation threshold for photothermal efficiency reported in previous literature [25–27]. After cooling to room temperature, different amounts of CNTs were added and stirred for an additional 30 min to yield five groups of PVA-CNT precursor solutions with varying CNT concentrations.

For the film coating process, a clean commercial PE-GO film was flattened and fixed onto a glass plate using adhesive tape. The PVA-CNT precursor solution was then drop-cast onto the PE-GO substrate and spread evenly using a four-sided applicator to control the wet thickness at 50 μm. The assembly was subsequently cured at 25 °C until the solvent completely evaporated. Finally, the bilayer PVA-CNT/PE-GO film was obtained. In this work, all bending experiments were repeated five times (n = 5, freshly prepared samples for each condition), and the fatigue tests were conducted at a controlled temperature of 25 °C and relative humidity of 50%. The schematic illustration of the preparation process is shown in Fig. 1.

Morphological structure of light-responsive flexible intelligent materials

The cross-sectional morphology of the PVA-CNT/PE-GO composite film was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Fig. 1. The bilayer structure is clearly distinguishable: the PE-GO layer appears gray, while the PVA-CNT layer appears black due to the incorporation of CNTs. The thickness of the PE-GO layer is approximately 100 μm, while the PVA-CNT layer measures about 75 μm, resulting in a total film thickness of approximately 175 μm. Minor gaps observed at the interface between the PVA-CNT and PE-GO layers are likely attributed to stress concentration during the sample cutting process, rather than inherent interfacial defects.

Results and discussion

Optical drive mechanism and finite element simulation of PVA-CNT/PE-GO films

The schematic illustration of the optical driving mechanism for the PVA-CNT/PE-GO film is presented in Fig. 2a. Upon NIR irradiation (wavelength 808 nm, beam diameter 30 mm, and irradiation distance 10 cm), the embedded CNTs and GO sheets act as efficient photothermal agents, rapidly converting light energy into thermal energy. Due to the significant mismatch in the coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) between PE and PVA, the PE-GO layer expands more drastically than the PVA-CNT layer. This asymmetric thermal expansion generates a mismatch in thermal stress at the interface, creating a bending moment that forces the bilayer film to bend toward the side with the lower CTE (the PVA-CNT layer). Conversely, when the light source is switched off, the photothermal conversion ceases, and the film cools down. The thermal stress dissipates as the materials contract, allowing the film to elastically recover its original shape.

To quantitatively verify this driving mechanism, finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics. A geometric model consistent with the experimental samples (PVA-CNT layer: 5 mm ×20 mm ×75 μm; PE-GO layer: 5 mm ×20 mm ×100 μm) was established. The simulation integrated the Solid Mechanics and Heat Transfer modules to calculate the temperature distribution and the resulting thermal stress under defined boundary conditions (Fig. 2b), and the property ranges (Young’s modulus, thermal expansion coefficient) are cited from references for the base materials (PVA and PE) [30, 31]. The simulation results successfully reproduced the bending behavior, confirming that the deformation is driven by the CTE-mismatch-induced thermal stress between the active and support layers.

Effect of CNT concentration on the light-response performance

The influence mechanism of CNT concentration gradient and PVA-CNT layer thickness on the bending performance of PVA-CNT/PE-GO films is systematically explored, where no formal statistical hypothesis testing was performed, and the reported optimal conditions represent the highest mean values observed within the tested parameter range. Five groups of samples with different concentrations of CNT (1/1000, 1/100, 1/50, 1/25 and 1/10 respectively) are prepared via the scraper coating process. The thickness of the PVA-CNT layer is constant at 50 μm. Subsequently, the material properties are tested and characterized by the standardized bending test method.

As shown in Fig. 3a and b, the bending performance of the films exhibited a non-monotonic trend dependent on CNT concentration. Specifically, the bending curvature increased significantly with CNT content at lower concentrations (≤ 1/50). However, beyond this best performance under the tested conditions threshold (> 1/50), the bending performance gradually degraded. Notably, the film with a CNT concentration of 1/50 demonstrated the best actuation performance under the tested conditions characterized by a rapid response. Under NIR irradiation, this film achieved a bending angle of 60° within 20 s, followed by a slower increase until reaching a maximum angle of approximately 80° at 104 s (corresponding to a surface temperature of 60 °C).

This dynamic behavior can be attributed to the interplay between the rapid photothermal conversion rate and the relatively slower thermal diffusion rate within the bilayer structure. During the initial stage (0–20 s), the significant temperature gradient generated by the fast light-to-heat conversion induces rapid differential expansion, resulting in swift bending. As irradiation continues, the internal temperature distribution gradually tends toward thermal equilibrium, reducing the rate of bending deformation. The system eventually reaches a steady state at approximately 104 s, where the bending angle stabilizes at its maximum value.

Upon turning off the light source, the temperature of the light-response film decreased rapidly. The bending angle recovered sharply from its maximum value to approximately 15° within 5–10 s and fully returned to the initial state within 20 s. Further analysis revealed the mechanism behind the concentration-dependent performance (Fig. 4a). When the CNT concentration was lower than 1/50, the photothermal conversion capability of the PVA-CNT layer was insufficient, resulting in inadequate thermal expansion of the PVA matrix and consequently limited bending deformation. Conversely, when the CNT concentration exceeded 1/50, excessive CNT content likely disrupted the structural integrity of the PVA polymer network, compromising its thermal expansion properties and leading to performance degradation. Based on these results, a CNT concentration of 1/50 was identified as best performance under the tested conditions, balancing photothermal efficiency with structural stability.

Effect of PVA-CNT layer thickness on light-response performance

Subsequently, the influence of the PVA-CNT layer thickness on the bending performance was investigated. Four groups of samples with varying PVA-CNT layer thicknesses (25, 50, 75, and 100 μm) were prepared via the scraper coating process, while the CNT concentration was maintained at the 1/50. The temperature variations over time for these samples are presented in Fig. 4b. The bending performance exhibited a non-monotonic trend with increasing thickness, as depicted in Fig. 5a and b. Specifically, the bending performance improved significantly as the thickness increased from 25 μm to 75 μm, but declined when the thickness reached 100 μm. The sample with a thickness of 75 μm demonstrated superior actuation performance. It responded rapidly within 2–3 s, comparable to the 50 μm film, and achieved a bending angle of approximately 65° within 15 s, outperforming the 50 μm sample. In contrast, the films with thicknesses of 25 μm and 100 μm exhibited relatively poor performance, with bending angles reaching only about 40° during the initial rapid growth stage. For the best performance under the tested conditions of 75 μm film, the bending angle continued to increase rapidly between 15 s and 40 s, eventually stabilizing at a maximum angle of 85° at 86 s. This thickness-dependent behavior is primarily attributed to the interplay between thermal equilibrium dynamics and bending stiffness. For thicknesses below 75 μm, the total photothermal content per unit area is relatively low, causing the film to reach thermal equilibrium rapidly with a reduced temperature gradient, thereby limiting the bending deformation. Conversely, at a thickness of 100 μm, the bending stiffness increases significantly—approximately 2.3 times that of the 75 μm film, which imposes substantial mechanical resistance to bending. Regarding recovery, upon cessation of irradiation, the 75 μm film recovered to approximately 15° rapidly and returned to its initial state within 15 s. While this recovery time is slightly longer than that of the 50 μm film, the 75 μm film (at 1/50 CNT concentration) still exhibits excellent comprehensive bending-recovery performance, meeting the requirements for subsequent application designs.

Finally, the influence of light power density on the bending performance of the optimized PVA-CNT/PE-GO film (CNT concentration: 1/50; thickness: 75 μm) was systematically investigated under varying intensities (0.03, 0.06, 0.1 and 0.15 W/cm2). As illustrated in Figs. 6a-c, the bending performance exhibited a positive correlation with light power density. Notably, the initial response speed remained consistent across all tested densities, with rapid bending observed within 2–3 s of irradiation. However, higher power densities intensified the thermal gradient across the film thickness, leading to significant differences in the maximum bending angle and the kinetics of the bending process.

Specifically, at a low power density of 0.03 W/cm², the film reached a bending angle of approximately 30° rapidly, before slowly increasing to a maximum of 46.5°. As the density increased to 0.06 W/cm², the maximum angle improved to 64.5°. At 0.15 W/cm², the film demonstrated the most pronounced deformation, rapidly reaching ~85° and peaking at 109°. Generally, the bending process exhibited a two-stage behavior: a rapid deformation stage within the first 5–10 s, followed by a slower progression toward the maximum angle. Recovery tests revealed that at power densities below 0.1 W/cm², the film could fully return to its initial state upon light removal, consistent with the elastic recovery behavior discussed previously. However, at a higher power density of 0.15 W/cm², the film exhibited irreversible deformation. As shown in Fig. 6d, the recovery process halted at approximately 33°, failing to return to the initial state. This phenomenon is attributed to the thermal limitations of the polymer matrix (PVA ≤ 80 °C, PE ≤ 60 °C). Under high-intensity irradiation, the photothermal conversion rate significantly exceeded the heat dissipation rate, causing rapid thermal accumulation (reaching 60 °C within 95 s). This temperature rise induced permanent structural changes in the PVA matrix, specifically the loss of bound water. The dehydration reduced the distance between polymer chains, densified the hydrogen bond network, and restricted chain mobility, thereby diminishing the material’s elastic recovery capability.

In conclusion, a balance exists between actuation magnitude and recovery reliability. While power densities below 0.1 W/cm² ensure full recovery, they yield limited bending angles. Conversely, densities exceeding 0.1 W/cm² enhance bending performance but compromise structural reversibility. Therefore, a power density of 0.1 W/cm² is identified as the best operating parameter under the tested conditions, balancing rapid response, significant bending deformation, and reliable recovery performance.

Demonstration of photothermal actuation and on-demand opening

Based on the best actuation characteristics identified above under the tested conditions, a prototype light-response drug release capsule was designed to demonstrate the photothermal actuation and on-demand opening. The schematic of the operational principle is presented in Fig. 8a. The capsule shell was fabricated using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), selected for its excellent biocompatibility and hydrophobicity. The capsule experiment serves strictly as a proof-of-principle demonstration of the actuator on-demand opening functionality, rather than a quantitative drug release study. Figure 8b shows the physical structure of the device. The capsule features an internal cavity for drug storage, with a square groove (4 mm ×20 mm ×1 mm) machined at the top. The PVA-CNT/PE-GO film was cut to the appropriate size and fixed within this groove to serve as the release switch.

Fig. 8a Schematic diagram of the principle of light-response drug release capsules. b Physical picture of the drug-releasing capsule driven by optical drive; c Light experimental drug release capsule

a Schematic diagram of the principle of light-response drug release capsules. b Physical picture of the drug-releasing capsule driven by optical drive; c Light experimental drug release capsule

Subsequently, a simulation experiment was conducted on the light-response drug release capsule. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 8c, and the light-controlled capsule demonstrated excellent functionality. Under NIR irradiation (0.1 W/cm²), the light-controlled switch responded rapidly within 2 s, ensuring the timeliness of drug release. With continuous irradiation, the opening angle of the switch reached approximately 45° within 8 s to 10 s. Due to the limitation of the fixation method of the film, the light-controlled switch failed to reach the maximum bending angle of 60o−80o during the previous individual sample test, but it was sufficient to expose the drug reservoir to the surrounding fluid for effective release. Upon turning off the light source, the switch completed the closing action within 8 s, and the function of on-demand opening has been achieved. Furthermore, the device exhibited good stability across five repeated release cycles. Given the potential toxicity associated with CNT and GO, we plan to implement a protective encapsulation strategy in future studies. Specifically, we propose coating the PVA-CNT and PE-GO layers with ultrathin films of pure PVA and PE, respectively. This design aims to establish a physical barrier that prevents the leaching of nanoparticles into biological tissues, thereby ensuring biosafety, while simultaneously retaining the superior photo-actuation performance of the composite films. Furthermore, the quantitative drug release kinetics would require specific permeability studies in future work. This light-response drug capsule offers a design strategy for light-response actuators with potential for further biomedical exploration.

Conclusions

In this study, a near-infrared light-response actuator based on a bilayer PVA-CNT/PE-GO composite film was successfully developed. The underlying driving mechanism was elucidated through a “light-thermal-mechanical” energy conversion model, attributing the rapid and significant bending deformation to the asymmetric thermal expansion between the active and support layers. The influence of CNT concentration gradients and PVA-CNT thickness on the performance of double-layer PVA-CNT/PE-GO flexible light-response films is studied, and the best one under the tested conditions presents excellent stability and fatigue resistance. Further, the optical driving performance of the composite film shows an increasing trend with the increase of power density. When the light power density is 0.1 W/cm2, the composite film exhibits excellent bending performance while its recovery performance is not affected. The optimized PVA-CNT/PE-GO film can maintain good driving performance and reliability in 100 driving tests. Ultimately, based on the optimized light response characteristics of PVA-CNT/PE-GO films, a new drug release capsule system is developed as a proof-of-principle demonstration of the actuators on-demand opening functionality. This work provides a design strategy for light-responsive actuators with potential for further biomedical exploration.
