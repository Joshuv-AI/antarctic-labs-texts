An online survey of the structure and delivery of postgraduate psychiatric training in Nigeria
Authors: Olatunji Alao Abiodun, Peter Omoniyi Ajiboye, Mumeen Olaitan Salihu, Dauda Sulyman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNigeria faces a severe shortage of mental health specialists, with fewer than 300 psychiatrists serving a population exceeding 234 million. Strengthening postgraduate psychiatric training is therefore critical, yet empirical evidence on the structure and implementation of residency programmes in Nigeria remains limited. This study aimed to systematically assess the structure and delivery of postgraduate psychiatric training across all accredited training centres in Nigeria, evaluate compliance with regulatory college standards, and identify actionable areas for improvement.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive online survey was conducted across all 30 institutions accredited for postgraduate psychiatric training by the National Postgraduate Medical College of Nigeria (NPMCN) and/or the West African College of Physicians (WACP) between June and July 2024. Heads of Department or designated trainers at each institution completed a researcher-developed, 35-item questionnaire via Google Forms. Twenty-nine institutions responded (response rate: 96.7%). Data were analysed using STATA version 17.0. Descriptive statistics summarised institutional characteristics and training practices. Responses to the open-ended question on Part II preparation strategies were analysed thematically.ResultsAmong 109 health institutions in Nigeria accredited for postgraduate medical training in various specialities, only 30 (27.5%) offered postgraduate training in psychiatry. The median duration of accreditation was 20 years (IQR: 12–26 years), with median figures of 30 psychiatric beds, 7 consultant psychiatrists, and 11 resident doctors per institution. Core psychiatric rotations (General Psychiatry (100.0%), Addiction psychiatry (96.6%), Consultation-Liaison psychiatry (93.1%), Child and Adolescent psychiatry (93.1%), old age psychiatry (72.4%) and Psychotherapy (72.4%)) and preparations for Part I and Part II examinations were widely available. However, community psychiatry was limited to only 24.1% of training centres while 37.9% of centres lacked formal training manuals. Weekly journal clubs were conducted by 41.4% of centres while 44.8% of centres conducted monthly case conferences. Real-time end-of-posting assessments were conducted in only 34.5% of centres. Thematic analysis of Part II preparation strategies identified seven approaches, of which dissertation and research supervision (57.1%) and mock examinations and oral practice (53.6%) were most prevalent. A small number of institutions reported no formal Part II preparation programme.ConclusionSignificant gaps exist in the distribution, capacity, and implementation of postgraduate psychiatric training across Nigeria, with marked variability in subspecialty exposure, academic activity, assessment practices, and research integration. Introduction

Nigeria, with a population exceeding 234 million, is projected to become the third most populous country in the world by 2050 [1]. Despite this rapid demographic growth, the country continues to face profound challenges in the provision of adequate mental health services. Approximately 20% of Nigerians are estimated to experience a mental disorder during their lifetime, with a reported lifetime prevalence of 12.1% for common mental disorders [2, 3]. Yet fewer than 300 practising psychiatrists currently serve the entire country [4]. This severe workforce deficit has major implications for access to specialist care, quality of treatment, and mental health outcomes nationwide.

This critical shortage is further compounded by ongoing healthcare workforce migration from low- and middle-income countries to higher-income settings [5]. A recent survey found that 85.8% of early-career psychiatrists (ECPs) in Nigeria had at some point considered emigrating [6], a figure comparable to the 83.7% reported among ECPs in Iran [7]. More than half (52.9%) had already taken concrete steps toward migration [6], nearly double the proportion reported in Iran (27.7%) [7]. Key push factors driving migration include poor working conditions, limited career development opportunities, income dissatisfaction, economic hardship, sociopolitical instability, insecurity, and concerns about living standards and children’s futures. Conversely, higher remuneration, improved career prospects, better working environments, enhanced quality of life, and active international recruitment serve as powerful pull factors [6–8]. Addressing these drivers is therefore inseparable from any meaningful effort to sustain and expand the psychiatric workforce.

Against this backdrop, strengthening postgraduate psychiatric training and expanding the specialist mental health workforce have become urgent national priorities. In Nigeria, postgraduate psychiatric training is regulated by two bodies: the National Postgraduate Medical College of Nigeria (NPMCN) and the West African College of Physicians (WACP). Both colleges administer fellowship programmes lasting four to six years, designed to equip resident doctors with the clinical knowledge, skills, and professional competencies required to qualify as specialist psychiatrists. NPMCN fellows are licensed to practise within Nigeria, whereas WACP fellows may practise across member states of the West African sub-region. Both certificates are, however, accorded equal standing under Nigerian law [9, 10]. Entry into psychiatric residency traditionally requires success in the primary fellowship examination in psychiatry, or exemption as provided under the regulations of either college [9, 10].

Training takes place in institutions accredited by the respective colleges, including standalone federal and state neuropsychiatric hospitals, psychiatric departments within federal and state teaching hospitals, and federal medical centres distributed across Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones. Accreditation — either partial or full, valid for two or five years respectively — is governed by guidelines set by each college [9, 10]. All trainees are registered with the relevant college at the outset of training, with progress monitored through individual portfolios and college files. Trainers are Fellows of the college or equivalently qualified specialists, supported by allied health professionals including clinical psychologists, social workers, psychiatric nurses, occupational therapists, and electrodiagnostic technicians [9, 10].

The residency programme is structured into two consecutive phases — junior and senior residency. Under the NPMCN curriculum, junior residency requires a minimum of 24 months of full-time rotations before a candidate becomes eligible to sit the Part I examination, followed by a further 24 months of training before eligibility for the Part II fellowship examination. The WACP equivalent — termed the membership training programme — requires 36 months for the junior phase (culminating in the Part I membership examination) and 24 months for the senior fellowship training phase (culminating in the Part II examination). Successful completion of the Part II examination of either college confers consultant specialist status and fellowship of the respective college [9, 10].

This training framework is broadly comparable to postgraduate psychiatric training models in other countries. In the United Kingdom, training comprises three years of core training followed by three years of higher specialty training [11]. Psychiatric residency spans five years in Canada [12], four years in the United States [13], and three years in India [14].

During residency, trainees acquire supervised clinical experience across all major psychiatric subspecialties, including General Psychiatry, Neurology, Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, Old Age Psychiatry, Forensic Psychiatry, Community Psychiatry, Substance Use Psychiatry, Emergency Psychiatry, and Consultation-Liaison Psychiatry, with individual rotations lasting three to twelve months depending on the subspecialty and the college’s curriculum requirements [9, 10].

Under the NPMCN curriculum, junior residency includes a six-month rotation in General Adult Psychiatry, three months in Neurology, and three months each in any five of the following seven core areas: Child Psychiatry, Psychiatry of Later Life, Forensic Psychiatry, Community Psychiatry, Substance Use Psychiatry, Emergency Psychiatry, and Consultation-Liaison Psychiatry. Senior residency includes six months to complete the two outstanding core rotations, three months in a subspecialty area other than the intended area of sub-specialisation, three months in an elective rotation — which may extend beyond core psychiatry but must be relevant to mental health and requires faculty approval — and a final twelve months in the area of intended sub-specialisation [9].

The WACP membership training programme (junior residency) spans 36 months and comprises 12 months of General Adult Psychiatry, six months of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, and three months each of Consultation-Liaison Psychiatry, Neurology, Old Age Psychiatry, Community Psychiatry, Addiction Psychiatry, and Forensic Psychiatry. The WACP fellowship programme (senior residency) requires a minimum of 24 months and comprises six months of General Adult Psychiatry, three months each from any two of the following subspecialties — Addiction, Child and Adolescent, Consultation-Liaison, Forensic, Old Age, or Community Psychiatry — with the final twelve months spent in one rotation, preferably the intended area of sub-specialization [10].

Beyond clinical exposure, the programme develops competencies in research, teaching, and administration. Residents are required to complete a supervised research project (dissertation) before the conclusion of senior residency. Training methods include supervised clinical practice, didactic lectures, seminars, case conferences, journal clubs, multidisciplinary team meetings, and structured mentorship [9, 10].

Both the NPMCN and WACP curricula specify clear minimum requirements for clinical and academic activity in each rotation. Junior residents are expected to complete five hours of clinical rotations daily (six days per week), supplemented by two hours of didactic lectures, 1.5 h of weekly journal club meetings, and two hours of monthly clinical case conferences across core rotation areas. Senior residency training entails similar clinical service requirements, in addition to mandatory two-week workshops on management and research methodology organised by the respective colleges, 15 weeks of medical education seminars at local training institutions, and completion of a dissertation project on a psychiatry-related topic [9, 10].

Assessment is both formative and summative. Formative assessments, conducted on an ongoing basis by institutional trainers, encompass clinical competencies, mini-clinical evaluations, direct observation of procedural skills, case-based discussions, teaching performance, and portfolio documentation. Completion of a stipulated number of formative assessments each year is mandatory for examination eligibility. Summative assessments for the WACP include theory papers, picture tests, objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs), long clinical cases, and a dissertation examination, while those for the NPMCN include multiple-choice questions, essay papers, long cases, manned OSCE stations, picture tests, oral examinations, patient management problems, and a dissertation defence. By the end of training, residents are expected to demonstrate competence across six core domains: medical knowledge, patient care, practice-based learning and improvement, interpersonal and communication skills, professionalism, and systems-based practice [9, 10].

Notwithstanding this structured framework, postgraduate psychiatric training in Nigeria faces several serious and escalating challenges. The migration crisis is not confined to early-career doctors [6]: senior psychiatrists and specialist trainers are increasingly leaving the country, further widening the consultant-to-trainee ratio and depleting expertise in already scarce subspecialties such as forensic psychiatry, community psychiatry, old age psychiatry and psychotherapy, among others. In several instances, departing trainers have left no replacements, with direct consequences for the breadth and quality of training available to the next generation of psychiatrists. Compounding this, many training institutions are now admitting prospective residents prior to their passing the mandatory primary fellowship entry examination — a departure from college regulations driven by severe shortages of trainee doctors. These pre-examination recruits are often deployed primarily for service delivery, only formally commencing residency upon subsequently passing the primary examination. While pragmatic, this practice risks subordinating training quality to service need.

Despite the recognised importance of a standardised, competency-based postgraduate training framework, empirical evidence on how psychiatric residency programmes across Nigeria are actually structured, implemented, and monitored — and the degree to which they comply with approved NPMCN and WACP guidelines — remains very limited. This national online survey therefore aims to systematically assess the structure and delivery of postgraduate psychiatric training in Nigeria, evaluate compliance with established college standards, and benchmark local practices against global counterparts, with the ultimate goal of identifying actionable areas for improvement, supporting harmonisation of training across institutions, and strengthening the quality of psychiatric specialist formation in Nigeria.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted across all 30 accredited postgraduate psychiatric residency training centres in Nigeria through an online survey. These institutions held either partial or full accreditation from the National Postgraduate Medical College of Nigeria (NPMCN), the West African College of Physicians (WACP), or both.

Study instrument and data collection

Data were collected using a researcher-developed questionnaire designed specifically for this study and administered via Google Forms. The self-administered instrument, written in English, comprised 35 items organised into two main sections. The first section captured basic information about each training institution, including its name, geographic location, type of facility, number of practising psychiatrists, and number of resident doctors. The second section examined the structure of postgraduate training, covering the mode of preparation for Part I and Part II fellowship examinations, available subspecialty rotations, end-of-posting assessment practices, and the availability of institutional training manuals, among other domains.

Participant recruitment

The Head of Department (HOD) or a designated psychiatrist actively involved in residency training at each institution was contacted individually via email or WhatsApp and invited to complete the questionnaire on behalf of their institution. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to survey completion. Participation was entirely voluntary, and all responses were handled with strict confidentiality. Twenty-nine institutions whose representatives provided informed consent and completed the questionnaire were included in the final analysis.

Pretesting and validation

To ensure clarity, relevance, and content validity, the questionnaire was pretested among five psychiatrists working in two emerging training hospitals who did not participate in the main study. Feedback from the pretest was used to refine the instrument before its final administration.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee of the Kwara State University Teaching Hospital (Protocol No: KWASUTH/IRC/246/VOL.II/45). The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed of the study objectives and their right to withdraw at any stage. Informed consent was obtained electronically, and all institutional responses were anonymised and stored securely to ensure confidentiality.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using STATA version 17.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the demographic characteristics of the training facilities and the structure and delivery of training programmes. For the open-ended question on preparation strategies for the Part II fellowship examination, responses from 28 training institutions were inductively coded and grouped into thematic categories using thematic analysis. As most responses referenced multiple activities, the themes were not mutually exclusive and total percentages therefore exceeded 100%. Two authors (MOS and DS) independently coded the responses and reached complete agreement. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Seven distinct thematic categories were identified: Theme 1 — Mock Examinations and Oral Practice (mock oral exams, mock dissertation defences, OSCE simulations, and structured oral question sessions); Theme 2 — Dissertation and Research Supervision (dissertation review, supervision of proposals, departmental proposal and thesis presentations, and dissertation progress meetings); Theme 3 — Seminar and Case Presentations (seminar presentations, and presentation of challenging cases); Theme 4 — Journal Club and Literature Reviews (journal reviews, and literature review sessions); Theme 5 — Didactic Teaching and Lectures (consultant-led lectures, teaching on research methods and statistics); Theme 6 — Research Mentorship and Involvement in Trainer or Departmental/Hospital Research (research mentorship and guidance, participation in departmental/hospital research groups, and proposal topic development); and Theme 7 — Supervised Clinical Engagement (taking charge of ward rounds under consultant supervision).

Results

At the time of the study, 109 health institutions in Nigeria were accredited for postgraduate medical training across various specialties (e.g. surgery, internal medicine, and paediatrics e.t.c). Of these 109 institutions, only 30 (27.5%) offered postgraduate training in psychiatry. Twenty-nine of the 30 eligible institutions participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 96.7%.

More than half (55.2%) of the accredited psychiatric departments were located within federal teaching hospitals; just over a quarter (27.6%) were standalone federal neuropsychiatric hospitals, and only four (13.8%) were situated within state teaching hospitals. The median duration of accreditation as a training institution was 20 years (IQR: 12–26 years). For Part II training, thematic analysis of responses from 28 institutions revealed seven distinct strategies employed in preparing residents for the fellowship examination. Dissertation and research supervision was the most frequently reported approach, identified in 16 institutions (57.1%), and encompassed departmental assessment of research proposals, dissertation critiques, correction of submitted work, and progress report meetings. Mock examinations and oral practice were nearly as prevalent, reported in 15 institutions (53.6%), and included simulated oral assessments, mock dissertation defences, and structured OSCE practice designed to replicate examination conditions. Seminar and case presentations featured in 13 institutions (46.4%), with residents required to formally present seminar and clinical cases to departmental audiences.

Journal club and literature review sessions were reported in 7 institutions (25.0%), while research mentorship and involvement in hospital or trainer-led research was identified in 5 (17.9%). Didactic teaching and lectures — primarily covering research methods and statistics — were reported in 4 institutions (14.3%). A small number of institutions (n = 2; 7.1%) reported supervised clinical engagement (i.e. monitored ward round leadership by senior residents) as a component of their senior residency preparation. Notably, one institution acknowledged the absence of a formal preparation programme, with residents participating informally in trainers’ research activities in lieu of a structured approach (Table 5).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive overview of the structure and delivery of postgraduate psychiatric training in Nigeria, revealing substantial variability in institutional capacity, training scope, and academic organisation across accredited centres. Although psychiatric residency training has expanded over time, its current scale, distribution, and infrastructure remain insufficient to meet Nigeria’s growing mental health needs.

Currently, only approximately one-quarter of health institutions accredited to train medical specialists in the country offer psychiatric residency programmes, with most training centres concentrated in federal teaching hospitals. In practical terms, Nigeria’s 30 accredited psychiatric training centres translate to roughly one programme per 7.8 million people, compared to 346 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME)-accredited psychiatric programmes in the United States (approximately 1 per 970,000 people) and 17 Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada (RCPS)-accredited programmes (approximately 1 per 2.4 million people) [15, 16]. These comparisons underscore the critical undersupply of psychiatric training capacity in Nigeria and highlight the risk of perpetuating regional and institutional disparities in specialist mental health workforce development — a pattern commonly observed in other low- and middle-income countries where specialist services and training are highly centralised in national or federal institutions [17]. While the median duration since accreditation (20 years) reflects a well-established foundation for psychiatric training in Nigeria, the slow pace of programme expansion underscores the urgent need for deliberate policy efforts to scale up training capacity. For instance, expanding accreditation from the current 27.5% to approximately 50% — to include more state-owned and emerging private tertiary institutions in the short to medium term — alongside targeted funding and capacity-building initiatives, could help reduce these disparities and strengthen the national supply of qualified psychiatrists.

Clear differences in training infrastructure and human resources were also evident across institutions. The median figures for bed capacity, consultant psychiatrists, and resident doctors suggest that many programmes operate on a relatively small scale, which may limit the breadth of clinical exposure, supervision, and mentorship available to trainees. Standalone federal neuropsychiatric hospitals, with their larger bed capacities and stronger staffing levels, offer clear advantages in terms of case diversity and training opportunities. However, an over-reliance on a small number of well-resourced centres restricts overall training output and reinforces regional imbalances. Additionally, standalone neuropsychiatric centres provide limited exposure to consultation-liaison psychiatry, integrated behavioural health models, and the psychiatric manifestations of medical disorders. Inter-institutional outside postings — where residents attend other accredited institutions to complete rotations not available at their home centre (such as neurology) — are common practice and are permitted under college curricula, and are expected to partially bridge this knowledge gap. Similar challenges have been documented across Africa and other resource-constrained settings, where psychiatric training programmes frequently struggle with faculty shortages, limited facilities, and heavy service demands that undermine competency-based education [18]. Addressing these gaps will require coordinated workforce planning, expansion of faculty positions, improved clinical infrastructure, protected time for teaching and supervision, and strengthened inter-institutional exchange programmes among all accredited training centres.

In terms of curriculum delivery, most institutions prepared residents for both Part I and Part II fellowship examinations, indicating adherence to the requirements of the NPMCN and the WACP. Core rotations in General Adult Psychiatry, Addiction Psychiatry, Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, and Consultation-Liaison Psychiatry were widely available. In contrast, exposure to Community Psychiatry was strikingly limited, with fewer than one-quarter of centres offering this rotation. This finding is particularly concerning given World Health Organisation recommendations that prioritise community-based and decentralised mental health care as key strategies for improving access to services in low-resource settings [19]. Strengthening community psychiatric training through structured rotations and partnerships with primary care and community-based services would better equip residents to address Nigeria’s mental health needs and lead system-level reforms.

Training in Old Age Psychiatry and Psychotherapy was also inconsistent, with more than one-quarter of institutions lacking formal psychotherapy training. This gap is especially significant, as psychotherapy skills enhance clinical competence and are highly valued by trainees and early-career psychiatrists [20, 21]. These findings are consistent with earlier Nigerian studies reporting limited psychotherapy exposure and supervision [22], as well as evidence from other low- and middle-income countries [23]. The current NPMCN and WACP curricula embed psychotherapy within General Psychiatry rather than defining it as a separate rotation or distinct competency area, which may contribute to variability in training delivery and difficulty in assessing competence. In contrast, many international training programmes recognise psychotherapy as a core training domain with structured supervision and clearly defined competency requirements [11, 12, 24–26]. Revising the curriculum to include structured, supervised psychotherapy training — either as a standalone posting or through clearly articulated competency benchmarks — would help address this important skills gap.

In addition, formalising inter-institutional outside postings would enable resident doctors to gain exposure to subspecialties such as forensic psychiatry and old age psychiatry that may not be available at their home institutions, thereby improving the overall scope and quality of training. Future qualitative studies employing in-depth interviews with trainers and trainees are warranted to explore how residents navigate core psychiatry subspecialty rotations that are prerequisite for examination eligibility yet unavailable at their home institutions — a pattern evident in the findings of the present study.

The World Psychiatry Exchange Programme of the World Psychiatric Association (WPA) presents an additional and readily available opportunity for early-career psychiatrists (ECPs) in Nigeria to gain international exposure in areas such as neuropsychiatry and diverse psychotherapeutic models not easily accessible locally [27, 28]. By facilitating the acquisition of transversal competencies and fostering a triad of education, research, and innovation, this programme can enhance learning, promote international networking, and strengthen global collaborations. It is therefore recommended that the NPMCN review its curriculum and reinstate the previously practised compulsory one-year overseas training for senior residents, while actively pursuing formal partnerships with the WPA under its exchange programme. Furthermore, neuropsychiatry should be developed as a formal subspecialty in Nigeria, with resident doctors undertaking dedicated neuropsychiatry rotations in addition to neurology postings, to better equip psychiatrists to manage complex behavioural presentations associated with brain disorders.

Further evidence of variability in training implementation was observed in the absence of formal residency training manuals in more than one-third of institutions. The lack of standardised documentation may undermine consistent competency development and weaken the integrity of national certification processes. International accreditation frameworks emphasise the importance of clearly documented, competency-based curricula supported by institutional training manuals [11, 12, 24]. Strengthening accreditation requirements to mandate the availability and regular review of such manuals, alongside closer monitoring of curriculum implementation, would promote greater standardisation across training centres.

Despite clearly defined academic and clinical requirements outlined by the NPMCN and WACP [9, 10], this study identified important gaps between policy and practice. While most institutions conducted weekly academic seminars, fewer than half held monthly journal clubs or clinical case conferences, and nearly one-quarter did not conduct case conferences at all. These deficiencies reduce opportunities for critical appraisal, reflective learning, and interdisciplinary engagement — all of which are central to high-quality postgraduate education. Structured workplace-based assessments, a cornerstone of competency-based training, were implemented in only approximately one-third of centres. The absence of real-time end-of-posting assessments in the majority of institutions diminishes the formative value of assessment and may compromise objectivity, echoing concerns raised in other Nigerian postgraduate training programmes where feedback was inconsistently delivered or perceived as punitive [29]. Embedding a culture of timely, structured formative assessment and constructive feedback after each rotation would strengthen mentorship and better align training with international standards [12, 24].

Although research training is mandated by both the NPMCN and WACP through compulsory dissertation requirements, it appears to be poorly integrated into the routine life of residency programmes. The findings on Part II preparation strategies reveal important additional dimensions of this variability. While dissertation and research supervision was the most frequently reported approach — employed in 57.1% of institutions — and mock examinations and oral practice were nearly as common (53.6%), a substantial proportion of institutions relied on only one or two preparation modalities. The relative infrequency of journal clubs and literature review sessions (25.0%), formal research mentorship (17.9%), and didactic teaching on research methods (14.3%) suggests that research literacy and critical appraisal skills are inconsistently cultivated during senior residency. Of particular concern, at least one institution acknowledged the complete absence of a formal Part II preparation programme, with residents informally participating in trainers’ research activities as the only form of preparation. Such variability risks producing cohorts of senior residents with markedly unequal readiness for the fellowship examination and for subsequent independent specialist practice. Standardising Part II preparation across institutions — through college-endorsed minimum requirements for mock examinations, structured dissertation supervision milestones, and formal research mentorship — would reduce these disparities and improve both the quality and equity of senior residency training. These findings are consistent with international evidence: variability in the structure and quality of research training has also been documented in Canada, the United States, and Australia, where approaches differ considerably across programmes [15, 24, 30–33]. More broadly, providing residents with protected research time, structured mentorship, and opportunities to participate in supervised departmental research projects would help build the research skills and culture necessary for a productive psychiatric academic workforce in Nigeria.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents one of the most comprehensive national assessments of postgraduate psychiatric residency training in Nigeria to date. By including nearly all accredited training institutions and achieving a response rate of 96.7%, the findings provide a reliable and representative snapshot of current training structures, resources, and practices across the country. The breadth of coverage across geopolitical zones and institution types — from standalone neuropsychiatric hospitals to federal and state teaching hospitals — strengthens the generalisability of the findings within the Nigerian context. The study also identifies practical, actionable areas for improvement that are directly relevant to policymakers, training institutions, and regulatory bodies.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the reliance on self-reported data from departmental representatives introduces the possibility of reporting bias or social desirability effects, as responses may not fully reflect actual day-to-day practice in some institutions. Second, although nearly all eligible accredited institutions participated, emerging training hospitals yet to receive formal accreditation for psychiatric training were not included; this may lead to an underestimation of the broader national gaps in psychiatric training capacity. Third, the cross-sectional design captures a single point in time and does not permit the assessment of changes or trends in training delivery over time. Fourth, the questionnaire format placed limits on the depth of information obtainable from the respondents. Furthermore, the study did not quantify the hours trainees spent on specific academic or clinical activities, which would have provided a more granular basis for international comparisons. Finally, the study focused primarily on structural and organisational dimensions of training — specifically, the availability and delivery of measurable academic activities, clinical duties, and subspecialty postings during different phases of residency — and did not directly assess trainee experiences, learning outcomes, or patient care impact. Domains such as interpersonal and communication skills, professionalism, practice-based learning, and administrative training are largely embedded within the training methods already evaluated, and are normally assessed on an ongoing individual basis by trainers through annual workplace-based assessments — areas that fall beyond the scope of the present study, but represent important directions for future research. Longitudinal or mixed-methods designs would provide deeper insights into training quality, outcomes, and the lived experiences of residents across Nigeria’s diverse training centres.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that, despite a longstanding framework for postgraduate psychiatric training in Nigeria, significant gaps persist in the distribution, capacity, and implementation of residency programmes. Training opportunities remain concentrated in a small number of federally funded institutions, with marked disparities in infrastructure, subspecialty exposure, academic activity, assessment practices, and research integration across accredited centres. Limited access to community psychiatry rotations, inconsistent use of formative assessments, and the poor integration of research training into routine residency practice risk hindering the development of a well-rounded, practice-ready psychiatric workforce. The variability observed in Part II preparation approaches — ranging from comprehensive, multi-modal strategies to the near or total absence of a formal programme in some institutions — further underscores the need for greater standardisation and regulatory oversight across the training centres in Nigeria. Addressing these challenges will require deliberate policy reforms, the expansion of accredited training centres, targeted investment in faculty and clinical infrastructure, improved curriculum standardisation, and closer monitoring of programme implementation by the NPMCN and WACP. Harmonising postgraduate psychiatric training with international best practices is essential not only for improving the quality of specialist training, but also for building a stronger and more equitably distributed mental health workforce capable of meeting Nigeria’s growing and largely unmet mental health needs.
