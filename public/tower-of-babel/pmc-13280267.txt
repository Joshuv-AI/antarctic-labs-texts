The State of the Art in Chronic Prurigo Nodularis
Authors: Esther Serra-Baldrich, Ángeles Flórez, Marc Julià Manresa, Francisco Javier Ortiz de Frutos, José-Juan Pereyra-Rodriguez, Bibiana Pérez, Ignasi Figueras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic prurigo nodularis (CPN) is a highly burdensome chronic inflammatory dermatosis characterized by intensely pruritic papulonodular lesions that may persist for years. Its pathophysiology reflects a neuro–immune–fibrotic axis in which type-2 cytokines (IL-4/IL-13/IL-31), neuropeptides, and profibrotic signaling interact to sustain the itch–scratch cycle and dermal fibrosis. CPN predominantly affects older adult women and is frequently associated with dermatologic, neuropsychiatric, and cardiometabolic comorbidities. Definition, nomenclature, and management remain areas of debate, with relevant differences between European and US consensus documents. The therapeutic landscape has expanded with dupilumab and nemolizumab, the only agents currently supported by phase III evidence, whereas JAK inhibitors remain promising but investigational options that require careful safety contextualization. This narrative review synthesizes current knowledge on CPN, from definition and epidemiology to etiopathogenesis and evolving therapy, while highlighting persistent unmet needs in diagnosis and management.Keywords: Fibrosis, Immune dysregulation, Neural dysregulation, Prurigo, Pruritus, Quality of life

Key Summary Points

Chronic prurigo nodularis (CPN) is a debilitating inflammatory skin disease defined by intense, chronic itch and nodular lesions that significantly impair a patient’s quality of life and psychological well-being.The disease is driven by a self-perpetuating “itch–scratch cycle” involving a complex neuro–immune–fibrotic axis where type-2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31) and neuropeptides promote persistent inflammation and skin fibrosis.CPN is a highly heterogeneous condition frequently associated with a broad range of comorbidities, including atopic dermatitis, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, and psychiatric disorders such as anxiety and depression.The therapeutic landscape is currently shifting from empirical, off-label treatments toward targeted biologics, with dupilumab and nemolizumab being the only agents currently supported by high-level phase III clinical evidence.There remains a critical need for standardized clinical guidelines and personalized “treat-to-target” strategies to address existing gaps in managing special populations and the neurogenic components of the disease.

Introduction

Chronic prurigo nodularis (CPN), referred to as prurigo nodularis (PN) in much of US literature, is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by intensely pruritic papulonodular lesions that may persist for years or even decades [1–9]. Patients experience not only intractable itch but also pain, stinging, burning, and tingling, which promotes repeated scratching, scarring, and a substantial disease burden. Moderate-to-severe disease can impair quality of life to a degree comparable with other chronic disorders such as hypertension or arthritis [10]. This burden is further amplified by frequent comorbidities, including atopic dermatitis, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and psychiatric disorders, which contribute to the marked heterogeneity of CPN [11].

Despite the diversity of associated conditions, all cases of CPN share a common pathophysiologic mechanism known as “the itch–scratch cycle”, in which uncontrollable itch leads to persistent scratching and the subsequent development of the nodular cutaneous lesions [12, 13]. Recent molecular and histopathological studies highlight the role of a neuro–immune–fibrotic axis in which type-2 cytokines (IL-4/IL-13/IL-31), neuropeptides, and profibrotic pathways drive the itch–scratch cycle, epidermal acanthosis, and fibrosis [14].

Currently, there is no universally accepted definition of CPN and no standardized clinical guidelines for its management. Existing expert consensus documents propose largely empirical therapeutic strategies, with limited guidance on treatment sequencing, combinations, optimal duration, or management of special populations [1, 15]. One of the major unmet needs in CPN care is the development of clear therapeutic algorithms. Recent advances, particularly the approval of dupilumab and nemolizumab [16, 17], the only agents supported by phase III evidence, together with emerging JAK inhibitors, are reshaping the treatment landscape.

This narrative review synthesizes current knowledge on CPN, including its definition, epidemiology, etiopathogenesis, and clinical impact. It also integrates evidence from clinical trials and real-world studies to support clinicians in the diagnosis, assessment, and management of this challenging condition.

Methods

We performed a targeted literature search in MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, and the Cochrane Library from January 2015 to September 2025. The strategy combined MeSH/Emtree terms and free-text keywords related to CPN/PN, epidemiology, pathophysiology (including IL-4, IL-13, IL-31, neuroimmune pathways, and fibrosis), diagnosis, patient-reported outcomes, and treatments (dupilumab, nemolizumab, JAK inhibitors). Search terms also covered emerging biologics and their targets (e.g., vixarelimab/OSMRβ, barzolvolimab/CDX-0159/KIT, rocatinlimab/OX40, stapokibart/CM310/IL-4Rα, SHR-1819/IL-4Rα), alongside class terms (e.g., oncostatin-M/OSM, IL-4 receptor, OX40 antagonists, KIT inhibitors).

We included human studies in English or Spanish that addressed CPN definition or classification, epidemiology, comorbidities, pathophysiology, diagnosis, outcome measures, or treatment. We prioritized clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews or meta-analyses, randomized and prospective interventional studies, large observational cohorts, and selected seminal mechanistic studies. We excluded reports centered on other chronic pruritic disorders without extractable CPN data, nonprimary literature not relevant to the objectives, and isolated case reports except when they illustrated an emerging therapy with no higher-level evidence. Reference lists of key reviews and guidelines were hand-searched to identify additional records. The final narrative synthesis included 127 references. Because this review was conceived as a narrative review rather than a systematic review, a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-style screening flow, formal risk-of-bias assessment, and a prospectively recorded record-level denominator for all screened citations were not available; this limitation is now stated explicitly to avoid overstating methodological rigor. However, we describe the search process to enhance transparency and adhere to the principles of the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) [18].

Relevant Aspects of Chronic Prurigo Nodularis

Definition

Although dermatologists generally share a clear clinical understanding of what CPN is, its nomenclature has historically been a source of confusion. In the USA, the term prurigo nodularis (PN) is preferred and is used to encompass all chronic prurigo variants, as it is considered sufficiently descriptive (ICD-10: L28.1) [15]. In contrast, in Europe, chronic prurigo is employed as the umbrella term, with CPN designated as its nodular subtype [1, 2]. Chronic prurigo is therefore used as a broad category that includes several clinical morphologies, such as papular, nodular, plaque, or umbilicated types of the same disease. The term chronic prurigo was used in literature prior to the establishment of the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology Task Force in 2018 with the purpose of reaching expert consensus on the definition and classification of prurigo associated terms [1].

Epidemiology

Data on the incidence and prevalence of CPN remain scarce and heterogeneous, partly because the disease is underrecognized, and case ascertainment differs across datasets [19]. Reported prevalence ranges from 9 to 210 per 100,000 in Europe and the USA [8]. In large European and US cross-sectional studies, older adults predominate, with mean ages of 64 and 56 years, respectively, although cases also occur in other age groups, including children [10, 20–22]. No consistent sex association has been demonstrated, with rates similar to the general population or only slightly higher in women in some cohorts [8, 10, 20, 22, 23].

In England, epidemiological data indicate an increase in CPN prevalence from 2.49 per 10,000 inhabitants in 2007 to 8.8 per 10,000 inhabitants in 2019 [24]. These findings differ from those of Morgan et al., who reported a prevalence of 3.27 per 10,000 inhabitants in 2018 and an incidence of 2.88 per 100,000 inhabitant-years between 2008 and 2018 [25]. However, these studies used markedly different methodologies [25, 26].

Etiopathogenesis

The etiology of CPN remains unclear in most reports. The disease frequently occurs in association with dermatologic, systemic, neurologic, or psychiatric disorders, although it can also appear without an identifiable underlying condition [1, 6, 22]. Ortiz de Frutos et al. classified CPN according to etiological factors and comorbidities into four groups: (1) dermatological disease-related CPN, most commonly linked to atopic dermatitis and less often to psoriasis, lichen planus, or hypertrophic lupus; (2) systemic/metabolic disease-related CPN, including diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, hematologic and hepatobiliary disease, and drug-induced pruritus; (3) psychiatric disease-related CPN, associated with obsessive–compulsive, impulse control, or anxiety–depressive disorders; or (4) idiopathic CPN, defined as CPN without an associated disease, representing a very small proportion of cases [27].

These diverse comorbid profiles contribute to marked clinical heterogeneity. Etiology and comorbidities are often intertwined, and it is frequently unclear whether one condition precedes or results from the other or how CPN and comorbidities mutually influence each other [28].

Despite these differences, patients share a common pathophysiologic mechanism: the itch–scratch cycle, maintained by a complex interplay of immunologic, environmental, and disease-related factors (Fig. 1) [1, 6, 22]. Advances in molecular and histopathologic research have substantially improved our understanding of CPN, which is now considered a multifactorial disorder driven by immune dysregulation, neuronal sensitization, and—unlike most other chronic pruritic diseases—a prominent profibrotic response [14].

Immune Dysregulation

Changes in the skin of patients with CPN perpetuate the itch–scratch cycle, causing chronic nonhistaminergic pruritus and persistent inflammation [12, 29–31]. When the epithelium is damaged—by scratching or by proinflammatory cytokines and pruritogenic mediators—keratinocytes release itch-inducing mediators (thymic stromal lymphopoietin, IL-33, cathepsin S, kallikreins) that activate sensory neurons [12, 29]. Additional pruritogenic and type-2 cytokines from eosinophils, basophils, type-2 innate lymphoid cells, Th2 lymphocytes, and mast cells (IL-4, IL-13, IL-31, histamine, tryptase) further stimulate these neurons and contribute to producing itching (Fig. 1) [12, 32–34].

Within this microenvironment, eosinophils and mast cells are particularly relevant. Eosinophils release neuropeptides, eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (EDN), eosinophil protein X (EPX), and major basic protein; ECP and EDN, which are neurotoxic, are markedly elevated in CPN skin [35]. Mast cells are an important source of oncostatin M and show high IL-31RA expression in CPN, and mast cell–associated mediators and receptors (IL-4, IL-13, IL-31, MRGPRX2, PAR2) also contribute to disease pathogenesis [36].

Type-2 inflammation is central in CPN. IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31 directly activate sensory neurons via their receptors (Fig. 1) [37, 38]. IL-4 and IL-13 signaling through IL-4Rα sensitizes neurons to subthreshold levels of other pruritogens, including IL-31, a key pruritogen in CPN [12, 38–42]. Moreover, type-2 cytokines participate in feedback loops, as IL-4 induces IL-31 expression and release by Th2 cells, further amplifying pruritus and inflammation [38].

Neural Dysregulation

CPN is also characterized by structural and functional alterations of cutaneous nerves. In the epidermis, intraepidermal nerve fiber density is reduced but returns to normal after lesion resolution; nerve growth factor (NGF) receptor–immunoreactive fibers and protein gene product (PGP)-9.5+ fibers are likewise decreased [35, 43]. In the dermis, however, neuronal hyperplasia is observed, with PGP-9.5 and NGF receptor staining showing significantly denser immunoreactive structures than in healthy controls [35, 43, 44].

Chronic activation of sensory neurons—by scratching or pruritogenic mediators—induces neurogenic inflammation and peripheral sensitization [29, 30]. Activated neurons release neuropeptides (calcitonin gene-related peptide, substance P, vasoactive intestinal polypeptide) that stimulate immune cells to produce IL-4, IL-13, and other mediators, which in turn further activate neurons, creating a self-reinforcing loop. These signals are transmitted to the central nervous system, eliciting itch and perpetuating the itch–scratch cycle (Fig. 1) [45].

Fibrotic Response

Cutaneous fibrosis is a defining histopathological feature of CPN [9, 46]. It arises from repeated tissue injury and represents a reparative response to stimuli such as scratching [47]. Pruritus-related mediators, including neuropeptides and histamine, also promote dermal inflammation and fibrosis (Fig. 1) [46]. However, type-2 cytokines are the main fibrotic drivers: IL-4 and IL-13, essential for normal wound healing, become pathogenic when dysregulated, activating profibrotic pathways that induce excessive fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition [47, 48]. Fibroblasts in CPN display a WNT5A+ phenotype, are apoptosis-resistant, and enhance dermal fibrosis and type-2 inflammation via periostin release [49, 50]. IL-31 further activates IL-31RA on fibroblasts, altering genes involved in proliferation and collagen synthesis, thereby contributing to fibrosis [51].

CPN is also characterized by a hyperkeratotic epidermis with irregular epidermal hyperplasia, focal parakeratosis, papillary dermal fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltration [52]. IL-22, produced in type-3 (Th17/Th22) responses, is a key proinflammatory cytokine driving these epidermal changes. It promotes keratinocyte proliferation, fuels skin inflammation, and modulates wound healing and epidermal differentiation, with elevated levels detected in both skin and serum of patients with CPN [52, 53].

Comorbidities

A systematic review identified diseases related to CPN and grouped them into: (1) pruritic diseases that may lead to or coexist with CPN, (2) inflammatory diseases, and (3) age-related diseases, more frequent in older adults, in whom CPN is also more common [11]. Studies from several countries similarly show that many conditions are more prevalent in CPN, notably, atopic dermatitis, asthma, diabetes, hyperlipoproteinemia, heart failure, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic kidney disease [11, 27, 28, 54–56].

Up to 50% of patients with CPN have a history of atopy, and the term “atopic dermatitis with a prurigo phenotype” is often used, although the temporal relationship between dermatitis and prurigo is unclear [11]. Single-cell RNA sequencing has shown molecular differences: oligoclonal cytotoxic T cells are present in atopic dermatitis but not in CPN, and type 2 myeloid responses are stronger in atopic dermatitis, whereas CPN is dominated by a stromal response (fibrosis, angiogenesis, collagen remodeling, neurogenesis) [57].

Among systemic comorbidities, chronic kidney disease is particularly relevant. Although many patients with chronic kidney disease and pruritus do not develop prurigo, chronic kidney disease–associated pruritus and CPN share dermatologic, neuropathic, and immune dysregulation features [58]. Renin–angiotensin system dysregulation may also contribute to both CPN lesions and kidney disease [59, 60]. Other systemic associations include infectious, endocrinologic, cardiovascular, and neoplastic diseases such as lymphoma, likely linked to inflammatory imbalance, neural dysregulation, or a profibrotic tendency. In hepatic disease, Marani et al. found that patients with CPN (versus atopic dermatitis) showed gene signatures associated with liver disease, liver fibrosis, and chronic hepatitis [61]. The most prevalent metabolic comorbidities are type 2 diabetes (≈ 35%) and chronic liver disease (≈ 16%), both capable of causing peripheral neuropathy and supporting a role for neural dysregulation in CPN pathogenesis [20, 62].

Psychiatric comorbidities, especially anxiety (45.6%) and depression (16.4%), are also common [7, 63]. Sleep disturbance is frequent and associated with elevated C-reactive protein levels, which in turn increase cardiovascular risk [64]. In one analysis, patients with CPN were clustered into: class 1, atopy (high atopic burden, no psychiatric or cardiovascular disease, lower mean age 38.7 years); class 2, neuropsychiatric (high neuropsychiatric burden, 68.9% women); class 3, high comorbidity burden (multiple chronic pruritic, neuropsychiatric, cardiopulmonary, and atopic diseases); and class 4, chronic and cardiopulmonary diseases [65]. This stratification may aid clinical and therapeutic decision-making.

In a multicenter study of 513 patients with CPN with various comorbidities, itch intensity (worst itch intensity in the previous 24 h, worst itch numeric rating scale [WI-NRS]) was significantly associated only with hypertension—hypertensive patients reported more intense pruritus—whereas quality of life (Five-Item Life Quality Questionnaire, 5-PLQ) was significantly worse in those with concomitant depression [66].

Impact on Patients

Patients with CPN experience a multidimensional burden, affecting both physical and psychological health [10]. Beyond intense itch and papulonodular lesions with excoriations and scarring [1, 10, 13, 22], they frequently report pain and somatosensory symptoms such as stinging, burning, tingling, and heat/cold dysesthesia [13, 22]. Compared with other chronic conditions with pruritus, the itching experienced by patients with CPN is among the most severe, with an intensity of 7 out of 10 [7, 10, 22]. More than 53% report skin pain (skin pain-numeric rating scale (NRS) ≥ 3), and more than 54% report burning, stinging, or tingling (burning/stinging/tingling-NRS ≥ 3) [7]. Although nodules may gradually resolve, they often leave scarring and hyper- or depigmentation, especially if undertreated, adding considerable psychoaffective distress [67, 68].

This burden leads to marked impairment in quality of life, comparable to other pruritic dermatoses (notably contact dermatitis and lichen planus) and systemic diseases such as hypertension, stroke, coronary artery disease, and arthritis, through sleep disturbance, absenteeism, and disruption of daily activities, particularly in those with higher itch scores [1, 7, 10, 69–74]. Emotional well-being is also affected, with frequent anxiety, depression, anger, disgust, shame, and helplessness and increased use of antidepressants and anxiolytics [1, 7, 75]. Patients with CPN show higher odds than those with atopic dermatitis of behavioral and eating disorders, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, substance abuse, schizophrenia, and self-harm [19]. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that they are 1.3-, 1.9-, and 2-fold more likely to experience depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation/self-harm, respectively, than healthy controls, although causality remains unclear [76].

The overall burden is further amplified by comorbidities [3, 10, 15, 19, 22]. A higher Charlson Comorbidity Index in CPN (1.53) compared with atopic dermatitis (0.53) and psoriasis (1.16) is associated with greater use of specialist healthcare services [77].

Diagnosis and Evaluation of Chronic Prurigo Nodularis

CPN includes several lesion types, with papulonodular lesions being the most common [8]. Different prurigo lesions may coexist, and no subtype lesion necessarily reflects a more advanced or chronic stage [5, 6, 23]. Papulonodular lesions can range from a few to hundreds, typically distributed bilaterally and symmetrically on the extensor surfaces of the arms and legs [5, 6, 8, 78]. The “butterfly sign” may be observed, with sparing of areas inaccessible to scratching, classically the mid-back [6, 23]. Ulcerations may also occur, irrespective of CPN subtype [79].

There is no consensus on whether CPN is a distinct disease or a consequence of other conditions; therefore, current guidelines seek to harmonize diagnostic criteria. European and American documents diagnose CPN clinically using three main signs or symptoms, summarized in Table 1. Both require chronic pruritus lasting at least 6 weeks [1, 2, 15, 80]. The European guideline additionally emphasizes multiple localized or generalized pruritic lesions and a history or signs of repeated scratching, whereas the American consensus highlights nodular lesions plus repeated scratching, pinching, or rubbing [1, 15, 80]. Terminology also differs: “chronic nodular prurigo” in Europe versus “prurigo nodularis” in the USA (Table 1). Differential diagnosis remains essential, and Table 2 highlights the main alternatives and distinguishing features [2, 3, 15, 81–83].

Guidelines recommend a complementary diagnostic work-up that may include laboratory testing and skin biopsy. European and American documents largely concur on the core investigations, whereas Japanese guidance is less detailed; Table 3 compares these approaches and underscores where recommendations converge or diverge [15, 80, 84]. In 2024, Spanish dermatologists proposed a diagnostic algorithm aligned with European criteria [27]. It accepts all lesion types (not only nodules) and uses history and examination to guide both differential diagnosis and etiologic categorization, integrating signs and symptoms, quality of life, productivity, patient-reported outcomes, and comorbidities. Final diagnoses fall into four categories: CPN associated with dermatologic disease, systemic/metabolic disease, psychiatric disease, or idiopathic CPN. Disease Evaluation and Control Tools

CPN severity and disease control can be assessed with several tools and scoring systems (Table 4), which collectively capture lesion activity, itch intensity, quality of life, sleep, and patient-perceived control [2, 3, 68, 73, 85–90]. One of the most widely used clinician-reported measures is the Investigator’s Global Assessment for PN (IGA-PN) [2, 85]. Others tools, such as the Prurigo Activity Score (PAS), provide greater granularity but are less practical for routine care, although a shorter version is available [2, 86].

IGA-PN is popular because of its simplicity. It evaluates both disease activity (IGA-PN-A) and stage (IGA-PN-S) using a 5-point scale based on the number of active lesions (0, clear [no nodules] to 4, severe [> 100 dome-shaped palpable nodules]) [2, 85].

Quality of life is crucial in CPN. The most commonly used tool is the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), which is almost always impaired [88]. DLQI is a ten-item questionnaire assessing the impact of skin disease over the previous week; each item scores 0–3 (maximum 30), with higher scores indicating worse quality of life [88]. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is also useful; it includes 14 items (7 for anxiety, 7 for depression), each subscale scored 0–21 (0–7, normal; 8–10, borderline; 11–21, abnormal) [89, 90]. To date, only DLQI has been formally validated in CPN, on the basis of phase III dupilumab trials in adults; therefore, other phenotypes and settings may be underrepresented [91]. Therefore, when possible, DLQI should be complemented with prurigo-specific tools such as the Prurigo Control Test (PCT) and/or ItchyQoL.

One of the most recent instruments is the PCT, developed and validated to assess disease control from the patient’s perspective. It comprises five items covering the previous 2 weeks, each scored 0–4 (total 0–20; 0 = worst control, 20 = complete control) [92]. PCT is easy to use in routine practice and supports more focused, meaningful communication between patients and clinicians. The ItchyQoL is a pruritus-specific quality-of-life questionnaire that assesses symptoms, functional impairment, and emotional impact of chronic itch. It consists of 22 Likert-scale items scored from 1 (never) to 5 (all the time) and summed to generate domain and total scores, allowing sensitive monitoring of disease burden and treatment response [93].

Among sleep measures, the sleep NRS is widely used because of its simplicity: patients rate sleep quality over the previous 24 h on a 0–10 scale (10 = worst, 0 = best), although confusion about the direction of the scale can complicate interpretation.

Treatment of Chronic Prurigo Nodularis

The main goals of CPN treatment are to rapidly reduce pruritus, interrupt the itch–scratch cycle, heal lesions, and restore skin integrity. Contemporary consensus documents and treat-to-target proposals define meaningful response as clear or almost clear skin (IGA-PN 0/1) together with a clinically relevant reduction in itch intensity, usually at least 4 points on the WI-NRS or peak pruritus numeric rating scale (PP-NRS) [15, 27, 80, 94]. Because CPN burden is multidimensional, treatment assessment should also include quality of life, sleep, and patient-perceived control (Table 4), and management should incorporate evaluation of associated disease, psychiatric burden, and underlying triggers [3, 95].

For practical purposes, the therapeutic evidence can be divided into three tiers. High-quality evidence is currently limited to the phase III trials for dupilumab (PRIME/PRIME2) and nemolizumab (OLYMPIA1/OLYMPIA2), in which combined improvement in itch and lesion clearance represents a key response criterion [96, 97]. Intermediate-level evidence comes from phase II trials of agents such as JAK inhibitors. In routine practice, gabapentinoids and other neural-directed agents are particularly relevant for patients with prominent neuropathic symptoms or sleep disturbance. However, the evidence base for gabapentin, pregabalin, antidepressants, phototherapy, cyclosporine, methotrexate, and thalidomide remains largely nonrandomized, and these options should be interpreted as empirically used therapies rather than as equivalently validated alternatives [98–101].

Dupilumab

Dupilumab is a recombinant human IgG4 monoclonal antibody that blocks IL-4 signaling via the type I receptor (IL-4Rα/γc) and both IL-4 and IL-13 signaling via the type II receptor (IL-4Rα/IL-13Rα) [16]. PRIME and PRIME2 were randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase III trials in patients with CPN inadequately controlled with topical therapy [96]. They enrolled 151 and 160 patients, respectively, randomized to placebo or dupilumab 300 mg every 2 weeks (600-mg loading dose) for 24 weeks plus 12 weeks of follow-up; low-to-moderate potency topical corticosteroids and topical calcineurin inhibitors were allowed [96].

Clinically meaningful itch improvement was significantly more frequent with dupilumab than with placebo (≥ 4-point WI-NRS reduction at week 24: 60% versus 18% in PRIME, and 58% versus 20% in PRIME2; both p < 0.0001), as was clear/almost clear skin (IGA-PN 0/1 at week 24: 48% versus 18% in PRIME, and 45% versus 16% in PRIME2; both p < 0.001) (Table 6). These benefits were consistent across baseline lesion severity and were similar in patients who were atopic and who were nonatopic. No patients discontinued dupilumab, and no new safety signals emerged; conjunctivitis rates were low and comparable to placebo in PRIME (2.7% in both arms) and only slightly higher with dupilumab in PRIME2 (3.9% versus 0%) [96]. Together with nemolizumab, dupilumab currently represents one of the two therapies with the strongest evidence base in CPN.

Nemolizumab

Nemolizumab is a humanized IgG2 monoclonal antibody that inhibits IL-31 signaling by selectively binding IL-31RA [17]. Its efficacy and safety were evaluated in the phase III randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled OLYMPIA1 (24 weeks) and OLYMPIA2 (16 weeks) trials [97, 102]. OLYMPIA1 enrolled 286 patients and OLYMPIA2 274, who were randomized to nemolizumab (60-mg loading dose followed by 30 or 60 mg according to baseline weight) or placebo every 4 weeks.

At week 16, a ≥ 4-point reduction in PP-NRS was achieved by 58.3% of patients receiving nemolizumab versus 16.7% receiving placebo in OLYMPIA1 and by 56.3% versus 20.98% in OLYMPIA2 (both p < 0.0001), with maintenance through week 24 in OLYMPIA1. IGA 0/1 rates were 26.3% versus 9.4% and 37.7% versus 11.0%, respectively (p < 0.0001) (Table 6). Most adverse events were mild or moderate; headache (6.6% versus 4.4%) and atopic dermatitis (5.5% versus 0%) were the most common events reported with nemolizumab [97, 102]. These phase III data place nemolizumab alongside dupilumab at the top of the current therapeutic evidence hierarchy in CPN.

JAK Inhibitors

Oral JAK1 inhibitors include abrocitinib [103], upadacitinib [104], and povorcitinib [105]. Among them, povorcitinib currently has the strongest CPN-specific dataset, based on a randomized phase II study. Patients were assigned to povorcitinib 15 mg (n = 36), 45 mg (n = 36), 75 mg (n = 37), or placebo (n = 37) for 16 weeks. A ≥ 4-point reduction in WI-NRS was achieved by 54.1% of patients receiving povorcitinib versus 8.1% receiving placebo, and 48.6% achieved IGA-PN 0/1 versus 5.4% with placebo. Benefit was maintained through the extension phase up to week 40 [105] (Table 6).

Despite this promising signal, JAK inhibitors should currently be contextualized as investigational or selected-use options in CPN rather than as evidence-equivalent alternatives to dupilumab or nemolizumab. The available CPN-specific datasets remain early phase or derived from small real-world cohorts, and none have been specifically designed to assess long-term safety in this population. Importantly, the safety profile of JAK inhibitors warrants careful consideration in CPN, particularly given that the patient population is predominantly older (mean age 56–64 years) and carries a high comorbidity burden, including diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease [106]. Accordingly, when JAK inhibitors are considered in CPN, an individualized risk–benefit assessment and close monitoring of safety parameters are essential.

Emerging Therapies

Several emerging biologics are being investigated for CPN. The anti-OSMRβ monoclonal antibody vixarelimab achieved rapid itch reduction and clear or almost clear skin in about one third of patients in a phase IIa prurigo nodularis study [107]. IL-4Rα–blocking antibodies such as rademikibart (CBP-201) have shown robust efficacy in phase II atopic dermatitis trials and are being developed for type 2-driven dermatoses [108, 109]. The OX40 antibody rocatinlimab and the anti-KIT antibody barzolvolimab are also in phase II development in type-2 inflammatory or mast cell–driven diseases, with promising antipruritic activity that may translate to CPN [110, 111]. At present, however, these agents remain investigational in CPN and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Real-World Evidence

Real-world evidence complements clinical trials by reflecting broader case-mix and routine-use safety [112], but in CPN, it remains dominated by uncontrolled small studies. Using the eligibility criteria summarized in Table 7, we identified 44 real-world studies: 9 case series, 19 prospective series, and 16 retrospective series. Most cohorts were small, heterogeneous, and at risk of selection and reporting bias, therefore, these data should be used mainly to support feasibility, tolerability, and external validity rather than to infer comparative efficacy.

Recent retrospective studies largely confirm the rapid antipruritic and lesion-level benefit of dupilumab observed in clinical trials [117–122]. Limited real-world experience with nemolizumab also suggests meaningful improvement, but cutaneous adverse events have been reported and require monitoring [123, 124]. Overall, the main value of real-world evidence at present is to confirm the effectiveness of the approved biologics in more diverse patients and to highlight safety and access issues that are less visible in randomized trials.

Current Guidelines for the Treatment of Chronic Prurigo Nodularis

The International Forum for the Study of Itch (IFSI) guideline (2020) and the European and US expert consensuses propose stepwise approaches to CPN diagnosis and management, but they are consensus-based, nonpersonalized, and largely pre-phase III with respect to dupilumab and nemolizumab; consequently, they provide limited patient-tailored therapeutic guidance [15, 80, 84, 101, 125].

These documents consistently begin with trigger control and topical anti-inflammatory therapy and then escalate to phototherapy or systemic agents, but they differ in how explicitly they separate neural-directed from immune-directed options. One consensus proposes an algorithm that starts with high-potency topical corticosteroids for flares, calcineurin inhibitors for maintenance, and capsaicin for localized disease, with phototherapy or systemic agents added when response is inadequate [125]. The Japanese consensus is broadly similar but recommends reevaluating the diagnosis and considering biopsy if all treatment lines fail [84].

Notably, most guidelines were developed before the availability of phase III biologic data and therefore rely heavily on off-label treatments supported by lower-quality evidence. This helps explain why frequently used therapies such as antihistamines continue to appear prominently despite the limited mechanistic rationale for histamine blockade in CPN [24, 80, 126].

The recent SWITCH algorithm moves closer to a treat-to-target model by linking therapeutic decisions to pruritus, lesion count, quality of life, and sleep [94]. Building on that concept, Fig. 2 provides a simplified pragmatic algorithm that prioritizes high-level evidence when available, reserves off-label or investigational agents for selected circumstances, and emphasizes repeated reassessment every 3–6 months.

Unmet Needs in Chronic Prurigo Nodularis Management

Despite recent progress, the management of CPN is currently facing urgent unmet needs, which highlight the necessity for clearer therapeutic strategies:

First, harmonized terminology, diagnostic criteria, and therapeutic guidance are still needed across regions; current recommendations remain largely consensus-based and leave substantial room for variability in practice [127].

Second, treatment sequencing and combination strategies remain poorly defined. Clinicians still lack evidence-based guidance on when to escalate, switch, taper, or combine systemic and topical therapies.

Third, the neurogenic and fibrotic components of CPN remain undertargeted. Although these pathways are biologically central, therapies specifically directed at neural sensitization or fibrosis are still limited [127].

These gaps underscore the complexity of CPN and the need for more practical and personalized guidance. Future research should prioritize biomarker-informed phenotyping, pragmatic comparative trials, long-term safety data, and digital endpoints to better define CPN subtypes and treatment response.
