Self-reported temporomandibular disorder pain in a multi-ethnic, Southeast Asian population: prevalence and health impact
Authors: Eunice Siew Pei Lua, Peter Svensson, Christopher C. Peck, Yu Fan Sim, Hee Hon Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of painful temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) has been well studied in various countries, but not in multi-ethnic Singapore where pain development, presentation, impact, and reporting may be influenced by cultural, genetic and environmental factors. This study is an initial step toward assessing the prevalence of self-reported TMD pain amongst adult Singaporeans and its impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL).MethodsAdult participants (≥ 21 years) were randomly recruited using a two-stage stratified sampling. Data was collected in 2017 using a multilingual, structured questionnaire, including TMD screening, pain drawing, Graded Chronic Pain Scale (GCPS) and HRQoL using EQ-5D-5L and EQ-VAS. Non-parametric tests (p < 0.05), logistic regression, and mediation analyses were used to evaluate associations between TMD status, sociodemographic factors, and HRQoL.Results1600 participants (39.8% invitees) completed the survey. Depending on questions used, self-reported TMD pain prevalence was 1.7% or 13.0%. TMD pain was significantly associated with HRQoL dimensions (EQ-5D), although weakly associated with poorer overall health status (EQ VAS). GCPS increase was also associated with a decrease in EQ VAS health status. 22.7% of participants indicated pain in the face, head or neck region from the pain drawings, of which 99.7% also indicated body pain. There were no statistically significant differences in TMD pain prevalence between ethnic groups.ConclusionsTMD pain prevalence appeared strongly influenced by question wording bias, supporting the need of validated questions in Singapore. This study highlights that the choice of screening tool affects prevalence estimates, which could have direct influence on patient care, perceived diseased burden and the allocation of healthcare resources. Background

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) is a collective term that encompasses a group of musculoskeletal conditions commonly associated with pain and/or functional limitations in the masticatory muscles, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and related structures [1]. Key symptoms include pain with jaw functions such as eating and talking, as well as TMJ noises, catching or locking of the jaw [2]. TMD pain is also frequently accompanied by comorbid conditions such as depression, stress, anxiety and other chronic pain conditions [3]. Its multifactorial etiology and the limited understanding of its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms make TMDs challenging to manage [1]. In this context, the biopsychosocial model plays a crucial role in the understanding, assessment, and management of TMD pain [4].

TMD pain can significantly impact an individual’s quality of life, influencing both oral health-related quality of life (OHQoL) [5], and the overall quality of life [6]. Furthermore, it is associated with increased healthcare utilization and costs, posing a substantial burden on both individuals and healthcare systems [7]. Orofacial pain and TMDs have been assigned higher disability weights within the Global Burden of Disease framework compared to symptomatic caries and periodontitis [8]. This finding underscores the substantial public health burden posed by these conditions and reinforces the need for their prioritisation in public health policy.

TMD pain is the most common cause of non-odontogenic orofacial pain [9]. However, most prevalence data are drawn from high-income Western countries. Despite Singapore’s status as a high-income country, existing studies on TMD prevalence and distribution are few and dated [10, 11]. In addition, ethnic, cultural and genetic influences may influence the pain experience, limiting the generalizability of international findings to the multi-ethnic and multi-cultural Singapore context.

The impact of ethnicity and culture on the pain experience has long been established [12–15]. Cultural considerations in the assessment and management of orofacial pain have also been increasingly recognised in recent years. This includes emphasis on cross-cultural adaptations of existing diagnostic tools [16] and incorporation of culture in clinical implementation of the biopsychosocial model [17]. Furthermore, the definitions and distinctions among ethnicity, culture and race have been the subject of extensive discussion, as their differentiation is considered essential in pain research grounded in the biopsychosocial framework [18]. Edwards [18] proposes operational definitions, where race refers to groups delineated primarily by shared ancestry and physical traits, while ethnicity encompasses a broader array of characteristics – including behaviour, culture, history, experience, ancestry, and beliefs – spanning biological, social, psychological, cultural and political dimensions.

The aim of the study was to assess the prevalence of self-reported TMD pain in multi-ethnic Singapore and its impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL).

Methods

Study setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted as part of a larger project on overlapping pain conditions in Singapore. The study employed a two-stage disproportionate stratified sampling method, stratified by housing type, because housing type in Singapore is an established proxy for socioeconomic status (SES) and reflects the country’s unique residential landscape [19]. In addition, public housing (managed by the Housing & Development Board, HDB) is subject to Singapore’s Ethnic Integration Policy, which aims to preserve the nation’s multi-cultural identity and promote racial integration. Therefore, sampling was stratified by housing type to ensure that the sample reflected the underlying ethnic distribution of the population.

Both public housing and private landed properties were included in the sampling frame. Addresses from private apartments and condominiums could not be included because security controls and restricted entry posed significant logistic challenges for access to these premises. To ensure adequate representation of individuals residing in private housing overall, private landed properties were thus oversampled and used as a proxy for the broader private-housing group. Household addresses were proportionately selected according to the Urban Redevelopment Authority (URA) planning regions: Central, East, North, North-East, and West of Singapore.

In the first stage, a random stratified sample of 4500 household addresses was procured from the Singapore Department of Statistics, which maintains a sampling frame of all residential addresses nationwide for the selection of samples for household surveys. These households were invited to participate by mail. Of these, 241 addresses were found to be invalid. In the second stage, eligible individuals from each consenting household were randomly selected and invited to participate in the survey. A maximum of three contact attempts were made to reach the selected household.

Eligible participants were Singapore citizens and permanent residents, aged 21 years old or older. Participants were drawn from all major ethnic groups in Singapore (Chinese, Malay, Indian, and Others), and the ethnic composition of the sample broadly reflected that of the general population. Individuals were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) previous history of trauma to the head and neck region, (ii) headaches associated with medication, alcohol or substance abuse, (iii) pregnancy, (iv) diagnosed medical conditions including cerebrovascular disease, epilepsy or other seizure disorders, movement disorders, hypothyroidism, connective tissue disease, acute immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), regional or metastatic tumours, and (v) conditions that may hamper one’s mental and cognitive capacity to respond to the interview.

Interviewer-administered questionnaire

The survey was conducted via door-to-door personal interviews by a team of trained female interviewers. The interviewers were contacted regularly throughout the data collection process to ensure consistency in the way the interviewers administered the questionnaires and collected data. Data collection utilised both combination of computer-assisted personal interview and pen-and-paper interview methods. The questionnaire was made available in four languages (English, Malay, Chinese and Tamil) to ensure representation across the multi-ethnic Singapore population. Demographic information gathered from the questionnaire included the participants’ sex, age, ethnicity, highest educational attainment, housing type, and employment status (Refer to Additional file 1 for the variables gathered through the interview).

The questionnaire included several standardised pain-related components, derived from the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) protocol [20], including the TMD Pain Screener [21] that screens for pain and dysfunction, the pain drawing that localises the pain, and the Graded Chronic Pain Scale (GCPS) [22] that measures pain intensity and pain-related disability. The Generalised Anxiety Disorder 2-item (GAD-2) was used as an initial screening tool for anxiety disorders [23]. HRQoL was measured using the EQ-5D-5L instrument [24], which comprised quality of life descriptors (EQ-5D) in various domains, and a visual analog scale from 0 to 100 (EQ VAS) to measure a person’s own perception of their overall health. Additionally, a post hoc analysis identified specific items within the survey as proxy questions for the first two questions of the 3Q/TMD [25], that screen for TMD pain (Refer to Additional file 1). The computer-assisted personal interview method was used to capture questionnaire responses, and a pen-and-paper interview method was employed to document pain drawings.

The TMD Pain Screener is a self-report tool designed to identify individuals with TMD pain and/or dysfunction, who may require further clinical examination, diagnosis and management [21]. Scoring was conducted in accordance with the protocol established by Gonzalez et al. (2011), with a score of 3 or higher indicating a positive result for TMD pain. In contrast, the 3Q/TMD [25] consists of three screening questions encompassing a broader spectrum of TMD symptoms; the first two questions assess TMD pain, while the third addresses functional limitations such as jaw catching and locking. In the present study, four TMD pain-related questions from the survey were identified by three experienced pain clinicians retrospectively as proxy questions (2Q/P) for the 3Q/TMD questions screening for TMD pain. Consistent with the original 3Q/TMD protocol, a positive response to any of the 2Q/P questions was interpreted as indicative of TMD pain. The pain drawing was also included in the survey, as it serves as a useful screening tool that contributes to a comprehensive biopsychosocial assessment of pain [4]. The extent and distribution of marked pain areas have also been associated with greater psychosocial burden, providing valuable information for the evaluation and management of TMD pain [26].

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (NHG DSRB Reference Number 2016/00111). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and study variables. Fisher’s exact tests were first used to examine bivariate associations between TMD pain and sociodemographic variables, EQ-5D domains, and chronic pain grade. Age, sex, ethnicity and SES were retained for multivariable analyses regardless of bivariate significance to account for potential confounding. SES was operationalised using housing type and was included as a proxy indicator of SES in subsequent multivariable analyses [20]. Highest educational attainment and employment status were not included in regression models to avoid redundancy with SES. Binary logistic regression analyses were performed with TMD pain as the dependent variable. Age, sex, ethnicity and SES were entered simultaneously as covariates in all models. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and the amount of explained variance was assessed using Nagelkerke’s R2. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for each predictor. Prior to conducting mediation analyses, logistic regression was used to examine whether anxiety was predicted by sociodemographic variables. This ensured that potential confounding associations between the mediator and covariates were identified. For subsequent mediation analyses, anxiety was added as an additional predictor to the logistic regression model to test its role as a mediator between sociodemographic factors and the outcome variables. All statistical analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29.0.2.0).

Results

The survey was conducted between May and December 2017. Of the 4500 households invited by mail, 36.5% were unreachable after three contact attempts, 20.1% declined participation, 5.4% had invalid addresses, 2.2% were excluded based on eligibility criteria, and 0.2% were excluded due to language barriers. A total of 1600 eligible individuals successfully completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 35.6%.

Participant demographics

Among the 1600 adult respondents, the mean age was 47.0 years, and 54.6% were female, while 45.4% were male. The majority were Chinese (75.4%), had attained at least a diploma, professional or university qualification (50.6%) and were employed (67.3%). The study population was broadly similar to the national census distribution on sex and ethnicity [27]. Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Self-reported TMD pain

TMD pain, as assessed by the TMD Pain Screener [21], was reported by 1.7% (95% CI: 1.06%–2.32%) of the participants (n = 27). No demographic factors were significantly associated with this group. To further explore the low prevalence of TMD pain, a post hoc analysis of 2Q/P was conducted. Based on these questions, the prevalence of self-reported TMD pain was 13.0% (n = 208, 95% CI: 11.4%–14.7%). This TMD pain was significantly associated with demographic factors such as age (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.005, φc = 0.098), and housing type (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001, φc = 0.095), but not sex, ethnicity, education level and occupational status. Using Cohen’s kappa (κ), there was no agreement between the TMD Pain Screener and proxy-3Q/TMD sets of questions (κ = 0.09).

In subsequent analyses, participants were grouped into two cohorts based on their responses to screening questions. Cohort A was defined using the TMD Pain Screener (PS), with participants categorised as PS + for positive responses and PS- for negative responses. Cohort B was defined using the 2Q/P, with participants categorised as 2Q/P + for positive responses and 2Q/P- for negative responses.

Predictors of TMD pain – regression analyses

A binary logistic regression model was conducted to examine whether age, sex, ethnicity and SES predicted TMD pain (PS+). The model was not statistically significant (χ²=8.77, df = 10, p = 0.554), indicating that the set of predictors did not improve prediction of TMD pain relative to a model with no predictors. As no significant associations were found, subsequent mediation analyses were not conducted for this outcome.

In contrast, a logistic regression predicting TMD pain (2Q/P+) from age, sex, ethnicity and SES was statistically significant (χ²=32.370, df = 10, p < 0.001). The explained variance was low (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.037), although the Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated adequate model fit (χ²=7.412, df = 8, p = 0.493). Among the predictors, age (Wald χ²=13.91, df = 4, p = 0.008) and SES (Wald χ²=13.32, df = 2, p = 0.001) emerged as significant, whereas sex and ethnicity were not significant.

Preliminary logistic regression analysis showed that anxiety was significantly associated with age (Wald χ²=9.84, df = 4, p = 0.043), but not with sex, ethnicity or SES. Hence, anxiety was included in the regression model with age, sex, ethnicity and SES to test whether anxiety mediated the association between these demographic variables and TMD pain (2Q/P+). The extended model remained significant (χ²=51.531, df = 11, p < 0.001), with a modest improvement in variance (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.059). While the model demonstrated adequate fit according to the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (χ²=7.364, df = 8, p = 0.498), classification performance indicated limited predictive utility. The high overall accuracy (87.1%) of the model was largely driven by very high sensitivity (99.7%), but specificity was extremely low (2.4%), suggesting that the model failed to correctly identify TMD pain (2Q/P+). Nevertheless, anxiety was a significant predictor of TMD pain (2Q/P+) (OR = 4.62, 95% CI [2.42, 8.82], p < 0.001). The odds ratios for age and SES were slightly attenuated but remained significant, suggesting that anxiety partially mediated, but did not fully explain, the association of age and SES with TMD pain (2Q/P+) as presented in Table 4.

Discussion

The results of this cross-sectional survey in Singapore demonstrated that, when using one screening tool, the prevalence of self-reported TMD pain was remarkably low at 1.7%, with no significant associations with demographic characteristics. However, a post-hoc analysis using another screening tool, with proxy questions for TMD pain, revealed a substantially higher prevalence of 13.0%, with statistically significant associations with age and housing type.

Globally, the prevalence of TMD has been reported to be approximately 34%, with suggestions that geographical, cultural and methodological factors play a significant role in TMD prevalence [28]. Regional data from Southeast Asia and Asia remain limited. Most available studies have been conducted in more homogenous populations with no information on ethnic distribution, which limits direct comparison with Singapore’s multi-ethnic context [29–31]. A recent study in Singapore among military personnel reported a self-reported TMD pain symptom prevalence of 16.7% [32], but its generalisability is restricted by the specific nature of the study population and the absence of information on ethnicity. In addition, a study conducted in Hong Kong on symptoms associated with TMDs reported a prevalence of 33% [31]. These disparities highlight the complex interplay of sociocultural factors (including cultural norms and ethnicity) and genetic predispositions in modulating pain perception and reporting. Therefore, while comparisons are informative, they should be made with caution, particularly in light of methodological heterogeneity, including differing survey instruments and case definitions. It is important to note that the present study specifically assessed painful TMDs, while most prevalence studies in the literature do not distinguish between painful and non-painful TMDs [33]. This could account for the substantial discrepancies in reported prevalence rates.

The relatively low TMD pain prevalence observed in the present study may be accounted for, at least in part, to the applied exclusion criteria. Individuals with a prior history of trauma to the head and neck region were excluded. This is a relevant consideration, as traumatic injuries, such as trauma related to wisdom tooth extraction, significant external trauma, motor vehicle accidents [34] and whiplash [34, 35] are well-documented risk factors for the development of TMDs, including painful TMDs. In addition, individuals were also excluded if they had headaches attributed to medication overuse, alcohol or substance abuse. These types of headaches frequently co-occur with or share overlapping signs and symptoms with TMD-related pain [36, 37]. Therefore, their exclusion may have further contributed to the lower prevalence of TMD pain in the present sample.

In contrast to a previous local study reporting associations between general pain prevalence and demographic factors such as sex, age, ethnicity and household income [3], our initial findings using the TMD Pain Screener did not reveal any significant associations. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the small number of TMD-positive participants identified by the screener (n = 27). Notably, the use of proxy questions revealed more pronounced associations, particularly with age and indicators of SES, but not ethnicity. The overall age pattern observed in this study as demonstrated by the higher prevalence of painful TMDs in younger and middle-aged adults was also reported in other studies [38, 39]. Lower SES has also been associated with higher prevalence of painful TMDs – individuals of lower SES may face various psychosocial and environmental stressors related to income and occupation, that could increase their risk for developing painful TMDs [40, 41].

While the proxy questions identified more individuals with self-reported TMD pain, it is interesting to note that most of the 2Q/P + participants (80.3%) did not have a chronic pain grade as measured by the GCPS despite reporting TMD pain, and only 2.9% experienced moderate to severe limitations (Table 3). In contrast, 29.6% of PS + participants did not have a chronic pain grade, and 22.2% had moderate to severe limitations (Table 3). This could be attributed to different recall periods of the TMD Pain Screener, proxy questions to the 3Q/TMD and GCPS. Nonetheless, this suggests that although TMD pain may be more common with the proxy questions, severe or functionally limiting TMD pain remains less prevalent. This was also reflected in the results from the GCPS, where 97.1% of 2Q/P + participants and 77.8% of PS + participants did not report any functional limitations. This further emphasises the importance of understanding pain-related disabilities and limitations, along with the complexities of instrument selection and contexts in which they are applied. Overall, the results of this study corroborate with previous literature where GCPS was significantly associated with poorer self-rated of health on the EQ VAS [42].

The marked disparity in TMD pain prevalence between the two sets of screening questions likely reflects deeper methodological and contextual differences in how different instruments capture symptomatology. Beyond differences in case definitions, the wording structure and cognitive demands of each instrument could influence respondent interpretation, which could influence the validity and applicability of such screeners in the Singapore context. The TMD Pain Screener uses a 30-day recall period and requires multiple positive responses to items relating to both pain intensity and functional limitation, which effectively filters for more persistent of clinically significant symptoms – an approach well-suited to the specialist clinic context that it was developed for [21], but potentially underestimating cases in the community. In contrast, the 3Q/TMD, developed for population-level screening [25], adopts broader symptom definitions by focusing on the presence and frequency of pain, and a low response threshold as it identifies cases with a single positive response. The proxy questions diverge further by assessing only pain presence, without specifying a recall period and frequency, allowing respondents substantial interpretative latitude that likely inflates prevalence by capturing transient symptoms. These methodological differences may be amplified in Singapore’s multicultural population, where linguistic nuances, health literacy differences, and cultural norms surrounding pain expression can alter how respondents interpret the questions. These factors could explain the variation in prevalence rates observed across instruments and highlight the need for improved and culturally adapted and context-specific validation to ensure accurate screening tools.

Although both the TMD Pain Screener and the pain drawing are validated self-report Axis 1 instruments from the DC/TMD protocol, it is notable that only 1.7% of participants reported TMD pain, whereas 22.7% reported pain on the drawings of the face, head or neck regions. Unlike the TMD Pain Screener, the pain drawing used in this survey did not specify a recall period and lacked anatomical specificity, which makes it more sensitive to intermittent or transient symptoms. This raises the possibility that the prevalence derived from the pain drawings may be inflated by head and neck pain unrelated to, or overlapping with TMD pain.

Nevertheless, the inclusion of the pain drawing in this study provided added value by reflecting the broader distribution and burden of pain among participants. In this study, almost a quarter of the population reported pain in the face, head or neck region, with a high co-occurrence (99.7%) of body pain. These findings support existing literature on the nature of TMD pain and its association with other comorbid pain conditions [43]. The strong overlap reinforces the relevance of a biopsychosocial approach in the assessment and management of TMD. This suggests that pain drawing may provide insight to clinically meaningful pain experiences that may not be fully captured by diagnostic screening tools, further supporting its utility in the understanding of pain.

Strengths and limitations of study

A key limitation of this study is the absence of a formal sample size calculation prior to data collection. Although the final sample of 1600 participants provided a robust dataset, the lack of an a priori justification for sample size may limit the interpretability of the precision of our estimates. While the modest response rate (39.8%) raises concerns for non-response bias, which could affect the generalizability of the findings, this is consistent with contemporary population-based surveys and reflects challenges common to survey research conducted during this period, including difficulties contacting households [44].

As non-respondents are more likely to be younger, less health-engaged, and from more socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, non-response bias remains an important limitation [45]. If such patterns applied to the present study, the likely direction of bias would be an underestimation, as younger age and lower socioeconomic status has been associated with higher prevalence and greater severity of painful TMDs [41]. The magnitude of this potential bias cannot be reliably estimated with the available data. Although comparisons were made between respondent distributions and national census data for sex and ethnicity, it was not feasible to make meaningful direct comparisons for other variables such as age, education, household type and employment due to differences in category definitions. The sample size is also notably smaller than some local population-based surveys on general pain [46, 47].

Despite these limitations, a key strength of this study is the use of a stratified sampling design, which helps reduce selection bias. Furthermore, the sample size compares favourably with the most recent National Adult Oral Health Survey conducted in 2019, which included 1196 participants [48]. A key strength of the present study is the use of stratified sampling to lower the bias. It is also broadly consistent with sample sizes used in international studies on orofacial pain and TMD, including those by McMillan and colleagues in Hong Kong [29, 49].

An additional consideration is the time gap between the original data collection in 2017 and the present day. While this temporal distance may limit direct applicability of the findings to current population patterns, Singapore’s population characteristics have remained relatively stable, and major determinants of painful TMDs such as healthcare access and general lifestyle exposures are not known to have undergone large shifts [27, 50]. As such, meaningful population-level changes in the prevalence of painful TMDs are unlikely. Nonetheless, it should be acknowledged that some changes over time, particularly those related to psychosocial stress, could influence symptom prevalence, and that future surveillance using updated data would be valuable.

Furthermore, as a cross-sectional survey, this study may not have fully captured the fluctuating and intermittent nature of TMD. In addition, the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases related to recall, interpretation and subjectivity, particularly in the absence of clinical validation. Nevertheless, the findings of this study align with other TMD studies in highlighting the negative impact that TMD pain has on the individual, with potential implications at the societal level [8].

This study has provided preliminary insights to the prevalence of self-reported TMD pain and its health impacts amongst Singapore’s multi-ethnic adult population. Given the limited understanding of TMD pain in Singapore, population-based studies are essential to enhance knowledge of its burden within the Global Burden of orofacial pain project, and to inform evidence-based healthcare planning. Raising awareness of TMD pain and its impact may support better resource allocation and enhance management strategies for chronic orofacial pain in Singapore’s diverse population.

Conclusions

The prevalence of self-reported TMD pain in Singapore ranged from 1.7% to 13.0%, depending on the screening questions used. With these initial insights into TMD pain in a multi-ethnic population, future research should aim to validate TMD screening tools within the cultural context of Singapore, which offers a unique opportunity to further explore the ethnic and socio-cultural dimensions of pain perception and reporting. Although no consistent demographic predictors of TMD pain were identified, the findings reinforced that TMD pain negatively affects HRQoL, including daily activities, in a culturally and ethnically diverse Southeast Asian population.
