High-fidelity genome and prime editing enabled by the AI-designed openCRISPR-1
Authors: Hye-Yeon Hwang, Hwalin Yi, Yuju Gwon, Eunju Jeon, Daesik Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRNA-guided nucleases such as CRISPR-Cas9 systems have revolutionized genome engineering by enabling programmable DNA modifications. Although structure-guided and evolution-derived high-fidelity Cas9 variants improve target specificity, they often compromise on-target activity or constrain guide RNA (gRNA) design.MethodsWe performed head-to-head comparisons of OpenCRISPR-1 and Cas9 in human cells using amplicon sequencing, multiplex Digenome-seq, and off-target validation by targeted sequencing. Editing activity was assessed across 28 endogenous loci in HEK293T cells and further evaluated in human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and MRC-5 fibroblasts. To test clinically relevant delivery, Cas9 and OpenCRISPR-1 ribonucleoproteins were delivered using engineered virus-like particles (eVLPs). We also generated OpenCRISPR-based prime editors, OpenCRISPR-PE2 and OpenCRISPR-PE7, and compared them with PE2max and PE7 using pegRNAs and engineered epegRNAs.ResultsHere, we show that OpenCRISPR-1, an AI-designed, Cas9-like nuclease, retains Cas9-level editing efficiency across multiple genomic loci while significantly reducing off-target mutations. Using multiplex Digenome-seq and targeted deep sequencing, OpenCRISPR-1 exhibits up to a 553-fold reduction in off-target mutations compared to Cas9 and achieves off-target indices that match or surpass those of high-fidelity Cas9 variants. OpenCRISPR-1 also sustains robust editing across diverse gRNA formats (GX19, gX19, and gX20), highlighting its enhanced versatility. Furthermore, converting OpenCRISPR-1 into a prime editor yields comparable editing efficiencies while lowering the relative specificity ratio by up to 97%.ConclusionsThese findings establish generative AI-guided protein design as a powerful strategy to overcome the specificity-efficiency trade-off, expanding the genome editing toolkit for both research and therapeutic use, and ushering in a new era of rational protein design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-026-01682-2.

Background

The discovery of CRISPR-Cas systems as adaptive immune mechanisms in prokaryotes has sparked a revolution in genome engineering [1–5]. Cas9, directed by a programmable guide RNA (gRNA), introduces targeted double-strand breaks in DNA at sites adjacent to a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM), enabling precise and scalable genetic modification [1–5]. Its relatively high editing efficiency has facilitated widespread adoption across diverse fields, including basic research, biotechnology, and clinical applications [6–12]. Despite its broad utility, Cas9 exhibits off-target activity, often tolerating mismatches between the gRNA and DNA target, as well as recognizing noncanonical PAMs [13, 14]. These off-target effects remain a significant hurdle in therapeutic contexts, where even low-frequency unintended edits can lead to oncogenic transformation or other harmful outcomes. To address this challenge, numerous engineered Cas9 variants have been developed to enhance specificity, including eCas9 [15], Cas9-HF1 [16], and HypaCas9 [17], which utilize structure-guided mutations to reduce nonspecific interactions between Cas9 and the target DNA. Complementary efforts using directed evolution have produced variants such as EvoCas9 [18] and Sniper-Cas9 [19], further improving specificity through iterative selection in cellular environments. However, these high-fidelity nucleases often compromise on‐target activity in pursuit of enhanced specificity, highlighting the need for genome editors that maintain robust editing efficiency without elevating off-target risks [20].

Recent advances in protein language models and generative AI have opened promising new avenues for designing functional proteins beyond the constraints of natural sequences [21]. Leveraging large-scale modeling of CRISPR-Cas sequences, Ruffolo and colleagues developed OpenCRISPR-1, a de novo Cas9-like nuclease generated entirely through AI-guided design [22]. Although it diverges from Cas9 by > 400 amino acids, OpenCRISPR-1 retains comparable genome editing efficiency while exhibiting a markedly reduced off-target profile in human cells [22]. Preliminary analyses suggest that OpenCRISPR-1 features enhanced PAM selectivity and maintains similar on-target activity, attributes that may collectively contribute to its improved specificity [22]. However, conflicting evaluations of OpenCRISPR-1’s performance have recently emerged, raising key questions about its generalizability and reliability [22, 23]. While the original study by Ruffolo and colleagues reported that OpenCRISPR-1 achieves on-target editing efficiencies comparable to Cas9 with markedly reduced off-target activity [22], a subsequent investigation by Tian and colleagues observed significantly reduced on-target activity and increased off-target effects relative to Cas9 [23]. These discrepancies highlight the need for direct, head-to-head comparisons of AI-designed genome editors under standardized experimental conditions to clarify conflicting findings and establish their true functional capabilities.

In this study, we present a comprehensive, side-by-side evaluation of Cas9 and OpenCRISPR-1 with respect to genome-wide off-target activity in human cells. Employing a combination of Digenome-seq and targeted amplicon sequencing, we systematically assess off-target effects across a panel of endogenous genomic loci. Our results reveal that OpenCRISPR-1 not only maintains on-target efficacy comparable to Cas9 but also achieves notable improvements in editing specificity. Additionally, we expand the platform by engineering OpenCRISPR-PEs, a prime editing system derived from OpenCRISPR-1, and observe enhanced specificity compared to PEs, underscoring OpenCRISPR-1’s promise for high-fidelity therapeutic applications.

Methods

Plasmid construction

The plasmid OpenCRISPR-1 (Addgene plasmid #221565) was a gift from Ali Madani. Plasmids pCMV-PEmax (#174820), pU6-pegRNA-GG-acceptor (#132777), pU6-tevopreq1-GG-acceptor (#174038), pU6-tmpknot-GG-acceptor (#174038), pCMV-MMLVgag-3xNES-Cas9 (#181752) and v5 Cas9-eVLP (#228468) were gifts from David Liu. pCMV-PE7 was a gift from Brittany Adamson (# 214812). pMD2.G was a gift from Didier Trono (#12259). pBS-CMV-gagpol (# 35614) was a gift from Patrick Salmon. The OpenCRISPR-PE2 expression construct was generated by introducing an H850A point mutation into the OpenCRISPR-1 backbone via Q5 site-directed mutagenesis (New England Biolabs), followed by insertion of an engineered reverse transcriptase domain using Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs). pegRNA and epegRNA expression plasmids were assembled via Golden Gate cloning into the respective acceptor plasmids [24, 25]. The OpenCRISPR-PE7 expression construct was generated from the PE7 backbone by replacing the SpCas9 nickase with the OpenCRISPR-1 nickase fragment amplified from the OpenCRISPR-PE2 plasmid using Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs). The V5 OpenCRISPR-1-eVLP construct was generated from the V5 Cas9-eVLP plasmid by replacing the Cas9 coding sequence with OpenCRISPR-1 using Gibson Assembly. For bacterial expression, the OpenCRISPR-1 coding sequence was subcloned into pET-28a using Gibson Assembly to generate pET-OpenCRISPR-1. All transfection-grade plasmids were purified using the NucleoBond Xtra Midiprep Kit (Macherey-Nagel).

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells (ATCC, CRL-11268) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Welgene) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Welgene) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Welgene) at 37 °C. MRC-5 (ATCC, CCL-171) were maintained in MEM, with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C. Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (STEMCELL Technologies, SCTi003-A) were maintained on plates coated with Matrigel Growth Factor Reduced Basement Membrane Matrix (Corning) in mTeSR1 Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For transfection, 1.5 × 105 HEK293T cells were seeded per well in a 24-well plate. Human iPSCs were nucleofected at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per reaction using the P3 Cell Line Nucleofector X Kit (Lonza), and MRC-5 fibroblasts at 1 × 10⁵ cells per reaction using the SE Cell Line Nucleofector Kit on a 4D-Nucleofector system. Cells were co-transfected with 1,500 ng of a plasmid encoding Cas9, OpenCRISPR-1, PE2max, OpenCRISPR-PE2, PE7, or OpenCRISPR-PE7 along with 500 ng of a gRNA or pegRNA expression plasmid, using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA was extracted 72 h post-transfection using the DNeasy Tissue Kit (Qiagen).

Expression and purification of the OpenCRISPR-1 protein

BL21(DE3) cells were transformed with pET-OpenCRISPR-1 and cultured at 37 °C in LB medium containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, followed by incubation for 16 h at 16 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 250 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM DTT, 25 mM imidazole, 1 mM PMSF, 5% glycerol, 1 mg/mL lysozyme), and lysed by sonication. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation and incubated with Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen) for 12–16 h at 4 °C. Resin-bound protein was loaded onto a column, washed with buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 25 mM imidazole, 1 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM DTT), and eluted using a linear imidazole gradient (25–500 mM) in buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 5% glycerol). For further purification, pooled fractions were applied to a Heparin Sepharose column (Cytiva), washed with low-salt buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100), and eluted using a 600 mM to 2 M NaCl gradient in buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 0.1 mM DTT, 5% glycerol). The final protein product was concentrated using Amicon Ultra filters (Millipore), buffer-exchanged into storage buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 250 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 50% glycerol), and stored at − 80 °C.

Production of Cas9- eVLP and OpenCRISPR-1- eVLP

For production of Cas9-eVLPs and OpenCRISPR-1-eVLPs, 1 × 10⁷ Lenti-X 293T cells (Clontech) were seeded in 150-mm dishes one day before transfection. Cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding VSV-G (800 ng), gag-pol (6,750 ng), either gag-Cas9 (2,250 ng) or gag-OpenCRISPR-1, and an sgRNA expression plasmid (8,800 ng) using 120 µg polyethylenimine (Polysciences) per dish. At 72 h after transfection, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min to remove cellular debris, then passed through a 0.45-µm cellulose acetate filter (Sartorius). PEG-it Virus Precipitation Solution (System Biosciences) was added to the filtrate and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The precipitated particles were collected by centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the pellets were resuspended in 1× HIV-safe Manager (Lugen SCI) and stored at − 80 °C until use.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail and incubated on ice for 30 min. Lysates were subsequently clarified by centrifugation, and protein concentrations were determined using a Bradford assay. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with SDS sample buffer and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min. Samples were separated by SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBS-T for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against HA (anti-HA tag; Abcam, ab18181) and β-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-47778). After washing with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Sigma) for 1 h at room temperature and signals were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system.

Digenome-seq

For in vitro cleavage, ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) were assembled by pre-incubating 100 nM OpenCRISPR-1 with a pool of ten gRNAs (30 nM each) at room temperature for 10 min. The RNP mixture was then added to 10 µg of genomic DNA in 1 mL of reaction buffer (100 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris‐HCl pH 7.9, 10 mM MgCl2, 100 µg/mL BSA) and incubated at 37 °C for 8 h. Following digestion, DNA was purified using the DNeasy Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and treated with RNase A (50 µg/mL) to eliminate residual gRNA. Libraries were prepared for whole-genome sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6,000 platform at 30–40× coverage (Macrogen). Sequencing reads were aligned using the Isaac aligner, and genome-wide cleavage sites were identified with the Digenome toolkit (https://github.com/chizksh/digenome-toolkit2) [26, 27].

Targeted deep sequencing

Targeted genomic regions were PCR-amplified using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) and locus-specific primers (Additional file 1: Table S1 and Additional file 2: Table S2). Amplicons were barcoded with Illumina TruSeq HT dual-index adapters, purified, and sequenced on the Illumina iSeq 100 platform (150 bp paired-end). Each target locus achieved a minimum sequencing depth of 3,000 reads.

Statistical analysis

All results are reported as mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.1.1, with individual data points shown as dots. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

OpenCRISPR-1 exhibits efficient on-target editing and improved genome-wide specificity compared to Cas9 in human cells

To assess the genome editing efficiency of the AI-designed OpenCRISPR-1, we conducted side-by-side comparisons with canonical Cas9 across 28 endogenous target sites in HEK293T cells. Cells were transfected with plasmid DNA encoding either the Cas9 or OpenCRISPR-1 nuclease along with the corresponding gRNAs. PCR amplicon-based deep sequencing revealed that OpenCRISPR-1 achieved slightly higher average mutation frequencies (69.73% ± 2.89%) compared to Cas9 (68.34% ± 3.49%), demonstrating comparable on-target editing activity across diverse loci (Fig. 1A and Additional file 3: Fig. S1A). Consistently, cumulative distribution function (CDF) analyses of editing efficiencies at the 28 sites revealed highly similar mutation frequency distributions for OpenCRISPR-1 and Cas9 (Fig. 1B). Immunoblot analysis demonstrated comparable protein levels of OpenCRISPR-1 and Cas9, indicating that the observed differences in editing activity are unlikely to be attributable to altered expression efficiency but rather to intrinsic properties of OpenCRISPR-1 and Cas9 (Additional file 3: Fig. S1B).

We next compared Cas9 and OpenCRISPR-1 in human iPSCs. At the VEGFA1 locus, OpenCRISPR-1 showed higher editing efficiency than Cas9, whereas both nucleases displayed similar activity at EMX1 (Additional file 3: Fig. S1C). We also assessed editing in MRC-5 fibroblasts, a non-transformed human lung-derived cell line, where OpenCRISPR-1 exhibited somewhat reduced activity relative to Cas9 across the loci tested (Additional file 3: Fig. S1D). Together, these findings demonstrate that OpenCRISPR-1 can support efficient editing in human iPSCs, while also highlighting the importance of cellular context when comparing nuclease activities.

We next evaluated the genome-wide off-target profiles of both nucleases using multiplex Digenome-seq, a high-throughput method that enables simultaneous profiling of off-target cleavage across multiple gRNA targets [26]. In vitro digestion was carried out using recombinant Cas9 or OpenCRISPR-1 proteins complexed with each of the ten gRNAs, followed by whole-genome sequencing of the digested genomic DNA (Fig. 1C). Digenome-seq analysis revealed that Cas9 generated a significantly higher number of potential off-target sites (88.5 ± 33.2) than OpenCRISPR-1 (25.6 ± 13.3), underscoring the improved specificity of the OpenCRISPR-1 nuclease (Fig. 1D, E and Additional file 4: Table S3). Notably, Cas9 cleaved not only canonical NGG PAMs (81.36% ± 28.37%) but also a considerable fraction of noncanonical NGH (H = A, C, or T) PAMs (6.10% ± 2.64%), NHG PAMs (11.75% ± 4.66%), and NHH PAMs (0.79% ± 0.77%) (Fig. 1E). In contrast, OpenCRISPR-1 showed a strong preference for NGG PAMs (87.89% ± 11.50%) with limited activity at noncanonical PAMs (Fig. 1E). These findings suggest that OpenCRISPR-1 exhibits enhanced PAM selectivity and superior specificity compared to Cas9.

To validate the genome-wide off-target sites predicted by multiplex Digenome-seq, we conducted targeted amplicon deep sequencing at candidate loci across multiple gRNA targets. All predicted off-target sites associated with VEGFA1, HEK1, HEK2, HEK3, FANCF, RNF2, HBB, and EMX1 were PCR-amplified and sequenced in both Cas9- and OpenCRISPR-1-treated cells. Additionally, 14 of 108 candidate sites for VEGFA2 and 10 of 94 for HEK4 were selected for validation in both groups. Mutation frequency analysis at these validated off-target sites revealed a substantially lower off-target activity for OpenCRISPR-1 compared to Cas9 (Fig. 2A). For instance, at OT1 of HEK2 which harbors two mismatches relative to the on-target site Cas9 induced an indel frequency of 6.11% ± 0.18%, whereas OpenCRISPR-1 showed a substantially lower indel frequency of 0.01% ± 0.02%, representing a 553-fold improvement in specificity (Fig. 2A). Notably, OpenCRISPR-1 maintained higher on-target activity at HEK2 compared to Cas9, emphasizing its favorable balance between editing efficiency and precision.

We also validated off-target sites associated with noncanonical PAMs, such as OT2 of VEGFA2 and OT3 of EMX1, which contained single and double mismatches, respectively, adjacent to non-NGG PAM motifs. At these noncanonical PAM sites, OpenCRISPR-1 exhibited significantly reduced editing activity relative to Cas9, with undetectable indel frequencies at OT2 of VEGFA2 (Cas9: 2.38% ± 0.16%) and markedly reduced activity at OT3 of EMX1 (OpenCRISPR-1: 1.90% ± 0.19%; Cas9: 51.58% ± 0.71%) (Fig. 2A). These results confirm that OpenCRISPR-1 not only improves target specificity at canonical NGG PAM sites but also effectively reduces off-target mutations at noncanonical PAMs, contributing to its enhanced overall genome editing fidelity.

To further minimize off-target editing by Cas9 and OpenCRISPR-1, we next evaluated delivery of ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes using engineered virus-like particles (eVLPs) [28, 29]. Compared with plasmid DNA transfection, eVLP-mediated delivery of Cas9 and OpenCRISPR-1 RNPs resulted in higher on-target editing at VEGFA1 and EMX1. Moreover, OpenCRISPR-1-eVLP achieved up to a 257-fold improvement in specificity relative to Cas9-eVLP (Fig. 2B).

To quantitatively assess editing specificity between the two nucleases, we calculated the Off-Target Index (OTI), defined as the ratio of cumulative off-target indel frequency to on-target editing efficiency [26]. Across all gRNAs tested, OpenCRISPR-1 demonstrated a significantly lower OTI compared to Cas9, indicating superior editing precision relative to its on-target activity (Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, eVLP-mediated RNP delivery reduced OTI for both Cas9 and OpenCRISPR-1 (Fig. 2D). These results confirm that OpenCRISPR-1 not only produces fewer genome-wide off-target sites but also displays markedly reduced off-target effects in cellular contexts, and that eVLP-based RNP delivery further enhances specificity, underscoring its promise as a high-fidelity genome editing tool for precision applications.

OpenCRISPR-1 demonstrates enhanced PAM selectivity

Digenome-seq and targeted amplicon sequencing further demonstrated that OpenCRISPR-1 possesses superior PAM selectivity (Figs. 1E and 2A). To systematically characterize this enhanced specificity, we computationally screened endogenous genomic loci containing identical spacer sequences positioned adjacent to either canonical (NGG) or noncanonical (NGH and NHG) PAM variants (Fig. 3A). HEK293T cells were then cotransfected with gRNAs targeting these shared spacer sequences and plasmids encoding either Cas9 or OpenCRISPR-1. Editing efficiencies were subsequently quantified at each site by targeted deep sequencing. At canonical NGG PAM sites, Cas9 showed modestly higher editing efficiency (66.62% ± 4.15%) compared to OpenCRISPR-1 (57.68% ± 3.63%) (Fig. 3B and C). However, at noncanonical PAM sites, OpenCRISPR-1 editing was reduced by 86% at NHG PAMs and 70% at NGH PAMs (Fig. 3B, C and Additional file 3: Fig. S2). Specifically, at NHG PAM sites, Cas9 editing averaged 14.29% ± 6.06%, while OpenCRISPR-1 activity dropped sharply to 2.09% ± 1.08%, representing an approximate 5.1-fold decrease (Fig. 3B and C). Similarly, for NGH PAMs, Cas9 editing efficiency was 6.92% ± 6.32%, whereas OpenCRISPR-1 demonstrated a further reduced efficiency of 3.31% ± 3.46% (approximately 2.1-fold decrease) (Fig. 3B and C). These findings highlight OpenCRISPR-1’s increased PAM selectivity, demonstrating its ability to discriminate against near-cognate PAMs even when the spacer sequence remains identical. This enhanced PAM stringency likely contributes to the improved overall specificity of OpenCRISPR-1 relative to Cas9.

OpenCRISPR-1 vs. high-fidelity Cas9 variants

A well-known limitation of Cas9-based systems is the requirement for a 5′ guanine (G) in gRNAs transcribed under the U6 promoter, which is commonly used for gRNA expression [19, 20]. Depending on the spacer sequence, three gRNA formats are typically used: (I) GX19, where the spacer naturally begins with a G and is used unchanged; (II) gX19, where a non-G first nucleotide is replaced with a G; and (III) gX20, where an additional nontemplated G is appended to the 5′ end of the original 20-nt spacer (Fig. 4A). To evaluate the compatibility of OpenCRISPR-1 with these formats, we compared its editing performance to that of Cas9 and five high-fidelity Cas9 variants, including eCas9, Cas9-HF1, HypaCas9, EvoCas9, and Sniper-Cas9, across nine endogenous loci in HEK293T cells. Editing efficiencies were quantified using amplicon-based deep sequencing. Using GX19 gRNAs, OpenCRISPR-1 demonstrated editing efficiencies comparable to eCas9, Cas9-HF1, HypaCas9, and Sniper-Cas9 and consistently outperformed EvoCas9, which showed reduced activity across multiple targets (Fig. 4A and Additional file 3: Fig. S3). In the gX19 and gX20 configurations, where the initial guanine is either mismatched or nontemplated, OpenCRISPR-1 and Sniper-Cas9 retained significantly higher editing efficiencies than eCas9, Cas9-HF1, HypaCas9, and EvoCas9, which were more sensitive to changes in gRNA architecture (Fig. 4A and Additional file 3: Fig. S3). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that OpenCRISPR-1 is highly tolerant to gRNA format variation, maintaining robust editing activity across diverse gRNA designs. This format independence underscores its utility as a versatile genome editing platform compatible with a broad range of target sequences.

To characterize off-target editing profiles, we directly compared OpenCRISPR-1 with established high-fidelity Cas9 variants. Using targeted amplicon sequencing, we quantified off-target activity by calculating OTI, defined as the cumulative off-target editing frequency relative to on-target activity (Fig. 4B and Additional file 3: Fig. S4). Among the nucleases tested, Cas9 exhibited the highest OTI across all loci except FANCF, underscoring its relatively lower specificity (Fig. 4B). At individual genomic sites, OpenCRISPR-1 showed variable specificity: at some targets, such as HEK4, its OTI was slightly higher than that of eCas9, Cas9-HF1, and HypaCas9, whereas at others, like VEGFA2, its OTI was lower than these variants. To further assess specificity, we calculated the relative OTI, defined as the ratio of Cas9 OTI to that of each variant across eight target sites. Overall, OpenCRISPR-1 exhibited a relative OTI (0.18 ± 0.07) comparable to eCas9 (0.09 ± 0.04), Cas9-HF1 (0.10 ± 0.03), and HypaCas9 (0.12 ± 0.06) and showed notably improved values compared to Sniper-Cas9 (0.35 ± 0.09) and EvoCas9 (0.85 ± 0.77) (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these results indicate that OpenCRISPR-1 maintains robust specificity, outperforming Sniper-Cas9 and EvoCas9, and closely matching other high-fidelity Cas9 variants depending on the target locus. This combination of versatility and enhanced specificity underscores the potential utility of OpenCRISPR-1 for precise and reliable genome editing applications.

OpenCRISPR-based PEs retain efficient prime editing activity and exhibit improved specificity compared with existing prime editors

To evaluate the performance of OpenCRISPR-1 in the context of prime editing, we generated PE2- and PE7-type constructs based on the OpenCRISPR-1 backbone [25, 30]. Specifically, we generated OpenCRISPR-PE2 and OpenCRISPR-PE7 by converting OpenCRISPR-1 into a nickase and fusing it with an engineered reverse transcriptase, analogous to the PE2max and PE7 configuration. To assess prime editing performance, we synthesized canonical pegRNAs along with two engineered variants (tevo-epegRNA and mpk-epegRNA) [24], targeting three endogenous loci. Cells were transfected with plasmids encoding either OpenCRISPR-PE2, PE2max, OpenCRISPR-PE7, or PE7 together with the corresponding pegRNA or epegRNA. Across all tested sites, OpenCRISPR-PE2 exhibited prime editing activity comparable to PE2max, although editing efficiencies varied depending on the target locus and pegRNA type (Fig. 5A). Notably, when paired with engineered epegRNAs, OpenCRISPR-PE2 consistently achieved higher editing efficiencies than PE2max, and in most conditions, OpenCRISPR-PE7 further increased editing efficiency relative to OpenCRISPR-PE2 (Fig. 5A).

To assess off-target specificity, we analyzed editing outcomes at seven off-target sites, including five previously validated loci [31]. Across most loci examined, both OpenCRISPR-PE2 and OpenCRISPR-PE7 induced substantially fewer off-target modifications than their respective counterparts, PE2max and PE7, while maintaining high levels of on-target editing. (Fig. 5B and Additional file 3: Fig. S5). Together, these results demonstrate that OpenCRISPR-PE2 and OpenCRISPR-PE7 maintain high editing efficiency while markedly reducing off-target activity, particularly when used with optimized epegRNAs (Fig. 5B). These findings establish OpenCRISPR-based PEs as a promising next-generation prime editor with enhanced fidelity, well suited for precision genome engineering applications.

Discussion

In this study, we show that the AI-designed genome editor OpenCRISPR-1 marks a significant advancement in precision genome engineering, effectively combining high editing efficiency with exceptional specificity. As the first entirely AI-designed CRISPR nuclease validated in human cells, OpenCRISPR-1 delivers on-target editing performance comparable to Cas9 while substantially reducing off-target effects (Fig. 2). This notable enhancement in specificity directly addresses key safety concerns in clinical genome editing, particularly genotoxicity and oncogenic risks associated with unintended genomic alterations. Additionally, earlier studies have found that around 58% of healthy individuals harbor detectable antibodies against SpCas9, indicating widespread innate or acquired immune responses to this nuclease [32]. Immune recognition of Cas9 as a foreign antigen can trigger T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity or antibody-driven clearance, thereby compromising in vivo editing efficiency due to rapid protein elimination [33–35]. Importantly, OpenCRISPR-1’s sequence lacks the known immunodominant T-cell epitopes present in Cas9, pointing to a markedly reduced immunogenic profile [36, 37]. Moreover, transient delivery of Cas9 and OpenCRISPR-1 as RNP complexes via eVLP further decreased off-target editing while maintaining high on-target activity (Fig. 2) [28, 29]. In particular, OpenCRISPR-1-eVLP achieved markedly improved specificity relative to Cas9-eVLP, highlighting the compatibility of OpenCRISPR-1 with clinically favored RNP formats. Taken together, these findings establish OpenCRISPR-1 as a highly promising genome-editing platform for clinical use, offering an unmatched combination of efficacy, precision, and immunological safety.

A persistent challenge in genome editing has been achieving high specificity without sacrificing editing efficiency. Engineered Cas9 variants such as eCas9, Cas9-HF1, and HypaCas9 enhanced specificity by reducing nonspecific DNA interactions [15–17], while directed evolution strategies like EvoCas9 and Sniper-Cas9 improved nuclease fidelity [18, 19]. In addition to the variants evaluated in this study, other high-fidelity derivatives such as Cas9 HiFi have been reported to preserve near wild-type on-target activity while substantially reducing off-target editing [38, 39]. However, these methods often involved trade-offs, leading to diminished on-target activity or variable performance depending on the gRNA format (Fig. 4A) [20]. In contrast, OpenCRISPR-1 retains Cas9-level editing efficiency while markedly reducing off-target effects (Fig. 2), achieving a previously unattainable balance between precision and efficacy. As a result, OpenCRISPR-1 emerges as an ideal Cas9 variant, offering the strength and accuracy required for therapeutic genome editing. Moreover, highly precise platforms like base and prime editors impose stringent constraints on target site selection, limiting gRNA design flexibility [25, 40–42]. High-fidelity Cas9 variants (e.g., eCas9, Cas9-HF1, HypaCas9, and EvoCas9) further restrict versatility by requiring a guanine at the first position of the spacer sequence (Fig. 4A). In genome-wide CRISPR screens, tens of thousands of gRNAs are typically used, often following the gX20 spacer format [43–45]. Within these limitations, OpenCRISPR-1, owing to its relaxed sequence constraints and superior specificity, may substantially improve both accuracy and adaptability in large-scale CRISPR screens. Thus, OpenCRISPR-1 stands out as a highly promising genome-editing tool, capable of overcoming current barriers and enabling broader use in biomedical research and therapeutic settings.

Importantly, its successful adaptation into a prime editing format (OpenCRISPR-PE2 and OpenCRISPR-PE7) demonstrates that AI-generated nucleases can be seamlessly integrated into next-generation genome-editing platforms without compromising activity with high specificity (Fig. 5). As a result, OpenCRISPR-1 significantly expands the genome editing toolkit, enabling researchers to confidently tackle a broader range of genomic targets.

Conclusions

In summary, OpenCRISPR-1 not only addresses the long-standing trade-off between specificity and efficiency in genome editing but also marks a paradigm shift in protein engineering. AI-driven generative models offer a rational, robust strategy for designing biomolecules tailored to specific functions. This intentional transition from discovery-based biotechnology to design-driven innovation promises to greatly enhance our ability to edit genomes with unprecedented precision.
