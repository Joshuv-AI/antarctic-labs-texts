Nitrate improves plant growth by promoting nitrogen assimilation and photosynthetic performance in citrus seedlings
Authors: Hao Xu, Wenlang Hu, Kaiyuan Du, Yan Dong, Qingru Fan, Zengrong Huang, Lin-Tong Yang, Li-Song Chen, Jiuxin Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNitrate (represented by NN or NO3−-N) and ammonium (represented by AN or NH4+-N) are the two predominant nitrogen (N) forms utilized by plants; however, the physiological mechanisms underlying citrus response to different N forms are still poorly understood. In this study, seedlings of two citrus varieties (Citrus reticulata Blanco cv. Lugan and Citrus sinensis (L.) Osbeck cv. Xuegan) were cultivated under two N forms (NN and AN, at 4 mmol L− 1) to investigate their ultrastructural, physiological, and biochemical characteristics.ResultsCompared with AN treatment, NN treatment significantly promoted plant growth by regulating the physiological and morphological characteristics of roots and leaves. Under AN conditions, there was an excessive accumulation of free amino acids and soluble proteins in both leaves and roots, which suppressed the activity of N assimilation enzymes and consequently reduced plant N uptake. Furthermore, AN treatment inhibited photosynthetic performance by decreasing chlorophyll content, damaging chloroplast structure, and disrupting photosynthetic electron transport chain. Consequently, this led to the accumulation of non-structural carbohydrates in both leaves and roots, ultimately affecting the morphogenesis of citrus plants. Additionally, AN treatment induced an oxidative stress response, increasing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and eliciting an antioxidant response in citrus seedlings. Moreover, based on measured physiological parameters, principal component analysis showed an obvious separation between AN and NN treatments.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrated that NN supply enhanced plant growth owing to the improved coordination of carbon and N metabolism in citrus plants, thereby providing a basis for optimizing N management strategies in sustainable citrus production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07631-2.Keywords: Citrus plants, N forms, N assimilation, Photosynthesis, Ultrastructure

Background

Nitrogen (N) is a necessary element for plant tissues, as it is a component of proteins, amino acids and other organic compounds. It plays a crucial role in regulating physiological metabolism, gene expression, and plant development [1, 2]. The assimilation of N into plant systems is an extremely complex process that depends on genetic traits, environment conditions, and especially the type of N fertilizer applied [1, 3]. In recent years, the continuous application of chemical N fertilizers has been used to maintain soil fertility and maximize crop yield. Among them, nitrate (NO3−-N or NN) and ammonium (NH4+-N or AN) are two primary forms of inorganic N that have both economic and environmental impacts and play diverse roles in agricultural production [4–6].

The uptake of inorganic N by plants occurs through either a low-affinity or high-affinity transport system. NO3− is converted to NH4+ through the action of nitrite reductase (NiR) and nitrate reductase (NR), a process that requires a considerable amount of energy [7]. From this perspective, the assimilation of NH4+ is a key process in N metabolism, as it is the only form of reduced N that plants can use to synthesize amino acids. This process can reduce energy consumption and ultimately promote crop growth [8]. Two main pathways facilitate the assimilation of NH4+: glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase (GS/GOGAT) and glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) [1, 9]. However, due to the low affinity of GDH for NH4+, the GS/GOGAT pathway is considered the primary pathway for NH4+ assimilation [10]. Nevertheless, the distinct mechanisms of N assimilation in response to NH4+ and NO3− remain unclear, hindering the optimization of N management strategies aimed at enhancing the physiological functions of plants and meeting their specific needs.

Carbon (C) and N metabolism are fundamental physiological characteristic of plants, with their homeostasis and balance of these elements being essential for normal plant growth and serving as important indicators of stress tolerance. Approximately 75% of the N found in a leaf is incorporated into chlorophyll, which directly influences the photosynthetic system for C fixation, while various physiological and biochemical processes are further regulated by N sources [6]. Numerous studies have shown that an excessive supply of AN to plants triggers rapid changes in key cellular processes; specifically, these changes include alterations in cytoplasmic pH, gene expression, and post-translational modifications of proteins [4, 9, 11]. This leads to the acidification of the extraplastidial space, alterations in the oxidative and phytohormonal status, and inhibition of plant growth, particularly affecting root cell expansion, division, and early root elongation [7, 11–13]. In contrast, nitrate does not induce toxicity; rather, its availability can mitigate the toxic effects of AN, although the underlying mechanism remains largely unknown. Previous studies suggest that the NN-dependent mitigation of AN toxicity is mediated by inhibiting NH4+ uptake-mediated H+ efflux, as well as by forming a functional unit of the transporter protein NRT1.1 and the anion channel SLAH3 to alleviate AN toxicity [7, 11, 14]. Additionally, it has been confirmed that plants exhibit varying sensitivity to NH4+ and NO3− due to differences in species, genotypes, and soil characteristics [1, 15, 16]. Generally, plants can be categorized into those with AN preference (such as rice, blueberries and onions) and those with NN preference (such as potato, sugar beet and poplars), to which they exhibit distinct physiological and morphological responses for better adaptation [4]. However, limited information is available regarding how different N forms impact N uptake and plant development, particularly in citrus.

Citrus fruit is popular among consumers for its rich nutritional qualities and unique flavour, with approximately 8 million hectares of citrus plants cultivated in over 140 countries [3]. China plays a pivotal role in the global citrus industry, being abundant in germplasm resources and boasting excellent varieties, with China alone contributing about one-third of global citrus production. Among the widely planted citrus varieties in southern China, Citrus reticulata Blanco and Citrus sinensis (L.) Osbeck belong to different branches on the evolutionary tree and exhibit genetic disparities [17]. Meanwhile, C. reticulata, an ancient citrus variety, is renowned for its potential medicinal value, attributed to its rich nutrient content and bioactive compounds [18]. C. sinensis is the most prominent species within the Citrus genus, accounting for approximately 50% of global citrus production [19]. In the tropical and subtropical red soil regions of southern China, citrus is widely cultivated but often faces soil issues such as high acidity and low fertility [20, 21]. An adequate N supply is essential for improving fruit yield and quality in citrus production. However, existing research data indicated that the national average total N fertilizer input was 387 kg ha− 1 in Chinese citrus production, which is 1.5–3.5 times higher than that in other countries, resulting in a relatively low N partial productivity (54.6 kg kg− 1), as well as the overlooking of N form management [10, 22]– [23]. Notably, excessive N application does not result in a proportional increase in yield and can give rise to a series of ecological and environmental risks, such as exacerbating soil acidification, reducing soil biological activity, inhibiting the growth of nitrifying bacteria, disrupting soil NH4+/NO3− balance, and increasing C footprint [16, 22–27]. All these factors decrease N use efficiency (NUE) and disrupt the matching balance between soil N forms and citrus’ inherent N preferences, ultimately restricting fruit yield and causing a decline in fruit quality. Previous studies suggest that matching soil N supply with plant N demand is the primary approach to improving NUE for high-quality citrus production [6, 28]. This alignment depends on various factors, including citrus varieties and management practices, especially N dosage and form.

Xiong et al. [26] found that, compared to treatments without N application, appropriate N addition significantly enhances the activity of nitrate reductase (NR) and glutamate dehydrogenase in the spring shoot leaves of citrus trees, as well as the expression of related genes, excessive N application can impede the expression of GDH2. Quaggio et al. [29] revealed that the fruit yield of citrus trees fertilized with NN is significantly greater than that of those receiving AN, as well as NUE improved 14% to 38%. Our previous studies have confirmed that C. reticulata shows a preference for NN, positively influencing plant N uptake and photosynthetic performance [10, 30]. Nevertheless, the physiological mechanisms underlying the responses of different varieties, along with the ultrastructural changes in their photosynthetic organs in reaction to various N forms, warrant further investigation. Therefore, unravelling the effects of different N sources on the physiological characteristics of citrus plants is a crucial step towards improving the productivity potential and environmental sustainability of citrus production.

Based on the above concerns, we hypothesized that NN supply will enhance the activity of N assimilation enzymes to support high N uptake capacity in citrus plants, and further promote plant C fixation. This will balance C-N metabolism, ultimately resulting in greater biomass accumulation. In this study, our objectives were: (1) to explore how different N forms affect plant development and N uptake in two citrus cultivars, and (2) to clarify the physiological mechanisms underlying N metabolism, gas exchange, photosynthetic performance, chloroplast ultrastructure, assimilates production, and antioxidant system in response to various N forms. This study is expected to offer valuable insights into optimizing N management for sustainable citrus production.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials and culture conditions

This research utilized two citrus plant varieties, namely ‘Lugan (C. reticulata)’ and ‘Xuegan (C. sinensis)’, which were purchased in February from Yonghui, a regional hypermarket chain located in Fuzhou City, Fujian Province, China. Specifically, seeds were extracted from healthy citrus fruits and subjected to a sequential cultivation process, including sowing (conducted in March), germination, emergence, transplanting (carried out in May), and subsequent cultivation (maintained from May to October), respectively. Once the seedlings developed four to five leaves, the two comparable varieties were transferred to six-liter ceramic container for sand cultivation. Each ceramic pot was designated as an independent experimental unit, since the sand matrix, nutrient solution supply, and microenvironment of each pot were strictly controlled independently. Each treatment included 10 experimental units (10 pots), with 2 seedlings planted per pot and subsequently pooled as a single biological replicate for analysis.

Based on our previous laboratory studies regarding N forms and concentration gradients, the modified Hoagland’s nutrient solution was used to treat the seedlings, providing two N form conditions: AN supplied as (NH4)2SO4 and NN supplied as Ca(NO3)2, both at a 4 mmol L− 1 N supply [10, 30]. Meanwhile, CaCl2·2H2O was used to adjust the Ca2+ concentration in the nutrient solution of each treatment to 5 mmol L− 1, so as to eliminate the influence of Ca2+ on the plants. Additionally, dicyandiamide (DCD) was incorporated into the nutrition solution to prevent nitrification between treatments, with the solution pH maintained to 6.50 ± 0.05 using HCl or NaOH. A total of four treatments, comprising two citrus varieties and two N forms, with ten replicates each, were arranged under greenhouse conditions. During the culturing phase, the 1/4 strength nutrient solution was irrigated every two days for 14 days, followed by the application of the 1/2 strength nutrient solution every two days for 30 days. Subsequently, a full-strength nutrient solution was applied until the citrus seedlings were harvested.

Plant sampling and growth characteristics measurements

After five months of treatment, citrus plants from 5 experimental units (5 pots, 10 plants total; the 2 seedlings per pot were pooled as one sample) were harvested and divided into root, stem, and leaf sections. Fresh samples of white roots (0–1 cm from the root tip) and middle leaves (at 1/5 of the plant height) were immediately transferred at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Additionally, plant biomass was measured as the sum of each organ part, which leaf and root growth characteristics were determined following the methods described by Chen et al. [10]. Besides, tetrazolium chloride staining was employed to quantify the root activity. All measurements in this section were performed with a minimum of four biological replicates, where each biological replicate corresponded to one independent experiment unit (one pot).

Determining of chlorophyll (Chl) content, gas exchange, and Chl a fluorescence parameters

Chl concentration was determined by measuring the supernatant obtained from leaves separated with 80% (v/v) acetone [31]. Four biological replicates (each from an independent pot) were included; for quality control, two technical replicates were conducted to ensure measurement stability during analysis. Leaf gas exchange parameters were measured during the sample harvest stage using a portable photosynthetic system (CIRAS-2, PP System, Hertfordshire, UK), with settings as described by Chen et al. [30]. Measurements were taken from leaves positioned at 1/5 of the distance from the lower to the upper part of the plant on a sunny morning. The leaf temperature was recorded at 27 ± 0.5 °C, the relative humidity was 45 ± 1%, and the light intensity was 1000 µmol m− 2 s− 1. Additionally, water use efficiency (WUE, WUE = Pn/Tr, Pn represents net photosynthetic rate and Tr represents transpiration rate), carboxylation efficiency (CE, CE = Pn/Ci, Ci represents intercellular CO2 concentration), and limitation of stomatal (Ls=1-Ci/Ca, Ca represents ambient CO2 concentration) were analyzed and calculated [30, 32]. After 3 h of dark adaptation, the same leaves (at the defined position) were used to measure the chlorophyll a fluorescence transient (OJIP test) with a Handy Plant Efficiency Analyzer (Handy PEA, Hansatech Instruments, Norfolk, UK), which are described in Table S1. Measurements were conducted on seven independent biological replicates and two technical replicates.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses

The methodology outlined by Oustric et al. [33] was adopted for analyzing lower leaf and root samples using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). On a sunny day, samples were collected and immediately fixed with 5% glutaraldehyde. Following fixation, the samples were rinsed four times in PBS (pH = 7.0), with each rinse lasting 15 min. The samples were then fixed for two hours in osmium tetroxide and subsequently washed. Dehydration was carried out using a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), with each concentration applied for 15 min. The samples were subsequently treated with acetone twice, each for 10 min. A resin-acetone mixture was introduced in two steps: first in a 1:3 ratio for two hours, followed by a 3:1 ratio for two and a half hours. The samples were then embedded in pure resin overnight. Ultrathin Sects. (60–90 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome, stained, and examined under a Hitachi HT7700 TEM (Japan) operated at 80 kV. Ten leaf samples were used as biological replicates to ensure robustness.

Determining of non-structural carbohydrate (NSC), free amino acid (FAA) and soluble protein

Dried ground samples were analyzed to determine the NSC content using the anthrone-ethyl acetate colorimetry method [10, 31]. Briefly, samples were initially homogenized in 10% glacial acetic acid and centrifuged at 4 °C. Subsequently, supernatant was thoroughly mixed with a solution containing 0.9 mL water, 1.5 mL ninhydrin, and 0.25 mL of 1% ascorbic acid, and then incubated in a boiling water bath for 15 min. After cooling the mixture to 25 °C, 1.25 mL of 60% ethanol was added, and the absorbance was determined using a spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan) at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Soluble protein content was determined using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method [10, 31]. Briefly, frozen leaves and roots were homogenized in 1.6 mL of buffer (pH = 7.0) containing 50 mmol L− 1 NaH2PO4-Na2HPO4, and then centrifuged for 10 min at 3,000×g at 4 °C. To improve grinding efficiency, a small amount of quartz sand and insoluble polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP) may be added. After mixing 0.02 mL of supernatant with 0.98 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue at 25 °C for 5 min, the absorbance was determined using a spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan) at a wavelength of 595 nm, with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. All experiments were performed with at least four biological replicates.

To determine free amino acids (FAA), weigh 0.03 g of the fresh sample and grind it into a homogenate using 1.6 mL of 10% acetic acid [10, 31]. Centrifuge the mixture at 4 °C and 13,000×g for 10 min. Then, take 0.1 mL of the supernatant and mix it with 0.9 mL of distilled water, 1.5 mL of ninhydrin solution, and 0.25 mL of ascorbic acid. Mix thoroughly and incubate in a boiling water bath for 15 min. After cooling in cold water, add 1.25 mL of 60% ethanol solution, mix well, and centrifuge again. Finally, measure the absorbance at 570 nm. All measurements in this section were performed with a minimum of four biological replicates and two technical replicates.

Determining of N metabolism enzyme activity

The activities of N metabolism-related enzymes, including nitrate reductase (NR, EC1.6.6.1), glutamine synthetase (GS, EC6.3.1.2), glutamate synthase (GOGAT, EC1.4.1.13), and glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH; EC1.4.1.2), were measured using the method described by Chen et al. [10] and Xiong et al. [26], with four biological replicates and two technical replicates.

For the determination of NR, fresh samples were extracted by grinding in a buffer (pH = 8.7). To improve homogenization and accurate, a small amount of quartz sand and insoluble polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP) may be added. After centrifugation at 4,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, 0.4 mL of supernatant was added to a centrifuge tube containing 1.2 mL of 100 mmol L− 1 KNO3 (dissolved in 100 mmol L− 1 phosphoric acid buffer) and 0.4 mL of 2 mg mL− 1 NADH, and the mixture was reacted at 25 °C for 30 min. A blank control was performed by replacing NADH with 0.4 mL of 100 mmol L− 1 phosphoric acid buffer (pH = 7.5) under the same conditions for each sample. At the end of the reaction, 1 mL of p-aminobenzenesulfonamide (1% W/V) was immediately added to stop the reaction, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 0.02% (W/V) naphthylamine, which reacted at room temperature for 15 min before centrifugation at 4,000×g for 5 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan) at a wavelength of 540 nm, with KNO2 as the standard.

For the determination of GS, samples were homogenized in a buffer (pH = 7.6). Supernatant was added to a mixture containing Tris-HCl, MgCl2, sodium glutamate, and hydroxylamine hydrochloride. Finally, ATP solution was added, mixed thoroughly, and stored at 30 °C for 30 min. A blank control was included in each sample tube by omitting hydroxylamine hydrochloride. After the reaction, 1 mL of chromogenic agent containing 2% TCA (W/V), 3.5% FeCl3 (W/V), and 1.8% HCl (V/V) was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 5 min following an additional reaction time of 5 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at a wavelength of 540nm.

GOGAT activity was measured using a buffer (pH = 8.0). After thorough homogenization, the supernatant was thoroughly mixed with a solution containing 2-oxoglutarate, KCl, NADH, and Tris-HCl (pH = 7.6). Finally, glutamine was added as the starter, and the reaction rate was measured at 340 nm for 1 min using a spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan).

The GDH enzyme solution was extracted as described for GOGAT. Subsequently, 0.2 mol L− 1 of Tris-HCl, 0.1 mol L− 1 of α-ketoglutarate, 1 mol L− 1 of NH4Cl, and 3 mmol L− 1 of NADH were sequentially added to a glass cuvette. The kinetic assay was performed at 340nm for 1 min, using the enzyme solution as the starter.

Determining of NH4+-N and NO3−-N concentrations

The NH4+-N concentration in fresh citrus tissues (roots and leaves) was determined using a modified colorimetric method [32, 34]. Briefly, 0.05 g of fresh sample was homogenized with 0.5 mL of 0.3 mmol L− 1 H2SO4 on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to separate the supernatant from cellular debris. A 0.2 mL aliquot of the clear supernatant was transferred to a 15 mL test tube, followed by the sequential addition of 4.9 mL phenol solution and 4.9 mL sodium hypochlorite solution. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and incubated at room temperature for 1 h to allow the color reaction to complete. After incubation, the absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 625 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan), with a blank control used for instrument calibration. NH4+-N​ concentration was calculated using a standard curve generated from ammonium sulfate solutions of known concentrations.

The NO3−-N concentration was analyzed using a slightly modified salicylic acid-sulfuric acid colorimetric method [32, 34]. Briefly, 0.05 g of fresh sample was ground into a fine homogenate with 0.5 mL of deionized water. The homogenate was transferred to a test tube and immersed in a boiling water bath for 8 min. After extraction, the tube was immediately cooled under running tap water to stop the reaction, and the mixture was centrifuged at 13,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. A 0.1 mL portion of the supernatant was mixed with 0.4 mL of 5% (w/v) salicylic acid-sulfuric acid solution in a new test tube, and the mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 20 min. Subsequently, 9.5 mL of 8% (w/v) NaOH solution was added slowly to neutralize the acid and develop the yellow-orange color. Once cooled to room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 410 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan), with a blank control used for background correction. NO3−-N​ concentration was quantified using a standard curve prepared from potassium nitrate standard solutions.

Determining of antioxidant enzyme activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) content

Samples were homogenized in a 50 mmol L− 1 buffer of KH2PO4-KOH (pH = 7.5), and the supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract. According to the method described by Balfagón et al. [35], the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD; EC1.15.1.1), peroxidase (POD; EC1.11.1.17), and catalase (CAT; EC1.11.1.6) were measured at 240 nm, 470 nm, and 560 nm, respectively, after the supernatant was fully mixed with the reaction solution. Thiobarbituric acid (TBA) was used to determine MDA content. Briefly, fresh tissue samples (0.03 g) were homogenized in 1.5 mL of 80% ethanol and centrifuged at 3,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, with the supernatant collected for analysis. The reaction mixture was prepared using duplicate setups for both sample (+ TBA) and control (-TBA) groups, containing 0.9 mL of 22.2% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), 0.9 mL of 0.722% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) in the sample group, and 0.1 mL of 0.1% butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT). After incubation at 95 for 25 min to form MDA-TBA adducts, the mixture was cooled, centrifuged again, and the supernatant analyzed spectrophotometrically at 440 nm, 532 nm, and 600 nm [10]. Measurements were taken from four biological replicates and two technical replicates.

Determining of N concentration, accumulation, and distribution

For N concentration measurement, dried and ground samples of roots, stems, and leaves of citrus seedlings were placed in a H2SO4-H2O2 system at 260–270 °C until completely digested to obtain a clear solution [10, 30, 32], with four biological replicates and two technical replicates. The N concentration, accumulation and distribution were calculated based on the obtained digestion solution. The total N concentration of the obtained solution was determined using a continuous flow analyzer (AA3, Bran + Luebbe, Hamburg, Germany). N accumulation (N concentration × dry weight of each part) and N distribution (percentage of organ N accumulation relative to total plant N accumulation) for each organ were calculated.

Statistical analysis

The experimental results are expressed as mean values ± standard error (SE), with a minimum of four replicates (n ≥ 4). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way and two-way ANOVA in SPSS 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and the least significant difference (LSD) test was applied to identify significant differences among means at a significance level of P < 0.05. For multivariate analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using OriginPro 2022b (OriginLab Corp., MA, USA). Additionally, a partial least squares pathway analysis (PLS-PM) model was conducted using SmartPLS 4 (SmartPLS GmbH, Oststeinbek, Germany).

Results

Plant biomass and growth variables

The biomass of leaves, stems and roots were increased significantly following NN treatment. Among them, the biomass was 5.4 g·plant− 1 for Lugan plants and 11.9 g·plant− 1 for Xuegan plants, representing increases of 1.5-fold and 1.9-fold compared with AN treatment, respectively (Fig. 1a-b). With the exception of SLA, all leaf growth variables, including leaf weight, water content, area, and SLW, were consistently higher under NN treatment (Fig. S1). Similar trends were observed in root growth parameters and activity (Fig. S2).

N uptake, assimilation enzyme activity and products

Under NN conditions, N concentration (% N) in leaves and roots was significantly lower than under AN (due to dilution by greater growth), whereas stem N concentration was higher under NN (Fig. 1c). Despite these concentration differences, total plant N content (accumulation or uptake) was much higher with NN, being 1.61-fold higher in Lugan and 1.77-fold higher in Xuegan compared with AN (Fig. 1d). Additionally, N distribution among various organs of citrus seedlings was regulated by N form. For Lugan plants, NN supply increased the N proportion in roots while decreasing it in leaves. Conversely, for Xuegan plants, NN treatment resulted in a decrease in N proportion in roots and an increase in leaves and stems (Fig. 1e).

Changes in N uptake may be associated with differences in N metabolism characteristics. Compared with AN treatment, NN treatment enhanced NR and GS activities in roots and leaves of both citrus varieties (Fig. 2a-b). In contrast, GOGAT and GDH activities were consistently lower under NN treatment than that under AN treatment (Fig. 2c-d). Thus, AN-treated plants accumulated significantly more unused N compounds (free amino acids, soluble proteins) than NN-treated plants (Fig. 2e-f), consistent with the observed lower activities of NR and GS under AN condition. Furthermore, NH4+-N concentration in roots was notably higher under AN treatment than that under NN treatment, especially in Xuegan (Fig. 2g); meanwhile, NO3−-N concentration was drastically reduced in both leaves and roots under AN supply (Fig. 2h). However, these enzyme activities, biosynthesis products, and NH4+-N and NO3–N concentrations in different organs varied between citrus cultivars.

Chl content and photosynthetic performance

The alterations in N metabolism of citrus seedlings indicated that the specific forms of N significantly influenced leaf photosynthetic efficiency (Fig. 3). Compared with AN treatment, NN treatment led to a significant increase in Chl content, as evidenced by higher levels of both Chl a and Chl b (Fig. 3a-b). In terms of leaf gas exchange characteristics, NN treatment resulted in notable enhancements in Pn, gs, and Tr across all citrus cultivars (Fig. 3c-e). These improvements were associated with increased CE, WUE, and Ls (Fig. 3g-i). However, NN treatment also led to a decrease in Ci (Fig. 3f), likely due to the increased demand for C assimilation driven by higher photosynthetic activity.

Further investigation of light energy transduction in the photosynthetic apparatus is warranted based on the parameters indicated by the PSII reaction center fluorescence transient. These parameters of DIo/CSo, ABS/CSo, DIo/RC, TRo/RC, and ABS/RC significantly increased under AN treatment, while ETo/CSo, TRo/CSo, and ETo/RC declined compared with NN treatment, regardless of the citrus cultivars (Fig. 4g-h; Fig. S4). Except for an increase in Vj, AN treatment decreased all PSⅡ receptor side parameters, including ETo/TRo, ETo/ABS, Mo, and Sm, ultimately inhibiting the photosynthetic performance indices of Fv/Fm, Fv/Fo, and PIabs (Fig. 4g-h; Fig. S5).

NSC and antioxidant enzyme activity

Differences in photosynthetic performance between N forms induced changes in assimilate accumulation in various organs of citrus seedlings (Fig. 5). Generally, soluble sugar and starch contents in leaves (14.54% and 314.71% for Lugan and 17.35% and 71.38% for Xuegan), stems (100.64% and 50.51% for Lugan and 94.66% and 42.63% for Xuegan), and roots (54.34% and 38.69% for Lugan and 134.20% and 24.53% for Xuegan) under AN conditions were consistently higher than those under NN conditions, resulting in an increased NSC content (soluble sugar + starch). Moreover, TEM observations revealed that a large amount of starch accumulated in root and leaf cells of citrus seedlings supplied with AN (Fig. 6), and the chloroplast structure became loose and even disintegrated (Fig. 6e-h).

Discussion

Citrus is a nitrate-preferring plant

Numerous studies have investigated AN toxicity in plants, identifying root growth restriction and leaf chlorosis as the primary phenotypic indicators [4, 7]. Furthermore, AN toxicity impacts intracellular pH, osmotic balance, phytohormone and polyamine metabolism, and nutrient uptake [4, 34]. It also reduces available CO2, damages chloroplast ultrastructure, increases proton efflux, hampers transmembrane ammonium cycling, inhibits GDP-mannose pyrophosphorylase, and induces oxidative stress [7, 27]. Plants may employ various mechanisms to adapt to these challenges, such as enhanced NH4+ assimilation in leaves, increased activity of the alternative oxidase pathway in mitochondria, and reduced sensitivity to external pH acidification [36].

This investigation demonstrated that the growth and biomass accumulation of the two citrus species under NN treatment were significantly higher than those observed under AN treatment (Fig. 1). Moreover, higher SLW and lower SLA were observed in NN treatment across citrus species, indicating thinner leaves compared with AN treatment (Fig. S1). Under stress conditions, increasing leaf thickness is a strategy to enhance resistance and extend leaf longevity, but this comes at the expense of leaf area and photosynthesis [10, 30]. Furthermore, NN treatment significantly stimulated root morphogenesis and improved biomass allocation, particularly in Lugan plants (Fig. 1 and Fig. S2). These findings suggest that the form of N has a more pronounced impact on root growth and development in citrus plants, with NN treatment potentially enhancing nutrient and water uptake, ultimately improving biomass accumulation [9, 10, 37].

Nitrate improves the N metabolism of citrus plants

The N metabolism characteristics of citrus plants in response to AN and NN supply are directly related to their preferences for different N forms. In our study, NN supply led to greater overall N accumulation in all organs compared to AN supply, although the N concentrations (% N) in leaves and roots were actually lower under NN (due to dilution by increased growth) (Fig. 1). This indicates that while plants on nitrate took up more total N, that N was more effectively utilized for growth. However, the characteristics of N uptake, reduction, and assimilation are crucial for plants to convert inorganic into organic N forms within their physiological process. Generally, plant root cells possess specific transport proteins that efficiently absorb NH4+ from the soil [9, 12]. Through the GS/GOGAT pathway, organic N-containing compounds such as glutamine and glutamate are synthesized directly in the roots, serving as vital N sources for the plants [1, 9]. This process conserves energy and reduces the number of metabolic steps, enabling a rapid response to the plants’ N demands. However, when excessive NH4+ accumulate within the cells, they can disrupt the acid-base balance, interfere with N metabolism, inhibit root system growth and development, and induce oxidative stress [4, 38]. NO3− has several advantages over NH4+ as it can penetrate root cells, where it undergoes reduction or is stored in vacuoles before being transported to the above-ground parts for subsequent assimilation and storage. Consequently, variations in soil N forms mainly affect N assimilation processes in plants, further influencing relative physiological and biochemical processes. Hachiya and Sakakibara [9] reported that the reduction of NO3− requires a higher consumption of ATP, consequently affecting the selection of N forms by plants. These findings suggest an inherent trade-off and adaptability in citrus plant regarding energy consumption and toxicity tolerance when preferring NO3− over NH4+.

For most land plants, after absorbing NO3−, two distinct processes occur: a small portion of the nitrate ions is immediately assimilated in the roots, while the remainder is transported to the above-ground parts through the xylem and subsequently reduced in the leaves through photosynthesis [9, 39]. In the cytoplasm, NO3− is enzymatically reduced to NO2− by NR before being transported to plastids and chloroplasts, where it is further converted into NH4+ by NiR [6, 7]. Simultaneously, Amt/Mep/Rh family proteins facilitate the transmembrane movement of NH4+, which is then predominantly assimilated through GS [9, 40]. In our study, compared to NN treatment, AN treatment led to a significant increase in NH4+-N concentration in both the leaves and roots of the two citrus varieties (Fig. 2g-h). Concurrently, the activities of key enzymes NR and GS involved in N assimilation in the roots and leaves of the two varieties were significantly reduced under AN treatment, while the activity of GOGAT increased substantially (Fig. 2a-c). Plants detoxify NH4+ by incorporating it into organic compounds through the GS/GOGAT pathway [7], with GS playing a crucial role in converting NH4+ to glutamate. GS also serves as the rate-limiting step in the NH4+ assimilation process [27]. The reduced GS activity under AN treatment likely hindered the efficient conversion of accumulated NH4+-N into organic N; this not only explains the observed increase in NH4+-N concentration in various organs of citrus plants but may also trigger NH4+ toxicity when sufficiently severe. Previous studies have reported that the reduced activity of NR and GS may result from the depletion of carbon skeletons and energy supply caused by a decline in photosynthetic rate (Pn), as both enzymes are involved in photosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism [27, 40]. This decline may lead to excessive accumulation of NH4+ in the roots. Moreover, excessive NH4+ has been shown to increase the content of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in roots [38], resulting in reduced meristem size, inhibited root elongation, and compromised root vitality [6, 41]. Our results indicate that FAA and soluble protein levels increased significantly in both leaves and roots under AN treatment (Fig. 2). This may represent a negative feedback regulation of the NH4+ assimilation pathway, correlating with the decreased activity of NR and GS. In addition to the GS/GOGAT cycle, mitochondrial NAD(H)-dependent GDH utilizes NH4+ as a substrate to facilitate the reversible conversion of 2-ketoglutaric acid (2-OG) into glutamate. Consequently, GDH activity is significantly induced in plants subjected to stress or when NH4+ is the sole N source, thereby reducing internal NH4+ concentrations [27]. This finding is consistent with our results, indicating that excessive accumulation of FAA and soluble proteins in root and leaf tissues may negatively regulate GS activity, further promoting NH4+ accumulation and its associated toxic effects. Additionally, the inhibition of the root system due to excessive NH4+ may impair the plant’s ability to absorb essential nutrients, which in turn can reduce the photosynthetic capacity of the above-ground parts and the activity of related N assimilation enzymes.

Nitrate enhances the photosynthetic system of citrus plants

Plants utilize light energy to convert CO2 into organic compounds while simultaneously taking up N through their roots, demonstrating a clear coupling relationship [9, 42]. Among them, the reducing power (i.e. NADPH) and chemical energy (i.e. ATP) necessary for driving biochemical process in plants are generated by the photosynthetic electron transport chain during the photoreaction of photosynthesis [39]. Additionally, N sources influence plant photosynthetic efficiency by affecting the Pn of light response, light compensation point, light saturation point, and dark respiration rate. Gao et al. [43] reported that N forms affects plant growth and development regarding gas exchange capacity. Chen et al. [30] further found that the performance and stability of the photosynthetic electron transfer chain are regulated by both N form and dosage. This finding aligns with results from our study, which AN treatment primarily disrupts the reaction center and acceptor side of the photosynthetic electron transfer chain, thereby negatively impacting photosynthetic performance and reducing assimilate production (Figs. 3 and 4; Figs. S3-S5).

The chloroplast is one of the most crucial organelles in plant photosynthesis, and its status reflects the extent of stress experienced during this process. Under normal conditions, chloroplasts exhibit a fusiform and clear structure; however, they can be damaged by abiotic stress, such as inappropriate nutrient supply [33]. Our results showed that the chloroplast structure was complete, transparent and relatively small under NN treatment according to TEM analysis (Figs. 5 and 6). In contrast, the chloroplasts appeared deformed, scattered and more prominent under AN treatment, with significant starch accumulation observed. Also, the quantification results showed that the levels of soluble sugar and starch increased correspondingly (Fig. 5), which appears to the reduced photosynthetic capacity of citrus plants under AN supply. Li et al. [44] reported that leaf N content is related to chloroplast size, and the larger chloroplasts that increase with N availability can inhibit CO2 diffusion by reducing mesophyll conductance. Wang et al. [45] found that excessive carbohydrate accumulation can inhibit photosynthesis by suppressing the expression of genes involved in physiological process. Additionally, photosynthetic pigments play a crucial role in the photosynthetic system and may potentially influence N metabolism by reducing photosynthetic N utilization efficiency in citrus plants [30]. This aligns with our finding that the contents of Chl a and Chl b were significantly decreased under AN treatment compared with NN treatment, leading to inhibited Pn (Figs. 3 and 4). These results elucidate the reduction in photosynthetic system capacity induced under AN conditions, a process primarily regulated by non-stomatal factors in plant, including citrus.

Nitrate alleviates oxidative stress in citrus

The excessive accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA) in plants can cause membrane lipid peroxidation and damage to cellular membrane [10]. Plants, including citrus, have evolved an antioxidant system primarily composed of antioxidant enzymes and non-enzymatic antioxidants to counteract the detrimental effects caused by ROS and MDA [23, 35, 46]. Additionally, there is a close association between the plant’s N preference and its antioxidant enzyme system, which help maintain high antioxidant enzyme activities essential for regulating free radicals within normal ranges [8, 47]. Notably, the present study showed a significant increase in MDA levels under AN treatment compared with NN treatment, with higher activities of POD and CAT observed in leaves and roots (Fig. 7), as also reported by Fan et al. [23], which further deduced that plants are subjected to AN stress. This may be due to increased membrane lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress resulting from elevated fluorescent heat dissipation under a sole AN supply. Moreover, NO3− assimilation requires more reductants than NH4+, potentially reducing oxidative stress and antioxidant enzyme activities in plants [4, 8].

The processes through which plants utilize light for physiological functions can be summarized into three interrelated pathways: photosynthesis, chlorophyll a fluorescence, and energy dissipation, with a reduction in photosynthetic capacity corresponds to an increase in the other two pathways [48]. Also, the regulation of linear electron transport is crucial for protecting PSII from photosuppression [49]. It has been shown that applying of NH4+ leads to increase of NH4+ concentration in leaves, specifically affecting photosynthetic energy conversion and distribution [27]. Additionally, elevated levels of AN nutrition induce uncoupling of photophosphorylation and significantly increase in photorespiratory electron flux [27, 30]. Furthermore, AN can inhibit oxygen release and delay electron transfer to the reaction center by substituting chlorine at important binding sites within OEC [50]. In the present study, the O, J, I and P fluorescence levels significantly increased under AN treatment, indicating an enhanced fluorescence intensity in leaves along with a decline in the efficiency of the entire electron transport (Fig. 4). Notably, WK did not differ between N treatments (Fig. S3), indicating that the donor side (OEC) remained unaffected. Moreover, ABS/RC and ABS/CSo decreased and Mo increased under AN treatment, indicating inactivation of the reaction center (Fig. 4; Fig. S4 and S5). Concurrently, the increase in DIo/RC, DIo/CSo and DIo/ABS can be regarded as an adaptive mechanism to protect leaves from photooxidation damage [30, 51]. Overall, AN treatment reduces Fv/Fo, Fv/Fm and PIabs by inactivating the PSII reaction center and inhibiting the receptor side, which the disordered photosynthetic electron transport chain further induces photooxidative damage in citrus plants.

Moreover, previous studies integrating physiological, proteomic, and gene expression analyses have reported that key variables affecting N redistribution in citrus trees include enhanced N metabolism and photosynthesis, improved levels of proline and arginine, and delayed degradation of storage proteins in senescent leaves [26, 52]. Therefore, it is crucial to further elucidate the discrepancies in C/N metabolism, hormone balance, and ion homeostasis under different N forms using a multi-omics approach, including transcriptomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and microbiome analyses [7, 52–54], that will provide a theoretical foundation for undergoing plant N utilization as well as targeted N fertilization in citrus production.

Conclusion

In this study, we compared two citrus cultivars (C. reticulata and C. sinensis) under two N forms (ammonium vs. nitrate) to draw insights for N management in citrus production. Our results revealed significant differences in morphological and physiological features, demonstrating how N forms affect plant growth by regulating N assimilation, photosynthetic performance, and antioxidant system. In summary, citrus seedlings clearly prefer nitrate over ammonium. Nitrate supply enhanced growth by boosting N-assimilation enzyme activity and coordinating carbon–nitrogen metabolism, which in turn preserved chloroplast structure and photosynthetic efficiency while minimizing oxidative stress. Our findings provide a scientific basis for optimizing fertilizer N forms in citrus cultivation to improve plant performance and sustainability.
