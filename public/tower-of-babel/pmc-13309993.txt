Analysis of ElfBar Elfa vaping product aerosol compared with cigarette smoke and regulatory safety limits
Authors: Ian M. Fearon, Sairah Salim-Sartoni, Zhichao Chen, Yudan Yang, Xianfang Rong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySmoking-related disease is related to inhalational toxicant exposure. Here, we assessed levels of 17 analytes, which have a potential link to smoking-related disease, in aerosol from four variants of the ElfBar Elfa vaping product compared with cigarette smoke levels or safety limits. Carbon monoxide, NNK and NNN, acrolein, benzo[a]pyrene, 1,3-butadiene, benzene, cadmium and arsenic were below detectable or quantifiable levels in all vaping product aerosols. For formaldehyde and acetaldehyde, these were between 99.6% and 99.9% lower than levels in 3R4F reference cigarette smoke depending on the variant assessed. For metals quantifiable in the aerosols, assumed potential exposure levels among users were all significantly lower than USP permissible daily exposure levels for inhalational medicines or below NIOSH-recommended exposure limits. Overall, toxicant exposure among smokers completely switching to Elfa vaping product use would be significantly reduced, or eliminated, compared with continued smoking. Further, metal exposure is likely not of toxicological concern.

Introduction

Cigarette smoking is a leading cause of human disease, and is responsible for more than 7 million deaths per year globally1 from numerous causes including lung cancer, heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and reproductive/developmental disease.2 The primary cause of smoking-related diseases is exposure to chemical toxicants which are formed during the processes of combustion and pyrolysis of tobacco and inhaled by smokers,3,4,5 with the disease risk related to the degree (cigarette consumption) and length (years smoked) of cigarette smoking. More than 7,000 chemicals have been identified in tobacco and tobacco smoke,4 and a number of these are known toxicants which the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has classified as harmful and potentially harmful constituents (HPHCs) which are associated with the development of various smoking-related diseases.6

For smokers, the most effective way of reducing smoking-related disease risk is to stop smoking,2 which would serve to eliminate their exposure to chemical toxicants. However, despite large numbers of adult smokers reportedly wanting to stop smoking, it is estimated that less than 10% of adult smokers actually stop smoking each year.7 In smokers who are either unwilling or unable to stop smoking, complete switching to the use of vaping products (also known as e-cigarettes or electronic nicotine delivery systems) which deliver nicotine with fewer and substantially lower levels of toxicants compared with cigarette smoke8 has the potential to improve their health. This toxicant reduction approach to reducing but potentially not completely eliminating toxicant exposure was first described in the 2001 report by the US Institute of Medicine entitled “Clearing the Smoke: the Science Base for Tobacco Harm Reduction.”3,5 In this report, products which support tobacco harm reduction (THR) were defined as “ … harm-reducing if [they] lower total tobacco-related mortality and morbidity even though use of that product may involve continued exposure to tobacco-related toxicants.” Such a toxicant reduction approach to THR involving advising smokers to switch to using vaping products is supported by an increasing number of public health bodies, including the UK Office of Health Improvement and Disparities (formerly known as Public Health England), the UK Royal College of Physicians, the New Zealand Ministry of Health, and Health Canada.9,10,11,12,13,14 In support of this harm reduction role for vaping products, increasing amounts of evidence coming from both observational and interventional studies suggests that vaping products can support complete abstinence from cigarette smoking,15,16,17,18,19,20 and potentially to a greater extent than conventional nicotine replacement therapies.16,18,20

In this article, we report findings from an analytical chemistry study assessing levels of a number of analytes in the aerosols machine-generated from four flavored variants of a pod-system vaping product, the ElfBar Elfa, which vaporizes a liquid solution using an alloy mesh heating coil. The analytes assessed were chosen because they are present in global regulatory and quality standards and recommendations for analytes to assess in tobacco/nicotine products, and included the nine analytes which were mandated for lowering in cigarette smoke in the World Health Organization (WHO) Study Group on Tobacco Product Regulation (TobReg) proposal21 and analytes on the FDA HPHC list.6 Our findings are used to ascertain the potential for exposure to toxicants during use of the assessed vaping products, both in absolute terms and in relation to toxicant exposure during cigarette smoking or levels prescribed in relevant exposure safety limits for medicines or occupational exposure, and we further use the data to assess whether the vaping products assessed can contribute to THR.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the levels of 17 analytes (see Table S1) in the aerosol from four flavor variants of the ElfBar Elfa vaping product and compared these levels either to those reported in cigarette smoke or to levels prescribed in relevant exposure safety limits for medicines or occupational exposure. Analytes were chosen by mapping those which are recommended for measurement in tobacco and nicotine products by many global regulatory authorities, including those listed in the U.K. Tobacco and Related Products Regulations 2016, the EU Tobacco Products Directive, the British Standards Institute Publicly Available Specification 8855, the Association Française de Normalisation, the US FDA Final Guidance (Revised) for Premarket Tobacco Product Applications for Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems, and the product safety requirements issued by the New Zealand Ministry of Health. Additionally, those analytes which are mandated for lowering in cigarette smoke in the WHO TobReg proposal21 were also considered, and we also included a comprehensive set of metals in our analyses due to the nature of the Elfa vaping product design. Our findings show that a number of toxicants present in cigarette smoke were either undetectable or below quantifiable levels in vaping product aerosol. These included CO, the TSNAs NNK and NNN, acrolein, benzo[a]pyrene, 1,3-butadiene and benzene, all of which are present in cigarette smoke and listed in the WHO TobReg proposal,21 as well as cadmium and arsenic. The carbonyl compounds formaldehyde and acetaldehyde were found in the vaping product aerosols for all four flavors, but these were found at very low levels ranging from 99.6% to 99.9% lower than the levels previously reported in 3R4F reference cigarette smoke.22 Overall, these findings indicate elimination of, or highly significant reductions in, a number of toxicants with a prescribed link to smoking-related disease6 in the vaping product aerosols. These findings are of importance. Vaping product technologies are continually evolving, including those elements of vaping products which wick the liquid solution and heat it to generate an aerosol. Previous studies have reported aerosol chemistry assessments for vaping products which utilize other wicking and aerosolizing technologies, for example coiled metallic heating elements wrapped around a cotton wick,24,25 a metal nichrome coil wrapped around a silica wick26 or a ceramic wick and flat nichrome heating coil.27 The current study assessed vaping products which utilize mesh heating coils, a coil design which is increasingly prevalent in vaping products. Our findings represent, therefore, an indication of the exposure and harm reduction potential of a modern vaping product which uses an innovative means of aerosol generation.

While neither exposure or risk were directly assessed in this study, it is most likely that these aerosol chemistry differences would lead to significant reductions in both exposure and risk among smokers who exclusively switch to the use of these vaping products. While the vaping products assessed in the study deliver nicotine to users, and this likely will sustain dependence on the use of nicotine among users who switch to their use from cigarette smoking, the relative harms associated with such dependence are likely to be minimal and certainly far lower than the harms associated with cigarette smoking. In this regard, it has been suggested that in order to provide a switching benefit and to enable them to compete with combustible cigarettes, vaping products must possess some degree of dependence potential (i.e., addictiveness).28,29 Ideally THR is best supported by products which simultaneously optimize appeal and dependence potential while, as demonstrated in this study, minimizing potential harmfulness.28 Overall therefore, it is likely that the vaping products assessed in this study can meaningfully contribute to THR.

Regarding metals, some of those assessed in this study were also absent from the aerosol of all flavor variants the vaping products assessed, including cadmium and arsenic. Some metals, such as lead and nickel, were detected in the aerosols for all products, though these were only found at low or very low microgram levels which were just above their respective limits of quantification. Other metals were detected and quantified in some of the aerosols, including chromium, nickel, aluminum, iron, and tin, though again these were found only at low or very low levels. None of these metals were reported in cigarette smoke.22 Therefore, in order to contextualize our findings and to assess whether exposure to these metals at the levels found in our study is likely to be of toxicological concern, we performed calculations using an assumed level of number of puffs per day to derive potential exposure levels among users. These were then compared with either USP PDE levels for inhalational medicines,30 or the REL as defined by NIOSH.31 It should be noted that the estimated number of puffs (1,200) is likely a significant overestimate and was based on the potential for 600 puffs per day, and an uncertainty factor of two, as previously described.23 Based on these calculations, we deem that presence of low or very low levels of metals in the vaping product aerosols assessed is not of toxicological concern. For lead, chromium and nickel, levels were between approximately 3- and 46-times lower than PDE levels depending on the metal and the flavor. For aluminum, iron and tin, levels were between approximately 4,000- and 40,000-times lower than the RELs, also depending on the metal and the flavor. These findings are concordant with those of others, including an assessment of a wide range of published studies to determine aerosol metal levels in a variety of different vaping products.23

Given that our data indicate the potential for substantial reductions or elimination of exposure to toxic chemicals among those smokers who switch exclusively to the vaping products assessed, along with previous determinations of the significantly reduced toxicant burden of the use of vaping products compared with cigarette smoking,8,9,11,12,13,26,32,33,34 communication of our findings may reassure and support smokers in making an informed decision to switch to their use. Misperceptions of the absolute and relative risks of the use of vaping products have previously been reported to be a barrier to initiation among smokers.35 In this regard, Jackson et al.36 reported that approximately half of smokers perceive vaping product use to be just as harmful if not more harmful than cigarette smoking, leading the authors to conclude that this misperception is a health risk in and of itself as it may discourage smokers from substantially reducing their harm by switching to vaping products. Better communication about the health risks of vaping products, using data such as those reported in this study, is greatly needed so that adult smokers can make informed choices about the nicotine products they use. This has the potential to change the current status quo, in which large proportions of smokers report not having ever tried using vaping products,37 and subsequently lead to improvements in both individual and population-level health.

In summary, our assessment of a broad range of analytes demonstrates the absence of, or significant reductions in, a number of chemical toxicants with links to human disease in the aerosol from a vaping product compared with cigarette smoke. For smokers switching to the exclusive use of the vaping products assessed, this will likely result in reduced exposure and reduced risk. In addition, while we demonstrate that some metals were found in the vaping product aerosols, these are at such low levels so as not to give rise to toxicological concern. These findings have important implications for our understanding of the potential role for vaping products in supporting THR.

Limitations of the study

This study and its findings are subject to some limitations. Firstly, we only assessed analyte levels in the aerosols from a small number of flavor variants of a specific type of vaping product. Our findings may not be generalizable to other flavor variants of the same vaping product, or to the many different types vaping products that are available to consumers. Secondly, we used a standardized puffing regimen to generate aerosols for all the products assessed.38,39 Real-world use patterns, in which users puff on the vaping differently to the puffing regimen prescribed by the ISO/CORESTA standard used in this study, may give rise to different absolute and relative aerosol toxicant yields. In this regard, many puffing topography studies have described a variety of real-world use patterns among users of different device types with different physical and e-liquid characteristics.40 However, our use of a standardized puffing regimen facilitates future between-product comparisons among different vaping products, and also reduces concerns expressed by others that many vaping product aerosol assessments have used unrealistic regimens for the machine generation of vaping product aerosols.41 Thirdly, and related to the second point, our machine-derived aerosol toxicant yields may not be indicative of actual exposure among users, and human clinical studies assessing biomarkers of exposure are required in order to assess exposure from different types and variants of vaping products relative to exposure from cigarette smoking. However, given either the absence of, or the significant reductions in, a number of analytes including those mandated for lowering in cigarette smoke in the WHO TobReg proposal, it is likely that use of the assessed vaping products would greatly reduce or eliminate exposure to toxic chemicals among smokers who exclusively switch to using them. This suggestion is in accordance with many studies which have demonstrated significant reductions in biomarkers of exposure among smokers who switch to exclusive use of vaping products.42,43,44,45,46,47,48,49 While the current study suggests that exposure reductions are likely in smokers who switch to using the ElfBar Elfa vaping products, this would have to be verified in a clinical study assessing these products. Fourthly, we only measured 17 analytes in the vaping product aerosols, and our findings are therefore limited to potential exposure to those chemicals. Other studies have used untargeted analyses to assess vaping product aerosol composition,33,50,51,52,53 with the presence of some analytes attributable to flavoring ingredients, thermal degradation products and device constituents.33 Additional studies are required to determine the presence of further toxicants in the vaping products assessed in this study, as well as to assess their potential toxicological significance under conditions of assumed normal use using appropriate toxicological risk assessment methodologies. Fifthly, our use of occupational exposure standards in order to assess the potential for metal exposure relative to regulatory safety limits makes assumptions regarding the inhaled air volume and exposure context which may not reflect real-world use and exposure among users, although we did calculate the potential for metal exposure based on an extreme number of puffs (1,200 per day) which likely greatly overestimates actual use. Future studies may need to consider assessing user topography in order to determine real-world use patterns and their variability among users, in order to enable more accurate estimations, including worst-case scenarios, of the potential for exposure and risk. Similarly, assessing aerosol yields against regulatory safety limits, which refer to medicinal or occupational rather than general consumer and vulnerable population exposure, may also constitute a limitation. However, such an approach was taken in the absence of other means of assessing the potential risk associated with the use of the vaping products assessed in this study for the analytes assessed using this approach. And sixthly, we have used historical data for analyte yields in 3R4F reference cigarette smoke22 in order to compare cigarette smoke yields with vaping product aerosol yields. Due to the potential for analytical variances, for example between different laboratories or due to different calibration methods being used, this creates a potential limitation in the comparisons made. However, due to the large differences (≥99.6%) reported between vaping product aerosol and cigarette smoke analyte yields, any such variation is unlikely to affect the overall findings from these comparisons.

Method details

Study products

Images of the products assessed in this study can be found in Figure 1. The ElfBar Elfa vaping product is a closed-system pod device with a rechargeable battery section. The battery has a capacity of 500mAh and has a 9.3W output. Vaporisation of the e-liquid solution is by electronic heating of a 1.2Ω mesh coil, wrapped around a cotton wick which draws the e-liquid to the coil. The mesh coil is an alloy composed of iron, chromium and aluminium (FeCrAl). The e-liquid solution is comprised of 2% w/w tobacco-derived nicotine, benzoic acid as a protonating agent, propylene glycol (PG), vegetable glycerine (VG), and flavouring agents. The pH of the e-liquids was 4.32 (Strawberry Ice), 4.55 (Watermelon), 4.76 (Spearmint) and 4.51 (Super Tobacco). The PG:VG ratios for the e-liquids are 60:40 (Strawberry Ice, Watermelon and Super Tobacco) and 57:43 (Spearmint). Flavour selection was based on sales data, i.e., we studied those 4 products which account for the greatest sales volumes and therefore have the greatest use prevalence.

Vaping product aerosol generation

Vaping product aerosol generation and analysis were performed at Broughton Life Sciences (Earby, Lancashire, UK), an independent laboratory accredited by the United Kingdom Accreditation Scheme (UKAS; laboratory number 10636). All analytical methods were accredited to ISO/IEC 17025:2017.54

Aerosol from the study vaping products was generated in accordance with ISO 20768:2018.39 This regimen is consistent with CORESTA Recommended Method No. 81,38 a methodology which has previously been suggested as appropriate for assessing aerosol yields from pod-system vaping products.41 This regimen generated aerosol using 55 ml puffs of 3-second duration every 30 seconds. The puff profile was a square-wave, and the vaping product was held in the puffing machine at an angle of 45 degrees. Puffing was conducted using 4 puff blocks, each with 25 puffs. Between each aerosol generation run, 3 ‘clearing puffs’ were taken with no vaping product present to ensure that aerosol was removed from the flow path following collection puffs.

Analyte selection

Analyte selection was based on global regulatory and quality standards and recommendations for analytes to assess in tobacco/nicotine products. These included the U.K. TRPR, the EU TPD, the BSI PAS 8855, the Association Française de Normalisation, the US FDA Final Guidance (Revised) for PMTAs for ENDS, product safety requirements issued by the New Zealand Ministry of Health, and the WHO TobReg proposal. Full details of each of these standards/recommendations, as well as information concerning potential or known links to tobacco-related disease outcomes due to exposure to each of the analytes as described by the US FDA, can be found in Table S1.

Analytical methods

Details of each of the analytical methods used to measure analytes in the aerosol from the study vaping products are in Table S2. Blank (air) control samples were prepared for vaping product emissions by puffing empty ports on the same machines during collection of the vaping product aerosols and the blank controls were analysed with the test samples.

Comparator product aerosol collection & analysis

Mainstream smoke yields from 3R4F reference cigarettes were taken from a published study which generated cigarette smoke using the ISO55 machine smoking regimen (35 ml puff volume, 2-second puff duration, 60-second interpuff interval, and no blocking of filter ventilation), also at an ISO 17025 accredited laboratory.22

Quantification and statistical analysis

Aerosol generation and analytical chemistry assessments were performed in triplicate. Data are presented using descriptive statistics (means ± standard deviations [SD]) from the three replicates. Aerosol toxicant yields were background corrected using data from air blank (control) collections. For calculations, values of limit of detection (LOD)/2 were used for values which were <LOD, and the mean of the LOD and the limit of quantification (LOQ) was used for values which were >LOD but <LOQ. All data analyses were conducted using Excel for Mac version 16.108.2 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, US).

Comparison of ElfBar aerosol and 3R4F reference cigarette smoke yields

For the comparisons of ElfBar aerosol yields with 3R4F cigarette smoke yields, the calculations were performed per 100 puffs, since in the laboratory analyses yields were measured across 100 puffs (4 blocks of 25 puffs). To perform the calculations, initially per-puff yields for 3R4F cigarette smoke were calculated using data from Eldridge et al.,22 by taking the per-cigarette yields and dividing the yield by the number of puffs taken to generate that yield. This approach was taken because in Eldridge et al.,22 for the different groups of analytes (e.g., carbonyls, organics etc) the number of puffs taken to generate per-cigarette yields were slightly different. We then took the calculated per-cigarette yields and multiplied these by 100. Subsequent to this, we calculated the percentage differences in 100-puff yields between ElfBar aerosol and 3R4F cigarette smoke for each of the analytes of interest.

Comparison of potential exposure levels with regulatory safety limits

A previously described method was used to compare potential for exposure to metals during use of the study vaping products with regulatory safety limits.23 In brief, for the metals lead, chromium and nickel we used the Permissible Daily Exposure (PDE) values for inhalational medicines as defined by the USP.30 The PDEs refer to chronic exposure in adults of 50 kg body weight.

For the metals not included in the USP list (aluminium, iron and tin), we used the REL as defined by NIOSH.31 RELs represent a time-weighted average concentration in the air for up to a 10-hour working day during a 40-hour working week.

To evaluate daily exposure from study vaping product use, the average daily number of puffs was assumed to be 600 puffs per day and an uncertainty factor of 2 was applied,23 leading to our estimates of potential total daily exposure from 1,200 puffs based on our analytical findings of analyte levels per 100 puffs. For the RELs, the average daily intake was based on the amount of air inhaled over a period of 10 hours. For this, we used the European Medicines Agency average daily inhaled air volume of 20 m3 to calculate a 10-hour inhaled air volume of 8.3 m3. Thus, RELs were multiplied by 8.3 to calculate the acceptable daily exposure. For the PDEs, no assumptions were made regarding respiratory volumes since these safety limits are not associated with the amount of air inhaled.23
