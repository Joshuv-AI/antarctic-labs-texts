Tuberculosis-Related Outcomes in Dermatologic Patients Receiving Systemic Therapy
Authors: Kaixuan Liu, Ziyi Zeng, Wenjie Lu, Yin Yang, Tong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow does tuberculosis (TB) risk vary across systemic therapies used in treating dermatologic diseases?FindingsIn this systematic review and meta-analysis of 31 studies including 15 005 patients, pooled incidence of active TB was 1.0%, and of latent TB infection conversion, 4.3%. Conventional immunosuppressants were associated with higher TB incidence, whereas tumor necrosis factor inhibitors showed higher risk among biologics, particularly in high-burden regions.MeaningThese findings suggest that TB risk in dermatologic patients receiving systemic therapy varies by treatment mechanism and epidemiologic context, supporting risk-stratified screening and monitoring strategies.

Introduction

Patients with chronic inflammatory dermatoses, such as psoriasis, often require long-term systemic therapy. Conventional immunosuppressants, including cyclosporine and methotrexate, effectively control disease activity but exert broad immunosuppressive effects and have been associated with increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections, particularly tuberculosis (TB).1,2 The introduction of biologic agents—including tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, interleukin (IL)-17 inhibitors, and ustekinumab (IL-12/23 p40 inhibitor)—has revolutionized treatment.1,3 Given their selective immune targeting, TB risk profiles may differ substantially from conventional therapies and vary across biologic classes.4,5

Mechanistic evidence supports this heterogeneity among biologic agents. TNF-α is essential for granuloma maintenance6; consequently, TNF inhibition is consistently associated with TB reactivation.7,8 In contrast, IL-17 inhibitors and ustekinumab target pathways that are less central to antimycobacterial immunity, suggesting a comparatively lower risk. However, systematic quantitative comparisons across biologic classes remain limited.3,9 Furthermore, most studies prioritize active TB as an end point, giving less attention to latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) conversion (a change from a negative to a positive tuberculin skin test and/or interferon-gamma release assay result). As a sensitive early marker, LTBI conversion identifies treatment-associated risk more effectively than active TB, particularly given the shorter observation windows typical of some clinical trials. Incorporating this metric is critical for differentiating TB impact and burden across varying global geographic regions and developing refined risk-stratification strategies.

Evidence uncertainty has led to inconsistent clinical guidelines. While several national and international guidelines recommend LTBI screening before initiating biologic therapy,10,11,12,13 emerging statements suggest routine testing may be unnecessary for newer biologic classes.14,15 Differences also exist in recommendations regarding biologic use in patients with active TB or LTBI.8,16 To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the incidence of active TB and LTBI conversion among dermatologic patients receiving systemic therapy, compare risks across biologic classes, and evaluate the modifying effect of regional TB burden.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261293550). We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guideline.

Literature Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science (Core Collection), and the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL) from database inception to October 1, 2025. Gray literature sources and clinical trial registries were also screened, and reference lists of included studies were manually reviewed to identify additional eligible records. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in eMethods 1 to 4 in Supplement 1.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Eligible studies were randomized clinical trials and observational studies enrolling patients with dermatologic diseases receiving systemic therapy and reporting active TB or LTBI conversion. Detailed eligibility criteria, outcome definitions, and therapy classifications are provided in eMethods 5 to 8 in Supplement 1.

Study selection was independently performed by 2 investigators (K.L. and Z.Z.) through title and abstract screening, followed by full-text review. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third investigator (W.L.). Data extraction included study characteristics, patient demographic information, treatment regimens, follow-up duration, and event counts for tuberculosis-related outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

This study used single-arm incidence as the primary outcome. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to pool the incidence of tuberculosis-related events following systemic therapy, with corresponding 95% CIs. Incidence was calculated as the number of events divided by the population at risk. The LTBI conversion rate was defined as the proportion of newly identified LTBI cases among patients with negative TB test results at baseline.

Because incidence data tend to exhibit skewed distributions and unstable variances when proportions approach 0 or 1, logit transformation was applied to study-specific incidence estimates prior to pooling. After meta-analytic aggregation, inverse logit transformation was performed to obtain pooled estimates on the original incidence scale, thereby improving the accuracy and interpretability of the summary estimates.

Random-effects models were used to pool incidence estimates, accounting for both within-study sampling error and between-study heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran Q test and quantified with the I2 statistic, with the between-study variance (τ2) also reported. Study weights in the pooled analyses were determined using the inverse-variance method under the random-effects framework.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to biologic mechanism of action and regional TB burden (high-burden vs low-burden settings). We classified regions as high-burden if they were listed among the World Health Organization 30 high TB burden countries for 2021 to 2025.17 All other countries were considered to have low burden. In addition, leave-1-out sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimates.

When the number of included studies permitted, funnel plots were used to qualitatively assess small-study effects and potential publication bias. The definition and direction of incidence outcomes were kept consistent across all analyses to ensure comparability between results. All statistical analyses were performed using Python, version 3.13 (Python Software Foundation).

LTBI Conversion

Overall and Biologic Agent−Treated Subgroup Incidence

Twenty-two studies contributed to the analysis of LTBI conversion, yielding 27 independent cohorts. Among 9915 who had negative TST or IGRA screening prior to biologic therapy, 261 conversion events were observed, corresponding to a pooled incidence of 4.3% (95% CI, 3.0%-6.1%; I2 = 83%; eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). In patients treated with biologic agents (8767 with negative baseline results), 148 LTBI conversions occurred, with a pooled incidence of 4.0% (95% CI, 2.7%-5.9%; I2 = 81%; eFigure 2 in Supplement 1).

Stratified Analysis by Biologic Mechanism

After stratification by biologic mechanism of action, LTBI conversion risk differed across biologic classes (Figure 2). TNF inhibitors were associated with the highest incidence (4.9%; 95% CI, 2.9%-8.1%; I2 = 83%). IL-17 inhibitors showed a lower incidence (2.2%; 95% CI, 0.8%-6.0%; I2 = 79%), and ustekinumab showed the lowest incidence (1.8%; 95% CI, 0.4%-6.9%; I2 = 79%). For IL-23 inhibitors, only 1 study was available with no observed events; therefore, results were summarized descriptively.

LTBI Conversion in Patients Receiving Conventional Systemic Immunosuppressive Therapy

Among patients receiving anti-CD20 therapy, immunosuppressive agents, or glucocorticoids, 113 LTBI conversion events were observed among 1148 patients (9.8%). Although anti-CD20 is a biologic agent, it was excluded from the main biologic subgroup and analyzed here due to limited data and the inability to isolate its outcomes from concurrent conventional immunosuppressants. Consequently, these heterogeneous results were summarized descriptively (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Stratified Analysis by Regional TB Burden

To compare the distribution of LTBI conversion rates across different epidemiologic settings, subgroup analyses were conducted according to regional TB burden (high vs low; Figure 3). For TNF inhibitors, LTBI conversion rates were higher in high-burden regions (5.5%; 95% CI, 1.8%-15.4%; I2 = 78%) than in low-burden regions (4.6%; 95% CI, 2.4%-8.6%; I2 = 86%). For IL-17 inhibitors, the rates were 3.0% (95% CI, 1.0%-8.7%; I2 = 50%) in high-burden regions and 1.4% (95% CI, 0.2%-9.8%; I2 = 91%) in low-burden regions.

Active TB

Overall and Biologic-Treated Subgroup Incidence

Among all dermatologic patients receiving systemic therapy (38 independent cohorts totaling 15 005 participants), the pooled incidence of active TB was 1.0% (95% CI, 0.6%-1.8%; I2 = 91%; eFigure 3 in Supplement 1). When the analysis was restricted to patients treated with biologic agents (31 independent cohorts totaling 10 271 participants), the pooled incidence of active TB decreased to 0.6% (95% CI, 0.4%-0.9%; I2 = 28%; eFigure 4 in Supplement 1).

Stratified Analysis by Biologic Mechanism

Stratified by biologic mechanism, the distribution of active TB risk was similar to LTBI conversion, although absolute incidence rates were lower overall (Figure 4). TNF inhibitors had the highest pooled incidence (0.9%; 95% CI, 0.6%-1.4%; I2 = 1%), followed by ustekinumab (0.7%; 95% CI, 0.1%-4.4%; I2 = 68%) and IL-17 inhibitors (0.4%; 95% CI, 0.2%-1.1%; I2 = 17%). Active TB was evaluated among dermatologic patients receiving immunosuppressive agents or glucocorticoids (eFigure 5 in Supplement 1). The pooled incidence of active TB was 5.9% (95% CI, 2.6%-12.6%), with considerable between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 96%).

Sensitivity Analyses and Publication Bias

Leave-1-out sensitivity analyses demonstrated stable results across overall and stratified analyses. No individual study materially altered pooled incidence estimates or the direction of effect (eFigures 6-13 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated LTBI conversion and active TB among dermatology patients receiving systemic therapy and demonstrates that TB-related risk is heterogeneous. We found that risk differed between conventional immunosuppressants and biologic agents, varied across biologic mechanisms, and was modified by regional TB burden. Conventional therapies were associated with higher active TB incidence, whereas biologic agents showed lower overall rates. Among biologic agents, TNF inhibitors carried higher risks for both LTBI conversion and active TB, particularly in high-burden settings, whereas IL-17 inhibitors and ustekinumab showed lower pooled incidences. These findings support risk-stratified approaches to TB screening and monitoring rather than uniform management strategies.

LTBI Conversion as an Earlier TB-Related Outcome

Our pooled data show that LTBI conversion (4.3%) was more common than active TB (1.0%). This finding suggests that focusing only on active TB may underestimate the overall burden of TB-related outcomes. Because we included only patients who tested negative at baseline, the observed conversions likely represent de novo infections acquired during follow-up rather than reactivation of prior latent infection. Thus, LTBI conversion may serve as a sensitive, earlier indicator of TB-related risk. Importantly, it reflects not merely environmental exposure, but the dynamic interplay between this exposure and drug-induced immune vulnerabilities, capturing how distinct therapies alter host defenses.

Among treatment categories, conventional immunosuppressants showed a notably higher LTBI conversion rate (9.8%) than biologic agents (4.0%). However, LTBI data for conventional agents are sparse and derived from limited cohorts.29 Therefore, the high proportion should be viewed as a signal that warrants attention, rather than a stable risk estimate. Furthermore, socioeconomic confounding cannot be excluded because patients receiving biologic agents may have better access to health care and live in lower-exposure environments. The historical focus on active TB rather than LTBI conversion likely reflects limited emphasis on latent infection and variability in screening methods.

Within biologic agents, LTBI conversion showed a consistent ordering: highest for TNF inhibitors, followed by IL-17 inhibitors, and lowest for ustekinumab. This relative ordering persisted across all main and stratified analyses despite statistical heterogeneity. This consistency suggests that different biologic mechanisms differentially influence host susceptibility to infection establishment after exposure to Mycobacterium tuberculosis. However, the lower rates observed with ustekinumab should be interpreted with caution, as most studies of this drug class were conducted in low-burden regions, whereas IL-17 inhibitor studies included more high-burden settings.

Active TB Risk: Contrast of Conventional vs Biologic Therapies

Active TB is a clinically meaningful end point, and its distribution further reinforces differences among systemic therapies. Conventional immunosuppressants exhibited substantially higher rates of active TB (5.9%) than biologic agents, with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 96%). This high variance is likely driven by diverse treatment regimens and the inclusion of nonpsoriasis cohorts (eg, systemic lupus erythematosus) from high-burden settings. Detailed study-level characteristics are summarized in eTable 2 in Supplement 1 to facilitate interpretation.

Among biologic agents, active TB events were concentrated in TNF inhibitor cohorts. In contrast, IL-17 inhibitors and ustekinumab were associated with markedly fewer events, with numerous studies reporting zero cases. Notably, while ustekinumab showed a lower LTBI conversion rate than IL-17 inhibitors, the incidence of active TB was lower for IL-17 inhibitors (0.4%) than for ustekinumab (0.7%). This pattern is corroborated by recent routine clinical evidence49 and comprehensive expert consensus15 demonstrating no TB reactivations among patients receiving IL-17, IL-23 or ustekinumab, even in those with LTBI. However, this does not imply an absolute absence of risk for these newer classes. Rather, it suggests a significantly lower population-level risk, underscoring that active TB ultimately arises from a complex interplay of drug mechanism, regional TB exposure, and individual host susceptibility, rather than from treatment alone.

Biological Rationale for Mechanism-Specific Risk Differences

The observed risk gradient has immunologic plausibility. Conventional immunosuppressants (eg, cyclosporine, methotrexate) broadly diminish host defenses and thus increase susceptibility to various pathogens, including TB.50 Biologic agents produce more targeted immune modulation, and the role of each targeted pathway within anti-TB immunity differs. TNF-α is central to granuloma formation and maintenance; therefore, its inhibition has a clear mechanistic association with TB reactivation risk.7 Conversely, the IL-17 pathway mainly contributes to inflammation amplification and mucocutaneous barrier immunity; its direct role in controlling Mycobacterium tuberculosis may be limited, which accounts for the lowest observed active TB rates with IL-17 inhibitors.1,49

The outcomes for ustekinumab (an IL-12/23 p40 inhibitor) are consistent with this rationale. As IL-12 is central to Th1-mediated antimycobacterial immunity,51 p40 blockade may confer a higher reactivation risk than IL-17 inhibition. Accordingly, we observed a higher incidence of active TB with ustekinumab. Its lower LTBI conversion rate likely reflects methodologic constraints—namely, sparse events in low-burden settings—rather than a true protective effect.

Regional TB Burden as a Key Modifier of Risk

Regional TB burden emerged as an important modifier. Across identical biologic classes, both LTBI conversion and active TB rates were consistently higher in high-burden settings. This finding indicates that treatment-related risk is not isolated from exposure risk in the environment. It challenges the one-size-fits-all approach to screening and prevention. In low-burden regions, especially for patients receiving lower-risk biologic agents, the benefit of continued intensive screening and universal prophylaxis deserves reconsideration. Conversely, in high-burden regions, even biologic agents considered lower risk may not eliminate significant TB risk. For conventional immunosuppressants, this regional risk amplification appears particularly pronounced, although sparse data necessitates further investigation.

Clinical Implications

Although prior guidelines emphasize active TB,8,52 incorporating LTBI conversion as an early marker supports a shift toward risk-stratified management based on drug mechanisms and regional epidemiology. Rigorous screening and monitoring remain essential for conventional immunosuppressants and TNF inhibitors, particularly in high-burden settings; similarly, despite low observed event rates, the critical role of IL-12 in granuloma integrity warrants continued caution and preventive therapy with ustekinumab.15

In contrast, the very low TB incidence with IL-17 inhibitors in low-burden regions calls into question the need for uniform intensive screening, consistent with recent guidance against routine LTBI testing for IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors in psoriasis.14 Although a recent database study supported universal surveillance across biologic agents,53 this may be substantially confounded by screening bias and diagnostic misclassification54; moreover, as these data are largely derived from psoriasis cohorts, any relaxation of screening should be confined to psoriasis patients, with cautious extrapolation to other immune-mediated diseases.

Strengths and Limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in its concurrent evaluation of LTBI conversion and active TB across both conventional and biologic therapies, further stratified by drug mechanism and regional TB burden. However, several limitations must be acknowledged.

First, analyses were based on single-arm incidence and did not allow direct comparisons between therapies. Second, grouping by drug class rather than disease type assumes that TB risk is primarily driven by immunosuppressive mechanisms, whereas underlying disease may still confound the results. Third, no eligible studies reported tuberculosis outcomes for JAK inhibitors in psoriasis, and for cyclosporine and rituximab, drug-specific data could not be extracted because of concurrent therapies and lack of stratification. Fourth, data on IL-23 inhibitors were too limited for quantitative analysis; thus, our findings do not apply to this drug class.

Conclusions

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that TB-related risk among dermatologic patients receiving systemic therapy is heterogeneous and shaped by treatment category, drug mechanism, and regional TB burden. Overall, conventional immunosuppressants were associated with higher TB incidence, whereas biologic agents showed lower rates. Within biologic agents, TNF inhibitors carried relatively higher risk, particularly in high-burden settings; IL-17 inhibitors showed lower risk and consistent findings across analyses; and although overall event rates remained low, current evidence suggests ustekinumab carries a slightly higher risk of progression to active TB than IL-17 inhibitors.

These findings advocate for a paradigm shift from universal TB screening toward risk-stratified management tailored to specific therapies and epidemiologic contexts. Future prospective studies with standardized screening and individual-level data are essential to refine these estimates. Ultimately, as the use of ustekinumab and newer IL-23 inhibitors expands, studies of larger, longer-term cohorts will be crucial to inform definitive TB screening and monitoring guidelines.
