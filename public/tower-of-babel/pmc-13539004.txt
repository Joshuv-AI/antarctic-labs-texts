Individual and Organizational Level Barriers to Post-exposure Prophylaxis Uptake for Hepatitis B Virus Among Midwives in Northern Ghana: A Qualitative Enquiry
Authors: Mercy Tiko, Vivian Efua Senoo-Dogbey, Charles Ampong Adjei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) poses a significant occupational risk to healthcare workers (HCWs), particularly midwives in highly endemic settings such as Ghana, where frequent exposure to blood and bodily fluids during clinical practice increases their risk of occupational exposure and infection. Despite the availability of HBV vaccination and Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP), uptake remains suboptimal due to multiple barriers. This study explores individual and organizational-level barriers to HBV PEP in northern Ghana.MethodA qualitative exploratory study was conducted in the Kassena-Nankana West District of Ghana, involving 20 midwives recruited purposively from three healthcare facilities. Semi-structured interviews were conducted, and thematic analysis was used to identify key patterns in line with the socioecological model.ResultsTwo major overarching themes emerged across the individual and organizational socioecological levels. At the individual level, participants demonstrated inadequate knowledge of post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) for HBV, including inadequate knowledge of the concept of PEP and HBV-specific protocols and guidelines. At the organizational level, health facility barriers included unstructured exposure reporting and PEP management pathways, absence of facility-level HBV-specific protocols and guidelines, and inadequate training on PEP for HBV were the factors identified as affecting PEP uptake.ConclusionDespite the availability of a national occupational health and safety policy recommending structured HBV PEP, protocol implementation at the facility level was weak, leaving midwives unprotected. The study underscores the urgent need for improved HBV education, structured exposure management pathways, and strengthened facility-level policies to enhance HBV PEP uptake. Addressing these gaps is crucial to reducing the risk of occupational HBV infection and ensuring midwives' safety in highly endemic settings.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a global issue. Disease burden estimates reported in 2021 indicated that approximately 296 million people worldwide were living with chronic HBV infection, with the African region reporting the highest prevalence at 5.7%. 1 The infection is efficiently transmitted through percutaneous and per-mucosal exposures to contaminated blood and body fluids, and this is the basis for its classification as an occupational hazard to Healthcare Workers (HCWs). 2 It has been documented that 37% of all HBV infections among HCWs are due to occupational exposures to blood and body fluids contaminated with HBV. 3 The risk of exposure and infection with HBV is higher among HCWs who work in highly endemic regions of the world. 2 Ghana is highly endemic for HBV, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses reporting prevalence rates of between 12%- 14%,4,5 with a corresponding high prevalence of 5.9% among HCWs. 6 Ironically, despite the high HBV disease burden in endemic regions, studies have reported relatively low HBV vaccination rates among HCWs. 7 This, coupled with high occupational exposure to blood and body fluids, significantly increases the risk of HBV infection in this population.8,9 For instance, despite Ghana being an HBV-endemic country with a prevalence of 12–14% in the general population,4,5 HBV vaccination coverage among HCWs remains low, estimated at 44.4% to 53.4%.10,11 The combination of high endemicity, low vaccination rates, and frequent exposure to blood and body fluids through percutaneous and mucocutaneous routes12-14 places Ghanaian HCWs at an increased risk of HBV infection.

Midwives in northern Ghana face increased HBV exposure due to high regional endemicity and prevalence among pregnant women, 15 alongside frequent contact with potentially contaminated body fluids. 16 Staff shortages contribute to heavy workloads and greater risk of needlestick injuries, 17 while limited training, inadequate PPE, and low vaccination coverage heighten vulnerability.11,18 Elevated rates of occupational injuries further compound this risk, 19 and the need to reassess barriers to HBV PEP uptake in this sub-population of HCWs.

Occupational PEP refers to the preventive medical treatment administered to HCWs following potential exposure to infectious agents in the workplace. It typically involves timely intervention, including wound care, administration of prophylactic medications and close monitoring to reduce the risk of infection and disease transmission after exposure through needlestick injuries and mucocutaneous contact. 20

Knowledge alone does not guarantee HBV PEP uptake. Although awareness shapes risk perception, actual use depends on decision-making at the time of exposure and the availability of clear guidelines and institutional support. 21 System-level barriers often prevent knowledge from translating into timely and appropriate action.

Effective PEP for HBV programs in health facilities depends largely on the interaction between the health facility and the exposed HCW. 22 Health facilities are obliged to institute exposure reporting systems with very experienced and skilled focal persons who are trained and fully resourced to respond to the needs of the exposed HCWs. The availability of facility-level protocols and guidelines for effective implementation of PEP, coupled with an appropriate stock of recommended testing supplies and other PEP logistics, is a major component of an effective PEP programme in a health facility. 23 All these recommendations are explicitly outlined in Ghana’s Occupational Health and Safety Policy, which mandates all health facilities to implement its guidelines to protect employees from HBV infection. 24

Despite the existence and dissemination of Ghana’s Occupational Health and Safety Policy, adherence to the guidelines in the policy remains suboptimal, as reported in a recent study. 25

These factors, along with HCW-related factors, may have contributed to the 5.9% prevalence of HBV reported among Ghanaian HCWs. 6 Numerous studies have examined the uptake and barriers to HBV prevention practices among Ghanaian HCWs. However, most of these studies have primarily focused on HBV vaccination, standard precautionary practice, hand washing, PPE use and prevention of occupational injuries, with limited attention given to PEP for HBV. The very few studies conducted in the area of PEP focused only on PEP knowledge,11,26,27 with no attention to other factors that facilitate or hinder PEP uptake. This gap in research and practice may lead to missed opportunities for timely intervention following occupational exposure, increasing the risk of HBV transmission in this population. This study is guided by the Socioecological Model (SEM), which highlights how health behaviours are shaped by both individual and institutional factors. 28 This present study, therefore, sought to explore barriers to PEP uptake for HBV among a sub-group of HCWs in northern Ghana.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was a hospital-based study that utilized an exploratory descriptive qualitative approach to assess barriers to PEP for HBV uptake in northern Ghana. Exploratory research is conducted when enough is not known about a phenomenon, and the researcher wants to investigate a problem that has not been clearly defined.29,30 This study was conducted in the second half of the year 2024.

Study Setting

The study was conducted in the Kassena-Nankana West District of the Upper East Region of Ghana. The district is one of the fifteen districts in the Upper East Region of Ghana and has Paga as its capital town. The district was chosen because it is located in a region which is considered one of the highly endemic regions in Ghana in terms of HBV infections. 15 These three (3) health facilities were selected purposively from 63 public and privately owned facilities because they provide maternal and child health services and also have midwives in the majority compared to other categories of staff. The study area is characterised by a poor healthcare system, with a population coverage of 92,641 people. Two study sites with health centre status are 20-bed-capacity health facilities, with an average of 50 outpatients per day. The third facility is a district hospital which provides medical care services, including obstetrics and gynaecology services, surgical, ear-nose and throat services, and laboratory services. The provision of gynaecological and delivery services in the study facilities necessitates performing exposure-prone procedures by midwives, which increases the risk of HBV exposure. Specifically for this study area, a nationwide cross-sectional study revealed an HBV prevalence of 15.6 per cent, indicating a high disease burden in the general population. 15 Additionally, a quantitative study also reported inadequate knowledge of PEP for HBV among HCWs in Northern Ghana. 11 These factors present a strong contextual background for this present study.

Study Population and Participants

The research population was midwives working in the Kassena Nankana West District of the Upper East Region of Ghana. Even though other professionals work in the three study sites, this sub-group of HCWs was purposefully chosen as the target population because they represent the category with the highest number of staff in the selected health facilities. Additionally, they were selected because they are a high-risk group for HBV exposure due to their frequent contact with blood and body fluids during maternity care. Their work environment and duties make them particularly relevant for studying HBV PEP uptake.15,17,18 Additionally, focusing only on this subpopulation of HCWs also ensures more consistent findings by reducing variation in roles and exposure patterns across different healthcare cadres, allowing a clearer understanding of barriers to PEP use in this specific group.

Inclusion Criteria

The study targeted midwives working in the maternity units of the three selected health institutions. Eligible participants were midwives with a minimum of two years of continuous work experience at their current facility who were directly involved in patient care activities that placed them at risk of occupational exposure to blood and body fluids.

A minimum of two years of experience in the maternity unit was considered sufficient to ensure substantial contact with pregnant women, thereby increasing the likelihood of occupational exposure to hepatitis B virus (HBV). This duration was also considered adequate for participants to have become familiar with the facility’s post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) management pathways and protocols, where such systems existed. Furthermore, the two-year experience criterion aligns with recommendations from most nursing and midwifery regulatory bodies, which advocate a period of preceptorship for newly qualified healthcare workers to support their transition from students to autonomous practitioners. 31

Exclusion Criteria

Midwives whose roles did not involve direct patient care or exposure to blood and body fluids, such as those performing administrative duties, were excluded from the study. In addition, eligible midwives who were on annual leave, maternity leave, or sick leave during the data collection period were not included.

Sample Size Estimation and Sampling Procedure

This qualitative exploratory study aimed to interview 20 or more participants, in line with recommended sample sizes for qualitative research. 32 Data saturation was reached after the 20th interview, at which point no new insights emerged. 30 Participants were purposively selected from three facilities in a ratio of 4:7:9. Purposive sampling was used to recruit information-rich participants. 33

Data Collection Instrument

We developed a semi-structured interview guide solely for this study. The development of the research instrument was guided by the objectives of the study and the literature available on the processes and procedures for PEP management for exposed HCWs.

The research instrument comprised three primary sections. Section A gathered data on the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, providing insight into their background and demographics. Section B explored the knowledge of the concept of PEP. The final section, Section C, investigated facility-level factors affecting PEP uptake, examining the role of institutional settings and resources. To facilitate in-depth exploration, the questions were open-ended, allowing researchers to probe for additional information and clarify responses as needed. The instrument was subjected to pretesting procedures as well as expert review by an academic supervisor and an occupational health and safety expert at the district health directorate (the pretesting was done with 2 individuals working in health facilities located within the study district, but not one of the selected study sites). These procedures aimed to achieve content and face validity, which is a measure of the appropriateness of the instrument to answer the research questions.

Data Collection Procedure

The study received ethical clearance and adhered to established research ethics and guidelines. Permission was obtained from health facility authorities at the three study sites prior to data collection. Potential participants were informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, and schedule during Monday morning ward meetings and through facility-specific social media platforms. Issues of confidentiality, privacy, and voluntary participation were clearly communicated.

A purposive sampling approach was used, without stratification. Participants who expressed interest were approached, and interview appointments were scheduled. The principal investigator conducted face-to-face interviews at the end of participants’ shifts in their resting areas within the labour, antenatal, and postnatal wards at each study site. All interviews were conducted in English, with participants consenting to audio recording, and supplementary notes were taken. Each interview lasted approximately one hour.

Data Analysis

The data analysis process followed Braun and Clarke’s (2006) thematic analysis methodology. 34 The researchers utilized iterative thematic analysis approach, informed by the Socioecological Model. Following a verbatim transcription, the research team familiarized themselves with the data through repeated reading of transcripts. Initial codes were inductively generated from the data and subsequently reviewed and refined through team discussions to ensure consistency and relevance.

Codes were then grouped into broader categories, and themes were developed by identifying patterns and relationships across the data. The Socioecological Model was applied as an analytic framework during this stage to organize and interpret the findings across different levels of influence, particularly the individual and institutional levels. This facilitated the alignment of sub-themes. Themes and sub-themes were reviewed collaboratively by the research team, with discrepancies in coding or interpretation resolved through discussion and consensus. This iterative and theory-informed process enhanced the rigour of analysis and supported the development of coherent and contextually grounded conclusions.

Ethical Consideration

The study received ethical clearance and protocol approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Navrongo Research Centre, NHRC IRB 14/04/2023. Additionally, permission was obtained from the Regional Director of Health Services and the management of the District Health Directorate. Each participant received an information sheet outlining the study’s objectives, procedures, risks, and benefits. After reviewing the information sheet, participants voluntarily signed a written informed consent form, indicating their willingness to participate in the study. Confidentiality was ensured in this study. All the interviews were conducted privately in the participants' resting area, with only the principal investigator present. To ensure anonymity, pseudonyms were used instead of actual names, effectively de-identifying the study participants.

Trustworthiness

The study employed Guba and Lincoln’s criteria for ensuring the trustworthiness of the research study’s findings. 35 Methodological rigour was ensured through multiple complementary strategies. Credibility was enhanced by developing the semi-structured interview guide from the study objectives and relevant literature, followed by pretesting among similar participants outside the study sites. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed iteratively, with codes and themes reviewed and refined through consensus among the research team. Verbatim quotations were used to support the findings.

Dependability was ensured through the use of a standardized interview guide, consistent data collection by a single trained principal investigator, and continuous supervision throughout the study. Confirmability was strengthened through reflexive practice, whereby the principal investigator considered the potential influence of their professional background and interactions with participants on data collection and interpretation. Regular supervisory discussions further supported objective interpretation. Transferability was enhanced by providing a clear description of the study setting, participants, and research procedures, enabling readers to determine the applicability of the findings to similar contexts.

The study involved twenty (20) participants who were recruited from health facilities in the Kassena-Nankana West District of the Upper East Region of Ghana participated in this study. All participants were females and between 27 and 46 years old. Seventeen of the participants were Christians, while three of them were Muslims. Most of the participants (70%) had a diploma in midwifery, 4 (20%) had a Bachelor of Science in midwifery, and only 2 had a post-basic certificate in midwifery. Their working experience ranged between 2 and 8 years. Most of the participants were staff midwives. Only four (20%) of the participants had received at least one dose of HBV vaccine. Themes and Sub-themes of the Study

Figure 1 presents the key overarching themes, themes and sub-themes identified in the study, and they have been organized according to the levels of the Socioecological Model. At the individual level, the main theme of inadequate knowledge captures gaps in participants’ understanding of HBV PEP, including limited awareness of the concept and lack of familiarity with HBV-specific protocols and guidelines. At the organizational level, the theme of health facility-level barriers highlights systemic challenges such as unstructured exposure reporting and PEP management pathways, the absence of facility-specific protocols, and inadequate training on HBV PEP. Together, these themes illustrate how both individual and institutional factors shape the uptake and management of HBV PEP. Themes and sub-themes

Overarching theme 1: Individual Level Factors Affecting PEP Uptake

Inadequate Knowledge emerged as a key individual-level factor influencing the uptake of PEP for HBV among midwives. Participants demonstrated limited understanding of the concept of HBV PEP and its appropriate use following occupational exposure. They also reported inadequate knowledge of HBV-specific PEP protocols and guidelines, resulting in uncertainty regarding the appropriate actions to take after exposure to blood or body fluids. This overarching has one theme thus Inadequate knowledge and two sub-themes namely (1) inadequate knowledge of the concept of PEP for HBV and (2) inadequate knowledge of HBV-specific protocols and guidelines.

Theme (a) Inadequate knowledge

Two main sub-themes were identified under the inadequate knowledge theme. These include: 1. Inadequate knowledge of the concept of PEP for HBV and 2. Inadequate knowledge on PEP for HBV-specific policies and guidelines.

Overarching Theme 2. Organizational Level Factors Affecting PEP for HBV Uptake

Some important organizational factors were reported as major factors affecting the uptake of PEP for HBV among midwives. The main theme identified under this overarching theme is facility-level barriers. Participants described challenges within their health facilities that limited timely access to and use of PEP following occupational exposure. These barriers included unstructured exposure reporting and PEP management pathways, the absence of facility-level HBV-specific protocols and guidelines, and inadequate training on PEP for HBV. Together, these organizational constraints reduced awareness of established procedures and hindered the effective implementation of PEP for HBV after occupational exposure.

Theme (b): Health Facility-Level Barriers

Effective implementation of PEP for HBV in health facilities is highly dependent on the interaction between the health facility and the exposed HCW. Facilities are responsible for establishing robust exposure reporting systems, ensuring the presence of trained focal persons, and maintaining sufficient resources to manage occupational exposures efficiently. The availability of facility-level policies, protocols, and guidelines is critical for ensuring timely and appropriate PEP administration. Additionally, a well-stocked supply of recommended testing kits and other essential PEP logistics is necessary to facilitate PEP management. Three sub-themes emerged under this theme. These include3. Unstructured exposure reporting, Absence of facility-level HBV-specific protocols and guidelines and lack of training.

Unstructured Exposure Reporting and PEP Management Pathways

Despite the Ghana Health Service’s Occupational Health and Safety Policy mandating health facilities to establish structures and systems for exposure reporting and the proper management of HCWs exposed to HBV, these measures were inadequately implemented at the study sites. The study revealed that the facilities lacked formal structures for exposure reporting and PEP management for HBV. Some participants reported that after notifying the doctor in charge of their exposure, they were instructed to take HIV prophylaxis instead of receiving appropriate HBV management. According to the participants, the facilities had structured protocols only for managing HIV exposures, leading to cases where participants in need of HBV PEP were incorrectly given HIV PEP. This practice was not due to HIV exposure but rather stemmed from healthcare providers' greater familiarity with antiretroviral PEP management. Absence of Facility-level HBV-specific Protocols and Guidelines

Health facility-level HBV-specific policies are official guidelines established by facility leadership to protect HCWs from HBV exposure and mitigate its consequences. These policies are typically aligned with the Ghana Health Service’s Occupational Health and Safety Policy recommendations. However, this study found that the participating facilities lacked dedicated guidelines for managing HCWs exposed to HBV. In contrast, the Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) guideline is widely recognized at the facility level. This policy primarily focuses on hand washing, use of PPE, hospital waste management, decontamination, and sterilization, among other measures, and is derived from the Ghana Health Service’s Infection Prevention Policy.

Findings from this study revealed that participants were more familiar with general infection prevention protocols than with HBV-specific prevention guidelines. Many reported being unaware of facility-level protocols dedicated to HBV prevention and HBV PEP management. Some even believed that such protocols did not exist, stating that their awareness of such protocols was limited to PPE use. I do not have any idea. There are no policies at the facility on post-exposure prophylaxis for HBV (P2, F,35 Years) I only know of the policy on the use of PPEs. They only advise us to use gloves and all appropriate PPE to protect ourselves and avoid exposure to HBV. (P8, F,40 Years)

Discussion

The present study sought to explore the barriers to PEP uptake for HBV among the participants in a highly endemic setting. This study used the Socioecological Model to show that health behaviours are influenced by multiple levels, especially the individual and institutional levels. 28 Together, these factors show how personal knowledge gaps and system-level weaknesses interact to reduce HBV PEP uptake and shape outcomes. Similar findings were reported in studies conducted in both the southern and northern sectors of Ghana11,26

Individual-level Factors Affecting PEP for HBV Uptake

According to the Socioecological Model, individual-level factors are personal characteristics that influence an individual’s knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, skills, and behaviour. In the context of PEP for HBV uptake, the study identified inadequate knowledge of concepts and HBV-specific protocols as factors affecting PEP for HBV uptake.

Participants in this study reported inadequate knowledge of the concept of PEP for HBV, which represents a critical barrier to preventing HBV transmission, particularly among individuals who frequently experience occupational exposure. This gap is especially concerning in highly endemic settings where HBV prevalence is elevated, and vaccination coverage among HCWs remains low.4,10 In such contexts, the combination of high exposure risk and low immunity increases susceptibility to infection, making timely and appropriate use of PEP essential. Additionally, participants demonstrated limited knowledge of facility-specific protocols and guidelines for HBV PEP management. This finding is consistent with previous research that identified inadequate knowledge of occupational HBV prevention and PEP management among exposed HCWs. 25 . Although Ghana’s Occupational Health and Safety Policy provides clear recommendations for HBV prevention and the management of exposed HCWs, both awareness and implementation of these guidelines remain suboptimal. From an individual-level perspective within the socioecological model, these findings highlight how limited knowledge and awareness of HBV PEP protocols can directly influence health-seeking behaviour. Specifically, insufficient understanding may hinder individuals’ ability to recognize risk, follow appropriate procedures, and seek timely care after exposure, thereby increasing the likelihood of HBV transmission.

The individual-level factors identified in this study suggest that personal characteristics, particularly knowledge, play a critical role in shaping the uptake of PEP for HBV. These findings highlight the need for interventions that target modifiable individual-level factors through regular training, workshops, and continuing professional education to improve knowledge and ultimately enhance the uptake of PEP for HBV following occupational exposure.

Organisational Level Factors Affecting PEP for HBV Uptake

According to the Socioecological Model, institutional (organizational) level factors are characteristics of the workplace or institution that influence individual behaviour. These include organizational policies, procedures, resources, leadership, and the work environment that can either facilitate or hinder desired practices. In the context of PEP for HBV, this study identified unstructured exposure reporting and PEP management pathways, absence of facility-specific protocols and guidelines and lack of training on HBV as the institutional or organizational factors influencing PEP uptake.

In this study, participants highlighted the absence of clear systems and structures for exposure reporting and HBV PEP management. At the institutional level, gaps such as a lack of clear protocols, weak reporting systems, and insufficient training further restrict effective response. Instead, exposures were reported to facility HIV coordinators, who were much more familiar with the management of HIV exposures. As a result, neither the risk levels of the exposed HCWs nor the source patients were assessed. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in another HBV-endemic region in Ghana, which reported the lack of formal structures for HBV exposure reporting and PEP management. 25 However, it contradicts established recommendations that emphasize the need for clear reporting pathways in all health facilities to ensure timely exposure reporting and comprehensive risk assessment for both HCWs and source patients. 22 It has been established that health facilities have a critical responsibility to implement systems that prevent occupational HBV transmission and ensure the appropriate management of exposed HCWs. 36

This study found that HCWs who required HBV PEP were incorrectly given HIV PEP, receiving antiretroviral drugs instead of the appropriate HBV regimen. However, there is no evidence supporting antiretrovirals for HBV PEP, and they are specifically not recommended for this purpose, as they do not protect against HBV exposure. 37 The findings also highlight gaps in institutional systems, including the lack of clear reporting channels and designated personnel to assess and manage HBV exposures. These deficiencies weaken effective exposure management and highlight the need to strengthen HCW safety protocols.

The study highlights a clear imbalance in how PEP is handled for HIV and HBV in the health facilities. HIV PEP is well-organised, with established protocols, trained coordinators, and consistent access to treatment, largely due to strong and sustained global and national investment in HIV programmes. In contrast, HBV PEP is poorly developed or informally managed, often lacking dedicated systems and being overshadowed by HIV structures. Despite HBV being highly endemic in Ghana and posing significant occupational risks to healthcare workers, it has not received similar attention or resources. As a result, HIV PEP systems are sometimes used as a general response to all bloodborne exposure incidents, including those that should specifically involve HBV management.

Participants reported that inadequate training on occupational HBV prevention contributed to their poor knowledge and management of HBV exposures, consistent with earlier findings in Ghana. 38 Although IPC training was common, it appeared to lack sufficient coverage of HBV PEP, as reflected in participants’ incorrect acceptance of antiretroviral drugs for HBV exposure. This indicates that HBV prevention and PEP are not well integrated into IPC training programmes, highlighting a broader system gap in HCW education and preparedness. 39

The findings also suggest that training legacies within IPC programmes are more strongly aligned with HIV than HBV, leading to greater familiarity with HIV exposure management among frontline staff and coordinators. Consequently, HBV PEP appears under-integrated into routine occupational health practice, resulting in gaps in appropriate risk assessment and management. Overall, this contrast highlights how vertical programme structures can inadvertently shape institutional priorities, potentially leaving other equally important occupational health risks under-addressed.

Health facilities should strengthen and standardize occupational exposure reporting by implementing clear, stepwise protocols that define immediate actions, documentation, and referral pathways, and ensure these are integrated into routine practice. In addition, supply chain systems for hepatitis B vaccine and HBIG should be improved through routine stock monitoring, minimum stock level enforcement, and designated personnel responsible for timely replenishment to prevent stockouts. Finally, the role of occupational health focal persons should be clearly defined and institutionalized, with explicit responsibilities for coordinating exposure reporting, ensuring PEP availability, and supporting adherence to protocols, supported by regular training and supervision.

Study Limitations

The study relied on self-reported data, which may be affected by recall and social desirability biases, potentially influencing the accuracy of participants’ responses. In addition, the exclusive focus on midwives means the findings do not capture the perspectives of other key actors involved in HBV PEP management, such as coordinators responsible for prescribing and administering PEP. The absence of input from health facility managers also limits the ability to verify reported gaps, particularly regarding the existence of facility-level HBV PEP protocols, despite provisions in the Ghana Health Service Occupational Health and Safety Policy.

Future research can address these limitations by adopting a multi-cadre approach that includes laboratory staff, PEP coordinators, medical officers, and facility managers to provide a more comprehensive understanding of exposure management practices. Incorporating mixed methods, such as triangulating self-reports with document reviews, facility audits, and observational data, could help validate participants’ accounts and reduce bias. Longitudinal or prospective study designs may also minimize recall bias by capturing experiences closer to the time of exposure. Additionally, engaging institutional leadership would allow for verification of policy implementation and provide deeper insight into organizational processes affecting HBV PEP uptake.

Conclusion

This study explored barriers to HBV PEP uptake among the study participants in a highly endemic setting. The findings indicate gaps in knowledge of HBV PEP and challenges in facility-level implementation, including unclear reporting pathways, absence of HBV-specific protocols, and limited training. These factors may contribute to suboptimal management of occupational exposures among the participants. However, as the study focused solely on midwives, the findings reflect their perspectives and experiences and may not fully capture the roles of other key stakeholders involved in PEP implementation, such as facility managers or HIV coordinators. The reported gaps in practice should therefore be interpreted as indicative of potential implementation challenges at the facility level rather than definitive evidence of systemic failure. Strengthening HBV PEP uptake may require improved training, clearer institutional protocols, and better coordination among healthcare personnel. Future studies incorporating multiple stakeholder perspectives are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of HBV PEP implementation and inform targeted interventions.
