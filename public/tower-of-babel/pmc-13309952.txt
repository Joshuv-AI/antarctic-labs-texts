Morphological characteristics and soft tissue analysis of the naso-oropharyngeal airway in young adults with skeletal Class II anterior open bite: a CBCT retrospective study
Authors: Jiahui Sun, Jindong Chen, Yanting Zou, Yike Yao, Yuan Zhang, Xianghui Xing, Jun Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the naso-oropharyngeal airway morphology and soft tissue characteristics in adult patients with skeletal Class II anterior open bite (AOB) through cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) measurements, and to compare these findings with those of patients with skeletal Class II malocclusion and normal overbite.MethodsOne hundred subjects with skeletal Class II malocclusion were divided into two groups: the AOB group (n = 50) with an anterior overbite of less than − 3 mm, and the control group (n = 50) with a normal anterior overbite. The age and sex distributions were matched between the two groups. Three-dimensional reconstruction was performed based on the CBCT data of the patients, and the naso-oropharyngeal airway model was segmented into three parts for volume measurement. Measurements included the cross-sectional area at three anatomical levels, the minimum cross-sectional area, and the transverse and sagittal diameters of the airway at two levels. The height of the pharyngeal airway was also measured. The positions of the hyoid bone and tongue body were evaluated. The length, thickness, and area of the soft palate and tongue body were measured in the mid-sagittal plane. Statistical analyses were conducted using independent samples t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, chi-square tests, and multiple linear regression, with P < 0.05 indicating statistically significant differences.ResultsCompared to the control group, skeletal Class II AOB patients exhibited narrower nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal airway spaces and lower minimum cross-sectional area. The cross-section at the inferior border of the tongue base showed a more horizontally elongated elliptical shape in AOB patients. Additionally, the AOB group showed significantly greater tongue length, thickness, and area than the control group. The naso-oropharyngeal airway morphology was significantly influenced by several factors, including sex, soft palate area, tongue area, palatoglossal space volume, and H-C3v.ConclusionIn skeletal Class II patients, AOB is associated with a narrower oropharyngeal airway compared to normal overbites. An enlarged soft palate and tongue, alongside abnormal tongue posture, may be the primary causes. Clinically, incorporating airway and soft-tissue assessments into treatment planning is advisable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08526-w.Keywords: Skeletal Class II Malocclusion, Anterior Open Bite (AOB), Naso-oropharyngeal Airways, Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT)

Introduction

The morphology and function of the upper airway are closely related to craniofacial structures. For example, mouth breathing caused by a narrow airway may lead to “adenoid facies”. Conversely, craniofacial structures can also influence airway dimensions; patients with skeletal Class II malocclusion tend to have narrower upper airways, particularly in the oropharyngeal region and in patients with high-angle skeletal Class II [1, 2, 3, 4]. The upper airway is also associated with the surrounding soft tissues. In addition to the adenoids and tonsils, the airway is correlated with the morphology of the soft palate and tongue as well as the position of the hyoid bone and tongue [5, 6, 7].

Anterior open bite (AOB) can result from multiple etiologies, including excessive vertical growth, oral habits, and temporomandibular joint disorders [8]. AOB due to oral habits such as thumb sucking and tongue thrusting is more common in growing children than in adults. Although self-correction may occur after cessation of these habits, most patients still require orthodontic treatment. AOB is associated with distinctive oral and maxillofacial structures such as a highly arched palate, vertical growth pattern, and altered tongue posture [9, 10], suggesting that those with AOB may have narrower upper airways. Orthodontic treatment for open bites is prone to relapse due to the vertical growth pattern and the tendency for tongue thrusting, which may be exacerbated by the mouth breathing that typically results from upper airway obstruction. Therefore, evaluating the upper airway in AOB patients is clinically relevant. Concurrently, upper airway abnormalities may contribute to AOB or its relapse, potentially worsening the condition. While previous CBCT studies have explored airway morphology in AOB patients, their findings remain inconsistent due to a lack of strict matching of sagittal (ANB) and vertical (FMA) skeletal patterns, or the inclusion of mixed skeletal patterns [12, 13]. Skeletal Class II AOB patients present with a unique combination of mandibular retrognathia and vertical dysplasia that may synergistically narrow the airway; yet, this specific subpopulation has not been exclusively investigated to date. Moreover, most existing studies have focused on the isolated airway space, overlooking the crucial morphological contributions of surrounding structures such as the hyoid bone, soft palate, and tongue, which are explicitly evaluated in the present study.

In this study, we performed a 3D CBCT analysis of the naso-oropharyngeal airway and surrounding soft tissues in skeletal Class II AOB patients with strictly matched skeletal patterns to controls, aiming to: (1) characterize airway morphological changes in this specific population; (2) identify key soft tissue and skeletal factors associated with airway narrowing; and (3) provide evidence-based clinical guidance for orthodontic treatment of skeletal Class II AOB.

Methods

Study subjects

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School (Approval No.: KY-2021NL-062). This study utilized patient records from the Orthodontics Department database of Nanjing Stomatological Hospital, covering the period from 2019 to 2021.

A total of 100 subjects were divided into an AOB group and a control group, with 50 subjects in each group. The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 (effect size d = 0.73, α = 0.05, power = 0.90) based on previous airway volume differences in AOB patients [12], indicating a minimum of 41 subjects per group. Thus, 50 subjects were enrolled in each group to account for potential sample loss or exclusions. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) skeletal Class II malocclusion (ANB > 4.7°), with no significant differences in cephalometric measurements (ANB, SNA, SNB, and FMA); (2) age between 18 and 35 years; (3) bite contact present in the bilateral molar regions; and (4) no missing anterior teeth. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of orthodontic treatment or orthognathic surgery; (2) history of airway disease or surgery; (3) maxillofacial syndromes; (4) obstructive sleep apnea syndrome; (5) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m²; (6) recent onset of AOB; and (7) abnormal head posture.

The AOB group (n = 50) consisted of patients with skeletal Class II malocclusion (ANB > 4.7°) with an anterior overbite of − 3 mm or less. The control group (n = 50) consisted of individuals with a normal anterior overbite of 1–3 mm. There were no significant differences in age, sex, and cephalometric measurements (ANB, SNA, SNB, and FMA) between the two groups (Table 1). Additionally, to evaluate the potential influence of vertical facial divergence, a subgroup analysis was performed within the AOB group by stratifying patients into borderline high-angle (30° < FMA ≤ 35°, n = 16) and high-angle (FMA > 35°, n = 29) subgroups.

CBCT data acquisition

Images were acquired using a NewTom VGi CT scanner (QR Company, Italy) under standardized conditions. The scan parameters were set as follows: slice thickness = 0.25 mm; scan time = 10 s; tube current = 5 mA; and tube voltage = 110 kVp. Imaging was performed by professionals from the Department of Oral Radiology at Nanjing Stomatological Hospital. During CBCT scanning, patients were instructed to remain seated upright, with eyes looking straight ahead, teeth in maximum intercuspation, and the tongue and perioral muscles relaxed. The patients were advised to maintain steady breathing and avoid swallowing or tongue movement during the examination. CBCT data were exported in DICOM format via the medical imaging PACS system for subsequent analysis.

Airway assessment

The CBCT data were imported into Mimics Medical 21.0 (Materialise, Belgium) for three-dimensional reconstruction and measurement of the upper airway. The three-dimensional coordinate system was adjusted so that (1) the sagittal axis passed through both the anterior nasal spine and the basion, (2) the coronal axis was tangential to the lowest points of the infraorbital ridges bilaterally, and (3) the horizontal plane was made parallel to the Frankfort horizontal (FH) plane.

Measurements included the volume of each segment, the cross-sectional area of the boundary surfaces, and the minimum cross-sectional area of the oropharyngeal segment. The transverse and anteroposterior diameters of the velopharyngeal and glossopharyngeal boundaries were measured along with the height of the oropharyngeal airway, defined as the vertical distance from PLb to PLd. Additionally, the ratio of the transverse diameter to the anteroposterior diameter at the PLc and PLd interfaces was measured to evaluate changes in airway morphology [15]. The hyoid bone position measurements are shown in Fig. 2. Measurements related to the tongue body and soft palate included length, thickness, and area (Fig. 3). To optimize soft tissue visualization and ensure measurement reproducibility, image contrast and brightness levels were manually adjusted. Anatomical landmarks were precisely identified by cross-referencing multi-planar views and tracing continuous soft tissue contours. Based on the highly standardized resting posture achieved during image acquisition, specific anatomical protocols were established for localizing challenging soft tissue landmarks on the mid-sagittal slice. The tongue tip (T) was strictly defined as the most anterior and inferior point of the tongue dorsum, and the epiglottis-tongue junction (E) was defined as the exact intersection point where the base of the tongue meets the epiglottis. This rigorous anatomical definition, combined with the strict scanning instructions, effectively minimized intra-observer measurement errors that might arise from dynamic soft tissue variations in static CBCT imaging. In the mid-sagittal plane, an inferior tongue position was defined as separation between the tongue and palate, while a superior tongue position was defined as the tongue adhering closely to the palate. The volume of the palatoglossal space was also measured. All measurements were performed by the same investigator. Each measurement was taken three times, and the average value was recorded as the final result. Intra-observer reliability was assessed by calculating intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for linear, area, and volume measurements.

Statistical analysis

Measurement data were imported into SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corp., USA) for statistical analysis. All measurement data were described using the mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). Levene’s test and Shapiro–Wilk test were employed to assess the homogeneity and normality of the data. For continuous variables meeting normality assumptions, independent-sample t-tests were used to assess differences between groups. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U tests were employed. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. Furthermore, to isolate the independent effect of the anterior open bite from the potential confounding influence of the vertical skeletal pattern, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed. FMA was set as the covariate for the primary airway and soft tissue variables that initially showed significant differences between the groups. Estimated marginal means were calculated to compare the adjusted differences. Finally, multiple linear regression was applied to evaluate the associations between the independent variables and both airway volume and minimum cross-sectional area. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all included variables were below 5, indicating that multicollinearity remained within acceptable limits. Statistically significant differences were defined as those with P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Table 1, no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed in age, sex, or cephalometric parameters (SNA, SNB, ANB, and FMA) between the AOB group and the control group, indicating that the two groups were well-matched in terms of basic characteristics and craniofacial skeletal patterns. Regarding the dental parameters, the AOB group exhibited significantly greater labial inclination of the maxillary incisors compared to the control group (P < 0.05). However, no statistically significant difference was observed in the mandibular incisor inclination between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Airway morphology

All measurements demonstrated high intra-observer reliability, with ICC values ranging from 0.833 to 0.952 (Supplementary Table 1).

The volumes of the oropharyngeal, velopharyngeal, and glossopharyngeal segments in the AOB group were significantly smaller than those in the control group (P < 0.05). Significant differences were also observed in the cross-sectional areas between the two groups. Compared with the control group, the AOB group exhibited smaller cross-sectional areas at PLb, PLc, and PLd as well as a smaller minimum cross-sectional area in the oropharynx (P < 0.05). Among linear measurements, the transverse diameter and anteroposterior diameter of PLc as well as the anteroposterior diameter of PLd were significantly smaller in the AOB group than in the control group (P < 0.05). In addition, the ratio of the transverse diameter to the anteroposterior diameter at PLd was significantly larger in the AOB group than in the control group (P < 0.05). Subgroup analysis of the AOB group based on FMA

Baseline characteristics, except for FMA, were well-matched between the borderline high-angle and high-angle subgroups (Supplementary Table 2). Regarding airway and soft tissue morphology, the high-angle subgroup exhibited a significantly smaller anteroposterior diameter at the inferior velopharyngeal boundary (P < 0.05), a larger transverse-to-anteroposterior ratio at this level (P < 0.05), and a significantly decreased soft palate thickness (P < 0.05) compared to the borderline high-angle subgroup. However, no significant differences were observed in other measurements between the two subgroups (Supplementary Table 3).

Adjusted comparison controlling for FMA

After adjusting for FMA using an ANCOVA, all 14 airway and soft tissue parameters that initially showed significant inter-group differences remained statistically significant (P < 0.05). Specifically, the AOB cohort continued to exhibit distinct morphological adaptations, such as a decreased glossopharyngeal volume, a reduced oropharyngeal min-CSA, and alterations in tongue dimensions, independent of the vertical skeletal pattern. Discussion

Previous studies indicate that age, head posture, sex, BMI, and both sagittal and vertical skeletal patterns are significantly associated with pharyngeal airway morphology [16, 17, 18]. Therefore, the control group comprised patients matched for age and sex with the AOB group, all of whom had passed their peak growth period. Participants with a high BMI or abnormal head posture were excluded. Furthermore, skeletal Class II AOB subjects were specifically selected, as they are likely to have narrower pharyngeal airways.

Based on the three-dimensional analysis in this study, the skeletal Class II AOB patients exhibited narrower airways compared to the controls, and these narrower airways primarily manifested as reduced volume in the naso-oropharyngeal segments. This aligns with the findings of Kim et al. [12], who similarly observed that AOB patients had smaller volumes across all segments of the pharyngeal airway compared to patients with normal overbite. Significant differences were identified in the total pharyngeal volume as well as in the volumes of the nasopharyngeal and hypopharyngeal segments. Additionally, the minimum cross-sectional area of the pharyngeal airway in AOB patients was significantly smaller than in those with normal overbite. Laranjo et al. [11] also found that the sagittal diameters of the nasopharynx and oropharynx were narrower in AOB patients than in patients with normal overbite. In contrast, Vidal-Manyari et al. [13] found no significant differences in upper airway volume or linear measurements between AOB patients and controls, although they did observe larger airway areas in the AOB group. Notably, their study reported significant baseline differences in SNA and FMA between the two groups. Since sagittal and vertical skeletal patterns strongly influence airway dimensions, the lack of strict control over these variables in their sample likely explains the discrepancy between their findings and those of our study.

By matching the skeletal Class II AOB patients with the skeletal Class II normal overbite controls, we aimed to isolate the specific impact of the AOB phenotype on airway morphology. In this study, the AOB group predominantly exhibited a hyperdivergent phenotype, reflected by a marginally higher mean FMA (36.00° vs. 34.20°, P = 0.077). Our subgroup analysis revealed that while extreme hyperdivergence (FMA > 35°) is not associated with significant differences in overall airway volume compared to borderline cases, it correlates with localized sagittal narrowing of the lower velopharynx and a thinner soft palate. These findings suggest that the AOB phenotype is independently associated with general airway variations, whereas a severe vertical growth pattern acts as a compounding factor linked to further localized anatomical adaptations. While the subgroup analysis clarified how extreme vertical divergence exacerbates local boundaries within the AOB group, it was equally crucial to confirm the primary source of the differences between the AOB and control groups. Our subsequent ANCOVA revealed that, even when FMA was strictly controlled as a covariate, all major morphological differences in the airway and soft tissues remained highly significant. This finding indicate that the anterior open bite phenotype itself is independently associated with these specific naso-oropharyngeal and soft tissue adaptations, operating beyond the mere influence of overall vertical divergence.

In the present study, the AOB group exhibited a higher prevalence of inferior tongue position (albeit non-significant) and significantly larger tongue body. Furthermore, we observed a larger volume of palatoglossal space and greater soft palate size in the AOB group, although the differences were not statistically significant; these findings are corroborated by previous research [8, 19, 20, 21]. AOB patients predominantly present with a vertical growth pattern and a tendency for clockwise rotation of the mandible, which can cause the tongue to shift posteriorly and inferiorly. Compounding the effects, detrimental oral habits such as sucking and tongue thrusting can lead to a deepened palatal vault and constriction of the upper dental arch [19, 22]. The functional influence of soft tissues is clearly reflected in the present dental measurements. Despite controlling for basic skeletal sagittal parameters and ensuring comparable FMA between groups, the AOB group exhibited a significantly greater U1-SN angle. This indicates pronounced anterior dentoalveolar compensation alongside inherent vertical skeletal tendencies. An enlarged tongue, coupled with tongue thrusting or a low resting posture, likely exerts continuous excessive pressure on the maxillary anterior segment, resulting in upper incisor proclination. Interestingly, the IMPA did not differ significantly, suggesting this aberrant functional pressure predominantly affects the maxillary arch. However, despite this anterior dentoalveolar compensation, the oral cavity capacity remains inadequate to accommodate the hypertrophied soft tissues. Consequently, the enlarged tongue mass is forced posteriorly, encroaching upon the upper airway space. This spatial mismatch largely explains the narrower oropharyngeal airway, particularly in the glossopharyngeal segment, observed in AOB patients. This anatomical mechanism is robustly corroborated by the negative correlation between palatoglossal space volume and oropharyngeal airway dimensions: a larger palatoglossal space indicates a lower tongue posture, which mechanically displaces the tongue base backward, thereby reducing both the volume and minimum cross-sectional area of the oropharyngeal airway.

In the treatment of AOB in skeletal class Ⅱ, for patients exhibiting an enlarged tongue area or low tongue posture, these morphological characteristics highlight a more complex anatomical environment. Understanding these baseline morphological features can serve as an important anatomical reference before formulating orthodontic plans. For instance, rather than directly dictating treatment mechanics, these findings suggest that orthodontic movements potentially reducing oral cavity volume, such as extensive anterior retraction, should be planned with greater awareness of the pre-existing soft tissue conditions [23, 24, 25]. Furthermore, these anatomical variations suggest that morphological stability might be challenged, serving as a reminder that comprehensive evaluations, potentially including myofunctional assessments, could be relevant [26]. Given that AOB is often associated with upper airway structural abnormalities and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [27, 28], identifying such anatomical features on CBCT can act as an early morphological screen. When patients exhibit significant signs of airway narrowing or tongue enlargement on imaging alongside persistent mouth breathing or respiratory symptoms, timely referral to an otolaryngologist or a sleep medicine specialist for dynamic and functional assessment is advisable [29, 30].

We also found that compared to the control group, the AOB group exhibited a larger ratio of transverse diameter to anteroposterior diameter at the cross-section of the inferior glossopharyngeal boundary. This suggests that the morphological change in the upper airway of AOB patients is more pronounced in the glossopharyngeal segment, where the airway shape tends toward a transversely elongated ellipse. Additionally, although not statistically significant, we noted an increase in oropharyngeal airway length and a lowering of the hyoid position in the AOB group. Similarly, previous studies [11, 12, 31] have found significantly elongated pharyngeal airway length and inferior displacement of the hyoid bone in AOB patients. Pae et al. [31] suggested that airway length might serve as a convenient cephalometric indicator for diagnosing AOB, although other clinical information must be considered for accurate diagnosis. The hyoid bone position reflects respiratory efficiency; when breathing is compromised, the hyoid bone descends to enhance respiratory capacity [12]. Changes in hyoid bone position may be correlated with increased lower facial height in AOB patients, with compensatory adaptations in the suprahyoid and infrahyoid muscles inducing inferior displacement of the hyoid bone [11]. Olja et al. [32] reported that OSA patients exhibit lower and more posteriorly positioned hyoid bones, with a significantly greater distance to the C3 vertebra compared to controls. Similarly, we found that a decreased H-C3v distance was associated with a reduced minimum cross-sectional area in the oropharynx.

Multiple Linear Regression Analysis identified soft palate area, tongue area, palatoglossal space volume, and H-C3v distance associated with oropharyngeal airway morphology. These parameters may affect the airway through different mechanisms: an increased soft palate area can directly reduce the anteroposterior diameter of the airway at the retropalatal level; an enlarged tongue area may push the tongue body posterior‑inferiorly, while an increased palatoglossal space volume reflects a lower tongue position, both of which can compress the retroglossal airway [33, 34]. The H-C3v distance serves as an indirect indicator of hyoid bone position; a decrease in this distance may suggest posterior displacement of the hyoid, which could indirectly compromise the stability of the oropharyngeal airway by pulling the tongue base posteriorly [34, 35]. Although VIF analysis indicated that statistical collinearity among these variables was not strong in this sample, there are potential anatomical and functional interrelationships. For example, hypertrophy of both the tongue and soft palate may co-occur in individuals with obesity or certain syndromes [36, 37, 38]. Physiologically, they may exert a synergistic effect, with concurrent hypertrophy producing more pronounced airway compression than either factor alone. In predicting the minimum cross-sectional area of the oropharyngeal airway, the H-C3v distance demonstrated the largest effect size (B = 9.083), followed by the soft palate area (B = − 0.849), tongue area (B = − 0.166), and palatoglossal space volume (B = − 0.011). This suggests that in clinical assessment, direct radiographic measurement of the H-C3v distance or focused evaluation of the retropalatal and retroglossal morphology may facilitate identification of high‑risk oropharyngeal airway narrowing.

This study presents several methodological and clinical limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the current analysis was confined to static CBCT imaging, which inherently cannot capture the dynamic physiological behaviors of soft tissues such as the tongue and soft palate during swallowing or respiration. Second, precisely identifying soft tissue boundaries and specific anatomical landmarks (such as T and E) on CBCT is inherently challenging due to the lack of high radiodensity contrast. Although strict localization protocols and multi-planar cross-referencing were applied in this study to ensure measurement reliability, these assessments remain subject to potential variability, and maintaining an absolutely stable resting posture of these soft tissues during the acquisition process is practically difficult [39]. Third, and importantly, as an observational study, these morphological associations are not directly linked to longitudinal treatment outcomes, relapse rates, or specific clinical decision-making. Therefore, the findings and the clinical implications discussed herein should be strictly interpreted as baseline diagnostic indicators and morphological risk awareness, rather than definitive therapeutic guidelines. Future large-scale prospective studies incorporating long-term follow-ups and dynamic functional evaluations, such as real-time MRI or airflow analysis, are warranted to elucidate how these morphological variations directly impact orthodontic treatment stability and patient-centered outcomes.

Conclusion

Patients with skeletal Class II AOB exhibit narrower oropharyngeal airways than those with normal overbites, likely driven by enlarged soft palates and tongues, alongside abnormal tongue posture. Consequently, orthodontic treatment planning for these patients may benefit from comprehensive airway and soft-tissue evaluations.
