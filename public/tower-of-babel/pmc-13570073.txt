Protocol: Deterrence-Oriented Counterterrorism Interventions in OECD Countries for Domestic Terrorist Attacks: A Systematic Review
Authors: Esther Salama, Michael Wolfowicz, Badi Hasisi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives: This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to assess the effectiveness of nationally implemented, government-led, deterrence-oriented counterterrorism interventions targeting domestic terrorism in OECD countries. Methodology: The review will follow standard Campbell Collaboration methodological guidelines. Data permitting, multilevel meta-analyses will be used to estimate overall pooled effect sizes while accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data by modelling dependence among multiple effect sizes within studies. Moderator analysis and meta-regression will be instrumental in exploring potential sources of heterogeneity. Implications: The findings of the review hold significant implications for shaping effective evidence-based policies and interventions in the area of domestic counterterrorism. The results are also intended to inform future research by identifying gaps in the body of knowledge.

Background

The Problem, Condition or Issue

The events of 9/11 propelled the issue of countering terrorism to a central national priority for Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries. This has been accompanied by an exponential increase in government spending to ensure the development of counterterrorism interventions, including military responses, enhanced airport security, and the apprehension and legal prosecution of individuals suspected of terrorism (McCoy et al., 2021). In more recent years, countering violent extremism (CVE) interventions have been developed to combat the radicalization that underpins terrorism, specifically focusing on tackling the risk factors that give rise to radicalization in the first place (Wolfowicz, Litmanovitz, et al., 2021).

Recent advancements in the field have furthered our understanding of terrorism as a phenomenon, with research becoming more rigorous, increasingly drawing from traditional criminology theories, and using sophisticated quantitative and statistical methods (Freilich et al., 2024; Wolfowicz et al., 2021). This body of research has identified that while differences certainly exist, there is a significant degree of overlap between crime and terrorism. One point of overlap suggests that terrorists use cognitive strategies that are, in many respects, similar to the ones used by ordinary criminals, indicating the potential for deterrence strategies to be effective (e.g., Newman & Hsu, 2012; Perry & Hasisi, 2017; Salama & Wolfowicz, 2025).

Nonetheless, to date, our knowledge of whether terrorism responds to deterrent efforts in the same way crime does remains limited, notably due to an insufficient number of counterterrorism intervention evaluation studies (Freilich et al., 2024; Schuurman, 2020), limited statistical models (Enders & Sandler, 1993; Hsu & McDowall, 2020), and a general lack of research on the role of the criminal justice system in countering terrorism (Freilich et al., 2024; Sydes et al., 2023). Furthermore, existing quantitative studies have produced mixed evidence and, as a result, have given rise to concerns about backfire effects rather than the intended deterrent effects such interventions are designed to produce (Benmelech et al., 2015; Chae & Kim, 2024; Dugan et al., 2005). As in the broader area of CVE, the development of counterterrorism interventions and policies has for the most part been carried out in the absence of an evidence-based process, due to a lack of evidence synthesis (Freilich et al., 2024; LaFree & Freilich, 2019; Lum et al., 2006; Sageman, 2014; Sandler, 2025).

The proposed review aims to synthesize the evidence for the effectiveness of deterrence-focused interventions in reducing domestic terrorism. It will focus on criminal justice interventions and strategies of neutralization used in OECD countries to curb terrorism. The primary objective of the review is to assess whether nationally implemented deterrence-oriented counterterrorism interventions have an effect on terrorism trends, and if so, the magnitude and direction of this effect.

The Foundations of Deterrence-Based Counterterrorism

To date, there exists no consensus on the definition of terrorism, with academic, governmental, and supranational organizations offering divergent conceptualizations (Easson & Schmid, 2011; Freilich et al., 2024; LaFree & Schwarzenbach, 2021). Given that a substantial share of counterterrorism intervention evaluation studies rely on the Global Terrorism Database, this review adopts the database’s broad definition of terrorism as being: “The threatened or actual use of illegal force and violence by a non-state actor to attain a political, economic, religious, or social goal through fear, coercion, or intimidation” (Argomaniz & Vidal-Diez, 2017; Bove & Böhmelt, 2022; LaFree et al., 2009; START, 2022).

Despite ongoing definitional debates, most OECD governments approach terrorism primarily through its criminal component, addressing terrorist activity via domestic criminal justice systems. This reflects a widely shared premise: even in the absence of a universally accepted definition, terrorism at a minimum involves illegal acts, including the use or threat of violence, and therefore constitutes a violation of local criminal law. The State’s rationale for treating terrorism as a crime is reinforced by empirical evidence of substantial similarities with ordinary crime—and particularly organized crime—including prior criminal involvement, organizational structures, financing mechanisms, and overlapping recruitment pools (Grieco, 2023; Haymoz et al., 2023; LaFree, 2018; Rodermond & Thijs, 2023; Thijs et al., 2024; Valasik & Phillips, 2017). Research further indicates that terrorists, much like other criminals, can be understood as rational agents that respond predictably to changes in their constraints and opportunities (Newman & Clarke, 2006; LaFree & Dugan, 2009; Sandler, 2025).

With terrorism treated as a criminal offense in practice, counterterrorism policies have, to a large extent, drawn directly on deterrence-oriented strategies originally developed to address ordinary crime. Deterrence-based theories, grounded in rational choice perspectives, assume that criminal behavior can be reduced when potential offenders perceive a high probability of detection, face severe consequences, and anticipate swift punishment (Becker, 1968; Nagin, 2013). These theories posit that raising the expected costs of perpetrating crime, while increasing the expected utility of abstaining from crime, will result in lower offending rates. A large body of criminological research suggests that the certainty of punishment, particularly the perceived risk of apprehension and conviction, is more effective in deterring crime than the severity or swiftness of punishment (Bun et al., 2020; Chalfin & McCrary, 2017; Nagin, 2013). Similar dynamics have also been observed in the context of terrorism (LaFree & Dugan, 2009).

The argument that the criminal justice system serves as a deterrent to criminal offenders can be traced back to the 18th century (Beccaria, 1764/1963; Bentham, 1789). Criminal justice systems across democratic nations are designed to arrest, charge, convict, and sentence offenders. These activities seek to deter future crimes via four different mechanisms: general deterrence, specific deterrence, rehabilitation, and incapacitation (Marvell, 2010; Nagin, 2013). General deterrence refers to the assumption that an increased perception of the probability of being caught deters potential criminals. Specific deterrence is associated with the assumption that the experience of punishment affects the offender’s decision to reoffend (Chalfin & McCrary, 2017; Nagin, 2013). Incapacitation operates by physically removing offenders, typically through incarceration or neutralization, thereby preventing them from committing future offenses.

An important methodological challenge in assessing the effectiveness of deterrence-based counterterrorism strategies is the potential confounding between deterrent and incapacitation effects (Levitt, 2004; Nagin, 2013). Because terrorist activity is often highly concentrated among a small subset of offenders (McCauley & Moskalenko, 2017; Salama & Wolfowicz, 2025), observed reductions in attack frequency following counterterrorism interventions may be misinterpreted as a deterrent effect when they in fact reflect incapacitation. At the same time, confounding may occur in the opposite direction: offenders may be incarcerated or killed and thus incapacitated, yet observed reductions in violence may reflect general deterrent effects that such incapacitation has on other potential offenders, rather than a reduction in the incapacitated individuals’ own offending.

Whilst research must be cognizant of these issues, states and their security forces continue to employ incapacitation measures—such as incarceration and killings—within a fundamentally deterrence-oriented framework. These interventions typically operate through a dual logic. First, they involve targeted deterrence or incapacitation, whereby offenders are physically removed. Second, these measures are intended to generate general deterrent effects by increasing the perceived risks faced by other actual or potential offenders. As such, to the extent that observed outcomes reflect a combination of deterrent and incapacitation effects, this has no bearing on the deterrence-oriented rationale underpinning these approaches.

In the context of terrorism, deterrence-based theories and rational choice assumptions have long dominated both academic research and counterterrorism policy (LaFree & Dugan, 2009). From this perspective, terrorist behavior is shaped by perceived benefits, including personal gains (e.g., money, fame or revenge), religious rewards (e.g., eternity in paradise), or social rewards (e.g., social status or recognition) (LaFree & Dugan, 2009; Perry & Hasisi, 2017). The counterterrorism interventions examined in this review are therefore grounded in the assumption that terrorists act as rational—albeit bounded—agents who respond to changes in constraints and opportunities. Consistent with these assumptions, interventions that increase the costs of offending or perceived risk of being caught have been shown to alter terrorist behavior; for example, the installation of airport metal detectors substantially reduced the frequency of aviation attacks (Enders & Sandler, 1993; Hsu & Apel, 2015). The mechanisms underpinning these interventions will be further explained in the following background sections.

The Interventions

This review considers nationally implemented deterrence-oriented counterterrorism interventions as tactics designed to deter terrorism by increasing the certainty and/or severity of punishment, as well as the costs of offending (Beccaria, 1764/1963; Becker, 1968; Nagin, 2013). Governments of OECD countries often seek to deter terrorism using a mixed approach that combines offensive, defensive, and conciliatory strategies and that operates through both general and specific deterrence, as well as incapacitation.

Offensive deterrence-based approaches, such as drone strikes and military operations, target terrorists, their capabilities, and resources to disrupt activities and increase both the certainty and severity of punishment. These military tactics aim to signal state capacity and resolve. Accordingly, they operate primarily through general deterrence (e.g., potential terrorists respond to changes in the threat of punishment), but also through incapacitation (e.g., removing terrorists). Proactive policing strategies similarly seek to deter terrorism by increasing the risk of detection and enforcement certainty (Carson et al., 2020; Safer-Lichtenstein, 2019). Police crackdowns, for instance, have played a crucial role in both dismantling homegrown terror cells and thwarting imminent terror plots (Nesser, 2024).

Defensive approaches aim to increase the difficulty of launching an attack and to reduce the probability that an attack will be completed successfully (Lehrke & Schomaker, 2016). Situational prevention and target-hardening tactics, including security barriers and metal detectors, operate through a deterrence-by-denial mechanism. They are designed to raise the likelihood of failure, decrease target vulnerability, and mitigate opportunities for attack (Enders & Sandler, 1993; Freilich et al., 2019; Guler & Demir, 2024; Hsu & Apel, 2015; Perry et al., 2017).

Conciliatory actions, such as ceasefires, military withdrawals, and amnesty laws, are ‘softer’ interventions that have been utilized in OECD countries such as Turkey (Eser, 2007) and Israel (Dugan & Chenoweth, 2012). These tactics increase the expected benefits of not engaging in violence and have been described in the literature as ‘rewards for abstention’ measures whereby restraint is met with political, territorial, or legal concessions (Carson et al., 2020).

Traditional crime control measures across OECD nations such as arrests, charges, convictions, and sentencing are commonly applied to those who commit acts of terrorism (Landes, 1978; Wolfowicz & Salama, 2025; Ünal & Cafnik Uludağ, 2022). These measures operate through general and specific deterrence, consistent with the idea that both the threat and the experience of punishment influence offending and reoffending decisions (Chalfin & McCrary, 2017; Nagin, 2013), and through incapacitation—notably through incarceration. With respect to punishment severity, longer sentence lengths have been found to be associated with reduced recidivism (Hasisi et al., 2020). Despite their centrality, the role of criminal justice measures in combating terrorism has remained overlooked (Sydes et al., 2023).

Finally, post-9/11, most Western democracies enacted various types of counterterrorism legislation that expanded law enforcement powers and enhanced sanctions, thereby increasing the certainty and severity of punishment (Shor, 2016).

How the Interventions Might Work

“Deterrence is a theory of choice”, whereby a potential terrorist’s behavior is shaped by changes in the expected costs and benefits of offending (Nagin, 2013, p. 7). Deterrence-oriented counterterrorism interventions may operate in two distinct pathways: direct and indirect deterrence.

First, direct deterrence operates by influencing the behavior of actors who are directly targeted by an intervention. It may work through specific deterrence, whereby an offender who has already offended refrains from reoffending following the experience of punishment (e.g., an arrested terrorist who does not recidivate). Direct deterrence may also operate through general deterrence, whereby a targeted individual refrains from acting in anticipation of punishment (e.g., a potential terrorist under law enforcement surveillance abandons plans).

Second, indirect deterrence, which primarily works through general deterrence, affects individuals who are not the intervention’s direct targets. In this case, potential terrorists update their expectations as they observe others being punished (Levitt, 2004; Marvell, 2010; Nagin, 2013). For instance, increased police and security presence may create a perception of heightened risk of apprehension and/or punishment sufficient to prevent offenses from being committed in the first place (Nagin, 2013). Similarly, Yang and Jen (2018) find that focused policing strategies may lead to reductions in terrorist activity in areas adjacent to the intervention site. Both direct and indirect effects will be generally captured, and indistinguishably so, by the various studies eligible in this review.

In addition, incapacitation-type interventions are relevant to this review because many counterterrorism interventions simultaneously produce deterrent and incapacitative effects, which are thereby captured in the included studies. In the context of counterterrorism, incapacitation effects may work through incarceration but also by means of neutralization via injury or death, which often occurs at the scene of the attack (Ozdogan & Ozdogan, 2023; Salama & Wolfowicz, 2025; Ünal & Cafnik Uludağ, 2022). Targeted killings, leadership decapitations, and drone strikes are also employed to eliminate offenders, degrade organizational capabilities, and signal state resolve, thereby contributing to broader deterrent effects (Carson, 2017; Pollack & Byman, 2024; Price, 2012).

However, evidence from ordinary crime research shows that deterrence-based interventions can produce unintended and even counterproductive consequences. Aggressive policing tactics have been shown to generate iatrogenic effects (Escaño et al., 2025), while certain programs—such as Scared Straight—designed to increase the perceived costs of punishment have been found to be more harmful than no intervention at all (Petrosino et al., 2003). Research on imprisonment and sentence length yields mixed findings and, in some cases, suggests increased recidivism (Berger & Scheidegger, 2022; Kirk & Wakefield, 2018; Loeffler & Nagin, 2022).

Likewise, counterterrorism interventions may also generate iatrogenic, rather than deterrent, effects (Hsu & McDowall, 2020; LaFree et al., 2009). In some cases, such interventions may produce counterproductive outcomes by exacerbating grievances and altering incentives, thereby unintentionally encouraging violence (Dugan & Chenoweth, 2012). Research on policing has shown that stop-and-frisk measures, especially when perceived as unjust, are associated with increased risks of radicalization (Tankebe, 2020). In general, it has been suggested that hard or indiscriminate counterterrorism tactics, particularly when they result in collateral damage, may foster desires for revenge, generate grassroots support, and facilitate recruitment (Gill et al., 2016; Rosendorff & Sandler, 2004, 2010; Sandler & Siqueira, 2006). Evidence of such backfire effects has also been documented for targeted killings, where the removal of al-Qaida leaders and Islamic State Khorasan operatives has, in some cases, generated retaliatory pressures or mobilized broader support within global jihadist networks (Carson, 2017; Jadoon et al., 2023; Pollack & Byman, 2024). Further, this backfire dynamic is also found in the Israeli context, where precautionary house demolitions have been linked to political radicalization and subsequent rises in terrorist activity (Freedman & Klor, 2023; Hatz, 2020).

Softer tactics, such as legislation, may also yield backlash by creating perceptions of unfairness or disproportionate punishment, thereby fostering outrage and energizing potential offenders (LaFree et al., 2009; Pridemore & Freilich, 2007). Similarly, conciliatory actions targeted at one terrorist group may incentivize other groups to escalate violence in anticipation of receiving similar rewards in the future (Shkolnik, 2023). Likewise, prisoner releases may reduce the perceived severity of punishment when individuals are released early in the context of peace agreements, thereby potentially undermining deterrent effects (Ganor & Falk, 2013).

Why It Is Important to Do the Review

Several reasons underscore the importance of conducting this review. The primary terrorism threat faced by OECD governments today is domestic in nature, with domestic attacks far outnumbering transnational attacks in Western democracies (Chermak et al., 2024; Gaibulloev & Sandler, 2023). As such, in recent years, OECD governments have allocated substantial resources to counter domestic terrorism through a wide range of deterrence-oriented strategies, and in parallel the number of quantitative studies evaluating government-led counterterrorism interventions has increased. However, existing reviews have largely focused on radicalization and other upstream outcomes or on specific intervention types, rather than providing a systematic, field-wide synthesis of deterrence-oriented, government-led interventions and their effects on domestic terrorist attack trends. As such, there remains no comprehensive and up-to-date synthesis addressing this specific review question.

Existing Primary Research

Similar to findings in ordinary crime research, the effects of deterrence-based interventions in terrorism studies display substantial heterogeneity, paralleling the mixed results observed for anti-crime deterrence strategies (Braga et al., 2015, 2018; Dölling et al., 2009; Hinkle et al., 2020; Petersen et al., 2023; Pratt et al., 2017; Schell-Busey et al., 2016). Within the terrorism literature, this heterogeneity has given rise to the deterrence-backlash nexus debate (Argomaniz & Vidal-Diez, 2017; Dugan et al., 2005; Hsu & McDowall, 2020; Ozdogan & Ozdogan, 2023; Wolfowicz & Salama, 2025). Most studies find that ‘soft’ tactics, such as ceasefires, are associated with decreases in attacks, whereas ‘hard’ measures, such as military strikes, are often associated with increases in violence (Argomaniz & Vidal-Diez, 2017; Dugan & Chenoweth, 2012).

More specifically, several studies that quantitatively examine ‘hard’ tactics report evidence of backlash. For instance, Fisher and Becker (2021) find that the assassination of Osama bin Laden led to iatrogenic effects. Similarly, Chanpong (2025) and Kaplan et al. (2006) present evidence that targeted assassinations produce backlash effects. Some are concerned that ‘hard’ counterterrorism interventions are counterproductive as they create grievances that enable terrorist campaigns to gain traction (Dugan & Chenoweth, 2012; Hsu & McDowall, 2020).

In contrast, several longitudinal studies have found deterrence-oriented interventions to effectively reduce terrorism (Safer-Lichtenstein, 2019; Salama & Wolfowicz, 2025). Some studies based on descriptive statistics and various event-based regression models report reductions in attacks following situational crime prevention and target-hardening tactics (Hsu & Apel, 2015; Hsu et al., 2018; Perry et al., 2019). Additionally, Carson et al. (2020) found that both punitive laws and crackdowns (e.g., FBI operations) reduce the occurrence of eco-terrorism. Furthermore, in some cases, studies on counterterrorism interventions have reported nil effects on terrorism trends (Freedman & Klor, 2023; Hafez & Hatfield, 2006).

Despite the widespread use of anti-crime tactics to combat domestic terrorism in OECD countries, there are relatively few quantitative studies that assess the effectiveness of such tactics applied to terrorism (Salama & Wolfowicz, 2025; Sydes et al., 2023). The few studies that evaluate the impact of traditional crime control tactics on terrorism have produced conflicting findings. This deepens the ongoing deterrence–backlash debate (Bove & Böhmelt, 2022; Ozdogan & Ozdogan, 2023; Ünal & Cafnik Uludağ, 2022). Perhaps one of the least disputed counterterrorism strategies is arrest, as findings overwhelmingly show that the likelihood of apprehension leads to a reduction in terrorism trends (Hussain, 2010; Kaplan et al., 2006; Landes, 1978).

Mixed findings from prior studies have repeatedly been blamed on methodological and data limitations (Hsu & McDowall, 2020; Morris & LaFree, 2016; Schuurman, 2020). Analytical models often rely on oversimplified or dummy variables to represent government actions. Studies also tend to overaggregate independent variables, combining several counterterrorism measures into a single variable (Chenoweth & Dugan, 2011; Wolfowicz & Salama, 2025). As a result, these models are statistically limited in capturing such complex phenomena (Argomaniz & Vidal-Diez, 2017; Enders & Sandler, 1993; Hsu & McDowall, 2020; Sageman, 2014). In addition, there remains a lack of reliable data on counterterrorism, with studies often using limited data or open-source data (Cubukcu & Forst, 2018; Hsu & McDowall, 2020; Sageman, 2014; Sandler, 2025). Although the Global Terrorism Database has been overwhelmingly used in terrorism studies, it has its limitations, primarily because it relies mainly on English open sources. This reliance has led to underreporting of attacks, as demonstrated by Alimi and Maney (2022) and Perry et al. (2017) in the context of Israel.

Recently, a growing body of research has employed count or continuous measures (e.g., number of arrests or incarceration rates), rather than dichotomous variables, to examine domestically implemented counterterrorism interventions (Bove & Böhmelt, 2022; Ozdogan & Ozdogan, 2023; Salama & Wolfowicz, 2025; Ünal & Cafnik Uludağ, 2022). Despite this growth, to date, no comprehensive synthesis has been conducted that focuses specifically on the effectiveness of deterrence-oriented, government-led counterterrorism interventions in reducing domestic terrorism (Sydes et al., 2023). The present review seeks to address this gap by systematically synthesizing the existing evidence. Additionally, where data permits, moderator analyses will be conducted to explore various potential sources of heterogeneity in effects across and within studies. The review will also identify gaps in the evidence base to inform future research.

Why Is This Review Needed in Light of Existing Reviews?

To date, several reviews and protocols examine government-led interventions, including case management approaches, educational programs, communication campaigns, and multi-agency initiatives involving police (Duarte et al., 2025; Hassan et al., 2025; Lewis et al., 2025; Mazerolle et al., 2021). However, these reviews primarily assess the effectiveness of such measures in preventing radicalization to violence, rather than in reducing the occurrence of terrorist attacks, which is the outcome of interest in the present review. Other reviews adopt a more limited intervention scope and depart from the field-wide approach pursued here, focusing exclusively on specific intervention types, such as criminal justice responses (Sydes et al., 2023) or situational crime prevention measures (Freilich et al., 2019; Marchment et al., 2025).

Two reviews serve as foundational references for the present study. First, Lum et al. (2006)’s systematic review and meta-analysis examined the effectiveness of counterterrorism measures on terrorist attack counts. Although it did not specifically focus on domestic terrorism or government-led interventions and was published nearly two decades ago, it remains the most comparable synthesis in terms of outcome and other eligibility criteria to the present review. Second, LaFree and Freilich’s (2019) qualitative review of government-led counterterrorism interventions provides a field-wide synthesis and conceptual framework—classifying interventions from most repressive to most conciliatory—but adopts a broad scope that includes non-deterrence-oriented intervention types (e.g., disengagement programs) and outcomes (e.g., radicalization) that fall outside the scope of the current review.

Although existing reviews have significantly contributed to synthesizing the available evidence, none has systematically assessed the effects of deterrence-oriented, government-led counterterrorism interventions on domestic terrorism, despite substantial growth in the quantitative evidence base.

Policy Relevance

Domestic terrorism remains one of the main security challenges facing OECD nations. For example, in 2024, European Union member states experienced 58 terrorist incidents, encompassing completed, failed, and foiled attacks, of which 34 were completed. In response, member states arrested 449 individuals for terrorism-related offenses, many during various crackdowns, and convicted 426 individuals, mostly for membership in terrorist organizations or disseminating propaganda. Several countries also enacted new laws or expanded existing measures targeting terrorism-related offenses (TE-SAT report, 2025). However, the impact of these measures on future terrorism trends remains largely unknown.

At the same time, OECD governments devote substantial public resources to internal security. In 2023, public order and safety expenditure amounted to approximately €48.7 billion in France, £58.3 billion in the United Kingdom, and $513.9 billion in the United States, highlighting the tremendous financial investments in policing, courts, prisons, and other internal security functions—within which counterterrorism interventions are embedded (OECD, 2024). This reinforces the importance of determining whether counterterrorism interventions are effective.

Governments must also continually balance the imperative to protect public safety with the need to safeguard civil liberties and individual rights. Given this context, evidence-based findings on both the effectiveness and potential unintended consequences of counterterrorism interventions are therefore crucial for informed policymaking.

However, to date, counterterrorism evaluation research has remained limited and characterized by mixed findings (Argomaniz & Vidal-Diez, 2017; Carson et al., 2020; Drakos & Konstantinou, 2014; Hsu & McDowall, 2020; Perry et al., 2017). Specifically, the role of criminal justice responses in deterring domestic terrorism has remained underexplored, despite their widespread use across OECD countries (Freilich et al., 2024; Salama & Wolfowicz, 2025; Sydes et al., 2023).

The proposed systematic review seeks to address these gaps by synthesizing the current evidence and producing valuable insights into the effectiveness, or lack thereof, of deterrence-focused counterterrorism interventions. Ultimately, it aims to inform the development of more nuanced and evidence-driven counterterrorism policy.

Objectives

The main objective of the proposed systematic review is to answer the question: How effective are nationally implemented deterrence-oriented counterterrorism interventions applied in OECD countries in deterring domestic terrorism? Specifically, this review aims to assess the effect of counterterrorism interventions on both the frequency and timing of terrorist attacks.

A secondary objective is to examine whether the effectiveness of these interventions varies depending on study characteristics and to explore potential sources of heterogeneity both within and between studies. When possible, potential moderators will be explored, including, but not limited to, characteristics of the intervention, study context (e.g., geographic location), study design, and study sample characteristics (e.g., length of exposure period).

Methodology

Criteria for Including Studies in This Review

Types of Studies

The systematic review will be limited to quantitative studies only and will include the following study designs:1. Experimental or quasi-experimental studies;2. Observational studies employing either descriptive or inferential statistics.

As frequently noted in the literature, it is not expected that many randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or quasi-experimental studies (e.g., non-randomized controlled trials) will be identified in this review, if any. However, should any such studies exist, they will be included provided that they evaluate two or more units of analysis, comparing units exposed to the intervention with units exposed to ‘business-as-usual’ (i.e., pre-existing policies or practices), an alternative intervention, or different levels of exposure to the intervention (i.e., variation in treatment intensity or dosage). Accordingly, no restrictions will be imposed on the types of comparison conditions used. In addition, nationally implemented policies inherently lack randomization—as they are applied at the national level, rather than being assigned experimentally to randomly selected units. Nonetheless, should studies observing differences in how national policies are implemented across sites (e.g., states or regions) be retrieved, no specific inclusion or exclusion criteria will be imposed based on implementation or randomization characteristics. No restrictions will be imposed on follow-up periods.

For observational studies, which are expected to be the most common type of study, the eligible comparators comprise both between- and within-unit (e.g., country) variation in terrorism outcomes and variation in the application of one or more included interventions. Observational studies that use multiple time points for a single country (e.g., time-series analyses), as well as observational studies that use multiple time points across multiple countries (e.g., time-series cross-national or panel analyses), will be eligible. Observational cross-sectional studies that focus on a single country are also eligible, provided that they include multiple time points (e.g., Gill et al., 2016). Common observational designs therefore include time-series, interrupted time-series, and before–after designs. Studies without a temporal dimension will not be eligible. That is, included studies will be limited to longitudinal data, whether single-country or cross-national. No restrictions will be imposed on exposure period.

The classification of study designs will not rely on the terminology used by study authors, as design labels vary across disciplines and are often applied inconsistently. As such, evidence shows substantial divergence between author-reported design labels and the underlying design features of studies (Hartling et al., 2011; Seo et al., 2016). Accordingly, study designs will be classified using the structured tool developed by Seo et al. (2016) for systematic reviews of interventions and exposures. This tool classifies studies based on core design elements rather than labels, including how conditions or groups are formed, the temporal ordering of intervention/exposure and outcome measurement, and the presence or absence of a control or comparison group.

The primary language used for indexing and abstract is English. As such, all database searches will be carried out in English. However, if an indexed study written in a different language meets the criteria at the title and abstract screening stage, it will be sent for translation for further eligibility screening.

Types of Participants

The proposed systematic review will consider studies that examine the impact of counterterrorism interventions at the macro-level, using a single country or multiple countries as the unit(s) of analysis. This therefore excludes studies that focus on specific organizations, groups, or individuals.

For consistency in contextual analysis and intervention scope, the review will be limited to studies focusing on one or more OECD countries. To date, 38 countries are part of the OECD, as reported on the official OECD website (OECD, 2024) (see Table 1 and Figure 1). Although no country has ever been excluded from the OECD, several nations have joined the organization long after its establishment in 1961. For example, Chile, Estonia, Israel, and Slovenia became members in 2010, while Colombia, Costa Rica, Latvia, and Lithuania joined during the late 2010s or early 2020s. To gain OECD membership, countries must consistently uphold specific economic and political standards over several years. For instance, Colombia applied for membership in 2011, after negotiations that started in 2013, it was invited in 2018 and officially joined in 2020. This process suggests that applicant countries often align closely with OECD standards well before their formal inclusion. Therefore, we will include studies whose observation periods overlap with at least one year of the country’s OECD membership. For instance, the studies by Hafez and Hatfield (2006) and Dugan and Chenoweth (2012), set in Israel and both covering the years up to 2004, will be excluded due to the lack of overlap with Israel’s OECD membership start date.

For cross-national studies that combine both OECD and non-OECD countries without distinguishing between them, we will include the study as long as at least one OECD country is represented. When disaggregated OECD/non-OECD results are available (e.g., in supplementary analyses), these will be preferred (e.g., Shor, 2016). When separate estimates cannot be extracted, we will conduct moderator analyses comparing OECD-only studies with mixed OECD/non-OECD studies. We will also assess the robustness of our findings through sensitivity analyses that exclude mixed-sample studies to examine whether they affect the overall results.

Types of Interventions

In line with the deterrence-based framework discussed in earlier sections, the review will include measures capturing variation in the certainty and severity of punishment, including arrests, charges, convictions, sentence length, and offender neutralization. We will also include proxy measures frequently used to capture counterterrorism interventions, such as the enactment of counterterrorism legislation, expansions of police and military powers and resources, and focused operations targeting terrorism.

Eligible intervention measures may therefore take the form of count or continuous variables where the intervention varies across time units (e.g., months or years), whether within one unit (single-country analysis) or across multiple units (panel analysis). These are commonly used to measure indicators such as arrests or safety and defense spending (e.g., Buesa & Baumert, 2018; Drakos & Konstantinou, 2014).

Interventions may also be operationalized as binary (dummy) variables across time, capturing, for example, the implementation of a specific policy or the occurrence of a military intervention (e.g., Morehouse, 2015; Safer-Lichtenstein, 2019). In these cases, time periods prior to the intervention are coded as 0 and periods following implementation as 1, reflecting a time-varying exposure rather than a cross-sectional comparison between independent units.

When data permits, we will examine whether effect sizes differ systematically across these measurement approaches through moderator analysis. Differences in intervention measurement (e.g., binary or continuous) will be examined as a moderator to assess their potential contribution to heterogeneity.

The review will only consider intervention evaluation studies that examine nationally implemented government-led counterterrorism interventions. As a guide, the review considers studies that fall within the categorization of governmental responses as outlined by LaFree and Freilich (2019). Two categories of interventions outlined by LaFree and Freilich (2019) will be excluded, namely deradicalization and disengagement programs, as well as community-level and primary prevention programs. This is because studies assessing the effectiveness of such interventions tend to use individuals as the unit of analysis. Additionally, these types of interventions are often carried out by non-governmental entities, which constitute a criterion for exclusion. Further, these intervention categories are due to be synthesized by upcoming Campbell Collaboration reviews being devised at the time of submitting this proposal.

Interventions that are not implemented nationally but rather have an international scope, such as military operations conducted abroad, and foreign military aid or economic sanctions, will also be excluded from the analysis. For instance, studies assessing, in the context of the global war on terrorism, the effects of US drone strikes in Pakistan or Yemen on the occurrence of terror attacks will be excluded (e.g., Jadoon et al., 2023; Manna, 2015). Similarly, social, political, and economic interventions that do not primarily aim to counter terrorism via deterrence-focused approaches will be excluded from the review.

Interventions will be classified into predefined categories using a structured procedure designed to ensure transparency and consistency. First, classification will be guided by the language and descriptions provided by the study authors, including stated objectives, operational features, enforcement tools, and the institutional actors responsible for implementation. Second, the research team will conduct secondary qualitative research to examine how the intervention is described by the originating or implementing authorities (e.g., the FBI or the UK Home Office). This information will be used to supplement author-provided descriptions, particularly when they are limited or ambiguous. Classification decisions will therefore be informed by the substantive characteristics of the intervention rather than by terminology alone.

Types of Outcomes

The proposed review limits its focus to studies where the measured outcome is the frequency of terrorism—as defined by the commonly used Global Terrorism Database. This comprehensive definition enables the inclusion of a wide range of terrorism activities, as well as insurgent-type attacks. So too, its flexibility allows for the inclusion of studies that use other databases.

For inclusion in the systematic review, studies must include terrorism, and more specifically terror attacks, as the measured outcome. Some examples of how terror attacks can be measured include:(1) A measure of frequency of attacks (e.g., counts of events, rate of events); or(2) A measure of the time to failure (e.g., duration between events).

We will include outcome data that is based on both official and open-source event-based databases.

Studies that examine terrorism based on a measure of attack lethality, such as the number of fatalities or injuries resulting from attacks, will be excluded from the review. This is because the review is focusing on the occurrence of terror attacks and not on their lethality. Indeed, many terror attacks worldwide occur with no victims and are therefore unrecorded by studies that only include attacks that result in injury or death. Although deterrence measures may mitigate the severity of attacks, this aspect falls outside of the scope of the review and warrants a separate research question. Additionally, lethality is influenced by factors such as a state’s medical response quality, as well as a range of stochastic factors (Einav et al., 2004; Goldman et al., 2024; Shapira et al., 2006).

Although there is an ongoing debate regarding the conceptual distinction between terrorism and violence occurring within the context of war or civil war, many widely used terrorism databases have been criticized for conflating terrorism with other forms of political violence, including the Global Terrorism Database (LaFree, 2018; McCann, 2023). Given the absence of consensus on the definition of terrorism and the resulting measurement and classification issues, studies will not be excluded on the basis of the databases they employ. Instead, eligibility will be determined based on how the phenomenon is defined by the study authors themselves. Studies that explicitly examine war, civil war, or conventional military conflict will be excluded, whereas studies that explicitly frame the observed outcome as terrorism will be eligible for inclusion if they meet all other eligibility criteria.

Studies that rely solely on dichotomous prevalence outcomes (1 or 0) will also be excluded. For example, Asal et al.’s (2023) cross-sectional study, which examines whether counterterrorism interventions influence the onset of terrorism, coding “1” for years following the initiation of violence, will not be included in the analysis. Several reasons inform this decision. First, in the case of dichotomous outcomes, in particular when indicator variables are also dichotomous, such outcomes do not provide information on either the magnitude or the direction of effects (Nguyen & Loughran, 2018). In such studies, a country with 100 terrorist attacks is treated as being equivalent to a country with only 10 attacks. Second, as stated above, one of the aims of this review is to gain comprehensive insights into the impacts (or lack thereof) of counterterrorism interventions on the frequency of attacks and the duration between terrorist events, which is fundamentally different from the impact on the occurrence versus non-occurrence of terrorism (Petersen et al., 2023). This approach aligns with prior systematic reviews that have excluded studies based on methodology or outcome measures, focusing on the most relevant and prevalent ones for accuracy and comparability (Petersen et al., 2023; Schmucker & Lösel, 2011).

There will be no restriction on the type of terror attack included. As such, the review will include attacks, such as but not limited to: stabbings, bombings, suicide bombings, vehicular attacks, kidnappings, assassinations, hijackings, and shootings. It is important to note that while some studies incorporate a combination of attack types within their analytical models, others concentrate on one specific type, such as suicide bombing attacks (Benmelech et al., 2015). Nonetheless, studies examining exclusively threats, hoaxes, or non-violent acts will not be eligible for inclusion. No restriction will be placed on the ideological strain or motivation behind the incident.

The review focuses on the effects (or non-effect) of counterterrorism interventions on domestic terrorism. However, studies that aggregate both transnational and domestic terrorism without distinction will still be included in the review. Pilot searches have revealed many studies that rely on the Global Terrorism Database (GTD), which records both types of attacks. Although most relevant studies explicitly exclude transnational incidents from their analysis (e.g., Bove & Böhmelt, 2022), others do not exclude them or do not clearly state whether they have done so. This issue arises primarily in cross-national research. For instance, Lehrke and Schomaker’s (2016) study, which examines the effects of kill and capture operations on global terrorism trends, will be included in the review. Where studies provide disaggregated domestic and transnational results, we will extract domestic estimates (Enders et al., 1990). When separate estimates are not available, we will address this issue using statistical strategies consistent with our approach to mixed OECD/non-OECD samples, including moderator and sensitivity analyses to assess whether mixed-sample studies differ from domestic-only studies. Studies that solely observe the effects of nationally implemented counterterrorism interventions on transnational, or ‘overseas’, terrorism will be excluded (e.g., Le Vine & Salert, 1996).

Studies whose only outcome is a measure of radical attitudes or support for political extremism, usually measured through radicalization rates or voting patterns, will be strictly excluded from the review as this represents a distinctly separate line of inquiry.

Duration of Follow-Up or Observation Period

No restrictions will be imposed on the duration of follow-up or the length of the observation period; instead, duration will be accounted for analytically within the meta-analyses. For studies employing observational longitudinal designs, variation in observation period length will be accounted for analytically by modeling duration as a continuous moderator (e.g., through meta-regression). In studies in which outcomes are measured at defined intervals following an intervention (e.g., randomized or quasi-experimental designs), follow-up duration will be modeled as a categorical moderator (e.g., short-, medium-, and long-term) when data permits. The length of observation or follow-up period will thus be examined as a potential source of heterogeneity in effect sizes.

To accomplish this, the identification of studies relies on electronic searches of bibliographic databases, grey literature searches, and contact with experts. The full search strategy will be documented in the final review. The different phases of the search and study selection process will be reported in a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data Collection and Analysis

Description of Methods Used in Primary Research

Based on pilot searches and our familiarity with the literature, we expect included quantitative studies to be observational and to rely on longitudinal regression frameworks for count or rate outcomes (e.g., Poisson or negative binomial models), interrupted time-series designs, and time-to-event models (e.g., Cox proportional hazards models). While most studies assess the effectiveness of one or more counterterrorism interventions in a single-country analysis, others employ panel designs to assess the impact of one or more interventions across multiple countries. Most included studies will likely use official data from governmental institutions, open-source datasets such as the Global Terrorism Database, or a combination of both. For example, Buesa and Baumert (2018)—a study identified during the pilot search—examine the effects of two criminal justice counterterrorism interventions, namely arrests and charges, on the rate of domestic ETA terrorism in Spain between 2001 and 2015, using an observational design with Poisson and OLS regression models.

Criteria for Determination of Independent Findings

Selection of Studies

Search results will be imported directly into Rayyan, an online reference management and screening platform. Rayyan’s built-in duplicate detection function will be used to identify and remove potential duplicates prior to screening. Following de-duplication, Rayyan will be used to manage all stages of the screening process. Data Extraction and Management

Once full-text screening is completed, two trained reviewers will independently extract and code study-level characteristics. The coding workload will be randomly assigned and divided between the two reviewers, such that each study is coded by one reviewer. Data extraction will be conducted using a predefined codebook designed to ensure accurate and comparable information collection. The codebook encompasses over 50 items, pertaining to a range of domains including bibliographic characteristics, geographical context, intervention characteristics, effect size data, and more (see Table Extraction and Coding Sheet in the supporting information section). Coding will be conducted using Excel.

To assess inter-coder reliability, a random 10% subsample of studies will be independently coded by both reviewers. The results of the inter-coder reliability analysis will be presented using Cohen’s kappa (κ), with the threshold for acceptable reliability set at κ > .60. Coding instructions for items with relatively poor scores will be enhanced to improve clarity, and items exhibiting persistently low agreement may be removed. Any discrepancies will be resolved through collaborative discussions between the reviewers. The final review will present descriptive characteristics of the included studies in a comprehensive table, incorporating elements from Table 8 and additional relevant information.

Assessment of Risk of Bias in Included Studies

The credibility of the findings of included studies will be assessed through a thorough critical appraisal of their methodology. The choice of risk-of-bias tool will be determined by the design of each included study. For randomized controlled trials, we will use the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2). For quasi-experimental studies, we will use ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions). For longitudinal observational and survival analysis studies, which we anticipate being the most common study designs, we will use ROBINS-E (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Exposures) as a conceptual framework.

Although ROBINS-E provides a structured framework for assessing risk of bias in observational studies of exposures, its domains are primarily tailored to epidemiological research using individual-level data. The studies expected to meet the inclusion criteria differ in several important respects, as they rely on aggregate, event-based, and longitudinal data and face field-specific methodological challenges. While some ROBINS-E domains directly capture relevant sources of bias (e.g., confounding), others are less relevant (e.g., selection of participants), and key sources of bias in counterterrorism research—such as temporal ordering, overlapping datasets, and the use of proxy measures—are only implicitly addressed and therefore require more explicit operationalization.

Our approach follows that of previous Campbell Collaboration reviews and protocols that modified existing risk-of-bias tools to better reflect the methodological characteristics and limitations of political violence and terrorism research (e.g., Hassan et al., 2022; Wolfowicz et al., 2021, 2022). Accordingly, the ROBINS-E domains were adapted, aligning the operationalization of the bias domains with the data structure, study designs, and common methodological limitations of the field while preserving the conceptual framework of the original tool and improving its applicability. Risk of Bias Assessment

Upon completion of the assessment, a ninth column will be created to represent the overall risk of bias classification at the study level. This classification will be based on a domain-based “worst-case” rule, whereby the highest level of concern across domains determines the overall rating. Specifically, a study will be classified as high risk of bias if at least one domain is rated as high risk. It will be classified as low risk only if all domains are rated as low risk. If no domains are rated as high risk but at least one domain is rated as some concerns, the study will be classified as some concerns.

Studies will not be excluded based on the degree of bias. Instead, risk of bias will be examined in moderator analyses and investigations of heterogeneity. To do so, we will use the overall risk of bias classification as a categorical moderator.

Risk of bias assessment will follow the same assignment procedure as data extraction. A random 10% subsample of studies will be rated by both reviewers to check for inter-coder reliability. If disagreement arises, a senior reviewer will make the final decision. Critical appraisal assessments will be presented graphically in the final report and summarized in-text by domain across included studies.

Measures of Treatment Effect

This review will conduct multilevel meta-analyses assessing the impact of counterterrorism interventions on both the frequency and timing of terrorist attacks across multiple time points. Meta-analyses will only be conducted when at least two studies yield effect sizes for the same outcome construct. We anticipate encountering a range of statistical reporting formats across studies (e.g., regression coefficients (b), SE(b), t-values, hazard ratios, etc.), from which effect sizes will be derived. The appropriate methods for extracting and standardizing these effects—focused on the most common reporting formats—are detailed below.

To do so, we will follow established procedures to adequately extract effect sizes, predominantly from regression models but also from summary statistics (Borenstein et al., 2009; Lipsey & Wilson, 2001; Wolfowicz et al., 2022). Where applicable, calculations will be supported by the Practical Meta-Analysis Effect Size Calculator provided by the Campbell Collaboration. 1 For effect sizes not directly covered by available calculators—particularly partial estimates derived from multivariate models—standardization will be conducted using a range of accepted methods, acknowledging that no standard convention exists for such estimates (Wolfowicz et al., 2022).

Based on the study designs anticipated in this review, we expect two predominant forms of quantitative evidence: longitudinal observational studies that model incident frequency (e.g., time-series analyses, interrupted time-series analyses), and survival models that examine the timing of terrorist events (e.g., Cox proportional hazards models, series hazard models). These two approaches capture fundamentally different statistical constructs. Therefore, these effect sizes will not be combined, as they are not substantively comparable, and will be meta-analyzed separately (Borenstein et al., 2009).

Longitudinal observational studies typically employ a range of time-series regression approaches (e.g., OLS, Poisson, VAR) or interrupted time-series designs (e.g., ARIMA) to capture the strength of association between an intervention and subsequent terrorism outcomes over time. Given their observational and correlational nature, the preferred effect size measure will be Pearson’s correlation coefficient r. When a study reports the results of a linear regression (e.g., OLS) in which both the independent and dependent variables are continuous, r will be calculated as follows: r=SDx bSDy

When standard deviations (SDs) are not reported, when the independent variable is dichotomous and the dependent variable is continuous, or when the model is not strictly linear (e.g., Poisson regression models), r will be calculated based on the t-ratio (b/SE) using the following formula (Wilson, 2016): r=tt2+(n−2)

For these conversions, standard errors will be calculated, if possible, based on the following calculation: SEr=r×SEbb

Assuming that the confidence interval provided is a 95% CI, we will use the confidence intervals (CIs) to calculate the SE when it is not reported (Wolfowicz et al., 2022). The formula is as follows: SEb=CIupper−CIlower2×1.96

For other study designs, which are expected to be relatively rare, effect sizes will be derived as follows. For studies that use variables measured on a continuous scale to compare pre-post scores, we will calculate the standardized mean difference (Cohen’s d), using the means, standard deviations and sample sizes (or other data reported by authors).

For experimental studies, both randomized and non-randomized, that compare two independent groups, the standardized mean difference will likewise be calculated as Cohen’s d, following the formula outlined in Borenstein et al. (2009): d=X_1−X_2Spooled

Following Campbell Collaboration guidelines (Polanin & Snilstveit, 2016), when multiple compatible effect size metrics are derived from included studies, we will use the most commonly reported metric as the primary effect size and convert other extracted effect sizes to that metric using conventional formulas—provided that they are conceptually comparable (Borenstein et al., 2009). Given the predominance of observational longitudinal studies in this literature, correlation-based effect sizes are expected to be the most common; accordingly, Pearson’s r will serve as the primary metric for conversion where appropriate. Effect sizes arising from distinct modeling frameworks (e.g., HRs, IRRs) will not be converted to r but will be analyzed in appropriate metrics and in separate models, as described below.

Where appropriate, we will therefore convert effect sizes extracted as d to r as follows: r=dd2+4

We will then transform Pearson’s r to Fisher’s z, allowing for the correlation coefficient to be normally distributed (Borenstein et al., 2009; Rosenthal, 1984). As such, meta-analytic models will be estimated using Fisher’s z, while back-transformed r values will be reported in text for interpretability. The transformation from Pearson’s r to Fisher’s z is given by: z=0.5×ln(1+r1−r)

The variance of Fisher’s z will be calculated as: varFisherz=1n−3

The standard error will be calculated as the square root of the Fisher’s z variance: SEFisherz=varFisherz

For survival analysis studies, effect sizes will be calculated as the natural logarithm of the hazard ratio, ln(HR) (Parmar et al., 1998). Findings will be reported in ln(HR). Because ln(HR) arises from time-to-event models that estimate effects on the hazard function and r reflects associations from longitudinal models of event counts or rates, the two metrics are not comparable (Borenstein et al., 2009). Moreover, there is no recognized or validated method for converting ln(HR) to r. Accordingly, ln(HR) effect sizes will be synthesized in a separate multilevel meta-analysis.

Time-to-event effect sizes will be derived from reported hazard ratios. If the hazard ratio is already provided on the log scale, it will be used directly; otherwise, it will be transformed by taking the natural logarithm. The standard error for the log hazard ratio will be calculated based on the following calculation: SE(lnHR)=SE(HR)HR

For randomized controlled trials or quasi-experimental studies, effect sizes based on count outcomes may be reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs). In such cases, we will extract the natural logarithm of the IRR (ln(IRR)) and its corresponding standard error. These effect sizes will be synthesized in a separate analysis (Higgins et al., 2024).

Effect sizes commonly used for binary dependent variables, such as odds ratios (ORs) or risk ratios (RRs), are not relevant to this review, as studies relying on dichotomous outcomes are not considered eligible.

Multivariate (or adjusted) intervention effects will be preferred to bivariate (or unadjusted) ones for two key reasons related to enhancing comparability across studies. First, following Cochrane guidelines, adjusted estimates are generally more appropriate in non-randomized studies because they attempt to account for confounding (Chandler et al., 2019). Second, based on pilot searches, the eligible studies overwhelmingly report results as multivariate regression estimates, whereas bivariate associations are less commonly reported in this literature, making multivariate estimates the most comparable basis for effect size extraction.

When only unadjusted (or bivariate) estimates are available (e.g., from correlation matrices), and study authors do not respond or are unable to provide adjusted estimates, we will extract the unadjusted estimates and account for potential differences between bivariate and multivariate effects through moderator analyses.

All eligible studies will be included in the qualitative summary tables. However, studies for which effect sizes cannot be extracted—either from published data or from information provided by authors—will not be included in the quantitative analysis.

Effect sizes will be analyzed using the StataNow/SE 18.5 program under a random-effects model specification.

Unit of Analysis Issues

The unit of analysis in this review is the effect size, representing the association between exposure to a specific counterterrorism intervention and a terrorism-related outcome over time. Based on pilot searches, eligible studies employ a range of longitudinal designs with varying temporal structures, including analyses of incident counts or rates measured across repeated time intervals (e.g., daily, monthly, or yearly counts), repeated observations across geographic units over time (e.g., country-year, country-month), and time-to-event models estimating the duration until the next attack. All eligible units of analysis have a temporal dimension, capturing variation across successive time intervals.

Pilot searches indicate that many eligible studies contribute multiple relevant estimates. This occurs primarily because individual studies often examine more than one counterterrorism intervention within the same model (e.g., Argomaniz & Vidal-Diez, 2017; Lehrke & Schomaker, 2016). These multiple treatment effects will be retained, allowing multiple effect sizes per study, and their statistical dependence will be accounted for using the multilevel meta-analytic approach described in the following section.

In addition, studies frequently report multiple effect sizes resulting from repeated measurements across different time points, follow-up periods, or lag structures within the same study. These will also be incorporated into the analysis, with resulting dependencies handled as described in the following section.

Criteria for Determination of Independent Findings

A key methodological issue in meta-analysis concerns the independence of effect sizes and arises primarily from two sources: multiple reports of the same study and multiple outcomes within a single study.

First, studies—often produced by the same set of authors—based on identical datasets, samples, outcomes, and interventions, will be identified and linked. When directly overlapping estimates are reported, only one estimate will be retained, prioritizing those with the longest exposure or follow-up period or the most recent publication. All decisions will be transparently documented in the final review.

Second, studies frequently report multiple effect sizes resulting from the use of multiple outcomes (e.g., alternative measures of the same outcome at the same time point). As a result, effect sizes are frequently nested within studies and are therefore statistically dependent.

Failure to account for dependent effect sizes may result in disproportionate weighting of studies that contribute multiple estimates and biased pooled effects. Specifically, such dependence can lead to underestimated standard errors, artificially narrow confidence intervals, underestimated p-values, and an increased risk of Type I error (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001; Littell & Valentine, 2023).

To address this issue, two approaches are commonly used to account for dependent effect sizes: robust variance estimation (RVE) and multilevel meta-analysis. Simulation studies have shown that both methods produce similar estimates of the average effect size and associated standard errors, although each has distinct strengths and limitations (Moeyaert et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2025). RVE is appropriate when the dependence structure is unknown. In contrast, multilevel meta-analysis explicitly models dependence by specifying hierarchical levels that account for the covariance between effect sizes when the structure of the data is known. In the present review, dependence primarily arises from the hierarchical nesting of effect sizes within studies, as studies often contribute more than one effect size.

In addition, multilevel meta-analysis is well suited for examining heterogeneity. Given the field-wide scope of the review, substantial between- and within-study heterogeneity is expected. Multilevel meta-analysis enables the decomposition of variance within and between studies and supports the assessment of heterogeneity at both levels, which is not supported within the RVE framework (Fernández-Castilla et al., 2020; Moeyaert et al., 2017; Van den Noortgate et al., 2013; Polanin et al., 2022). This decomposition aligns with a central objective of the review, namely to assess variation in effects and identify sources of heterogeneity.

Accordingly, dependence among effect sizes will be handled using a multilevel meta-analysis approach. To assess the robustness of the findings to alternative assumptions regarding dependence, RVE will be implemented as a sensitivity analysis.

This three-level meta-analytic framework models the hierarchical structure of the data by accounting for clustering of effect sizes within studies and allows studies to contribute one or more effect sizes without exerting disproportionate influence on the pooled results (Assink & Wibbelink, 2016; Polanin et al., 2022; Van den Noortgate et al., 2013, 2015).

Effect sizes will be weighted using the following adapted inverse-variance weighting equation that accounts for within-study dependence (Hedges et al., 2010): Wij=1kjv ¯·j where Wij represents the weight assigned to a specific effect size i within study j; kj refers to the number of effect sizes extracted for study j; and the average variance v ¯·j accounts for the variability of effect sizes within study j.

When studies report multiple estimates that differ only in model specification (e.g., fixed-versus random-effects models) or in the set of control variables, we will conduct an internal meta-analysis and include the resulting pooled estimate in the main analysis (Higginson et al., 2018). This procedure is intended to avoid duplication of substantively equivalent estimates and prevent arbitrary selection of outcomes. It will not be applied to substantively distinct estimates. All instances of pooling will be explicitly documented to ensure transparency and replicability.

Additional dependence may arise from overlapping samples across studies, as some studies rely on the same open-source datasets—notably the Global Terrorism Database—and observe overlapping countries, time periods, and underlying terrorist events. Such overlaps will be identified and documented, and where feasible, addressed through subgroup analyses and bias assessments.

Dealing With Missing Data

Some studies included in the review may omit specific measures, endpoints, or subgroups from their reporting. Reporting bias could potentially arise within studies if authors choose not to report non-significant data points, very weak effects, or measures related to subgroups. We will attempt to retrieve missing data for any applicable included study. To do so, when creating the extraction Excel file, the reviewers will carefully keep track of missing data. Missing data should be coded to distinguish between items that were skipped by coders, missing data supplied by authors, partial reporting (e.g., missing data for effect size calculations or missing outcomes or subgroups), and data that is not available (Littell et al., 2021). The reviewers will attempt to retrieve missing study-level characteristics and effect sizes following the set of actions below (Wolfowicz, Litmanovitz, et al., 2021):(1) Searching for supplementary materials online;(2) Searching for other reports of the same study;(3) Contacting the study authors with concise missing data requests.

If the missing data cannot be retrieved using the action items above, imputation methods will be used when possible, including mean imputation or regression imputation (Pigott, 2009). It is important to note that these methods can lead to a misestimation of the variance among effect sizes. Therefore, reviewers should be aware of the potential bias that arises from imputation methods. To address this concern, sensitivity analyses will be performed to assess how the findings may be affected by missing data and the applied imputation methods.

In cases where there is insufficient data to calculate an imputed value, the effect size will not be included in the quantitative analysis, although the study will be included in the description of included studies and summary tables. Methods employed to deal with missing data will be reported in the final review to ensure transparency and to document the presence of potential reporting bias.

Assessment of Heterogeneity

The three-level meta-analytic framework allows for the decomposition of heterogeneity across multiple levels of the data hierarchy, distinguishing between variability within and between studies (Nakagawa & Santos, 2012). Overall heterogeneity will be summarized using the total Higgins-Thompson multilevel (Beccaria, 1764/1963) I 2 statistic, which reflects the joint proportion of total variance attributable to heterogeneity across all hierarchical levels. Level-specific Higgins-Thompson multilevel I 2 statistics will also be reported to quantify the proportion of variance attributable to heterogeneity at each level of the model: the effect-size level (L2; within-study heterogeneity) and the study level (L3; between-study heterogeneity) (Cheung, 2014; Holt et al., 2015). Estimates of the random-intercept variance components, τL22 and τL32 , representing between-effect-size variance and between-study variance, respectively, will also be reported. Residual heterogeneity will be measured using the multilevel Cochran QM test statistic.

Moderators may also exert an effect on the relationship between counterterrorism interventions and subsequent terrorist activity, for instance depending on the type of intervention, the attack ideology, or the geographic context (LaFree & Freilich, 2019; Shkolnik, 2023). Accordingly, subgroup and moderator analyses will be used to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, as detailed in Section 3.4.12.

Assessment of Reporting Biases

Publication bias is a critical threat to the validity of overall findings if the included studies are not representative of all studies conducted (Pustejovsky & Rodgers, 2019; Song et al., 2009). To mitigate this issue at the search stage, extensive searches of grey literature will be conducted to identify unpublished or non-peer-reviewed studies.

At the analysis stage, reporting bias will be examined using complementary diagnostic approaches. Because most publication-bias methods assume independent effect sizes, all diagnostics will be exploratory given the multilevel structure of the data.

First, funnel plots will be produced for descriptive purposes, including a funnel plot with all effect sizes and a study-level funnel plot using one summary estimate per study (e.g., a study-level mean) (Nakagawa et al., 2022, 2023). Given the violation of the independence assumption, evidence of asymmetry will be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect publication bias or true heterogeneity. Conventional Egger tests will be reported for exploratory purposes only.

Second, small-study effects will be assessed using Egger-type precision-based meta-regression, operationalized through PET and, where appropriate, PEESE models, in which effect sizes are regressed on measures of study precision (the standard error and its square) within a multilevel framework (Nakagawa et al., 2022, 2023). These regressions account for effect-size dependence and heterogeneity and will be interpreted as indicators of small-study effects rather than definitive evidence of publication bias.

Taken together, these approaches constitute an exploratory assessment of the robustness of the findings to potential publication bias and selective reporting, rather than formal tests of bias.

Data Synthesis

For the main models, three-level random-effects meta-analyses will be carried out using StataNow/SE 18.5 software (StataCorp, 2023). This software supports all aspects of the analysis, including meta-regression, moderator analyses for both continuous and categorical moderators, sensitivity analyses, and the visualization of results using tables and figures.

Effect sizes derived from models of event frequency (e.g., Pearson’s r), time-to-event analyses (e.g., ln(HR)), and other non-comparable metrics will not be combined and will instead be meta-analyzed and presented separately. Given the multilevel structure of the data, with multiple effect sizes nested within studies, standard forest plots may not adequately represent the data. Accordingly, results will be presented primarily in summary tables, alongside adapted visual displays where appropriate.

For each analysis, the pooled effect size and corresponding 95% confidence interval will be reported. In addition, prediction intervals will be presented to indicate the range within which the true effect size is expected to fall in 95% of comparable populations, thereby capturing the potential scope of true effects in future similar studies (Borenstein et al., 2021; IntHout et al., 2016). Statistical significance of the pooled effects will be assessed using z-tests. Moderator analyses will be conducted and reported, as detailed in the following section.

Subgroup Analyses and Investigation of Heterogeneity

Through moderator analyses and meta-regressions, the proposed review will assess potential sources of heterogeneity in effect sizes. Within the deterrence-backlash discourse, a contrast is often drawn between soft (e.g., conciliatory responses) and hard interventions (e.g., military responses), with some arguing that softer approaches are more likely to yield deterrent effects (Argomaniz & Vidal-Diez, 2017; Hsu & McDowall, 2020). Accordingly, the review will examine whether intervention type functions as a moderator of effect sizes. Intervention categories will be operationalized using the typology proposed by LaFree and Freilich (2019), enabling the classification of counterterrorism interventions into analytically meaningful groups (e.g., criminal justice responses, military responses, situational prevention measures). These moderator analyses will examine whether intervention types contribute to between-effect heterogeneity, and whether different categories systematically produce different effect estimates.

We will also investigate whether attack type is a potential source of heterogeneity. Research in ordinary crime shows that different forms of offending, such as property crime and violent crime, may respond differently to deterrence-oriented strategies (Bun et al., 2020; Marvell, 2010). Similarly, terrorism research indicates that attack types may exhibit distinct reactions to counterterrorism strategies. For example, Ozdogan and Ozdogan (2023) find that both conciliatory and repressive counterterrorism policies are associated with small increases in the rate of high-profile terror incidents but reduce the rate of low-profile incidents.

In addition, extremist ideology will be examined as a potential moderator. While many overlaps exist across ideologies, important differences exist, such as in levels of violence or in socio-demographic characteristics at the individual level (Chermak & Gruenewald, 2015; Jasko et al., 2022). These variations in extremist ideologies may also manifest in how they respond to deterrence efforts. Notably, a recent study in the Israeli context has highlighted that terror organizations with different ideological agendas vary in their reactions to counterterrorism interventions (Shkolnik, 2023). Moderator analyses will therefore be used to assess whether ideology type explains between-effect heterogeneity.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses will be carried out to test the robustness of the findings to alternative analytical decisions and modeling assumptions.

First, robust variance estimation (RVE) models will be estimated as a sensitivity analysis for the main models. RVE provides an alternative approach to handling dependent effect sizes and does not require specification of the underlying dependence structure (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022). Comparing RVE estimates with the main results will inform the sensitivity of the findings to assumptions regarding effect-size dependence.

Second, we will conduct outlier diagnostics using study-level random-effects predictions (best linear unbiased predictions; BLUPs). Each random intercept is a study-specific deviation from the overall pooled effect size. Primary models will be re-estimated excluding influential studies as a robustness check.

Third, sensitivity analyses may be conducted by re-estimating models after excluding specific groups of studies to assess sensitivity to inclusion decisions (Nakagawa et al., 2023). For instance, analyses may be re-run excluding studies with mixed OECD/non-OECD samples or those combining domestic and transnational terrorism outcomes.
