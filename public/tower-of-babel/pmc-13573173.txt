High prevalence of pes planus in children with Fontan circulation
Authors: Lisheng Lin, Junko Shiono, Shinpei Goto, Yusuke Yano, Yuta Tsukagoshi, Yukiko Ban, Masakazu Abe, Hitoshi Horigome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe musculoskeletal system is important for maintaining Fontan circulation. However, little is known about the foot morphology in this population. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of pes planus and its association with the patients’ physical characteristics after the Fontan procedure.MethodsThis single-center, cross-sectional study evaluated patients who underwent the Fontan procedure. Pes planus was assessed via physical examination. Physical measurements, including height, weight, body mass index, and fat-free mass index (FFMI), were collected and compared between patients with and without pes planus. Multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusted for age and sex, and time since the Fontan procedure was performed.ResultsForty-seven patients (median age: 13 years) were included. Pes planus was observed in 27 (57%) patients and was present in all age groups. The patients with pes planus had a significantly lower height SDS (P = 0.011) and tended to have a lower FFMI (P = 0.066). In the adjusted analysis, a lower FFMI showed a trend toward an association with pes planus, although this did not reach significance (OR 0.648, 95% CI 0.38–1.12, P = 0.118).ConclusionsPes planus is highly prevalent in patients with Fontan circulation and may represent an under-recognized musculoskeletal phenotype. Further studies are required to clarify the etiology and clinical significance.Keywords: Pes planus, Fontan circulation, Congenital heart disease, Musculoskeletal system, Growth

Introduction

The Fontan procedure is a palliative surgical strategy for patients with single-ventricle physiology, in which systemic venous return is directed to the pulmonary arteries without a sub-pulmonary ventricle. As pulmonary blood flow partly depends on peripheral muscle pump function, the musculoskeletal system plays an important role in supporting Fontan circulation [1].

After the Fontan procedure, patients present with abnormalities in somatic growth, bone mineral metabolism, and muscle mass [1]. These abnormalities may affect musculoskeletal development, including foot arch formation, and could potentially contribute to pes planus. During routine rehabilitation assessments of patients with Fontan circulation, we frequently observed flattening of the foot arch. However, despite these clinical observations, the prevalence of pes planus in this population has not been systematically investigated.

Pes planus (flatfoot), characterized by collapse of the medial longitudinal arch, is associated with leg pain, altered lower limb function, and reduced physical performance [2,3].

Therefore, a cross-sectional study was conducted to evaluate the prevalence of pes planus and its association with the physical characteristics of patients after the Fontan procedure.

Methods

This was a single-center, cross-sectional study. All patients who underwent the Fontan procedure > 6 months earlier and were followed up at our institution were included. Patients with paralysis or severe developmental disorders that made physical examinations difficult were excluded from this study. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Ibaraki Children's Hospital (approval no. 3IRB-23), and written informed consent was obtained from the patients and/or their parents.

Pes planus was defined as the disappearance of the medial longitudinal arch of the foot as evaluated by a physical therapist during a standing examination.

Physical measurements, including height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), were recorded. The z-scores of these measurements were calculated according to age- and sex-specific Japanese reference standards [4,5].

Fat-free mass (FFM) was measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody 570; InBody Co., Ltd). The fat-free mass index (FFMI) was calculated as FFM divided by height squared (kg/m2).

Pes planus prevalence was evaluated in the study population. Furthermore, physical measurements were compared between patients with and without pes planus using Student's t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between FFMI and the presence of pes planus after adjusting for age and sex, and time since the Fontan procedure. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 16.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Forty-seven patients who underwent the Fontan procedure were included in the study. The median age was 13 years (range, 3–22 years), the median time since the Fontan procedure was 120 months (range, 6–223 months), and 24 patients (51%) were male.

The z-scores of height, weight, and BMI were −1.00 ± 1.27, −0.73 ± 1.14, and −0.28 ± 0.92, respectively.

Pes planus was observed in 27 (57%) patients. The prevalence was 52% in patients aged 6–12 years and 50% in those aged 12–22 years, indicating that pes planus was present across all age groups rather than being limited to younger children (Fig. 1). Excluding children younger than 6 years, pes planus remained prevalent, affecting 20 of 38 patients (53%).

Age distribution and prevalence of pes planus in Fontan patients. Stacked bar chart showing the number of patients with and without pes planus across age groups. Pes planus was observed in 27 of the 47 patients (57%) and was present across all age groups.

Patients with pes planus had significantly lower height-SDS compared with those without pes planus (height-SDS -1.40 ± 1.27 vs −0.47 ± 1.10, P = 0.011). FFMI tended to be lower in patients with pes planus, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (median 13.2 [IQR 12.1–14.6] vs 14.2 [IQR 13.2–15.5], P = 0.066). No significant differences were observed in the weight SDS or BMI SDS between the groups.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, and time since the Fontan procedure, lower FFMI tended to be associated with the presence of pes planus, although the association did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.648, 95% CI 0.38–1.12, P = 0.118).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates a high prevalence of pes planus in patients with Fontan circulation. Importantly, pes planus was observed across all age groups rather than being limited to younger children. The high prevalence persisted even after excluding children younger than 6 years, suggesting that the findings were not simply attributable to the inclusion of younger children whose medial longitudinal arch may still be developing. To our knowledge, the prevalence of pes planus has not been reported in the broader congenital heart disease population. In the general pediatric population, the prevalence of pes planus has been reported to be approximately 13% in Japanese school-age cohorts and up to 25% overall, with a decreasing trend with increasing age [6,7]. However, direct comparison with the present study should be interpreted with caution because of differences in study populations and assessment methods.

Although this cross-sectional study could not establish causality, the findings suggest that foot arch formation may be delayed or altered in patients with Fontan circulation.

Several mechanisms may have contributed to this phenomenon. Reduced physical activity and delayed motor development, commonly observed in children with complex congenital heart disease [8], may impair normal arch development. Additionally, growth disturbances, reduced muscle mass, and abnormalities in bone metabolism associated with Fontan physiology [1] may influence foot morphology.

Consistent with this hypothesis, patients with pes planus in the present study had a significantly lower height SDS and a trend toward a lower FFMI.

Although most patients do not report direct symptoms such as foot pain, early recognition of pes planus may allow early intervention for potential motor development delay or musculoskeletal dysfunction.

This study has several limitations. The diagnosis of pes planus was based on clinical assessment without standardized objective criteria. In addition, flexible and rigid pes planus were not differentiated, and interobserver reliability was not assessed. Furthermore, the lack of an age-matched control group limits direct comparison with the general pediatric population. Because no assessment of foot morphology before the Fontan procedure was available, it could not be determined whether pes planus developed or progressed after the Fontan procedure. In addition, the potential influence of underlying genetic factors cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a high prevalence of pes planus in patients with Fontan circulation.

Further studies are needed to clarify the etiology of pes planus in this population and its relationship with musculoskeletal function and clinical outcomes.
