The cylindrical devices with tunable positive, infinite, and negative capacitance for dynamic random access memory
Authors: Hyeon Woo Park, Won-Tae Koo, Dong Ik Suh, Junseong Seo, Panithan Sriboriboon, Yunseok Kim, Seungyong Byun, Youngmo Kim, Ja-Yong Kim, Seung Wook Ryu, Heeyoung Jeon, Ki Vin Im, Gwangyeob Lee, Taeone Youn, Seho Lee, Jaeyun Yi, Seon Yong Cha, Cheol Seong Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to a stray field formation from the alternating ferroelectric bound charges of the multidomain state of the ferroelectric thin films, the negative capacitance effect can emerge in the ferroelectric/dielectric bilayer devices. However, the capacitance behavior of the thin ferroelectric/dielectric stacked films in the nanoscale cylindrical structure deviated from the negative capacitance state to unpredicted other states. This work confirmed that the thin (2.0 – 3.0 nm) ferroelectric Hf0.33Zr0.67O2 layer could have a positive capacitance, infinite capacitance, or negative capacitance by controlling the thickness of the stacked dielectric Al2O3 layer thickness (1.0 – 3.0 nm). A detailed analytical model and numerical simulation based on phase-field modeling, considering the ferroelectric polarization bound charge compensation by the injected/trapped interfacial carriers and the geometry effect of the cylinder, precisely identified the change of the inhomogeneous stray field energy, which played a crucial role in determining the capacitance state. Further analysis of the models revealed that the capacitance variation is strongly correlated to the extent of the initial polarization charge compensation of the ferroelectric domains and leakage mechanisms. This work provides guidelines for developing the next-generation capacitors in dynamic random access memory.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Electronic devices

Introduction

Thin ferroelectric (FE) films showed several critically different properties from the bulk FE material1–5. These differences could be primarily attributed to the involvement of the high depolarizing effect due to the insufficient compensation of the FE-bound charge, even when the chemical and crystal structures were of high quality2–4,6. A typical example is the metal-ferroelectric-insulator-metal (MFIM) structure, where the insulator layer interferes with the FE-bound charge compensation by the free carriers in the metal layer, which may depolarize the FE layer under critical circumstances. Nonetheless, such an adverse effect might be used positively. A notable example is the emergence of the negative capacitance (NC) effect from the MFIM structure7–16. In this case, the FE layer experiences a voltage with an opposite polarity to the applied one, increasing the voltage applied to the insulator or dielectric (DE) layer to a value higher than the applied value7,14,17. This effect can boost the charge storage capacity of the MFIM structure over the metal-insulator-metal structure. These effects were initially interpreted using the one-dimensional Landau–Ginzburg–Devonshire (LGD) theory7–9,18,19. However, the original LGD models with a single domain lack justification for the thin films with a multidomain structure. In contrast, recent theoretical and experimental works revealed that the inhomogeneous stray field energy (ISE) related to the domain wall (DW) motion could precisely explain the phenomenon6,14,20–22. The inhomogeneous stray field originates from alternating polarization-bound charges in a multidomain ferroelectric configuration, which generate spatially varying lateral electric fields near domain boundaries, as shown in Fig. 1. Formally, it corresponds to the spatially varying component of the electrostatic field obtained after subtracting the averaged vertical potential across the ferroelectric layer6. Unlike the macroscopic depolarization field, which represents an averaged vertical field component, the inhomogeneous stray field can persist even at zero net polarization20.

However, the NC effect necessarily increases the field across the DE layer, which invokes reliability concerns of the layer due to the increased leakage current12,18,23. An alternative approach could be an electrostatically transparent FE layer where a negligibly small average field is applied to the FE layer. Incorporation with the transparent FE layer may prevent serious leakage current degradation by reducing the field across the adjacent DE layer while maintaining the total capacitance of the bilayer the same as that of the DE layer. However, this requires an infinite capacitance (IC) or a huge positive capacitance (PC) of the FE layer rather than the NC effect, corresponding to the flattening of the internal energy vs. polarization curve.

Figure 1a shows the domain structures inside the FE/DE stacked structure (right panel) and the corresponding stray-field-related ISE (left panel). It shows that the electrostatic ISE density, fes,ih has the highest value when the two antiparallel domains have equal portions (depolarized state)6,20,22, and decreases with the DW motion (toward poled state) when no screening charges are involved. This effect induced the negative curvature in their ISE vs. FE polarization (fes,ih−Pf) curve, which is the fundamental asset for the NC effect manifested as a single S-shaped polarization vs. electric field (P–E) curve in the multidomain structure with the abrupt DW (hard DW) as shown in Fig. 1b. However, according to the extended ISE model that incorporates the coupling between the FE bound charges and injected charges, compensating the FE bound charge, at the FE/DE interfaces, the Λ-shaped fes,ih−Pf curves can be transformed to the V-shaped curve, and the double S-shaped P–E curve appears (Fig. 1c, d)24–28. This finding suggests that the P–E curve of the FE layer within the stacked structure, initially expected to manifest only as a single S-curve based on the phenomenological LGD theory, is subject to engineering where capacitance can dynamically change depending on the device configuration and voltage application conditions.

Moreover, the ISE model further predicts that the capacitance of the FE layer within the stacked structure can continuously vary from negative to positive value depending on the relative density of its screening charge25. This work adopts the extended ISE formalism to explain the experimentally observed capacitance transition effect (from PC to IC to NC) of the state-of-the-art dynamic random access memory (DRAM) pillar capacitors with a diameter of <20 nm. The FE Hf0.33Zr0.67O2 (HZO), DE Al2O3 (AO) thin films, and the TiN top and bottom electrodes comprised the cylindrical capacitor structure. Varying the FE and DE thicknesses can adjust the charge compensation mechanism, leading to a versatile transition between PC, IC, and NC effects. In addition, introducing curvature as a geometric parameter of the cylindrical stack further enhances the NC effect, since increased curvature strengthens the stray-field-driven depolarization and thus amplifies the capacitance boosting. Incorporating the cylindrical geometry effect of the capacitor with a comparable diameter and film thickness was critical in precisely identifying the capacitance transition effect.

This work examines various possible charge–voltage (Q–V) effects of the TiN/HZO/AO/TiN capacitor structure, and its mechanism was revealed both by the analytical ISE model derived in polar coordinates, and the numerical simulations based on the phase-field model for the structure25,29,30. Within this framework, the curvature-dependent enhancement of the NC effect was quantitatively verified, demonstrating that increased curvature amplifies the depolarization-driven ISE energy and consequently boosts the capacitance. It was experimentally confirmed that the bilayer’s equivalent oxide thickness (EOT) did not increase from that of the AO layer, while the leakage current could be decreased by four orders of magnitude by adding the HZO layer with the near-IC modulation. It therefore becomes essential to understand how the suppression of leakage current is correlated with the capacitance state of the FE/DE bilayer. In particular, while NC states are often accompanied by increased leakage due to enhanced fields across the DE, the present study demonstrates that IC states can enable dramatic leakage reduction, thereby establishing a direct link between capacitance transition and leakage behavior.

Results and discussion

Curvature-driven enhancement of NC in cylindrical FE/DE stack

While the extended ISE framework feasibly explains the capacitance transitions in planar FE/DE stacks (Fig. 1), applying the same method to the state-of-the-art DRAM architecture, where capacitors have a cylindrical pillar geometry, introduces the curvature of the cylinder as a new knob to control the performance. In this respect, FE/DE cylindrical stacks were examined while varying the inner radius, R, thereby tuning the curvature value κ. As shown in Fig. 2a, increasing curvature rotates the S-curve toward a more horizontal orientation. This trend originates from the increased ISE density at the depolarized state with increasing κ (Fig. 2b). Figure 2c clarifies the physical origin of the increased ISE density. For the same FE volume, higher curvature effectively increases the FE/DE interfacial perimeter for a given lateral domain period. This geometrical effect suppresses the self-screening of FE-bound charge by the antiparallel domain configuration, leading to stronger inhomogeneous potentials and stray fields. Figure 2d quantifies the slope of the S-curve at the origin (i.e., the negative dielectric constant, εih) for a fixed total FE and DE thickness of 5 nm. A smaller magnitude of the negative dielectric constant corresponds to a more horizontal S-curve. As the FE thickness decreases, the ISE energy per unit FE volume increases, driving the dielectric constant to smaller negative values and thereby strengthening the NC-driven capacitance boosting. However, excessive boosting through FE thickness scaling may destabilize the ferroelectric orthorhombic phase, suggesting that curvature modulation offers a more favorable route.

It should be noted that even at the given FE thickness tf = 3 nm, (along the black line in Fig. 2d), decreasing the inner radius R still drives the dielectric constant to a smaller negative value. Consistently, Fig. 2e shows that, along this line, decreasing the inner radius can produce up to a twofold capacitance enhancement compared with a planar capacitor of the same thickness. In contrast, the inverted configuration with FE on top of DE (Supplementary Fig. 1) exhibits the opposite trend: increasing curvature shortens the FE/DE interfacial length, strengthens lateral screening, lowers the ISE energy density, and consequently weakens the NC effect.

Impact of asymmetric interfacial screening on capacitance

The preceding analysis on cylindrical geometries revealed that cylinder curvature provides an additional degree of freedom for modulating the NC effect, supplementing the primary control mechanism of layer thickness. While such geometric factors are significant, the underlying physics remains rooted in the ISE, which critically depends on the charge compensation at the FE/DE interface. The physical origin of this compensating charge (σit) is understood as the injection of carriers from the electrode, which tunnel through the thin dielectric layer and are captured by trap states at the interface. This process is driven by the strong local electric field generated by the uncompensated FE-bound charges. Specifically, positive ferroelectric bound charges (σf=+Ps) are screened by electrons captured in acceptor-like traps, while negative bound charges (σf=−Ps) are screened by holes (or emitted electrons) from donor-like traps. To quantitatively describe the extent of this screening, the compensating parameter λ is introduced, defined as λ=σf+σit/σf. This parameter represents the ratio of the net bound charge (after screening) to the original ferroelectric bound charge. Thus, λ=0 indicates that the ferroelectric bound charge is completely compensated by the interfacial charge, while λ=1 signifies no compensation effect at all.

The original ISE models implicitly assume a symmetric screening, where λ is identical for both upward and downward domains. However, this may not be physically accurate. The fabrication process and inherent material properties of the FE/DE interface do not guarantee a symmetric distribution of these trap states. It is physically plausible that the injection barriers for electrons and holes from the electrode into the dielectric are significantly different. Furthermore, the density, energy level, and capture cross-section of acceptor-like traps can differ from those of donor-like traps. Such an imbalance would naturally lead to an asymmetric charge screening, where the degree of compensation for upward-pointing domains differs from that for downward-pointing domains. Therefore, to build a more comprehensive model, it is crucial to extend the investigation beyond the simplified assumption of symmetric screening. To account for this, two independent parameters are introduced in the model: λ+ for the upward-pointing domains (where the bound charge is +Ps) and λ− for the downward-pointing domains (where the bound charge is −Ps) as shown in Fig. 3.

This analysis also uncovers a practically significant consequence. In the FE/DE bilayer structure, asymmetric screening results in a net average interfacial charge, σit,avg=−Psζ where ζ=λ+−λ−2. For example, in the λ+=0,λ−=0.5 case, a net positive screening charge exists (σit,avg=0.25Ps), which induces an internal electric field within the FE layer. This internal field acts as a built-in bias, causing a horizontal shift of the initial operation point on the P–E curve, which is an effect analogous to imprint. This is represented by the red and blue diamond markers (with the inset schematics of the multi-domain configuration in Fig. 3a) in Fig. 3a, b, which show the initial state at zero external voltage is slightly shifted from the depolarized state Pf=Psδ=0. Although the device’s operating point is shifted, it still follows the same universal P–E and ISE curves. This finding suggests a strategy for device engineering: by intentionally designing an interface that promotes specific asymmetric screening, the initial operating point could be precisely set. For instance, the zero-bias state could be positioned directly in the IC region, enabling a device that exhibits near-IC characteristics from the onset of low-voltage operation.

Figure 5a describes the incremental pulse measurement setup to extract the Q–V curves of the pillar-shaped capacitors. In this measurement, the Qdis means the reversibly discharged charge density for the applied pulse height in the x-axis with a duration of 50 μs (Supplementary Fig. 4). Figure 5b, c show the Q–V curves for bilayers consisting of HZO(3.0 nm)/AO(2.0 nm), and HZO(2.0 nm)/AO(3.0 nm) for positive and negative bias, respectively. The dashed lines corresponding to the 2.0 and 3.0 nm AO single layers were obtained by calculating the k values (~5.37 and ~ 5.16 for positive and negative bias, respectively) from the experimental measurement data of these single layers (Supplementary Fig. 5). The negligible residual charge density (Qres) indicates the hysteresis-free behaviors of the capacitors. The slopes of the Q–V curves correspond to the differential capacitance density of the specific stacked HZO/AO or single AO layer. A high leakage current density (J) prohibited the data acquisition for the AO single layer below 2.0 nm. However, stacking the 3.0 nm-thick HZO film on the 2 nm-thick AO film decreased the J by ~four orders of magnitude (Fig. 5d), allowing the precise Q–V estimation. While the slopes of the Q–V curves of the stacked device are initially smaller than that of the single 2.0 nm AO layer, the difference becomes negligible as the pulse height increases, indicating the IC effect in the HZO layer. The capacitance enhancement over the single 3.0 nm AO layer was observed in the HZO(2.0 nm)/AO(3.0 nm) sample, indicating a transition from the PC (HZO(3.0 nm)/AO(2.0 nm)) to NC (HZO(2.0 nm)/AO(3.0 nm)). However, as expected from the NC effect, the leakage current suppression, in this case, was much weaker (Fig. 5e) than in the near-IC state (Fig. 5d), demonstrating the over-voltage across the AO 3.0 nm layer due to the involvement of the NC effect in the HZO(2.0 nm). The data in Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 6 prove the resilience of the near-IC and NC states of the samples over the repeated voltage stresses. The endurance of the Q–V tests up to 109 cycles was demonstrated even at the high pulse voltage of 1.4 V, almost twice the capacitor voltage of conventional DRAM. In addition, Supplementary Fig. 7 demonstrated the thermal stability of the NC and IC capacitors. The NC and IC capacitors exhibited stable operation over a wide range of temperatures (−30, −10, 0, 25, 50, 75, 95, and 125 °C) and voltage amplitudes (0.6, 1.0, and 1.4 V), without notable deterioration. The NC and IC capacitors further exhibited stable charging/discharging properties at 0.6, 1.0, and 1.4 V under typical cold (−10 °C) and hot (125 °C) temperatures up to 109 cycles with very low variations (σ/μ for cycle to cycle, ≤ 0.7% for the NC capacitor and ≤ 1.3% for the IC capacitors). Furthermore, Supplementary Fig. 8 displays the data for pulse lengths ranging from 1 to 100 μs. The Qdis under short pulse conditions (1.19 μC/cm2 for NC capacitor and 1.42 μC/cm2 for the IC capacitor at 0.6 V and 1 μs) were slightly lower than those of long pulses (1.24 μC/cm2 for NC capacitor and 1.44 μC/cm2 for the IC capacitor at 0.6 V and 100 μs), but there was no significant degradation. These results demonstrated that the NC and IC capacitors can be used in a wide range of pulse lengths. In addition, the error bars in Fig. 5b–e obtained from different devices confirm the reliability and repeatability of the capacitors. In particular, the relative standard deviation (σ/μ) of NC and IC capacitors is 2.49 and 2.25%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating the high repeatability of the NC and IC capacitors. Supplementary Fig. 10 compares the physical thickness ranges of this work to the previous studies on MFIM-based NC capacitors10,12,13,31–37. To implement the stacked layers into the tiny gap between the storage nodes of the state-of-the-art DRAM with a design rule <15 nm, the combined film thickness range must be within the lower-left corner region of the graph bounded by the pink line. Only this work’s result meets the stringent criteria.

However, the capacitance enhancement observed in Fig. 5b, c could also be attributed to the Maxwell–Wagner effect, which occurs due to leakage current passing through each layer. Further analyses demonstrated that this was not the case (Supplementary Note 1), and the consistent change in capacitance variation with device thickness can be explained solely by the unique effects of multiple domains in the ferroelectric layer and the compensating charges. In addition, Polarization-Voltage (P–V) measurements obtained from a bipolar triangular sweep on the same HZO/AO stacks Supplementary Fig. 11) reproduce the quasi-static Q–V response extracted from the incremental-pulse method (Fig. 5b, c), in the absence of macroscopic hysteresis, indicating that the quasi-static IC condition is consistently realized under bipolar excitation. Furthermore, consistent with the pulse results, small-signal capacitance–voltage measurements also revealed enhanced capacitance in NC-like stacks and only modest reduction in IC-like stacks compared with AO single layers (Supplementary Note 2).

Independent measurements on a structurally equivalent planar 3.0-nm HZO film formed on an amorphous-AO(1.0 nm)/amorphous-HfO2(1.5 nm) dielectric layer (Supplementary Fig. 12) further confirm the presence of nanoscale ferroelectric domains and orthorhombic phase signatures through piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) and XRD analyses. The corresponding hysteresis-free Q–V characteristics also exhibit clear capacitance enhancement over the amorphous-AO/amorphous-HfO2 reference layer, reproducing the same nonlinear polarization response observed in the pillar capacitors and demonstrating that the IC/NC behavior persists even in the planar geometry. These independent planar characterizations reinforce that the capacitance modulation observed in the cylindrical capacitors arises from ferroelectric domain redistribution rather than from leakage-driven or defect-related artifacts.

The double S-curve behavior of the HZO(3.0 nm)/AO(2.0 nm) thin film is attributed to a V-shape ISE landscape, as shown in Fig. 6b. In contrast, the HZO(2.0 nm)/AO(3.0 nm) device has the ISE landscape with Λ-shape, indicating a negative curvature across the entire polarization range.

The difference in the ISE landscape is attributed to the coupling of the injected compensating charges and the polarization switching. Phase field modeling was conducted for this cylindrical structure to show this effect visually, and Fig. 6c, d show the domain structure (within the ferroelectric layer) and the distribution of trapped interfacial compensating charge (σit, at the HZO/AO interface) for the HZO(2.0 nm)/AO(3.0 nm) and HZO(3.0 nm)/AO(2.0 nm) devices, respectively, under different applied voltages to the top electrode (outer ring in these figures). Detailed methods for the formulation of the phase field model and simulation parameters can be found in the “Methods” section and Supplementary Note 6. As the applied voltage increases, inward polarization domains grow via the DW motion. However, the σit can also impact the change in energy of the inhomogeneous stray field resulting from the domain structure.

A relatively high λ value (λ = 0.7 ~ 0.9) corresponds to weak compensation, where σit is opposite in sign but small in magnitude compared to Ps. Under this condition, the ISE decreases as poling proceeds, as shown in the magenta curves in Fig. 6b. This decrease can be visualized as the evolution of the inhomogeneous potential (φih) directly calculated from the phase-field model, as shown in Fig. 6e, which is consistent with the analytical ISE model in Fig. 1a. In contrast, the double S-curve and V-shape ISE landscape observed in the HZO(3.0 nm)/AO(2.0 nm) device occurs when λ is small (ca. λ = 0.28). As shown in Fig. 6d, when the initial compensating charge has already compensated for a significant amount of FE-bound charge, the initial φih in the depolarized state is relatively small. If the polarization switching can be induced without changing the σit distribution by the short pulse application10,12,13, a substantial charge imbalance between the initial compensating charge and the newly formed bound charge in each domain will be induced. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 6f, this imbalance will increase further as poling progresses toward a single-domain state, generating higher φih and increased ISE as the poling proceeds, which is also consistent with Fig. 1c. As the same effect will manifest under opposite bias conditions, the increase in initial compensating charge causes the capacitance of the ferroelectric layer to change from a single NC to IC on both bias polarities. Other energy contributions, such as the Landau bulk energy and the gradient (domain-wall) energy, are explicitly included in the phase-field model (Supplementary Note 6). However, in HZO films with hard DWs, these terms remain almost constant when varying dielectric film thickness and, therefore, cannot account for the observed dielectric film thickness-dependent transition of the ISE landscape22.

Finally, it is essential to distinguish the NC effect observed in this work from the apparent NC phenomena reported in the literature39–43. The term apparent NC is often used to describe measurement artifacts caused by transient effects such as delayed nucleation and imprint effect, resulting in reverse switching. However, our experimental results exhibit several key features inconsistent with such a dynamic, non-equilibrium mechanism. Specifically, the NC effect was observed symmetrically at both positive and negative voltages. It appeared immediately from the zero external voltage, rather than exhibiting the unidirectional and threshold behaviors typical of reverse switching. Most importantly, the Qres remains negligible and stable across a wide range of pulse widths and voltages (Supplementary Fig. 8). A dynamic, transient artifact would be highly sensitive to timing conditions. In contrast, this stable, near-zero Qres indicates that the measurement is probing a genuine (near) equilibrium state. These results strongly support that the phenomenon observed here is not a transient artifact, but rather a quasi-static NC/IC effect that is well-described by the stable ISE landscape. Accordingly, the NC/IC effect originates from a steady-state inhomogeneous stray-field distribution and can electrically persist provided that the effective multidomain period remains unchanged25.

Capacitance tuning in thin HZO/AO stacks

Given the significant impact of compensating charge on the capacitance of the ferroelectric layer, it is worth controlling externally the charge injection to modify the capacitance. Analytical ISE models and numerical phase field models both revealed that the transition from a single S-curve for HZO(2.0 nm)/AO(3.0 nm) to a double S-curve for HZO(3.0 nm)/AO(2.0 nm) originates from an increase in the magnitude of σit. In this regard, previous studies have shown that the high electric field generated at the interfacial DE layer between the ferroelectric layer and the electrode can induce the injection of compensating charge from the electrode18,23,44,45. Therefore, it is possible to externally control the magnitude of the accumulated compensating charge by varying the thickness of the interfacial AO layer.

Figure 7a shows the extracted Pf–Ef curves when the AO layer thickness increases from 1.0 to 2.5 nm while keeping the 3.0 nm of the HZO layer. As the AO thickness increases, the double S-curve rotates anticlockwise. Figure 7b indicates that the change is related to a decrease in the curvature of the ISE near the origin with the AO thickness increase. This decrease in curvature signifies a substantial ISE increment in the initial depolarized state. To further illustrate the theoretical basis of these thickness-driven transitions, the cylindrical ISE response as a function of the interfacial compensation factor λ was additionally analyzed under the assumption of symmetric screening (Supplementary Note 7). As shown in Fig. S6, decreasing λ from unity to zero gradually transforms the ferroelectric P–E curve from a single-S to a double-S shape, with the corresponding ISE landscape evolving from Λ- to V-type curvature. This finding directly complements the AO-thickness dependence shown in Fig. 7b, c, confirming that the observed capacitance transitions are fundamentally governed by the degree of interfacial charge compensation.

Figure 7c shows the change in λ values as a function of the thickness of each device in the analytical model and the numerical model. Both models showed that the AO thickness increase leads to a decrease in σit (i.e., an increase in λ), further increasing the ISE in the initial state. The relationship between the AO layer thickness and the screening factor λ, or, more specifically, the interfacial charge density σit can be understood by examining how large an electric field is applied to the AO layer from the initial antiparallel domain structure. As shown in the band diagram in the inset of Fig. 7c, the uncompensated FE-bound charge of the FE domain distorts the internal potential, facilitating the injection of σit through the AO layer. Assuming the same domain width and uncompensated FE bound charge, the magnitude of the electric field induced in the AO layer of 1.0 nm thickness reaches ~ 8 MV/cm under the zero-bias condition. This high field causes carrier tunneling across the AO layer, allowing the FE-bound charge to be almost completely compensated by the charges σit. Increasing the thickness of the AO layer decreases the electric field down to 4.7 MV/cm at a thickness of 2.5 nm, effectively suppressing the charge injection.

Interestingly, the change in the ISE landscape caused by σit plays a crucial role in understanding the transition of capacitance, even in the LGD model11,24,26. If screening charges are introduced through the thin AO layer, the depolarization field significantly decreases, destabilizing the intrinsic NC described in the LGD model and potentially forming antiparallel domains24–26. In this case, a double S-curve can also be derived from the LGD model24,25. Further analysis of the LGD model shows that the landscape of ISE changes depending on the amount of charge compensation in each domain, which, along with LGD internal energy, can cause the capacitance variation from PC to IC to NC (Supplementary Note 8). While the LGD model and the ISE model may utilize different mechanisms—homogeneous switching within domains for LGD and lateral DW motion for ISE—they agree that changes in stray field energy within antiparallel structures are critical drivers of capacitance transitions. This coincidence suggests a universal underlying physics in FE/DE thin films, where the variation in the degree of charge compensation, governed by the thickness of the DE layer, induces the observed capacitance changes.

If the ferroelectricity of HZO films can be retained even below 2 nm, the AO thickness can be increased to over 3 nm for the given total thickness margin of state-of-the-art DRAM cell capacitors (5 nm). The thicker AO film significantly suppresses the charge compensation, ultimately leading to the capacitance transition to an NC. Figure 7d shows the correlation between the EOT and the leakage current density at a driving voltage of 0.8 V for a cylindrical thin HZO/AO capacitor. Generally, the leakage current of dielectric thin films is inversely proportional to the EOT since a decrease in EOT typically results from thinner physical thickness. Techniques such as introducing a very thin insulating layer of AO in between, like ZrO2/Al2O3/ZrO2 (ZAZ), have been widely applied to prevent the leakage current increase46–49. However, this approach has the drawback of significantly increasing the EOT value due to the serial connection with the additional capacitance.

However, this work shows that adding a thin FE film with variable capacitance on top of the DE layer can minimize the EOT increase. When the thickness of the AO layer is relatively thick, between 2.0 and 2.5 nm, the near-IC state of the FE layer manifests, resulting in no increase in the EOT even with the stacking of the FE layer. Actually, the EOT slightly decreases. On the other hand, the leakage current density is significantly decreased by ~105 times in the near-IC devices.

In the case of a 3.0 nm-thick AO layer, the compensation effect is further suppressed with the stacking of HZO thin films, resulting in the manifestation of the NC effect. A substantial EOT decrease (~ 0.7 nm) is expected in this case. However, the boosted voltage over the FE layer by the NC effect diminishes the effect of mitigating leakage current.

Correlation of capacitance transition with leakage pathways

Figure 7d clearly showed that the suppression of leakage current in HZO/AO bilayers is strongly dependent on the capacitance state of the ferroelectric layer. A significant reduction is consistently observed in the near-IC regime, whereas little suppression is obtained in the NC regime. An alternative interpretation could be that these trends reflect the thickness dependence of the AO dielectric, rather than the capacitive state of the HZO layer. To address this possibility, the leakage mechanisms of AO single-layer devices and HZO/AO bilayers were systematically analyzed, as described in Supplementary Note 9. The results confirmed that while the J–V curves can be fitted by a combination of direct tunneling (DT) and trap-limited space charge limited conduction (SCLC), the extracted SCLC and DT parameters in the bilayers show abnormal trends that cannot be explained by a simple serial conduction of the dielectric bilayers.

Based on these findings, a FE domain-resolved numerical trap-limited SCLC conduction model in the bilayer was constructed to capture the essential mechanism responsible for the observed behavior. Supplementary Note 10 explains the detailed formulation of the numerical model. In this model, the screening of ferroelectric bound charges at the FE/DE interface was explicitly taken into account to be consistent with the IC or NC state of the ISE model, allowing the correlation between capacitance transition and leakage pathways to be quantitatively established, as presented in Fig. 7d. In this framework, the HZO/AO bilayer is described in cylindrical geometry and the conduction is governed by trap-limited SCLC based on Mark-Helfrich (MH) model50–53, while the ferroelectric polarization of HZO introduces bound charges at the FE/DE interface.

The domain-resolved mechanism of leakage conduction in HZO/AO bilayers was first examined in the NC regime (HZO(2.0 nm)/AO(3.0 nm)). Figure 8a, b show the simulated potential and electron concentration, along with the current distribution under these conditions. It is observed that conduction depends strongly on the sign of the interfacial bound charge. The upward domains, carrying positive charges, promote electron injection, thereby enhancing SCLC conduction, whereas the downward domains, carrying negative charges, suppress electron injection and create carrier depletion, leading to a significantly reduced local current. As a result, the global leakage is governed primarily by the upward-domain regions, which form the dominant conduction pathways. Figure 8c confirms this picture, showing that the simulated J–V of the NC device closely matches the experiment, with leakage currents only slightly reduced compared to the AO 3.0 nm single layer. In a higher bias, the leakage even exceeds that of a dielectric-only bilayer (dashed curves) due to the facilitation of the leakage through the upward-domain regions by the unscreened positive bound charges. This explains why the leakage current is reduced by only about a factor of three compared to the AO 3.0 nm single layer in the HZO(2.0 nm)/AO(3.0 nm) bilayer.

Having established how bound charge polarity dictates the leakage channels, the IC regime (HZO(3.0 nm)/AO(2.0 nm)) with more effective screening of the bound charges was next considered. In this case, it is crucial to understand how the leakage pathways were modified if the screening of bound charges was asymmetric. Specifically, if positive charges are fully compensated, while negative charges remain unscreened. This condition was modeled by setting the upward-domain charges to vanish (σnet,↑=0μC/cm2) while retaining the downward-domain charges (σnet,↓=−11.5μC/cm2). The resulting distributions are shown in Fig. 8d, e. Notably, the negative potential barriers generated at the downward domains extended laterally into the upward-domain regions, narrowing their conduction channels as well. In this configuration, the global leakage pathways were almost entirely blocked, and the current density was drastically reduced compared to the case without ferroelectric polarization. Figure 8f demonstrates that this asymmetric screening scenario reproduces the experimentally observed more than 10⁴-fold leakage suppression in the HZO(3.0 nm)/AO(2.0 nm) bilayer. By contrast, a dielectric-only bilayer of the same total thickness can account for at most ~10²-fold suppression, underscoring the inadequacy of the AO thickness scaling scenario alone. In addition, a quantitative evaluation of the possibility that the leakage reduction originates from HZO-induced modifications in the AO defect density was performed. As shown in Supplementary Note 11, realistic variations of the AO trap density cannot reproduce the ~10⁴-fold leakage suppression, confirming that AO defect fluctuations are not responsible for the observed trend. Importantly, asymmetric screening does not alter the IC capacitive behavior itself, as shown previously (Fig. 3), since IC depends only on the average compensation of bound charges.

The physical origin of this asymmetric screening can be attributed to the intrinsic carrier injection barriers at the metal/oxide interfaces. Electron injection into the conduction band is energetically much more favorable than hole injection into the valence band, owing to the large band offset between the electrode Fermi level and the oxide valence band edge. As a result, positive bound charges at the FE/DE interface are readily compensated by electron injection. In contrast, negative bound charges remain only weakly screened because the corresponding hole injection is strongly suppressed. This fundamental asymmetry in carrier injection thus provides a natural explanation for the selective screening assumed in our model.

This mechanism is also consistent with the polarity-dependent asymmetry observed in the J–V curves of the bilayers, where higher leakage currents are observed under negative bias (Fig. 5d, e). Under negative bias on the outer-radius electrode, the fraction of upward domains increases, and conduction is facilitated through more positive bound charges at the FE/DE interface. Conversely, under positive bias, downward domains dominate, and leakage is further suppressed. Such asymmetry is absent in AO single layers, reinforcing that it originates from the ferroelectric polarization in the bilayer.

Taken together, these results establish a direct causal link between capacitance state and leakage suppression: IC states correspond to preferential screening of positive bound charges, leading to global suppression of SCLC pathways. In contrast, NC states correspond to incomplete screening of both positive and negative bound charges, leading to upward-domain-dominated conduction enhancement, which yields little leakage reduction in the bilayer. The clear correlation between the charge distribution responsible for capacitance transition and the observed leakage pathways provides strong additional support for the physical mechanism underlying the ISE model, which explains the NC/IC behavior in HZO/AO bilayers.

The experimental demonstration of the NC (or NC-like) effect in several thin-film systems, including the FE layer, became feasible when the adverse charge injection during the measurements was suppressed10,12,13,36. Although the original suggestion of such an intriguing effect was based on the one-dimensional LGD formalism7,12,13, the presence of DWs (or domains) in the experimental systems required other mechanisms than the original suggestion. The recent theoretical model based on the inhomogeneous electrostatic energy related to the DW motion provided a crucial advancement for the theory and experiment14,20,22,25. Recently, the original ISE model has been extended to show that the versatile transition between PC, IC, and NC is viable considering the external coupling effect with the compensation charges25. In this study, it was experimentally demonstrated that such transitions can be realized in thin FE/DE bilayer vertical capacitors with a cylindrical structure and total thicknesses on the order of ~5 nm by varying the relative thicknesses of the FE and DE layers. This study also revealed that analyzing the NC effect in the cylindrical capacitor structure using the conventional planar model can lead to an overestimation due to the differing field distributions38. Furthermore, cylindrical geometries were shown to enhance the ISE energy compared with planar structures, amplifying and stabilizing NC effects. This result demonstrates that ISE landscape transitions in antiparallel systems can similarly manifest in cylindrical configurations, opening the way for cylindrical DRAM capacitors to exploit stronger NC boosting than planar models.

At the same time, the capacitance transition observed in thin FE/DE bilayers was shown to originate from the ISE mechanism under the influence of statically injected charges, which must be distinguished from adverse dynamic injection during electrical, which must be differentiated from the above-mentioned adverse charge injection during the measurements10,13,36. The injected charges substantially modify the ISE landscape. The degree of injected charge must depend on the thickness of the intervening DE layer. The detailed simulation of the domain and compensating charge configurations based on the phase-field method reproduced the experimental results accurately. As a result, the observed IC effect results from the subtle coupling between the FE-bound charge and the static compensating charge during the DW motion, which results in the inverse W-shaped energy landscape. When the compensating effect is negligible, the NC effect is achieved. In contrast, the continuous transition from the NC to IC to PC can be realized when the charge injection becomes more fluent.

In addition to the geometrical and electrostatic insights, the present study establishes a direct causal link between capacitance transition and leakage suppression in FE/DE bilayers, while also clarifying the role of static charge injection. Through a systematic analysis of AO single-layer and bilayer devices, it was confirmed that simple AO thickness scaling cannot explain the approximately 10⁴-fold reduction in leakage observed in IC states. A FE domain-resolved SCLC numerical model incorporating asymmetric screening of ferroelectric bound charges was therefore developed, which quantitatively reproduced the experimental J–V curves. The results revealed that upward domains with positive charges promote electron injection while downward domains with negative charges suppress conduction. In NC devices, where positive FE-bound charges remain unscreened, leakage is dominated by upward-domain conduction and is decreased only slightly compared to AO single layers. By contrast, in IC devices, preferential screening of positive charges results in the global blocking of conduction pathways, reproducing the significant leakage suppression observed experimentally. This strong correlation between charge distribution, capacitance transition, and leakage current provides compelling and independent support for the physical basis of the ISE model.

Using the near-IC characteristics of the FE layer, the experimental results suggest an approach for decreasing the EOT of the DRAM cell capacitor without increasing the leakage current. Nevertheless, as the capacitance variation depends on the specific injection mechanism through the dielectric layer, more comprehensive future works are required to apply the suggested methods using higher dielectric constant thin films than the Al2O3 film.

Methods

Sample preparation and structural analysis

Arrays of silicon (Si) cylinders were prepared using the state-of-the-art fabrication process of commercialized DRAM cell capacitors with a diameter of <20 nm. Then, bottom electrode (BE) TiN, hafnium zirconium oxides (HZO), aluminum oxides (AO), and top electrode (TE) were sequentially deposited on the sidewall of Si cylinders using atomic layer deposition process (Supplementary Fig. 2). HZO thin films were deposited at a deposition temperature of 300 °C and a pressure of 1.7 Torr using atomic layer deposition (ALD), adopting a supercycle concept for precise composition control. The subcycle and supercycle of HZO layers were modulated to achieve optimal o-phase HZO layers. Hf[N(CH3)2]3(C5H5) and Zr[N(CH3)2]3(C5H5) were used as precursors, and ozone was used as an oxygen source. After the HZO deposition, post-deposition annealing (PDA) was performed for 1 h at 500 °C in N2 atmosphere to crystallize the HZO films. Subsequently, amorphous AO layers were deposited on the top of the HZO films at 300 °C using trimethylaluminum (Al(CH3)3) as the Al precursor and ozone as the oxygen source for ALD. The thickness of HZO and AO layers was controlled by the modulation of ALD cycles. Lastly, 3.5 nm of TiN was deposited as a top electrode on HZO/AO stacks by the ALD process using TiCl4 and NH3. Both bottom and top electrodes are connected to the metal lines.

The TEM analysis on pillar-type FE/DE capacitors with a bilayer of HZO 3.0 nm and AO 2.0 nm was conducted for material characterization. The TEM specimens were prepared using a focused ion beam system. The TEM images and the dark-field scanning TEM (STEM) analyses were conducted by aberration-corrected TEM (JEM-ARM200F, JEOL) equipped with electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). The plan view of the FE/DE capacitor arrays exhibits the uniform fabrication of FE/DE capacitors (Fig. 4b). The fabricated device has a MFIM configuration with pillar-type capacitor structures (Fig. 4c). The high-resolution TEM image of the FE/DE capacitor exhibits the crystalline HZO layers and amorphous AO layers (Fig. 4d), with the desired thicknesses of the HZO/AO layers. In addition, the fast Fourier transform patterns of HZO layers indicate the presence of the ferroelectric orthorhombic phase (inset image in Fig. 4d). EELS mapping images and line profiles further confirm the thin FE/DE capacitor structure (Fig. 4e, f). The pillar-type capacitor structure with HZO 2.0 nm and AO 3.0 nm was also confirmed by the TEM analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore, The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted by using a high-resolution powder X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab 9 kW, Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) to investigate the crystal structures of the samples. The XRD analysis of pillar-type FE/DE capacitors confirms that the FE layers of HZO are polycrystalline with a mixture of orthorhombic and tetragonal phases (Fig. 4g). In order to demonstrate the phase ratio of the samples further, the XRD data of HZO layers was deconvoluted to the peaks of o-, t-, and m-phases (Supplementary Fig. 13). As a result, the o-phase, t-phase, and m-phase ratios were identified as approximately 75.9%, 13.4%, and 10.7%, respectively.

Electrical measurement and extraction of Df–Ef and ISE landscape

The pulse measurements of the capacitors were conducted using a Keithley 4200. The incremental single-pulse measurement method was used for the capacitance extraction. The applied voltage pulses as a function of time are described in Supplementary Fig. 4. The rising, duration, and falling time of the pulses and the delay time between the pulses are 50 μs. To calculate the stored charges (Qch), reversible discharging charges (Qdis), and residual charges (Qres) of the capacitors, the preset negative voltage pulse (Pre pulse), the first positive or negative voltage pulse (P pulse) were applied consecutively. Qch is obtained from the accumulated charges during the P pulse, and Qres is directly obtained from the residual charge density after the discharging of the capacitor. Qdis is calculated as the difference between Qch and Qres (Qdis = Qch – Qres). I–V measurements of the capacitors were carried out using Agilent 4082 F.

PFM measurements
