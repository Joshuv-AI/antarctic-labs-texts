The role of fractional derivatives and magnetic fields in shaping MHD newtonian flow behavior in injected diverging channels
Authors: M. Tolami, A. Nazari-Golshan, S. S. Nourazar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study comprehensively analyzes how fractional-order calculus and externally applied magnetic fields synergistically govern the hydrodynamic behavior of electrically conducting Newtonian fluids in divergent geometries by injecting flow through the walls at varying tangential velocities. Using Caputo fractional derivatives, the nonlinear boundary value problem is addressed through a dual approach: an innovative implementation of the Adaptive Fractional Method (AFM) for analytical solutions and a high-precision finite difference scheme for numerical validation. Key observations reveal that enhancement of the Reynolds number (Re) or the fractional differentiation order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} suppressing near-wall velocities while amplifying core-region flow magnitudes. In contrast, increased Hartmann numbers (Ha) lead to significant attenuation of peak velocities due to interactions with the Lorentz force, which reveal a dual effect when reaching the Hartmann number of approximately 17: (i) the radial velocity profile begins to show uniformity at the center of the channel, and (ii) the velocity profile indicates the presence of two peak velocities near the channel walls. As well as when the Hartmann number surpasses approximately 26, the memory and non-local effects within the velocity field are mitigated. An increase in Ha results in the expansion of the streaklines. When the Hartmann number is low (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ha \approx 2$$\end{document}), an increase in the channel divergence angle leads to a decrease in the maximum velocity and a refinement of the velocity profile. Conversely, at high Hartmann numbers (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ha \ge 7$$\end{document} ), augmenting the channel divergence angle produces effects on the velocity field akin to those observed with an increase in the Hartmann number, specifically reducing the velocity at the channel’s center and resulting in an M-shaped velocity profile.

Introduction

Magnetic fields significantly modulate the dynamics of conductive fluids, including plasmas and liquid metals, a phenomenon central to magnetohydrodynamics (MHD). The field of MHD investigates these interactions, examining how magnetic fields influence properties like velocity and pressure distributions, heat transfer, and related phenomena. These effects have been harnessed in applications ranging from astrophysics and plasma physics to engineering systems for controlling backflow and stabilizing flow in microfluidic devices1,2. The study of MHD flow regulation holds significant experimental and practical importance. By altering the velocity field, magnetic fields can enhance flow symmetry and stability, which is crucial for optimizing fluid performance3,4. This capability enables precise, contact-free control of fluid motion, offering distinct advantages in biomedical applications such as pumping and mixing, where mechanical components are undesirable5. Moreover, MHD flow control is vital in aeronautical engineering, including MHD-assisted ramjets, turbine engines, and boundary-layer management, where tailoring flow properties through magnetic fields and channel design provides a versatile tool for improving efficiency and performance6. The capacity to adjust flow characteristics, including velocity and temperature distributions, as well as to prevent flow separation and manage boundary layer properties via magnetic fields and injection/suction techniques, can significantly improve the precision and effectiveness of drug delivery systems7,8. MHD flow models are effective in managing heat transfer, which is essential in plasma systems where temperature control is critical7,9.

Fractional derivatives provide an advanced mathematical framework for capturing fluids’ complex behavior. Unlike integer-order derivatives, fractional derivatives model memory effects and long-range interactions, making them essential for studying non-Newtonian fluids, anomalous diffusion, turbulence, and boundary layers10–14. Their applications extend to diverse areas, including polymer science, biological fluid dynamics, and viscoelastic materials15–19. Numerous studies have leveraged the efficacy of fractional calculus in simulating flows within porous media and under the influence of magnetic effects, which possess extensive applications in engineering and industrial systems. Guo et al.20 investigated mass and heat transfer over an inclined porous surface using Atangana-Baleanu (AB) and Caputo fractional derivatives, highlighting applications in thermal processes and energy production. Raza et al.21 studied Maxwell fluid flow with AB and Caputo-Fabrizio (CF) fractional derivatives, noting that CF-based models showed declining temperature and velocity profiles. Further, Raza et al.22 applied Prabhakar fractional derivatives to MHD fluid flow, demonstrating how fractional parameters influence momentum profiles. Their work on Casson nanofluids23 revealed that Prabhakar derivatives decelerate thermal and momentum profiles, with practical implications for nanoparticle-enhanced heat transfer. Ali et al.24 explored Jeffrey nanofluid flow in porous microchannels using fractal-fractional derivatives, showing enhanced heat transfer with hybrid nanofluids. Raza et al.25 analyzed bioconvective micropolar fluid flow using numerical methods, emphasizing the role of chemical reactions and radiation in porous media. Abbas and Nazar26 examined Hall current effects on Jeffrey flow using CPC fractional operators, emphasizing controlled velocity and temperature profiles. Abbas et al.27,28 extended this to Casson fluids, combining AB and CPC derivatives with neural networks for high-accuracy predictions. Ramzan et al.29 integrated Prabhakar derivatives with ANN to model Maxwell fluid flow, achieving precise thermal and concentration profiles. Abbas et al.30 studied fractionalized Brinkman fluid flow with CPC derivatives, showing how diffusion-thermo effects and magnetic fields influence velocity profiles. Rafique et al.31 employed Caputo time-fractional derivatives to examine stratified second-grade fluid flow, highlighting the impact of thermal and mass stratification on industrial heat exchangers. Abbas et al.32 modeled MHD Casson fluid over an oscillating porous plate using Laplace transforms, revealing how fractional parameters enhance fluid behavior in electronic cooling processes. Puneeth et al.33 analyzed nanofluid flow with bioconvection, while Anwar34,35 focused on MHD Casson and Williamson fluids, using fractional calculus to capture viscoelastic memory effects. Ali et al.36 applied fractional calculus to Josephson junction phase dynamics, providing insights into superconducting systems under magnetic fields. Irfan et al.37 investigated magneto-bioconvection in Carreau nanofluids, demonstrating how bio-convection and nanoparticle shapes affect thermal performance in bioengineering applications. Anwar et al.38 demonstrated a 40.39% thermal efficiency boost in hybrid nanofluids using AB and CPC models. Asifa et al.39–41 applied Caputo-Fabrizio and Prabhakar derivatives to blood flow and engine oil, respectively, noting shape-dependent thermal enhancements. Anwar et al.42 developed a fractional model for hybrid nanofluids (MoS₂/Fe₃O₄ in engine oil), showing a 17.4% thermal efficiency improvement, with implications for industrial cooling systems. Muhammad et al.43 used Prabhakar operators to model vacuum pump oil, achieving a 44% thermal improvement. These studies collectively highlight the versatility of fractional derivatives—such as Caputo, CPC, and Prabhakar operators—in capturing memory effects, flow penetration through porous walls, and heat transfer enhancement. The combination of fractional calculus and numerical methods (such as Laplace transforms, artificial neural networks, and finite difference techniques) has facilitated accurate modeling of intricate fluid dynamics, enhancing design optimization for engineering purposes and promoting advancements in energy systems, biomedical devices, and industrial operations.

Jeffery-Hamel flow has been a focal point for studying fluid dynamics in converging and diverging channels since its introduction by Jeffery and Hamel44,45. Numerous studies have explored its applications in industrial systems, such as chemical vapor deposition reactors, gas compressors, and pipelines46,47. Modern investigations have expanded their scope to include MHD flow, heat transfer, and nanofluid behavior, addressing challenges in energy technologies, electronic cooling, and drug delivery systems48–57. For example, Hashim et al.57 examined nanofluid MHD flow using numerical techniques, while Ochieng et al.55 analyzed unsteady flow influenced by a magnetic field. Similarly, Ara et al.53 studied heat transfer in micropolar fluids, and Meher and Patel52 focused on non-Newtonian Eyring-Powell fluids under MHD conditions. These studies highlight the interplay between magnetic fields, fluid rheology, and thermal properties. Recent advancements include innovative methods for analyzing MHD flows, such as the Adaptive Fraction Method (AFM), spectral techniques, and homotopy approaches48–50,56,58. These methods enable accurate solutions to nonlinear equations governing fluid motion, revealing insights into flow stability, velocity profiles, and the effects of injection and suction59–65. Additionally, studies like those by Taklifi66 and Kaminski67 have explored the influence of magnetic fields on bubble dynamics and particle trajectories in complex fluids, further demonstrating the potential for MHD applications in modern engineering.

Despite the extensive contributions of previous works, a comprehensive understanding of how fractional derivatives and magnetic fields jointly influence injected MHD Jeffery–Hamel flow remains underexplored. The novelties of the present study can be summarized as follows: (1) the effect of tangential wall injection is incorporated, with injection velocity varying across the channel width; (2) fractional derivatives in the radial direction replace the conventional integer derivatives in the governing equations; and (3) a new similarity transformation is introduced to reformulate the problem. To address this model, the Navier–Stokes equation is reformulated with a fractional derivative of arbitrary order, and similarity method is applied to reduce it to an ordinary differential equation (ODE). Analytical solutions are obtained using the Adomian Fractional Method (AFM) and validated against numerical results. Parametric studies on the Reynolds number (Re), Hartmann number (Ha), and fractional derivative order are then conducted to evaluate their influence on velocity distribution, and overall characteristics. The study is organized as follows: Section "Flow formulation" formulates the governing equations, integrating fractional derivatives and MHD effects. Section "Solution methodology" details the hybrid analytical–numerical solution methodology, employing AFM and finite difference schemes. Section "Results and discussions" analyzes parametric results on flow behavior, while Section "Conclusion" concludes with key findings.

In the problem, several key points concerning velocity are outlined: 1: The primary flow enters the channel in a radial manner, while the injection flow enters tangentially from the channel walls. The radial velocity of the primary flow exceeds the tangential velocity of the injection. 2: A: At the walls, in accordance with the no-slip condition, the radial velocity is zero, and the tangential velocity is maximum. Consequently, in the regions adjacent to the channel walls, the tangential velocity resulting from the injection flow prevails over the radial velocity. B: At the center of the channel, due to symmetry, the tangential velocity is zero, and the radial velocity is at its maximum. Thus, in the central regions of the channel, the radial velocity from the primary flow dominates the tangential velocity. In summary, the problem exhibits dual conditions in the regions near the walls and in the central areas of the channel. 3: A boundary condition is imposed on the walls of the channel, characterized by a tangential velocity equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{i}$$\end{document} and a radial velocity of zero. When addressing the governing differential equation, the boundary condition at the wall influences the equation’s response, with this influence being more significant near the walls. As one moves further from the walls, the impact of the equation on the response becomes more pronounced. 4: Besides the momentum equation, the continuity equation governs the relationship between tangential and radial velocities throughout the field. Taking these points into account, as an initial step towards deriving an approximate solution through analytical methods for the flow within the channel characterized by variable tangential velocity, only the dominant tangential velocity term is considered, while the other tangential velocity terms are disregarded.

In this research the subsequent assumptions have been considered: The model considers (1) 2D, steady, Newtonian fluid, incompressible flow with negligible gravity, and (2) Radially dominant velocity. Therefore, we focus solely on the predominant component of tangential velocity, disregarding the other components. (3) Neglecting the induced magnetic field based on the premise that the Eckert number and the magnetic Reynolds number are both minimal64,68. (4) Disregarding Hall effects. This is due to the assumption that the frequency of electron-atom collisions is extremely low69.

Fractional derivative modeling

This makes them effective for describing real-world processes such as diffusion, viscoelasticity, and biological dynamics, where classical integer-order models often fall short. By capturing gradual transitions and nonlocal influences, fractional models provide a closer match to many experimentally observed behaviors. In steady MHD flow, the selection of fractional parameters significantly impacts the stability of the solution by affecting the dissipation of perturbations and energy. Solution methodology

Adaptive fraction method (AFM) formulation

The analytical solution is obtained through the Adaptive Fraction Method (AFM), which is a fast, flexible and robust method against the input noises72–74. This Method is designed to efficiently solve nonlinear differential equations by combining the strengths of analytical representation and numerical optimization. The key idea is to approximate the solution not with a simple polynomial or series, but with a rational expression (fractional form) that can capture nonlinear behavior more accurately. AFM employs a systematic four-stage approach for nonlinear differential equations. The equation is decomposed into linear and nonlinear components, with exact solutions constructed for the linear operator. The field variables are then represented through a rational expression. The expansion coefficients are optimized through constrained minimization of the residual norm, ensuring satisfaction of the governing equation and boundary conditions. When using six or more basis terms in the expansion, the method demonstrates desired convergence characteristics. Results and discussions

The governing dynamics are characterized by three fundamental dimensionless parameters: Re, Ha and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} which govern the behavior of four key dimensionless variables: F,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{v}_{r}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{v}_{\theta }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{V}$$\end{document}. The Reynolds number (Re) represents the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in a fluid, serving as a key indicator of flow regime. This makes Re essential for predicting flow stability, heat and mass transfer characteristics, and designing systems ranging from microfluidic devices to large-scale pipelines. The Hartmann number (Ha), by contrast, quantifies the ratio of electromagnetic to viscous forces in electrically conducting fluids under the influence of a magnetic field. High Ha values indicate strong magnetic damping of velocity fluctuations and boundary layer suppression. Together, these dimensionless numbers provide complementary insights into fluid flow control, stability, and transport phenomena in engineering and scientific systems. Figure 7 demonstrates the parametric sensitivity of the flow field, revealing that enhanced inertial effects (Re) produce a slight increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| F \right|$$\end{document}, which raises \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| {\,\hat{v}_{\theta } } \right|$$\end{document}. However, symmetry makes this increase negligible at the channel’s center, where the velocity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{v}_{\theta }$$\end{document} is zero.
