Human In Vivo Validation of Frequency‐Dependent QTI
Authors: Svenja Niesen, Marten Veldmann, Ali Ajouz, Tony Stöcker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe aim of this work is to investigate if q‐space trajectory imaging (QTI) waveforms can be designed to probe QTI metrics at a single centroid frequency under realistic experimental conditions for in vivo human brain imaging.MethodsRealistic diffusion encoding waveforms based on double‐rotation gradient waveform and magic‐angle spinning of the q‐vector with varying encoding bandwidth, alignment of encoding spectra across encodings (tuning) and across axes (spectral isotropy) were designed to investigate the accuracy of the centroid frequency approximation. QTI metrics were computed for idealized as well as realistic oscillating and pulsed gradient waveforms using analytical diffusion spectra with 1D and 2D short‐range disorder along and perpendicular to axons. For in vivo validation, the waveforms were integrated into a multiband spiral spin‐echo sequence.ResultsThe simulations demonstrate frequency‐dependent QTI metrics. The combination of tuning and spectral isotropy led to metrics close to the simulated ground‐truth at the centroid frequency of the encoding spectrum. Realistic waveforms with broader bandwidth compared to idealized waveforms introduce little additional error in QTI metrics due to truncation of the cumulant expansion. In agreement with literature, omitting tuning and spectral isotropy reduces isotropic variance and increases microscopic fractional anisotropy when LTE contains lower frequencies, and vice versa for PTE. This observation is confirmed qualitatively by the in vivo measurements.ConclusionThe accuracy of the centroid frequency approximation depends on the combination of encoding spectra and investigated tissue. Using similar encoding spectra for tuning and spectral isotropy leads to metrics, characteristic to the centroid frequency.

Introduction

Diffusion tensor imaging [1, 2] enables investigation of the human brain structure beyond the resolution typically gained with MRI. The application of diffusion sensitizing gradients allows the definition of a voxel‐dependent diffusion tensor, that describes the averaged movement of molecules in that voxel and assumes this diffusion to be time‐independent (Gaussian diffusion). This allows the computation of macroscopic diffusion metrics such as the mean diffusivity (MD) and the fractional anisotropy (FA).

As Gaussian diffusion does not describe diffusion in complex tissue in the human brain sufficiently, other diffusion MRI (dMRI) methods emerged.

Oscillating gradients [3, 4, 5] were used to target diffusion times in the short‐time regime, yielding surface‐to‐volume ratios [6, 7], or the long‐time regime, in which time‐dependence results from the structural disordering of the tissue [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. In particular, it was shown [8, 9, 10, 11, 12] that the one‐dimensional (1D) short‐range disorder along axons due to axon caliber variations as well as the two‐dimensional (2D) short‐range disorder of the extra‐axonal space perpendicular to the principal fiber orientation have a strong time‐influence on diffusion metrics.

Tensor valued encoding (TVE) [13] assumes a diffusion tensor distribution (DTD) composed of multiple Gaussian diffusion compartments within a voxel. Exploiting various b‐tensor shapes allows the computation of the DTD [14] and is achieved by shaping the diffusion gradients such that the desired q‐space trajectory is traversed.

However, due to the multiple unknowns arising from the components of the diffusion tensors in different compartments, many volumes with different diffusion encodings have to be acquired. Q‐space trajectory imaging (QTI) [15] offers a faster solution by the estimation of the covariance tensor of the DTD with a minimum of 28 diffusion volumes to compute microscopic metrics such as isotropic variance (Cmd) and microscopic fractional anisotropy (μFA). Each diffusion volume has to resemble a different b‐tensor with a specific b‐value and shape, which is defined by the b‐tensor's rank [15] (Rank 1: linear tensor encoding (LTE), Rank 2: planar tensor encoding (PTE), Rank 3: spherical tensor encoding (STE)).

Time‐dependent diffusion processes can be modeled with the Gaussian‐phase approximation [16], which leads to a frequency‐dependent DTD [17, 18, 19]. The complete quantification of the DTD requires different b‐tensor shapes designed to encode multiple frequencies. However, restricting the investigation to double‐rotation gradient waveforms at the centroid frequency [17, 18, 20]—the first moment of the encoding distribution—makes the approach applicable for in vivo measurements [21, 22].

Introducing time‐dependence in the TVE diffusional variance decomposition approach [23, 24, 25], it was demonstrated that matching the power spectra across different encodings (tuning) yields time‐dependent metrics that differ from those obtained without tuning. In that context, matching power spectra across axes within one encoding (spectral isotropy) [24] was introduced as well. Shifting the encoding spectra across frequencies results in time‐dependent MD estimates and altered μFA sensitivity, where the most affected frequencies depend on the compartment radius [25]. Monte‐Carlo simulations demonstrated that omitting tuning leads to overestimated μFA values using QTI, however to a less extent in white matter (WM) [26]. Additionally, time‐dependent μFA was shown with double (oscillating) diffusion encoding in both monkey [27] and mouse brains [28], including an error arising from overstepping the convergence radius [28]. Recently, matching the power spectra's trace across encodings was found to be essential to not over‐ or underestimate microscopic anisotropy due to different MD weighting across encodings in ex vivo rat heart [29]/brain [30] as well as in the prostate [31].

In this work, we follow up on the idea of Lasič et al. [30] by investigating if QTI waveforms for human brain MRI can be designed with encoding spectra narrowly distributed around the centroid frequency, such that the approximation of solely probing the centroid frequency can be used for deriving QTI metrics related to this frequency.

Different DTDs are simulated by using the analytical diffusion spectra [8, 9, 10, 11, 12] of the 1D and 2D short‐range disorder along and perpendicular to the axons (ω0.5 and ω1 power laws) as well as radial diffusion inside the axons (ω2 power law). Idealized oscillating and pulsed gradient echo waveforms (OGE and PGE) with varying centroid frequencies and spectral bandwidths are created to investigate under which conditions the ground‐truth QTI metrics can be measured at the centroid frequency. The bias due to this centroid frequency approximation is compared to the error arising from the cumulant expansion.

To investigate the practicality of the centroid frequency approximation, three QTI waveforms for in vivo human brain measurements are designed. Their encoding spectra differ, reflecting the use of tuning and spectral isotropy.

The three QTI waveforms are applied in vivo by incorporating them into a multiband spin‐echo sequence with a spiral readout. It is investigated if the simulation results correlate with the in vivo results.

Theory

The explanation of the centroid frequency approximation in QTI starts with a brief summary of the time‐independent QTI‐approach [15], which assumes a DTD that only includes Gaussian diffusion within each compartment. Here, the term “compartment” includes all particles whose diffusion can be described by the same diffusion tensor [14]. The time‐independent QTI‐approach [15] provides the signal attenuation for multiple Gaussian diffusion compartments: (1) S(b)=S0·⟨exp(−<b,d>)⟩=S0·∑iwi·exp−<b,di> where b is the b‐tensor, d i are the time‐independent diffusion tensors of the DTD, S0 is the signal without diffusion weighting and wi is the weight of the i‐th compartment. Following the formalism of Westin et al. [15], <.,.> denotes the inner product between tensors and ⟨…⟩ the mean over compartments. The cumulant expansion of Equation (1) yields a signal attenuation depending on the averaged diffusion tensor ⟨d⟩ and a fourth order covariance tensor C, which leads to the time‐independent QTI‐equation [15]: (2) Sc(b)=S0·exp−<b,⟨d⟩>+12<B,C>. Determination of C enables the computation of QTI metrics (MD, FA, Cmd, μFA) [15]. Measuring metrics with regards to time‐dependent diffusion within the compartments considers the Gaussian‐phase approximation for multiple compartments [16, 18]. Its Fourier domain representation is given by [18]: (3) S(ω)=S0·∑iwi·exp−∫−∞∞<b(ω),di(ω)>dω, where d i(ω) is the tensor of the diffusion spectrum of the i‐th compartment and b(ω) is the encoding spectrum of the diffusion gradients g→ during the diffusion encoding time τ [17]: (4) b(ω)=12πq→(ω)⊗q→(ω) with the outer product ⊗ and the dephasing spectrum q→(ω) defined as: (5) q→(ω)=∫0τγ∫0tg→t′dt′eiωtdt. Equation (3) can be simplified to Equation (1) if d(ω) is constant across ω for nonzero b(ω) [18]. In this work, we investigate whether the simplification to Equation (1) remains valid when b(ω) has nonzero values narrowly distributed around the centroid frequency ωc, such that d(ω) = d(ωc). The centroid frequency ωc is defined as [17, 20]: (6) ωc=1b∫−∞∞|ω|b(ω)dω. Here, b(ω) is the frequency‐dependent trace of b(ω) and b is the integral of b(ω) across frequencies. Thus, Equation (3) can be simplified to: (7) Sωc=S0·∑iwi·exp−<b,diωc>. The cumulant expansion of S(ωc) is denoted as Sc(ωc) as in Equation (2), but the averaged diffusion tensor ⟨d⟩ and the covariance tensor C now depend on ωc: (8) Scωc=S0·exp−<b,dωc>+12<B,Cωc>. Estimation of C(ωc) leads to QTI metrics specific for the centroid frequency.

Methods

Frequency Modulated QTI Waveforms

To investigate the validity of the centroid frequency approximation, idealized oscillating and pulsed gradient waveforms (OGE and PGE) with varying centroid frequency and spectral bandwidth were designed. The OGE waveforms (Figure 1A) were created with trapezoidal gradients of alternating polarity. The first and last trapezoidal gradients had duration τ, while gradients in the center of the train had duration 2τ. The duration τ was varied to create waveforms with frequencies ranging from 1 to 21 Hz. Increasing the number of gradients in the middle of the train decreases the spectral bandwidth, defined here as the frequency interval containing 90% of the total area under the encoding spectrum. The PGE waveforms (Figure 1B) were created with the gradient duration δ fixed at 12.2 ms and varying the gradient spacing Δ. Increasing the spacing increases both ωc and spectral bandwidth.

The optimized PTE waveform shows strong similarity to the previously published circular polarized OGSE sequence [36] (Figure S1A,B). Ideally, a single peak would appear at ωc. However, a second peak is observed in the encoding spectra due to a finite duration of the refocusing pulse and crusher gradients [28]. The magnitude of this secondary peak is proportional to the ratio of refocusing duration and TE. As the refocusing duration was limited by SAR and peripheral nerve stimulation due to the crushers and TE was limited by T2 decay, the second peak could not be fully eliminated. It is tested, if the splitting into two peaks leads to the same errors in the centroid frequency approximation as for an OGSE sequence with same waveform duration, ωc and bandwidth, but establishing from one peak (Figure S1A,C). The LTE waveform was optimized to match the encoding spectrum of the PTE considering the trace of the PTE waveform and its centroid frequency (Table 1B). Nonlinear constraints were imposed to prevent exceeding the scanner's maximum gradient amplitude and slew rate per axis, while ensuring the desired b‐tensor shape [14], the maximum b‐value, the spin‐echo condition and no diffusion gradients during the refocusing duration. In Vivo Experiments

Sequence Parameters

Four healthy subjects (gender: four males, one female; age: 24–33) were scanned with given consent. Subject one was measured twice with a break of 8 days in between.

Measurements were conducted on a MAGNETOM 7T Plus scanner (Siemens Healthineers AG, Forchheim, Germany) with a 32/8 channel receive/transmit coil (Nova Medical), a maximum gradient strength of 70 mT/m and a maximum slew rate of 200 T/m/s.

The three QTI waveforms were inserted into a single‐shot spiral spin‐echo sequence [42, 43]. Sequence design was done with Pulseq [44] and the sequence parameters were: matrix size = 140 × 140, slices = 70, resolution = 1.5 mm isotropic, volume TR = 5.46 s, TE = 119.2 ms, total acquisition time = 15 min, in‐plane acceleration = 3, multiband‐factor = 2, acquired in transversal orientation. The Q2 scheme [45] was used as the diffusion protocol with a total amount of 151 diffusion volumes: encodings = [PTE, LTE], b‐values = [[0, 1400], [0, 100, 800, 1400]] s/mm2, directions per b‐values = [[9, 36], [22, 6, 30, 48]]. The excitation pulse duration was set to 6 ms and the refocusing pulse duration to 8 ms, leading to a total refocusing duration of 11.5 ms due to crushers and a 1 ms pause to decrease peripheral nerve stimulation. Fat unfocusing was achieved by adjusting the excitation and refocusing pulse bandwidths [46]. Additionally, a fat saturation pulse before the excitation pulse with a flip angle of 70° was applied.

A T1‐weighted (T1w) image was acquired with an MPRAGE sequence (FOV = 256 × 256 × 179.2 mm3; isotropic resolution = 0.8 mm, total acquisition time = 4 min) to enable a region of interest analysis in structural space.

Reconstruction & Preprocessing Pipeline

The reconstruction workflow [42, 43] used a higher order encoding model [47], which included a B0 map, coil sensitivity maps (both derived from a GRE‐prescan), dynamic field monitoring [48] data of up to 3rd spatial order and compressed sensing regularization [49, 50].

Preprocessing included denoising [51], Gibbs ringing correction [52], motion correction with FSL “eddy” [53, 54, 55], gradient unwarping [56, 57] and a correction for thermal drift [58, 59, 60]. The thermal drift correction [60] was modified to apply region‐wise signal drift correction [61] with 10 k‐means clustered regions from a mean non‐diffusion weighted image. QTI metric computation was done with the QTI ± algorithm [41, 62, 63] with an upper diffusion limit of 3.1μm2/ms, used for the algorithm [41, 62, 63]. The b‐tensor calculation included imaging gradients and considered the systems response by applying the gradient transfer function (GTF) [64, 65] to the gradient waveforms. We assessed potential vibration‐induced bias in diffusion metrics by analyzing LTE signals at the maximum b‐value across b‐vector components for each QTI sequence as done in literature [66].

Registration [67, 68, 69] from diffusion space to structural space was done with the mean of the non‐diffusion weighted images and afterwards applied onto the QTI metric maps. The FSL MNI152‐template [67] was registered to structural space and region of interest analyses were performed for the splenium and the anterior corona radiata, by applying the registration from MNI to structural space on the labeled regions from FSL atlases [70, 71]. Boxplots containing the metric values within the two regions were computed for each subject and sequence. A linear mixed‐effects model with random intercepts [72] for subjects was fitted to assess the effect of the different sequences on the measured QTI metrics. Absolute and relative differences between the sequences were estimated from the fitted model and reported together with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Centroid Frequency Approximation on 1D Diffusion Spectra

The relative error ε (Equation 14) vanishes for 2D short‐range disorder (power law ω1) for each encoding spectra, whereas diffusion itself vanishes for movement perpendicular to and inside an axon (power law ω2) (Figure 1C).

1D short‐range disorder (power law ω0.5) demonstrates lower deff than d(ωc) with increasing bandwidth and decreasing ωc of the idealized PGE/OGE waveforms (Figure 1D). The PGE spectrum with the largest bandwidth has the largest error (ε ≈ −0.8%). The error behavior of the idealized waveforms also holds for the in vivo waveforms (Figure 1D). The in vivo PGSE waveform error falls within the PGE range (largest error for LTE of qMAS and detuned gDOR), while the OGSE waveform error falls within the OGE range (smallest error for PTE of tuned and detuned gDOR). The PTE of qMAS, combining PGSE and OGSE, lies between both ranges and shows the second‐largest error.

Centroid Frequency Approximation on 3D Diffusion Spectra for QTI

The signal comparison between S(ω) and S(ωc) shows accurate centroid frequency approximation for an isotropic tensor, if PTE and LTE contain the same ωc regardless of the bandwidth (Figure 3B). In contrast, the anisotropic tensor or multiple tensors show increasing RMAE with larger spectral bandwidth but the same ωc (Figure 3C–E).

Higher RMAE is observed if PTE and LTE differ in ωc (Figure 3B–E), especially for the isotropic tensor with diffusivity following the power law ω1. If diffusion waveforms contain larger frequencies than the combined frequency of all volumes, the signal level is smaller than the signal at the combined frequency (LTE/PTE volumes in the orange/purple marked cases in Figure 4A) and vice versa (PTE/LTE volumes in the orange/purple marked case in Figure 4A).

Discussion

We investigated the design of QTI waveforms in terms of their encoding spectra for human in vivo applications. The spectra determine if QTI metrics specific to a centroid frequency can be measured (centroid frequency approximation). If the approximation is met, the Gaussian‐phase approximated signal S(ω) meets the signal defined by the tissue's DTD at the centroid frequency S(ωc). The approximation was first validated using idealized QTI waveforms and a comparison of analytical signal equations, and then applied in vivo by accordingly designing realistic and suitable QTI schemes. First, the theoretical investigations based on the idealized waveforms are discussed with respect to the frequency‐dependent properties of the encoded b‐tensors.

Same Centroid Frequency and Same Bandwidth Across b‐Tensor Shapes

Different encoding spectra with the same ωc and bandwidth were designed. In that case, both tuning and spectral isotropy have to be applied as spectral anisotropy of the PTE waveform leads to different ωc between the total PTE and LTE waveform. The approximation is expected to improve with more narrowly distributed encoding spectra around ωc. However, this depends on the tissue type's DTD.

A DTD consisting of one isotropic tensor with diffusivity following the power law ω1 does not lead to an approximation error for both a small and a large bandwidth due to the linear behavior of the power law (Figure 3B). In contrast, one anisotropic tensor with diffusivities following the power laws ω1 and ω0.5 leads to a larger deviation from the centroid frequency metrics due to the changing curvature of the power law ω0.5, especially at frequencies close to zero (Figure 3C and Figure S6: Cmd: 0.06%, μFA: 6%).

Adding parallel fibers to the anisotropic tensor results in growing differences of the diffusivities perpendicular to the principal fiber orientation with frequency. This is due to the intra‐axonal perpendicular diffusivity not being detectable for the investigated diffusion times [11], whereas the perpendicular diffusivity of the extra‐axonal compartment increases linearly with frequency [8, 11]. Therefore, the higher order terms in the cumulant expansion increase, and the cumulant signal Sc(ωc) deviates from the signal at centroid frequency S(ωc) (Figure 3D and Figure S5). We refer to this as the truncation error of the cumulant expansion. Crossing instead of parallel fibers increase the truncation error as the diffusion differs more strongly across directions, influencing more diffusion volumes with different b‐tensor eigenbasis (Figure 3E). As a result, the truncation error becomes sufficiently large that a larger bandwidth no longer yields greater deviations from the centroid frequency metrics than a smaller bandwidth. Still, the deviations are small (Figure 5A, Cmd: 4%–8%, μFA: 3%–6%).

Different Centroid Frequency and Same Bandwidth Across b‐Tensor Shapes

Encoding spectra across b‐tensor shapes can differ in terms of their centroid frequencies, leading to a combined frequency as the mean of their centroid frequencies.

Lasič et al. [29, 30] showed that a varying spectral trace across b‐tensor shapes samples different MD across encodings, leading to over‐ or underestimated TVE metrics in ex vivo rat heart [29]/brain [30] as well as in the prostate [31]. This work builds up on that idea by comparing the over‐and underestimated metrics to the metrics at the combined frequency in case of human brain tissue. It is shown (Figure 4) that if PTE/LTE waveforms sample smaller/larger frequencies than the combined frequency, the signal level of the signal S(ω) of the PTE volumes is much larger than the signal level of the signal S(ωc) at the combined frequency due to a decreased MD at smaller frequencies and the other way around for the LTE volumes. This approximation error of sampling at the combined frequency enlarges with increased MD frequency‐dependence. Therefore, the error is most pronounced for the isotropic tensor with the strongly increasing power law ω1 and less pronounced for the DTD including crossing fibers with the slower increasing power law ω0.5 (Figure 3B,E).

The difference of the signal level of the signal S(ω) across b‐tensor shapes gets wrongly interpreted, leading to large deviations from the metrics at combined frequency. For the macroscopic metrics, the deviation stays below 8% in the crossing fiber region (Figures 4B,C and 5). As LTE volumes outnumber PTE volumes twofold, the direction of the sequence MD deviation is predominantly determined by the LTE volumes. For the microscopic metrics, small differences in the signal levels for the different b‐tensor shapes result in erroneous Cmd and μFA values as pointed out in Ref. [29, 30, 31]. Omitting positivity constraints on d and C could result in microscopic metrics outside of their defined boundaries (Figure 4B,C). Including constraints (Figure 5), lower frequencies in the PTE volumes than the combined frequency over‐/underestimate Cmd/μFA (Cmd: 90%, μFA: −20%) and vice versa for the lower frequencies in the LTE volumes (Cmd: −90%, μFA: 37%).

In the future, it should be tested how the numbers of PTE and LTE volumes impact deviations from centroid frequency metrics. Here, we started from an optimized protocol [45] and showed that matching encoding spectra (tuning and spectral isotropy) result in metrics closer to the combined (centroid) frequency.

Different Bandwidth Across b‐Tensor Shapes

Similar to spectra with different ωc, b‐tensors with different bandwidths encode different diffusivity strengths, causing signal differences and deviations from the combined frequency (Figure 5). However, in comparison to the deviation of different ωc across b‐tensor shapes, these deviations are negligible.

In Vivo Validation

QTI metrics were successfully measured with a multiband spiral sequence at 7T for three sequences differing in encoding spectra (Figures 6 and 7). For the given sequence parameters (TE = 120 ms, volume TR > 5 s) and T2 values of WM (3T: 62 ms, 7T: 45 ms) [73] the SNR at 7T is approximately 1.95 times higher compared to 3T assuming a factor of B01.65 (Ref. [74]). The measurement would greatly benefit from a high‐performance gradient system [75, 76, 77, 78] as this would drastically reduce the length of the QTI waveforms and result in lower echo times. However, such a system was not available. Additionally, parts of the GM, such as the pallidum and caudate, contain lower SNR than WM due to the combination of iron content and diffusion behavior [79], leading to strongly biased QTI metrics in these regions.

In vivo, for the ACR (Figure 8 and Table 2) and the splenium (Figure S7 and Table S3), tuned gDOR leads to higher MD and Cmd and lower μFA values, but similar FA values compared to the other two sequences. The significant strength of the differences between sequences (Table 2) meets the results of the simulated crossing fiber region.

Analytical spectra, created by results from literature [8, 9, 10, 11, 12], were sought to resemble the time‐dependent diffusion in the ACR. As the gradient waveforms used for human in vivo imaging contain small frequencies (ωc < 18 Hz), the analytical spectra defined in the low frequency regime could be used as the ground‐truth effective diffusivity. The MD differs between intra‐ and extra‐axonal spaces because intra‐axonal radial diffusivity remains near zero even at higher frequencies, whereas extra‐axonal diffusivity increases linearly with frequency perpendicular to the fibers, increasing Cmd (Figures 4C and 5B). MD is increased as well as parallel and perpendicular diffusion in each space increase with frequency [27]. Crossing fibers decrease the FA due to orientational averaging of the diffusion tensor. Though the anisotropy of each of the tensors of the DTD increases with increased frequency, the denominator in the formula for FA and μFA contains the increased diffusivity, which results in a decrease of FA and μFA with increased frequency [27, 28]. However, the simulated analytical spectra only considered fitting values for the diffusion spectra of the ACR. In vivo, sequence‐related differences were greater in the ACR than in the splenium and may result from the larger extra‐axonal volume fraction in the crossing fiber region. Due to CSF contamination bias in the region of the splenium [12] and the need to account for an orientation dispersion inside a fiber bundle in the simulated DTD to yield an FA below one, only the ACR was simulated.

According to simulation (Table 2A), tuned gDOR has the most alike PTE and LTE spectra and therefore leads to metrics closer to metrics at the combined frequency (Cmd: −18%, μFA: 14%) compared to detuned gDOR having larger frequencies for PTE than LTE and therefore strongly under‐/overestimating Cmd/μFA (Cmd: −94%, μFA: 38%). The sequence qMAS falls between the gDOR sequences in terms of deviation (Cmd: −52%, μFA: 19%), as the LTE has lower frequencies than the spectrally anisotropic PTE, but not to that amount of detuned gDOR. The encoding spectrum of tuned gDOR is shifted towards larger frequencies, increasing sensitivity to the higher terms. As tuned gDOR contains the largest combined frequency, MD is largest, followed by detuned gDOR and qMAS.

As demonstrated, encoding spectra with same ωc and bandwidth lead to minimal deviation from the centroid frequency metrics. Here, this was enabled with the optimized tuned gDOR sequence, ensuring not overstepping scanner limits and a reasonable echo time. Despite the two peaks in the encoding spectrum instead of one, the approximation error remains unchanged compared to one peak in the encoding spectrum for same bandwidth. The larger bandwidth (22.7 Hz) does not lead to additional bias due to the truncation error. However, due to a slight mismatch of LTE and PTE spectra in the optimization, the tuned gDOR leads still to slight deviations from the centroid frequency metrics (Table 2A: Cmd: −18%, μFA: 14%). An improvement would be to use the OGSE sequence from Figure S1C to match precisely PTE and LTE spectra. However, as PTE waveforms are set to the maximum gradient amplitude for minimal TE, tuning the LTE would result in overstepping the maximum gradient amplitude and therefore, the LTE waveform required separate optimization.

Care must be taken for in vivo measurements as scanner physical phenomena could lead to misinterpretation of the results in terms of time‐dependence. However, as differences of the metrics between the three sequences are prominent across the whole brain, they most likely do not originate from flow and partial‐volume effects of CSF [80]. B0/B1 inhomogeneities, background‐gradients or gradient‐nonlinearity effects would lead to local instead of global differences in the calculated metrics. Additionally, concomitant field effects are relatively low at 7T and eddy current effects were addressed by using the GTF in the b‐tensor computation. We also rule out water exchange as a major influence on the metrics, given the slow exchange rates in healthy white matter [81]. We observed both a signal and a frequency drift during diffusion measurements at high gradient duty cycle, which could lead to incorrect diffusion metrics and underestimated restriction [61]. While signal drift was corrected retrospectively [59] (Figure S8), the frequency drift can lead to reduced fat suppression for later acquired volumes [58]. However, we observed no fat artifacts in later acquired volumes. Finally, no vibration‐related signal attenuation resulting from the diffusion encoding was observed in any sequence (Figure S9), indicating minimal metric bias.

In summary, the simulations demonstrated that short‐range disorder induces time dependence in QTI metrics in human brain tissue when using the low‐frequency diffusion encoding spectra typical of in vivo imaging. Idealized waveforms with matched centroid frequency and bandwidth satisfied the centroid frequency approximation. In contrast to detuned gDOR and qMAS, tuned gDOR was optimized to match the encoding spectra of the idealized waveforms while considering hardware constraints. Despite a broadened spectral peak in the encoding spectra caused by the hardware constraints, this optimization enabled in vivo measurement of QTI metrics that were close to those obtained at the centroid frequency.

Conclusion

QTI waveforms were designed which ensure matching encoding spectra across b‐tensor shapes, including both tuning and spectral isotropy, and encoding spectra distributed around a centroid frequency. This enables probing QTI metrics at this specific frequency in complex tissue as in the human brain. Conversely, metrics of different encoding spectra across b‐tensor shapes deviate from centroid‐frequency metrics by a factor depending on the tissue's diffusivity, strongly over‐ or underestimating microscopic metrics. Because of scanner and timing constraints, spectra of the designed realistic waveforms are more broadly distributed around the centroid frequency. In simulated WM, however, truncation of the cumulant expansion causes spectra with different bandwidths to yield similar small deviations from the centroid frequency metrics.
