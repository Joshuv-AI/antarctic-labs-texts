Abstract
. Carleson and sparse collections of sets play a central role in dyadic harmonic analysis. We employ methods from optimization theory to study such collections. First, we present a strongly polynomial algorithm to compute the Carleson constant of a collection of sets, improving on the recent approximation algorithm of Rey [ Rey24 ] . Our algorithm is based on submodular function minimization. Second, we provide an algorithm showing that any Carleson collection is sparse, achieving optimal dependence of the respective constants and thus providing a constructive proof of a result of Hänninen [ Hän18 ] . Our key insight is a reformulation of the duality between the Carleson condition and sparseness in terms of the duality between the maximum flow and the minimum cut in a weighted directed graph.

1.
Introduction

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a measure space and
\mathcal{F}
a collection of sets in
\Sigma
, where we use the word
collection
for an unordered sequence of sets that allows for repeated elements. We call
\mathcal{F}

\eta
-sparse
for
\eta\in(0,1]
if for every
Q\in\mathcal{F}
there exists a measurable subset
E_{Q}\subseteq Q
such that
\mu(E_{Q})\geq\eta\mu(Q)
and the sets
\{E_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
are pairwise disjoint. The number
\eta
provides a scale-invariant way to quantify the overlap of the sets in
\mathcal{F}
. In particular, a
1
-sparse collection is pairwise disjoint (up to
\mu
-null sets).

Closely related to
\eta
-sparseness is the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition. The collection
\mathcal{F}
is said to satisfy the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition
for
\Lambda\geq 1
if for every subcollection
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we have

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(Q)\leq\Lambda\mu\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}}Q\bigr).

Every
\eta
-sparse collection
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
\eta^{-1}
-Carleson condition, as for any subcollection
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we have

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(Q\right)\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(E_{Q}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\mu\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}}Q\bigr),

where the last inequality holds because the sets
\{E_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
are pairwise disjoint.

In the converse direction, it is known in many cases that if
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition, then
\mathcal{F}
is
\frac{1}{\Lambda}
-sparse. For
\mathcal{F}
consisting of dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mu
a non-atomic, locally finite Borel measure, this was proven by Verbitsky in
[
Ver96
, Corollary 2]
, using a result of Dor
[
Dor75
]
. Verbitsky’s proof was extended to general collections of Borel measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by Hänninen
[
Hän18
]
, see also
[
Bar19
, Theorem 3.3]
.

The equivalence between sparseness and the Carleson condition is fundamental in applications in harmonic analysis: the Carleson condition is often straightforward to verify, while sparseness is incredibly useful for obtaining sharp estimates. We refer to the survey
[
Per19
]
and the references therein for an overview of the use of sparse collections in harmonic analysis.

The remarkably elegant Dor–Hänninen–Verbitsky proof is based on duality and the Hahn–Banach separation theorem and is thus non-constructive, even if the collection
\mathcal{F}
is finite. In stark contrast, if
\mathcal{F}
is a finite collection of dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one can start by choosing any subset
E_{Q}\subseteq Q
with measure
\frac{1}{\Lambda}\mu(Q)
for all minimal cubes in
\mathcal{F}
. Afterwards, one can go up “layer by layer”, choosing subsets
E_{Q}
of measure
\frac{1}{\Lambda}\mu(Q)
in
Q\setminus\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{F}:R\subsetneq Q}E_{R}
. This will always be possible since

\mu\Bigl(\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{F}:R\subsetneq Q}E_{R}\Bigr)=\tfrac{1}{\Lambda}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{F}:R\subseteq Q}\mu(R)-\tfrac{1}{\Lambda}\mu(Q)\leq(1-\tfrac{1}{\Lambda})\mu(Q),

where in the first step the nestedness property of dyadic cubes is used and in the last step the

\Lambda
-Carleson condition. Hence, for dyadic cubes one can construct the
E_{Q}
’s using a greedy algorithm. This argument was extended to infinite collections of dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Lebesgue measure by Lerner and Nazarov
[
LN19
, Lemma 6.3]
and subsequently with any non-atomic, locally finite Borel measure
\mu
by Cascante and Ortega
[
CO17
, Theorem 4.3]
.

The constructive proof for dyadic cubes does not generalize to more general collections
\mathcal{F}
, not even to axis-parallel dyadic rectangles in two dimensions. This is due to the fact that the Carleson condition is non-local, as shown by Carleson in
[
Car74
]
.
The first constructive algorithm beyond dyadic cubes was recently obtained by Rey
[
Rey24
]
, who proposed a greedy
approximation
algorithm to construct the sets
\{E_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
for a finite
\Lambda
-Carleson collection in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This algorithm constructs sets
\{E_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
such that

\mu(E_{Q})\geq\frac{c}{\Lambda\log(\mathrm{e}+\Lambda)}\mu(Q)

for some absolute constant
0<c<1
. Under a geometric condition on
\mathcal{F}
, the logarithmic loss can be removed, recovering the optimal, non-constructive result from
[
Hän18
]
up to an absolute constant. The algorithm of Rey can also be used to approximate the optimal Carleson condition constant of a finite collection
\mathcal{F}
in polynomial time.

Given a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
and a finite collection
\mathcal{F}
of sets in
\Sigma
, the goal of this article is twofold:

(1)

We provide a strongly polynomial algorithm to compute the minimum
\Lambda\geq 1
such that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition. This will be done in Section
2
, using the fact that the Carleson condition is submodular.

(2)

Given that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition, we construct sets
\{E_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
such that
\mu(E_{Q})\geq\frac{1}{\Lambda}\mu(Q)
, i.e. show that
\mathcal{F}
is
\frac{1}{\Lambda}
-sparse. To do so, we will reformulate the duality between the Carleson condition and sparseness in terms of the duality between the maximum flow and the minimum cut in a weighted directed graph, see Section
3
.

Compared to
[
Rey24
]
, we remove the geometric assumption on
\mathcal{F}
and achieve
c=1
, i.e., we calculate instead of approximate the Carleson constant and our sets
\{E_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
are optimal. This provides a constructive proof of the result from
[
Hän18
]
for finite collections
\mathcal{F}
. We will make some remarks on countable collections
\mathcal{F}
in Remark
3.8
. Moreover, we extend the theory from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a locally finite Borel measure to a general measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
, which we fix throughout the paper.

Remark 1.1
.

One can replace
\{\mu(Q)\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
by an arbitrary sequence of nonnegative real numbers
\{\lambda_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
in the definition of the Carleson condition and sparseness, as done in, e.g.,
[
CO17
,
Hän18
,
Ver96
]
. Our proofs carry over verbatim to this generality.

2.
The optimal Carleson constant

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite, nonempty collection of sets in the
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma
with
0<\mu(Q)<\infty
for all
Q\in\mathcal{F}
to avoid trivialities. Suppose we want to calculate the minimum
\Lambda\geq 1
such that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition, i.e., calculate

\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}}:=\sup\Bigl\{\frac{\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(Q)}{\mu\bigl(\textstyle\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}Q\bigr)}:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\text{ nonempty}\Bigr\}.

A naive way is to use brute force and evaluate the right-hand side for all
2^{\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert}
subcollections of
\mathcal{F}
, which is exponential in
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert
and hence impractical for large collections. We would prefer to have an algorithm that is polynomial in
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert
and furthermore does not depend on the size of e.g.
\mu(Q)
for
Q\in\mathcal{F}
.

This leads us to the question how to represent the input of such an algorithm. Indeed, to calculate
\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}}
we need to have access to the measure of
\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}Q
for
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. If we explicitly list all these values, only reading the input would already take exponential time. To avoid this, we assume to have an
evaluation oracle
available:

(1)

: Given a subcollection
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, return
\mu\bigl(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}Q\bigr)
.

We aim for an algorithm with a polynomial number of calls to
1
and a polynomial amount of other computational steps. The time needed for one call to the oracle
1
is denoted by
\mathrm{EO}_{1}
.

Our algorithm will be based on
submodular function minimization
. Let us quickly recall the definition of submodularity. We denote the powerset of
\mathcal{F}
by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{F})

Definition 2.1
.

A set function
f\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{R}
is called
\emph{submodular}
if for every
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we have

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{A})+f(\mathcal{B})\geq f(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B})+f(\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}).

There is an extensive literature on the minimization of submodular functions, i.e., finding
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
such that
f(\mathcal{A})=\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}f(\mathcal{B})
. In particular, there exist strongly polynomial algorithms to find such minimizers, see the surveys
[
Iwa08
,
McC05
]
, the more recent
[
JLSZ24
,
Jia23
]
and the references therein.

The submodular function we will use is

f_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{A}):=\Lambda\cdot\mu\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}}Q\bigr)-\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(Q),\qquad\Lambda\geq 1.

Lemma 2.2
.

The function
f_{\Lambda}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{R}
is submodular for
\Lambda\geq 1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mu\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}}Q\bigr)+\mu\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{B}}}Q\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mu\Bigl(\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}}Q\bigr)\cup\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{B}}}Q\bigr)\Bigr)+\mu\Bigl(\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}}Q\bigr)\cap\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{B}}}Q\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mu\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}}}Q\bigr)+\mu\Bigl({{\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}}}Q}\Bigr).

Moreover, we trivially have

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(Q)+\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{B}}\mu(Q)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}}\mu(Q)+\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}}\mu(Q),

which proves the lemma.
∎

Algorithm 1

Compute
\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}}
.

The algorithm to compute
\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}}
we propose is given in Algorithm
1
. The idea is to iteratively find the collection
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
violating the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition the most and then update
\Lambda
accordingly. We force the violating collections to be shrinking, ensuring that Algorithm
1
does at most
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert
iterations of the while loop.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite, nonempty collection in
\Sigma
with
0<\mu(Q)<\infty
for all
Q\in\mathcal{F}
. The output
(\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
of Algorithm
1
satisfies

(2.1)

\Lambda_{\mathcal{F}}=\Lambda=\frac{\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(Q)}{\mu\bigl(\textstyle\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{A}}Q\bigr)},

Algorithm
1
is strongly polynomial, with a runtime of

O(n^{4}\log n\cdot\mathrm{EO}_{1}+n^{5}\log n),

where
n=\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert.

Proof.

By line
1
of Algorithm
1
, we have
f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j-1})=0
for all
j\geq 1
. Therefore, if
\mathcal{A}_{j}=\mathcal{A}_{j-1}
for some
j\geq 1
, we have
r=0
and the while loop terminates. Thus, since
\mathcal{A}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{j-1}
by construction, the while loop is iterated at most
n
times. Denote the number of iterations by
m
. Moreover,
f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j-1})=0
also ensures that we can always take
\mathcal{A}_{j}
nonempty.

Let
(\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
be the output of Algorithm
1
. The second equality in (
2.1
) follows directly from the fact that
\mathcal{A}
is a nonempty collection satisfying
f_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{A})=r=0
when the algorithm terminates. The first equality in (
2.1
) is equivalent to

(2.2)

\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}f_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{B})=0.

We claim that
\mathcal{A}_{j}
is a minimizer for
\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{B})
for all
1\leq j\leq m
, which for
j=m
implies (
2.2
), since
f_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{A})=f_{\Lambda_{m}}(\mathcal{A}_{m})=r=0
when the algorithm terminates.

We prove the claim inductively. The case
j=1
is clear since
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{F}
. Suppose that the claim holds for some
j\geq 2
. By line
1
of Algorithm
1
and
f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j-1})=0
we have

\Lambda_{j}\cdot\mu\bigl(\textstyle\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}Q\bigr)-\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}\mu(Q)=f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j})=\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{j-1}}f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{B})\leq 0.

It follows that

(2.3)

\Lambda_{j+1}=\frac{\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}\mu(Q)}{\mu\bigl(\textstyle\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}Q\bigr)}\geq\Lambda_{j},\qquad j\geq 1.

Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be a minimizer for
\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{B})
. Then we have by the submodularity from Lemma
2.2
, (
2.3
) and the induction hypothesis

\displaystyle f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{A}_{j}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime})-f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{A}_{j})-f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{A}_{j}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j})-f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-(\Lambda_{j+1}-\Lambda_{j})\Bigl(\mu\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}_{j}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}}Q\bigr)-\mu\bigl({\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}}Q\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j})-f_{\Lambda_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\leq 0.

We conclude

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{B})=f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\geq f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{A}_{j}\cap\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\geq\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{j}}f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{B}),

which means that, since
\mathcal{A}_{j+1}
is a minimizer for
\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{j}}f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{B})
,
\mathcal{A}_{j+1}
is a minimizer for
\min_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}f_{\Lambda_{j+1}}(\mathcal{B})
as well. This finishes the proof of the claim, and thus the proof of (
2.1
).

It remains to show the claimed runtime. As noted at the start of the proof, the while loop is executed at most
n
times. The time one iteration takes is determined by line
1
of Algorithm
1
, the minimization of the submodular function
f_{\Lambda_{j}}
. Using the s algorithm from
[
JLSZ24
]
, a minimizer for a submodular function
f
can be found with runtime
O(n^{3}\log n\cdot\mathrm{EO}_{f}+n^{4}\log n)
, where
\mathrm{EO}_{f}
denotes the runtime of one function evaluation of
f
.
If we initialize by storing the value of
\mu(Q)
for all
Q\in\mathcal{F}
, a function evaluation of
f_{\Lambda_{j}}
has a runtime of
O(\mathrm{EO}_{1}+n)
.
Hence, the runtime of line
1
of Algorithm
1
is

O(n\cdot\mathrm{EO}_{1}+n^{3}\log n\cdot(\mathrm{EO}_{1}+n)+n^{4}\log n)=O(n^{3}\log n\cdot\mathrm{EO}_{1}+n^{4}\log n).

This finishes the proof.
∎

.

The runtime of Algorithm
1
is, roughly speaking,
n
times the best available algorithm for submodular function optimization on a set with
n
elements. If one is interested in the minimum number of calls to the oracle
1
, one can replace the submodular function optimization algorithm from
[

]

3.
The Carleson condition implies sparseness

Having established an algorithm to compute the optimal
\Lambda
such that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition, we now turn our second goal: Constructing the sets
\{E_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}
such that
\mu(E_{Q})\geq\frac{1}{\Lambda}\mu(Q)
for all
Q\in\mathcal{F}
. In particular, we will provide a constructive proof that a finite collection satisfying the
\Lambda
-Carleson condition is
\frac{1}{\Lambda}
-sparse.

Without any assumptions on the measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
, not every
\Lambda
-Carleson condition is
\frac{1}{\Lambda}
-sparse. This is easily seen by taking
S=\{1,2\}
,
\mu
the counting measure and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{P}(S)
. This
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the
2
-Carleson condition, but is not
\frac{1}{2}
-sparse. This problem cannot arise when
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
is non-atomic, which is assumed in, e.g.,
[
Hän18
,
Ver96
]
. Alternatively, to include the case of atomic measures, one can extend the notion of sparseness as in
[
Rey24
, Definition 1]
.

Definition 3.1
.

We call
\mathcal{F}

generalized
\eta
-sparse
for
\eta\in(0,1]
if for every
Q\in\mathcal{F}
there is a function
\varphi_{Q}\colon\Omega\to[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\varphi_{Q}\hskip 2.0pt\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\geq\eta\mu(Q),\qquad Q\in\mathcal{F},

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{F}}\varphi_{Q}

\displaystyle\leq 1.

Any
\eta
-sparse collection
\mathcal{F}
is generalized
\eta
-sparse by setting
\varphi_{Q}=\ind_{E_{Q}}
for
Q\in\mathcal{F}
. Conversely, if
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
is non-atomic, a convexity argument as in
[
Dor75
, Lemma 2.3]
shows that a generalized
\eta
-sparse collection is
\eta
-sparse.

Given a finite, nonempty collection
\mathcal{F}
of sets in

3.3.
The maximum flow problem

Before we continue, we provide some background on the maximum flow problem. Let
G=(V,E,c)
be a weighted directed graph with a source
s\in V
and a sink
t\in V
. A function
f\colon E\to[0,\infty)
is called an
flow
through the graph
G
if it satisfies
flow conservation

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in V:(u,v)\in E}f(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V:(v,w)\in E}f(v,w)

\displaystyle v\in V\setminus\{s,t\}

and the
capacity constraint
imposed by
c

(3.3)

\displaystyle f(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq c(u,v),

\displaystyle(u,v)\in E.

The
value of a flow
f
is the amount of flow from
s
to
t
, which is given by

(3.4)

\lvert f\rvert:=\sum_{v\in V:(s,v)\in E}f(s,v)=\sum_{v\in V:(v,t)\in E}f(v,t)

where the equality follows from flow conservation.

Finding a maximum flow of a graph is a classical optimization problem, for which (strongly) polynomial algorithms date back to Ford–Fulkerson
[
FF56
]
, Edmonds–Karp
[
EK72
]
and Dinic
[
Din70
]
. The best runtime for our purposes is provided by Orlin’s algorithm
[
Orl13
]
, which can find a maximum flow through a graph with runtime
O(\lvert V\rvert\lvert E\rvert)
.

To make a connection with the Carleson condition, we also need to introduce cuts. An
(s,t)
-cut
is a partition
(S,T)
of
V
(so
T=V\setminus S
) such that
s\in S
and
t\in T
. The
capacity
of the
(s,t)
-cut is given by

c(S,T)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E:u\in S,v\in T}c(u,v).

We will see that a minimum capacity
(s,t)
-cut of the graph constructed in Subsection
3.2
corresponds to a
maximizer in the Carleson condition.

Intuitively, the value of a flow through a graph can never exceed the smallest bottleneck in the graph, i.e., the minimum capacity of a
(s,t)
-cut. In fact, the maximum value of a flow is equal to the minimum capacity of a
(s,t)
-cut, which is the famous max-flow min-cut theorem.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G=(V,E,c)
be a weighted directed graph with source
s\in V
and sink
t\in V
. Then

\displaystyle\max\bigl\{\lvert f\rvert:f\text{ is a flow}\bigr\}=\min\bigl\{c(S,T):(S,T)\text{ is an $(s,t)$-cut}\bigr\}.
