A Polylogarithmic Algorithm for Stochastic Online Sorting
Dimitris Fotakis, Andreas Kalavas, Charalampos Platanos, Thanos Tolias

Abstract
In the Online Sorting Problem , an array of n n initially empty cells is given. At each time step t t , a real number x t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] x_{t}\in[0,1] arrives and must be placed immediately and irrevocably into an empty cell. The objective is to minimize the sum of absolute differences between consecutive entries. The problem was introduced by Aamand, Abrahamsen, Beretta, and Kleist (SODA 2023) as a technical tool for proving lower bounds in online geometric packing problems. In follow-up work, Abrahamsen, Bercea, Beretta, Klausen, and Kozma (ESA 2024) studied the Stochastic Online Sorting Problem , where each x t x_{t} is drawn i.i.d. from 𝒰 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) \mathcal{U}(0,1) , and presented a O ~ ​ ( n 1 / 4 ) \widetilde{O}(n^{1/4}) -competitive algorithm, showing that stochastic input enables much stronger guarantees than in the adversarial setting. They also introduced the Online Travelling Salesperson Problem (TSP) as a multidimensional generalization. More recently, Hu [ Hu25 ] , independently and in parallel, obtained a log ⁡ n ⋅ 2 O ⁡ ( log ∗ ⁡ n ) \log n\cdot 2^{O(\log^{*}n)} -competitive algorithm together with a logarithmic lower bound for the Stochastic Online Sorting Problem . We give an O ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{2}n) -competitive algorithm for the Stochastic Online Sorting Problem that succeeds w.h.p., achieving an exponential improvement over the O ~ ​ ( n 1 / 4 ) \widetilde{O}(n^{1/4}) bound of Abrahamsen et al. (ESA 2024). Our approach further extends to the Stochastic Online TSP in fixed dimension d d , where it achieves an O ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{2}n) -competitive ratio.

Introduction

Online decision-making under uncertainty is a central theme in algorithm design, capturing the tension between irrevocable actions and incomplete information. In many applications, elements must be assigned to positions or resources immediately upon arrival, before future inputs are known. Examples include packet placement in memory buffers, layout problems in VLSI design, and dynamic task scheduling. The cost incurred often depends not just on individual assignments, but on the global structure of the final configuration.

The
Online Sorting Problem
, introduced by Aamand, Abrahamsen, Beretta, and Kleist
[
AABK23
]
(SODA 2023), offers a minimalist abstraction of such scenarios: real-valued elements arrive one by one and must be irrevocably placed into an array, with the goal of minimizing the sum of absolute differences between consecutive entries. Despite its simplicity, the model captures the core algorithmic difficulty of a variety of geometric online problems. Aamand et al. used it as a technical foundation to derive lower bounds for online strip packing, bin packing, and perimeter packing, and provided a tight
O(\sqrt{n})
-competitive algorithm in the adversarial setting. Their lower bound applied to deterministic algorithms; subsequently, Abrahamsen, Bercea, Beretta, Klausen, and Kozma
[
ABB+24
]
(ESA 2024) showed that even randomized algorithms cannot beat this guarantee in the worst case.

The connection to geometric optimization extends further: Online Sorting can be seen as a special case of the
Online Travelling Salesperson Problem (Online TSP)
, where arriving points must be irrevocably inserted into a growing tour. This generalization was introduced in the same ESA 2024 paper by Abrahamsen et al., who gave a dimension-dependent algorithm for the adversarial Online TSP. Soon after, Bertram
[
Ber25
]
(ESA 2025) demonstrated that dimension-independent bounds are possible in the adversarial model, further highlighting the richness of the problem space.

Motivated by the large
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
lower bound in the adversarial setting, Abrahamsen
et al.
[
ABB+24
]
initiated the study of a stochastic variant of Online Sorting, where each input
value is drawn i.i.d. from a known distribution. Their goal was to leverage stochasticity to obtain
qualitatively stronger guarantees, by exploiting typical-case structure rather than defending against
worst-case inputs. Focusing on the uniform distribution, they designed a

\widetilde{O}(n^{1/4})
-competitive algorithm, demonstrating that even mild probabilistic
assumptions can lead to exponential improvements over the adversarial baseline.

In this work, we present an
O(\log^{2}n)
-competitive algorithm for stochastic online
sorting, achieving an exponential improvement over the
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/4})
bound of Abrahamsen
et al.
[
ABB+24
]
. Furthermore, we show that our approach extends naturally to the multidimensional setting of stochastic Online TSP with (asymptotically) the same competitive ratio. In recent parallel and independent work, Hu
[
Hu25
]
obtained a

\log n\cdot 2^{O(\log^{*}n)}
-competitive algorithm along with a nearly matching lower
bound of
\Omega(\log n)
for the stochastic version of Online Sorting.

Remark 1.1
.

To the best of our understanding, Hu’s
O(\log^{20}n)
high-probability algorithm
[
Hu25
]
can be extended to the multidimensional setting using techniques similar to those developed in Section 3. By contrast, whether his recursive
\log n\cdot 2^{O(\log^{*}n)}
-competitive algorithm extends beyond the one-dimensional case remains unclear.

1.1
Formal Problem Definition

We study the
Online Sorting Problem
, introduced by Aamand et al.
[
AABK23
]
. The input consists of a sequence of
n
real numbers
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\in[0,1]
, which arrive one at a time in an online fashion. There is an array
A
of length
n
with initially empty cells
A[1],A[2],\dots,A[n]
. Upon the arrival of each number
x_{j}
, an online algorithm must immediately and irrevocably assign
x_{j}
to an empty cell of the array. After all
n
numbers have been placed, the algorithm incurs a total cost equal to the sum of absolute differences between consecutive entries in the array:

\text{Cost}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|A[i+1]-A[i]|.

(2)

The objective is to minimize this cost.

Aamand et al.
[
AABK23
]
first considered the
adversarial model
, where the input sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
is selected by an adaptive adversary with full knowledge of the algorithm. The performance of an algorithm
Alg
is measured by its competitive ratio:

\max_{\mathcal{I}}\frac{\textsc{Alg}(\mathcal{I})}{\textsc{Opt}(\mathcal{I})},

(3)

where
\textsc{Opt}(\mathcal{I})
denotes the cost of the optimal offline algorithm on instance
\mathcal{I}
, and the maximization is over all valid input sequences.

Abrahamsen et al.
[
ABB+24
]
later introduced the
stochastic model with known distribution
, in which the inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are drawn i.i.d. from a known distribution with cumulative distribution function (CDF)
F:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
. The goal is again to minimize the competitive ratio relative to the optimal offline solution on the realized instance. Formally, the algorithm seeks to minimize:

\frac{\textsc{Alg}(\mathcal{I})}{\textsc{Opt}(\mathcal{I})}

(4)

with high probability over the random draw of the instance
\mathcal{I}\sim F^{\otimes n}
.

1.2
Technical Overview and Contributions.

A Polylogarithmic Algorithm for Stochastic Online Sorting.

Before describing our algorithm, we will briefly review the previous work on this problem by Abrahamsen et al.
[
ABB+24
]
The first main idea is to divide the larger part of the array into buckets, hash elements into them using a standard hash function and solve the problem separately in each bucket. The second main idea is the introduction of a backyard. Since each bucket has a fixed capacity, some elements will not fit in their hashed bucket and so an additional (small) part of the array in the end is used as a place to store this overflow (backyard). Their first algorithm that uses the algorithm for the adversarial algorithm from Aamand et al.
[
AABK23
]
in each bucket achieves
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/3})
complexity. When they use a recursive version of their first algorithm inside each bucket they improve the time complexity to
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/4})
. We continue by seeing the above algorithm from the lens of the balls-into-bins literature in order to understand its limitations.

Abrahamsen et al.’s algorithm (especially the first version) can be thought an instance of the balls-into-bins problem, since each number is placed in each bucket with the same probability. For their algorithm to work, it is crucial that all the buckets are filled before the backyard is filled. This argument is necessary to show that the numbers in each bucket are only those hashed into them and not leftover numbers that do not fit anywhere else in the array at the end of the algorithm. Then, they argue that the cost of placing numbers into buckets is small, since each bucket contains "close" numbers, while the backyard contains numbers that are placed arbitrarily. The
O(n^{1/3})
algorithm is obtained by optimizing the size of the buckets and the backyard to balance the aforementioned costs. The main limitation of the above approach is that the balls-into-bins instance is fixed at the start of the algorithm and thus the size of the backyard must be large in order to cover for imbalances in the buckets with high probability.

Main Idea and Intuition.

Our main idea is that we can view the online sorting problem as a series of balls-into-bins instances, adjusting the capacities of the buckets dynamically in each new instance to handle previous imbalances. We partition the original array
A
in a series of (decreasing in length) subarrays
A_{i}
and treat filling each subarray as a different instance. More specifically, we start placing numbers into buckets with a hashed-based logic in a subarray
A_{1}
of the original array. This is our first balls-into-bins instance. During the first overflow in our current balls-into-bins instance we initiate a new one. In this new instance our bins consist of the empty cells of
A_{1}
and subarray
A_{2}
. The size and number of the buckets in the new subarray are selected so that the new balls-into-bins instance can handle previous imbalances.

In order to gain more intuition we consider a small toy example. Suppose for simplicity’s sake, that subarray
A_{1}
has
100
cells and is divided into
4
buckets, with capacity
25
each, that handle numbers in
(0,0.25),(0.25,0.5),(0.5,0.75),(0.75,1)
respectively. Assume that, the bucket in the first balls-into-bins instance that contains numbers from
(0,0.25)
is overflowed and numbers in each bucket are
(25,20,10,15)
at the time exactly before the overflow. Then, the up to this point empty, subarray
A_{2}
will be brought into action to take care of the overflows of subarray
A_{1}
. Let’s assume that
A_{2}
has
50
cells and two buckets that handle numbers
(0,0.5),(0.5,1)
. Our goal is to set the lengths of each bucket in
A_{2}
in a way that ensure that an overflow is occurred relatively late, for instance after
A_{1}
is completely filled. If we allocate
25
cells for each bucket in
A_{2}
then we expect the first one to overflow significantly faster than the second one since in
A_{1}
there are less empty cells for elements lying in
(0,0.5)
than for elements lying in
(0.5,1)
. Thus in order to handle the imbalances in
A_{1}
we will allocate more cells in the first bucket in
A_{2}
. More specifically, the total number of empty cells in
A_{1}
is
0+5+15+10=30
and since we add
50
cells with
A_{2}
we have in total
80
cells. If we allocate
35
cells for the first bucket in
A_{2}
and
15
cells for the second one then we can consider a new balls-into-bins instance with
2
bins that handle numbers from
(0,0.5)
and
(0.5,1)
respectively. The first bin contains the first two buckets of
A_{1}
and the first bucket of
A_{2}
and has
40
empty cells, thus capacity
40
. The second bin contains the last two buckets of
A_{1}
and the second bucket of
A_{2}
and has
40
empty cells, thus capacity
40
. As a result, we have created a new balanced balls-into-bins instance since the bins have equal capacity and thus we expect that the overflow of this instance occurs later than if we divided
A_{2}
into two equal sized buckets. The balls-into-bins perspective and specifically the power of changing dynamically capacities of the balls-into-bins instances to handle imbalances are the core ideas that let us to make an exponential improvement upon the best-known algorithm.

Uniform Stochastic Online Euclidean TSP.

The main challenge when trying to extend this problem to higher dimensions is that we suddenly do not have a trivial offline solution - our optimal solution is the cost of the TSP tour. To address this issue, we divide the space into blocks, and consider the best tour that visits the input points block by block, where consecutive blocks are adjacent. Here, the order of the blocks is inspired by space-filling curves, which have been widely used for the universal TSP problem in the past (see
[
PB89
,
BG89
,
HKL06
,
CS17
]
). We show that this special tour is worse only by a constant factor from the TSP tour, and then we adjust our online sorting algorithm to find an approximate solution of this specific high-dimensional tour by utilizing Bertram’s
[
Ber25
]
algorithm. This way, we manage to maintain the
O(\log^{2}n)
-competitive factor in higher dimensions. Finally, we note that we only consider the uniform distribution.

2.2
Algorithm Description

First, we note that, for the sake of clarity of presentation, our analysis does not aim to obtain the tightest possible bounds. In fact, a more favourable competitive ratio can be established with a refined argument. We refer the reader to
Remark

2.10
,
Remark

Remark

2.12

Phase
1
.

First, define
\ell
as the unique positive integer such that
\frac{n}{4\log^{2}n}<2^{\ell}\leq\frac{n}{2\log^{2}n}
. Then, let
A_{1}
as the subarray containing the first
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
elements of the original array. In the first phase we will place elements inside this subarray using a hash-based logic. First, we divide the
A_{1}
elements into
K_{1}=2^{\ell}
contiguous sub-subarrays, that we call buckets, of size
C_{1}=\frac{A_{1}}{K_{1}}
elements each. Note that
C_{1}
may not be an integer; consequently, some buckets will have capacity
\lfloor C_{1}\rfloor
while others will have capacity
\lfloor C_{1}\rfloor+1
. Either case, the capacity of each bucket is
\Theta(\log^{2}n)
. As we receive elements online, we place an element
x\in(\frac{i-1}{K_{1}},\frac{i}{K_{1}}]
for
i\in[K_{1}]
into bucket
i
(element
0
is placed in bucket
1
). Inside each bucket, the placement of the elements is done using
\mathcal{A}_{adv}
. Since the input is drawn from
\mathcal{U}(0,1)
and each bucket has the same mapped-length, each sample has equal probability of being placed into each bucket. Placement of numbers into the buckets of
A_{1}
at the first phase of our algorithm can be thought as a balls-into-bins instance where we throw balls into
K_{1}
bins, uniformly at random. When an element arrives and its designated bucket is full, we say that an overflow has occurred, triggering a transition to Phase
2
.

Phase
2
.

In order to handle overflows from subarray
A_{1}
we allocate a subarray
A_{2}
of size
\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor
next to
A_{1}
. Subarray
A_{2}
is divided into
K_{2}=K_{1}/2=2^{\ell-1}
buckets and bucket
i
handles elements from interval
(\frac{i-1}{K_{2}},\frac{i}{K_{2}}]
. When a number cannot be placed into its designated bucket in
A_{1}
(because the bucket is full) we place it into its designated bucket in
A_{2}
. Inside each bucket, the placement of the elements is done using
\mathcal{A}_{adv}
. To fully describe
A_{2}
, we must specify the size of each bucket. Crucially, these sizes are not the same for each bucket, as discussed in the introduction.

To gain a better understanding of how we will select the capacities of the buckets, we will define a second balls-into-bins instance, which is created alongside Phase
2
. Consider the empty cells of buckets
2i-1
and
2i
of subarray
A_{1}
together with the
i
-th bucket of subarray
A_{2}
(which is empty and its size will be defined shortly). Together, these
3
buckets create the
i
-th bin of our second balls-into-bins instance, consisting of
K_{2}
bins. More specifically, bin
i
receives elements from the intervals that buckets
2i-1
and
2i
of subarray
A_{1}
map, together with the interval of the
i
-th bucket of subarray
A_{2}
, which are the same:
(\frac{i-1}{K_{2}},\frac{i}{K_{2}}]
. As a result, since the mapped interval in each bin has the same size each sample has equal probability of being placed into each bin, thus letting us use the balls-into-bins modelling.

When a bin overflows in this second balls-into-bins instance, this means a bucket in
A_{2}
has overflowed since even if buckets of
A_{1}
are full we can still place an element in its designated bucket in
A_{2}
. Ideally, we would like the first overflow in subarray
A_{2}
to occur late. To achieve this, we would like the second balls-into-bins instance to be somewhat balanced by forcing bins to have the same capacity. This fact determines the size of each bucket. More specifically, if the overflow in
A_{1}
occurred exactly after
N_{1}
elements, then we have
A_{1}-N_{1}
empty positions in
A_{1}
and thus together with
A_{2}
we have
A_{1}+A_{2}-N_{1}
total empty positions. This is the total size of the second balls-into-bins instance, i.e. the total number of balls that all bins can collectively hold. Since the number of bins is
K_{2}
, for the capacity of each bin we have
C_{2}=\frac{A_{1}+A_{2}-N_{1}}{K_{2}}=\Theta(\log^{2}n)
. Note, again, that
C_{2}
may not be an integer thus the capacities of the bins in the balls-into-bins instance may differ by at most 1 (
\lfloor C_{2}\rfloor
and
\lfloor C_{2}\rfloor+1
). Using the capacity of each bin we can find the size of each bucket in
A_{2}
. More specifically, for bucket
i
of
A_{2}
we subtract from the capacity of bin
i
the remaining empty cells in buckets
2i-1
and
2i
of
A_{1}
. When an element arrives and its designated bucket in
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
is full, we transition to Phase
3
.

Final Phase.

When

B

B
using the
\mathcal{A}_{adv}

has size

2.3
Analysis

In the following, we will show that our algorithm does not fail with high probability. First, we present the following lemma, which plays a key role in analysing the balls-into-bins instances encountered in our problem.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose a balls-into-bins instance with
K
total bins, each with capacity
C=\Theta(\log^{2}n)
and let
M=K\cdot C\leq n
the total number of balls that all bins can collectively hold. If the probability that an element belongs to each bin is the same, then the first overflow of a bin happens after
M-\frac{M}{\Theta(\log^{1/2}n)}
balls are thrown w.p. at least
1-K/n^{2}
. Also, after throwing
M+\frac{M}{\Theta(\log^{1/2}n)}
balls, all bins are full w.p. at least
1-K/n^{2}
.

Proof.

We will use standard Chernoff bounds along with a union bound over bad events. For each
i\in[K]
, let
L_{i}^{(t)}
denote the random variable representing the number of balls in the
i
-th bin after
t
balls have been thrown. Note that
\mathbb{E}[L_{i}^{(t)}]=t/K
. Also, let
X_{i}^{(t)}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1/K)
be the indicator random variable that is equal to
1
if and only if the
t
-th ball lands in bin
i
. Thus,
L_{i}^{(t)}=\sum_{j\in[t]}X_{i}^{(j)}
, where
X_{i}^{(j)}
are independent. First, to bound the time of the first overflow, we apply a standard upper tail Chernoff bound. After throwing
T=M-c\cdot\frac{M}{\log^{1/2}n}=M-\frac{M}{\Theta(\log^{1/2}n)}
balls for some positive constant
c
, we obtain that (
c
is not absorbed into
\Theta(\cdot)
term):

\mathbb{P}\!\left[L_{i}^{(T)}\geq C\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\frac{M-T}{T}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{T}{K}}{\frac{M-T}{T}+2}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{(M-T)^{2}C}{(M+T)M}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\Theta(\log n)\right)\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}

(5)

for some choice of constant
c
, depending on
C=\Theta(\log^{2}n)
. Taking a union bound over all
K
bins, we get:

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\exists i\in[K]:L_{i}^{(T)}\geq C\right]\leq\frac{K}{n^{2}}.

(6)

This shows that w.p. at least
1-\frac{K}{n^{2}}
, no bin has exhibited overflow after
T=M-o(M)
balls have been thrown, completing the first part of the lemma.

For the second part of the lemma, we want to show that after throwing
T^{\prime}=M+c\cdot\frac{M}{\log^{1/2}n}=M+\frac{M}{\Theta(\log^{1/2}n)}
balls for some positive constant
c
, all bins are full w.p. at least
1-\frac{K}{n^{2}}
. From a standard lower tail Chernoff bound we obtain that (again
c
is not absorbed into
\Theta(\cdot)
term):

\mathbb{P}\!\left[L_{i}^{(T^{\prime})}<C\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\frac{T^{\prime}-M}{T^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{T^{\prime}}{K}}{2}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{(T^{\prime}-M)^{2}C}{2T^{\prime}M}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{4}\Theta(\log n)\right)\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}

(7)

for some choice of constant
c
, depending on
C=\Theta(\log^{2}n)
. Taking a union bound over all
K
bins, we get:

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\exists i\in[K]:L_{i}^{(T^{\prime})}<C\right]\leq\frac{K}{n^{2}},

(8)

finishing the proof.
∎

3
Uniform Stochastic Online Euclidean TSP

In this section, we extend our ideas for stochastic online sorting to the
Online Euclidean TSP
problem, which arises by increasing the dimensionality of the input. The input sequence consists of
d
-dimensional vectors
x_{i}
(or points) and the cost function becomes the sum of the Euclidean distances (symbolized with
\|\cdot\|
) between vectors in neighbouring cells in array
A
. As we aim to minimize the length of the total distance between consecutive points, this is the online setting of the well-studied TSP problem. Interestingly, one can think of the online sorting problem as the online TSP problem in the space
[0,1]
.

Online Euclidean TSP
was introduced by
[
ABB+24
]
who also showed an
\Omega({\sqrt{n}})
lower bound for the adversarial setting. Recently, Bertram
[
Ber25
]
presented an algorithm with
O(\sqrt{n})
competitive ratio, and thus closed the gap for the adversarial setting. In this paper, we explore what happens in the
Stochastic
setting, where the input is drawn from the
Uniform
distribution in
[0,1]^{d}
. Formally, we have:

Definition 3.1

(Uniform Stochastic Online Euclidean TSP)
.

Consider an array
A
of length
n
with initially empty cells
A[1],A[2],\dots,A[n]
. An input sequence consisting of
n
points
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\in[0,1]^{d}
drawn i.i.d. from the Uniform Distribution
U
in
[0,1]^{d}
arrives online one by one. Upon the arrival of each point
x_{j}
, an online algorithm must immediately and irrevocably assign
x_{j}
to an empty cell in the array. After all
n
points have been assigned, the algorithm incurs a total cost equal to the sum of euclidean distances between points in consecutive cells:

\text{Cost}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\|A[i+1]-A[i]\|.

(16)

The objective is to minimize the ratio
\frac{\text{ALG}(\mathcal{I})}{\textsc{Opt}(\mathcal{I})}
that holds with high probability over the random choice of the instance
\mathcal{I}
.

As we saw in
Section

2
, our algorithm for online sorting matches intervals to buckets. In higher dimensions we aim to match
blocks to buckets. However, in contrast with the online sorting problem, the cost of the best tour that visits the points block by block (we call this a
block tour
), is not necessarily optimal, whereas in the online sorting problem the best order of numbers when grouped interval by interval equals the optimal. To accommodate this, it suffices to show that the best block tour has constant factor approximation from the optimal tour with high probability. Then, by running a variant of the online sorting problem (that utilizes the adversarial algorithm by Bertram
[
Ber25
]
to accommodate higher dimensions), we get an
O(\log^{2}n)
-competitive ratio from the optimal block tour, which is
O(1)
away from the TSP tour. Consequently, we get a final
O(\log^{2}n)
-competitive ratio.

3.2
Approximability of Block Tours

First, we show that the best tour that visits the points block by block is a constant factor away from the optimal TSP, which allows as to run the online sorting algorithm in higher dimensions without losing in competitive ratio.

Figure 4
:
The domain
[0,1]\times[0,1]
is partitioned into four blocks.
The left panel shows the block-by-block tour produced by our algorithm,
while the right panel shows the optimal TSP tour on the same instance.
Although the two tours may differ, our partitioning ensures that their lengths
remain close with high probability.

We assume that the number of dimensions is not very high - specifically we assume
d\leq\ell
. This is needed so that the blocks in the partition have side lengths strictly less than
1
. We will see later that this assumption is not strong, as we will show that for greater dimensions the competitive ratio of a random tour is constant w.h.p.. We then bound the cost of subtours of the points in each of the blocks (these correspond to the intra-bucket costs) and the cost of connecting all subtours (this corresponds to the inter-bucket cost).

We start by showing the correctness of
Algorithm

2
, i.e. that it traverses all the blocks, and that consecutive blocks are all adjacent.

Lemma 3.3
.

Algorithm

2
traverses all blocks, and consecutive blocks are adjacent.

Proof.

Let
c
be a block with coordinates
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{d}
. We will find exactly the order number that it is visited. Let
n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{d}
the number of blocks in each dimension. By the algorithm, for each move in dimension
i
,
n_{i-1}
moves are required in dimension
i-1
(if
i\neq 0
). Additionally, we observe that while the algorithm runs, if
v_{i}
is increasing, then there is an even number of dimensions among those with index
j>i
for which
v_{j}
is even, and if
v_{i}
is decreasing, then there is an odd number of dimensions
j>i
for which
v_{j}
is even. This happens because all
n_{i}
are even as they are powers of
2
. Thus, we get that the order of the block is:

o(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{d})=1+\sum_{i\in[d]}\left((\text{num\_even)}\cdot(v_{i}-1)+(\text{num\_even)}\cdot(n_{i}-v_{i}))\cdot\prod_{j\in[i-1]}n_{j}\right)

where num_even is
1
if the number of dimensions with even
v_{j}
among those with index
j>i
is even, or
0
otherwise.

Regarding the adjacency of consecutive blocks, this can be seen directly from the algorithm, as we always move one dimension by
1
, and in a direction that does not take us off boundary. The only exception is the first and last block, which are both corner blocks. Therefore, our algorithm is correct.
∎

In one dimension, we could relate the cost of a tour in an interval directly to the cost of
Opt
. In higher dimensions, however, there is no analogous way to compare the cost of a tour confined to a bucket with the global optimum. To make progress, we need a structural guarantee showing that the block-by-block tour is nevertheless a good proxy for
Opt
. The next lemma provides exactly this, by invoking classical results from the TSP literature to bound the gap between the two tours. We note that this is the only step in our analysis where we exploit the fact that the underlying distribution is uniform; all other parts of the argument extend to arbitrary known distributions.

Lemma 3.4
.

The best tour that visits the points block by block has cost at most
O(1)
times the TSP tour with probability at least
1-(\frac{1}{n\log^{2}n}+2e^{-c^{\prime}\cdot d\cdot n})
when the points are drawn i.i.d. from the Uniform distribution.

Proof.

We will first find the expected cost of the TSP in each block and show that it is concentrated.

In
[
BHH59
]
, Beardwood, Halton and Hammersley show that the expected value of a TSP tour with
n
points drawn i.i.d from
U[0,\Delta]^{d}
is
\beta_{d}\cdot n^{1-1/d}\cdot\Delta
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
\beta_{d}\rightarrow\sqrt{\frac{d}{2\pi e}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We notate this quantity with
\text{TSP}(n,d,\Delta)
. Clearly,
\text{TSP}(n,d,\Delta)
is also an upper bound for the expected value of a TSP with
n
points drawn i.i.d from a subspace of
U[0,\Delta]^{d}
, so we will now find an upper bound for the longest side length of a block. As we split the grid
\ell
times, we split at least
\lfloor\frac{\ell}{d}\rfloor
times in each direction. Thus, the maximum side length is at most
(\frac{1}{2})^{\lfloor\frac{\ell}{d}\rfloor}
. As
\ell
and
d
are both integers then
(\frac{1}{2})^{\lfloor\frac{\ell}{d}\rfloor}\leq(\frac{1}{2})^{\frac{\ell-d}{d}}<(\frac{4\log^{2}n}{n})^{1/d}\cdot 2^{\frac{d}{d}}\leq 4(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n})^{1/d}
. Thus, for the cost of each subtour in block
C_{i}
, let it be
T_{i}
, we get
E[T_{i}]\leq\text{TSP}(\log^{2}n,d,\Delta)\leq\beta_{d}\cdot(\log^{2}n)^{1-1/d}\cdot\Delta<\beta_{d}\cdot(\log^{2}n)^{1-1/d}\cdot 4(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n})^{1/d}=4\beta_{d}\cdot n^{-1/d}\cdot\log^{2}n
.

Furthermore, from Chapter 2 of
[
Ste97
]
we get the concentration bound:

P(|T-\text{TSP}(n,d,\Delta)|\geq t)\leq 2e^{-\frac{ct^{2}}{n^{1-2/d}\Delta^{2}}}

where
T
is the cost of the actual TSP tour and
c
is a constant. For a block
C_{i}
in our grid this becomes
P(|T_{i}-E[T_{i}]|\geq t)\leq 2e^{-\frac{ct^{2}}{(\log^{2}n)^{1-2/d}(4(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n})^{1/d})^{2}}}=2e^{-\frac{c^{\prime}t^{2}n^{2/d}}{\log^{1+\varepsilon}n}}
. By setting
t=8\beta_{d}\cdot n^{-1/d}\cdot\log^{2}n\geq E[T_{i}]
we get the probability that the TSP has cost more than 3 times the expected (if
E[T_{i}]
is minimum). This probability is at most
2e^{-\frac{c^{\prime\prime}d\cdot n^{-2/d}\cdot(\log^{2}n)^{2}\cdot n^{2/d}}{\log^{2}n}}\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}
. By applying a union bound over all
2^{\ell}\leq\frac{n}{2\log^{2}n}
blocks, we get that with probability
1-\frac{1}{n\log^{2}n}
all block subtours are at most
12\beta_{d}\cdot n^{-1/d}\cdot\log^{2}n
each (when
E[T_{i}]
is maximum).

Now, we will bound the cost of connecting the block subtours. As we connect only neighbouring blocks, the cost of the edge that connects the blocks is at most twice the diagonal of a block. The diagonal of a block is the maximum side length times
\sqrt{d}
, which is at most
2\sqrt{d}\cdot 4(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n})^{1/d}
. This is less than
2\beta_{d}\cdot n^{-1/d}\cdot\log^{2}n
and thus we can charge each connecting edge to the block it starts from, making now the attributed cost of each block at most
14\beta_{d}\cdot n^{-1/d}\cdot\log^{2}n
with the same probability as before. When connecting the last block to the first, we can bound the length of this edge with the TSP tour length (and thus yield
+1
in the approximation factor).

By summing over all blocks we get that the best tour over all points, when the tour visits them block by block is at most the number of blocks times the attributed cost of each block, which is at most:

\frac{n}{2\log^{2}n}\cdot 14\beta_{d}\cdot n^{-1/d}\cdot\log^{2}n=7\beta_{d}\cdot n^{1-1/d}

Using the same tools, we get that the expected TSP tour over
n
points in
[0,1]^{d}
(without the block order condition) is
E[T]=\text{TSP}(n,d,1)=\beta_{d}\cdot n^{1-1/d}
, and is additionally well concentrated. Using the previous bound we get:

P(E[T]-T>\frac{1}{2}\beta_{d}\cdot n^{1-1/d})\leq 2e^{-\frac{c^{\prime}\cdot d\cdot n^{2-2/d}}{n^{1-2/d}}}\leq 2e^{-c^{\prime}\cdot d\cdot n}

Therefore, the best tour that visits the points block by block has cost at most
15
times the TSP tour (14 by dividing the upper bound for the block tour with the lower bound for the TSP tour,
+1
for connecting the last to the first block). The probability is at least
1-(\frac{1}{n\log^{2}n}+2e^{-c^{\prime}\cdot d\cdot n})
.
∎

Next, we show that in higher dimensions, the cost of a random tour over the points is close to the expected TSP tour cost. As the points arrive independently from the uniform distribution, we place them in
A
in the order they arrive, i.e. the first point in
A[1]
, the second in
A[2]
and so on.

Lemma 3.5
.

When
d\geq\ell
and the points are drawn i.i.d. from the Uniform distribution, the cost of the tour that visits the points in the order they arrive is at most
O(1)
times the TSP tour cost with probability
1-1/n
.

Proof.

Let
C_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-x_{i+1}\|
, where
x_{n+1}=x_{1}
. As the points are independently drawn, the expected cost of the tour is approximately:

\mathbb{E}[C_{n}]\approx n\cdot\mu_{d},\quad\text{where }\mu_{d}:=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1]^{d})}[\|X-Y\|].

(17)

Anderssen et al.
[
ABDM76
]
showed that:

\frac{\sqrt{d}}{3}\leq\mu_{d}\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{6}}.

(18)

Therefore:

n\cdot\frac{\sqrt{d}}{3}\leq\mathbb{E}[C_{n}]\leq n\cdot\sqrt{\frac{d}{6}}.

(19)

Changing any one point
x_{i}
in the tour affects at most two distances in the cost function. Since the maximum distance between any two points in
[0,1]^{d}
is
\sqrt{d}
, changing one point can affect the total cost by at most
2\sqrt{d}
. Applying McDiarmid’s inequality yields the following concentration bound:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|C_{n}-\mathbb{E}[C_{n}]\right|>\varepsilon\right)\leq 2e^{\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2dn}\right)}.

(20)

By plugging
\varepsilon=n\sqrt{d/6}
we get that the random tour is at most
(1+\sqrt{3/2})
times the expected with probability at least
1-2e^{-n/12}
.

As in the proof of the
Lemma

3.4
, we use the fact that the TSP tour has cost at least
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\beta_{d}\cdot n^{1-1/d}
with probability at least
1-2e^{-c^{\prime}\cdot d\cdot n}
. Therefore, the cost of a random tour over the points divided by the TSP tour cost is at most

\frac{(1+\sqrt{3/2})\cdot n\sqrt{\frac{d}{6}}}{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{d}{2\pi e}}\cdot n^{1-1/d}}=c\cdot n^{1/d}\leq c\cdot n^{1/\ell}=O(1)

.

with probability at least
1-(2e^{-n/12}+2e^{-c^{\prime}\cdot d\cdot n})
. The lemma follows.

∎

Now we are ready to prove
Theorem

3.2
.

3.3
An
O(\log^{2}n)
Algorithm for USOE TSP

As our algorithm for the TSP is very similar to the algorithm for online sorting, the proof of
Theorem

3.2
will focus on the key element that is needed in order for the algorithm to work: we show that the cost of the best block tour in every phase is good enough (as the geometry of the regions that correspond to buckets and the distribution over them changes, it is not trivial that block tours remain good).

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

Let
T_{b}
be the best tour that visits the points block by block and
\textsc{Opt}_{b}
be its cost. By
Lemma

3.4
we know that
\textsc{Opt}_{b}=O(1)\textsc{Opt}
with high probability. We start by showing that the optimal possible cost in every subarray
A_{i}
is at most
T_{b}
.

Claim 3.6
.

The cost of the TSP tour of points in each subarray
A_{i}

is at most
\textsc{Opt}_{b}
.

Proof.

Let
T_{i}
be the best tour of the points in subarray
A_{i}
that visits them bucket by bucket. The difference between
T_{i}
and
T_{b}
is that
T_{i}
has less points, as well as some of the blocks might be merged (in order to correspond to a smaller number of buckets). Additionally, the distribution of arrivals in the merged blocks is no longer uniform, as if the buckets of some corresponding blocks in the previous subarray are fully filled, there is greater probability to put a point in that bucket. Surprisingly, this will not affect us, as we will see.

Consider the blocks that correspond to a bucket in
A_{i}
. Let
S_{i}^{c}
be the best tour that visits the points of bucket
c
block by block (primitive block, not merged). In each block the distribution is uniform (by the algorithm) and the number of points in each one is at most
\log^{2}n
(as in total they are
\log^{2}n
). Therefore, by knowing that each block follows the uniform distribution and that they have at most
\log^{2}n
points, the cost of
S_{i}^{c}
is at most
T_{b}^{c}
, where
T_{b}^{c}
is the fraction of the cost of
T_{b}
that corresponds to bucket
c
. Additionally, the best subtour in the
\log^{2}n
points of the merged blocks has at most cost
S_{i}^{c}
. Tour
T_{i}
consists of the aforementioned sections which have at most cost
S_{i}^{c}
, which gives us that
T_{i}
has cost at most
\textsc{Opt}_{b}
(the cost of tour
T_{b}
).
∎

3.6
was the main part of the proof. The rest of the claims follow directly from previous lemmas.

Claim 3.7
.

The first overflow of phase
i
happens after the arrival of
\frac{n}{2^{i}}(1-1/\Theta(\sqrt{\log n}))
points with probability at least
1-\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}
. Additionally, after the arrival of
\frac{N}{2^{i}}(1+1/\Theta(\sqrt{\log n}))
points, all buckets become full with probability at least
1-\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}
.

Proof.

As our buckets have size
\Theta(\log^{2}n)
, they can collectively hold
n/2^{i}
points and the probability that a point belongs to each bucket is the same, then we get the result directly from
Lemma

2.1
.
∎

Claim 3.8
.

Let
k=\log\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}
. For each
i\in[k-1]
, conditioned on the event that
3.7
holds for every
A_{y}
for
y\in[i+1]
, every subarray
A_{j}
for
j\in[i]
becomes completely filled before any bucket in
A_{j+1}
overflows. Moreover,
3.7
holds for
A_{i}
for
i\in[k]
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}
.

Proof.

Directly from
Lemma

2.7
.
∎

Claim 3.9
.

Our algorithm does not fail with probability at least
1-1/n
.

Proof.

By
3.8
, when array
A_{k}
is filled, then all arrays
A_{i}
for
i\in[k-1]
are filled w.p. at least
1-1/n
. Therefore, the remaining
O(\log^{2}n)
are guaranteed to fit into subarray
B
, completing the proof.
∎

Finally, we find the cost incurred by decomposing it into the following components:

\bullet

Buckets:
Consider subarray
A_{i}
. Since we use Bertram’s algorithm
[
Ber25
]
for placement in each bucket, we incur total cost for the subarray:
\sum_{j=1}^{K_{i}}\sqrt{C_{i}}\cdot T_{i}=\Theta(\log n)\textsc{Opt}
and since we have

subarrays in total we incur total cost

.

\bullet

Between buckets inside a subarray:

3.4
and

, the total cost to "connect them" is at most

, as this cost is included in the cost of subarray

. Thus, in total for all subarrays:
O(\log n)\textsc{Opt}
.

\bullet

Between subarrays:
Between subarrays we incur cost at most
Opt
and since we have
k=O(\log n)
transitions this cost is
O(\log n)\textsc{Opt}

\bullet

Subarray
B
:
The elements are inserted in
B
using
[

]
thus we incur total cost

4
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the stochastic versions of the Online Sorting and Online TSP problems.
Together with the recent independent work of Hu
[
Hu25
]
, our results establish that
polylogarithmic competitiveness is achievable for stochastic online sorting under the uniform
distribution, leaving little room for improvement in this setting. In one dimension, both algorithms
naturally extend to general known distributions, but in higher dimensions such an extension is far
from obvious. A natural follow-up question is therefore whether our bounds can be extended to
general known distributions in the multidimensional case.

At the same time, the current model crucially relies on full knowledge of the distribution.
Relaxing this assumption raises several compelling directions for future work. What guarantees
can be obtained when points are drawn i.i.d. from an
unknown
distribution, or when they
arrive in uniformly random order without distributional assumptions? More broadly, it would be
interesting to explore whether our dynamic balls-into-bins perspective can be applied to other
online problems beyond sorting and TSP.
