Spatial posture perception method combined with ball-tip technique improves pedicle screw placement in adult degenerative scoliosis: a dual-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xin Xu, Linyang Song, Kun Wang, Fuxin Wang, Zheng Zhang, Ruoxian Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFreehand pedicle screw placement is challenging in adult degenerative scoliosis owing to vertebral rotation and pedicle deformity. We evaluated whether the SPPM+ball-tip workflow was associated with improved intraoperative performance and screw accuracy versus the freehand approach.MethodsIn this dual-center, retrospective study, 44 adults who underwent posterior thoracolumbar deformity correction (January 2018–January 2024) were assigned to SPPM+ball-tip (n = 22; 320 screws) or freehand (n = 22; 340 screws). The primary outcome was initial pedicle screw placement accuracy on early postoperative CT according to the Gertzbein–Robbins classification. Secondary outcomes included screw insertion time, intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures, blood loss, delayed screw-related mechanical complications, and radiographic correction at 1 week and 1 year.ResultsThe SPPM+ball-tip workflow was associated with higher accuracy than freehand (Grade A 90.6% [290/320] vs. 70.9% [241/340]; clinically acceptable Grades A–B 96.0% [307/320] vs. 87.1% [296/340]; poor C–E 4.0% vs. 12.9%; all P < 0.001). Mean insertion time was shorter (2.45 ± 1.02 vs. 5.12 ± 1.67 min/screw; P < 0.001), fluoroscopy exposures fewer (4.1 ± 2.2 vs. 11.1 ± 3.3; P < 0.001), and blood loss lower (280.7 ± 102.3 vs. 450.4 ± 118.7 mL; P < 0.001). Screw cut-out occurred in 0 vs. 5 cases (P = 0.044). Cobb angle correction at 1 week and 1 year was comparable between groups (both P > 0.05).ConclusionThe SPPM+ball-tip workflow was associated with higher pedicle screw accuracy and greater operative efficiency, while showing lower radiation exposure and lower intraoperative blood loss, without compromising deformity correction. These findings support the SPPM+ball-tip workflow as a potentially practical alternative to freehand placement. However, because the intervention was evaluated as a combined workflow rather than as isolated components, the independent contribution of SPPM itself cannot be determined from the present study. Introduction

Adult degenerative scoliosis (ADS) is a common spinal condition primarily affecting the elderly, characterized by a progressive, three-dimensional deformity involving the sagittal, coronal, and axial planes. For patients exhibiting clinical symptoms, surgical intervention remains the mainstay of treatment [1]. Osteotomy and pedicle screw fixation is the predominant surgical method employed for spinal deformity correction [2, 3].

The accuracy rate of traditional freehand pedicle screw placement in the thoracolumbar region is approximately 86.2%. However, in degenerative scoliosis, due to vertebral rotation, dislocation, and pedicle dysplasia, the difficulty and risk of misplacement increase significantly. Consequently, the conventional technique is challenged by limited precision, high dependence on intraoperative fluoroscopy, and elevated complication rates [4, 5].

To mitigate these risks, navigation systems and robotic assistance have been increasingly utilized. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis compared robot-assisted, navigation-based, and conventional freehand pedicle screw placement in scoliosis surgery, reporting that robot-assisted techniques had significantly greater accuracy but longer operative times [6]. Similarly, other meta-analyses demonstrated that robotic assistance improves accuracy and reduces radiation exposure compared with freehand methods [6, 7]. Nonetheless, these advanced techniques have limitations. Steep learning curves, registration errors, prolonged setup and registration times, intraoperative workflow disruption, and high costs remain major obstacles to widespread clinical use. Moreover, although robotic or navigated approaches improve screw accuracy, their benefits on patient-centered outcomes such as pain relief, function, and complication reduction are less consistent [6, 7]. Performance is also affected by curve severity, vertebral rotation, pedicle size, and bone quality. Thus, despite promising results, there is still an unmet need for methods that combine high accuracy, low complication risk, adaptability to severe anatomical variation, and minimal dependence on costly infrastructure.

Gaines introduced the “funnel technique” for pedicle screw placement, which evolved into the ball-tip technique. Utilizing the elastic properties of the ball-tip probe allows navigation through the cancellous bone and helps determine the optimal trajectory, thereby increasing insertion accuracy. Despite its efficacy, this method has limitations in patients with severe osteoporosis or significantly deformed pedicles [8].

Although robotic navigation and augmented reality have dramatically enhanced accuracy, these technologies are not widely adopted due to the complexity of preoperative planning, high cost of acquisition, maintenance, and operation [9, 10]. To further improve screw placement accuracy and safety using a simpler and more efficient technique, our clinical observations revealed significant differences in outcomes between surgeons with varying levels of experience under identical equipment conditions. Senior surgeons were able to place screws rapidly and accurately with minimal fluoroscopic assistance based on pre- and intraoperative spatial understanding.

Drawing from these insights, we developed the Spatial Posture Perception Method (SPPM), a composite intraoperative strategy grounded in cross-sectional anatomy, digital imaging, surgical positioning, and surgeon’s spatial orientation. It forms a three-dimensional mental model for real-time spatial perception, enabling the surgeon to intuitively guide pedicle trajectory. This study evaluated the clinical performance of the SPPM+ball-tip workflow for pedicle screw placement in adult degenerative scoliosis, compared with conventional freehand insertion. Because the intervention was assessed as a combined workflow rather than as isolated components, the present study was designed to examine the performance of the integrated technique rather than the independent effect of SPPM alone.

Methods

General information

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 44 patients diagnosed with adult degenerative scoliosis who underwent posterior thoracolumbar scoliosis correction and instrumentation between January 2018 and January 2024 at two tertiary spine centers. All patients had complete clinical and radiographic data available for analysis.

Based on the surgical screw placement technique used, patients were divided into two groups: 22 patients (320 screws) in the experimental group treated with the SPPM+ball-tip workflow, and 22 patients (340 screws) in the control group treated with the traditional freehand technique. There were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05), confirming the comparability of cohorts.

Surgical team and experience

All procedures were performed by the same senior spine surgeon with more than 15 years of surgical experience, assisted by a consistent surgical team. The lead surgeon was proficient in both freehand and ball-tip pedicle screw placement techniques, thereby minimizing bias related to surgical expertise.

Surgical procedure

Experimental group (SPPM+ball-tip workflow)

SPPM was structured as a four-step protocol that integrates preoperative angular data with intraoperative posture and tactile feedback to guide pedicle access in ADS, where rotation and pedicle asymmetry are common.

Step 1 – Preoperative angular quantification. For each level, the medial–lateral (ML) and cranial–caudal (CC) angles derived from CT were tabulated on a level-by-level worksheet. A level-specific angle worksheet was completed for each instrumented level (see Supplementary Material S1). Facet-based entry landmarks (Weinstein/Roy-Camille/Krag) were noted, with adjustments anticipated for rotated segments and concave/convex differences.

Before instrumentation, the team documented table tilt, patient rotation (shoulder/pelvis symmetry), and segmental lordosis/kyphosis after positioning and temporary rod leveling. The preoperative ML/CC angles were translated to the intraoperative coordinate frame using the patient’s current posture (e.g., if the segment was lordosed by ~10°, the CC working angle was adjusted accordingly). Step 4 – Tactile-guided advancement with ball-tip. After cortical opening at the planned entry point, an elastic ball-tip probe (≈2.0–2.5 mm) was advanced along cancellous bone with minimal hammering. Five-cortex palpation (medial, lateral, superior, inferior, anterior) was performed at 15–20 mm to verify containment. Acceptance cues included uniform cancellous resistance and elastic “spring-back” of the ball-tip; deviation cues included a hard stop (suspected cortical contact), skiving, or sudden “give.” Deviation recognition and management followed a predefined algorithm (see Supplementary Material S3).Any deviation prompted immediate re-centering within the modeled corridor, with redirection before tapping. For very narrow pedicles, progressive dilation with a small tap was performed after secure cancellous trajectory confirmation. Final tapping and screw insertion followed standard practice. Radiation-sparing workflow

The SPPM-guided workflow encourages a “single-confirmation” strategy: one AP check to verify entry alignment and one lateral check to confirm depth/CC vector during the early adoption phase. Fluoroscopy checkpoints for this phase are summarized in Supplementary Material S3; experienced users may rely on a single plane unless cues suggest otherwise.

Control group (freehand technique)

Screw placement was performed using conventional anatomical landmarks without adjunctive guidance. Entry points were selected based on vertebral level: at thoracic segments, the junction of the superior articular facet and transverse process; at lumbar levels, the lateral border of the superior articular facet or the pars interarticularis. Trajectory angulation was estimated from preoperative CT or radiographs and transposed intraoperatively relative to the lamina and spinous process. A gearshift probe was advanced through the pedicle under tactile control. Any abnormal resistance suggested cortical breach and prompted redirection. The integrity of the pedicle walls was confirmed by palpation with a ball-tip feeler. Intraoperative fluoroscopy was intermittently applied to verify trajectory, especially in rotated or narrow pedicles. After satisfactory probing and tapping, screws were inserted along the prepared channel, and final fluoroscopy was used to confirm position.

Observation Indicators

To evaluate the clinical performance of the SPPM+ball-tip workflow, the following indicators were compared between the two groups:

Accuracy of pedicle screw placement (screw-level outcome)

Early postoperative three-dimensional computed tomography (CT) of the thoracolumbar spine was performed within 1 week after surgery to evaluate initial pedicle screw positioning. The Gertzbein–Robbins classification system (Grades A–E) [11] was used as follows: Grade A, screw fully contained within the pedicle; Grade B, cortical breach < 2 mm; Grade C, breach < 4 mm; Grade D, breach < 6 mm; and Grade E, breach > 6 mm. Grades A and B were considered clinically acceptable, whereas Grades C–E were defined as poor insertions. Postoperative CT images were independently reviewed by two observers using predefined Gertzbein–Robbins criteria. Interobserver reliability analysis is provided in Supplementary Table S5. The 1-year follow-up imaging assessment was used to evaluate maintenance of deformity correction and delayed screw-related mechanical events, rather than initial screw placement accuracy.

Screw placement time (screw-level outcome)

The time required to complete placement of one pedicle screw, measured from the first breach/opening of the cortical bone at the planned entry point using a pedicle entry awl/pedicle opener to final seating of the screw at the intended depth with the screwdriver disengaged. This interval included any probing, redirection, and tapping performed for that screw, and the same start/end landmarks were applied in both the SPPM+ball-tip and freehand groups. Timing was recorded using a stopwatch by an independent operating-room staff member.

Fluoroscopy frequency (procedure-level outcome)

Number of intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy events recorded from start to end of surgery.

Intraoperative blood loss (procedure-level outcome)

The total amount of intraoperative blood loss was calculated by summing the volumes collected in the suction canister and the blood absorbed by surgical gauze.

Screw-related complications (patient-level outcome)

Delayed screw-related mechanical events were recorded at the patient level and included postoperative screw cut-out/back-out events identified during follow-up. These events were analyzed separately from early postoperative CT-based screw placement accuracy.

Correction outcome (patient-level outcome)

Measured using the Cobb angle of the main curve and correction rate, both one week and one year postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables conforming to a normal or approximately normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Intergroup comparisons of continuous data were performed using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on data distribution. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Outcomes were analyzed according to their measurement level. Screw-level outcomes included pedicle screw accuracy and screw insertion time. Patient-/procedure-level outcomes included fluoroscopy exposures, intraoperative blood loss, screw-related complications, and radiographic correction outcomes. To account for clustering of multiple screws within the same patient, we additionally fitted a mixed-effects logistic regression model (generalized linear mixed model) with a random intercept for patient. Clinically acceptable screw placement was defined as Gertzbein–Robbins grades A–B (coded as 1) versus grades C–E (coded as 0). Surgical technique (SPPM+ball-tip vs. freehand) was included as a fixed effect. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The variance (and standard deviation) of the patient-level random intercept was reported to quantify between-patient heterogeneity. Because all surgeries were performed by a single senior surgeon, a surgeon-level random effect was not included.

Fluoroscopy frequency

The experimental group exhibited a significantly lower mean number of intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures per procedure (4.1 ± 2.2) compared to the control group (11.1 ± 3.3) (P < 0.001, Table 4; Fig. 6).

Intraoperative blood loss

The experimental group demonstrated a significantly lower mean intraoperative blood loss per procedure (280.7 ± 102.3 mL) compared to the control group (450.4 ± 118.7 mL) (P < 0.001, Table 4; Fig. 6).

Delayed screw-related mechanical complications

At the patient level, five cases of delayed screw cut-out/back-out were observed in the control group, whereas no such events occurred in the experimental group (P = 0.044, Table 4; Fig. 6). These events were treated as postoperative mechanical fixation-related complications rather than as evidence of initial screw misplacement. Given the small number of events, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Clinically, symptom severity among these five cases ranged from none (incidental radiographic finding) to focal mechanical back pain or mild radicular-like discomfort; no new postoperative neurologic deficit was identified in any case. Four patients were managed conservatively (observation, bracing/activity restriction, and analgesics/NSAIDs with or without a short course of neuropathic agents, with close radiographic follow-up). One patient with early postoperative back-out and significant focal pain underwent revision on postoperative day 5 (screw replacement with augmentation/longer screw), with symptom relief and stable fixation at the last follow-up. Case-level details of these five events are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Radiographic correction

Radiographic correction outcomes were analyzed at the patient level. There was no statistically significant difference in deformity correction outcomes between the two groups. The one-week postoperative Cobb angles were 10.3 ± 3.1° for the experimental group and 11.2 ± 3.3° for the control group, while the one-year postoperative Cobb angles measured 10.9 ± 3.7° and 11.8 ± 3.9°, respectively. Similarly, the correction rates at one week and one year showed no significant variation between the groups, recorded as 66.3 ± 8.5% versus 64.8 ± 9.0%, and 65.9 ± 8.7% for both groups, respectively (P > 0.05, Table 4; Figs. 7 and 8).

Discussion

Analysis of findings

In degenerative scoliosis correction surgery, vertebral rotation, facet joint degeneration, spinal canal stenosis, and three-dimensional deformities substantially increase the difficulty of pedicle screw placement [15]. Multiple structural interferences during surgery can lead to trajectory errors and elevate the risk of neurovascular injury [16]. Previous studies have reported that complex pedicle morphologies—such as Watanabe type D—significantly impair the judgment of screw trajectory and contribute to high error rates in freehand techniques [17].

In the present study, the SPPM+ball-tip workflow was associated with significantly higher pedicle screw placement accuracy than the conventional freehand method. The clinically acceptable rate (96.0%) was comparable to that reported by He et al. for the ball-tip technique alone (96.7%) [18]. However, our cohort focused specifically on degenerative scoliosis, a population with severe vertebral rotation, asymmetric pedicle anatomy, and reduced bone quality. Achieving similar accuracy under these more demanding conditions suggests that the integrated workflow may perform favorably in anatomically challenging settings; however, reproducibility across surgeons cannot be determined from the present single-surgeon design.

In addition to accuracy, the SPPM+ball-tip workflow was associated with shorter operative time and lower intraoperative fluoroscopy frequency relative to freehand placement. These improvements are likely attributable to the surgeon’s ability to construct a real-time three-dimensional mental model of the pedicle trajectory, thereby decreasing the need for repeated probe adjustments and imaging confirmation. Reduced operative time and radiation exposure not only improve intraoperative efficiency but also enhance safety for both patients and the surgical team.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the SPPM+ball-tip workflow may help maintain high accuracy in anatomically complex cases while simultaneously improving procedural efficiency, which represents a clinically meaningful advantage over conventional freehand placement. We also observed lower intraoperative blood loss in the SPPM+ball-tip group. However, blood loss is a multifactorial procedural outcome and may be influenced by operative duration, number of instrumented levels, deformity severity, and overall surgical complexity. Therefore, although the between-group difference may partly reflect greater procedural efficiency, it should not be interpreted as being solely attributable to the screw placement workflow itself.

Comparative analysis of screw insertion techniques

Freehand screw placement does not require specialized equipment and remains widely used in primary-level hospitals. However, its accuracy is highly dependent on surgeon experience and is associated with longer operative time, higher blood loss, and greater fluoroscopy exposure [2, 7, 19]. Previous reports indicated misplacement rates of 10–20% with freehand techniques, particularly in patients with scoliosis or rotational deformity [16, 17].

The ball-tip technique, introduced as a modification of the funnel method, improves tactile feedback and allows the probe to deflect along cancellous bone, thereby identifying a safer path. Studies such as Watanabe et al. [20], Zhou et al. [21], and He et al. [18] have supported the feasibility and accuracy advantages of ball-tip-assisted screw placement over conventional freehand techniques in different spinal fixation scenarios, with reported accuracy rates of around 90–97%. However, this approach still faces challenges in cases of severe osteoporosis, extremely narrow pedicles, or high apical vertebral rotation, where the probe may deflect excessively or fail to advance.

Advanced technologies such as computer-assisted navigation and robotic systems have further improved accuracy. Zhang et al. and Fan et al. [6, 7] demonstrated higher accuracy rates (94–96%) compared with freehand, accompanied by reduced intraoperative fluoroscopy. Nonetheless, these systems require expensive equipment, prolonged setup, and specialized training. Their utility may be limited in low-resource settings or when intraoperative registration errors occur. Furthermore, several meta-analyses suggest that while navigation and robotic assistance improve screw accuracy, improvements in clinical outcomes such as pain relief or functional recovery are less consistent [6, 7].

Against this background, the SPPM combined with the ball-tip technique offers an attractive compromise. SPPM+ball-tip differs from ball-tip alone by creating a cognitive-navigation loop that links preoperative information to real-time tactile guidance: (1) quantifying level-specific medial–lateral and cranial–caudal angles from CT, (2) integrating those angles into the intraoperative coordinate frame after positioning, (3) modeling a 3-D pedicle corridor relative to facet-based landmarks while anticipating vertebral rotation, and (4) applying tactile-guided advancement with five-cortex palpation. Prior literature notes that experienced surgeons may achieve high accuracy even without advanced equipment because of well-developed spatial perception skills; the SPPM framework may be viewed as an attempt to codify this expertise into a more structured workflow [7, 16]. Conceptually, this differs from imaging-based navigation or robotics, which rely on external systems to display or dictate trajectories; SPPM instead emphasizes perception–integration–action within the surgeon’s mental model (i.e., “cognitive navigation”), helping to reduce probe redirections and reliance on repeated fluoroscopy—consistent with our efficiency and radiation findings. However, the present study compared the combined SPPM+ball-tip workflow with conventional freehand placement rather than isolating SPPM as a standalone intervention. Therefore, the observed between-group differences cannot be attributed to SPPM alone and may instead reflect the combined effects of the cognitive workflow, the ball-tip instrumentation, and surgeon familiarity with the integrated technique.

Thus, the SPPM framework may be viewed as an attempt to codify expert spatial reasoning into a more structured workflow. However, whether this framework independently improves reproducibility, reduces surgeon dependence, or facilitates teaching across surgeons cannot be determined from the present single-surgeon study. This perspective addresses concerns that SPPM is “not new” by framing it as a systematic integration of perception and tactile guidance into a more structured workflow, rather than as a separately validated technology. A posture-awareness surgical tool has likewise shown feasibility and clinical benefit in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, supporting the general principle that posture-informed workflows can enhance screw placement [22].

Technical considerations and proposed implementation framework

Operational rules

(1) Map planned ML/CC angles to the intraoperative posture (tolerance ~ ± 5°; assistant read-back). (2) Use a fixed five-cortex sequence at ~ 15–20 mm (medial→lateral→superior→inferior→anterior) and advance only after two successive containment-positive cycles. (3) Treat hard stop/skiving/sudden give as stop-signals: halt → re-center within the modeled corridor → re-advance in smaller steps; abandon the tract if deviation recurs. (4) For Watanabe C/D or marked apical rotation, confirm a cancellous path, dilate with a smaller tap, bias slightly medial, or switch to extrapedicular/cortical trajectories. (5) During early adoption, obtain one AP plus one lateral check; record fluoroscopy exposures per screw.

Proposed training pathway

Module 1: angle worksheets, facet mapping, supervised CT measurements (κ/ICC on a subset). Module 2: bench-top simulation (goniometer-guided drills; scripted deviation-and-correction tasks). Module 3: OR proctoring with a five-item SPPM checklist and read-back protocol, then taper support. Module 4 (optional): VR/AR angle–posture calibration.

Potential implementation metrics

An OSATS-style checklist (0–2 per item; target ≥ 18/20 on two consecutive cases) may be used to support structured implementation; a proposed checklist is provided in Supplementary Material S2. Potential monitoring targets include an A–B rate ≥ 95%, fluoroscopy ≤ 5 per screw, and insertion time, with learning patterns explored using CUSUM or segmented regression where appropriate.

Fidelity & note

Templates for angle planning, intraoperative fidelity, and deviation management are provided in Supplementary Materials S1–S3. These materials are intended to make tacit spatial steps more explicit and potentially easier to standardize. However, the present study did not formally validate teachability, reproducibility, inter-surgeon generalizability, or learning-curve shortening across multiple surgeons.

Limitations and future directions

This study has limitations. It is a dual-center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample, and all procedures were performed by a single senior surgeon, which limits external generalizability and precludes assessment of inter-surgeon reproducibility or surgeon-level variance components, as well as a formal learning-curve evaluation. The supplementary angle worksheet, OSATS-style checklist, and deviation-management algorithm should therefore be interpreted as proposed implementation tools rather than validation evidence. Allocation to technique was nonrandom, so residual confounding cannot be excluded. In particular, the retrospective, nonrandomized, single-surgeon design remains vulnerable to differences in case mix, deformity severity, pedicle morphology, side-specific anatomy, apical versus non-apical level characteristics, bone quality, and temporal evolution of surgical practice, all of which may have influenced the observed between-group differences. Although a mixed-effects model was used to account for within-patient clustering of screws for the primary accuracy endpoint, this approach does not eliminate residual confounding inherent to the study design. In addition, because the intervention evaluated in this study was the combined SPPM+ball-tip workflow rather than SPPM in isolation, the independent contribution of SPPM itself cannot be determined from the present data. Furthermore, intraoperative blood loss is influenced by multiple factors, including instrumented levels, operative complexity, and deformity severity. Because we did not adjust for these potential confounders, the observed lower blood loss in the SPPM+ball-tip group should be interpreted cautiously and should not be attributed solely to the screw placement technique. Moreover, pedicles were analyzed en bloc without prespecified subgroup stratification according to thoracic versus lumbar level, concave versus convex side, apical versus non-apical level, pedicle morphology (e.g., Watanabe type), or bone quality, all of which may influence screw placement difficulty and risk. Initial screw placement accuracy was evaluated on early postoperative CT, whereas 1-year follow-up imaging was used to assess maintenance of deformity correction and delayed screw-related mechanical events. Accordingly, delayed screw cut-out/back-out events should be interpreted as postoperative mechanical complications rather than direct indicators of initial placement accuracy. Nevertheless, radiographic grading remains a surrogate outcome, and long-term endpoints such as fusion status, hardware loosening, neurologic outcomes, and patient-reported outcome measures were not comprehensively captured. Finally, we did not perform a head-to-head comparison with navigation or robotic systems, which are contemporary benchmarks in complex deformity correction [6, 7].

Future work should include prospective multicenter cohorts or randomized/matched trials with prespecified subgroup analyses, incorporation of PROMs and neurologic outcomes, longer follow-up for structural endpoints, and objective evaluation of learning curves and implementation fidelity. Multi-surgeon validation studies are also needed to quantify between-surgeon variability and better define the reproducibility and practical role of the SPPM+ball-tip workflow relative to navigation or robotic assistance.

Conclusion

The SPPM+ball-tip workflow was associated with higher pedicle screw accuracy and greater intraoperative efficiency, while showing lower fluoroscopy use and lower intraoperative blood loss in this cohort, with radiographic correction comparable to freehand placement. As an integrated, device-light workflow, it may represent a practical alternative where advanced guidance systems are unavailable or impractical. However, because the intervention was evaluated as a combined workflow rather than as isolated components, the independent contribution of SPPM itself cannot be determined from the present study. Prospective multicenter validation—ideally including patient-centered outcomes, prespecified subgroup analyses, and cost-effectiveness—will help define its role in contemporary deformity correction.
