FAM30A Induces Inflammation and Oxidative Damage in PDLSCs by Targeting miR-424-5p
Authors: Wang Zhi, Lina Huang, Jianan Kang, Zhengyi Chu, Minsong Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the diagnostic value and regulatory role of FAM30A in patients with chronic periodontitis (CP).MethodsThe study included 104 CP patients. Saliva and periodontal ligament tissue samples were stored at -80°C. Logistic regression predicted risk factors for CP. Periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS to establish an in vitro model. RT-qPCR assessed gene expression. CCK-8 and flow cytometry evaluated cell proliferation and apoptosis. ELISA measured inflammatory cytokine expression, while kits quantified oxidative stress products. Dual luciferase reporter assays and RNA immunoprecipitation examined gene-target relationships.ResultsFAM30A was upregulated in saliva and periodontal ligament tissue from CP patients. FAM30A demonstrated diagnostic value in both sample types and was identified as a risk factor for CP. LPS stimulation increased FAM30A expression in cells. si-FAM30A promoted cell proliferation, reduced apoptosis, and suppressed inflammatory and oxidative stress levels. miR-424-5p was downregulated in CP patients. Furthermore, FAM30A competitively binds miR-424-5p to modulate PDLSCs' function. Transfection with miR inhibitor counteracts si- FAM30A-induced cellular dysfunction and inflammatory responses.ConclusionFAM30A induces CP by targeting miR-424-5p to inhibit PDLSCs proliferation, promote apoptosis, and increase inflammatory and oxidative stress levels.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic disease that diminishes quality of life. Chronic periodontitis (CP) typically progresses slowly in its early stages,1 often leading to neglect. Surveys indicate that 5% to 20% of periodontitis sufferers are adults, though many exhibit symptoms during adolescence.2 CP is predominantly caused by microbial infection,3 which can compromise the integrity of the bone and connective tissue surrounding the teeth.4 Additionally, poor oral hygiene habits, smoking,5 a history of diabetes,6 and bone loss7 may accelerate disease progression. In advanced stages, patients commonly experience gum bleeding and tooth loosening, noticeably impairing masticatory function and overall health. Radiographic parameters are one of the key indicators for detecting periodontal progression.8 However, other clinical periodontal examinations are also indispensable. Periodontal examinations9 and microbiological tests10,11 are also considered important reference indicators for identifying CP. However, dynamic biomarkers in blood or other bodily fluid samples have garnered attention due to their advantages of being readily accessible and providing timely responses.12 For instance, studies have revealed correlations between CP and the expression of oxidative stress products,13 superoxide dismutase, and bone morphogenetic factors14 in saliva. Nevertheless, few oral fluid biomarkers currently predict CP progression. Consequently, advancing CP diagnosis, and precision treatment must be prioritized.

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) participate in regulating multiple signalling pathways within periodontal cells,15 and their abnormal expression is crucial in inflammatory diseases.16 Research has revealed that dysregulation of various long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) correlates with the pathogenesis of CP.17 Fausto et al.18 reported that lncRNA OIP5-AS1 is downregulated in periodontitis patients, demonstrating significant predictive value for disease progression. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that silencing lncR-APDC alters the proportion of immune cells within periodontal tissues.19 Proteomics analysis identified 11 lncRNAs upregulated in CP patients, including FAM30A (also known as KIAA0125).20 Subsequently, Lin et al.21 also detected increased FAM30A expression in periodontitis patient datasets. Soudeh et al.22 demonstrated FAM30A dysregulation in ameloblastoma, positioning it as a regulator of odontogenic diseases. Additionally, studies indicate that FAM30A expression levels correlate positively with plasma cells in CP patients.23 It also participates in B-cell regulation in lupus nephritis patients through a competitive endogenous RNA mechanism in the IFN-λ pathway.24 Second, elevated FAM30A expression serves as an unfavourable prognostic factor for survival in patients with myelodysplastic syndromes.25 It also functions as a pivotal gene involved in bone destruction/resorption within osteoarthritis.26 These findings align closely with the core pathological mechanisms of CP.27,28 However, to date, studies investigating the relationship between FAM30A and oral diseases remain scarce, and the underlying molecular mechanisms have not been thoroughly elucidated. Beyond lncRNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs) are increasingly recognized for their roles in inflammatory diseases.29 Bioinformatics analysis indicates that miR-424-5p may be one of the core miRNAs regulating osteogenesis in periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs).30 Moreover, it possesses a targeted binding site for FAM30A. Xia et al.31 detected that degradation of mesenchymal stem cells leads to miR-424-5p downregulation. Talha et al.32 reported that miR-424-5p inhibitors exacerbated inflammatory responses in endometrial epithelial cells. However, the mechanisms by which FAM30A and miR-424-5p participate in CP regulation remain unclear.

Based on the above information, we collected baseline data and clinical characteristics from 189 volunteers to gain further insights into the periodontal status of CP patients. Through RT-qPCR detection and in vitro cell experiments, we preliminarily revealed the diagnostic value and regulatory role of lncRNA in CP. This adds new directions for deepening the understanding of CP pathogenesis and developing potential therapeutic targets in subsequent studies.

Materials and methods

Patient inclusion

Using probing depth (PD) as the primary endpoint,33 the sample size was assessed using PASS software. A 2-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 and test power (1-β) of 0.8 were set. Based on departmental clinical data and previous reports,34 the between-group PD difference δ was set at 0.6 mm, with a pooled standard deviation σ = 1.0 mm. After accounting for a 10% dropout rate, each group ultimately required 92 participants. However, the actual study enrolled 104 CP patients and 85 orthodontic patients as the control group. All participants were patients treated at the dental department of Harbin Stomatological Hospital between May 2022 and April 2023. One mL of saliva was collected from all participants. Periodontal ligament tissue was obtained from healthy tooth roots extracted for orthodontic purposes or from diseased teeth of CP patients. This study was conducted with approval from the Ethics Committee of Harbin Stomatological Hospital. All patients or their guardians provided written informed consent. Healthy subjects exhibited periodontal probing depths (PD), bleeding indices (BI), and plaque indices (PLI) within normal ranges. Additionally, subjects had undergone no dental treatment within the preceding three months. Participants retained ≥20 teeth intraorally, including at least four molars. However, individuals with defective restorations, occlusal trauma, systemic immune disorders, cardiovascular disease, or long-term corticosteroid use were excluded.

For clinical indicators such as PD, BI, and PLI, we measured them as follows: (1) For PD: Using a Florida electronic pressure-sensitive probe (20g force), we measured the distance from the gingival margin to the pocket apex at six sites (buccal/lingual mesial, central, and distal) on each tooth throughout the entire mouth.35 For BI: Observed for bleeding at the site 30 s after probing completion.36 BI was only assessed in CP patients, as control subjects had clinically healthy periodontium with no bleeding on probing. For PLI: Assessed plaque thickness in the gingival margin area by sweeping the probe over the tooth surface and graded according to the Silness & Löe index.37 All periodontal clinical examinations were performed by 2 experienced dentists (10+ years of practice). Subsequently, intra- and inter-examiner reliability calibration was conducted before the formal experiment. Ten CP patients not included in this study were randomly selected. Two examiners independently measured PD, BI, and PLI at six sites per tooth for all teeth. One week later, one primary examiner repeated measurements on the same patients to assess intra-examiner reliability (ICC). Reliability of measurements was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients and Kappa values. For PD: Inter-rater ICC was 0.92, and intra-rater ICC was 0.95. For BI and PLI: Weighted Kappa values for inter-rater agreement were 0.85 and 0.88, respectively; weighted Kappa values for intra-rater agreement were 0.90 and 0.92, respectively.

Cell culture and processing

Human periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs, SNP-H324) were procured from Wuhan Sunncell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Cells were cultured in the corresponding human periodontal ligament stem cell complete medium (SNPM-H324, Sunncell, Wuhan), supplemented with adequate serum, dual antibodies, glutamine, folic acid, and other components. Cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2. PDLSCs were induced for 12 hours using 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide from E. coli O55:B5 (LPS, HY-D1056, MCE, U.S.) to establish an in vitro cellular model.

Cell transfection

Transfection was performed using Liposomal 2000 Transfection Reagent (40802, YEASEN, Shanghai). Cells at 70% confluence in a 24-well plate were selected for transfection. Prepare a fresh centrifuge tube. Add 0.5 μg of oligonucleotide (si-NC, si-FAM30A, inhibitor NC, miR inhibitor) to 50 μL of basal medium for mixing. In a separate centrifuge tube, dilute 2 μL of nucleic acid transfection reagent with 50 μL of basal medium. Combine the diluted transfection reagent with the diluted oligonucleotide solution and incubate at 25°C for 20 minutes. Add 100 μL of the transfection reagent mixture to each well. Place the 24-well plate in a CO2 incubator and incubate for 24 hours.

RT-qPCR

Frozen tissue was thawed at -80°C and homogenized using a Biopple high-speed cryogenic tissue grinder (-30°C), then placed in an ice bath. RNA was extracted from saliva, periodontal ligament tissue, and cells using the TRIzol Plus RNA Purification System (12183555, Thermo Fisher, USA). RNA quality was assessed using a Qubit Fluorometer. Reverse transcription of LncRNA was performed using HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT Super Mix for qPCR (R433, Vazyme, Nanjing). GAPDH served as an internal control, and LncRNA expression quantification was analysed via the SYBR Green One-Step qPCR Kit (D7268S, Beyotime, Shanghai). miRNA quantification was performed using the miRNA Stem-Loop Reverse Transcription Kit (abs60269, Absin, Shanghai) and SYBR Green miRNA Fluorescent Quantitative PCR Kit (AP282, YaJi, Shanghai), with U6 as the reference gene. Experimental data were recorded using the Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch real-time quantitative PCR instrument and calculated according to the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The primer sequences involved in the quantitative reaction are as follows: FAM30A, forward primer (F), 5′-TGGCAAAGGCAAGTGAC-3′, reverse primer (R), 5′-GGCAGAAGGATGAACCC-3′. GAPDH, F, 5′-TGACAACTTTGGTATCGTGGAAGG-3′, R, 5′-AGGCAGGGATGATGTTCTGGAGAG-3′. miR-424-5p, F, 5′-GCCAGCAGCAATTCATGT-3′, R, 5′-TATGGTTTTGACGACTGTGTGAT-3′. U6, 5′-ATTGGAACGATACAGAGAAGATT-3′, 5′-GGAACGCTTCACGAATTTG-3′.

CCK8

The effect of transfection on cell proliferation was assessed using the Enhanced Cell Counting Kit 8 (E-CK-A362, Elabscience, Wuhan). A 100μL aliquot (2 × 103 cells) of the transfected cell suspension was seeded into a 96-well plate. Blank wells contained only an equal volume of culture medium without cells. Subsequent procedures were performed once cells had adhered and reached 70% confluence. Add 10 μL of CCK8 solution to each well, mix thoroughly, and incubate in a culture incubator for 4 hours. Measure the absorbance of the solution at 450 nm using a multi-mode microplate reader.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-FITC/PI dual staining kit (BL107B, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Cells were gently washed with PBS, then detached using a cell scraper. The pellet was collected by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 5 minutes. Cells were then resuspended in 500 μL Binding Buffer to a concentration of approximately 1 × 106 cells/mL. A 100 μL aliquot of the cell suspension was added to an opaque microplate well, with an equal volume of Binding Buffer added to the blank well. 5μL Annexin V-FITC solution was added to the aforementioned wells and mixed thoroughly, followed by the addition of an equal volume of Propidium Iodide reagent. In single-stain tubes, add only one corresponding staining reagent. Incubate the microplate at room temperature in the dark for 10 minutes. Before analysis, filter the cell suspension through a 200-micron mesh filter and mix thoroughly. Finally, collect apoptotic signals using a BriCyte E6 flow cytometer. The excitation wavelength is 488 nm, and the emission wavelength is 530 nm.

ELISA

The cellular inflammatory cytokine kits were procured from MULTI SCIENCES Biotech. These comprised the Human TNF-α ELISA Kit (EK182), Human IL-1β ELISA Kit (EK101B), and Human IL-10 ELISA Kit (EK110). The specific procedure is as follows: Add 300 μL washing buffer to the microplate wells and allow them to soak for 30 seconds. Discard the liquid from the wells. Add 100 μL cell culture medium to the blank wells, 10-fold serial dilutions of the standard to the standard wells, and cell supernatant to the sample wells. Subsequently, add 50μL antibody detection working solution to the aforementioned wells. Block the microplate, place it on a shaker at 100 rpm/minutes, and incubate for 2 hours. Discard the liquid, add 300 μL wash buffer, wash the plate 6 times, and tap dry. Add 100 μL streptavidin working solution to the wells, incubate under the same conditions for 45 minutes, discard the liquid, and wash the plate 6 times. Add 100 μL of color development substrate to the wells and incubate in the dark for 20 minutes. Directly add an equal volume of stop solution to the wells and gently mix. Detect the absorbance values of the solutions using a microplate reader at dual wavelengths of 450 nm and 630 nm. The final result is the concentration of the inflammatory cytokine corresponding to the difference between the dual-wavelength absorbance values.

Oxidative stress assay

Malondialdehyde (MDA) in cells was measured using a lipid oxidation assay kit (S0131S, Beyotime, Shanghai). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assessed via a total SOD activity assay kit (S0101S, Beyotime, Shanghai). Catalase (CAT) activity was assessed using the CAT activity assay kit (BC0205) purchased from Solarbio. Transfected cells were washed with pre-chilled PBS, lysed with an appropriate volume of lysis buffer, and then centrifuged at 11,800 rpm for 5 minutes. The supernatant was collected for subsequent experiments. Samples and reagents were added according to the respective kit manufacturers' protocols. MDA detection employed excitation light at 532 nm. SOD detection utilized excitation light at 450 nm with 650 nm as the reference wavelength. CAT detection employed excitation light at 520 nm.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

The dual luciferase reporter assay kit (BRK0044, ABclonal, Wuhan) was employed to detect gene targeting relationships. Transfected cells were harvested, old medium removed, and cells washed twice with PBS. Add 30 μL of 1 × Passive Luciferase Lysis Buffer to a 96-well plate and vortex for 10 minutes. Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm for 3 minutes and collect the supernatant. Transfer 20 μL of cell lysis supernatant to a detection tube and add 100 μL of Firefly Luciferase Working Solution. After thorough mixing, measure the luminescence intensity of the firefly luciferase using a multifunctional microplate reader. Subsequently, add 100 μL of sea squirt luciferase reagent to the same system and measure the luminescence intensity of the solution again. The final result is normalized based on the luminescence intensity of the sea squirt luciferase.

RNA immunoprecipitation

Target proteins were enriched using the Classic Magnetic Protein A/G IP/Co-IP Kit (YJ201, Epizyme, Shanghai). Cells were washed with PBS and dislodged using a cell scraper. Collect into a centrifuge tube, add 1 mL lysis buffer (containing protease inhibitor, GRF101), and incubate on ice for 20 minutes. Centrifuge at 4°C, 13,000 rpm for 10 minutes; collect and store the supernatant on ice. Transfer 500 μL supernatant to a centrifuge tube, and add 5 μg target antibody or isotype IgG. Incubate at room temperature for 1 hour to form antigen-antibody complexes. The following day, wash 25 μL of magnetic bead suspension with 500 μL lysis buffer. Place on a magnetic stand for 1 minute, then discard the supernatant. Repeat this step 5 times. Combine the antigen-antibody complexes with magnetic beads and incubate overnight on a shaking incubator at 4°C. Wash and resuspend with 500 μL lysis buffer, allow to stand for 1 minute, discard the supernatant, and repeat twice. Subsequently, add 100 μL elution buffer to the aforementioned complex and incubate at room temperature for 10 minutes. Transfer the supernatant to a fresh tube, add neutralization buffer at a 10:1 ratio, and proceed for subsequent analysis.

Database sources

First, we queried the CTD website (https://ctdbase.org/detail.go?type=disease&acc=MESH%3AD055113) to identify potential target genes involved in CP regulation. Simultaneously, we utilized the ENCORI (https://rnasysu.com/encori/agoClipRNA.php?source=mRNA), miRDB (https://mirdb.org/), miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/), and Targetscan (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) to collectively gather downstream target gene information for miRNAs. Genes enriched across all five databases were identified as overlapping genes. Subsequently, we constructed the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for overlapping genes using the STRING software (https://cn.string-db.org/). Additionally, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on overlapping genes via the Micro Bioinformatics website (https://bioinformatics.com.cn/?keywords=pathway).

Data analysis

Experimental results were collated using SPSS 25.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. All results comprised three valid replicates. Continuous variables following a normal distribution (age) were compared using independent samples t-tests, while categorical variables (gender, smoking history, diabetes history, PD, BI, PLI) were analysed via chi-square tests. For cellular experiments, intergroup differences were assessed using independent samples t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Additionally, multivariate logistic regression was employed to examine the potential influence of multiple factors on CP. ROC curves were employed to evaluate the diagnostic value of lncRNAs for CP across different samples. Pearson correlation analysis examined the relationship between LncRNA and miRNA expressions. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Diagnostic value of FAM30A for CP patients

Test results indicate that FAM30A is significantly upregulated in the saliva and periodontal ligament tissue of CP patients (P < .0001, Figures 1A, 1B). ROC analysis revealed that the AUC for predicting CP using salivary FAM30A was 85.44% (P < .0001, 95% CI: 0.8016-0.9077, Figure 1C), with a sensitivity of 77.85% and specificity of 76.65%. Conversely, the AUC for predicting CP using FAM30A in periodontal tissue was 86.47% (P < .0001, 95% CI: 0.8131-0.9163, Figure 1D), with a sensitivity of 73.08% and specificity of 85.88%. Further correlation analysis (Figure 1E-G) demonstrated that FAM30A expression in periodontal tissues of CP patients was positively correlated with PD (r = 0.7338), BI (r = 0.7422), and PLI (r = 0.6808). Logistic regression analysis indicated that FAM30A in periodontal tissues (P = .018) constituted a risk factor for inducing chronic periodontitis (Table 2). Effects of si-FAM30A on LPS-stimulated PDLSCs function and inflammatory levels

Results demonstrated that FAM30A expression progressively increased in a concentration-dependent manner with LPS, stabilizing at 200 ng/mL. Consequently, we selected 100 ng/mL LPS for cell treatment (P < 0.0001, Figure 2A). Transfection with si-FAM30A obviously suppressed FAM30A expression (P < .0001, Figure 2B). LPS treatment inhibited PDLSCs' proliferation and promoted apoptosis. However, si-FAM30A promoted cell proliferation and reduced apoptosis (P < .0001, Figures 2C, 2D). Secondly, we observed that LPS induced elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (particularly TNF-α) and downregulation of anti-inflammatory factors in PDLSCs (P = .0200, Figure 2E). However, inhibition of FAM30A reduced inflammatory cytokine expression in cells. miR-424-5p is a downstream target gene of FAM30A

Bioinformatics prediction revealed a target binding site between miR-424-5p and FAM30A (Figure 3A). When we transfected miR inhibitor into wild-type (WT-FAM30A) cells, the solution fluorescence activity increased, whereas transfection with miR mimic markedly diminished it (P = .0016, Figure 3B). However, no difference was detected upon transfection in mutant (MT-FAM30A) cells. Furthermore, RNA immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated significant co-enrichment of FAM30A and miR-424-5p (P < .0001, Figure 3C). Detection revealed low expression levels of miR-424-5p in both saliva and periodontal ligament tissue from CP patients (P < .0001, Figures 3D, 3E). Moreover, an obvious negative correlation between FAM30A and miR-424-5p expression was observed in both sample types (P < .0001, Figures 3F, 3G). Discussion

Enrichment analysis of gene expression profiles in periodontitis surgery patients revealed that non-coding RNAs, transcription factors, and endogenous genes potentially regulate the disease.38 Separately, an immune infiltration analysis indicated that multiple differentially expressed genes possess potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for periodontitis.39 Current research predominantly addresses periodontitis treatment through immunomodulation or bone development promotion. Yang et al.40 reported a scaffold containing aspirin and growth peptides for regulating periodontal bone regeneration. Xue et al.41 introduced a multifunctional hydrogel for immunomodulation in CP patients. Wei's team42 identified melatonin carbon dots (MT-CD) with radical scavenging properties that promote regeneration of damaged periodontal tissues. However, our findings indicate that FAM30A upregulation and miR-424-5p downregulation are present in both salivary and periodontal ligament tissue samples from CP patients. Secondly, we found that FAM30A expression in both biological samples holds diagnostic value for CP. Furthermore, FAM30A likely exacerbates periodontitis progression via miR-424-5p.

It has been reported that FAM30A plays a crucial regulatory role in bone metabolism and formation. Hong et al.25 discovered that FAM30A exhibits abnormal expression in patients with myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), leading to poor prognosis. Chromosomal microarray analysis revealed that FAM30A expression correlates with physiological mechanisms in patients with idiopathic short stature.43 Furthermore, aberrant FAM30A expression has been associated with multiple cancers.44 Andre et al.45 suggested FAM30A may function as an oncogene in acute lymphoblastic leukemia patients. Wang et al.46 reported that elevated FAM30A levels constitute a susceptibility factor for chemotherapy in leukemia patients. Zhang et al.47 demonstrated that FAM30A is an upregulated gene in ovarian cancer patients, potentially promoting cancer cell metastasis. Furthermore, Marina et al.48 observed elevated FAM30A transcript levels in ependymoma patients, suggesting potential as a therapeutic target. However, the findings of this study indicate that FAM30A levels in patients with chronic periodontitis demonstrate good sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing the condition. Furthermore, the analysis revealed a positive correlation between FAM30A and periodontal assessment metrics (PD, BI, PLI) in these patients. This result suggests that FAM30A holds potential as a clinical diagnostic biomarker for chronic periodontitis.

This study revealed that miR-424-5p was markedly downregulated in both CP patients and LPS-treated PDLSCs. This suggests that abnormal expression of miR-424-5p may contribute to CP progression. Cell functional assays demonstrated that inhibition of miR-424-5p promoted apoptosis and elevated oxidative stress levels. This suggests that miR-424-5p functions as a negative regulator in CP modulation. Furthermore, miR-424-5p is a key miRNA involved in both osteogenesis and inflammatory regulation. Reports indicate that miR-424-5p targets HSP90AA1 to inhibit skeletal muscle differentiation.49 It also attenuates osteogenic effects by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin axis mediated by WIF1.50 Furthermore, bioinformatics analysis revealed reduced miR-424-5p expression in patients with ischemic stroke51 and thyroid cancer.52 Xie et al.53 demonstrated that inhibiting miR-424-5p noticeably reduced endothelial cell injury induced by middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO). Zhao et al.54 reported that the miR-424-5p/TLR4 axis exacerbates LPS-induced lung injury via the NF-κB signaling pathway. However, bioinformatics analysis revealed that the MAPK, cellular senescence, and mTOR signaling pathways downstream of the FAM30A/miR-424-5p axis may contribute to the progression of CP. The MAPK pathway serves as an ‘initiator of inflammation and stress’. Research indicates that periodontal pathogens and their virulence factors activate the MAPK signaling pathway by engaging host surface receptors,55 leading to the massive release of inflammatory mediators that destroy periodontal tissues.56 The mTOR signaling pathway acts as a ‘regulator of cellular metabolism and fate’, exhibiting dual effects.57 Persistent inflammatory stimulation simultaneously inhibits autophagy, exacerbating inflammatory responses,58 while its sustained activation induces cell cycle arrest.59 Cellular senescence, in turn, acts as an ‘amplifier of inflammation’.60 Under prolonged influence of pathways such as MAPK and mTOR, cells within periodontal tissues (eg, fibroblasts, osteoblasts) enter a senescent state.61 They secrete substantial quantities of inflammatory cytokines and proteases, exacerbating disease progression while impeding periodontal tissue regeneration.

However, due to experimental design and resource constraints, we must discuss the limitations currently present in this study. (1) Insufficient support for FAM30A in diagnosing CP. There remains a lack of assessment of FAM30A across patients with varying degrees of CP severity. (2) Limitations of the in vitro cell model. Restrictions on cell passage numbers and simplified culture conditions may affect result stability, whilst static culture overlooks the dynamic nature of inflammation. (3) The signaling pathways downstream of the FAM30A/miR-424-5p axis involved in CP regulation remain unvalidated. (4) Animal studies are absent. Future research will address these limitations through the following approaches: (1) First, expanding independent cohorts to conduct stratified analyses and validate FAM30A expression across different subgroups. (2) Optimize in vitro models (eg, multi-species co-infection) and validate using alternative cell lines (gingival fibroblasts, osteoblasts, etc.). (3) Validate gene expression or activation within relevant signaling pathways via transcriptomics and proteomics. (4) Develop conditional gene knockout mouse models to validate findings in a more vivo-like environment. This will enable a more comprehensive elucidation of the target gene's mechanism of action and therapeutic potential in CP. Furthermore, research indicates that diabetes is associated with the progression of periodontitis.62,63 Yet, this differential was not evident among the subjects we enrolled in. To control for the influence of confounding variables (diabetes), we deliberately controlled the allocation of diabetic patients during the inclusion of healthy individuals and CP patients. Consequently, there was no significant difference in diabetes history between the 2 groups. Nevertheless, considering the randomized nature of the study, we will subsequently incorporate diabetes as a relevant variable and conduct stratified analysis experiments.

In summary, this study demonstrates that FAM30A is upregulated in the saliva and periodontal ligament tissues of CP patients and possesses diagnostic value. Meanwhile, miR-424-5p participates in CP regulation as a negative regulatory factor. This is primarily manifested as follows: FAM30A attenuates PDLSCs proliferation by targeting and inhibiting miR-424-5p, thereby promoting apoptosis, elevating inflammatory and oxidative stress levels, and exacerbating CP.
