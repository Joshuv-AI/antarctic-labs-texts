Cluster-Aware Causal Mixer for Online Anomaly Detection in Multivariate Time Series
Md Mahmuddun Nabi Murad, Yasin Yilmaz

Abstract
Early and accurate detection of anomalies in time series data is critical, given the significant risks associated with false or missed detections. While MLP-based mixer models have shown promise in time series analysis, they lack a causality mechanism to preserve temporal dependencies inherent in the system. Moreover, real-world multivariate time series often contain numerous channels with diverse inter-channel correlations. A single embedding mechanism for all channels does not effectively capture these complex relationships. To address these challenges, we propose a novel cluster-aware causal mixer to effectively detect anomalies in multivariate time series. Our model groups channels into clusters based on their correlations, with each cluster processed through a dedicated embedding layer. In addition, we introduce a causal mixer in our model, which mixes the information while maintaining causality. Furthermore, we present an anomaly detection framework that accumulates the anomaly evidence over time to prevent false positives due to nominal outliers. Our proposed model operates in an online fashion, making it suitable for real-time time-series anomaly detection tasks. Experimental evaluations across six public benchmark datasets demonstrate that our model consistently achieves superior F1 scores.

Introduction

Time series data consists of sequential measurements collected from multiple sources (e.g., sensors) over time. When there is an anomaly such as sensor malfunction or malicious data manipulation, the resulting patterns often deviate from normal behavior. Accurate and timely detection of such anomalies is critical in a wide range of applications, including the identification of cyberattacks
[
29
]
or sensor failures in critical infrastructure systems, such as water distribution networks
[
13
]
. Given the limited availability of labeled anomalies in real-world datasets, most research in time series anomaly detection has focused on unsupervised methods
[
4
]
. These approaches generally involve training models to learn the temporal (and spatial for multivariate analysis) characteristics of nominal training data.

Classical methods such as K-Nearest-Neighbors (KNN) and K-Means
[
23
,
37
]
detect anomalies by measuring the distance between a test point and nominal training data, flagging points that exceed a predefined threshold. With advances in computational power, deep learning-based approaches have become increasingly popular for time series anomaly detection. These methods typically consist of two stages: prediction or reconstruction, followed by anomaly detection. Prediction-based models forecast future values while reconstruction-based models aim to replicate input data. Anomalies are identified based on the resulting prediction or reconstruction error. Some approaches also incorporate uncertainty estimation, where nominal data yields low uncertainty and anomalous data results in higher uncertainty levels
[
21
]
.

In recent years, transformer-based models have gained popularity in time series analysis, with many studies adopting transformer variants as the core architecture
[
6
,
36
,
32
,
30
,
17
,
11
]
. Among them, NPSR
[
17
]
and SensitiveHUE
[
11
]
further enhance performance by refining anomaly scoring mechanisms.
In addition, recent studies, including
[
38
]
, question the effectiveness of transformer-based models for time series forecasting, showing that simple MLPs can achieve comparable performance. Furthermore, MLP-Mixer models have shown superior performance against transformer variants in time series forecasting tasks
[
24
,
5
]
. However, real-world sequential systems require causal processing. While transformers enforce causality via attention masking, MLP-Mixers currently lack mechanisms to ensure causal constraints.

Our contributions are threefold:

\bullet
Motivated by the strong performance of MLP-Mixer models and addressing their limitations regarding causality, we propose
a novel causal MLP-Mixer-based model
that mixes information in a strictly causal manner, ensuring that the representation at any given time step is influenced only by past information.

\bullet
In addition, we introduce a
cluster-aware multi-embedding mechanism
to capture inter-channel correlations effectively. Time series data often includes multiple channels; some are highly correlated, while others are completely independent. Employing a single, shared embedding layer across all channels can unintentionally impose false correlations. To address this, our proposed method groups channels based on their correlation and assigns each group a dedicated embedding layer, preserving meaningful dependencies while reducing spurious ones.

\bullet
Furthermore, we also propose a
sequential anomaly scoring method
that accumulates the anomaly evidence over time to enhance the anomaly detection performance.

3
Proposed Method

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}_{1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}_{t}\}
represent a multivariate time series, where each
\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C}
denotes a data point at time step
t
and
C
refers to the number of channels. Each data point
\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}_{t}
has an associated label
y_{t}\in\{0,1\}
, where
y_{t}=1
and
y_{t}=0
refer to anomalous and nominal data points. Our objective is to predict the label
y_{t}
for each time step
t
.

The training dataset consists solely of the nominal data, whereas the test dataset contains both nominal and anomalous points. At each time step
t
, a sliding window
\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{L}=\{\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}_{t-L+1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}_{t}\}
of length
L
is passed through the model to reconstruct the most recent data point
\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}_{t}
. The reconstruction loss between
\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}_{t}
and the reconstructed
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}}_{t}
is then used to make a decision. For subsequent predictions, the sliding window moves forward by one time step, and the process repeats iteratively.

3.1
Overall Model Architecture

The architecture of our model is depicted in Figure
1
. Our model consists of two key components: cluster-aware multi-embedding and causal mixer. The input to our model
\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times C}
is min-max normalized data. At the beginning of our model, we pass the input
\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{L}
through the cluster-aware multi-embedding module (Section
3.2
) to get embedded output
\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
.
We then apply 1D batch normalization to the output of the embedding module, resulting in
\underline{\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
.
\underline{\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{d}}
is then processed through multiple causal mixer layers. Each causal mixer layer (Section
3.3
) comprises a causal temporal mixer module and an embedding mixer module. The causal temporal mixer module consists of two causal linear layers connected by a non-linear GELU activation. The operations of causal temporal mixer module are summarized as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{c}=\mathcal{P}(\underline{\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{d}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times L}

(1)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}^{{}^{\prime}}_{c}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{c})))\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times L}

(2)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{e}=\mathcal{BN}(\mathcal{P}(\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}^{{}^{\prime}}_{c})+\underline{\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{d}}))\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d},

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}_{1}
,
\mathcal{L}_{2}
,
\mathcal{G}(.)
,
\mathcal{P}(.)
, and
\mathcal{BN}(.)
represent the two causal linear layers, GELU activation, permute operation, and 1D batch normalization, respectively. After the causal temporal mixer, the data passes through the embedding mixer module:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}^{{}^{\prime}}_{e}=\mathcal{L}^{{}^{\prime}}_{2}(\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{L}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{e})))\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}

(4)

\displaystyle\underline{\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}^{{}^{\prime}}_{d}}=\mathcal{BN}(\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}^{{}^{\prime}}_{e}+\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{e}+\underline{\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{d}})\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}

(5)

where,
\mathcal{L}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{L\times d\cdot d_{f}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{L\times d\cdot d_{f}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
represent regular linear layers,
d_{f}
is a hyperparameter (expansion factor).

After the data is processed through a series of causal mixer layers, it is combined with
\underline{\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{d}}
and subsequently normalized, yielding
\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
. This representation serves as the input to the head layer, which consists of a single linear transformation
\mathcal{L}_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{L\times C}
, mapping the
d
dimensional data to
C
reconstructed output channels. We consider the last reconstructed data point
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath${x}$}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C}
to detect the anomalies (Section
3.4
).

3.2
Cluster-Aware Multi-Embedding

In this paper, we propose a cluster-aware multi-embedding method, which includes channel clustering and multi-embedding, to enhance the channel representation. First, we compute a cluster index for each channel based on its correlation pattern, derived from the training dataset. This cluster index for each channel is then utilized in the clustering module during training and inference to group the input channels into clusters. Then, a separate embedding layer is employed for each channel cluster. The outputs of all embedding layers are concatenated to obtain the final high-dimensional representation. This cluster-aware multi-embedding technique mitigates spurious correlations that arise when channels with heterogeneous relationships are embedded jointly.

3.2.1
Channel Clustering

Real-world time series data often contains a mix of highly correlated, weakly correlated, and uncorrelated channels. A single shared embedding layer cannot distinguish these relations and may inadvertently impose artificial dependencies during the learning process. This can lead to degraded generalization performance, especially in anomaly detection tasks that require precise modeling of nominal patterns. To address this, we propose a cluster-aware multi-embedding technique that utilizes spectral clustering
[
31
]
to cluster the channels into
M
clusters based on their correlations and assigns a dedicated embedding layer to each group.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath${\Phi}$}\in[-1,1]^{C\times C}
denote the Pearson correlation matrix and
\mbox{\boldmath${\Phi}$}_{\text{abs}}\in[0,1]^{C\times C}
represent its element-wise absolute value. Channel
i
’s correlations with other channels, given by the
i
th row
\mbox{\boldmath${\phi}$}_{i}\in[0,1]^{C}
of
\mbox{\boldmath${\Phi}$}_{\text{abs}}
, is used as its correlation profile. Some of the channels in the time series may remain constant throughout the series, resulting in undefined (NaN) correlation values with other channels. To address this, we replace all NaN entries in
{\Phi}
with zeros before proceeding. During the initial clustering phase, we group all channels with zero-valued correlation profiles in the
M
th cluster. Let the number of remaining channels be
C^{{}^{\prime}}
and the corresponding absolute correlation matrix be
\mbox{\boldmath${\Phi}$}^{{}^{\prime}}_{\text{abs}}\in[0,1]^{C^{{}^{\prime}}\times C^{{}^{\prime}}}
with rows
\mbox{\boldmath${\phi}$}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}
.

To group
C^{{}^{\prime}}
channels with similar correlation profiles into
M^{{}^{\prime}}=M-1
clusters, we construct a similarity-based weighted adjacency matrix
\mbox{\boldmath${W}$}\in[-1,1]^{C^{{}^{\prime}}\times C^{{}^{\prime}}}
, where each off-diagonal entry
w_{ij}
represents the cosine similarity between the correlation profiles of channels
i
and
j
and is given by

\displaystyle w_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{(\phi_{i}^{{}^{\prime}})^{T}\phi_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}}{\left\|\phi_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}\right\|\left\|\phi_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}\right\|},&\text{if }i\neq j\\
0,&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

(6)

Here,
\left\|.\right\|
denotes the Euclidean norm. Next, we compute the normalized graph Laplacian matrix
\mbox{\boldmath${L}$}_{n}
as,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${L}$}_{n}=\mbox{\boldmath${I}$}-\mbox{\boldmath${D}$}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath${W}$}\mbox{\boldmath${D}$}^{-1/2}

(7)

where,
{I}
is the identity matrix and the diagonal matrix
{D}
is the degree matrix with diagonal element
d_{ii}=\sum_{j}w_{ij}
. We then perform spectral embedding by extracting the first
M^{{}^{\prime}}
non-trivial eigenvectors of
\mbox{\boldmath${L}$}_{n}
, corresponding to the smallest non-zero eigenvalues.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath${V}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{C^{{}^{\prime}}\times M^{{}^{\prime}}}
contains these eigenvectors as columns. To normalize the embedded representations, we compute,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${U}$}=\mbox{\boldmath${D}$}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath${V}$}

(8)

where the
i
th row of
{U}
, given by
\mbox{\boldmath${u}$}_{i}=d_{ii}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath${v}$}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{{}^{\prime}}}
, represents the spectral embedding of channel
i
. Finally, K-Means clustering is applied to the rows of
{U}
to cluster the channel embeddings into
M^{{}^{\prime}}
clusters and get the cluster index for each channel as,

\displaystyle k^{{}^{\prime}}=\textsc{K-Means}(\mbox{\boldmath${U}$},\ M^{{}^{\prime}}).

(9)

A pseudocode for the spectral clustering algorithm is given in Algorithm
1
in Appendix
A
.

3.2.2
Multi-Embedding Layer

After obtaining the cluster indices based on the training dataset, we construct
M
clusters of channels for both the training and the test datasets.
We employ multiple embedders—one for each cluster—to learn channel representations in a cluster-specific manner. This approach allows the model to embed channels with different correlation profiles in separate latent subspaces, mitigating the risk of entangling unrelated features and enabling more expressive representations.

Let
d
denote the desired final embedding dimension. Since the number of channels assigned to each cluster is not uniform, we assign a different embedding dimension
d_{i}
for each cluster
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
, proportional to the number of channels in the cluster
C_{i}
, using the following formula,

\displaystyle d_{i}=\begin{cases}\left\lfloor\dfrac{C_{i}}{C}d\right\rfloor,&\text{for }i=1,\dots,M-1\\
d-\sum_{j=1}^{M{-}1}d_{j},&\text{for }i=M,\end{cases}

(10)

which ensures
\sum_{i=1}^{M}d_{i}=d
. Each channel cluster is processed through its respective embedding layer of dimension
d_{i}
. These are subsequently concatenated to form the final unified
d
-dimensional embedding to feed into the next module of the model.

The use of multiple embedders not only enhances the model’s capacity to learn localized representations within each cluster, it also reduces the number of trainable parameters in the embedding layer. In a conventional single linear embedding layer, the number of trainable parameters (weights) is
C\times d
. In contrast, our approach partitions the channels into
M
clusters, and each cluster is embedded independently using a reduced embedding dimension. Assuming uniform cluster sizes, each cluster contains
O\left(\frac{C}{M}\right)
channels and is assigned an embedding dimension of
O\left(\frac{d}{M}\right)
. Therefore, the number of trainable weights in each embedding layer is
O\left(\frac{C\cdot d}{M^{2}}\right)
. Since we employ
M
embedding layers, the total number of trainable parameters becomes
O\left(\frac{C\cdot d}{M}\right)
. Thus, the proposed method substantially reduces, by a factor of
M
, the number of trainable parameters in the embedding layer when
M>1
.

3.3
Causal Mixer

MLP-mixer-based models have gained popularity in time series analysis due to their ability to capture the dependencies along various dimensions—temporal, embedding, and channel directions. However, a data point at each time step in the input sequence is allowed to interact with all other data points within the look-back window. But, in real-world applications, systems are causal, where the present data is only influenced by the past data points. Considering this, we propose a causal mixer, which is a combination of a causal temporal mixer and an embedding mixer. The causal temporal mixer mixes the data along the temporal dimension and ensures that each time step is influenced by the present and past observations only. On the other hand, the embedding mixer mixes the data along the embedding dimension without considering causality.

3.3.1
Causal Temporal Mixer Module

This module consists of two causal linear layers connected by GELU activation. The input to the causal temporal mixer module is denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times L}
, where
L
is the look-back window length and
d
is the embedding dimension. To introduce causality in the linear layer, we perform masking on the weight
\mbox{\boldmath${\Theta}$}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
by an upper-triangular mask
\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
given by

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1,1}&\gamma_{1,2}&\cdots&\gamma_{1,L}\\
0&\gamma_{2,2}&\cdots&\gamma_{2,L}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\gamma_{L,L}\end{bmatrix},\quad\gamma_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{j},&\text{if }i\leq j\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(11)

to get the final masked weight
\mbox{\boldmath${\Theta}$}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${\Theta}$}_{u}=\mbox{\boldmath${\Gamma}$}\odot\mbox{\boldmath${\Theta}$}_{c},

(12)

where
\odot
refers to element-wise multiplication.
The operation in the first causal linear layer can be written as,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{c1}=\mbox{\boldmath${X}$}_{c}\mbox{\boldmath${\Theta}$}_{u}+\mbox{\boldmath${b}$},

(13)

where
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times L}
is the learnable bias broadcasted along the embedding dimension. To enforce causality, the causal linear layer restricts each output neuron
j
to depend only on input neurons
i\leq j
, effectively preventing information flow from future neurons. We have another causal linear layer in the causal temporal mixer module, with operations similar to the first one.

3.3.2
Embedding Mixer

The embedding mixer employs two linear layers with a GELU activation in between. It projects the input from
d
to
(d\cdot d_{f})
dimensions and then back to
d
, following the design in
[
24
]
.

3.4
Anomaly Detection Method

Anomalies in time series data often exhibit sequential dependencies, where the likelihood of a point being anomalous increases if preceding points are also anomalous. Leveraging this temporal association, we propose an anomaly detection method that considers not only the anomaly evidence of the current data point but also that of preceding points. Our approach accumulates anomaly evidence over time, and when the accumulated evidence exceeds a predefined threshold, the corresponding instance is identified as anomalous. Furthermore, by analyzing the rise and fall of the anomaly evidence, our method effectively delineates the full extent of the anomaly sequence.

Let the original training and test datasets be denoted by
\mathcal{X}_{N}
and
\mathcal{X}_{T}
, with sizes
N
and
T
, respectively. We partition the training dataset
\mathcal{X}_{N}
into two subsets: a training subset
\mathcal{X}_{N_{1}}
and a validation subset
\mathcal{X}_{N_{2}}
, such that
N_{1}+N_{2}=N
. Our reconstruction model is trained on
\mathcal{X}_{N_{1}}
to minimize the reconstruction loss. After training, we compute the reconstruction loss for each sample in
\mathcal{X}_{N_{2}}
. The reconstruction losses are then sorted in ascending order to form a reconstruction loss series denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath${e}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}}
. Similarly, the reconstruction loss series for the test dataset is denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath${g}$}=\{g_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.

To evaluate the anomaly likelihood of a test point
g_{t}
, we compute its p-value (i.e., percentage of greater values) in
{e}
as
p_{t}
.
The anomaly evidence
\beta_{t}
is then computed using a statistical significance parameter
\alpha\in[0,1)
as:

\displaystyle\beta_{t}=\log\left(\frac{\alpha}{p_{t}+\epsilon}\right),

(14)

where
\epsilon
is a small value that avoids division by zero. We then accumulate the anomaly evidence
\beta_{t}
over time using the following formula to get the accumulated evidence series,

\displaystyle s_{t}=\begin{cases}\max(s_{t-1}+\beta_{t},0),&\text{if }\neg\left(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{\delta}\left(\beta_{t-i}<0\right)\right)\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad s_{0}=0,

(15)

where
\bigwedge
and
\neg
are the logical AND and NOT, respectively.
\delta
is a predefined window length that controls the reset condition. An instance is flagged as anomalous if
s_{t}
exceeds a predefined threshold
h
. This cumulative strategy enhances the detection of anomaly segments, particularly capturing the middle and bottom regions of an anomaly sequence where the evidence tends to peak. Let
[t_{a},t_{b}]
denote the detected anomaly portion where all the instances from
t=t_{a}
to
t=t_{b}
are flagged as anomalies. To detect the full span of an anomaly sequence—including its onset and offset—we implement a backward search. The optimal onset time
t_{a}^{*}

and offset time
t_{b}^{*}
are determined by

\displaystyle t_{a}^{*}=\arg\max\{t<t_{a}\mid s_{t}=0\}

(16)

\displaystyle t_{b}^{*}=\arg\max\{t_{a}<t\leq t_{b}\mid\beta_{t}>0\}

(17)

The optimal onset time
t_{a}^{*}
denotes the point at which the accumulated evidence begins to rise from zero before the alarm triggered by
s_{t}>h
, while the optimal offset time
t_{b}^{*}
represents the point at which the accumulated evidence starts to decline near the end of the anomaly sequence.

5
Conclusion

Time series anomaly detection is critical for preventing significant losses caused by missed or delayed detection. In this paper, we propose a cluster-aware causal mixer model for time series anomaly detection (CCM-TAD). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to introduce causality into the MLP-Mixer architecture. We demonstrate that incorporating causality improves anomaly detection performance. Additionally, to mitigate spurious correlations among channels, we employ spectral clustering to group channels and apply separate embeddings, which significantly enhances model performance and reduces computational complexity compared to the conventional single embedding approach. Furthermore, we adopt a sequential anomaly detection approach that accumulates anomaly evidence over time, effectively reducing errors and improving F1 score.

Appendix C
Data processing

Following the approach of SensitiveHUE
[
11
]
, we downsampled the SWAT and WADI datasets by a factor of 5 to ensure fair comparisons. Data normalization was performed using min-max scaling, with parameters computed from the training set. To prevent extreme values from affecting the model, we clipped the normalized test data to the range
[-4,+4]
, as recommended by NPSR
[
17
]
. We adopted the following data-specific preprocessing steps based on methods
[
17
,
11
]
:

•

For the SWAT dataset, the P201 and LIT401 channels exhibited inconsistencies between the training and test sets; hence, we set their values to zero
[
11

. To reduce noise, we applied a moving average filter to the entire dataset.

•

For the WADI dataset (2017 version), we ignored columns with excessive missing values and forward-filled a small number of remaining NaNs. One channel was also set to zero based on the same reference. Additionally, we excluded the first 21,600 data points from the training set to account for system initialization
[
17

.

•

For the PSM dataset, we applied a moving average for noise reduction, and forward-filled all missing values

Appendix F
Extended Ablation Study: Channel Clustering Variants

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed spectral clustering method based on correlation profiles, we conduct a comprehensive ablation study comparing three alternative channel clustering strategies. These strategies isolate the influence of different similarity metrics and clustering paradigms on the structural organization of features and their downstream impact on anomaly detection performance.

(1) Channel Similarity-Based Spectral Clustering:

In this variant, we construct the similarity-based adjacency matrix
\mbox{\boldmath${W}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}
using the cosine similarity between channel time series. The similarity score
w_{ij}\in[0,1]
is computed as,

\displaystyle w_{ij}=\begin{cases}\max\left(0,\frac{\langle\mathcal{X}_{N(:,i)},\mathcal{X}_{N(:,j)}\rangle}{\left\|\mathcal{X}_{N(:,i)}\right\|\left\|\mathcal{X}_{N(:,j)}\right\|}\right),&\text{if }i\neq j\\
0,&\text{if }i=j\end{cases}

(18)

where,
\mathcal{X}_{N(:,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
is the
i^{th}
channel of the training data
\mathcal{X}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}
.
N
and
C
are the length and the number of channels of the training data, respectively. We then apply a similar spectral clustering approach described in Section
3.2.1
to the resulting similarity weight matrix.

(2) Absolute Correlation-Based Spectral Clustering:

In this configuration, the adjacency matrix
\mbox{\boldmath${W}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}
is directly derived from the absolute Pearson correlation matrix
\mbox{\boldmath${\Phi}$}_{\text{abs}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}
, where
w_{ij}=\phi_{ij}
. Spectral clustering is then performed on this graph to determine channel groupings. This method captures the pairwise correlation without considering the full correlation profile.

(3) K-Means Clustering on Correlation Matrix:

Here, instead of forming a graph and applying spectral clustering, we directly cluster the rows of
\mbox{\boldmath${\Phi}$}_{\text{abs}}
using the K-Means algorithm. Each row
\phi_{i}
can be interpreted as the correlation profile of the
i
th channel, representing its relationship with all other channels. This method evaluates the efficacy of clustering in the original correlation profile space, independent of the properties of the spectral graph.

The anomaly detection results of all these approaches are shown in Table
5
of the main paper. Our proposed approach, which utilizes spectral clustering on the full correlation profiles, consistently outperforms these baselines, highlighting the importance of preserving inter-channel relationships during clustering.

Appendix H
Limitations

The performance of our anomaly detection method is sensitive to the value of
\alpha
. Since we determine the statistical position of the test reconstruction loss relative to the validation loss, the effectiveness of the method depends on the model being optimally trained on nominal data. Consequently, if the dataset size is small, the results may become inconsistent.

Additionally, we use an expansion factor
d_{f}
in the embedding mixer; however, we do not explicitly mention any expansion factor for the causal temporal mixer. Though we do not mention the expansion factor for the causal temporal mixer, it is effectively 1. An expansion factor greater than 1 in the temporal mixer does not maintain causality. In future work, we will explore alternatives that will maintain causality with a higher expansion factor.

From Appendix
G
, we observe that clustering reduces the spurious correlation for the nominal prediction. However, it does not fully mitigate the spurious correlation. One of the reasons for this is that we use a common embedding mixer module and a common head layer, which eventually mixes all the channel embeddings. However, employing a separate embedding mixer for each cluster could be a possible solution. But the common embedding mixer is required to extract the spatial information. We will also study this issue in a future work.
