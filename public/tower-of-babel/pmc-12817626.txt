Engineered extracellular vesicles-mediated curcumin delivery in brain microenvironment modulating lysosomes, mitochondria, and microglia reprogram for parkinson’s disease therapy
Authors: Ming-You Shie, Mei-Chih Chen, Yeh Chen, Shi-Wei Huang, Yen-Hong Lin, Min-Hua Yu, Chih-Ming Pan, Cheng-Yu Chen, Yi-Wen Chen, Ching-Liang Hsieh, Shao-Chih Chiu, Shung-Te Kao, Jaung-Geng Lin, Der-Yang Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the loss of dopaminergic neurons, aggregation of α-synuclein (α-Syn), lysosomal dysfunction, and mitochondrial impairment. Curcumin has demonstrated neuroprotective effects against PD pathology; however, its poor bioavailability, rapid systemic clearance, and limited blood-brain barrier permeability remain significant challenges to be overcome. An extracellular vesicle (EV)-based dopamine transporter (DAT)-targeted drug delivery system, derived from genetically engineered HEK293T cells, expressing DAT-targeting single-chain variable fragments (scFv) on the EV surface, is developed. Curcumin is encapsulated into the DAT-targeting EVs (αDAT EVs) for precise delivery into dopaminergic neurons. In the PD cell model, significant EV uptake is observed, with a reduced accumulation of α-Syn, alongside restored expression of DJ-1, TH, and PARKIN following treatment with curcumin-loaded DAT-targeting EVs (Cur@αDAT EVs). In a 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)-induced PD rat model, Cur@αDAT EVs significantly enhanced motor and cognitive function, protected dopaminergic neurons, and attenuated neuroinflammation, with microglial activation considered a downstream paracrine/bystander effect following neuronal rescue. Accumulation of curcumin in the substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area confirms precise αDAT-EV-mediated delivery, addressing the pharmacokinetic challenges of free curcumin. Overall, DAT-targeting EVs represent a promising precision delivery platform for combating PD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03911-z.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide and represents a significant and rising global health burden. Clinically, PD manifests with motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and postural instability, alongside non-motor features including cognitive impairment and autonomic dysfunction. Pathologically, PD is characterized by progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and accumulation of Lewy bodies primarily comprising aggregated alpha-synuclein (α-Syn) [1, 2]. Lysosomal dysfunction has emerged as a key pathogenic factor in PD, as it disrupts cellular proteostasis and impairs the degradation of misfolded proteins like α-Syn, leading to their accumulation and neurotoxicity [3]. Moreover, genetic mutations in lysosomal enzymes, such as GBA1, further implicate lysosomal impairment in PD pathogenesis. Similarly, mitochondrial dysfunction plays a central role in PD by reducing ATP production and compromising neuronal survival [4]. Critically, mitochondrial defects result in the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which causes oxidative stress and further damages cellular components [5]. Defects in mitochondrial quality control mechanisms, including mitophagy, contribute to the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons. Importantly, recent evidence highlights that mitochondria and lysosomes communicate through direct contact sites, and disturbances in this inter-organelle crosstalk may exacerbate neurodegenerative processes in PD [6, 7]. These interconnected processes ultimately result in progressive neurodegeneration, synaptic dysfunction, and the loss of dopaminergic tone. Dopamine transporter (DAT) expression is markedly reduced in PD, reflecting the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons. Therefore, DAT serves as a critical biomarker for disease diagnosis and progression monitoring, enabling the precise visualization of its distribution and providing invaluable insights into disease staging and differential diagnosis [8, 9].

Given the multifactorial nature of PD pathology, targeted therapeutic strategies that can mitigate α-Syn aggregation, restore lysosomal function, and protect dopaminergic neurons from oxidative stress are urgently needed. Curcumin, a polyphenolic compound derived from Curcuma longa, has demonstrated potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects in preclinical studies [10, 11]. It inhibits α-Syn aggregation, enhances lysosomal clearance, and ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction [12–14]. However, curcumin suffers from poor bioavailability, rapid systemic clearance, and limited blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, significantly restricting its clinical application [15, 16]. To address these pharmacokinetic limitations, the development of advanced drug delivery systems (DDS) capable of precise, sustained, and targeted curcumin release within the central nervous system (CNS) is crucial. In recent decades, significant progress has been made in developing nanocarriers for DDS [17, 18]. A wide range of DDS has been explored for tumor treatment, including nanoscale formulations such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, or metallic nanocarriers [19–21]. However, these nanoparticles often suffer from high immunogenicity and limited ability to cross the BBB [22, 23]. Additionally, the byproducts of polymeric nanoparticles could be toxic and challenging to eliminate, whereas the surface charge of liposomes may influence their stability in the bloodstream.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs), naturally secreted lipid bilayer nanoparticles, are a promising drug delivery platform because of their biocompatibility, stability, and capability to cross biological barriers [24, 25]. EVs facilitate intercellular communication by transporting bioactive molecules, including proteins, RNAs, and lipids [26–28]. Specifically, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived EVs have been widely investigated for neurological applications due to their abundance of neurotrophic factors and low immunogenicity [29, 30]. Although EVs hold promise for treating neurodegenerative diseases, they face challenges such as short systemic circulation and non-specific tissue accumulation [31–33]. Recent work has advanced EV-based and biomimetic nanocarriers for neurorepair. For Parkinson’s disease, Wang et al. devised independent-module/cascading-function EVs by conjugating an NO-generating, ROS/iNOS-responsive nanomotor to MSC–derived EVs, enabling chemotaxis across the diseased microenvironment, mitochondrial access, and multistage intervention [33]. In spinal cord injury, macrophage membrane–camouflaged curcumin biomimetic nanoparticles have been reported to home to inflamed lesions and attenuate neuroinflammation and ferroptosis, while ROS-responsive hybrid exosomes restore mitochondrial function and improve locomotor outcomes [34, 35]. These strategies primarily exploit microenvironmental cues or immune-mimetic membranes to achieve lesion-level homing and broad anti-inflammatory actions. Distinct from microenvironment-responsive carriers, a receptor-level and cell-type specific strategy is adopted in which EVs display an anti-DAT single-chain variable fragment to enable selective uptake by dopaminergic neurons while curcumin serves as the therapeutic payload. In the present study, HEK293T cell-derived EVs were employed as a DDS for curcumin therapy. In addition to their scalability for producing homogeneous EV populations with optimized drug-loading capacity, HEK293T cells offer high transfection efficiency for genetic engineering [36–38]. Accordingly, the HEK293T-derived EVs were engineered with anti-DAT specificity to enable targeted curcumin delivery into dopaminergic neurons, enhancing its bioavailability and prolonging therapeutic effects.

The neurotoxin 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) is a hydroxylated analogue of dopamine widely used to generate Parkinson’s models both in vivo and in vitro [39–41]. 6-OHDA not only inhibits mitochondrial respiratory complex I after entering cells, but also induces oxidative stress, autophagy, and the accumulation of α-synuclein [39]. This phenomenon can also be observed in many in vivo models. Therefore, in this study, we used a 6-OHDA-induced model of Parkinson’s disease. In vitro and in vivo data demonstrated that precise and sustained release of curcumin from engineered αDAT EVs significantly mitigated PD pathology and slowed disease progression by inhibiting α-Syn aggregation, restoring lysosomal function, and protecting mitochondria from oxidative stress. This study integrates the neuroprotective properties of curcumin with the precision targeting of engineered EVs, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for improving dopaminergic neuron survival and alleviating PD progression (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and Ham’s F-12 Nutrient Mix (F-12) (both from Thermo Fisher), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, US-sourced Hyclone, GE), 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% GlutaMAX™ Supplement, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (all from Thermo Fisher) in a humidified 37 °C/5% CO₂ environment. Immortalized human microglia-hTERT cells were cultured in microglia medium (MM, Sciencell) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a humidified 37 °C/5% CO₂ environment.

Construction and expression of CD63-anti-DAT fusion proteins

αDAT-specific scFv was screened using the HuScL-6® Human Single Domain Antibody Library (Creative Biolabs). Briefly, after four rounds of ELISA binding affinity selection, the αDAT scFv-phage fusion clone from the 4th eluate was selected. Then, this scFv was used to build the αDAT scFv-CD63 chimeric construct by synthesizing DNA corresponding to the leader peptide sequence 5¢-ATGGCCCTCCCTGTCACCGCCCTGCTGCTTCCGCTGGCTCTTCTGCTCCACGCCGCTCGGCCC-3¢. Regarding the αDAT scFv-CD63 construction, full-length αDAT scFv was inserted into CD63 at the 135th amino acid, and the chimera was linked with six-repeated His-tag and AausFP1 and cloned into a pEXO plasmid (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA).

Generation of DAT scFv-CD63-overexpressing HEK293T stable cell line

To generate αDAT scFv-CD63-overexpressing HEK293T cells (referred to as HEK293T-αDAT cells), we transfected pEXO plasmids encoding αDAT scFv-CD63 chimeric protein into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen). After selection using puromycin (1 ~ 4 µg/ml for HEK293T cells) for ≥ 20 days. The purity of αDAT expression in HEK293T cells was further evaluated by flow cytometry using an anti-scFv antibody (GenScript, Piscataway, NJ, USA). Cells were labeled with 2 µL of iFluor 647-conjugated anti-scFv antibody (GenScript) and 5 µL of FITC-conjugated anti-CD63 antibody (Abcam). The mixture was incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 1 h. Flow cytometry analysis was then performed using a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD FACS, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with 488-nm and 640-nm lasers. Unmodified EVs served as background controls. Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo software to generate and interpret detailed plots.

EV manufacture, isolation, and concentration

The wildtype HEK293T or HEK293T-αDAT cells (2 × 108 cells) were uniformly distributed on the surface of carriers placed in a CelCradle® benchtop bioreactor (ESCO Aster, Singapore) and cultured with 500 mL of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with exosome-depleted FBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and a 1% penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin B solution (Gibco). After 3–4 days of culture, the medium was collected and filtered through a 0.22-µm filter, followed by tangential flow filtration (MAP.03-plus TFF System; Lefo Science, Taipei, Taiwan) with a 300-kDa cut-off membrane. The final EV-enriched sample was collected and purified through a Protein L-based ScFv-capturing resin (GenScript). The αDAT scFv-CD63-expressing EVs (αDAT EVs) were then eluted by a chromatography system (ÄKTA™, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). The eluted αDAT-EV supernatants were subjected to concentration through a 30-kDa cut-off membrane and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco).

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis for the binding affinity of αDAT EVs

SPR analysis (BIAcore T200, Biacore-GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA) was performed using CM5 chips. The αDAT EVs (3 × 1010 particles) were resuspended in PBS, immobilized on the chips with maximum surface retention, followed by the surface preparation process, and the condition with a higher surface concentration of recombinant human or rat DAT proteins (25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.125, 1.5625, and 0.78125 nM) on the chip was selected. The regeneration method mode was performed for the regeneration scouting and surface performance test, followed by binding analysis and direct binding assessments to determine protein binding. Finally, kinetic analysis and mass transfer were selected for the kinetic assay concomitantly with the binding experiments. The data were analyzed using kinetic constants to determine the KD values.

Protein analysis via western blotting

The EVs or cells were lysed by adding an equal volume of 2X lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 ℃ to clarify the lysate. The supernatant was mixed with 6X sample buffer and denatured in a 95 ℃ dry bath for 10 min. Each sample, containing 20 µg of protein, was subjected to electrophoresis on a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies against CD9 (abclonal, MA, USA), CD63 (abclonal), CD81 (abclonal), Alix (abclonal), TSG101 (abclonal), ScFv (GenScript), α-Syn (abclonal), PARKIN (abclonal), TH (abclonal), DJ-1 (abclonal), NF-B (abclonal), p-NF-B (abclonal), STAT6 (abclonal), p-STAT6 (abclonal), PPARγ (abclonal), NRF-2 (abclonal), and β-actin (abclonal) and incubated overnight at 4 ℃, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for protein detection. Chemiluminescent reagents were applied for signal visualization, and the Western Blot Imaging System Azure c400 (Azure Biosystems Dublin, CA Inc., Dublin, CA) was utilized for signal detection.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

NTA measurements were conducted using the ZetaView instrument (Particle Metrix, Microtrac, Meerbusch, Germany) to evaluate the size distribution, concentration, and zeta potential of free and curcumin-loaded EVs. Calibration was performed using 100 nm polystyrene beads according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Samples were filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane filter and diluted in PBS to a final volume of 1 mL, ensuring a particle count of 50–200 per field of view. Analyses were performed at 11 distinct positions to obtain precise size distribution curves and concentration data. Zeta potential measurements were conducted at two positions for each sample to ensure consistency.

Preparation and analysis of curcumin-loaded αDAT EVs

Curcumin loading of EVs by sonication in combination with human albumin has been described previously.28 Briefly, the unmodified or DAT-targeting EVs were mixed with human albumin, and the albumin-EV (1 µg albumin: 1 µg of EV protein) mixture was sonicated (Tekmar sonic disruptor) using 6 cycles of 30 s on/off with 2-min cooling on ice between each cycle. After being filtered through a 100 kDa cut-off membrane, curcumin was added into the albumin-EV resuspensions (1 µg albumin: 1 µg of EV protein: 1 µg curcumin) and then sonicated using 6 cycles of 30 s on/off with 2-min cooling between each cycle. Subsequently, the albumin-curcumin-EV mixtures were filtered with a 30 kDa cut-off membrane twice and then resuspended in PBS for subsequent experiments. The encapsulated curcumin in each reaction was evaluated by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis using the plots of curcumin standard curves. A UV-visible spectrophotometer was employed to analyze the samples for curcumin absorption values. The absorbance values of αDAT EVs, curcumin, curcumin-loaded EVs (Cur@EVs), and Cur@αDAT EVs were measured across a wavelength range of 300–700 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer to assess the encapsulation efficiency and stability of curcumin within the EVs. Additionally, nanoflow cytometry (CytoFLEX nano, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) was used to analyze the loading efficiency of curcumin into αDAT EVs. αDAT EVs were labeled with the ExoGlow™-Protein EV labeling kit (System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, αDAT EVs were incubated with ExoGlow™ dye, which specifically binds to exosomal proteins, for 30 min at 37 °C. The free dye was separated by 3k concentrator columns at 12,000 g. Acquisition settings were then configured as follows: Small Particle Mode = ON, Flow Rate = SLOW (3.66 µL/min), Thresholds = ZERO, Trigger Channels = NONE, FSC/SSC Laser Power = 1%, 488 nm Laser Power = 25%, and if antibodies were used, 642 Laser Power = 50% and 405 Laser Power = 50%. Stopping criteria were set to TIME, and data were collected for specified durations.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) preparation and staining

For TEM analysis, a phosphotungstic acid solution (4%) was prepared by dissolving phosphotungstic acid in deionized water (ddH2O). This solution was then filtered through a 0.22-µm filter to ensure purity. TEM samples were prepared by placing 5.5 µL of the sample upside down on a carbon-coated copper grid and allowing it to sit for 10–15 min. Excess liquid was gently absorbed using filter paper. Subsequently, the grids were fixed with 5.5 µL of 4% glutaraldehyde for 10 min. The grids were then washed with ddH2O, and excess water was removed using filter paper. The samples were dried at 37 °C overnight before TEM imaging.

In vitro release profile of Cur@EV and Cur@αDAT EVs

First, the prepared Cur@EV and Cur@αDAT EVs were placed in dialysis bags with a molecular weight cutoff of 3.5 kDa and immersed in a release human plasma (pH 7.4, BioIVT, Westbury, NY, USA) to mimic physiological conditions. The release system was maintained at 37 °C in a constant temperature shaker with gentle shaking (100 rpm). At predetermined various time-points, 50 µL of the release plasma was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh plasma. Based on the curcumin concentrations, the cumulative release profile was calculated and plotted to compare Cur@EV and Cur@αDAT EVs.

6-OHDA-induced SH-SY5Y PD in vitro model

SH-SY5Y cells were differentiated into a neuronal-like phenotype using a standard protocol. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, neuronal differentiation was initiated by treating the cells with 50 µM retinoic acid (RA) for two days, with medium changes every other day. After RA pre-treatment, the medium was replaced with one containing 50 nM 12-O-Tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate (TPA) for an additional five days to further promote neuronal differentiation. Then, the cells were treated using 6-OHDA concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 150 µM for 24 h and the cytotoxicity was evaluated using PrestoBlue. Subsequent experiments all used 6-OHDA-treated SH-SY5Y cells for 24 h to induce the PD model in vitro.

Visualization of EVs uptake

αDAT EVs were tagged with the ExoGlow-Protein EV Labeling Kit (System Biosciences). The EV pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of PBS, followed by adding 1 µL of 500X ExoGlow-Protein labeling dye. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 350 rpm for 20 min to ensure efficient labeling. After incubation, the labeled EVs were collected by centrifuging at 21,000 ×g for 10 min, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 200 µL of sterile PBS. For imaging, 1 × 104 cells were plated in an 8-Well µ-Slide (ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany) and exposed to the fluorescently labeled EVs. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with DAPI for nuclei visualization and Alexa Fluor™ 594 Phalloidin (Invitrogen) for cytoskeleton visualization at various time points. Imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8 X, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Immunofluorescence assays

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min to allow antibody and dye penetration. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in the blocking solution overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, cells were labeled with Goat anti-Mouse IgG, IgM (H + L) secondary antibody tagged with Alexa Fluor™ 488 for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, cells were mounted using a mounting medium and DAPI (Abcam) to stain nuclei, and fluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope with appropriate excitation and emission settings.

Live cell imaging

Cells were stained with pHrodo™ Red AM, CellMask™ Actin Deep Red Tracking Stain, and Hoechst 33,342 (Invitrogen) to visualize intracellular pH, the actin cytoskeleton, and nuclei, respectively. For pHrodo™ Red AM staining, the dye was first prepared by diluting pHrodo™ Red AM Ester in PowerLoad™ concentrate, followed by further dilution in Live Cell Imaging Solution. Cells were incubated with the staining solution at room temperature for 30 min and then washed with LCIS before imaging. To visualize F-actin, CellMask™ Actin Deep Red Tracking Stain was dissolved in DMSO to create a 1 mM stock solution and subsequently diluted to 1 µM in an imaging buffer. Cells were then incubated with this solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, Hoechst 33,342 was added at a final concentration of 5 µg/mL, and cells were incubated for 15 min at 37 °C to stain nuclei. Following staining, cells were washed with PBS and imaged using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8 X).

Cells were incubated with MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos, a membrane-permeable dye that selectively accumulates in active mitochondria based on membrane potential. The staining solution was prepared in a complete culture medium at the recommended working concentration and prewarmed to 37 °C. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow efficient mitochondrial labeling. After MitoTracker™ staining, cells were co-stained with Hoechst 33,342 to visualize the nuclei. Following staining, cells were washed with PBS to remove excess dye and immediately imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope with appropriate excitation and emission filter settings. In addition, cells were incubated with TMRM (Abcam) to assess mitochondrial membrane potential. The dye was prepared in a serum-free medium at a final concentration of 50 nM, balancing sensitivity and minimizing cytotoxicity. After 30 min of incubation at 37 °C in the dark, excess dye was removed by washing with PBS. Imaging was performed using a confocal microscope. In addition, the flow cytometry analysis of the fluorescence intensity of JC-1 green and red was used to determine the integrity of the mitochondrial membrane.

Analysis of cell bioenergetics

Mitochondrial function of 6-OHDA-induced SH-SY5Y cells received a 24 h treatment and was analyzed by the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test and Seahorse XF Real-Time ATP Rate Assay (Agilent Technologies) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. After plating at an ideal density into Seahorse XF Cell Culture Microplates, the cells were left to adhere overnight. Seahorse XF assay medium was used instead of the culture medium on the day of the experiment. The cells were then incubated for an hour at 37 °C in a non-CO₂ incubator before being measured. The Mito Stress Test measured important mitochondrial parameters such as basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, proton leak, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity by administering successive injections of oligomycin (1.5 µM), FCCP (2.0 µM), and a mixture of rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 µM each). Oligomycin and rotenone/antimycin A were used in a Real-Time ATP Rate Assay to differentiate the contributions of glycolysis and mitochondria to ATP synthesis. A Seahorse XF Pro Analyzer was used for all the measurements.

Establishment of the 6-OHDA-induced PD rat model

Animal experiments were conducted in compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Medical University (Approval No. CMUIACU-2024-). SD rats were anesthetized for surgery with 2% isoflurane. Desipramine (25 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 30 min before 6-OHDA injection to prevent injury to noradrenergic neurons [42]. Subsequently, the rats were placed on a stereotaxic frame, and 11 µg of 6-OHDA (dissolved in 3.2 µL of freshly prepared deionized distilled water) was injected into the right and left dorsal striatum (caudate–putamen complex) using a 10-µL Hamilton syringe (coordinates for bregma: medial-lateral, 3.5 mm; anteroposterior, − 0.8 mm; dorsoventral, 5 mm following the stereotaxic atlas stated in a previous study).30 The needle was left in the brain for an extra 5 min before and after the injection, and the solutions were infused at a rate of 0.2 µL/min. The sex of animals used was male only. Unless otherwise stated, group size was n = 6 animals per group. The following behavioral assessments and histological quantifications were conducted without blinding. All analysts used standardized operating procedures established prior to unmasking group labels for data assembly and statistics.

Biodistribution study

To evaluate the biodistribution of therapeutic agents in vivo, an in situ PD model was employed as described above. αDAT EVs were administered into PD model via i.v. injection. Two weeks after 6-OHDA injection, EVs were labeled with XenoLight DiR (PerkinElmer) at a concentration of 1 × 1010 particles and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min to ensure robust labeling. Following two PBS washes to remove unbound dye, the labeled EVs were resuspended in 100 µL of PBS and administered intravenously to the rat through tail vein injection. The IVIS imaging system, equipped with fluorescence filters set to 745 nm excitation and 800 nm emission, was employed at predetermined intervals to track the in vivo distribution of the injected EVs.

Assessment of motor capacity in PD animal models

To evaluate whether Cur@αDAT EVs can improve motor and cognitive functions in PD rat models, rats were acclimatized for four weeks prior to the experiment. In the rotarod test, rats were placed on a rotating rod that gradually accelerated. The apparatus comprised a horizontal rod that rotated at a pre-set speed, which can be increased incrementally. The primary measure was the time each rat can remain on the rotating rod before falling off. Before the test, rats underwent a training period to familiarize themselves with the apparatus. During the actual test, each rat was placed on the rod, and the rotation speed was gradually increased from a starting speed of 4 rpm to a maximum of 40 rpm over 300 s. The latency to fall was recorded for each trial, typically with three trials per session and an inter-trial rest period of 15 min. The performance of the rats was assessed at multiple time points, such as baseline (before treatment) and at various intervals post-treatment, to monitor changes in motor function over time. All experiments were independent with six replicates.

Open field test (OFT)

The OPF is a reliable method to assess locomotor activity. The rats were placed in an open black chamber (60 × 60 × 60 cm length-width-height). The rats were individually placed in the center of the chamber, and behaviors were measured for 30 min using a digital video camera (Panasonic WV-CP300) and analyzed using EthoVision XT 15 software (Noldus Information Technology, Leesburg, VA, USA). Total movement distance (cm), movement duration (s), rest time (s), center entry (s), time of rotation turns (s), and supported rearing (s) were analyzed. The chambers were cleansed with 70% ethanol to prevent scents from previous assays from influencing the results.

Two-object recognition task

The two-object recognition task (ORT) has been used to assess recognition memory in rats.21 The ORT was performed the day after the OFT in the same open black chamber. The rats were allowed to explore two identical objects (T1) for 10 min to ensure they could recognize them. After 2 h, the rats were recorded in the chamber for 20 min, and one of the familiar objects (T1) was replaced with an unfamiliar object (T2). The location of the new object was balanced across the group. The amount of time each rat spent interacting with each object was recorded using EthoVision XT 15 software (Noldus Information Technology). Exploratory behavior was considered only when the rat’s nose made contact with or was oriented toward an object at a distance of ≤ 2 cm; turning around or sitting on the object did not qualify as exploratory behavior. In each trial and the within-subject trials, we counterbalanced T1 or T2 object positions (left or right side of the arena). We calculated the time spent by rats exploring the T1 and T2 objects (ΔT = T2 − T1). The chambers and objects were cleaned with 70% ethanol before the ORT to prevent scents from previous assays from influencing the results.

Determination of cur in serum and substantia Nigra

The rat blood serum and proteins lysed from the substantia nigra were subjected to dilution with two volumes of methanol, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected for analysis. Quantification of Cur was performed using an Agilent 1260 HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) equipped with a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The detection wavelengths were set at 420 nm for Cur.

Immunohistochemical analysis

After 8 weeks of treatment, the rats were sacrificed to collect the striatum and substantia nigra for evaluation. Following euthanasia, rat brains were excised and fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight. The tissues were then dehydrated using a series of graded alcohol concentrations and embedded in paraffin. Thin sections of 3 μm were prepared from these paraffin blocks for subsequent immunohistochemical analysis. The expression and localization of DAT, α-Syn, and TH were assessed using IHC. In brief, tissue sections were initially treated with a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 15 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by a blocking step to minimize non-specific binding. Primary antibodies were applied at a 1:100 dilution and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Following primary antibody incubation, sections were exposed to a biotinylated secondary antibody and amplified using a room-temperature polymer system for 20 min. Color development was achieved using a DAB substrate, and counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to visualize nuclear and cytoplasmic details. In addition, the fluorescence image was also used to confirm the location of curcumin in the slices.

Antibody array assay of substantia Nigra

For protein samples from the substantia nigra, the tissue was harvested from the sacrificed animals. The protein expression levels in the localized substantia nigra were measured with rat neuron Discovery antibody array C1 (RayBiotech, Norcross, GA, United States), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, protein extracts were diluted to 500 mg/mL with blocking buffer and added to the array pools printed with corresponding anti-cytokine antibodies for overnight incubation. After washing, a biotin-conjugated anti-cytokine mix was incubated with the pools for 2 h. Finally, the gallery arrays were performed with Cy3-conjugated streptavidin, and the membrane series arrays were incubated with HRP-conjugated streptavidin for another 2 h. Chemiluminescent reagents were applied for signal visualization, and the Imaging System Azure c400 was utilized for signal detection. The signals were read and normalized using an internal positive control.

ELISA

The concentrations of α-Syn, TNF-α, IFN-γ and IL-10 in the plasma were measured using ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocols (Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA). Samples were collected as plasma. Plasma samples were collected using EDTA-Na₂ as an anticoagulant and centrifuged within 30 min of collection at 2000×g for 15 min. The reaction was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance among treatment groups was determined using one-way ANOVA, followed by Scheffe’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses, ensuring the reliability of the observed differences between experimental conditions.

Results

Generation and characterization of the anti-DAT scFv-expressing EVs

An anti-DAT scFv-CD63 chimeric construct was developed by inserting the anti-DAT scFv into the extracellular loop between the third and fourth transmembrane domains of the exosomal tetraspanin protein CD63 (Fig. 2A). This fusion construct was transfected into HEK293T cells to establish a stable clone. The surface expression of αDAT-scFv-CD63 was further confirmed through flow cytometry analysis using fluorescent dye-labeled antibodies specifically targeting CD63 and scFv on HEK293T cells (Fig. S1A and S1B) and EVs (Fig. S2A and S2B). The supernatants harvested from the stable clone were subjected to a tangential flow filtration (TFF) system and a chromatography system with a Protein L-based ScFv-capturing resin column to obtain the αDAT scFv-expressing EVs. EVs were then loaded with curcumin by sonication. The binding affinity of αDAT EVs to recombinant human DAT protein was assessed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis, with a KD value of 6.4 × 10− 9 M (Fig. 2B). Exosomal markers, including CD9, CD63, CD81, and TSG101, were monitored. The CD63 blot showed multiple bands in the DAT-targeting EV sample compared to unmodified EVs, suggesting the expression of the αDAT-scFv-CD63 fusion protein (Fig. 2C). HPLC confirmed curcumin encapsulation in αDAT EVs, showing a prominent peak co-eluting with the curcumin standard and absent in unloaded EVs (Fig. 2D). Curcumin loading into αDAT EVs yielded an encapsulation efficiency (EE) of 31.3 ± 4.2% across independent preparations (Fig. 2E). In addition, the nanoflow cytometry analysis also showed that 88.3% of αDAT EVs were loaded with curcumin (Fig. 2F). These characteristics suggest that the drug loading and modifications did not significantly affect the inherent properties of EVs. The excellent drug loading (DL) efficiency (Cur@EVs: 0.25 ± 0.04 pg/EV and Cur@αDAT EVs: 0.26 ± 0.05 pg/EV) also indicated their high drug loading capacity, especially for the poorly soluble curcumin, thereby improving its dissolution rate and maximizing the therapeutic effect. To further observe the situation of loading the curcumin, high-resolution fluorescence imaging revealed that the αDAT signal was located on the outside of EVs, while curcumin was located inside EVs (Fig. 2G). The results demonstrated that curcumin was encapsulated in the αDAT EV. The maintenance of spherical characteristics (Fig. 2H and Fig. S3A) and size distribution (133.5 ± 8.1 nm, Fig. 2I and Fig. S3B) were assessed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Of the aforementioned characteristics, the engineered scFv-CD63 modification does not significantly affect the fundamental characteristics of the EV. However, the charge of the EVs after loading Cur was slightly reduced (Fig. S3C). Since Cur itself is negatively charged, it is speculated that some Cur is absorbed onto the EV surface during loading [43]. We further determined the release profile of curcumin from EV and αDAT EV at 37 °C for 72 h in a pH 7.4 buffer. The results indicated that the cumulative release of Cur@EV and Cur@αDAT EVs reached ∼40% within 12 h, and reached ∼65% a 48 h (Fig. 2J).

Targeted delivery of Cur@αDAT EVs effectively protected 6-OHDA-induced neurotoxicity in vitro

An in vitro PD neuron model was established using differentiated and 6-OHDA-induced SH-SY5Y cells [44]. In order to simulate the in vivo blood-brain barrier (BBB), primary microvascular brain endothelial cells (HBMECs), human brain vascular pericytes (HBVPs), and human astrocytes (HA) were also laden in collagen to establish an in vitro BBB model (Figure S4A). The trans-epithelial/endothelial electrical resistance (TEER) value of the BBB layer was measured to be 2144 Ω.cm², indicating robust barrier formation. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the integrity of the BBB model, showing F-actin (red) and nuclei (blue) (Fig. S4B). A cross-sectional view of the BBB model illustrated the organization of HBMECs, HBVPs, and HAs (Fig. S4C). This model was used for all in vitro experiments in this study. After administering Cur@αDAT EVs into the upper insert of the BBB model, the precision delivery of curcumin across the BBB towards the differentiated SH-SY5Y cells in the bottom well was further evaluated (with 94.3% of curcumin-encapsulated αDAT EVs, Fig. S5). To determine the suitable concentration of 6-OHDA for establishing the in vitro PD model, differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were treated with various concentrations of 6-OHDA (12.5–150 µM) for 24 h. A dose-dependent decrease in cell viability was observed, and 50 µM was selected for subsequent experiments as it consistently induced moderate neurotoxicity without causing excessive cell death (Fig. 3A). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed high DAT expression on SH-SY5Y cells, validating them as an appropriate recipient cell model for Cur@αDAT EVs delivery (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analysis showed efficient uptake of αDAT EVs by the SH-SY5Y cells, with higher levels of curcumin delivery compared to that by unmodified EVs (Fig. 3C). The protective effect of the targeted curcumin delivery mediated by αDAT EVs was subsequently evaluated in the in vitro PD cell model. After Cur@αDAT EVs treatment, ROS production was reduced compared to that by curcumin alone and unmodified EV treatment in the 6-OHDA-treated SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 3D), and the level of malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation, was also significantly reduced (Fig. 3E), supporting its antioxidative protective effect. Western blot analysis further revealed that the level of phosphorylated α-synuclein (p-α-Syn, Y129), which was abnormally elevated in PD cells, was effectively reduced by Cur@αDAT EVs. In addition, the expression levels of PARKIN, TH, and DJ-1 were notably restored (Fig. 3F). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated the restored morphology of the SH-SY5Y cells, the reduction of α-Syn accumulation, and enhanced tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) expression in the Cur@αDAT EV treatment group compared with those in the other treatment groups (Fig. 3G and H). Additionally, the relative viability of the 6-OHDA-treated SH-SY5Y cells was significantly higher in the Cur@αDAT EVs group than in the other groups (Fig. 3I), implying that Cur@αDAT EVs may effectively reduce neuronal damage and improve their survival rate. Moreover, the extracellular contents of α-Syn, TNF-α, and IFN-γ were reduced, whereas IL-10 levels were increased in Cur@αDAT EVs-treated SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. S6). To further validate the targeted delivery of Cur@αDAT EVs, PD iPSC-derived neurons identified by MAP2 and NeuN expression (Fig. S7A) were used as an alternative in vitro model. Western blot analysis demonstrated a higher abundance of α-Syn in PD neurons compared to those in healthy controls (Fig. S7B). Notably, αDAT EVs exhibited significantly higher uptake (53.2%) by PD neurons than unmodified EVs (4.8%) (Fig. S7C). This selective uptake of Cur@αDAT EVs in PD iPSC-derived neurons aligns with the reduction of α-Syn and restored levels of PARKIN and DJ-1 in SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. S7D). Taken together, the in vitro models demonstrated that the DAT-targeting EV delivery platform may enhance the precise delivery and efficacy of curcumin in DAT-expressing neurons in response to neurotoxic agents.

Cur@αDAT EVs enhance the autophagy-lysosomal pathway by modulating the mTOR-TFEB axis in the 6-OHDA-treated SH-SY5Y cells

TFEB, a key regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy, is crucial in clearing pathological protein aggregates in PD. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that Cur@αDAT EVs treatment in PD-induced SH-SY5Y cells significantly promoted TFEB nuclear translocation compared with the vehicle control (PD) and other treatment groups (Fig. 4B). Additionally, the cytoplasmic TFEB level in the Cur@αDAT EVs group was significantly decreased. In contrast, the nuclear TFEB level was sharply increased than in the other groups (Fig. 4C). The relative levels of p-AKT to total AKT (pAKT/AKT) and p-mTOR/mTOR indicate the degree of activation of the AKT-mTOR signaling pathway. Compared with the PD group and the other treatment groups, the levels of p-AKT/AKT and p-mTOR/mTOR were the lowest after Cur@αDAT EVs treatment, suggesting that the AKT-mTOR signaling pathway was inhibited (Fig. 4D). Immunofluorescence imaging further demonstrated restored cytoskeletal integrity (F-actin, green) and increased acidic (pHrodo fluorescence, red) in 6-OHDA-induced SH-SY5Y cells following Cur@αDAT EVs treatment, suggesting improved endosomal uptake and intracellular trafficking compared to treatments with free curcumin and curcumin loaded in unmodified EVs (Cur@EVs) (Fig. 4E). Specifically, lysosomal pH analysis using pHrodo showed a marked shift toward increased lysosomal acidity after Cur@αDAT EVs treatment, with the proportion harboring acidic lysosomes rising from 4.5% at Day 0 to 67.9% at Day 3 (Fig. 4F). This shift reflects enhanced lysosomal function and autophagy in 6-OHDA-induced SH-SY5Y cells post Cur@αDAT EVs treatment. These findings demonstrate that Cur@αDAT EVs effectively regulated the TFEB-mTOR axis, promoting autophagy and lysosomal function and offering a promising strategy for PD treatment.

Cur@αDAT EVs ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic capacity in the 6-OHDA-induced SH-SY5Y cells

Mitochondrial impairment is a central pathological feature of PD, contributing to neuronal degeneration through bioenergetic failure and oxidative stress. MitoTracker fluorescence analysis demonstrated that Cur@αDAT EVs markedly increased mitochondrial content compared to the vehicle PD and other treatment groups, indicating enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and turnover (Fig. 5B and C). Tetramethylrhodamine, methyl ester (TMRM) staining further revealed a significant restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential in the Cur@αDAT EVs group, surpassing the effects of free curcumin and unmodified EVs treatments (Fig. 5D and E). Furthermore, western blot analysis confirmed the upregulation of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), a key regulator of mitochondrial transcription and replication, supporting the role of Cur@αDAT EVs in promoting mitochondrial homeostasis (Fig. 5F). In addition, flow cytometry analysis indicated that Cur@αDAT EVs significantly increased the proportion of mitochondria-active cells (Fig. 5G). Cellular ATP profiling revealed a higher proportion of mitochondrial-derived ATP (MitoATP) in the Cur@αDAT EVs group relative to glycolytic ATP (GlycoATP), suggesting improved mitochondrial respiration efficiency (Fig. 5H). Seahorse assay data further showed that Cur@αDAT EVs enhanced the oxygen consumption rate (OCR), indicating improved oxidative phosphorylation capacity (Fig. 5I). These findings suggest that Cur@αDAT EVs mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction and improve energy metabolism in PD, thereby preserving neuronal viability and bioenergetic stability.

Cur@αDAT EVs accumulate in the brain and improve motor function in the 6-OHDA-induced PD rats

Efficient brain-targeted delivery is essential for therapeutic efficacy in PD. Hemolysis assay results showed that EVs did not cause hemolysis in vitro, supporting their biocompatibility and safety for intravenous administration (Fig. S8). Biodistribution analysis revealed that αDAT EVs preferentially accumulated in the brain, with reduced off-target distribution to peripheral organs such as the liver and spleen compared with unmodified EVs (Fig. 6A). In SPR analysis to evaluate the binding affinity of αDAT EV to recombinant rat DAT protein, the KD value was 2.0 × 10− 9 M (Fig. S9). In addition, sustained retention of αDAT EVs in the brain for up to seven days post-injection was observed (Fig. 6B). Immunofluorescence staining of brain tissues further validated enhanced accumulation of αDAT EVs in MAP2-positive neurons, with clear co-localization of ExoGlow signals and DAT expression, confirming targeted neuronal delivery (Fig. 6C). To assess functional recovery, behavioral assessments were conducted during the eight weeks following treatment (Fig. 6D). There was no difference in the body weight of rats in all treatment groups (Fig. S10). The rotarod performance assessment demonstrated that Cur@αDAT EVs significantly improved motor coordination, allowing PD rats to sustain a longer running time compared to the other groups (Fig. 6E and supplement Movie 1). Locomotion analysis in the open field test revealed increased total movement distance and velocity in the Cur@αDAT EVs group, indicating enhanced motor function and reduced PD-related deficits (Fig. 6F and supplement Movie 2). Additionally, increased speed (Fig. 6H) and decreased total rest time (Fig. 6I) in cur@αDAT EVs treated rats indicate improved mobility and exploratory behavior. Moreover, the results of the object recognition memory test showed a positive trend in time changes at weeks 4 and 8, suggesting that the cognitive decline in PD rats was alleviated (Fig. 6J). These results underscore the therapeutic potential of Cur@αDAT EVs for PD, emphasizing their precise brain-targeting capability and ability to restore motor function, making them a promising candidate for neurodegenerative disease interventions.

Cur@αDAT EVs mitigate pathological protein accumulation and promote neuroprotection in 6-OHDA-induced PD rats

To further evaluate the therapeutic effects of Cur@αDAT EVs on dopaminergic neuron integrity in PD rats, the rats were sacrificed and their brains were collected after Cur@αDAT EVs therapy, as shown in Fig. 7A. Pharmacokinetic analysis showed that curcumin encapsulated in αDAT EVs or unmodified EVs had prolonged circulation and higher systemic retention compared to free curcumin, indicating improved bioavailability and sustained drug delivery (Fig. 7B). Histological analysis revealed that Cur@αDAT EVs significantly reduced α-Syn accumulation while restoring TH and DAT expression in the substantia nigra, suggesting enhanced dopaminergic neuron survival (Fig. 7C). Additionally, DAT and TH performance in the ventral tegmental area and striatum remained higher, whereas α-Syn accumulation was reduced in Cur@αDAT EVs-treated PD rats compared to other groups (Fig. S10). Moreover, curcumin was retained in the striatum of Cur@αDAT EVs-treated rats, suggesting improved bioavailability and targeted delivery. HPLC analysis further confirmed higher curcumin retention in brain tissues of Cur@αDAT EVs-treated rats, supporting the efficacy of this targeted DDS (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, cytokine profiling indicated that Cur@αDAT EVs upregulated neurotrophic factors and anti-inflammatory cytokines, including BDNF, NGF, VEGF, IL-10, and CNTF (Fig. 7E). TUNEL staining of major organs revealed no significant apoptotic cell death in peripheral tissues across all treatment groups, supporting the systemic safety of Cur@αDAT EVs (Fig. S12). Meanwhile, pro-inflammatory markers (IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) were significantly reduced, demonstrating a shift toward an anti-inflammatory microenvironment. The Cur@αDAT EVs group exhibited a significant reduction in plasma α-Syn, TNF-α, and IFN-γ levels, indicating effective suppression of neuroinflammation (Fig. S13A–C). Concurrently, IL-10 levels were markedly elevated, suggesting a shift toward an anti-inflammatory state that may support neuronal survival (Fig. S13D). Notably, immunofluorescence staining showed increased expression of Nestin and Ki67, suggesting enhanced neurogenesis and regenerative potential in the substantia nigra of the Cur@αDAT EVs-treated rats (Fig. 7F). These results highlight the therapeutic potential of Cur@αDAT EVs in PD, demonstrating their ability to mitigate protein aggregation, reduce neuroinflammation, and promote neuronal survival and regeneration.

Discussion

By genetically engineering HEK293T cells, we successfully generated modified EVs displaying anti-DAT scFv-CD63 chimeric proteins on their surface, enabling specific targeting of dopaminergic neurons and overcoming the critical challenge in CNS drug delivery. The enhanced targeting capability and superior neuronal uptake of Cur@αDAT EVs align with recent advances highlighting the importance of precise targeting in neurodegenerative therapies [45, 46]. These improvements resulted in substantial therapeutic benefits, including reduced oxidative stress, decreased α-Syn accumulation, and the restoration of neuroprotective activities by upregulating critical neuroprotective proteins such as PARKIN and DJ-1.

Curcumin is well-known for its antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties and has previously shown promise in neurodegenerative disease treatment. However, its clinical utility is limited by challenges such as bioavailability and BBB permeability [47, 48]. Curcumin is rapidly degraded in the bloodstream, necessitating a delivery strategy that prolongs its circulation time and ensures brain-specific accumulation [49]. Our DAT-targeting EVs-based drug-delivery system effectively overcomes the pharmacokinetic barriers of curcumin. Encapsulation within αDAT-targeting EVs not only prolongs its circulation time but also enhances its delivery to the substantia nigra via DAT-mediated targeting. Specifically, Cur@αDAT EVs treatment showed a marked enhancement in lysosomal function, as indicated by increased pHrodo signals (from 4.5% to 67.9%, Fig. 4F), reflecting successful activation of the cellular degradation machinery. Cur@αDAT EVs treatment significantly promoted nuclear translocation of TFEB and activation of the autophagy-lysosome pathway (Fig. 4), facilitating the clearance of pathological protein aggregates and restoring mitochondrial functions (Fig. 5), both critical factors in PD pathogenesis [50–52]. Furthermore, curcumin’s emerging role in promoting mitochondrial quality control via the PINK1/PARKIN pathway aligns with our findings (Fig. 3 and Fig. S7), reinforcing its potential as a neuroprotective agent [53]. The dual modulation of autophagy-lysosomal pathways and mitochondrial homeostasis highlights the efficacy of our strategy. By effectively delivering curcumin to dopaminergic neurons, the DAT-targeting EVs platform provides a comprehensive therapeutic strategy with broad applicability beyond PD, offering potential treatment avenues for other neurodegenerative diseases.

In the histological analyses, Cur@αDAT EVs treatment significantly preserved TH expression within the brain tissues (Fig. S11), implying its neuroprotective effect based on the function of TH in dopaminergic neurons [54, 55]. The preservation of TH is known to correlate directly with restored striatal dopamine level and reduced neuroinflammation [56]. The reduced microglial activation and lower pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion after Cur@αDAT EVs treatment emphasize the importance of this DAT-targeting EVs platform in delivering curcumin precisely, thereby protecting dopaminergic neurons and restoring neurotransmitter balance as well. Consistently, behavioral assessments further validated the therapeutic efficacy of Cur@αDAT EVs. The motor function analysis including assessments of motor coordination and bradykinesia has been demonstrated in previous studies to evaluate the motor neuron activities in neurodegenerative animal models [57]. In the rotarod test, Cur@αDAT EVs-treated rats maintained a relatively high improvement of motor performance compared with the vehicle PD rats and other treatment groups (Fig. 6D). In a separate validation cohort, we also assessed the therapeutic efficacy of Cur@αDAT EVs in PD rats with more advanced motor impairments. The rotarod and open field tests in these severely affected animals demonstrated comparable improvements in motor coordination and locomotor activity to those observed in the standard PD model (Fig. S14 and movie 3). These findings reinforce the robustness of Cur@αDAT EVs across different stages of disease severity, supporting their translational potential in diverse PD populations. Additionally, Cur@αDAT EVs significantly reduced microglial activation and pro-inflammatory responses, correlating with improved cognitive function and reduced anxiety-like behaviors across multiple paradigms, including open-field and object recognition tests (Fig. 6J). This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that curcumin can ameliorate cognitive deficits in neurodegenerative models by modulating neuroinflammation and enhancing neurogenesis [58]. These comprehensive histological, biochemical, and behavioral findings strongly support Cur@αDAT EVs treatment as a promising therapeutic strategy, offering neuronal protection and functional recovery in PD.

In recent years, treatment strategies for PD have expanded beyond neuronal repair to include regulation of the surrounding immune environment, particularly microglial modulation [59]. Recent studies further support the presence of functional DAT in peripheral immune cells, including human monocyte-derived macrophages and PBMCs. Notably, PD patients exhibit increased DAT⁺/TH⁺ immune cells, suggesting a disease-associated dopaminergic immunophenotype that may inform microglia-targeting strategies [60, 61]. Moderate DAT immunoreactivity was detected in Iba1⁺ microglia within the substantia nigra of PD brain tissue (Fig. S15A), suggesting that a subset of microglia may acquire DAT expression under pathological conditions. To further evaluate receptor dependence and direct anti-inflammatory actions, DAT-expressing PD-mimic microglia were generated as a mechanistic probe (Fig. S15B), exhibiting high levels of DAT (67.2%, Fig. S15C) and CD86 (83.4%, Fig. S15D). αDAT EVs were internalized by these cells more efficiently than unmodified EVs (Fig. S15E). Additionally, Cur@αDAT EVs significantly reduced ROS (Fig. S16A) and downregulated p-NF-κB while upregulating p-STAT6, PPARγ, and NRF2, indicating an anti-inflammatory shift (Fig. S16B). Furthermore, the surface markers of microglial revealed that Cur@αDAT EVs promoted a phenotypic shift to CD206+-expressing microglia (M2 type), further supporting their immunomodulatory effects (Fig. S16C). This anti-inflammatory modulation is consistent with previous studies showing that PPARγ activation inhibits NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammation and attenuates microglial activation and inflammatory responses [62]. In addition, in vivo immunofluorescence analysis of the substantia nigra (Fig. S17) demonstrated that Cur@αDAT EVs treatment markedly reduced the number of CD86⁺ (M1-type) microglia while increasing CD206⁺ (M2-type) cells. The shift in M1/M2 marker distribution indicates a local anti-inflammatory transition within the lesioned tissue, complementing the in vitro evidence shown in Fig. S16. M2 microglia can directly stimulate the synthesis of neurotrophins as well as the expression and function of their receptors, which not only stimulates neuronal survival but also prevents neuronal apoptosis [63]. Notably, Cur@αDAT EVs-treated microglia exhibited the highest levels of BDNF secreted into the culture medium (Fig. S16D). In a previous study, M2 microglia promote phagocytosis of cell debris and misfolded proteins, promote tissue repair, and support neuron survival by various neurotrophic factors, such as BDNF [64, 65]. The elevated BDNF expression further underscores the system’s neuroprotective potential by supporting neuronal survival, regeneration, and synaptic plasticity [66]—critical factors for long-term functional recovery in PD, implying that Cur@αDAT EVs may play a role in promoting neuronal survival and repair mechanisms through immune regulation of microglia. Furthermore, many astrocytes in the substantia nigra also expressed DAT (Fig. S18A), and the number of astrocytes in the substantia nigra increased after treatment with Cur@αDAT EVs, most of which expressed the marker of A2 type astrocytes (S100A10, Fig. S18B). In vitro, flow cytometry confirmed DAT expression in primary astrocytes (Fig. S19A) and demonstrated a clear shift from the A1 (C3⁺) to the A2 (S100A10⁺) phenotype following Cur@αDAT EVs exposure (Fig. S19B). Correspondingly, the Cur@αDAT EVs-treated group showed the highest secretion levels of BDNF, VEGF, and NGF (Fig. S19C–E). Collectively, these findings highlight the supportive role of astrocytes in shaping the neurotrophic and inflammatory environment. The increased GFAP⁺/S100A10⁺ astrocyte population and elevated secretion of BDNF, VEGF, and NGF after Cur@αDAT EVs treatment suggest that astrocytes contribute to creating a more regenerative and less inflammatory milieu. This interpretation aligns with recent studies showing that astrocytes actively regulate dopaminergic neuron survival, neuroinflammation, and oxidative balance in PD models [61, 67]. Although astrocytic changes were not the primary focus of this work, these results indicate that Cur@αDAT EVs may also indirectly modulate astrocyte activity, thereby enhancing the overall trophic support within the lesion area. These findings should be interpreted as suggestive, and a dedicated study dissecting EV–astrocyte interactions in PD models is underway.

Conclusion

Collectively, we developed an innovative approach utilizing modified EVs derived from genetically engineering HEK293T cells overexpressing α DAT chimeric CD63 to precisely deliver curcumin for PD treatment. The αDAT EVs target DAT-positive neurons, enhancing curcumin internalization and consequently modulating inflammation and regeneration of these cells. Compared to free curcumin treatment, the Cur@αDAT EVs exhibit enhanced efficacy by activating the autophagy-lysosome pathway, improving mitochondrial bioenergetics, and facilitating cellular clearance. Additionally, Cur@αDAT EVs modulate the immune microenvironment by promoting M2 microglia polarization and support neuronal repair by stimulating BDNF levels. Taken together, these findings highlight αDAT EVs as a targeted drug delivery platform with the potential to revolutionize PD treatment through precise and efficient drug administration.
