A qualitative approach to interrogating the age and gender divide in digital SRHR platforms in Kenya
Authors: Joan Njagi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescent sexual and reproductive health and rights (ASRHR) programmes for 10–17-year-old adolescents in many African contexts are school-based and often aim to manage the risks associated with adolescent sexuality. Such programmes are critiqued for adopting pathologising, biomedical, moralistic and/or authoritarian approaches. Critiques of conventional ASRHR initiatives and technological advancements have resulted in an increased use of digital technologies to facilitate ASRHR. However, there is a gender and age divide whereby adolescent minors, particularly girls, remain excluded, unreached, or unable to exercise their agency. Despite the existence of a gender and age divide in digital SRH platforms, the divide remains uninterrogated. Data were collected in four phases from November 2019 to April 2022 using a qualitative mixed methods design that included review of online information and communication technology (ICT)-based sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) websites and platforms, 18 interviews with implementers of the platforms, and seven focus group discussions with adolescent girls. Even though ICTs have demonstrated their ability to navigate socio-cultural tensions surrounding SRHR, an age and gender divide poses a barrier for them to achieve this anticipated potential as adolescent girls below the age of 18 have the least access in Kenya. Socio-cultural norms surrounding adolescent sexuality in Kenya continue to inform ASRHR policies and programmes that exclude 10–17-year-old girls, particularly those in low-income urban contexts. Such exclusionary norms permeate digital spaces, inhibiting the potential of digital spaces to offer comprehensive and holistic services to adolescent girls who are not legally adult.Keywords: adolescent girls, age, gender, digital technologies, Kenya, socio-cultural norms

Background and introduction

A dearth of research exists demonstrating why conventional adolescent sexual and reproductive health and rights (ASRHR) programmes fall invariably short of their goals. Socio-cultural norms surrounding young people’s sexuality have been found to pose a barrier to providing sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) information and services to young people. These norms include age-based restrictions surrounding young people’s sexuality and the information and services that they should access, resulting in SRHR interventions that target youth and young adults, leaving out young adolescents below the age of 15 years.1

The prevalence of laws that criminalise adolescents’ sexuality and prohibit young people from accessing SRHR services without third-party consent, is a further reflection of a set of socio-cultural norms that reinforce the expectation that adolescents should not engage in sexual activity.2–4 Such policy restrictions dictate what can be taught in school and the services that can be provided, and in some cases provide explicit prohibitions against some information, including condom demonstration and distribution.5

Moreover, due to the dangers associated with sexual activity among young people, discussions around young people’s sexuality, particularly those below the age of 18, tend to focus on their sexual innocence, vulnerability to sexual abuse, and the risks associated with sexual activity, particularly teenage pregnancy and HIV infection.6,7 Many interventions are therefore designed by adults with the aim of preventing and controlling sexual activity while often failing to consider young people’s experiences of sexuality.3,8

Efforts to provide young people with SRHR information through comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) face opposition from conservative groups due to notions that providing SRHR information and services encourages sexual experimentation, in addition to encouraging lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ) and abortion agendas.3,9,10 Heteronormative socio-cultural norms that limit sex to socially sanctioned relationships therefore infiltrate CSE interventions, leading to the omission of culturally sensitive content such as adolescent sexuality, sexual orientation and gender identity, abortion, and masturbation, except when discussed negatively.11,12

The cultural tensions surrounding ASRHR implementation lead to misinformation of young people in relation to their sexuality, their exclusion from SRHR services, marginalisation of LGBTQ youth, poor implementation of CSE, and criminalisation of young people’s sexuality.

With the limitations posed by conventional ASRHR interventions and the increased use of digital tools in many fields including the health sector, this study sought to understand the role of digital technologies in addressing the socio-cultural norms surrounding SRHR for young people. This interest stems from the success of digital tools in breaking barriers of access to information and limitations to exercising voice and agency among marginalised groups, and an expectation that these successes can also be replicated for ASRHR.13 By reducing the limitations of physical spaces including distance, cost, and time, digital technologies can enhance accessibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of SRHR interventions for young people, and facilitate the design of relevant, people-centred programmes and policies.13–17

While digital tools have the potential to amplify voice and increase participation and consequently social change for marginalised groups, digital inequalities resulting from unequal distribution of digital resources and skills can limit access.18 The concept of digital inequality further recognises that access and digital skills are not sufficient for social change.13 This realisation stems from a growing understanding that digital tools are not value-neutral and can be influenced by power and cultural dynamics to perpetuate negative gender norms.8,18,19 Tacchi18 argues that for digital tools to contribute to social change, they need to be viewed less universally and instead pay attention to socio-cultural contexts, as well as the needs and circumstances of marginalised and excluded communities. Without this, technological innovations risk mirroring conventional development practice by perpetuating gender and age-related hierarchies and power dynamics.18

The age and gender divide is evident in information, communication, and technology (ICT)-based SRHR platforms, where adolescent girls and adolescent minors aged between 10 and 17 years remain excluded, unreached, or unable to exercise their agency. This is particularly puzzling as digital technologies are considered ideal in providing an alternative to formal sexuality education and an opportunity for adolescents, including sexual and gender minority youth, to discuss sexual experiences and explore sexual feelings on their own terms and without shame.16,20–22

For instance, Waldman and Amazon-Brown17 demonstrate that even though Every1Mobile’s Smart Sex – a programme that offers online sex and relationship advice – reached more than 100,000 young people, female adolescents who are minors had lower access compared to their male and adult counterparts. Only a quarter of the users were female and only 6% of the users were between 12 and 17 years.17 Similarly, a study of the mobile for Reproductive Health (m4RH) programme in Kenya found that only 22% of users were below 19 years old.23 In urban Tanzania, Pfeiffer et al.24 found that despite high internet usage among 15–19-year-olds, male adolescents had higher access compared to female adolescents. This was due to social norms encouraging internet use among boys, while discouraging girls from using the internet to protect them from risky encounters. They also found that girls spent more time on domestic chores and had less time to learn how to use the internet.

Despite the existence of a gender and age divide in ICT-based SRH platforms, the divide remains uninterrogated. This article is driven by the gender and age divide in ICT-based SRH platforms and the silences surrounding them. It seeks to examine whether, how, and for whom ICTs are reconceptualising adolescent sexual health interventions. It also seeks to understand how 10–17-year-old adolescent girls are positioned in this re-conceptualisation, where it exists. It further seeks to analyse the ways in which ICT-based SRHR platforms are navigating the socio-cultural tensions that surround ASRHR, and whether such navigation entails conforming to or challenging these tensions. The aims of the study are therefore to examine the role of ICTs in navigating the socio-cultural tensions that surround ASRHR, including those related to gender and age.

Methods and materials

Study design

The study collected data using a mixed-methods qualitative design that involved review of ICT-based SRHR platforms and their websites, key informant interviews (KIIs) with implementers of the platforms, and focus group discussions (FGDs) with adolescent girls.

Study settings

Online review involved selected ICT-based SRHR platforms mostly operating in Kenya and in some cases, extending to other East African countries.

Data collection methods

Data were collected in four phases from November 2019 to April 2022, using a qualitative mixed methods design that included review of online ICT-based SRHR platforms and their websites from November 2019 to March 2021, interviews with implementers of the platforms between September 2020 and January 2021, and FGDs with adolescent girls in April 2022.

Data collection methods involved both online and offline activities, with online review as the primary data collection method and KIIs and FGDs supplementing the data by confirming the findings of the online review, providing additional information to better understand the review’s findings.

Online review

A total of 18 ICT-based SRHR platforms were identified by the study. I (the researcher and author) identified 13 ICT-based SRHR platforms that I was familiar with as a result of my SRHR research in Kenya. While conducting KIIs, five more platforms were referred to me by some of the key informants.

The online review involved examination of seven of the most visible out of 18 ICT-based SRHR platforms in Kenya. These are: Shujaaz, Love Matters Kenya, #formnigani, Marie Stopes Kenya, Aunty Jane Hotline, Miss Sophie Bot, and Liverpool VCT Health (LVCT Health). Table 1 provides a description of the platforms.

Description of reviewed ICT-based SRHR platforms

These platforms were selected for being the most prominent and therefore most accessed in the Kenyan social media space and for having the highest number of followers/users and high levels of engagement of Twitter, Facebook, and/or Instagram, compared to other platforms.

The online review sought to conduct a preliminary review of the platforms’ political and ideological positioning, funding, the sexuality models that ICT-based ASRHR platforms promote, and their approach and work in relation to rights-based approaches. The online review also sought to examine issues emanating from young people and how they are addressed, to understand whether/how ICT-based SRHR platforms are reconceptualising adolescent sexuality and to navigate the tensions surrounding ASRHR for adolescent girls. The online review further sought to examine access and confirm the age and gender divide revealed in the literature review, and to examine how cognisant these platforms are of these divides and, if they are, to further understand how they address the age and gender divide.

The online review was conducted by the researcher (author), who read most of the content on the websites and on social media pages (Facebook, Twitter, and/or Instagram) of the selected ICT-based SRHR platforms. The researcher sought to understand their operations in terms of target audience, objectives, approaches, and reach, as well as user demographics such as education level, marital status, and geographic location of users, and reach and considerations in relation to the age and gender divide. The researcher took written notes on the content of the online review and on the issues that were of interest to the study throughout the entire process.

Key informant interviews

To bridge the information gaps in the online review, KIIs taking between 45 minutes and one hour were held with implementers of 18 SRHR platforms operating in Kenya, with some extending to East Africa. The KIIs sought to interrogate the age and gender divide further by understanding why this gap existed, and whether, how and why ICT-based SRHR platforms include adolescent girls below the age of 18 years, and the experiences, successes, and challenges in doing so.

Focus group discussions

In addition, a series of seven one-hour-long FGDs were conducted with 15 adolescent girls aged 10–17 years residing in Mathare Area, a low-income urban settlement in Kenya. Each FGD session was built on the previous one and involved the same 15 girls. The FGDs were held in a community centre in Mathare Area and were facilitated by the researcher (author) over a period of one month. The FGDs sought to understand the perspectives and experiences of adolescent girls in relation to existing ICT-based SRHR platforms in Kenya, including whether they interacted with them, factors influencing their ability to access these platforms and the relevance of the platforms in relation to the SRHR needs and experiences of adolescent girls below the age of 18 in low-income urban contexts in Kenya.

Ethics

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the United States International University (USIU) in Kenya (USIU-A/IRB/1-06-2020 issued on 7 January 2020) and research approval was obtained from the National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI) in Kenya (License No: NACOSTI/P/20/3539 issued on 24 January 2020). Parental/guardian consents were obtained from the parents and guardians of all the girls who participated in the study, while both verbal and written assents were obtained from all the girls who participated in the study. Verbal assent was obtained before the beginning of each FGD session with adolescent girls involved in the study.

Data management and analysis

All the interviews and FGDs were audio-recorded with the permission of the research participants. The audio recordings were stored in a secure computer, which only I as the researcher had access to. I transcribed all the audio recordings, and deleted them after transcription. Using the Braun and Clark32 6-step for thematic analysis, namely: (1) familiarisation with the data; (2) coding; (3) identifying themes; (4) reviewing themes; (5) defining and naming themes; and (6) writing, I reviewed, analysed and organised all the data according to identified themes.

Results and discussion

Increasing access

The review of ICT-based SRHR platforms revealed that they have a wide reach, thereby increasing access. Some platforms have reached several hundred thousand or a few million followers. For example, Shujaaz reaches millions of young people in Kenya and Tanzania through various media platforms including comic books, radio, social media, and events. Shujaaz distributes more than 10 million comic books a year, broadcasts Jongo Love, a weekly radio drama hosted on multiple nationwide shows in Kenya and Tanzania, with themes exploring relationships, gender-based violence, hustling, substance abuse, preventing unplanned pregnancy and contraception to protect one’s plans, dreams and future.25,33 Shujaaz also engages millions of fans on social media through Facebook and Twitter and through short message service (SMS).25,33 Jongo Love was also adapted into a 13-episode TV drama and later edited into a feature-length movie that generated more than 100,000 views on YouTube within three weeks of its release, was screened in cinemas and was widely distributed to video dens across Kenya.33

Similarly, the first phase of #Formnigani, a Kenyan-focused ICT-based SRHR platform, experimented on the use of creative communication to support ongoing family planning advocacy initiatives in Kenya by “amplifying the true voice of the people”, an initiative that generated more than 385 million online impressions.34 Miss Sophie Bot on the other hand has been asked and answered over 40,000 questions, from 4,000 users, mostly from Kenya but also from other parts of the world. Liverpool Voluntary Counselling and Testing (LVCT)'s one2one platform reaches more than six million young people annually.30 More than 3.4 million adolescents and young people access one2one through social media platforms, in addition to over 40,000 calls and “over 6,000 young people [are] being linked to SRH, HIV and gender-based violence (GBV) services through a robust countrywide referral network”.31 The Love Matters approach involves engaging young people, “using rights- and science-based information, and working closely with local partners to develop a customized mix of online platforms and offline actions”.35 The programme uses digital data analysis to understand the specific needs of each of its target regions. In response to those needs, Love Matters tailors its platforms, which run on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and a web-based discussion forum, to attract and reach millions of users.36

As the figures demonstrate, ICTs offer highly accessible avenues for SRHR engagement with young people. This is demonstrated by the high reach that some of these platforms have, with some engaging with several million users annually. These platforms provide on-demand access to SRHR services and information to young people.

However, even though ICTs have demonstrated their ability to greatly increase access to SRHR information and services for young people, girls and young women have limited access and make up the lowest number of users, as most of these platforms, particularly those that are highly visible and popular, are accessed by adult male youth. Adolescent girls who are not legally adults (between 10 and 17 years old) have the lowest access, more so in interventions that provide SRHR content for a general population, that is, without being intentional about reaching out to girls or young women. Such ICT-based SRHR platforms indicated having a male population of between 60% and 70% and a female population of between 30% and 40%. This was often the case for six of the 18 platforms involved in the study, more so those that were not intentional on reaching female audiences or specifically targeting girls and women. An interviewed staff member of one such organisation (KII Respondent 7) reported having a male audience ranging between 70% and 75% and a female audience of between 25% and 30%. The same platform indicated that 75% of its audience is 18 years and over and only 25% of its users comprise 13–17-year-olds. This means that even though adolescent girls are most affected by negative SRHR outcomes, they are the group with the least access to online SRHR platforms, which would provide them with the information and resources to navigate their sexuality in a safe and healthy manner. It also demonstrates the need to go beyond setting up a platform, to take an intersectional approach that seeks to reach those who would otherwise be excluded because of gender, age, socioeconomic status, and disability, among other exclusionary factors.

Interviews with key informants further established that many adolescent girls do not have access to online platforms, and they live with their parents and guardians which poses a challenge for them to access ICT gadgets. Moreover, when they have access, it may be under strict supervision, unless they are from wealthier families as indicated by KII Respondent 1. Adolescent girls in FGDs confirmed that they have limited access to phones and when they access phones, which often belong to their parents, they use them to play games and sometimes to watch YouTube videos. The videos are however not on SRHR-related issues.

Increasing access for those with limited access by combining online and offline activities

Some ICT-based SRHR platforms recognise that while ICTs increase access, in contexts like Kenya many people are still excluded from accessing the internet or lack ownership of individual ICT tools such as mobile phones or computers. This recognition has led to a combination of offline activities to reach such people, as well as the use of platforms that are not digital such as radio and comic books. Some of them also use language that increases their accessibility. Interviews with key informants indicated the need to reach populations with limited access as a reason for engaging in offline activities. Offline engagement includes school outreach, community engagement activities, mainstream media, magazines, comics, and branded communication material (KII Respondents 1, 7, 9, 10, and 11).

Some of the ICT-based SRHR platforms are addressing the challenge that girls’ limited access to ICTs presents, by going offline to reach the target group through partnership. For example, to strengthen its online work and enhance its visibility and outreach, Love Matters also engages in offline activities in partnership with local and international partners: “These activities include campaigns, concerts, installations (graffiti art, virtual reality), research projects and presentations at international conferences”.36 Love Matters has also used offline platforms such as running an article with a local newspaper, The Star, Kenya. The article is no longer in publication. Offline platforms are useful in reaching young people who may not have access to online platforms. Aunty Jane Hotline collaborates with Straight Talk to disseminate the Young Talk Magazine which targets 9–12-year-olds and the Straight Talk Magazine which targets 13–18-year-olds. Aunty Jane Hotline also collaborates with Zana Afrika’s magazine for adolescent girls to promote the Aunty Jane Hotline services. Some of the platforms involved in this research target adolescent and teenage girls through magazines such as Nia Teen (by Zana Africa), Shujaaz (by Shujaaz Inc.), Straight Talk Magazine (by Straight Talk Kenya), and Iamfree2be.com which includes a website for adolescent girls and access through a USSD text application.

Some platforms have moved away from a generalised to a highly targeted approach in terms of gender. For example, Aunty Jane Hotline provides CSE information and reproductive health care services targeting women and girls, supporting them to exercise sexual freedom and informed choices as indicated through an interview with KII Respondent 1. The hotline seeks to provide confidential, non-judgmental information to women and girls of reproductive age (14–65 years). The platform was set up in 2013 following conversations with women and girls aged between 14 and 65 in informal settlements which revealed gaps in accessing comprehensive reproductive health services, leading to unplanned pregnancies.

“Since most girls own phones, a hotline was considered an ideal avenue to bridge the gap and provide information and services while guaranteeing anonymity, safety and confidentiality.” (KII Respondent 1)

It is interesting to note that despite the Hotline’s focus on adolescent girls and young women, most of the 300–400 monthly callers, according to an interviewed key informant, are adult women aged between 18 and 35 years, who call to discuss issues such as contraception, access to safe abortion and relationships. This points to the importance of reflecting on the assumption that an ICT platform will be accessible to all girls and women, and the need to be even more specific in targeting adolescent girls who are not legally adults.

Many of the platforms targeting adolescent girls are inaccessible and even unknown to adolescent girls involved in this study. The girls involved in FGDs had only heard of and/or accessed Shujaaz magazine, which they found interesting and informative but at the time of the study it was no longer available in their community. The girls involved in the study indicated that they access most SRHR information through offline spaces, that is, school, parents, and NGO programmes in the community, as they had not been exposed to most of the online platforms. Even though the girls indicated that the content in the material presented to them during the research was interesting and relevant, they had no information about these platforms nor access to them as they had limited access to the internet. The platforms had also not been made available to them, pointing to constraints leading to the girls’ exclusion, even though it may not be intentional on the part of the platforms which also face resource constraints in ensuring wide reach of the content they generate. The girls are therefore confined to conventional avenues for SRHR information, which often tends to be provided by parents, teachers and, in some cases, NGOs that promote abstinence or are risk-management-focused in their approach, thereby failing to provide CSE to girls.

Some of the NGOs operating in the community produce publications that allow girls to access SRHR content through some form of media, combined with face-to-face sessions. Dream Girls, a USAID-funded HIV programme for adolescent girls, is the most prominent in Mathare area. The exclusion of girls from ICT-based SRHR platforms means that they have limited options for obtaining information about their sexuality. Moreover, by being confined to conventional spaces for SRHR information, girls are further constrained from accessing information that could provide more holistic notions of sexual health. This is particularly the case, as the study established that ICT-based platforms are challenging socio-cultural norms surrounding young people’s sexuality as discussed in the next section.

How ICT-based SRHR platforms are navigating the socio-cultural tensions surrounding ASRHR for adolescent girls

This section demonstrates that even though ICT-based SRHR platforms are using non-conventional approaches that are sex-positive and confront or challenge socio-cultural norms, they often exclude adolescent girls. A review of content provided by these platforms reveals that they are not only increasing access, but some are indeed challenging socio-cultural norms surrounding ASRHR. Such platforms are reconceptualising SRHR for young people by moving away from conventional approaches to ASRHR, which tend to be abstinence-focused and often take a biomedical and risk-management approach when discussing young people’s SRH. Some platforms are therefore taking a more comprehensive approach, acknowledging young people’s sexuality and their sexual agency and the need for accurate information to promote young people’s well-being.

Love Matters for instance, provides an example of a platform that acknowledges the taboo nature of discussing sexuality in many African contexts and makes an active effort to counter such norms. Love Matters is an initiative of RNW Media, a Dutch-based organisation, funded by several development partners, with the Dutch Ministry of Foreign Affairs as the main one and other partners such as Amplify Change, Ford Foundation, Packard Foundation, CHOICE for Youth and Sexuality, IPPF, and WGNRR.35

Love Matters operates in “restrictive settings” across the world, also described as “fragile or socio-politically repressive countries”.37 In 2016, RNW Media focused on 22 countries in sub-Saharan Africa, Middle East, North Africa, Asia and Latin America, characterised by “legal restrictions or taboos [that] make it difficult to find information on subjects such as sexuality, democratic participation and international justice”.35 Love Matters outlines masturbation, sex before marriage, condom use and abortion as some of the taboo topics and pays attention to the role of “religion”, “politics”, “gender norms and other social pressures” in reinforcing these taboos.36

As a global online platform, Love Matters “provides easy-to-access information and news on sexuality and sexual health for young adults around the world”.36 Its mission is “to identify young people’s needs and bring them together in user-owned digital communities where they can safely engage on taboos and sensitive topics, generating strong stories for advocacy to unleash their potential for social change”.37 Love Matters’ approach involves engaging young people, “using rights- and science-based information, and working closely with local partners to develop a customized mix of online platforms and offline actions”.35 The programme uses digital data analysis to understand the specific needs of each of its target regions. In response to those needs, Love Matters tailors its platforms, which run on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and a web-based discussion forum, to attract and reach millions of users.36

The platform describes itself as sex- and pleasure-positive, open, honest about all aspects of love and relationships and non-judgmental in its attitude.36 Love Matters considers its approach different from the rest due to the focus on pleasure, rather than the conventional focus on risk management, avoiding diseases and dysfunction. Moreover, the use of online space moves away from the conventional adult-facilitated approach, by allowing young people to engage and share their thoughts with their peers and to pose questions to (s)experts.36 Such an approach provides young people with the tools to help them make informed decisions and to explore and enjoy sex in a safe and healthy manner.36

Love Matters discusses a variety of topics that are not discussed on conventional platforms and even on other ICT-based SRHR platforms. The platform offers a more comprehensive approach to sexuality, by incorporating a wide array of topics beyond contraception, to include topics such as sexual pleasure, orgasms, varied ways of having sex including oral and anal sex, sexting, pornography, sexual diversity, masturbation, and penis and vagina shapes and sizes. Unlike conventional platforms that discuss sexual diversity in a negative light, Love Matters takes a sex-positive approach. The approach thereby goes beyond the risk management approach and heteronormativity.

However, Love Matters explicitly targets adults aged 18–35 years, as stated on the website and in a key informant interview. For this group, there is greater social acceptance of being in relationships since they are legally adult, thereby minimising the risk of negative backlash. As stated by KII Respondent 9, “we encourage young people to explore their sexuality but safely, but this [discussing young people’s sexuality] created a lot of problems with the government and other actors, hence the reason for targeting 18–35-year-olds”. In addition, the use of English has potential to pose a language barrier, attracting a more educated audience and excluding many young people, more so those that are most marginalised.

At the time of the interview, Love Matters had begun a discussion on engaging younger adolescents and using more inclusive language, which has resulted in the development of a YouTube channel for younger adolescents. Despite this development, Love Matters38 still indicates that more than 96% of its 1.55 million followers are 18–34 years old, an indication that young people below the age of 18 are still left out.

Moreover, while Love Matters and other platforms rightly acknowledge the taboo nature of discussions on sexuality, they do not pay attention to the fact that this is more pronounced for younger adolescents. None of the seven platforms that were initially reviewed targets young people who are younger than 15 years. This is a highly conventional approach in most SRHR programmes, which often target 15–19-year-old girls. In addition, research shows that women and girls are most excluded from ICT and internet access. The platforms have not, however, taken into serious consideration how age and gender exclude younger adolescent girls the most.

Key informant interviews seeking to understand the exclusion of adolescents who are minors established that even though practitioners are aware that adolescents who are not legally adults are sexually active, the legal and social environments pose restrictions in providing accurate information and SRHR services to this group. Several practitioners cited conflict between the law and reality, particularly in matters related to their sexuality for adolescents who are below the age of 18 years. To avoid backlash that may include shutting down operations by the government and legal authorities, these platforms choose to target adult audiences.

“Considering Ezekiel Mutua* and his policies, we had to be clear that we were targeting adults – to avoid crossing the line. In the Kenyan context it’s hard to find a way to share information with adolescents in a holistic manner.” (KII Respondent 9)

Restrictive laws around the age of consent play a role in restricting ICT-based SRHR platforms from providing CSE particularly for adolescents who are not legally adult. Many of these restrictive laws, particularly age of consent laws and laws that criminalise adolescent sexuality, are however not challenged by implementers, as established by KIIs. As KII Respondent 9 indicated, “we avoid backlash at all costs” while KII Respondent 15 stated, “when the NGO sector engages politically, it becomes targeted by the NGO council”. The approach to avoid backlash is common even with platforms, such as Love Matters, that explicitly seek to challenge taboos surrounding sexuality. In addition, several implementers involved in the study hold a similar position to the legal one, that minors cannot consent. The implication of conforming to the legal environment which does not allow certain content to adolescent minors is the exclusion of adolescents.

“10–14 are missing out on these kinds of information and the benefits of online platforms, but you need to understand that consent has always been a barrier to young people or adolescents receiving SRHR services and information. Consent acts as a precondition, which is informed generated by societal attitudes and it’s very difficult to conduct a value clarification program to mitigate these particular personal attitudes and values that are of course now propagating consent, which is demanded by the parents. Adolescents miss out because no parent is comfortable to leave a child with their mobile phones. Even mine is 10 years old, and I am not comfortable – even me even if I am in this program, I have the same predicament as any other parent.” (KII Respondent 3)

Consequently, due to legal restrictions many online platforms targeting adolescent minors promote abstinence and focus on acceptable topics such as menstruation, life skills, and puberty from a biological perspective. Some platforms use models that aim to build girls’ agency to demand SRHR services, but do not ensure that this becomes a reality by creating an enabling environment for girls to access such services without restrictions. This is a form of conformity to socio-cultural norms as these platforms shy away from confronting the legal and policy environment that creates barriers for young people’s SRHR and instead targets a population that will not generate opposition or tension. Unable to confront the restrictive legal and policy environments, some platforms implement empowerment initiatives which pass on to girls the burden of demanding services and claiming their rights. As Miedema et al.39 and Roodsaz40 argue, these are flaws of rights-based approaches, which place undue burdens on young people rather than on the system, particularly in cases where the context does not recognise young people as sexual beings.

Non-conventional approach but with a pre-determined agenda such as pregnancy prevention

The online review found that on some online platforms, non-conventional conversations are taking place, but they tend to be narrow in approach. For instance, even though some platforms have moved away from a purely abstinence-focused approach, they are mostly focused on promoting contraceptive use, largely driven by an agenda to prevent teen and early pregnancies. One such platform is #formnigani (a Sheng (Kiswahili slang)) word meaning “what’s the plan?”. #formnigani describes itself as “a creative platform and movement for all Kenyans to express themselves, discuss and make decisions about contraception”.41,p.8

Similarly, the Shujaaz theory of change is an overall audience journey from exposure to Shujaaz media, to developing trust in the media, to engaging in large-scale, taboo-confounding conversations to experiencing shifts in perceived norms, to – ultimately – changing behaviour.33 In relation to SRH, the taboo-confounding conversations seem restricted to contraception, leaving out a host of other relevant topics. A key component of Shujaaz SRHR work involves mapping where young people are on a contraceptive use rejection scale and moving them from a journey of complete rejection to complete adoption.42 While the focus on contraception is a shift away from the abstinence-only approach to SRHR for young people, the agenda is still predetermined to promote contraceptive use.

On the agenda to prevent teen and early pregnancies, contraceptives are presented as the silver bullet to SRHR. In many of these pregnancy-prevention-driven platforms, information on SRHR such as consent, violence, or bodily autonomy tends to be missing. These platforms therefore fail to provide CSE to the adolescent girls and young women whom the contraceptive agenda targets. Therefore, unlike platforms accessed by older youth, platforms designed for adolescent girls fail to provide more holistic information on their sexuality.

As such, even though there is a move beyond abstinence-only approaches, this is not driven by an acknowledgement of adolescent girls as sexual beings with the right to comprehensive SRHR information. It is instead driven by an agenda to manage the high pregnancy rates among adolescent girls, similarly to conventional spaces, which are driven by a risk management and pregnancy prevention agenda.43,44 Nanda and Tandon13 argue that even though technology can influence societal and gender norms, it can also be shaped by them to reflect and perpetuate social hierarchies, regressive social norms, and stratifications.

Therefore, while ICT-based interventions present an opportunity for a user-driven agenda, this is not necessarily the case. Both the problem and solution are determined by the platform developers, just as with conventional ASRHR platforms. The push for hormonal contraceptive use is also decontextualised and therefore takes a top-down assumptive approach. Contraceptive use among adolescents in many African contexts is still frowned upon and the value accorded to childbearing poses a barrier for adolescents’ access to hormonal contraceptives.45 The use of hormonal contraceptives is highly discouraged among young people who have never given birth, due to the fear that contraceptives may lead to infertility, and in most cases, hormonal contraceptive use is often discouraged until after at least a first birth.45

Another example of a non-conventional approach towards discussing SRHR is the discussion around pregnancy. There is a notable shift in some platforms from the pathologising approach taken earlier, where early pregnancy was largely perceived as disastrous for girls. During FGDs, girls confirmed that even in the community, pregnancy is normalised. Indeed, girls indicate facing pressure to get pregnant from their peers who are already teen mothers.

“Some girls get pregnant willingly. For example, if two sisters are living together and one gets pregnant, the other one sees that the sister has been supported and doesn’t suffer any consequences, so she decides to get pregnant too … Girls with children carry their children with pride, making it look good and those without children get a lot of pressure and wish they could get children. Sometimes the new mothers will wear matching outfits with their children, and they look good … and the children are so cute … a girl without a child will start to envy and the one with the child will encourage her to get a child. Sometimes the new mothers mock girls who do not have children.” (16-year-old adolescent girl in FGD session)

This is an emerging and unexpected finding that needs further understanding, particularly because it deviates from the assumption in policy and practice that teenage pregnancies are unwanted and a source of shame and stigma and suggests that there are privileges associated with or experienced by girls and young women who give birth. It could also point to a need to discuss peer pressure in a different way beyond the normative “avoid peer pressure” message which was present in the booklets available for girls as part of life skills education.

In some ICT-based SRHR platforms, girls are encouraged to continue with life after pregnancy, by accepting the pregnancy and seeking support. In most cases, accepting the pregnancy and raising the child is the only option presented. Emergency contraception and abortion are not often considered or provided as options and girls are encouraged to carry pregnancies to term in many cases including cases of sexual violence. This is another example of how ICT-based SRHR platforms conform to socio-cultural norms surrounding sexuality for adolescent girls who are minors. In some of the platforms reviewed in this study, abortion is still cast as dangerous and illegal. This resembles conventional spaces where certain topics such as abortion are only discussed in a negative light. FGD participants also confirmed that girls who get pregnant in their community are encouraged to give birth and raise the child. However, the girls indicate that in instances where the parents have money, girls are able to access abortion.

“No. Not all girls are made to carry the pregnancy to term. If a parent has money, they will take the girl for abortion. The parent will pay some money for abortion. Some parents force their daughters to abort.” (17-year-old adolescent girl in a FGD session)

Emergence of positive deviants embracing less conventional approaches to SRHR

Some ICT-based SRHR platforms are finding ways to navigate some socially unacceptable issues such as abortion and gender and sexual diversity. There is some visible shift around discussion of abortion, with some platforms moving beyond the norm by discussing abortion in a positive light, which would not be the case in conventional spaces, demonstrating the emergence of positive deviants.

The Aunty Jane website, for example, is organised, simple, resourceful, encouraging, and positive, particularly on abortion and contraception. Abortion is covered in a comprehensive, supportive, and non-stigmatising manner. While other platforms shy off from discussing abortion, in some cases to avoid backlash by government or due to donor restrictions, Aunty Jane hotline is open about it.

Some platforms also attempt to navigate socially unacceptable issues such as abortion through partnership. Platforms such as Shujaaz and Straight Talk tend to refer consumers to Aunty Jane for discussion on unintended pregnancies. Although such approaches do not take a clear stand on abortion, this could be interpreted as an indirect way of supporting access to abortion, particularly in a context where the norm is to cast abortion negatively. Some platforms may also not take a clear stand due to funding restrictions.

These approaches present pockets of hope and there is need to explore more deeply how such approaches can be supported and scaled up, how other platforms can adopt similar approaches, how to increase adolescent girls’ access to such platforms, and how to enhance such approaches to counteract dominant approaches and narratives.

However, other than abortion, efforts to engage adolescent girls effectively or to confront the socio-cultural tensions surrounding young people’s sexuality are minimal. Moreover, even though sexual violence was a common theme in the FGDs with adolescent girls, there are minimal efforts by ICT platforms to provide support related to sexual violence. Therefore, while the emergence of positive deviants is encouraging, there are many gaps in providing holistic and comprehensive sexuality education to adolescents.

Implications for policy and practice

The age and gender divide in ICT-based ASRHR platforms as well as the mimicking of conventional SRHR platforms when targeting adolescent girls, calls for deeper reflection by SRHR implementers and policy makers on the socio-cultural norms surrounding young people’s sexuality. It further requires a deeper understanding and engagement with barriers to promoting CSE in both conventional spaces and ICT-based platforms. While the emergence of positive deviants, particularly on the topic of abortion, is positive, it is limited in providing a holistic approach to sexual health for young people beyond abortion. However, it provides a useful starting point to draw lessons and advance ASRHR to provide more holistic and comprehensive support to young people’s sexual health and well-being. This study demonstrates the need for policy makers and practitioners to be cognisant of the exclusion that exists in ICT-based SRHR spaces and to make deliberate efforts to include 10–17-year-old girls in ASRHR interventions. For ASRHR interventions to be relevant, adolescent girls and young people must be meaningfully engaged in the processes of understanding their realities. Engagement with adolescents should also be intersectional, understanding that adolescent experiences of sexuality are not homogenous.

This study demonstrates the importance of understanding how socio-cultural norms permeate both offline and online spaces to exclude adolescent girls aged between 10 and 17 years in Kenya and similar settings, and of designing and testing interventions that counter such norms and create more inclusive ASRHR platforms.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study used multiple sources of data collection, which allowed the researcher to bridge information gaps and interrogate issues in more depth. Moreover, by conducting online review and involving implementers and adolescent girls in KIIs and FGDs respectively, the study was able to obtain different perspectives pertaining to digital technologies and SRHR broadly, and ASRHR more specifically.

However, while the online review sought to examine other characteristics of the users including education level, marital status, and geographic location of users, this was however not possible to establish due to the limitation of data available online. These platforms did not have data sets of their users available online and attempts to obtain data sets from platforms were not fruitful due to confidentiality policies in the organisations, as well as lack of systematic data collection of the demographic details of the users. The study was also unable to establish whether adolescents who are restricted due to age still go ahead and access the platforms and how this is addressed.

Conclusion

This article discusses the role of ICTs in navigating the socio-cultural tensions surrounding ASRHR and further seeks to understand the inclusion or exclusion of adolescent girls who are below 18 years old in ICT-based SRHR platforms. The study finds that some platforms are sex-positive, seeking to normalise discussions around sexuality and confront sexual taboos; however, these platforms are most accessible to adult youth – that is, young adults above the age of 18 years, particularly adult male youth. Therefore, as much as sex is normalised, this is only the case for adults, and even though some ICT-based SRHR platforms are challenging socio-cultural norms surrounding ASRHR, they may still not offer CSE to adolescent girls.

Moreover, in discussing SRHR issues affecting adolescent girls, ICT-based SRHR platforms tend to conform to socio-cultural norms surrounding ASHR and mimic conventional SRHR spaces. Even in ICT-based spaces, SRHR information targeting adolescent minors often takes a conventional approach by promoting abstinence and taking a risk management approach to preventing pregnancy and HIV infection, largely due to restrictions posed by the policy environment and socio-cultural norms. Such restrictions include age-of-consent laws that prohibit and even criminalise adolescent sexuality and socio-cultural norms that deny young people’s sexuality and their right to SRHR information and services.

Therefore, the unconventional and/or comprehensive approach to sexuality is limited to adults and in some cases to certain topics (especially when adolescent girls are the target) and is thus not comprehensive for adolescent girls.
