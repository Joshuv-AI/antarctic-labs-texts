SLIT2 inhibits lung adenocarcinoma progression by suppressing epithelial-mesenchymal transition
Authors: Zhimeng Chen, Hu Chen, Yuan Cui, Jun Chen, Weibiao Zeng, Jian Yang, Yu Li, Xiaobei Peng, Xin Tong, Jun Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most common subtype of lung cancer, is a major contributor to worldwide cancer deaths. Although the slit guidance ligand (SLIT) protein family is implicated in both normal biological processes and disease states, the specific role of SLIT2 in LUAD development remains unclear. To address this gap, the present study integrated bioinformatics analyses and experimental studies to investigate the functional significance and clinical relevance of SLIT2 in LUAD. The present analysis revealed that SLIT2 expression is significantly reduced in LUAD tissues and cell lines, and this decrease is associated with poorer patient outcomes. Functional experiments showed that inhibiting SLIT2 expression enhances LUAD cell growth, migration, invasion and epithelial-mesenchymal transformation of LUAD cells, whereas overexpression of SLIT2 can reverse these carcinogenic effects. Furthermore, it was observed that SLIT2 expression levels correlate with distinct patterns of immune cell infiltration in the tumor environment. These results suggested that targeting SLIT2 pathways could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for LUAD and further research is needed to explore the specific mechanisms through which SLIT2 exerts its tumor-suppressive effects and modulates immune responses. The current study enhances the understanding of LUAD biology and underscores the potential of SLIT2 as a biomarker and therapeutic target in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03948-4.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a highly fatal malignant tumor, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for 85% of cases. NSCLC includes lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and large cell carcinoma [1]. Immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment (TME) is pivotal for effective treatment [2]. With the discovery of more and more immune infiltration-related genes, including cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 and programmed death-ligand 1, their pivotal role in immune cell infiltration was confirmed [3]. The primary mechanism of immunotherapy is the activation of the immune system through autoimmune responses, thereby targeting and eliminating cancer cells. Within LUAD, there’s a correlation between the amount of T cells infiltrated and the immune reaction [4]. Nonetheless, the TME often hinders the effective activation of the immune response [5, 6]. Several studies confirmed the various functions of immunoinfiltration-related genes in solid tumors [7]. The prognostic significance of immunoinfiltration-associated genes in LUAD remains unclear; therefore, further investigation is needed. The present study uncovered the function of slit guidance ligand 2 (SLIT2) as an immunoinfiltration-related gene in patients with LUAD, identifying its tumor suppressing role and unveiling its potential as a therapeutic option in the management of LUAD. SLIT2, a secreted protein, was initially discovered as an axon-directed signal that preferentially binds to the roundabout receptor 1 (Robo1).The Slit-Robo signaling pathway plays an important role not only in neuronal guidance but also in cell migration in a wide range of cell types [8]. It is now known that members of the SLIT/ROBO family also regulate the proliferation, cell adhesion and survival of a variety of tissues and are involved in organogenesis, angiogenesis and tumour progression [9].Studies have shown that SLIT2 is associated with tumourigenesis in various human cancers. For example, Zhang et al. suggested that hypermethylation of the SLIT2 promoter leads to SLIT2-IT1/miR-218 inhibition, which results in leukaemia and predicts poor prognosis of myelodysplastic tumours [10], and that SLIT2 drives the metastasis of gastric cancer cells through activation of NEK9 [11]. More importantly, up-regulation of SLIT2 inhibits small cell lung cancer (SCLC) by affecting β-catenin [12]. In the current study, a downregulation of SLIT2 expression was observed in LUAD samples, which was associated with an unfavorable prognosis for patients with LUAD. The current Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) highlighted the significant enrichment of SLIT2 in cell adhesion pathways and tumor metabolism-related signaling pathways, suggesting its crucial involvement in LUAD biology. Furthermore, the current tumor immune cell analysis showed a strong link between SLIT2 expression and the infiltration of iDCs (immature Dendritic Cells), Memory T cells, eosinophils, macrophages, NK and Mast cells. Given the identified tumor suppressor role of SLIT2, the present findings not only underscore its potential as a valuable molecular marker for assessing the effectiveness of LUAD immunotherapy but also point to its potential application as a therapeutic target in the development of novel treatments targeting tumor metabolism and immune modulation.

Materials and methods

Gene expression omnibus (GEO) and the cancer genome atlas (TCGA) analysis

Gene chip expression data were sourced from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), and RNA sequencing info & clinical specifics on LUAD patients were obtained from TCGA (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/).

Materials

The primary antibodies used in the western blot analysis included Rabbit SLIT2 antibody (cat. no. ab134166; Abcam) and mouse β-actin antibody (cat. no. GB15001; Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.). Antibodies against mouse Snail (cat. no. L70G2; Cell Signaling Technologies, Inc.), mouse Vimentin (cat. no. sc-6260; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc.), mouse N-cadherin (cat. no. 610920; BD Transduction Laboratories; BD Biosciences), mouse E-Cadherin (cat. no. 610181; BD Transduction Laboratories; BD Biosciences) along with anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies (cat. nos. AS014 and AS017; ABclonal Biotech Co., Ltd.) were applied at dilutions of 1:1000–5000, while the secondary antibodies were used at a 1:5000–10,000 dilution.

Immunohistochemical staining

The Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org) provided information on the relative expression of the SLIT2 protein in LUAD and LUSC, considering both normal and pathological tissue [13]. The tissue samples from six distinct patients, including a 49-year-old female (Patient ID: 2268), a 21-year-old male (Patient ID: 2101), a 59-year-old male (Patient ID: 2222), a 49-year-old male (Patient ID: 3003), a 64-year-old male (Patient ID: 1847) and a 65-year-old female (Patient ID: 2403), served as a direct illustration of the overall expression pattern of SLIT2 in this context. Immunohistochemical staining was conducted using the specific antibody HPA019511 to visualize the expression of SLIT2.

Cell culture and transfection

The NSCLC cell lines A549, H1299, H226, H1650 and the bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B and HBE cells were sourced from the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (HyClone; Cytiva) or Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) with 10% FBS, in a 37 ℃, 5% CO2 incubator, and the medium was changed every 2–3 days. Small interfering (si)RNAs targeting SLIT2 (si-SLIT2-1 and si-SLIT2-2) and siRNA negative control (si-NC) sequences were obtained from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. These were then introduced into cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent (cat. no. L3000015; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). The cancer cells were harvested 48 h after transfection and used in the following functional experiments. The siRNA sequences were as follows: si-SLIT2-1, 5’-AAUACAGGUUGUUUGAAUGCAGUCG-3’; si-SLIT2-2, 5’-UGAAGAACUCUUAGAUGUCUA-3’; and si-NC, 5’-TTCCTCGAACGTGTCACTG-3’. WZ Biosciences, Inc. synthesized a SLIT2-His overexpression vector, which incorporated the coding sequence of SLIT2 along with a His tag.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

According to the instructions of the RNeasy Mini Kit (cat no. R0027; Beyotime), total RNA was extracted from the NSCLC cells A549, H1299, H226, H1650, as well as the bronchial epithelial cells BEAS-2B and HBE. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, 1 µg of total RNA was subjected to first-strand complementary DNA synthesis using the SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase Kit (cat no. 18080051; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). qRT-PCR reactions were performed using the SYBR Green kit in an ABI 7900 sequence detection system (cat no. 11736051; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The RT-qPCR assay utilized the following primers: SLIT2 forward, 5’-CAATTTACACTGGTTCCCAAGG-3’ and reverse, 5’-GAAGCTCTGATTAGAAAGCGTG-3’; reference gene β-actin, forward 5’-CACAGAGCCTCGCCTTTGCC-3’ and reverse, 5’-CATGCCGGAGCCGTTGTCG-3’. The reaction parameters included a denaturation program (5 min at 95 ℃, followed by an amplification and quantification program over 45 cycles (30 s at 95 ℃ and 30 s at 60 ℃). Each sample was tested in triplicates, and each sample underwent a melting curve analysis to check for the specificity of amplification. The relative expression of mRNA was determined by the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to the endogenous expression of β-actin mRNA.

Western blot analysis. Cells were washed twice with cold PBS before collection. Subsequently, the cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (cat. no. YF369700; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (cat. no. 1215471; Millipore). At room temperature, a protein-free solution (cat. no. PS108P; EpiZyme, Inc.) was used to block membranes for 40 min, followed by incubation with a primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After being washed with TBS-Tween, membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody for 2 h at ambient temperature.The untrimmed strips are shown in Figure S1.

Generation of LUAD cell lines stably overexpressing SLIT2

The lentivirus purchased from Anzhenbio was constructed based on the vector pCDH-GFP-PURO-HA-C. Then, 10 µl of the lentivirus suspension was added to infect A549 and H1299 cells, and the culture medium was replaced 16 h after infection. Finally, stable A549 and H1299 cell lines overexpressing HA-tagged SLIT2 were selected using 2 µg/mL puromycin (ST551, Beyotime).

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cells were digested, centrifuged and resuspended in PBS (BioVision, Inc.; Abcam) at 4 × 104 cells/100 µl. For migration assays, 5 × 104 cells were seeded into the upper chamber (cat. no. 353097; FALCON) of the transwell plate in 200 µl RPMI 1640 medium with 1% FBS, and 800 µl complete medium was added to the lower compartment. After incubation, cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, washed three times with PBS, wiped with cotton swabs and photographed under an inverted microscope. For the invasion assay, a 4:1 mixture of medium and Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences) was used, with the remaining steps being the same as for the migration assay.

Wound-healing assay

The cells were plated in a 6-well plate and cultured until they were ~ 100% confluent. A 200 µl pipette tip was used to draw a straight line to form a ‘scratch’ and the cells were then rinsed with PBS three times and imaged using six random fields of view. After 24 h, the cells were washed and imaged utilizing ImageJ 1.54 g software (National Institutes of Health).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

A549 and H1299 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 2 × 103 cells/well. CCK-8 solution (10 µl; APExBIO Thecnology LLC) was added after incubation for 0, 24, 48, 72–96 h. After 2 h of further incubation, a microplate reader (CMax Plus; Molecular Devices LLC) was used to quantify the optical density at 450 nm (OD450) at each indicated time point.

5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the EdU assay, which detects DNA replication activity. The procedure involved inoculating cells in the logarithmic growth phase, followed by incubation with 50 µM EdU-containing medium for 2–8 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized and stained with Apollo dye. Nuclear counterstaining was performed and the samples were analyzed using fluorescence microscopy.

Bioinformatics analysis

Utilizing the DESeq2 R software package (version 1.32.0), differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened across five GEO datasets (GSE32863, GSE10072, GSE68465, GSE31210 and GSE75027). Additionally, the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) tool was employed (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn) to conduct a comparative analysis of RNA expression between LUAD and normal tissues [14]. Subsequently, a survival analysis was performed utilizing the GEPIA tool. Additionally, clusterProfiler in R was utilized to analyze Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways of DEGs. Finally, the infiltration of immune cells was assessed by utilizing the ssGSEA R package.

Statistical analysis

All datasets and mappings underwent comprehensive analysis employing the R software package (version 4.2.1) alongside the Xiantao Academic Platform (www.xiantaozi.com). For the execution of statistical analysis and data manipulation, GraphPad Prism 6 software (Dotmatics) was utilized. The findings were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons amongst datasets were performed suing the Student’s t-test. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Result

Identification of SLIT2 in GEO datasets

In the present study, we first utilized Venn diagrams to analyze the intersection of down - regulated genes among the GSE32863, GSE10072, GSE68465, GSE31210, and GSE75027 datasets. This approach allowed us to identify a set of genes that were consistently down - regulated across multiple datasets. Through this analysis, we pinpointed 1 common differentially expressed gene (DEG), namely SLIT2 (Fig. 1F).

Subsequently, to comprehensively validate and further explore the expression pattern of SLIT2, we identified and analyzed DEGs between healthy and LUAD tissues within each of the five datasets, employing a threshold of P < 0.01 and a fold change > 1 or < − 1. The results consistently revealed a downregulation of SLIT2 in LUAD tissue (Fig. 1A-E). Therefore, the subsequent investigation focused on SLIT2 to clarify its role in LUAD.

In LUAD, SLIT2 expression is low and associated with poorer patient outcomes

To investigate SLIT2 expression in LUAD and LUSC, data from TCGA and GEPIA were used. Both databases demonstrated reduced SLIT2 expression in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 2A–C). SLIT2 in LUAD tissues was further examined through immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 2D) that indicated a decrease in the protein expression compared with adjacent non-cancerous tissues, suggesting a potential involvement in the progression and pathogenesis of LUAD. Subsequently, the difference in overall survival (OS) between the groups exhibiting high and low expression levels of SLIT2 showed that the high-expression group had an improved prognosis in NSCLC and LUAD patients (Fig. 2E and F). The present results demonstrated the potential of SLIT2 as a prognostic indicator. However, in LUSC, there was no definitive association between the expression level of SLIT2 and the survival time (Fig. 2G).

To further strengthen these observations, western blotting and RT-qPCR were applied to investigate SLIT2 expression levels in bronchial epithelial cells and various NSCLC cell lines. Aligning with GEO and TCGA data, the current findings indicated that bronchial epithelial cells, exemplified by BEAS-2B and HBE, exhibited relatively elevated levels of SLIT2 expression compared with the NSCLC cell lines (Fig. 2H and I). Having confirmed pan-NSCLC trends, subsequent analyses focused on LUAD to exploit its therapeutic implications.

Genetic variation of SLIT2 gene and PPI network in LUAD

Using the online tool c-BioPortal, 6 datasets with a total of 2276 samples were selected for the analysis of SLIT2 genetic alterations in LUAD (Fig. 3A). It was found that the overall mutation rate of SLIT2 was 7%, with nonsense mutations being the most prevalent type (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the prognostic outcomes between patients with mutant and non-mutant SLIT2 status were compared. The results indicated no statistically significant difference in OS or disease-free survival (DFS) (Fig. 3C). While mutations in SLIT2 may contribute to alterations in tumor progression, the current findings do not conclusively demonstrate a significant association between these mutations and a meaningful impact on patients’ overall prognosis. In addition, to further analyze the proteins that interact with SLIT2, a PPI network was constructed. First, the top 20 interacting proteins were identified using the GeneMANIA database (Fig. 3D). Subsequently, the STRING database was used to identify the top 25 interacting proteins related to SLIT2 (Fig. 3E) and the results showed that SLIT2 had potential interaction with ROBO1, SEMA3A, GPC1, ROBO2 and RAC1. Hence, SLIT2 is likely implicated in diverse biological functions, encompassing cell migration, neural development and signal transduction, through its potential interactions with proteins such as ROBO1, SEMA3A, GPC1, ROBO2 and RAC1. This suggested that SLIT2 is a key player in coordinating and regulating these diverse cellular activities by engaging in intricate protein-protein interactions.

GO and KEGG analysis of genes co-expressed with SLIT2 in LUAD

LinkedOmics’ LinkFinder was used to explore the genes interacting with SLIT2 in LUAD. The heat map of the top 20 genes negatively associated with SLIT2 is shown in Fig. 4A. Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed (Fig. 4B). The enrichment analysis of the first five biological processes showed that SLIT2 was mainly related to extracellular structural organization, regulation of ion transmembrane transport, positive regulation of secretion, humoral immune response and cell-cell adhesion via plasma-membrane adhesion molecules. The GO analysis of the top five cellular components showed that SLIT2 was mainly related to the collagen-containing extracellular matrix, synaptic membrane, ciliary part, postsynaptic membrane and presynapse. The GO analysis of the top 5 molecular functions showed that SLIT2 was mainly related to the receptor ligand activity, passive transmembrane transporter activity, substrate-specific channel activity, DNA-binding transcription activator, RNA polymerase II-specific and growth factor activity.

The KEGG enrichment analysis focused on SLIT2 indicated a significant association with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) processes, specifically the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and ECM-receptor interaction. This association was further visualized in a KEGG network map (Fig. 4C).

To gain further insights, GSEA was performed on TCGA-LUAD data to examine the molecular pathways altered in LUAD patients exhibiting high SLIT2 expression. The current analysis revealed the enrichment of several pathways, including ‘adhesion molecules cams’, ‘focal adhesion’, ‘pathways in cancer’ and ‘tight junction’, in patients with elevated SLIT2 expression (Fig. 4D–I). These findings highlight the potential impact of differences in SLIT2 expression on outcomes in patients with LUAD.

Knockdown of SLIT2 promotes the proliferation and migration of LUAD cells

To delve into the functional role of SLIT2 in LUAD cells, a gene-silencing strategy was implemented using siRNA transfection. The two siRNAs used in the present study, namely si-SLIT2-1 and si-SLIT2-2, were validated to effectively downregulate SLIT2 expression, both at the transcriptional and translational levels, in LUAD cell lines (Fig. 5A–C). Upon SLIT2 knockdown, distinct alterations in the expression of EMT markers were observed, indicating a potential role for SLIT2 in modulating EMT processes. Specifically, it was found a decreased E-cadherin expression, a hallmark of epithelial cells, and concomitant increases in N-cadherin, Snail and vimentin expression, all markers associated with mesenchymal cells and enhanced cell migration (Fig. 5D and E). These findings strongly suggested that SLIT2 acts as a suppressor of EMT in LUAD cells, further underscoring its importance in maintaining epithelial integrity. To directly assess the impact of SLIT2 knockdown on LUAD cell proliferation, two sensitive methods were employed, such as EdU staining (Fig. 5F) and the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 5G). The results showed that the proliferation of both cell lines increased significantly after SLIT2 knockdown.

The wound healing assay indicated a significant increase in wound recovery after SLIT2 knockdown (Fig. 5H and I). In addition, after SLIT2 knockdown, migration and invasion of A549 and H1299 cells were also significantly enhanced (Fig. 5J and K). The results showed that SLIT2 silencing boosted LUAD cell proliferation, migration and invasion, suggesting that SLIT2 may act as a tumor suppressor when downregulated. By harnessing the natural ability of SLIT2 to inhibit tumor growth and metastasis, effective treatments that target the underlying causes of LUAD progression could be developed.

Overexpression of SLIT2 inhibits the proliferation and migration of LUAD cells

SLIT2 overexpression experiments were performed to investigate the functional consequences of the SLIT2 upregulation in LUAD cells. SLIT2 overexpression, at both the transcriptional and translational levels, was confirmed in LUAD cell lines utilizing overexpression constructs (Fig. 6A–C). SLIT2 overexpression led to notable changes in EMT markers, indicating a potential role of SLIT2 in modulating EMT processes. Notably, the overexpression of SLIT2 resulted in increased E-cadherin levels, characteristic of epithelial cells, while simultaneously decreasing N-cadherin, Snail and vimentin expression, all markers associated with mesenchymal phenotypes and migratory capacity (Fig. 6D and E). These findings suggested that SLIT2 may function as an EMT inhibitor in LUAD cells, emphasizing its role in maintaining epithelial homeostasis.

EdU staining (Fig. 6F) and the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 6G) revealed a significant decrease in LUAD cell proliferation upon SLIT2 overexpression. Similarly, wound healing and migration assays demonstrated a notable reduction in the LUAD cell migration upon increased SLIT2 expression (Fig. 6H–K). Therefore, the overexpression of SLIT2 inhibited invasion and migration in LUAD. These findings underscore the pivotal role of SLIT2 in modulating LUAD cell behavior. They conclusively show that enhancing SLIT2 expression effectively reduces proliferation, migration and invasion of LUAD cells, positioning SLIT2 as a tumor suppressor in this context. Loss or downregulation of SLIT2, conversely, promotes aggressive tumor traits. Further exploration of the underlying mechanisms by which SLIT2 exerts its tumor suppressive functions will advance the current understanding of LUAD pathogenesis and potentially reveal novel therapeutic targets to combat this aggressive cancer subtype.

Immune cells infiltration analyses of SLIT2

Immune cells crucially influence cancer prognosis in the immune microenvironment. However, the question remains open regarding whether SLIT2 modulates the recruitment of these cells. To address this issue, the ssGSEA technique from the R package was used to examine the relationship between SLIT2 expression and the infiltration of immune cells. Using the TCGA-LUAD database, the scores for 24 immune cell types were calculated, revealing a positive association between SLIT2 expression and immune cell infiltration, excluding Th2 cells (Fig. 7A).

Delving deeper into this correlation, the TCGA database was mined and it was observed a significant positive correlation was observed between SLIT2 and macrophages (r = 0.410; P < 0.001), NK cells (r = 0.410; P < 0.001) and Mast cells (r = 0.510; P < 0.001) (Fig. 7B–D). Our previous study demonstrated that SLIT2 may regulate the polarization direction of macrophages in the tumor microenvironment and promote their differentiation into M1-type macrophages with anti-tumor activity. Specifically, the CIBERSORT algorithm’s analysis of the transcriptome data of LUAD reveals a significant positive correlation between SLIT2 expression level and the proportion of M1-type macrophage infiltration (r = 0.22, P < 0.01) (Fig. S2). This finding suggests that SLIT2 may act as an immunomodulatory molecule and reshape the immune balance of the tumor microenvironment by enhancing the recruitment or local activation of M1-type macrophages. This mechanism may provide new intervention targets for developing cancer immunotherapy strategies based on macrophage polarization.

Moreover, while analyzing the correlation between SLIT2 expression and the abundance of these infiltrating cells, a consistent positive trend with Mast cells, NK cells and macrophages was discovered (Fig. 7E–G). The present findings indicate that SLIT2 may inhibit tumor progression by altering the tumor microenvironment by influencing immune cell recruitment.

Discussion

NSCLC represents a challenging health issue globally and LUAD is the most common subtype [15], accounting for 50% of all lung cancer diagnoses and with increasing incidence [16, 17]. Therefore, identifying predictive biomarkers is important to provide new insights, diagnoses and treatments. The present study aimed to clarify the low expression of SLIT2 in NSCLC and its impact on cell proliferation, invasion and metastasis.

Recent studies emphasized the comprehensive role of the SLIT family in numerous crucial physiological and pathological processes. Among these, the SLIT2-ROBO1 signaling pathway inhibits macrophage proliferation [18]. A series of signal transduction mechanisms involving SLIT2 acting on target cells can modulate various downstream pathways (β-catenin/LEF/TCF, PI3K/Akt, CXCL12/CXCR4 and RAFTK/Pyk2), thereby regulating tumor invasion and metastasis [19–22]. Wang et al. identified SLIT2 as a target gene of miR-423-5p. Additionally, PGM5-AS1 has been shown to suppress NSCLC development by regulating the miR-423-5p/SLIT2 axis [23]. Consistent with the current findings, Wang et al. demonstrated that the downregulation of SLIT2 in LUAD is associated with a favorable prognosis [24], suggesting its potential as a prognostic marker. Furthermore, reduced methylation of SLITs genes may serve as potential biomarker for detecting NSCLC and predicting recurrence-free survival [25]. SLIT2 also functions as a suppressor in SCLC (Small Cell Lung Cancer)and LSCC (Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma) [12, 26]. Finally, blockade of the SLIT2/Robo4 axis has been found to effectively enhance angiogenic function in SSc-MVEC [27], further highlighting the multifaceted role of the SLIT family in biological processes.

Metastasis of cancer cells to distant body parts is the main killer in lung cancer. Several early-stage patients relapse because distant metastasis is not detected [28]. Emerging studies suggest that SLIT2 may play context-dependent roles in cancer, acting as a tumor suppressor in certain settings (e.g., lung adenocarcinoma), but potentially promoting metastasis in others (e.g., gastric cancer). Specifically, evidence suggested that the downregulation of SLIT2 can effectively inhibit the metastasis and progression of tumor cells. For instance, cancer-associated fibroblasts were shown to increase the expression and functional activity of cyclomethyline through the serine/threonine kinase NEK9, mediated by SLIT2, thereby enabling gastric cancer cells to acquire heightened migratory capabilities via cytoskeletal remodeling [11]. Furthermore, SLIT2 interacts with receptors ROBO1 and ROBO2, promoting liver fibrosis through the activation of downstream PI3K/Akt signaling pathways [29], highlighting its broader involvement in cell signaling and disease progression. These studies indicated that SLIT2 may have a cancer-promoting role; however, the present study found that SLIT2 in cancer tissues is downregulated compared with normal lung tissues. These findings were validated not only through online datasets like GEO and TCGA but also in LUAD tissues and cell models. At the same time, it was found that the downregulation of SLIT2 can promote EMT and enhance the migration and invasion capabilities of LUAD cells.

Accumulating evidence underscores the role of the immune system in inhibiting the initiation, progression, metastasis and dissemination of LUAD [30]. Understanding the role and mechanism of the immune response in LUAD is helpful to improve the clinical use of immunotherapy drugs. Therefore, the present study analyzed the immune scores by R-encapsulated ssGSEA and discovered a positive association between SLIT2 expression and certain immune cell subsets, including NK cells, dendritic cells and macrophages. SLIT2 exhibited a broad influence on LUAD inflammatory infiltration and high SLIT2 expression may predict a more favorable response to immunotherapy.

Overall, the present findings confirmed that SLIT2 is downregulated in LUAD. The prognostic significance of SLIT2 and its relationship with LUAD immunoinfiltration were verified. In LUAD cell lines, SLIT2 knockdowns promote migration and invasion. We speculate that SLIT2 could serve as a potential therapeutic target for LUAD rather than solely as a new molecular target for early diagnosis. Its exploitation may offer targeted therapeutic options for this disease. However, more confirmatory experiments are needed to explore and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

The present study presents some limitations. First, more tissue samples should be obtained to support the TCGA findings. Secondly, the mechanism through which SLIT2 regulates EMT remains unclear. Third, although SLIT2 expression is positively associated with immune cell infiltration, direct mechanistic evidence of SLIT2 regulating immune cell function remains to be elucidated.

In conclusion, the current study indicated that SLIT2 is dysregulated as a tumor suppressor gene in LUAD cell lines and clinical tissues. In addition, siRNA silencing of SLIT2 promotes proliferation, migration and invasion of LUAD cells. These findings imply that enhancing SLIT2 activity may represent a promising therapeutic approach for LUAD treatment, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target. This could provide new insights into the pathogenesis of NSCLC and improve its clinical prognosis. Therefore, further investigation into the specific role of SLIT2 in LUAD progression is warranted. Future studies should aim to identify key downstream effectors of SLIT2, which may serve as actionable molecular targets for therapeutic development in LUAD.
