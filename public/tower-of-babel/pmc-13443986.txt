Bridging gaps in diabetic neuropathy care: A Delphi‐based regional consensus from the Gulf Cooperation Council

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionDiabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is a common complication of diabetes in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) region, associated with delayed diagnosis, significant pain burden, and preventable complications. This Delphi‐based consensus aimed to identify regional gaps in screening and management to develop practical, evidence‐based recommendations tailored to GCC healthcare systems.Materials and MethodsA three‐round Delphi process was conducted involving 12 diabetes experts from six GCC countries, who reviewed international guidelines, identified unmet clinical needs, and voted on consensus statements addressing epidemiology, screening, diagnosis, and treatment, with consensus defined as ≥75% agreement.ResultsThe panel reached strong agreement on shifting from symptom‐driven to proactive care, recommending routine annual screening for all individuals with diabetes and prediabetes. Experts also emphasized early, integrated management combining lifestyle modification, cardiovascular risk reduction, pathogenetic therapies, and individualized pharmacologic treatment of neuropathic pain. Key barriers identified included limited consultation time, high clinical workload, suboptimal awareness of DPN among clinicians and patients, and restricted access to guideline‐recommended therapies due to regulatory constraints.ConclusionsThis regional consensus highlights the need for standardized, proactive care of patients with DPN, across the GCC. Implementation of systematic screening, early multimodal management, and policy‐level improvements in access to therapies may improve patients' quality of life and reduce complications.Keywords: Consensus Statement, Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy, Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) affects approximately 589 million adults globally (11.1%) and is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050, with a parallel increase in complications. 1 , 2 The prevalence of DM across the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries—Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and United Arab Emirates—is particularly high, ranging from 17.0% to 25.6%, 2 driven by obesity and sedentary lifestyles. 3 Indeed, four Gulf countries rank among the top 10 globally for diabetes prevalence. 2

Diabetic neuropathy (DN) encompasses a spectrum of nerve disorders in people with diabetes. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) occurs due to peripheral nerve involvement 4 , 5 , with distal symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy (DSPN) affecting 75% of those with diabetic neuropathy. 6 Painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN) is characterized by neuropathic pain and affects 20%–40% of people with diabetes.

The prevalence of diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) ranges from 20% to 90%, due to different diagnostic methods and risk factors. 7 , 8 , 9 We have previously demonstrated a much higher prevalence of both DSPN and PDN in the Gulf region, which was associated with poorer glycemic control, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity, and reduced physical activity. 10 DSPN increases the risk of falls, fractures, foot ulceration, amputation, and mortality, 6 , 11 with 25% developing foot ulcers and approximately 5% progressing to amputation, which is associated with a 5‐year mortality that exceeds most common cancers. 12 , 13 PDN is associated with inadequate sleep and depression, with two to three times higher annual healthcare costs compared with nonpainful neuropathy. 1 , 14 , 15 , 16

This Delphi consensus highlights the urgent need to address the underdiagnosis and treatment of DPN and PDN and proposes strategies for earlier detection and improved management of DPN and PDN in the GCC.

In this manuscript, “gaps” refer to discrepancies between current evidence and real‐world clinical practice, as well as unmet diagnostic and therapeutic needs in DPN. These gaps were identified through structured Delphi expert discussions, where clinicians highlighted their challenges in routine clinical practice across GCC countries. The identified gaps were cross‐checked against international guidelines and published literature.

CONSENSUS FINDING PROCESS

Panel composition and selection criteria

The expert panel comprised 12 clinicians with recognized expertise in diabetic peripheral neuropathy, with at least one expert from each GCC country (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Oman, Kuwait, Qatar, and Bahrain). Several panel members are also affiliated with regional societies involved in diabetes care and clinical research in the GCC region.

Consensus methodology

Experts identified major gaps in screening, diagnosis, and management, and deficiencies in regional clinical practice relative to international guidelines and developed consensus on improved diagnosis and management of DPN in the GCC. The panel sought to develop actionable, evidence‐based, and region‐appropriate recommendations after reviewing the evidence from systematic reviews, meta‐analyses, and RCTs. Where data were limited, consensus relied on collective clinical expertise.

A three‐round Delphi process was used to ensure a structured and rigorous approach: Delphi voting was conducted anonymously using structured online forms, ensuring that individual responses were not visible to other panelists. After each round, aggregated results (percentage agreement) were shared with the group to guide discussion, without disclosing individual ratings. Panel discussions were conducted to refine statements; however, voting remained independent and anonymous throughout the process.

Round 1: An online questionnaire (Google Forms) combining open and multiple‐choice questions was completed by all 12 experts. The responses were analyzed to generate the initial set of consensus statements and clinical algorithms.

Round 2: Experts voted on the drafted statements, and agreement was scored on a 5‐point scale (strongly disagree to strongly agree).

Round 3: Before the final voting round (round 3), statements that did not reach a strong consensus (≥75% agreement) in round 2 were discussed in depth during a structured expert meeting, focusing on GCC‐specific gaps, practical regional challenges, and adaptations needed for local implementation, leading to refined GCC‐specific statements.

To minimize dominance bias, only aggregated agreement levels were shared without disclosure of individual responses, and no voting was conducted during the discussion phase. Statements were refined in wording where appropriate and subsequently re‐evaluated through an independent and anonymous final round of voting.

The agreement levels presented in the manuscript reflect the final voting results for all statements. For statements that reached a strong consensus in Round 2, the reported values correspond to that round, whereas for statements with lower initial agreement, the reported values correspond to the final round after refinement. No statements were omitted from reporting.

One panelist was unable to participate in the final round due to scheduling constraints. Therefore, the final round included 11 participants. Consensus rates were calculated based on the number of respondents in each round, with no imputation for missing data. All analyses were conducted using available responses.

Consensus levels

Consensus levels for each statement were predefined as follows:

Strong consensus: ≥75% of experts agreed

Strong consensus: ≥75% of experts agreed

Moderate consensus: 60%–74% agreement

Moderate consensus: 60%–74% agreement

Low consensus: <60% agreement

Low consensus: <60% agreement

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF DIABETIC POLYNEUROPATHY

Diabetic neuropathy (DN) has a multifactorial etiology in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Hyperglycemia is a major driver, 17 with “metabolic memory” leading to progression of both somatic, 18 and autonomic neuropathy. 19 DN also has a vascular basis with reduced nerve blood flow 20 and vascular insufficiency. 21 Multiple vascular risk factors, including hyperlipidemia, hypertension, obesity, and smoking, are associated with DPN and PDN in the GCC. 10 Early pathology occurs in the unmyelinated C fibers, with later demyelination and axonal degeneration of the myelinated fibers. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25

PREVALENCE AND BURDEN OF DIABETIC NEUROPATHY IN THE GCC

Prevalence of diabetic neuropathy

Globally, at least 50% of people with diabetes have DPN, 26 with a prevalence of PDN around 21% in studies from the United States and Europe. 27 Our recent studies from the GCC reveal a DPN prevalence of 33.3%, with specific country‐level prevalence of 23.9% in Qatar, 35.9% in KSA, and 40.3% in Kuwait, of whom 52.2% had advanced neuropathy and were at risk of DFU, but 53.6% were undiagnosed. The prevalence of PDN was 2‐fold higher (43.3%), but 54.3% were undiagnosed. 10 Alshammari et al. reported a DPN prevalence of 40.2% in Saudi Arabia. 28

Our recent systematic review revealed a prevalence of 2%–77% for neuropathy in prediabetes, but the majority of studies reported a prevalence ≥10%. 29 A study from the UAE identified a PN prevalence of 11.5% among individuals with prediabetes. 30

This suggests that neuropathy begins in prediabetes and is driven by multiple vascular risk factors beyond just poor glycemic control, underscoring the need for proactive early screening and early multi‐risk factor intervention.

Asymptomatic DSPN: A hidden epidemic‐diagnostic delay

In the GCC, DSPN is common but often undiagnosed with a clinical focus on “positive” sensory symptoms (e.g., burning or tingling). In contrast, “negative” symptoms such as numbness and sensory loss are often overlooked, and a study from Qatar reported that 82% of patients with DN attending secondary care had not been previously diagnosed. 31 Similar findings have been reported across multiple studies in the region, consistently demonstrating a high proportion of undiagnosed DPN. 32 According to the American Diabetes Association, up to 50% of diabetic peripheral neuropathies may be asymptomatic. 6

Higher prevalence of painful diabetic neuropathy in the GCC‐modifiable risk factors

Painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN) is more prevalent in the GCC region. Cross‐sectional studies from Saudi Arabia have reported PDN prevalence rates of at least 35%, 33 , 34 and a study from Qatar reported a prevalence of 34.5%. 35 Our previous study from the Gulf region of 3,021 individuals with T2DM showed a DPN prevalence of 33.3% and a PDN prevalence of 43.3%, the AOR for DPN increased 2.2–5.0‐fold with diabetes duration (P ≤ 0.0001), 2.0‐fold with obesity (P ≤ 0.0001), 1.6‐fold with poor glycemic control (HbA1c >7%; P ≤ 0.0001), 1.4‐fold with hyperlipidemia (P = 0.01), and decreased 1.7‐fold with physical activity (P ≤ 0.0001). 10 These figures are much higher than the 26.4% prevalence reported in the UK population, 36 highlighting a regional disparity likely driven by poor glycaemic control, high rates of obesity, metabolic syndrome, and sedentary lifestyles. SCREENING AND DIAGNOSIS OF DIABETIC POLYNEUROPATHY

Symptoms and signs

Diabetic neuropathy typically presents with distal sensory symptoms in the feet and hands. Symptoms may be positive with pain, tingling, and paresthesia, negative with numbness, or due to altered sensory processing resulting in allodynia/hyperalgesia. 6 Motor involvement begins with toe weakness and progresses to more proximal ankle and calf weakness, which, with sensory loss, can lead to imbalance and falls. 24 , 37

Clinical signs include reduced or loss of sensation to pinprick, temperature (especially cold), vibration, and proprioception, loss of ankle reflexes, and weakness of the dorsiflexors. 9

Validated scoring systems such as the Toronto Clinical Neuropathy Score (TCNS) and the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI) combine a symptom questionnaire with a brief physical exam, enabling screening and assessment of DPN severity. 9

Importance of early detection and routine screening

Early detection is key to preventing morbidity from PDN. Any screening strategy for the detection of early PDN has to identify small‐fiber damage, which precedes large fiber involvement. 6 , 24 , 38 , 39 Indeed, small fiber neuropathy has been shown to affect 10%–30% of individuals with prediabetes or metabolic syndrome. 6

The ADA recommends annual neuropathy screening in all patients with diabetes, regardless of symptoms. 40 Routine screening and diagnostic tests must be rapid, reliable, and easy to perform and should accurately identify small nerve fiber dysfunction/damage. However, assessment with the 10 g monofilament, vibration sensation using a 128‐Hz tuning fork, or reflexes identifies large fiber damage and advanced DN in those at risk of ulceration and amputation. Tests like the 10 g monofilament are not fit for purpose for early neuropathy detection and yet continue to be advocated. 41 , 42

A careful history may identify painful symptoms, but the assessment of temperature or pinprick sensation to evaluate small‐fiber damage is relatively insensitive and rarely performed in a busy clinic. 43 Rapid, accurate, sensitive, and reproducible tests are required to identify small‐fiber damage and repair. Assessment of sudomotor function and skin biopsy enables identification of small fiber dysfunction and damage but has considerable variability 44 and is not widely available. 45 Corneal confocal microscopy (CCM) is an ophthalmic imaging technique that has robust evidence that it can identify early and more advanced DPN, 46 especially utilizing AI. 47 It has also been used to identify a range of other peripheral neuropathies, 48 and could be deployed alongside retinal screening. 49 Indeed, more recently, a deep learning based multi‐class system, Neuropath‐AI, has been deployed to differentiate diabetic from other major peripheral neuropathies from CCM images. 50 A comparison between current GCC clinical practice and international best‐practice standards for screening and assessment of diabetic peripheral neuropathy is shown in Figure 1.

Brief assessment tools for neuropathy

Questionnaires like DN4, LANSS, and NTSS‐6 are validated for identifying PDN but are time‐consuming and lack consistent diagnostic precision. To address this, we have recently developed ACT, a 5‐item patient‐led questionnaire and a 2‐item physician‐led sensory test to enable quicker, more efficient identification and referral of patients with neuropathy. 51

Differential diagnosis

Not all neuropathies in diabetic patients are due to diabetes, especially with atypical features, e.g., motor predominance, asymmetry, and rapid progression. 9 , 52 Alternative causes include vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, renal insufficiency, alcohol use, neurotoxic medications, HIV, hepatitis, vasculitis, monoclonal gammopathy, amyloidosis, or hereditary neuropathies. 9 , 53 Consensus statements related to current screening and assessment practices for DPN in the GCC are detailed in the Supplementary Material.

CURRENT PRACTICES AND DIAGNOSTIC GAPS IN THE GCC

Despite national and international guidelines and recommendations, screening for DN in the GCC is largely reactive, initiated only after symptom onset. Instruments such as the DN4 questionnaire, Neuropathy Symptom Score (NSS), Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI), Toronto Clinical Neuropathy Score (TCNS), and McGill Pain Questionnaire, 10‐g monofilament, and 128‐Hz tuning fork are rarely used in routine clinical practice. Nerve conduction studies (NCS) are sensitive and specific, 54 , 55 but are reserved for atypical presentations after referral to neurology. The more advanced and precise method of CCM can identify early subclinical nerve damage but is currently not widely available.

Proposed integrated care approaches

The one‐stop shop model of screening for all three microvascular complications at one visit has gained considerable interest recently, given the reduction in blindness following the implementation of diabetic retinal screening, 56 especially utilizing AI. 57 WHO‐accredited training of primary care staff to perform foot exams during retinal visits increased screening uptake to ~80%. 32

Role of patient education

Patient education is key to encouraging early symptom reporting to improve screening. ACT, a short 5‐item patient‐led questionnaire, has been developed recently to change the screening paradigm, enabling rapid identification of neuropathy. 51

Challenges in the GCC region

The high prevalence of DSPN due to increased obesity and vascular risk factors in the GCC is compounded by operational barriers that affect screening, delaying diagnosis, and timely management. Healthcare system constraints

Time constraints

High patient volumes in outpatient clinics limit clinicians' ability to perform a neurological assessment for DN. Primary care physicians perceive the diagnosis of peripheral neuropathy as time‐consuming and complex, favoring short, simple, and accurate screening tools. 43 In Kuwait, even though neuropathy screening takes less than 5 min per patient, understaffed clinics with large numbers of patients limit deployment of effective DPN screening. 58 In Qatar, despite a protocol for annual referral to podiatry for neuropathy screening, ~80% of patients remained undiagnosed. 35

Lack of Screening and Delayed Diagnosis

Assessment is largely reactive, with neuropathy evaluation only undertaken in patients with more advanced DPN. 6 In a study of 3,021 adults with type 2 diabetes in Qatar, Kuwait, and Saudi Arabia, 53.6% of patients with DPN and 54.3% of patients with PDN were undiagnosed. 10 In primary care in Riyadh, almost none of the patients were aware they had PDN. 34

Training and Awareness Gaps

Healthcare professionals do not systematically screen for DN, resulting in delayed or underdiagnosis. 59 Other healthcare professionals, such as nurses, are minimally involved in the care of patients with DPN, and there is a lack of adequately trained podiatrists. 60 Comprehensive training programs for both physicians and other health care workers are critical to improving early detection and improving the management of patients with DPN. 32

Treatment Constraints

Most treatment options for the management of PDN have limited efficacy, 61 due to side effects, which is especially relevant in the Gulf region. Furthermore, some FDA‐approved therapeutic options like capsaicin are unavailable, and gabapentinoids face regulatory restrictions in many GCC countries due to misuse concerns, with increased prescription of tramadol (21%) and acetaminophen/codeine (16%), 62 which has a considerable potential for addiction and increased harm.

The Need for Local Studies

Current data on DPN in the GCC are limited, and guidelines on the diagnosis and management are based on studies from Europe and the United States, which may not reflect local genetic, behavioral, and healthcare dynamics. Robust epidemiological studies and RCTs are needed from the region to characterize disease patterns, inform on screening and treatment protocols to guide resource allocation and public health strategies.

Population constraints

Cultural and Social Factors

Cultural norms and social barriers limit engagement in preventive care, especially to address obesity and reduced physical activity, a key risk factor for DPN and PDN. 10 Outdoor activity is constrained by conservative social expectations and hot weather. 63 Patients often underreport symptoms, seeking medical advice only when daily life is affected, and mild symptoms are dismissed due to cultural perceptions of pain. 43

Public Awareness and Education

Limited awareness of DPN delays diagnosis, with many patients misattributing symptoms such as numbness or tingling to aging, delaying diagnosis by up to 5 years. 43 In Saudi Arabia, only 37.2% of patients were aware of DPN. 64 Culturally tailored public health campaigns and patient education are key to early recognition and timely care‐seeking.

Lifestyle

There is clear evidence of suboptimal glycemic control with an HbA1c <7% in only 20%–40% of patients 65 and high rates of overweight/obesity and metabolic syndrome. 66 A regional meta‐analysis estimated the pooled prevalence of metabolic syndrome to be 28% in the GCC, ranging from 22% in Kuwait to 39% in the UAE. 67

Physical inactivity is high, with most adolescents failing to meet recommended activity levels due to cultural norms and inadequate physical health programs. 68 , 69 This underscores the need for targeted patient education, lifestyle interventions, and culturally sensitive public health initiatives to reduce the burden of risk factors for DPN in the GCC. TREATMENT OF PAINFUL DIABETIC NEUROPATHY

For the management of PDN, therapy should be individualized, taking into consideration symptom severity, comorbidities, and availability of therapies. 70 In the GCC, clinicians prioritize minimizing adverse effects when selecting pharmacologic options. Furthermore, local availability impacts significantly on the choice of medication and while cost is relevant, it plays a lesser role in decision‐making. Suboptimal treatment responses can result from patient‐related challenges, for example, noncompliance and unrealistic expectations; disease‐related factors, such as chronicity of neuropathy; and treatment‐related issues, including limited efficacy and adverse events, 71 with fewer than one‐third of patients achieving adequate pain relief with current pharmacotherapies. 72

Treatment failure is typically defined by persistent or worsening symptoms despite adequate dosing or intolerable side effects. In such cases, clinicians may switch to a different pharmacologic class or add an agent from another class to enhance efficacy and tolerability. 70 Setting realistic goals, reinforcing adherence, and implementing vigilant monitoring for side effects and treatment efficacy are critical to achieving optimal patient outcomes. 73 , 74

Disease‐modifying treatment

DN is currently believed to be irreversible, and improved glycemic control has been shown to delay progression in type 1, but not type 2 diabetes. 75 However, a recent systematic review showed that CCM identified nerve regeneration after improved glycemic control following simultaneous pancreas‐kidney transplantation and closed‐loop delivery of insulin in type 1 diabetes 76 , 77 , 78 and after GLP‐1/GLP‐1/GIP therapy, 79 , 80 SGLT2i, 81 and bariatric surgery 82 in type 2 diabetes.

Dietary measures and increased physical activity, 83 treatment of hyperlipidemia, 84 and ACE inhibition, 85 also improve DPN. A combination of exercise and dietary changes has also been shown to improve intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD), nerve conduction, and neuropathic symptoms 9 and exercise alone may also confer benefit in DPN. 6

These measures are particularly relevant in the GCC, where obesity, physical inactivity, metabolic syndrome, and poor glycemic control are highly prevalent and contribute significantly to DN. Pharmaco‐complementary therapy

Pharmaco‐complementary (pathogenetic) treatments may provide additional benefit even in patients with inadequate glycemic control. 86 Treatment with vitamin B1 (benfotiamine), vitamin B12, and alpha‐lipoic acid (ALA) may have a disease‐modifying effect alongside pharmacotherapy for symptom control. 87 While these agents show an improvement in neuropathic pain, the effectiveness of combining them remains uncertain, and further clinical trials are needed to assess their individual and combined roles in managing painful diabetic neuropathy.

The efficacy of ALA has been evaluated in large, randomized trials, and meta‐analyses report overall efficacy for neuropathic pain, 88 , 89 , 90 , 91 but with limited benefits on quantitative sensory testing and nerve conduction. Two recent randomized placebo‐controlled studies have also shown efficacy with vitamin B12 92 and a combination of B1, B6, and B12 93 with an improvement in neuropathic symptoms, nerve conduction, and autonomic function. B vitamins—especially benfotiamine and vitamin B12—are widely used in clinical practice in GCC, due to their long‐standing familiarity among physicians and patients, low cost, and favorable safety profile. Current practices in the use of pharmaco‐complementary therapies in the GCC are summarized in the Supporting Information.

Alpha‐lipoic acid

Alpha‐lipoic acid (ALA) improves redox balance, reduces lipid peroxidation, and enhances antioxidant enzyme activity, while modulating neuroinflammation through suppression of NF‐κB activation. 88 , 89 A meta‐analysis of 10 RCTs (1,242 patients; 600–1800 mg/day) showed a dose‐dependent improvement in total symptom score (TSS) and global satisfaction compared with placebo. 88 Further, a systematic review of 16 RCTs (2,693 patients) reported significant reductions in TSS, especially stabbing pain, burning, paraesthesia, and numbness. 91

The German Diabetes Association Clinical Practice Guidelines recommend ALA for mild to moderate neuropathic pain. 94 Expert opinion supports that ALA alleviates pain, burning sensation, and numbness with an overall improvement in quality of life. 52 , 95 ALA is most effective in early‐stage neuropathy, where patients are more likely to experience symptom relief and partial reversal of deficits with a favorable safety profile. 89 , 95 Symptom improvement may occur within 2 weeks, though benefits are gradual over several months. Patient education regarding timelines for improvement is essential to support adherence and optimize outcomes.

ALA is typically prescribed as a daily oral dose of 600 mg with higher doses of 1,200–1,800 mg daily, based on clinical judgment and patient tolerance. 88 Assessment of the efficacy of combination therapy with pregabalin, duloxetine, or tapentadol requires further studies. 96 ALA's favorable safety and tolerability make it suitable for long‐term treatment, as a 4‐year multicenter RCT of 460 patients showed that daily ALA 600 mg was well tolerated. 90 Expert consensus on the clinical use of ALA in DPN is summarized in the Supporting Information.

Aldose reductase inhibitors

Aldose reductase inhibitors (ARIs) represent a pathogenetic approach targeting the polyol pathway implicated in diabetic complications. 97 Examples include Epalrestat and Ranirestat, with availability largely limited to certain regions. Epalrestat—approved in Japan—has shown efficacy in slowing the progression of diabetic neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy. 98 However, evidence from randomized trials remains inconsistent, with some analyses showing no significant benefit over placebo in diabetic polyneuropathy, 99 emphasizing the need for further well‐designed trials using more potent and selective ARIs.

Vitamin B

Vitamin B supplementation is widely prescribed for DPN, especially in the GCC.

Vitamin B1

Benfotiamine, a fat‐soluble thiamine (B1) derivative, is preferred for its superior bioavailability 100 and is a reasonable pathogenetic treatment option. 101 Daily doses typically range from 100 to 600 mg. A randomized study of 165 patients with DSPN showed improvement with 600 mg/day, with benefits increasing over time. 102

Vitamin B12

Vitamin B12 is recommended for people with B12 deficiency due to metformin. 103 Monitoring of B12 is recommended, especially in patients with neuropathic symptoms. 104 Typical doses range from 500 to 1,000 mcg/day, with intramuscular administration considered in those with severe deficiency, malabsorption, or after bariatric surgery. A meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials showed that B12 improves neuropathic symptoms and reduces pain in diabetic neuropathy. 105 , 106 , 107 A combination of B1, B6, and B12 has been shown to significantly improve symptom scores in patients with peripheral neuropathy. 108

Cyanocobalamin, Thiamine, alongside SNRIs, TCAs, and anticonvulsants may have synergistic effects. 107 Hydroxycobalamin is more retained in the body compared to cyanocobalamin due to its extensive binding to plasma transcobalamin. Following a 1,000 μg dose, about 30% is retained, providing sufficient coverage for the body's requirements for 2 to 10 months. 109 A randomized controlled trial reported that a combination therapy of nucleotides + hydroxycobalamin is significantly better than hydroxycobalamin monotherapy for pain reduction in neuralgia. 110

Methylcobalamin: In a study of oral methylcobalamin, 1,000 μg/day over 1 year, normalization of B12 levels led to a significant improvement in pain and paresthesia and improvement in nerve conduction. 106

A 1‐year RCT of oral B12 (methylcobalamin) 1,000 μg/day in patients with diabetic neuropathy (DN) showed a significant increase in vitamin B12 levels and clinical symptoms, electrophysiology, and quality of life. 92

Symptomatic treatment of painful diabetic neuropathy

Gabapentinoids, such as gabapentin and pregabalin, and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), such as duloxetine, are recommended first‐line treatments for PDN, 6 , 40 with duloxetine preferred when depressive symptoms are present. 111 However, the use of gabapentinoids may be limited in the GCC due to regulatory restrictions because of concerns about misuse and reduced accessibility.

Gabapentin is typically started at 300–600 mg/day and titrated over several weeks to a maximum of ~1,800 mg/day in divided doses. If intolerance occurs, clinicians may slow titration, adjust the dose, or discontinue therapy. 6 , 9 Pregabalin has a more favorable pharmacokinetic profile and requires minimal titration. 6 Duloxetine is typically prescribed at a dose of 60 to 120 mg/day. 111 TCAs also show efficacy but are not FDA‐approved for PDN. 40 , 70

In the GCC, pregabalin and gabapentin are the most commonly prescribed anticonvulsants, duloxetine is the most frequently prescribed antidepressant, amitriptyline is used less often, and other TCAs, venlafaxine, and SSRIs are generally avoided. Pharmacological treatment practices for painful diabetic neuropathy in the GCC are summarized in the Supporting Information.

Opioids

Opioids such as tramadol, tapentadol, and oxycodone have shown efficacy in PDN but have an unfavorable risk–benefit profile due to dependence, misuse, and common adverse effects like sedation, constipation, and cognitive impairment. 40 , 70 The AAN advises against routine opioid use for PDN due to limited evidence of long‐term efficacy 70 and data from the CDC indicate that dependence may develop within days of initiating opioid therapy. 112 Accordingly, opioids should be reserved for treatment resistance to recommended first and second line options, used for the shortest possible duration, and closely monitored for misuse and adverse events. In the GCC, opioid use requires specialist referral. The role and limitations of opioid therapy in painful diabetic neuropathy are summarized in the Supporting Information.

Topical analgesics

Several topical treatments are available for PDN, with the main advantage being localized pain relief with minimal systemic absorption and reduced risk of CNS adverse effects. 40 , 70 Topical options may be used as monotherapy in patients with mild to moderate neuropathic pain, and in more severe cases, they are often combined with systemic pharmacological treatments to enhance symptom control. The AAN recommends that clinicians consider patient preference with topical therapy, for example, capsaicin, lidocaine, or glyceryl trinitrate spray. 70

The capsaicin 8% patch has demonstrated efficacy in large multicenter trials and was approved by the FDA in 2020 for neuropathic foot pain in adults. While it has fewer systemic adverse effects than oral agents, it causes local transient burning, erythema, and pain, and is contraindicated in patients with active skin lesions or highly sensitive skin. 96 , 111 Despite approval, its availability in many GCC countries is limited.

Lidocaine, a sodium channel blocker, is also an effective topical option with minimal systemic absorption and a favorable safety profile. However, the application should not exceed 12 h per day to avoid local irritation or toxicity. 40 Consensus statements on topical treatments for painful diabetic neuropathy are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Combination treatment

Real‐world data show that 45% of patients receive two or more medication classes, reflecting the widespread use of combination therapy. 113 , 114 Combination therapy is typically considered when patients show only partial improvement or adverse side effects with higher doses. Clinicians may switch to a different class or add an agent with a complementary mechanism of action, allowing lower doses and improved tolerability. 70 Current practice frequently employs two or three agents from different classes that can offer synergistic effects and reduce adverse events compared with high‐dose monotherapy. 6 , 96 , 113 , 115

Common combinations include pregabalin or gabapentin with duloxetine or tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), particularly when moderate‐dose monotherapy fails. 116 Although the duloxetine–pregabalin combination shows clinical benefit, the largest comparative trial found no significant advantage over high‐dose monotherapy, despite trends favoring combination use. 117

Combination strategies may extend beyond systemic therapy; pairing oral agents with topical capsaicin or lidocaine may provide benefit while minimizing systemic effects. Benfotiamine and ALA have also been explored as adjuncts, though robust randomized trial evidence remains limited. 96

Despite growing use, high‐quality comparative studies on the effectiveness, tolerability, and cost‐effectiveness of various combinations are lacking. 115 Further research is needed to define optimal combinations and identify patients most likely to benefit. STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

Strengths

This structured, multi‐round Delphi methodology ensured a rigorous, evidence‐based evaluation process, allowing experts to integrate the best available data with regional clinical realities. Consensus was representative with participation of experts in diabetic neuropathy from all GCC countries. This enhances the relevance, credibility, and practical applicability of the final recommendations across diverse GCC healthcare settings.

Limitations

The panel size was relatively small, which may limit the breadth of perspectives and the generalizability of some statements. The limited availability of robust, region‐specific epidemiological data restricted the ability to formulate evidence‐based recommendations, highlighting the need for studies in the GCC.

CONCLUSIONS

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy is a major and growing challenge in the GCC, driven by high rates of diabetes and obesity. Screening and treatment of DPN in the GCC remain reactive, with significant underdiagnosis of DPN and PDN and increased morbidity, leading to foot ulceration and amputations.

Through a structured Delphi process, this consensus highlights critical regional gaps and provides practical, evidence‐based recommendations tailored to GCC healthcare systems. Priorities include shifting from symptom‐driven screening to systematic annual assessments for all patients with diabetes and prediabetes, using validated clinical tools. Screening utilizing CCM alongside diabetic retinal screening would be ideal. Primary care capacity needs to be improved, and awareness among both clinicians and patients needs to be enhanced. Management should combine lifestyle optimization, vascular risk factor reduction, early use of pathogenetic therapies, and individualized pharmacologic treatment of neuropathic pain, with early combination strategies used when monotherapy is insufficient. These recommendations reflect regional regulatory restrictions and limited access to first‐line agents—such as gabapentinoids—which influence prescribing patterns and require pragmatic, region‐appropriate treatment adaptations.

Addressing DPN in the GCC requires coordinated action from clinicians, health systems, and policymakers. Implementing these recommendations would enable earlier detection, more effective management, and ultimately better quality of life for people living with diabetic neuropathy across the region.
