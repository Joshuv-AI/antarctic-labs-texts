Challenges in the New Zealand (NZ) healthcare system and health disparities in the Māori population
Authors: Kamran Rostami, Adele Rostami
Published: 2025

This piece is dedicated to New Zealand (NZ) people, who are very kind and deserve a better public healthcare system. We have come to know Kiwi’s as flexible, hardy people who get along with others. They also worry about complaining as they are often not heard, and it can make matters worse for them. This is our story of emigration to NZ which highlights the challenges we faced in NZ healthcare system.

Around 2017, we started the procedure of emigrating to NZ as we were hoping for a better life/work balance. The National Health Service (NHS) was under pressure, and the idea of migrating to Australia or NZ felt attractive. We emigrated to NZ (my wife and I and 2 of our 3 boys, aged 9 and 13 at the time). I was appointed as a gastroenterologist in Palmerston North Hospital, Manawatu Region. Palmerston North is on the North Island, 2 hours North of Wellington (the capital of NZ). I started my job in September 2018 to work in a public Hospital, MidCentral District Health Board (DHB). We were very excited about this move and determined to give it our all to make it work for our family.

The link below demonstrates my enthusiasm and passion for our new life in NZ and serving our community (I ride because the scenery takes my breath away | Palmerston North City Council).

NZ is beautiful with clean air and breathtaking nature. It has numerous hiking trails and year-round sports facilities with little traffic. However, the roads are mostly single carriage ways, poorly maintained, sign posted, and fatal accidents happen often.

We became a one-car household family, as I was able to commute to work with my bicycle. I love cycling. After a month of our arrival, we purchased a beautiful lifestyle property (a large home with 3 acres of land), and we couldn’t believe how the stars aligned for us. Palmerston North is often referred to as ‘easy living’, and we felt that this was indeed the case.

On my first workday, I received training on how to deal with referrals. The hospital policy was that we were only allowed to accept a certain number of patients in public sector as there were capacity issues. I had to change the way I worked as I was used to the National Health Service (NHS) in the UK, which would accept all patients who require medical care and prioritize accordingly. We would discuss patients nearly every week, until I understood the selection criteria. The public sector had limited capacity for only a small number of patients; for instance, 20 colonoscopies, 10-15 gastroscopies, and 10 patients for outpatients a week. That meant we were only seeing patients with life-threatening, complicated medical history. This number has never changed despite increased referrals and increased complexity of patients over the years. During the 7 years that we were there, the number of referrals increased to between 120-170 a week. However, unlike the NHS, there was no provision to develop services according to increased demand. We were instructed that patients not at risk of serious disease and less severe presentation should be rejected with a letter to General practitioner (GP) as there was no capacity or any plan to increase the capacity. This policy made me feel uncomfortable from the very first day. It felt as if the ambulance was waiting at the bottom of the cliff because the fence wasn’t fixed at the top of the hill.

The physician who was on-call for GI emergencies, had to deal with the referrals for the week on-call. A full-time working physician was on-call for nearly 10-14 times a year (average 12 weeks a year). After an audit of our service, it was found that foreign doctors were rostered to do more on-calls than our Kiwi counterparts.

On average, I was seeing not more than 7 patients in the outpatient clinic. Most of the time was spent on writing rejection letters to GPs. Most New Zealanders cannot afford private medical insurance and rely heavily on publicly funded health care. When their referrals were declined, out of desperation, they would visit their GP surgeries again, several times, and we would receive the same referrals again. This was wasting valuable GP time considering the shortage of GPs in NZ.

The rejection of referrals took hours, having to investigate patient records in detail and having to justify why patients couldn’t be seen. This time could have been better spent seeing those patients.

Some desperate patients would ask their GP for a private referral. Over 30% of the private patients I saw didn’t have private medical insurance. As most of them should have been entitled to publicly funded services, it created a moral dilemma, and I therefore ended up seeing them for free or giving a 50% discount.

It was costing the health organisation (Te Whatu Ora) thousands of dollars to pay consultants for unnecessary admin time instead of using consultants’ valuable skills and experience to care for these patients. This rejection of referrals led to my moral injury. I certainly became a doctor because I wanted to help people, not deny care to people who needed it. These neglected patients were then presented with more severe illness to the Emergency Department (ED). This increased the volume of ED patients disproportionally. They end up being assessed in the ED and referred for expensive investigations that exhaust the inpatient's resources. Some of these patients were presenting with advanced disease, like cancer, which could have been prevented. This policy adds huge expenses to the healthcare system and proves to be a false economy. This is why the mortality rate is high in NZ, especially in deprived areas (1).

I was astonished to see that my local hospital, MidCentral DHB, had stopped doing surveillance colonoscopy. The question is, who would be responsible for people presenting with advanced colon cancer? This could explain the high mortality figures related to cancer overall in NZ (2).

Before our emigration to NZ, I worked at Milton Keynes University Hospital, UK. We were only 5 gastroenterologists, caring for a population of 350,000. This is close to 4x time more than Palmerston North population. We didn’t reject or deny patients in need. The department had 2 dedicated IBD nurses who were doing only IBD, 2 hepatitis nurses, and 2 endoscopy nurses, all exclusively doing their specific jobs, under the guidance of a Gastroenterologist. We were seeing more patients in a clinic session, but that didn’t make me ill. There was adequate administration support, and there were transparency and honesty. Managers and staff had the same agenda, and that was to help patients in the best possible way. This is why, despite workforce pressures, NHS staff are proud to be a part of this organisation. I felt that bullying was addressed through robust policies and procedures in the NHS. Incident reporting was taken seriously, and issues were addressed, ultimately to improve services and care of patients.

The health system in NZ is governed by several inflexible managers, who seem to be out of touch with real clinical life. Many of these managers have been in positions for a long time and, at times, have secured these positions through nepotism. There seems to be a toxic work culture, which cascades down to front line staff. Employees have reported an atmosphere of siege mentality as reported by a psychologist assessment. https://www.pressreader.com/new-zealand/manawatu-standard/20230602/281487870734098?srsltid=AfmBOopX3Qc20z-uXYL909rYtw-r9o9qkvrPQV_jGmgjR8Z0DcuimNjK

In addition, there is an expression called "Tall poppy syndrome," and this translates into a social phenomenon, where successful individuals are criticized, resented, or ostracized for their perceived success, often with the intention of "cutting them down to size". This is sad as the individuals joining the health system cannot bring their wealth of experience towards improving the NZ health system. My wife, a dietitian with 20 years of UK experience in various specialist areas of nutrition, was required to re-sit the NZ dietetic exam (written and oral) before she was allowed to be registered as a dietitian. She also didn't survive more than a year at the DHB due to this unhealthy culture and was ostracized by the ultra-hierarchical culture. She could have contributed enormously to the dietetic team with her wealth of knowledge and experience. I had a similar experience. None of my suggestions to improve services were ever considered or even reflected on by the managers.

My suggestions were working towards educating specialist nurses for liver disease, IBD, and endoscopy nurses under consultant supervision, that would increase the capacity for seeing more patients in a cost-effective manner. Trying to improve the system is not welcomed as the government doesn’t invest in the healthcare system. Instead, it seemed that managers were micromanaging staff, which, unfortunately, doesn't leave any room for innovation and creativity that may result in positive transformation. The atmosphere was tedious, dull and lacked stimulation. With the new health reform under Te Whatu Ora, the restriction policies became even more pronounced as the local hospitals do not even have the authority to employ a cleaner without approval from the Wellington board. The managers dictate the policies and that has affected the reporting of incidents too. NZ hospitals have a system called RiskMan for reporting clinical incidents and improving patients’ safety. However, this system is broken and is no more than a tick box exercise. Staff are reluctant to report incidents due to fear of blame or repercussions. Delay in reviewing and acting on reports reduces the effectiveness of the system, as there's no dedicated clinical governance body to ensure the incidents are dealt with in an effective manner. Therefore, the lack of leadership and resistance to change in this domain result in repeated risks and errors. In my experience, nothing ever changed despite my reporting multiple incidents, until I lost faith in the system. Instead, I was reprimanded and discouraged by managers from using RiskMan. Similarly, there is no grievance procedure and policy in place for staff who are being treated unfairly. Staff are treated according to their relationship with managers. An atmosphere of fear of speaking out and bullying dominates, as seen in a reported study by Jonathan Black, a psychologist: (https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/national/490934/damning-report-into-palmerston-north-hospital-follows-death-of-pregnant-woman-with-sepsis).

On a personal level, I experienced moral injury, having to decline care for patients who needed to be seen, and burnout due to the lack of support from managers.

Māori Health and their socioeconomic state

Māori people are disproportionately represented in areas of high deprivation. They estimate that socioeconomic disadvantage explains about 50% of the ethnic inequity in mortality for Māori adult males, and just over 40% for females (3). This quantifies how much of the health gap is statistically accounted for by socioeconomic position. Māori had the highest age‐standardised prevalence of cardiovascular disease compared with non-Māori/non-Pacific (4). There was a strong gradient with deprivation.

Māori suffer a disproportionate burden of inequities in health, wellbeing, and disability relative to non-Māori. Estimates of the economic cost of health inequities among Māori children, note that life expectancy is shorter for Māori, and higher unmet health need and mortality rates (4). Māori record higher prevalence of chronic diseases (e.g., infectious disease, kidney disease, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease) and poorer outcomes after disease onset (4-7).

I would like to make the following recommendations to the healthcare organisation (Te Whatu Ora) to help change working culture:

Appointing kind managers who listen and can be flexible, who can identify the strengths of their employees, make the best use of their skills, identify training needs, and provide extra training/ support where necessary.
