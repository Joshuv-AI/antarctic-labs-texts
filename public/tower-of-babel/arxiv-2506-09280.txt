TTrace: Lightweight Error Checking and Diagnosis for Distributed Training
Haitian Jiang, Shaowei Zhu, Zhen Zhang, Zhenyu Song, Xinwei Fu, Zhen Jia, Yida Wang, Jinyang Li

Abstract
. Distributed training is essential for scaling the training of large neural network models, such as large language models (LLMs), across thousands of GPUs. However, the complexity of distributed training programs makes them particularly prone to silent bugs, which do not produce explicit error signal but lead to incorrect training outcome. Effectively detecting and localizing such silent bugs in distributed training is challenging. Common debugging practice using metrics like training loss or gradient norm curves can be inefficient and ineffective. Additionally, obtaining intermediate tensor values and determining whether they are correct during silent bug localization is difficult, particularly in the context of low-precision training. To address those challenges, we design and implement TTrace, the first system capable of detecting and localizing silent bugs in distributed training. TTrace collects intermediate tensors from distributing training in a fine-grained manner and compares them against those from a trusted single-device reference implementation. To properly compare the floating-point values in the tensors, we propose novel mathematical analysis that provides a guideline for setting thresholds, enabling TTrace to distinguish bug-induced errors from floating-point round-off errors. Experimental results demonstrate that TTrace effectively detects 11 existing bugs and 3 new bugs in the widely used Megatron-LM framework, while requiring fewer than 10 lines of code change. TTrace is effective in various training recipes, including low-precision recipes involving BF16 and FP8.

Introduction

The field of machine learning (ML) is increasingly characterized by a drive towards larger, more complex models.
Guided by scaling laws
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
,
the sizes of language and multi-modal models continue to grow,
demonstrating increasingly strong capabilities in multiple fields
(
Zhao et al. 2023b
;
Yang et al. 2023b
)
.
A recent DeepSeek-V3 model
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
has over 671 billion parameters,
and the latest Llama 4
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
even reaches 2 trillion.

Such relentless pursuit of larger models has transformed modern training pipelines into intricate, resource-intensive systems.
Various distributed training techniques have been proposed to accommodate
the training workload, such as data parallelism
(
Li et al. [n. d.]
)
, tensor parallelism
(
Shoeybi et al. 2019
)
, pipeline parallelism
(
Narayanan et al. 2019
)
, distributed optimizer
(
Rajbhandari et al. 2020
)
etc., resulting in complicated system implementation.

These complex distributed training systems bring about new reliability issues.
An increasing amount of silent bugs
(
Zulkifli et al. 2025
;
Tambon et al. 2024
)
have been observed as more and more system optimizations get
implemented in pursuit of better performance.
Silent bugs
(
sla 2023
;
te- 2025
;
cp- 2025
;
moe 2023
)

refer to bugs that do not produce crashes, hangs, or any explicit error signal, but lead to an incorrect training outcome that is unexpected by the system developer.
Such silent bugs might be due to wrong computation
(
sla 2023
;
cp- 2025
)
, or
incorrect communication operations for cross-device synchronization
(
te- 2025
;
moe 2023
)
in the training framework.
These bugs may degrade the quality of the trained model, thereby wasting precious long training time and massive GPU resources.
Therefore, it is important to detect and localize silent bugs in distributed training program before
the actual training process.

Given a distributed training program, how to tell whether it is correct?
A promising approach is to use a smaller version of the model for which a reference single-device implementation exists, since it is less likely to make mistakes in
single-device training programs.
However, the question remains whether the distributed program has deviated meaningfully from the reference implementation.
The common practice is to examine the patterns in the loss and gradient norm curves as indicators for issues, because these quantities can be seen to reflect the combined semantics of the forward and backward computation, respectively. As there is no guideline for how large a discrepancy is large enough to indicate bugs, developers typically use a few percentage points as an ad-hoc threshold.
Unfortunately, training programs with silent bugs may demonstrate similar loss and gradient norm patterns when compared with the reference program.

(

)

remains close to the reference program for many training steps. It takes 4,000 iterations (more than 6 hours) for the loss curve to show a 3% discrepancy.

Figure 1
.

This paper aims to address the following challenge:

Given a candidate distributed training program
and a reference implementation,
how to determine whether the execution of the candidate program matches that of the reference implementation?

As we have seen in Figure
1
,
relying on training loss and gradient norm curves to detect silent bugs can be inefficient and ineffective.
Developers need to train both the reference and the candidate implementations to examine the results for potential discrepancies.
Furthermore, even if a potential issue is spotted in this way, the developer still faces the onerous task of manually locating the whereabouts of the silent bug.
To do so, developers typically step through the code and compare intermediate tensor values against those in the reference implementation to bisect the bug location.
However, this process can be challenging, as the autograd system in modern deep learning frameworks handles backward propagation automatically, making it difficult to set breakpoints or insert checking code properly.
Even after obtaining intermediate results, it still remains nontrivial to determine whether the tensor values from the reference and the candidate implementations are
close
enough to be considered as equivalent.
Moreover, state-of-the-art training recipes all employ low-precision floating-point (FP) numbers like BF16
(
Kalamkar et al. 2019
)
and FP8
(
Micikevicius et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2024b
;
NVIDIA 2025
)
.
The FP round-off errors of such representations are large, making it difficult to differentiate FP round-off errors and bug-induced errors.

In this work, we propose TTrace, a streamlined and user-friendly system to detect and localize silent bugs in distributed training.
It ensures that the model parameters are initialized in
the exact same way in the reference and the candidate training programs,
and the same data are passed into these programs to compare the training outcomes.
Intermediate tensors are automatically collected and compared, which include
the inputs and output tensor in the forward and backward computation for each
neural network submodule.
Such granularity enables TTrace to localize the bug after detecting a discrepancy.
To properly compare the FP values in the intermediate tensors,
we also develop a guideline for how to set a discrepancy threshold based on a formal mathematical analysis that bounds the FP round-off errors in neural network training.

Developers working with the widely adopted distributed training framework Megatron-LM
can easily integrate TTrace with the framework,
requiring
fewer than 10 lines of code
.
To use TTrace,
developers simply need to annotate the sharding dimensions
to specify the model structure and the intended distributed training strategy.
These annotations inform TTrace’s tensor collection and comparison logic.

In summary, this work makes the following contributions:

•

We identify the silent bug problem as a pain point in distributed training by collecting and reproducing a taxonomy of real silent bugs from the widely used Megatron-LM framework.

•

We implement TTrace,
the first
system that enables detection and localization of silent bugs in diverse distributed training settings, including the emerging FP8 precision.

•

We systematically studied differentiating FP round-off errors from bug-induced errors in distributed training, and proposed a novel thresholding method grounded in theoretical analysis.

•

We conducted comprehensive evaluations and validated the effectiveness of TTrace by finding
11 existing silent bugs and 3 new silent bugs in Megatron-LM.

2.
Background and Challenges

Checking the execution of a distributed program against its reference implementation faces two technical challenges:
(1) In order to compare intermediate tensors between the two programs, how to map each tensor in the distributed training to its counterpart in the reference implementation?
(2) As training involves floating point operations,
how to distinguish “expected” FP round-off errors from bug-induced errors?
In this section, we provide the background necessary to understand the two challenges in more details.

2.1.
Challenge #1: Mapping of Semantics

The first challenge is to
establish a connection between the
semantics of candidate implementation and that of the reference
.
More specifically, we need to study how tensors
of the candidate implementation correspond to those in the reference.
In this work, we consider distributed training implementations based on PyTorch,
as these are the most widely used systems today.

Neural network module structure.

Popular neural network frameworks like PyTorch and TensorFlow
organize the neural network layers and sub-layer structures using
modules
.
Modules are the smallest unit in accessing and managing
the parameters, input / output tensors, and gradients.
These modules in the neural network are distinguished and accessed by their names,
and we assume that corresponding modules in the reference and the candidate share the same name
so that we can align these modules and their tensors.

Distributed training.

To align the distributed candidate with the single-device reference,
we also need to build up the mapping of neural network layers and
the tensor shards in distributed training
to the reference counterparts.
Building such mapping is challenging for two reasons.
On one hand, different layers in the model can be partitioned across GPUs using techniques like pipeline parallelism (PP),
so we may need to collect and rearrange the layers to
restore the correct ordering of layers in the single-device reference implementation.
On the other hand, tensors can also be partitioned in the distributed implementation
under techniques like tensor parallelism
(
Shoeybi et al. 2019
)
(TP),
sequence parallelism
(
Korthikanti et al. 2023
)
(SP), and context parallelism
(
Liu et al. 2024c
)
(CP).
So we need to assemble the shards according to the particular
parallelization strategy applied
in order to match them to the tensors in the reference implementation.

Our approach.

We built a tensor canonical mapping system to align the tensors
from the reference and candidate implementations.
And we use a simple user-written annotation to model the semantics of the distributed training framework
to form such tensor canonical mapping,
as will later be illustrated in the running example in
Section

3
.

2.2.
Challenge #2: FP round-off Errors

The second challenge is to

differentiate FP round-off errors from bug-induced errors.

Since we aim to compare the tensor values extracted from
candidate and reference implementations,
we have to cope with the semantics of low-precision FP operations.
A direct upcast to higher-precision like FP32 is not feasible
because widely-used kernels like FlashAttention
(
Dao et al. 2022
)
have hard-coded optimizations that require the use of low-precision data types like BF16 and FP8.

Why not use a verification based approach?

In order to get around the complexity of FP semantics, we have
considered an alternative approach that
traces the computation in the candidate and reference implementations into symbolic expressions whose variables are modeled as real numbers and then decides their equivalence using SMT solvers.
However, complex distributed programs are infeasible to trace, especially in the presence of custom kernels implemented in low-level languages
such as CUDA. Furthermore, deciding the equivalence of the resulting symbolic expressions that involve non-linear operations and exponentiation can be prohibitively expensive, due to the high complexity of the decision procedures for non-linear real arithmetic
(
Collins 1974
)
.

Round-off errors in FP representations.

Due to the discrete nature of FP numbers,
the numbers not representable will be rounded to a close representable number.
IEEE standards ensure numbers are correctly rounded under arithmetic operations and format conversions
(
Overton 2001
)
.
The relative error of the round-off error in a particular FP representation like FP32
is also called machine epsilon (
\varepsilon_{\text{mch}}
) or machine precision for that represention.

Since we need to compare the tensor values for discrepancy, we calculate the relative error of tensors as a single number.
The relative errors of matrices and tensors are computed with the help of matrix norms
(
Demmel 1997
)
,
i.e.,
\text{rel\_err}(A,B)=\frac{\|A-B\|}{\|A\|}
.
For convenience and generality, we use Frobenius norm in our work,
as it can be calculated regardless the tensor dimension.

Can machine epsilon be used as a fixed threshold to check discrepancy?

Setting a fixed threshold solely based on the machine epsilon of the underlying FP representation is inadequate for differentiating FP round-off errors from bug-induced errors.
First, FP round-off errors accumulate as tensor computations progress through the model. As a result, FP round-off errors in tensors from deeper layers can exceed the machine epsilon, even in the absence of bugs. Second, modern large models are trained using mixed-precision
(
Micikevicius et al. 2022
;
Micikevicius et al. 2018
;
Kalamkar et al. 2019
)
techniques to save GPU memory. Because lower-precision representations correspond to larger machine epsilons, a fixed threshold cannot accurately capture the varying numerical accuracy across different parts of the model.

Our approach.

We start from a theoretical analysis to understand the behavior of FP round-off errors in
neural networks.
The mathematical analysis shows that the bounds on FP round-off errors relate to the smoothness properties of
the neural network layers.
We thus propose an empirical threshold estimation procedure to distinguish bug-induced errors from FP round-off errors.
The threshold is obtained by running the reference implementation twice;
one run uses randomly selected input (data) tensors and the other run uses random perturbations of the same inputs.
Under weak assumptions that assume numerically stable training,
we can show that a reasonable threshold on the FP round-off errors can be obtained by
selecting perturbations close to machine epsilon.
We present our analysis of expected FP round-off errors in
Section

5
.

3.
Debugging Workflow Using TTrace

We use a running example to show how various components of TTrace work together to detect and localize a silent bug.
Assuming the user found that the loss curve of training with TP is higher than that of single-device training,
as shown in
Figure

1
. To find out the root cause of this discrepancy, users can use TTrace to conduct a differential analysis between the two runs.

Step 1. TTrace estimates the expected FP round-off errors in a model.

To find suitable thresholds for differentiating FP round-off errors from bug-induced errors, TTrace first estimates the magnitude of expected FP round-off errors for intermediate tensors within a given model.
TTrace first runs the reference implementation and
then run the reference again with the input tensors randomly perturbed
at the magnitude of machine epsilon.
This is to simulate the effect of FP differences and study how such differences
can be amplified by the model computations (more discussion in
Section

5
).
TTrace then computes a series of thresholds,
which will be later used to compare candidate and reference tensors in the
equivalence checker
(
Section

4.4
), based on the observed
relative errors between the intermediate tensors recorded from these two runs.

Figure 2
.
Example annotation clips for a GPT model.

Step 2. User annotates the model.

TTrace requires
module annotations
to know which intermediate tensors to trace and compare. The right half of
Figure

2
gives an example of module annotation for
linear_qkv
module (part of the transformer layer). A module is typically an instance of
torch.nn.Module
.
It can be an operator or a layer, like self-attention, normalization, and linear layer.
The module annotation marks which modules’ input and output tensors the user would like to be traced and compared.
The annotations also include how activations and gradients are partitioned by different parallel strategies.

To compare tensors in the candidate and reference implementation, TTrace needs to compute the
tensor canonical mapping
, a mechanism to relate tensors in the candidate and reference implementation (
Section

4.1
).
TTrace relies on
parameter annotations
to understand how the weight parameters are partitioned in the candidate implementation. For example, the left of
Figure

2
tells TTrace that the word embedding weights are sharded along the first dimension
in the candidate implementation.

Figure 3
.
The root cause of the bug in the case study.

Step 3. TTrace runs candidate and reference.

To enable proper interaction between the training framework and TTrace, the user needs to pass the annotations (that describes the expected semantics of the candidate implementation) to TTrace, and also make minimal code changes in the model implementation (invoke TTrace APIs at model initialization, and before and after parameter updates). The integration requires adding
less than 10 lines of code
in total. TTrace uses the
consistent distributed tensor generator
(
Section

4.2
) to generate the same input tensors provided to the candidate and reference, and then runs the candidate and reference with the
trace collector
(
Section

4.3
)
collecting traces, e.g., the input and output tensors of modules prescribed by the user in the forward propagation and the gradients in the backward propagation.
TTrace runs the training for 1 iteration to collect the traces.

Step 4. TTrace conducts differential testing to detect bugs.

TTrace generates a report by comparing the two traces of the (single-device) reference run and (multi-device) candidate run. The report provides numeric value discrepancies of two runs and marks unexpected
differences that is larger than the computed threshold. From the report, the user can know which tensors in the candidate diverge from their reference counterparts.

Figure 4
.
The system overview of TTrace.

Step 5. TTrace rewrites module inputs to localize bugs.

One can imagine that if there is a bug in the candidate implementation of the first module that
leads to wrong results, then all subsequent tensors values would also be very different from the reference.
We illustrate one such bug in
Figure

8
a, where the user would observe that activations and gradients are difference for almost every layer.
To help further localize the bug, TTrace implements a
consistent distributed tensor generator
(
Section

4.2
) that facilitates module-wise testing,
that can
overwriting
the input tensors to all modules in the candidate
and reference implementation in a consistent manner (
Section

4.3
).
This prevents errors originated in one module from propagating to successive computations,
and also remove the accumulated floating-point differences from previous modules.

In the example of
Figure

3
, the bug is in the forward computation of the word embedding module.
A wrong mask is calculated to select wrong word embeddings from the token indices when tensor parallelism is on.
The error caused by the bug is most prominent in the output of the buggy module,
however, the error also propagates and affects the subsequent layers even if they are correctly implemented.
With the tensor rewriting feature, TTrace can automatically pinpoint this buggy module.

4.
System Design

We have briefly described the functionality of the major system components in
Section

3
.
Now we explain the design of each component in detail.

4.1.
Tensor Canonical Mapping

To compare tensors in the candidate and reference implementation,
it is essential to establish an unambiguous correspondence between these tensors.
The notion of a
logical full tensor
in the candidate (distributed) runs is useful for the discussion.
Such a tensor is assembled from multiple physical shards on multiple devices, and
is expected to match a physical tensor in the (single-device) reference trace (see
Figure

6
).
We therefore need to solve two problems with the mapping:
1) how to uniquely identify a tensor in the reference trace or a logical full tensor in the candidate trace; and
2) how to map the local tensor shards in the candidate trace to their corresponding logical full tensors.

For the first problem, we introduce a unique canonical identifier for each tensor.
When comparing two traces, the tensors with the same canonical identifier can be compared against each other,
and there shall not be two tensors with the same identifier in the same trace.
This identifier is a function of
iteration number
,
batch index
,
tensor type
,
and a
canonical module name
of the module that this tensor belongs to. The
canonical module name
involves modeling the behavior of pipeline parallelism,
as every pipeline stage has its own layer id starting from 0,
and we need to map them back to the real layer id in the reference computation model.
Therefore, the canonical module name is a function of

PP size
,
PP rank
,
VPP size
,
VPP rank
,
local module name
.

Figure

5
shows a simplified case by mapping the local layer indices in an interleaved pipeline to the canonical ones in the single-device reference.

Figure 5
.
Illustration of the layer index mapping in pipeline parallelism (PP) and virtual pipeline parallelism (VPP) for
canonical module name
. Each colored box is one layer. The purple example means layer 0 in the 2nd virtual pipeline of the 1st pipeline stage maps to layer 4 in the reference.

Figure 6
.
Illustration for shard mapping.

For the second problem,
we maintain a data structure to record
the mapping from the slices in local shards to those in the corresponding logical full tensor,
as shown in
Figure

6
.
A tensor shard on one device can consist of multiple non-contiguous slices in the logical full tensor,
e.g., striped attention in context parallelism.
In this example, we will use the shards from the two ranks to compose the full tensor according to the mapping,
and then compare with the physical full tensor from the single-device run.
When merging the shards into the full tensor, TTrace will check to ensure there is no overlap nor omission.

TTrace does not require its users to supply all the information to construct the mapping but rather
uses the user-provided tensor annotations to infer the shard mapping.
For example, in
Figure

2
, the annotation for the parameters and the input or output activations
contain the dimensions on which particular parallelism strategies split.
The mapped slice is inferred from the sharding dimension, parallel rank, and the semantic of the framework.

4.2.
Consistent Distributed Tensor Generator

As demonstrated in
Section

3
, we need to generate
the
same
tensor as input to the candidate and reference modules in order to compare their behavior to identify bugs,
and also when trying to localize bugs in candidates.
If we generate at random a tensor for the reference (single-device), then
we also need to ensure that the same logical complete tensor will be generated for the candidate (distributed).
To achieve this, we hash the canonical identifier of the tensor as seed for the random number generator in generating tensors for the reference implementation and the corresponding logical complete tensors
for the candidate. The actual distributed tensors supplied to the candidate are then taken out from
the generated logical complete tensor as slices or shards.
In addition to generating consistent input tensors to the modules,
this mechanism can also be used to generated consistent main gradients
to examine the optimizer behavior in the candidate and reference implementation.

4.3.
Trace Collector and Tensor Rewrites

The trace collector interfaces with the training framework and the underlying DL framework
to collect the values of tensors to be compared.
We implement it in a way that achieves this without requiring significant code change from the user
in the model implementation, utilizing the PyTorch module and tensor hook API.
This also requires us to handle different tensors in a different manner.

•

activations
and
activation gradients
:
We use module hooks (forward and backward, respectively) to trace them,
since tensor hooks will be discarded along with the activation tensors
in the forward propagation if activation check-pointing is on.

•

parameter gradients
:
Since the parameter shards will always be on the device,
we can safely use tensor hook for them.

•

main gradients
:
Distributed mixed-precision training systems often keep an FP32 copy of the gradients
used for gradient accumulation and parameter update, also known as
main gradients
.
They can only be accessed before the optimizer update step,
and there is no automatic way of having them,
so TTrace designed an API to trace them before the optimizer step.

•

parameters
:
Obtaining the parameters right after the reinitialization is trivial.
What matters is the parameters after each optimizer update step,
as we can use them to see whether the optimizer is correctly implemented.
Similar to main graidents, there is no automatic way to do this,
so we also rely on the API to be called after the optimizer step.

The rewrite functionality for localizing the bugs is implemented similarly:
it uses the same kind of hooks as the trace collector,
but instead of recording the tensor values, it also overwrites them.

Besides the tensor value, canonical identifier, and the shard mapping,
we also store other auxiliary information in the trace,
like the name and class of the module consuming or producing the tensor,
to help the checking and bug localization process.
The traces are stored in the main memory and dumped to disk automatically when the training ends.

4.4.
Equivalence Checker

After obtaining the candidate and reference traces, TTrace needs to decide whether
the two traces are “equivalent”,
in the sense that the candidate implementation is likely correct and the differences in the
tensor values is likely due to the (accumulated) FP round-off errors that are expected for correct computations.
We will first merge the tensors obtained from a distributed candidate run to reconstruct
the corresponding logical full tensors, which will
facilitate the comparison with reference tensors.
Then we will compare the merged tensors according to the thresholds obtained in
Section

5
.

Tensor merger.

The merger will use the shard mapping in the traces to merge the tensor shards
into the logical full tensor.
When doing the merge, it can also detect and report bugs.
For example, when ZeRO is not used, the main gradients from each DP rank should be the same.
If the merger finds conflicts in the merging, it will report the bug and identify the bug location in terms of “conflicting tensor”, i.e., a shard in the logical full tensor in the candidate run that does not correspond to its reference counterpart.
A missing all-reduce before the gradient update may cause such issues.

Differential testing.

Given the merged traces from the reference and the candidate,
the equivalence checker will then conduct differential testing and evaluate the differences between the reference and the candidate.
It checks whether the differences come from floating point round-off (meaning the tensors are equivalent) or from a bug.
If an inequivalence is detected, it will use the auxiliary information in the trace to localize the bug.

5.
Analysis and Estimation of Expected FP Round-off Errors

As a core contribution of our system, we introduce a principled method to distinguish between the
expected tensor differences
, i.e., the differences in the tensor values that can be attributed to the nature of FP semantics, and the differences due to incorrect computations. Such difficulty arises naturally when one needs to reason about the correctness of distributed training, since parallelization techniques inherently introduce changes to the evaluation order of FP expressions, eventually leading to different numerical results due to the non-associativity or other intricacies of FP operations. To make it worse, these differences in the numerical result are often further
amplified by subsequent computations, making it extremely challenging to tell whether a computation is correct just by comparing the contents of the intermediate tensors in a bit-by-bit manner. As a result, developers are often forced to set some coarse bounds on the expected differences between the tensor values based on their own experience, which leads to bug-finding procedures
that are neither precise nor complete for silent bugs. Our work addresses this challenge by offering a more complete picture on the nature of the
expected
FP round-off errors within neural networks, backed by both theoretical derivations (this section) and comprehensive empirical evaluations (Section
6
).

5.1.
Analysis of Expected FP Differences in the Forward and Backward Propagation

Suppose that we view an ideal neural network layer
as a mathematical function
Y=f_{l}(X)
in this simplified presentation. Let
F_{L}
denote the composition
of
L
layers:
F_{L}=f_{L}\circ f_{L-1}\circ\dots\circ f_{1}
.
For convenience, we also recursively define the intermediate tensors in a multi-layer neural network as
Y_{0}=X
,

Y_{l}=f_{l}(X_{l-1})
. In parallel, we use
\hat{f_{l}}
and
\hat{F_{L}}
to denote an FP implementation of
f_{l}
and
F_{L}
, respectively, and also use
\hat{Y_{l}}
for the FP tensor that corresponds to
Y_{l}
.

Our theoretical analysis assumes smoothness properties of neural network layer function
f_{l}
.
We assume each
f_{l}
is Lipschitz continuous, i.e., it satisfies
that
\|f_{l}(X+\Delta X)-f_{l}(X)\|\leq C_{l}\|\Delta X\|
for all
X
in a neighborhood with a small random perturbation
\Delta X
and some constant
C_{l}
close to
1
. In other words, such a layer is
smooth
in that
it is guaranteed to not amplify small perturbations to its input by too much.
We have the following theorem that states that transformer layers are likely to be smooth when
they are just initialized:

Theorem 5.1 (Smoothness of Transformer Layers at Initialization).

A transformer layer
f_{l}
is smooth with high probability under standard initialization of the weights.
More specifically, it has a Lipschitz constant
C_{l}\leq 1+\mathcal{O}(d^{-\frac{1}{2}})
where

d
is the hidden dimension.

Let
\hat{f_{l}}
denote an FP implementation of the function
f_{l}
.
We also assume that a
reasonable
FP implementation
\hat{f_{l}}
of function
f_{l}
, whether distributed or not,
should satisfy that
it is also locally regular (with approximately the Lipschitz constant, i.e.,
\|\hat{f_{l}}(X+\Delta X)-\hat{f_{l}}(X)\|\leq C_{l}\|\Delta X\|
) and for most
X
it holds that

\|\hat{f_{l}}(X)-f_{l}(X)\|\leq C_{l}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{\text{mch}}
, where
C_{l}^{\prime}
is some constant and
\varepsilon_{\text{mch}}
is the machine epsilon.

Under technical assumptions on the smoothness of the neural network layer function and
the characteristics of FP errors introduced within the computation of each layer, we have the following theorem on
the accumulated FP error of activations.

Theorem 5.2 (Expected Activation Difference Bound).

Let
F=f_{L}\circ\dots\circ f_{1}
and
\hat{F}=\hat{f}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\hat{f}_{1}
be the ideal and a reasonable FP neural network implementations where
{Y}_{l}
and
\hat{{Y}}_{l}
are their intermediate activations. The expected relative difference of the difference norm when comparing the final outputs is bounded by:

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\lVert\hat{{Y}}_{L}-{Y}_{L}\right\rVert}{\left\lVert{Y}_{L}\right\rVert}\right]\approx\mathcal{O}\left(L\cdot\varepsilon_{\text{mch}}\right)\ .

We prove another theorem on the expected relative difference of gradient norms in transformers as follows.
Future work is needed to generalize this analysis to other neural network architectures.

Theorem 5.3 (Expected Gradient Difference Bound).

Let
G_{{W}^{l}}
and
\hat{G}_{{W}^{l}}
be the ideal and FP gradients for a parameter matrix
{W}^{l}
in layer
l
of
a transformer. Given the additional assumption that the backward propagation is numerically stable, the expected relative difference norm of the gradient is bounded by

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\hat{G}^{{W}^{l}}-G^{{W}^{l}}\|}{\|G^{{W}^{l}}\|}\right]\lessapprox\mathcal{O}\left(C^{L+1-l}\cdot\varepsilon_{\text{mch}}\right)

where
C
is a constant bound on the backward Jacobian that is close to
1
.

5.2.
Estimating the Expected FP Difference

Apart from the theoretical derivations above, we also provide a practical lightweight method to estimate
the expected FP differences.
Assuming the standard random initialization of weights and bounded FP error properties for the FP implementation
\hat{f_{l}}
of neural network layer,
we can use
\|\hat{f_{l}}(X+\Delta X)-\hat{f_{l}}(X)\|
to approximate the
expected difference
\|\hat{f_{l}}(X)-f_{l}(X)\|
, as long as the magnitude of
the perturbation
\|\Delta X\|
is chosen to be on the same order as
\varepsilon_{\text{mch}}
.

In practice, we select at random a small perturbation
\Delta X
to the reference input
X
with controlled norm
such that
\hat{f_{l}}(X+\Delta X)-\hat{f_{l}}(X)
is observed to be close to the FP error for a layer
l
.
We then measure the induced difference between
\hat{F_{L}}(X+\Delta X)-\hat{F_{L}}(X)
as
L
changes
to use as a guideline for what the expected FP differences look like for correct computations,
as the number of layers grows.
Let
\hat{H_{L}}
be the (distributed training) candidate that we are testing.
We conclude that
\hat{H_{L}}
is not a reasonable FP implementation of
f_{l}
if
the observed difference
\hat{H_{L}}(X)-\hat{F_{L}}(X)
behaves drastically different from
the perturbation difference
\hat{F_{L}}(X+\Delta X)-\hat{F_{L}}(X)
in the reference implementation.
Empirical evaluation of this strategy on some popular transformer architecture is detailed in Section
6
.
It is worth noting that we had restricted our empirical study to a number of layers
L\leq 128
since most
popular transformer architectures have less than that number of layers
(
Dubey et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024b
)
, and it is known that
transformers can suffer from the
rank collapse
problem if the number of layers becomes too large
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
.

6.
Implementation and Evaluation

TTrace is implemented in about 3000 lines of Python code.
The differential testing part is written in about 100 lines of C++ code to
bypass the Python global interpreter lock and enable multi-threaded parallel execution.
The TTrace implementation is based on PyTorch,
utilizing the hook mechanism and autograd system provided by PyTorch.
To evaluate the practicality and effectivenss of TTrace,
we focus on the following questions.

•

Can TTrace discover silent bugs in distributed training frameworks?

•

How long does it take for TTrace to detect and localize a bug
compared to the common practice mentioned in
Section

1
?

•

How accurate is the FP round-off error estimation?

•

How effective is TTrace on low-precision FP formats?

6.3.
Bug Case Study

We use the bug ID in the first column of
Table

1
to refer to the bugs.
Bug 1 is the bug we used in
Section

3
as the running example.
We summarized three types of silent bugs:

•

Wrong Computation (W-CP)
:
The input tensor of some operation is not what is expected.
This includes the input being outdated (bug 2);
the input being wrongly scaled (bug 3, 4);
the input being in a wrong layout (bug 12), etc.

•

Wrong Communication (W-CM)
:
The order or pattern of the collective communication is wrong,
so the result tensor is not what is expected.
This includes cases like a wrong communication order (bug 5),
wrong communication groups (bug 7),
a wrong communication pattern when computation-communication overlapping is enabled (bug 9), etc.

•

Missing Communication (M-CM):

One or more collective communication operation is missing, so that the result tensor is not correct.
A classical example of this kind is a missing allreduce that causes the tensor to be a partial sum (bug 6, 11).

6.4.
System Overhead

To answer the second question, we conducted the time cost comparison experiment
for TTrace against the common debugging practice mentioned in
Section

1
.
We will refer to it as
naïve approach
in the following text.
We use bug 1
(
sla 2023
)
as an example.
The naïve approach needs to train both the reference and candidate implementation long enough to identify the difference in loss.
As shown in
Figure

1
,
it took more than 4000 iterations for the loss curve to show a 3% relative error.
However, TTrace only need to run for one single iteration.
We measured the time spent on training the reference for the corresponding iterations,
as the single-device run is the bottleneck.
It took the naïve approach 6 hours and 40 minutes, while TTrace only needs 54 seconds
to identify the bug.
We recognize that there is room for performance improvement, e.g., overlapping
computation and communication when tracing, which might further reduce this overhead. Yet the optimizations themselves might introduce new bugs.

6.5.
FP Round-off Errors and Smoothness of Actual Transformer Models

In the following part, we will study the behavior of the FP errors and
the smoothness properties of transformer layers in large model training.
In practice, large models including LLMs like Llama seldom go beyond more than 100 layers.
For example, Llama3 405B is 126 layers
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
.
Therefore, we studied the relation of FP round-off errors and bug-induced errors with 128 layers,
a number that is more than enough for the purpose of correctness checking.

We will first study the smoothness of layers in a BF16 model,
and then show that our threshold estimation method can effectively capture
the characteristics of FP round-off errors in a correct candidate implementation.
Finally, we visualize difference in the FP round-off errors and bug-induced errors.

FP round-off errors regarding other factors.

There are other factors like sequence length, head dimension, and number of heads
that potentially have an effect on the FP round-off errors.
However, from our experiment, the FP round-off errors are not affected by these factors.
The reason is that transformers mostly use small head dimensions like 64 or 128,
the change in head dimension is small enough that it will make barely any difference
in FP round-off errors; and different heads are calculated independently, so adding number of
heads will not change the reduction dimension.
Sequence length serves as reduction dimension only in calculating the attention score,
however, the relative error of attention output is shrunk due to the numerical property
of attention, so the effect of sequence length is also negligible.

6.7.
FP Round-off Error Estimation and Smoothness of FP8 models

Figure

9
shows the estimated FP round-off errors in training with FP8 in TransformerEngine,
obtained through perturbations on the order of the machine epsilon of BF16.
FP8 training conducts tiled matrix multiplication in tensor cores using FP8,
and conducts reduction accumulations in higher precisions like BF16 or FP32
(
Liu et al. 2024b
;
NVIDIA 2025
)
.
Moreover, the intermediate results are stored in BF16.
Therefore, it is still feasible to use the machine epsilon of BF16 when estimating
the expected FP round-off errors.
We can observe that the estimated threshold does not exhibit an exponential blow-up in magnitude,
indicating that these layers are still well-conditioned and smooth.

There are different recipes for FP8 training.
For example, the quantization scaler can be shared by the whole neural network,
or per tensor
(
Micikevicius et al. 2022
)
, or only across a 128
\times
128 tile
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
.
And the used format can be e4m3 only
(
Liu et al. 2024b
;
NVIDIA 2025
)
, or a hybrid of e4m3 and e5m2
(
NVIDIA 2025
)
.
Therefore, these different recipes will incur different levels of FP round-off errors
although they are all FP8 training.
The recipe we use in
Figure

9
is e4m3 in TransformerEngine
(
NVIDIA 2025
)
.
It uses a global quantization scaler.
Therefore, more advanced recipes the one used in DeepSeek-V3
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)

will have smaller FP round-off errors due to the more fine-grained quantization.

As such, we show that the method that TTrace employed to distinguish FP round-off errors and bug-induced errors
will work with the SOTA FP8 low-precision training, since the numerical experiments
ensure that these layers are still approximately smooth.

7.
Discussions and Future Work

Extension to inference and other frameworks.

As a research prototype, TTrace currently only supports Megatron-LM
(
Narayanan et al. 2021
)

and TransformerEngine
(
NVIDIA 2025
)
as target frameworks.
Our methodology including the canonical tensor mapping and the threshold estimation method
naturally extends to neural network inference and other training frameworks, e.g., DeepSpeed
(
Rasley et al. 2020
)
or PyTorch FSDP
(
Zhao et al. 2023a
)
,
as long as the framework semantics are specified using the TTrace interface,
which we leave as future work.

Executing reference implementation.

If the target model is large, a single compute device might not have
enough memory to execute the reference implementation without any modifications to
the model itself or the ML runtime.
It is beyond the scope of this current work to study how to execute
the reference implementation on specialized hardware efficiently
while ensuring the semantics remain exactly the
same as if it were running on a single device.
In our evaluation, we used models whose reference implementation
can be executed on a single device, and we notice that many
bugs in the distributed training framework can still be discovered.
We point out that there are potential methods to get around this limitation:

CPU offloading: since the device memory is limited,
we can utilize CPU memory by either running the entire model on CPU
or offloading some computations and intermediate results to CPU.
There are two limitations of this approach.
First, this might introduce a non-trivial slowdown for larger models.
Also, one needs to make sure the offloading process itself is correct.
Verified distributed techniques: we may constrain the reference to use certain distributed techniques that have already been proven or tested to be correct.
For example, only allowing pipeline parallelism and activation checkpointing
could make large model runnable, and developers can use it to run the reference implementation in order to check other techniques.

8.
Related Work

Testing ML frameworks and compilers.

Researchers have developed a plethora of testing-based techniques to
exhibit and locate bugs in ML frameworks
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)

and compilers
(
Shen et al. 2021
;
Ma 2023
)
.
ML framework and library testing and fuzzing
(
Pham et al. 2019
;
Guo et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2023a
;
Zhang et al. 2021a
;
Zhang et al. 2021b
)
is
mainly interested in checking the implementation of APIs and operators through
techniques such as differential and mutation testing. In particular, these techniques often
focus on constructing a variety of valid API usages with input data to
compare the execution results against each other or some ground truth reference implementation, with a main focus on achieving a high coverage on the
APIs or the framework implementation.
Similarly for ML compiler testing and fuzzing
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Ma et al. 2023
)
,
one usually focus on constructing a variety of valid input programs represented
as computational graphs or some intermediate representations, in order to
detect issues in the lowering or optimization process inside the compilers.
Our work can be distinguished from these prior work in the following aspects.
First, in terms of the problem domain, our work is among the first to test distributed training frameworks, which involves unique challenges such as mapping intermediate results
in the candidate implementation to the reference.
Secondly, our approach enables testing and bug localization on a complete forward and backward propagation in training, making it necessary to reason about the propagation of FP round-off errors that arise in the computations. The intricacies of FP semantics are less of a problem when testing a single operator or small programs.
Finally, we focus on techniques for testing whether
one
particular distributed training run of a given model is correct in this work. However, our technique can potentially be used in conjunction with fuzzing and coverage-guided techniques to cover more code paths in the distributed training frameworks.

FP semantics.

As low-bit FP data types like FP16 becomes more popular, there are emerging works characterizing the numerical effects of FP operations. On the theory side, there are works
studying distribution of errors or bounds on the errors induced by FP operations
(
Dahlqvist et al. 2019
;
Ipsen and Zhou 2020
;
Higham and Mary 2019
)
from a probabilistic point of view, often with simplifying
assumptions on the actual FP hardware behaviors. More rigorous
modeling of the FP semantics
(
Goubault 2001
;
Solovyev et al. 2018
;
Das et al. 2020
)
is possible
yet expensive, making it challenging to scale to practical models.
Our work does not directly require precise modeling of FP differences, yet our technique and
theoretical derivations assume numerical stability of the single-device
and distributed FP implementation of neural network computations.

Sensitivity, smoothness, and robustness.

Lipschitz properties characterize how the output of a neural network changes in response to input perturbations
(
Scaman and Virmaux 2019
;
Qi et al. 2023a
)
. It is a desirable property to ensure training stability
(
Qi et al. 2023a
)
and robustness against adversarial attacks
(
Tsuzuku et al. 2018
)
. In the context of transformers, it is well-known that the standard dot-product transformer is not Lipschitz continuous
(
Kim et al. 2021
)
, yet we can still derive bounds on Lipschitz constants of transformer layers
assuming compactly supported input
(
Kim et al. 2021
;
Castin et al. 2024
)
or local smoothness properties
(
Havens et al. 2024
)
. There are also works studying
Lipschitz continuous variants of transformers
(
Kim et al. 2021
;
Qi et al. 2023b
;
Havens et al. 2024
)
.
Our work does not aim to perform a comprehensive study on Lipschitz constants
in neural networks, yet we rely on the ideal
and FP implementations of neural networks having certain smoothness properties, which
makes it possible to distinguish between expected FP differences and incorrect computations.

Verification techniques.

Verified computational graph rewrites
(
Jia et al. 2019
;
Arora et al. 2025
;
Zulkifli et al. 2025
)
and verified tensor compilers
(
Liu et al. 2024a
)
offer formal guarantees on the correctness of performance-improving
transformations for ML. Comparing to these techniques, our method can tell whether for a particular model
and configuration the training framework behaves as expected and detect bugs, but does not guarantee functional correctness of the framework implementation,
especially when the model or configuration changes. Yet our approach has
unique advantages. First, the verification based approaches often do not ensure correctness of the
whole stack, e.g., having a verified computational graph does rule out bugs
in the runtime or the compute kernels invoked. Our method captures and compares the actual tensor contents in the computation and is able to detect and help localize unexpected behavior no matter where the root cause of the problem resides.
Secondly, our approach based on testing is practical and lightweight. TTrace can be used by
framework maintainers to detect regressions if integrated into the testing pipelines.
Also, it requires much less effort for framework
maintainers to maintain the interface between the framework and TTrace, compared to
what would be required to construct and maintain proofs of correctness of the transformation rules.
We have also shown (Section
3
) that TTrace does not
require significant code changes by framework users, who want to make sure their custom models
or configurations can be executed as expected using the distributed training framework.

9.
Conclusion

We present TTrace, the first system designed to detect and localize silent bugs in distributed training.
TTrace collects intermediate tensors from distributing training in a fine-grained manner and compares them against those from a trusted single-device reference implementation.
We systematically study how to distinguish FP round-off errors from bug-induced errors in distributed training, and propose a novel thresholding method grounded in theoretical analysis.
TTrace detected 14 (3 new) silent bugs in widely used Megatron-LM framework.
