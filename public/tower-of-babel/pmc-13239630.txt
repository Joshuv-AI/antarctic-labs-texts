Underlying aetiology of acute intracerebral haemorrhage and associations between outcome measures, findings on initial computed tomography scan and ICH classification systems: an observational historical cohort study
Authors: Valeriia Nielsen, Trine Nielsen, Sofie Ronja Petersen, Fabiana Andrade Melchiori, Samuel Kilian Rodriguez Dreis, Harro Bitterling, Poul-Erik Braad, Egon Stenager, Sepehr Mamoei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionIntracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) accounts for approximately 15% of all strokes in Denmark and remains associated with high mortality and morbidity. It is challenging to distinguish neoplastic from non-neoplastic causes of ICH in the acute setting, and CT findings that may aid early differentiation have not been fully characterised. Existing ICH-classification systems (SMASH-U, H-ATOMIC and CLAS-ICH) have not been directly compared for diagnostic accuracy in this setting. Identifying radiological and clinical factors associated with underlying aetiology may support faster diagnosis, reduce time to workup related to potential underlying cancer and facilitate early targeted treatment of the underlying cause of ICH.Methods and analysisThis study is a retrospective observational cohort including all patients admitted with acute ICH to the Department of Neurology, University Hospital of Southern Denmark, Aabenraa between January 2014 and December 2024 (estimated approximately n=610). Medical records and initial non-enhanced CT scans will be reviewed. Two neurologists and two radiologists, blinded to final diagnosis, will independently extract clinical presentation, topographical and volumetric haemorrhage characteristics, and classify each case using the abovementioned ICH-classification systems. Primary analyses will assess associations between clinical and radiological features and underlying neoplastic vs non-neoplastic aetiology. Secondary analyses will compare diagnostic performance of classification systems using sensitivity, specificity and receiver operating characteristic curves. Multivariate logistic regression models will be applied with Holm correction for multiple comparisons.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been submitted to the National Danish Research Ethics Committee and the Danish Data Protection Agency. As data derive from completed disease courses, no patient contact is expected. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conferences and scientific presentations.Keywords: Cerebral Hemorrhage, Diagnostic Imaging, Cancer, Neuroradiology, Neurological oncology

Introduction

Annually, 12 500 persons are affected by stroke in Denmark,1 with 15% of all cases comprising intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH), the most fatal and second most frequent subtype of stroke.2 3 This is underlined by 30-day post-ICH mortality, estimated at 27% in 2022, which is significantly higher than the 7% estimated for ischaemic stroke.4 Clinical symptoms of ICH and ischaemic stroke are indistinguishable and can consist of palsy, paresthesia, diplopia, ataxia and dysphagia, among others. CT or MRI is, therefore, necessary to distinguish these two subtypes of stroke, with both scanning modalities being equivalent for the detection of ICH in the acute setting, while MRI is more accurate in detecting chronic ICH (ie, microbleeds).5 However, CT imaging of the brain is more appropriate for detecting ICH in acute settings due to its speed, availability and high sensitivity for acute detection of cerebral haemorrhage.

Underlying aetiologies of ICH include hypertensive parenchymal haemorrhage, angiopathies, primary neoplastic brain lesions and metastases,26,10 which may complicate and potentially prolong the diagnostic work-up, particularly in cases of malignancy.2 6 11 Previous studies suggest that 5–20% of cases of non-traumatic ICH are attributable to neoplasia, while other causes include arteriovenous malformations (43%), aneurysms (24%) and venous sinus thrombosis (19%).2 12 A few etiological ICH classification systems have been proposed, such as the SMASH-U, H-ATOMIC and the most recent CLAS-ICH classification system but these three systems have not yet been compared directly regarding sensitivity and specificity.10 13

The workup aiming at identifying the underlying aetiology of ICH is still under debate.14 However, further workup in the acute setting is determined by the localisation of the ICH outside of the basal ganglia, thalamus and pons (which may suggest other pathology than hypertensive parenchymal lesions), which may warrant CT/MRI with angiography or digital subtraction angiography to visualise potential vascular anomalies.14 Usually, a follow-up MRI is done after a few months due to the possibility of an ICH masking an underlying neoplastic lesion, which potentially can be overlooked on the initial CT scan.

Currently, studies primarily focused on ICH due to neoplastic lesions have shown that half of patients demonstrate stroke-like lesions, lung cancer is the most common cause of underlying metastatic ICH and glioblastoma is the most common cause of ICH from primary neoplastic brain lesions.6 Furthermore, volumetric analyses of the initial CT scans have shown larger volumes of perihematomal oedema (PHE) (figure 1) and haemorrhage in primary neoplastic brain lesions, when compared with non-neoplastic ICH2 and metastases,6 where the latter also were more prevalent in the occipital lobes.6 Utilisation of a cut-off of >0.50 of the relative PHE has been suggested to identify neoplastic causes of acute ICH.2

As of yet, associations between radiological findings on the initial CT scan of the acute ICH, clinical findings at presentation and final diagnosis have not yet been fully elucidated. This can potentially prolong workup and timely diagnosis of the underlying aetiology of the acute ICH, especially if the final diagnosis is malignancy.

Therefore, we will investigate findings on the initial non-enhanced CT and clinical symptoms and their associations with the neoplastic and non-neoplastic lesions in patients presenting with acute ICH admitted in the last 10 years to the Department of Neurology, University Hospital of Southern Denmark, Aabenraa, Denmark. Subsequently, we will perform subgroup analysis within the neoplastic (malformations or metastases) and non-neoplastic group (non-neoplastic structural lesion, medication, amyloid angiopathy, systemic disease, hypertension, undetermined, coagulopathy).

Furthermore, we will compare the SMASH-U, H-ATOMIC and the most recent CLAS-ICH classification systems.

This study aims to investigate associations between clinical presentation and findings on initial CT of the brain at admission and the underlying aetiology of ICH, with a particular focus on distinguishing neoplastic from non-neoplastic causes. These findings may contribute to improved early identification of underlying aetiology and inform clinical decision-making in the diagnostic work-up.

Methods and analysis

Study design, selection of participants and data collection

This study will be an exploratory observational historical cohort based on evaluation of medical records, initial CT scans and ICH-classifications of patients with acute ICH admitted to the Department of Neurology, University Hospital of Southern Denmark, Aabenraa, Denmark in the last 10 years, from 1 January 2014 to 31 December 2024. The Department of Neurology at the University Hospital of Southern Denmark in Aabenraa is a secondary care hospital covering the southern region of the Jutland peninsula with a population base of approximately 225 000 people and admits 60–65 patients with ICH annually. Patients with suspected acute stroke, including ICH, are typically admitted through the Emergency Department and managed in collaboration between neurologists and radiologists, with access to neurosurgical consultation when required. Patients may also be referred or transferred from surrounding hospitals within the region.

The diagnostic work-up of patients with suspected ICH follows established clinical practice, including immediate neuroimaging with non-enhanced CT of the brain and subsequent evaluation based on clinical presentation and imaging findings. Additional investigations, such as CT or MRI angiography, are performed when indicated to determine underlying aetiology.

In this study, the hospital chart database will be used to identify all medical records on all patients admitted with acute ICH over a period of 10 years (1 January 2014 to 31 December 2024), expecting approximately 610 patients. CT scans of the brain will be analysed by radiologists in the Department of Radiology, University Hospital of Southern Denmark, Aabenraa. Radiologists will assess the topography of ICH and volumetric analysis of the ICH, PHE using Analyze 15.0 (AnalyzeDirect, Overland Park, Kansas, USA) based on previously published methods for PHE and ICH volumetric analysis.2 15

All procedures, including data collection, variable definitions and statistical analyses, will be predefined prior to data collection and will be conducted according to a structured study protocol to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

Data collection will be based on predefined variable definitions and standardised procedures and will be performed by two neurologists and two radiologists with experience in stroke and neuroimaging. All reviewers involved in classification and imaging assessments will be blinded to the final etiological diagnosis during the independent reviews. Specifically, the two neurologists assigning cases according to SMASH-U, H-ATOMIC and CLAS-ICH will review only information available at admission, including presenting clinical features and the initial radiology report, and will be blinded to results from later diagnostic work-up and final diagnosis. Likewise, the two radiologists performing topographical and volumetric analyses will be blinded to final etiological diagnosis and clinical outcome data. Data extraction related to the final etiological diagnosis will be performed separately after these blinded assessments have been completed. Prior to data collection, all reviewers will undergo standardised training using pilot cases and a predefined data collection form (online supplemental table 1) with standardised variable definitions (see Variables) to ensure consistency. A standardised data collection form (online supplemental table 1) including all variables has been developed to guide data collection (online supplemental table 1). To assess intra-rater reliability, each reviewer will re-evaluate 20 cases after a time interval of 2 weeks while blinded to the initial assessment.

To assess consistency in data collection, 15% of cases (approximately 90 cases) will be independently reviewed by at least two reviewers and inter-rater reliability will be evaluated. In cases of ambiguous or conflicting interpretations, data will be discussed and resolved by consensus among reviewers.

Missing data will be handled as specified in the Data analysis plan section.

Sample size calculation

A formal sample size and power calculation was performed to assess whether the anticipated sample size would be sufficient for the planned analyses.

According to the Danish Stroke Registry data, 61 patients and 63 patients with acute ICH were admitted to the University Hospital of Southern Denmark, Aabenraa in 2023 and 2024, respectively.16 17 We therefore expect to enrol approximately 610 patients with acute ICH over 10 years, assuming that 10% have a neoplastic aetiology of ICH (consistent with estimates of 5–20% of neoplastic aetiology of all ICH cases as published by Nawabi et al)18 On the basis of previous studies, we expect 5–20% of cases (approximately 30–122 patients) to be attributable to neoplastic aetiology. The remaining cases are expected to represent non-neoplastic causes, including hypertensive haemorrhage, vascular malformations, aneurysms and other etiologies.12 18 Given the sample size of approximately 610 patients, we expect a sufficient number of cases for the primary analyses. Subgroup analyses will be conducted on an exploratory basis and interpreted with caution, particularly for smaller groups. To formally assess whether the anticipated sample size provides sufficient statistical power to detect a significant difference in the variables between neoplastic and non-neoplastic etiologies, we conducted a Monte Carlo simulation to estimate the minimal detectable difference expressed as Cohen’s d=M1− M2SDpooled with M1 and M2 being the mean for the neoplastic and non-neoplastic group, respectively. The pooled SD is calculated as SDpooled=(SD12+SD22)2. With 80% power and a significance level of α = 0.05, we expect to detect d≥0.56, given a prevalence of 10% neoplastic lesions. This is deemed realistic for variables such as relative PHE, which has previously shown an effect size of d=0.73.18

The final diagnosis of the underlying aetiology of ICH is determined through review of the full diagnostic work-up documented in the medical records, including discharge diagnoses, imaging findings and relevant investigations. International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes may be used for identification of eligible ICH admissions but will not alone define etiological classification. For analysis, individual etiological diagnoses will, where appropriate, be grouped into broader categories, including neoplastic causes (primary brain tumours and metastases), structural vascular causes (eg, arteriovenous malformations, aneurysms, venous thrombosis), small-vessel disease related causes (eg, hypertension, cerebral amyloid angiopathy), medication/coagulopathy-related causes, systemic/other determined causes and undetermined causes.

Data analysis plan

As the second objective is to investigate the diagnostic value of the combined variables, we aim to construct a multivariate model. Based on established methodological recommendations for logistic regression models, including studies suggesting that fewer than 10 events per variable may be acceptable under certain conditions,19 we expect to have a sufficient number of cases per variable to support the planned analyses.

Descriptive statistics will be used to assess baseline demographics of the patients admitted with acute ICH and stratified into the underlying neoplastic or non-neoplastic aetiology with a subsequent subgroup analysis in the neoplastic and non-neoplastic group as specified in 3. Significance and Research Objectives. For continuous variables, normality will be assessed using Q–Q plots and data are presented with mean (SD) or median (IQR) as appropriate. Intra-rater agreement will be assessed using Cohen’s kappa for categorical variables and intraclass correlation coefficients for continuous variables. Missing data will be categorised as missing or unclear.

Univariate generalised linear models will be applied to determine a mean difference or OR between continuous or categorical variables, respectively. Effect sizes will be displayed along with their 95%CIs. ICH volume, PHE volume and relative PHE volume will be assessed by receiver operating characteristic analysis with assessment of differences in area under the curve (AUC) in neoplastic and non-neoplastic ICH.

To control the family-wise error rate in investigations of association, we will apply Holm’s method for multiple testing. Results will be interpreted with caution regarding potential type I errors, implying that results will be checked for clinical plausibility and focusing on the width of the CIs.

To account for potential temporal changes in clinical practice and imaging over the 10-year study period, year of admission will be included in the analyses, including exploration of temporal trends and, where appropriate, adjustment for time in multivariate models.

In a multivariate logistic model, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with estimation of AUC will be used to evaluate model performance. Candidate predictor variables will be selected based on clinical relevance and effect size. The number of variables included will be limited according to the number of outcome events.

Other predictive modelling approaches will be considered if they seem appropriate.

Given that the SMASH-U, H-ATOMIC and CLAS-ICH classification systems differ in scope, level of detail, etiological attribution and reliance on imaging modalities, all classification systems will be applied in a standardised manner based on the available clinical and CT imaging data at admission. Comparisons between the classification systems will focus on their diagnostic performance in identifying the underlying aetiology of ICH, including sensitivity, specificity and ROC analyses, rather than direct conceptual equivalence between the systems. Where appropriate, etiological categories will be harmonised into broader groupings to support cross-system comparisons as also specified in Final diagnosis of the underlying aetiology of ICH in the Variables section.

STATA/BE V>18.0 will be used for statistical analysis, with p values below 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Coefficients from regression models will be reported with a 95% CI.

This study protocol is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Standardised data collection forms (online supplemental table 1) and blinded assessments will be applied to minimise potential sources of bias.

Limitations

This study is subject to limitations inherent to retrospective cohort designs. Selection bias may be present, as inclusion depends on available medical records and imaging, and patients with incomplete data are excluded. Information bias may occur due to missing or inconsistently recorded clinical data, and misclassification bias may arise from reliance on existing medical records when determining the cause of ICH.

To mitigate these limitations, standardised data collection forms (online supplemental table 1), predefined variable definitions and blinded independent assessments are applied.

Furthermore, retrospective review of existing medical records cannot fully capture clinical reasoning and decision-making, which may limit the direct translation of findings into clinical practice. This limitation may reduce the ability to account for contextual factors and clinician judgement influencing diagnostic work-up, and findings should therefore be interpreted with caution when applied to real-time acute care settings.

Finally, as this is a single-centre study conducted at a secondary hospital centre, the findings may have limited generalisability to other populations and healthcare settings, particularly community or rural hospitals with different patient populations, referral patterns and access to imaging specialist expertise. This may influence the distribution of underlying aetiologies and the applicability of the findings in other clinical contexts. Changes in clinical practice and imaging techniques over the 10-year study period may also influence the results.

However, the use of standard data collection procedures (online supplemental table 1), predefined variable definitions and objective radiological assessments may enhance reproducibility and support external validation in future multi-centre studies.

Differences in design, required inputs and intended use of the SMASH-U, H-ATOMIC and CLAS-ICH classification systems may affect their comparability and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations

Since this study is based on a retrospective review of already completed disease courses of patients with ICH, there are no side effects, risks or disadvantages associated with this study. Individual consent was not obtained, as this study is a retrospective observational study based on review of completed disease courses and routinely collected clinical and imaging data. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the National Danish Research Ethics Committee in accordance with Danish law and applicable regulations. No patients are expected to be contacted, as we do not expect to obtain new information relevant to the treatment of the included patients, since their hospital stays were concluded at the time this study was initiated.

An expert panel, comprising specialist neurologists and radiologists independent of this project and the authors, will be established to assess potential secondary findings beyond the project’s objectives but potentially relevant to participants’ health and well-being. The panel will be appointed based on clinical and academic expertise.

When secondary findings are identified, the panel will convene to discuss and assess their potential clinical significance. If necessary, the panel will consult relevant specialists with expertise in the field of the secondary finding.

Feedback to the participant on secondary findings may be provided if it is certain or highly likely that the findings indicate a serious or life-threatening disease, which (1) can be prevented, treated or alleviated, (2) has significant impact for the health and well-being of the participant, (3) can be clinically validated and (4) the method used to detect secondary findings is reliable.

Feedback on significant secondary findings will be provided in accordance with Danish law (Tilbagemeldingsbekendtgørelsens §9), while respecting the participants’ right not to receive such information. If the expert panel determines that a secondary finding may have a substantial impact on the participants’ health and well-being, the participants will be contacted and informed that we have identified a health-related finding, without disclosure of the nature or content of the finding. This allows the participant to have the option to decide whether they wish to receive further information or to decline to be informed. Participants who choose to receive further information will be offered detailed information about the finding. Additionally, the participants will be offered a meeting where they can ask questions and receive information about the implications of the finding and potential treatments.

The authors have obtained approval for the study protocol from the National Danish Research Ethics Committee (ID: 3083080).

Data protection

The patients admitted with acute ICH will not be identified by name in the research project and will be anonymised. Ethics committees may inspect the records of the study. Utmost care will be taken to keep the subjects’ medical data confidential.
