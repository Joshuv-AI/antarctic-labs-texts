Lateral transpetrosal approaches for brainstem cavernous malformations: tailoring the operative trajectory according to lesion location. Illustrative cases
Authors: Kazumi Ohmori, Taku Takeuchi, Shiduka Kamiyoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Brainstem cavernous malformations (BSCMs) are among the most challenging vascular lesions because complete resection must be balanced against the risk of permanent neurological deficits.1–3 Although the shortest route to the lesion is often considered desirable, direct dorsal approaches may place the medial longitudinal fasciculus and other dorsal tegmental structures at risk, whereas ventral approaches may require transgression of the corticospinal pathways. We therefore favor lateral transpetrosal approaches whenever feasible to maximize functional preservation, even when the lesion is not located immediately beneath the lateral surface. In this study, we describe our surgical strategy using posterior and combined transpetrosal approaches according to the rostrocaudal location of lateral BSCMs. We propose that preservation of functional anatomy should take precedence over selection of the shortest anatomical route.

Illustrative Cases

Between 2016 and 2024, 3 consecutive patients with lateral BSCMs underwent microsurgical resection at our institution. Two lesions were located in the lateral pons and one in the upper lateral midbrain. The surgical approach was selected according to lesion location. The choice between a posterior transpetrosal (retrolabyrinthine presigmoid) approach and a combined transpetrosal approach with anterior petrosectomy (Kawase approach)4 was determined by the rostrocaudal location of the lesion and the required surgical trajectory.

Standard intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring included motor evoked potentials, somatosensory evoked potentials, auditory brainstem responses, and continuous facial nerve electromyography. Neuronavigation was used in all cases. Gross-total resection was confirmed on early postoperative MRI, and neurological outcomes were evaluated at discharge and during follow-up.

Three consecutive patients (ages 22, 47, and 72 years; all male) underwent microsurgical resection for lateral BSCMs. Two lesions were located in the pons and one in the upper lateral midbrain. All patients had experienced multiple symptomatic hemorrhages (three or four episodes) before surgery. All 3 lesions were deep-seated and did not reach the pial surface of the brainstem. Preoperative symptoms included diplopia due to trochlear palsy in the midbrain case and transient disturbance of consciousness with hemiparesis in both pontine cases. One pontine patient had previously undergone Gamma Knife radiosurgery.

Gross-total resection was achieved in all patients. No new permanent neurological deficits occurred. Hearing was preserved in all 3 patients, with no preoperative hearing impairment or new postoperative hearing deficit. Diplopia improved in the midbrain patient, and both pontine patients recovered from impaired consciousness with improvement of hemiparesis. Case 1

A 22-year-old man presented after three episodes of hemorrhage with diplopia caused by trochlear nerve palsy. During outpatient ­follow-up, a recurrent hemorrhage resulted in radiographic enlargement of the lesion. MRI demonstrated an 18.5-mm upper lateral midbrain cavernous malformation (Fig. 1). A combined transpetrosal approach with anterior petrosectomy was selected to obtain an upward operative trajectory through the lateral mesencephalic sulcus. Case 2

A 72-year-old man experienced three hemorrhagic episodes despite previous Gamma Knife radiosurgery. The lesion was a ­29.4-mm cavernous malformation located in the ventrolateral pons. No radiographic enlargement of the lesion was documented between hemorrhagic episodes. A posterior transpetrosal approach through the lateral pontine zone provided sufficient exposure for complete resection. Hemiparesis improved, and no permanent neurological deterioration occurred.

Case 3

A 47-year-old man experienced four hemorrhagic episodes, the last occurring during hospitalization, resulting in radiographic enlargement of the lesion. MRI demonstrated a 22.0-mm ventrolateral pontine cavernous malformation. Because the lesion was located at the lateral pontine zone, a posterior transpetrosal (retrolabyrinthine presigmoid) approach was selected. Gross-total resection was achieved without new permanent neurological deficits. The patient’s disturbance of consciousness resolved, and hemiparesis gradually improved during follow-up.

Discussion

Observations

BSCMs remain among the most challenging vascular lesions because complete resection must be balanced against preservation of neurological function.5,6 Following symptomatic hemorrhage, the risk of recurrent bleeding is substantial, and repeated hemorrhages frequently result in progressive neurological deterioration.2,7 Therefore, microsurgical resection remains the only treatment capable of achieving complete lesion removal and definitive elimination of the risk of future hemorrhage.3,5–7

Although stereotactic radiosurgery has been proposed as an alternative treatment for surgically inaccessible BSCMs, its role remains controversial.8–11 Several studies have reported a reduction in hemorrhage rates following radiosurgery; however, interpretation of these results is complicated by the natural history of cavernous malformations. The risk of rebleeding is known to decline over time after the initial hemorrhage (temporal clustering),8 making it difficult to distinguish a true treatment effect from the natural course of the disease. Furthermore, radiosurgery does not eliminate the lesion itself and carries a potential risk of delayed radiation-induced neurological complications. Consequently, microsurgical resection remains the treatment of choice whenever an acceptable surgical corridor can be established.5,6

The principal challenge in BSCM surgery is not simply reaching the lesion but preserving the densely packed functional pathways within the brainstem. Previous anatomical studies have primarily focused on defining brainstem safe entry zones based on surface anatomy and fiber tract relationships.12–15 Direct dorsal approaches may require transgression of the dorsal tegmentum, where the medial longitudinal fasciculus, ocular motor pathways, ascending sensory tracts, and reticular formation are densely concentrated. Conversely, ventral approaches may place the corticospinal pathways at risk. Thus, when anatomically feasible, a lateral safe entry corridor may avoid critical structures encountered through either dorsal or ventral trajectories. Injury to these structures may result in disabling neurological deficits, including persistent ocular motility disorders, sensory impairment, and disturbance of consciousness. Therefore, whenever an anatomically feasible lateral corridor is available, we prefer to avoid direct dorsal transgression of the brainstem. Accordingly, the operative corridor should be selected not only to reach the lesion but also to preserve functional brainstem anatomy. Rather than simply selecting the shortest route to the lesion, the operative corridor should be tailored to maximize preservation of eloquent neural pathways while maintaining adequate surgical exposure.

This philosophy is consistent with the concept of established lateral brainstem safe entry zones.12–15 The lateral mesencephalic sulcus provides an effective corridor for lateral midbrain lesions, whereas the lateral pontine and peritrigeminal zones allow access to pontine cavernous malformations through relatively less eloquent regions (Fig. 2).12–15 These lateral corridors minimize transgression of major long tracts and cranial nerve nuclei while providing direct access to the hematoma cavity. In all 3 patients, gross-total resection was achieved through these lateral safe entry zones without new permanent neurological deficits, supporting the anatomical rationale of this strategy. Our experience further suggests that the choice of transpetrosal approach should be determined primarily by the rostrocaudal location of the lesion rather than by the pathological diagnosis itself. Posterior transpetrosal (retrolabyrinthine presigmoid) exposure provided an adequate operative trajectory for both pontine lesions.16 In contrast, the upper lateral midbrain cavernous malformation required a steeper superior viewing angle that could not be sufficiently obtained through the posterior transpetrosal approach alone. Addition of an anterior petrosectomy (Kawase approach) expanded the superior operative trajectory and facilitated safe access to the lateral mesencephalic sulcus through an upward surgical corridor.4,16 Thus, posterior and combined transpetrosal approaches should be regarded as complementary techniques, with the optimal corridor selected according to the anatomical location of the lesion (Fig. 3). From a technical standpoint, the key consideration is to tailor the extent of petrosal exposure to the required operative trajectory. For ventrolateral pontine lesions, a retrolabyrinthine posterior transpetrosal exposure provided sufficient access to the lateral pontine zone while minimizing cerebellar retraction. In contrast, for the upper lateral midbrain lesion, addition of anterior petrosectomy and tentorial division was necessary to obtain a sufficiently steep upward trajectory to the lateral mesencephalic sulcus. Thus, extensive petrosectomy is not required in every case; rather, the bony and dural exposure should be tailored to the location of the lesion and the intended safe entry zone (Video 1). This study has several limitations. It represents a retrospective analysis of only 3 patients without a comparative cohort. Consequently, definitive conclusions regarding the superiority of one surgical approach over another cannot be drawn. Nevertheless, all 3 patients underwent gross-total resection without new permanent neurological deficits despite multiple preoperative hemorrhages. Although the present series is limited in size, it illustrates an anatomical strategy that emphasizes preservation of functional brainstem pathways while tailoring the transpetrosal approach to lesion location.

Lessons

We believe that the shortest anatomical route may not always represent the safest functional corridor. Rather than advocating a single transpetrosal approach for all BSCMs, we propose that preservation of eloquent brainstem pathways should guide surgical planning, with the operative corridor tailored according to both the safe entry zone and the rostrocaudal location of the lesion.14
