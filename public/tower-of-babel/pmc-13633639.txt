Cooperation between Adh and OR5M11 contributes to the lung pathogenicity of Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae by facilitating the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response
Authors: Bo Wen, Xiaoli Yin, Xinhe Zhang, Chunling Zhu, Xiaofeng Li, Yilin Bai, Huarun Sun, Zhiyi Liu, Shuwen Ji, Ruibiao Wang, Chenru Yu, Jiaqi Wang, Longfei Zhang, Jiyuan Shen, Jianhe Hu, Yueyu Bai, Huihui Zhang, Ke Ding, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae (A. pleuropneumoniae) is the etiologic agent of porcine pleuropneumonia, a severe respiratory disease characterized by hemorrhagic, necrotizing lung lesions that cause significant economic losses on the global swine industry. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the lung pathogenicity of A. pleuropneumoniae remain unclear. Here we demonstrate that A. pleuropneumoniae infection induces lung injury through activating the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response mediated by the YadA-like head region (Adh) of Apa1, a bacterial adhesin that mediates attachment to host cells. Importantly, we show that Adh triggers the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response by engaging olfactory receptor family 5 subfamily M member 11 (OR5M11), a host receptor previously shown to interact with Adh. Furthermore, OR5M11 contributes to the Adh-activated inflammatory response by promoting mitochondrial DNA release. Taken together, our results provide new insights into the pathogenesis of A. pleuropneumoniae, particularly regarding NLRP3-mediated inflammatory pathology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-026-01845-4.

Introduction

Porcine pleuropneumonia is a highly contagious fatal disease which causes substantial economic losses to the pig industry worldwide by affecting swine of all ages [1]. The causative agent of porcine pleuropneumonia, A. pleuropneumoniae, belongs to the family Pasteurellaceae, and has been divided into 19 serotypes [2]. A lack of cross-protection between the main serovars results in slow progress in the development of vaccines, making the infection very difficult to control [3]. A. pleuropneumoniae is a respiratory pathogen and usually induces dyspnea, cough, high fever, and peripheral cyanosis of extremities. A. pleuropneumoniae exhibits typical lung pathogenicity characterized by severe congestion and fibrosis in the lungs, with necrotic lesions commonly observed, accompanied by considerable inflammatory cell infiltration and severe structural damage of the alveoli [4, 5]. The excessive inflammatory immune response triggered by A. pleuropneumoniae in the lungs is a key cause of the serious lung injury [1, 6–9]. However, the molecular mechanisms that underpin the lung pathogenicity of A. pleuropneumoniae remain unclear.

Trimeric autotransporter adhesins (TAAs) are bacterial adhesion structures that participate in adhesion, biofilm formation, autoagglutination, and early bacterial pathogenic processes [10, 11]. TAAs have been observed on many bacteria, such as YadA on the surface of Yersinia enterocolitica, NadA on Neisseria meningitidis, UpaG on uropathogenic Escherichia coli, BadA on Bartonella henselae, BtaE on Brucella, and HadA on the cell surface of Haemophilus influenzae [12–17]. A. pleuropneumoniae also possesses two TAA proteins, Apa1 and Apa2, which mediate bacterial autoagglutination and adherence to host cells [18, 19]. Amino acids 68–612 located in the head region of Apa1, named Adh, have been demonstrated to constitute the main functional domain, playing an important role in A. pleuropneumoniae adhesion to host cells, autoagglutination, and biofilm formation [20]. Furthermore, Adh contributes to the early-phase pathogenicity of A. pleuropneumoniae, particularly lung pathogenicity, through excessive inflammatory immune responses [20, 21]. Interestingly, olfactory receptor family 5 subfamily M member 11 (OR5M11) was identified to interact with Adh, which mediates Adh-induced release of inflammatory cytokines [20].

Inflammasomes are macromolecular multimeric protein complexes composed of intracellular pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), the adaptor protein apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and the effector pro-Caspase−1 (pro-CASP-1) [22]. The nucleotide-binding domain and leucine-rich repeat containing receptor (NLR) family PYRIN domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome is one of the best-characterized inflammasomes. Upon activation, the NLRP3 inflammasome serves as a platform for caspase−1 activation, resulting in caspase−1-dependent proteolytic maturation of pro-interleukin-1β (IL-1β) [23]. As an essential innate immune defense mechanism, inflammasomes are usually activated during cellular stress and infections [24, 25]. However, aberrant activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome triggers excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-18, and IL-6, potentially leading to a “cytokine storm”, skewing the host response toward pathological inflammatory events [26–28]. Although many studies highlight that excessive activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome results in hyperinflammation and severe tissue damage, whether the inflammatory lung injury induced by A. pleuropneumoniae is mediated by aberrant activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome has not been determined.

In this study, we investigated the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and its role in A. pleuropneumoniae infection-induced inflammatory lung injury in vitro and in vivo, and identified the mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of A. pleuropneumoniae infection.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal studies were conducted according to the experimental practices and standards of the Animal Welfare and Research Ethics Committee at Zhengzhou University. The study was also approved by the Animal Center of Zhengzhou University (IACUC license number: no. 41003100024648). All the animals were humanely handled.

Bacterial strains and cells

Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae serotype 5 strain L20 (5b WT) was obtained from the Shanghai Entry-Exit Inspection and Quarantine Bureau. The trimeric autotransporter adhesins-deletion strain (5b ΔAdh) was constructed [21] and stored by our laboratory. Primary PAMs were collected from the BALF of healthy weaned piglets, which were purchased from a pig-breeding farm. The PAM cell line (ATCC CRL-2845) was kindly provided by Professor Feng (China Agricultural University). These cells were grown in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin.

Experimental design and groupings

All in vitro experiments were performed with PAMs unless otherwise specified. For infection experiments, PAMs were infected with A. pleuropneumoniae 5b WT or 5b ΔAdh at an MOI of 10 for 12 h. Untreated cells served as the negative control (NT or NC). Antibodies and reagents

Primary antibody against caspase−1 (24232), Cle-caspase−1 (24232), AIM2 (53491), IL-1β (12426), Cle-IL-1β (12426), and ASC (67824) were purchased from cell signalling technology. Primary antibody against GSDMD (PU224937) and Cle-GSDMD (PU224937) were purchased from abmart. Primary antibody against OR5M11 (DF5110) was purchased from affinity. Primary antibody against β-actin (HC201) was purchased from Transgen. HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (A9917), and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (A0545) were from Sigma. Cell Mitochondrial isolation kit (C3601) was purchased from Beyotime. DNA Isolation kit (69504) was purchased from QIAGEN. DNase I (E127–01B) was purchased from Novoprotein. MCC950 (HY-12815) and Z-YVAD-FMK (HY-P1009) were purchased from MedChemExpress.

Western blotting

The harvested cells were treated with RIPA lysis buffer containing phenylmethyl sulfonylfluoride (PMSF) for generating cell lysates. Protein samples were produced by adding 5× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) sample buffer to lysed cells. The samples were boiled for 10 min, separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and then transferred onto 0.22 µm polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5–10% nonfat milk in TBST for 2 h and then probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, the membranes were reacted with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. At last, the bound antibodies were detected with enhanced chemiluminescence immunoblotting detection reagents (Millipore). Images were obtained with a CanoScan LiDE 100 scanner (Canon).

RNA isolation and Realtime quantitative RT-PCR

qRT-PCR was used to verify the cytokine, apoptotic signaling pathway-associated protein. The primer sequences are listed in Additional file 1. RNA extraction and reverse transcription was performed with the TaKaRa RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time, lot no. RR047A) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transient transfections and chemical treatment

All plasmids and siRNA (Ribo, Guangzhou, China) transfections were performed using TurboFect Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R0531) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. MCC950 (MCE, HY-12815) was used to pre-treat cells. The treatments were applied 2 h before bacterial strains infection and maintained until the end of the experiments.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining

The fixed lung samples were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and serial sectioned (4 mm). Three sagittal sections from each lung, and six sections per animal, were stained with hematoxylin–eosin and Masson’s trichrome. The severities of histopathological changes and pulmonary fibrosis of the lungs were assessed and scored. All the sections were numbered randomly and interpreted by three experimenters blinded to the experimental conditions. Histopathological changes were observed and scored under an Olympus microscope (Olympus Optical Co., Ltd.).

Determination of mtDNA copies in cytosolic extracts

The cytoplasmic fraction of PAMs is isolated using the Cell Mitochondrial Isolation Kit (C3601, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the instructions. Briefly, 107 cells were incubated in 0.5 mL of mitochondrial lysis buffer for 10 min and then homogenized 30 times with Dounce homogenizer. The supernatants are collected and determined the cytoplasmic mtDNA concentration using qRT-PCR.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times. The data are represented as the mean ± SD or typical photographs and are representative of three independent experiments. For data with two groups, unpaired Student’s t-tests were used under the assumption of normality. Data with more than two groups were analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) under assumption of normality. Values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All quantitative data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, inc. USA).

Results

Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae induces the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response

The NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response (mainly the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome) has been shown to be involved in inflammatory lung injury [29–31]. However, the relationship between the lung pathogenicity of A. pleuropneumoniae and the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response remains unknown. First, we evaluated the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome-related molecules in A. pleuropneumoniae (serotype 5 strain L20, 5b WT)-infected porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs). We detected elevated levels of ASC, mature caspase−1 (p10), and IL-1β (p17) by western blot in 5b WT-infected PAMs at 12 hpi, and in the positive group (LPS-treated), compared with the mock-infected group (NC) (Figure 2A and Additional file 2). Consistent with these findings, the mRNA levels of IL-1β in 5b WT-infected PAMs were upregulated in a time-dependent manner relative to the control group (Figure 2B). ELISA analysis showed that IL-1β protein levels in the supernatant of 5b WT-infected PAMs began to increase dramatically at 6 hours post-infection (hpi), peaking at 12 hpi (Figure 2C). It has been reported that K+ efflux is a common trigger of NLRP3 inflammasome activation by bacterial toxins [32]. Here, we found that inhibition of K⁺efflux by culturing PAMs in high-K+ medium decreased IL-1β levels in the supernatant of 5b WT-infected PAMs (Figure 2D). To examine the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in 5b WT-induced inflammatory responses, we used RNA interference to suppress NLRP3 expression in PAMs. When NLRP3 was knocked down (Figure 2E), pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-18, IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1) were downregulated (Figure 2F–J), and the cleavage of caspase−1, GSDMD, and IL-1β was attenuated compared with the negative control (NC) group for RNA interference during 5b WT infection (Figure 2K and Additional file 2).

Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae causes lung injury via NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response

The demonstrated role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in 5b WT-induced inflammatory responses prompted us to investigate whether the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response contributes to 5b WT-induced lung injury. To this end, we employed MCC950, a specific inhibitor of the NLRP3 inflammasome, in a mouse model of infection (1 × 108 CFU per mouse). MCC950 treatment significantly improved survival rates compared with the vehicle-treated group (80% versus 20%) (Figure 3A). Furthermore, both lung weight (Figure 3B) and bacterial burden in the lungs (Figure 3C) were reduced in MCC950-treated mice. Histological analysis revealed that 5b WT-infected mice exhibited severe lung lesions characterized by alveolar rupture, pulmonary congestion, and extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells into the pulmonary interstitium, alveoli, and peribronchial regions—pathological features consistent with bronchopneumonia (Figure 3G). In contrast, MCC950-treated mice displayed relatively milder lung pathology with reduced interstitial thickening; inflammatory cells were primarily localized around the bronchi, with minimal alveolar infiltration (Figure 3G).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that 5b WT induces lung injury through activation of the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response.

To further define the role of Adh in A. pleuropneumoniae-induced NLRP3 activation, we infected PAMs with an Adh deletion mutant (5bΔAdh) and compared outcomes with the wild-type (5b WT) strain. 5bΔAdh infection significantly reduced levels of mature caspase−1 and IL-1β (Figure 4D and Additional file 2) and decreased expression of TNF-α, NLRP3 and IL-1β (Figure 4E–G) compared with the 5b WT strain. Subsequently, we performed a complementation assay by treating 5b ΔAdh-infected PAMs with purified Adh protein to further assess its role in NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses. The reduced levels of mature IL-1β (Figure 4H and Additional file 2) and mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6, MCP-1, caspase−1 and NLRP (Figure 4I–M) observed in the 5b ΔAdh-infected group were restored upon Adh complementation. Additionally, MCC950 further decreased levels of mature caspase−1 and GSDMD (Figure 4N and Additional file 2) and expression of IL-18, MCP-1, IL-6 and caspase−1 (Figure 4O–R) in 5b ΔAdh-infected PAMs, implying that other virulence factors in A. pleuropneumoniae also mediate NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses.

Taken together, these data demonstrate that the Adh domain is a key, but not exclusive, region for NLRP3 inflammasome activation during A. pleuropneumoniae infection.

Our data showed that antibody treatment inhibited not only the maturation of NLRP3 inflammasome–related proteins, including cleaved IL-1β (Figure 5C and Additional file 2), but also the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines such as caspase−1, NLRP3, TNF-α and MCP-1 (Figure 5D–H) in 5b WT-infected PAMs.

Additionally, we knocked down OR5M11 in PAMs via RNA interference to further evaluate its role in NLRP3 inflammasome activation. qRT-PCR was performed to verify knockdown efficiency. Treatment with OR5M11-specific siRNA (si-OR5M11) at various concentrations reduced OR5M11 levels in a dose-dependent manner, with 10 μM producing the most pronounced effect (Figure 5I and Additional file 2). Moreover, si-OR5M11 at 10 μM significantly suppressed the maturation of caspase−1 and GSDMD (Figure 5I and Additional file 2) and inhibited TNF-α expression (Figure 5J).

OR5M11 participates in the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response by promoting mitochondrial DNA release

It has previously been shown that Adh affects mitochondrial homeostasis, as evidenced by reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in PAMs [27], indicating mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). As a major mitochondrial damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), mtDNA leaking into the cytoplasm during mitochondrial dysfunction can directly engage pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to enhance pro-inflammatory responses [34]. Given the interaction between Adh and OR5M11, we hypothesized that Adh triggers NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses through OR5M11-mediated mtDNA release. To test this, we first investigated whether A. pleuropneumoniae infection induces mtDNA release into the cytosol by quantifying cytosolic mtDNA using qRT-PCR. The mtDNA content (including cyt b, COX I, D-loop, and ND1) in the cytoplasm of 5b WT-infected PAMs was significantly increased compared with that in control cells (Figure 7A). Subsequently, we explored the role of mtDNA released from mitochondria in NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses by using DNase I to degrade cytosolic mtDNA in A. pleuropneumoniae-infected cells and then assessed the effects on NLRP3 inflammasome activation and inflammatory cytokine expression. qRT-PCR results revealed that DNase I decreased not only cytosolic mtDNA levels (Figure 7B) but also the excessive expression of inflammasome cytokines, such as IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-18 (Figure 7C–E), induced by 5b WT infection. Additionally, NLRP3 inflammasome–related proteins, including mature GSDMD, IL-1β, and AIM2, were downregulated in DNase I-treated PAMs during 5b WT infection (Figure 7F and Additional file 2).

To investigate the involvement of OR5M11 in mediating mtDNA release, OR5M11 was knocked down using siRNA, and qRT-PCR was performed to measure mtDNA levels. The data indicated that the release of mtDNA (cyt b, COX I, D-loop, and ND1) into the cytosol was significantly inhibited by knocking down OR5M11 during 5b WT infection in PAMs (Figure 7G). Similar results were obtained in PAMs pretreated with an OR5M11-specific antibody (Figure 7H). Conversely, overexpression of OR5M11 by transfection with a pCMV-OR5M11 plasmid further increased the level of mtDNA (cyt b, COX I, D-loop, and ND1) released into the cytosol during 5b WT infection (Figure 7I). Importantly, overexpression of OR5M11 in 5b ΔAdh-infected PAMs failed to further promote mtDNA release (Figure 7J), indicating that Adh is essential for OR5M11-mediated mtDNA release; in other words, cooperation between Adh and OR5M11 promotes mtDNA release.

Collectively, our results demonstrate that OR5M11 cooperates with Adh to facilitate A. pleuropneumoniae-triggered mtDNA release, which triggers NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses.

Adh is a critical virulence factor that contributes to the lung pathogenicity of A. pleuropneumoniae

Given that Adh and OR5M11 participate in A. pleuropneumoniae-induced, NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses in vitro, we sought to explore the contributions of these bacterial and host factors to A. pleuropneumoniae pathogenicity, particularly pulmonary inflammatory injury. Therefore, we established four experimental groups in a mouse model: a negative control group (NC), a 5b WT-infected group (5b), a 5b ΔAdh-infected group (5b ΔAdh), and a complementation group in which mice received intraperitoneal Adh injection prior to infection with the 5b ΔAdh strain (5b cΔAdh), to verify the role of Adh in A. pleuropneumoniae pathogenicity.

As shown in Figure 8A, the survival rate of the 5b group was significantly lower than that of the 5b ΔAdh group (20% versus 80%, respectively). Notably, the 5b cΔAdh group exhibited reduced survival between 42 and 84 hpi compared with the 5b ΔAdh group; however, survival ultimately equaled that of the 5b group. Clinical scores showed a similar trend to the survival data (Figure 8B). Lungs from the 5b group were the heaviest, whereas those from the 5b ΔAdh group were significantly lighter (Figure 8C). Consistently, serum levels of IL-1β (Figure 8D), IL-18 (Figure 8E), and IL-6 (Figure 8F) in the 5b ΔAdh group were significantly lower than those in the 5b group but higher than those in the NC group at 12 and 24 h post-infection. Additionally, histopathological analysis revealed marked inflammatory exudates and widened interstitial spaces in the 5b group compared with the 5b ΔAdh group (Figure 8G). Furthermore, extensive red blood cell infiltration in the alveoli of the 5b group indicated severe pulmonary hemorrhage (Figure 8G). The role of OR5M11 in the pathogenicity of A. pleuropneumoniae was also investigated by intraperitoneal administration of an OR5M11-specific antibody to mice. Discussion

Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae-induced porcine contagious pleuropneumonia is characterized by severe lung damage, including marked congestion and fibrosis. Furthermore, high levels of inflammatory cytokines are present in the lungs of A. pleuropneumoniae-infected pigs, accompanied by considerable inflammatory cell infiltration and severe alveolar structural damage, indicative of typical inflammatory lung injury [4, 5, 8]. This is a novel study to elucidate the underlying mechanism by which A. pleuropneumoniae infection induces pathological inflammation in the lung; we demonstrate that the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response contributes to the pulmonary pathogenicity of A. pleuropneumoniae. However, as a major pathogen of the Porcine Respiratory Disease Complex (PRDC), A. pleuropneumoniae frequently co-occurs with other pathogens, particularly viruses, thereby exacerbating lung lesions [35, 36]. Therefore, investigating the role of the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response during co-infection with A. pleuropneumoniae and other pathogens represents an important direction for future research.

Two trimeric autotransporter adhesins (TAAs), designated Apa1 and Apa2, were identified in A. pleuropneumoniae in 2011 and 2012, respectively, and mediate autoagglutination and host cell adherence [18, 19]. Subsequently, amino acids 68–612 in the head region of Apa1, designated Adh, were demonstrated to constitute the primary functional domain mediating A. pleuropneumoniae adhesion [21]. In addition to mediating adherence, Adh binds to OR5M11 to regulate proinflammatory cytokine expression, particularly IL-8, which plays a critical role in neutrophil recruitment and contributes to lung inflammation and fibrosis when highly expressed [20]. The present study elucidates that the interaction between Adh and OR5M11 drives excessive activation of the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response, ultimately resulting in lung damage. Notably, the Adh–OR5M11 interaction represents a critical process that enhances pulmonary pathogenicity. Therefore, determining the structure of the Adh–OR5M11 complex and designing small-molecule inhibitors targeting this interaction will be crucial for preventing and treating lung inflammatory damage caused by A. pleuropneumoniae infection.

Olfactory receptors (ORs), which belong to class A G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), constitute the most important chemosensory receptor family responsible for olfaction in the nasal olfactory epithelium [37]. Interestingly, ORs are also expressed in macrophages, including alveolar macrophages, where they can modulate inflammatory responses and have been implicated in various lung diseases [38, 39]. In our previous study, we demonstrated that OR5M11, an OR family member, is expressed in porcine alveolar macrophages and mediates the release of inflammatory cytokines, contributing to A. pleuropneumoniae pathogenicity through its interaction with Adh [20]. However, the mechanism by which OR5M11 regulates A. pleuropneumoniae-induced inflammatory responses remains unknown. Recently, an important study revealed that olfactory receptor 2 in vascular macrophages drives atherosclerosis through NLRP3-dependent IL-1 production [40]. Here, we demonstrate that OR5M11 also participates in NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses, ultimately contributing to lung damage during A. pleuropneumoniae infection through the aberrant release of mitochondrial DNA. Whereas, how does OR5M11 function in causing abnormal release of mitochondrial DNA? As known, in the process of G protein-coupled receptors related signal transduction, an influx of calcium ions (Ca2+) into the cells, which is able to trigger a series of downstream signaling pathways, especially is related to mitochondrial homeostasis [40–42]. Additionally, ORs can also activate the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-Akt pathway and the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, which regulate some biological process of cells [43, 44]. Therefore, we make a hypothesis that influx of calcium ions (Ca2+), and PI3K-Akt and MAPK pathway may be involved in OR5M11-mediated mitochondrial DNA release during A. pleuropneumoniae infection. But this need to be further studied.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is a major mitochondrial damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) that escapes from stressed mitochondria into the cytoplasm and provokes inflammation, particularly NLRP3 inflammasome activation [45–47]. NLRP3 inflammasome activation follows a two-step process: priming and activation [48]. Priming is initiated by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that sense pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), leading to NF-κB-mediated transcription of NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β. Activation involves NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, caspase−1 activation, and IL-1β maturation. mtDNA plays a key role in this two-step activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. On the one hand, cytosolic mtDNA, sensed by cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) synthase (cGAS)—a PRR—activates the cGAS-STING pathway, which subsequently promotes the transcription of genes encoding NLRP3 inflammasome activation–related proteins, such as IL-1β and NLRP3, through NF-κB activation [49]. On the other hand, mtDNA binds to cytosolic NLRP3, thereby triggering inflammasome activation [37]. In the present study, we found that mtDNA released from A. pleuropneumoniae-infected PAMs participates in NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses. However, the roles of the cGAS-STING pathway and the direct binding of mtDNA to NLRP3 in the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response triggered by OR5M11-promoted mtDNA release during A. pleuropneumoniae infection were not investigated in this study and require further verification.

A schematic model illustrating how the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response, activated through the interaction of Adh with OR5M11, contributes to the pulmonary pathogenicity of A. pleuropneumoniae is presented in Figure 10. In this model, Adh binding to OR5M11 triggers mtDNA release, resulting in dysregulated NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses that ultimately exacerbate lung injury during A. pleuropneumoniae infection. The findings presented herein advance our understanding of A. pleuropneumoniae pathogenesis and may inform the development of attenuated vaccines against this pathogen. It must, however, be recognized that, although the mechanism by which the A. pleuropneumoniae virulence factor Adh engages OR5M11 to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and thereby induce lung inflammatory injury has been characterized in this study, the use of a murine model is unlikely to fully reflect the pathophysiological processes that occur in the natural host, the pig, due to interspecies differences in pulmonary architecture, immune cell composition, and cytokine dynamics.
