Coracoid process morphometry in Asians: a systematic review of computed tomography-based studies
Authors: Brandon Lim, Gennie Lim, Junren Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurately measuring the coracoid process is essential to coracoid transfer procedures. This systematic review synthesizes computed tomography (CT)-based studies of coracoid morphometry in Asians, summarises measurement parameters, and establishes pooled-mean dimensional estimates.MethodsCT-based studies reporting coracoid morphometry in Asians were identified. Pooled estimates were calculated from study-level sample sizes, means, and standard deviations using a combined-group variance approach. Study quality was assessed using the Anatomical Quality Assurance checklist.ResultsFourteen studies comprising 1,581 coracoid processes were included. The pooled mean age was 46.38 ± 14.62 years (9 studies). The pooled mean measurements were: total coracoid length 39.82±3.17 mm (5 studies); tip to flexion point distance 23.31±3.50 mm (3 studies); tip to coracoclavicular ligament distance 22.66±2.99 mm (2 studies); thickness halfway between the tip and base 11.28±1.39 mm (3 studies); and thickness 1 cm from the tip 10.03 ± 0.95 mm (3 studies). In females, the pooled mean measurements were total length 38.79±3.95 mm (5 studies); tip to flexion point distance 19.98±1.91 mm (2 studies); thickness halfway between the tip and base 10.58±1.08 mm (3 studies); and thickness 1 cm from the tip 9.52±0.83 mm (2 studies).ConclusionsAlthough heterogeneity in anatomical definitions limits cross-study comparability, pooled coracoid dimensions in Asian females suggest that they approach previously proposed fracture-risk thresholds. Well-designed studies using standardised morphometric definitions and measurement protocols are needed to further evaluate this finding.

INTRODUCTION

The coracoid process, sometimes called the “lighthouse of the shoulder,” is a hook-like structure projecting from the anterosuperior scapula and plays the following critical anatomical and surgical roles: it is where several muscles (pectoralis minor, coracobrachialis, short head of biceps brachii) and ligaments (coracohumeral, coracoacromial, coracoclavicular, and superior transverse scapular) attach; it is a palpable bony landmark that can be easily identified when approaching the shoulder to avoid vital neurovascular structures running medial to it, such as the brachial plexus, axillary artery, and axillary vein; and it is used as a bone graft in coracoid transfer procedures in the management of anterior shoulder instability, most notably the Bristow and Latarjet procedures [1].

Accurately interpreting the anatomy and size of the coracoid process on preoperative imaging, such as computed tomography (CT), is essential for surgical planning because its dimensions determine the maximum osteotomy length, implant selection, and screw trajectory [2,3]. Effective surgical planning also enhances procedural safety during a coracoid osteotomy and graft transfer, minimising the risk of cortical breach or intraoperative graft fracture by selecting appropriately sized fixation devices [4]. Previous studies have suggested that coracoids that are <10 mm thick or usable grafts <20–22 mm might be associated with an increased risk of intraoperative fracture [5,6].

Several studies, cadaveric or imaging-based with CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have reported coracoid process dimensions (length, width, height, and thickness) across different sexes, ages, and ethnic populations [2,7-9]. Even though surgical techniques rely on accurate measurements of coracoid process dimensions, published studies vary in their measurement methodologies, including differences in anatomical landmarks, imaging planes, and dimensional definitions, limiting comparisons across studies and translation into clinical practice [7,10].

This systematic review synthesises CT-based evidence on coracoid process morphometry, examines differences in measurement methodologies, and provides pooled estimates of coracoid dimensions across demographic subgroups. We hypothesise that pooled data from Asian populations will demonstrate coracoid dimensions that approach or fall within previously proposed thresholds associated with an increased risk of intraoperative graft fracture, highlighting the importance of standardised measurement protocols and population-specific surgical planning.

METHODS

This systematic review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11].

Search Strategy

A search was performed in the following databases from their respective inceptions to 29 January 2026: Embase, Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library. We used the following search string: (coracoid process) AND (morphology OR morphologic OR morphometry OR morphometric OR anatomy OR anatomical OR anthropometry OR anthropometric OR length OR height OR width) AND (computed tomography or CT). A hand search of bibliographies from selected articles was also performed. Search results were imported into the Covidence online software tool (Veritas Health Innovation), and duplicates were automatically removed.

Study Selection

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the search results, followed by the full texts. Disagreements were resolved by a discussion between reviewers. The exclusion criteria were: (1) animal studies, finite element studies, technical reports, thesis papers, systematic reviews, letters to the editor, conference abstracts, and book chapters; (2) articles not published in English or translated into English; (3) studies that did not report the morphometry of the coracoid process measured using CT imaging; (4) studies that reported on CT imaging of coracoid fractures; and (5) studies not carried out on an Asian population. For this review, Asian populations were defined as those originating from East Asia, Southeast Asia, South Asia and the Pacific Islands [12]. During full-text screening for this systematic review, only full-length manuscripts reporting the morphometry of the coracoid process on CT images from live patients or cadaveric specimens from an Asian population were included. Randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, and case reports/series were included. When multiple studies reported data from overlapping patient populations, we included only the most recent study to prevent double-counting subjects.

Data Extraction

The following outcomes were extracted into Microsoft Excel spreadsheets: authors, year of study, origin of study, study design, sample size, sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and dimensions of the coracoid process measured on CT. Data presented as median (interquartile range) was converted to mean±standard deviation (SD) according to approximation methods previously established in the literature [13,14]. Pooled means and SDs were calculated from study-level sample sizes, means, and SDs using a combined-group variance approach. For morphometric parameters, the coracoid process was used as the unit of analysis, whereas demographic variables such as age were pooled using the number of patients or specimens. For the pooled analyses, only in vivo CT-derived measurements were included. Studies evaluating cadaveric specimens were retained within the descriptive synthesis but excluded from the pooled estimates. The number of contributing studies and coracoid processes was recorded. For consistency, similarly described anatomical measurements were grouped for the pooled analysis. Superior–inferior coracoid dimensions described as either “height” or “thickness” are collectively referred to as “thickness” throughout the text, but the original study terminology is preserved in the tables. Total coracoid length refers to the whole coracoid tip-to-base distance. Parameters that are relevant to coracoid transfer procedures include the coracoid thickness, the distance from the tip to the flexion point, and the tip to the coracoclavicular ligament insertion. Other parameters were summarized and pooled only when anatomical landmarks were clearly defined and considered equivalent. Due to heterogeneity in study populations and variability in measurement definitions, no formal meta-analysis was performed.

Quality Assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the methodologic quality of the included studies using the Anatomical Quality Assurance (AQUA) checklist, which emphasizes explicit descriptions of techniques, instruments, and measurement protocols to ensure replicability [15]. Disagreements were resolved by a discussion between the reviewers.

RESULTS

Literature Search Results

Our search strategy retrieved 689 studies from Embase (n=308), Scopus (n=169), PubMed (n=132), Web of Science (n=79), and the Cochrane Library (n=1). After 309 duplicates were removed, 380 articles underwent title and abstract screening, after which a further 341 studies were excluded. Of the 39 that underwent full-text screening, 14 studies met the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1).

Study Characteristics

In total, 14 studies were included [7,10,16-27], comprising 1,162 live patients and 66 cadaveric specimens. The studies reported measurements from 1,581 coracoid processes (bilateral shoulders were included where available). CT scans were conducted on live patients in 13 studies [7,10,16-22,24-27], and one study conducted CT scans on cadaveric specimens [23]. For the mixed study by Joshi et al. [27], only the CT-derived measurements from 108 shoulders were included in the pooled analyses, with the cadaveric measurements obtained using Vernier calipers excluded. Geographically, five studies were East Asian [10,20-23], five were Southeast Asian [7,17,19,24,26], and four were South Asian [16,18,25,27]. The study characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Characteristics of the Included Subjects

Age was reported in nine of the 13 in vivo studies [16-18,20-22,24-26], representing 875 live patients. The pooled mean age was 46.38±14.62 years. Sex was reported in 12 of the 14 studies [7,10,16-24,26], although the unit of reporting varied between patients and coracoid processes across studies. In particular, Kawamata et al. [22] presented sex distribution according to coracoid processes rather than individual patients.

Coracoid Morphometric Parameters

The anatomical definitions and measurement techniques used to quantify coracoid dimensions varied across studies. For example, most studies referred to “length” as the tip-to-base length [7,18,20,21,23-26], but one study referred to “length” as the distance from the coracoid tip to the flexion point [17]. Due to variability in those definitions, only parameters that were clearly defined with comparable measurements and reported in at least two studies were pooled. The definitions of parameters and their respective results are summarized in Table 2.

Pooled estimates for commonly reported coracoid dimensions were calculated using sample-size weighted means and SDs. The pooled estimates are summarized in Table 3. The total coracoid tip-to-base length was reported in five studies [7,18,20,24,26], representing 438 coracoid processes. The pooled mean total coracoid length was 39.82±3.17 mm. Kim et al. [23] reported a substantially shorter tip-to-base length (19.2±2.0 mm). Based on the magnitude of the reported values and comparison with other studies included in this review, this measurement likely corresponds to the distal horizontal segment of the coracoid, rather than the entire coracoid process. Because this definition was not anatomically equivalent to the whole-coracoid tip-to-base length reported in other studies, it was excluded from the pooled analysis. Ji et al. [21] defined length as the straight-line distance from the superior pillar tip to the inferior-pillar/scapular-notch junc­tion, and this measurement was thus also excluded.

The tip to flexion point distance was reported in three studies [16,21,26], representing 305 coracoid processes. The pooled mean distal coracoid segment length was 23.31±3.50 mm. The tip to coracoclavicular ligament distance was reported in two studies [21,27], representing 250 coracoid processes. The pooled mean osteotomy safety distance was 22.66±2.99 mm. Three studies were excluded from the pooled analysis due to non-equivalent anatomical constructs [10,17,22]. Fathi et al. [17] measured length from the tip to the “end of the horizontal part,” yielding a mean value of 38.29±3.17 mm, consistent with the near-total horizontal segment length, rather than the osteotomy-relevant distal segment. Kawamata et al. [22] reported a “distal length” of 10.20 mm, representing only a short distal portion of the coracoid. Tan et al. measured the “length of the horizontal portion,” which extends beyond the flexion point landmark [10]. Because those definitions did not correspond to the predefined harvestable graft construct, they are analyzed narratively and were excluded from the pooled estimates to preserve anatomical concordance.

The thickness halfway between the coracoid tip and base was recorded in three studies [18,24,26], representing 324 coracoid processes. The pooled mean thickness halfway between the coracoid tip and base was 11.28±1.39 mm. The thickness 1 cm from the coracoid tip was reported in three in vivo studies [10,17,18], representing 288 coracoid processes. The pooled mean thickness 1 cm from the coracoid tip was 10.03 ± 0.95 mm. The thickness halfway between the coracoid tip and flexion point was recorded in two studies [16,21], representing 265 coracoid processes. The pooled mean thickness halfway between the coracoid tip and flexion point was 11.18±1.89 mm.

The length halfway between the coracoid tip and base was reported in two studies [18,24], representing 284 coracoid processes. The pooled mean length halfway between the coracoid tip and base was 19.68±1.19 mm. The width halfway between the coracoid tip and base was recorded in three studies [18,24,26], representing 324 coracoid processes. The pooled mean width halfway between the coracoid tip and base was 13.50±1.26 mm. The width halfway between the coracoid tip and flexion point was recorded in three studies [10,16,21], representing 277 coracoid processes. The pooled mean width halfway between the coracoid tip and flexion point was 13.03±2.02 mm.

Sex Differences

Among the studies included in this systematic review, eight reported their results according to sex [7,17-21,24,26]. The results of those studies are summarized in Table 4. Pooled estimates divided by sex are summarized in Table 5. The total coracoid length from tip to base was reported by sex in five studies [7,18,20,24,26], representing 239 male coracoid processes and 199 female coracoid processes. The pooled mean total coracoid length was 41.13±2.90 mm in males and 38.79±3.95 mm in females. The tip to flexion point distance was reported by sex in two studies [21,26], representing 80 male coracoid processes and 102 female coracoid processes. The pooled mean tip to flexion point distance was 22.1±2.46 mm in males and 19.98±1.91 mm in females. Both studies reporting the length halfway between the coracoid tip and base reported the results by sex [18,24], representing 152 male coracoid processes and 132 female coracoid processes. The pooled mean length halfway between the coracoid tip and base was 20.19±0.85 mm in males and 19.08±1.25 mm in females. All three studies reporting the width halfway between the coracoid tip and base reported the results by sex [18,24,26], representing 172 male coracoid processes and 152 female coracoid processes. The pooled mean width halfway between the coracoid tip and base was 13.88±1.21 mm in males and 13.06±1.17 mm in females. All three studies reporting the thickness halfway between the coracoid tip and base reported the results by sex [18,24,26], representing 172 male coracoid processes and 152 female coracoid processes. The pooled mean thickness halfway between the coracoid tip and base was 11.90±1.32 mm in males and 10.58±1.08 mm in females. The thickness 1 cm from the coracoid tip was reported by sex in two studies [17,18], representing 158 male coracoid processes and 118 female coracoid processes. The pooled mean thickness 1 cm from the coracoid tip was 10.43±0.80 mm in males and 9.52±0.83 mm in females. These female-specific estimates should be interpreted with caution because the pooled distal coracoid segment length and thickness 1 cm from the coracoid tip were each derived from only two studies and represent population-level mean values.

Ethnic Differences

Only Fathi et al. [17] performed a formal comparative analysis of coracoid dimensions across multiple Chinese and Malay ethnic groups, reporting that Chinese subjects had larger coracoid process lengths (38.75±3.32 mm vs. 37.36±2.68 mm, P=0.09), thicker coracoid process tips (10.13±1.39 mm vs. 9.45±1.37 mm, P=0.06), and higher coracoid process base thicknesses (14.90±1.52 mm vs. 13.77±1.87 mm, P=0.01) [17]. Although Imma et al. [7] included Chinese, Malay and Indian subjects, they did not carry out a formal analysis of results among ethnicities.

Age Differences

Only Gulia et al. [18] explicitly analyzed coracoid measurements by age groups and did not identify any significant correlation between age and any of the coracoid parameters that they measured.

Reliability of Measurement Protocols

Interobserver reliability statistics were reported in three studies [16,21,26] and ranged from fair to excellent. Only one study reported intraobserver reliability statistics and reported that it was excellent [21]. The reported reliability statistics are summarized in Table 6.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the 14 included studies was assessed using the AQUA tool across five domains (Fig. 2). The results of this assessment can be found in the Supplementary Material 1. The quality assessment identified methodological concerns across the included studies, with a substantial proportion of assessments rated as unclear, particularly due to insufficient reporting of methods and inter- and intra-observer reliability, which limited assessment of reproducibility.

DISCUSSION

During a Latarjet procedure to treat bone defects seen in anterior shoulder instability, the coracoid process is transferred and typically fixed to the glenoid using screws [28]. This bone graft is intended to restore joint curvature and concavity, reduce contact pressure, and achieve joint stability to prevent recurrent dislocations [29]. This graft needs to be large enough to both restore stability and allow for sufficient distance between screws to prevent iatrogenic fractures [29]. The size of the coracoid thus directly influences both the amount of bone available for grafting and the space available to accommodate two screws (3.5 mm or 4.0 mm) spaced 10 mm apart [18]. Coracoid thickness is especially important because thin coracoids have less bone around the screw tunnels and are prone to fracture during drilling or tightening [5]. Although no high-level evidence supports an accepted threshold for coracoid dimensions that predict intraoperative graft fracture, Lamplot et al. [5] and Young et al. [6] suggested that, for congruent-arc Latarjet, coracoids that are <10 mm thick or offer usable grafts <20–22 mm are at a high risk of intraoperative fracture. Previous studies have also estimated that the safety margin for coracoid process harvesting ranges from 26.4 mm to 28.5 mm [30,31]. However, those thresholds were derived from American and Brazilian populations [30,31], and given that Asian coracoid processes are generally shorter than those of Westerners, achieving the same safety margins during graft harvesting for Asians, particularly females, might be unrealistic [23]. Furthermore, those standards apply to coracoid fixation done with screws. There is insufficient evidence to determine thresholds for suture fixations because the bone tunnels are much smaller than with screws.

This systematic review synthesizes CT-based studies that reported the dimensions of the coracoid process in Asian populations. Eight studies reported results according to sex [7,17-21,24,26], consistently demonstrating larger dimensions in males than females. However, only one study analyzed coracoid measurements by age group [18], and only one study analyzed differences between ethnic groups (Chinese and Malay) [17]. Our pooled analysis shows that the mean tip to flexion point distance was 19.98±1.91 mm in 102 females, the mean thickness halfway between the coracoid tip and base was 10.58±1.08 mm in 152 females, and the mean thickness 1 cm from the tip was 9.52±0.83 mm in 118 females. Although unclear measurement methods precluded its inclusion in the pooled analysis, Imma et al. [7] reported a mean tip thickness of 8.61±0.56 mm among nine females. In non-Asians, du Plessis et al. [32] reported a mean tip thickness of 10±1.5 mm among 50 females, and Abdelrahman et al. [33] reported a mean thickness of 10.5±1.5 mm among 16 females. Rohman et al. [34] reported a mean harvestable graft length of 24±3.4 mm in 100 American shoulders. In general, the smaller coracoid process dimensions reported in several Asian populations, compared with Western cohorts, have been attributed to genetics, environmental influences, dietary habits, and lifestyle [20]. This review suggests that coracoid dimensions in Asian females appear closer to previously proposed “high-risk” thresholds for intraoperative graft fracture than those observed in Asian males, suggesting that heightened preoperative awareness and intraoperative vigilance might be warranted during graft preparation and fixation in this subgroup. Alternative grafting choices might need to be prepared when operating on Asian female patients. The three female-specific estimates for the mean tip-to-flexion point distance, mean thickness halfway between the coracoid tip and base, and mean thickness 1 cm from the tip were derived from two, three, and two studies, respectively, and represent pooled mean values. These estimates should thus be interpreted with caution. Consequently, a proportion of individual coracoids might fall below commonly cited graft length and thickness thresholds, even though the pooled means only approach those values.

This review also identified substantial heterogeneity in measurement definitions across studies, such that only a few parameters were defined with sufficient consistency to allow for a pooled analysis. Although studies used common labels such as “length,” “width,” “height,” or “thickness,” they often measured them using different anatomical landmarks and reference points that were non-interchangeable. Total coracoid length, defined as the tip-to-base length, was the most frequently reported parameter (8 studies [7,18,20,21,23-26]), followed by the length from the coracoid tip to the flexion point (3 studies [16,21,26]). Pooled analyses of parameters were conducted only if the definitions of individual parameters were concordant. This heterogeneity and lack of strict anatomical equivalence limited cross-study comparability and quantitative synthesis. This review highlights the potential value of a standardised technique for measuring coracoid process dimensions on a CT scan. Future studies should clearly define how parameters of interest are measured relative to anatomical landmarks. Adopting a uniform methodology has the potential to improve cross-study comparability and allow for more substantial pooled analyses.

Recognising the morphometric differences in the coracoid process across ethnicities and sexes, a safety margin or recommended fixation technique most appropriate for small coracoid processes, such as those in Asian populations, is a gap in the literature. Relatively new methods of graft fixation, such as suture buttons, which allow for smaller drill holes of 2.8 mm, have been demonstrated by Boileau et al. [35] to be safe and reliable alternatives to traditional screws when larger safety margins are needed. Systematic reviews consistently demonstrate that suture button fixation has functional scores and range of motion outcomes similar to screw fixation while also having significantly lower complication and reoperation rates [36,37]. Furthermore, the systematic review by Rossi et al. [38] reported a 0.8% risk of graft fracture in screw fixation and only a 0.5% risk of graft fracture in suture button fixation (P=0.62). Although suture buttons are recommended for patients with glenoid bone loss >15%–20%, an Instability Severity Index Score of 3–6, or failed prior Bankart repair and contact or overhead athletes with moderate bone loss [36], whether suture button fixation might be appropriate in cases with small coracoid dimensions or graft thickness remains a gap in the literature [36-38]. Future research should evaluate the safety and clinical outcomes of suture-button fixation in patients with small coracoids, such as Asian females, because the reduced diameter of the drill holes might improve the safety of graft preparation and fixation at a relatively small length and thickness.

Preoperative CT imaging and measurements of individual coracoid processes are vital to prevent intraoperative issues such as graft fracture or inadequate fixation [18]. Apart from CT imaging, plain X-rays have also been used effectively in preoperative imaging, demonstrating a significant correlation between the coracoid process length on a true anteroposterior view and the intraoperative bone block length [39]. MRI, particularly when done with bone sequences, has also been used effectively to identify anatomical landmarks such as the anterior edge of the trapezoid ligament, allowing an accurate prediction of the usable coracoid length that can be harvested during coracoid transfer procedures [2]. The length from the coracoid tip to the trapezoid ligament might not correlate with the bony length from the tip to the elbow, so preoperative imaging with imaging modalities that allow accurate prediction of the size of the harvested graft is important [2]. Such accurate prediction of the coracoid graft size is especially important for Asian females because their small coracoid dimensions might limit the feasibility of certain coracoid transfer procedures, potentially reducing the advantages of the dynamic sling effect of conjoined tendons (tendons of coracobrachialis and the short head of the biceps) and capsular reconstruction that repairs the coracoacromial ligament to the capsule [40].

This review is not without limitations. First, although only CT-based studies were included, methodological heterogeneity among studies was still substantial, with definitions varying between studies and some studies relying only on figure-based definitions or implied definitions, which limited the number of parameters suitable for a pooled analysis. Defining the dimensions of the coracoid process is challenging because of its tortuous shape, making it difficult to identify bony landmarks on CT images without expert manipulation [16]. Furthermore, the included studies used both standard two-dimensional CT measurements and three-dimensional CT reconstruction techniques. Differences in image acquisition, reconstruction, and measurement protocols might have contributed additional heterogeneity to the pooled estimates. Second, most of the included studies were cross-sectional and descriptive, so no direct correlations between coracoid dimensions and outcomes such as intraoperative fractures were available for interpretation. Third, although ethnic differences were suggested by several of the included studies, only one performed a formal analysis between ethnic groups. Asians are a heterogeneous population; thus, studies originating in Asia also need to further analyse differences in data between ethnic groups within a geographical population.

CONCLUSIONS

CT-based studies of the coracoid process vary in anatomical definitions, limiting comparison among studies. Standardising measurement protocols with clearly defined anatomical landmarks is key to improving reproducibility and clinical applicability. Several pooled coracoid dimensions in Asian females approached previously reported fracture-risk thresholds. However, those findings should be interpreted cautiously because they were derived from a small number of studies. Well-designed studies using standardised morphometric definitions and measurement protocols are needed to further evaluate this finding and establish population-specific safety margins for coracoid transfer procedures, particularly in Asian females.
