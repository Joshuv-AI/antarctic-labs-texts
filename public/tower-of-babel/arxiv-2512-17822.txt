Fluid-inertia torques from particle-shape symmetry
L. Sundberg, F. Candelier, N. Fintzi, G. Voth, J. L. Pierson, K. Gustavsson, B. Mehlig

Abstract
Numerical simulation of particle motion in fluids at low particle Reynolds numbers is often based on empirical force and torque models obtained by fitting force and torque from ab-initio computations for simple particle shapes such as spheres, spheroids, or cylindrical disks and fibres. To do the same for more complex particles shapes, one needs to first know how particle shape constrains the dependence of force and torque on flow velocity, its gradient, and on particle orientation. Here we use symmetry analysis and perturbation theory to determine the form of the hydrodynamic torque on a particle settling in a quiescent fluid at low but non-zero particle Reynolds numbers, for particle shapes with different point-group symmetries. The symmetry conclusions are verified by comparing with explicit calculations for nearly spherical particles.



I
Introduction

Consider a small particle moving in a fluid. How does particle shape constrain the hydrodynamic force and torque experienced by the particle? This is an important question, because
numerical studies of particle motion in complex flows tend to rely on models for force and torque, which allow for efficient computer simulation. A common way to derive such models is to use parameterisations of force and torque obtained by ab-initio simulations
Sanjeevi et al. 2018
;
Ouchene et al. 2015
;
Ouchene et al. 2016a
;
Ouchene 2020
;
Hölzer and Sommerfeld 2009
;
Zastawny et al. 2012
;
Fröhlich et al. 2020
, requiring an
ansatz
for how force and torque depend on the flow velocity and its gradients. Any such ansatz, in turn, depends on particle shape.
In Stokes flow, it is known how particle-shape symmetries determine force and torque on small particles in a viscous fluid
Bretherton 1962
;
Brenner 1964a
;
Happel and Brenner 1983
;
Fries et al. 2017
;
Witten and Diamant 2020
;
Collins et al. 2021
;
Miara et al. 2024
;
Huseby et al. 2025
.
In many applications, however, the particles are too large or move too rapidly for the Stokes approximation to apply,
because it neglects corrections to hydrodynamic force and torque that arise at non-zero particle Reynolds number

{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
.
Examples include ice crystals in cold clouds
Heymsfield 1973
;

;

Atmospheric particles are frequently modelled using highly symmetric shapes

Bhowmick et al. 2024
,
but often atmospheric particles are asymmetric, meaning that certain rotation and reflection symmetries are broken

;
Cai et al. 2020
.

At small but non-zero particle Reynolds number
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
, hydrodynamic forces and torques on axisymmetric particles (such as straight fibres and flat disks) settling in a quiescent fluid have been calculated in perturbation theory

Brenner 1961
;
Khayat and Cox 1989
;

,

Brenner and Cox 1963

derived general expressions for the steady force on

of tensors and pseudo-tensors of ranks two and three. They also considered how shape symmetries (reflections and
\pi/2

Here we analyse how particle-shape symmetries constrain the general form of the non-zero contributions to the hydrodynamic torque on a particle with arbitrary shape settling in a quiescent fluid, and determine in particular how many parameters are needed to fully parameterise the torque to order
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
(the general conclusion is: the more shape symmetries are broken, the more parameters are needed).
We verify our symmetry considerations by comparing with explicit calculations for nearly spherical particles

Collis et al. 2024

II
Background

Consider a particle moving with velocity
v
and
angular velocity
\omega
in a quiescent fluid (Figure
1
). Hydrodynamic force and torque upon a particle in a fluid are determined by integrals of components of the stress tensor over
the particle surface
\mathscr{S}
:

F

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathscr{S}}\!\!\bbsigma\cdot{\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$s$}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$T$}}=\int_{\mathscr{S}}\!\!\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\wedge(\bbsigma\cdot{\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$s$})\,.

(1)

Here
r
is the vector from the chosen reference point to a point on the particle surface (we take the centre-of-mass as the reference).
The elements of the stress tensor
\bbsigma
are given by
\sigma_{mn}=-p\delta_{mn}+2\mu S_{mn}
, where
\mu
denotes the viscosity, and
S_{mn}
are the elements of the strain-rate matrix, the symmetric part of the matrix of disturbance-velocity gradients
\partial w_{i}/\partial x_{j}
. In the frame translating with the particle, the disturbance flow
w
satisfies the Navier-Stokes equations:

{{\rm Re}_{p}\,{\rm Sl}\,\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}{\partial t}}-{\rm Re}_{p}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}+{\rm Re}_{\rm{p}}\>\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=-\bm{\nabla}p+\bm{\triangle}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\,,\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=0\,.

(2)

We impose no-slip boundary conditions on the particle surface,
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=\mbox{\boldmath$v$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\wedge\mbox{\boldmath$r$}
, and require that
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}\to\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
as
|\mbox{\boldmath$r$}|\to\infty
.
Equation (
2
) was non-dimensionalised using an estimate
v_{0}
of the particle velocity, the particle size
a
, the time scale
\tau_{0}
at which the particle boundary conditions change, the angular-velocity scale
\omega_{0}=v_{0}/a
, and the pressure scale
\mu v_{0}/a
. Force and torque are non-dimensionalised as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$F$}/(\mu av_{0})\to\mbox{\boldmath$F$}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{\boldmath$T$}/(\mu a^{2}v_{0})\to\mbox{\boldmath$T$}\,.

(3)

From here on, we quote all expressions in non-dimensional variables.
The non-dimensional numbers in Eq.(
2
) are the particle Reynolds number
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
and the Strouhal number Sl (a non-dimensional measure
of the time scale at which the particle boundary conditions change):

\displaystyle{\rm Re}_{\rm p}=av_{0}/\nu\,,\quad{\rm Sl}=a/(v_{0}\tau_{0})\,.

(4)

For
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}=0
, the left-hand side of Eq. (
2
) vanishes, and one obtains the steady Stokes approximation to the disturbance flow. Integrating the resulting
\bbsigma^{(0)}
over the particle surface as specified in Eqs. (
1
) yields Stokes force
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(0)}
and torque
\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(0)}
. Both are linearly related to the particle velocity
v
and its angular velocity
\omega
. In a quiescent fluid, this relation is written as
Kim and Karrila 1991

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(0)}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(0)}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{A}&{\mathbb{B}}^{\sf T}\\
\mathbb{B}&\mathbb{C}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(5)

Here
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B},
and
\mathbb{C}
are Stokes resistance tensors which depend on particle orientation, on particle shape, but not on particle size
a
. The superscript
\sf T
denotes the transpose.

Happel and Brenner 1983
analysed how the resistance tensors in Eq. (
5
) depend on particle shape (for corresponding results in shear flow, see Refs.
Bretherton 1962
;
Brenner 1964b
;
Fries et al. 2017
;
Ishimoto 2020
).
Using the fact that the energy dissipation is a scalar, they determined how the Stokes resistance tensors transform under orthogonal coordinate transformations, and concluded the following: if the boundary conditions imposed by the particle upon the flow are invariant
under an orthogonal coordinate transformation
\mathbb{O}
, this constrains
the elements of the Stokes tensors as follows
Happel and Brenner 1983
:

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle=O_{mi}A_{mn}O_{nj}\,,

(6a)

\displaystyle B_{ij}

\displaystyle=\det[\mathbb{O}]O_{mi}B_{mn}O_{nj}\,,

(6b)

\displaystyle C_{ij}

\displaystyle=O_{mi}C_{mn}O_{nj}\,.

(6c)

Alternatively, these transformation rules can be derived from the covariance of Eq. (
5
). Transform
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath$v$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$T$}(\mbox{\boldmath$v$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
as
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{\prime}=\mathbb{O}\mbox{\boldmath$F$}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{\prime}=\det[\mathbb{O}]\mathbb{O}\mbox{\boldmath$T$}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{\prime}=\mbox{\boldmath$F$}(\mathbb{O}\mbox{\boldmath$v$},\det[O]\mathbb{O}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{\prime}=\mbox{\boldmath$T$}(\mathbb{O}\mbox{\boldmath$v$},\det[O]\mathbb{O}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
, and
\mathbb{O}
is a particle symmetry
Fries et al. 2017
.
Eq. (
6b
) shows that the translation-rotation coupling tensor
\mathbb{B}
transforms in a different way from
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{C}
.
The factor
\det[\mathbb{O}]
ensures that
\mathbb{B}=0
for particles with sufficient numbers of reflection symmetries (spheroid, ellipsoid).

For non-zero particle Reynolds number
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
, the left side of Eq. (
2
) matters. To first order in
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
, general expressions for the steady hydrodynamic force and torque (
{\rm Sl}=0
) were derived in Refs.
Brenner 1961
;
Brenner and Cox 1963
;
Cox 1965
.

Lovalenti and Brady 1993
derived
the first-order force corrections in
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
for unsteady flow. Here
we obtain the corresponding expressions
for the unsteady torque,
for particles of arbitrary shapes. In the steady limit, our results simplify to those in Refs.
Brenner 1961
;
Brenner and Cox 1963
;
Cox 1965
.

To compute the effects of weak fluid inertia upon hydrodynamic
force and torque, one must account for the fact that the perturbation
problem in
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
is singular, due to the slow spatial decay of the disturbance flow
Childress 1964
;
Saffman 1965
. We use matched asymptotic expansions
Childress 1964
;
Saffman 1965

to deal with this problem.
In this approach, an inner solution valid near the particle is matched to an outer solution valid far from the particle. In the following, it is assumed that
1\gg{\rm Re}_{\rm p}\gg{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}
.
Far from the particle, its boundary condition is replaced by a point-source forcing, so that Eq. (
2
) becomes

{\rm Re}_{p}\,{\rm Sl}\,\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize out}}}{\partial t}-{\rm Re}_{p}\,\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize out}}+{\rm Re}_{p}\,\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize out}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize out}}=-\bm{\nabla}p_{\rm out}+\bm{\triangle}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize out}}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}\,\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$r$})\>.

(7)

Here
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(0)}
denotes the force exerted by the particle on the fluid in the Stokes limit.
This outer problem
can be solved by Fourier transform.
The outer solution in Fourier space can be expanded as a series in the small parameter
\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{p}\,{\rm Sl}}
, keeping in mind that this expansion must be carried out in the sense of distributions
(
Meibohm et al. 2016
;
Redaelli et al. 2023
)
.
In configuration space, the corresponding series expansion takes the form

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize out}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\frac{\mathbb{1}}{r}+\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{r^{3}}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}-{\rm Re}_{\rm{p}}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\left[\frac{3r}{32\pi}\left(\mathbb{1}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{r^{2}}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}\right]+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\,\,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}\,.

(8)

The term
\mathcal{U}
corresponds to a uniform flow and originates from a singular contribution to the expansion of the outer solution in Fourier space, namely, a term proportional to the Dirac distribution
\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})
.
This uniform contribution takes the form

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}=\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\int_{0}^{t}\!{\rm d}\tau\;{\mathbb{K}}^{(1)}(t,\tau)\cdot\frac{{\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}(\tau)}{{\rm d}\tau}+{\rm Re}_{\rm p}\int_{0}^{t}\!{\rm d}\tau\;{\mathbb{K}}^{(2)}(t,\tau)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}(\tau)\,.

(9)

The kernels
{\mathbb{K}}^{(1)}(t,\tau)
and
{\mathbb{K}}^{(2)}(t,\tau)
are given in Appendix
A
. They are equivalent to the kernel involved in the
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
-correction to the force in unsteady flow, derived by
Lovalenti and Brady 1993
. The kernels also appear
in the work of
Redaelli et al. 2023
, except that there the expressions were simplified using the fact that the force
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}
and the velocity
v
are collinear for a spherical particle.

In the inner region, disturbance velocity and pressure are expanded as

{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}={\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(0)}+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\;{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}+\ldots,\qquad p_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}=p_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(0)}+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\;p_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}+\ldots\,.

(10)

These inner fields are then matched order by order with their counterparts in the outer solution. The leading-order term
{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(0)}
is the solution of the steady Stokes problem. The term
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}\,\,\partial_{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}^{(0)}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}
can be neglected here because it is of higher order in the regular inner expansion.
This leading-order solution matches the first term of the outer expansion (
8
).
The next correction
{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}

satisfies the inhomogeneous Stokes problem
Redaelli et al. 2023

\sqrt{\frac{{\rm Re}_{p}}{{\rm Sl}}}\left[-\,\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(0)}+\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(0)}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(0)}\right]=-\bm{\nabla}p^{(1)}+\bm{\triangle}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}\>.

(11)

The function
{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}
satisfies no-slip condition on the particle surface (i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
) and matches the last two terms of the outer expansion (
8
).
The solution of the inhomogeneous problem Eq. (
11
) is sought in the form

\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}=(\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\rm p}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$})+\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{h}
,
that is as the sum of a particular (forced) solution
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\rm p}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}
, and a homogeneous solution
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{h}
. Here,
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\rm p}
satisfies (
11
), while
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{h}
satisfies the Stokes equations together with the boundary condition

\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{h}=-(\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\rm p}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$})
on the particle surface,
ensuring that
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}
fulfills the no-slip condition. The particular solution
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\rm p}
depends quadratically on
v
and
\omega
, due to the quadratic convective term in (
11
). Similarly, the component of
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{h}
linked to
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{\rm p}
inherits this quadratic dependence because of the boundary condition imposed on the homogeneous part. Consequently, the total force and torque at order
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
are

\mbox{\boldmath$F$}=\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(0)}+{\rm Re}_{\rm p}\,\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(1)}_{\rm reg}+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(1)}_{\rm sing}\,,\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$T$}=\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(0)}+{\rm Re}_{\rm p}\,\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(1)}_{\rm reg}+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(1)}_{\rm sing}\,,

(12)

where the so-called ’singular’ contributions come from
the singular part of the outer expansion,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(1)}_{\rm sing}=\mathbb{A}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(1)}_{\rm sing}=\mathbb{B}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}\,,

(13)

and the ’regular’ contributions take the form

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(1)}_{\rm reg}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}^{(vv)}:{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}+\mathbb{M}^{(\omega\omega)}:{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}+\mathbb{M}^{(v\omega)}:{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}},

(14a)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(1)}_{\rm reg}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}:{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}+\mathbb{N}^{(\omega\omega)}:{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}+\mathbb{N}^{(v\omega)}:{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}.

(14b)

Here the
colon denotes double contraction
(the elements of
\mathbb{M}:\mathbb{S}
with the second
order tensor
\mathbb{S}
are
\sum_{jk}M_{ijk}S_{jk}
).
We remark that terms of the form

\mathbb{M}^{(\omega v)}:\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$v$}

and

\mathbb{N}^{(\omega v)}:\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$v$}
can be omitted since they have the same structure and transformation rules as
\mathbb{M}^{(v\omega)}:\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}

and
\mathbb{N}^{(v\omega)}:\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
.
Because the contributions (
14
) are determined by the inner solution, they are valid instantaneously, also for non-zero angular velocities. Such quadratic terms were obtained in Refs.
Brenner 1961
;
Brenner and Cox 1963
;
Cox 1965
using a reformulation of the reciprocal theorem.

In the steady limit, Eq. (
9
) simplifies.
In order to obtain a steady disturbance flow,

v
,
\omega
, and

must be independent of time (or at least vary very slowly).
Equation (
5
) relates
these three vectors:

\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(0)}=\mathbb{A}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}+\mathbb{B}^{\sf T}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
.
Accounting for a possible time dependence of
\mathbb{A}
, one finds:

\frac{{\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}}{{\rm d}t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\wedge\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}-\mathbb{A}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\wedge\mbox{\boldmath$v$})+\mathbb{A}\cdot\frac{{\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}{{\rm d}t}+\mathbb{B}^{\sf T}\cdot\frac{{\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}{{\rm d}t}\>.

(15)

We conclude that it is not possible in general to simultaneously satisfy

\tfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}=\tfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=\tfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
, at least when
\mathbb{B}\neq\mathbb{0}
. An exception to
this rule is the following example: an axisymmetric pear-shaped particle that rotates around its symmetry axis
with constant angular velocity

, so that

,

, and

are collinear.

A second example is a particle moving
at constant velocity with zero angular velocity. In this case, Eq. (
15
)
allows for both
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}
and

to be constant. When
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}
varies slowly (so that its time scale of variation is much larger than

, or equivalently
{\rm Sl}\to 0
), Eq. (
9
) simplifies. The first integral on the right-hand side of Eq. (

) vanishes, and
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}
can be pulled out from the second integral. Upon performing the remaining integral, one obtains

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}=-\sqrt{\frac{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}}{{\rm Sl}}}\,\frac{3v}{32\pi}\left(\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{3}\,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\otimes\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}\,,

(16)

where
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}
denotes the unit vector in the direction of

,
and

is the identity matrix.
Since
\mathcal{U}
corresponds to a uniform flow at infinity,
the singular contributions simplify to

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(1)}_{\rm sing}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}}{{\rm Sl}}}\frac{3v}{32\pi}\mathbb{A}\cdot\big[\mathbb{A}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-\tfrac{1}{3}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\otimes{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}})\cdot\mathbb{A}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\big]\,,\,\quad\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(1)}_{{\rm sing}}=-\sqrt{\frac{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}}{{\rm Sl}}}\frac{3v}{32\pi}{\mathbb{B}}\cdot\big[\mathbb{A}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}-\tfrac{1}{3}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\otimes{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}})\cdot\mathbb{A}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\big]\,.

(17)

These contributions to the steady force and torque approximation were first obtained in Refs.
Brenner 1961
;
Brenner and Cox 1963
;
Cox 1965
using the reformulation of the reciprocal theorem mentioned above.

Cox 1965
includes terms with
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\neq 0
in his
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
-contributions for force and torque. As pointed out above, this is consistent in certain special cases, but not in general.

Asymptotic matching, employed here, offers additional insights. First, it gives a clear interpretation of where the different terms to the fluid-inertia forces and torques come from. The contributions (
17
) originate from the far field and may contain transient, time-dependent terms, while

14
) are instantaneous contributions deriving from the inner solution. Second, asymptotic matching allows to compute the transient, time-dependent terms (
9
), so-called history forces and torques that affect the transient dynamics of the particle. Third, the derivation of Eqs. (
17
) from the unsteady
\mathcal{U}
highlights that Eqs. (
17
) assume that the particle velocity is steady. Upon a change of centre of reference at non-zero
\omega
this assumption may fail. Therefore one needs to go back to (
9
) and (
13
) in order to see that the particle dynamics does not depend on the arbitrary choice of the centre-of-reference.

{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
. The tensors

and

)

). This in turn implies the same for
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(1)}_{\rm reg}
and

,

as follows:

\displaystyle N_{ijk}^{(vv)}

\displaystyle=\det[\mathbb{O}]\,O_{mi}N_{mnl}^{(vv)}O_{nj}O_{lk}\quad\,\mbox{and}\quad M_{ijk}^{(vv)}=O_{mi}M_{mnl}^{(vv)}O_{nj}O_{lk}\,,

(18a)

\displaystyle N_{ijk}^{(\omega\omega)}

\displaystyle=\det[\mathbb{O}]\,O_{mi}N_{mnl}^{(\omega\omega)}O_{nj}O_{lk}\quad\mbox{and}\quad M_{ijk}^{(\omega\omega)}=O_{mi}M_{mnl}^{(\omega\omega)}O_{nj}O_{lk}\,,

(18b)

\displaystyle N_{ijk}^{(v\omega)}

\displaystyle=O_{mi}N_{mnl}^{(v\omega)}O_{nj}O_{lk}\,,\hskip 22.76219pt\quad\mbox{and}\quad M_{ijk}^{(v\omega)}=\det[\mathbb{O}]\,O_{mi}M_{mnl}^{(v\omega)}O_{nj}O_{lk}\,.

(18c)

Note that the rank-3 tensors
\mathbb{M}
and
\mathbb{N}
arise in the first
order of a systematic expansion in
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
. So their elements are determined by Stokes solutions, but the tensors depend on particle shape in a more complex way than solely through the rank-two Stokes resistance tensors
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
, and
\mathbb{C}
. Equation (
18
) implies that

\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}
,
\mathbb{N}^{(\omega\omega)}
, and
\mathbb{M}^{(v\omega)}
transform as the Stokes tensor
\mathbb{H}
that
couples
\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(0)}
to the
strain
\mathbb{S}

Bretherton 1962
;
Brenner 1964b
;
Fries et al. 2017
;
Ishimoto 2020
.
Likewise,
\mathbb{M}^{(vv)}
,
\mathbb{M}^{(\omega\omega)}
, and
\mathbb{N}^{(v\omega)}
have the same transformation properties.

Brenner and Cox 1963

and
Cox 1965
determined
under which circumstances
the components of the vectors
\mathbb{M}^{(vv)}:\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$v$}
and
\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}:\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$v$}
(and the corresponding contributions for the other rank-3 tensors) vanish, by considering

\tfrac{\pi}{2}
- and
\pi
-rotations as well as reflections. In general, these contributions do not vanish. Here we use Eqs. (
6
), (
14
), (
17
), and (
18
) to derive constraints
on the non-zero components of the fluid-inertia torque for asymmetric particles, obtained from symmetric shapes by breaking rotation and/or reflection symmetries. This allows to understand similarities and differences in the dynamics of asymmetric particles settling in a quiescent fluid
Candelier and Mehlig 2016
;
Roy et al. 2019
;
Candelier et al. 2024
;
Miara et al. 2024
;
Flapper et al. 2025
.

III.1
Prismatic symmetry
D_{2h}

The cuboid shown in Figure
2
(
a
) has three reflection symmetries in the planes orthogonal to the three principal axes (point group symmetry
D_{2h}
).
The three orthogonal reflections constrain the three Stokes resistance tensors to be
\mathbb{A}={\rm diag}[A_{pp},A_{qq},A_{nn}]
,
\mathbb{C}={\rm diag}[C_{pp},C_{qq},C_{nn}]
, and

\mathbb{B}=0
. Further, the reflections constrain the inertial rank-3 tensors to
\mathbb{N}^{(v\omega)}=0
, and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{N}}_{p::}^{(vv)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&{N}_{pqn}^{(vv)}\\
0&{N}_{pnq}^{(vv)}&0\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad{\mathbb{N}}_{q::}^{(vv)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&{N}_{qpn}^{(vv)}\\
0&0&0\\
{N}_{qnp}^{(vv)}&0&0\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad{\mathbb{N}}_{n::}^{(vv)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&N_{npq}^{(vv)}&0\\
N_{nqp}^{(vv)}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\,.

(19)

It follows from the discussion in Section
II
that the tensors
\mathbb{N}^{(\omega\omega)}
and
\mathbb{M}^{(v\omega)}
have the same form as
\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}
, but with independent parameters. The tensors
\mathbb{M}^{(vv)}
and
\mathbb{M}^{(\omega\omega)}
vanish. The above constraints yield the following expressions for the
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
-corrections to the torque

\displaystyle T_{p}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=2N_{p(nq)}^{(vv)}v_{n}v_{q}+2N_{p(nq)}^{(\omega\omega)}\omega_{n}\omega_{q}\,,

(20a)

\displaystyle T_{q}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=2N_{q(pn)}^{(vv)}v_{p}v_{n}+2N_{q(pn)}^{(\omega\omega)}\omega_{p}\omega_{n}\,,

(20b)

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=2N_{n(pq)}^{(vv)}v_{p}v_{q}+2N_{n(pq)}^{(\omega\omega)}\omega_{p}\omega_{q}\,

(20c)

in the basis
\mbox{\boldmath$p$},\mbox{\boldmath$q$},\mbox{\boldmath$n$}
(Figure
2
).
Here,
N_{p(nq)}^{(vv)}=\tfrac{1}{2}(N_{pnq}^{(vv)}+N_{pqn}^{(vv)})
,…are symmetrised coupling-coefficients.
Since
\mathbb{B}=0
, the singular contributions to the inertial torque, Eq. (
17
), vanish. Thus, only the instantaneous terms from the inner solution contribute, meaning that Eqs. (
20
) are valid in the unsteady case, in particular for non-zero values of
\omega
.

III.2
Prismatic symmetry (
D_{nh}
for
n>2
)

Straight columns with a regular
n
-gon cross sections with
n>2
(
prismatic symmetry
D_{nh}
with
n>2
) possess discrete
n
-fold rotational symmetry (
C_{n}
) about the
n
-axis.
The case
n=4
is illustrated in Fig.
2
(
b

n=6
) cross sections
Nakaya 1954
;
Furukawa and Wettlaufer 2007
. Imposing
C_{n}
-rotation symmetry with
n>2

around
n
imposes additional constraints on the non-zero coefficients in Eq. (
20
) compared with
n=2
:

\displaystyle A_{pp}=A_{qq}=A_{\perp},\quad A_{nn}=A_{\parallel},\quad C_{pp}=C_{qq}=C_{\perp},\quad C_{nn}=C_{\parallel},

(21a)

\displaystyle N_{pqn}^{(vv)}=-N_{qpn}^{(vv)}=N_{1}^{(vv)}\,,N_{pnq}^{(vv)}=-N_{pnq}^{(vv)}=N_{2}^{(vv)}\,,N_{npq}^{(vv)}=-N_{nqp}^{(vv)}=N_{3}^{(vv)}\,.

(21b)

The elements of
\mathbb{N}^{(\omega\omega)}
are constrained in the same way. This leaves six undetermined parameters in the
\mathbb{N}
-tensors. The
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
-contributions to the torque simplify to:

\displaystyle T_{p}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=(N_{1}^{(vv)}+N_{2}^{(vv)})v_{q}v_{n}+(N_{1}^{(\omega\omega)}+N_{2}^{(\omega\omega)})\omega_{q}\omega_{n}\,,

(22a)

\displaystyle T_{q}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-(N_{1}^{(vv)}+N_{2}^{(vv)})v_{p}v_{n}-(N_{1}^{(\omega\omega)}+N_{2}^{(\omega\omega)})\omega_{p}\omega_{n}\,,

(22b)

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=0

(22c)

in the basis
\mbox{\boldmath$p$},\mbox{\boldmath$q$},\mbox{\boldmath$n$}
(Figure
2
). Similar to Eqs. (
20
), these expressions are valid in the unsteady case.
The rotational symmetry around
n
causes the torque
T_{n}
to vanish. This follows because
{\mathbb{N}}_{n::}^{(vv)}
and
{\mathbb{N}}_{n::}^{(\omega\omega)}
are anti-symmetric, and contracted with the symmetric
\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v}
and
\bm{\omega}\otimes\bm{\omega}
, respectively.
If
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=0
, the torque is orthogonal to
n
, and proportional to the velocity component
v_{n}
:
\mbox{\boldmath$T$}=(N_{1}^{(vv)}+N_{2}^{(vv)})(\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$n$})(\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\wedge\mbox{\boldmath$n$})
, consistent with Refs.
Khayat and Cox 1989
;
Dabade et al. 2015
;
Jiang et al. 2021
.

III.3
Full octahedral symmetry (
O_{h}
)

Now consider particles with
full octahedral symmetry

O_{h}
[(Figure
2
(
c
)]. An example is the cube, but also the
sphere with continuous rotation symmetry.
For both examples, rotation and reflection symmetries further constrain Eqs. (
21
). The Stokes tensors
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{C}
become proportional to the identity matrix, and the tensors
\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}
and
\mathbb{N}^{(\omega\omega)}
become proportional to the anti-symmetric Levi-Civita tensor

\epsilon
:
A_{\perp}=A_{\parallel}=A
,
C_{\perp}=C_{\parallel}=C
,
\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}=N^{(vv)}\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}
, and
\mathbb{N}^{(\omega\omega)}=N^{(\omega\omega)}\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}
.
The latter conditions cause the torque (
22
) to vanish.

As discussed in Section
II
,
\mathbb{M}^{(v\omega)}
is constrained in the same way as
\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}
, so
\mathbb{M}^{(v\omega)}=M^{(v\omega)}\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}
, yielding a contribution proportional to
\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\wedge\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
, the Rubinow-Keller lift force for a spherical particle (Eq. (71) in Ref.
Rubinow and Keller 1961
), and its generalisation to particles with
O_{h}
symmetry. See Appendix
B
for details.

Using that
\mathbb{A}=A\mathbb{I}
and that
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}
is a unit vector, the singular contribution to the force (
17
) is isotropic, proportional to
\tfrac{2}{3}A^{2}v^{2}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}

Brenner and Cox 1963
;
Cox 1965
. The theory to linear order in
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
is isotropic, but does not rule out the existence of anisotropic terms at higher orders in in the Reynolds number.
It is instructive to compare this conclusion with a more general symmetry argument that assumes only that force and torque to order
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
are third-order polynomials in the components of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}
with coefficients that are constrained by symmetry. Requiring invariance of force and torque under particle-shape symmetry transformations yields results consistent with the above conclusions, but they constrain force and torque less because the relation between force and torque encoded in Eq. (
13
) is not accounted for.

III.4
Orthorhombic pyramidal symmetry (
C_{2v}
)

Figure
2
(
d
) shows a particle that has the same symmetry as
D_{2h}
, but with broken reflection symmetry in the
n
-direction
(orthorhombic pyramidal symmetry
C_{2v}
). Examples of such particles are curved planar fibres with constant radius of curvature
Candelier et al. 2024
, symmetrically bent disks
Miara et al. 2024

(Fig.
1

Flapper et al. 2025

The tensors
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}
, and
\mathbb{N}^{(\omega\omega)}
of particles with
C_{2v}
symmetry have the
same form as for particles with
D_{2h}
symmetry (discussed above).
But the broken reflection symmetry results in a non-zero Stokes translation-rotation coupling:

\mathbb{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&B_{pq}&0\\
B_{qp}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\,,

(23)

as well as a non-zero
\mathbb{N}^{(v\omega)}
:

{\mathbb{N}}_{p::}^{(v\omega)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&{N}_{ppn}^{(v\omega)}\\
0&0&0\\
{N}_{pnp}^{(v\omega)}&0&0\end{bmatrix}\,\quad{\mathbb{N}}_{q::}^{(v\omega)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&{N}_{qqn}^{(v\omega)}\\
0&{N}_{qnq}^{(v\omega)}&0\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad{\mathbb{N}}_{n::}^{(v\omega)}=\begin{bmatrix}{N}_{npp}^{(v\omega)}&0&0\\
0&{N}_{nqq}^{(v\omega)}&0\\
0&0&{N}_{nnn}^{(v\omega)}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(24)

Now consider the corresponding
expression for the fluid-inertia correction to the hydrodynamic torque.
Since the general symmetry analysis
of the unsteady contribution
\mathcal{U}

is complicated, we only consider
the steady limit, which requires

\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=0
, as discussed in the previous Section. For the steady
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
-contributions to the torque we find:

\displaystyle T_{p}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{32\pi}vB_{pq}[3A_{qq}-(A_{pp}{\hat{v}_{p}}^{2}+A_{qq}{\hat{v}_{q}}^{2}+A_{nn}{\hat{v}_{n}}^{2})]v_{q}+2N_{p(nq)}^{(vv)}v_{n}v_{q}\,,

(25a)

\displaystyle T_{q}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{32\pi}vB_{qp}[3A_{pp}-(A_{pp}{\hat{v}_{p}}^{2}+A_{qq}{\hat{v}_{q}}^{2}+A_{nn}{\hat{v}_{n}}^{2})]v_{p}+2N_{q(np)}^{(vv)}v_{n}v_{p}\,,

(25b)

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=2N_{n(qp)}^{(vv)}v_{q}v_{p}

(25c)

in the basis
\mbox{\boldmath$p$},\mbox{\boldmath$q$},\mbox{\boldmath$n$}
(Figure
2
).
Comparing with Eq. (
20
) for the ellipsoid, we see that the form of the quadratic terms is unaffected by the reflection symmetry for
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=0
. Broken reflection symmetry still changes the fluid-inertia torque, due to the non-zero translation-rotation coupling
\mathbb{B}
in the Stokes limit, yielding a non-zero singular contribution from Eq. (

III.5
Pyramidal symmetry (

Particles with
n

Maches et al. 2024

(26a)

as well as

\displaystyle{N}_{ppn}^{(v\omega)}

\displaystyle={N}_{qqn}^{(v\omega)}=N_{1}^{(v\omega)},\quad{N}_{pnp}^{(v\omega)}={N}_{qnq}^{(v\omega)}=N_{2}^{(v\omega)},\quad{N}_{npp}^{(v\omega)}={N}_{nqq}^{(v\omega)}=N_{3}^{(v\omega)},\quad{N}_{nnn}^{(v\omega)}=N_{4}^{(v\omega)}\,,

\displaystyle N_{pqn}^{(vv)}

\displaystyle=-N_{qpn}^{(vv)}=N_{1}^{(vv)},\quad N_{pnq}^{(vv)}=-N_{pnq}^{(vv)}=N_{2}^{(vv)},\quad N_{npq}^{(vv)}=-N_{nqp}^{(vv)}=N_{3}^{(vv)}\,.

(26c)

The tensor

-contributions to the torque components are

\displaystyle T_{p}^{(1)}

\displaystyle T_{q}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{32\pi}vB[A_{\perp}(3-({\hat{v}_{p}}^{2}+{\hat{v}_{q}}^{2}))-A_{\parallel}{\hat{v}_{n}}^{2}]v_{p}-(N_{1}^{(vv)}+N_{2}^{(vv)})v_{p}v_{n}\,,

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(27c)

Comparing with Eq. (

constraining the coefficients

III.6

V
Discussion

The results summarised in Section
III
show how particle-shape symmetries constrain the hydrodynamic torque, including fluid-inertia contributions to order
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
, extending earlier work by Brenner and Cox on corresponding constraints on the hydrodynamic force
Brenner and Cox 1963
. They determined under which circumstances the contributions from the
\mathbb{M}
tensor to the hydrodynamic force vanish, by analysing how
reflections as well as
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
- and
\pi
-rotations constrain
\mathbb{M}^{(vv)}:\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$v$}
.
In a similar fashion,
Cox 1965
considered
under which conditions
the components
of the vectors
\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}:\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$v$}
,

\mathbb{N}^{(v\omega)}:\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
and
\mathbb{N}^{(\omega\omega)}:\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
to vanish, because in that case steady force and torque are solely determined by the Stokes resistance tensors
\mathbb{A}
(drag)
and
\mathbb{B}
(translation-rotation coupling).

For general shapes, these contributions do not vanish. In this case, the question is: how many parameters are needed to parameterise the dependence of the torque (and the force) upon particle velocity and angular velocity? Here we used symmetry analysis
to answer this question. We determined how particle-shape symmetry
constrains the non-zero elements of the coupling tensors. This determines the form of the torque, and in particular how many parameters are needed in parameterisations.
The general structure of the torque becomes more complex (involves more parameters to be determined)
as more shape symmetries are broken. In particular, any given shape symmetry implies that the resistance tensors
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{C}
commute with the generator of the corresponding symmetry transformation.
Full octahedral symmetry
O_{\rm h}
requires that
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
, and
\mathbb{C}
commute with all rotational generators. This implies that
these tensors are proportional to the unit matrix (isotropy). Now consider how rotations constrain the rank-3 tensors: the symmetries in the point-group
O_{\rm h}

require that
all rank-3 tensors are proportional to the Levi-Civita tensor
\epsilon
, the only antisymmetric rank-3 tensor.
Reflection symmetries constrain the rank-3
tensors further, namely
\mathbb{N}^{(v\omega)}

as well as
\mathbb{M}^{(vv)}
and
\mathbb{M}^{(\omega\omega)}
must vanish. Reflection symmetries also cause
\mathbb{B}
to vanish.
The last two statements apply to all particles in the top row of Fig.
2
.

For nearly spherical particles with different shape symmetries (Section
IV
), we found some cases where the symmetry analysis
from Section
III
allows a non-zero coupling, but the coupling nevertheless vanishes to a certain order in perturbation theory. For
C_{\infty v}
-symmetry for example,
the translation-rotation coupling
\mathbb{B}
vanishes to order
\varepsilon^{2}
, although allowed by symmetry. In general, one expects that all coupling allowed by symmetry are non-zero unless there are additional symmetries not considered. The elements of
\mathbb{B}
vanish to order
\varepsilon^{2}

because the parameterisation
r=1-\varepsilon(\sin^{2}\vartheta+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\cos\vartheta)
corresponds to a shifted spheroid. Expressing the torque w.r.t. the centre of mass cancels any potential contributions to the torque up to order
\varepsilon^{2}
.
This happens also for
C_{2v}
-symmetry. In the latter case, we also find that
N_{n(pq)}^{(vv)}
and
N_{n(pq)}^{(\omega\omega)}
evaluate to zero to order
\varepsilon
. We expect that higher orders in
\varepsilon
are non-zero.

Candelier et al. 2024
used the slender-body approximation to calculate the steady hydrodynamic force and torque on curved atmospheric fibres with
C_{2v}
symmetry, settling in quiescent air.
In slender-body theory
Khayat and Cox 1989
, force and torque are expressed as

F

\displaystyle=\int\!\!{\rm d}s\,\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(s)\,,\quad\mbox{\boldmath$T$}=\int\!\!{\rm d}s\,\mbox{\boldmath$r$}(s)\wedge\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(s)\,,

replacing Eqs. (

Here
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(s)

the force on a small segment of a slender body.
It is obtained by asymptotic matching, as an expansion in

The results of
Khayat and Cox 1989

It has been shown that the predictions of

i.e.

v_{0}a/\nu\leq 0.1

To order

the resulting hydrodynamic force and torque contain
at most third powers of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}

Let us compare the slender-body results for the torque on curved fibres with

Eq. (
25

Here, the reflection symmetry
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}\to-\mbox{\boldmath$n$}

\mbox{\boldmath$p$}\to-\mbox{\boldmath$p$}

However,
the slender-body approximation has fewer terms than allowed by the symmetry analysis,
similar to the nearly-spherical particles discussed above,
although different terms are zero in that case.
The allowed terms that vanish in the slender-body approximation are proportional to
B_{qn}A_{nn}
, while allowed terms
proportional to
B_{nq}A_{qq}
appear. This can be understood as follows. Computing the Stokes resistance tensors for planar fibres with constant radius of curvature using
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}(s)

from Ref.
Khayat and Cox 1989

A_{qq}
and
A_{nn}

are of order
1/\log\kappa
, and so is
B_{nq}

Cox 1970

B_{qn}

1/(\log\kappa)^{2}

1/(\log\kappa)^{3}

Khayat and Cox 1989

The above dependencies on
\kappa

are confirmed by
numerical results for

obtained by modeling the fibres as chains of hydrodynamically interacting, identical beads

\kappa=40

This shortcoming of slender-body theory stems from approximations for the inner solution, namely that hydrodynamic interactions
between different segments of the fibre are neglected (one has to go to higher orders in

1/(\log\kappa)
to account for these interactions

for the bead model

Candelier et al. 2024

\mathbb{B}=0
without hydrodynamic interactions in the centre-of-mass of the chain. This is no longer true if the beads have different sizes, or in the
slender-body approximation where the fibre segments have different orientations.

In slender-body theory, higher orders in
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}=av_{0}/\nu
result in higher powers of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}

It is likely that
higher-order singular contributions to force and torque are entirely determined by the Stokes tensors
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
, and
\mathbb{C}
. If this is the case, any other symmetry-breaking effects at larger particle Reynolds numbers must come from the inner solution.

At small but finite
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
, fluid inertia torques tend to align highly symmetric particles with the slip velocity. This is important in the atmospheric sciences, where the alignment of symmetric ice crystals (

(sphere).

Andersen et al. 2005
used an empirical torque model to describe fluttering and tumbling of platelets settling in a quiescent fluid at

of order

. For planar motion,

Analysis of ab-initio data for force and torque on a thin disk (aspect ratio

The laboratory experiments of

The previous paragraph discussed the steady-state problem, the orientation of a steadily settling particle. Now consider how the particle orientation changes as the steady state is approached. As explained in Section
II
, the transient is
affected by history forces and torques arising from inertial fluid acceleration (the
term
\partial_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}
in the unsteady Stokes equation.
In this limit, the history force is well understood for a spherical particle settling in a quiescent fluid. The history force arises from the outer solution and decays slowly, as
t^{-1/2}
, after a sudden change of the particle dynamics
Landau and Lifshitz 1987
. Fluid-inertia corrections at small
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
modify the power-law decay in a non-universal fashion
Lovalenti and Brady 1993
, but the decay remains slow at small Reynolds numbers. The history torque, by contrast, decays rapidly for a sphere. Its kernel is essentially a Dirac delta function
Candelier et al. 2023

{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
corrections do not change this fact. Eqs. (
9

different for particles
with non-zero translation–rotation coupling
\mathbb{B}

\mathbb{B}
results
in a slow decay of the history torque as well. This history torque is expected to affect the transient dynamics of asymmetric particles settling at non-zero Reynolds numbers
Candelier et al. 2024
.
For spheroids (
\mathbb{B}=0
) the relaxation of the orientational dynamics is well described
Bhowmick et al. 2024
, but as yet there is no reliable theory for the relaxation of particles with non-zero translation-rotation coupling
outside the Stokes limit
Candelier et al. 2024

Note that we computed the torque w.r.t. the centre-of-mass of the particle, and assumed a homogeneous mass-density distribution. An inhomogeneous distribution of the particle-mass density can shift the centre-of-mass, breaking further shape symmetries. Adjusting its centre-of-mass allows gliders settling in a quiescent fluid to navigate to land on a certain spot

We determined how shape symmetry constrains the hydrodynamical torque

VI
Conclusions

In this paper we used symmetry analysis to constrain the hydrodynamic torque on particles moving in a quiescent fluid at small but non-zero particle Reynolds number. For highly symmetrical particles, such as slender fibres with circular cross section and spheroids, fluid inertia torques were well understood
Brenner 1961
;
Khayat and Cox 1989
;
Dabade et al. 2015
, and known to describe how highly symmetric particles align as they settle in quiescent or weakly turbulent fluids
Bhowmick et al. 2024
.

The present study was motivated by recent interest in the angular dynamics of asymmetrical atmospheric particles
Candelier and Mehlig 2016
;
Roy et al. 2019
;
Ravichandran and Wettlaufer 2023
;
Maches et al. 2024
;
Candelier et al. 2024
, such as curved fibres, asymmetric ice crystals and ash particles. Here we show how breaking of shape symmetry allows new terms to appear in the hydrodynamic torque (and force), that can significantly change how particles align as they settle.
The analysis shows that the bent disks of
Miara et al. 2024
, planar curved
atmospheric fibres with constant radius of curvature
Candelier et al. 2024
, and the oloid
Flapper et al. 2025
obey the same equations of motion, albeit with different parameters. The form of the equation
of motion determines the possible dynamical
behaviours by constraining the bifurcations that may occur as the parameters are varied. For example, in the Stokes limit the transition from alignment at small
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}
(curved fibre) to oscillations (bent disk)
occurs when the product
B_{pq}B_{qp}
of the non-zero translation-rotation coupling coefficients in Eq. (
23
) changes sign
Candelier et al. 2024
. The results summarised here allow to determine how the bifurcations change for larger ribbons, as fluid-inertia corrections become important.

Regarding the hydrodynamic torque, our results generalise the theory of
Cox 1965
to include unsteady effects, leading to slowly decaying history torques for asymmetrical particles with translation-rotation coupling (for symmetrical particles, history torques decay rapidly). Such unsteady effects influence the transient angular dynamics. In order to understand the transient angular dynamics for asymmetrical, curved atmospheric fibres
Bhowmick et al. 2024
, we expect history torques to play a role. Similar conclusions are expected for bent disks
Miara et al. 2024
, which have the same point-group shape symmetry as planar fibres with constant radius of curvature.

Even in the absence of history torques, one expects complex settling trajectories even for symmetrical particles settling in quiescent air. For example, our theory shows that the transient dynamics
of ellipsoids is governed by three couplings (the six non-zero elements of the rank-3 tensor

\mathbb{N}^{(vv)}
which take different values depending on the aspect ratios of the ellipsoidal particle. This – together with the fact that all three components of the hydrodynamical torque are non-zero – explains the rich variety of settling behaviours observed in the experiment
Bhowmick et al. 2025
, but
a detailed computation of the transient dynamics remains a question for the future. Regarding the steady limit, our calculations show that nearly spherical ellipsoids settle with their broadest side down, as observed in the experiment
Bhowmick et al. 2025
. To confirm that this conclusion is valid for ellipsoidal particles with arbitrary aspect ratios, one needs to compute the three symmetrised coefficients in Eq. (
20
) for the torque by direct numerical simulation.

The results discussed here were obtained in the limit of small Reynolds numbers,
{\rm Re}_{\rm p}\ll 1
. An important question is how the particle dynamics changes as the Reynolds number increases. For spheroids in shear flow, bifurcations that occur at larger shear Reynolds numbers Re
s
have been mapped out, and it turns out that the small-Re theory remains qualitatively valid for Re
s
up to order unity. For spheroids settling in a quiescent fluid, one can obtain quantitative agreement with experiments using empirical torque models similar to those discussed here
Bhowmick et al. 2024
. For particles with broken shape symmetry
Candelier et al. 2024
, the
expressions derived here yield qualitative but not quantitative agreement with the experiments. How the accuracy of small-Re approximations breaks down for highly asymmetric particles as the Reynolds number increases remains an open question. It may be instructive to compare the results of the present analysis with the potential-flow limit, the limit considered by Lord Kelvin in his analysis of the isotropic helicoid
Kelvin 1871
.
Corresponding expressions for hydrodynamic force and torque can be obtained for particles of arbitrary shape using either the Lagrangian formalism
(
Lamb 1924
)
, or by direct integration of the pressure on the body
(
Howe 1995
)
. However, the results of
Candelier et al. 2023
indicate that even for a spherical particle, hydrodynamic force and torque can be quite different in the two limits. As a consequence it may be challenging to find convincing interpolation formulae between the two limits.

Last but not least, symmetry analysis as described here can be used to constrain hydrodynamical forces and torques on motile micro-organisms. The standard model assumes that the organisms are spherical, but this is an idealisation. In the future we will analyse how the interaction of microswimmers with the surrounding flow changes when shape symmetries are broken. To this end, one needs to generalise the present analysis to account for strain and vorticity of the undisturbed flow. For special cases this is known
Bretherton 1962
;
Brenner 1964b
;
Fries et al. 2017
;
Ishimoto 2020
, but not in general.

{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}={\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(0)}+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\>{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}+{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}\>{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(2)}+\ldots\quad\mbox{and}\quad{p}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}={p}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(0)}+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\>{p}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}+{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}\>{p}_{\mbox{\scriptsize in}}^{(1)}+\ldots\,.

(46)

To second order, the outer flow equations
require two source terms,
f^{(0)}
and
f^{(1)}
,

{{\rm Re}_{p}\,{\rm Sl}\,\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}{\partial t}}=-\bm{\nabla}p+\bm{\triangle}\mbox{\boldmath$w$}+\left(\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(1)}\right)\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$r$})\>.

(47)

The solution of this outer equation takes the form

\begin{split}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize out}}=&\frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\frac{\mathbb{1}}{r}+\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{r^{3}}\right)\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{\rm p}{\rm Sl}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(1)}\right)+\sqrt{{\rm Re}_{p}{\rm Sl}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{1}\\
&+{\rm Re}_{p}{\rm Sl}\,\left(\frac{3r}{32\pi}\left(\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{r^{2}}\right)\frac{{\rm d}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}}{{\rm d}t}+\,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{2}\right)\end{split}

(48)

where

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{1}=-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{6\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(t-\tau)}}\frac{{\rm{d}}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(0)}}{{\rm{d}}\tau}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{2}=-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{6\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(t-\tau)}}\frac{{\rm{d}}\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(1)}}{{\rm{d}}\tau}\>.

(49)

The matching procedure leads us to
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}^{(1)}=-\mathbb{A}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{1}
.
The corresponding
singular contributions to force and torque read:

\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(1)}_{\rm sing}=\mathbb{A}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{1},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{(2)}_{\rm sing}=\mathbb{A}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{2},

\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(1)}_{\rm sing}=\mathbb{B}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{1},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$T$}^{(2)}_{\rm sing}=\mathbb{B}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}_{2}\>.
