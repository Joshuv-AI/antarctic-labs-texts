Photoreactive Pantetheinyl Mimics Reveal Design Limitations for Crypto‐Acyl Carrier Proteins
Authors: Anna‐Kay R. West, Tawakalitu A. Afolabi‐Balogun, Christian C. Martin, Lizbeth Cambron, Mujeeb A. Wakeel, Joshua A. Baccile, Constance B. Bailey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyketide synthases (PKS), fatty acid synthases (FAS), and non‐ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS) span a variety of protein architectures and catalytic modalities. Common to all these biosynthetic modalities is the centrality of a phosphopantetheinylated carrier protein, which shuttles substrate either inter‐ or intramolecularly between discrete domains that catalyze enzymatic transformations within the assembly line, resulting in these biosynthetic classes being termed “thiotemplated.” In all thiotemplated biosynthetic assembly lines, transient protein–protein interactions mediated by the carrier protein are essential for chain extension and catalysis. In recent years, synthetic analogs of phosphopantetheine (Ppant), known as “crypto‐ACPs,” have become powerful tools for probing certain protein–protein interactions in thiotemplated systems. These crypto‐ACP probes are usually created through a chemoenzymatic process. Most existing crypto‐ACP probes are designed around mechanism‐based approaches, which are aimed at capturing specific protein interactions within these pathways. To develop a more versatile probe capable of capturing a broader range of transient protein–protein interactions, we designed and synthesized a series of diazirine‐based phosphopantetheinylated mimics suitable for photocrosslinking using a commonly employed beta‐alanine linkage for synthetic tractability. We then evaluated the efficiency of loading Escherichia coli AcpP with these diazirine probes as well as crosslinking with Escherichia coli FabD. Examination of both loading and crosslinking revealed that deviations from the structure of the natural substrate (4′‐phosphopantetheine) of the phosphopantetheinyl (Ppant) arm of the carrier protein resulted in inefficient loading, leading to poor or no crosslinking. These results highlight the limitations of synthetic and structural modularity in crypto‐ACP design and will assist future strategies for interrogating protein–protein interactions of this nature.

Introduction

Polyketide synthases (PKSs), fatty acid synthases (FAS), and non‐ribosomal peptide synthases (NRPS) span a range of catalytic modalities to catalyze the formation of important and structurally diverse metabolites—polyketides [1, 2], fatty acids [3], and non‐ribosomal peptides, respectively [4]. Despite encompassing various catalytic modalities and structural architectures of these proteins, their associated biosynthetic pathways are linked due to the presence of a central catalytic domain, the carrier protein, which contains a phosphopantetheinyl (Ppant) prosthetic group (otherwise termed the “Ppant arm”). This p‐pant arm possesses a free thiol, wherein thioester linkages carry acyl intermediates and undergo chain elongation [5, 6, 7]. This commonality among the biosynthetic pathways leads to them being collectively referred to as “thiotemplated” pathways. Investigations of such thiotemplated biosynthesis have ramifications for synthetic biology, as engineering these pathways can create molecules, ranging from therapeutic natural products [8] to diverse bioproducts such as biofuels, commodities, and industrial chemicals [9, 10, 11]. Thiotemplated pathways are often broadly classified as type I, where there is a megasynth(et)ase that consists of discrete catalytic domains tethered by linkers [3], or type II, where discrete proteins create a complex that docks on the acyl carrier protein (ACP) [12]. Many iterations of these two paradigms exist, with key examples including the trans‐acyltransferase (trans‐AT) PKSs, which are type I PKSs, where the acyltransferase (AT) domain, and sometimes other domains, are standalone, discrete enzymes [13], so‐called on‐line non‐PKS/NRPS tailoring enzymes that have specificity for carrier protein substrates [14, 15, 16] and domains that cross‐talk with primary metabolism [17]. Furthermore, many interactions remain mysterious, for example, some type I PKSs that have inactive acyltransferase domains but still perform chain extension [18, 19, 20, 21].

To capture these key fleeting interactions that mediate biosynthetic transformations, Burkart and coworkers developed a strategy that exploits modification of the carrier protein to create probes harboring unnatural Ppant linkages. These loaded ACPs are called “crypto‐ACPs” or an ACP with an unnatural modification at the thiol end [22]. Such crypto‐ACP modifications include mechanism‐based probes with pantetheine‐linked analogs that are functionalized to covalently react with the catalytic domain of interest (Figure 1A) [23, 24]. In this approach, Burkart and colleagues employ a combination of three enzymes: Escherichia coli CoaA (pantethenate kinase), CoaD (phosphopantetheine adenylyltransferase), and CoaE (dephosphocoenzyme A kinase) from the coenzyme A biosynthesis pathway [25] to convert their pantetheine probes into coenzyme A analogs. These enzymes are tolerant to changes distal to the hydroxyl moiety of the pantoic acid portion of pantetheine and thus tolerate a range of mimics at the region on the thiol end [23, 26, 27]. The resulting unnatural Ppant analogs are then attached to carrier proteins by the action of the promiscuous Ppant transferase (PPTase) enzyme, Bacillus subtilis Sfp [26, 28]. Since the development of this approach, other researchers have implemented this technique to investigate the interaction between catalytic proteins in PKS and NRPS using mechanism‐based probes, rendering this method a valuable state‐of‐the‐art technique for studying thiotemplated assembly lines [29, 30].

While mechanism‐based crosslinkers of pantetheinyl mimics on the carrier protein have proven to have wide utility for structural and functional investigation of polyketide and fatty acid pathways [23, 31, 32], the downside to chemical crosslinkers is that they have specific, narrow applications in the targeted chemistry. Furthermore, the use of a mechanism‐based cross‐linker requires knowledge of the chemistry involved in catalysis, meaning it cannot be used to discover novel enzymatic transformations. An alternative approach often used for dissecting protein complexes is photo‐crosslinking. Unlike mechanism‐based approaches, photo‐crosslinking probes can be utilized in studying a wide variety of interactions, including those with unknown chemistry [33, 34, 35]. The three common photo‐reactive functional groups used in protein–protein interaction studies include benzophenone, aryl azides, and alkyl diazirines. The use of photoreactive probes to investigate interactions between enzymes in the FAS and PKS pathways has led to work by Williams and coworkers, in which they used a benzophenone photoreactive group to investigate ACP–KS interactions. In their approach, a photocrosslinking amino acid was incorporated at the conserved serine residue site on the ACPs from the type II FAS pathway in E. coli and the DEBS type I PKS pathway [36]. The results from their study showed efficient crosslinking between the photoreactive E. coli AcpP and its cognate partner enzymes, FabB and FabF, while the type I PKS ACP displayed no crosslinking interaction between the enzymes. While each photo‐reactive group has its advantages, alkyl diazirine has been frequently selected for photo‐crosslinking studies due to its chemical and photophysical properties. Its small size reduces steric interactions and limits potential blocking of the cross‐link between proteins. In addition, alkyl diazirine is activated at a favorable wavelength of light that is not damaging to proteins [35]. It also exhibits high photo‐crosslinking efficiency through the formation of a reactive carbene intermediate, while demonstrating good chemical stability and undergoing rapid photolysis to form its reactive species [37]. Another major advantage of diazirines is that they are nonspecific and are known to insert rapidly into C─H and X─H bonds (X = N, S, and O) [35]. Although a diazirine‐linked crypto‐ACP appears to be a tool with broad utility for investigating protein–protein interactions in thiotemplated pathways, to our knowledge, such a probe has not been synthesized or utilized in any mechanistic studies.

While diazirines exhibit properties that make them suitable for photo‐crosslinking studies of biosynthetic pathways, it is important to consider their potential drawbacks, especially synthetically. Given their sensitivity to light and potential volatility and explosiveness, pragmatic considerations must be addressed regarding synthetic feasibility, especially with the high cost of low quantities of diazirine precursors that are available commercially. In selecting the appropriate diazirine precursor pool for cross‐linking investigations, it is also important to consider the type of linkage that will produce a more stable and synthetically accessible bioisostere. As such, there have been increased efforts in pursuing beta‐alanine linkages for the crypto‐ACP probe design instead of the more unstable thioester linkage [38]. The large commercial availability of amines also plays a major role in why β‐alanine linkages are pursued. To broaden the utility of the diazirine‐based probes, the attachment of a biorthogonal handle, such as an alkyne group, allows for further detection and visualization of potential cross‐links when paired with an azide fluorophore through click chemistry. With the alkyne group being very compact and small, the incorporation of this biorthogonal handle should not affect the structure of the diazirine probe or its reactivity [35]. Hence, with these considerations in mind, we sought to explore the efficiency of photo‐crosslinking in capturing the interactions between proteins within thiotemplated pathways. We first synthesized three diazirine‐based photo‐crosslinking pantetheinyl mimics, 1a, 1b, and 1c (Figure 1B), with clickable analogs (alkyne) from commercially available starting materials. Adopting the approach developed by Burkart and coworkers, diazirine probes 1a–c were then subjected to a coenzyme A conversion reaction via the action of the E. coli enzymes CoaA, CoaD, and CoaE. We explored the feasibility of this approach using one of the most fundamental thiotemplated reactions from primary metabolism: loading of E. coli acyl carrier protein (AcpP) via B. subtilis Sfp (Figure 1B). These newly generated diazirine‐based crypto‐ACPs were then exposed to UV irradiation in the presence of a key reaction partner for AcpP, the E. coli malonyl‐CoA acyl‐carrier protein transacylase, FabD (Figure 1C). Loading efficiency and crosslinking efficiency were evaluated by liquid chromatography–high resolution mass spectrometry (LC–HRMS).

Results and Discussion

To initiate our investigation, photo‐crosslinking pantetheinyl probes Dia (1a), Alkyne‐Dia (1b), and Amine‐Alkyne‐Dia (1c) were synthesized using a modified version of a synthetic protocol established by Burkart and coworkers [26, 27]. Briefly, d‐pantothenic acid hemi‐calcium saltS1 was protected by reacting with p‐anisaldehyde dimethyl acetal, producing the protected carboxylic acid S4 in a 40% yield. The protected acid S4 was then subjected to a condensation reaction with 2‐azidoethyl amine S5 to generate the protected azide S6 in a 62% yield. Staudinger reduction of S6 using triphenylphosphine led to the formation of the protected amine 2 in an 88% yield (Scheme S1). The cross‐coupling reaction of 2 with SDA (NHS)‐diazirine 3, 3‐(3‐(but‐3‐yn‐1‐yl)‐3H‐diazirine‐3‐yl) propanoic acid 4, and 3‐(but‐3‐yn‐1‐yl)‐3‐(2‐iodoethyl)‐3H‐diazirine 5 produced the protected probes 6a, 6b, and 6c, respectively. Deprotection of 6a– c under mildly acidic conditions yielded the desired pantetheinyl probes 1a–1c in yields of 41%, 26%, and 33%, respectively (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of diazirine‐based pantetheine mimics 1a–c.

Following the successful synthesis of 1a–c, we then proceeded to perform an in vitro enzymatic conversion of the diazirine‐based pantetheine probes with the purified coenzyme A biosynthetic enzymes CoaA, CoaD, and CoaE from E. coli (Figure 2A). After the heterologous expression and affinity purification of CoaA, CoaD, and CoaE in E. coli (Figure S2), the activity and promiscuity of the enzymes were examined through the conversion of 1a–c to their respective coenzyme A analogs. Conversion was verified by negative‐mode electrospray ionization (ESI−) liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC–MS). LC–MS analysis from the biocatalytic conversion experiment showed the successful conversion of 1a (Dia), 1b (Alkyne‐Dia), and 1c (Amine‐Alkyne‐Dia) to their corresponding coenzyme A analog, showing singly and doubly charged mass‐to‐charge (m/z) values of 859.1922/429.0898, 897.2083/448.102, and 869.2157/434.1033, respectively (Figure 2B–D). To determine if any residual amounts of unconverted 1a–c were present, an extracted‐ion chromatogram (EIC) was obtained for each of the reactions and compared with the EICs of pure 1a–c. The results showed no evidence of the presence of the unconverted probes 1a and 1b (Figures S3 and S4) in the conversion reaction sample; however, the Coenzyme A conversion reaction of 1c showed a high abundance of the unconverted 1c probe (Figure S5).

Once we successfully converted 1a–c into their respective coenzyme A analogs, our focus shifted to attaching these converted probes to the E. coli acyl carrier protein, AcpP. To achieve loading, coenzyme A analogs 1a (Dia), 1b (Alkyne‐Dia), and 1c (Amine‐Alkyne‐Dia) were incubated with the carrier protein along with the promiscuous PPTase, Sfp (Figure 3A). Carrier protein loading was assessed using positive‐mode ESI+ LC–HRMS. As a natural substrate of Sfp and the primary donor of the 4′‐phosphapantetheinyl group to the serine residue of the carrier protein [39, 40], coenzyme A hydrate served as a positive control for this reaction. LC–HRMS analysis was initially performed on the recombinant apo‐AcpP protein, which shows a mass of 8508 Da (Figure S3). The recombinant carrier protein expressed and used for these experiments was from the overproducing strain designed by Cronan and coworkers [41]. This is because attempts at the overexpression of the E. coli carrier protein, AcpP, in a multicopy plasmid showed toxicity to the expression host, as observed and reported by Cronan Jr. and team [41]. After the enzymatic reactions, LC–HRMS analysis of the reaction samples with coenzyme A hydrate, 1a, 1b, and 1c, revealed intact masses of 8848, 8941, 8979, and 8951 Da, respectively (Figure 3B–E). Further analysis of the loading experiment highlighted a mass of 8873 Da, which was observed in the spectrum for the diazirine probe 1b (Figure 3D). The likelihood that this peak represents the attached 1b probe to the carrier protein appears low, given its mass difference of 106 Da, which does not correspond to a typical ionization structure of the diazirine probe. This peak is more likely a variant of the active form of the carrier protein, holo‐Acp, which has an experimental mass of 8848 Da with a 25Mg ion attached. This interpretation is supported by the presence of holo‐AcpP traces in the spectrum of probe 1c (Figure 3E). This suggests a potential competition between the loading of the diazirine probes and the natural substrate, coenzyme A, which would be present in trace amounts relative to AcpP in E. coli cell lysates. To reduce the occurrence of holo‐AcpP in our sample runs, we performed the holo‐to‐apo AcpP conversion reaction during the purification of the carrier protein and several rounds of dialysis for each enzyme used in this study. However, our attempts were unsuccessful.

To investigate the crosslinking ability of 1a, 1b, and 1c, the probes were subjected to a photo‐irradiation reaction with recombinant malonyl‐CoA‐acyl carrier protein transacylase, FabD, from the type II FAS pathway in E. coli. FabD is a key enzyme in bacterial fatty acid biosynthesis. It catalyzes the transfer of the malonyl group from malonyl‐CoA to holo‐ACP, supplying the extender unit for subsequent decarboxylative condensation and beta‐keto processing [42]. FabD was heterologously expressed in E. coli and purified to homogeneity (Figure S6). LC–HRMS analysis of the purified FabD revealed an intact mass of 34,314 Da (Figure S7). Based on literature reports [43], the equilibrium dissociation constant (K D) for E. coli FabD with its cognate, acyl carrier protein (AcpP), is proposed to be within the sub‐micromolar range, indicating a high binding affinity. However, due to the transient nature of the interaction between the two enzymes, a definitive K D value has not been reported. Therefore, to assess the photo‐crosslinking efficiency of each probe, varying molar ratios of crypto‐ACP 1a–1c (1): FabD (0.5, 2, 1, 10) were reacted. Irradiation of crypto‐AcpPs 1a–c with FabD reveals the release of nitrogen gas (N2), indicating the formation of the reactive carbene species for 1a, 1b, and 1c (Figure 4A), yielding masses of 8913, 8950, and 8922 Da, respectively (Figure 4B–D). Data from the photo‐crosslinking experiment also showed quenching of the reactive carbene species of 1a‐c through their insertion into water molecules, resulting in variants with masses of 8931, 8967, and 8942 Da, respectively (Figure 4B–D). A difference in the irradiation efficiency of each probe was also observed across the different concentrations of FabD investigated, with a concentration of 2.5 µM showing the highest conversion efficiency for all three crypto‐AcpPs, followed by 5 µM (Figures S8 and S9). This was also observed in the non‐deconvoluted spectra for each of the crypto‐AcpPs. Examination of the non‐deconvoluted spectra for the crypto‐AcpPs reacted with 2.5 µM FabD showed multiple charged peaks representing FabD, ranging from a m/z value of approximately 750 to 1320 (Figure S8B–D). The multiple charged peaks representing the irradiated crypto‐AcpPs 1a–1c range between m/z values of 1250 and 1800. For crypto‐AcpP 1a, the non‐deconvoluted spectrum showed m/z values representing the presence of the holo‐AcpP (1262.4886 (+7) and 1475.5554 (+6)) and charged peaks that likely represent the irradiated AcpP‐Dia probe 1a (1486.5614 (+6)). Charged variants of holo‐AcpP were also observed in the non‐deconvoluted mass spectra for crypto‐AcpPs 1b and 1c. Peaks with m/z values around 1490 (1492.9147 (+6)) likely represent a variant of the irradiated crypto‐AcpP 1b (Figure S8C). The non‐deconvoluted spectrum of crypto‐AcpP 1c showed a m/z value of 1488.0768, which could likely represent a variant of the irradiated AcpP‐Amine‐Alkyne‐Dia probe 1c. From the non‐deconvoluted spectra obtained, we did observe some carryover that occurred during the mass spec. analysis, even though a blank was included between each reaction sample run. Although LC–HRMS analysis confirmed that 1a–c were successfully irradiated to their reactive carbene intermediates, we found no evidence that the probes crosslinked to the target protein, FabD, even in the presence of an excess of FabD.

The lack of a crosslink between crypto‐AcpPs 1a– 1c and FabD is likely due to the probes not being able to access the small, compact active site of FabD [42, 43]. To determine whether this could be the reason, we sought to investigate the crosslinking interaction between crypto‐AcpPs 1a– 1c with the E. coli 3‐hydroxydecanoyl‐ [acyl carrier protein] dehydratase enzyme, FabA. Unlike FabD, the active site of FabA is large and elongated and can bind substrates with a chain length of up to 10 carbons (C10) [44, 45], hence making it an appropriate candidate for our investigation. Following the heterologous expression, purification, and characterization via LC–HRMS of E. coli FabA (Figures S10 and S11), photocrosslinking assessments were performed with crypto‐AcpPs 1a– 1c following the same procedure outlined for FabD. To evaluate whether crosslinking occurred between crypto‐AcpP 1a –1c and FabD or FabA, we performed a sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) analysis for each micromolar concentration of FabD and FabA tested. To maximize detection sensitivity, the gels were visualized using silver staining. As controls, the purified apo‐AcpP, E. coli FabD, E. coli FabA, and the photocrosslinking experiment with CoA hydrate were also included on the gel. A comparison between the concentrations of FabD and FabA tested showed the presence of the bands representing FabD, FabA, and AcpP in each reaction sample when compared to the control bands highlighted in the different colored boxes. However, in a similar fashion to FabD, there is no clear indication that a cross‐link product was formed in any of the reaction samples (Figure S12), further supporting this hypothesis.

To further assess the cross‐linking between crypto‐AcpPs 1a–1c and FabA, we performed LC–HRMS analysis on the irradiated samples. In each reaction, 5 μM of FabA was irradiated with an equivalent amount of crypto‐AcpPs 1a–1c. As a control, FabA was irradiated with the active, functional form of the carrier protein, holo‐AcpP. From the non‐deconvoluted spectra obtained, we observed the charged variants representing the FabA protein between m/z values of 650 and 1200, which was prominent in the spectrum of holo‐AcpP and crypto‐AcpP 1b (AcpP‐Alkyne‐Dia) (Figure S13A,C). From the non‐deconvoluted spectra of each of the crypto‐AcpPs, there were no definitive peaks observed that indicate the presence of the respective irradiated probes. The observed peaks were from the apo‐form of the carrier protein, giving m/z values of 1216 (+7) and 1418 (+6) (Figure S13).

A likely explanation for the lack of crosslinking is the structural changes to the carrier protein that occur following the attachment of diazirine probes 1a–c. Burkart and colleagues investigated the influence of modifying the thioester linkage on the structure of AcpP [38]. From their study, they demonstrated that replacing the natural thioester linkage with an amide or ester linkage resulted in significant structural changes in the carrier protein. They also reported that the amide linkage perturbed residues in the “recognition helix” (helix II) of AcpP [38]. With this knowledge, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the synthesized diazirine probes, which have an amide linkage (1a and 1b) and an amine linkage (1c), may result in a conformation that is distinct from the natural conformation with Ppant. This, in turn, might result in a failure to cross‐link due to the spatial positioning of the diazirine. This hypothesis is further supported by the observation of evidence of carbene formation via nitrogen extrusion (Figure 4).

As ACPs have a characteristic conserved four‐helix bundle structure, conformational differences could be easily monitored by changes in the circular dichroism (CD) spectra. Following the chemoenzymatic loading of probes 1a–1c onto ACP, the resulting crypto‐ACP products (1a–1c) were purified using Ni‐NTA affinity chromatography, which selectively binds and removes the His‐tagged proteins Sfp, E. coli Coa A, Coa D, and Coa E present in the reaction mixture. The samples were then analyzed via CD, and the resulting data were normalized to molar ellipticity relative to the carrier protein concentration and processed using Dichroweb [46]. As a control and to ensure that the Ni‐NTA workup was not interfering with the CD results, we performed the Ni‐NTA purification on the 50 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.0 used for this experiment and conducted CD analysis on the buffer prior to running all other samples (Figure S14). CD analysis was also performed on the pure, inactive form of the protein, apo‐AcpP, and the active form with the natural Ppant moiety attached to it, holo‐AcpP (Figure 5A). As reported in the literature, E. coli holo‐ACP undergoes a more compact folding due to the attachment of the Ppant moiety [47], which is what we observed in the CD spectrum in Figure 5A. CD analysis of the carrier protein after the attachment of diazirine probes 1a–1c showed a significant decrease in the helical content of the protein, especially a complete abolishment of helical content for AcpP‐Alkyne‐Dia and AcpP‐Amine‐Alkyne‐Dia, when compared to holo‐AcpP (Figure 5B–D, Table S2). This data suggests that the protein loses a substantial secondary structure upon the attachment of 1a–1c. Thus, this result supports the hypothesis that the carrier protein is likely not in the correct conformation to facilitate a crosslink with the target protein, FabD, although we cannot exclude the possibility that increased protein aggregation is causing the change in the CD signal.

Conclusion

We sought to create diazirine‐based photocrosslinking crypto‐ACPs to expand the suite of tools for elucidating polyketide, fatty acid, and non‐ribosomal peptide pathways with unknown chemistry and/or unknown reaction domains. Our experience in the design and evaluation of such a probe is illustrative of the limitations of such an approach. Using commercially accessible precursors, we synthesized three novel diazirine‐based Ppant mimics, 1a–c, to have an amide (1a–b) or amine (1c) group, replacing the typical thiol group of the pantetheine molecule of coenzyme A. The loading of diazirine‐based probes 1a–c indicates that deviation from the structure of the typical pantetheine moiety in coenzyme A likely leads to inefficient loading, as observed with 1b and 1c. While the beta‐alanine linkage has been successfully employed for many crypto‐ACP probes [27, 32, 48, 49] due to its increased stability and availability of synthetic precursors, in support of what we observed, a prior investigation from Burkart and coworkers indicated that single‐atom replacement of the thioester sulfur to oxo esters or amide bioisosteres resulted in noticeable conformational changes in E. coli AcpP. While the conformational change from single‐atom substitution was rigorously assessed, and it was noted that the use of bioisosteres of the thioester should be pursued with caution, the functional relevance was not established in this previous work [50]. Williams and coworkers have also reported the possibility of a small perturbation to the structure of the type I PKS carrier protein, ACP2, upon installation of a photoreactive amino acid in its active site, which resulted in a lack of crosslinking with the E. coli type II FAS and ketosynthase enzymes [36]. Given the small active site of FabD, we wanted to investigate other partner enzymes of E. coli AcpP with larger active sites to assess whether the probes may just be incompatible with FabD. The dehydratase, FabA, is known to have a large and elongated active site compared to that of FabD; however, photocrosslinking analysis of FabA with crypto‐AcpPs 1a–1c still did not show any evidence that crosslinking occurred based on silver‐stain SDS‐PAGE analysis. Our lack of evidence of the occurrence of photocrosslinking between FabD, FabA, and AcpP‐linked probes 1a–c, combined with detectable differences in secondary structure between holo‐AcpP and AcpP‐linked to probes 1a–c, is suggestive of downstream consequences of these conformational differences. While it is unusual in the field to report probes that ultimately do not work as intended, we strongly believe it is important to provide such information to help guide others in the chemical biology community who may be interested in pursuing similar work, as more transparency would further advance the field.
