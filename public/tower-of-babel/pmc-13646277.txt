Exploratory associations between co-occurring opioid and alcohol use disorders and GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment
Authors: Fares Qeadan, Ashlie McCunn, Benjamin Tingey, Philip J Kroth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCo-occurring opioid and alcohol use disorders (OUD + AUD) are associated with morbidity, yet medications for these disorders remain underused. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonists (RA), prescribed for metabolic disease, may influence substance-related outcomes. This study aims to estimate associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions, first-line AUD and OUD medications and several adverse healthcare outcomes among those with co-occurring OUD + AUD.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using de-identified electronic health records from Oracle Health Real-World Data (149 U.S. health systems; 2014-2024). Among 107,217 patients aged ≥12 years with dual OUD + AUD diagnoses, time-varying exposures included medications for OUD (MOUD), medications for AUD (MAUD), and GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions. Outcomes over 2 years included overdoses and intoxications, SUD-related hospitalizations, detoxification, positive drug screens, mental health events, incident liver conditions, and liver biomarkers. Marginal structural survival models with stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting estimated adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) overall and stratified by MOUD/MAUD status, type 2 diabetes (T2DM), and obesity.ResultsGIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions are associated with lower risk of all-drug overdose among patients not receiving MOUD/MAUD (aHR 0.61; 95% CI 0.32-0.98). In stratified analyses, lower overdose risk is observed among patients with T2DM receiving MAUD only (aHR 0.15; 95% CI 0.01-0.41) and among those receiving both MOUD and MAUD (T2DM: aHR 0.46; 95% CI 0.24-0.87; obesity: aHR 0.19; 95% CI 0.04-0.97). Protective associations for SUD-related hospitalization are observed across most strata.ConclusionsIn this large EHR cohort, GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions are linked to reduced overdose and hospitalization risk among patients with co-occurring OUD + AUD. Findings are exploratory and limited by small subgroup sizes; prospective studies are needed to confirm benefit and evaluate safety.Subject terms: Combination drug therapy, Epidemiology

Introduction

The convergence of opioid use disorder (OUD) and alcohol use disorder (AUD) presents a formidable challenge in public health. The United States (U.S.) has seen rises in alcohol-related (~178,000) and opioid-related (~80,000) mortality in recent years1,2. The dual diagnosis of OUD and AUD is a growing concern in U.S. patient populations. Some estimates show that between 14.0% and 27.1% of individuals with opioid dependence or OUD also have current comorbid alcohol dependence or an AUD diagnosis3,4. Other studies have found that over half of individuals with OUD have also experienced AUD at some point in their lifetime3,5. Importantly, those with both OUD and AUD experience increased morbidity and mortality, worse treatment outcomes, and have significantly higher rates of anxiety, depression and substance dependence at baseline6,7. Individuals with co-occurring OUD and AUD also have a greater risk of opioid overdose, accident, and injury7, greater odds of opioid-related poisoning and suicidal ideation8, and increased risk of relapse, overdose, emergency room visits and are less likely to receive medication treatment compared to those with just one SUD3,5,6,9. The co-occurrence of OUD and AUD not only complicates clinical management but also exacerbates the severity and prognosis of each disorder.

Pharmacotherapy for treatment of OUD and AUD has proven to improve patient morbidity10. First-line medications for treatment of moderate to severe AUD include naltrexone, acamprosate, and disulfiram. Naltrexone has been found to reduce the number of heavy drinking days11 while acamprosate and disulfiram significantly increase alcohol abstinence12,13. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved the use of buprenorphine, methadone, and naltrexone to reduce opioid use and treat OUD. Extensive evidence has proven these pharmacotherapies benefit people by reducing substance cravings and use, increasing cumulative abstinence, and preventing overdose injury and death10,14–17. Despite the numerous medications available, in 2023, only 1.9% of the 28.9 million people with AUD and 18.0% of the 5.7 million people with OUD received pharmacological treatment for their substance use disorders18. Systemic and demographic barriers, including referral challenges19, provider service issues20, limitations in insurance coverage21, and treatment stigmatism17,22 prevent individuals from receiving the substance use treatment they need.

Further evidence shows that individuals with OUD and AUD are even less likely to receive pharmacotherapy compared to those with just one of the disorders. Among a group of patients with opioid dependence, those with co-occurring alcohol dependence were 25% less likely to receive medication treatment compared to those without AUD3. In another study among individuals with OUD, those with one or more additional SUD diagnoses were significantly less likely to receive buprenorphine or methadone compared to those with only OUD23. Even when patients do receive medication treatment, those with co-occurring SUD experience lower treatment retention, increased legal consequences, and poorer health outcomes overall24–26. Current pharmacotherapies, while effective independently for OUD or AUD, face limitations and potential contraindications when used together6. Additionally, the integration and utilization of medications for OUD and AUD (MOUD and MAUD) in clinical practice are suboptimal27. This gap in effective treatment modalities for co-occurring disorders highlights a critical unmet medical need.

Recent research has begun exploring the potential benefits of alternative therapies in the treatment of substance use. Significant attention surrounds glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA), which are primarily prescribed to treat type 2 diabetes, obesity, and other weight-related medical conditions28. Specifically, our recent epidemiological study has found that among people with OUD and AUD, those with GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions have lower rates of overdose and alcohol intoxication29. Another study using electronic health record (EHR) data found semaglutide (a common GLP-1 RA) to be associated with reduced risk of opioid overdose in patients with comorbid type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and OUD compared to other antidiabetic medications30. Similarly, semaglutide has been associated with a lower risk of AUD incidence and recurrence compared to other anti-obesity medications31 and a significantly lower risk of tobacco use diagnosis compared to other antidiabetic medications32. GLP-1 RA medications have also indicated better outcomes for patients with the combination of T2DM, obesity, and cannabis use disorder, compared to other anti-obesity and anti-diabetic medications33.

Importantly, emerging evidence is consistent with preclinical studies that have documented various GLP-1 RA drugs reducing the rewarding effects of nicotine, cocaine, alcohol, and opioids in animal models of addiction34,35. In a case study series of six patients with AUD taking semaglutide for weight loss, all six patients displayed a significant reduction in AUD symptoms following semaglutide treatment36. A recent randomized controlled trial examined the effects of once-weekly semaglutide dose on alcohol consumption patterns. Compared to the placebo, researchers found that semaglutide significantly reduced weekly alcohol cravings and drinks consumed on drinking days but did not affect the total number of drinking days in the study37. Another clinical trial for patients with AUD found exenatide, another common GLP-1 RA, significantly reduced alcohol cue activity in the reward pathway in the brain; however, no significant difference was detected in the number of heavy drinking days between the exenatide and placebo groups38. While this GIP/GLP-1 RA and substance use research provides substantial evidence, much of the current literature reports outcomes for a single substance or SUD. There is an urgent need to estimate how GIP/GLP-1 RA associates with substance use outcomes among patients with co-occurring diseases, especially OUD and AUD, and how these associations fit within the realm of current first-line MOUD and MAUD treatment.

Also, while current literature mainly focuses on substance use outcomes, much less is known about the relationship between these treatment approaches and liver functioning. While some studies have estimated the impact of MOUD and MAUD individually on liver function39–44, it is not well understood how these medications affect liver function when taken simultaneously. Additionally, no studies have evaluated liver enzyme levels among individuals with co-occurring OUD and AUD. Importantly, emerging evidence has recognized a potential for GIP/GLP-1 RA medications in the treatment of various metabolic liver diseases45–47. Investigating the impact of GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions on liver functionality in patients with co-occurring OUD and AUD, a group of individuals already at an increased risk of liver dysfunction, would fill a gap in understanding medication safety issues related to integrative treatment approaches for substance use. There is a growing body of evidence that treatment of co-occurring SUD, especially OUD and AUD, is fraught with complexities and requires more integrated treatment approaches than previously believed.

Utilizing a large, multi-institutional EHR database, this research will investigate the relationships between integrated substance use treatment approaches and adverse healthcare outcomes for patients with co-occurring OUD and AUD. Specifically, our study aims to (1) provide characteristics of patients with co-occurring OUD and AUD stratified by obesity and T2DM, (2) estimate associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA with first-line AUD and OUD medications and several adverse healthcare outcomes, and (3) compare liver enzyme laboratory values by GIP/GLP-1 RA and MOUD/MAUD treatment status. Despite the relatively rare occurrence of comorbid OUD and AUD, leveraging patient data from multiple health systems across the entire U.S. provides a sufficiently large sample to examine this somewhat rare patient group. Results of this study may encourage researchers to further explore the potential benefit of GIP/GLP-1 RA in substance use treatment and help with overcoming many of the current barriers to SUD treatment.

Methods

Data source

This study was conducted using retrospective, de-identified, EHR data from Oracle Health Real-World DataTM (OHRWD). As of the February 2025 refresh, 149 health systems across the U.S. contribute to OHRWD, comprising all encounter types (e.g., inpatient, emergency, outpatient, in-person, primary care, telehealth, etc.). Contributing systems have provided data for over 115 million patients, leading to a total of approximately 2 billion encounters. OHRWD is extracted from the EMR of hospitals in which Oracle has a data use agreement. Encounters may include pharmacy, clinical and microbiology laboratory, admission, and billing information from affiliated patient care locations. All admissions, medication orders and dispensing, laboratory orders and specimens are date and time stamped, providing a temporal relationship between treatment patterns and clinical information. Oracle has established Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-compliant operating policies to establish de-identification for OHRWD48.

Sample

Patients were included in this study if they had a qualifying OUD and AUD diagnosis, defined by qualifying codes (Supplementary Data 2). For each OUD and AUD, the first inpatient or emergency encounter with a qualifying code or the first of ≥2 encounters, of any type, occurring within a span of two years, with qualifying codes, was chosen as the OUD/AUD respective date49. Qualifying OUD and AUD diagnoses were required each to occur within a span of two years, where the most recent date of the two was chosen as the index date. Patients were restricted to those ≥12 years old at the index encounter. Index encounters spanned from 1 January 2014 to 30 November 2024. Inclusion began in 2014 to coincide with the initial U.S. FDA approval of many GLP-1 RA medications50. Supplementary Fig. 1 displays the inclusion/exclusion criteria of the cohort. This study was reviewed and determined to be exempt by the Loyola University Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB) (LU# 217901) under 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4) for secondary research using de-identified data and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines51.

Study design

This study used a longitudinal retrospective cohort design that started enrollment of patients at the index OUD & AUD date (1 January 2014 to 30 November 2024) and assessed treatment usage/outcomes from one day post-index date up to 2 years later. Treatment and outcomes were assessed at repeat 14-day intervals, extending up to two years. Intervals of 14 days were chosen to balance the ability to detect changes over time while also allowing a sufficiently large period to capture meaningful changes. The time interval was also informed by literature identifying the first week to 2 weeks after a non-fatal overdose (index event) to be the greatest period of risk for a subsequent overdose52–54. Literature on subsequent alcohol adverse events has longer risk periods55, but shorter opioid risk windows were chosen due to the intersection of OUD & AUD conditions and to allow for greater ability to detect changes over time. Patients were included until 30 November 2024 to ensure those lastly eligible had at least three months of follow-up ending at the data refresh in February 2025. Thus, those lastly eligible did not have a full 2 years of follow-up, which was done to maximize the cohort sample. While this approach expanded the study population, it introduced variation in treatment period and follow-up duration; therefore, we conducted sensitivity analyses restricting patients with a full 2 years of follow-up to assess the robustness of results. Associations were stable, and thus we used the maximized cohort of patients. Due to patients having variable time-on-study, “person-months” were incorporated in all analyses comprising the time (in months) from the start of follow-up to the maximum follow-up date over 14-day periods. For incident (first-occurrence) outcomes, person-months comprised the time from start of follow-up to incident outcome (for those experiencing the outcome) or the time from start of follow-up to the maximum follow-up date (for those not experiencing the outcome), again over 14-day periods. Any total person-month time span exceeding the follow-up period of interest was capped at the maximum follow-up amount (e.g., 24 months). Patients with a maximum follow-up time that ended before the conclusion of the 2-year follow-up period were censored at their maximum follow-up. Due to treatment and outcome assessments being captured in similar 14-day windows, and to avoid possible time-dependent biases, the temporality of both was assessed, and in instances where outcomes preceded treatment in a given window, the treatment assignment for that window was imputed with the previous window’s value or assigned as “0” if the treatment had not yet been achieved. Sensitivity analyses further reclassified exposure statuses in each 14-day window as the lagged 14-day window assignment and revealed similar associations. Supplementary Fig. 2 displays a visual schematic of the study design. Additional analyses exploring temporal trends in liver functioning via biomarkers were assessed among those with ≥3 liver function labs each on separate days (to establish a trajectory) within 24 months of follow-up.

Ethics and consent

This study involved secondary data analysis of de-identified patient information, with no direct intervention or interaction with individuals. In accordance with ethical guidelines, the requirement for informed consent and ethical approval for this component was waived by the Loyola University Chicago IRB (LU# 217901). Consent to participate was not applicable as it involved secondary data analysis using de-identified patient information with no direct involvement of individuals. Consent to publish was not applicable. This manuscript does not contain any individual person’s data in any form (including any individual details, images, or videos). The data analyzed in this study are de-identified and secondary in nature.

Outcomes

The outcomes of interest comprised time-varying repeat 14-day window binary markers of adverse health outcomes across the two years of follow-up. These included all-drug overdose, opioid overdose (OOD), and alcohol intoxication (qualifying codes in Supplementary Data 3). OOD was defined by encounters of any type, with accompanying diagnosis codes that comprised indications of opioid use with intoxication or opioid-specific indications with poisoning. OOD was also defined by emergency encounters with a prescription of naloxone. Alcohol intoxication was defined by encounters of any type, with accompanying diagnosis codes that comprised indications of alcohol use with intoxication or toxic effects of alcohol-specific indications. All drug overdose indications extended to all substances. Other outcomes of interest included SUD-related hospitalization, positive drug screening, substance detoxification, mental health crises, and antidepressant prescription (Rx; Supplementary Tables 1–2, Supplementary Data 2, 4–6). All these outcomes allowed possible repeat occurrences per patient within the follow-up window. Additional outcomes included time-varying 14-day window incident liver conditions, depression, and anxiety (codes in Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Data 5) focusing on the first occurrence per patient within the follow-up window. Additional outcomes comprised liver functioning via repeated continuous biomarker labs and specifically included albumin (grams per deciliter [g/dL]), total protein (g/dL), alkaline phosphatase (ALP; units per liter [U/L]), alanine transaminase (ALT; U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; U/L), and bilirubin (milligrams per deciliter [mg/dL]; Supplementary Table 4).

Exposures

The exposures of interest were time-varying repeat 14-day window binary treatment indications captured across the two years of follow-up. These included MOUD, MAUD, and GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx defined by qualifying codes (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Data 7 and 8). Prescriptions either initiated during a given window or overlapping with the window (comprising ongoing prescriptions) were designated as the presence of treatment.

Additional measures

Baseline characteristics comprised demographics of age (in years), sex, race/ethnicity, census region, urbanicity, year of encounter, and insurance. Clinical/behavioral characteristics included the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)56, chronic pain, sleep apnea, mental health conditions, other SUD conditions, procedure history, opioid Rx dosing (morphine milligram equivalents [MME]) and duration57, and recurrent outcome histories (e.g., all-drug overdose, SUD-related hospitalization, etc.; Supplementary Tables 1–2 and 6 and Supplementary Data 2–6 and 9–12). Time-varying characteristics (similarly captured as binary indications in repeat, 14-day windows) included benzodiazepine Rx, outpatient SUD-related services, and complementary medicine usage (Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Data 13 and 14). Additionally, baseline T2DM and obesity were treated as stratification variables (Supplementary Data 15) along with inclusion of time-varying T2DM medication Rx (e.g., insulin, biguanides, sodium-glucose cotransporter two inhibitors, etc.) and obesity medication Rx (e.g., phentermine, orlistat, setmelanotide, etc.; Supplementary Data 16) to account for additional, condition-specific, treatments utilized over the study period.

Statistics and reproducibility

Characteristics were presented overall as well as stratified by T2DM and obesity. Incident rates (IRs), defined as the count of incident outcomes on study divided by the person time on study, per 10,000 person-months, were presented overall as well as by treatment status (i.e., receipt of MOUD, MAUD, GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx), for the sample overall as well as among those with T2DM and obesity. To quantify associations between treatment and recurrent outcomes, marginal structural Andersen-Gill regression models with inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were fit and adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) were calculated. The sample was balanced on joint time-varying treatments of GIP/GLP-1 Rx, MOUD, and MAUD via stabilized IPTW, and weights were accounted for in models to accommodate time-varying variables that were simultaneously possible confounders of the association of interest and possibly influenced by previous treatment status, while also accounting for possible selection bias due to non-random treatment assignment58–60. Covariate balance, post weighting, was confirmed in Supplementary Table 8. Robust standard errors clustered by patients as well as health systems. Marginal structural Cox models with IPTW quantified associations between treatment and first outcomes. Model goodness of fit was assessed by Cox-Snell (CS) residuals, and the PH assumption was assessed by scaled Schoenfeld residuals along with a global Schoenfeld test61,62. All models involved interactions of time-varying GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx with MOUD and MAUD status, and estimates were presented with estimated marginal means. Variability of estimates was captured with 95% Wald confidence intervals (CIs). Models and IPTW calculation were conducted on the sample overall as well as among those with T2DM and obesity.

To explore the temporal relationship between treatment and liver functioning, multilevel, mixed-effects, linear regression models were utilized with liver function labs as outcomes and involved the health system and patient ID as crossed random-effects. Models involved the interaction of GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx, MOUD/MAUD, and the time (in months) since follow-up start, while being adjusted for previously mentioned characteristics. Predicted mean biomarkers and 95% CI trends were presented over time in paneled line plots with different lines (confidence bands) for GIP/GLP-1 Rx groups and different panels for MOUD/MAUD treatment groups. Residual diagnostics confirmed model GOF. Due to limited sample sizes, these analyses were only conducted on the sample overall. All hypothesis tests were two-sided with a significance level of 5%. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.0.2 (The R Foundation).

Sensitivity/supplemental analyses

A sensitivity analysis excluded patients with GIP/GLP-1 RA contraindicating condition histories inclusive of bariatric surgery, pancreatitis, type 1 diabetes, thyroid cancer, multiple endocrine neoplasia type II, or gastroparesis (Supplementary Table 9) and additionally a supplemental analysis assessed associations between treatments and noted adverse GIP/GLP-1 RA side-effect conditions, inclusive of recurrent antiemetic Rx and incident acute pancreatitis and gallbladder disease (Supplementary Table 10).

Results

Descriptive statistics

Characteristics of patients with concurrent OUD and AUD are displayed in Tables 1–3. There were a total of 107,217 patients meeting the inclusion criteria, with a mean (standard deviation) of 45.2 (14.9) years, and 65.3% (69,946) were male. There were 41.0% of patients who had Medicaid, nearly one-third were prescribed benzodiazepines, and 18.8% had chronic pain. Over half of patients had ≥1 mental health diagnosis, and more than 60% had ≥1 other SUD, with over 7% having ≥4 other SUDs. Over the study period, there were 5.1% of patients (5438) who used outpatient SUD-related services, 21.4% (22,895) treated with MOUD, 7.1% (7570) treated with MAUD, 2.6% (2837) utilized complementary medicine, and 1.1% (1184) prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA medication. Supplementary Table 11 displays the distribution of MOUD & MAUD combinations and GIP/GLP-1 RA, MOUD, and MAUD combinations in the sample overall as well as restricted to those with T2DM and those with obesity.

There were 67.3% (72,113) of patients that had no T2DM or obesity, 11.2% (12,020) that had T2DM alone, 14.5% (15,600) that had obesity alone, and 7.0% (7484) that had T2DM and obesity (Tables 1–3). Those with T2DM or obesity were older and had higher comorbidity, mental health conditions, procedure history, healthcare encounters, and higher opioid dosing/duration history relative to those without either condition. Those with T2DM and obesity had the highest occurrence of all these characteristics compared to other groups. A larger share of patients with T2DM were prescribed some form of T2DM medication, whereas very few obesity patients (0.3–0.4%) were prescribed obesity medication. Those without either T2DM or obesity were most likely to receive MOUD; however, those with both conditions were most likely to receive GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx’s.

Inferential statistics

Associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx, MOUD/MAUD and drug overdose/alcohol intoxication are displayed in Table 4. Among those without MOUD or MAUD treatment, patients prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA had a 39% lower risk of all-drug overdose (aHR [95% CI]: 0.61 [0.32, 0.98]) compared to those not prescribed. Among patients treated with MAUD, those with GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx had 89% lower risk of alcohol intoxication (aHR [95% CI]: 0.11 [0.01, 0.86]). Lower risk of all outcomes was observed for those with GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx’s, compared to those without, among all other MOUD and MAUD treatment groups (with the exception of only MOUD treatment for alcohol intoxication), but none of these findings was significant. When looking among patients with T2DM, those prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA had significantly lower risk of all-drug overdose among those treated with MAUD only (aHR [95% CI]: 0.15 [0.01, 0.41]) and among those treated with MOUD and MAUD (aHR [95% CI]: 0.46 [0.24, 0.87]). Among patients with obesity, those prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA had a significantly lower risk of all-drug overdose compared to those treated with MOUD and MAUD (aHR [95% CI]: 0.19 [0.04, 0.97]).

Associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx, MOUD/MAUD, and other adverse health outcomes are displayed in Table 5. Patients with GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx’s, compared to those without, had significantly lower risk of SUD-related hospitalization across all MOUD/MAUD treatment groups. There were no significant associations between treatments and positive drug screening. Among patients without any MOUD/MAUD treatment, those prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA had significantly lower risk of detoxification (aHR [95% CI]: 0.48 [0.11, 0.88]) while no significant associations were found for other treatment groups. Among patients with both MOUD and MAUD treatment, those prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA had a significantly higher risk of incident liver conditions (aHR [95% CI]: 4.29 [1.39, 8.19]) while no significant associations were found for other treatment groups. Associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA and SUD-related hospitalization were not significant among those with T2DM, and associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA and positive drug screening were similarly not significant among those with T2DM.

When repeating main analyses while excluding those with GIP/GLP-1 RA contraindicating conditions, results remained similar (Supplementary Data 17–19). When assessing associations between treatments and adverse GIP/GLP-1 RA side-effect conditions (Supplementary Data 20), those prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA, compared to those not prescribed, had a significantly higher risk of antiemetic Rx among those without any MOUD or MAUD treatment. Among those with MOUD or MAUD treatment, no associations were found between treatment and antiemetic Rx. No associations were found in T2DM and obesity subgroups or between treatments and acute pancreatitis and gallbladder disease, overall or in any subgroup.

Discussion

Among a sample of patients with co-occurring OUD and AUD, this study identified protective associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA Rx and adverse substance use outcomes with varying differences among the MOUD and MAUD-treated subgroups. When the overall patient sample was stratified by T2DM and obesity, differential protective trends were observed for overdose, alcohol intoxication, and drug screening, while hospitalization and detoxification protective trends were fairly consistent. Patients with GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions also displayed significantly lower liver biomarker levels for ALT, AST, and bilirubin compared to those with no prescriptions, with greater differences among the MOUD and MAUD untreated or partially treated subgroups. The findings of this study augment the growing evidence that GIP/GLP-1 RA may improve substance use outcomes and further emphasize the need to investigate these drugs as an alternative therapy option in substance use treatment.

Specifically, GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions were found to be significantly protective of all-drug overdose among those without any MOUD or MAUD treatment, and significantly protective of alcohol intoxication among those with only MAUD treatment. These associations are consistent with and extend the findings in our previous research, which demonstrated that patients with existing OUD or AUD who were prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA had significantly lower rates of opioid overdose (aIRR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.43–0.83) and alcohol intoxication (aIRR = 0.50; 95% CI: 0.40–0.63) compared to those who were not prescribed29. While directionality of trends was largely consistent across several treatment group strata, associations were not significant and additional research is needed to confirm whether these results are clinical signals. Notably, the protective associations found for all-drug overdose were strongest when restricted to patients with T2DM being treated with only MAUD as well as both MOUD and MAUD, and also when restricted to patients with obesity treated with both MOUD and MAUD. Patients with OUD & AUD and active T2DM and obesity conditions comprise a population in which all three treatments are clinically relevant and may therefore experience greater reductions in overdose risk when their co-occurring conditions are treated more comprehensively. The lack of significant findings in GIP/GLP-1 RA associations across MOUD/MAUD groups for OOD and alcohol intoxication among those with T2DM and obesity could be attributed to a lack of sample size to achieve sufficient statistical power when restricting all-drug overdose to more granular components63. In general, the question of therapeutic incompatibility between GIP/GLP-1 RA and MOUD/MAUD can be posed. Although incompatibility is unlikely, as methadone is the only medication for substance use that is cautioned when taken with GLP-1 RA. When taken together, methadone and GLP-1 RA may exhibit an additive effect on glycemic control, causing low blood sugar, and GLP-1 RAs delay gastric emptying, which can impact absorption of oral medication (i.e., methadone)64,65. Additionally, there are a number of barriers preventing individuals in need from obtaining MOUD and MAUD19–22; and furthermore, adherence to and retention of substance use treatment medications among those actually being able to obtain treatment is low, with estimates around 50%66,67. This phenomenon has likely further contributed to lower capture of sample sizes among MOUD/MAUD-treated patients and is further restricted to those with T2DM or obesity. Notwithstanding, this study has revealed that GIP/GLP-1 RA may be a potential alternative treatment method among those with comorbid OUD and AUD, with the highest potential efficacy when combined with traditional SUD treatment methods in certain subgroups. However, in scenarios where traditional MOUD and MAUD are not viable options, GIP/GLP-1 RA may still be another potential alternative treatment method. We are cautious to note that randomized clinical trials are needed to confirm the comparative efficacy of these treatment methods in populations with co-occurring OUD and AUD.

When looking at other outcomes, MOUD/MAUD-treated subgroups exhibited stronger protective associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA and SUD-related hospitalization compared to those with no MOUD or MAUD treatment. The stronger protective effect seen in the treated subgroups suggests GIP/GLP-1 RA combined with MOUD or MAUD treatment may be particularly beneficial for preventing SUD-related hospitalizations. Independently, MOUD is associated with fewer hospitalizations68,69, MAUD significantly increases substance abstinence13,14, and GLP-1 RA reduce substance cravings and consumption37,70,71. Logically, patients taking these medications together may experience a compounding effect of reduced substance use, resulting in fewer hospitalizations. However, those treated singly with MOUD or MAUD or with both MOUD and MAUD had no significant associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA and positive drug screening and detoxification. While MOUD and MAUD have been proven to reduce substance use72,73, evidence does not indicate that these therapies often result in total substance detoxification. Research also shows that urine drug screening may decrease long-term retention in MOUD treatment programs74. It is possible that positive drug screens and detoxification are not the most suitable outcomes to evaluate success for individuals taking MOUD/MAUD. Future studies should investigate the impact of these medications on various substance use outcomes among patients with co-occurring SUD.

Regarding the liver function analyses, the biomarker trends suggest that some liver function indicators may be altered with GIP/GLP-1 RA use, with varying results among MOUD and MAUD-treated subgroups. Among GIP/GLP-1 RA patients, total protein and ALP levels were variable between treatment subgroups, while albumin displayed slightly higher values among non-treated and MAUD-only treated patients. Our results also show that patients with GLP-1 RA Rx display lower liver enzyme levels for ALT, AST, and bilirubin in all MOUD and MAUD treatment subgroups. Overall, these results indicate there is no strong evidence of sustained hepatic harm, which is consistent with the current understanding of side effects for MOUD, MAUD, and GIP/GLP-1 RA10,44,75. While our study provides useful foundational estimates, future research should explore in-depth short- and long-term liver functioning effects of GIP/GLP-1 RA independently and in conjunction with standard substance use treatments. Liver function monitoring is recommended annually for most individuals76; however, people with liver impairment and damage undergo more frequent monitoring of liver functioning and drug levels while taking MOUD or MAUD77,78. Heavy alcohol use is a possible confounder in this relationship that our analyses could not account for. Patients included in our liver biomarker analyses retained repeat laboratory values and likely consisted of a higher burdened subgroup of individuals undergoing more frequent monitoring. Additional studies should compare the effect of GIP/GLP-1 RA in individuals with normal liver function to those with liver impairment and liver damage.

The results of this study indicated that GIP/GLP-1 RA prescribed patients had a higher risk of liver condition diagnoses compared to non-prescribed patients among the subgroup with both MOUD and MAUD treatment in the overall sample and those with T2DM. The greater indications of liver conditions for GIP/GLP-1 RA users in the MOUD and MAUD-treated subgroup could be explained by increased diagnoses due to more frequent medical monitoring79 or pre-existing liver risk caused by genetic predisposition, diet/lifestyle behaviors, T2DM, or other conditions80. Also, among patients with MOUD and MAUD treatment, ALT and AST exhibited initial spikes for those prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA; however, these biomarker levels normalized over time. These results suggest GIP/GLP-1 RA may offer longer-term stabilization or improvement, even while possibly requiring an initial adjustment phase, rather than permanent biochemical deterioration. While this exploratory analysis has revealed novel associations between GIP/GLP-1 RA and liver outcomes to the best of our knowledge, these results should not be interpreted as clinical improvements in comorbid OUD and AUD. Together, the biomarker data suggest that GIP/GLP-1 RA use is not associated with sustained hepatotoxicity and may offer liver-related benefits in certain contexts. However, longitudinal research with histologic or imaging validation is needed to clarify clinical implications.

While the exact mechanisms of action are not fully understood, it is likely that GIP/GLP-1 RA medications modulate the dopamine reward pathway in the brain by interacting with the GLP-1 receptors found in this system81. The dopamine reward pathway is responsible for regulating reward processing and the development and maintenance of addictive behaviors through the release of dopamine82. The involvement of the reward pathway in food and drug consumption83,84, likely explains why GIP/GLP-1 RA are effective at reducing food consumption85,86 and drug consumption in animal models87–89. Additional research would further delineate the mechanistic pathways between GIP/GLP-1 RA drugs and substance use in humans, better informing the usage of these medications in substance use treatment.

There is also growing interest in using GIP/GLP-1 RA to treat nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), a common complication of liver disease90. A recent planned interim analysis from an ongoing phase 3 clinical trial among patients with biopsy-defined metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH, formerly termed NASH) and stage 2 or 3 fibrosis found that once-weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg improved liver histologic outcomes, including higher rates of steatohepatitis resolution without worsening of fibrosis and reduction in liver fibrosis without worsening of steatohepatitis, compared with placebo91. Another clinical trial also displayed improved liver enzyme levels, insulin sensitivity, and NASH resolution in patients receiving liraglutide with obesity compared to a placebo92. The direct mechanisms linking GIP/GLP-1 RA medications to the attenuation of NASH and other liver diseases are uncertain, largely because of the lack of GLP-1 receptor expression in hepatocytes93. Several studies suggest that the indirect metabolic effects of GIP/GLP-1 RA medications, such as weight loss, better glycemic control, reduced inflammation, decreased lipotoxicity, and improved metabolic function, could explain the improvements in NASH and other liver conditions92–94. More research is needed to understand the direct actions of GIP/GLP-1 RA in liver functioning.

We also observed a statistically significant increase in depression and anxiety diagnoses among GIP/GLP-1 RA users, particularly in the subgroup treated with both MOUD and MAUD, with consistent findings in both the overall sample and among patients with T2DM and obesity. The only significant increased risk for antidepressant prescriptions among GIP/GLP-1 RA users was seen among those not treated with MOUD or MAUD. Additionally, mental health crises, defined as inpatient or emergency diagnoses of depression or anxiety or any suicide-related diagnosis, were significantly lower among GIP/GLP-1 RA users treated with MAUD and marginally lower among those with obesity treated only with MAUD or untreated. These signals raise important questions about the potential neuropsychiatric effects of GIP/GLP-1 RA in substance-using populations. While increased rates of diagnosis may reflect underlying mood changes, they could also result from surveillance bias, a form of detection bias in which more frequent healthcare engagement among GIP/GLP-1 RA users increases the likelihood of mood-related assessments and documentation, even without true increases in underlying pathology95. Although our findings are consistent with a recent large-scale observational study that has reported increased risks of depression, anxiety, and suicidal behavior in GLP-1 RA users with obesity96, some studies identified GLP-1 RA as a protective factor for incident depression in patients with T2DM or controls97–99. Research also indicates metabolic, inflammatory, or central nervous system mechanisms influenced by GLP‑1 receptor activity may contribute to altered affective regulation, as GLP‑1 receptors are expressed in mood‑relevant brain regions and GLP‑1 RA modulate neurotrophic signaling, neurotransmitter systems, neuroinflammation, and neuronal survival100–102. However, the context of substance use disorder remains largely underexplored, and it is unclear whether such psychiatric effects differ by indication or comorbidity burden. Therefore, these findings underscore the need for targeted investigation into the neuropsychiatric safety and therapeutic potential of GIP/GLP-1 RA among patients with co-occurring substance use and metabolic conditions.

When assessing notable side-effect conditions of GIP/GLP-1 RA, those prescribed GIP/GLP-1 RA had a significantly higher risk of antiemetic prescriptions compared to those not prescribed, among the subgroup without any MOUD or MAUD treatment, but no associations were found in subgroups treated with MOUD or MAUD. This may reflect a higher propensity to experience side effects when not robustly treating one’s concurrent OUD & AUD condition. Furthermore, no associations were found between treatments and acute pancreatitis or gallbladder disease. Notwithstanding the exploratory nature of this analysis, it is again crucial to note that the potential advantages of GLP-1 RA must be weighed against the risks of adverse side-effects103,104, while also considering the impact of other SUD treatment options.

The results of this study build on the growing evidence that GIP/GLP-1 RA medications may have a wider range of application than originally believed. Our findings should encourage researchers to further investigate the effects of GIP/GLP-1 RA in substance use treatment. With support of additional confirmatory findings, our results suggest that GIP/GLP-1 RA could be considered for harm-reduction or adjunctive therapy in SUD patients, both untreated and those treated with MOUD/MAUD. As stated in our previous work29, these results encourage a re-evaluation of current SUD treatment strategies and policies to potentially include GIP/GLP-1 RA medications. Prospective clinical trials are needed to determine if our exploratory findings persist in rigorously controlled trials and to explore the efficacy and safety of GIP/GLP-1 RA in diverse populations.

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting the findings of this study. First, our study populations were limited by the health systems contributing to the OHRWD, and thus, the findings are not nationally representative. It is possible that certain groups of patients may be over- or underrepresented in our analyses due to various reasons, such as receiving care outside of contributing health systems, facing barriers to treatment, or having a higher comorbidity burden105–107. Second, most patients receiving GIP/GLP-1 RA medications have comorbid T2DM and obesity. Future studies should measure the effects of GIP/GLP-1 RA on substance use outcomes specifically among individuals without T2DM and obesity, as currently, results are largely generalizable to metabolically vulnerable OUD and AUD populations. Third, the OHRWD allows the analysis of patient prescriptions but does not provide information on the filling or usage of medications. Fourth, it is possible that a “Prescribing Physician Bias” may impact GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions, resulting in a reduced number of exposure indications in our examined patient sample. It is possible clinicians may be more likely to prescribe GIP/GLP-1 RA to patients they perceive as more stable or compliant, which introduces a “healthy user” bias that is difficult to adjust for statistically. Fifth, many other important confounders, such as socioeconomic status and specific SUD severity, are not routinely captured in EHR and cannot be explicitly accounted for in analyses. However, we attempted to capture alternative proxies for these variables where applicable. Sixth, liver biomarker analyses could not control for important confounders, like heavy alcohol consumption, and may only be generalizable to adverse cases rather than the full sample of those with OUD and AUD. Seventh, we did not account for follow-up opioid prescribing but rather controlled for a history of prescription up to 1-year prior to the index OUD and AUD. Lastly, the results of this study are only as accurate as the data entered by clinicians and the algorithms used to define all conditions and treatments.

Conclusions

The results of this study, examining the associations of MOUD, MAUD, and GIP/GLP-1 RA medications with adverse health outcomes, underscore the potential for innovative treatment strategies in substance use care. The protective associations observed between GIP/GLP-1 RA prescriptions and outcomes such as all-drug overdose, SUD-related hospitalizations, and detoxification expand upon previous findings in this area. While these results offer promising evidence for alternative therapeutic options, further research is needed to confirm and extend these findings across diverse patient subgroups with varying comorbidities. Importantly, the observed increases in liver-related diagnoses, depression, and anxiety raise safety concerns that require cautious interpretation and merit further investigation.
