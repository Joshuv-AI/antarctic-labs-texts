Introduction

Determining whether a graph contains a Hamilton cycle (i.e., a cycle that visits every vertex of the graph) is one of the classical NP-complete problems listed in Karp’s seminal paper
[
31
]
. Therefore, it has been an important direction of research in graph theory to study natural conditions that ensure Hamiltonicity. An early and influential such result was obtained in 1952 by Dirac
[
19
]
who showed that every
n
-vertex graph with minimum degree at least
\frac{n}{2}
contains a Hamilton cycle. This minimum degree condition is best possible. Inspired by Dirac’s theorem, numerous results have appeared in the literature throughout the last few decades (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
13
,
17
,
18
,
36
]
).

1.1.
‘Discrepancy’ results on Hamilton cycles

Discrepancy
is a fundamental notion in mathematics that studies the ‘irregularity’ in a distribution. This notion has found applications in diverse areas such as ergodic theory, number theory, statistics, geometry, and computer science.
In combinatorial discrepancy theory, the central question is to study, for any vertex-colouring of a given hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
with two colours, existence of an unbalanced hyperedge (i.e., a hyperedge with significantly more vertices in one colour than the other).
In this paper, we study such questions in the context of graphs, where the vertex set of
\mathcal{H}
is the edge set of some given graph
G
and the hyperedges of
\mathcal{H}
correspond to subgraphs of
G
with a given type. These questions have a rich and influential history dating back to Erdős
[
20
,
21
]
.

We will consider colourings with two or more colours. An
r
-edge-colouring
of a graph
G
refers to an assignment of
r
colours to the edge set of
G
.
In this paper, we are interested in the following problem. Given graphs
G
and
H
, determine the largest
t
such that any
r
-edge-colouring of
G
contains a copy of
H
with at least
t
edges of the same colour. This problem has been recently studied in
[
5
,
22
,
26
]
when

is a Hamilton cycle or a spanning tree. For Hamilton cycles, the following result is established.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
5
,

,

]
)

Let

.
For all

\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r}+\varepsilon\right)n

\left(\frac{1}{r}+\delta\right)n
times.

This was shown to be best possible by constructing
n

\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r}\right)n
with an

-edge-colouring of it such that there is no Hamilton cycle where some colour appears more than
\frac{n}{r}
times. In this context, there is a recent stability result

Let

1.2.
Resilience of Hamilton cycles in random graphs

Studying Hamiltonicity has been an active area of research in the field of random graphs. In this paper, we will focus on the Erdős–Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
on
n
vertices where every possible edge is present independently with probability
p
.
The study of Hamiltonicity in the random graph
G(n,p)
was pioneered by the breakthrough work of Pósa
[
42
]
, who introduced the versatile
Pósa’s rotation-extension technique
, and was subsequently advanced by Koršunov
[
34
]
. Their results were sharpened by Bollobás
[
8
]
and Komlós–Szemerédi
[
33
]
who proved that the threshold for
G(n,p)
to contain a Hamilton cycle is at
p=\frac{\log{n}+\log\log{n}}{n}
.
A striking result in this area says that the ‘bottleneck’ for the random graph to contain a Hamilton cycle is having minimum degree
2
. This concept is made precise in the hitting time results of Ajtai–Komlós–Szemerédi
[
1
]
and Bollobás
[
8
]
. For more on this, see
Section

1.4
. For a nice introduction to this area along with shorter proofs of many of these results, see the survey
[
35
]
. For other related problems and a historical overview of this area, we suggest the readers to consult the survey
[
23
]
.

One way to view Dirac’s theorem is that if one starts with a complete graph on
n
vertices and deletes fewer than half of the edges incident to every vertex, then the residual graph contains a Hamilton cycle. Such a phenomenon was also shown to be true for random graphs above the Hamiltonicity threshold by Lee and Sudakov
[
37
]
. Sudakov and Vu
[
45
]
initiated the systematic study of such phenomena in random graphs using the terminology of ‘resilience’ of a given graph property. For some results on this topic, see, e.g.
[
2
,
4
,
6
,
12
,
16
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
37
,

]

There are two main types of resilience that are studied: (i) global resilience and (ii) local resilience. In this paper, we focus on the latter and say a graph

is

if for every subgraph

, the graph

. In our paper, the property we are interested in is Hamiltonicity. Lee and Sudakov
[
37
]
showed that the random graph

with

above the Hamiltonicity threshold is
(1/2-o(1))
-resiliently Hamiltonian w.h.p.

1.4.
Hitting time result

We adapt the techniques we used to prove
Theorem

1.7
in order to prove a similar statement in the random graph process, leading to a more precise version of the theorem. Before stating that, we start with some basic definitions and classical hitting time results on Hamiltonicity.

The
n
-vertex random graph process
is a sequence of graphs
\{G_{n\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptM}\}_{M\geq 0}=G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{\binom{n}{2}}
, each with vertex set
V
of size
n
, such that
G_{0}
contains no edges and, for each
i\in[\binom{n}{2}]
,
G_{i}
is formed by adding an edge to
G_{i-1}
uniformly at random from the non-adjacent pairs of vertices of
G_{i-1}
. We say that an event
holds in almost every random graph process
if w.h.p. the event holds in every graph of the random graph process
\{G_{n\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptM}\}_{M\geq 0}
.

Clearly, we can also form the above random graph process by giving an ordering of the pairs of vertices uniformly at random, and then adding the edges to the empty graph with vertex set
V
in this order. All sets of
M
edges are equally likely to appear as the first
M
edges in this ordering; thus for each fixed

,

is a random graph on
n
vertices with
M
edges chosen uniformly at random.

As briefly mentioned earlier, Ajtai–Komlós–Szemerédi
[
1
]
and Bollobás
[
8
]
showed that the following happens in almost every random graph process: the edge that increases the minimum degree from
1
in
G_{n,M-1}
to
2
in
G_{n,M}
, for some
M
, also creates a Hamilton cycle in
G_{n,M}
. It turns out that this obstacle not only makes the graph Hamiltonian, but also makes it resiliently Hamiltonian as shown by Montgomery
[
39
]
and Nenadov–Steger–Trujić
[
40
]
. They proved the following result, which is a hitting time version of
Theorem

1.4
. The minimum degree of a graph
G
is denoted by
\delta(G)
.

Theorem 1.9

(
[
39
,
40
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then, in almost every random graph process
\{G_{n\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptM}\}_{M\geq 0}
, if

, then every
(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon)
-residual spanning subgraph of

contains a Hamilton cycle.

The hitting time version of
Theorem

1.7
is as follows.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let

,
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r},1]
, and

. Then, in almost every

, then for every

(1-\varepsilon)\min\{(2\alpha-1)n\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\frac{2\alpha n}{r}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\frac{2n}{r+1}\}

1.5.
Ramsey-type results on matchings

A key ingredient of our proofs of the main results is a Ramsey-type statement about finding large monochromatic matchings in edge-coloured graphs with a given minimum degree. Before stating it, we briefly mention some known results along this line.

A classical result of Cockayne and Lorimer
[
15
]
implies that every
r
-edge-colouring of the complete graph
K_{n}
contains a monochromatic matching of size at least
\frac{n-1}{r+1}
. Gishboliner, Krivelevich, and Michaeli
[
25
]
extended this to almost complete host graphs and used that to prove their
Theorem

1.6
. We strengthen these results and obtain the following pleasing statement.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
G
be an
r
-edge-coloured
n
-vertex graph with minimum degree
d
. Then
G
contains a monochromatic matching with at least
\min\left\{\frac{d}{r},\frac{n-1}{r+1}\right\}
edges.

This is best possible as shown by matching constructions in
Section

2
. A particularly nice corollary of
Theorem

1.11
is that every
r
-edge-coloured
n
-vertex graph with minimum degree at least
\frac{r(n-1)}{r+1}
contains a monochromatic matching of size at least
\frac{n-1}{r+1}
. This significantly improves the previously mentioned result of Cockayne and Lorimer
[
15
]
. On a different note, a related ‘Ramsey-Turán’ variant of this result is established in a recent preprint
[
32
]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
n,r\geq 2
be integers,
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r},1)
,
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let also

-edge-colouring of an
\alpha
-residual spanning subgraph of

(1+\varepsilon)k_{\alpha}

Fix
r\geq 2
,
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r},1)
,
\varepsilon>0
, and let
n
be a sufficiently large integer relative to
r,\alpha,\varepsilon
. Let
\delta=\min\{\frac{1-\alpha}{4\alpha}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\frac{(2\alpha-1)\varepsilon}{8\alpha}\}
and let
\alpha^{\prime}
be such that
(1+\delta)\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}\leq(1+2\delta)\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}n
is an integer. Note that
\alpha^{\prime}\in[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r},1)
. Let

be the
r
-edge-coloured graph on the same vertex set as

obtained from applying
Proposition

2.5
with

(in place of

be the

, where the colouring of

is the restriction of the colouring of

to the edges of

. We claim that w.h.p.

is an
\alpha
-residual spanning subgraph of

. Indeed, by Chernoff’s bound (see,

), w.h.p. the following hold for every vertex

\displaystyle d_{G^{\prime}}(v)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{\delta}{3}\right)d_{F}(v)p,

and

\displaystyle d_{G}(v)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\delta}{3}\right)np.

If

\mathbb{P}\left(|X-\mathbb{E}X|\geq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}X\right)\leq 2\exp{\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}X}{3}\right)}.\qed

\delta_{i}\in\{1\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt2\}

Lemma 3.4

Let

4.
Large monochromatic matchings in dense graphs

We prove
Theorem

1.11
in this section. For this, we will prove the following equivalent result.

Theorem 4.1

(Large monochromatic matching)
.

Let
G
be an
r
-edge-coloured
n
-vertex graph with minimum degree
\delta(G)\leq\frac{rn}{r+1}
. Then
G
contains a monochromatic matching with at least
\frac{\delta(G)}{r}
edges.

We first show how
Theorem

1.11
follows from
Theorem

4.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.11
assuming
Theorem

4.1
.

Let
G
be an
r
-edge-coloured
n
-vertex graph with minimum degree
d
. If
d\leq\frac{rn}{r+1}
, apply
Theorem

4.1
on
G
to obtain a monochromatic matching with at least
\frac{d}{r}\geq\min\{\frac{d}{r}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\frac{n-1}{r+1}\}
edges, as desired. Otherwise,
G
has minimum degree
d>\frac{rn}{r+1}
. Then, first consider a spanning subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
such that
\delta(G^{\prime})=\lfloor\frac{rn}{r+1}\rfloor
. (Such a subgraph can be obtained by removing edges of
G
one by one, and noting that removal of an edge can decrease the minimum degree by at most
1
.) Now, we apply
Theorem

4.1
on
G^{\prime}
to obtain a monochromatic matching with at least
\left\lceil\frac{\lfloor\frac{rn}{r+1}\rfloor}{r}\right\rceil\geq\frac{n-1}{r+1}
edges. Thus,
G
also contains a matching with at least
\frac{n-1}{r+1}\geq\min\{\frac{d}{r}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\frac{n-1}{r+1}\}
edges, as desired.
∎

We now proceed to prove
Theorem

4.1
. For that, we will first introduce some shorthands and state some lemmas that will later facilitate our arguments. For a graph
G
, denote by
\mu(G)
the matching number of
G
, denote by
c(G)
the number of connected components of
G
, and denote by
\odd(G)
the number of connected components of
G
whose size is odd.

Theorem 4.2

(Tutte-Berge formula)
.

Every graph
G
satisfies

\mu(G)=\frac{|V(G)|}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\max_{U\in V(G)}\left(\odd\left(G-U\right)-|U|\right).

Lemma 4.3
.

For every
n
-vertex graph
G
, there exists a set of vertices
U\subseteq V(G)
of size
|U|\leq\mu(G)
such
that
c(G-U)\geq n-2\mu(G)+|U|
.

Proof.

By the Tutte-Berge formula, we have

\mu(G)=\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\max_{U^{\prime}\in V(G)}\left(\odd\left(G-U^{\prime}\right)-|U^{\prime}|\right)\geq\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\max_{U^{\prime}\in V(G)}\left(c\left(G-U^{\prime}\right)-|U^{\prime}|\right).

Then
\max_{U^{\prime}\in V(G)}\left(c\left(G-U^{\prime}\right)-|U^{\prime}|\right)\geq n-2\mu(G)
. Let
U
be the subset of
V(G)
that maximizes the left-hand side of this expression. We then have that
c(G-U)\geq n-2\mu(G)+|U|
. Also, clearly
n-|U|\geq c(G-U)\geq n-2\mu(G)+|U|
, which implies that
|U|\leq\mu(G)
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Every graph
G
satisfies

c(G)\leq\sum_{v\in V(G)}\frac{1}{d_{G}(v)+1}.

Proof.

For a vertex
v
of
G
, denote by
C_{v}
the size of the connected component of
G
that contains
v
. Clearly
|C_{v}|\geq d_{G}(v)+1
. Therefore,

\sum_{v\in V(G)}\frac{1}{d_{G}(v)+1}\geq\sum_{v\in V(G)}\frac{1}{|C_{v}|}=c(G).

∎

Lemma 4.5

(Lemma 1 in Section 2 from
[
25
]
)
.

Every graph
G
satisfies

e(G)\leq\binom{|V(G)|-c(G)+1}{2}.

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

4.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Let
G
be an
r
-edge-coloured
n
-vertex graph with minimum degree
\delta(G)\leq\frac{rn}{r+1}
. For each
i\in[r]
, let
G_{i}
denote the graph with vertex set
V(G)
and whose edges are all edges of
G
with colour
i
. Thus,
E(G)=\bigcup_{i\in[r]}E(G_{i})
. Let
\mu=\max_{i\in[r]}\mu(G_{i})
denote the size of the largest monochromatic matching of
G
.

Note that
Theorem

4.1
is trivial if
\delta(G)=0
and so we may assume
\delta(G)\geq 1
. Suppose for contradiction that
\mu<\frac{\delta(G)}{r}
. Hence,

(4.1)

\mu\leq\frac{\delta(G)-1}{r}.

In addition, if
\delta(G)\geq 1
then clearly
\mu\geq 1
, which implies

(4.2)

r+1\leq\delta(G)<n.

By applying
Lemma

4.3
to the graphs
G_{i}
, with
i\in[r]
, there is a set
U_{i}\subseteq V(G)
of size
|U_{i}|\leq\mu
such that
c(G_{i}-U_{i})\geq n-2\mu+|U_{i}|
.
Let
U=V(G)\setminus\cup_{i\in[r]}U_{i}
. We have

(4.3)

|U|\geq n-\sum_{i\in[r]}|U_{i}|.

We also have the following for every
i\in[r]
,

\displaystyle c(G_{i}[U])\geq c(G_{i}-U_{i})-c\left(\left(G_{i}-U_{i}\right)-U\right)

\displaystyle\geq n-2\mu+|U_{i}|-\left(n-|U_{i}|-|U|\right)

(4.4)

\displaystyle=|U|-2\mu+2|U_{i}|.

We next make a few claims.

Claim 4.5.1
.

n-r\mu+1\leq|U|\leq 4\mu+1.

Proof of
claim.

Since
|U_{i}|\leq\mu
for every
i\in[r]
, we have
|U|\geq n-\sum_{i\in[r]}|U_{i}|\geq n-r\mu
. By
Lemma

4.5
, for every
i\in[r]
, we have

\displaystyle 2e(G_{i}[U])\leq\left(|U|-c(G_{i}[U])+1\right)\left(|U|-c\left(G_{i}[U]\right)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:low_bound_CGiU}}}{{\leq}}\left(2\mu-2|U_{i}|+1\right)\left(2\mu-2|U_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(2\mu+1\right)\left(\mu-|U_{i}|\right).

Using this, we also have that

\displaystyle r\mu|U|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:up_bound_mu}}}{{<}}\delta(G)|U|\leq\sum_{v\in U}d_{G}(v)

\displaystyle\leq|U|\left(n-|U|\right)+\sum_{i\in[r]}2e(G_{i}[U])

\displaystyle\leq|U|\left(n-|U|\right)+2(2\mu+1)\left(r\mu-\sum_{i\in[r]}|U_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:low_bound_U}}}{{\leq}}|U|\left(n-|U|\right)+2(2\mu+1)\left(r\mu-n+|U|\right).

Therefore,
|U|\left(r\mu-n+|U|\right)<2(2\mu+1)\left(r\mu-n+|U|\right)
. Since
r\mu-n+|U|\geq 0
, this implies that
|U|>n-r\mu
and
|U|<2(2\mu+1)
, which concludes the proof of the claim.

\blacklozenge

Claim 4.5.2
.

For all
i\in[r]
, we have
c(G_{i}[U])>1
.

Proof of
claim.

We have

\displaystyle c(G_{i}[U])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:low_bound_CGiU}}}{{\geq}}|U|-2\mu+2|U_{i}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:low_bound_U}}}{{\geq}}n-\sum_{j\in[r]}|U_{j}|-2\mu+2|U_{i}|

\displaystyle\geq n-2\mu-\sum_{j\in[r]\setminus\{i\}}|U_{j}|

\displaystyle\geq n-2\mu-(r-1)\mu

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:up_bound_mu}}}{{\geq}}n-(r+1)\left(\frac{\delta(G)-1}{r}\right)

\displaystyle\geq n-\frac{r+1}{r}\left(\frac{r}{r+1}n-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{r+1}{r}>1.

\blacklozenge

Claim 4.5.3
.

For every vertex
v\in U
and every
i\in[r]
, we have
d_{G_{i}[U]}(v)\leq d_{G[U]}(v)-2
.

Proof of
claim.

Let
t=\sum_{j\in[r]}\left(\mu-|U_{j}|\right)
. Observe first that every vertex
v\in U
satisfies

(4.5)

d_{G[U]}(v)\geq\delta(G)-\sum_{j\in[r]}|U_{j}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:up_bound_mu}}}{{\geq}}1+\mu r-\sum_{j\in[r]}|U_{j}|=1+\sum_{j\in[r]}\left(\mu-|U_{j}|\right)=1+t.

For every vertex
v\in U
and every
i\in[r]
, denote by
C_{i,u}
the vertex-set of the connected component of
G_{i}[U]
that contains
v
.

Suppose for a contradiction that there is some
v\in U
and
i\in[r]
such that
d_{G_{i}[U]}(v)\geq d_{G[U]}(v)-1
. By
4.5.2
, the graph
G_{i}[U]
has at least two components. Let
u
be a vertex in
U\setminus C_{i,v}
, so that
C_{i,u}\neq C_{i,v}
.
Observe that

(4.6)

|C_{i,v}|\geq 1+d_{G_{i}[U]}(v)\geq d_{G[U]}(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:low_deg_GU_v}}}{{\geq}}1+t.

Furthermore, we have

(4.7)

\sum_{j\in[r]}|C_{j,u}|\geq\sum_{j\in[r]}\left(1+d_{G_{j}[U]}(u)\right)=r+d_{G[U]}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:low_deg_GU_v}}}{{\geq}}r+t+1.

Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[r]}c(G_{j}[U])=c(G_{i}[U])+\sum_{j\in[r]\setminus\{i\}}c(G_{j}[U])

\displaystyle\leq 2+\left(|U|-|C_{i,v}|-|C_{i,u}|\right)+\sum_{j\in[r]\setminus\{i\}}\left(1+|U|-|C_{j,u}|\right)

\displaystyle=r+1+r|U|-|C_{i,v}|-\sum_{j\in[r]}|C_{j,u}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:low_C_iv},\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:low_sum_C_ju}}}{{\leq}}r|U|-2t-1.

However, we also have that

\sum_{j\in[r]}c(G_{j}[U])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:low_bound_CGiU}}}{{\geq}}\sum_{j\in[r]}\left(|U|-2\mu+2|U_{j}|\right)=r|U|-2t,

a contradiction. This concludes the proof of
4.5.3
.

\blacklozenge

Claim 4.5.4
.

2(n-r\mu)\leq\left(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{|U|-n+r\mu}\right)|U|.

Proof of
claim.

We denote
d_{i}(v)=d_{G_{i}[U]}(v)
. By
Lemma

4.4
, we have

c(G_{i}[U])\leq\sum_{v\in U}\frac{1}{d_{i}(v)+1}
for every
i\in[r]
, and therefore

\sum_{i\in[r]}c(G_{i}[U])\leq\sum_{i\in[r]}\sum_{v\in U}\frac{1}{d_{i}(v)+1}=\sum_{v\in U}\sum_{i\in[r]}\frac{1}{d_{i}(v)+1}.

Observe that
\sum_{i\in[r]}d_{i}(v)=d_{G[U]}(v)
, and by
4.5.3
, we have
d_{i}(v)\leq d_{G[U]}(v)-2
for all
i\in[r]
.
Note that for any numbers
0<x\leq y
, we have
\frac{1}{x+1}+\frac{1}{y+1}<\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{y+2}
. This tells us that for
r
variables
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
with a fixed sum satisfying
x_{i}\leq z
for every
i\in[r]
, the expression
\sum_{i\in[r]}\frac{1}{x_{i}+1}
attains its maximum value when every
x_{i}
except at most one equals
z
or
0
.
In particular, we conclude that the maximum of
\sum_{i\in[r]}\frac{1}{d_{i}(v)+1}
is at most the value of this expression when
d_{i}(v)
are all zero except for one equal to
2
and one equal to
d_{G[U]}(v)-2
. Thus,

\sum_{i\in[r]}\frac{1}{d_{i}(v)+1}\leq\frac{r-2}{1}+\frac{1}{2+1}+\frac{1}{d_{G[U]}(v)-2+1}\leq r-2+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{|U|-n+r\mu},

where we have used that
d_{G[U]}(v)-1\geq\delta(G)-1-(n-|U|)\geq r\mu-n+|U|
by (
4.1
) (note that
|U|-n+r\mu>0
by
4.5.1
). In particular,

\sum_{i\in[r]}c(G_{i}[U])\leq\sum_{v\in U}\left(r-2+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{|U|-n+r\mu}\right)=|U|\left(r-2+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{|U|-n+r\mu}\right).

We also have that

\sum_{i\in[r]}c\left(G_{i}[U]\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle~\eqref{eq:low_bound_CGiU}}}{{\geq}}\sum_{i\in[r]}\left(|U|-2\mu+2|U_{i}|\right)=r|U|-2r\mu+2\sum_{i\in[r]}|U_{i}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle~\eqref{eq:low_bound_U}}}{{\geq}}(r-2)|U|+2(n-r\mu).

Combining the above inequalities, we obtain

(r-2)|U|+2(n-r\mu)\leq|U|\left(r-2+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{|U|-n+r\mu}\right),

and canceling the
(r-2)|U|
from both sides completes the proof of the claim.

\blacklozenge

We will now show that the two inequalities of
4.5.1
and
4.5.4
cannot hold simultaneously. Let
x=n-r\mu
, noting that

(4.8)

x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:up_bound_mu}}}{{\geq}}n-\delta(G)+1\geq\frac{n}{r+1}+1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:bound_n}}}{{>}}2.

Let
y=|U|-n+r\mu
, noting that by
4.5.1
,

1\leq y\leq 4\mu+1-n+r\mu.

By (
4.1
), we have
\mu\leq\frac{\delta(G)-1}{r}\leq\frac{n}{r+1}-\frac{1}{r}
. Substituting this in, we have

(4.9)

1\leq y\leq(r+4)\left(\frac{n}{r+1}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+1-n<\frac{3n}{r+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:bound_x}}}{{<}}3x.

Now, the inequality of
4.5.4
can be rewritten in terms of
x
and
y
as

2x\leq\left(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{y}\right)(x+y),

which we can rearrange to get

0\leq y^{2}-5yx+3y+3x=-(y-1)(3x-y)-2y(x-2).

By (
4.9
), we see that
y-1\geq 0
and
3x-y>0
. Moreover, by (
4.8
) we also have

. Therefore we conclude that
-(y-1)(3x-y)-2y(x-2)<0
, which is a contradiction.

4.1
.
∎

5.
Large monochromatic linear forests and proof of
Theorem

1.2

In this section, we execute Step 2 as described in
Section

2.2
. Recall that a forest is called a
linear forest
if each of its connected components is a path. The main result of this section is
Theorem

5.2
, which finds a large monochromatic linear forest in an edge-coloured pseudorandom graph. Similar to
[
25
]
, instead of random graphs, we work with more general pseudorandom graphs.

We will now introduce some useful notation. Suppose
G
is a graph. For two disjoint non-empty sets
V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq V(G)
, the density of
(V_{1},V_{2})
is defined as
d_{G}(V_{1},V_{2})=\frac{e_{G}(V_{1},V_{2})}{|V_{1}||V_{2}|}
. For
\gamma,p\in(0,1]
, the graph
G
is called
(\gamma,p)
-pseudorandom
if for every disjoint non-empty sets
V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq V(G)
with
|V_{1}|,|V_{2}|\geq\gamma|V(G)|
, we have
|d_{G}(V_{1},V_{2})-p|\leq\gamma p
. Define the density of
V
as
d_{G}(V)=\frac{e_{G}(V)}{\binom{|V|}{2}}
. We also note the following well-known fact (for a proof, see e.g.
[
25
, Section 3]
).

Lemma 5.1

(
[
25
]
)
.

If
G
is a
(\gamma,p)
-pseudorandom graph, then every set
V\subseteq V(G)
of size at least
2\gamma|V|
satisfies
|d_{G}(V)-p|\leq\gamma p
.

For convenience, we will use the usual hierarchy notation throughout this section. For example, we say
\alpha\ll\beta,\gamma
to mean that there is a non-decreasing function
g:(0,1]^{2}\rightarrow(0,1]
such that
\alpha\leq g(\beta,\gamma)
. When there are multiple numbers in a hierarchy, they are chosen from right to left. We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose
0<\frac{1}{n}\ll\frac{1}{C},\gamma\ll\alpha,\varepsilon,\frac{1}{r}\leq 1
. Let
G
be an
n
-vertex
(\gamma,p)
-pseudorandom graph for some
p\in(0,1]
, and
H
be an
\alpha
-residual spanning subgraph of
G
. Then, for every
r
-edge-colouring of
H
, there is a monochromatic linear forest of size
(1-\varepsilon)\min\{\frac{2\alpha n}{r}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\frac{2n}{r+1}\}
consisting of at most
C
paths.

We use the above result to prove
Theorem

1.2
at the end of this section. Before proving
Theorem

5.2
, we first prove the following corollary, which will later be useful in the proof of
Theorems

1.7
and
1.10
. Note that the range of probability

is relaxed below compared to what we assume in
Theorem

1.7
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
r
be a positive integer, and
\alpha,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Then there exists
C>0
such that if
p=p(n)\geq\frac{C}{n}
and
G\sim G(n,p)
, then with probability
1-o(n^{-3}),
for every
r
-edge-colouring of every
\alpha
-residual spanning subgraph of
G
, there is a monochromatic linear forest of size
(1-\varepsilon)\min\{\frac{2\alpha n}{r}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\frac{2n}{r+1}\}
consisting of at most
C
paths.

To prove this, we will use the following standard lemma, which says that random graphs are pseudorandom with appropriate parameters.

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose
0<\frac{1}{C}\ll\gamma\leq 1
. Let
G\sim G(n,p)
with
p\geq\frac{C}{n}
. Then with probability
1-o(n^{-3})
, the graph
G
is
(\gamma,p)
-pseudorandom.

Proof.

Every two disjoint sets
A,B\subseteq V(G)
with
|A|,|B|\geq\gamma n
satisfy

p|A||B|\geq p\gamma^{2}n^{2}\geq C\gamma^{2}n\geq\frac{100n}{\gamma^{2}},

where the last inequality uses the fact that
\frac{1}{C}\ll\gamma
. Thus, by applying
Lemma

3.5
with
\lambda=\gamma
, we obtain that the following happens with probability
1-o(n^{-3})
. Every disjoint
A,B\subseteq V(G)
with
|A|,|B|\geq\gamma n
satisfy
e_{G}(A,B)=(1\pm\gamma)p|A||B|
. This proves the lemma.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

5.3
.

Let
r,\alpha,\varepsilon
be given as in the statement of
Corollary

5.3
. Choose
C,\gamma
such that
0<\frac{1}{C}\ll\gamma\ll\alpha,\varepsilon
. If
p\geq\frac{C}{n}
,
Lemma

5.4
implies that with probability

, the graph
G
is
(\gamma,p)
-pseudorandom. Then applying

5.2
gives the result.
∎

We next focus on proving
Theorem

5.2
, for which we use the following ‘multicolour’ variant of the sparse regularity lemma. The same version was also used in
[
25
]
.

We now introduce a few definitions for convenience. Consider a graph
G
. A pair
(V_{1},V_{2})
of disjoint vertex-sets is called
(\delta,q)
-regular
if for every
V^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq V_{1}
and

with

for all

|d(V^{\prime}_{1},V^{\prime}_{2})-d(V_{1},V_{2})|\leq\delta q
. A partition of a set is called an

if the sizes of any two parts differ by at most
1
.
Let
G_{1},\dots,G_{r}
be graphs on the same vertex set

\mathcal{P}
, we may (and do) assume that the monochromatic linear forest
\mathcal{P}
spans a set
S\subseteq V(H)
of size
|S|=(1-\varepsilon_{0})k
and
\mathcal{P}
still has at most
C
paths. Thus,
e(\mathcal{P})\geq(1-\varepsilon_{0})k-C
.

We now check the hypotheses of
Theorem

6.2
about
H
, namely
A1
and
A2
. To see
A1
, we use our assumption that
\delta(G)\geq 2
to deduce that every vertex
v\in V(H)
satisfies
d_{H}(v)\geq\alpha d_{G}(v)\geq 2\alpha>1
. To see
A2
, first note that every vertex
v\in V(H)
with
d_{H}(v)\leq\frac{np}{5000}
satisfies
d_{G}(v)\leq\frac{d_{H}(v)}{\alpha}\leq\frac{np}{2500}\leq\frac{M}{1000n}
. This observation, together with the assumption of (ii), implies the validity of
A2
.

While the remainder of the proof is identical to that of
Theorem

1.7
, we include the details again for completeness. By the conclusion of
Theorem

6.2
with
(\varepsilon,C,\delta)=(\varepsilon_{0},C,\varepsilon_{1})
, we obtain a linear forest
\mathcal{P}^{*}
covering a set
T\supseteq S
such that, by letting
U=V(H)\setminus T
, the properties in
E1
–
E6
hold.

We now define a matching
\mathcal{M}
on
U
by adding an edge between two vertices in
U
if they are endpoints of a path in
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. Observe that, since
E5
holds and
\mathcal{P}^{*}
covers
T
, if we find a Hamilton cycle in the graph
H[U]+\mathcal{M}
that contains all edges of
\mathcal{M}
, we can then replace each edge of the cycle that is also an edge of
\mathcal{M}
by its corresponding monochromatic path in
\mathcal{P}^{*}
to obtain a Hamilton cycle in
H
containing all the paths in
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. Then, by
E3
, all but at most
\varepsilon_{1}n
edges of
\mathcal{P}
are included in this Hamilton cycle, implying that the number of edges of
\mathcal{P}
in the cycle is at least
e(\mathcal{P})-\varepsilon_{1}n\geq(1-\varepsilon_{0})k-\varepsilon_{1}n\geq(1-\varepsilon)k
, and all of these edges are of the same colour. Therefore, it is enough to find a Hamilton cycle in the graph
H[U]+\mathcal{M}
that contains all edges in
\mathcal{M}
.

We now wish to apply the conclusion of
Theorem

7.1
with
(\varepsilon,K)=(\varepsilon_{1},K)
on the set
U
in place of

and the graph
H[U]
in place of
H
. The required hypotheses follows from
E1
,
E2
,
E4
with the fact that
|V(\mathcal{M})|
is at most twice the number of paths in
\mathcal{P}^{*}
, and finally
E6
with the fact that
W=V(\mathcal{M})
. Thus, the conclusion of
Theorem

7.1
obtains a Hamilton cycle in the graph
H[U]+\mathcal{M}
that contains all edges of
\mathcal{M}
, as desired. This completes the proof of
Theorem

1.10
.
