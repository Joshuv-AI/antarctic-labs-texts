Academic Stress and Temporomandibular Disorder Symptoms in Dental Students: Preliminary Cross-Sectional Study Findings on Prevalence, Severity, and Associated Factors
Authors: Basem H AL-Huthaifi, Ehab M Abdu, Abdullmalik N Al-Hamati, Mohammed M Al Moaleem, Khalid Aldhorae, Baker M Abdullah, Ali H Al-Surri, Helal Y Al-sabri, Saleh L Alnamer, Awab M Al-Hattami, Talal M Alghurbani, Mohannad A AL-Safani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTemporomandibular disorders (TMD) are prevalent among dental students who are exposed to significant academic stressors. However, the relationship between stress severity and TMD symptomatology remains poorly characterized. This study assessed the prevalence of TMD symptoms, academic stress, and their associations among dental students.MethodsThis multicenter, classroom-based survey invited 850 undergraduate dental students at five universities. Of 792 returned questionnaires (return rate, 93.2%), 756 were included in the final analysis (88.9% of those invited). TMD symptoms were screened with the Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI), academic stress with a locally adapted 10-item Dental Environment Stress Scale (DESS)-derived measure, and six selected Brief COPE items for coping strategies. The multivariable logistic model included age, gender, year of study, marital status, sleep duration, prior TMD diagnosis, screen time, caffeine intake, and physical activity.ResultsFAI-positive TMD symptoms (FAI ≥20) were reported by 56.6% of participants. Academic stress was moderate in 43.8% and severe in 27.5% of the participants. In adjusted analysis, female gender (AOR=1.62, 95% CI: 1.15–2.29, p=0.006) and a self-reported prior TMD diagnosis (AOR=3.32, 95% CI: 2.15–5.12, p<0.001) were associated with current FAI screen positivity. Sleep deprivation (<5 h/night) was associated with higher stress (p<0.001), but not with TMD risk. Model performance was modest (Nagelkerke R2=0.105; Hosmer-Lemeshow p=0.724). The most frequently employed coping strategies were religious/spiritual beliefs (mean 3.38±0.75) and concentrating efforts (3.15±0.72).ConclusionSelf-reported TMD symptoms and academic stress were common and co-occurred in this sample. Because exposure and outcome were measured concurrently and no clinical TMD examination was performed, the associations are preliminary and should not be interpreted as causal, predictive, or diagnostic. Longitudinal studies incorporating DC/TMD examinations are needed before preventive or screening effects can be inferred.Keywords: temporomandibular disorders, academic stress, dental students, Fonseca Anamnestic Index, dental environment stress scale, coping strategies

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) comprise a heterogeneous group of musculoskeletal and neuromuscular conditions affecting the temporomandibular joints (TMJ), masticatory muscles, and associated structures.1,2 Pain, joint sounds, and mandibular functional limitations are common manifestations. Global syntheses indicate substantial symptom burden, although prevalence varies by population and, importantly, by whether self-report screening or standardized clinical diagnosis is used.3–5

Dental education combines intensive coursework, prolonged study, fine-motor training, examinations, and clinical responsibility, all of which may contribute to psychological strain.6–9 Published estimates of TMD among dental students vary widely, including 36.99% in a Saudi sample assessed with DC/TMD and 77.5% in a Turkish sample studied during the COVID-19 period.10,11

A biopsychosocial model is more appropriate than a unidirectional mechanical explanation of TMD. Biological susceptibility, pain processing, psychological distress, sleep, parafunctional behaviors, and social context may interact.12–18 Stress may accompany muscle tension, clenching, and altered pain perception; conversely, persistent craniofacial pain and TMD symptoms may disrupt sleep and intensify anxiety or academic stress. Psychosocial impairment also correlates with clinically assessed TMD in some populations.19

Evidence from Yemen remains scarce. In particular, multicenter estimates of self-reported TMD symptoms and academic stress among Yemeni dental students, together with adjusted analyses of demographic and health-related correlates, are limited. Coping behaviors may also contextualize students’ experience, but heterogeneous coping behaviors should be described individually rather than treated as a single latent construct.20–22

This study aimed to: (1) determine the prevalence and severity of TMD symptoms and academic stress among dental students; (2) examine the associations between TMD symptoms, academic stress, and demographic/health-related factors; (3) identify independent predictors of TMD risk; and (4) describe the coping strategies used by dental students in relation to TMD symptoms.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted among undergraduate dental students in Sana’a, Yemen, between February and March 2026. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Ibn Al-Nafis University for Medical Sciences, Sana’a, Yemen (Approval No. 198, dated December 27, 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.23 All participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed about the study objectives, procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time.

Selection Criteria

Undergraduate students enrolled in five-year dental programs at five universities in Sana’a City (Ibn Al-Nafis University, University of Sciences and Technology, Al-Rasheed Smart University, Sana’a University, and Saba University) were approached in classroom sessions. Students present and available during scheduled visits were invited, constituting a multicenter convenience sampling. Participants were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: they were aged ≥18 years, actively enrolled in any academic year (1st to 5th), and willing to provide written informed consent. Students who had received orthodontic or cosmetic treatment, were born with congenital craniofacial deformities, or had submitted incomplete responses (>10% missing data) were excluded from the final analysis. The treatment-related exclusion was intended to reduce potential confounding from treatment-associated occlusal or jaw changes; because such treatment is common in young adults, this criterion may also introduce selection bias and reduce representativeness. Sample Size Determination

A total of 850 undergraduate dental students from five universities in Sana’a were invited to participate in the study. The minimum required sample size was calculated a priori, assuming a 50% expected prevalence of TMD symptoms, a 5% margin of error, and 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), yielding 385 participants. To account for an anticipated non-response or exclusion rate of 30–40%, the target was set at 650. In total, 792 questionnaires were returned (questionnaire return rate, 93.2%), and 756 participants remained after exclusions, corresponding to 88.9% of all invited students.

Questionnaire Development, Validity, Reliability, and Pilot Testing

The structured and self-administered questionnaire contained three main standardized instruments: the Dental Environment Stress Scale (DESS), Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI), and six selected items from the Brief COPE inventory. It was developed from previously validated survey tools used in many published studies,24–27 with some modifications.

The content validity of the questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of five multidisciplinary experts (three dental public health specialists and two clinical psychologists). Each reviewer independently rated the relevance and clarity of each item on a 4-point scale. The Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion of experts rating an item as 3 or 4; items with I-CVI ≥0.78 were retained. The Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI/Ave), averaged across all items, was 0.92, indicating very good content validity, however, the panel did not include an orofacial pain specialist, which limits content-validation claims for the TMD component and is acknowledged as a study limitation.

A pilot study was conducted with a subsample of 30 dental students from one university (not included in the main sample) to determine the feasibility of the questionnaire, its clarity, and average completion time (10–12 minutes). Based on the pilot study, minor edits were made to the wording of some items after reviewing the collected data. These local procedures assessed comprehensibility and content relevance; they did not constitute external or construct validation.

Internal consistency was determined using Cronbach’s α, demonstrating good-to-excellent reliability for the total scores: DESS (α = 0.89), FAI (α = 0.86), and the six selected Brief COPE items (α = 0.78). In addition, test-retest reliability was assessed in a second sub-sample of 30 participants at a two-week interval, showing excellent temporal stability for the total FAI score (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC = 0.91) and for the DESS total score (ICC = 0.89). Questionnaire Parts

The final instrument contained 35 questions organized into four sequential parts, each designed to systematically evaluate the prevalence and severity of TMD symptoms and academic stress among dental students. All measures were self-reported; no physical TMD examination, imaging, or record verification was performed. The complete instrument is available as Supplementary File 1.

The first covered sociodemographic and health-related characteristics. It includes nine items covering age (≤25, 26–30, 31–35, 36–40 years), gender, academic year (1st to 5th), marital status (single, married, divorced), average sleep hours per night (<5, 5–6, 7–8, >8 hours), prior TMD diagnosis (yes/no/not sure), daily screen time, daily caffeinated beverage intake (0, 1–2, 3–4, ≥5 cups), and weekly moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (none, <30, 30–150, >150 minutes).

The second part was related to academic stress measurements. Academic stress was assessed using a locally adapted 10-item measure derived from the Dental Environment Stress questionnaire,24,25 which assesses academic stress across five domains: living accommodation, personal factors, educational environment, academic work, and clinical factors. Each item is rated on a four-point Likert scale (1 = not stressful, 2 = slightly stressful, 3 = moderately stressful, and 4 = very stressful). The total score ranged from 10 to 40. Based on distribution-based cutoffs, scores were categorized as mild stress (≤22), moderate stress (23–30), or severe stress (≥31), with higher scores indicating greater stress. The shortened adaptation underwent local content review, pilot testing, and reliability assessment but has not been externally validated as a standalone scale; category-based findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. The Cronbach’s α for the DESS in this sample was 0.89.

The third part is related to TMD symptoms. The 10-item Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI) is a validated questionnaire that screens for TMD signs and symptoms across multiple domains,26,27 including: difficulty opening the mouth, jaw mobility limitations, muscle pain on chewing, headache, neck pain, ear/jaw joint pain, TMJ noise, tooth clenching/grinding, occlusal discomfort, and self-perceived tension. Each item was scored as “n” (0 points), “sometime” (5 points), or “ye” (10 points). Total scores range from 0 to 100, and TMD severity is classified as follows: 0–15, no TMD; 20–40, mild TMD; 45–65, moderate TMD; and 70–100, severe TMD. The FAI has demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.758–0.805) and validity in multiple populations.26 In the present sample, Cronbach’s α was 0.86.

The fourth part was related to six Brief COPE-derived behaviors were selected to keep the survey brief while representing four commonly used adaptive behaviors (concentrating efforts, seeking emotional support, religious or spiritual coping, and turning to work) and two potentially maladaptive behaviors (self-criticism and alcohol or drug use).25 Each item is rated on a 4-point scale: 1 = I have not been doing this at all, 2 = a little bit, 3 = a medium amount, and 4 = a lot. The Cronbach’s α for the six items in this sample was 0.78.

Data Collection

Data were collected through paper-based questionnaire sessions held at university lecture halls after regular class hours. Trained research assistants distributed printed questionnaires after obtaining written informed consent. Participants completed the questionnaire (10–12 min) and placed them in a sealed, unmarked collection box to ensure anonymity. Of 850 invited students, 792 returned a questionnaire (93.2% return rate). Thirty-six incomplete or ineligible questionnaires were excluded, leaving 756 participants in the final analysis (88.9% of those invited). All exposures and outcomes were self-reported.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were calculated for all variables. Group comparisons were performed using chi-squared tests for categorical variables and independent t-tests or one-way -way ANOVA for continuous variables, as appropriate. For multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied; statistical significance was set at p < 0.01 for those analyses. Prior to multivariable modeling, multicollinearity among predictor variables was assessed using tolerance and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) statistics; all VIF values were < 5, indicating no significant collinearity. Multivariable binary logistic regression was conducted to identify the independent predictors of TMD risk (dependent variable: TMD, FAI ≥20). Variables with p < 0.20 in univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model. The adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke R2 and the Hosmer–-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The statistical significance for the primary regression model was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Missing data (caffeine intake, n=6, 0.79%) were handled by list-wise deletion for analyses involving that variable; for all other variables, no missing data were present.

Results

A total of 792 students returned questionnaires (93.2% return rate). After exclusions, 756 were included in the analysis (88.9% of all invited students). Associations with Demographic and Health-Related Factors

Table 4 presents the associations between demographic/health-related characteristics and both the FAI and DESS total scores. Multivariable Logistic Regression Analysis

Table 6 presents the results of the multivariate binary logistic regression analysis for TMD risk (FAI ≥20). After adjusting for all potential confounders, female gender remained associated with higher odds of FAI screen positivity (AOR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.15–2.29, p = 0.006), and a self-reported prior TMD diagnosis was associated with higher odds of current screen positivity (AOR = 3.32, 95% CI: 2.15–5.12, p < 0.001). Age group, year of study, marital status, sleep duration, screen time, caffeine intake, and physical activity were not significantly associated with TMD risk in the adjusted model. Nagelkerke R2 was 0.105 and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was non-significant (p=0.724), indicating acceptable calibration but modest explanatory performance.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study of 756 dental students revealed a high prevalence of TMD symptoms (56.6%) and academic stress (71.3%, moderate to severe). The findings demonstrate a significant, graded association between academic stress severity and TMD symptomatology. Female gender and self-reported prior TMD diagnosis were adjusted cross-sectional correlates of FAI screen positivity. Notably, sleep deprivation was strongly associated with higher stress levels but not with TMD risk after multivariable adjustment. However, these findings are preliminary because the design was cross-sectional, measures were self-reported, and no clinical TMD examination was conducted.

The observed TMD prevalence of 56.6% exceeds the 36.99% reported among Saudi dental students assessed with DC/TMD,10 and is lower than the 97% Helkimo-index estimate in another Saudi student sample.28 A recent global meta-analysis by Zieliński et al reported a worldwide TMD prevalence of 34%, with marked continental variation (South America 47%, Asia 33%, Europe 29%),5 another Saudi population study using RDC/TMD clinical examinations found a 27.2% prevalence among children and adolescents.29 while Dantas et al observed that 78.6% of Brazilian medical and dental students reported at least one TMD symptom.30 In India, Chinthalapalli et al reported a TMD prevalence of 61.2% among dental students.31 In contrast, the global adult population prevalence was approximately 34%,5 indicating that dental students experience a substantially higher symptom burden, likely due to academic and clinical stressors. However, differences in age, sampling, study period, instrument, threshold, and clinical verification substantially limit direct prevalence comparisons. The present estimate should therefore be interpreted as symptom-screen positivity in Yemeni students rather than as a directly comparable clinical diagnosis.

The FAI has high sensitivity but only moderate specificity in several validation studies, which can inflate screen-positive estimates relative to DC/TMD examination.26,32 Clinical studies that combine standardized TMD examinations with objective measures further illustrate that symptom questionnaires and physical diagnosis capture related but non-equivalent constructs.33 This distinction is especially important here because no physical examination was performed.

The item-level pattern is compatible with a biopsychosocial account of TMD-related symptoms, not a one-way causal pathway. Headache, tension, chewing-muscle pain, and clenching varied substantially across stress categories, whereas joint clicking remained stable. Shared psychological distress, somatic symptom amplification, pain-related stress, altered sleep, or parafunctional behavior could contribute in either direction. Similar correlations between psychosocial profiles and physical TMD diagnoses have been reported, but cross-sectional associations do not establish temporal sequence.19,26,34–37

The overlap between the FAI item asking whether a participant considers themselves tense and the construct of academic stress may also strengthen the observed association through shared content. Consequently, the results do not establish that stress produces mechanical overload or structural disc displacement. They instead identify a concurrent symptom pattern that should be tested with longitudinal designs, validated psychological measures, and DC/TMD Axis I and II assessment.

Female students reported higher absolute FAI scores compared to males (19.47 vs 17.30), this difference did not meet the adjusted threshold for statistical significance (p = 0.025). Female predominance has consistently been reported. For example, Zieliński et al reported female-to-male ratios from 1.09 (Europe) to 1.56 (South America).5 Dantas et al found that females comprised 76.9% of their sample with higher rates of severe anxiety and depression.30 Alqarni et al reported gender as a significant predictor of OHIP-TMD scores (β=−0.268, p=0.016) and PROMIS anxiety scores (β=−0.213, p=0.035).38 AL-Hamad also found higher rates of muscular pain during chewing in females (p=0.005).27 This predominance is attributed to biological factors (estrogen influences TMJ laxity, pain perception, and inflammation), anatomical differences (smaller mandibular condyles), and psychosocial factors (differential pain reporting and coping).

Interestingly, despite higher FAI scores, females did not report significantly higher DESS scores (p=0.064), suggesting that the gender difference in TMD may not be fully explained by differential stress perception. This observation aligns with studies suggesting that biological susceptibility may play a more prominent role than stress exposure in explaining gender disparities in TMD.

A self-reported prior TMD diagnosis showed the largest adjusted association with current FAI screen positivity (AOR=3.32, 95% CI: 2.15–5.12). This finding has several potential implications. First, TMD may follow a chronic or recurrent course with symptoms persisting or recurring over time. Lin et al observed that, while the overall prevalence of self-reported TMD symptoms was 49.7%, most symptoms were self-limiting and not aggravated by functional activities,34 suggesting that many students experience subclinical or intermittent TMD that may recur or persist. Second, individuals with prior TMD may have a heightened awareness or vigilance regarding orofacial symptoms, leading to increased reporting. Kadoğlu et al reported that 76% of dental students had possible bruxism, with those reporting bruxism demonstrating significantly higher FAI scores (effect size, r=0.44),24 supporting the chronic/recurrent nature of stress-related oral parafunctions and TMD symptoms. Fan et al also found that the more severe the students’ own TMD-related problems, the more they educated themselves about TMD, the more motivated they were to learn, and the more positively they evaluated treating patients with TMD,39 highlighting the bidirectional influence of personal experience and clinical awareness.

The strong association between sleep deprivation (<5 h/night) and higher academic stress (p<0.001) was clinically significant. However, sleep duration was not found to be independently associated with TMD risk in the multivariate model. This pattern suggests that the relationship between sleep and TMD may be mediated through stress pathways, rather than being directly causal. Kadoğlu et al identified “lack of relaxation time” as one of the strongest individual stressors differentiating students with and without bruxism (Hedges’ g=0.51, p=0.001).24 Medeiros et al, during the COVID-19 social isolation period, found high rates of anxiety (49.6%) and depression (38.9%) among dental students, with a positive correlation between oral behaviors and symptoms of anxiety (r=0.312, p=0.001) and depression (r=0.216, p=0.021).40 Tănculescu et al reported that morning jaw pain or stiffness, often considered an indicator of nocturnal bruxism, was significantly associated with orofacial pain (χ2=34.1, p<0.001),41 further linking sleep-related parafunctions to TMD symptoms. The absence of a direct association in our adjusted model may indicate that stress is a proximal determinant of TMD symptoms in this population.

Religious/spiritual beliefs emerged as the most frequently endorsed coping strategy (mean 3.38±0.75, 50% responding “a lot”), whereas alcohol or drug use was uncommon. These six behaviors were selected for brief descriptive coverage and were analyzed separately; they do not support conclusions about a unitary coping profile or moderation of the stress-TMD association. However, Alqarni et al found that higher anxiety levels (PROMIS scores) were strongly correlated with psychosocial issues (partial η2=0.801, p=0.003) and moderately correlated with physical function impairments (partial η2=0.307, p=0.035) among dental students,38 suggesting that psychological resilience and coping mechanisms may modulate the stress-TMD relationship. In addition, Fan et al observed that dental students with personal TMD experiences were more motivated to educate themselves about TMD (r=0.13, p<0.05) and were more likely to want to learn more (r=0.15, p<0.01),39 indicating that personal exposure can be a catalyst for proactive coping and knowledge acquisition. Future studies should examine whether specific coping strategies moderate the relationship between stress- and TMD.

These findings have practical implications in dental education. First, the high prevalence of TMD symptoms and academic stress warrants the integration of stress management programs into dental curricula, including mindfulness-based interventions and cognitive-behavioral training. Second, early TMD screening, particularly for students entering clinical training, may facilitate timely interventions. Third, addressing modifiable risk factors such as sleep hygiene and parafunctional habits could be incorporated into student wellness initiatives. Fourth, students with prior TMD diagnosis may benefit from ongoing monitoring during their training.

Kadoğlu et al identified “inconsistent feedback from academic staff” as the most prominent modifiable stressor (g=0.50), along with examinations (g=0.49), fear of failing specialty examinations (g=0.42), and shortage of clinical time (g=0.32).24 These findings support curriculum-level interventions including structured feedback systems and resilience-building programs. Fan et al further emphasized that only 29.5% of students were satisfied with their TMD education, and only 49.9% felt well-prepared to diagnose TMD.39 Thus, both didactic and clinical TMD education need substantial improvement, with a focus on “hands-on” experiences and live patient encounters.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size (N=756), participation across five universities, a 93.2% questionnaire return rate, an 88.9% final-analysis proportion, and transparent reporting of adjusted estimates with confidence intervals. However, this study has several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences regarding the direction of the associations between academic stress and TMD symptoms. Second, TMD was measured by FAI self-report without DC/TMD examination, imaging, or clinical-record verification; therefore, prevalence may be overestimated and subtypes cannot be identified. The DESS-derived 10-item adaptation was not externally or construct validated, and the expert panel did not include an orofacial pain specialist. The classroom-based convenience sample from one geographic region (Sana’a, Yemen), may limit the generalizability to dental student populations in other cultural or educational settings. Excluding students with prior orthodontic or cosmetic treatment may introduce selection bias. The Nagelkerke R2 of 0.105 indicates modest model explanatory performance but should not be interpreted literally as 89.5% of individual risk being caused by unmeasured factors. Other unmeasured variables may include clinical bruxism, occlusion, pain history, trauma, systemic conditions, medication use, and psychological comorbidity. Finally, prior TMD diagnosis was based on self-reporting rather than verified clinical records, introducing possible recall bias. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable epidemiological data on TMD symptom prevalence and its association with academic stress in an understudied population, and the findings can inform targeted screening and stress-reduction interventions in dental education.

Longitudinal studies are needed to establish the temporal relationship between academic stress and TMD symptom development. Multicenter studies across diverse educational and cultural contexts have enhanced generalizability. Future research should incorporate clinical DC/TMD examinations to validate self-reported findings and enable classification of TMD subtypes. Investigating interventions targeting stress reduction and their effects on TMD outcomes would inform evidence-based prevention strategies. Finally, examination of the potential moderating role of coping strategies in the stress-TMD relationship warrants further investigation. As suggested by Minervini et al,36 continued validation of the DC/TMD Axis I and II in different populations will improve diagnostic accuracy and treatment personalization.

Conclusions

Self-reported TMD symptoms and academic stress were common and showed concurrent associations in this sample of Yemeni dental students. Female gender and self-reported prior TMD diagnosis were adjusted correlates of FAI screen positivity, but the model had modest explanatory performance. Because the study was cross-sectional and lacked clinical TMD diagnosis, the findings are preliminary and do not establish causation, prediction, or treatment benefit. Longitudinal studies incorporating DC/TMD examinations are required.
