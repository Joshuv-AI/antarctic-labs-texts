ON STRUCTURAL CONNECTIONS BETWEEN SANDPILE MONOIDS AND WEIGHTED LEAVITT PATH ALGEBRAS
Roozbeh Hazrat, Tran Giang Nam

Abstract
. In this article, we establish the relations between a sandpile graph, its sandpile monoid and the weighted Leavitt path algebra associated with it. Namely, we show that the lattice of all idempotents of the sandpile monoid SP ​ ( E ) \text{SP}(E) of a sandpile graph E E is both isomorphic to the lattice of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of E E , the lattice of all order-ideals of SP ​ ( E ) \text{SP}(E) and the lattice of all ideals of the weighted Leavitt path algebra L 𝗄 ​ ( E , ω ) L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega) generated by vertices. Also, we describe the sandpile group of a sandpile graph E E via archimedean classes of SP ​ ( E ) \text{SP}(E) , and prove that all maximal subgroups of SP ​ ( E ) \text{SP}(E) are exactly the Grothendieck groups of these archimedean classes. Finally, we give the structure of the Leavitt path algebra L 𝗄 ​ ( E ) L_{\mathsf{k}}(E) of a sandpile graph E E via a finite chain of graded ideals being invariant under every graded automorphism of L 𝗄 ​ ( E ) L_{\mathsf{k}}(E) , and completely describe the structure of L 𝗄 ​ ( E ) L_{\mathsf{k}}(E) such that the lattice of all idempotents of SP ​ ( E ) \text{SP}(E) is a chain. Consequently, we completely describe the structure of the weighted Leavitt path algebra of a sandpile graph E E such that SP ​ ( E ) \text{SP}(E) has exactly two idempotents. 1.
Introduction

The notion of abelian sandpile models was invented in
1987
by Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld
[
9
]
. While defined by a simple local rule, it produces self-similar global patterns that call for an explanation. The models have been used to describe phenomena such as forest fires, traffic jams, stock market fluctuations, etc. The book of Bak
[
10
]
describes how events in nature apparently follow this type of behaviour. In
[
14
]
Dhar systematically associated finite commutative monoid and groups to sandpile models, now called respectively
sandpile monoids
and
sandpile groups
, and championed the use of them as an invariant which proved to capture many properties of the model. These algebraic structures constitute one of the main themes of the subject and they have been extensively studied (see, e.g.,
[
23
,
8
,
12
]
and references there). The abelian sandpile model was independently discovered in
1991
by Björner, Lovász and Shor
[
11
]
, who called it
chip-firing
. Indeed, in the last two decades the subject has been enriched by an exhilarating interaction of numerous areas of mathematics, including statistical physics, combinatorics, free boundary PDE, probability, potential theory, number theory and group theory; refer to Levine and Peres’s recent survey on the subject
[
20
]
in more details.

In a different realm, the notion of Leavitt path algebras
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
associated to directed graphs
E
were introduced by Abrams and Aranda Pino in
[
1
]
, and independently Ara, Moreno, and Pardo in
[
6
]
. These are a generalization of algebras introduced by William Leavitt in 1962
[
19
]
as a “universal” algebra
A
of type
(1,n)
, so that
A\cong A^{n}
as right
A
-modules, where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. It was established quite early on in the theory that Leavitt path algebras only produce rings of ranks
(1,n)
. In
[
19
]
Leavitt further constructed rings of type
(m,n)
, where
1<m<n
and showed that these algebras surprisingly are domains. When
m\geq 2
, this universal ring is not realizable as a Leavitt path algebra. With this in mind, the notion of
weighted
Leavitt path algebras
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,w)
associated to weighted graphs
(E,w)
were introduced by the first author in
[
15
]
(see
[
22
]
for a nice overview of this topic). The weighted Leavitt path algebras provide a natural context in which all of Leavitt’s algebras (corresponding to any pair
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
) can be realized as a specific example.

The study of the commutative monoid
\mathcal{V}(A)
of isomorphism classes of finitely generated projective left modules of a unital ring
A
(with operation
\oplus
) goes back to the work of Grothendieck and Serre. For a Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
, the monoid
\mathcal{V}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))
has received substantial attention since the introduction of the topic. Furthermore, the monoid
\mathcal{V}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,w))
has been described completely by the first author in
[
15
]
, and subsequently by Preusser in
[
21
]
.
In
[
4
]
Abrams and the first author established that these monoids could be naturally related to the sandpile monoids of graphs. This relationship allows us to associate weighted Leavitt path algebras to the theory of sandpile models, thereby opening up an avenue by which to investigate sandpile models via the structure of weighted Leavitt path algebras, and vice versa.

In this article, we investigate the relations between a sandpile graph, its sandpile monoid and the weighted Leavitt path algebra associated with it. In particular we concentrate on order-ideals and idempotents of a sandpile monoid and establish a relation between them and certain ideals and the structure of weighted Leavitt path algebras.

In Section
2
, we describe the structure of sandpile monoid
\text{SP}(E)
of a sandpile graph
E
in terms of its sandpile submonoids
\text{SP}(E_{H})
, where
E_{H}
is the restriction graph of
E
to an nonempty saturated hereditary subset
H
of
E
, and show that the lattice of all order-ideals of
\text{SP}(E)
is isomorphic to the lattice of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E
(Theorem
2.10
). Consequently, we obtain that every isomorphism between two sandpile monoids
\text{SP}(E)
and
\text{SP}(F)
induces an isomorphism between the lattices of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E
and
F
(Corollary
2.11
).

In Section
3
, based on Babai and Toumpakari’s result
[
8
, Theorem 4.13]
and Theorem
2.10
, we describe all idempotents and all order-ideals of the sandpile monoid
\text{SP}(E)
of a sandpile graph
E
in terms of the combinatorics of
E
. In particular, we obtain that they are not only isomorphic to each other as lattices, but also isomorphic to the lattice of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E
(Theorem
3.6
). Also, we prove that a sandpile group
G(E)
is the direct limit of the Grothendieck groups of archimedean classes of
\text{SP}(E)
and obtain that the Grothendieck group of an archimedean class
[x]
is exactly the sandpile group of the sandpile graph arising from the support of
x
, as well as show that all maximal subgroups of
\text{SP}(E)
are exactly the Grothendieck groups of archimedean classes of
\text{SP}(E)
(Theorem
3.9
).

In Section
4
, we make a bridge between the structure of sandpile graphs and the algebraic structure of Leavitt path algebras. Based on Theorem
3.6
,
we show that the lattice of all idempotents of the sandpile momoid of a sandpile graph
E
is both isomorphic to the lattice of all nonzero graded ideals of the Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
and the lattice of all ideals the weighted Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
generated by vertices (Theorem
4.4
). Also, we give the structure of the Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
of a sandpile graph
E
via a finite chain of graded ideals being invariant under every graded automorphism of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
, which extends Zelmanov et al.’s result
[
5
, Theorem 1]
to the sandpile graph setting, and completely describe the structure of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
such that the lattice of all idempotents of
\text{SP}(E)
is a chain (Theorem
4.10
). Consequently, we completely describe the structure of the (weighted) Leavitt path algebra of a sandpile graph
E
such that
\text{SP}(E)
has exactly two idempotents (Theorem
4.12
).

Throughout we write
\mathbb{N}
for the set of non-negative integers, and
\mathbb{N}^{+}
for the set of positive integers.

2.
Sandpile monoids and groups

The main aim of this section is to describe the structure of sandpile monoid
\text{SP}(E)
of a sandpile graph
E
in terms of its sandpile submonoids
\text{SP}(E_{H})
, where
E_{H}
is the restriction graph of
E
to an nonempty saturated hereditary subset
H
of
E
, as well as to show that the lattice of all order-ideals of
\text{SP}(E)
is isomorphic to the lattice of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E
(Theorem
2.10

\text{SP}(E)
and
\text{SP}(F)
yields an isomorphism between the lattices of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E

In order to define sandpile monoids we need to recall the notion of a graph.

A
directed graph
is a quadruple
E=(E^{0},E^{1},s,r)
, where
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
are sets and

are maps. Throughout, “graph” will always mean “directed graph”. The elements of
E^{0}
are called

vertices
and the elements of
E^{1}

edges
. (We allow the empty set to be viewed as a graph with
E^{0}=E^{1}=\emptyset.
) If
e
is an edge, then
s(e)
is called its
source
and
r(e)
its
range
. If
v
is a vertex and
e
an edge, we say that
v

emits

e
if
s(e)=v
, and
v

receives

e
if
r(e)=v
. An edge
e
is called a
loop at

v
in case
s(e)=v=r(e)
.
A vertex is called a

sink
if it emits no edges, and is called
irrelevant
in case it emits exactly one edge. A graph is called
reduced
if it contains no irrelevant vertices.
A vertex is called
regular
if it is not a sink and does not emit infinitely many edges. The subset of
E^{0}
consisting of all the regular vertices is denoted by
E^{0}_{\operatorname{reg}}
. Similarly, the subset of
E^{0}
consisting of all the sinks is denoted by
E^{0}_{\operatorname{sink}}
.

The
out-degree
of a vertex
v
, denoted by
\text{out-deg}(v)
, is defined as
|s^{-1}(v)|
. A graph is called
row-finite
if any vertex emits a finite number (possibly zero) of edges. The graph
E
is called
finite
if
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
are finite sets.

A
path

p
in
E
is
a sequence
p=e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{n}
of edges in
E
such that

r(e_{i})=s(e_{i+1})
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. We define
s(p)=s(e_{1})
, and
r(p)=r(e_{n})
.
By definition, the
length

|p|
of
p
is
n
. We assign the length zero to vertices. A
closed path
(based at
v
) is a path
p
such that
s(p)=r(p)=v
. A
cycle
(based at
v
) is a closed path
p=e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{n}
based at
v
such that
s(e_{i})\neq s(e_{j})
for any
i\neq j
. An edge
f\in E^{1}
is called an
exit
of a cycle
e_{1}\cdots e_{n}
if there is an
1\leq i\leq n
such that
s(f)=s(e_{i})
and
f\neq e_{i}
.

For every strongly connected cyclic component
C
of
E
, we denote by
C^{0}
the set of all vertices in
E
which lie on
C
.

Definition 2.3

(

Let
E
be a sandpile graph. There is an explicit description of the congruence on the free commutative monoid
\langle v\mid v\in E^{0}\rangle
given by the defining relations of
\text{SP}(E)
in Definition
2.3
, as follows. A nonzero element of
\langle v\mid v\in E^{0}\rangle
can be written uniquely up to permutation as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}v_{i}
, where
v_{i}
are distinct vertices and
k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Define a binary relation

\rightarrow_{1}
on the free commutative monoid
\langle v\mid v\in E^{0}\rangle
by

\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}v_{i}\ \longrightarrow_{1}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\sum_{i\neq j}k_{i}v_{i}&&\text{if }v_{j}=s\\
\Big(\sum_{i\neq j}k_{i}v_{i}\Big)+(k_{j}-|s^{-1}(v_{j})|)v_{j}+\sum_{e\in s^{-1}(v_{j})}r(e))&&\text{if }v_{j}\neq s,\end{array}\right.

(1)

whenever
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and

k_{j}\geq|s^{-1}(v_{j})|
. Let
\rightarrow
be the transitive and reflexive closure of
\rightarrow_{1}

on
\langle v\mid v\in E^{0}\rangle
. Namely

a\rightarrow b\ \ \ \text{ if }a=b,\ \mbox{or}\ a=a_{0}\rightarrow_{1}a_{1}\rightarrow_{1}\dots\rightarrow_{1}a_{k}=b.

(2)

Finally, let
\sim
be the congruence on
\langle v\mid v\in E^{0}\rangle
generated by the relation

. That means,
a\sim b
in case there is a string

such that

or

for each

\text{SP}(E)=\mathbb{\langle}v\mid v\in E^{0}\rangle/\sim.

To avoid cumbersome equivalence class notation, it is standard (but not technically correct) to denote the elements of

The following proposition shows that sandpile monoids have “Confluence Property”, which was established by Abrams and the first author in
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
)
.

Let
E
be a sandpile graph and
a,b\in\rm{SP}
(E)
nonzero elements. Then
a=b
in
\rm{SP}
(E)
if and only if there is an element
c\in\mathbb{\langle}v\mid v\in E^{0}\rangle
such that
a\rightarrow c
and
b\rightarrow c
.

Let
E
be a graph and
H\subseteq E^{0}
. We say
H
is
hereditary
if for any
e\in E^{1}
,
s(e)\in H
implies
r(e)\in H
. We say
H
is
saturated
if whenever
v\in E_{\operatorname{reg}}^{0}
with the property that
r(s^{-1}(v))\subseteq H
, then
v\in H
. The
hereditary saturated closure
of
H
, denoted by
\overline{H}
, is the smallest hereditary and saturated subset of
E^{0}
containing
H
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}_{E}
the set of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E^{0}
. If
E
is a sandpile graph, then the set of all vertices in
E
which do not connect to any cycle in
E
is the smallest element in
(\mathcal{H}_{E},\subseteq)
, and so
(\mathcal{H}_{E},\subseteq)
is a complete lattice with
H\vee H^{\prime}=\overline{H\cup H^{\prime}}
and
H\wedge H^{\prime}=H\cap H^{\prime}
.

For any
H\in\mathcal{H}_{E}
, we denote by
E_{H}
the
restriction graph

E_{H}^{0}:=H
,
E_{H}^{1}:=\{e\in E^{1}\mid s(e)\in H\}

and the source and range functions in
E_{H}
are exactly the source and range functions in
E
, restricted to
H
.

The following remark is easy and useful.

Remark 2.6
.

Let
E
be a sandpile graph. Then
E_{H}
is a sandpile graph for all
H\in\mathcal{H}_{E}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a nonempty saturated hereditary subset of
E^{0}
. Then, there exists a vertex
v\in E^{0}
such that
v\in H
. Since
E
is a sandpile graph, there exists a path
p
in
E
such that
v=s(p)
and
s=r(p)
. This shows that
s\in H
(since
H
is hereditary). It is obvious that for every vertex
w
in
H
, there is a path
q
in
E_{H}
such that
w=s(q)
and
r(q)=s
. Thus,
E_{H}
is a sandpile graph. ∎

As an application of Proposition
2.5
, we obtain the following useful proposition.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
E
be a sandpile graph and
H\in\mathcal{H}_{E}
. Then, the map

\psi_{H}:\rm{SP}
(E_{H})\longrightarrow\rm{SP}
(E)
, defined by

, is an injective homomorphism of monoids. Consequently,

\rm{SP}
(E_{H})
is a submonoid of
\rm{SP}
(E)
.

Proof.

We note that
E_{H}
is a sandpile graph, by Remark

.
Let
a,b\in\text{SP}(E_{H})
such that
a=b
in
\text{SP}(E)
. By Proposition
2.5
, there is an element

such that
a\rightarrow c
and
b\rightarrow c
. Since

is hereditary, all the transformations occur in

, and so
c\in\langle v\mid v\in H^{0}\rangle
. This implies that
a=b
in
\text{SP}(E_{H})
. Hence,
\psi_{H}
is an injective homomorphism of monoids, thus finishing the proof.

Recall (see, e.g.,

) that an

of a commutative monoid

which is hereditary with respect to the canonical preorder

on

and

, where the preorder

on

The set

forms a complete lattice such that the join of two elements

generated by

.

An element

. We denote by

.

Let

(E_{H})

\rm{SP}
(E_{H^{\prime}})

(2)

\rm{SP}
(E_{S_{H}})=Z(\rm{SP}
(E))
, where

(E))
is the unit group of
\rm{SP}
(E)
;

(3)
For all

,
\rm{SP}
(E_{H})
is an order-ideal of
\rm{SP}
(E)
containing

(E))

\rm{SP}
(E_{H})=\langle H\rangle

(4)
For every order-ideal

\rm{SP}
(E)

(E_{H})
, where

(5)

\mathcal{H}_{E}\cong\mathcal{L}(\rm{SP}
(E))

(6)

(E_{H}))

(7)
For all

(E_{H})

is a maximal subgroup of
\rm{SP}
(E)

Proof.

(1) Let
H
and
H^{\prime}
be two elements of
\mathcal{H}_{E}
with
H\subseteq H^{\prime}
. Then,
H
is a saturated hereditary subset of
(E_{H^{\prime}})^{0}
. By Proposition
2.7
, there exists an injective monoid homomorphism
\psi_{H,H^{\prime}}:\text{SP}(E_{H})\longrightarrow\text{SP}(E_{H^{\prime}})
such that
\psi_{H,H^{\prime}}(a)=a
for all

, and so
\rm{SP}
(E_{H})
may be considered as a submonoid of
\rm{SP}
(E_{H^{\prime}})
.

(2) It follows from
[
4
, Proposition 3.3]
.

(3) Let
H
be an element in
\mathcal{H}_{E}
. We then have
S_{E}\subseteq H
. By items (1) and (2),
\text{SP}(E_{H})
is a submonoid of
\text{SP}(E)
containing
Z(\text{SP}(E))
. Assume that
x
any
y
are elements in
\text{SP}(E)
with
x\leq y
and

in
\text{SP}(E)
for some

. If

, then

and so

, as desired.

Considering the case when

.
By Proposition
2.5
, there exists an element

such that

and

. Since

,

, and so
\text{supp}(c)\subseteq E^{0}_{H}
. Assume that

. Then, there exists a vertex

. Since

is saturated,

must connect to a cycle which is not in

. This shows that

. Thus
\text{SP}(E_{H})
is an order-ideal of
\text{SP}(E)
and
\rm{SP}
(E_{H})=\langle H\rangle
.

(4) Let

be an order-ideal of
\text{SP}(E)
. Since

and

. Let

. We claim that

. Indeed, let

Let

. Therefore,

is saturated, showing the claim.

We next prove that

. Since

\text{SP}(E)
, we have

. Let

\text{SP}(E)

(6) It is clear that

is directed, since the join of two elements

.

Let
H
and

(7) By Proposition

3.
Idempotents and archimedean classes of sandpile monoids

In this section, based essentially on Babai and Toumpakari’s result
[
8
, Theorem 4.13]
and Theorem
2.10
, we describe all idempotents and all order-ideals of the sandpile monoid
\text{SP}(E)
of a sandpile graph
E
in terms of the combinatorics of
E
. In particular, we obtain that they are not only isomorphic to each other as lattices, but also isomorphic to the lattice of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E
(Theorem
3.6
). Also, we prove that a sandpile group
G(E)
is the direct limit of the Grothendieck groups of archimedean classes of
\text{SP}(E)
and obtain that the Grothendieck group of an archimedean class
[x]
is exactly the sandpile group of the sandpile monoid arising from the support of
x
, as well as show that all maximal subgroups of
\text{SP}(E)
are exactly the Grothendieck groups of archimedean classes of
\text{SP}(E)
(Theorem
3.9
).

Let
(P,\leq)
be a partially ordered set. We say a nonempty subset
I
of
P
is an
ideal
of
P
if
x\in I
and
y\leq x
then
y\in I
. We say a set
F
is a
filter
of

if
x\in F
and
x\leq y
implies
y\in F
.

Let
E
be an arbitrary graph. Let
\mathcal{C}_{E}
be the set of all strongly connected cyclic components of
E
, i.e., strongly connected components which contain cycles. Consider
\mathcal{C}_{E}
as a partially ordered set with
C\leq C^{\prime}
if there is a path connecting
C
to
C^{\prime}
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{E}
the set of all filters of
\mathcal{C}_{E}
. It is obvious that
(\mathcal{F}_{E},\subseteq)
is a complete lattice such that
F\vee F^{\prime}=F\cup F^{\prime}
and
F\wedge F^{\prime}=F\cap F^{\prime}
for all
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{E}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
E
be a sandpile graph,
S_{E}
the set of all vertices in
E
which do not connect to any cycle in
E
,
\mathcal{F}_{E}
the complete lattice of all filters of
\mathcal{C}_{E}
. Then, the corresponding
\phi_{E}:\mathcal{F}_{E}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{E}
, defined by
\emptyset\longmapsto S_{E}
and
\emptyset\neq F\longmapsto\overline{\cup_{C\in F}C^{0}}
, is a lattice isomorphism, where
C^{0}
is the set of all vertices of

.

Proof.

It is obvious that
\phi_{E}
is a lattice homomorphism. Let
H
be a nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E^{0}
. We denote by

the set of all strongly connected cyclic components of the restriction graph

of

onto

. Assume that

, and

Let

are the same. Since

\psi_{E}(\{c_{1}\})=\{S_{E},H_{c_{1}}\}
and
\psi_{E}(\{c_{1},c_{2}\})=\{S_{E},H_{c_{1}},H_{c_{2}}\}
.

In
[
8
]
, Babai and Toumpakari provided us with a method to read the idempotent structure of the sandpile monoid
\text{SP}(E)
of a sandpile graph
E
via the cycle structure of
E
.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
8
, Theorem 4.13]
)
.

For every sandpile graph
E
, the correspondence
\delta_{E}:\rm{Idem}(SP(E))\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{E}
, defined by
\delta_{E}(0)=\emptyset
and
\delta_{E}(x)
is the set of all strongly connected cyclic components of
E_{H}
, where
H=\overline{\rm{supp}(x)}
, for all
0\neq x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
, is a lattice isomorphism.

We are now in a position to establish the first main result of this section describing all idempotents and all order-ideals of
\text{SP}(E)
in terms of the combinatorics of
E
, which yields that they are isomorphic to each other as lattices.

Theorem 3.6
.

For every sandpile graph
E
, there is a lattice isomorphism between the following lattices:

(1)
The lattice
\rm{Idem}(
\rm{SP}
(E))
of all idempotents of
\rm{SP}
(E)
;

(2)
The lattice
\mathcal{F}_{E}
of all filters of
\mathcal{C}_{E}
;

(3)
The lattice
\mathcal{H}_{E}
of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of

;

(4)
The lattice
\rm{Ideal}(\mathcal{H}^{s}_{E})
of all ideals of
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{E}

(5)
The lattice
\mathcal{L}(\rm{SP}
(E))
of all order-ideals of
\rm{SP}
(E)

Proof.

By Theorem
3.5
,
\rm{Idem}(
\rm{SP}
(E))\cong\mathcal{F}_{E}
as lattices via the isomorphism

. By Lemma
3.1
,
\mathcal{F}_{E}\cong\mathcal{H}_{E}
as lattices via the isomorphism

. By Lemma

2.10
(5), we obtain that

∎

We may construct an explicit isomorphism between the lattice of all idempotents of
\text{SP}(E)
and the lattice of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of

.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
E
be a sandpile graph and
S_{E}
the set of all vertices in
E
which do not connect to any cycle in
E
. Then, the map
\varsigma_{E}:\rm{Idem}(SP(E))\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{E}
, defined by
0\longmapsto S_{E}
and
0\neq x\longmapsto\overline{\rm{supp}(x)}
, is a lattice isomorphism.

Proof.

It immediately follows from the proof of Theorem
3.6
that
\varsigma_{E}=\phi_{E}\delta_{E}
.
∎

For any commutative monoid
(M,+)
, its canonical preoder induces the following equivalence relation on
M
, the now-so-called
archimedean equivalence relation
.

Fact 3.8
.

For every commutative monoid
M
, the binary relation, defined by

x\asymp y
if and only if
x\leq my
and
y\leq nx
for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

is an equivalence relation on
M
.

Proof.

It is obvious that
\asymp
is reflexive and symmetric.

Let
x,y,z
be elements of
M
such that
x\asymp y
and
y\asymp z
. Then
x\leq my
,
y\leq nx
,
y\leq kz
and
z\leq ly
for some
m,n,k,l\in\mathbb{N}
, and so
x\leq(mk)z
and
z\leq(ln)x
, that means,
x\asymp z
. This implies that
\asymp
is transitive, and so
\asymp
is an equivalence relation on
M
. ∎

For any commutative monoid
M
and for any
x\in M
, the
\asymp
-equivalence class of
x
, denoted by
[x]
, is called the
archimedean class
of
x
; that means,
[x]=\{y\in M\mid y\asymp x\}
.

We are now in a position to establish the second main result of this section describing the sandpile group and all maximal subgroups of a sandpile monoid via archimedean classes.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
E
be a sandpile graph and
S_{E}
the set of all vertices in
E
which do not connect to any cycle in
E
. Then, the following statements hold:

(1)
For all
x\in\rm{SP}
(E)
,
[x]
is a subsemigroup of
\rm{SP}
(E)
containing a unique idempotent;

(2)

\rm{SP}
(E)=\bigsqcup_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}[x]
;

(3)

G(E)=\varinjlim_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}G([x])
, where
G([x])
is the Grothendieck group of
[x]
;

(4)
For all
x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
,

G[x]\cong x+[x]=\begin{cases}G(E_{S_{E}})&\text{if }x=0,\\
G(E_{H})&\text{if }x\neq 0,\end{cases}

where
H=\overline{\rm{supp}(x)}
. In particular,
G(E)\cong e_{\max}+[e_{\max}],
where
e_{\max}=\sum_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}x
;

(5)
For every maximal subgroup
G
of
\rm{SP}
(E)
,
G\cong x+[x]
for some
x\in\rm{Idem(SP(E))}
. Consequently, the set of all maximal subgroups of
\rm{SP}
(E)
is exactly the set

\{x+[x]\mid x\in\rm{Idem(SP(E))}\}.

Proof.

(1) Let
y
and
z\in[x]
. We then have
x\leq ny
,
y\leq mx
,
z\leq kx
and
x\leq lz
for some
m,n,k,l\in\mathbb{N}
, and so
y+z\leq(m+k)x
and
x\leq ny\leq n(y+z)
. This implies that
y+z\in[x]
, an hence
[x]
is a subsemigroup of
\text{SP}(E)
. Since
[x]
is finite and by
[
12
, Lemma 2.10]
,
tx
is an idempotent for some
t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
; that means,
[x]
always contains an idempotent.

Assume that
e
and
f
are two idempotents of
[x]
. We then have
e\asymp f
, and so
e=f+x
and
f=e+y
for some
x,y\in\text{SP}(E)
. This implies that
e+f=f+x+f=f+x=e
and
e+f=e+e+y=e+y=f
, and so
e=f
, proving (1).

(2) By Fact
3.8
and Item (1), we immediately obtain that
\text{SP}(E)=\bigsqcup_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}[x]
.

(3) Let
e
and
f
be two idempotents of
\text{SP}(E)
with
e\leq f
. We then have
f=e+x
for some
x\in\text{SP}(E)
, and so
e+f=e+e+x=e+x=f
.

Let
x\in[e]
. Then,
e=x+a
and
nx=e+b
for some
a,b\in\text{SP}(E)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and so
f=f+e=f+(x+a)=(f+x)+a
, that means,
f+x\leq f
. It is obvious that
f\leq f+x
, and so
f+x\in[f]
. Therefore, the corresponding
\phi_{e,f}:[e]\longrightarrow[f]
, defined by
x\longmapsto f+x
, is a map. Moreover, we have

\phi_{e,f}(x+y)=f+x+y=f+x+f+y=\phi_{e,f}(x)+\phi_{e,f}(y)

for all
x,y\in[e]
, and so
\phi_{e,f}
is a semigroup homomorphism. Also, it is not hard to check that

\phi_{f,g}\circ\phi_{e,f}=\phi_{e,g}
for all
e,f,g\in\rm{SP(E)}
with
e\leq f\leq g
.

Every semigroup homomorphisms
\phi_{e,f}
induces a group homomorphism
\phi_{e,f}:G([e])\longrightarrow G([f])
such that
\phi_{e,f}([(x,y)]_{0})=[(\phi_{e,f}(x),\phi_{e,f}(y))]_{0}
for all
[(x,y)]_{0}\in G([e])
. We note that
\phi_{e,e}([(x,y)]_{0})=[(\phi_{e,e}(x),\phi_{e,e}(y))]_{0}=[(e+x,e+y)]_{0}=[(x,y)]_{0}+[(e,e)]_{0}=[(x,y)]_{0}
, and so
\phi_{e,f}=id_{G([e])}
. Therefore, the pair
(G[e],\phi_{e,f})
is a direct system of finite abelian groups over
\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
. Let
\psi_{e}:G([e])\longrightarrow\varinjlim_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}G([x])
be the canonical homomorphism.

For every
e\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
, since
[e]
is a subsemigroup of
\text{SP}(E)
, there exists a group homomorphism
\alpha_{e}:G([e])\longrightarrow G(E)
such that
\alpha_{e}([(x,y)]_{0})=[(x,y)]_{0}
for all
[(x,y)]_{0}\in G([e])
. Let
e
and
f
be two idempotents of
\text{SP}(E)
with
e\leq f
. Then, for each
[(x,y)]_{0}\in G([e])
, we have

\alpha_{f}\phi_{e,f}([(x,y)]_{0})=[(f+x,f+y)]_{0}=[(x,y)]_{0}+[(e,f)]_{0}\text{ in }G(E)

Since
e=e+e
and
f=f+f
, we must have
[(e,f)]_{0}=[(e+f,e+f)]_{0}
in
G(E)
; that means,
[(e,f)]_{0}
is the identity of
G(E)
. Hence, we obtain that

\alpha_{f}\phi_{e,f}([(x,y)]_{0})=[(x,y)]_{0}+[(e,f)]_{0}=[(x,y)]_{0}=\alpha_{e}([(x,y)]_{0})\text{ in }G(E),

that means,
\alpha_{f}\phi_{e,f}=\alpha_{e}
. By the Universal Property of
\varinjlim_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}G([x])
, there is a unique group homomorphism
\beta:\varinjlim_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}G([x])\longrightarrow G(E)
such that
\beta\psi_{e}=\alpha_{e}
for all
e\in\rm{Idem(SP(E))}
. Then, for each
[(x,y)]_{0}\in G([e])
, we have
[(x,y)]_{0}=\beta([(x,y)]_{0})
in
G(E)
. Since
\text{SP}(E)=\bigsqcup_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}[x]
, we immediately get that
\beta
is surjective.

Let
[(x,y)]_{0}\in G([e])
with
\beta([(x,y)]_{0})=[(x,y)]_{0}=0_{G(E)}
. We then have
[(x,y)]_{0}=[e,e]_{0}
in
G(E)
, that means,
x+e+z=y+e+z
for some
z\in\text{SP}(E)
. By item (2), there exists an idempotent
f\in\text{SP}(E)
such that
z\in[f]
. Then, since
x+e,y+e\in[e]
and
z+f\in[f]
, we get that
x+e+f,y+e+f,z+f+e\in[e+f]
. Since
x+e+z+f=y+e+z+f
,
0_{G[e+f]}=[(x+e+f,y+e+f)]_{0}=\phi_{e,e+f}([(x,y)]_{0})
, and so
[(x,y)]_{0}
is zero in
\varinjlim_{x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))}G([x])
. Therefore,
\beta
is injective, and so it is an isomorphism.

(4) Let
x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
. We claim that
x+[x]
is a group with identity
x
. Indeed, by Item (1),
x+[x]
is clearly a monoid with identity
x
. Let
a
be an arbitrary element of
[x]
. Then, by
[
12
, Lemma 2.10]
,
na
is an idempotent of
[x]
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. By item (1), we get that
x=na
, and so
x+a+(n-1)a=x+na=x+x=x
. This shows that
x+[x]
is a group with identity
x
, proving the claim.

Let
\iota_{x}:[x]\longrightarrow G([x])
be the canonical semigroup homomorphism, that means,
\iota_{x}(y)=[y,y+x]_{0}
for all
y\in[x]
. We note that
\phi_{x,x}:[x]\longrightarrow[x]
,
y\longmapsto x+y
, is a semigroup homomorphism with
\phi_{x,x}([x])=x+[x]
. By the Universal Property of
G([x])
, there is a unique group homomorphism
\delta:G([x])\longrightarrow x+[x]
such that
\delta\iota_{x}=\phi_{x,x}
, and so

\delta([y,z]_{0})=\delta([y,y+z]_{0})-\delta([z,y+z]_{0})=\delta\iota_{x}(y)-\delta\iota_{x}(z)=x+y-(x+z)

for all
y,z\in[x]
. This implies that
\delta([y,z]_{0})=0

\Longleftrightarrow x+y=x+z
in
[x]
, and so
[(y,z)]_{0}=[(x,x)]_{0}=0_{G([x])}
. Therefore,
\delta
is injective.

For every
x+y\in x+[x]
, we have
\delta([(y,x+y)]_{0})=\delta\iota_{x}(y)=\phi_{x,x}(y)=x+y
, and so
\delta
is surjective. Therefore, we obtain that
G([x])\cong x+[x]
.

If
x=0
, then
G([0])=Z(\text{SP}(E))=\text{SP}(E_{S_{E}})=G(E_{S_{E}})=[0]
, as desired. Suppose
x\neq 0
and
H:=\overline{\rm{supp}(x)}
. It is obvious that
x+x=x\in\text{SP}(E_{H})
. Then, since
\text{SP}(E_{H})
is an order-ideal of
\text{SP}(E)
(by Theorem
2.10
(3)),
x+[x]\subseteq\text{SP}(E_{H})
. By Corollary
3.7
,
x
is the biggest idempotent of
\text{SP}(E_{H})
. According to Theorem
2.10
(7), we immediately obtain that
G(E_{H})=x+\text{SP}(E_{H})
, and so

x+[x]\subseteq x+\text{SP}(E_{H})=G(E_{H})
. Conversely, for every
y\in G(E_{H})
, by Proposition
2.9
,
y=x+z
for some
z\in\text{SP}(E_{H})
. By
[
12
, Lemma 2.10]
,
mz
is an idempotent of
\text{SP}(E_{H})
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and so
mz\leq x
. This implies that
x+z\leq x+x=x
, and so
x+z\in[x]
. Then,
y=x+z=x+(x+z)\in x+[x]
, that means,
G(E_{H})\subseteq x+[x]
.

(5) Let
G
be a maximal idempotent of
\text{SP}(E)
, that means,
G=Z(x+\text{SP}(E))
, the unit group of
x+\text{SP}(E)
, for some
x\in\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
. If
x=0
, then
G=Z(\text{SP}(E))=\text{SP}(E_{S_{E}})=G(E_{S_{E}})=[0]
. Consider the case when
x\neq 0
. Let
H=\overline{\rm{supp}(x)}
. By Corollary
3.7
,
x
is the biggest idempotent of
\text{SP}(E_{H})
. By the proof of Theorem
2.10
(7),

G=Z(x+\text{SP}(E))=x+\text{SP}(E_{H})=G(E_{H})
. By Item (4), we have
G=G(E_{H})=x+[x]
. Using this observation, Item (4) and
Theorem
2.10
(7), we immediately obtain that the set of all maximal subgroups of
\rm{SP}
(E)
is exactly the set
\{x+[x]\mid x\in\rm{Idem(SP(E))}\},
thus finishing the proof.
∎

4.
A connection between sandpile monoids and weighted Leavitt path algebras

In this section, based on Theorem
3.6
,
we show that the lattice of all idempotents of the sandpile momoid
\text{SP}(E)
of a sandpile graph
E
is both isomorphic to the lattice of all nonzero graded ideals of the Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
and the lattice of all ideals the weighted Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
generated by vertices (Theorem
4.4
). Also, we give the structure of the Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
of a sandpile graph
E
, extending Zelmanov et al.’s result
[
5
, Theorem 1]
to the sandpile graph setting, and completely describe the structure of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
such that the lattice of all idempotents of
\text{SP}(E)
is a chain (Theorem
4.10
). Consequently, we completely describe the structure of the (weighted) Leavitt path algebra of a sandpile graph
E
such that
\text{SP}(E)
has exactly two idempotents (Theorem
4.12
).

We begin this section by recalling some basic notions of weighted graphs.

Definition 4.1
.

A
weighted graph
is a pair
(E,\omega)
, where
E
is a graph and
\omega:E^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}^{+}
is a map. If
e\in E^{1}
, then
\omega(e)
is called the
weight
of
e
. A weighted graph
(E,\omega)
is called
row-finite
if the graph
E
is row-finite. A weighted graph
(E,\omega)
is called
finite
if the graph
E
is finite.

For each regular vertex
v
in a weighted graph
(E,\omega)
we set
\omega(v):=\max\{\omega(e)\mid e\in s^{-1}(v)\}
. This gives a map (called
\omega
again)
\omega:E_{\operatorname{reg}}^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

A row-finite weighted graph
(E,\omega)
is called a
vertex weighted graph
if for any
v\in E^{0}_{\operatorname{reg}}
,
\omega(e)=\omega(e^{\prime})
for all
e,e^{\prime}\in s^{-1}(v)
.

A vertex weighted graph is called a
balanced weighted graph
if
\omega(v)=|s^{-1}(v)|
for all
v\in E_{\operatorname{reg}}^{0}
.

It is worth mentioning the following note.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that any row-finite directed graph
E
can be given the structure of a balanced weighted graph
(E,\omega)
by assigning
\omega(v)=|s^{-1}(v)|
for all
v\in E_{\operatorname{reg}}^{0}
, and the now-so called
balanced weighted graph associated with

E
.

We next recall the notion of weighted Leavitt path algebras. These are algebras associated to row-finite weighted graphs. We refer the reader to
[
15
]
and
[
22
]
for a detailed analysis of these algebras.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
(E,\omega)
be a row-finite weighted graph and
\mathsf{k}
a field. The free
\mathsf{k}
-algebra generated by
\{v,e_{i},e_{i}^{*}\mid v\in E^{0},e\in E^{1},1\leq i\leq\omega(e)\}
subject to relations

(i)

uv=\delta_{uv}u,\text{ where }u,v\in E^{0}
,

(ii)

s(e)e_{i}=e_{i}=e_{i}r(e),~r(e)e_{i}^{*}=e_{i}^{*}=e_{i}^{*}s(e),\text{ where }e\in E^{1},1\leq i\leq\omega(e)
,

(iii)

\sum_{e\in s^{-1}(v)}e_{i}e_{j}^{*}=\delta_{ij}v,\text{ where }v\in E_{\operatorname{reg}}^{0}\text{ and }1\leq i,j\leq\omega(v)
,

(iv)

\sum_{1\leq i\leq\omega(v)}e_{i}^{*}f_{i}=\delta_{ef}r(e),\text{ where }v\in E_{\operatorname{reg}}^{0}\text{ and }e,f\in s^{-1}(v)
,

is called the
weighted Leavitt path algebra
of
(E,\omega)
over
\mathsf{k}
, and denoted
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
, where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta. In relations (iii) and (iv) we set
e_{i}
and
e_{i}^{*}
to be zero whenever
i>\omega(e)
.

Note that if the weight of each edge is
1
, then
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
reduces to the usual Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
of the graph
E
. It is easy to see that the mappings given by
v\longmapsto v
for all
v\in E^{0}
,
e_{i}\longmapsto e^{*}_{i}
and
e^{*}_{i}\longmapsto e_{i}
for all
e\in E^{1}
and
1\leq i\leq\omega(e)
, produce an involution on the
\mathsf{k}
-algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
. Also,
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
\mathsf{k}
-algebra with grading induced by
\deg(v)=0
for all
v\in E^{0}
,
\deg(e_{i})=1
and
\deg(e^{*}_{i})=-1
for all
e\in E^{1}
and
1\leq i\leq\omega(e)
.

Let
\mathsf{k}
be a field,
E
a sandpile graph and
(E,\omega)
the
balanced weighted graph associated with
E
. Let
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
be the Leavitt path algebra of
E
and
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
the weighted Leavitt path algebra of
(E,\omega)
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))
the set of nonzero graded ideals of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
, and denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ver}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega))
the set of all ideals of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
generated by vertices. It is well-known that every nonzero graded ideal
I
of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
always contains a vertex
v\in E^{0}
. Since
E
is a sandpile graph, there is a path
p
in
E
such that
v=s(p)
and
s=r(p)
, and so
s=r(p)=p^{*}p=p^{*}vp\in I
, that means,
I
always contains the sink
s
. This implies that
(\mathcal{L}_{\text{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)),\subseteq)
is a lattice. Similarly, we have that
(\mathcal{L}_{\text{ver}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)),\subseteq)
is a lattice. Moreover, the following theorem shows that these lattices are isomorphic to the lattice of all idempotents of
\text{SP}(E)
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathsf{k}
be a field,
E
a sandpile graph and
(E,\omega)
the balanced weighted graph associated with
E
. Then, there is a lattice isomorphism between the following lattices:

(1)
The lattice
\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
of all idempotents of
\rm{SP}
(E)
;

(2)
The lattice
\mathcal{F}_{E}
of all filters of
\mathcal{C}_{E}
;

(3)
The lattice
\mathcal{H}_{E}
of all nonempty saturated hereditary subsets of
E^{0}
;

(4)
The lattice
\mathcal{L}(\rm{SP}
(E))
of all order-ideals of
\rm{SP}
(E)
;

(5)
The lattice
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))
of all nonzero graded ideals of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
;

(6)
The lattice
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{ver}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega))
of all ideals of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
generated by vertices.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.6
, we obtain that

\rm{Idem}(SP(E))\cong\mathcal{F}_{E}\cong\mathcal{H}_{E}\cong\mathcal{L}(\text{SP}(E))

as lattices. By
[
3
, Theorem 2.5.8]
, we have

\mathcal{H}_{E}\cong\mathcal{L}_{\text{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))

as lattices.

We next claim that

\mathcal{H}_{E}\cong\mathcal{L}_{\text{ver}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega))

as lattices. Indeed, let
I
be an ideal of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)
generated by vertices. It is obvious that
I\cap E^{0}\neq\emptyset
.
We show that
I\cap E^{0}
is a hereditary and saturated subset of
E^{0}
. Let
e\in E^{1}
with
v:=s(e)\in I
. We then have
e_{i}=ve_{i}\in I
for all
1\leq i\leq\omega(e)
, and so
r(e)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq\omega(v)}e_{i}^{*}e_{i}\in I
. This implies that
I\cap E^{0}
is hereditary. Let
v\in E_{\operatorname{reg}}^{0}
with the property that
r(s^{-1}(v))\subseteq I\cap E^{0}
. We then have
r(e_{1})\in I
for all
e\in s^{-1}(v)
, and so
e_{1}=e_{1}r(e_{1})\in I
for all
e\in s^{-1}(v)
. Therefore, we obtain that
v=\sum_{e\in s^{-1}(v)}e_{1}e_{1}^{*}\in I
, and so
I\cap E^{0}
is saturated, as desired.

On the other hand if
H
is hereditary and saturated and
I(H)
is an ideal of
L(E,\omega)
generated by
H
, then
I(H)\cap E^{0}=H
by
[
16
, Theorem 2.10]
. From these observations, we immediately obtain that the corresponding
\alpha:\mathcal{L}_{\text{ver}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega))\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{E}
, defined by
I\longmapsto I\cap E^{0}
, is a lattice isomorphism, thus finishing the proof.
∎

As a corollary of Theorem
4.4
, we have the following useful fact.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\mathsf{k}
be a field,
E
a sandpile graph and
(E,\omega)
the balanced weighted graph associated with
E
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)
The lattice
\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
is a chain;

(2)
The lattice
\mathcal{F}_{E}
is a chain;

(3)
The partially ordered set
\mathcal{C}_{E}
is a chain;

(4)
The lattice
\mathcal{H}_{E}
is a chain;

(5)
The lattice
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{gr}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))
is a chain;

(6)
The lattice
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{ver}}(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega))
is a chain.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.4
, it suffices to show the equivalence of (2) and (3). It is obvious that if the partially ordered set
\mathcal{C}_{E}
is a chain, then so is the lattice
\mathcal{F}_{E}
. Assume that the lattice
\mathcal{F}_{E}
is a chain, but
\mathcal{C}_{E}
is not a chain. Then, there exist strongly connected cyclic components
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
of
E
such that
C_{1}\nleq C_{2}
and
C_{2}\nleq C_{1}
. Let
F_{C_{1}}=\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{E}\mid C_{1}\leq C\}
and
F_{C_{2}}=\{D\in\mathcal{C}_{E}\mid C_{2}\leq D\}
. It is obvious that
F_{C_{1}}
and
F_{C_{2}}\in\mathcal{F}_{E}
. Since
C_{1}\nleq C_{2}
and
C_{2}\nleq C_{1}
, we have
F_{C_{1}}\nleq F_{C_{2}}
and
F_{C_{2}}\nleq F_{C_{1}}
, that means,
\mathcal{F}_{E}
is not a chain, a contradiction. Therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{E}
is a chain, thus finishing the proof.
∎

The following lemma is useful for describing the structure of Leavitt path algebras of sandpile graphs.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathsf{k}
be a field,
E
an arbitrary graph and
H
a hereditary subset of
E^{0}
. Then, the following statements hold:

(1)

L_{\mathsf{k}}(E_{H})
is a
\mathsf{k}
-subalgebra of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H))
;

(2)
If
H
is simply a cycle based at
v
, then
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H))\cong\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda_{v}}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
, where
\Lambda_{v}
is the set of all paths in
E
which end at
v
, but which do
not contain all the edges of
H
;

(3)
If
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E_{H})
is purely infinite simple, then
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H))
is also purely infinite simple.

Proof.

(1) By the graded uniqueness theorem (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Theorem 2.2.15]
), the map
\phi:L_{\mathsf{k}}(E_{H})\longrightarrow L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H))
, defined by:
v\longmapsto v
for all
v\in H
and
e\longmapsto e
,
e^{*}\longmapsto e^{*}
for all
e\in s^{-1}(H)
, is an injective homomorphism of
\mathsf{k}
-algebras, and so
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E_{H})
can be considered as a
\mathsf{k}
-subalgebra of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H))
.

(2) It immediately follows from Theorem
4.8
and
[
3
, Lemma 2.7.1]
.

(3) Assume that
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E_{H})
is purely infinite simple. Then, by
[
2
, Theorem 11]
,
E_{H}
has a cycle, and so
E(H)
has a cycle, since
E(H)
contains
E_{H}
as a subgraph. Note that if
c
is a cycle in
E(H)
, then
c
lies in
E_{H}
. Since
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E_{H})
is purely infinite simple and by
[
2
, Theorem 11]
, every cycle in
E_{H}
has an exit. This implies that every cycle in
E(H)
has an exit.

Let
G
be a nonempty saturated hereditary subset of
E(H)^{0}
. If there exists an element
\alpha\in F(H)
such that
\alpha\in G
, then
r(\alpha)\in H\cap G
. Otherwise, since
H\neq\varnothing
,
G
must contain a vertex which lies in
H
. In any case,
G
contains a vertex
v\in H
. Let
\overline{\{v\}}
be the saturated and hereditary closure of
\{v\}
in
E_{H}
. Since
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E_{H})
is purely infinite simple and by
[
2
, Theorem 11]
,
H=\overline{\{v\}}
, and so
H\subseteq G
. Then, since every
\alpha\in F(H)
is a regular vertex in
E(H)
and connects to
H
via the edge
\overline{\alpha}
, it lies in
G
(since
G
is saturated). This shows that
G=(E(H))^{0}
, that means, the only hereditary and saturated subsets of
(E(H))^{0}
are
\varnothing
and
(E(H))^{0}
. From these observations and by
[
2
, Theorem 11]
, we obtain that
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H))
is purely infinite simple, thus finishing the proof.
∎

Let
E
be an arbitrary graph and
\mathcal{C}_{E}
the partially ordered set of all strongly connected cyclic components of
E
. A sequence of distinct elements
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
of
\mathcal{C}_{E}
is a
chain of length

k
if
C_{1}\leq\cdots\leq C_{k}
. An edge

is called an

to a strongly connected cyclic component

of

if
s(e)\in C^{0}
and
r(e)\notin C^{0}
, where
C^{0}
is the set of all vertices in

which lie on

.

For a hereditary subset
H
of

, we define the
quotient graph

E/H
as follows:

(E/H)^{0}=E^{0}\setminus H\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ (E/H)^{1}=\{e\in E^{1}\;|\ r(e)\notin H\}.

The source and range maps of
E/H
are the source and range maps restricted from
E
.

We are now in position to present the second main result of this section describing the structure of Leavitt path algebras of sandpile graphs, which extends Zelmanov et al.’s result
[
5
, Theorem 1]
to the sandpile graph setting.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
\mathsf{k}
be a field,
E
a sandpile graph and let
t
be the maximal length of a chain of elements in
\mathcal{C}_{E}
. Then
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
has a finite chain of graded ideals,
0<I_{0}<I_{1}<\cdots<I_{t}=L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
, satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

I_{0}=\rm{Soc}
(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))\cong\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda_{s}}(\mathsf{k})
, where
\Lambda_{s}
is the set of paths in
E
ending at
s
,

(2)

I_{i+1}/I_{i}

(0\leq i<t)
is a finite sum of purely infinite simple Leavitt path
\mathsf{k}
-algebras and matrix algebras
\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
, where
\Lambda
is a nonempty set,

(3)

I_{t}/I_{t-1}
is a finite sum of unital purely infinite simple Leavitt path
\mathsf{k}
-algebras and matrix algebras
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
;

(4)
The ideals
I_{i}
are invariant under every graded automorphism of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
.

Consequently, the lattice
\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
is a chain if and only if
I_{i+1}/I_{i}
is either a purely infinite simple Leavitt path
\mathsf{k}
-algebra or a matrix algebra
\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
for all
0\leq i\leq t-1
.

Proof.

Let
I_{0}
be the ideal of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
generated by
s
. Let
S_{E}
be the hereditary saturated subset of
E^{0}
including all vertices which do not connect to any cycle. It is obvious that the hereditary saturated closure of
\{s\}
is
S_{E}
, and so
I_{0}=I(S_{E})
, the ideal of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
generated by
S_{E}
. By
[
3
, Corollary 2.6.5]
,
I_{0}\cong\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda_{s}}(\mathsf{k})
, where
\Lambda_{s}
is the set of paths in
E
ending at
s
. By
[
7
, Theorem 5.2]
,
I_{0}=I(S_{E})
is exactly the socle of the Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
. By
[
3
, Theorem 2.4.15]
,
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)/I_{0}\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E/S_{E})
. Let
\{C_{11},C_{12},\ldots,C_{1k_{1}}\}
be the set of all strongly connected cycle components of
E/S_{E}
without exits. We denote by
C^{0}_{1i}
the set of all vertices which lie on
C_{1i}
. Let
I_{1}
be the ideal of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
generated by
\cup^{k_{1}}_{i=1}C^{0}_{1i}
. Since every vertex in
E
connects to
s
, we must have
I_{0}<I_{1}
. Let
H_{1}
be the hereditary saturated closure of
\cup^{k_{1}}_{i=1}C^{0}_{1i}
. By Theorem
4.8
,
I_{1}=I(H_{1})\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H_{1}))
, and so
I_{1}/I_{0}\cong\oplus^{k_{1}}_{i=1}I^{i}_{1}
, where
I^{i}_{1}
is the ideal of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E/S_{E})
generated by
C^{0}_{1i}
. Clearly, each
C^{0}_{1i}
is a hereditary subset of
E^{0}\setminus S_{E}
. By Theorem
4.8
,
I_{1}/I_{0}\cong\oplus^{k_{1}}_{i=1}L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{1i}))
.
If
C_{1i}
is simply a cycle, then
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{1i}))\cong\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda_{v}}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
, where
\Lambda_{v}
is the set of all paths in
E
which end at
v:=s(C_{1i})
, but which do not contain all the edges of
C_{1i}
, by Lemma
4.9
(2). If
C_{1i}
has at least two distinct cycles, then we claim that
L_{\mathsf{k}}(C_{1i})
is purely infinite simple. Indeed, since
C_{1i}
is strongly connected,
C_{1i}
has only the trivial hereditary and saturated subsets. Since
C_{1i}
has both at least two distinct cycles and is strongly connected, every cycle in
C_{1i}
has an exit. By
[
3
, Theorem 3.1.10]
,
L_{\mathsf{k}}(C_{1i})
is purely infinite simple, showing the claim. Then, by Lemma
4.9
(3), we immediately obtain that
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{1i}))
is purely infinite simple.

By
[
3
, Theorem 2.4.15]
,
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)/I_{1}\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E/H_{1})
. Let
\{C_{21},C_{22},\ldots,C_{2k_{2}}\}
be the set of all strongly connected cycle components of
E/H_{1}
without exits. Let
I_{2}
be the ideal of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
generated by
\cup^{k_{2}}_{i=1}C^{0}_{2i}
. Similar to the above argument, we have

I_{1}<I_{2}
,
I_{2}\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H_{2}))
and
I_{2}/I_{1}\cong\oplus^{k_{2}}_{i=1}L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{2i}))
, where
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{2i}))
is either a purely infinite simple Leavitt path
\mathsf{k}
-algebras or isomorphic to
\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
. We continue to repeat this process
t+1
times. We then obtain that
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
has a finite chain of graded ideals,
0<I_{0}<I_{1}<\cdots<I_{t}=L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
, such that
I_{i+1}/I_{i}
is a finite sum of purely infinite simple Leavitt path
\mathsf{k}
-algebras and matrix algebras
\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
, where
\Lambda
is an infinite set, for
0\leq i\leq t-2
. For
i=t-1
, we have
I_{t}/I_{t-1}\cong\oplus^{k_{t}}_{i=1}L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{ti}))
, where
\{C_{t1},\ldots,C_{tk_{t}}\}
is the set of all minimal elements of the partially ordered set
(\mathcal{C}_{E},\leq)
. Hence,
F(C^{0}_{ti})
is finite for all
1\leq i\leq k_{t}
. This implies that
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{ti}))
is either a unital purely infinite simple Leavitt path
\mathsf{k}
-algebras or isomorphic to
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

Let
\alpha
be a graded automorphism of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
. Then, since
I_{0}=\rm{Soc}
(L_{\mathsf{k}}(E))
,
I_{0}=\alpha(I_{0})
. We next claim that
I_{1}=\alpha(I_{1})
. Indeed, since
I_{0}<I_{1}
,
I_{0}=\alpha(I_{0})<\alpha(I_{1})
. Since
I_{1}
is a graded ideal of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
, so is
\alpha(I_{1})
. By
[
3
, Theorem 2.5.8]
,
\alpha(I_{1})
is equal to the ideal
I(H)
of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
generated by a hereditary and saturated subset
H
of
E^{0}
. By Theorem
4.8
,
\alpha(I_{1})\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H))
, and so
\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H)/S_{E})
. Since
\alpha
is a graded automorphism of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
,
\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}\cong I_{1}/I_{0}
as
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
\mathsf{k}
-algebras. Therefore,
\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}\cong\oplus^{k_{1}}_{i=1}L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{1i}))
. Assume that
C^{0}_{ij}\subset H
for some
2\leq i\leq t
and
1\leq j\leq k_{i}
. Then,
C_{ij}
connects to
C_{1h}
for some
1\leq h\leq k_{1}
. Let
\overline{C^{0}_{1h}}
be the hereditary and saturated subset of
(E(H)/S_{E})^{0}=E(H)^{0}\setminus S_{E}
. Let
c
be an arbitrary cycle in
C_{ij}
, and
p
the infinite path
c^{\infty}=cc\cdots c\cdots
in

. Clearly, the set
c^{0}
of all vertices which lie on

is contained in
(E(H)/S_{E})^{0}\setminus\overline{C^{0}_{1h}}
, and the set of all paths

such that

and

is different from the empty set. By
[
13
, Theorem 5.2]
,
\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H)/S_{E})
is indecomposable. This implies that

, that means,
\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}
is either a purely infinite simple Leavitt path

-algebras or isomorphic to
\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
. Hence,

is graded simple. On the other hand,
\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H)/S_{E})
always contains a proper graded ideal

, a contradiction. Therefore, we obtain that
C^{0}_{ij}\nsubseteq H
for all
2\leq i\leq t
and
1\leq j\leq k_{i}
; equivalently,
C^{0}_{ij}\cap H=\emptyset
for all
2\leq i\leq t
and
1\leq j\leq k_{i}
(since

is a hereditary subset of
E^{0}
). Since
\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}\neq 0
,
C^{0}_{1i}\subset H
for some
1\leq i\leq k_{1}
. We note that every strongly connected cycle component of
E(H)
is exactly a strongly connected cycle component of
E
. Therefore, we have

\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}\cong L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(H)/S_{E})\cong\bigoplus_{j\in J}L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{1j}))

for some nonempty subset

, and so

\bigoplus_{j\in J}L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{1j}))\cong\alpha(I_{1})/I_{0}\cong I_{1}/I_{0}\cong\bigoplus^{k_{1}}_{i=1}L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{1i})).

Then, since each
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E(C^{0}_{1i}))
is indecomposable and by
[

, Lemma 3.8]
, we must have

, that means,

for all

. This shows that

, proving the claim. Similar to the above argument, we obtain that

for all

.

Assume that
\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
is a chain. Then, by Corollary

, the partially ordered set
\mathcal{C}_{E}
is also a chain. By Items (2) and (3), we immediately obtain that

is either a purely infinite simple Leavitt path

-algebra or a matrix algebra
\mathbb{M}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{k}[x,x^{-1}])
for all

. Conversely, by the construction of all the ideals

, the partially ordered set
(\mathcal{C}_{E},\leq)
is a chain. By Corollary
4.5
, the lattice
\rm{Idem}(SP(E))
is a chain, thus finishing the proof.
∎

We close the article by describing completely the structure of the (weighted) Leavitt path algebra of a sandpile graph

such that the sandpile monoid of

has exactly two idempotents.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
\mathsf{k}
be a field,
E
a sandpile graph and
S_{E}
be the set of vertices in
E
which does not connect to any cycle. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{SP}(E)
has exactly two idempotents;

(2)
The Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E/S_{E})
is either isomorphic to
\mathbb{M}_{n}(K[x,x^{-1}])
for some
n
or purely infinite simple;

(3)
The weighted Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E/S_{E},\omega_{r})

Proof.

(1)
\Longrightarrow
(2). Assume that
\operatorname{SP}(E)
has only two idempotents. By Theorem
4.4
,
E
has only one strongly connected cyclic component
\mathcal{C}
. By Theorem
4.10
, we obtain that
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E/S_{E})
is either isomorphic to
\mathbb{M}_{n}(K[x,x^{-1}])

n

(2)
\Longrightarrow
(3). By our hypothesis and
[
3
, Proposition 3.1.14 ]
, the Leavitt path algebra

, the lattice

L_{\mathsf{k}}(E,\omega)

. By

.

(3)
\Longrightarrow
(1). Assume that
