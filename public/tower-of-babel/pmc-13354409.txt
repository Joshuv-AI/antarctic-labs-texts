Glans reconstruction utilizing a skin flap in posterior hypospadias with a small glans: a retrospective case study
Authors: Hung Thanh Le, Trong Le Duc Vo, Cuong Trung Ho, Linh Thi Truc Nguyen, Chan Cong Huynh, Phu Dai Tran, Dao Thi Anh Nguyen, Quynh Tran Minh Nguyen, An Tai Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Surgical management of posterior hypospadias remains a challenge for pediatric urologists, even for surgeons with extensive experience. The primary reason for this is the frequent presence of a small glans and severe chordee in posterior hypospadias cases. We conducted this study to evaluate the surgical outcomes of two-stage urethroplasty with Glans Reconstruction utilizing a Skin Flap (GRSF) in posterior hypospadias with severe chordee and a small glans.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed 58 posterior hypospadias patients with severe chordee and small glans (glans width ≤12 mm), who underwent two-stage urethroplasty with GRSF at Children’s Hospital 1 during the study period from January 2021 to September 2024.Results:According to Duckett’s classification, there were 10 cases of penoscrotal type (17.2%), 31 cases of midscrotal type (53.4%), and 17 cases of perineal type (29.3%). The mean glans diameter was 7.3 mm (range: 3–12 mm). Forty-two cases (72.4%) achieved penile straightening after urethral plate incision. The mean age at the first operation was 29.9 months and at the second operation was 39.7 months. Late complications were observed in 14 cases (24.1%), including urethrocutaneous fistula (5 cases), urethral dehiscence (7 cases), meatal stenosis (1 case), and urethral stricture (1 case). Glans size and other factors did not influence the outcomes of this technique.Conclusion:Two-stage urethroplasty with GRSF is an effective approach, yielding positive outcomes and acceptable complication rates for cases of posterior hypospadias with severe chordee and a small glans.

Introducing

Hypospadias is one of the most common congenital abnormalities, occurring in approximately one in 150 to one in 300 male live births[1]. As described by Duckett, hypospadias is classified into three categories based on the position of the urethral opening after correction of the chordee: anterior, mid-shaft, and posterior[2]. The posterior type accounts for about 20% of cases and is often associated with severe chordee (≥30 degrees)[3]. Chordee correction is the first priority in hypospadias repair and must be completed before urethroplasty[4]. Posterior hypospadias typically presents with a small glans (Fig. 1)[5]. It has long been established that the size of the glans influences surgical outcomes for hypospadias repair. A small glans complicates circumferential glanuloplasty around the neourethra, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative complications[5,6]. Until now, the management of hypospadias with a small glans remains inconsistent and poses a significant challenge for pediatric urologists[5,7]. At Children’s Hospital 1, we have repaired these cases with two-stage urethroplasty and had promising results. Therefore, we are conducting this study with the aim of evaluating our center’s surgical outcomes in the management of posterior hypospadias with severe chordee and a small glans (defined as glans width ≤12 mm), applying two-stage urethroplasty with Glans Reconstruction utilizing a Skin Flap (GRSF) from ventral penile skin.

Patients and methods

We retrospectively reviewed 58 patients with posterior hypospadias, severe chordee, and a small glans who underwent two-stage urethroplasty with GRSF at Children’s Hospital 1 during the study period from January 2021 to September 2024. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital 1. Posterior hypospadias was classified according to Duckett’s classification, based on the location of the urethral opening after correction of the chordee, which includes peno-scrotal, scrotal, and perineal types. Severe chordee was defined as residual chordee of 30 degrees or greater after penile degloving. In our study, a small glans diameter was defined as 12 mm or smaller.

Information was collected and analyzed through medical charts. The follow-up period was at least 6 months after the second-stage operation. Patients who received preoperative hormone therapy, had a history of penile surgery, or had their first-stage procedures performed at other centers were not included in this study.

IBM SPSS version 25 was used for the analysis. Chi-squared and ANOVA tests were used to compare the differences between the complication and non-complication groups. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Surgical technique

All procedures within this study were performed by a single surgeon, ensuring consistency in surgical technique and indications. Following general anesthesia and caudal block, a 4/0 absorbable suture (Monosyn/Vicryl) was placed through the tip of the glans as a traction suture. The incision line was marked, and regional anesthesia was administered with a solution of lidocaine 1% and adrenaline. The glans diameter was measured, and the degree of penile chordee was evaluated.

First stage of the procedure

We performed a two-stage urethroplasty using the Byars technique. In the first stage of the procedure, a skin incision is made circumferentially 3–5 mm proximal to the coronal sulcus, extending into a U-shaped incision on either side of the urethral plate and around the edge of the urethral opening. After completely degloving the penis, severe chordee has been determined using Horton’s artificial erection technique. Then, the urethral plate is incised and released from the corpora (Fig. 2a). If penile chordee remains, the persistent chordee is corrected by three ventral corporotomies (Fig. 2b)[8]. Once the penile chordee is completely corrected, we divide the glans deeply in the midline and separate the glans from the corpora cavernosa to the sides. The foreskin pedicle flaps are created by dividing the dorsal foreskin in the midline and unfolding them by dissecting the superficial fascia from the skin layer. A suture is placed at the 12-o’clock position between the dorsal skin and the glans to prevent penile torsion. Then, we rotate the foreskin flaps to the ventral aspect. The most distal points of the two foreskin flaps are sutured to the top of the glans incision. The flaps are then sutured to the glans mucosa and closed in the midline with 7/0 Maxon/PDS (Fig. 2c). We use an 8Fr Foley catheter or feeding tube for bladder drainage. Second stage of the procedure

The second stage was performed at least 6 months later. In the second stage, a U-shaped incision is made on both sides of the new urethral plate, from above the glans to below the ectopic meatus. The width of the new urethral plate should be ≥10 mm (Fig. 2d). The neourethra is constructed by tubularizing the new urethral plate over an 8–10Fr feeding tube and then covered using the dartos fascia flap (Fig. 2e). Finally, glans reconstruction is achieved by using a ventral penile skin flap. We separate the dartos fascia from the ventral skin flap to ensure mobility and a tension-free skin closure while still maintaining good vascularization. The neourethral meatus is enveloped posteriorly by the glans and anteriorly by the skin flap (Fig. 2f). This is the key difference in our technique compared to the traditional Byars procedure[9]. The urethra and the skin are closed in a single layer using 7/0 Maxon/PDS. The superficial fascia flap is sutured using 6/0 Maxon/PDS/Vicryl.

Postoperative care

We use a roll dressing for both stages of the procedure, with an inner layer of Vaseline gauze and multiple layers of gauze on the outside. The dressing is changed every 3–4 days. We do not routinely use postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis after hypospadias repair. The transurethral catheter is usually maintained for about 7–10 days in the first stage and 10–14 days in the second-stage procedure. Follow-up visits are scheduled at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively.

Results

A total of 58 cases were included in this study. The mean age at the first operation was 29.9 months (12 months–11 years), and at the second operation was 39.7 months (18 months–12 years). Comorbidities were noted in 14 cases (24.1%), including 11 cases of cryptorchidism (four unilateral and seven bilateral), two cases of inguinal hernias or hydroceles, and two cases of penoscrotal transposition. All cases with cryptorchidism underwent karyotype analysis, with results showing 46, XY.

According to Duckett’s classification, there were 10 cases of the penoscrotal type (17.2%), 31 cases of the midscrotal type (53.4%), and 17 cases of the perineal type (29.3%). The mean glans diameter was 7.3 mm (3–12 mm). Among these, 51 cases (87.9%) had a significantly small glans diameter (≤10 mm). All cases presented with severe chordee, with a mean degree of chordee after degloving of 32.8 degrees (30–50 degrees). After urethral plate incision, 42 cases (72.4%) achieved penile straightening, while 16 cases (27.6%) still had chordee requiring corporotomies. There was no recurrent chordee after either the first or the second stage of the surgery. The mean neourethra length was 3.7 cm (1.5–7.5 cm). The mean operation time for the first stage was 69.6 minutes (45–120 minutes), and for the second stage was 59.1 minutes (35–120 minutes).

The mean duration of postoperative urinary catheterization was 8.3 days (7–13 days). The decision regarding the duration of postoperative catheterization was based on the surgeon’s assessment and the wound condition. Early postoperative complications following the second stage occurred in nine cases (15.5%), all of which were surgical site infections. The follow-up period ranged from 6 to 48 months, during which late complications were observed in 14 cases (24.1%), including urethrocutaneous fistula (five cases), urethral dehiscence (seven cases), meatal stenosis (one case), and short distal urethral stricture (1 case). All cases underwent re-urethroplasty or double-knot fistula ligation after a minimum interval of 6 months.

We assessed factors that may influence treatment outcomes (late complications). The results showed no significant differences in the assessed factors between the two groups (Table 1). Discussion

Surgical management of posterior hypospadias remains a challenge for pediatric urologists, even for surgeons with extensive experience. The primary reason for this is the frequent presence of a small glans and severe chordee in posterior hypospadias cases[5,10]. According to Sennert, there are two treatment approaches for hypospadias patients with small glans: (1) preoperative hormonal therapy or (2) applying an appropriate surgical technique[5]. Preoperative hormonal therapy remains controversial and inconclusive regarding indications, androgen type, dosage, treatment efficacy, and further investigation is still needed[7,11]. The main goal of hormonal therapy is to achieve an adequate glans size suitable for urethroplasty (glans diameter ≥15 mm)[12]. Snodgrass also reported that 66% of posterior hypospadias patients are androgen resistant and 57% fail to reach the target glans size with hormonal therapy[12]. In our study, the mean glans diameter was 7.3 mm, with 87.9% of cases having very small glans (diameter ≤10 mm). This was significantly smaller than the glans size reported in most previous studies[5,10,12,13]. Fawzy believes that it is unrealistic to expect more than a 50% increase in glans size with hormonal therapy in hypospadias patients with very small glans[10].

Considering the above reasons, we chose to apply two-stage urethroplasty with GRSF to repair cases of posterior hypospadias with severe chordee and small glans. The traditional procedure was first reported by Byars in 1955[9]. This procedure allows us to completely correct the chordee in the first stage, while also being able to prepare the new urethral plate wide enough for urethroplasty in the second stage. For severe penile chordee, dorsal plication methods are ineffective due to high recurrence and penile shortening[14]. We performed urethral plate incision, excision of hypoplastic fascia, and corpus spongiosum. Subsequently, we made three ventral corporotomies to correct the chordee if necessary. Snodgrass first applied this technique to correct the penile chordee while preserving the urethral plate[8]. We have applied this technique since 2020 with promising results and no recurrent cases[15]. This chordee correction technique can be effectively combined with first-stage urethral plate reconstruction methods and does not affect subsequent urethroplasty[16]. Compared to other severe chordee correction methods, ventral corporotomies are more effective and standardized than the superficial “fairy-cut” technique and easier to perform, with less time-consuming compared to corporeal reconstruction with grafts, while still achieving penile straightening[17]. If the chordee still remains, dorsal plication can be performed. Though only 27.6% of our cases used the ventral corporotomies technique, no cases required additional dorsal plication.

Despite ongoing debate over single-stage versus two-stage repair in posterior hypospadias, the general global trend in the past 30 years favors staged urethroplasty[18,19]. The two common two-stage urethroplasty methods involve using either a foreskin pedicle flap, such as the Byars technique, or a foreskin free graft, such as the Bracka technique. Studies show similar long-term outcomes between these two methods[20]. We chose to use the Byars technique due to the surgeon’s greater experience with this technique. Our study included children with a glans diameter of <12 mm; therefore, we modified the procedure by using a ventral penile skin flap to reconstruct the glans. While several reports have described using skin flaps to augment the glans, those primarily address anterior or midshaft hypospadias in which the urethral plate is preserved[21,22]. Shaker mobilized and advanced a penile skin flap to be interpositioned between the glanular wings after Snodgrass and Mathieu procedures with low complication rates and good cosmetic outcomes[21]. Similarly, our flap is harvested from the ventro-lateral penile skin. The glans, after reconstruction, maintained its naturally triangularized shape, and the meatal position was acceptable (Fig. 3). In addition, the use of the flap shifted skin closure off the midline to avoid overlapping suture lines, provided neourethral coverage, and reduced fistula rates. Our overall complication rate was 24.1% (14 cases). The reported complication rates for the Byars technique vary widely, ranging from 3.3% to 81%[23,24]. A 2021 meta-analysis of 11 studies involving a total of 1310 patients reported a complication rate of 20%[18]. Urethral dehiscence was the most common complication (12%), which is consistent with the present study population of small glans. All cases of urethral dehiscence had a very small glans diameter (4–10 mm). During the re-operation, we used the Duplay urethral tubularization technique and required an additional penile skin flap for glans reconstruction. Two cases of meatal stenosis and distal urethral stricture also presented with a very small glans (preoperative glans diameters were 4 mm and 7 mm, respectively). Both cases exhibited straining to void and underwent redo-urethroplasty at 13 months and 7 months postoperatively. We believe that a smaller glans and a more distal location of the reconstructed meatus would contribute to increased glans tension, thereby increasing the risk of meatal stenosis. We used a dartos fascia flap placed over the neourethral suture as a waterproofing layer to prevent fistula in the second stage. Urethrocutaneous fistula occurred in five cases (8.6%) and all were small fistulas (<2 mm). These cases were repaired using double-knot fistula ligation at least 6 months after the second-stage surgery. The glans size did not affect our technical results (Table 1). Postoperative care plays a critical role in minimizing the complication rate. We used a roll dressing with an inner layer of Vaseline gauze to maintain moisture, promote wound healing, and reduce pain. This dressing helped reduce swelling and bleeding but was not too tight to ensure good flap perfusion. Dressings were changed every 3–4 days and completely removed upon catheter removal. We used an 8–10Fr feeding tube for the urethral catheter to avoid tension on the reconstructed glans. The catheter was left in place for approximately 10–14 days postoperatively to protect the neourethra and facilitate skin flap healing. Furthermore, we did not observe long urethral strictures or urethral diverticula, which are common complications after the traditional Byars technique[18].

According to Hadidi, posterior, especially perineal, hypospadias can be considered a syndrome often including severe penile chordee, small glans, undescended testes, and sometimes penoscrotal transposition. The author suggests that this is a special group of hypospadias patients requiring a distinct approach[25]. Hadidi and Fawzy reported a staged urethroplasty with chordee excision and distal urethroplasty (CEDU) in the first stage and reconstruction of the remaining perineal urethral segment in the second stage, with low postoperative complication rates[10,25]. The glans size in this study was larger than in our study (10.7 mm vs. 7.3 mm), despite the age at surgery in our study being older. These results lead us to believe there are clear differences in the clinical characteristics of hypospadias across various global regions and races. Although we lack experience with this specific technique, we are concerned that glans reconstruction in cases of significantly small glans (<9–10 mm) without glans augmentation would be very challenging.

Our study has some limitations. First, this is a retrospective study design with a small sample size. Second, the follow-up period was quite short and inconsistent, ranging from 6 to 48 months. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer postoperative follow-up are needed to more accurately evaluate the efficacy of this surgical technique.

Conclusion

Two-stage urethroplasty with GRSF is an effective approach with positive outcomes and acceptable complication rates for cases of posterior hypospadias with severe chordee and a small glans.
