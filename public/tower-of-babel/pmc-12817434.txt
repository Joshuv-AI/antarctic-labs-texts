The SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein induces microglia senescence-mediated cognitive impairment via Glycolysis
Authors: Nan-Shi-Yu Yang, Han-Xi Sha, Chen-Yu Zhang, Hui Chen, Yu-Biao Liu, Jian-Bing Xiong, Jia-Xi Duan, Jun Zhang, Cha-Xiang Guan, Yong Zhou, Feng Su, Wen-Jing Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic has precipitated a surge in neurocognitive dysfunction, with long-term implications for global health systems and socioeconomic stability. Despite growing clinical recognition of post-COVID cognitive deficits (“brain fog”), the molecular mechanisms driving these impairments remain poorly understood. Our study addresses this critical gap by identifying SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (SARS-CoV-2 N protein), a core structural component of the virus, as a novel etiological factor in senescence-mediated cognitive decline. We observed that SARS-CoV-2 N protein caused microglial senescence both in vivo and in vitro. Mechanistically, SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced metabolic shifting toward glycolysis initiated a cascade of microglial senescence, which propagated cognitive impairment. We found that glycolysis inhibition reduced SARS-CoV-2 N protein-triggered microglial senescence and attenuated cognitive impairment in mice. Disrupted mitochondrial dynamics impaired oxidative phosphorylation capacity, forcing glycolytic reprogramming that ultimately triggered cellular senescence activation. We found that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein promoted excessive mitochondrial dysfunction in microglia, resulting in mitochondrial fragmentation. Inhibition of mitochondrial fission effectively rescued SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced microglial senescence. In conclusion, our study suggests that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein induces senescence-mediated cognitive impairment by promoting glycolysis in microglia. Therapeutic targeting of glycolysis in SARS-CoV-2 N protein-triggered microglial senescence could be beneficial for treating or preventing cognitive impairment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10020-025-01410-3.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2 N protein, Cognitive impairment, Microglia, Senescence, Glycolysis

Introduction

Coronaviruses are enveloped, positive-sense RNA viruses that are globally distributed, among which severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) is a member of the family Coronaviridae (V’Kovski et al. 2021; Qin et al. 2024; Babalghith et al. 2022). Post-infection cognitive impairment—including memory loss, attention deficits, and “brain fog”—affects over 30% of patients, with elderly and severe cases showing even higher rates (up to 59% at 6-month follow-up) (Hampshire et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2021; Langa and Levine 2014). Elderly individuals demonstrate heightened susceptibility to cognitive dysfunction following COVID-19 infection (Crivelli et al. 2022). These deficits impose substantial societal and healthcare burdens, yet their mechanisms remain unclear. Microglia are central immune cells whose senescence has emerged as a key driver of cognitive dysfunction (Gulen et al. 2023). The elimination of senescent microglia alleviates cognitive impairment in aged mice (Ogrodnik et al. 2021). Despite clinical recognition of post-COVID cognitive impairment, no studies have yet identified whether SARS-CoV-2 viral proteins directly induce microglial senescence. Therefore, investigating the mechanisms underlying COVID-19-associated microglial senescence is crucial for both mechanistic understanding and clinical applications.

The SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein, the most abundant and conserved viral component, serves multiple essential functions in the viral life cycle (Zhao et al. 2024a, b; El-Saber Batiha et al. 2022; Al-Kuraishy et al. 2021). The N protein not only mediates viral RNA packaging into ribonucleoprotein complexes but also facilitates multiple aspects of the viral life cycle, from transcription and replication to host immune evasion, via dynamic protein-protein interactions (Cubuk et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2023; Al-Kuraishy et al. 2024; Mostafa-Hedeab et al. 2022). Notably, it is released extracellularly during infection and traffics to the central nervous system (CNS), accumulating preferentially in memory-related brain regions (Gao et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2020, 2021). Further researches indicate that the N protein exhibits selective tropism for memory-associated brain regions, where it may directly modulate neural function (Zhang et al. 2020; Soung et al. 2022). Notably, anti-N protein IgG was detectable in the cerebrospinal fluid of SARS-CoV-2-infected patients presenting with neurological symptoms (Remsik et al. 2021). While these findings establish the SARS-CoV-2 N protein’s neurotropic potential, two fundamental questions remain unresolved: (1) whether SARS-CoV-2 N protein deposition directly causes cognitive impairment, and (2) through what specific pathophysiological mechanisms this may occur. The potential for SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced microglial senescence to mediate post-COVID cognitive decline, and its mechanistic basis, represents a crucial therapeutic target requiring exploration.

Recent investigations have revealed a fundamental connection between glycolytic flux alterations and the induction of senescent phenotypes (Bie et al. 2024; Stabenow et al. 2022). Glucose metabolic reprogramming refers to the transformation in intracellular glucose metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis under pathological conditions, involving changes in metabolism-related enzymes, metabolic pathways, and metabolites (Xu et al. 2025). Metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis has been shown to drive cellular senescence in microglial populations (Zhao et al. 2024a, b). Furthermore, senescent microglia drive brain aging and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) progression via glycolysis-derived lactate, which activates NF-κB and stimulates pro-inflammatory IL-6/IL-8 secretion (Wei et al. 2023). Intermediate metabolites of glycolytic reprogramming (e.g., succinate, PKM2) act as signaling molecules to promote cellular senescence (Zhao et al. 2024a, b; Wu et al. 2023). The fission-fusion dynamics of environmentally sensitive mitochondria critically govern cellular metabolic regulation (Hsu et al. 2025). Mitochondrial dynamic dysfunction caused by pathological fission or fusion defects can disrupt oxidative phosphorylation, thereby trigger a bioenergy crisis and further activate the senescence pathway (You et al. 2023; Hara et al. 2013). While post-COVID cognitive impairment is well-established clinically, the mechanisms by which SARS-CoV-2 viral proteins like the N protein induce microglial senescence, particularly through metabolic reprogramming, involving mitochondrial fission and aerobic glycolysis, remain unknown.

Current research on COVID-19-associated cognitive decline focuses primarily on neuroinflammation (Al-Kuraishy et al. 2024; Vanderheiden and Klein 2022), leaving the metabolic drivers of microglial senescence unexplored. In this study, we demonstrated that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein triggered microglial senescence, which subsequently mediated cognitive impairment in mice. Mechanistically, the SARS-CoV-2 N protein promoted a glycolytic shift, driving microglial senescence and associated neurocognitive dysfunction. Our research indicates that SARS-CoV-2 N protein-mediated metabolic dysregulation represents a potential strategy to prevent microglial senescence and subsequent neurocognitive deficits in post-COVID syndrome.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

The 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were provided by Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Hunan, China). Mice were individually kept in ventilated, temperature- and humidity-controlled environments (24–26 °C, 50%−60%) under a 12:12 h light: dark cycle. All animal protocols were approved by the Clinical Medical Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (2021101127).

In vivo viral infection

To perform in vivo viral infection studies, the C57BL/6J mice were intranasally inoculated with the pSLenti-EF1-EGFP-P2A-Puro-CMV-2019-nCov-N(Optimized)−3xFLAG-WPRE (Lot: H29311, OBiO Technology, Shanghai, China) at a dosage of 1 × 105 TCID50 every two days, or vector for the uninfected control group. Mice were monitored daily for clinical symptoms and weight loss for up to 14 days post-infection.

Animal treatments

C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into three groups: Control, SARS-CoV-2 N protein, and SARS-CoV-2 N protein + 2-Deoxy-D-arabino-hexose (2-DG, an inhibitor of glycolysis). Mice in the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group received infections of SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Optimized). To assess the role of glycolysis in SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced cognitive impairment, mice in the SARS-CoV-2 N protein + 2-DG group were injected with 2-DG (50 mg/kg, dissolved in saline solution, Lot: D8375, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) intraperitoneally 2 h before intranasal delivery with SARS-CoV-2 N protein. 2-DG was injected intraperitoneally every 2 days during the 14 days of SARS-CoV-2 N protein-infected mice. On Day 14, all mice were euthanized, and their brains were harvested for further analysis. All procedures were conducted under anesthesia with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (80 mg/kg). Open field test (OFT)

OFT can be used to assess the locomotor ability of mice. Mice are allowed to freely explore a square exercise box (100 cm long, 100 cm wide, 40 cm high) for 5 min. The time mice stayed in the central area of the open field and the frequency of mice crossing the central area of the open field were observed and recorded. At the end of the experiment, the mice were removed, and the entire arena box was cleaned with 75% alcohol. After the boxes dried, the experiment was repeated with different mice.

Y-maze test (YMT)

YMT (8 mice per group) is widely used to study spatial learning and memory (Yang et al. 2024). The test is conducted using a Y-shaped maze with three wooden arms, which was performed by marking each arm as a starter arm, a familiar arm, and a novel arm. The recognition abilities of the mice were measured by blocking off one arm of the Y-maze (the novel arm), and their spatial working memory was assessed by recording the spontaneous alternations. In this study, the test consists of training and test sessions. For the training session, the novel arm was closed, and the mice were placed in the starter arm, allowing free access to the familiar arm for 5 min. After 2 h, during the test phase, the animal was again placed on the starter arm, and its preference for the novel arm or known arms was observed for 5 min. The Smart3.0 was used to record how long the mice stayed in the novelty arm, the number of times they entered it, and the number of times they entered the unrepeated consecutive arm (i.e., ACB, BAC, CAB).

H&E staining and inflammatory injury score

The brain tissue was fixed in a 4% neutral buffered formaldehyde solution for a minimum of 24 h at 4 °C. Following fixation, the brains were embedded in paraffin wax, and serial coronal sections of approximately 4–5 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome. The brain slices were subsequently deparaffinized and rehydrated before undergoing hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Lot: G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China), a standard histological technique used to visualize general tissue architecture and cellular morphology. Hematoxylin stains nuclei blue, while eosin stains cytoplasm and extracellular matrix components in shades of pink or red, enabling clear differentiation of cellular structures and detection of abnormalities, such as inflammation, necrosis, or changes in cell morphology. After staining, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with a coverslip using a resin-based mounting medium to preserve the tissue integrity and enhance optical clarity. The stained brain sections were then examined under a digital slide scanner, and high-resolution images were captured using the Pannoramic Scan system (3Dhistech, Hungary, Budapest).

Cell culture

Murine BV2 microglia were provided by Procell (Wuhan, China). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Lot: 12491015, DMEM, Gibco, USA) containing 4.5 g/L glucose was supplied with 10% fetal bovine serum (Lot: 164250, Procell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Lot: PB180120, Procell) for maintaining cells in a humid environment containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Plasmids and cloning

Codon-optimized pLJM1-SARS-CoV-2 N-FLAG was cloned from SinoBiological (cat# VG40588-NF) using BamHI and EcoRI.

Transient transfections

Transient transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Lot: L3000001, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, Murine BV2 microglia were seeded in 12-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and transfected with 1 µg of Codon-optimized pLJM1-SARS-CoV-2 N-FLAG and 1.5 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 for 48 h.

Cell treatment

Cells were planted into the 12-well plate and transfected with 1 µg of Codon-optimized pLJM1-SARS-CoV-2 N-FLAG and 1.5 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 for 48 h. To assess the role of glycolysis in SARS-CoV-2 N-protein-induced senescence in BV2 cells, we treated cells with the glycolysis inhibitor (2-DG, 5 mM, Sigma-Aldrich) 30 min before Codon-optimized pLJM1-SARS-CoV-2 N-FLAG transfection. To evaluate the role of mitochondrial division in SARS-CoV-2 N-protein-induced senescence in BV2 cells, we treated cells with the DRP1 inhibitor (Mdivi-1, 100 nM, Lot: HY-15886, MedChemExpress, USA) 30 min before Codon-optimized pLJM1-SARS-CoV-2 N-FLAG transfection.

Western blot

RIPA lysate (containing a protease inhibitor, Lot: R0010, Solarbio) was added to the hippocampus or cells. The samples were completely lysed at 4 ℃ for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was absorbed, and the total protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA, Lot: BCA01, Ding Guo Prosperous, China). After denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, 30 µg of protein was loaded on SDS-PAGE gels for electrophoresis. A total of 30 µg protein underwent separation on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel and was subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, Lot: 256638/237437, Merck, Germany) membrane. Then, blocked with 5% skim milk or bovine serum albumin (BSA, Lot: 143184, Biofroxx, Germany) for 1 h. The primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4 ℃. The next day, the secondary antibody was incubated with the TBST wash film at room temperature for 1 h, after rewashing the PVDF membrane with TBST. Protein detection was carried out using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Lot: CW0064M, JiangsuCowinBiotechCo., Ltd., China) detection kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The membranes were incubated with the ECL reagent, and the chemiluminescent signals were captured using the ChemiDoc XRS imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). The resulting images were analyzed using Image Lab Analyzer software (Bio-Rad), which allowed for precise quantification of protein band intensities. The relative expression levels of target proteins were normalized to internal loading controls and subjected to statistical analysis to evaluate significant differences between experimental groups. All primary and secondary antibodies used in this study were validated for their specificity and suitability in Western blot applications. Lactate production

The concentration of lactate was measured using a lactate assay kit (Lot: MAK570, Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The main reaction mixture consists of 2 µL lactate probe, 26 µL sample solution, 26 µL lactate assay buffer, and 2 µL lactate mix. The sample was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured calorimetrically at 570 nm and fluorometrically at λex = 535 nm and λem = 587 nm.

Immunofluorescence

Lung tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-p21 and anti-IBA1, at 4 ℃ overnight. The next day, after washing with PBS buffer, homogeneous fluorescence double-labeling staining was performed using the Tyramide Signal Amplification Kit (Lot: AFIHC024, AiFang Biological, Hunan, China). Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Lot: C0065, Solarbio) for 10 min. Sections were blocked with an anti-fluorescence quencher (Lot: S200, Solarbio). Images were captured using Panoramic Scan (3Dhistech, Hungary, Budapest). The antibodies used in the study are shown in Table 1.

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential

The JC-1 assay kit (Lot: C2006, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to measure the mitochondrial membrane potential (Yang et al. 2022). Cells cultured in 48-well plates were rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove any residual culture medium and non-adherent debris before mitochondrial membrane potential assessment. Following the wash steps, the cells were incubated with JC-1 staining solution for 20 min at 37 °C in a dark environment to prevent fluorescence degradation. After staining, the cells were washed three times with JC-1 staining buffer to remove any unbound dye and minimize background fluorescence, which is essential for obtaining clear and specific signals. Fluorescent images were then captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with appropriate excitation and emission filters for both red and green fluorescence channels. The resulting images allowed for qualitative analysis of mitochondrial membrane potential changes across different experimental conditions.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining

The senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining kit was from Beyotime Biotechnology (Lot: C0602, Shanghai, China) (Zhang et al. 2023). The cells were washed twice with PBS. One milliliter of fixative solution was added to the 24-well plate, and the cells were fixed at room temperature for 8 min. The cells were washed thrice with PBS, each for 5 min. The staining mixture was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The cells were washed with PBS, and the staining results were observed under a microscope. The number of SA-β-Gal-positive cells, identified by their bluish-green coloration, was quantified by counting three randomly selected microscopic fields, and the results were expressed as a percentage of the total cell population.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as means ± standard deviation and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired t-test was employed. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences among multiple groups, followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. All experiments were independently replicated three times. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SARS-CoV-2 N protein drives cognitive impairment in mice

To determine whether the SARS-CoV-2 N protein induces cognitive impairment, we investigated its effects using the LV-brain-N C57BL/6J mouse model. C57BL/6J mice were administered LV-SARS-CoV-2 N protein via intranasal administration, and cognitive impairment was assessed 14 days later. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, mice overexpressing the SARS-CoV-2 N protein displayed significant hypoactivity in the OFT, including reduced average moving speed, shorter total ambulatory distance, and fewer central zone entries relative to the LV-vector group (Fig. 1B-E). The Y-maze test was employed to assess spatial learning and memory. During the training phase, the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group exhibited significantly reduced total distance traveled and prolonged immobility time compared to the LV-vector group, indicating that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein impairs exploratory motivation in novel environments (Fig. 1F-H). During the Y-maze test phase, the spontaneous alternation rate of the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group was lower than that of the LV-vector group (Fig. 1J). Additionally, the N protein-overexpressing mice demonstrated reduced exploratory behavior, as evidenced by fewer entries into the novel arm (Fig. 1K) and significantly decreased time spent in the novel arm (Fig. 1L). These behavioral deficits collectively suggest that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein impairs spatial working memory and novelty-seeking behavior in mice.

Subsequently, we focused on the effects of the SARS-CoV-2 N protein on neurons, the cellular basis of cognitive function. H&E staining showed normal neuronal architecture in the hippocampal neurons of the LV-vector group. At the same time, the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group exhibited significant histopathological changes, including disrupted cytoarchitecture and reduced neuronal density in the DG, CA1, and CA3 regions of the hippocampus (Figs. 1M–N). SARS-CoV-2 N protein treatment led to a significant decrease in the expression of ZO-1 in the mouse cerebral cortex, indicating a potential association with neuronal damage (Figs. 1O–P). We further assessed the levels of amyloid precursor protein (APP), Tau, and phosphorylated Tau at threonine 231 (p-TauT231), which are closely linked to cognitive impairment, in hippocampal tissue. The results revealed that, compared to the LV-vector group, the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group exhibited elevated levels of APP and p-TauT231 (Figs. 1Q–R). Collectively, these data reveal that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein drives cognitive impairment in mice.

SARS-CoV-2 N protein triggers senescence in hippocampal microglia of mice

Microglial senescence represents a key pathogenic mechanism in cognitive decline (Shea and Villeda 2025). We found that SARS-CoV-2 N protein induced upregulation of senescence-associated genes (p16, p19, p21, and p53) and proteins (p16, p53, and p21) in the hippocampal tissue of mice (Fig. 2A-H). Importantly, we further observed higher expression levels of hippocampal microglia activation marker IBA1 and senescence-associated protein p21 in the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group. In addition, more activated IBA1+ microglia co-localized with p21 protein in the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group (Fig. 2I-K). We also used immunofluorescence to detect colocalization of N protein and IBA1 in the hippocampus of the mice. The results showed that the level of N protein was significantly increased in the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group (Figure S1A-B). Overall, the above data suggest that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein triggers senescence of hippocampal microglia in mice.

SARS-CoV-2 N protein triggers senescence in BV2 microglial cells in vitro

Microglia, as the principal innate immune effectors in the CNS, demonstrate activation phenotypes and functional adaptations that critically contribute to the pathogenesis of cognitive dysfunction (McGroarty et al. 2025). To investigate whether the SARS-CoV-2 N protein triggers microglial senescence, BV2 microglial cells were stimulated by transfection with Codon-optimized pLJM1-SARS-CoV-2 N-FLAG. Our results demonstrated a significant increase in the proportion of β-gal-positive cells in the SARS-CoV-2 N-protein-treated BV2 microglia cells (Fig. A-B). Further analysis revealed up-regulated expression of senescence-associated genes and downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines in BV2 microglia cells treated with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 3C-G). Furthermore, elevated levels of senescence-associated proteins were observed in the SARS-CoV-2 N-protein-treated BV2 microglia cells (Fig. 3H-L). Senescent cells displayed significantly reduced expression of the proliferation marker Ki67, consistent with their growth-arrested state. The SARS-CoV-2 N protein treatment demonstrated a significant decrease in Ki67 expression in BV2 microglia cells (Fig. 3M-N). In conclusion, these findings suggest that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein induces cellular senescence in BV2 microglial cells.

Glycolysis mediates the SARS-CoV-2 N protein-driven senescence in BV2 microglial cells

Several previous studies have indicated that glycolytic reprogramming is closely associated with cellular senescence (Bie et al. 2024; Park et al. 2023). We found that the levels of lactate, the product of aerobic glycolysis, were increased in the BV2 microglial cells treated with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the expression of glycolysis-related genes and proteins was up-regulated in BV2 microglial cells treated with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 4B-D and S1B). These results indicate that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein promotes glycolytic reprogramming in BV2 microglial cells. To further investigate the role of glycolytic reprogramming in SARS-CoV-2 N-protein-induced BV2 microglial cell senescence, we used the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG. We found that 2-DG reduced lactate content in the BV2 microglia culture supernatant caused by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, 2-DG attenuated the expression of glycolysis-related proteins (Figs. 4F–G) and decreased the number of β-gal-positive BV2 microglial cells induced by SARS-CoV-2 N protein treatment (Figs. 4H–I). We also observed that 2-DG reduced the expression of senescence-associated genes and downstream pro-inflammatory factors in BV2 microglia cells induced by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein l0(Fig. 4J and S1D). Moreover, 2-DG mitigated the expression of senescence-associated proteins in BV2 microglia cells induced by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. M-N). Additionally, 2-DG significantly increased the expression of Ki67 in the SARS-CoV-2 N-protein-treated BV2 microglia cells, indicating enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. 4K-L). Overall, inhibiting glycolytic reprogramming attenuates SARS-CoV-2 N-protein-induced senescence in BV2 microglial cells.

Inhibition of Glycolysis alleviates cognitive impairment induced by SARS-CoV-2 N protein in mice

Then, we investigated whether inhibiting glycolysis could alleviate cognitive impairment induced by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein in mice. Using the LV-brain-N C57BL/6J mouse model, we treated the mice with 2-DG. The timeline of the animal experimental procedures is summarized in Fig. 5A. In the OFT, 2-DG treatment significantly increased the average moving speed, moving distance, and times of crossing the central area compared with the N protein-overexpressing mice (Fig. 5B-E). These results demonstrate that glycolytic inhibition mitigated the reduction in exploratory ability induced by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein. During the training phase of the Y-maze test, 2-DG treatment significantly increased total distance and reduced the total resting time compared with the mice overexpressing the SARS-CoV-2 N protein, suggesting that glycolytic inhibition enhances the motivation to explore novel environments (Fig. 5F-H). During the Y-maze test phase, 2-DG treatment increased the spontaneous alternation rate compared with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group (Fig. 5J). Furthermore, 2-DG treatment significantly increased both the number of entries into the novel arm (Fig. 5K) and the duration spent in the novel arm (Fig. 5L) relative to the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group. These Y-maze results demonstrate that 2-DG significantly alleviated SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced cognitive impairment. H&E staining revealed that 2-DG attenuated neuronal damage in the hippocampal region of mice overexpressing the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 5M-N). Western blot analysis showed that 2-DG treatment decreased the expression levels of APP, Tau protein, and p-TauT231 in hippocampal tissue compared with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group (Fig. 5O-P). In summary, inhibiting glycolysis significantly alleviates cognitive impairment induced by SARS-CoV-2 N protein in mice.

Inhibition of Glycolysis attenuates SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced microglial senescence in the hippocampal region of mice

Next, we verify whether the SARS-CoV-2 N protein promotes cellular senescence in hippocampal microglia through glycolytic reprogramming. Treatment with 2-DG recovered blood-brain barrier permeability compared to the SARS-CoV-2 N protein group (Fig. 6A-B). And we found that glycolytic inhibition reduced the expression of senescence-associated genes and pro-inflammatory factors in the hippocampal tissue of the N protein-overexpressing mice (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, we also observed that glycolytic inhibition markedly reduced the expression of senescence-associated proteins in the hippocampal microglia of mice overexpressing the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 6D-E). In addition, 2-DG significantly reduced the colocalization of p21 and IBA1 in the hippocampal microglia of the N protein-overexpressing mice (Fig. 6F-H). Taken together, inhibiting glycolysis attenuates SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced cellular senescence in hippocampal microglia of mice.

SARS-CoV-2 N protein drives the senescence of BV2 microglial cells by triggering mitochondrial dysfunction

Impaired mitochondrial dynamics compromise oxidative phosphorylation, driving a metabolic shift toward glycolysis that subsequently activates cellular senescence (Pang et al. 2025). To examine mitochondrial dysfunction in BV2 microglial cells after SARS-CoV-2 N protein treatment, we measured mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), a standard marker of mitochondrial health. JC-1 staining results showed that the mitochondrial membrane potential was lost by the treatment with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 7A and S2A), suggesting that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction of microglia. Additionally, we used an antibody targeting the mitochondrial outer membrane protein TOM20 to visualize the mitochondrial network morphology through immunofluorescence. Significant mitochondrial fragmentation was observed in BV2 microglial cells treated with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 7B and S2B-C). Furthermore, we observed elevated phosphorylation levels at the 616 site of the mitochondrial fission protein DRP1 in BV2 microglial cells treated with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 7C-D). These results demonstrate that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein induces increased mitochondrial fission, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction in BV2 microglial cells. Then we used the mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1. We observed that Mdivi-1 restored the loss of MMP in BV2 microglial cells induced by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 7E and S2D). Moreover, Mdivi-1, mitochondrial fission inhibition, alleviated the expression of senescence-associated genes and downstream pro-inflammatory factors in BV2 microglial cells treated with SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Fig. 7F and S2E). Subsequently, Mdivi-1 decreased the number of β-gal-positive BV2 microglial cells induced by SARS-CoV-2 N protein treatment (Figs. 7G and S2F). Mdivi-1 significantly up-regulated Ki67 expression in the SARS-CoV-2 N protein-treated group (Fig. 7H and S2G), indicating enhanced cell proliferation. Inhibition of mitochondrial fission also suppressed the expression of senescence-associated proteins in BV2 microglial cells following exposure to SARS-CoV-2 N protein (Figs. 7I–J). Additionally, Mdivi-1 treatment decreased lactate levels and expression of glycolytic enzymes in SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced microglia cells, as shown in Figure S3. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that mitochondrial fission inhibition alleviates senescence in BV2 microglial cells triggered by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein.

Discussion

While COVID-19 no longer constitutes a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, a significant proportion of convalescent patients experience persistent cognitive impairment. This study revealed the SARS-CoV-2 N protein, the fundamental architectural element of viral particles, as a novel molecular driver of neurocognitive impairment (Fig. 8). Our study provided the first experimental evidence that the N protein of SARS-CoV-2 induced microglial senescence and subsequent cognitive impairment in mice. Mechanistically, SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced metabolic shifting toward glycolysis initiates a cascade of microglial senescence that propagates cognitive impairment. Collectively, this work elucidates previously unrecognized mechanisms underlying post-COVID neurocognitive dysfunction, revealing the N protein of SARS-CoV-2 as a critical pathogenic determinant.

Schematic illustration. SARS-CoV-2 N protein promotes excessive mitochondrial division to enhance glycolysis, thereby inducing microglia senescence, leading to cognitive impairment in mice

SARS-CoV-2 can reach brain regions in various ways, such as by damaging the basal membrane of the blood-brain barrier (Kaufer et al. 2022), through cranial nerves that innervate the olfactory mucosa (Meinhardt et al. 2021), and via the vagus nerve into the brainstem (Bulfamante et al. 2021). The N protein of SARS-CoV-2, the pathogen of COVID-19, is one of the most abundant viral structural proteins, with multiple functions in viral particles, host cell environments, and in vitro assays (Ye et al. 2020). Studies have shown that N protein antibodies persist in the plasma of patients infected with COVID-19 for up to 12 months, increasing the possibility that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein can reach the brain (Guo et al. 2022). Previous studies have also demonstrated that the hippocampal tissue is particularly susceptible to viral infections (Figueiredo et al. 2019). Brain scans of patients recovering from COVID-19 revealed significant changes in the volume of grey and white matter in the hippocampus, as well as alterations in metabolism (Lu et al. 2020; Douaud et al. 2022). The SARS-CoV-2 N protein can induce hyperphosphorylation of Tau, leading to cognitive impairment in mice (Orsini et al. 2024), and can also co-aggregate with αS to form composite αS/N-protein fibrils, thereby accelerating the development of synucleinopathy (Semerdzhiev et al. 2023). These results suggest that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein plays a central role in cognitive impairment related to post-COVID-19 syndrome. Microglia are the most abundant type of immune cell in the CNS, and microglial senescence plays a crucial role in cognitive impairment (Wei et al. 2023). Senescent microglia release pro-inflammatory factors, which aggravate neuroinflammation and thereby damage the nerves in the brain (Yang et al. 2022). Our research observed that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein could induce cognitive impairment in mice concomitant with obvious senescence of microglia in the mouse hippocampal tissue. Current understanding of COVID-19-related neurocognitive dysfunction has been primarily attributed to several distinct yet potentially interconnected pathogenic mechanisms. Neuroinflammatory pathways have emerged as a major contributor, where microglial activation and the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) drive synaptic pruning and neuronal damage through the activation of the complement cascade, particularly involving the C1q and C3 components (Fernandez-Castaneda et al. 2022). Parallel research has demonstrated the significance of cerebrovascular dysfunction in this context, wherein SARS-CoV-2 infection of endothelial cells leads to blood-brain barrier impairment through downregulation of tight junction proteins such as claudin-5, ultimately resulting in hypoperfusion and white matter injury (Lee et al. 2021). Additionally, growing evidence supports the involvement of autoimmune mechanisms, where molecular mimicry between viral antigens and neuronal proteins enables cross-reactivity of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies with critical neurotransmitter receptors, including NMDA and GABAA receptors, thereby disrupting normal synaptic transmission (Monje and Iwasaki 2022). While these mechanisms have been extensively studied, our current findings reveal an additional, previously underappreciated dimension of metabolic dysregulation in COVID-19-associated neurocognitive impairment, mediated through viral protein-induced microglial senescence. This metabolic axis appears to operate distinctly from, yet potentially synergistically with, these established pathways, offering new insights into the complex pathophysiology of post-COVID neurological sequelae. Our research found, at the cellular level, that the SARS-CoV-2 N protein can also cause senescence of BV2 cells, and the results are consistent with those at the animal level. Our experimental results strongly suggest that microglial senescence induced by SARS-CoV-2 N protein is a key pathogenic mechanism of cognitive impairment following SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Emerging evidence demonstrates that SARS-CoV-2 infection disrupts mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), resulting in increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) production (Al-Kuraishy et al. 2022; Xie et al. 2024). The elevated mtROS stabilizes HIF-1α, which redirects carbon molecules from mitochondrial oxidation through glycolysis, providing substrates for viral biogenesis (Huang et al. 2022; Guarnieri et al. 2024). Notably, such glucose metabolic reprogramming has become a key trigger of cellular senescence. Microglial metabolic reprogramming plays a critical role in the neuroinflammation and cognitive dysfunction associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection (Alves et al. 2023; Zhong et al. 2024). Metabolically reprogrammed microglia release IL-6 and TNF-α, further exacerbating blood-brain barrier disruption and neuronal apoptosis (Hu et al. 2020; Ma et al. 2023). This study also found that the N protein induced senescence of BV2 cells by promoting glycolysis. In contrast, inhibiting glycolysis can alleviate N protein-induced senescence in mouse microglia and cognitive impairment in mice. These findings indicate that viral infection triggers microglial glycolysis and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby amplifying neurotoxicity and contributing to memory deficits. The significance of mitochondrial health in the aging process and the development of age-related diseases (Zhong et al. 2023a, b). Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a dual role in cellular senescence, acting both as an initiating trigger and a downstream consequence. Initially, mitochondrial respiratory chain defects disrupt ATP production, forcing cells to shift toward glycolysis, a process that elevates reactive ROS levels. This ROS accumulation damages mitochondrial DNA and proteins, further impairing mitochondrial function in a self-perpetuating cycle. Over time, senescence-associated metabolic reprogramming, such as increased glycolysis and reduced oxidative phosphorylation, exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction. Thus, while early mitochondrial dysfunction may trigger senescence via ROS-mediated stress, the senescent state itself reinforces mitochondrial decline through metabolic alterations. Similar to our observations in microglia, researchers have found that overexpression of Drp1 leads to abnormal mitochondrial division in pulmonary artery endothelial cells, generating ROS that activate HIF-1α signaling and glucose metabolic reprogramming (Sun et al. 2025). During the aging process, the enhancement of glycolytic activity is often accompanied by a decline in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation function, resulting in an insufficient mitochondrial energy supply and promoting the maintenance of dynamic balance through mitochondrial division (Ly et al. 2020). Our research also observed that the N protein induced senescence of BV2 cells by promoting excessive mitochondrial division. The above results tentatively suggest that the N protein promotes excessive mitochondrial division to enhance glycolysis, thereby inducing microglial senescence, which leads to cognitive impairment in mice. These results suggest a novel mechanism where viral proteins exploit mitochondrial dynamics-glycolysis crosstalk. The complex interplay among glycolysis, mitochondrial quality control, and cellular senescence may explain neurological symptoms in post-COVID-19 syndrome, echoing recent findings of metabolic dysregulation in long COVID patients (Codo et al. 2020; Guarnieri et al. 2023).

Our study identifies three promising druggable targets along the N protein-glycolysis-senescence axis, while also highlighting drug repurposing opportunities, including metformin (currently in long COVID trials, NCT05576662, for mitochondrial improvement (Bramante et al. 2022) and senolytic combinations (dasatinib plus quercetin (Gu et al. 2025) for clearing senescent microglia. For patient stratification, we propose a multimodal biomarker panel encompassing CSF lactate levels (to monitor glycolytic flux) and plasma senescence-associated secretory phenotype (including IL-6 and MMP-3). Our research still has some limitations. Firstly, the involvement of other neural cell types in the model treated with the SARS-CoV-2 N protein was not assessed. The experimental outcomes showing that 2-DG alleviates N protein-induced cognitive dysfunction cannot exclude potential contributions from other neural cell populations. Research into other neural cell types in people infected with SARS-CoV-2 would be an important area to focus on in the future. Second, although we established that mitochondrial dysfunction drives microglial senescence, the precise mechanistic relationship between mitochondrial dynamics (particularly fission) and glycolytic reprogramming requires further investigation. While the current literature supports the notion that mitochondrial fragmentation precedes a glycolytic shift in other cell types (Codo et al. 2020), we acknowledge that our study did not directly demonstrate this temporal relationship in microglia. This important aspect will be addressed in our ongoing work, which utilizes live-cell imaging of mitochondrial morphology in conjunction with real-time metabolic flux analysis. Third, regarding glycolysis-induced senescence, while we confirmed the critical role of glycolytic activation, the downstream signaling mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Previous studies have shown that glycolytic enzymes, such as HK2 and PKM2, can promote senescence through non-metabolic functions (Zhang et al. 2021; Guarnieri et al. 2023). However, whether the SARS-CoV-2 N specifically modulates these moonlighting activities requires further investigation. Our future work will employ glucometabolic approaches to systematically characterize the key metabolic alterations induced by the SARS-CoV-2 N protein. In addition to its inhibitory effect on glycolysis, 2-DG may also exert multiple biological functions, including competitive inhibition of glucose transport, elevation of circulating glucose levels, induction of apoptosis or autophagy, and neuroprotective activities. The potential involvement of these mechanisms in SARS-CoV-2 N protein-induced cognitive dysfunction will be systematically investigated in future research. While current literature confirms SARS-CoV-2 neuroinvasion, direct evidence of human microglial infection remains limited. It remains unclear whether microglial cells are directly infected by SARS-CoV-2 in patients with COVID-19, or if the SARS-CoV-2 N protein enters microglia via alternative mechanisms (e.g., exosomal transfer, cell fusion). This distinction has fundamental implications for brain health and long COVID-19 pathogenesis, necessitating further investigation.

In summary, this study reveals the critical role of the N protein in cognitive impairment after SARS-CoV-2 infection by inducing microglia senescence. Our findings also reveal that microglia senescence is a key target for intervention strategies for cognitive impairment after SARS-CoV-2 infection.
