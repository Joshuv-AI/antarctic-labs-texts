PRS-BC313 integration for tailored breast cancer prevention in female patients and their healthy relatives
Authors: Rosanna H E Krakowsky, Stephan Drukewitz, Alexandra Händel, John Wiedenhöft, Bahriye Aktas, Johannes R Lemke, Vincent Strehlow, Julia Hentschel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecise breast cancer risk assessment (BCR) is essential for personalised prevention in women with a family history of hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC). The CanRisk model integrates monogenic variants with reproductive, lifestyle and familial factors and can be extended by Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS). We evaluated the impact of PRS-BC313 in three groups: (1) healthy carriers of (likely) pathogenic variants (LP/P), (2) affected LP/P carriers and (3) healthy female relatives from variant-negative HBOC families.In healthy LP/P carriers, median 10-year breast cancer (BC) risk remained stable, while individual estimates ranged from+25% to −16% compared with calculations without PRS. Among affected LP/P carriers, contralateral BC risk shifted by+24% to −11%, indicating PRS effects even in high-risk individuals.In healthy relatives, applying the recently introduced German threshold of an 8% BC risk between ages 40 and 50 years resulted in escalation to intensified surveillance in ~6% (5/86) (2.4–14%, Wilson binomial CI with Yates’ correction) and de-escalation in ~1% (1/86) (0.061–7.2%).Here, we provide new evidence of PRS clinical impact under the updated German threshold.Keywords: Clinical Decision-Making, Evidence-Based Practice, Genetic Association Studies, Genetic Counseling, Gynecology

Accurate breast cancer risk assessment (BCR) in women with genetic predisposition is essential for guiding preventive strategies, individualising screening recommendations and fostering informed decision-making. While high-penetrance variants in genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 confer substantial risk, they explain only a fraction of hereditary breast cancer (BC) cases. In recent years, the concept of polygenic inheritance characterised by the cumulative effect of numerous common variants has significantly expanded our understanding of BC susceptibility.1 2 Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS) quantify an individual’s genetic predisposition by aggregating the effects of genomic variants. One of the most extensively validated PRS is the PRS-BC313, which incorporates 313 variants associated with BC risk.1 3 4 Integrated into models such as CanRisk, PRS-BC313 enhances individual risk prediction by adding polygenic information to established risk factors. In CanRisk, the PRS is combined with reproductive, hormonal, family, lifestyle factors and breast density. This multifactorial modelling enables more precise and individualised BC risk stratification, potentially guiding preventive measures such as intensified screening or prophylactic surgery.5

Despite increasing validation, clinical implementation of PRS remains heterogeneous, and data regarding its impact across clinically relevant subgroups in hereditary cancer care are limited.6 In Germany, for example, only a minority of centres within the German Consortium for Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer (GC-HBOC) offer PRS-based risk assessments as part of routine diagnostics. Importantly, BC risk is not determined solely by monogenic predisposition but also by polygenic background.2 6 Thus, an individual carrying a likely pathogenic/pathogenic (LP/P) variant in a moderate-risk gene may have a substantially higher overall risk if accompanied by a high PRS than initially assumed based on monogenic status alone. Conversely, a low PRS may attenuate the overall risk estimate, potentially modifying an individual decision regarding risk-reducing surgery.

Within the GC-HBOC framework, unaffected female relatives from HBOC families without identified LP/P variants previously qualified for intensified surveillance if their calculated 10-year BC risk reached ≥5%. Since March 2026, the threshold in Germany has been set to a ≥8% risk of breast cancer (BC) between ages 40 and 50 years, reducing the number of women eligible for intensified screening. Accordingly, the clinical impact of PRS for this subgroup depends not only on their predictive performance but also on the chosen risk threshold and the health policy framework governing access to intensified surveillance.

Building on previous findings that PRS-BC313 modifies clinical recommendations in ~12% of HBOC cases,6 we analysed its impact in three groups: (1) healthy women carrying LP/P variants, (2) affected LP/P carriers and (3) unaffected female relatives of patients with HBOC without identified LP/P variants. Risk assessment included monogenetic, ancestry, clinical, hormonal, familial and lifestyle data using the CanRisk model; breast density was included where available. Ancestry was inferred using a predefined set of 79 ancestry-informative markers, which have previously been demonstrated to reliably distinguish major continental ancestry groups (online supplemental figure 1; https://www.internationalgenome.org/).

Of 547 initially assessed patients, 225 were included after applying exclusion criteria (online supplemental figure 2). The final cohort comprised 38.2% (86/225) healthy relatives under the age of 50 from families without identified LP/P variants, 38.7% (87/225) healthy LP/P carriers and 23.1% (52/225) LP/P carriers affected by HBOC.

In healthy and affected LP/P carriers, median 10-year BC risk estimates remained stable, but individual predictions varied notably (online supplemental figure 3, figure 1). Among healthy women carrying LP/P variants, 10-year BC risk estimates showed a considerable range, with individual differences from 25% higher to 16% lower than initially calculated without PRS-BC313 (figure 1A). For instance, a woman aged 45–50 with a LP/P PALB2 variant experienced an increase in her 10 year risk from 26% to 40.5% after PRS integration (patient 5, figure 1A), while a woman aged 30–35 with a LP/P BRCA2 variant had her risk decrease from 31.2% to 14.8% (patient 88, figure 1A). These PRS-informed adjustments could meaningfully influence individualised decisions regarding risk-reducing surgery.

In affected LP/P carriers, the predicted 10-year risk for contralateral BC varied substantially after PRS-BC313 integration, rising by up to 23% or decreasing by approximately 11% following PRS-BC313 integration (figure 1B). For example, patient 2 with a LP/P BRCA1 variant, aged 35–40 years at the time of the study, showed an increase in her risk from 12.6% to 26.2%, whereas patient 53 with a LP/P BRCA1 variant, also aged 35–40 years old, experienced a decrease from 43% to 31.7%. This mirrors observations made in unaffected carriers of LP/P variants, suggesting that PRS exerts a comparable modifying effect across both affected and unaffected individuals harbouring pathogenic variants. These PRS-informed risk modifications could meaningfully influence personalised decision-making of healthy carriers, particularly with respect to risk-reducing surgical interventions and their timing. Our findings underscore the broad modifying potential of PRS beyond monogenic risk stratification and highlight its prospective impact on preventive and predictive medicine.

In healthy relatives from variant-negative HBOC families, median 10-year risk estimates remained largely unchanged (online supplemental figure 3); however, PRS-BC313 integration led to meaningful individual BC risk reclassification. Applying the former 5% threshold for 10-year BC risk, ~10% (9/86) (5.2–19% Wilson binomial CI with Yates’ correction) exceeded the threshold for intensified early detection, while 7% (6/86) (2.9%–15%) had risk reductions sufficient to de-escalate surveillance (figure 2A). This indicates that some women previously considered at unremarkable genetic risk may newly qualify for intensified surveillance, whereas others may no longer meet criteria for it.

Specifically ~10% (9/86) [5.2-19%] of women exceeded the 5% 10-year risk threshold used to recommend intensified surveillance, while approximately 7% (6/86) [2.9%–15%] dropped below this threshold, suggesting potential de-escalation of screening.

B) PRS-BC313-driven reclassification of screening recommendations based on the updated GC-HBOC threshold of 8% BC risk between ages 40 and 50 (applicable as of March 2026). At the new 8% BC risk threshold, PRS integration led to six changes in screening recommendations: ~6% (5/86) [2.4-14%] women newly qualified for intensified surveillance and ~1% (1/86) [0.061-7.2%] underwent de-escalation, highlighting reduced reclassification compared to the 5% threshold.

Notably, under the new 8% threshold for BC risk between ages 40 and 50 years in Germany, five women newly qualified for intensified surveillance, while one no longer met criteria following PRS integration, highlighting a meaningful shift compared with the former 5% threshold (figure 2B). Previously, 15 out of 86 women had PRS-driven changes in screening recommendations; under the updated threshold, this number decreased to 6 out of 86, reflecting more restrictive risk stratification.

Among the five women reclassified to intensified surveillance, one would already have qualified under the previous 5% threshold without PRS-BC313 measurement (z-score 1.7093); two had also met escalation criteria on PRS-BC313 measurement previously (z-scores 1.8109 and 2.5626). The remaining two represent newly identified candidates who would not have been captured under the former approach: their 10-year BC risks incorporating PRS were 4.7% and 1.2%, respectively, and they were aged 30–35 and 20–25 years at the time of consultation (z-scores 2.0091 and 2.1529). This underscores a key advantage of the updated guidelines: by anchoring the risk assessment window to the decade between ages 40 and 50 years, clinically relevant risk elevations in younger women are no longer systematically missed due to the absence of an applicable risk window using older frameworks.

The single woman reclassified to de-escalation further illustrates the impact of PRS integration. Under the former 5% threshold, her 10-year BC risk was 13% without PRS-BC313 and 7.9% with PRS-BC313, both still qualifying her for intensified surveillance. However, under the updated 8% threshold, her estimated BC risk between ages 40 and 50 years decreased from approximately 12% to 7% with PRS incorporation, placing her below the 8% cut-off and resulting in a de-escalation of her screening recommendation.

All in all, this illustrates that PRS-BC313 integration may enable both escalation and clinically meaningful de-escalation. Whether PRS-guided reclassification improves outcomes remains to be shown prospectively.

Beyond statistical refinement, accurate risk classification carries profound psychosocial implications. Women incorrectly classified as high-risk may undergo unnecessary MRI screenings and may experience heightened anxiety. Given that patients with early-detected BC have a better overall survival than patients with advanced-stage disease, the potential benefits of PRS-guided risk stratification appear plausible and warrant further investigation.7 8

The predictive performance of PRS-BC313 is strongest for oestrogen receptor–positive BC, moderate for oestrogen receptor–negative tumours, and limited for triple-negative subtypes, underscoring the need for subtype-specific PRS tools. Since tumour subtype cannot currently be predicted in unaffected individuals, application across all subtypes remains a pragmatic interim strategy.5 9 10 Until outcome-based evidence becomes available, PRS should be considered a quantitative risk modifier rather than a stand-alone clinical decision instrument, consistent with currentAmerican College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidance.11

While PRS integration results in measurable risk reclassification, the clinical consequences of such reclassification remain unknown. Prospective studies with long-term follow-up are required to assess whether PRS-informed surveillance strategies improve clinically meaningful endpoints, including cancer stage at diagnosis, quality of life and mortality.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that PRS-BC313 modifies BC risk estimates in healthy as well as affected variant carriers and extend these observations to a clinically under-represented group: unaffected relatives from HBOC families without LP/P variants. Apart from limited prior reports, this subgroup remains insufficiently studied, and our data provide novel insight into risk stratification in scenarios where monogenic testing and family history-based models often offer limited discriminatory power.1 2 6 Comparative data on the impact of the former and updated risk thresholds on screening recommendations in this specific subgroup are, to our knowledge, currently limited.

Important limitations of our study must be acknowledged. First, a subset of SNPs within PRS-BC313 required imputation rather than direct genotyping; while these variants are characterised by relatively common allele frequencies and modest effect sizes and were systematically excluded across all participants, this may represent a minor source of imprecision in individual risk estimates.

Second, sample sizes were modest, and participants were of European ancestry, limiting generalisability. This reflects broader challenges in PRS research, as most models have been developed and validated in populations of European descent. The retrospective design precludes assessment of clinical endpoints. Furthermore, although CanRisk represents the most comprehensive risk prediction model currently available, absolute risk estimates remain sensitive to the accuracy of family history and lifestyle data, as well as to operator-dependent input variability.

Finally, several barriers hinder the routine clinical implementation of PRS. These include limited reimbursement, the absence of standardised clinical guidelines and variable clinician familiarity with polygenic risk assessment. Standardised workflows, quality control measures and structured user training are essential to ensure reproducibility and minimise technical bias. Technical challenges further complicate implementation, as PRS are typically derived from SNP-array data, whereas monogenic variant identification relies on next-generation sequencing (NGS). For reasons of efficiency and methodological consistency, deriving both PRS and monogenic data from a unified NGS platform would be preferable. Currently, imputation is performed according to the consortium’s standard protocol, although the integration of NGS-based polygenic scoring into routine clinical pipelines remains an important area for future development.12 Validation across diverse ancestries remains critical to mitigate the perpetuation of health inequities in risk stratification. Standardised workflows, quality control measures and structured user training, including consistent application of tools such as CanRisk, are essential to ensure reproducibility and minimise technical bias.13

Despite these challenges, PRS-BC313 meaningfully stratified BC risk across patient groups, producing clinically relevant changes in absolute risk estimates consistent with prior reports.2 4 6 Importantly, PRS complements rather than replaces established models, refining estimates when integrated with family history, clinical, hormonal and lifestyle factors within tools such as CanRisk.4 14 Our data further provide novel insight into a clinically under-represented subgroup: unaffected relatives from variant-negative HBOC families. Here, monogenic testing and pedigree-based models alone offer limited discriminatory power. Precise risk reclassification in this context may improve clinical outcomes by enabling timely intensified surveillance in high-risk individuals while reducing unnecessary interventions in those with lower risk. While prospective evidence on clinical outcomes remains to be established, the accumulating data support an optimistic perspective on PRS integration. Ongoing and future studies are expected to further substantiate the utility of PRS-informed risk assessment in guiding preventive decision-making.11 15 Standardised, transparent and reproducible risk assessment pathways will be essential to ensure that high-risk individuals are appropriately identified and that preventive care is optimally allocated.

Overall, PRS-BC313 holds meaningful potential to refine preventive recommendations and to support individualised clinical decisions regarding intensified surveillance or risk-reducing strategies, representing a significant step forward in the realisation of precision medicine in hereditary BC care.

Methods

This study aims to evaluate how the inclusion of the PRS based on 313 genomic variants (PRS-BC313) influences BC risk estimation by refining existing risk prediction models.

Study population

The study cohort comprised three groups of women:

Healthy female relatives under the age of 50 years of BC index cases with no identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (genetically unremarkable).

Healthy female relatives under the age of 50 years of BC index cases with no identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (genetically unremarkable).

Healthy women carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in genes associated with hereditary BC.

Healthy women carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in genes associated with hereditary BC.

Women with HBOC (affected) carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in genes associated with hereditary BC.

Women with HBOC (affected) carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in genes associated with hereditary BC.

Participants were recruited through the genetic counselling unit and clinical genetics services of the Institute of Human Genetics at Leipzig University Hospital. Recruitment occurred during routine counselling sessions in 2024 as well as through re-contact of eligible patients up to 2 years after their initial presentation. Over this period, all individuals meeting inclusion criteria for one of the three groups were consecutively selected.

Information on BC status was based on clinical history obtained during genetic counselling. Because detailed histopathological reports were not available for all affected participants, we could not systematically distinguish between invasive BC and in situ disease in all cases. Accordingly, all self-reported or clinically documented BC diagnoses were included.

Regarding ancestry, the cohort consisted of individuals of European descent, which aligns well with the reference population used for the validation of the PRS-BC313 model (online supplemenfigure S1).

In total, 547 women were approached for study participation and invited to provide written informed consent. Of these, several groups were excluded based on predefined criteria. Eighteen individuals diagnosed exclusively with ovarian cancer (OC) were excluded, as the CanRisk model does not currently offer validated parameters for estimating BC risk in OC-affected patients. Two additional women were excluded because their age exceeded the model’s applicable prediction range (limited to individuals up to 80 years). A further 301 probands were excluded due to incomplete data on key lifestyle, hormonal or reproductive risk factors required for comprehensive BC risk estimation within the CanRisk framework. Of these, approximately 10 individuals actively declined participation, while the remaining exclusions were due to missing information essential for model-based risk calculation. One individual of East Asian ancestry was excluded.

The final cohort composition and the sequential exclusion process are illustrated in online supplemental figure S2.

Genetic analysis

Women from families with suspected HBOC either underwent in-house panel diagnostics, which included PRS-BC313 testing, or were referred with an external report of targeted Sanger testing or panel diagnostics. For women where a pathogenic variant (PV) was known within the family, targeted testing was conducted as part of the in-house panel diagnostics. In cases where external diagnostics had been performed or a healthy female relative from families with suspected HBOC had not yet been genetically tested, PRS-BC313 measurement was performed via a panel.

Of the known lower-penetrance CHEK2 variants, p.I157T was included, as it is incorporated into the PRS-BC313 model and contributes to risk estimation within CanRisk. In contrast, p.S428F—classified in Germany as likely benign—was not included in the monogenic category, nor in the PRS, and therefore was not reported. p.T476M, a recognised risk allele, was reported as a monogenic variant. Accordingly, only p.I157T was treated as a contributory variant within the polygenic and combined risk assessment framework.

Molecular genetics

Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood using the MagCore Kit 101 and MagCore instrument (RBC Bioscience, New Taipei City, Taiwan). DNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and Qubit (Thermo Scientific). NGS was performed after sample preparation with the Twist Library Preparation EF Kit 2.0, and the Twist Universal Adapter System—TruSeq Compatible, 96 Samples Plate A–D, along with enrichment using the Twist Custom Panel (design name: Cancer_PRS_HUGV6; Twist Design ID: TE-96674869 or HousePanel v2/v2.1, Twist Design ID TE-96574338/TE-91078956, Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, California, USA). All panels covered the coding sequence of all known genes predisposing for HBOC (BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, TP53, ATM, CHEK2, RAD51C, RAD51D, BARD1, BRIP1, SMARCA4, STK11, PTEN, CDH1 as well as genomic variants to calculate the PRS-BC313 and 79 genomic variants to determine the ancestry). Sample identification was conducted using the Nimagen RC-PCR assay. Sequencing was performed on a NextSeq500/550 Mid Output v2.5 kit (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA; sequencer: Illumina NextSeq550, Illumina). The mean coverage was at least 300×, with all target regions covered at least 20×. Both single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and copy number variants (CNVs) were detected.

Variant classification and panel sequencing

The raw data were analysed using the Varfeed software (Limbus, Rostock, Germany), and variants (SNVs and CNVs) were annotated with the Varvis software (Limbus). All variants were described according to GRCh38 and classified following the latest ACMG criteria and ClinGen VCEPs, if available. Databases such as ClinVar, HGMD and HerediCare were used for classification, considering factors such as gene and variant attributes, population frequency (gnomAD), predicted impact on protein function, in silico prediction tools (primarily BayesDel, Revel and SpliceAI-lookup), conservation and phenotype.

Targeted sequencing for PRS calculation

Raw reads were quality checked using fastqc, and remaining adapter sequences and low-quality data were removed using trimmomatic. The processed data were aligned to hg19 using minimap2, and visual duplicates were flagged with samtools. Haplotypes for the BCAC-313 PRS model were called using freebayes, and positions with coverage below 20× or with conflicting haplotype signals were imputed using twice the allele frequency as described in the BCAC-313 model. The resulting haplotypes were then used to calculate the normalised z-score via the CanRisk API. The final z-scores were combined with the corresponding BOADICEA file to estimate the 10-year risk for each participant. It is important to note that the original PRS was derived from SNP-array data; however, the simultaneous analysis of monogenic variants and PRS via NGS is more efficient. This approach requires careful identification and exclusion of array-specific artefacts from the PRS calculation to ensure accuracy. Currently, imputation adheres to the GC-HBOC consortium’s standard protocol.12 16

Risk calculation and data handling

Risk calculations were performed via the CanRisk API, incorporating available clinical, familial, ancestral and genetic data. For each case, risk was calculated both with and without inclusion of PRS-BC313, allowing for direct comparison. The analogue CanRisk questionnaire was digitised using an Excel template, in which a trained study staff member manually entered participants’ responses. These digital entries were then imported into the CanRisk interface for automated risk calculation. The transfer from the analogue to the digital format was carefully monitored and checked to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Due to the frequent unavailability of PRS data from relatives, we were unable to include PRS information of relatives in the model. Additionally, genetic data from family members carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were often missing. There was also inconsistency in whether relatives had undergone targeted single-gene testing or broader multigene panel diagnostics. For these reasons, detailed genetic test results for family members were not incorporated into the model (but for the index). Instead, familial risk was included in a simplified form, based solely on whether relatives were reported as ‘affected’ or ‘not affected’, along with their age at diagnosis.

Pedigree information was available for at least three generations, depending on participant knowledge. Birth and death years were either provided or estimated when unavailable. Only relatives with sufficient age information were included in CanRisk calculations, as the model requires this for accurate risk estimation.

Gene panel data were incorporated wherever available. For participants in groups 2 and 3 (carriers of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants), individual diagnostic results from gene panels—including BRCA1, BRCA2 and other HBOC-associated genes listed in CanRisk—were directly included in the risk calculations. For women in the variant-negative group, gene panel information from the tested index patient or counselled proband was used to confirm the absence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the family.

In this manner, monogenic findings were systematically integrated into the CanRisk model for the index participant, ensuring accurate representation of carrier status in combined PRS and monogenic risk estimation.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism, SPSS and Excel software. CIs were computed using the R base library.

Ancestry Prediction prediction

Genotypes of 79 variants (online supplemental information) that allow for a differentiation between the superpopulations of European, East Asian, South Asian and African ancestry were used as an input to create a principal component analysis model. The genotyping of the samples was done using FreeBayes V.1.3.7. The genotypes were mapped to the principal component analysis model, followed by logistic regression to predict the ancestry of the sample.

To benchmark the performance of our ancestry prediction we used the 2156 samples from the 1000 Genome project with known ancestry (https://www.internationalgenome.org/).

Samples from the 1000 Genome project were filtered to the regions of the 79 SNPs after that the ancestry of the sample was predicted with our approach. For the superpopulations of European, East Asian, South Asian and African, the model correctly predicted 2115 out of 2156 (98.09%) of the 1000 genome samples.

For each sample included in the presented study, the ancestry was predicted using the described model. Additionally, we visualised the results of ancestry prediction for each sample together with the labelled data of 1000 genome project samples as a reference.

Allele frequencies

Discrepancies between the allele frequencies observed in our cohort and those reported in population reference databases (gnomAD v3/v4) were noted for a subset of SNPs. These deviations most likely reflect a combination of low-complexity genomic regions at certain loci and array-specific technical artefacts rather than true biological divergence from the reference population. As detailed by Baumann et al, NGS-based genotyping of PRS loci is subject to well-characterised technical limitations that can contribute to such discrepancies. Importantly, affected SNPs were systematically excluded from analysis across all participants; their impact on the overall validity of risk estimates is therefore expected to be minimal.12

Statistical testing

Distribution equivalence between our local cohort and the PRS base cohort was evaluated by testing reported PRS percentiles against the standard uniform distribution (on the interval (0, 1)) using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.
