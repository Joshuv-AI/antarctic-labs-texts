Revised safety evaluation of the food enzyme catalase from the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus niger strain CTS2093
Authors: EFSA Panel on Food Enzymes (FEZ), Holger Zorn, José Manuel Barat Baviera, Claudia Bolognesi, Francesco Catania, Gabriele Gadermaier, Ralf Greiner, Baltasar Mayo, Alicja Mortensen, Yrjö Henrik Roos, Marize L M Solano, Henk Van Loveren, Laurence Vernis, Cristina Andreoli, Andrea Terron, Ana Criado, Jaime Aguilera, Magdalena Andryszkiewicz, Yi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe food enzyme catalase (hydrogen‐peroxide:hydrogen‐peroxide oxidoreductase; EC 1.11.1.6) is produced with the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus niger strain CTS2093 by Shin Nihon Chemical Co., Ltd. In a previous evaluation, the Panel could not exclude safety concerns due to an insufficient margin of exposure (MoE). As a follow‐up, the applicant updated the intended uses, conducted an in vitro micronucleus test using a new food enzyme batch and updated the homology search for allergenicity. In the present evaluation, the dietary exposure was calculated to be up to 0.032 mg total organic solids (TOS)/kg body weight (bw) per day in European populations. When combined with the no observed adverse effect level previously reported (56 mg TOS/kg bw per day), the Panel derived an MoE of at least 1750. Based on the new data, the revised MoE and the previous evaluation, the Panel concluded that this food enzyme does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.Keywords: Aspergillus niger, caperase, catalase, EC 1.11.1.6, EFSA‐Q‐2016‐00532, EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00165, food enzyme, hydrogen‐peroxide:hydrogen‐peroxide oxidoreductase

INTRODUCTION

Article 3 of the Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 1 provides a definition for ‘food enzyme’ and ‘food enzyme preparation’.

‘Food enzyme’ means a product obtained from plants, animals or microorganisms or products thereof including a product obtained by a fermentation process using microorganisms: (i) containing one or more enzymes capable of catalysing a specific biochemical reaction and (ii) added to food for a technological purpose at any stage of the manufacturing, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of foods.

‘Food enzyme preparation’ means a formulation consisting of one or more food enzymes in which substances such as food additives and/or other food ingredients are incorporated to facilitate their storage, sale, standardisation, dilution or dissolution.

Before January 2009, food enzymes other than those used as food additives were not regulated or were regulated as processing aids under the legislation of the Member States. On 20 January 2009, Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes came into force. This Regulation applies to enzymes that are added to food to perform a technological function in the manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of such food, including enzymes used as processing aids. Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 2 established the European Union (EU) procedures for the safety assessment and the authorisation procedure of food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings. The use of a food enzyme shall be authorised only if it is demonstrated that:

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

there is a reasonable technological need;

there is a reasonable technological need;

its use does not mislead the consumer.

its use does not mislead the consumer.

All food enzymes currently on the European Union market and intended to remain on that market, as well as all new food enzymes, shall be subjected to a safety evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and approval via an EU Community list.

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background as provided by the European Commission

Only food enzymes included in the Union list may be placed on the market as such and used in foods, in accordance with the specifications and conditions of use provided for in Article 7(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes.

On 10 December 2024, a new application was introduced by the applicant ‘Shin Nihon Chemical Co., Ltd’ for the authorisation of the food enzyme Catalase from non‐genetically modified Aspergillus niger (strain CTS2093).

This application is a follow‐up of a negative opinion adopted by EFSA on 25 January 2023 (EFSA‐Q‐2016‐00532). EFSA concluded that the margin of exposure is insufficient to exclude safety concerns for the food enzyme under the intended conditions of use. The new application contains revised information on the intended used and on the estimated dietary exposure.

Following the requirements of Article 12.1 of Regulation (EC) No 234/2011 implementing Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008, 3 the Commission has verified that the application falls within the scope of the food enzyme Regulation and contains all the elements required under Chapter II of that Regulation.

Terms of Reference

The Commission requests the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) to verify the requirements of Article 32b of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, whether the suitability of the new data is in line with the requirements of Article 12(2) of Regulation (EU) No 234/2011. The Commission also requests EFSA to carry out the safety assessment and the assessment of possible confidentiality requests on the new data for the following food enzyme Catalase from non‐genetically modified Aspergillus niger (strain CTS2093), in accordance with Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 establishing a common authorisation procedure for food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings.2

Interpretation of the Terms of Reference

In January 2023, EFSA could not conclude on the safety evaluation of the food enzyme catalase from a non‐genetically modified Aspergillus niger strain CTS2093 (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a). Other aspects of the safety of the food enzyme were addressed in the previous Scientific Opinion.

In the previous application (EFSA‐Q‐2016‐00532), the CEP Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level of 56 mg food enzyme‐total organic solids (TOS)/kg body weight (bw) per day, the mid‐dose tested, which, when compared with the estimated dietary exposure, resulted in a margin of exposure (MoE) of 16. Based on the data provided, the Panel considered the MoE as insufficient to exclude safety concerns under the intended conditions of use. Consequently, the Panel could not conclude on the safety of the food enzyme (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

As a follow‐up, the applicant withdrew five of the intended uses and updated the use levels for the remaining four food manufacturing processes. Furthermore, the applicant conducted an in vitro micronucleus test using a new food enzyme batch and updated the literature search for the allergenicity assessment. The present opinion assessed these new data provided in the application EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00165 and reaches a conclusion on the safety of the food enzyme.

Toxicological data

In the previous evaluation, the Panel concluded that genotoxicity tests (bacterial reverse mutation test and in vitro mammalian chromosomal aberration test) did not indicate a safety concern and identified a no observed adverse effect level of 56 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the mid‐dose tested (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

In the current application, the applicant has provided an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test using a new food enzyme batch. It has activity of 246,000 U/g, 3.4% of protein, 1.0% of ash, 93.3% of water, which results in 5.7% TOS and an enzyme activity/TOS ratio of 4316 U/mg TOS. 7 This new batch has a similar enzyme activity/TOS ratio as all four batches evaluated in EFSA CEP Panel (2023a); thus, the Panel considered it a suitable test item. 8

Furthermore, the lead content of this batch was below 0.05 mg/kg 9 which complies with the specification for lead as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). In addition, arsenic concentration was below the limit of quantification (LoQ) of the employed method. 10

This batch complies with the microbiological criteria for total coliforms, Escherichia coli and Salmonella, as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). 11 No antimicrobial activity was detected. 12

The presence of aflatoxins (B1, B2, G1, G2), ochratoxin A, sterigmatocystin, T‐2 toxin, zearalenone and fumonisins (B1, B2) was examined and found below the LoQ of the applied methods. 13 , 14

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test

The in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test was carried out according to the OECD Test Guideline 487 (OECD, 2016) and following good laboratory practice (GLP). 15 A preliminary range‐finding and two main experiments were performed with duplicate cultures of human peripheral whole blood lymphocytes. The cell cultures were treated with the food enzyme with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix).

Based on the results from the range‐finding test, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for the frequency of binucleated cells with micronuclei (MNBN) at concentrations of 500, 1000 and 1250 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (3‐h exposure and 21‐h recovery period) without S9‐mix or at concentrations of 500, 1000, 1500 and 1750 μg TOS/mL in the presence of S9‐mix. Cells were also exposed to the food enzyme and scored for MNBN at concentrations of 250, 500 and 1000 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment (24‐h exposure and 24‐h recovery period) without S9‐mix.

No cytotoxicity was seen either in the short‐term treatment with or without S9‐mix or in the long‐term treatment. In the short‐term treatment, the frequency of MNBN was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at all concentrations tested.

The frequency of MNBN was statistically significantly different to the negative controls at concentrations ≥ 250 μg TOS/mL in the long‐term treatment, with a concentration‐related response. All but one single replicate culture values were within the 95% of the historical control range.

In a confirmatory experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for MNBN at concentrations of 400, 600, 800 and 1000 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment without S9‐mix. Cytotoxicity of 55% and 19% (based on replication index) was observed at 800 and 1000 μg TOS/mL. The frequency of MNBN was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at any concentrations tested.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme did not induce an increase in the frequency of MNBN under the test conditions applied in this study.

Allergenicity

The allergenicity of the food enzyme has been assessed before; see the previous evaluation (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

The applicant provided an updated homology search with a newer version of the AllergenOnline database (version 24, January 2026), which confirmed the original findings.

In conclusion, the Panel confirmed its previous conclusion that, under the intended conditions of use, a risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to this food enzyme cannot be excluded, but that the likelihood is low.

This food manufacturing process is not included in the ‘Food manufacturing processes and technical data used in the exposure assessment of food enzymes’ (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023b).

The Panel noted a change in the use levels for two food manufacturing processes when compared to the previously recommended use levels. The applicant ascribed these changes to the availability of actual use levels reported by food manufacturers. 18

In the production of cheese and in the production of fish roe, following a treatment with hydrogen peroxide, the catalase is added to the raw materials (rennet 19 and herring roe, 20 respectively) to catalyse the removal of residual amounts of hydrogen peroxide. The food enzyme–TOS remains in the final foods. According to the applicant, repeated washing steps could theoretically remove the food enzyme–TOS from the fish roe. However, in the absence of analytical data to demonstrate the extent of such removal, 21 the Panel opted for a conservative scenario, assuming that 100% of the food enzyme–TOS are transferred in the fish roe.

In the processing of eggs and egg products, 22 and in the production of coffee extracts, 23 the raw materials are treated with glucose oxidase and the food enzyme is added to catalyse the removal of hydrogen peroxide. The food enzyme–TOS remains in the final foods.

The food enzyme has a temperature optimum around 50°C (pH 7.0) and a pH optimum around 7.0 (30°C). Thermostability was tested by pre‐incubation of the food enzyme for 15 min at different temperatures (pH 7.0). Enzyme activity decreased above 50°C, showing no residual activity above 75°C (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a). Based on these data and the downstream processing within the respective food manufacturing processes, the Panel concluded that the food enzyme is inactivated during the processing of eggs and egg products and during the production of coffee extracts. However, it may remain in its active form in fish roe and cheese, depending on the heat treatment conditions.

Dietary exposure estimation

Chronic exposure to the food enzyme–TOS was calculated using the FEIM webtool 24 by combining the maximum recommended use level with individual consumption data (EFSA CEP Panel, 2021). The estimation involved selection of relevant food categories and application of technical conversion factors (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023b) together with the information provided in Appendix C.

Table 2 provides an overview of the derived exposure estimates across all surveys. Detailed mean and 95th percentile exposure to the food enzyme–TOS per age class, country and survey, as well as contribution from each FoodEx category to the total dietary exposure are reported in Appendix A – Tables 1 and 2. For the present assessment, food consumption data were available from 51 dietary surveys (covering infants, toddlers, children, adolescents, adults and the elderly), carried out in 27 European countries (Appendix B). The highest dietary exposure was estimated to be 0.032 mg TOS/kg bw per day in toddlers at the 95th percentile.

The conservative approach applied to estimate the dietary exposure to the food enzyme–TOS, in particular assumptions made on the occurrence and use levels of this specific food enzyme, is likely to have led to an overestimation of the exposure.

Margin of exposure

In the previous evaluation, the Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of 56 mg TOS/kg body weight (bw) per day, the mid‐dose tested, resulting in a MoE of at least 16 (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

A comparison of the NOAEL with the newly derived exposure estimates of 0–0.010 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the mean and from 0 to 0.032 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the 95th percentile resulted in an MoE of at least 1750.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on the previous and newly submitted data, and the updated MoE, the Panel concludes that the food enzyme catalase produced with the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus niger strain CTS2093 does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.
