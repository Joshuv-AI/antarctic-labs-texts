MedicalAgentsBench for Complex Medical Reasoning: Comparing Internalized Reasoning Models versus Externalized Agent-based Frameworks
Yanjun Shao, Xiangru Tang, Jiwoong Sohn, Jiapeng Chen, Yuxuan Liao, Jiayi Zhang, Jinyu Xiang, Fang Wu, Yilun Zhao, Chenglin Wu, Wenqi Shi, Arman Cohan, Mark Gerstein ORCID: 0000-0002-9746-3719

Abstract




SUMMARY

Complex medical reasoning requires integrating heterogeneous clinical evidence across multiple inference steps. Large language models (LLMs) now approach this through two routes: internalized reasoning and externalized agent scaffolding (frameworks that decompose problems collaboratively amongst multiple LLMs). To determine whether these routes are exclusive or complementary, we introduce MedicalAgentsBench, a filtered benchmark of 862 complex clinical questions drawn from the union of eight medical datasets via difficulty-aware curation and contamination screening. Evaluating three internalized reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, o1-mini, and o3-mini), seven base models, and nine externalized agent-based methods, we find that internalized and externalized approaches each independently improve performance, and that their benefits compound: the highest accuracy is achieved by layering agent workflows onto an internalized reasoning model (i.e., o3-mini + MDAgents with 35.1%). Pareto analysis shows this combination dominates the cost–performance frontier; moreover, lightweight optimization on inexpensive models offers an entry point for resource-constrained settings. Our benchmark is at
https://github.com/gersteinlab/MedicalAgentsBench
.

To address these challenges, we introduce MedicalAgentsBench, a benchmark specifically designed to enable a principled comparison between internalized reasoning models and externalized agent frameworks on complex medical reasoning tasks.
Unlike existing benchmarks that either focus on general medical knowledge or suffer from ceiling effects, MedicalAgentsBench employs a pipeline that:
(1)

(4)

(a)
both externalized agent scaffolding and internalized reasoning independently improve performance, and their benefits are complementary: layering agent workflows on internalized reasoning models (e.g., o3-mini + MDAgents) yields the highest overall accuracy;

(b)
the Pareto frontier of cost–performance trade-offs is dominated by reasoning-model–agent combinations at the high end and by lightweight workflow optimization methods (e.g., SPO) at the low end;
and
(c)
open-source reasoning models achieve competitive accuracy, though their extended reasoning chains do not necessarily reduce per-sample inference cost.

Quantitative evaluations

Most models struggle with our challenging benchmark, with even powerful systems like GPT-4o achieving only 32.2% accuracy on MedQA and 18.1% on MedExQA in our Hard subset. This substantial performance drop confirms that our selection criteria effectively identify questions that require advanced reasoning. Amid these challenges, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates remarkable performance, achieving the highest scores on five datasets (MedMCQA: 30.9%, MMLU: 43.7%, MMLU-Pro: 36.8%, MedExQA: 26.0%, and MedXpertQA-U: 26.3%). Similarly, o3-mini excels on two datasets (MedQA: 52.9% and MedBullets: 50.3%).

The central finding from our method comparison (Table
4
) is that externalized and internalized reasoning are complementary. Agent frameworks substantially improve base models: for instance, GPT-4o + AFlow (29.8% average) outperforms bare GPT-4o zero-shot by a wide margin. Reasoning models independently advance the frontier: bare o3-mini (27.8%) already surpasses most agent-augmented base-model configurations. Critically, layering agent workflows on top of reasoning models yields further gains: o3-mini + MDAgents achieves the highest overall accuracy (35.1%), followed by o3-mini + MultiPersona (34.1%) and o3-mini + CoT (32.7%), all surpassing bare o3-mini by 5–7 percentage points. This indicates that agents add value even when the underlying model is already strong, likely by introducing diverse reasoning paths and catching errors that a single model pass would miss.

Among individual methods, automated workflow optimization (AFlow) and multi-persona prompting (MultiPersona) achieve the strongest results when applied to base models, while discussion-based agent frameworks (MedAgents, MDAgents) show their greatest value when paired with reasoning models. Traditional prompting gains from CoT-SC over CoT are inconsistent on this hard subset, and domain-specific methods like MedPrompt lack consistency across datasets (see Table
4
for full per-dataset results). Additional qualitative error analysis and representative failure cases are provided in Supplemental Note S1.

Benchmark quality assessment

We assess benchmark quality along two dimensions: reasoning depth to verify cognitive difficulty and data contamination to ensure fair and reliable model comparison.

Reasoning depth analysis

To directly assess whether MedicalAgentsBench emphasizes multi-step clinical reasoning rather than superficial difficulty or obscure knowledge retrieval, we conducted an expert-annotated analysis of reasoning depth using a standardized five-level rubric detailed in the human validation protocol in Methods. Four medical students in their clinical years independently annotated a stratified sample of 20 questions per dataset (10 from the Full split and 10 from the Hard split), covering all eight source datasets across nine evaluation subsets (180 questions total). This design enables a direct comparison of the cognitive demands imposed by the original datasets versus the curated benchmark. Figure
2
reveals a clear structural shift in cognitive demands between the two splits. In the full distributions of generalist benchmarks such as MMLU and MMLU-Pro, reasoning depth is bottom-heavy, with a predominance of Level 1 and Level 2 questions solvable via direct fact retrieval or single-step deduction. In contrast, the Hard subset exhibits a pronounced shift toward Levels 4 and 5, corresponding to multi-hop clinical reasoning that requires synthesizing heterogeneous evidence or resolving competing hypotheses.

This pattern is consistent across diverse medical domains and is not driven by a small number of outlier datasets. For example, in MedXpertQA, the Hard subset effectively removes direct-recall questions and concentrates on scenarios that require integrating patient history, vital signs, and diagnostic findings to arrive at a diagnosis or management decision. Even for case-based datasets such as MedBullets, which already operate at relatively high reasoning levels, the Hard split further emphasizes Level 5 reasoning involving conflicting clinical evidence or subtle contraindications.
Inter-annotator agreement is substantial (overall Fleiss’
\kappa=0.62
, Supplemental Table S3), indicating that these distinctions in reasoning depth are clinically meaningful and reproducible rather than subjective.

Data contamination analysis

We assessed potential data contamination using the MELD (Memorization Effects Levenshtein Detector) framework
28
. MELD splits each question into two halves: the first half is provided as context, and the model is prompted to generate the second half. The Levenshtein distance ratio between the generated output and the original unseen portion quantifies memorization; higher similarity indicates that the model has likely encountered the question during training. This approach is particularly well-suited to multiple-choice benchmarks, where verbatim reproduction of question text, rather than answer choices, serves as the primary indicator of training exposure (Figure
3
). MELD has high precision but unknown recall: a detected match strongly indicates memorization, but absence of a match does not guarantee clean data.

The analysis reveals a clear divide between commercial and open-source models. OpenAI models show consistently low similarity scores (median 20–25%), suggesting minimal verbatim memorization. In contrast, several open-source models (DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-32B, Llama-3.3-70B) exhibit substantially higher scores, with some reproducing question texts at over 95% character-level accuracy on MMLU and MMLU-Pro (60–90% similarity range). Importantly, our Hard subset demonstrates significantly lower MELD scores across all models than the corresponding Full sets, confirming its utility for more reliable cross-model comparison. A manual review of high-similarity cases in MedMCQA and MedExQA attributed some elevated scores to shared standard medical terminology rather than direct data leakage. Given these contamination concerns, our primary method comparisons (Table
4
) focus on OpenAI models (GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, o1-mini, o3-mini), which exhibit the lowest contamination risk and therefore provide the most reliable basis for comparing reasoning strategies. Search-based optimization methods (SPO and AFlow) were evaluated only for GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4o.

Cost-performance analysis

Our evaluation examines both model architecture efficiency and reasoning method effectiveness through a cost-performance lens. We analyze these dimensions separately using two complementary visualizations.

Base model cost-efficiency

As shown in Figure
4
, the ten base models occupy markedly different positions in accuracy–cost space across the eight datasets. The marker encoding and Pareto frontier are described in the figure caption; here, we focus on the substantive pattern. In each panel, the red dashed frontier identifies non-dominated models that achieve the best accuracy attainable at a given inference cost, whereas points below the frontier are strictly inefficient relative to another model on the same dataset. DeepSeek-R1 and o3-mini recur on or near this frontier across many datasets, indicating that they frequently define the strongest empirical trade-offs among the base models.

The frontier nevertheless varies by task. MedQA and MedBullets show steep gains as inference cost increases, whereas PubMedQA exhibits a flatter profile with more modest returns to expensive models. Across several of the more challenging datasets, internalized reasoning models occupy stronger positions than non-reasoning models at comparable cost levels, suggesting that explicit training for multi-step reasoning can improve deployment-time efficiency as well as accuracy.
Open-source models demonstrate competitive performance. On the Full test set, DeepSeek-R1 achieves the highest accuracy among all models on several benchmarks, including MedMCQA (82.2%) and MMLU-Pro (79.7%), rivaling or surpassing closed-source alternatives. However, its extended reasoning chains result in higher per-sample inference costs than compact closed-source models such as o3-mini, illustrating a trade-off between reasoning depth and cost efficiency.

Reasoning method cost-efficiency

Figure
5
extends our analysis to the full space of model–method combinations. The Pareto frontier, together with the annotated transitions in the figure, reveals two distinct mechanisms for improving medical reasoning, with opposite cost signatures.

Externalizing reasoning trades cost for performance.
Adding agent scaffolding to a fixed base model consistently improves accuracy but increases inference cost. For instance, moving from bare GPT-4o-mini (11.0%) to GPT-4o-mini + SPO (20.1%) nearly doubles performance at a modest cost increase. However, not all externalization is equally efficient: GPT-4o-mini + MedAgents (18.3%) costs far more than SPO yet achieves lower accuracy, illustrating that heavier agent scaffolding can yield diminishing or even negative returns on weak base models. The same pattern holds at the top of the frontier: o3-mini zero-shot (27.8%) improves to o3-mini + CoT (32.7%) and then to o3-mini + MDAgents (35.1%), each step adding both accuracy and cost.

Internalizing reasoning improves performance while reducing inference cost.
Switching from an agent-augmented base model to a bare reasoning model can simultaneously increase accuracy and decrease per-sample inference cost. The most striking example is the transition from the cluster of GPT-4o + agent configurations (25–30% accuracy at 10–100
\times
higher inference cost) to bare o3-mini (27.8%): the reasoning model achieves comparable or higher accuracy at an order of magnitude lower inference cost. This reflects the efficiency of internalized reasoning at deployment time, where multi-step inference is compiled into the model’s parameters rather than orchestrated through repeated API calls. We note, however, that this analysis considers only inference cost; the training cost required to develop reasoning capabilities (e.g., reinforcement learning, chain-of-thought distillation) is not publicly disclosed by model providers and may be substantial.

These two mechanisms are complementary and together define the Pareto frontier. The optimal strategy traces a path that first externalizes reasoning on cheap models (GPT-4o-mini + SPO), then internalizes by upgrading to a reasoning model (o3-mini), and finally externalizes again by layering agents on the reasoning model (o3-mini + MDAgents). Agent frameworks applied to non-reasoning models (e.g., GPT-4o + MedAgents) fall well below this frontier, as their high inference cost is not justified by commensurate accuracy gains. Practitioners should therefore view internalized reasoning as the primary investment for improving accuracy per dollar, with externalized agent scaffolding as an additive enhancement best applied on top of already-strong reasoning models.

DISCUSSION

The central finding of this study is that internalized reasoning and externalized agent scaffolding are not substitutes but complements. Agents lift base-model accuracy (GPT-4o to GPT-4o + MedAgents), internalized reasoning models do the same at lower inference cost (GPT-4o + agents to o3-mini), and agents on top of internalized reasoning models push accuracy further still (o3-mini to o3-mini + MDAgents, 35.1%). Neither approach subsumes the other. Internalized reasoning models internalize multi-step inference through training-time optimization, while agent frameworks introduce diverse viewpoints and iterative error correction at inference time. The two mechanisms operate on different failure modes, which explains why their gains compound rather than overlap.

The Pareto frontier analysis further clarifies when each approach is most valuable. At the budget-conscious end, lightweight workflow optimization methods such as SPO applied to inexpensive base models (e.g., GPT-4o-mini + SPO) provide the most cost-efficient entry point. At the high-performance end, internalized reasoning models combined with agent methods (o3-mini + CoT, o3-mini + MDAgents) dominate the frontier. Discussion-based frameworks (MedAgents, MDAgents) show their greatest value when paired with internalized reasoning models, where the additional inference cost is justified by meaningful accuracy gains. Our error analysis (Supplemental Note S1) confirms this mechanistically: multi-agent aggregation amplifies shared biases when agents lack diverse reasoning priors, whereas structured self-critique and role-specialized disagreement can recover from localized errors.

For deployment, these results suggest a simple decision rule. If the budget allows an internalized reasoning model, use one; agents on top will further improve accuracy. If not, lightweight workflow optimization (e.g., SPO on GPT-4o-mini) offers the best accuracy per dollar. In either regime, the choice is not “agents or internalized reasoning models” but “how much of each.” This trade-off is especially salient in healthcare, where privacy requirements and local infrastructure constraints may preclude sending sensitive clinical data to frontier proprietary systems
42
,
33
. In practice, the key question is which combination of internalized reasoning models and externalized workflows best satisfies privacy, compute, latency, and accuracy requirements.

More broadly, MedicalAgentsBench demonstrates the value of decision-oriented benchmarking that jointly evaluates accuracy, cost, and reasoning quality. By filtering for genuinely difficult questions and screening for data contamination, our benchmark exposes meaningful performance differences that standard medical QA datasets obscure through ceiling effects.

Limitations of the study

Our benchmark draws exclusively from multiple-choice QA datasets derived from educational resources, which do not capture the full complexity of clinical practice (e.g., longitudinal patient management, ambiguous presentations, multi-modal evidence integration). Validating these findings on real-world clinical decision-making tasks remains an important next step.

The 50% accuracy threshold used for difficulty filtering introduces a potential circularity: the “hard” subset is defined relative to the specific models evaluated. We mitigate this through architectural diversity in the model pool and independent human validation of reasoning depth, but future work should explore task-intrinsic difficulty measures that do not depend on model performance.

Our cost comparisons focus on inference-time expense. For proprietary internalized reasoning models such as o3-mini, the training cost required to produce these reasoning capabilities is not publicly disclosed, making it difficult to compare the full end-to-end economic trade-off between internalizing reasoning during training and externalizing it through agent scaffolding at inference time.

Finally, our evaluation covers a fixed set of nine agentic methods. More sophisticated strategies, such as step-wise verification, heterogeneous model ensembles, and dynamic agent collaboration, remain unexplored and could alter the comparative landscape between internalized and externalized reasoning.

METHODS

Evaluation tasks and datasets

MedicalAgentsBench is a carefully curated benchmark designed to evaluate complex medical reasoning tasks. We draw from eight established medical datasets, described below.

MedQA

MedQA
18
is a large-scale open-domain medical question answering dataset collected from professional board exams such as the USMLE. In our benchmark, we use 1,273 English multiple-choice questions that test a broad range of clinical and foundational medical knowledge.

PubMedQA

PubMedQA
19
is a biomedical question answering dataset constructed from PubMed abstracts to evaluate reasoning over primary research literature. We use 500 expert-annotated yes/no/maybe questions, where each item pairs a research question with its corresponding abstract.

MedMCQA

MedMCQA
31
is a large-scale multiple-choice dataset built from Indian medical entrance exams (AIIMS and NEET PG), covering many subjects and topics. Our benchmark includes 2,816 questions sampled from MedMCQA, focusing on diverse real-world exam-style problems.

MedBullets

MedBullets
5
comprises USMLE Step 2/3-style clinical questions sourced from the MedBullets online learning platform. We use 308 challenging case-based questions, each designed to simulate realistic clinical decision-making scenarios with expert-authored explanations.

MedExQA

MedExQA
23
is a benchmark designed to evaluate medical question answering with a focus on explanation quality across underrepresented specialties. We extract 935 questions spanning Audiology, Nutrition, Occupational Therapy, Physical Therapy, and Speech-Language Pathology, each paired with multiple expert explanations.

MedXpertQA

MedXpertQA
54
is a recent benchmark targeting expert-level medical knowledge and advanced clinical reasoning across multiple specialties and body systems. We include 2,450 text-based questions from MedXpertQA, covering both understanding-oriented and reasoning-intensive scenarios.

MMLU

The Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark
14
includes several medically relevant subjects. Following the Med-PaLM setting, we retain only the medical fields clinical_knowledge, professional_medicine, college_medicine, medical_genetics, anatomy, and college_biology, yielding 1,089 questions.

MMLU-Pro

MMLU-Pro
45
is an enhanced, more challenging extension of MMLU that emphasizes reasoning and harder distractors. We include only subjects whose source (src) belongs to the health category, specifically clinical_knowledge, professional_medicine, college_medicine, medical_genetics, nutrition, human_aging, anatomy, and virology, resulting in 818 questions.

These source datasets vary significantly in size (from 308 to 2,816 questions), average token length (18.7 to 316.1), and number of options (3 to 10), providing diverse evaluation contexts (Table
2
).

Benchmark construction

As illustrated in Figure
6
, we construct MedicalAgentsBench by aggregating questions from multiple medical QA datasets and applying a multi-stage filtering and verification process to ensure difficulty, reasoning depth, and clinical validity. Our hard-set selection process is based on three key criteria:

Model performance distribution

As visualized in Figure
7
, we analyze the proportion of models that correctly answer each question (
k/N
ratio). Questions where less than 50% of models provide correct answers (left of the dashed line in Figure
7
), are categorized as hard candidates. This ensures our benchmark focuses on truly challenging questions that current models struggle with.

Dataset sampling strategy

We carefully balanced our benchmark to ensure comprehensive coverage across various source datasets. Our Hard subset includes precisely 100 questions each from MedQA, PubMedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU-Pro, and MedExQA. From MedXpertQA, we incorporated both its Reasoning (MedXpertQA-R) and Understanding (MedXpertQA-U) subsets (100 questions each), which were annotated in the original paper to distinguish between questions requiring complex clinical reasoning versus those primarily testing medical knowledge. Additionally, we included 89 questions from MedBullets and 73 questions from MMLU. This sampling distribution maintains proportional representation across the source datasets while ensuring sufficient sample size to evaluate model performance across different medical question types and their associated reasoning requirements.

Reasoning depth

We prioritize questions that require multi-step reasoning processes, where solving the problem involves a sequence of logical deductions or inferences rather than simple fact retrieval. This type of reasoning is typically demonstrated in long chain-of-thought approaches, where models must work through several intermediate steps to reach a conclusion. To systematically assess reasoning depth, we developed a five-level classification rubric and engaged medical professionals to annotate each question (see the human validation protocol below for the complete rubric and validation details). The need for such complex reasoning is evidenced by the significant performance gap between base models and agent-based approaches. As shown in Table
3
, while models achieve high accuracy on the Full set (e.g., GPT-4o: 88.2% on MedQA), their performance drops significantly on our Hard subset (e.g., GPT-4o: 32.2% on MedQA-Hard), confirming that these selected questions demand deeper analytical processing and medical reasoning capabilities.

In summary, we construct MedicalAgentsBench through a multi-stage pipeline designed to ensure question difficulty, diversity, and evaluation validity. Specifically, we first evaluate each candidate question across multiple model architectures (as shown in Table
3
) to ensure architecture-independent difficulty. Second, we apply a difficulty-aware dataset sampling strategy to balance coverage across source benchmarks. Third, we incorporate human annotations to verify that selected questions require multi-step medical reasoning rather than surface-level recall. Finally, we conduct a data contamination analysis using MELD (Memorization Effects Levenshtein Detector)
28
to identify questions that may have been memorized during model training. This analysis involves splitting each test item (including the question text but excluding answer options) into two halves, providing models with only the first half, and measuring the similarity between their generated continuations and the original second half. By filtering out potentially memorized items, this step safeguards the fairness and validity of the benchmark for evaluating genuine medical reasoning capabilities.
As shown in Figure
3
, the Hard subset selected for our MedicalAgentsBench benchmark consistently demonstrates lower similarity scores (20-40%) compared to the original datasets, suggesting our filtering process effectively selects questions that test genuine reasoning rather than memorization.
Complete details of our MELD methodology and its limitations are discussed in the data contamination analysis under Results. Finally, four medical professionals (M.D. students) review the final question set to verify clinical relevance and reasoning complexity through the validation protocol described below. The resulting MedicalAgentsBench benchmark contains 862 questions with an average token length of 147.4.

Human validation protocol

To ensure the quality and clinical validity of benchmark questions, we conducted a human validation study using four medical students in their clinical years with formal clinical training as annotators. The validation process served two primary objectives: assessing the level of reasoning depth required to answer each question and verifying the correctness and clinical relevance of the questions. Each annotator received standardized training materials and participated in a calibration session, during which they jointly reviewed and discussed 10 representative sample questions to ensure consistent interpretation and application of the evaluation criteria. Following calibration, annotators independently reviewed assigned question subsets, with overlapping samples included to enable estimation of inter-annotator agreement.

Reasoning depth assessment

For reasoning depth assessment, we developed a systematic rubric that defines reasoning depth as the minimum number of distinct cognitive inference steps required to arrive at the correct answer. Each step corresponds to a discrete inference, calculation, or clinical judgment that builds upon previously derived information. Supplemental Table S1 summarizes the five-level reasoning depth classification system and provides representative clinical examples for each level. Annotators independently reviewed each question, documented the required reasoning steps, and assigned depth scores according to this rubric. To enable reliable estimation of inter-annotator agreement, we sampled 20 questions from each dataset (10 from the original dataset and 10 from the curated hard subset) for comparative annotation by multiple reviewers.

Inter-annotator reliability was quantified using Fleiss’
\kappa
, which measures agreement beyond chance among multiple raters. Specifically, for each item, we compute the proportion of rater pairs that assign the same category and average this quantity across items to obtain the observed agreement
\bar{P}
. The expected agreement
\bar{P}_{e}
is computed from the squared category frequencies averaged across all ratings. Fleiss’
\kappa
is then defined as

\kappa=\frac{\bar{P}-\bar{P}_{e}}{1-\bar{P}_{e}}.

Across all datasets, the resulting inter-annotator agreement is
\kappa=0.62
(Supplemental Table S3), which is commonly considered substantial agreement for complex cognitive annotation tasks.

Question validity assessment

Concurrent with reasoning depth assessment, annotators evaluated question validity using strict exclusion criteria.
Questions were flagged for removal if they contained wrong answers contradicting current medical guidelines, lacked sufficient clinical context for definitive answers, tested outdated or clinically irrelevant knowledge that could be reasonably interpreted multiple ways.
Supplemental Table S2 summarizes our question exclusion criteria and the review process.

Our quality control protocol implemented several measures to ensure annotation consistency.
Following the initial calibration phase, any question flagged by at least one annotator underwent group review.
Questions were removed from the final dataset if two or more annotators agreed on the validity of the concern, with all exclusions documented with a specific rationale.
Of the initial candidate questions, 21 questions were removed due to validity concerns: 5 for wrong answers, 5 for poor clinical context, and 11 for clinical irrelevance.

), we used deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3, deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1, meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo, and Qwen/QwQ-32B-Preview. Note that Together AI has since updated DeepSeek-V3 to V3.1 and retired QwQ-32B-Preview.

Reasoning methods

Additionally, we evaluate 9 distinct agentic reasoning approaches spanning three categories. The first category includes baseline prompting methods such as Chain-of-Thought and Self-Consistency. The second encompasses advanced prompting techniques: Multi-Persona, Self-Refine, and MedPrompt. The third category covers agent-based frameworks, including medical-specific collaboration frameworks (MedAgents and MDAgents, which we reimplemented to ensure accurate parsing of multiple-choice responses) and search-based agent methods (SPO and AFlow) with search strategies consistent with the original setting. Because agentic pipelines require extensive multi-step reasoning, evaluating them on all existing medical benchmarks would be computationally prohibitive. Therefore, Table
1
reports agentic results solely on MedicalAgentsBench, while Table
2
provides cross-benchmark comparisons for single-call model evaluations. Our implementations of these baseline methods and evaluation framework are publicly available at
https://github.com/gersteinlab/MedicalAgentsBench/
.

Our data contamination analysis (Figure
3
) revealed that OpenAI models demonstrate consistently lower memorization metrics across all datasets compared to other model families. This finding guided our decision to use GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and o3-mini as primary models for method comparison to minimize performance advantages stemming from potential training data contamination.

Standardized evaluation

All experiments utilize identical prompt templates and evaluation protocols to ensure a fair comparison. We implement a standardized two-round inference protocol per query for agent-based methods requiring multiple inference rounds (e.g., MedAgents). Multi-agent approaches (e.g., MDAgents) consistently employ three distinct agent roles for all evaluations.
This standardization mitigates confounding factors arising from implementation variations, allowing us to more accurately attribute performance differences to the methods themselves rather than to differences in their implementations.

Cost analysis

To analyze cost-performance trade-offs, we followed a standardized evaluation protocol.
Understanding these trade-offs is particularly important given the significant computational resources required by advanced reasoning models, which often come with substantially higher inference costs and longer processing times than traditional models. Similarly, multi-agent frameworks require multiple rounds of API calls for agent interactions, further increasing computational costs and inference time.
In this analysis, we focus exclusively on inference-time cost and latency, as training costs are highly model-dependent, often proprietary, and difficult to attribute fairly on a per-task or per-sample basis.

For API-based commercial models (OpenAI and Claude), we calculated costs using their published pricing rates based on total token usage (input + output). For open-source models (such as DeepSeek, Llama, and QwQ), we estimated costs based on Together AI’s hosting rates (
https://www.together.ai/
), a platform that provides API access to open-source models. This approach enables fair cost comparisons between proprietary and open-source alternatives across different deployment scenarios.
The total cost of experimentation was $226.17. We measured inference time as wall-clock time per sample, including prompt construction and model inference, across agent-based methods that included their complete interaction cycles.

Supplemental Note S1. Error Analysis

We analyze five representative clinical cases (Tables
S4
–
S8
) to characterize failure modes and success patterns across reasoning paradigms. Each table presents side-by-side reasoning traces from base models, reasoning-optimized models, and agent-based systems. Red-highlighted segments mark critical decision pivots where hypotheses are introduced, overturned, or revised.

Case 1 (Table
S4
): Convergent reasoning in IRIS management.

An HIV patient on ART develops worsening pulmonary symptoms despite rising CD4 counts, a classic presentation of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS). GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4o both mismanage this case: the former favors drug sensitivity testing, while the latter considers discontinuing therapy. DeepSeek-R1, by contrast, entertains alternative diagnoses (infection relapse, drug resistance) during its extended chain-of-thought but systematically eliminates each against the clinical timeline (symptom onset 4 weeks post-ART, improving immunologic markers). Each re-evaluation step narrows the differential rather than broadening it, and the model correctly selects continuation of current therapy (A). The key observation is that deliberation length per se does not determine accuracy; what matters is whether successive reasoning steps converge toward or diverge from the strongest hypothesis.

Case 2 (Table
S5
): Refinement versus consensus amplification in osteomyelitis.

A patient presents with osteomyelitis following a cat bite. The clinically relevant pathogen is
Pasteurella multocida
, which requires ampicillin–sulbactam (B). The initial chain-of-thought selects vancomycin (E), overweighting generic severity cues (osteomyelitis suggesting MRSA) while neglecting the pathogen-specific exposure history (cat bite indicating
Pasteurella
).

Under Self-Refine, the model re-examines pathogen likelihood across refinement rounds and shifts from broad MRSA coverage to pathogen-directed therapy, ultimately converging on the correct answer. Under MultiPersona, however, three independent agents generate similar MRSA-focused rationales. During aggregation, inter-agent agreement reinforces the shared error rather than correcting it. This distinction is clinically consequential: iterative self-critique recovers from anchoring bias by forcing re-examination of premises, whereas naive aggregation amplifies correlated mistakes when agents share the same reasoning blind spot.

Case 3 (Table
S6
): Hypothesis reversal in small bowel obstruction.

A patient with prior abdominal surgery and diabetes presents with symptoms consistent with adhesive small bowel obstruction. GPT-4o and o3-mini correctly identify adhesions (A) through straightforward pattern matching (surgical history + obstructive symptoms). DeepSeek-R1 initially reaches the same conclusion but then revisits the differential: the model notes the patient’s diabetes and explores autonomic neuropathy (B) as an alternative etiology for dysmotility. Without an explicit stopping criterion or confidence threshold, this secondary hypothesis displaces the original, despite weaker supporting evidence.

The failure mechanism is distinct from knowledge gaps or reasoning errors in the traditional sense. The model possesses sufficient clinical knowledge to reach the correct answer and does so early in its reasoning chain. The error arises from unconstrained hypothesis exploration: additional deliberation destabilizes an already-correct conclusion. Compared with Case 1, where re-evaluation converges, this case shows that the same extended reasoning process can diverge when intermediate steps introduce plausible but weaker alternatives without calibrating their relative likelihood.

Case 4 (Table
S7
): Disagreement-driven correction in septic shock.

A patient in refractory septic shock requires vasopressor escalation. The single-model chain-of-thought selects hydrocortisone (C), reasoning from persistent hypotension to adrenal insufficiency. This represents a plausible but premature inference that bypasses the standard vasopressor escalation protocol.

Within MultiPersona, agents initially disagree: some advocate corticosteroids, others argue for epinephrine as the guideline-recommended second-line vasopressor. Critically, this disagreement triggers explicit comparison of competing clinical rationales rather than simple majority voting. The synthesized response identifies that vasopressor escalation should precede steroid therapy per Surviving Sepsis guidelines, converging on epinephrine (B). The correction depends on
structured
disagreement: agents with different initial hypotheses are forced to reconcile their reasoning against shared clinical criteria, exposing the premature inferential leap in the corticosteroid rationale.

Case 5 (Table
S8
): Role-specialized revision in neonatal galactosemia.

A neonate presents with findings consistent with classic galactosemia. Within MedAgents, the initial synthesized report (v0) correctly identifies the diagnosis but introduces a spurious dietary recommendation (avoiding fruit juice). This is mechanistically incoherent, since galactosemia involves defective galactose metabolism, not fructose intolerance. Domain-specialized agents (ophthalmology, hepatology, clinical nutrition) flag this inconsistency: cataracts and hepatotoxicity in galactosemia are driven by galactitol and galactose-1-phosphate accumulation, not by generic sugar exposure. The revised synthesis (v1) narrows the intervention to eliminating lactose-containing feeds and switching to soy-based formula (B), correctly targeting the underlying metabolic defect. The correction arises not from agent diversity alone, but from the combination of domain-specific knowledge with an explicit revision mechanism that forces the synthesis to address identified inconsistencies.

Summary.

Three recurring failure and recovery mechanisms emerge across these cases:

1.

Convergence versus divergence in extended reasoning.
Longer chain-of-thought improves accuracy when successive steps narrow the differential (Case 1), but degrades accuracy when unconstrained exploration displaces well-supported hypotheses (Case 3). The outcome depends on whether intermediate re-evaluation is convergent or diffusive.

2.

Consensus amplification.
Multi-agent aggregation without structured disagreement handling reinforces shared reasoning errors (Case 2, MultiPersona). When agents share the same anchoring bias, agreement increases confidence in the wrong answer.

3.

Structured correction.
Both iterative self-critique (Case 2, Self-Refine) and role-specialized disagreement resolution (Cases 4–5) can recover from reasoning errors, but only when the correction mechanism forces re-examination of specific premises rather than generating independent rationales from the same prior assumptions.

These patterns are consistent with our quantitative findings: stronger base reasoning reduces the marginal value of agentic scaffolding, but targeted correction mechanisms (disagreement detection, premise re-examination, domain-specialized review) remain beneficial when they address specific failure modes rather than adding undifferentiated deliberation.

Clinical Explanation:

This patient who presents with failure to thrive, hepatosplenomegaly, and bilateral cataracts most likely has classic galactosemia. Patients with this disorder should avoid lactose-containing products by changing to a soy-based formula. Classic galactosemia is an autosomal recessive defect in galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase. This enzyme is involved in the conversion of galactose to glucose, and a deficiency of this enzyme results in the accumulation of galactose 1-phosphate in the liver, kidney, and brain. This metabolite acts as a phosphate sink, meaning that it traps all free phosphate in the cytosol and inhibits the formation of other phosphate-dependent metabolites such as adenosine triphosphate. This results in failure to thrive, vomiting, lethargy, and hepatosplenomegaly after ingestion of galactose-containing products such as milk. Patients will also develop bilateral cataracts if the deficit is not detected in the first few weeks after birth. Avoiding galactose by changing to a soy-based formula will avoid the complications of this disorder. Turck discusses using soy protein for infant feeding. The author notes that there are safety concerns with regards to the high phyto-estrogenic isoflavone content of these formulas. The author recommends that despite these safety concerns, these formulas should still be used in patients with classic galactosemia.
