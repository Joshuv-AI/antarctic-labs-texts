Direct evidence for the absence of coupling between shear strain and superconductivity in Sr 2 ​ RuO 4 \text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
Giordano Mattoni, Thomas Johnson, Atsutoshi Ikeda, Shubhankar Paul, Jake Bobowski, Manfred Sigrist, Yoshiteru Maeno

Abstract
The superconducting symmetry of Sr 2 ​ RuO 4 \text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} has been intensely debated for many years. A crucial controversy recently emerged between shear-mode ultrasound experiments, which suggest a two-component order parameter, and some uniaxial pressure experiments that suggest a one-component order parameter. To resolve this controversy, we use a new approach to directly apply three different kinds of shear strain to single crystals of Sr 2 ​ RuO 4 \text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} and investigate the coupling to superconductivity. After characterising the strain by optical imaging, we observe variations of the transition temperature T c T_{\mathrm{c}} smaller than 10 mK % − 1 10\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}\text{\,}{\mathrm{\%}}^{-1} as measured by low-frequency magnetic susceptibility, indicating that shear strain has little to no coupling to superconductivity. Our results are consistent with a one-component order parameter model, but such a model cannot consistently explain other experimental evidence such as time-reversal symmetry breaking, superconducting domains, and horizontal line nodes, thus calling for alternative interpretations.



I
Introduction

Strains in a crystal have well-defined irreducible representations (irreps) under the symmetry of a lattice.
Strains are precisely coupled to the superconducting (SC) order parameter (OP) according to their symmetries, and induce changes in
T_{\mathrm{c}}
.
Thus, the application of strains to a superconductor and the observation of the changes in
T_{\mathrm{c}}
is a precise method of gaining information on the SC OP.
In addition to isotropic hydrostatic pressure, it has recently become clear that uniaxial strains are a powerful tool to investigate superconducting symmetry, since they can profoundly alter crystal symmetry
hicks2014strong
;
steppke2017strong
;
hicks2025probing
.

The OP of unconventional superconductivity in
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

maeno1994superconductivity
is still unresolved despite 30 years of extensive research efforts
maeno2024still
;
maeno2024thirty
.
Spin-singlet-like behaviour was newly established from spin susceptibility measured by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
pustogow2019constraints
;
ishida2020reduction
;
chronister2021evidence
and polarised neutrons
petsch2020reduction
.
Time-reversal symmetry breaking (TRSB), evidenced by an emerging internal magnetic field below
T_{\mathrm{c}}

luke1998time
, has been reported by several groups
shiroka2012mu
;
higemoto2014investigation
;
grinenko2021split
.
Magneto-optic Kerr effect (MOKE) also supports the presence of TRSB in the superconducting state
xia2006high
.
Anomalous switching behaviour in superconducting junctions
anwar2021review
point to the presence of SC domains that arise with multi-component OP with one-dimensional (1D) or 2D irreducible representations (irreps).

Uniaxial compression in
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
enhances
T_{\mathrm{c}}
from 1.5 to
3.5\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, and it is associated with the changes in the Fermi surface topology (Lifshitz transition)
steppke2017strong
;
sunko2019direct
.
A splitting between
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and appearance of the TRSB phase was observed by muon spin relaxation (
\mu
SR) under uniaxial strain
grinenko2021split
, pointing to a chiral
superconducting OP.
However, the splitting is not observed in specific heat and elastocaloric-effect measurements under uniaxial strain
li2021high
;
li2022elastocaloric
, leaving the issue of TRSB unsettled at present.

Another peculiar behaviour of
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
is the jump at
T_{\mathrm{c}}
of the shear-mode ultrasound velocity related to the elastic modulus
c_{66}

benhabib2021ultrasound
;
ghosh2021thermodynamic
.
This unusual coupling between SC OP and shear strain
implies that the OP has two components:
either
chiral with 2D irreps (e.g.,
d_{yz}\pm id_{zx}
),
nematic with 2D irreps (e.g.,
d_{yz}
only,
d_{zx}
only, or
d_{yz}\pm d_{zx}
),
or a combination of 1D irreps (e.g.,
d+ig
,
s+id
).
Results of uniaxial stress and hydrostatic pressure experiments can be combined to compare with ultrasound experiments
jerzembeck2024tc
, but such comparison leads to both qualitative and quantitative discrepancies
maeno2024thirty
.
Specifically, a V-shaped kink in
T_{\mathrm{c}}
as a function of uniaxial strain along the [110] direction was not observed, in contrast with the expectations from the observed jump in the ultrasound
c_{66}
.
It is crucial to resolve this discrepancy in order to reach a conclusion about the superconducting state of
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
.

In this study, we investigate the direct effect of shear strain on the
T_{\mathrm{c}}
of
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
.
The magnitudes of sample strains at low temperatures are measured with optical imaging down to
30\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
.
Kinks, linear slopes, and quadratic changes in
T_{\mathrm{c}}
are evaluated by applying three different kinds of shear strain and comparing the results with the estimates of the Ehrenfest relations from the jumps and slope changes of the elastic-moduli at
T_{\mathrm{c}}
.
We find that
T_{\mathrm{c}}
does not change within the experimental precision of
\partial{T_{\mathrm{c}}}/\partial{\varepsilon_{xy}}=$\pm 6\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}\text{\,}{\mathrm{\%}}^{-1}$
.
This result seriously challenges the interpretation of SC in
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
in terms of a two-component OP.

II
Results

II.1
Direct shear-strain quantification at low temperature

To apply shear strain, we construct piezo-sample assemblies by rigidly gluing thinly polished
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
crystals directly onto the surface of piezoelectric devices, as shown in
Fig.

(see Methods for construction details).
Deformation of a piezo-sample assembly is monitored by an optical microscope which allows us to calculate the point-by-point in-plane displacements (
u_{x}
,
u_{y}
) and the in-plane shear strain

\varepsilon_{xy}=(\partial u_{x}/\partial y+\partial u_{y}/\partial x)/2
.
We note that this definition is symmetric (
\varepsilon_{xy}=\varepsilon_{yx}
) and that
\varepsilon_{xy}=\gamma_{xy}/2=\varepsilon_{6}/2

\gamma_{xy}
is the engineering strain and
\varepsilon_{6}
is the

We show in

.
By means of digital image correlation

;
mccormick2010digital

As indicated by the arrows, the bottom of the piezo-sample assembly is moving to the right (red) while the top is moving to the left (blue) due to the applied shear strain.
We note that the piezoelectric device we use predominantly generates a shear displacement component
\partial u_{x}/\partial y
, while the complementary component
\partial u_{y}/\partial x
is small due to the device’s geometry and boundary constraints (
Fig.

S1
in the Supplementary Information).
We comment on the importance of measuring both components because relaxation of shear strain could lead to insurgence of a negative
\partial u_{y}/\partial x
at the sample surface, which corresponds to an overall sample rotation (
Fig.

S2
).

We calculate the average shear strain over the surface of the piezo device and the

At room temperature, the piezo device can apply a maximum of about
\varepsilon_{xy}=$\pm 0.05\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$

\varepsilon_{xy}=$\pm 0.04\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$

We emphasize the advantage of this optical technique, which enables non-contact measurement of the effective shear strain directly on the sample surface, allowing us to estimate the shear strain transferred to the bulk of the sample and to neglect the uncertainty due to glue-mediated strain transfer from the piezoelectric device.
The shear–voltage characteristics in
Fig.

1c

While this hysteresis is generally negligible for the purposes of this study, it introduces an uncertainty in the shear strain that is most significant around zero applied voltage, where the upper bound estimated from the vertical width of the hysteresis is
\delta\varepsilon_{xy}\approx$0.02\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
.
Importantly, this uncertainty is minimal at the largest applied voltages, where the strain response is well defined and reproducible.
As shown in
Fig.

1d
, the shear applied at a certain voltage becomes gradually smaller at lower temperature due to the decreasing capacitance of the piezoelectric device (
Fig.

S3
).
We compensate for this effect by applying a larger voltage to the piezo device at low temperature, thus achieving similar values of maximum shear strain at all temperatures.

We show in
Fig.

1e
that the strain transferred from the piezoelectric device to the
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
sample is as large as
75\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
at lower temperature, where a weak enhancement is possibly caused by a stiffening of the glue used to attach the sample to the piezo surface.
This strain transfer is consistent with the value predicted by our finite element simulations (
Fig.

S2
) for samples of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
thickness.
A direct correlation between the travel distance of the piezoelectric device and its capacitance (
Fig.

S3
) allows us to calculate a conversion factor
\varepsilon_{xy}/V_{\mathrm{piezo}}=$0.007\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1}$
at
2\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
.
Because the samples investigated in this study had similar thicknesses, we found this conversion factor to be approximately constant.

II.2
Response of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
to shear strain

We now consider changes in
T_{\mathrm{c}}
by shear strain.
To detect the superconducting transition, we insert the piezo-sample assembly of
Fig.

1a
into the mutual-inductance coil set shown in
Fig.

2a
.
The mutual-inductance technique allows us to measure the imaginary
\chi^{\prime\prime}
and real
\chi^{\prime}
parts of the alternating current (AC) susceptibility extracted from the in-phase and out-of-phase lock-in voltages
V_{x}
,
V_{y}
, respectively.
These are measured as a function of temperature for different applied voltages
V_{\mathrm{piezo}}
in
Figs.

2c
and
2b
.
We use
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
samples with a sharp superconducting transition that appears as a dissipation peak in
\chi^{\prime\prime}
, with full width at half maximum of about
20\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
, and as a drop in
\chi^{\prime}
that indicates the onset of diamagnetic susceptibility, with a transition width smaller than
40\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
.
We compare in
Fig.

2d

: from the position of the peak in
\chi^{\prime\prime}
(squares), and from the mid-point of the drop in
\chi^{\prime}

The values of

Since the peak in
\chi^{\prime\prime}
is about an order of magnitude smaller than the drop in

We use several samples from at least two different batches of crystals for the measurements.

The data in

III
Discussion

Our results show that the
T_{\mathrm{c}}
of
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
does not change under the direct application of shear strains
\varepsilon_{xy}
,
\varepsilon_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}^{110}
, and
\varepsilon_{zx}
.
This is different from the
T_{\mathrm{c}}
variations that occur under compressive strains.
In the following, we compare our results with the prediction of shear-strain response estimated by combining results of uniaxial compressions and hydrostatic pressure.
We also examine the limit of compatibility with the jumps in ultrasound velocities at
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and with the change in their slope.
Finally, we compare with the jumps in thermal expansion at
T_{\mathrm{c}}
.

The couplings between different strains and superconductivity are dictated by symmetry.
Due to the

For the two-component order parameter

\displaystyle f_{\eta\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=r_{1}\varepsilon_{A_{\mathrm{1g,1}}}|\bm{\eta}|^{2}+r_{2}\varepsilon_{A_{\mathrm{1g,2}}}|\bm{\eta}|^{2}

(1)

\displaystyle+r_{3}\varepsilon_{B_{\mathrm{2g}}}(\eta_{x}^{*}\eta_{y}+\eta_{x}\eta_{y}^{*})+r_{4}\varepsilon_{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}(|\eta_{x}|^{2}-|\eta_{y}|^{2}),

up to second order in
\eta_{x},\eta_{y}

\displaystyle=\pm\left|r_{3}\right|\>\>(\pm\ \mathrm{sign\ for}\ \varepsilon_{B_{\mathrm{2g}}}\gtrless 0),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\varepsilon_{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}}

III.2
Comparison with thermal expansion

Another thermodynamic comparison can be made with jumps in thermal expansion coefficient

using the different Ehrenfest relation

\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{ii}}=-T_{\mathrm{c}}\frac{\Delta\alpha_{i}}{\Delta C},

(5)

where

is the specific heat, and
\Delta

Note that, unlike

From the negative jumps

\Delta\alpha_{a}=$-0.42\text{\times}{10}^{-7}\text{\,}{\mathrm{K}}^{-1}$

\Delta\alpha_{c}=$-0.52\text{\times}{10}^{-7}\text{\,}{\mathrm{K}}^{-1}$

\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}/\partial p_{\mathrm{hydro}}=$-360\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}\text{\,}{\mathrm{GPa}}^{-1}$
and

\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}/\partial\sigma_{zz}=$+140\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}\text{\,}{\mathrm{GPa}}^{-1}$
.
These estimates are within a factor two in agreement with the results of
hydrostatic pressure
-210\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}\text{\,}{\mathrm{GPa}}^{-1}

forsythe2002evolution
and

c
-axis uniaxial-stress
+76\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}\text{\,}{\mathrm{GPa}}^{-1}

jerzembeck2022superconductivity

Thus, the strong disagreements of ultrasound jumps in both shear and compression modes suggest that the ultrasound jumps may contain additional contributions which are not properly included in the Ehrenfest relation of

IV
Conclusions

We investigated the coupling of
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
superconductivity to shear strain.
For this purpose, we developed a technique where thin sample crystals were glued on shear-strain piezoelectric devices that allowed us to directly apply static shear strains along three symmetry channels.
The magnitudes of strains were evaluated from optical imaging down to
30\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
.
We found no detectable change of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
, with a resolution limit smaller than
10\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}\text{\,}{\mathrm{\%}}^{-1}
.
This poses strict limits on the possible nature of the superconducting order parameter, pointing at ruling out a two-component order parameter.
We note, however, that there remains an unresolved discrepancy between the results of ultrasound and static deformation (stress/strain) experiments, which requires further investigation.
Our analysis indicates that
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
has a one-component OP, or it is a host of more exotic OPs that are required to fully explain its superconducting state.
The new method developed in this study can be readily applied to other superconductors for which a two-component OP is suggested, such as
\text{UPt}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, or to other materials showing different kinds of phase transitions where a two-component order parameter may emerge.

V
Methods

V.1
Piezo-sample assemblies and sample crystals

In order to apply various shear strains to
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
samples, we used a shear piezoelectric device (Thorlabs, PL5FB) consisting of a single piezoelectric ceramic layer of
\text{PbZr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{x\/}}}\text{Ti}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{1\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417ptx\/}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(PZT) with gold-plated electrodes on the top and bottom surfaces.
The device provides a free-stroke displacement of
1.3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
with the application of
\pm 200\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
at room temperature.
We cut the commercial
5\text{\times}5\text{\times}0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
shear devices into four pieces of about
2\text{\times}2\text{\times}0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
with a diamond-disc cutter, and polished the cut planes using a polishing machine (Musashino Denshi, MA-200) with a diamond slurry of
3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
.
We left a suitable surface roughness to secure strong adhesion to the sample crystal and to provide a clear optical contrast for measuring actual strain.
For the control experiment, we used a tensile strain device (Thorlabs, PA3CE, dimension
2\text{\times}2\text{\times}2\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
) consisting of piezoelectric layers stacked in series.
The device provides a free-stroke expansion displacement of
2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
with the application of
+100\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
at room temperature.

The
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
single crystals used in this study were grown by a floating-zone method using an image furnace (Canon Machinery, SC-E15HD)
bobowski2019improved
.
The cystal orientation was determined by X-ray Laue patterns (Rigaku, RASCO BL-II with a CCD camera).
We selected
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
crystals with a sharp superconducting transition at
T_{\mathrm{c}}=$1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
, ensuring the absence of metallic-
Ru
inclusions that would induce broad enhancement of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
up to
3.5\text{\,}\mathrm{K}

maeno1998enhancement
.
The
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
crystals were cut into a typical size of
0.4\text{\times}1\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
and polished down to a thickness of
30\pm\pm 5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
with a diamond slurry of
3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
.
Each thin crystalline sample was glued to the active side surface of a piezo device with epoxy (EPO-TEK, 353ND) cured at
110120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
for
20\text{\,}\mathrm{min}
.
The lap shear of EPO-TEK 353ND (
13.5\text{\,}\mathrm{MPa}
)
is much larger than the typical lap shear of other low-temperature epoxy such as Stycast 2850FT (
3\text{\,}\mathrm{MPa}
, with catalyst 24LV),
ensuring good adhesion between the
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and the piezo element.

High-voltage electrodes for the piezo elements were provided by copper wires (diameter
0.14\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
) attached using silver paste (EPO-TEK, H20E) cured at
120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
for
2\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
.
For SC measurements, each piezo-sample assembly was mounted on a bamboo holder on which ten insulated copper wires (diameter
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
) were used for thermal anchoring, glued with Varnish (GE, 8530) as shown in
Fig.

2a
.

V.2
Image correlation to optically measure the strains

We employed an optical-imaging technique to quantify the shear and tensile strain applied by the piezoelectric devices to
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
samples.
Digital photographs were taken with different
V_{\mathrm{piezo}}
using an optical microscope (MicroSupport, SS306) with
1040\text{\,}\times
zoom.
Digital image correlation (DIC) with DICe software was employed to compute in-plane displacements in the
x
and
y
directions
schreier2009image
;
mccormick2010digital
.
The average strain over the sample and piezo surfaces was calculated line-by-line averaging the displacement data, while the error was estimated from the standard deviation of the data.

V.3
Calibration of strain

The conversion factor from
V_{\mathrm{piezo}}
to shear
\varepsilon_{xy}
and uniaxial
\varepsilon_{xx}
was measured at different temperatures between 30 and
295\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
in an optical cryostat (Thermalblock, custom design).
Since the piezo displacement per unit volt depends on temperature, the capacitance of the piezo was used to bridge between the measurements at
30\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, lowest temperature accessible by our optical cryostat, and the measurements of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
which were performed at and below
2\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
.
The strain transfer ratio discussed in
Fig.

1e
was estimated on different samples and found to be rather constant among the several samples due to their comparable thicknesses.

V.4
Determination of
T_{\mathrm{c}}

Evaluation of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
with exceptionally high precision and accuracy was crucial in this study.
We adopted alternating-current (AC) susceptibility measurements using a mutual-inductance coil set
yonezawa2015compact
and a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research Systems, SR830) to probe both the imaginary
\chi^{\prime\prime}
and real
\chi^{\prime}
parts of the AC susceptibility defined as
\chi=\partial M/\partial H=-i\chi^{\prime\prime}+\chi^{\prime}
.
By driving an alternating current of the form

I_{\mathrm{AC}}(t)=I_{0}\cos(2\pi ft)

in the excitation coil, the resulting voltage in the pickup coil is

V_{\mathrm{AC}}(t)=(1/\alpha)V_{\mathrm{sample}}I_{0}f(-\chi^{\prime\prime}\cos(2\pi ft)+\chi^{\prime}\sin(2\pi ft))
,
with
\alpha
a constant related to the geometry of the coils,
V_{\mathrm{sample}}
the sample volume,
I_{0}
the amplitude of the excitation current, and
f
its frequency
nikolo1995superconductivity
.
The coil set consisted of a pair of in-series pick-up/reference coils
(400 turns each, internal diameter
5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
, length
5.5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
, copper wires diameter
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
)
wound in opposite directions,
and an excitation coil
(
2400\text{\,}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
, internal diameter
6.0\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
, length
36\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
, copper wires diameter
100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
),
producing a magnetic field of
84\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro T}\text{\,}{\mathrm{mA}}^{-1}
.
An alternating current (frequency
17.7\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
, root mean squared amplitude of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{mA}
) was sourced to the excitation coil using the voltage output of the lock-in amplifier and a series resistance of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{k\SIUnitSymbolOhm}
.
In-phase (
V_{x}
) and out-of-phase (
V_{y}
) lock-in components were calibrated at
2\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
by considering the phase delay of the circuit involving only the excitation coil.
Several values of current were tested to ensure that the effect of heating was below
1\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
near
T_{\mathrm{c}}
.
The sample temperature was measured with a thermometer (Lake Shore, Cernox CX-1030-SD-HT) mounted on the copper plate to which each piezo-sample assembly and mutual-inductance coil set were thermally anchored.

Appendix A
Ginzburg-Landau theory for shear strain

To understand the phase diagram of a two-component SC, we use a Ginzburg-Landau (GL) theory.
Landau theory provides a simple model based on symmetry that can qualitatively describe OP behaviour near a phase transition.
We use a GL free energy to derive the expected strain dependence of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and compare the results of different experiments.

The GL free energy is written as a power series expansion of an OP near a phase transition
maeno2024still
.
The free energy is an analytic function of the OP and it is invariant under the symmetries of the Hamiltonian.
Because the free energy is a scalar quantity, it belongs to the
{A_{\mathrm{1g}}}
irreducible representation (irrep).
As an example, we consider the two-component OP
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{x},\eta_{y})
which belongs to the 2D
{E_{\mathrm{g}}}
irrep of the
D_{\mathrm{4h}}
point group with components
\{d_{xz},d_{yz}\}
.
Because the free energy is a scalar quantity and an analytic function of the OPs, the lowest order terms are quadratic in the OPs.
The square of an
{E_{\mathrm{g}}}
order parameter has
{A_{\mathrm{1g}}}
,
{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}
, and
{B_{\mathrm{2g}}}
components; only combinations of the OP with
{A_{\mathrm{1g}}}
symmetry are permitted in the free energy.
Because we are interested in a minimal model that qualitatively describes the OP behaviour, we go only up to quartic order in the OP and consider the GL free energy density

\displaystyle f_{\eta}

\displaystyle=a_{0}(T-T_{\mathrm{c},0})|\bm{\eta}|^{2}+\beta_{1}|\bm{\eta}|^{4}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}(\eta_{x}^{2}\eta_{y}^{*2}+\eta_{x}^{*2}\eta_{y}^{2})+\beta_{3}|\eta_{x}|^{2}|\eta_{y}|^{2},

(S1)

where
|\bm{\eta}|^{2}=|\eta_{x}|^{2}+|\eta_{y}|^{2}
and
a_{0}
and
\beta_{1}
are positive constants
sigrist2002ehrenfest
.
The
\beta_{2}
term affects the relative phase between the SC order parameters, and thus governs whether the equilibrium state breaks time reversal symmetry.
For example, the chiral state
d_{xz}+id_{yz}
breaks time reversal symmetry, whereas the nematic state
d_{xz}+d_{yz}
does not.
Both the
\beta_{2}
and
\beta_{3}
terms affect whether these SC order parameters coexist or compete.
If the
\beta_{3}
term is positive and has a larger magnitude than
\beta_{2}
, then the free energy is minimized by having one component equal to zero.
For our analysis, we neglect spatial variation of the order parameter.

The GL free energy density that describes the coupling between a two-component SC OP and lattice strain is given by

\displaystyle f_{\eta\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=r_{1}\varepsilon_{A_{\mathrm{1g,1}}}|\bm{\eta}|^{2}+r_{2}\varepsilon_{A_{\mathrm{1g,2}}}|\bm{\eta}|^{2}+r_{3}\varepsilon_{B_{\mathrm{2g}}}(\eta_{x}^{*}\eta_{y}+\eta_{x}\eta_{y}^{*})+r_{4}\varepsilon_{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}(|\eta_{x}|^{2}-|\eta_{y}|^{2}),

(S2)

where we go only to second order in
\eta_{x}
and
\eta_{y}
and first order in strain
sigrist2002ehrenfest
. These strains, labelled in terms of their irreps, are given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A_{\mathrm{1g,1}}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{xx}+\varepsilon_{yy},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A_{\mathrm{1g,2}}}=\varepsilon_{zz},

(S3)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{B_{\mathrm{2g}}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{xy},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}=\varepsilon_{xx}-\varepsilon_{yy}.

Here the strains are components of the linear strain tensor
\varepsilon_{ij}=(\partial u_{i}/\partial x_{j}+\partial u_{j}/\partial x_{i})/2
where
\bm{u}
is a local displacement vector.
Note that each free energy term has
{A_{\mathrm{1g}}}
symmetry resulting from the combination of the strain and the OP-combination symmetries.
There are no symmetry-allowed linear couplings to
{E_{\mathrm{g}}}
strains
\varepsilon_{xz}
and
\varepsilon_{yz}
, so to lowest order these strains should not affect SC.
The
{A_{\mathrm{1g}}}
strains correspond to compressions or dilations of a sample that preserve the
C_{4z}
rotational symmetry of the lattice; the
{B_{\mathrm{2g}}}
and
{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}
strains correspond to volume-preserving shear distortions that break the
C_{4z}
rotational symmetry of the lattice.

We restrict our discussion here to the chiral
{E_{\mathrm{g}}}
SC phase to illustrate the coupling of a two-component OP to strain;
{E_{\mathrm{g}}}
is the only 2D even-parity irrep of the point group
D_{\mathrm{4h}}
. Other two-component OPs, discussed in literature, are composite of two different one-dimensional representations, such as the
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}+ig_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
state
kivelson2020proposal
;
ghosh2021thermodynamic
;
maeno2024thirty
, combining
{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}
for
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
and
{A_{\mathrm{2g}}}
for
d_{xy(x^{2}-y^{2})}
, assumed to have nearly the same transition temperature.
The advantage of considering the
{E_{\mathrm{g}}}
OP is that all examined strain channels can couple and allow us to derive the corresponding Ehrenfest relations as listed in Table 1 (up to some prefactors of order one). This is not the case for any of the composite two-component OPs. With this choice, we do not intend to promote any interpretation of the realized OP for this SC, as our experimental results are not supporting specifically a two-component order parameter.

In the following, we provide some basic definitions that are useful when dealing with elasticity problems.
The Voigt notation allows to transform the
3\times 3
stress and strain matrices (in three-dimensional space) of elements
\sigma_{ij}
,
\varepsilon_{ij}
into
6\times 1
vectors of elements
\sigma_{k}
,
\varepsilon_{k}
defined as

\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{1}\\
\sigma_{2}\\
\sigma_{3}\\
\sigma_{4}\\
\sigma_{5}\\
\sigma_{6}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{xx}\\
\sigma_{yy}\\
\sigma_{zz}\\
\sigma_{yz}\\
\sigma_{zx}\\
\sigma_{xy}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}\\
\varepsilon_{2}\\
\varepsilon_{3}\\
\varepsilon_{4}\\
\varepsilon_{5}\\
\varepsilon_{6}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_{xx}\\
\varepsilon_{yy}\\
\varepsilon_{zz}\\
\gamma_{yz}\\
\gamma_{zx}\\
\gamma_{xy}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_{xx}\\
\varepsilon_{yy}\\
\varepsilon_{zz}\\
2\varepsilon_{yz}\\
2\varepsilon_{zx}\\
2\varepsilon_{xy}\end{bmatrix},

(S13)

where the factors 2 on the shear components are necessary to preserve the calculation of the elastic energy density

\sigma
and

Appendix C
Derivation of
Eq.

3
in the main text by combination of three stress–strain experiments

To calculate the changes in
T_{\mathrm{c}}
of
\text{Sr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{RuO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, we now consider the derivatives with respect to stress and strain.
For the
{A_{\mathrm{1g,1}}}
and
{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}
, using the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{A_{\mathrm{1g,1}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}\frac{\partial\sigma_{i}}{\partial\sigma_{A_{\mathrm{1g,1}}}}=\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{xx}}+\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{yy}},

(S21)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}\frac{\partial\sigma_{i}}{\partial\sigma_{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}}=\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{xx}}-\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{yy}}.

(S22)

In the case of hydrostatic pressure, which is defined with opposite sign to stresses, we have

dp_{\mathrm{hydro}}=-d\sigma_{i}
, with
i=1,2,3
(the other components are zero) and calculate the total differential of stress as

dT_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}d\sigma_{i}=-\left(\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{xx}}+\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{yy}}+\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{zz}}\right)dp_{\mathrm{hydro}},

(S23)

from which we obtain

\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{A_{\mathrm{1g,1}}}}=-\frac{dT_{\mathrm{c}}}{dp_{\mathrm{hydro}}}-\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{zz}}.

(S24)

To calculate the effect of stress along the [110] in-plane direction, it is useful to consider the three-dimensional rotation matrices.
We can thus consider a uniaxial stress of the kind
\sigma_{xx}
with magnitude
\sigma^{*}
applied to a reference system rotated by
-45\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
along the
z
axis and then rotated back by
+45\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}^{110}=R_{z}(+45^{\circ})\sigma_{xx}R_{z}(-45^{\circ})=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&0\\
1&1&0\\
0&0&\sqrt{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma^{*}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\
-1&1&0\\
0&0&\sqrt{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma^{*}&\sigma^{*}&0\\
\sigma^{*}&\sigma^{*}&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(S25)

Thus, the uniaxial stress
\sigma_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}^{110}
involves

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}=\sigma_{yy}=\sigma_{xy}=\frac{\sigma^{*}}{2}.

(S26)

We can calculate the total change of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
as

\displaystyle dT_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}d\sigma_{i}=\left(\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{xx}}+\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{yy}}+\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{xy}}\right)\frac{1}{2}d\sigma_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}^{110},

(S27)

and then apply
Eq.

S21
to obtain the final result

\displaystyle\frac{dT_{\mathrm{c}}}{d\sigma_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}^{110}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{A_{\mathrm{1g,1}}}}+\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{xy}}\right).

(S28)

Similarly, we can calculate the effect of
xy
shear strain of magnitude
\sigma^{*}
along the [110] in-plane direction as

\displaystyle\sigma_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}^{110}

\displaystyle=R_{z}(+45^{\circ})\sigma_{xy}R_{z}(-45^{\circ})=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&0\\
1&1&0\\
0&0&\sqrt{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&\sigma^{*}&0\\
\sigma^{*}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\
-1&1&0\\
0&0&\sqrt{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}-2\sigma^{*}&0&0\\
0&2\sigma^{*}&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix},

(S29)

which involves

\sigma_{xx}=-\sigma^{*},\quad\sigma_{yy}=+\sigma^{*},

(S30)

leading to

\displaystyle dT_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{i}}d\sigma_{i}=\left(-\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{xx}}+\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{yy}}+\right)d\sigma_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}^{110}.

(S31)

By making use of
Eq.

S22
, we get the final result

\displaystyle\frac{dT_{\mathrm{c}}}{d\sigma_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}^{110}}=-\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\sigma_{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}},

(S32)

which shows that
\sigma_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}^{110}
shear stress is equivalent to pure
{B_{\mathrm{1g}}}
stress, except for the minus sign.
