Effect of 10 cm H₂O positive end-expiratory pressure on right internal jugular vein cross-sectional area in obese patients under general anesthesia: a prospective observational study
Authors: Xiaofan Zheng, Xiaoqiong Cao, Shiwen Shen, Dongliang Pei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity can complicate central venous catheterization by increasing skin-to-vessel depth and impairing ultrasound visualization. Optimizing internal jugular vein (IJV) cross-sectional area (CSA) is a key strategy to facilitate cannulation. Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is known to promote IJV distension in non-obese individuals and constitutes a component of lung-protective ventilation in obese patients; however, its efficacy for IJV distension and hemodynamic safety in the obese population remain uncertain. This study investigated the association between 10 cm H₂O PEEP and right IJV CSA, as well as the hemodynamic tolerability of this intervention, in obese patients under general anesthesia.MethodsThis prospective observational study enrolled 40 adult patients with body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m² undergoing elective surgery at Tongren Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine between June and October 2025. Following standardized anesthesia induction and hemodynamic stabilization, each patient underwent ultrasound measurement of right IJV CSA at both zero end-expiratory pressure (ZEEP) and PEEP 10 cm H₂O in a randomized, self-controlled design. The outcome assessor was blinded to PEEP levels. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR) were recorded concurrently. The primary outcome was the difference in IJV CSA between ZEEP and PEEP 10 cm H₂O. Paired Student’s t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used as appropriate, and multivariable linear regression explored factors associated with the change in CSA (ΔCSA).ResultsAll 40 patients (median BMI 32.19 [IQR 31.17–33.59] kg/m²; 52.5% male) completed the study with no dropouts or missing data. PEEP 10 cm H₂O was associated with a significant increase in right IJV CSA from 1.33 ± 0.48 cm² to 1.62 ± 0.63 cm² (mean paired difference [ΔCSA] 0.28 ± 0.42 cm², 95% CI 0.15 to 0.42, p < 0.001), representing a relative increase of approximately 21%. MAP showed a modest decrease (median paired difference [ΔMAP] − 7 mmHg, IQR − 13.25 to -2.42, p < 0.001), and HR decreased correspondingly (mean paired difference [ΔHR] 5.68 ± 7.40 bpm, 95% CI 3.30 to 8.04, p < 0.001). No patient experienced severe hypotension (MAP < 65 mmHg) or required vasopressor support. Exploratory multivariable regression did not identify significant associations between ΔCSA and BMI, neck circumference, age, sex, or ASA status (all p > 0.05); however, statistical power was limited by the sample size.ConclusionsThe application of 10 cm H₂O PEEP was associated with increased right IJV CSA in moderately obese patients under general anesthesia. Introduction

Obesity has reached pandemic proportions worldwide [1, 2], posing substantial challenges to anesthesia and critical care medicine. Obesity is an independent risk factor for central venous catheterization failure and complications, primarily because distorted anatomical landmarks and excessive subcutaneous adipose tissue may increase the skin-to-vessel depth and may complicate ultrasound visualization [3–6]. When the IJV is small or inadequately distended, the technical difficulty of cannulation increases markedly, leading to a higher incidence of multiple needle passes, inadvertent carotid artery puncture, local hematoma formation, pneumothorax, and catheter malposition [6, 7]. These complications are of particular concern in obese patients, in whom rescue strategies such as alternative vascular access sites (e.g., subclavian or femoral veins) are also technically challenging due to body habitus, and in whom uncontrolled hemorrhage from arterial puncture may be more difficult to manage given the depth of anatomical structures [3, 4]. Optimizing internal jugular vein (IJV) cross-sectional area (CSA) prior to cannulation is therefore a critically important strategy to maximize first-pass success rates and minimize procedural complications in this vulnerable population [8, 9].

In non-obese individuals, physiological maneuvers such as the Valsalva maneuver, Trendelenburg position (head-down tilt), passive leg raising, and application of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) are routinely employed to enhance IJV filling [10–13]. Nevertheless, evidence indicates that these commonly used interventions have limited efficacy—or may even be ineffective or counterproductive—in obese patients [14, 15], with some studies yielding conflicting results. Validating the utility of these maneuvers for IJV distension in the obese population is therefore of considerable clinical importance for optimizing cannulation success and reducing procedural complications.

In our recent prospective study conducted in 2025, we demonstrated that PLR effectively increases IJV CSA in obese patients [16], offering a feasible alternative for venous distension. Mechanistically, PEEP elevates intrathoracic pressure, which affects cardiovascular function through multiple interrelated pathways: it impedes systemic venous return, increases right ventricular (RV) afterload by compressing alveolar vessels, reduces pulmonary blood flow, and consequently decreases left ventricular preload and systemic cardiac output [17]. The resultant venous congestion upstream of the thorax theoretically promotes IJV distension. In obese patients, PEEP 10 cm H₂O is frequently applied as part of lung-protective ventilation strategies to counteract reduced chest wall compliance and promote alveolar recruitment. However, its hemodynamic safety remains a major concern, particularly given the combined effects of increased RV afterload and reduced preload, as previous reports have documented significant circulatory instability, including hypotension, at this level in obese individuals [14].

This prospective observational study therefore aimed to investigate the association between PEEP 10 cm H₂O (compared with zero end-expiratory pressure) and right IJV CSA in obese patients. A secondary objective was to evaluate the hemodynamic tolerability of this intervention.

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective observational study employing a within-subject (self-controlled) design to investigate the association between PEEP levels during mechanical ventilation and the CSA of the right IJV in obese patients. Although PEEP levels were actively adjusted as part of the study protocol, both ZEEP (0 cm H₂O) and PEEP 10 cm H₂O represent standard-of-care ventilatory settings routinely applied during general anesthesia in obese patients and do not constitute novel experimental interventions. The study was therefore classified as observational within the framework of routine clinical practice, consistent with similar published physiological studies examining the effects of standard clinical maneuvers on IJV dimensions. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. All procedures followed a predefined protocol in a controlled clinical setting to minimize variability and ensure reproducibility. The PEEP sequence was randomized to mitigate potential order effects, and the outcome assessor was blinded to PEEP levels.

Setting

The study was conducted at Tongren Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, from June to October 2025. All measurements were performed in the operating room during elective surgeries under general anesthesia.

Participants

Adult patients scheduled for elective surgery were recruited consecutively upon arrival to the operating room.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m², consistent with the World Health Organization definition of obesity; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status II–III; (4) scheduled for elective non-cardiac, non-thoracic surgery under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation; and (5) supine surgical positioning anticipated throughout the measurement period.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of neck surgery or cervical radiation therapy that may have altered the anatomy of the right IJV; (2) known right IJV thrombosis, stenosis, or prior ipsilateral central venous catheterization within the preceding 30 days; (3) severe cardiovascular disease, defined as New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III–IV heart failure, unstable angina, uncontrolled cardiac arrhythmias, or left ventricular ejection fraction < 40% on preoperative echocardiography (if available); (4) moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease or known pulmonary hypertension (estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure > 40 mmHg); (5) end-stage renal disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 15 mL/min/1.73 m² or requiring renal replacement therapy); (6) severe infection or sepsis as defined by the Sepsis-3 criteria (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA] score increase ≥ 2 points); (7) significant coagulopathy (international normalized ratio [INR] > 1.5 or platelet count < 100 × 10⁹/L); (8) known severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease [GOLD] stage III–IV) or uncontrolled asthma, which may confound the hemodynamic response to PEEP; (9) pre-existing pneumothorax or subcutaneous emphysema; and (10) inability to provide written informed consent.

Variables

The primary outcome was the difference in right IJV CSA between zero end-expiratory pressure (ZEEP; 0 cm H₂O) and PEEP 10 cm H₂O (PEEP10). Secondary outcomes included the change in CSA (ΔCSA; CSA at PEEP10 minus CSA at ZEEP) and its associations with baseline factors (neck circumference, BMI, age, sex, ASA status, baseline heart rate at ZEEP), as well as differences in mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate between ZEEP and PEEP10.

All variables were defined a priori. Continuous variables (e.g., CSA in cm², neck circumference in cm, BMI in kg/m², age in years, heart rate in beats per minute) were analyzed in original units without transformation. Categorical variables (e.g., sex as male/female, ASA as II/III) were coded as binary or nominal.

Data sources and measurement

Standard monitoring was initiated upon operating room arrival, including non-invasive blood pressure (mmHg) and heart rate (beats per minute) via Mindray BeneVision N22 monitor, plus anthropometric measurements. Neck circumference was measured at cricoid cartilage level (head neutral) using a soft tape (nearest 0.1 cm). BMI was calculated as weight (kg, calibrated scale) divided by height squared (m, stadiometer). Drug doses were calculated based on ideal body weight. Hemoglobin (g/L) was obtained from preoperative blood tests. Fasting time and preoperative fluid volume were extracted from anesthesia records.

Anesthesia induction was standardized: propofol 2 mg/kg, rocuronium 0.7 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.2 µg/kg, followed by endotracheal intubation. Measurements started 10 min post-intubation to allow hemodynamic stabilization. All study measurements were completed prior to surgical incision; no surgical procedure, pneumoperitoneum, or surgical stimulation was present during the measurement period. No continuous vasoactive infusions (e.g., norepinephrine, phenylephrine, or inotropic agents) were used at any point during the study. Intermittent bolus doses of ephedrine (6–12 mg intravenously) were administered to 12 of 40 patients (30%) during or immediately following anesthesia induction to treat transient induction-related hypotension, consistent with standard clinical practice. All study measurements were initiated only after the 10-minute post-intubation hemodynamic stabilization period, during which hemodynamic parameters were confirmed to have returned to stable and acceptable levels (MAP > 65 mmHg) without ongoing vasoactive support. No vasoactive drugs of any kind were administered from the onset of the stabilization period through the completion of all ZEEP and PEEP10 measurements. Given the short duration of action of ephedrine (approximately 5–10 min) and the self-controlled study design—in which both ZEEP and PEEP10 measurements were obtained under identical post-stabilization conditions within each patient—the transient induction-phase use of ephedrine is unlikely to have confounded the primary within-subject comparison.

Following endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation was initiated in volume-controlled ventilation (VCV) mode for all patients with the following standardized settings: tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg (ideal body weight), respiratory rate of 12–14 breaths/min (adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide [EtCO₂] at 35–45 mmHg), inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio (I: E) of 1:2, and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) of 0.5–0.6. These ventilator settings remained constant throughout the study measurements, with only the PEEP level being changed between ZEEP and PEEP 10 cm H₂O as per the study protocol.

Each patient underwent right IJV CSA assessment at both ZEEP and PEEP10 in a self-controlled design. The sequence of PEEP application was randomized via a computer-generated list with concealed allocation (sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes) to mitigate potential carryover or order effects. An anesthesiologist opened the envelope intraoperatively and adjusted ventilator PEEP accordingly. Each level was maintained for at least 2 min to achieve steady-state ventilation, with a 1-minute washout interval at ZEEP between levels. Hemodynamic stability was continuously monitored throughout the study period. Hypotension was defined as MAP < 65 mmHg or a decrease in systolic blood pressure > 20% from baseline. In the event of significant hypotension during the PEEP maneuver, the protocol mandated immediate termination of the intervention and administration of hemodynamic support. In addition, ventilator-displayed airway pressures were continuously monitored as part of standard anesthetic care. A plateau pressure exceeding 30 cm H₂O—the widely accepted upper safety limit for lung-protective ventilation [18]—was established as a respiratory safety threshold mandating immediate termination of the PEEP maneuver and return to baseline ventilatory settings. Any patient requiring rescue intervention for hemodynamic instability during the measurement period would be excluded from the final analysis.

To mitigate the risk of atelectasis formation during ZEEP exposure, several protective measures were incorporated into the study protocol. Following endotracheal intubation, a baseline PEEP of 5 cm H₂O was maintained throughout the entire 10-minute hemodynamic stabilization period; patients were therefore not exposed to ZEEP until the onset of the randomized measurement window. The fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) was reduced from 1.0 (used during preoxygenation and induction for apnea safety) to 0.5–0.6 immediately after successful intubation and maintained at this level throughout the stabilization and measurement periods, well below the threshold associated with clinically significant absorption atelectasis. ZEEP exposure was limited to a brief 2-minute measurement window and occurred only in patients randomized to the ZEEP-first sequence. The 2-minute equilibration interval was selected to allow hemodynamic steady-state with respect to intrathoracic pressure transmission and venous capacitance redistribution.

A standardized fluid management protocol was followed from induction through the measurement period. All patients received lactated Ringer’s solution via a peripheral intravenous catheter at a maintenance rate of approximately 5–7 mL/kg/h (calculated based on ideal body weight), consistent with institutional protocol for elective surgery. No bolus fluid challenges, colloid solutions, or blood products were administered during this period. Given that the interval from anesthesia induction to the initiation of study measurements was approximately 15 min (including the 10-minute post-intubation stabilization period), the estimated total crystalloid volume infused was approximately 100–175 mL, representing less than 3–4% of the estimated circulating blood volume (~ 65–70 mL/kg ideal body weight). This minimal volume is unlikely to have produced clinically significant alterations in preload status. Furthermore, the self-controlled study design ensured that both ZEEP and PEEP10 measurements were performed under identical volume loading conditions within each patient, thereby mitigating any potential confounding effect of fluid administration on the primary within-subject comparison.

All measurements were performed with the patient in a standardized supine position on a horizontal operating Table (0° inclination, no Trendelenburg or reverse Trendelenburg tilt). The patient’s arms were positioned alongside the body. No pillows, head rings, or neck supports were used during the measurement period to avoid any external influence on cervical venous anatomy. This position was strictly maintained throughout the entire measurement period and was not altered between ZEEP and PEEP10 measurements. Ultrasound imaging was performed using a 7.5 MHz linear-array probe (Mindray M8, Shenzhen, China) positioned transversely at the cricoid cartilage level, with the head rotated 20° to the left for anatomical standardization. The probe was oriented perpendicular to the skin surface, and its angulation was carefully adjusted to obtain a true transverse cross-section of the IJV, confirmed by visualization of the most circular (least elliptical) vessel profile. Once optimal perpendicular alignment was achieved, the probe was held steady without tilting, fanning, or sliding during image acquisition. To minimize probe-induced venous compression, a standardized “minimal pressure” technique was employed: a generous amount of ultrasound gel was applied to create an acoustic standoff layer, and the probe was placed on the skin with only sufficient pressure to maintain adequate skin contact and image quality. The operator was specifically instructed to observe the IJV lumen in real time during probe positioning and to progressively reduce the applied pressure until no visible deformation or compression of the venous wall was observed on the ultrasound display. This criterion was verified prior to each image acquisition at both ZEEP and PEEP10 time points. Importantly, both ZEEP and PEEP10 images were acquired sequentially with the probe maintained in the same position and orientation, ensuring that any residual angulation-related artifact would affect both conditions equally and would not bias the within-subject comparison. Images were acquired at end-expiration (verified by the ventilator waveform display) with the probe held in a steady position. All ultrasound images throughout the study were acquired by a single experienced anesthesiologist (Operator A) who was blinded to the PEEP level to eliminate inter-operator acquisition variability. Concurrent heart rate and blood pressure were recorded at each measurement time point. Images were stored digitally and de-identified; CSA was measured offline via ultrasound software planimetry (inner vessel wall tracing) by a second anesthesiologist (Assessor B) who was blinded to the PEEP level at which each image was acquired. This double-blinded acquisition and assessment design was implemented to minimize both inter-operator and inter-assessor variability.

Bias control

Several measures were implemented to minimize potential sources of bias. Selection bias was mitigated by consecutively enrolling all eligible patients meeting predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period. Performance bias was addressed by standardizing the anesthesia induction protocol, measurement conditions (patient positioning, probe placement at the cricoid cartilage level, head rotation of 20° to the left), and timing (measurements initiated 10 min post-intubation to ensure hemodynamic stabilization). The sequence of PEEP application (ZEEP-first vs. PEEP10-first) was randomized using a computer-generated random number list with concealed allocation via sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, thereby mitigating potential carryover or order effects. A 1-minute washout interval at ZEEP was applied between measurements. Detection bias was minimized through a double-blinded design: the ultrasonographer acquiring the images (Operator A) was blinded to the PEEP level — a separate anesthesiologist adjusted the ventilator settings without informing the ultrasonographer — and the outcome assessor performing offline CSA measurements (Assessor B) was independently blinded to the PEEP allocation of each stored image. Attrition bias was not applicable, as all 40 enrolled patients completed the study with no dropouts and no missing data.

Study size

Sample size was calculated using PASS software (version 15.0, NCSS LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA) for the primary outcome (paired difference in CSA between ZEEP and PEEP10). Based on prior data reporting a mean supine IJV CSA of approximately 1.73 cm² with a standard deviation of 0.72 cm², we assumed a minimum clinically meaningful relative increase of 20%. This threshold was selected based on two considerations: (1) Tartière et al. demonstrated that physiological maneuvers produced relative increases in IJV CSA of approximately 20% in adult patients, establishing this magnitude as a clinically relevant benchmark for IJV distension studies [19]; and (2) our previous study investigating passive leg raising in obese patients similarly adopted a 20% relative increase as the minimal clinically important difference and successfully detected a significant effect [16]. Using a two-sided paired t-test with α = 0.05 and 80% power, the required sample size was 36 participants. Accounting for an anticipated dropout rate of 10%, the target enrollment was set at n = 40.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed or median (interquartile range, IQR) if not, with normality assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, complemented by visual inspection of quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots. Q–Q plots for all primary and secondary continuous outcomes are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Paired differences between ZEEP and PEEP10 were analyzed as follows: differences in right IJV CSA and heart rate were normally distributed and analyzed using the paired Student’s t-test, with results reported as mean differences and 95% confidence intervals (CI); differences in MAP were non-normally distributed and analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with results reported as median differences and p-values.

Multivariable linear regression was used to explore factors associated with ΔCSA, including neck circumference and BMI as primary independent variables, and age, sex, ASA physical status, and baseline heart rate (at ZEEP) as covariates. Regression coefficients (β), 95% CIs, and p-values are reported for all variables regardless of statistical significance.

All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). There were no missing data in this study. All 40 enrolled patients completed both ZEEP and PEEP10 measurements with complete recording of all primary and secondary outcome variables. A prespecified plan for handling missing data (listwise deletion for < 5% missing values) was included in the study protocol but was not required.

Primary outcome

Application of PEEP 10 cm H₂O significantly increased the CSA of the right IJV compared with ZEEP. Mean CSA rose from 1.33 ± 0.48 cm² under ZEEP to 1.62 ± 0.63 cm² under PEEP 10 cm H₂O. The mean paired difference (ΔCSA) was 0.28 ± 0.42 cm² (95% CI 0.15 to 0.42, p < 0.001, paired Student’s t-test), representing a relative increase of approximately 21% (Fig. 2a).

Secondary outcomes

Hemodynamic parameters changed significantly with the application of PEEP 10 cm H₂O (Fig. 2b and c).

MAP decreased significantly from a median of 83.84 (IQR 73.33 to 92.91) mmHg at ZEEP to a median of 77.33 (IQR 65.00 to 83.08) mmHg at PEEP 10 cm H₂O. The median paired difference (ΔMAP) was − 7(IQR − 13.25 to -2.42) mmHg (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). The reduction was modest in magnitude; no patient experienced severe hypotension (MAP < 65 mmHg) or required vasopressor support during the study period.

Heart rate decreased from 74.83 ± 13.11 bpm during ZEEP to 69.15 ± 11.99 bpm during PEEP 10 cm H₂O. The mean paired difference (ΔHR) was 5.68 ± 7.40 bpm (95% CI 3.30 to 8.04, p < 0.001, paired Student’s t-test).

Respiratory safety

No adverse respiratory events were observed during the study period. No patient exceeded the predefined plateau pressure safety threshold of 30 cm H₂O during PEEP 10 cm H₂O application. No episodes of oxygen desaturation (SpO₂ <92%), clinical signs of barotrauma (pneumothorax or subcutaneous emphysema), or ventilation-related complications occurred in any of the 40 patients.

Multivariable analysis

Exploratory multivariable linear regression did not identify statistically significant associations between ΔCSA and patient characteristics including age, BMI, baseline heart rate, baseline mean blood pressure, fasting time, or neck circumference (all p > 0.05). However, given the relatively small sample size (n = 40) in relation to the number of covariates included (k = 6), yielding approximately 6–7 observations per variable, the statistical power of this analysis was limited. These results should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and do not exclude the possibility of clinically meaningful associations that may be detected in larger studies.

Discussion

The principal finding of this prospective observational study is that the application of 10 cm H₂O PEEP significantly increased the CSA of the right IJV in obese patients under general anesthesia. Although PEEP had statistically significant effects on MAP and heart rate (HR), no episodes of severe hypotension requiring protocol termination or rescue vasopressor therapy occurred during the measurement period.

In clinical practice, PEEP serves not only as a tool to improve IJV puncture conditions, but also as an essential component of lung-protective ventilation strategies [20, 21]. In the anesthetic and critical care management of obese patients, lung-protective ventilation is of paramount importance; therefore, establishing the hemodynamic safety of PEEP in this population is clinically necessary. While application of PEEP was associated with decreases in MAP and HR, these changes remained within clinically acceptable ranges [18]. Importantly, our findings contrast with those of Downey et al. [14], who reported that 10 cm H₂O PEEP was poorly tolerated in obese anesthetized adults, with a considerable proportion of patients developing hypotension requiring intervention. We postulate that this discrepancy is primarily attributable to differences in obesity severity between the two cohorts. Participants in the Downey et al. study had higher BMI levels consistent with morbid or super-obesity (typically BMI > 40 kg/m² in Western populations), whereas our cohort had a median BMI of 32.19 kg/m² (Class I/II obesity). Patients with super-obesity may exhibit greater hemodynamic vulnerability to PEEP due to more pronounced reductions in preload from elevated intrathoracic pressure and potential baseline right ventricular strain, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation.

This study extends our previous investigation demonstrating the efficacy of passive leg raising for IJV distension in awake obese patients prior to anesthesia induction [16]. The present findings demonstrate that PEEP 10 cm H₂O is similarly effective for IJV distension under general anesthesia — a physiological environment characterized by reduced vascular tone, altered cardiac contractility, and positive-pressure ventilation — thereby broadening the repertoire of available maneuvers across different phases of perioperative care. Despite the hemodynamic perturbations introduced by general anesthesia, PEEP still effectively increased IJV CSA, demonstrating the robustness of this effect in the obese population.

Regarding the choice of ventilation mode, all patients were ventilated using volume-controlled ventilation (VCV) throughout the study. In VCV mode, the application of PEEP directly increases mean airway pressure and, consequently, intrathoracic pressure, which is the primary physiological mechanism underlying IJV distension. However, this elevation in intrathoracic pressure has a dual effect on the cardiopulmonary system: while it promotes venous congestion upstream (facilitating IJV distension), it simultaneously increases RV afterload by compressing alveolar vessels, which may reduce cardiac output [22]. The net effect on IJV CSA therefore depends on the balance between upstream venous congestion and downstream cardiac output reduction [17]. In our cohort of moderately obese, hemodynamically stable patients, the venous congestion effect predominated, resulting in a net 21% increase in IJV CSA. However, in patients with pre-existing RV dysfunction or morbid obesity, the RV afterload effect may predominate, potentially limiting or reversing the expected IJV distension — a consideration that underscores the importance of our exclusion criteria regarding severe cardiovascular disease. Different ventilation modes may result in varying degrees of intrathoracic pressure transmission; our findings are therefore most directly applicable to obese patients ventilated with VCV, and whether similar results would be obtained under pressure-controlled ventilation warrants further investigation.

Regarding head positioning, we standardized head rotation at 20° to the left for all patients throughout all measurements. Prior studies have demonstrated that excessive head rotation (beyond 40°) reduces IJV CSA through mechanical compression by the sternocleidomastoid muscle and increases the overlap between the IJV and the carotid artery, thereby elevating the risk of inadvertent arterial puncture [23]. Conversely, minimal or no rotation may limit ultrasound probe access to the right IJV. A rotation of approximately 20° represents a widely adopted clinical compromise that provides adequate ultrasound visualization while preserving venous patency, and is consistent with the positioning described in prior IJV imaging studies [8, 9]. This angle was kept constant across both ZEEP and PEEP10 measurements, ensuring that head position did not confound the within-subject comparison.

These findings carry practical clinical implications for the management of obese patients. The increase in IJV CSA induced by 10 cm H₂O PEEP offers a reliable method to improve vascular visualization and facilitate central venous catheterization in this population, where anatomical challenges often make IJV access more difficult.

Our study has several limitations that warrant careful consideration.

First, as a single-center observational study conducted under controlled mechanical ventilation, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Our cohort represented a highly selected subgroup of hemodynamically stable, moderately obese patients (median BMI 32.19 kg/m²; predominantly ASA II), with rigorous exclusion of individuals with severe cardiovascular disease, pulmonary hypertension, end-stage renal disease, sepsis, and significant coagulopathy. Furthermore, all measurements were performed under standardized general anesthesia with volume-controlled ventilation, and findings may differ in spontaneously breathing patients, emergency settings with less predictable hemodynamic conditions, or patients with morbid or super-obesity (BMI > 40 kg/m²), decompensated cardiac disease, or hemodynamic instability, in whom the application of PEEP may carry a substantially different risk–benefit profile. Future multicenter studies specifically targeting these higher-risk subgroups and broader clinical settings are warranted.

Second, we used IJV CSA as a surrogate marker for cannulation ease and did not evaluate actual puncture success rates, number of needle passes, or procedure-related complications. Additionally, because central venous catheterization was not performed during the study period, we were unable to obtain central venous pressure (CVP) measurements that would have provided direct physiological evidence of intrathoracic pressure transmission and strengthened the mechanistic interpretation of PEEP-induced IJV distension. The clinical translation of the observed 21% CSA increase into tangible improvements in cannulation outcomes, and the precise relationship between PEEP-induced venous distension and central venous hemodynamics, remain to be validated in future interventional studies incorporating both cannulation outcome data and CVP measurement.

Third, we acknowledge that the active adjustment of PEEP levels introduces elements characteristic of quasi-experimental designs, which may challenge the purely observational classification. However, given that both PEEP settings are within the spectrum of standard intraoperative ventilatory management for obese patients and no experimental therapy was introduced, we maintain that the observational framework is appropriate. Readers should interpret our findings as associations rather than established causal relationships, consistent with the principles outlined by D’Amico et al. [24].

Fourth, we used ZEEP as the comparator condition to isolate the specific contribution of PEEP to IJV distension. However, even brief exposure to ZEEP in obese patients may promote alveolar derecruitment and increase pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) by reducing lung volume below functional residual capacity, as described by Disselkamp et al. [17]. This U-shaped relationship between lung volume and PVR means that the ZEEP condition may not represent a true hemodynamic “baseline” but rather a state of mildly elevated right ventricular afterload. Although several protocol features mitigated this risk — including maintenance of 5 cm H₂O PEEP during the pre-measurement stabilization period, brief (2-minute) ZEEP exposure, moderate FiO₂ (0.5–0.6), and enrollment of non-critically ill patients at the lower end of the obesity spectrum — we cannot exclude that partial derecruitment occurred. This may have attenuated the observed difference in IJV CSA between conditions and partly explains the variable individual responses, including cases where CSA paradoxically decreased with PEEP application. Future studies should consider using a low-PEEP comparator (e.g., 5 cm H₂O) rather than ZEEP to better reflect clinical practice and avoid confounding from derecruitment-induced PVR changes.

Fifth, despite standardization of the ultrasound technique — including perpendicular probe orientation, single anatomical landmark, and single-operator blinded acquisition — minor variations in probe angulation between patients cannot be entirely excluded and may have introduced measurement variability. However, the self-controlled design, in which the probe was maintained in a fixed position between ZEEP and PEEP10 acquisitions within each patient, ensures that such variability would not systematically bias the primary within-subject comparison.

Sixth, the multivariable regression analysis was exploratory in nature, and the ratio of observations to covariates (approximately 6–7:1) was below the commonly recommended threshold of 10–15:1, limiting the reliability and generalizability of these findings. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to adequately assess the influence of patient characteristics on the PEEP-induced change in IJV CSA.

Conclusion

The application of 10 cm H₂O PEEP was associated with increased right IJV CSA in moderately obese patients under general anesthesia. This maneuver was well tolerated with clinically acceptable hemodynamic changes in this specific cohort. However, individualized hemodynamic assessment remains essential before extrapolating these findings to patients with morbid obesity, hemodynamic instability, or right ventricular dysfunction.
