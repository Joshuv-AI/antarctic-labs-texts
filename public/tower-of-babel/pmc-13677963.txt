Metabolomics reveals the effects of different carbon sources on the quality and flavor profile of low-salt shrimp paste
Authors: Ping Hong, Meiqi Gu, Chuanhai Tu, Wenxiong Zheng, Shanshan Shui, Jialiang Li, Soottawat Benjakul, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To elucidate carbon source effects on low-salt shrimp paste quality and bioactivity, glucose and glutinous rice flour were added pre-fermentation, physicochemical indicators monitored, and flavor and metabolic compound changes analyzed via combined HS-SPME-GC-MS and non-targeted metabolomics. The results showed that the addition of glucose and glutinous rice flour significantly enhanced the quality and flavor of low-salt shrimp paste following 12 days of fermentation. In total, 709 volatile compounds and 2089 non-volatile compounds were putatively annotated. The top 50 volatile compounds in shrimp paste included 7 alcohols, 9 hydrocarbons, and 6 ketones. Sensory analysis showed carbon source-supplemented shrimp paste exhibited enhanced sweetness and fruity aromas. Among the top 20 non-volatile metabolites, organic acids and their derivatives, lipids, and lipoid molecules emerged as primary metabolites. Unique metabolic pathways identified in the carbon-supplemented group—such as cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis—promoted flavor development in low-salt shrimp paste.

Introduction

Shrimp paste is a traditional fermented food commonly consumed directly as an appetizer or used as a flavor enhancer during cooking, improving the palatability of target foods (Yu et al., 2022). Low-salt shrimp paste contains only 10% to 20% salt—significantly less than traditional shrimp paste (25% ∼ 30%). The reduced salt content improves the physicochemical properties and aligns better with current dietary recommendations (Huang et al., 2024). The fermentation of traditional shrimp paste is a spontaneous process and is prone to spoilage, especially during low-salt fermentation. Most existing studies have focused on the microbial community composition of low-salt shrimp paste and the quality control risks associated with salt reduction (Qu et al., 2026). Exogenous carbon source supplementation is widely recognized as an effective strategy for regulating microbial metabolism and improving flavor quality in carbohydrate-rich fermentation systems. For example, the addition of glucose or sucrose has been shown to increase the production of key organic acids, including acetic and lactic acid (Wang, Chi, et al., 2025). In bread-making, sugar addition not only contributes sweetness but also enhances fermentation performance and final product quality (Woo et al., 2025). However, shrimp paste is a fundamentally different matrix—high in protein and extremely low in endogenous sugars—and the metabolic fate and effects of added carbon sources in this system remain unknown.

Research indicates that carbon sources significantly influence the composition and activity of microbial communities during food fermentation, thereby enhancing the flavor profile of the final product. For instance, glucose serves as a readily available carbon source, promoting the growth of certain beneficial microorganisms, such as lactic acid bacteria and Bacillus species (Castillo-Alfonso et al., 2022). Glutinous rice flour, as a complex carbon source containing higher levels of starch and fiber, may exert distinct effects on microbial metabolism during fermentation compared to monosaccharides, potentially fostering the growth of microorganisms such as yeasts and lactic acid bacteria (Dan et al., 2022). Consequently, adding additional carbon sources will alter the metabolites and flavor compounds of low-salt shrimp paste, necessitating further analysis.

Non-targeted metabolomics has been widely applied in fermented foods in recent years and has proven useful for characterizing flavor-related metabolites (Chen et al., 2023). This approach enables the global profiling of key metabolites that determine product flavor and provides new insights into metabolic pathways. Shrimp paste exhibits greater aromatic complexity compared to other fermented products, with its core sensory attributes defined by three aroma components: meaty notes, soy sauce-like flavors, and shrimp-specific aromas (Hu et al., 2024). Headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) has high extraction efficiency and operational simplicity, enabling the precise separation and qualitative identification of volatile compounds in complex samples. It has been widely used in flavor analysis, especially in food processing and storage (Cheng et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025). The combined use of non-targeted metabolomics and volatile compounds analysis has also become common practice in food fermentation research (Qin et al., 2024; Zhang, Wang, et al., 2025).

This study explored the effects of additional carbon sources on the fermentation quality by adding glucose and glutinous rice flour. A non-targeted metabolomics approach was adopted to identify changes in metabolites and analyze key metabolic pathways. Meanwhile, HS-SPME-GC-MS technology was utilized to investigate the evolution of volatile flavor compounds during the fermentation process. The findings are expected to support improvement and process control in low-salt shrimp paste fermentation and provide insights and to provide data support for future industrial application.

Materials and methods

Samples collection

Fresh red shrimp (Solenocera crassicornis) were immediately placed in an insulated container packed with ample ice following procurement. They were then transported to the laboratory via express delivery and processed without delay. Edible salt and glutinous rice flour were purchased from local supermarkets in Zhoushan City, Zhejiang Province, China. Food-grade glucose was procured from Want Want Food Additives Co., Ltd.

Preparation of low-salt shrimp paste

Fresh red shrimp were first rinsed three times with sterile water to remove surface impurities. They were then processed into a uniform shrimp paste using a meat grinder at room temperature. The homogenized shrimp paste was portioned into sterile fermentation tanks, with each tank containing 250 g. The homogenized paste was divided into three groups: Group Y (added 12% edible salt, blank control), Group P (added 12% edible salt and 6% food-grade glucose), and Group F (added 12% edible salt and 6% glutinous rice flour). After thorough mixing, each group was covered with sterile gauze and incubated at 25 °C for 12 days. Samples were collected at 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 days and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Three independent fermentation batches were prepared for each group as biological replicates. The samples used for metabolomic, volatile, and biogenic amine analyses were obtained from an independent fermentation experiment conducted separately from Gu et al. (2025). These datasets have not been reported previously.

Determination of physicochemical properties

Salt content was measured using a salinity meter. Total viable count was determined according to the Chinese national standard GB 4789.2-2016.

Protein concentration was determined using the biuret method (Miranda, 2024). Briefly, 1 g of shrimp paste was mixed with 9 mL of physiological saline, homogenized on ice, and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min. A 50 μL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 2.5 mL of biuret reagent, incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and cooled under running water. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm, and the protein concentration was calculated from a standard curve.

SDS-PAGE was performed according to Song et al. (2025) with minor modifications. Briefly, 3 g of shrimp paste was mixed with 27 mL of 5% SDS solution and digested at 85 °C for 1 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was diluted, mixed with protein loading buffer (4:1, v/v), and heated in boiling water for 5 min. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue and destained until the background became clear.

Biogenic amine levels in shrimp paste were measured by high-performance liquid chromatography. A 1 mL aliquot of the biogenic amine extract was transferred into a 10 mL centrifuge tube and mixed with 1.5 mL of pH 11 buffer solution. After adding 1 mL of Dns-Cl solution (10 mg/mL in acetone), the tube was incubated at 40 °C in a water bath in the dark for 1 h. The mixture was then supplemented with 0.2 mL of proline solution (100 mg/mL), mixed, and allowed to stand at room temperature for another hour. For extraction, 3 mL of n-heptane was added and the tube was vortexed thoroughly. Once the layers had separated, 1 mL of the upper organic phase was removed and evaporated to dryness under a gentle nitrogen stream at 40 °C. The resulting residue was redissolved in 1 mL of chromatographic methanol and filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane before analysis. The separation used a Shiseido Capcell Pak C18 MGII column (4.6 mm I.D. × 150 mm, 5 μm) with a mobile phase of methanol and ultrapure water. The column was maintained at 35 °C, the flow rate at 0.80 mL/min, and the injection volume at 10 μL. The gradient program is provided in Supplementary Material Table S1.

LC-MS analysis

Metabolite extraction

Sample preparation followed the procedure in Section 2.2. Three sets of low-salt shrimp paste samples (Y, P, F) collected at fermentation days 0 and 12 were selected for non-targeted metabolomics analysis. Briefly, 50 mg of sample was placed in a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and 400 μL of extraction solvent (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v) was added. The mixture was homogenized at −10 °C (50 Hz, 6 min) and ultrasonicated at 5 °C (40 kHz, 30 min). The sample was then allowed to settle at −20 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatant was collected for further analysis. To ensure reproducibility, a total of three independent biological replicates were prepared.

Chromatography and mass spectrometry conditions

Chromatography conditions: 2 μL of sample was separated on an HSST3 column before entering mass spectrometry detection. Mobile phase A: 95% water +5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid).

Mobile phase B: 47.5% acetonitrile +47.5% isopropanol +5% water (containing 0.1% formic acid).

MS Conditions: Mass signals were acquired in dual-ion mode with a mass range of 70–1050 m/z. Ion spray voltage: positive ion voltage 3500 V, negative ion voltage 2800 V. Sheath gas 40 psi, auxiliary heating gas 10 psi. Ion source heating temperature 400 °C. Collision energy cycling: 20–40-60 V. MS1 resolution 70,000, MS2 resolution 17,500.

HS-SPME-GC-MS and sensory evaluation

Volatile analysis

Sample preparation followed the procedure in Section 2.2. For volatile compound analysis, low-salt shrimp paste samples from Groups Y, P, and F were taken at 0 d and 12 d. At each time point, three independent fermentation batches were sampled per group as biological replicates (n = 3).

Headspace Solid-Phase Microextraction Operating Parameters: Weigh 2.00 g of shrimp paste was weighed into a 20 mL headspace vial, sealed, and equilibrated at 50 °C for 30 min. The SPME fiber was then inserted and extraction was performed for 30 min. The fiber was desorbed at 250 °C for 4 min.

Data processing and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in three independent biological replicates unless otherwise noted, and all results are presented as mean ± SD. Metabolomics data were preprocessed by imputing missing values with half the minimum positive value, log10-transformation, and Pareto scaling. Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed by Shapiro-Wilk and Levene's tests. Group differences were tested by one-way ANOVA with LSD post-hoc test, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data for flavor and untargeted metabolite assays are provided in the supplementary materials (Tables S2 and S3). All subsequent analyses were performed based on these datasets. For multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly altered. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0, Excel 2021, and SIMCA 14.1. Plots were generated with Origin 2024.

Results and discussion

Changes in physicochemical properties during fermentation of low-salt shrimp paste

The increase in salt content (relative) observed during fermentation resulted from moisture loss, which concentrated the salts in the matrix (Fig. 1a). Salt content increased gradually in all three groups throughout fermentation, but remained consistently lower in Groups P and F than in Group Y. This difference was likely due to the addition of 6% glucose or glutinous rice flour before fermentation, which increased the total mass of the paste and thus diluted the relative salt concentration.Fig. 1Changes in salt content (a), TVC (b), total protein (c), and SDS-PAGE (d) during the fermentation of low-salt shrimp paste. Within the same color, different lowercase letters denote significant differences within groups (p < 0.05), while different uppercase letters indicate significant differences at the same fermentation time (p < 0.05).Fig. 1

Changes in salt content (a), TVC (b), total protein (c), and SDS-PAGE (d) during the fermentation of low-salt shrimp paste. Within the same color, different lowercase letters denote significant differences within groups (p < 0.05), while different uppercase letters indicate significant differences at the same fermentation time (p < 0.05).

The fermentation of low-salt shrimp paste depends on the microorganisms present in the paste (Surya et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 1b, total viable counts in all three groups followed a similar trend: slow growth during the early stage (0–3 d), probably reflecting low initial populations and an adaptation period, followed by rapid increases during the mid-to-late stages, driven by abundant nutrients and favorable conditions for microbial proliferation. By the end of fermentation, the total colony count in Group Y shrimp paste increased from 3.43 lg(CFU/g) to 9.55 lg(CFU/g), while Group P rose from 3.32 lg(CFU/g) to 9.11 lg(CFU/g). While Group F increased from 3.24 lg(CFU/g) to 9.05 lg(CFU/g). Group Y exhibited significantly higher total colony counts than Groups P and F, likely due to the addition of glucose/glutinous rice flour, which enabled lactic acid bacteria to rapidly utilize fermentable sugars, producing lactic and acetic acids, thereby lowering pH. This reduced the growth rate of aerobic spoilage bacteria dominant in shrimp paste, such as Pseudomonas and Enterobacteriaceae (Helmi et al., 2022), consequently slowing the increase in total colony count. In addition, the addition of carbon sources lowers the water activity of shrimp paste, which suppresses microbial growth to extend shelf life and causes marked changes in textural properties (Kilinc et al., 2006). The microorganisms involved in shrimp paste fermentation secrete proteases and lipases that hydrolyze proteins and lipids into small-molecule peptides, free amino acids, and free fatty acids, thereby playing a key role in the formation of the characteristic flavor profile of shrimp paste (Song et al., 2024).

Total protein content decreased with fermentation time in all three groups (Fig. 1c), a trend consistent with previous reports on fermented aquatic products (Prihanto et al., 2024). This occurs because the low-salinity fermentation environment, coupled with ample carbon sources in Groups P and F, provides favorable growth conditions for most microorganisms, leading to faster protein utilization in these groups. By the end of fermentation, total protein content decreased from 127.09 mg/g to 83.80 mg/g in Group Y, from 119.27 mg/g to 70.24 mg/g in Group P, and from 112.52 mg/g in the early fermentation stage to 60.72 mg/g in Group F. The amount of protein degradation in groups P and F was significantly higher than in group Y. Protein hydrolysis occurring during fermentation plays a decisive biochemical role in shaping flavor characteristics and optimizing nutritional properties (Zhu et al., 2019).

SDS-PAGE provided a visual representation of protein molecular weight changes during fermentation (Fig. 1d). At the same fermentation time point, the target protein bands (Bands I, II, III, actin, and troponin T) in Group Y were more intense than those in Groups P and F, indicating that more intact proteins were retained in the control group. In contrast, the markedly fainter and more diffuse bands observed in Groups P and F reflected a higher degree of protein hydrolysis in these carbon-source-supplemented groups, which was consistent with the results of total protein content. Collectively, these findings confirm that the addition of glucose and glutinous rice flour significantly facilitates protein degradation during low-salt shrimp paste fermentation. No evident degradation of myosin light chain (MLC) was detected in any group over the entire fermentation period. These band variations reflect ongoing protein degradation throughout fermentation, which facilitates the formation of volatile flavor compounds (Li et al., 2024).

Analysis of biogenic amine content in fermented low-salt shrimp paste

Biogenic amines (BAs) are a major risk factor in foodborne illness, and controlling their content is critical for ensuring the quality and safety of low-salt shrimp paste (Sang et al., 2020). Six biogenic amines—tyramine, putrescine, cadaverine, tryptamine, spermine, and spermidine—were detected in all three groups (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Changes of Tyramine (a), Putrescine (b), Cadaverine (c), tryptamine (d), spermine (e), and Spermidine (f) during fermentation of low-salt shrimp paste.Fig. 2

Changes of Tyramine (a), Putrescine (b), Cadaverine (c), tryptamine (d), spermine (e), and Spermidine (f) during fermentation of low-salt shrimp paste.

Among fermented foods, tyramine is the most prevalent and harmful biogenic amine. In shrimp paste, tyramine levels ranged from 5.86 to 126.29 mg/kg (Li et al., 2021). In the present study, tyramine levels in all three groups increased with fermentation time (Fig. 2a), with Group F consistently showing lower levels than Groups P and Y. This occurs because the branched starch in glutinous rice flour can adsorb free tyrosine through hydrogen bonding or hydrophobic interactions, thereby reducing tyramine formation (Wang, Jiang, et al., 2025). Putrescine levels in Group Y followed a three-stage pattern of increase, stabilization, and a late-stage rise (Fig. 2b). In contrast, Groups P and F showed fluctuating trends, with an initial increase, a subsequent decrease, and a final rebound. This pattern can be explained as follows: during early fermentation, active microbial metabolism associated with glucose and glutinous rice flour degradation led to rapid putrescine production. As fermentation progressed, some putrescine may have been converted by other microorganisms into alternative metabolites such as spermine, causing a temporary decline. In the late fermentation stage, the re-accumulation of putrescine might be related to continued protein hydrolysis by microbes harbouring decarboxylase-associated genes (e.g., speA and aguA), which would provide precursor amino acids for putrescine synthesis (Tan et al., 2025).

Cadaverine and tryptamine levels in all three groups exhibited a similar “rise–fall–rise” pattern (Fig. 2c and d). Levels of both amines in Groups P and F remained consistently lower than those in Group Y throughout fermentation. This is likely because glucose competes with free amino acids in Maillard reactions, reducing the availability of amino acid substrates for decarboxylation and thus limiting biogenic amine production. Reducing sugars generated from the degradation of polysaccharides and starch can react with side chains of proteins and amino acids to form early Maillard reaction products and reversible Schiff bases. This reduces the content of free amino acids and suppresses the formation of heterocyclic amines in meat products (Teng et al., 2023).

Spermine levels declined gradually in all three groups (Fig. 2e), whereas spermidine levels increased gradually over the fermentation period (Fig. 2f). Group F maintained higher spermidine levels than Groups Y and P throughout fermentation. The addition of glucose and glutinous rice flour appeared to shift microbial metabolism from protein degradation toward glycolysis, reducing the precursor supply for spermidine synthesis and thereby selectively inhibiting spermidine accumulation. No significant effect on spermine levels was observed.

After preprocessing and PLS-DA analysis, the samples from each shrimp paste group on day 12 were clearly separated into three distinct clusters in the scatter plot, indicating a good model fit. In Fig. 3g, h, and i, blue points on the left indicate metabolites with reduced expression levels, while red points on the right indicate metabolites with increased expression levels. Group Y exhibited 537 total upregulated metabolites and 339 total downregulated metabolites; The P group had 615 total up-regulated metabolites and 355 total down-regulated metabolites in low-salt shrimp paste; the F group had 608 total up-regulated metabolites and 345 total down-regulated metabolites. All three low-salt shrimp paste groups exhibited more total up-regulated metabolites than total down-regulated metabolites. The proteases inherent to shrimp and microbial proteases remain active in low-salt (10–12%) environments, continuously hydrolyzing myofibrillar proteins into peptides and free amino acids (Liu et al., 2020). In low-salt fermentation matrices, consistent “net upregulation” metabolomic profiles have been quantitatively confirmed. In a 5% NaCl-fermented shepherd's purse experiment, the number of upregulated metabolites exceeded that of downregulated metabolites across all three time points. Similarly, in 3% NaCl-fermented pickles supplemented with Lactobacillus plantarum, 70.78% of differential metabolites showed an upward trend (Li et al., 2025). Although not an aquatic food, low-salt mixed-strain fermentation of mulberry juice exhibited a parallel trend—36 of 92 differentially metabolized compounds showed significant upregulation (Han et al., 2025). Collectively, low-salt fermented foods often operate within a net synthetic metabolome environment, favoring the accumulation of flavor- and nutrition-oriented small molecules.

Eight of the significantly downregulated differential metabolites at day 12 in the three shrimp paste groups belonged to lipid and lipoid molecules. Lipidomic analysis of shrimp revealed that dried shrimp are rich in glycerophospholipids (GP), glycerolipids (GL), sphingolipids (SP), fatty acyls (FA), and sterol lipids (ST) (Zheng, Yang, et al., 2025). Glycerophospholipids are among the most abundant lipids in shrimp and constitute a vital component of biological membranes. Glycerophospholipids among the differential metabolites included phosphatidylcholine (PC), lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC), and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LysoPE). LysoPC and LysoPE originate from PC and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). At day 12 of fermentation for all three shrimp paste groups, the extent of downregulation followed the order P > F > Y. This indicates that adding a carbon source during shrimp paste fermentation is more effective than traditional fermentation methods in reducing the production of inflammatory lipids, and that adding glucose is more advantageous than adding glutinous rice flour.

As shown in Fig. 4a, the metabolites most significantly upregulated primarily included organic acids and their derivatives, oligopeptides, and lipid/lipid-like molecules. On day 12 of fermentation, lotaustralin and glutarylcarnitine were significantly more up-regulated in the P group than in the F group, and the F group showed higher levels than the Y group. Under low-salt and carbon-rich conditions, the low-salt environment altered microbial community structure, activating polyketide and fatty acid metabolism (Phewpan et al., 2020). Microbial activity in shrimp paste also elevated acetyl-CoA levels through glycolysis, while supplying malonyl-CoA for polyketide synthesis and glutaryl-CoA for carnitine acyltransferase reactions, directly boosting lotosatalin and glutarylcarnitine production. Lotaustralin has been shown to release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) and exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity in plant–insect systems (Bjarnholt et al., 2008). Based on this, it is speculated that this component may contribute to the overall antibacterial function of shrimp paste, though further validation in in vivo and in vitro models is required. Glutarylcarnitine, a key intermediate in mitochondrial β-oxidation, has been confirmed in mammalian studies to maintain CoA pool homeostasis and enhance Nrf2-mediated antioxidant capacity (Luo et al., 2022). Therefore, the addition of glucose may indirectly help maintain antioxidant homeostasis in low-salt shrimp paste during fermentation.

Fig. 4(b/c/d) shows hierarchical cluster analysis and VIP score analysis. The left three columns represent samples from each group at 0 days, while the right three columns represent samples from each group at 12 days. Each row corresponds to a metabolite. In Group Y shrimp paste (Fig. 4b), 14 metabolites were upregulated while 36 were downregulated. In Group P shrimp paste (Fig. 4c), 18 metabolites were upregulated and 32 were downregulated. In Group F shrimp paste (Fig. 4d), 16 metabolites were upregulated and 34 were downregulated. As shown in Fig. 4b and c, compared to day 0 of fermentation, day 12 exhibited significant upregulation of oligopeptide content (Phe-Val, Ile-Phe, Phe-Phe) in the shrimp paste group Y and Ile-Phe in group P. Oligopeptides typically exhibit superior absorption rates and biological functions compared to amino acids. Due to their smaller molecular weight, oligopeptides can impart diverse flavors such as sweetness, sourness, saltiness, umami, and bitterness, significantly enriching the sensory characteristics of fermented products (Zhao et al., 2016). Furthermore, many oligopeptides exhibit health-promoting functions, enhancing protein nutritional value and improving functional properties, thereby positively affecting human health (Wan et al., 2025).

As important flavor compounds in aquatic foods, organic acids primarily impart sourness to food (Wang et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 4c, organic acids and their derivatives were significantly upregulated in Group P shrimp paste at day 12 of fermentation. As shown in Fig. 4d, organic acids and their derivatives were also significantly upregulated in Group F shrimp paste at day 12 compared to day 0. During the fermentation of low-salt shrimp paste, acidic compounds not only enhance flavor but also inhibit the growth of undesirable microbial populations. The acidic compounds in shrimp paste primarily originate from two sources: one part comes from the raw material, the red shrimp, and the other from microbial metabolism during fermentation.

Ketones play a crucial role in enhancing the flavor of meat products. Ketone compounds can react with proteins via irreversible covalent bonds (Shen et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. 4b, c, and d, during the fermentation of low-salt shrimp paste, compared to Day 0, the shrimp paste in Group Y on Day 12 primarily contained (2S,3S,3Ar)-2-(2H-1,3-benzodioxol-5-yl)-5-methoxy-3-methyl-3a-(prop-2-en-1-yl)-3,3a-dihydro-1-benzofuran-6(2H)-one and Milbemycin beta1 as the primary differential ketone metabolites; Group P shrimp paste also showed significant upregulation of Milbemycin beta1; In Group F shrimp paste, Epothilone A and (2S,3S,3Ar)-2-(2H-1,3-benzodioxol-5-yl)-5-methoxy-3-methyl-3a-(prop-2-en-1-yl)-3,3a-dihydro-1-benzofuran-6(2H)-one were significantly up-regulated.

Lipids play a crucial role in the nutritional value, sensory characteristics, and flavor development of fermented meat products (Xu et al., 2018). As shown in Fig. 4b, during the fermentation of shrimp paste in Group Y, the content of lipid and lipoid molecules (Armillaripin, PS (22:5(4Z,7Z,10Z,13Z,16Z)/14:1(9Z)), CDP-DG(i-16:0/i-18:0), etc.) was significantly downregulated. As shown in Fig. 4c and d, P group shrimp paste exhibited significantly reduced levels of lipid and lipoid molecules (CDP-DG (18:0/18:1(11Z)), Cinnamoside, Isoeruboside B, etc.), while F group shrimp paste showed significantly reduced levels of lipid and lipoid molecules (Propionylcarnitine, IV2Fuc-nLc4Cer, Armillaripin, etc.).

Higher VIP values indicate greater contributions to group discrimination. VIP analysis revealed extensive degradation of lipids and lipid-like molecules in all three groups. However, Groups P and F showed significant upregulation of oxidized organic compounds, possibly because the additional carbon sources provided sufficient energy for microbial metabolism. Groups Y and F exhibited significant upregulation of phenylpropanoids and polyketides. Phenylpropanoids such as 4-vinylguaiacol can enhance the antioxidant capacity of shrimp paste (Terpinc et al., 2011), while polyketone methyl ketones (e.g., 2-heptanone, 2-nonanone) contribute fruity-floral notes (Huang et al., 2025). Certain polyketone cyclisation products possess conjugated double bond structures. The conjugated double bond polyketone skeleton absorbs orange-red light at low pH (Spirache et al., 2023), partially replacing the coloring effect of nitrites to enhance the brightness and redness of shrimp paste.

Analysis of metabolic pathway enrichment for differentially expressed metabolites during low-salt shrimp paste fermentation

The large number of differentially expressed metabolites in shrimp paste indicates that the fermentation process is highly complex. To identify the metabolic pathways most closely associated with metabolite differences during shrimp paste fermentation, enrichment and topological analyses of differential metabolites were conducted based on the KEGG database. With a significance threshold of p < 0.05, 10, 9 and 10 metabolic pathways were identified in Groups Y, P, and F, respectively, on days 0 and 12 of shrimp paste fermentation (Fig. 5). The three shrimp paste groups shared seven metabolic pathways at p < 0.05: linoleic acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, D-amino acid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, and tryptophan metabolism. This overlap likely stems from their identical fermentation substrates. Linoleic acid metabolism is a crucial mechanism for fatty acid synthesis and degradation, primarily in the liver and muscle. During this process, fatty acids are oxidized into lower-energy linoleic acid, providing cells with necessary energy and supporting various metabolic activities. Glycerophospholipid metabolism is a complex process that converts glycerophospholipids into energy while generating post-processing metabolites. Arachidonic acid serves as the precursor for esterification products formed when glycerophospholipids bind fatty acids at the Sn-2 position. The production of biogenic amines depends on three key factors: First, the presence of corresponding amino acids as precursors is required. Second, microorganisms capable of secreting amino acid decarboxylases must be present (De Cesare et al., 2025). Finally, suitable environmental conditions promote microbial growth, enhance the secretion of amino acid decarboxylases, and increase their activity, thereby effectively facilitating decarboxylation (Bover-Cid et al., 2001). Compared to the control group (Y-group), the P-group shrimp paste differed in that (at p < 0.05) it lacked the arginine and proline metabolism pathways and the lysine degradation pathway. This indicates that adding glucose to shrimp paste helps reduce biogenic amine content. The biosynthesis of cutin, suberine, and waxes in shrimp paste from Group F represents a major metabolic pathway second only to linoleic acid metabolism. Cutin and suberin are hydrophobic lipid biopolyester components that reduce water evaporation and lipid oxidation during late fermentation, thereby maintaining the stability of flavor precursors (Zhu et al., 2023).

As a significant volatile component, alcohols primarily originate from the conversion of unsaturated fatty acids. They play a pivotal role in flavor development, contributing significantly to the distinctive meaty aroma, mushroom-like notes, and a mild oily aroma (Li et al., 2022). Although the sensory thresholds for alcohol compounds are generally high, limiting their direct contribution to the overall flavor profile of shrimp products, these compounds may still interact synergistically with other flavor substances, potentially influencing the final sensory characteristics of the product (Zheng, Zhan, et al., 2025). (E)-Octenol, 2-octen-1-ol, and (Z)-2-octyl-1-ol were the most abundant detected alcohols. (Z)-2-octyl-1-ol exhibits a sweet floral aroma, showing a slight increase at day 12 of shrimp paste fermentation compared to the unfermented state. 3-Hexanol, the second most abundant alcohol, showed an increasing trend across all three shrimp paste fermentation groups, with the most significant increase observed in Group F shrimp paste on day 12 of fermentation.

Hydrocarbons likely originate from the oxidation and degradation of fatty acids, typically presenting aromatic and sweet notes. Research indicates they may serve as key intermediates in the formation of heterocyclic compounds, which play a crucial role in developing meat flavor and exert a significant base effect on meat taste (Zhao et al., 2024). Under specific conditions, alkenes may convert into aldehydes and ketones, which could be potential sources of the fishy odor in shrimp paste. The total relative contents of hydrocarbons in Groups Y, F, and P increased by 36.15%, 39.64%, and 21.74%, respectively. After fermentation, glutinous rice flour can enhance the overall aromatic flavor of shrimp paste, but may also increase the fishy odor at the same time.

The ketones present in shrimp paste originate from the oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids and the degradation of amino acids. These compounds often emit floral and fruity aromas and are a major factor contributing to the cheesy aroma in fish sauce (Han et al., 2024). During fermentation, the 4-heptanone content in Group Y shrimp paste increased from 1.12 ± 0.10 μg/g to 1.33 ± 0.08 μg/g, while that in Group P rose from an initial 1.12 ± 0.14 μg/g to 1.26 ± 0.07 μg/g. In Group F, the content increased from 1.11 ± 0.17 μg/g to 1.57 ± 0.11 μg/g. A significant upward trend was observed before and after fermentation, and by day 12 of fermentation, the relative content in Group F was significantly higher than in Groups Y and P. This indicates that adding glutinous rice flour enhances the sweet and aromatic flavor profile of shrimp paste. The concentration of Methyl α-ionone increased markedly in all groups: from 0.23 ± 0.04 μg/g to 0.45 ± 0.03 μg/g in Group Y, from 0.29 ± 0.06 μg/g to 0.44 ± 0.11 μg/g in Group P, and from 0.25 ± 0.06 μg/g to 0.54 ± 0.06 μg/g in Group F. This indicates that adding glucose during fermentation enhances the expression of floral and fruity aromas in shrimp paste.

During the fermentation of shrimp paste, 12 heterocyclic compounds have been detected, among which pyrazine compounds are closely associated with the roasted and nutty flavor characteristics. At day 12 of fermentation, the relative contents of isobutylmethoxypyrazine in groups Y, P, and F were 9.41 ± 0.38 μg/g, 7.45 ± 0.34 μg/g, and 10.80 ± 2.86 μg/g, respectively. The relative content in the shrimp paste from group F is significantly higher than in the other two groups, possibly due to the addition of glutinous rice flour.

Most ester compounds exhibit pronounced fruity and floral aromas. Esters mitigate the pungent taste of fatty acids and the bitterness of amino acids. During shrimp paste fermentation, ethyl benzoate increased from 0.18 ± 0.03 μg/g to 0.34 ± 0.04 μg/g in Group Y, from 0.21 ± 0.06 μg/g to 0.33 ± 0.05 μg/g in Group P, while in Group F, it increased from 0.15 ± 0.04 μg/g to 0.31 ± 0.04 μg/g. Ethyl ester compounds impart fruity and creamy aromas to fermented products and play a role in shaping shrimp paste flavor. The relative content of 2-hydroxybenzoate in shrimp paste from Group Y increased to 0.71 ± 0.22 μg/g by the 12th day of fermentation, while that in Group P increased to 0.68 ± 0.20 μg/g and in Group F to 0.98 ± 0.68 μg/g. This difference stems from varying carbon sources affecting microbial ester synthesis capacity during fermentation.

The aldehydes detected were E-2-octenal and 4-pentylbenzaldehyde, which are associated with yeast metabolites produced during fermentation. The addition of a carbon source had little effect on the levels of these aldehydes. They primarily originate from the oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids such as linoleic acid and linolenic acid, and may also be generated through the Strecker degradation of amino acids (Xiao et al., 2024). They play a significant role in enhancing shrimp paste flavor, typically imparting pleasant aromas reminiscent of grass, malt, fruit, and cheese. Overall, shrimp paste made with added carbon sources contains a richer variety of flavor compounds than shrimp paste made without them.

The principal component contributions of PC1 and PC2 were 54.51% and 10.98%, respectively. At day 0, the samples clustered tightly, indicating high similarity in volatile profiles. At day 12, the samples separated along PC1, suggesting that significant changes in volatile compounds occurred during fermentation. Fig. 6c presents the PCA analysis of the control group and Group F at day 12 of fermentation. The principal component contribution rates for PC1 and PC2 were 54.09% and 20.79%, respectively. The samples from Groups Y and F were distinctly distributed on opposite sides of the positive and negative axes along the PC2 dimension. The PCA analysis results for the fermented shrimp pastes from the two groups showed differences, likely due to the addition of glutinous rice flour affecting microbial growth and metabolism, resulting in variations in the types and levels of volatile flavor compounds in the pastes.

Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) effectively analyzes variables with weaker correlations, providing more comprehensive results. As a supervised multivariate statistical method, PLS-DA more effectively highlights intergroup differences than PCA, aiding in the identification of metabolites that differ significantly. In Fig. 6, a greater distance between two samples indicates larger differences between them, and vice versa. Fig. 6d, f, h, j, and l present the OPLS-DA score plots for five shrimp paste groups: Y-0 vs Y-12, P-0 vs P-12, F-0 vs F-12, P-12 vs Y-12, and F-12 vs Y-12. Fig. 6e, g, i, k, m display the corresponding OPLS-DA validation plots for each group. Fig. 6d, f, and h reveal that samples from the three shrimp paste groups at fermentation day 0 and day 12 are distinctly distributed on opposite sides of the positive and negative axes, indicating significant differences. This suggests that certain specific flavor compounds in shrimp paste undergo changes as fermentation time increases. Fig. 6j and l compare the P and F groups at day 12 with the control group (Y group) at day 12. The greater distance between points indicates analytical variability, suggesting that adding glucose and glutinous rice flour alters volatile compounds in low-salt shrimp paste. An OPLS-DA was used to construct a sample discrimination model. The model parameters show that the R2X values for each group range from 0.506 to 0.829, R2Y was greater than 0.994, and Q2 ranged from 0.702 to 0.973, meeting the validity threshold of R2 and Q2 > 0.5. To rule out the risk of model overfitting, 200 permutation tests were conducted to verify the reliability of the models (Fig. 6e, g, i, k, m). The results of the permutation tests indicate that the R2Y and Q2 values of all models generated by random permutations were significantly lower than those of the original OPLS-DA model; no random model had a Q2 value exceeding that of the original model. The permutation test yielded p < 0.005 for all groups. Collectively, these results demonstrated that the established OPLS-DA models were free of overfitting and possessed satisfactory discrimination and predictive capacity for characterizing intergroup differences.

Conclusion

The addition of exogenous carbon sources was associated with lower relative salt content, greater protein hydrolysis, and less accumulation of major biogenic amines, including tyramine and cadaverine. Metabolomics analysis showed that organic acids and their derivatives, lipids and lipid-like molecules, and organic heterocyclic compounds were the main non-volatile metabolites among the top 20 differentially expressed compounds. Seven metabolic pathways were common to all three groups and appeared to form the core framework for material transformation and energy supply during fermentation. The carbon-supplemented groups showed additional pathway features that may relate to biogenic amine metabolism and flavor precursor stability. Both carbon sources were linked to greater aromatic complexity and higher sensory acceptability in the final products. Glucose-supplemented samples exhibited enhanced sweet, minty, fruity, honey, and caramel notes, whereas glutinous rice flour showed a stronger association with sweet, fruity, herbal, and coconut notes. Future work is needed to uncover the mechanisms behind these effects and to examine how varying addition ratios influence flavor development and storage stability. Such studies would help guide the formulation optimization of low-salt shrimp paste for industrial production.
