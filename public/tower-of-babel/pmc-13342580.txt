SSD-DIS: A Semi-Synthetic Shadow Dataset for Document Images
Authors: Bingshu Wang, Jia Li, Ze Wang, Aihong Shangguan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With rising demand for remote work and education, smartphones and other portable photographic devices are increasingly used to capture physical documents, which are then shared as electronic files. However, shadows in such images hinder reading. Currently available shadow removal datasets exhibit certain limitations. This paper creates a semi-synthetic dataset (SSD-DIS) with 12,224 image sets. Using Blender for shadow masks, multi-source shadow-free images, and adjusted shadow intensity/color, it simulates real-world shadow scenarios. Experiments show SSD-DIS enhances neural networks’ learning of document shadow features; models trained on it outperform those using traditional datasets, supporting document shadow removal algorithm research.

Background & Summary

Documents play an important role as an integral part of our daily lives. In today’s digital era, the importance of digital documents is becoming more and more significant in order to meet the needs of modern scenarios such as telecommuting and digital learning. In the past, people mainly used scanners to access high-quality digitized documents. However, with the popularity of portable imaging devices, more and more people are choosing to use devices such as smartphones to capture document images. While this shift has brought convenience, it has also made captured document images more susceptible to outside interference. Compared to traditional scanners, these portable devices often result in uneven shadows on captured document images when the light source is blocked by the device or the user’s hand, or is affected by uneven lighting in real-world environments. These shadows not only affect the image quality, but also may cause obstacles to reading, optical character recognition and other application scenarios1–3. Although several datasets have been proposed for shadow removal in natural images4,5, specialized resources for document shadows remain limited, and directly applying these generic datasets to document images often yields suboptimal results due to domain discrepancies. Therefore, how to remove shadows from document images and improve the quality of document images has become a much-needed research topic in the field of computer vision6–8.

Data-driven deep learning methods have become the mainstream approach to address this problem, where high-quality training datasets serve as the cornerstone for achieving desirable outcomes. In the field of document shadow removal, publicly available benchmark datasets are crucial for advancing algorithm development. However, existing mainstream datasets exhibit inherent limitations in their construction methodologies, which may compromise the models’ generalization capability in real-world scenarios9. Early research laid an important foundation for the document shadow removal task, but the datasets constructed were typically limited in scale. For instance, the datasets such as Bako, Jung, Shah, Kligler and OSR contain a relatively small number of samples and cover only a narrow range of document types and shadow scenarios. This limited scope can significantly impact network performance10. Training on data with such constrained scale and diversity makes it difficult for models to adequately learn complex and variable shadow features, often leading to overfitting and consequently restricting their generalization capability to new scenes and content. Therefore, constructing a large-scale, high-quality benchmark dataset is crucial for training robust and generalizable document shadow removal models.

The recent emergence of large-scale synthetic datasets has, to some extent, addressed the issue of data quantity but still exhibits notable drawbacks. For example, the FSDSRD dataset is large in scale, yet its document content is entirely synthetically generated using computer graphics methods. This approach introduces a potential issue: a significant number of samples in the dataset are derived from the same virtual document base, resulting in highly repetitive text content and layout. When trained on such data, models may implicitly memorize specific content patterns rather than learning general shadow removal features, leading to performance degradation when confronted with novel and diverse real documents. Similarly, although the SDSRD dataset comprises over 8,000 image sets, its original shadow-free document base originates from only 970 distinct synthetic templates, posing a similar “content memorization” risk.

The strength of the RDD dataset lies in its images being entirely captured from real scenes, ensuring authenticity and diversity in document content. However, this dataset does not provide corresponding shadow masks. Shadow masks are crucial for the training of supervised learning models and serve as key references for evaluating shadow detection and removal accuracy. This absence limits its application in advanced algorithm research that requires precise supervisory signals.

In summary, the limitations of existing document shadow removal datasets may hinder the advancement of removal methods in this field. To overcome the aforementioned limitations, this paper adopts a semi-synthetic approach to construct a new dataset, named SSD-DIS (A Semi-synthetic Shadow Dataset for Document Images). The dataset comprises 12,224 sets of shadowed document images, of which 7,334 sets are selected as the training set, 2,444 sets as the validation set, and the remaining 2,446 sets as the test set. Each set of images includes document images with shadows, corresponding non-shadowed document images, and shadow masks. By introducing the SSD-DIS dataset, we are able to significantly increase the size of the training data, providing richer and more diverse samples for the neural network. This not only helps to improve the generalization ability of the model, but also allows us to train more complex and robust neural network structures, thus improving the performance of the document image shadow removal task. This innovative dataset construction method provides richer data support for research in the field of document image shadow removal and lays a solid foundation for future research and applications.

To systematically evaluate the effectiveness of SSD-DIS as a training resource, we designed a fair benchmark evaluation: by keeping the model architecture constant and only swapping the training data, we directly measure the impact of different datasets on model performance. Quantitative and qualitative analyses on public benchmarks such as Jung demonstrate that training with SSD-DIS can significantly and consistently enhance the shadow removal performance of diverse models. This independently validates the intrinsic value of our dataset in improving model generalization capability. We believe that the release of SSD-DIS will provide the community with a high-quality benchmark and facilitate subsequent research in the field of document image processing.

Methods

Training document image shadow removal models requires precisely paired sets of shadowed and shadow-free images. Although collecting such real paired data in controlled environments is feasible, the process is typically time-consuming, labor-intensive, and struggles to achieve sufficient scale and diversity across dimensions such as document content, lighting conditions, and shadow complexity. Given that current physics-based rendering techniques can synthesize shadows with high fidelity11, this study proposes a semi-synthetic framework for constructing the large-scale document shadow removal dataset SSD-DIS.

The overall construction process of the SSD-DIS dataset is shown in Fig. 1. During the construction process, a series of well-designed steps were taken to ensure that the generated document images were both diverse and close to the real scene, including three main steps: shadow mask generation, non-shadowed document image collection, and resizing and shadow attachment. Specifically, the curated shadow masks undergo scale adjustment and intensity modulation before being applied to the shadow-free document base images to synthesize the final shadowed document images.Fig. 1Flowchart of the SSD-DIS dataset construction process.

Shadow mask generation

To construct data that can effectively train models to understand the physical properties of shadows in the real world, we employed physically based rendered shadow masks. Unlike techniques that rely on simple 2D image processing (e.g., alpha blending), we utilized high-quality shadow masks generated using the open-source 3D creation suite, Blender, sourced from the publicly available dataset by Matsuo11. The core motivation for this choice is that Blender provides comprehensive 3D scene construction and physically based ray-tracing capabilities. This allows for the precise simulation of complex shadow phenomena arising from light-object interactions—specifically, effects like penumbra and variations in intensity/color temperature caused by indirect lighting or multiple light sources, which are difficult to replicate through 2D means. This ensures that the utilized shadows are consistent with natural shadows in terms of both geometric and photometric properties.

The mask generation was a procedural and parameterized process where a three-dimensional scene was constructed in Blender. This process involved creating a set of occluder objects and a receiving plane representing the document surface. Then, one or more light sources, simulating lamps of varying sizes, positions, and intensities, were configured. To adapt these raw materials for our specific document shadow removal task and address empirical observations of real-world photography, a key step involved the systematic curation and filtering of these masks based on parameters—including the occluders’ shapes and positions, as well as the light source properties—within plausible ranges. Each selected mask corresponds to a unique 3D scene rendered using Blender’s Cycles rendering engine. By calculating light paths, this engine produced high-resolution grayscale images containing accurate shadow information, namely the raw shadow masks (Maskshadow). The brightness value of each pixel in these masks represents the degree of shadow attenuation at that point, thereby naturally capturing the continuous transition from umbra to penumbra. In designing the occluder shapes, we adopted a strategy that balances realism with generalization by harmonizing common scenarios with morphological diversity. While real-world document shadows typically originate from hands, writing instruments, or electronic devices, we intentionally introduced parameterized random shapes alongside simulated common objects. This design choice aims to expand the distribution of shadow geometries, thereby compelling deep learning models to learn universal physical representations of shadows rather than overfitting to specific occluder contours. By encompassing a wide spectrum of occlusion patterns, the dataset enhances the model’s capability to generalize to complex, previously unseen shadow morphologies in diverse real-world environments.

Collection of shadow-free document images

The document image base (Ifree) of this dataset was sourced from two complementary streams to ensure both diversity and legal compliance: self-captured photography and web crawling of public resources. The majority of the images were obtained by high-definition photography of various paper-based documents using a smartphone in an indoor environment with uniform, diffuse lighting, guaranteeing the high quality and shadow-free nature of the original images. To further expand the diversity of document types, we systematically crawled a collection of document images from open platforms under Creative Commons licenses, using search engines with keywords including “document scan” and “textbook page”.

To construct a high-quality and ethically sound dataset base, we implemented a rigorous multi-level manual and automated screening pipeline for all collected shadow-free document images. The screening criteria were applied sequentially. First, based on clarity, all images exhibiting severe blurring, motion artifacts, or out-of-focus issues were removed to ensure superior visual quality. Second, to safeguard the dataset’s diversity, we consciously curated images to cover a wide range of document types, including plain text documents, documents containing both text and graphics, multilingual documents such as Chinese and English, handwritten documents, and documents with various printed layouts. Third, given that these images serve as the base for shadow synthesis, we required the primary document area to be free of obvious shadows, uneven illumination, or strong specular reflections. Finally, and most importantly, we conducted a thorough legal and ethical compliance review. All images containing any personally identifiable information—such as faces, identification numbers, home addresses, handwritten signatures, or phone numbers—were manually removed. This process ensures the complete anonymization of all data, aligning with established academic research ethics standards.

Resizing and shadow attachment

Considering the size difference of different document images, it was necessary to adjust the size scaling of the shadow mask to ensure the smoothness of the subsequent shadow attachment process. In order to simulate the different intensities of document shadows in the real scene, this paper introduced Seedintensity as a random shadow intensity seed, which made the shadow intensity attached to each document image have a certain degree of dynamics, and further increased the diversity of the dataset. Based on several experimental analyses, this paper concluded that limiting the shadow intensity values to the range of (0.15, 0.8) would better match the human eye’s perception of shadow intensity. As shown in Fig. 2, too low or too high an intensity value resulted in an image that was too dark or overexposed, which did not correspond to real shadows attached to the document. In order to avoid image color casting and to ensure that the resulting document image with shadows was more natural and realistic in color, the RGB color channel of the document image was processed independently. Dataset features

Through the aforementioned pipeline, we constructed the SSD-DIS dataset comprising 12,224 rigorously paired image sets, designed to address the limitations of existing data in terms of scale, authenticity, and generalization capability. The core contributions and salient advantages of this dataset are manifested in the following four aspects:

First, guaranteed scale and diversity. The dataset provides over ten thousand paired samples, establishing a necessary data foundation for training modern deep networks. More importantly, its diversity stems from authentic and varied document image bases, fundamentally avoiding the “content repetition” problem common in fully synthetic data. This compels models to learn generalizable features rather than memorizing specific templates.

Second, strong authenticity of data images. The authenticity of this dataset is reflected in two aspects. It employs a wide range of genuine shadow-free document images as the synthesis base, ensuring that the text, layout, and textures adhere to real-world distributions. Furthermore, it utilizes 3D software to generate shadows and their corresponding masks by simulating the interaction between light and occluders, capable of accurately reproducing the intensity gradients and soft/hard edges found in natural shadows. The combination of an authentic base and a physics-based synthesis method significantly enhances the data fidelity and the model’s potential for generalization.

Third, high annotation precision and informational richness. The dataset provides continuous-value shadow masks, as opposed to traditional binary 0-1 masks. Each pixel value represents the precise degree of shadow attenuation, capable of depicting the smooth transition from the shadow core to shadow-free areas. This provides models with finer supervisory signals, enabling them to learn the complex physical properties of shadows. Consequently, it holds the potential for generating removal results with more natural visual effects and smoother boundary transitions.

Fourth, pronounced openness and extensibility. We have not only made the complete paired dataset publicly available but have also open-sourced the shadow masks and high-quality synthesis code. This offers a dual key value to the research community: firstly, developers can directly use the high-quality masks to train their models; secondly, subsequent researchers can easily utilize our masks and synthesis code to combine them with any shadow-free document images, rapidly generating new, customized training data. This significantly promotes the reproducibility, comparability, and sustained innovation of research in this field.

In summary, the SSD-DIS dataset, through its large-scale authentic base, physically-accurate 3D shadow generation, high-precision continuous-value annotation, and complete open-source protocol, provides a new resource for the field of document shadow removal that combines high quality, strong generalizability, and high practical utility. Its release is poised to facilitate the development of more robust and generalizable next-generation shadow removal algorithms.

Data Records

The SSD-DIS12 dataset comprises a total of 12,224 uniquely paired samples. The dataset has been shared via figshare (link: 10.6084/m9.figshare.29401811) and Baidu Netdisk (link: https://pan.baidu.com/s/5ZmiPhoGZz1kqI0KxFl93aQ).

It is organized into three core directories and one README file: shadow_free_images contains the clean, shadow-free document images used as the synthesis base; shadow_masks contains the corresponding continuous-value shadow masks; shadow_images contains the final synthesized document images with shadows; and README.md provides detailed documentation of the dataset, including the file structure, correspondence rules, generation methodology, and license information.

The files from the three image directories are paired by matching identifiers. To illustrate, a sample set is formed by grouping the files with the same ID, such as XYH00002.jpg, from the “shadow_free_images/train”, “shadow_masks/train”, and “shadow_images/train” directories. This structure ensures perfect alignment during both the training and evaluation phases.

Technical Validation

In this section, we employ the widely used document shadow removal algorithm, BGShadowNet13, as a baseline model to validate both the utility and the challenging nature of the SSD-DIS dataset. The experimental design consists of two parts: first, augmenting existing datasets with SSD-DIS for training to verify improvements in model generalization; and second, evaluating models trained on various data sources against the SSD-DIS test set to demonstrate the dataset’s uniqueness and difficulty. This section introduces and analyzes the experimental platform, implementation details, evaluation metrics, and experimental results.

Experimental platform

All experiments were conducted on a desktop workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7-12700K CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24GB memory). The software environment included the Windows 11 operating system, Python 3.7, PyTorch 1.8.0, and CUDA 11.1.

Implementation details

We selected BGShadowNet as the representative model. All models were trained from scratch using different training set configurations. The training sets involved include the existing Jung, Kligler, OSR, and RDD datasets, as well as the proposed SSD-DIS dataset.

Evaluation metrics

We employed three widely used metrics to quantitatively evaluate shadow removal performance: Structural Similarity Index (SSIM): This metric accounts for image luminance and structural information. It measures the similarity between two images by comparing their structures; a higher SSIM value indicates better structural preservation. Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR): This metric evaluates the quality of the processed image by comparing it to the original image (Ground Truth). A higher PSNR value indicates less distortion and higher image quality. Root Mean Square Error (RMSE): This metric assesses the level of difference between the original image and the processing result. A lower RMSE value signifies a smaller difference, corresponding to higher fidelity to the ground truth.
