Deep Linear Network Training Dynamics from Random Initialization: Data, Width, Depth, and Hyperparameter Transfer
Blake Bordelon, Cengiz Pehlevan

Abstract
We theoretically characterize gradient descent dynamics in deep linear networks trained at large width from random initialization and on large quantities of random data. Our theory captures the “wider is better” effect of mean-field/maximum-update parameterized networks as well as hyperparameter transfer effects, which can be contrasted with the neural-tangent parameterization where optimal learning rates shift with model width. We provide asymptotic descriptions of both non-residual and residual neural networks, the latter of which enables an infinite depth limit when branches are scaled as 1 / depth 1/\sqrt{\text{depth}} . We also compare training with one-pass stochastic gradient descent to the dynamics when training data are repeated at each iteration. Lastly, we show that this model recovers the accelerated power law training dynamics for power law structured data in the rich regime observed in recent works.

Introduction

Understanding the training dynamics and scaling properties of neural networks is critical to deriving parameterizations and optimizers that achieve stable dynamics across model sizes
(
Yang et al., 2021
;
Bordelon et al., 2024c
;
Yang et al., 2023
;
Vankadara et al., 2024
;
Everett et al., 2024
)
, which in turn lead to significant computational savings in hyperparameter search via optimal hyperparameter transfer from smaller to larger sized models
(
Achiam et al., 2023
)
. However, characterizing the training dynamics of neural networks is generally complicated due to nonlinear dynamics that depend on the parameterization of the network, the weight initialization strategy, optimizer, and data structure.

Despite the complexity of the dynamics induced by gradient descent from random initial conditions, significant theoretical insight into deep network training can be gained by studying tractable limits (large width or large depth networks) or specialized architectures (such as deep linear networks) or the behavior of training under specific data distributions (such as random isotropic data). While the literature on various special cases of training contains numerous works which we review in Section
1.1
below, a theoretical model that can capture simultaneously the effect of varying width, depth, richness, data quantity on the learning dynamics in randomly initialized networks is missing. Of specific interest is that no existing theory can theoretically characterize the two effects necessary for successful hyperparameter transfer
(
Yang et al., 2021
)
: (1) monotonic improvement in performance with model width or model depth and (2) approximately consistent optimal learning rates when appropriately parameterizing the model and optimizer.

Is there a simple theoretical model of deep networks that captures the influence of random initialization, network parameterization, width, and depth in order to characterize the hyperparameter transfer effect?

In this work, we provide such a theory, though for a limited model class of deep linear networks, by characterizing the training dynamics from random initialization on random data which captures both the effect of network width, depth, dataset size, and feature learning strength (richness). Concretely our contributions are

•

We develop a novel dynamical mean field theory (DMFT) which is specific for deep linear neural networks that enables a prediction of typical case loss and representation trajectories over both initial weights and random data, or stochastic gradient descent (SGD) samples, as the disorder. This theory completely characterizes the train and test loss dynamics in terms of a closed set of equations for correlation and response functions at each hidden layer of the network.

•

We show that our theory provides a minimal model that captures the impact of parameterization on training dynamics. In particular, our results capture both the “wider is better” effect of deep networks throughout training as well as the hyperparameter transfer effect for networks in
\mu
P. Conversely, our theory captures that wider networks can train more slowly and hyperparameters can fail to transfer in NTK parameterization.

•

We extend our theory to provide a solution to the training dynamics of deep
residual networks
, enabling the study of the large depth limit, though in a tractable setting. In this setting, we reproduce that hyperparameter transfer across depths by scaling the residual branch by
1/\sqrt{\text{depth}}
, consistent with prior works.

•

We also show that our theory can capture the behavior of linear networks trained on structured data, such as power-law covariance eigenvalues. This results in power law scaling of the loss with training iterations, though with improved exponents compared to lazy learning, consistent with recent findings in the shallow case
(
Bordelon et al., 2024b
)
.

\displaystyle\bm{v}(t)=\bm{w}^{\star}-\frac{\sqrt{D}}{N\gamma_{0}}\bm{W}^{0}(t)^{\top}\bm{g}^{1}(t).

\displaystyle h^{\ell}(t)=u^{\ell}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[R^{\ell-1}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C^{\ell-1}_{h}(t,t^{\prime})\right]g^{\ell}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle g^{\ell}(t)=r^{\ell}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[R^{\ell+1}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C^{\ell+1}_{g}(t,t^{\prime})\right]h^{\ell}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle R^{\ell}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\delta h(t)}{\delta r^{\ell}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R^{\ell}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\delta g^{\ell}(t)}{\delta u^{\ell}(t^{\prime})}\right>.

(12)

Intuitively, these functions measure the response of the variables at time

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{s<t}\sum_{s^{\prime}<t}R_{vr}^{0}(t,s)R^{0}_{vr}(t,s^{\prime})}_{\text{Bias}}+\underbrace{\sigma^{2}}_{\text{Label Noise}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\nu\gamma_{0}^{2}}\sum_{s<t}\sum_{s^{\prime}<t}R_{vr}^{0}(t,s)R^{0}_{vr}(t,s^{\prime})C_{g}^{1}(s,s^{\prime})}_{\text{Limited Width Variance}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{s<t}\sum_{s^{\prime}<t}R_{vu}^{0}(t,s)R_{vu}^{0}(t,s^{\prime})C_{\Delta}(s,s^{\prime})}_{\text{Limited Data Variance}}

(13)

While the focus in the paper so far has been on data drawn from an isotropic distribution and on the proportional limit, we stress that our methods can also characterize the dynamics of training on structured data. For example, suppose that our covariates

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\sim k^{-\alpha}\ ,\ (w^{\star}_{k})^{2}\lambda_{k}\sim k^{-\alpha\beta-1},

Discussion

In this work, we developed an asymptotic mean field description of the training dynamics of randomly initialized deep linear networks trained on random data. The theory was able to capture both vanilla MLP architectures and deep residual neural networks and characterize the impact of parameterization, learning rate, richness parameter, width, depth, dataset size, and batchsize for online SGD. This is the first work to also numerically verify the asymptotic description of training dynamics in infinite width residual networks. We demonstrated that our theory can capture the hyperparameter transfer effect for properly parameterized networks across both widths and depths which have been observed empirically prior works. For structured and trace-class covariates, we modify our theory to approximately capture the impact of finite batchsize
B
and finite width
N
.

Limitations and Future Directions

The current theory, while a signficant improvement in complexity compared to prior works which do not average over the random data (such as
Bordelon & Pehlevan (2022)
;
Bordelon et al. (2024c)
), becomes costly to compute for very deep networks trained for a large number of iterations
T
and very deep networks
L\gg 1
. We summarize the memory and compute requirements to compute our theory in Table
1
. Because of the
\sim L^{2}
number of response functions required to compute our residual network DMFT, the costs are higher than for a standard non-residual network (which is linear in
L
). Our DMFT solver is more efficient than full batch training in a finite width
N
linear ResNet provided that
LT^{2}\ll N^{2}P
. In addition, the function approximation power of this model is quite limited as we are only considering deep linear networks in the present study. However, despite the failure to capture nonlinear function approximation, many of the dynamical effects from initialization, SGD, and parameterization persist. Some future directions that could be analyzed within this framework include characterizing the impact of learning rate schedules and other optimizers on the dynamics, studying the effect of normalization layers, Hessian spectra, and characterizing how nonlinearity changes this theoretical picture.

Appendix A
Derivation of Mean Field Equations

In this section, we provide a mathematical derivation of the DMFT equations that govern the train and test loss dynamics of the deep linear network. We will start by analyzing the non-residual architecture trained with full batch gradient descent and then proceed to study online SGD and the residual network architecture, which involves a larger set of cross-layer response functions in the residual stream.

A.1
Non-Residual Deep Linear Network Trained with Full Batch GD

A.1.1
Isolating Dependence on The Disorder

We start by considering the feedforward depth
L
network with no residual skip connections defined as

\displaystyle f(\bm{x})=\frac{\sqrt{D}}{N\gamma_{0}}(\bm{w}^{L})^{\top}\prod_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{W}^{\ell}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\bm{W}^{0}\right)\bm{x}.

(22)

In this section, we consider weights are updating these weights with gradient descent on the empirical (training) loss

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}(t)=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\left[\Delta_{\mu}(t)\right]^{2}\ ,\ \Delta_{\mu}(t)\equiv y_{\mu}-f(\bm{x}_{\mu})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\bm{w}^{\star}\cdot\bm{x}_{\mu}+\sigma\epsilon_{\mu}-f(\bm{x}_{\mu}).

(23)

The update steps are performed using a raw learning rate of
N\eta\gamma_{0}^{2}
following
Bordelon & Pehlevan (2022)
, which gives

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{L}(t+1)=\bm{w}^{L}(t)-N\eta\gamma_{0}^{2}\nabla_{\bm{w}^{L}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(t)\ ,\ \bm{W}^{\ell}(t+1)=\bm{W}^{\ell}(t)-N\eta\gamma_{0}^{2}\nabla_{\bm{W}^{\ell}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(t)\ ,\ \ell\in\{0,...,L-1\}.

(24)

Using the chain rule at each step
t
, we can decompose the weights at time
t
into their initial values and all of their updates

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{0}(t)=\bm{W}^{0}(0)+\frac{\eta\gamma_{0}}{\sqrt{D}}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\bm{g}^{1}(t^{\prime})\bm{h}^{0}(t^{\prime})^{\top}

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{\ell}(t)=\bm{W}^{\ell}(0)+\frac{\eta\gamma_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\bm{g}^{\ell+1}(t^{\prime})\bm{h}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})^{\top}

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{L}(t)=\bm{w}^{L}(0)+\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\bm{h}^{L}(t^{\prime})

(25)

where the vectors
\bm{g}^{\ell}(t),\bm{h}^{\ell}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
that we introduced defined in the following way

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{0}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{D}}{P}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}(t)\quad,\quad\bm{\Delta}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\bm{X}\bm{v}(t)+\sigma\bm{\epsilon}\quad,\quad\bm{v}(t)=\bm{w}_{\star}-\frac{\sqrt{D}}{N\gamma_{0}}\bm{W}^{0}(t)^{\top}\bm{g}^{1}(t)

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{\ell+1}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{W}^{\ell}(t)\bm{h}^{\ell}(t)\ ,\ \ell\in\{0,1,...,L-1\}

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{L}(t)=\bm{w}^{L}(t)

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{\ell}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{W}^{\ell}(t)^{\top}\bm{g}^{\ell+1}(t)\ ,\ \ell\in\{1,...,L-1\}

(26)

In the above definitions, we have introduced the data matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times D}
and the noise in the target values
\bm{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. We can now isolate the dependence of these vectors on the random initial conditions
\bm{W}^{\ell}(0)
and the updates from gradient descent. Expanding out the weight dynamics, we can close the
\bm{v}(t),\{\bm{h}^{\ell}(t),\bm{g}^{\ell}(t)\}_{\ell\in[L]}
dynamics as

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{\ell+1}(t)=\bm{\chi}^{\ell+1}(t)+\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{h}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{g}^{\ell+1}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{\ell}(t)=\bm{\xi}^{\ell}(t)+\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{g}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{h}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\bm{v}(t)=\bm{w}_{\star}-\frac{\sqrt{D}}{N\gamma_{0}}\bm{W}^{0}(0)^{\top}\bm{g}^{1}(t)-\eta\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{g}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{h}^{0}(t^{\prime})

where we introduced the following correlation functions

\displaystyle C^{\ell}_{h}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{h}^{\ell}(t)\cdot\bm{h}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ C^{\ell}_{g}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{g}^{\ell}(t)\cdot\bm{g}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})

(27)

and the following fields that depend on the initial conditions
\bm{W}^{\ell}(0)

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{0}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{D}}{N\gamma_{0}}\bm{W}^{0}(0)^{\top}\bm{g}^{1}(t)\ ,\ \bm{\chi}^{1}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\bm{W}^{0}(0)\bm{h}^{0}(t)

\displaystyle\bm{\chi}^{\ell+1}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{W}^{\ell}(0)\bm{h}^{\ell}(t)\ ,\ \bm{\xi}^{\ell}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{W}^{\ell}(0)^{\top}\bm{g}^{\ell+1}(t).

(28)

These variables isolate the dependence on all of the random initial conditions for the weights. Combined with the defining equations for the variables
\bm{\Delta}(t),\bm{v}(t),\bm{h}^{0}(t)
, we will now aim to characterize the typical case dynamics over draws of the disorder
\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{X},\bm{\epsilon},\bm{W}^{0}(0),\bm{W}^{1}(0),...,\bm{w}^{L}(0)\}.

(29)

Our goal is to characterize the typical case dynamics of the system over random draws of
\mathcal{D}
.

A.1.2
Computing the Dynamical Distribution Over Draws of the Disorder

Moment Generating Function

We now aim to characterize the joint distribution of
\bm{v}(t),\bm{\Delta}(t),\bm{h}^{0}(t),\{\bm{h}^{\ell}(t),\bm{g}^{\ell}(t)\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
over draws of the random disorder
\mathcal{D}
. To do so, we will start by calculating the moment generating function of these fields with a Martin-Siggia-Rose integral
(
Martin et al., 1973
)
over the trajectories

\displaystyle Z[\bm{j}_{v},\bm{j}_{\Delta},\{\bm{j}_{h^{\ell}},\bm{j}_{g^{\ell}}\}_{\ell\in[L]}]=\left<\exp\left(\sum_{t}\left[\bm{j}_{v}(t)\cdot\bm{v}(t)+\bm{j}_{\Delta}(t)\cdot\bm{\Delta}(t)+\sum_{\ell}\bm{j}_{h^{\ell}}(t)\cdot\bm{h}^{\ell}(t)+\bm{j}_{g^{\ell}}(t)\cdot\bm{g}^{\ell}(t)\right]\right)\right>_{\mathcal{D}}.

(30)

This object, once computed, enables computation of arbitrary moments from derivatives with respect to the
\bm{j}
variables at
\bm{j}=0
. For example, the two-point correlation between the
\bm{v}(t)
variables has the form

\displaystyle\left<v_{i}(t)v_{k}(t^{\prime})\right>_{\mathcal{D}}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial j_{v,i}(t)\partial j_{v,k}(t^{\prime})}Z[\bm{j}_{v},\bm{j}_{\Delta},\{\bm{j}_{h^{\ell}},\bm{j}_{g^{\ell}}\}_{\ell\in[L]}].

(31)

Why No Replicas?

At this point, we stress that the dynamics for our variables
\bm{v}(t),\bm{\Delta}(t),\bm{h}^{\ell}(t),\bm{g}^{\ell}(t)
provide us a
complete and normalized joint distribution
unlike in static (replica) mean field calculations where the distribution of the variables of interest are only known up to a normalization constant (eg, where one must perform a quenched average of an observable
\mathcal{O}(\bm{\theta})
for a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{D})
which depends on disorder
\mathcal{D}
:
\left<\frac{\int d\bm{\theta}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{D})\right)\mathcal{O}(\bm{\theta})}{\int d\bm{\theta}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}(\bm{\theta},\mathcal{D})\right)}\right>_{\mathcal{D}}
where we see the disorder appears in both numerator and denominator). This form of average is usually tackled with the replica method. In our case, this is not necessary since
Z
directly captures the distribution of our trajectories over draws of disorder
\mathcal{D}
.

\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{h}^{0}(t)\delta\left(\bm{h}^{0}(t)-\frac{\sqrt{D}}{P}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}(t)\right)=\int\frac{d\bm{h}^{0}(t)d\hat{\bm{h}}^{0}(t)}{(2\pi)^{D}}\exp\left(i\hat{\bm{h}}^{0}(t)\cdot\left[\bm{h}^{0}(t)-\frac{\sqrt{D}}{P}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\Delta}(t)\right]\right)

(32)

(47)

where the relevant order parameters are

\displaystyle C_{h}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{h}^{\ell}(t)\cdot\bm{h}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ C_{g}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\bm{g}^{\ell+1}(t)\cdot\bm{g}^{\ell+1}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle R^{\ell}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv-\frac{i}{N}\bm{h}^{\ell}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{\xi}}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ R^{\ell+1}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv-\frac{i}{N}\bm{g}^{\ell+1}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{\chi}}^{\ell+1}(t^{\prime})

(48)

As before, we introduce these order parameters with dual Fourier variables.

Proportional Scaling Regime

We notice that many of the terms that arise in the integrals depend on ratios such as
\frac{N}{D}
and
\frac{P}{D}
. To deal with these terms we introduce definitions for these ratios

\displaystyle\alpha\equiv\frac{P}{D}\ ,\ \nu\equiv\frac{N}{D}.

(49)

One can interpret, at fixed
D
, as
\alpha
and
\nu
representing an increase in the quantity of data or the width of the network respectively. With these constants
\alpha,\nu
introduced, we can now consider how the moment generating function
Z
behaves as
D\to\infty
. However, before this, we must organize and simplify the remaining integrals to enable taking the
D\to\infty
limit.

Decoupling The Single Site Integrals

Now we aim to express the integrals over the variables
\bm{\Delta}(t),\bm{v}(t),\bm{\chi}^{\ell}(t),\bm{\xi}^{\ell}(t)
and their conjugates

\displaystyle\int\prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{d\bm{\Delta}(t)d\hat{\bm{\Delta}}(t)}{(2\pi)^{P}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\bm{\Delta}}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{\Delta}}(t^{\prime})C_{v}(t,t^{\prime})+i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\bm{\Delta}}(t)\cdot\left(\bm{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})-R^{0}_{vu}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{t}\bm{j}_{\Delta}(t)\cdot\bm{\Delta}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{C}_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{\Delta}(t)\cdot\bm{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\mu=1}^{P}\mathcal{Z}_{\Delta}[C_{v},\hat{C}_{\Delta},R^{0}_{vu},j_{\Delta,\mu}(t)]

(50)

where we introduced the
single site moment generating function
for the
\Delta(t)
fields that has the form of an integral over a single entry (scalar stochastic process) of the original
\bm{\Delta}(t)
vector

\displaystyle Z_{\Delta}=\int\prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{d\Delta(t)d\hat{\Delta}(t)}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\Delta}(t)\hat{\Delta}(t^{\prime})C_{v}(t,t^{\prime})+i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\Delta}(t)\left(\Delta(t^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})-R^{0}_{vu}(t,t^{\prime})\Delta(t^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(\sum_{t}j_{\Delta}(t)\Delta(t)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{C}_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})\Delta(t)\Delta(t^{\prime})\right)

(51)

From our disorder average, we also see that there is statistical coupling between
\bm{v}(t),\bm{\xi}^{0}(t)
and
\bm{h}^{0}(t)
variables. We let the joint moment generating function for this set of variables be
\mathcal{Z}_{0}
(for layer
\ell=0
), and express it as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{0}=\int\prod_{t=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{dv(t)d\xi^{0}(t)d\hat{\xi}^{0}(t)dh^{0}(t)d\hat{h}^{0}(t)}{4\pi^{2}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{h}^{0}(t)\hat{h}^{0}(t^{\prime})C_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2\gamma_{0}^{2}\nu}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\xi}^{0}(t)\hat{\xi}^{0}(t^{\prime})C_{g}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{h}^{0}(t)\left(\delta(t-t^{\prime})h^{0}(t^{\prime})-R_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})v(t^{\prime})\right)+\sum_{t}j_{h^{0}}(t)h^{0}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\xi}^{0}(t)\left(\delta(t-t^{\prime})\xi^{0}(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}R_{gu}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})h^{0}(t^{\prime})\right)+\sum_{t}j_{v}(t)v(t)\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{t}\delta\left(v(t)-w^{\star}+\xi^{0}(t)+\eta\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{g}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})h^{0}(t^{\prime})\right)

(52)

For the first hidden layer (
\ell=1
), we have the following joint single site MGF

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\int\prod_{t}

\displaystyle\frac{d\chi^{1}(t)d\hat{\chi}^{1}(t)d\xi^{1}(t)d\hat{\xi}^{1}(t)dh^{1}(t)dg^{1}(t)}{4\pi^{2}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\chi}^{1}(t)\hat{\chi}^{1}(t^{\prime})C_{h}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\xi}^{1}(t)\hat{\xi}^{1}(t^{\prime})C_{g}^{2}(t,t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\chi}^{1}(t)\left(\delta(t-t^{\prime})\chi^{1}(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\nu\gamma_{0}}R^{0}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})g^{1}(t^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\xi}^{1}(t)\left(\delta(t-t^{\prime})\xi^{1}(t^{\prime})-R^{2}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})h^{1}(t^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{t}\left[j_{h^{1}}(t)h^{1}(t)+j_{g^{1}}(t)g^{1}(t)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{t}\delta\left(h^{1}(t)-\chi^{1}(t)-\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{h}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})g^{1}(t^{\prime})\right)\delta\left(g^{1}(t)-\xi^{1}(t)-\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{g}^{2}(t,t^{\prime})h^{1}(t^{\prime})\right)

(53)

For the hidden layers
\ell\in\{2,...,L-1\}
we have the following single site MGFs

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}=\int\prod_{t}

\displaystyle\frac{d\chi^{\ell}(t)d\hat{\chi}^{\ell}(t)d\xi^{\ell}(t)d\hat{\xi}^{\ell}(t)dh^{\ell}(t)dg^{\ell}(t)}{4\pi^{2}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\chi}^{\ell}(t)\hat{\chi}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})C_{h}^{\ell-1}(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\xi}^{\ell}(t)\hat{\xi}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})C_{g}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\chi}^{\ell}(t)\left(\delta(t-t^{\prime})\chi^{\ell}(t^{\prime})-R^{\ell-1}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})g^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\xi}^{\ell}(t)\left(\delta(t-t^{\prime})\xi^{\ell}(t^{\prime})-R^{\ell+1}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})h^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{t}\left[j_{h^{\ell}}(t)h^{\ell}(t)+j_{g^{\ell}}(t)g^{\ell}(t)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{t}\delta\left(h^{\ell}(t)-\chi^{\ell}(t)-\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{h}^{\ell-1}(t,t^{\prime})g^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\right)\delta\left(g^{\ell}(t)-\xi^{\ell}(t)-\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{g}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})h^{1}(t^{\prime})\right)

(54)

The final layer
\ell=L
has the following single site MGF

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{L}=\int

\displaystyle\frac{dh^{L}(t)d\hat{\chi}^{L}(t)d\chi^{L}(t)dg^{L}(t)}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\chi}^{L}(t)\hat{\chi}^{L}(t^{\prime})C^{L-1}_{h}(t,t^{\prime})+i\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\chi}^{L}(t)\left(\chi^{L}(t^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})-R^{L-1}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})g^{L}(t^{\prime})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{t}\delta\left(h^{L}(t)-\chi^{L}(t)-\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{h}^{L-1}(t,t^{\prime})g^{L}(t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\left<\prod_{t}\delta\left(g^{L}(t)-g^{L}(0)-\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}h^{L}(t^{\prime})\right)\right>_{g^{L}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{t}\left[j_{h^{L}}(t)h^{L}(t)+j_{g^{L}}(t)g^{L}(t)\right]\right)

(55)

Now we have reduced all of the field path integrals into single site moment generating functions.

DMFT Action

We now group all of the correlation and response functions, as well as their conjugate order parameters into a list
\bm{q}
and combine all of the source variables into a list
\bm{j}
. The moment generating function can be written as

\displaystyle Z[\bm{j}]=\int d\bm{q}\exp\left(-D\mathcal{S}[\bm{q},\bm{j}]\right)

(56)

where
\mathcal{S}
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
DMFT action which has the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\left[C_{v}(t,t^{\prime})\hat{C}_{v}(t,t^{\prime})+\alpha C_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})\hat{C}_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})\right]-\frac{\nu}{2}\sum_{\ell}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\left[C_{h^{\ell}}(t,t^{\prime})\hat{C}_{h^{\ell}}(t,t^{\prime})+C_{g}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})\hat{C}_{g}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{tt^{\prime}}R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})R_{\Delta}(t^{\prime},t)+\gamma_{0}^{-1}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}R_{hr}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})R_{gu}^{1}(t^{\prime},t)+\nu\sum_{\ell}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}R_{hr}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})R_{gu}^{\ell+1}(t^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{D}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\ln\mathcal{Z}_{\Delta}[j_{\Delta,\mu}]-\frac{1}{D}\sum_{k=1}^{D}\mathcal{Z}_{0}[j_{h^{0},k},j_{v,k}]-\frac{1}{D}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}[j_{h^{\ell},i},j_{g^{\ell},i}]

(57)

Saddle Point Equations

We are now in a position to take the
D\to\infty
limit. In this limit, the integral over
\bm{q}
is dominated by the saddle point of
\mathcal{S}
. The resulting saddle point equations
\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial\bm{q}}=0
. We start by differentiating with respect to the conjugate order parameters for the correlation functions which give

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial\hat{C}_{v}(t,t^{\prime})}=-\frac{1}{2}C_{v}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{k=1}^{D}\left<v(t)v(t^{\prime})\right>_{k}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial\hat{C}_{h}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})}=-\frac{1}{2}C_{h}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{k=1}^{D}\left<h^{0}(t)h^{0}(t^{\prime})\right>_{k}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial\hat{C}_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}C_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\left<\Delta(t)\Delta(t^{\prime})\right>_{\mu}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial\hat{C}_{h}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})}=-\frac{\nu}{2}C_{h}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left<h^{\ell}(t)h^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\right>_{i}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial\hat{C}_{g}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})}=-\frac{\nu}{2}C_{h}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left<g^{\ell}(t)g^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\right>_{i}=0

(58)

where
\left<\right>_{\mu}
represents an average over the single site distribution defined by the moment generating function
\mathcal{Z}_{\Delta}[j_{\Delta,\mu}]
. Similarly
\left<\right>_{k},\left<\right>_{i}
denote averages over the distributions defined by the single site moment generating functions
\mathcal{Z}_{0}[j_{k}],\{\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}[j_{i}]\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
. We note that in the limit of zero source
\bm{j}\to 0
these averages all become identical (we will examine this zero source limit shortly). Next, we can work out the saddle point equations for the response functions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial R_{vu}^{0}(t^{\prime},t)}=R_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{i}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\left<\Delta(t)\hat{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\right>_{\mu}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial R_{\Delta}(t^{\prime},t)}=R_{vu}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{i}{D}\sum_{k=1}^{D}\left<v(t)\hat{h}^{0}(t^{\prime})\right>_{k}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial R_{hr}^{0}(t^{\prime},t)}=\gamma_{0}^{-1}R^{1}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{i}{N\gamma_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left<g^{1}(t)\hat{\xi}^{1}(t^{\prime})\right>_{i}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial R_{gu}^{1}(t^{\prime},t)}=\gamma_{0}^{-1}R^{0}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{i}{D\gamma_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{D}\left<h^{0}(t)\hat{\chi}^{0}(t^{\prime})\right>_{k}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial R_{hr}^{\ell}(t^{\prime},t)}=\nu R^{\ell+1}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{i}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left<g^{\ell+1}(t)\hat{\xi}^{\ell+1}(t^{\prime})\right>_{i}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial R_{gu}^{\ell+1}(t^{\prime},t)}=\nu R^{\ell}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{i}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left<h^{\ell}(t)\hat{\chi}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\right>_{i}=0

(59)

Lastly, we have a collection of saddle point equations
\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial C}=0
that define the conjugate order parameters
\hat{C}=0
which must vanish at the saddle point
(
Crisanti & Sompolinsky, 2018
;
Mignacco et al., 2020
;
Bordelon & Pehlevan, 2022
)
. Thus we have the following remaining set of equations

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})=0\ ,\ \hat{C}_{v}(t,t^{\prime})=0\ ,\ \hat{C}_{h}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=0\ ,\ \hat{C}_{g}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=0.

(60)

Hubbard Transformation

Now that we have argued that the correlation and response functions take on deterministic values in the
D\to\infty
limit, we can represent the quadratic terms in log-density in
\hat{\Delta},\hat{\chi},\hat{\xi}
fields as linear averages Gaussian random variables
u_{\Delta}(t),u^{\ell}(t),r^{\ell}(t)
, giving

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\Delta}(t)\hat{\Delta}(t^{\prime})C_{v}(t,t^{\prime})\right)=\left<\exp\left(-i\sum_{t}\hat{\Delta}(t)u_{\Delta}(t)\right)\right>_{u_{\Delta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{v})}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{h}^{0}(t)\hat{h}^{0}(t^{\prime})C_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})\right)=\left<\exp\left(-i\sum_{t}\hat{h}^{0}(t)u^{0}(t)\right)\right>_{u^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\alpha^{-1}C_{\Delta}\right)}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\chi}^{\ell}(t)\hat{\chi}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})C_{h}^{\ell-1}(t,t^{\prime})\right)=\left<\exp\left(-i\sum_{t}\hat{\chi}^{\ell}(t)u^{\ell}(t)\right)\right>_{u^{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,C_{h}^{\ell-1}\right)}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\gamma_{0}^{2}\nu}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\xi}^{0}(t)\hat{\xi}^{0}(t^{\prime})C_{g}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})\right)=\left<\exp\left(-i\sum_{t}\hat{\xi}^{0}(t)r^{0}(t)\right)\right>_{r^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\gamma_{0}^{-2}\nu^{-1}C_{h}^{\ell-1}\right)}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tt^{\prime}}\hat{\xi}^{\ell}(t)\hat{\xi}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})C_{g}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})\right)=\left<\exp\left(-i\sum_{t}\hat{\xi}^{\ell}(t)r^{\ell}(t)\right)\right>_{r^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,C_{g}^{\ell+1}\right)}

(61)

After introducing these Gaussian random variables, we can now perform integrals over the Fourier variables
\hat{\Delta},\hat{h}^{0},\hat{\chi}^{\ell},\hat{\xi}^{\ell}
to obtain defining equations for the random variables of interest. For example, for the
\Delta(t)
random variable,

\displaystyle\int\prod_{t}\frac{d\hat{\Delta}(t)}{2\pi}\left<\exp\left(i\sum_{t}\hat{\Delta}(t)\left[\Delta(t)-u_{\Delta}(t)-\sum_{t^{\prime}}R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})\Delta(t^{\prime})\right]\right)\right>_{u_{\Delta}}

\displaystyle=\left<\delta\left(\Delta(t)-u_{\Delta}(t)-\sum_{t^{\prime}}R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})\Delta(t^{\prime})\right)\right>_{u_{\Delta}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{v})}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\Delta(t)=u_{\Delta}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}}R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})\Delta(t^{\prime})\ ,\ u_{\Delta}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{v})

(62)

We can repeat this for all of the remaining fields which give defining stochastic processes for them. The general result is that the random fields are driven by a noise component (like
u_{\Delta}(t)
above) as well as a memory term involving a response function (like
R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})
above).

Simplifying the Response Functions

We note that the response functions involve averages over the
\hat{\Delta},\hat{\chi}^{\ell},\hat{\xi}^{\ell}
variables, which we now argue can be viewed as derivatives with respect to the Hubbard variable. For simplicity, we will now examine the zero source limit where all averages are identical.

\displaystyle R_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i\left<\Delta(t)\hat{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=-i\int\left[\prod_{t}\frac{d\Delta(t)d\hat{\Delta}(t)}{2\pi}\right]\Delta(t)\hat{\Delta}(t^{\prime})\left<\exp\left(i\sum_{t}\hat{\Delta}(t)\left[\Delta(t)-u_{\Delta}(t)-\sum_{t^{\prime}}R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})\Delta(t^{\prime})\right]\right)\right>_{u_{\Delta}}

\displaystyle=\int\left[\prod_{t}\frac{d\Delta(t)d\hat{\Delta}(t)}{2\pi}\right]\Delta(t)\left<\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{\Delta}(t^{\prime})}\exp\left(i\sum_{t}\hat{\Delta}(t)\left[\Delta(t)-u_{\Delta}(t)-\sum_{t^{\prime}}R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})\Delta(t^{\prime})\right]\right)\right>_{u_{\Delta}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime\prime}}\left<\Delta(t)[C_{v}]^{-1}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})u_{\Delta}(t^{\prime\prime})\right>_{u_{\Delta}}=\left<\frac{\partial\Delta(t)}{\partial u_{\Delta}(t^{\prime})}\right>_{u_{\Delta}}

(63)

which holds via integration by parts and Stein’s lemma. Repeating this for each of the fields, we find the following response function definitions from the saddle point equations

\displaystyle R_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial\Delta(t)}{\partial u_{\Delta}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial v(t)}{\partial u^{0}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R^{0}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial h^{0}(t)}{\partial r^{0}(t^{\prime})}\right>

\displaystyle R^{\ell}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial h^{\ell}(t)}{\partial r^{\ell}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R^{\ell}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial g^{\ell}(t)}{\partial u^{\ell}(t^{\prime})}\right>.

(64)

A.2
Final DMFT Equations

Combining the above equations at zero source
\bm{j}\to 0
after our Hubbard transformations, we arrive at the final single site equations for our fields. The DMFT equations that describe the single site stochastic processes are

\displaystyle v(t)=w^{\star}-r^{0}(t)-\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}R^{1}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{g}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})\right]h^{0}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ r^{0}(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}\left(0,\frac{1}{\nu\gamma_{0}^{2}}G^{1}\right)

\displaystyle\Delta(t)=u_{\Delta}(t)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})\Delta(t^{\prime})+\sigma\epsilon\ ,\ u_{\Delta}(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}\left(0,C_{v}\right)\ ,\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

\displaystyle h^{0}(t)=u^{0}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}R_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})v(t^{\prime})\ ,\ u^{0}(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}\left(0,\frac{1}{\alpha}C_{\Delta}\right)

\displaystyle h^{1}(t)=u^{1}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[\frac{1}{\nu\gamma_{0}}R_{hr}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})\right]g^{1}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ u^{1}(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,C_{h}^{0})

\displaystyle h^{\ell}(t)=u^{\ell}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[R_{hr}^{\ell-1}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}^{\ell-1}(t,t^{\prime})\right]g^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ u^{\ell}(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,C_{h}^{\ell-1})

\displaystyle g^{\ell}(t)=r^{\ell}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[R_{gu}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{g}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})\right]h^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ r^{\ell}(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,C_{g}^{\ell+1})

(65)

From these equations, all necessary response functions can be computed by differentiating over the noise variables
\{u,r\}
.

\displaystyle R_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial\Delta(t)}{\partial u_{\Delta}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R_{vu}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial v(t)}{\partial u^{0}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R^{0}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial h^{0}(t)}{\partial r^{0}(t^{\prime})}\right>

\displaystyle R^{\ell}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial h^{\ell}(t)}{\partial r^{\ell}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R^{\ell}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial g^{\ell}(t)}{\partial u^{\ell}(t^{\prime})}\right>

(66)

Similarly, the correlation functions can be computed as averages over these noise variables

\displaystyle C_{v}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<v(t)v(t^{\prime})\right>\ ,\ C_{\Delta}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\Delta(t)\Delta(t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle C_{h}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<h^{\ell}(t)h^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\right>\ ,\ C_{g}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<g^{\ell}(t)g^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\right>

(67)

We note again that the test and train losses can be obtained from the time-time diagonal of the correlation functions.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t)=C_{v}(t,t)+\sigma^{2}\ ,\ \mathcal{\hat{L}}(t)=C_{\Delta}(t,t)

(68)

A.3
Centering: Subtracting the Initial Predictor for Lazy Limit Stability

At finite values of
\nu
our theory will diverge if the lazy limit
\gamma_{0}\to 0
is taken. This is due to a divergence of the predictor variance
C_{v}(t,t)
which can be eliminated with a
centering
operation. Under centering we define the vector
\bm{v}(t)
as

\displaystyle\bm{v}(t)=\bm{w}^{\star}-\bm{\xi}^{0}(t)+\bm{\xi}^{0}(0)+\eta\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{g}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{h}^{0}(t^{\prime})

(69)

The key adjustment to the DMFT equations from this operation is

\displaystyle v(t)=w_{\star}-r^{0}(t)+r^{0}(0)-\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}R^{1}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})h^{0}(t^{\prime})-\eta\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}C_{g}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})h^{0}(t^{\prime})

The correlation functions that need to be tracked are
\{C_{v},C_{h}^{0},C_{h}^{1},...,C_{h}^{L-1},C_{g}^{1},C_{g}^{2},...,C_{g}^{L}\}
while we need the following response functions
\{R_{\Delta},R_{hr}^{0},...,R^{L-1}_{hr},R_{gu}^{1},...,R_{gu}^{L}\}
.

Appendix B
Comparing Parameterizations

In this section, we briefly comment on how the maximal stable learning rate scales with
\nu
in NTK and in mean field/
\mu
P parameterization. NTK parameterization is defined as scaling the network so that
\gamma_{0}=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu}}\right)
. However, a consequence of this choice is that the change in the correlation functions
C_{g}(t,t^{\prime})
under a fixed number of steps of optimization will decrease with
\gamma_{0}
at fixed learning rate. As a consequence, higher learning rates can be tolerated at larger widths. However, once the network is wide enough to be sufficiently close to the kernel regime, the maximum stable learning rate stabilizes as the dynamics become asymptotically linear. A comparison showing this trend is provided in
8
.

\nu
for different parameterizations.

For very rich dynamics
\gamma_{0}\gg 1
, the optimal learning rate approximately scales as

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{max}}\sim\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{\nu}\left(1\right)&\text{MF}\ (\text{Fixed}\ \gamma_{0})\\
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}\left(\nu^{1-\frac{1}{L}}\right)&\text{NTK}\ (\text{Fixed}\ \gamma_{0}\sqrt{\nu}),\end{cases}

(70)

based on a simple argument about the scale of the kernel. On the other hand, in the lazy learning regime
\gamma_{0}\ll 1
, both MF and NTK parameterizations approach constant optimal learning rate as they approach linear models.

C.1
The Large
L
Limit

The infinite depth limit with
\beta=\beta_{0}/\sqrt{L}
can be interpreted as a collection of ODEs for the response functions and a collection of ODEs for the correlation functions along the hidden layers.

First, we note that under the scaling
\beta=\beta_{0}/\sqrt{L}
, that for any
k>1
, the responses scale as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h^{\ell}(t)}{\partial u^{k}(t^{\prime})}=\Theta\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\sqrt{L}}\right)\ ,\ \frac{\partial g^{\ell}(t)}{\partial u^{k}(t^{\prime})}=\Theta\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\sqrt{L}}\right)

(83)

and similarly that for all
k<L
, the response functions for the
r^{k}
fields scale as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h^{\ell}(t)}{\partial r^{k}(t^{\prime})}=\Theta\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\sqrt{L}}\right)\ ,\ \frac{\partial g^{\ell}(t)}{\partial r^{k}(t^{\prime})}=\Theta\left(\frac{\beta_{0}}{\sqrt{L}}\right)

(84)

To take the large
L
limit, we introduce a notion of “layer time”

\displaystyle\tau=\frac{\ell}{L}\in[0,1]

(85)

and we recognize that the finite
L
ResNet update equations approximate a SDE with a discretized process over
\tau\in[0,1]
with step size
d\tau\sim 1/L
. We define the limited

\displaystyle h(\tau,t)=\lim_{L\to\infty}h^{\ell}(t)|_{\ell=L\tau}\ ,\ g(\tau,t)=\lim_{L\to\infty}g^{\ell}(t)|_{\ell=L\tau}

(86)

The functions we aim to characterize in the limit are the limiting correlation functions

\displaystyle C^{\ell}_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\equiv\lim_{L\to\infty}C^{\ell}_{h}(t,t^{\prime})|_{\ell=L\tau}\ ,\ C^{\ell}_{g}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\equiv\lim_{L\to\infty}C^{\ell}_{g}(t,t^{\prime})|_{\ell=L\tau}

(87)

and the following
\Theta(1)
limiting response functions

\displaystyle R_{hr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\equiv\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{L}}{\beta_{0}}R^{\ell}_{hr}(t,t^{\prime})|_{\ell=L\tau}\ ,\ R_{gu}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\equiv\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{L}}{\beta_{0}}R^{\ell}_{gu}(t,t^{\prime})|_{\ell=L\tau}

(88)

With these functions introduced, the single site equations in the “body” of the ResNet take the form of coupled SDEs

\displaystyle dh(\tau,t)=\beta_{0}du(\tau,t)+\beta_{0}^{2}d\tau\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[R_{hr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\right]g(\tau,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle dg(\tau,t)=-\beta_{0}dr(\tau,t)-\beta_{0}^{2}d\tau\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[R_{gu}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{g}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\right]h(\tau,t^{\prime})

(89)

The noise sources in these SDEs have the correlation structure

\displaystyle\left<du(\tau,t)du(\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>=\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})C_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})d\tau d\tau^{\prime}\ ,\ \left<dr(\tau,t)dr(\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>=\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})C_{g}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})d\tau d\tau^{\prime}

(90)

The
h
SDE is to be integrated forward in time from
\tau=0
to
\tau=1
and the
g
SDE is to be integrated backwards in time from
\tau=1
to
\tau=0
. The necessary response and correlation functions can be computed from this SDE as

\displaystyle R_{hr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial h(\tau,t)}{\partial du(\tau,t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R_{gu}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial g(\tau,t)}{\partial dr(\tau,t^{\prime})}\right>

\displaystyle R_{hu}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial g(\tau,t)}{\partial du(\tau,t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R_{gr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial g(\tau,t)}{\partial dr(\tau,t^{\prime})}\right>

\displaystyle C_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\left<h(\tau,t)h(\tau,t^{\prime})\right>\ ,\ C_{g}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\left<g(\tau,t)g(\tau,t^{\prime})\right>

(91)

However, we note that the single-layer response functions do not actually close, but rather, all possible pairs of layer-times must be considered, ie we must compute the full collection

\displaystyle R_{hr}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial h(\tau,t)}{\partial du(\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R_{gu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\partial g(\tau,t)}{\partial dr(\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\right>

(92)

These four response functions form a set of ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}R_{hu}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\delta(t-t^{\prime})+\beta_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime\prime}<t}\left[R_{hr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\right]R_{gu}(\tau,t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}R_{hr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\beta_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime\prime}<t}\left[R_{hr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\right]R_{gr}(\tau,t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}R_{gu}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=-\beta_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime\prime}<t}\left[R_{hr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\right]R_{hu}(\tau,t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}R_{gr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=-\delta(t-t^{\prime})-\beta_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime\prime}<t}\left[R_{hr}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})\right]R_{hr}(\tau,t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime}).

(93)

Again from these response functions, we can again obtain the final correlation functions,

\displaystyle C_{h}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau^{\prime}\left[R_{hu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t,s)R_{hu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime},s^{\prime})C_{h}(\tau^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})+R_{hr}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t,s)R_{hr}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime},s^{\prime})C_{g}(\tau^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle C_{g}(\tau,t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau^{\prime}\left[R_{gu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t,s)R_{gu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime},s^{\prime})C_{h}(\tau^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})+R_{gr}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t,s)R_{gr}(\tau,\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime},s^{\prime})C_{g}(\tau^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})\right]

(94)

and the test loss dynamics have the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t)=C_{h}(0,t,t).

(95)

D.2

From our theory and simulations, we see that for sufficiently large learning rates and sufficiently small width and batch size, the test loss can initially
increase
with iterations
t

\displaystyle R_{gu}^{1}(t,t^{\prime})\approx\eta\gamma_{0}L\Theta(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle G^{1}(t+1,t+1)-G^{1}(t,t)\approx[G^{2}(t+1,t+1)-G^{2}(t,t)]+4\eta^{2}L^{2}\gamma_{0}^{2}C_{h}^{0}(t,t)

\displaystyle\approx\eta^{2}C_{h}^{0}(t,t)\sum_{k=1}^{L}k^{2}=\frac{\eta^{2}\gamma_{0}^{2}L(L+1)(2L+1)}{6}C_{h}^{0}(t,t)

C_{v}(t)=\left<v(t)^{2}\right>

\displaystyle C_{v}(t+1)\approx\left[\left(1-L\eta\right)^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}L^{2}}{\alpha_{B}}+\frac{\eta^{2}L(L+1)(2L+1)}{6\nu}+\frac{\eta^{2}L(L+1)(2L+1)}{6\nu\alpha_{B}}\right]C_{v}(t).

(104)

\displaystyle\eta L>\frac{2}{1+\frac{1}{\alpha_{B}}+\frac{(L+1)(2L+1)}{6L\nu}+\frac{(L+1)(2L+1)}{6L\alpha_{B}\nu}}.

(105)

Indeed from simulations, we see that the early dynamics diverge for sufficiently small
\alpha_{B}
and
\nu
.

(107)

The definition of the correlation functions for these fields changes to

\displaystyle C_{v}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\left<v_{k}(t)v_{k}(t^{\prime})\right>\ ,\ C_{h}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left<h^{0}_{k}(t)h^{0}_{k}(t^{\prime})\right>

(108)

while the response function for the
h^{0}
and
v
variables change to

\displaystyle R_{hr}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left<\frac{\partial h^{0}_{k}(t)}{\partial r_{k}^{0}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R_{vu}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\left<\frac{\partial v_{k}(t)}{\partial u^{0}_{k}(t^{\prime})}\right>

(109)

which are both finite due to the trace class condition
\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}<\infty
. To express the test loss, we can introduce the
k
-th mode’s transfer function
\mathcal{H}_{k}(t,t^{\prime})
which has the following definition

\displaystyle v_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\mathcal{H}_{k}(t,t^{\prime})\left[w^{\star}_{k}-r^{0}_{k}(t^{\prime})-\sum_{t^{\prime\prime}<t^{\prime}}\left[\gamma_{0}^{-1}R_{gu}^{1}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})+C_{g}^{1}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\right]u^{0}_{k}(t^{\prime\prime})\right]

(110)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv\delta(t-t^{\prime})-\lambda_{k}\sum_{t^{\prime\prime}<t}\left[\gamma_{0}^{-1}R_{gu}^{1}(t,t^{\prime\prime})+\eta C_{g}^{1}(t,t^{\prime\prime})\right]\mathcal{H}_{k}(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime}).

(111)

The other hidden layers of the network have the same single site dynamics as before,

\displaystyle h^{1}(t)

\displaystyle=u^{1}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[\frac{1}{N\gamma_{0}}R_{hr}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}^{0}(t,t^{\prime})\right]g^{1}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ u^{1}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{h}^{0})

\displaystyle h^{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle=u^{\ell}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[R_{hr}^{\ell-1}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{h}^{\ell-1}(t,t^{\prime})\right]g^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ u^{\ell}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{h}^{\ell-1})

\displaystyle g^{L}(t)

\displaystyle=r^{L}(t)+\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}h^{L}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle g^{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle=r^{\ell}(t)+\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\left[R_{gu}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})+\eta\gamma_{0}C_{g}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})\right]h^{\ell}(t^{\prime})\ ,\ u^{\ell}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{g}^{\ell+1})

E.1
Scaling Laws for Easy and Hard Tasks

We now examine the scaling law behavior with respect to time
t
for the
N,B\to\infty
limit. We also study the small learning rate regime, where we can study a continuous time gradient flow dynamics

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{k}(t)\approx-\lambda_{k}K(t)v_{k}(t)

(112)

where
K(t)\approx C_{g}^{1}(t,t)+\gamma_{0}^{-1}R_{gu}^{1}(t,t)
is related to the
Neural Tangent Kernel
for the model
(
Jacot et al., 2018
)
. We make the following ansatz for the small
\gamma_{0}
dynamics for
K(t)

\displaystyle K(t)\sim 1+\gamma_{0}^{2}t^{\chi-1}\ ,\ \gamma_{0}\to 0

(113)

where the above is understood to disregard prefactors constants. Following general arguments of
Bordelon et al. (2024b)
, we must try identifying solutions to the self consistent equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{k}(t)=-\lambda_{k}[1+\gamma_{0}^{2}t^{\chi-1}]v_{k}(t)\implies v_{k}(t)=\exp\left(-\lambda_{k}[t+\gamma_{0}^{2}t^{\chi}]\right)w^{\star}_{k}

(114)

Following the self-consistency argument, we find that the kernel must scale as
K(t)\sim t^{1-\beta\chi}
. Combining this with the assumption implies that

\displaystyle\chi=\frac{2}{1+\beta}\ ,\ \forall\beta<1.

(115)

This implies the following loss scalings

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t)\sim\begin{cases}t^{-\beta}&\beta>1\\
t^{-\frac{2\beta}{1+\beta}}&\beta<1\end{cases}

(116)

which we show in Figure
10
. The case where
\beta<1
is known as the “hard task” regime while
\beta>1
is the “easy task” regime for linear networks trained on powerlaw features.

Scaling laws for the hard task regime are well predicted by the adjusted exponent.

Appendix G
Multiple Output Channels

The case of multiple (but
\mathcal{O}(1)
) output channels
N_{c}
can also be handled within our formalism. The cost of this analysis is to track correlation and response functions across all possible pairs of output channels as well as correlations across timesteps. We let the output channel
c
have readout
\bm{w}^{L}_{c}
as before

\displaystyle f_{c}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{N\gamma_{0}}\bm{w}_{c}^{L}\prod_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{W}^{\ell}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\bm{W}^{0}\right)\bm{x}

(119)

In this case, we assume a collection of target vectors
\bm{\bm{w}}^{\star}_{c}
for
c\in\{1,...,N_{c}\}
. In this case, the test error is governed by the following weight discrepancy

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{w}^{\star}_{c}-\frac{1}{N\gamma_{0}}\bm{W}^{0}(t)^{\top}\bm{g}^{1}_{c}(t)

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{\ell}_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{W}^{\ell}(t)^{\top}\bm{g}^{\ell+1}_{c}(t)\ ,\ \bm{g}^{L}_{c}(t)=\bm{w}^{L}_{c}(t)

(120)

The dynamics for the weights are

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{0}(t+1)=\bm{W}^{0}(t)+\frac{\eta\gamma_{0}}{\sqrt{D}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\bm{g}^{1}_{c}(t)\bm{h}^{0}_{c}(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{\ell}(t+1)=\bm{W}^{\ell}(t)-\frac{\eta\gamma_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\bm{g}_{c}^{\ell+1}(t)\bm{h}_{c}^{\ell}(t)^{\top}

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{L}_{c}(t+1)=\bm{w}^{L}_{c}(t)+\eta\gamma_{0}\bm{h}_{c}(t)

(121)

where the variables
\bm{h}^{\ell}_{c}(t)
are defined as

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{\ell+1}_{c}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{W}^{\ell}(t)\bm{h}_{c}^{\ell}(t)\ ,\ \bm{h}^{0}_{c}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{D}}{P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\Delta_{\mu,c}(t)\bm{x}_{\mu}

(122)

Expanding out the dynamics we find

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{\ell}_{c}(t)=\bm{\chi}^{\ell}_{c}(t)+\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\sum_{c^{\prime}}C_{h,cc^{\prime}}^{\ell-1}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{g}_{c^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\bm{g}^{\ell}_{c}(t)=\bm{\xi}^{\ell}_{c}(t)+\eta\gamma_{0}\sum_{t^{\prime}<t}\sum_{c^{\prime}}C_{g,cc^{\prime}}^{\ell+1}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{h}_{c^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})

(123)

As before we can carry out the DMFT computation but the order parameters that we need to track include cross-output channel correlations

\displaystyle C_{v,cc^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<v_{c}(t)v_{c^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\right>\ ,\ C_{\Delta,cc^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\Delta_{c}(t)\Delta_{c^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle C_{h,cc^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<h^{\ell}_{c}(t)h^{\ell}_{c^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\right>\ ,\ C_{g,cc^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<g^{\ell}_{c}(t)g^{\ell}_{c^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\right>

(124)

as well as cross channel response functions

\displaystyle R_{vu,cc^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\delta v_{c}(t)}{\delta u_{c^{\prime}}^{0}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R_{\Delta,cc^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\delta\Delta_{c}(t)}{\delta u_{\Delta,c^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})}\right>

\displaystyle R_{hr,cc^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\delta h_{c}^{\ell}(t)}{\delta r_{c^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})}\right>\ ,\ R_{gu,cc^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t,t^{\prime})=\left<\frac{\delta g_{c}^{\ell}(t)}{\delta u_{c^{\prime}}^{\ell}(t^{\prime})}\right>

(125)

These correlations and response functions close in the obvious generalization of the scalar output case.
