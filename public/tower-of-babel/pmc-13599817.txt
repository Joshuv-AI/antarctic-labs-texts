Factors promoting and impeding exercise behaviour in patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes during chemotherapy in Zhejiang, China: a qualitative study
Authors: Huan Liu, Junxiang Sun, Ting Wang, Huihua Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore factors influencing exercise behaviour during chemotherapy in patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes, providing reference for developing management plans and clinical practice.DesignThis was a qualitative study using a descriptive phenomenological approach. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with a guide informed by existing literature on exercise behaviour in chronic disease populations; the Health Belief Model (HBM) served as a sensitising framework to ensure broad coverage of relevant experiential domains. Data were analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method, and the inductively derived themes were subsequently organised using the HBM as a post-hoc interpretive framework.SettingDepartment of Medical Oncology, Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China), in May and June 2023.Participants11 patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes undergoing chemotherapy.ResultsThe mean age of the 11 patients was 58.09±7.70 years. The mean duration of colorectal cancer was 0.88±0.91 years, and the mean duration of diabetes was 9.24±7.75 years. Based on the HBM, three themes and six sub-themes were identified: Perceived Benefits of Exercise (physical benefits, psychosocial benefits), Perceived Barriers to Exercise (health status and chemotherapy-related side effects, psychological apprehension) and Cues to Action for Exercise (social support, professional guidance).ConclusionGiven the multifaceted challenges faced by patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes in exercise management, healthcare professionals should prioritise health education and diversify the formats of patient instruction. Providing effective social support and professional guidance may help address the identified barriers and facilitate exercise adherence during chemotherapy. However, as this is a small, single-centre qualitative study, the relationship between exercise adherence and long-term prognosis requires further investigation in larger, longitudinal studies.

Introduction

With advances in cancer diagnosis and treatment, the number of cancer survivors continues to increase annually, leading to a growing population with cancer comorbidities.1 Among colorectal cancer comorbidities, chronic diseases like diabetes and hypertension predominate. The ‘colorectal cancer-diabetes’ dual comorbidity pattern ranks highly, representing the most common comorbidity combination in clinical settings, accounting for 20%–30% of cases.2 A core driver of their high comorbidity rate is shared health risk factors, with physical inactivity established as a common independent risk factor for both.3 4 Existing research indicates that exercise can alleviate adverse symptoms such as cancer-related fatigue and anxiety/depression during chemotherapy in colorectal cancer patients by downregulating systemic inflammatory responses.5 Simultaneously, exercise effectively enhances insulin sensitivity and improves glucose metabolism in diabetic patients.6 Owing to this dual regulatory effect, exercise exerts a positive influence on the development and progression of colorectal cancer with comorbid diabetes and has been recognised globally as a core non-pharmacological intervention strategy for preventing and managing multiple chronic diseases.6 7 However, the current state of exercise management for Chinese patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes is not optimistic, with over half failing to meet guideline-recommended exercise volumes.8 9 While existing exercise research and guidelines predominantly focus on single-disease scenarios, the complexity of disease pathophysiology and the heterogeneity of individual physiological characteristics in comorbid settings present significant challenges,10 increasing the difficulty of exercise management for these patients. The Health Belief Model (HBM), a key psychological theory explaining health behaviours, has been well-applied in exercise adherence research.11 Domestic studies exploring exercise management factors in patients with multiple comorbidities from a comorbidity perspective remain scarce, predominantly relying on quantitative approaches like regression and correlation analyses. No qualitative research focusing on exercise determinants for colorectal cancer patients with diabetes has been reported. Therefore, this study employs a phenomenological research approach. Through semi-structured interviews with patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes, it delves into the factors influencing their exercise behaviour amid the challenges of managing multiple coexisting conditions, aiming to provide insights for developing intervention strategies and clinical practice.

This qualitative study aimed to elucidate the facilitators and barriers to exercise behaviour from the perspective of patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes during chemotherapy, to inform the development of tailored, evidence-based exercise support interventions.

Methods

This study employed a qualitative, phenomenological research design to gain an in-depth understanding of the lived experiences of patients. The research was guided by the HBM as its overarching theoretical framework. The HBM, a key psychological theory for explaining health behaviours, informed the development of the interview guide to ensure a systematic exploration of the core constructs (eg, perceived benefits, barriers, cues to action) relevant to exercise behaviour. A phenomenological approach was adopted to prioritise the participants’ subjective perspectives. Accordingly, the data collected from semi-structured interviews were analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method, a rigorous procedure designed to derive themes essentially grounded in the participants’ narratives.

Participants and setting

A qualitative study was conducted at the Department of Oncology, Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, between May and June 2023. The target population was patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid type 2 diabetes undergoing chemotherapy.

Sampling and recruitment: Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling strategy. The process involved: (1) daily screening of the hospital’s electronic medical record system for eligible inpatients; (2) preliminary eligibility assessment; (3) face-to-face approach and final verification; (4) obtaining informed consent. This ensured recruitment of information-rich participants actively experiencing the phenomenon. Inclusion criteria were (1) pathologically confirmed colon/rectal adenocarcinoma; (2) pathological Tumor-Node-Metastasis staging (pTNM) stage II–III after radical surgery; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG-PS) score of 0–2; (4) meeting the diagnostic criteria for type 2 diabetes as per the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes (2020 edition); (5) age≥18 years; (6) basic literacy; (7) voluntary participation in the study. Exclusion criteria included (1) presence of tumours other than colorectal tumours; (2) presence of an ostomy; (3) concurrent multi-organ dysfunction; (4) communication barriers or psychiatric disorders.

Sample size and thematic saturation: We planned a flexible sample, anticipating approximately 10–15 participants in line with published qualitative samples for similar patient-groups and interview density.12 Interviews were transcribed and analysed iteratively (Colaizzi’s method). We tracked emerging codes/themes per interview and judged saturation against three pragmatic criteria used in health services qualitative research: (1) no new themes appeared in two consecutive interviews; (2) theme boundaries and relationships remained stable on re-review; and (3) participant diversity on key descriptors (age, gender, colorectal cancer type 2 diabetes mellitus (CRC/T2DM) duration, exercise level) had been reasonably covered.13

Development of interview guide

The initial draft of the semi-structured interview was developed through a targeted literature review. This review was conducted with a focus on three key areas to ensure the guide’s comprehensiveness, theoretical grounding, and methodological rigour. (1) Population-specific evidence: empirical studies (both qualitative and quantitative) investigating exercise behaviours, facilitators and barriers specifically among patients with colorectal cancer, type 2 diabetes or their comorbidity were examined. This review helped identify broad experiential domains pertinent to the population (eg, the experience of managing concurrent treatment and chronic disease symptoms, navigating safety concerns), ensuring these areas were proactively included in the preliminary outline for exploration; (2) theoretical framing: theoretical frameworks commonly used in health behaviour research, including the HBM, which served as a sensitising framework to alert the researcher to potentially relevant experiential domains (eg, perceived benefits, barriers, sources of support). The HBM was not used to predetermine question wording or to impose a deductive structure; rather, it helped ensure the interview covered a broad range of topics while maintaining open-ended questioning to allow participants to introduce novel or unexpected themes. Probing strategies were guided by participants’own narratives, not by HBM constructs; and (3) methodological guidance: best practice guidelines for developing qualitative interview questions in health research were reviewed to formulate effective, open-ended and non-leading prompts. Subsequently, a multidisciplinary panel of experts was invited to participate in a focus group discussion to review and refine the preliminary semi-structured interview guide (ie, the draft outline). Experts provided feedback on the relevance, clarity and comprehensiveness of the questions, which was used to finalise the guide. Prior to formal data collection, a convenience sample of two patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes undergoing chemotherapy was recruited for pilot interviews. Feedback from these pilot interviews was used to further revise the guide, resulting in the final version. The core outline is as follows: (1) How do you view engaging in physical exercise given your current disease status? (Is it necessary? What are the benefits/drawbacks?) (2) How did you approach physical exercise before and during chemotherapy? (eg, type of exercise, duration, frequency, intensity) Could you describe your experiences and feelings about exercise during these two periods? What do you believe you can gain from exercising during chemotherapy? Have you noticed any physical or psychological changes? (3) What factors do you believe influence your ability to maintain regular exercise during chemotherapy? (Facilitators? Barriers?) (4) Have you encountered any difficulties while trying to exercise during chemotherapy? If so, could you describe them and how you managed them? (5) Have you received any help, encouragement, support, companionship or praise from others regarding your exercise efforts? Have you had any such experiences? (6) What kind of support or assistance do you most wish to receive to help you exercise during chemotherapy? The semi-structured interview guide used in this study is provided as a supplementary file (see online supplemental file 1: Interview Guide). Furthermore, probing questions were strategically employed based on participants’ responses and guided by the HBM constructs to elicit deeper insights. For instance, when a participant mentioned fatigue as a barrier, we might probe, “Can you tell me more about how the fatigue affects your desire or ability to exercise on a day-to-day basis?” (exploring Perceived Barriers). If a participant noted that family encouragement helped, we might ask, “What specifically did your family say or do that made a difference for you?” (exploring Cues to Action). Standard open-ended prompts such as ‘Could you elaborate on that?’ or ‘Is there anything else you would like to add regarding your experience with exercise during treatment?’ were consistently used to encourage participants to expand on their narratives and ensure comprehensive data collection. Open-ended questions such as ‘Is there anything else you would like to add?’ were also used to deepen the conversation.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face, semi-structured in-depth interviews, using an interview guide developed through expert focus group discussion. Data were collected through face-to-face, semi-structured in-depth interviews. All interviews were conducted solely by the principal researcher (HL), who is trained in qualitative methods. Two research assistants, Zhuojun Yang and Jing He (both nursing students), were present to support the data collection process. Their primary roles were to operate the audio-recording equipment (a dedicated voice recorder) and document detailed field notes on non-verbal cues and the interview context. This team-based approach allowed the interviewer to maintain full focus on participant engagement while ensuring accurate and comprehensive data capture. The Principal Investigator (PI, HL) did not know any participant prior to study initiation. All participants were aware of the interviewer’s (ie, the PI’s) professional background and role as outlined in the participant information sheet prior to the interview. Prior to interviews, researchers explained the study objectives and procedures to participants. Written informed consent was obtained after the participant fully understood the information. The interview location was chosen by the participant to ensure a comfortable and relaxed setting (eg, patient bedside, demonstration room), with utmost efforts made to maintain a quiet environment. During the interviews, the researcher practised active listening and maintained a receptive and non-judgemental attitude. Follow-up questions were asked based on the participant’s responses and the context of the conversation, while the researcher consistently maintained a neutral stance. Leading questions and suggestive language were strictly avoided throughout, and participants were neither interrupted nor evaluated. Clarification was sought by summarising points and asking additional questions as needed. Each interview lasted between 30 and 50 min. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent. Following data collection, the audio files were transcribed verbatim into textual format using WPS Office software to organise the interview materials for analysis. All data collection instruments and procedures remained consistent throughout the study. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point where no new themes emerged from subsequent interviews. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved. After the ninth interview, preliminary analysis indicated theme repetition. Two confirmatory interviews (participants 10 and 11) were then conducted. The research team reviewed these and concluded that no new themes emerged, confirming saturation was reached with 11 participants.

Data analysis

Following interviews, both researchers collaboratively organised the data. Verbatim transcripts were completed within 24 hours. Verification with the participant was sought in cases where the audio was unclear, where there was uncertainty about the specific wording or meaning of a statement, or to clarify the context of a particular experience mentioned. This process aimed to ensure the accuracy and authenticity of the data and is a recognised technique for enhancing credibility in qualitative research (member checking). The data were analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological analysis method.14 Two researchers first immersed themselves in the data by repeatedly reading the transcripts independently to gain familiarity with the content. To bracket preconceptions and enhance reflexivity, both researchers maintained reflective memos throughout the study, documenting initial impressions and personal reflections that might influence interpretation. The initial coding phase was conducted independently by the two researchers on the same two transcripts. They highlighted significant statements, extracted meaningful units and assigned preliminary codes. To ensure analytical rigour and consistency, the researchers collaboratively developed and maintained a shared analytical framework. This framework, which evolved throughout the analysis, documented all codes, their definitions, exemplary quotes, and the rationale for thematic clustering. The researchers then met to compare their independent codes, discuss any discrepancies in interpretation, and refine code definitions until consensus was reached. This iterative discussion and consensus-building was the primary method for resolving coding disagreements. After establishing coding consistency, the remaining transcripts were divided and coded independently, with regular meetings held to discuss any emergent or ambiguous segments and to ensure adherence to the evolving analytical framework. This process created a comprehensive audit trail of the analytical decisions, from initial coding to final theme generation. The coded data were then grouped into clusters of meaning, and relationships among clusters were examined to form themes and sub-themes.

Several strategies were employed to enhance the trustworthiness of the findings. First, independent double-coding was performed: as described above, two researchers independently coded all transcripts and resolved discrepancies through consensus discussion. Second, peer debriefing was conducted: this qualitative study formed part of the first author’s master’s thesis; accordingly, the analytical process and emerging themes were periodically reviewed by the supervising professor and were also scrutinised by external examiners during the thesis defence. Third, an audit trail was maintained, including original interview audio recordings and verbatim transcripts, to ensure transparency and allow for verification of the analytical process.

In the final stage of analysis, after the thematic structure had been inductively derived, we observed that the emergent themes resonated strongly with three core constructs of the HBM (perceived benefits, perceived barriers and cues to action). To enhance the theoretical coherence and interpretability of the findings, we adopted the HBM as a post-hoc organising framework for presenting the results. Importantly, the HBM was not used as a deductive coding framework; themes were generated purely inductively. The mapping process involved two researchers independently matching each final theme to the most relevant HBM construct, reaching consensus through discussion. All inductively derived themes mapped onto one of the three HBM constructs; no themes were forced into categories that did not naturally align. The remaining HBM dimensions (perceived susceptibility, perceived severity and self-efficacy) did not emerge as distinct themes in this study, reflecting the specific experiential focus of this patient population during chemotherapy—a finding that underscores the inductive integrity of our approach.

Patient and public involvement

Study participants or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

The analysis revealed a complex interplay of factors influencing exercise behaviour. To enhance the theoretical coherence and practical utility of these findings, the emergent themes were subsequently organised and presented within the framework of the HBM15, which served as a post-hoc interpretive lens. The factors influencing exercise behaviour in patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes were categorised into three main themes and six sub-themes (see figure 1): Perceived Benefits of Exercise (with two sub-themes: Physical Benefits and Psychosocial Benefits), Perceived Barriers to Exercise (with two sub-themes: Health Status and Chemotherapy-Related Side Effects and Psychological Concerns) and Cues to Action for Exercise (with two sub-themes: Social Support and Professional Guidance).

Perceived benefits of exercise

Physical benefits

The interview results indicated that perceived physical benefits served as a facilitator for exercise behaviour. Most patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes acknowledged that exercise was beneficial for their overall health. Some participants with established exercise routines reported experiencing positive physical effects, such as alleviating fatigue, improving glycaemic control and stimulating intestinal motility and bowel movements. However, it is noteworthy that while the majority recognised the general health benefits of exercise, none could accurately specify its particular advantages during the chemotherapy period when probed further.

P1: I always exercise for a period before each chemotherapy session, so I basically experience no side effects. But if I go for chemotherapy without exercising beforehand, I feel listless and nauseous. As for other specific benefits of exercise, I'm not very clear.

P3: After exercising, my metabolism gets a boost, which aids digestion. When my blood sugar isn't well-controlled, I start to worry and think of ways to lower it through exercise. That’s about it.

P5: For patients like us, if we just lie in bed and sleep all the time, we feel even more fatigued and lethargic. Also, for colon cancer, it’s important to move around to stimulate bowel movements. It’s beneficial.

P8: It’s good for controlling blood sugar and for bowel movements. My chemotherapy was initiated because of bowel issues discovered due to poor stool habits. Now, during chemotherapy, you must move; if you don't, you won't have regular bowel movements.

P8 also mentioned: I feel much more relaxed and in a better mood after dancing. After these four cycles of chemotherapy, my doctor said the treatment response has been very good and I should continue with this regimen. I feel it might be related to my dancing.

Psychosocial benefits

Several interviewees reported that exercise positively impacted their mental well-being and spousal relationships, which in turn motivated their engagement in physical activity. Research indicates that during exercise, the brain releases endorphins—substances with analgesic and euphoric effects—which can alleviate negative emotions such as anxiety and depression.16 Furthermore, exercise can help divert patients’ attention away from their illness towards the activity itself, thereby reducing the perceived impact of disease- and chemotherapy-related discomfort. Additionally, purposeful and regular exercise may enhance cognitive function, assisting patients in cultivating a positive and optimistic self-image within a more mature cognitive framework, ultimately promoting exercise adherence.17

P3 stated during the interview: Also, the air outside is better than at home. Going out for some fresh air makes me feel much more comfortable inside.

P5 shared: For me, exercise is more like recharging. After working out, I feel 'fully charged,’ ready to face everything. My mood improves, and I feel more refreshed and clear-headed.

P8 mentioned: When my wife and I go out for walks or dancing, we get to talk a lot. She helps me plan the next steps in my treatment. Even though I have cancer, exercising gives us more opportunities to spend quality time together.

Perceived barriers to exercise

Health status and chemotherapy-related side effects

The interview findings revealed that health status and chemotherapy-related side effects were identified as major barriers to exercise management. Participants reported a decline in overall physical function and activity levels compared with their pre-diagnosis state, making it difficult to maintain their previous exercise volume. They emphasised that chemotherapy-induced side effects experienced during treatment cycles—such as fatigue, nausea and vomiting, peripheral neuropathy and alopecia—directly reduced their motivation and capacity for exercise. Furthermore, the cumulative effect of these side effects often exacerbated the difficulty of engaging in physical activity in the later stages of the treatment cycle.

P2: Compared to before I had cancer, I've reduced my exercise significantly. My physical capacity isn't what it used to be; being ill just makes you feel unwell. Also, in the days immediately following chemotherapy, I always feel more fatigued, which has an impact. My legs feel weak and numb, so I just don't want to move. At other times, I still take walks after meals and do square dancing.

P7: The week right after chemotherapy, the fatigue is more noticeable. I might only manage one or two kilometers a day, walking about three thousand steps, roughly half an hour.

P9: But right after chemotherapy, I feel my strength is low, and I can't walk much. Sometimes I don't go out to exercise for two days.

P9 also added: The most distressing thing after chemotherapy is the heavy hair loss. Now, whenever I go out for a walk, I have to wear a wig.

P11: The doctor advised me to walk five kilometers daily. However, in the first few days after chemotherapy, I feel genuinely unwell, weak, and feeble. I have to exercise within my current capacity; otherwise, I can't do it. The exercise volume should be lighter in the days right after chemo. Walking five kilometers daily is achievable for me in the following days.

Psychological apprehension

Most patients exhibited a degree of psychological barrier towards exercising during chemotherapy, manifesting as kinesiophobia. The apprehension stemmed from concerns such as potential displacement of an implantable port, fear of infection, risk of falls and worry about negative impacts on their disease condition.

P1: I only take walks, not daring to do any vigorous exercise. Especially after chemotherapy, the whole body feels terrible. Exercising more wouldn't be good for the illness.

P4: But I haven't walked much these past couple of days because the device in my chest was just removed recently. Also, the weather has been quite hot, and I'm afraid sweating might lead to an infection.

P6: I'm worried that exercising might cause a fall and result in a fracture. If any accident happens, I might end up bedridden.

P8: Dancing won't affect this needle in my chest, right? I never dare to dance too vigorously.

Cues to action for exercise

Social support

The interviews revealed that receiving social support served as a positive factor in enhancing patients’ confidence in exercise management, whereas a lack of such support acted as a barrier. Patients placed trust in healthcare professionals, whose exercise recommendations played a pivotal motivational role. Those with established exercise habits consistently reported having received such advice from their clinicians. For some patients, motivation stemmed from family and friends. On one hand, patients noted that exercising with partners or friends helped alleviate chemotherapy-related side effects. On the other hand, patients whose families advised against exercise demonstrated poorer exercise management. The concern, support and supervision from family members, friends and healthcare providers effectively counteracted feelings of loneliness and complacency during exercise, thereby promoting adherence—a finding consistent with the research of Yangqing et al.18

P1 stated: When I visit the doctor for my diabetes, he also says to exercise more. It’s good for what you've eaten; you need to burn it off. If you don't, it will be bad for the condition.

P2 shared: My family often reminds me to go out for walks more. Sometimes after meals, they accompany me for walks in the residential area. Exercising is good for blood sugar; it becomes more stable. It would also be good if you could create a group chat for us patients to share exercise experiences together.

P3 explained: I go for walks in the park both morning and evening. In the evening, my daughter accompanies me, and in the morning, I go with a few friends. My friends are very concerned about me; they call me every day even while I'm hospitalized, asking how I'm doing.” P3 added: "This time it was detected in my liver, so my mood hasn't been great, and I don't really feel like going out. Also, my feet feel numb when I walk, but not when I'm lying still. However, when I arrange to walk with friends, I don't pay much attention to the numbness anymore. It distracts me, and it doesn't bother me as much.

P8 mentioned: My family is very supportive. I go dancing together with my wife.

P9 reported: Professor Xu is very strict; it’s a mandatory requirement. You must exercise, walking five kilometers every day.

P10 expressed: My family doesn't allow me to go out to exercise. They are worried and believe that being ill requires rest and recuperation. P10 further noted: The doctor never said that I should start exercising after chemotherapy. The doctor is always very busy. The outpatient appointments are fully booked; you only get a minute or two inside, so when would there be time to tell you to exercise?

Professional guidance

During the interviews, the researchers learnt that while patients reported receiving encouragement from healthcare professionals to exercise during chemotherapy, they also indicated that the guidance provided was often too general. Many of their specific concerns remained unaddressed, and they expressed a desire for more detailed, personalised exercise instructions. From the patients’ perspective, being provided with a professional, specific and safe exercise plan by their physician could significantly enhance their exercise self-efficacy during treatment. Furthermore, some patients reported encountering conflicting advice regarding exercise for diabetes vs cancer, which led to confusion and difficulty in decision-making.

P3: The doctor only says that I should be more active after chemotherapy, at least walking five kilometers a day. But there are no specific recommendations for other types of exercise, and I wasn't told to what extent I should exercise. When I asked if exercising could affect my venous access port, the doctor didn't seem to have specific details either. It would be quite useful if there were videos, pamphlets, or lectures explaining these things in detail.

P7: On the Huashan Hospital public account, I read that people with diabetes should exercise more because it’s good for blood sugar. But then I saw another article saying that cancer patients with hand and foot numbness are advised not to exercise due to a higher risk of falls. So, which one should I follow?

P11: Before I got sick, I exercised regularly—playing ball games, running, and so on. But after getting ill, I've been afraid to move. When I ask the doctor how I should exercise and if there are any precautions, they always say, 'Do what you can manage based on your own condition; if you feel up to it, move more.’ I've seen on my phone that foreign doctors prescribe exercise prescription. It would be really good if China could learn from that practice as well.

Discussion

This study, through in-depth interviews, identified a multifaceted array of factors influencing exercise behaviour in patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes during chemotherapy. These factors span knowledge deficits, symptom burden, motivational challenges and systemic gaps in clinical support. Critically, our findings suggest that the current clinical approach, which often manages CRC and diabetes in isolation, fails to address the unique, compounded barriers faced by this comorbid population. Effective intervention requires a paradigm shift towards integrated, personalised and support-based exercise management. The following discussion elaborates on three pivotal, interlinked domains derived from our data: (1) personalisation of exercise prescription; (2) management of the symptom-affect-exercise cycle; and (3) mobilisation of multidimensional social support.

Developing personalised exercise management plans based on comorbidity characteristics to improve exercise experience in patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes

Research indicates that developing individualised aerobic combined with resistance exercise programmes—tailored to exercise type, duration, frequency and intensity for patients with different conditions—may help ensure exercise effectiveness, improve adherence and subsequently enhance functional status.19 The findings of this study reveal that although patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes acknowledge the benefits of exercise, their understanding of its specific significance for diabetes, colorectal cancer and particularly for this comorbidity is limited. They lack knowledge regarding appropriate exercise types, frequency and intensity for their comorbid condition. Consequently, their exercise level is low, the type of activity is monotonous, the intensity often falls below the recommended safe threshold and their overall volume falls far short of guideline recommendations. Patients reported during interviews that healthcare professionals prioritise dietary and pharmacological therapies while glossing over exercise management. This was vividly described by P10, who noted the clinical focus and time constraints that marginalised exercise advice: “My family doesn’t allow me to go out to exercise; they think I need rest. But at the hospital, it’s the same. The doctor is always very busy. In the short minute or two during the follow-up, the conversation is only about the medication—whether the drugs are working, if we need to adjust the dose. It’s all about the pills. Never once has the doctor said I should start exercising or even asked about my activity.” The cumulative impact of chemotherapy side effects is not considered, and targeted guidance is lacking, which aligns with the findings of Chunying et al.20 This situation leads to a knowledge gap and may even foster misconceptions due to the absence of scientific, personalised exercise advice. The interviews also revealed that when patients encounter conflicting exercise recommendations from separate diabetes and colorectal cancer guidance, they often face decision paralysis, resulting in exercise avoidance. While evidence is growing both domestically and internationally regarding the role of exercise in improving clinical outcomes for both diabetes and colorectal cancer, existing published guidelines provide only generic advice on exercise type, intensity, frequency and duration. They fail to account for the cumulative effect of chemotherapy side effects on exercise intolerance, are designed for single diseases (thus ineffective or even contradictory for patients with comorbidities) and lack practicality, hindering effective exercise management by clinical staff. Compared with patients with other chronic disease comorbidities, implementing exercise management holds even greater significance for those with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes. The non-pharmacotherapeutic role of exercise management in halting disease progression and delaying complications should be fully leveraged. Therefore, it is imperative to develop personalised, practical exercise management protocols for the chemotherapy period in this patient population, specifically based on the characteristics of their comorbidity.

Therefore, bridging this knowledge and guidance gap with personalised protocols is the foundational step. However, knowledge alone is insufficient if patients are incapacitated by treatment-related symptoms and negative affect.

Enhancing symptom management and creating positive affective experiences to foster intrinsic motivation for sustained exercise in patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes

Patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes in the mid-to-late stages face compounded challenges, including negative emotions arising from illness and chemotherapy-related side effects. These significantly undermine treatment confidence and quality of life, acting as major barriers to continued home-based exercise, a finding consistent with our prior research.21 In this study, most patients reported a substantial decline in physical capacity compared with pre-illness levels, making it difficult to adhere to exercise recommendations due to symptoms such as cancer pain, fatigue, nausea and vomiting and peripheral neuropathy. Some patients also exhibited reluctance to exercise outdoors due to appearance-related anxiety, such as that caused by alopecia. However, the role of exercise in alleviating chemotherapy-related fatigue and other issues has been well-established and is recommended as an effective tool for managing chemotherapy side effects in worldwide clinical practice guidelines.7 22 Consequently, effectively addressing these symptoms emerges as a crucial, yet challenging, aspect of supporting exercise during chemotherapy. This study also found that patients with higher levels of exercise knowledge and better health information literacy, who held positive perceptions about exercise, demonstrated greater exercise adherence. Furthermore, when exercise yielded positive affective experiences such as pleasure, relaxation and a sense of accomplishment, it enhanced exercise self-efficacy.23 This serves as intrinsic motivation to pursue higher exercise goals and maintain long-term engagement, helping to alleviate chemotherapy-induced fatigue and anxiety, thereby forming a virtuous cycle. Conversely, negative past healthcare experiences or a loss of confidence in rehabilitation strategies like exercise can make it difficult to initiate an exercise routine, aligning with the findings of Mengxiao et al.24 Consequently, guiding patients effectively may need to begin with a comprehensive understanding of their past exercise experiences. A multi-faceted approach is necessary to address the complex barriers identified. Providing correct information on the value of exercise and health literacy, improving knowledge, correcting misconceptions (eg, that exercise might dislodge an implantable port), creating positive affective experiences, identifying and reinforcing positive traits, and alleviating psychological concerns are all necessary. A multi-faceted approach is required to establish a sustainable mechanism for promoting exercise during chemotherapy in this patient population.

While optimising the individual’s capacity and motivation is crucial, sustaining exercise behaviour long-term often depends on the reinforcing ecosystem surrounding the patient.

Mobilising multidimensional social support systems, including family and healthcare, to empower long-term exercise management in patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes

Literature suggests that creating socially supportive conditions is a significant factor in helping home-based cancer patients adhere to regular exercise.25 Our interviews revealed that the traditional belief among family members that patients should ‘rest and recuperate’ during chemotherapy often conflicts with the lifestyle modifications required for patients with colorectal cancer and diabetes, thereby hindering exercise behaviour. Conversely, patients living with spouses or children tended to exhibit higher exercise adherence. Therefore, the scope of health education should extend beyond the individual patient to include family members and close friends, mobilising them to become an intrinsic motivator for patient exercise. Furthermore, research indicates that exercise advice from healthcare professionals is a critical link in transitioning patients from ‘awareness’ to ‘action’.23 The interviews highlighted a core issue: compared with standardised dietary and pharmacological interventions, exercise management is not afforded equal importance in clinical practice and is severely marginalised during patient education. When patients with comorbidities are referred, concerns about exercise safety often lead both physical therapists and the patients themselves to reduce the frequency and intensity of exercise to levels unlikely to be effective.26 Consequently, an urgent priority is to foster a profound shift in the mindset of healthcare providers—from passively acknowledging the benefits of exercise to actively empowering patients to engage in it—and to formally integrate exercise into the core management protocol for these patients. The role of healthcare professionals extends far beyond merely offering advice; it is fundamentally about empowering patients through professional, scientific exercise prescriptions, motivational interviewing and continuous encouragement, thereby helping them build self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation for sustained exercise. Some patients also expressed a need for peer support groups. Thus, multi-channel engagement through methods such as establishing WeChat groups for patients, creating support groups for family and friends, forming exercise teams and organising experience-sharing sessions with role models can collectively enhance exercise efficacy and improve adherence.

Implications for practice and future research

Promoting exercise in patients with CRC and T2DM during chemotherapy is a complex, multidimensional challenge. Our findings advocate for a shift in clinical practice towards a comprehensive, patient-centred model that simultaneously addresses several key barriers. This model should (1) educate and empower through comorbidity-specific, personalised exercise planning; (2) equip and motivate by integrating proactive symptom management with the cultivation of positive exercise experiences; and (3) engage and sustain by actively involving both family and healthcare systems as continuous pillars of support. Future clinical guidelines must move beyond a disease-siloed approach to embed such an integrated model, transforming exercise from a marginal recommendation into a core component of care.

To build a stronger evidence base for this model and extend its applicability, future research is needed. The in-depth, contextual insights generated by this qualitative study—focused on the chemotherapy phase—provide a crucial foundation. The next essential step involves large-sample, mixed-methods and longitudinal studies that can follow patients across the broader disease trajectory. Such research will be vital to quantify the relative importance of the factors we identified, rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of tailored interventions and establish definitive, phase-specific protocols for integrated cancer and diabetes care.

Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method was employed to ensure analytical rigour, we did not fully execute Step 7 of this approach, which involves returning the complete thematic structure to all participants for member checking. Due to the clinical context of active chemotherapy and patient attrition after discharge, full verification with the entire sample was not feasible. To mitigate this limitation, alternative credibility-enhancing strategies were implemented, including independent double-coding by two researchers, peer debriefing with the supervisory team, and maintenance of a comprehensive audit trail. Second, this study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital in Shanghai, China, with a relatively small sample of 11 participants. Consequently, the findings may not be generalisable to other settings, regions or patient populations with different demographic or clinical characteristics. Third, the study focused exclusively on the chemotherapy treatment phase, and the identified factors may be context-specific to this period, limiting their applicability to other phases of the cancer care continuum. Fourth, despite rigorous protocols to maintain neutrality, the presence of the researcher during face-to-face interviews may have influenced participant responses. Future multi-centre, longitudinal studies with larger and more diverse samples are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This qualitative study provides the first in-depth exploration of the factors influencing exercise behaviour in patients with colorectal cancer and comorbid diabetes during chemotherapy. The findings illuminate a complex interplay of knowledge gaps, symptom burdens, motivational challenges and systemic support shortfalls. This study identifies critical, modifiable targets for intervention—specifically, the need for comorbidity-tailored education, proactive symptom management to facilitate activity and the mobilisation of multidimensional support. These insights are crucial for informing the development of future, evidence-based and patient-centred support strategies. By addressing these identified barriers and leveraging the facilitators, healthcare professionals can be better equipped to design interventions that aim to enhance exercise adherence and, potentially, the overall care experience for this patient population.
