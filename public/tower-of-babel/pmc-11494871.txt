Clinical analysis of 20 cases of perinatal tuberculosis
Authors: Ying Zhu, Houxi Bai, Mingbo Zhao, Xiaotao Yang, Yi Huang, Lu Xu, Haifeng Jin, Houyu Chen, Penghao Cui, Yonghan Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo analyze the clinical manifestations, diagnostic and therapeutic processes of perinatal tuberculosis in children, providing reference for clinicians in the diagnosis and treatment of this disease.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the epidemiological history, clinical manifestations, laboratory and imaging findings, and treatment follow-up of 20 cases of perinatal tuberculosis diagnosed in the Second Department of Infectious Disease, Kunming Children’s Hospital, from February 2014 to September 2021.ResultsOf the 20 cases, 13 were male (65.0%) and 7 were female (35.0%). The average age at onset was 35.35 ± 23.03days, with an average time from onset to diagnosis of 24.75 ± 15.55days. Tuberculin skin test (TST) was positive in 1 out of 4 cases (25.0%). Gamma interferon release assays (IGRAs) were positive in 9 out of 17 cases (52.9%).acid-fast staining was positive in 7 out of 16 cases (43.7%), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid polymerase-chain-reaction(PCR) was positive in 14 out of 20 cases (70.0%). Chest CT showed miliary changes in 4 out of 19 cases (21.0%), multiple nodular and patchy opacities in 6 out of 19 cases (31.6%), and pulmonary consolidation and atelectasis in 10 out of 19 cases (52.6%). After anti-tuberculosis treatment, 16 out of 20 cases (80.0%) improved, and no recurrence of tuberculosis was observed during follow-up periods ranging from 9 months to 3 years. The growth and development of these children were similar to those of healthy children.ConclusionThe clinical manifestations and chest imaging features of perinatal tuberculosis are nonspecific. In suspected cases, it is crucial to investigate the mother’s condition thoroughly and complete etiological examinations to achieve early diagnosis and timely treatment, which can improve prognosis.Keywords: Perinatal tuberculosis, Neonate, Diagnosis, Treatment

Introduction

Perinatal tuberculosis is a rare but serious disease where Mycobacterium tuberculosis can disseminate to multiple organs, posing a life-threatening risk [1, 2]. Early diagnosis and prompt treatment are essential to saving patients [3, 4]. Neonatal tuberculosis can be congenital or postnatally acquired. Given the difficulty in precisely determining the time and circumstances of infection, the term “perinatal tuberculosis” has been widely used to describe both types, specifically referring to tuberculosis acquired in utero, during birth, or shortly after birth [5, 6]. Beyond temporal definitions, the clinical symptoms of perinatal tuberculosis are atypical, making diagnosis challenging [7]. Delays in diagnosis and the absence of timely anti-tuberculosis therapy contribute to the high mortality associated with perinatal tuberculosis. This study retrospectively analyzes the clinical data of 20 cases of perinatal tuberculosis treated at Kunming Children’s Hospital from 2014 to 2021, providing reference for the diagnosis and treatment of similar cases.

Materials and methods

Study population

A retrospective study method was employed to collect the clinical data of 20 cases of perinatal tuberculosis diagnosed and treated at Kunming Children’s Hospital from February 2014 to September 2021.

Inclusion Criteria: Based on the revised criteria by Cantwell et al. [1]. in 1994 and related literature, patients were included if they met at least one of the following criteria, in addition to having a confirmed tuberculosis diagnosis: (1 Tuberculosis manifestation within 60 days of birth in full-term infants or corrected age for preterm infants; (2 Primary intrahepatic syndrome or intrahepatic caseous granulomas; (3 Maternal tuberculosis of the placenta or reproductive system; (4)Maternal diagnosis of smear-positive active pulmonary tuberculosis.

Exclusion Criteria:1) Postnatal infection possibly caused by confirmed tuberculosis in family members or healthcare workers, other than the mother;

2) Significant data loss; 3) Loss to follow-up.

Data extraction

General data of the children (gender, gestational age, birth weight, age at onset, BCG vaccination history, maternal tuberculosis history), clinical manifestations, major laboratory tests (including blood routine, hypersensitive C-reactive protein, liver function, tuberculin skin test, gamma interferon release assays, acid-fast staining, Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid PCR, cerebrospinal fluid routine and biochemistry), imaging examinations (including chest X-ray, chest computed tomography (CT), bronchoscopy, abdominal ultrasound, abdominal CT, cranial CT, cranial MRI), treatment, and prognosis were extracted.

Definition

Degree of liver function impairment: According to the 2019 guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of antituberculosis [8] drug-induced liver injury, the severity was classified as mild (grade 1), moderate (grade 2), and severe (grade 3).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25 were used for statistical analysis. The continuous variables of a normal distribution were compared by t-test and expressed as X ± standard deviation, while the continuous variables of a non-normal distribution were compared by Mann-Whitney test and expressed as the median of quartile [M (P25, p75)]. Categorical variables were analyzed by χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test and expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Outcome

General clinical data

Among the 20 pediatric patients, 13 were male (65.0%) and 7 were female (35.0%). The age at onset ranged from 0 to 84 days, with an average of 35.35 ± 23.03 days(see Table 1). The mean duration from disease onset to the diagnosis of perinatal tuberculosis was 24.75 ± 15.55 days. Natural conception occurred in 18 cases (90.0%), and in vitro fertilization was the method of conception in 2 cases (10.0%). Both full-term and preterm births were observed equally, each accounting for 10 cases (50.0%). Thirteen infants (65.0%) had a normal birth weight, whereas 7 infants (35.0%), all of whom were preterm, had low birth weight. Twelve infants (60%) had received the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccination, with no abnormal reactions noted at the vaccination site or in the ipsilateral lymph nodes.

Clinical manifestations

Of the 20 infants, 15 (75.0%) exhibited fever, with temperatures ranging from 37.8 to 40.0 °C. Thirteen cases (65.0%) presented with cough, 11 cases (55.0%) had moist rales in the lungs, 9 cases (45.0%) had tachypnea, respiratory distress, or cyanosis, 2 cases (1.0%) had pallor, 3 cases (15.0%) exhibited jaundice, 10 cases (50.0%) had hepatomegaly, 7 cases (35.0%) had hepatosplenomegaly, 1 case (5.0%) had lymphadenopathy, and 8 cases (40.0%) showed neurological symptoms such as irritability, hyperexcitability, and lethargy. Additionally, 6 cases (30.0%) had abdominal distension, 4 cases (20.0%) experienced vomiting, and 4 cases (20.0%) showed no weight gain after the onset of the illness.

The same group also demonstrated a significant decrease in neutrophil count and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein levels, while changes in lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, and platelet count were not statistically significant. However, 3 patients developed severe bone marrow suppression during treatment, characterized by significant reductions in white blood cells, hemoglobin, and platelets. See Table 2.

Immunological Diagnostics: Among the 4 patients who underwent the tuberculin skin test (TST), one case tested positive. Additionally, 17 patients were tested using the interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA), with 9 cases (52.9%) returning positive results.see Table 1.

Bacteriological and Molecular Diagnostics: Out of 16 patients who underwent acid-fast bacilli smear staining, 7 (43.7%) tested positive. Specifically, 6 out of 13 patients (46.2%) tested positive for acid-fast bacilli in sputum smears, and 1 out of 3 patients (33.3%) tested positive in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid smears. Among the 20 patients who underwent Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid PCR testing, 14 (70.0%) were positive. Of these, sputum PCR was positive in 11 cases, gastric aspirate PCR was positive in all 3 cases, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid PCR was positive in 5 out of 6 cases.see Table 1.

Cerebrospinal Fluid Biochemistry: Among the 17 patients who underwent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination, 5 (29.4%) had elevated CSF cell counts. One patient’s CSF showed a predominance of monocytes, with slightly reduced glucose and elevated protein levels, while the other 4 patients had normal glucose and protein levels, and their CSF was not further classified.

Chest CT scans were conducted on 19 patients, showing miliary changes in 4 cases (21.0%), multiple nodular and patchy consolidations in 6 cases (31.6%), pulmonary consolidation and atelectasis in 10 cases (52.6%), pleural effusion in 4 cases (21.0%), pleural thickening and adhesions in 7 cases (36.8%), and enlarged hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes in 3 cases (15.7%) (See Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Discussion

Tuberculosis remains a significant public health challenge in China and globally, posing a severe threat to human health. Among newly diagnosed tuberculosis cases, adult females constitute 33.0% of the patient population. Tuberculosis is relatively common among pregnant women. in high-burden countries, the prevalence of active tuberculosis in pregnant and postpartum women can reach as high as 60/100,000 per year, whereas in low-burden countries, the prevalence is below 20/100,000 per year, and often even lower than 10/100,000 per year [9]. Maternal tuberculosis poses a high risk of transmission to neonates and doubles the risk of preterm birth and low birth weight in infants. Cantwell et al. [1]. found that 60.0–70.0% of mothers of children with tuberculosis were asymptomatic, leading to a delay in the diagnosis of the mother compared to the child. In this study, 25.0% of the mothers exhibited symptoms and were diagnosed with tuberculosis before delivery, while 40% were diagnosed only after their child was diagnosed with perinatal tuberculosis, likely due to the mothers’ reluctance to undergo chest X-rays during pregnancy out of concern for fetal radiation exposure, thereby delaying the diagnosis for both mother and child.

Genital tuberculosis is a form of extrapulmonary tuberculosis and is a leading cause of primary infertility in women from countries where tuberculosis is endemic [10]. With the widespread use of assisted reproductive technologies, the incidence of tuberculosis during pregnancy has gradually increased, posing a serious threat to the health and safety of both the pregnant woman and the fetus [11]. Studies [12]have shown that tuberculosis following in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer, predominantly occurs within the first 8 to 12 weeks of pregnancy. Early pregnancy tuberculosis and its treatment with anti-tuberculosis drugs are unfavorable for pregnancy outcomes, often resulting in spontaneous or induced abortion.

Perinatal tuberculosis is a rare but severe form of tuberculosis, with neonatal mortality rates as high as 50.0% if untreated. Sen et al. [13].reported that the symptoms of congenital pulmonary tuberculosis typically manifest within the first three weeks of life, with a mean onset age of 28 days. However, Vogel et al. [14].noted that some infants with congenital pulmonary tuberculosis do not develop symptoms until three months or later, with the longest duration from birth to symptom onset being 154 days, which is consistent with the report by Schaaf et al. [15]. In this study, the onset age of perinatal tuberculosis ranged from 1 to 84 days, with a median onset age of 35.35 days, and 40% of the children developed the disease within the first four weeks of life.

Before treatment, the children in this study exhibited elevated white blood cell counts, neutrophils, and C-reactive protein levels, similar to sepsis caused by other neonatal infections, indicating a strong inflammatory response. Neutrophils are the first innate immune cells to migrate from the blood to the site of infection in response to inflammatory stimuli [16]. Upon activation, neutrophils secrete large amounts of chemokines and cytokines to combat the invasion of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Additionally, Muefong et al. [17].demonstrated that an increase in neutrophils is closely related to the severity of tuberculosis. In this study, three children presented with significant splenomegaly, and two of them developed pancytopenia. However, bone marrow cytology was not performed, leaving the cause of pancytopenia unclear. Based on clinical symptoms and treatment outcomes, it is hypothesized that bone marrow infiltration by Mycobacterium tuberculosis may be responsible. Therefore, in cases where pancytopenia occurs, bone marrow cytology and histiocytic disease-related examinations are recommended to further clarify the etiology.

Among the children in this study, six exhibited hepatic dysfunction before treatment, with abdominal ultrasound and/or CT scans revealing hepatomegaly in four of these cases. The hepatic impairment observed in these perinatal tuberculosis patients is likely associated with hematogenous dissemination of tuberculosis. Following anti-tuberculosis treatment, 13 children developed varying degrees of hepatic dysfunction, which normalized after the discontinuation of hepatotoxic anti-tuberculosis drugs and the administration of hepatoprotective therapy. This underscores the critical importance of drug selection and liver function monitoring in ensuring the safety of treatment. Thus, it is recommended that after the diagnosis of perinatal tuberculosis, the choice of anti-tuberculosis regimen should be made based on liver function and disease severity, with consideration given to discontinuing hepatotoxic drugs until hepatoprotective treatment proves effective before reintroducing anti-tuberculosis medications.

In this study, 70.0% of the 20 children tested positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA via sputum PCR, and all four gastric aspirate samples were positive. The higher positivity rate in gastric aspirates compared to sputum is likely due to the tendency of infants to swallow rather than expectorate sputum, suggesting that gastric aspirates may be a more reliable specimen for pathogen detection in infants and young children. Seventeen children underwent gamma interferon release assays, with a positive rate of 52.9%, which is lower than that of molecular biological tests, possibly due to the young age and immature immune function of the patients. Therefore, even if the gamma interferon release assay is negative in infants, tuberculosis cannot be ruled out. Additionally, cerebrospinal fluid examination in 17 children revealed elevated white blood cell counts in five cases, which may be associated with the incomplete development of the blood-brain barrier in young children and the hematogenous dissemination characteristic of perinatal tuberculosis.

In this study, abdominal ultrasound in all 20 children revealed hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and multiple hypoechoic nodules in some patients, strongly suspected to be tuberculous lesions. It is hypothesized that Mycobacterium tuberculosis forms intrauterine infection foci in the fetal liver via the umbilical vein. During follow-up, calcification of the liver was observed, further supporting the pathological features of tuberculous lesions.

The recommended treatment regimen for neonatal tuberculosis in China includes isoniazid (H) and rifampicin (R), with isoniazid administered for over a year and rifampicin for 9 months to a year. For severe cases, ethambutol (E) or pyrazinamide (Z) may be added. In this study, treatment plans were tailored based on the severity of the disease and liver function. Six children received the H-R-Z-E quadruple regimen, 11 were treated with H-R-E, 2 with H-R-Z, and 1 with H-E. For nine critically ill children with disseminated tuberculosis, linezolid was added, and two of these also received levofloxacin. After initiating treatment, 12 children developed liver dysfunction, leading to the discontinuation of pyrazinamide and the administration of hepatoprotective agents such as reduced glutathione and glucuronolactone. Additionally, 16 children received anti-inflammatory therapy with methylprednisolone or prednisone. Among the 20 children in this study, 17 adhered to regular treatment, and 16 were successfully cured after 12 to 18 months of therapy, with growth and development comparable to their peers. One child, currently under regular follow-up, has been treated for 9 months. Furthermore, 17 children were regularly monitored for fundus and vision during and after ethambutol therapy, with no cases of retrobulbar optic neuritis observed. This suggests that ethambutol is relatively safe for perinatal tuberculosis, especially since it lacks hepatotoxicity, making it a good choice for children with liver dysfunction.

Treatment durations varied based on the severity of the disease and response to therapy, with most cases requiring prolonged anti-tuberculosis therapy. After treatment, 16 of the 20 children showed significant improvement, and during follow-up (9 months to 3 years), no relapses were noted. These findings highlight the effectiveness of early, tailored anti-tuberculosis treatment in improving the prognosis of perinatal tuberculosis patients. In conclusion, perinatal tuberculosis, although rare, presents a serious health risk to neonates. Its clinical manifestations are nonspecific, often mimicking other neonatal infections, which can lead to delays in diagnosis and treatment. Early identification through thorough maternal screening, use of advanced diagnostic tools like PCR, and prompt initiation of anti-tuberculosis therapy are critical to improving outcomes. Careful monitoring of liver function and adjusting drug regimens based on individual responses are essential in managing these vulnerable patients.

However, this study is limited by its small sample size, as it is based on patients from a single hospital, which may not be representative. Additionally, our hospital currently lacks a qualified laboratory for Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture, drug susceptibility testing, and GeneXpert testing, thus no specimens could be tested for these.

Conclusion

The clinical manifestations and chest imaging characteristics of perinatal tuberculosis are nonspecific. For suspected cases, it is important to thoroughly evaluate the mother’s condition and perform comprehensive pathogen testing to ensure early diagnosis and prompt treatment, which generally leads to a favorable prognosis.
