Some limit theorems for locally stationary Hawkes processes
Thomas Deschatre 1 , Pierre Gruet 1 , Antoine Lotz 1,2,†

Abstract
. We prove a law of large numbers and functional central limit theorem for a class of multivariate Hawkes processes with time-dependent reproduction rate. We address the difficulties induced by the use of non-convolutive Volterra processes by recombining classical martingale methods introduced in Bacry et al. [ 3 ] with novel ideas proposed by Kwan et al. [ 19 ] . The asymptotic theory we obtain yields useful applications in financial statistics. As an illustration, we derive closed-form expressions for price distortions under liquidity constraints. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) : 60F05, 60G55, 62M10, 62P05. Keywords : Hawkes processes. Locally stationary processes. Limit Theorems.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation and relation to other works

Hawkes processes are a class of self-interacting point processes. Their applications span numerous fields ranging from neuroscience
[
24
]

[
5
]
to financial econometrics
[
4
]

[
13
]
. In all generality, a multivariate point process
(\boldsymbol{N}_{t})=(N_{1,t},\ldots,N_{p,t})
records discrete event-times
(t^{k}_{i})
occurring in continuous time along
p
components according to

(1)

N_{k,t}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\{t^{k}_{i}\leq t\}}.

The law of such process is characterised by its predictable intensity
\lambda_{t}=(\lambda_{1,t},\cdots,\lambda_{p,t})
, informally understood as an instantaneous probability of event-time arrival

(2)

\mathbb{P}\big[N_{k,t}\textup{ jumps in }[t,t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t]\big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{t}\big]=\lambda_{k,t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
is a history of the process. The intensity of the Hawkes process typically takes the form

\lambda_{k,t}=\Phi_{k}\Big[\mu_{k}+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{kl}(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{l,s}\Big],

where the
\Phi_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+}
are
activation functions
, the
\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}

baseline intensities
and the function
\varphi=(\varphi_{kl})\colon[0;\infty)\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
the
kernel
of the process. We refer to section
2
for a rigorous definition. When
p=1
and
\Phi_{1}(x)=x
, the dynamic of event-times arrival is that of a population process with Poisson immigration at intensity
\mu_{1}
and Galton-Watson reproduction at rate
\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
(see Hawkes & Oakes
[
16
]
). Under the condition
\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)}<1
, the process is known to reach stationarity as
T\to\infty
, yielding useful approximations and convergence guarantees in statistical inference procedure (see e.g Ogata
[
23
]
). It proves limiting in practice however, as the distribution of the phenomenon at hand often fluctuates in time.

Generalisations of the Hawkes process have thus arisen in an effort to accommodate time- dependent dynamics. The process is then most often coerced to live on a compact timeframe
[0,T]
and depends on the double index
(t,T)
. Chen & Hall
[
9
]
first considered an univariate linear Hawkes process with varying baseline
t\in[0,T]\mapsto\mu(\frac{t}{T})\in[0,\infty)
, allowing the rate of cluster formation to oscillate in time. Roueff
et al.

[
26
]

[
25
]
further generalised the model, allowing
\varphi
to depend on
\frac{t}{T}
as well – resulting in the so-called
locally stationary
Hawkes process. This paper explores asymptotic properties of a class of locally stationary Hawkes processes with intensities

(3)

\lambda^{T}_{k,t}=\mu_{k}\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)+\int_{0}^{t}g\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)\varphi_{kl}(t-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{l,s},\hskip 5.69046ptt\in[0,T],

where, for any
k,l=1\ldots p
,
\mu_{k}\colon[0,1]\mapsto[0,\infty)
,
g\colon[0,1]\mapsto[0,\infty)
and
\varphi_{kl}\colon[0,\infty)\mapsto[0,\infty)
. The asymptotic behaviour of the process is well-known in the standard case where
g
is constant. Functional limit theorems were first proved by Bacry
et al.

[
3
]
for the multivariate linear Hawkes process with constant baseline
(\mu_{k})
, and later extended by Deschatre & Gruet
[
13
]
to varying baselines. Their results take the form of a uniform convergence for the normalised process
T^{-1}(N_{Tu})_{u\in[0,1]}
and of the weak convergence of the rescaled process
T^{-\frac{1}{2}}(N_{Tu}-\mathbb{E}[N_{Tu}])_{u\in[0,1]}
towards a diffusive limit. Analogous results were proven under the mild finite first moment assumption
\int_{0}^{\infty}t\lVert\varphi\rVert(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty
for non linear activation functions and a constant baseline, by Zhu
[
27
]
in the univariate strictly self-exciting case
\varphi>0
and by Cattiaux
et al.

[
8
]
in the more general multivariate and possibly inhibiting case. Strong mixing rates covering the varying baseline case were moreover obtained by Cheysson
et al.

[
10
]
under a similar assumption. The asymptotic theory of the varying baseline case is completed by the three recent works of Kwan
et al.

[
19
]

[
21
]

[
20
]
, whom derive general ergodic theorems for statistical inference motives, and introduce a general approximation strategy for non stationary point processes.

Regarding varying kernels finally, a central limit Theorem was obtained by Fierro
et al.

[
14
]
in the context of a cluster-like construction of
(N_{t})
, wherein the kernel is replaced at each generation by a new function. As for locally stationary Hawkes processes, neither functional approximations nor ergodic Theorems are known, owing perhaps to the difficulty associated with treating non-convolutive Volterra processes.

1.2.
Main contribution

We aim to reconcile the model (
3
) with the general asymptotic theory for Hawkes processes.
Our main results in section
3
are a functional law of large number (Theorem
1
) and a central limit Theorem (Theorem
2
). Theorem
1
states the uniform convergence

\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{T}N_{Tu}-\int_{0}^{u}(\boldsymbol{Id}-\boldsymbol{K}(x))^{-1}\mu(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\Big\rVert\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}0,

where
\boldsymbol{K}(x)=g(x)\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
, and can be regarded as a generalisation of the law of large numbers of Bacry
et al.

[
3
]
and of the ergodic Theorem of Kwan
et al.

[
21
]
. Theorem
2
establishes the weak convergence in Skorokhod topology of the rescaled process
T^{-\frac{1}{2}}\{N_{Tu}-\mathbb{E}\big[N_{Tu}]\}
,
u\in[0,1]
, towards the diffusive limit

(4)

\int_{0}^{u}(\boldsymbol{Id}-\boldsymbol{K}(x))^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{x},

where
(W_{x})
is a standard Brownian motion, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
maps
x\in[0,1]
to the diagonal matrix with coefficients
(\boldsymbol{Id}-\boldsymbol{K}(x))^{-1}\mu(x)
. This again extends the limit Theorems of Bacry
et al.

[
3
]
, and further generalises their prior extension by Deschatre & Gruet
[
13
]
. Our framework however differs from these two prior approaches, in that the kernel
\kappa(t,s)=g(\nicefrac{{t}}{{T}})\varphi(t-s)
escapes the convolution-specific techniques on which they depend. We rely instead on the same broad principle as Kwan, Chen & Dunsmuir
[
21
]
. More specifically, while the technical basis for our proofs differs fundamentally from the coupling-like arguments in
[
21
]
, we find that their approximation strategy based on a grid refining at rate
o(T)
can be repurposed to produce asymptotic results for the class of non-convolutive Volterra resolvent associated with our kernel. We isolate in Proposition
3
this deterministic result, from which Theorem
1
deduces. Obtaining Theorem
2
is more involved. The key to the proof consists in remarking the sampling scheme in Theorem 3 of Bacry
et al.

[
3
]
refines at the exact same speed as the approximation grid of the
kcd
approach. This analogy proves quite productive, as it enables a careful recombination of the two approaches from which Theorem
2
follows.

2.
Locally stationary Hawkes processes

2.1.
Definition.

We work on a rich enough probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
where a family of integer-valued random measures
\boldsymbol{N}=(N_{1},\cdots,N_{p})
on the real Borel sets
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
are defined by

N_{k}(C)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\{t_{n}^{k}\in C\}},

where
(t^{1}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}},\ldots,(t^{p}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
are random sequences with values in
[0,\infty]
, such that
0<t^{k}_{n}<t^{k}_{n+1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
and
k=1\ldots p
.
The multivariate point process
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}=(N_{1,t},\cdots,N_{p,t})
with counting measures
\boldsymbol{N}
is then defined by
N_{k,t}=N_{k}([0,t])
consistently with (
1
). We will also write
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}N_{k,s}
for the measure
N_{k}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)
. The history of
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}
is the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
generated by the process until
t
, that is
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\{N(C)\hskip 2.84544pt\lvert\hskip 2.84544ptC\subset[0,t]\cap\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}
. For any
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-progressively-measurable process
\lambda_{t}=(\lambda_{1,t},\cdots,\lambda_{p,t})
,
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}
is said to admit
(\lambda_{t})
as its intensity if, for any
t>s>0
,
k=1\ldots p
,

(5)

\mathbb{E}\big[N_{k}((s,t])\big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{t}\big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{s}^{t}\lambda_{k,u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big].

Up to some extension of the underlying probability space, we may suppose
p
independent Poisson measures
\pi_{1},\cdots\pi_{p}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with unit intensity are defined on
(\Omega,\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
, and denote by
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\pi})
their natural filtration. We then have the following existence result (see Brémaud & Massoulié
[
6
]
).

Proposition 1
.

For any
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\pi})
–progressively measurable process
(\lambda_{k,t})
, the process defined by

(6)

N_{k,t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\{x\leq\lambda_{k,t}\}}\pi_{k,t}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)

admits
(\lambda_{k,t})
as its intensity.

The process
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}
is then said to be
embedded
into the Poisson base
\pi
. The integral in (
3
) is taken over
[0,t)
with
t
specifically excluded from its domain. The system

\begin{cases}N^{T}_{k,t}&=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\{x\leq\lambda_{k,s}\}}\pi_{k,s}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)\\
\lambda^{T}_{k,t}&=\mu_{k}\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{[0,t)}g\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)\varphi_{kl}(t-s)N_{l}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s),\end{cases}

recursively defines
(\lambda_{k,t})
at each
t\in[0,T]
as a function of the past jumps of the Poisson base
(\pi_{k})
. Proposition
1
therefore ensures the well-posedness of the following definition.

Definition 1
.

A locally stationary Hawkes process is any point process
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}
of the form (
6
) with intensity

\lambda_{k,t}=\mu_{k}\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{[0,t)}g\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)\varphi_{kl}(t-s)N_{l}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s).

Definition
1
transmits certain properties of the Poisson base to the process itself. Two distinct coordinates of
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}
are in particular
\mathbb{P}
-a.s prevented from jumping simultaneously. Definition (
5
) of the intensity also implies the process

(7)

\boldsymbol{M}_{t}=(M_{k,t}):=\Big(N_{k,t}-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{k,s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big),\hskip 2.84544ptt\in[0,T],

is a local martingale. In fact, for any predictable process
(H_{k,t})
such that,
\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{t}\lvert H_{k,s}\rvert N_{k}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)]<\infty
for any
k=1\ldots p
, one has

\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{t}H_{k,s}N_{l}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{t}H_{k,s}\lambda_{k,s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big\lvert\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big],

and we refer the reader to Daley & Vere-Jones
[
11
]
and Brémaud
[
7
]
for a comprehensive introduction to point processes with stochastic intensities. Taking
H_{k,\cdot}
to be the indicator function
\mathbb{1}_{[t,t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t]}
of some interval
[t,t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t]
, this implies (

).

2.2.
Stability of the process.

For any
d\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
and any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{q}
the
\ell^{q}
-norm
\lVert x\rVert_{q}=(\sum_{i}^{d}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{q})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}

\sup_{x\in(a,b)}\lVert f(x)\rVert_{1}

from the notation where there is no ambiguity. Likewise, we use the notation
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{q}}=(\int_{(a,b)}\lVert f(x)\rVert_{q}^{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}
for the
L^{q}(a,b)
-norm. Finally,
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is the set of

Assumption 1
.

The baseline and reproduction functions
\mu\colon[0,1]\mapsto[0,\infty)^{p}
,
g\colon[0,1]\mapsto[0,\infty)
are respectively continuous and continuously differentiable over
[0,1]
.

Assumption 2

(Stability)
.

For any
k,l=1\ldots p
,
\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{kl}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty
, and the matrix with coefficients

\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{kl}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Big)

has spectral radius below
1
.

Remark 1
.

When
\mu
and

are constants, Assumption

reduces to the classical stability condition in Bacry et al.
[
3
]
or Brémaud & Massoulié
[
6
]
. Note also that while we have not used the cluster representation of Roueff et al.
[

]
, Assumption

is comparable to
[
26

.

For any
k,l=1\ldots p
, define the integrable function

(8)

\bar{\varphi}_{kl}\colon t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\varphi_{kl}(t),

and, accordingly,
\bar{\varphi}=(\bar{\varphi}_{kl})\colon[0,\infty)\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
. Assumption
2
guarantees the stability of the process in the sense that

for any bounded

and any

. The random measures

are then said to be locally finite. This is clear from the fact the jumps

of

[

. In particular, one has Lemma

Lemma 1
.

Suppose Assumptions

2
hold. Then, the counting measures

With all existence guarantees provided we proceed to our main results.

Let

(Law of Large Numbers)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1

2

\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\Big\lVert\frac{1}{T}N_{Tu}^{T}-\int_{0}^{u}(\boldsymbol{Id}-\boldsymbol{K}(x))^{-1}\mu(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\Big\rVert\to 0

and
\mathbb{P}

As we move to the weak convergence of the rescaled process, Assumption

Assumption 3
.

There exists
\epsilon\in(0,\infty]

\varphi\in L^{1+\epsilon}[0,\infty)

Theorem 2

(Central limit theorem)

Under Assumption
1
to
2

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\big\{N_{Tu}^{T}-\mathbb{E}[N_{Tu}^{T}]\big\}\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{P})}\int_{0}^{u}(\boldsymbol{Id}-\boldsymbol{K}(s))^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}

.

4.
Application to financial statistics

4.1.
A model for prices under liquidity constraints

We revisit the microstructure model of Bacry
et al.

[
4
]
. Suppose one observes the price
(P_{t})
of an asset over
t\in[0,T]
. In the context of high-frequency trading,
(P_{t})
is recorded at the tick-by-tick level and the price trajectory bears a discrete nature. A similar property is retained at lower frequencies by the less liquid assets. A fitting models class then takes the form
P_{t}=N^{+}_{t}-N^{-}_{t}
, where
(N^{+}_{t})
, respectively
(N^{-}_{t})
, is a pure jump process recording positive, respectively negative, price increments. In
[
3
]
,
(N^{+}_{t},N^{-}_{t})
is a bivariate Hawkes process with baseline intensity and kernel

(9)

\mu=\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{+}\\
\mu^{-}\end{bmatrix}\textup{ and }\varphi(t)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha\\
\alpha&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\Phi(t,\beta),

where
\Phi(t,\beta)=\beta\exp(-\beta t)
with
(\beta,\alpha)\in[0,\infty)\times[0,1)
, and
\mu^{+}=\mu^{-}
. The antidiagonal structure of
\varphi
mimics microstucture noise and introduces reversion effects in price dynamics, meaning that an upward price increment is more likely followed by a downward price move and conversely, see the references in
[
3
]
. Such behaviour is expected in the presence of a diversified pool of traders implementing broadly uncorrelated strategies. It may also be partially attributed to market makers quoting at both sides of the order book. Should a large fraction of all participants retreat from the market, one would expect
\alpha
to decrease as the remaining traders’ views may align more often. For low values of
\alpha
, prices tend to move in the same direction one wishes to execute in, resulting in a worse outcome for liquidity takers. In this sense, the reproduction rate
\alpha
measures the rate at which liquidity replenishes.

That participation in the market may vary as time passes is not a purely theoretical scenario. In the context of commodities futures, some agents may be specifically adverse to the physical risk associated with taking delivery, and the typology of traders involved in the market may thus evolve as expiry looms by. What is empirically known and recognised as a stylised fact of commodities markets is the existence of the
Samuelson effect
, which describes a decreasing relation between volatility and time to maturity. We will return to this precise matter further down this section, and refer for now to Jaeck & Lautier
[
17
]
and Aïd
et al.

[
1
]
for more details.

We consider situations where some price-insensitive directional flow moves
(P_{t})
, corresponding to an imbalance
\mu^{+}\neq\mu^{-}
in
\mu

t\to T
with

(10)

\alpha(x)=\alpha_{0}(1-\eta x^{2}),

. The function
\alpha

\alpha(0)=\alpha_{0}

\alpha(1)=(1-\eta)\alpha_{0}<\alpha(0)

Remark 4

The proof scheme for Proposition

Remark 5
.

The term "slippage" is borrowed from the financial literature, where it refers to the
difference between the price at which one intends to execute a marginal order, and the price at which it is actually filled. Simple models express slippage as a function of volatility, and the present one does in fact establish such a link. However,
\alpha
being a rate of change rather than an absolute measure of liquidity, the relation is not quite straightforward. From the same arguments as Bacry et al.
[
3
]
, the limit volatility
\sigma
of
(P_{t})
expresses as

\sigma(u)=\frac{\mu^{+}+\mu^{-}}{(1-\alpha(u))(1+\alpha(u))^{2}},

a decreasing function of
\alpha
for
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{3}
and an increasing function for
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{3}
. The somewhat indirect link with volatility suggests some form of trade-off. When
\alpha
decreases, directional price movement become more persistent, and
\sigma
decreases too. If
\alpha
decreases further however, the amplitude of directional variations increases beyond a certain threshold, and the relation reverts.

A consequence of remark
5
is that variations in
\alpha
primarily affect the persistence of trends in the dynamics of
(P_{t})
, whilst their relation with volatility is more ambiguous. Hence Proposition
2
should
not
be understood as a fundamental basis for the Samuelson effect. An increase in volatility as
t\to T
is better represented by an increase in the amplitude of
\mu(x)
as
x\to 1
, which bears an unequivocal amplifying effect on
\sigma
, and which our results do allow. We refer to Deschatre & Gruet
[
13
]
for such a model, where
\mu(x)\propto\exp(\kappa x)
for some
\kappa>0
. In conclusion, a price model of the type (
9
) will only clearly attribute the Samuelson effect to exogenous price moves, and we expect that this characteristic reflects actual market dynamics to some degree. While we are not immediately able to confront our results with market data, we may still evaluate our approach via numerical simulations, to which we now proceed.

5.
Discussion

We have established in section
3
novel functional law of large number and central limit Theorem for locally stationary Hawkes processes, and presented in Section
4
some of their applications to statistical finance. The theoretical side of our work opens some interesting perspectives. Theorem
2
provides a proof of the existence of a globally defined weak limit for a class of Hawkes processes admitting no stationary distribution, such feature being of possible interest in the context of statistical inference. It is also possible to envision a
marked
extension of Theorem
2
, within which external covariates are allowed to influence the law of the process. As a matter of fact, the introduction of a time-dependent reproduction rate may be regarded as the adjunction of non-stationary deterministic marks to the kernel of the process.

Regarding the applied part of the article, we acknowledge that Proposition
2
is dependent upon our supposition of a quadratic decay in the amplitude of the reproduction rate. Some further statistical work and estimation on market data are thus required to assess the credibility of such hypothesis. We refer to Roueff
et al.

[
25
]
and Mammen & Müller
[
22
]
for existing developments on the matter.

6.1.
On Volterra equations

We first recall, mainly from Gripenberg
[
15
, Chapter 9]
, some classical results and notations for Volterra integral equations in general, and renewal equations in particular. A Volterra kernel is any function
k\colon(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
such that
k(t,s)=0
when
s>t
. If
a
and
b
are two Volterra kernels and
f
some Borel function over
[0,T]
, the
\star
products over Volterra kernels are defined by

\displaystyle(a\star b)(t,s)=\int_{s}^{t}a(t,u)b(u,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\hskip 5.69046pt\textup{and}\hskip 5.69046pt(a\star f)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}a(t,u)f(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

We will be concerned with functional equations of the form

x(t)=m(t)+k\star x(t),\hskip 2.84544ptt\in[0,T],

where
k\colon[0,T]^{2}\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
is a Volterra kernel and
m\colon[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{p}
a bounded function. When it is defined, the resolvent of a Volterra kernel
k
is the series
K=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}k^{\star i}
where
k^{\star i}
is the
i
-fold
\star
product of
k
with itself, with the convention
k^{\star 1}=k
. In the sequel, we will work with the kernel

k^{T}(t,s)=g\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)\varphi(t-s),

where we have identified
\varphi
to a function of
\mathbb{R}
with support in
[0,\infty)
. The kernel
k^{T}
verifies for any
(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}
the coordinate-wise inequality
k^{T}(t,s)\leq\bar{\varphi}(t-s)
, where the bounding kernel
\bar{\varphi}
is defined in (
8
). Thus
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(k^{T})^{\star i}(t,s)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\varphi}^{\star i}(t-s)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
. As
\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\varphi}^{\star i}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=(\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\varphi}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)^{i}
, the series of the
\bar{\varphi}^{\star i}
converges in
L^{1}[0,\infty)
. Hence the series of the
(k^{T})^{\star i}(t,s)
converges for almost every
(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}
, and the kernel
k^{T}
admits a well defined resolvent
K^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(k^{T})^{\star i}
.

Lemma 2

(Theorem 3.6 in Chapter 9 of Gripenberg
[
15
]
)
.

Let
T>0
, and
m\colon[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{p}

such that

also verifies

. If furthermore equality holds in (

Remark 6
.

The notation

is consistent with the usual convolution product, which corresponds to the special case where the values

and

of the two Volterra kernels depend only on

Remark 7

When

Lemma 4

Suppose

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\{(k^{T})^{\star(n+1)}(t+h,s+h)

\displaystyle-(k^{T})^{\star(n+1)}(t,s)\}=

\displaystyle+

otherwise, one recovers the coordinate-wise renewal inequality

f^{T}(t,x,s)\leq m^{T}(t,x,s)+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\varphi}(u)f^{T}(t-u,x,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u,\hskip 2.84544ptt\in[0,T].

Inequation (
30
) resolves into

f^{T}(t,x,s)\leq m^{T}(t,x,s)+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\Psi}(t-u)m^{T}(u,x,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u,\hskip 2.84544ptt\in[0,T],

where
\bar{\Psi}=\sum_{i\geq 1}^{\infty}\bar{\varphi}^{\star i}
is the resolvent of the convolution kernel
t,s\mapsto\bar{\varphi}(t-s)
. Integrating, using Fubini’s Theorem and Young’s convolution inequality,

\displaystyle\big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}f^{T}(t,x,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\big\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}m^{T}(t,x,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\Psi}(t-u)m^{T}(u,x,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\big\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}m^{T}(t,x,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\Psi}(t-u)\Big(\int_{0}^{T}m^{T}(u,x,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\big\rVert

\displaystyle\leq(1+\int_{0}^{\infty}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}m^{T}(u,x,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\big\rVert.

The Lemma follows from the last multiplicative factor hereinabove tending to
0
as
x\to 0
, as we now establish. Let
u\in[0,T]
. Firstly,

\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Big(g\big(\frac{u+x}{T}\big)-g\big(\frac{u}{T}\big)\Big)\varphi(u+x-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\rVert\leq\omega(g,x)\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{1}},

where the modulus of continuity
\omega(g,x)
of
g
at
x
goes to
0
as
x\to 0
.
Secondly,

(31)

\displaystyle g(\frac{u}{T})\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(u+x-s)-\varphi(u-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=g(\frac{u}{T})\int_{u}^{u+x}\varphi(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

By Hölder’s inequality we have some
\nu\geq 1
such that
\nu^{-1}+(1+\epsilon)^{-1}=1
and, for any
v>0
,
\lVert\int_{v}^{v+x}\varphi(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\rVert\leq x^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{1+\epsilon}[0,\infty)}
, hence

\displaystyle\lVert g(\frac{u}{T})\int_{0}^{T}\varphi(u+x-s)-\varphi(u-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\rVert\leq x^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}[0,1]}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{1+\epsilon}[0,\infty)}\xrightarrow[x\to 0]{}0.

Applying again Young’s convolution inequality to the third term on the right-hand side of (
29
) yields a bound of the form

\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Big(g\big(\frac{u+t}{T}\big)-g\big(\frac{u}{T}\big)\Big)\int_{s}^{u+x}\varphi(u+x-v)K(v,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big\rVert\leq\omega(g,x)\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{1}[0,\infty)}\lVert\bar{\Psi}\rVert_{L^{1}[0,\infty)},

which goes to
0
as
x\to\infty
. Likewise, using Fubini’s Theorem with the fourth term,

\lVert\int_{0}^{T}g(\frac{u}{T})\int_{s}^{s+x}\varphi(u+x-v)K(v,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\rVert\leq\lVert\bar{\varphi}\rVert_{L^{1}[0,\infty)}\int_{u-x}^{u}\rVert\bar{\Psi}(u-v)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v,

where
\int_{u-x}^{u}\rVert\bar{\Psi}(u-v)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v=\int_{0}^{x}\rVert\bar{\Psi}(v)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\to 0
as
T\to\infty
, ending the proof.

∎

Proposition 4
.

Work under Assumptions
1
to
3
. The family of processes

\Big\{T^{-\frac{1}{2}}(N^{T}_{Tu}-\mathbf{E}[N_{Tu}]),\hskip 2.84544ptu\in[0,1]\Big\}_{T>0}

is tight in
D[0,1]
equipped with the topology of uniform convergence.

Proof.

Recall that for any
T>0
and
u\in[0,1]
,

(32)

N^{T}_{Tu}-\mathbf{E}[N_{Tu}]=M_{Tu}^{T}+\int_{0}^{Tu}\int_{0}^{t}K^{T}(t,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}M^{T}_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=M^{T}_{Tu}+Y^{T}_{Tu}.

The martingale
T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(M^{T}_{Tu})
is tight as per its convergence in uniform topology towards a
\mathbb{P}
-a.s continuous limit. Hence it suffices to show the tightness of
T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(Y^{T}_{Tu})
, which reads

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sup_{\lvert u-v\rvert<\delta}\lVert X^{T}_{Tv}-X^{T}_{Tu}\rVert>\eta\Big]=0\textup{ for any }\eta>0.

For any
u\in[0,1]
then, integrating by parts,

\displaystyle Y^{T}_{Tv}=\int_{0}^{Tu}\Big(K^{T}(Tu,s)-\int_{s}^{Tu}(\partial_{s}+\partial_{t})K^{T}(t,s)\Big)\frac{M^{T}_{s}}{\sqrt{T}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Hence for any
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
,

\displaystyle Y^{T}_{Tv}-Y^{T}_{Tu}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{Tu}\Big(\int_{Tu}^{Tv}(\partial_{s}+\partial_{t})K^{T}(t,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Big)\frac{M^{T}_{s}}{\sqrt{T}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int_{Tu}^{Tv}\Big(\int_{s}^{Tv}(\partial_{s}+\partial_{t})K^{T}(t,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Big)\frac{M^{T}_{s}}{\sqrt{T}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{Tv}K^{T}(Tv,s)\frac{M^{T}_{s}}{\sqrt{T}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{Tu}K^{T}(Tu,s)\frac{M^{T}_{s}}{\sqrt{T}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

\displaystyle=\textbf{{i}}+\textbf{{ii}}+\textbf{{iii}}

Let
\delta>0
. Let
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
such that
0\leq v-u\leq\delta
. Firstly,

\textbf{{i}}\leq\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\lVert M^{T}_{s}\rVert\int_{Tu}^{Tv}T^{-1}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\lVert\tilde{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(\delta).

Secondly, for any
T>0
,

\textbf{{ii}}\leq\sup_{s\in[Tu,Tv]}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\lVert M^{T}_{s}\rVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{Tu}^{\infty}\lVert\tilde{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Let
\eta\in[0,1)
. When
v\in[0,\eta]
,

\textbf{{ii}}\leq\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sup_{s\in[0,\eta T]}\lVert M^{T}_{s}\rVert_{1}=\mathcal{O}(\eta),

whereas if
v\in(\eta,1]
,

\textbf{{ii}}\leq\Big(\int_{Tu}^{\infty}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\lVert M^{T}_{s}\rVert_{1}=\mathcal{O}\big(\int^{\infty}_{(\eta-\delta)T}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\big).

The second term
ii
is thus arbitrarily small as
T\to\infty
. We may in fact proceed in a similar fashion for the third and remaining term. Let
\eta\in(0,1]
. On one hand,

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{T(v\land\eta)}^{Tv}K^{T}(Tv,Tv-s)\frac{M^{T}_{Tv-s}}{\sqrt{T}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\int_{T(v\land\eta)}^{Tv}K^{T}(Tv,Tv-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\rVert\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\frac{M^{T}_{Tv-s}}{\sqrt{T}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\int_{T(v\land\eta)}^{Tv}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\frac{M^{T}_{t}}{\sqrt{T}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{T\eta}^{\infty}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\frac{M^{T}_{t}}{\sqrt{T}}\rVert,

and likewise,

\displaystyle\lVert\int_{T(u\land\eta)}^{Tu}K^{T}(Tu,Tu-s)\frac{M^{T}_{Tu-s}}{\sqrt{T}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\rVert\leq\Big(\int_{T(\eta-\delta)}^{\infty}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\frac{M^{T}_{t}}{\sqrt{T}}\rVert,

where
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}M^{T}_{t}\rVert=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and the two bounds hereinabove vanish as
T\to\infty
. On the other,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T(u\land\eta)}K^{T}(Tu,Tu-s)\frac{M^{T}_{Tu-s}}{\sqrt{T}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\Big(\int_{0}^{T(u\land\eta)}K^{T}(Tu,Tu-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\frac{M^{T}_{Tu}}{\sqrt{T}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\sup_{\lvert x-y\rvert\leq\eta}\Big\lVert\frac{M^{T}_{Tx}-M^{T}_{Ty}}{\sqrt{T}}\Big\rVert,

which can be made arbitrarily small as
\eta
is chosen close to
0
on account of the tightness of
(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}M^{T}_{Tu})
. A similar Cesàro argument shows that

\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\lVert\Big(\int_{0}^{T(x\land\eta)}K^{T}(Tx,Tx-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\frac{M^{T}_{Tx}}{\sqrt{T}}-\Big(\int_{0}^{T}K^{T}(Tx,Tx-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\frac{M^{T}_{Tx}}{\sqrt{T}}\rVert\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0,

and one retrieves

\sup_{\lvert u-v\rvert<\delta}\Big\lVert\textbf{{iii}}-\Big\{\Big(\int_{0}^{T}K^{T}(Tv,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\frac{M_{Tv}}{\sqrt{T}}-\Big(\int_{0}^{T}K^{T}(Tu,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\frac{M_{Tu}}{\sqrt{T}}\Big\}\Big\rVert\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

Then,
\lVert(\int_{0}^{T}K^{T}(Tv,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}M^{T}_{Tv}-(\int_{0}^{T}K^{T}(Tu,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}M^{T}_{Tu}\rVert
is bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{\lvert x-y\rvert\leq\delta}\big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}K^{T}(Ty,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{T}K^{T}(Tx,s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\big\rVert

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\frac{M^{T}_{t}}{\sqrt{T}}\rVert

\displaystyle+\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\lVert\bar{\Psi}(s)\rVert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big)\sup_{\lvert x-y\rvert\leq\delta}\Big\lVert\frac{M^{T}_{Tx}-M^{T}_{Ty}}{\sqrt{T}}\Big\rVert,

for any
T>0
and any
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
such that
\lvert v-u\rvert<\delta
. The desired result then deduces from Lemma
18
and the tightness of

(T^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}M^{T}_{Tu})
.

∎

7.4.
Further remarks on Theorem
2

Following remark
3
, replacing
k^{T}_{ij}(t,s)=g(\frac{t}{T})\varphi_{ij}(t-s)
with the coordinate-dependent kernel
k^{T}_{ij}(t,s)=g_{ij}(\frac{t}{T})\varphi_{ij}(t-s)
wherein
g\colon[0,1]\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
is only a matter of rephrasing: we have only relied on the regularity properties of
g
and the existence of a resolvent
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(k^{T})^{\star i}
, which is guaranteed under the condition that
(\lVert g_{ij}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{ij}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)_{ij}
has spectral radius below
1
. This is with the exception of Lemma
4
, where the recursion step and thus the constant in our bound should be modified into

(\partial_{t}+\partial_{s})(k^{T})_{ij}^{\star n}(t,s)\leq C(g,g^{\prime})\frac{n}{T}\bar{\varphi}_{ij}^{\star n}(t-s)

where
\bar{\varphi}_{ij}(t)=\lVert g_{ij}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\varphi_{ij}(t)
and
C(g,g^{\prime})=\max_{ij}\lVert g_{ij}^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert g_{ij}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{-1}\mathbb{1}_{g_{ij}\neq 0}
. In addition to this technical detail, some increased attention must then be placed on the non-commutativity of
g
in the
\star
-iterates of the kernel. This results in more cumbersome notation for the bounds appearing throughout our proofs, hence we have kept our main results in the context of an univariate reproduction rate for the sake of simplicity.
