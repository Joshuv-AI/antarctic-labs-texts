Effects of lingual and labial orthodontic treatment on the airway in adult with extraction of first premolars: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ruijie Zhang, Ziyu Guo, Shanmin Fu, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate changes in the upper airway of adult patients after lingual orthodontic treatment with premolar extractions and analyze the correlation between airway changes and dentomaxillary indices.MethodsFor this retrospective study, 40 adult patients were included: 20 treated with lingual appliances and 20 adults treated with labial appliances. All patients underwent extraction of four first premolars and had pretreatment and posttreatment cone-beam computed tomography scans. CBCT data were subjected to three-dimensional reconstruction to measure volumes and minimal cross-sectional areas of the oropharyngeal, palatopharyngeal, glossopharyngeal, and laryngopharyngeal airway segments, as well as changes in incisor retraction, maxillary/mandibular arch width, mandibular position, and hyoid bone position.ResultsIn the lingual group, glossopharyngeal airway volume decreased by 1127.19 mm³, while the minimal cross-sectional areas of the glossopharyngeal, oropharyngeal, and overall upper airway decreased by 31.28 mm², 28.14 mm², and 26.25 mm², respectively (all P < 0.05). These changes differed significantly from those in the labial group, which showed no significant alterations in any airway segment. Glossopharyngeal airway changes were not significantly correlated with incisor retraction or arch width but were significantly associated with mandibular position.ConclusionsFor adult patients undergoing extraction of four first premolars, lingual orthodontic treatment results in significant reductions in glossopharyngeal airway volume and cross-sectional area. These reductions are not significantly associated with incisor retraction or arch width changes but may be associated with mandibular descent.

Background

Orthodontic treatment not only aligns teeth and improves facial profile but may also influence upper airway dimensions, which are closely linked to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and overall health [1–4]. Anatomical factors such as craniofacial morphology, mandibular position, and dental arch dimensions play important roles in airway size [5]. For example, skeletal Class II patterns and mandibular retrognathia are associated with smaller airways, whereas maxillary transverse deficiency can contribute to airway obstruction [6, 7]. Orthodontic interventions, including rapid maxillary expansion and mandibular advancement, can enlarge airway space and improve OSA symptoms [8, 9]. By modifying maxillary width [10] and mandibular position [11], orthodontic treatment may positively affect airway dimensions, thereby contributing to the treatment of OSA. This further supports the link between dentofacial parameters and upper airway size, as well as the potential impact of dentofacial changes on upper airway dimensions.

Extraction of four first premolars is a common approach in adult patients with crowding or bimaxillary protrusion. Premolar extraction reduces arch length and may alter arch width, potentially affecting oral cavity volume, tongue position, and airway dimensions. Some studies suggest that premolar extraction reduces oral volume and airway dimensions due to posterior tongue displacement [12], while others argue that extraction has no significant effect [13]. Additional studies [14] suggest that the impact on the airway depends on treatment objectives: significant retraction of anterior teeth may lead to upper airway stenosis; mesial movement of molars may affect the size of the upper airway. Currently, there is no consensus on whether orthodontic treatment with tooth extraction exerts an impact on the upper airway.

Lingual orthodontics has gained popularity in adult patients due to its esthetic advantages. Compared with labial appliances, lingual appliances apply forces closer to the tooth’s center of resistance, which may produce different dental and skeletal effects [15, 16]. For example, lingual appliances may increase intercanine width and reduce anchorage loss of maxillary first molars during space closure [17], show different torque expression [18], and even help reduce gingival recession and root prominence [19]. Despite these biomechanical differences, most previous studies have focused on the effects of labial appliances on the airway, with little attention given to lingual appliances.

Based on previous research, changes in oral space may induce positional shifts of the tongue and soft palate, thereby affecting airway dimensions. Dental arch length, arch width, and mandibular position represent dentofacial parameters in the sagittal, transverse, and vertical dimensions, respectively. These parameters are closely associated with oral space, and their alterations may directly contribute to upper airway changes. Therefore, in the present study, the impact of extraction-based orthodontic treatment on the airway was evaluated not only by the amount of incisor retraction, but by integrating changes in three-dimensional dentofacial parameters. Given the potential relationship between hyoid bone position and upper airway morphology, hyoid position was also included in the analysis [20]. Most previous studies have relied on a single key parameter. For example, the retraction distance of anterior teeth is frequently used to assess the airway effect of extraction treatment [21], which primarily reflects changes in sagittal dentofacial parameters.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of extraction-based lingual orthodontic treatment on upper airway dimensions in adult patients, using labial appliances as a control. Additionally, we analyzed the correlation between airway changes and 3D dentofacial indices, including mandibular and hyoid bone positions, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how orthodontic treatment may influence airway morphology.

Methods

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the School of Stomatology, Air Force Medical University, Xi’an, Shaanxi, China (Approval No. IRB-REV-2020064). All patients were treated in the Department of Orthodontics, Air Force Medical University Hospital between 2018 and 2023.

Sample size

The sample size calculation is based on previous studies [21] and performed by the PASS software (version 11). The significance level (α) is set at 0.05 and the power (1-β) is set at 0.90. The power analysis indicates that a total sample size of 20 questions would be sufficient to detect an effect size with 90% power.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 18–35 years at treatment initiation; (2) skeletal Class I relationship defined by ANB (0° < ANB ≤ 4.7°), with normal SNA and SNB values and an average vertical growth pattern (normal SN-MP angle); (3) no missing teeth except third molars; (4) extraction of four first premolars; (5) availability of complete pre- and post-treatment CBCT scans; and (6) primary indications for extraction including mild-to-moderate anterior crowding and/or bimaxillary protrusion requiring space for incisor retraction and profile improvement.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of orthodontic treatment or orthognathic surgery; (2) history of cleft lip and palate; and (3) history or clinical diagnosis of snoring or sleep apnea. A total of 40 patients met the criteria and were included in the analysis.

A total of 40 patients met the criteria and were included in the analysis. The sample was divided into a lingual appliance group and a labial appliance group, with 20 patients in each group. In lingual group, all patients received treatment with the Active Wing Lingual Orthodontic Technology, with active wing lingual orthodontic bracket bonded to the lingual surfaces of the teeth. En-masse retraction of the anterior teeth was performed using a sliding mechanics approach with 0.017 × 0.025-inch stainless steel archwires. In labial group, all patients were treated with the MBT straight-wire appliance technique, using 0.022 × 0.028-inch straight-wire appliances bonded to the labial-buccal surfaces of the teeth. For extraction space closure, all patients were treated with 0.019 × 0.025-inch stainless steel rectangular wires. The mean total treatment duration across all patients was approximately 2.5 years.

CBCT acquisition

All CBCT scans were performed following the ALARA principle to minimize radiation exposure, and acquired using the same CBCT machine (Galileo, Sirona Dental Systems, Germany) with a voltage of 85 kV, current of 21 mA, and slice thickness of 0.15 mm. During acquisition, patients were positioned in a seated upright posture with the Frankfort horizontal plane aligned parallel to the floor, and head stabilization devices integrated into the CBCT unit. Patients were instructed to maintain centric occlusion, keep their lips gently closed, the tongue resting on the floor of the mouth, and avoid swallowing or moving the jaw. Additionally, three-dimensional cranial reorientation was performed during image processing to further standardize head position and ensure consistency of soft tissue and tongue position across scans.

All CBCT data in DICOM format were imported into Dolphin 11.8. All images underwent standardization: Three-dimensional cranial positioning was performed with reference to the recommendations for systematic upper airway analysis [22]. A three-dimensional rectangular coordinate system was established using 3-matic 19.0 software (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). The sagittal plane was defined by the facial midline passing through the nasion and anterior nasal spine, the coronal plane was determined by the bilateral orbital points, and the axial plane was defined by the horizontal line passing through the right ear point and bilateral orbital points. Calibration of the coordinate system was further performed from three viewing angles: in the frontal view, the horizontal line was aligned with the bilateral orbital points and the facial midline passed through the nasion and anterior nasal spine; in the right lateral view, the horizontal line was aligned with the right ear point and the corresponding orbital point; and in the superior view, the vertical line was aligned with the crista galli and the midpoint of the anterior edge of the foramen magnum.

Dolphin 11.8 was used to measure the volume and cross-sectional area of the upper airway. Since the minimum cross-sectional area (minCSA) of the airway is the anatomical feature most closely associated with the pathogenesis of OSA [23], changes in minCSA were defined as the primary outcome variable, while airway volume served as the secondary outcome [11].

The measured parameters included palatopharyngeal volume (PPV) and minCSA (PP-minCSA), glossopharyngeal volume (GPV) and minCSA (GP-minCSA), oropharyngeal volume (OPV) and minCSA (OP-minCSA), laryngopharyngeal volume (LPV) and minCSA (LP-minCSA), total oropharyngeal and laryngopharyngeal volume (TV), and overall airway minCSA (OA-minCSA).

Airway regions were defined using horizontal axial planes parallel to the Frankfort horizontal plane. The palatopharyngeal airway extended from the anterior nasal spine to the tip of the uvula; the glossopharyngeal airway from the uvular tip to the most anterior-inferior point of the third cervical vertebra (C3) and the laryngopharyngeal airway from C3 to the most anterior-inferior point of the fourth cervical vertebra (C4) [22]. The oropharyngeal airway comprised the palatopharyngeal and glossopharyngeal segments.

After manual delineation of the airway boundaries, three-dimensional reconstruction was performed. Airway volume (mm³) and minCSA (mm²) were automatically calculated by the software.

Measurement of hyoid bone and dentomaxillary indices (Fig. 2)

Measurements of dentomaxillary parameters. a Overlap of 3D models; b–d set up coordinate system; e changes after orthodontic treatment; f point of dentomaxillary parameters; red: pretreatmen, yellow: posttreatment

Mimics Research 19.0 and 3-matic Research 11.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) were used to measure the hyoid bone position and dentomaxillary indices. Three-dimensional reconstruction of bone tissue from CBCT images was performed using Mimics Research 19.0. The obtained 3D models were imported into 3-matic Research 11.0, where pre-treatment and post-treatment 3D models of each patient were superimposed [21].

Meanwhile, a coordinate system was established with the nasion (Point N) as the origin of the coordinate system and the Frankfort horizontal plane as the axial plane. The coordinate axes were defined as follows: The X-axis was perpendicular to the midsagittal plane, with the left direction as positive; The Y-axis lay on the midsagittal plane, with the posterior direction as positive; The Z-axis was perpendicular to the Frankfort horizontal plane, with the upward direction as positive. All coordinate values were expressed in millimeters (mm).

Measurements included: (1) Hyoid bone position: defined as the coordinates of the most anterosuperior point of the hyoid body. (2) Incisor retraction: measured as the sagittal displacement of the midpoint of the mesial incisor edge along the Y-axis. (3) Arch width: intercanine width (U3W, L3W) and intermolar width (U6W, L6W) measured between crown centers [24]. (4) Mandibular position: represented by menton coordinates.

All data were measured and recorded by a single trained researcher. Repeated measurements were performed within 1 month after the initial measurement, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to evaluate measurement consistency, with all ICC values exceeding 0.90, indicating excellent reliability. The average values of the two measurements were used for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were processed using SPSS software (version 24; IBM, Armonk, NY). Measurement results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

First, normality test and homogeneity of variance test were performed on the data.

If the data satisfied the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, the independent samples t-test was used to compare inter-group differences between the labial group and the lingual group. The paired t-test was applied to compare changes in various indices before and after treatment in the same patient. If the data did not meet the normality assumption, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used instead. For results with significant changes, the Pearson correlation test was conducted to examine the correlation between airway changes and changes in dentomaxillary indices. The two-tailed test significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Upper airway volume

Measurements of the volume of each upper airway segment are presented in Fig. 3a. In the labial group, there were no significant changes in any airway segment after treatment compared with before treatment. However, in the lingual group, the glossopharyngeal volume (GPV) decreased by an average of 1127.19 mm³ after treatment compared with before treatment (P = 0.008). There were no significant differences in pre-treatment and post-treatment upper airway volumes between the two groups. Nevertheless, the changes in GPV, oropharyngeal volume (OPV), and total volume (TV) showed statistically significant differences between the two groups (P < 0.05).

Upper airway minimum cross-sectional area (minCSA)

Measurements of the minCSA of each upper airway segment are presented in Fig. 3b. In the lingual group, the minCSA of the glossopharyngeal, oropharyngeal, and overall airway decreased by 31.28 mm² (P = 0.011), 28.14 mm² (P = 0.008), and 26.25 mm² (P = 0.006), respectively; the changes in these indices were statistically significantly different from those in the labial group (P < 0.05). No significant changes were observed in the palatopharyngeal minCSA (PP-minCSA) or laryngopharyngeal minCSA (LP-minCSA) in the lingual group, nor in the minCSA of any airway segment in the labial group. Except for LP-minCSA, the changes in minCSA of all other airway segments showed statistically significant differences between the two groups (P < 0.05).

Changes in hyoid bone and dentomaxillary index positions

Changes in the hyoid bone position and dentomaxillary indices are presented in Fig. 3c. The midpoint of the superior border of the hyoid body was used to represent the hyoid bone position. No significant change in the hyoid bone position was observed after treatment in the lingual group. In the labial fixed appliance group, the hyoid body descended by 0.93 mm along the Z-axis after treatment compared with before treatment (P = 0.040), but this change showed no significant difference between the two groups.

The sagittal movement of teeth was represented by the change in the Y coordinate of the midpoint of the incisal edge of the incisor, while the vertical movement was represented by the change in the Z coordinate. After orthodontic treatment: In the lingual group, the maxillary central incisors were horizontally retracted by 5.44 mm (P < 0.001), and the mandibular central incisors were horizontally retracted by 3.57 mm (P < 0.001). In the labial group, the maxillary central incisors were horizontally retracted by 5.11 mm (P < 0.001), and the mandibular central incisors were horizontally retracted by 4.40 mm (P < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference in the amount of central incisor retraction between the two groups.

After orthodontic treatment, changes in arch width were observed as follows. In the lingual group, the maxillary canine width increased by 1.09 mm (P = 0.007) and the mandibular canine width increased by 1.84 mm (P < 0.001). In the labial group, the maxillary canine width increased by 1.03 mm (P = 0.019) and the mandibular canine width increased by 1.29 mm (P < 0.001).

No significant change in the first molar width was observed in the lingual group after treatment. In the labial group, the maxillary first molar width decreased by 1.25 mm and the mandibular first molar width decreased by 2.12 mm after treatment (both P < 0.001). Nevertheless, there was no significant inter-group difference in the changes in dental arch width.

The menton coordinates were used to reflect the mandibular position. In the lingual group, the menton moved downward by 0.89 mm after treatment (P = 0.004), with no significant change in the sagittal direction. No significant change in the menton position was observed in the labial group after treatment. There was a significant inter-group difference in the vertical positional change of the menton (P = 0.013), but no significant difference in the sagittal positional change between the two groups.

Comparison of upper airway changes between the two groups and correlation analysis

When comparing upper airway changes between the two groups, significant differences were observed in the minimum cross-sectional area (minCSA) of the glossopharyngeal, oropharyngeal, and overall upper airways, all of which include the glossopharyngeal segment. Furthermore, a significant intergroup difference was detected only in the volume change of the glossopharyngeal airway. Therefore, the observed intergroup differences in airway changes were primarily concentrated in the glossopharyngeal segment.

To explore the relationship between dentomaxillary index changes and glossopharyngeal airway changes, data from the two groups were pooled for Pearson correlation analysis. The results are as Fig. 3d.

The change in glossopharyngeal airway volume was potentially associated with two parameters: Z coordinate of the menton (Me): Correlation coefficient (r) = 0.495 (P = 0.001); Y coordinate of the hyoid bone: Correlation coefficient (r) = 0.388 (P = 0.013).

The change in glossopharyngeal airway minCSA was potentially associated with two parameters: Z coordinate of the menton (Me, Z-axis): Correlation coefficient (r) = 0.580 (P < 0.001); Z coordinate of the hyoid bone (Z-axis): Correlation coefficient (r) = -0.365 (P = 0.020).

In contrast, no significant correlation was observed between the change in glossopharyngeal airway (volume or minCSA) and either horizontal incisor retraction or arch width change.

Discussion

OSA is characterized by recurrent upper airway collapse, with anatomical factors playing a key pathogenic role [4]. Upper airway size, tongue position, and mandibular position influence OSA development [25]; additionally, the anterior cranial base length (S-N) and mandibular length (Me-Go) exhibit a negative correlation with the Apnea-Hypopnea Index [26]. Craniofacial morphology is closely related to airway dimensions: skeletal Class II patients with mandibular retrognathia tend to have smaller airways [27], and craniofacial abnormalities such as cleft lip and palate also affect airway size [28]. Mandibular molar inclination has likewise been associated with pharyngeal airway size [29]. Because orthodontic treatment alters tooth position, inclination, and mandibular position, it may influence pharyngeal airway dimensions.

Previous studies on extraction-based orthodontic treatment have reported inconsistent findings. Steegman et al. [30] suggested orthodontic treatment may alter airway size, whereas Orabi et al. [31] found no strong evidence that premolar extractions significantly affect the adult pharyngeal airway. Thus, the effect of extraction treatment on airway size remains controversial.

Age-related airway changes must also be considered. Upper airway volume increases before adulthood, remains relatively stable, and declines gradually after 40 years of age [32] To minimize this confounding factor, only patients aged 18–35 years were included.

CBCT acquisition conditions can influence airway measurements. Because airway-related soft tissues are posture-sensitive and supine positioning may induce airway narrowing [33]. Therefore, all patients in this study underwent CBCT scanning in the seated position. Compared with lateral cephalograms, CBCT enables 3D reconstruction and more accurate assessment of airway volume and minCSA. However, inconsistent segmentation protocols limit comparability across studies [30]. Therefore, the protocol proposed by Guijarro-M R et al. [22] was adopted to enhance reproducibility.

No significant posttreatment airway changes were observed in the labial group. In contrast, the lingual group showed reductions in glossopharyngeal airway volume and in the minCSA of the glossopharyngeal, oropharyngeal, and overall airways. As the narrowest airway region is commonly located in the glossopharyngeal or palatopharyngeal segment [34], the observed changes were primarily concentrated in the glossopharyngeal region. These changes were significantly different from those in the labial group.

The difference between appliance systems may relate to biomechanics. In lingual orthodontics, the force application point is closer to the tooth’s center of resistance [16], potentially resulting in different dentomaxillary adaptations. In the sagittal plane, both groups exhibited significant anterior tooth retraction, with no intergroup difference in retraction amount and no correlation between incisor retraction and glossopharyngeal airway changes.

In the sagittal plane, previous studies typically measured tooth movement relative to the maxilla or mandible. However, because mandibular position may change, the relative position between the airway and mandible is not fixed, whereas the relative position between the airway and skull remains stable. Drawing on Yu Chen et al. [21], who measured horizontal incisor retraction after 3D reconstruction, this study incorporated all dentomaxillary indices into a coordinate system and measured their changes relative to the skull. This approach better reflects dentomaxillary changes in relation to the upper airway and may more accurately reveal their impact.

Both groups exhibited significant posterior movement of the upper and lower anterior teeth after treatment. Although lingual orthodontics is advantageous for reducing molar anchorage loss, there was no significant intergroup difference in the amount of anterior tooth retraction. Furthermore, no significant correlation was observed between glossopharyngeal airway size changes and the amount of anterior tooth retraction.

In the transverse plane, both groups showed an increase in canine arch width, likely due to the alignment of mild anterior crowding, which widened the intercanine distance. The decrease in first molar arch width resulted from slight mesial movement of molars during extraction space closure. The lingual group showed a slightly greater increase in canine width and a slightly smaller decrease in molar width than the labial group, consistent with Spyridon N Papageorgiou et al. [17], who reported that lingual appliances may increase intercanine width and reduce anchorage loss of the maxillary first molars. However, no significant intergroup differences were observed in canine or molar width changes, and no significant correlation was found between changes in arch width and glossopharyngeal airway changes.

This finding aligns with Emad Al Maaitah et al. [35], who concluded that extraction of the first premolars in patients with bimaxillary protrusion does not affect the airway despite incisor retraction and arch width changes. Some studies [10] have suggested that maxillary expansion can impact the airway, likely because those studies included patients with relatively narrow arches and airways, where transverse compression or hypoplasia contributed to airway narrowing. In such cases, expansion relieves compressive factors or promotes airway development. In contrast, the present study included adult patients without arch narrowing, whose airways were not significantly compressed transversely and had limited growth potential; therefore, minimal changes in arch width did not induce significant airway changes.

In the vertical plane, the menton descended by an average of 0.89 mm in the lingual group after treatment, indicating a potential downward shift in mandibular position. This change was significantly different from that in the labial group (where no significant mandibular position change was observed). This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in force application points between the two appliance types: in lingual orthodontics, the force applied during anterior tooth intrusion is closer to the tooth’s center of resistance. In contrast, labial appliances are more likely to cause labial inclination of anterior teeth, while lingual appliances tend to correct mesially inclined molars toward distal inclination. Differences in occlusal plane changes may thus have led to intergroup differences in mandibular position.

Pearson correlation analysis between glossopharyngeal airway changes and mandibular position changes revealed a moderate positive correlation, with a downward shift in mandibular position associated with a decrease in glossopharyngeal airway volume and minCSA. Xiaoxin Shi et al. [11] reported that counterclockwise rotation of the mandible increases airway size, consistent with our findings; however, other researchers have argued that vertical control does not significantly affect the airway [36].

Hyoid bone position may be influenced by mandibular position, as the mylohyoid muscle connects the hyoid bone to the mandible. Omid Mortezai et al. [37] proposed that extraction-based orthodontic treatment affects upper airway size and hyoid bone position. In the present study, only the labial group showed a statistically significant vertical change in hyoid bone position, but this change was not significantly different between the two groups. Although there was an intergroup difference in sagittal hyoid bone position, neither group exhibited a statistically significant sagittal change after treatment. Thus, there is insufficient evidence to confirm intergroup differences in hyoid bone position changes.

Although our results suggested a moderate correlation between hyoid bone position and glossopharyngeal airway changes, it is important to recognize that airway morphology is influenced by multiple craniofacial factors. In particular, the cranial base configuration and soft palate morphology have been shown to significantly affect pharyngeal airway dimensions and may interact with orthodontic treatment mechanics, including incisor retraction and mandibular positioning [38, 39]. These factors may help explain individual variability in airway response following extraction-based lingual or labial orthodontic treatment. Future studies should incorporate assessment of cranial base angles, soft palate length and thickness, and other relevant craniofacial parameters to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how orthodontic mechanics influence upper airway morphology over time.

Compared to labial orthodontics, lingual orthodontics is associated with a significant reduction in glossopharyngeal airway size, which may be related to changes in mandibular position. The findings of this study suggest that ignoring mandibular position changes during extraction-based orthodontic treatment may be a potential source of research bias. Previous studies on extraction-based orthodontics have primarily focused on the relationship between the amount of incisor retraction and airway changes [12]; incorporating mandibular position measurements may help reduce bias between studies.

This study has several limitations. First, although the sample size met the statistical power requirement, the total number of patients was relatively small and derived from a single institution. The strict inclusion criteria may also limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, this was a retrospective study, and potential selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to further validate these findings. In addition, the present study included only skeletal Class I patients with an average vertical growth pattern. This restriction was applied to minimize confounding effects of craniofacial morphology on upper airway dimensions and to improve internal validity. However, patients with skeletal Class II or hyperdivergent patterns are known to have narrower upper airways and may be more vulnerable to airway compromise [40]. Finally, the current study only assessed immediate post-treatment airway changes. Long-term follow-up studies have reported that airway dimensions may partially recover or stabilize over several years after orthodontic treatment [11, 12]. Therefore, future research should include extended follow-up to evaluate whether the reductions in glossopharyngeal airway observed in the short term persist, improve, or further progress over time.

Conclusions

In adult Angle Class I patients with an average mandibular plane angle who underwent extraction of four first premolars, lingual orthodontic treatment resulted in significant reductions in glossopharyngeal airway volume and minimum cross-sectional area, whereas no significant upper airway changes were observed in the labial group. Correlation analysis revealed that the glossopharyngeal airway reduction in the lingual group was significantly associated with the vertical descent of the menton (mandibular downward shift) but not with incisor retraction or arch width changes. Although the labial group showed a minor vertical change in hyoid bone position, there was insufficient evidence to confirm a causal link between hyoid bone movement and upper airway alterations. Clinically, these findings emphasize the need to monitor mandibular vertical position during lingual orthodontic treatment, particularly in patients at risk of obstructive sleep apnea. Additionally, we suggest that incorporating mandibular position assessments may help mitigate potential bias in studies evaluating the impact of extraction-based orthodontics on the upper airway given that prior studies have often centered primarily on incisor retraction.
