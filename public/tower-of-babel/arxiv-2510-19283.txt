Mohammad Al-Jarrah, Bamdad Hosseini, Niyizhen Jin, Michele Martino, Amirhossein Taghvaei

Abstract
We present a systematic analysis of estimation errors for a class of optimal transport based algorithms for filtering and data assimilation. Along the way, we extend previous error analyses of Brenier maps to the case of conditional Brenier maps that arise in the context of simulation based inference. We then apply these results in a filtering scenario to analyze the optimal transport filtering algorithm of [ 5 ] . An extension of that algorithm along with numerical benchmarks on various non-Gaussian and high-dimensional examples are provided to demonstrate its effectiveness and practical potential.

Introduction

This paper outlines a systematic analysis of a class of optimal transport (OT)
algorithms for filtering of nonlinear systems. Our analysis
provides quantitative estimation rates for such algorithms combining
statistical errors due to finite samples as well as approximation errors
due to parameterization of transport maps.
Numerical experiments further highlight the applicability of our OT methods
and investigate the manifestation of our theory in practical benchmarks.

Nonlinear filtering is the problem of inferring the state of a
dynamical system from partial and noisy observations. This task is
achieved via a probabilistic formulation leading to
a sequence of conditional distributions for the state of the dynamical
system obtained by successive applications of Bayes’ rule.
Early engineering applications of filtering include target tracking
[

[

1.2
Literature survey

Classical nonlinear filtering algorithms, such as the Kalman filter and its nonlinear extensions
[

,
66
,
12

, and sequential importance resampling (SIR) particle filters (PF)
[

are widely used in practical problems and commercial applications. However, they
are subject to fundamental limitations that prohibit their application to modern high-dimensional problems with strong nonlinear effects: Kalman filters are sensitive to initial conditions and fail to represent multi-modal distributions
[
97
,
22
]
while SIR
suffers from the particle degeneracy phenomenon that becomes severe in high-dimensional problems, an issue known as the curse of dimensionality
[
15
,
92
,
14
,
13
]
.

These limitations motivated the development of alternative coupling or transport-based methods in recent years with the aim of overcoming the curse of dimensionality
[
39
,
37
,
93
,
121
,
52
,
94
,
40
,
120
,
80
,

,

,
26
]
see also
[
102
]
and
[
106
]
for a recent survey of these topics.
The coupling method proposed in
[

]
is the closest method to the OT method
studied in this paper as well as the previous works
[

,
104
,
59
]
.
Both approaches are similar since they are considered likelihood-free and amenable to neural network parameterizations.
A key distinction lies in the structure of the transport map: while our method seeks to compute the OT
map from the prior to the posterior by solving a min-max problem,
[
102
]

constructs the Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement using a maximum likelihood estimator.

We would also note that an active area of research around conditional simulation and its
application for the solution of inverse problems has been developed recently; see
[

,

,

,
38

. By observing the connection between Bayesian inference and the Bayesian update step in
filtering we realize the opportunity for extending the aforementioned works to the
filtering problem, revealing a diverse set of potential algorithms.

The error analysis of coupling/OT-based nonlinear filtering algorithms is challenging, as the algorithm involves an interacting particle system. Results are available for EnKF in the linear Gaussian setting
[

,

,

In this paper, we avoid the complications due to the interacting particle system using either of the following two procedures: (i) we generate independent particles at each time step using
empirical OT maps;
and (ii) generate independent samples with an additional resampling step

Section

3.4

The second idea is also used in the analysis and implementation of SIR
[

]
.

Our analysis of conditional OT map estimation is based on the extension of recent works on statistical

2.3
Statement of main results

3.1
Problem setup

We continue with the notation and terminology introduced in
Section

1
,
where the sequence of posteriors
\{\pi^{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
follows a recursive update defined by
\pi^{t}=(\mathcal{B}_{Y_{t}}\circ\mathcal{A})[\pi^{t-1}]
,
with
\mathcal{A}
representing the state/dynamic update and

\mathcal{B}_{Y_{t}}
the Bayesian update for the data
Y_{t}
.
In this light, we introduce the notation

\mathcal{T}_{y}:=\mathcal{B}_{y}\circ\mathcal{A},\qquad\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t,\tau}}=\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t}}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{T}_{Y_{\tau+1}},\qquad t>\tau,

(19)

with the convention that
\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t,t}}
is the identity map.
With this notation, we have
\pi^{t}=\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t}}[\pi^{t-1}]
, and, by extension,
\pi^{t}=\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t,\tau}}[\pi^{\tau}]
. We emphasize that the maps
\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t,\tau}}

are defined in terms of the observed process
{Y}_{t}
and are hence random objects.

A numerical filtering algorithm can then be viewed as a method to approximate
\mathcal{T}_{y}
with the maps
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{y}
. Similarly,
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{Y_{t,\tau}}
defines the approximate sequence of posteriors
\widehat{\pi}^{t}=\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{Y_{t}}[\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}]
. To analyze the error of such approximations, it is necessary for the true sequence of posteriors to satisfy stability properties
that prevent errors from accumulating over time. To this end,
we introduce the following
filter stability
assumption.
In what follows,
D:\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{U})\times\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{U})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq}0

denotes a statistical divergence in the parlance of
eq.

13
.

Assumption 12
(Filter stability).

The true filter
\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t,\tau}}
is uniformly geometrically stable with respect to the
divergence
D
in the sense that there exist constants
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
C_{\rm stab.}>0
such that, for any pair of measures
\pi_{1}
,
\pi_{2}
and
t>\tau\geq 0
, it holds that

D(\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t,\tau}}[\pi_{1}],\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t,\tau}}[\pi_{2}])\leq C_{\rm stab.}(1-\lambda)^{t-\tau+1}D(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}),\quad\text{uniformly with respect to }Y_{t,\tau}.

(20)

This stability assumption is familiar in the error analysis of sequential importance
sampling particle filters
[
42
]
. It is valid if the
dynamic transition kernel
a
is bounded below and above by positive and bounded constants. In particular, if there exists a constant
c
such that the density associated with the dynamic update kernel satisfies

\sqrt{c}\leq a(u^{\prime}\mid u)\leq 1/\sqrt{c}
, for all
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
, then
eq.

20

holds with
C_{\rm stab.}=c^{-1}

[
91
, Thm. 3.7]
.
This condition leads to the so-called minorization condition that ensures geometric
ergodicity of Markov processes
[
83
]
, which is shown to be inherited by
the filter. We note that this condition is relatively strong and can only be verified
for a restricted class of systems, e.g., when
\mathcal{U}
is compact.
A complete characterization of systems with uniform geometric stable filters is a
challenging open problem in the field. More insight is available for the weaker
notion of asymptotic stability of the filter, which holds when the system is
“detectable” in a sense that is suitable for nonlinear stochastic dynamical
systems
[
113
,
33
,
112
,
69
]
.
For more discussion and survey of the results on filter stability we
refer the reader to
[
36
]
.

3.2
Statement of main results and overview of proofs

The OT representation of conditional distributions, as established in
proposition

5
, allows us to replace the Bayesian update with the push-forward of conditional Brenier maps. In particular, the filtering operator

\mathcal{T}_{y}
, for any probability measure
\pi
, can be written as

\displaystyle\text{(exact filter operator)}\qquad\mathcal{T}_{y}[\pi]\coloneqq\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}[\pi](y,\cdot)\#\eta_{\mathcal{U}}[\pi],\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\eta_{\mathcal{U}}[\pi]\coloneqq\mathcal{A}\pi\,.

(21)

Here,
\phi^{\dagger}[\pi]
denotes the operator acting on
\pi
that yields the minimizer of the dual problem
eq.

11
, where the marginal distributions
\eta
and
\nu
are defined in terms of
\pi
according to

\displaystyle\eta[\pi]\coloneqq\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}[\pi]\otimes\eta_{\mathcal{U}}[\pi]\,,\qquad\nu[\pi](y,u)\coloneqq h(y\mid u)\eta_{\mathcal{U}}[\pi](u).

(22)

As the notation implies, the probability measures and the conditional Brenier potential depend explicitly on the input measure
\pi
. This formulation motivates the following approximation:

\displaystyle\text{(approximate filter operator)}\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{y}[\pi]\coloneqq\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}[\pi](y,\cdot)\#\eta_{\mathcal{U}}[\pi].

(23)

The difference is that, instead of
\phi^{\dagger}[\pi]
, we use
\widehat{\phi}[\pi]
, which is the minimizer of the empirical dual problem
eq.

12
constructed from independent samples of the marginal distributions
defined in
eq.

22
. With
eq.

21
and
eq.

23
at hand, the exact and approximate posteriors evolve as

\displaystyle\pi^{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{Y_{t}}[\pi^{t-1}]=\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}[\pi^{t-1}]\#\eta_{\mathcal{U}}[\pi^{t-1}]\,,

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}^{t}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{Y_{t}}[\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}]=\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}[\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}]\#\eta_{\mathcal{U}}[\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}]\,,\qquad\widehat{\pi}^{0}=\pi^{0}\,.

To simplify notation, let us write

\displaystyle{\phi_{t}^{\ddagger}}\coloneqq\phi^{\dagger}[\pi^{t-1}]\,,\qquad\phi_{t}^{\dagger}\coloneqq\phi^{\dagger}[\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}]\,,\qquad\widehat{\phi}_{t}\coloneqq\widehat{\phi}[\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}]\,,

as well as

\displaystyle\eta^{t}\coloneqq\eta[\pi^{t-1}]\,,\qquad\nu^{t}\coloneqq\nu[\pi^{t-1}]\,,\qquad\widehat{\eta}^{t}\coloneqq\eta[\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}]\,,\qquad\widehat{\nu}^{t}\coloneqq\nu[\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}]\,.

We highlight that
{\phi_{t}^{\ddagger}}
corresponds to the exact filter applied to the exact posterior
\pi^{t-1}
, while
\phi_{t}^{\dagger}
corresponds to the exact filter applied to the approximated posterior
\widehat{\pi}^{t-1}
.

With this setup, our goal is to bound the approximation error of the posteriors. In particular, we consider the following quantity:

\displaystyle\text{{(exact mean filtering error)}}\qquad\mathbb{E}^{\textnormal{train}}_{1:t}\mathbb{E}_{{Y}_{t}}D(\widehat{\pi}^{t},\pi^{t})\,,

(24)

where the outer expectation is with respect to the empirical samples used to train/compute the potentials
\{\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{t}
, and the inner expectation is with respect to the observed data
{Y}_{t}
.
However, as will be clear in
Lemma

14
, controlling this error is challenging. We therefore consider the approximate error:

\displaystyle(\text{approximate mean filtering error})\quad\mathbb{E}_{1:t}^{\textnormal{train}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{{Y}}_{t}}D(\widehat{\pi}^{t},\pi^{t})\,,

(25)

where the inner expectation is now taken with respect to the observations
\widehat{{Y}}_{t}=\{\widehat{Y}_{1},\widehat{Y}_{2},...,\widehat{Y}_{t}\}
, where
\widehat{Y}_{\tau}\sim\widehat{\nu}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\tau}
for all
\tau\leq t
, and
\widehat{\nu}^{\tau}=h(y\mid u)\mathcal{A}\widehat{\pi}^{\tau-1}
.
We will bound both of these quantities.

Our first goal is to relate the exact and approximate mean filtering error to the difference between the empirical estimate of the potential function
\widehat{\phi}_{t}
and the exact potential
\phi^{\dagger}_{t}
. For this, we make the following assumption on the divergence
D
, extending the notation of
eq.

13
.

Assumption 13
.

The divergence
D
is uniformly stable over
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathcal{U})
, the space of probability measures with bounded second moments, in the sense that

D(T_{1}\#\mu,T_{2}\#\mu)\leq C_{D}\|T_{1}-T_{2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}},\qquad\forall\;T_{1},T_{2}\in L^{2}_{\mu}\text{ and }\mu\in\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathcal{U}),

for a uniform constant
C_{D}>0
.
Moreover,
D
satisfies the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle D(\mu_{1},\mu_{3})\leq D(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+D(\mu_{2},\mu_{3})\ ,\qquad\forall\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{U})\,.

(26)

In
Appendix

A
, we present
several commonly used divergences that satisfy
13
, including the Wasserstein-2 metric, as well as the maximum mean discrepancies with appropriate
kernels. We are now ready to present a lemma that allows us to control
the mean filtering errors in terms of the estimation error of the Brenier potentials.

Lemma 14
.

Suppose
12
and
13
holds. Then, the exact and approximate mean filtering errors satisfy the bounds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1:t}^{\textnormal{train}}\mathbb{E}_{{{Y}}_{t}}D(\widehat{\pi}_{t},\pi_{t})\,

\displaystyle\leq C_{D}C_{\rm stab.}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}(1-\lambda)^{t-\tau}\mathbb{E}_{1:\tau}^{\textnormal{train}}\mathbb{E}_{{{Y}}_{\tau-1}}\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}\|_{L_{\widetilde{\eta}^{\tau}}^{2}},

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1:t}^{\textnormal{train}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{{Y}}_{t}}D(\widehat{\pi}_{t},\pi_{t})\,

\displaystyle\leq C_{D}C_{\rm stab.}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}(1-\lambda)^{t-\tau}\mathbb{E}_{1:\tau}^{\textnormal{train}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{{Y}}_{\tau-1}}\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}\|_{L_{\widehat{\eta}^{\tau}}^{2}},

(28)

where
\widetilde{\eta}^{\tau}\coloneqq\widehat{\eta}^{\tau}_{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\nu^{\tau}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, and we
recall
\widehat{\eta}^{\tau}=\widehat{\eta}^{\tau}_{\mathcal{U}}\otimes\widehat{\nu}^{\tau}_{\mathcal{Y}}
.

The proof of
lemma

14
follows the approach of
[
2
, Prop. 2]
: We decompose the filtering errors via the repeated use of the triangle inequality coupled with the uniform geometric stability of the filter in
eq.

20
and the stability of
D
in
eq.

13
. The details are supplied in
Section

C.1
.

Lemma

14
enables us to apply
Theorems

7
and
10
to bound the mean filtering errors. However, this is not directly possible for the exact error due to the discrepancy in the probability distribution that appears in the
L^{2}
-norms on the
right hand side. In particular, the application of
Theorems

7
and
10
concludes a bound for
\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}\|_{L_{\widehat{\eta}^{\tau}}^{2}}
while the bound
eq.

27
is in terms of
\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}\|_{L_{\widetilde{\eta}^{\tau}}^{2}}
, i.e., we have a mismatch in the
\mathcal{Y}
-marginals. On the other hand,
the correct
L^{2}
-norm appears on the right hand side of
eq.

28
, motivating our choice to work with the approximate mean filtering error.

We now present our first main theorem that provides quantitative
control of the approximate mean filtering error.

Theorem 15
.

Suppose
12
and
13
hold. Then,
for any
t>0
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

{}_{1:t}^{\textnormal{train}}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{{Y}}_{t}}D(\pi^{t},\widehat{\pi}^{t})\leq C_{\rm filter}e_{t},

(29)

where

C_{\rm filter}=\frac{C_{D}C_{\rm stab.}}{\lambda}
and
e_{t}
takes different forms
depending on further assumptions:

1.

(Slow rate) If
6
holds, then

\displaystyle e_{t}=(\textnormal{const.})\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{\text{max}}}{\alpha_{\text{min}}}}\max_{1\leq\tau\leq t}\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\|\nabla_{u}\phi-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}\|_{L_{\widehat{\eta}^{\tau}}^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{{C_{\mathcal{F}}R\beta_{\text{max}}}}{N^{1/2}}}\right).

2.

(Fast rate) If
6
and
9
hold for
\widehat{\eta}^{\tau}
and
\widehat{\nu}^{\tau}
for all
\tau\leq t
, then

\displaystyle e_{t}=(\textnormal{const.})\left(\max_{1\leq\tau\leq t}C_{\textnormal{est.}}(\tau)\left(\frac{\log(N)}{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2+\gamma}}\right),

where
C_{\textnormal{est.}}(\tau)>0
is the constant appearing
in
theorem

10
that may change in time due to variations in the
Poincaré constants of
\widehat{\eta}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\tau}
and
\widehat{\nu}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\tau}
.

Remark 16
.

As pointed out in
Remark

8
, the slow rate is derived under assumptions pertaining to the hypothesis class with minimal requirements on the underlying filtering system. In contrast, the fast rate requires us to verify additional assumptions that might be challenging to preserve over time throughout the evolution of the filtering system. We investigate these questions further in
Section

3.3
.

The proof of the theorem follows by the application of
Theorems

7
and
10
to the right hand side of
eq.

28
; see
Section

C.2
for full details. Note that both fast and slow rate results require time-uniform control over the approximation error
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\|\nabla_{u}\phi-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}\|_{L_{\widehat{\eta}^{\tau}}^{2}}
(for the fast rate, this requirement is implicit and follows from
9
(4)). Moreover, the fast rate requires uniform control over the time-varying constants
C_{\textnormal{est.}}(\tau)
.

We now bound the exact mean filtering error under additional assumptions.

Assumption 17
.

It holds that:

1.

There exists
L_{\mathcal{Y}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}(y,\cdot)-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}(y^{\prime},\cdot)\|&\leq L_{\mathcal{Y}}\|y-y^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{Y}},\\
\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}(y,\cdot)-\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}(y^{\prime},\cdot)\|&\leq L_{\mathcal{Y}}\|y-y^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}.\end{split}

(30)

2.

For any pair of probability measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{U})

define the measures

\rho_{1}(y)=\int h(y\mid u)\mathcal{A}\mu_{1}({\,\rm{d}}u),\qquad\rho_{2}(y)=\int h(y\mid u)\mathcal{A}\mu_{2}({\,\rm{d}}u).

Then there exists a coupling
\Gamma\in\Pi(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and a
constant
C_{D}^{\prime}(h,\mathcal{A})>0
so that

\mathbb{E}_{(Y_{1},Y_{2})\sim\Gamma}\|Y_{1}-Y_{2}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C_{D}^{\prime}D(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}).

(31)

The first Lipschitz condition, familiar to
9
(2), can be relaxed to a local one by taking
L_{\mathcal{Y}}=L_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y^{\prime})

at the expense of slightly more technical arguments in the proof of

Theorem

18
below. The second condition
is valid under regularity assumptions on the dynamic operator
\mathcal{A}
and
the observation kernel
h
and for appropriate choices of
D
.
In
Section

C.4
we verify this condition
for the Wasserstein-1 metric and a practical choice of
\mathcal{A}
and
h
.

Theorem 18
.

Suppose the exact filter satisfies
12
, the divergence
D
satisfies
13
, and
17
holds. Then, for any
t>0
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

{}_{1:t}^{\textnormal{train}}\mathbb{E}_{{Y}_{t}}D(\pi_{t},\widehat{\pi}_{t})\leq\,C_{\rm filter}^{\prime}\max(1,\varrho^{t})e_{t},

(32)

where

\displaystyle C_{\rm filter}^{\prime}\coloneqq\frac{C_{D}C_{\rm stab.}}{\lambda}\left(1+\frac{2L_{\mathcal{Y}}C_{D}^{\prime}C_{D}C_{\rm stab.}}{|\lambda-2L_{\mathcal{Y}}C_{D}^{\prime}C_{D}C_{\rm stab.}|}\right),\qquad\varrho\coloneqq 2L_{\mathcal{Y}}C_{D}^{\prime}C_{D}C_{\rm stab.}+1-\lambda,

and

e_{t}
has the same form as in
theorem

15
.

To obtain
theorem

18
, we first further decompose each term of the mean filtering error bound in
eq.

27
as

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}(Y_{\tau},\cdot)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}(Y_{\tau},\cdot)\|_{L_{\widehat{\eta}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\tau}}^{2}}\leq\text{I}(\tau)+\text{II}(\tau)+\text{III}(\tau),

where
\text{I}(\tau)\coloneqq\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}(\widehat{Y}_{\tau},\cdot)-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}(\widehat{Y}_{\tau},\cdot)\|_{L_{\widehat{\eta}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\tau}}^{2}}
,

\text{II}(\tau)\coloneqq\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}(Y_{\tau},\cdot)-\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}(\widehat{Y}_{\tau},\cdot)\|_{L_{\widehat{\eta}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\tau}}^{2}}
, and

\text{III}(\tau)\coloneqq\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}(\widehat{Y}_{\tau},\cdot)-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}_{\tau}(Y_{\tau},\cdot)\|_{L_{\widehat{\eta}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\tau}}^{2}}

For
1\leq\tau\leq t
, the expectations of the
\textnormal{I}(\tau)
terms are bounded using the rates from
theorem

7
and
theorem

, as in
theorem

15

\textnormal{III}(\tau)

. After achieving such recursive relations, a discrete Grönwall-type argument then yields the desired result. Full details are provided in
section

C.3
.

Remark 19
.

As briefly introduced in

.

The result of

provides the first statistically complete error analysis of the OTF, improving upon

In doing so, we track the key constants depending on the dynamic and observation models

.

3.3
An example application

We now verify some of our assumptions in the context of a practical filtering model. We start by considering a class of filtering systems for which we can guarantee that, at each time step, conditions (i) and (ii) in the static example of
Section

2.4
can be preserved as time evolves. This ensures that the distributions
\widehat{\eta}_{\mathcal{U}}^{t}
and
\widehat{\nu}^{t}
remain strongly log-concave and log-smooth.
To this end, we characterize the filtering system
eq.

1
by update equations

\displaystyle U_{t}\sim\exp(-{a}(\cdot|U_{t-1})),\quad U_{0}\sim\pi_{0},\quad Y_{t}\sim\exp(-{h}(\cdot|U_{t}))\,,

(35)

under the following set of assumptions:

Assumption 22
.

(i)

(Initial distribution)

\pi_{0}(u)=\exp(-{W}_{\pi_{0}}(u))
and there are
positive and bounded constants
\sigma_{\min}({W}_{\pi_{0}})
,

\sigma_{\max}({W}_{\pi_{0}})
so that

\sigma_{\min}({W}_{\pi_{0}})I\preceq\nabla^{2}{W}_{\pi_{0}}(u)\preceq\sigma_{\max}({W}_{\pi_{0}})I,

(Data likelihood) For positive and bounded constants
\theta_{\min}
and
\theta_{\max}

\theta_{\min}I\preceq\nabla^{2}{h}(y\mid u)\preceq\theta_{\max}I.

(Dynamic kernel)
There are positive and bounded constants
\sigma_{\min}(\cdot)

\sigma_{\max}(\cdot)
so that for all

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(a_{u})I\preceq\nabla_{u}^{2}{a}(u\mid u^{\prime})\preceq\sigma_{\max}(a_{u})I\,,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(a_{u^{\prime}})I\preceq\nabla_{u^{\prime}}^{2}{a}(u\mid u^{\prime})\preceq\sigma_{\max}(a_{u^{\prime}})I\,,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(a_{uu^{\prime}})I\preceq\nabla_{uu^{\prime}}^{2}{a}(u\mid u^{\prime})\preceq\sigma_{\max}(a_{uu^{\prime}})I\,.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\text{quad}}:=\bigr\{(y,u)\mapsto\frac{1}{2}u^{\top}Q(y)u+u^{\top}b(y)&\big|b(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,Q(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\,,\\

To simplify the notation, we also introduce the functions

\displaystyle\begin{split}m(x)\coloneqq\left(\sigma_{\min}(a_{u})-\frac{\sigma_{\max}^{2}(a_{uu^{\prime}})}{\sigma_{\min}(a_{u^{\prime}})+x}\right)\,,\quad M(x)\coloneqq\left(\sigma_{\max}(a_{u})-\frac{\sigma_{\min}^{2}(a_{uu^{\prime}})}{\sigma_{\max}(a_{u^{\prime}})+x}\right)\,.\end{split}

Consider the filtering system of (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma_{t}I&\preceq\nabla_{u}^{2}{W}_{\widehat{\eta}_{\mathcal{U}}^{t}}(u)\preceq\Gamma_{t}I\,,\\

(40)

the update functions
m(\cdot/\sigma_{max}^{2}(Q))
and

have positive fixed points and the Hessians of the potentials

remain uniformly bounded in time

) for the explicit bound.

3.4
Towards algorithms

We briefly discuss how our earlier analysis can be connected to
an idealized algorithm.
In order to produce the samples that are required to learn
\widehat{\phi}_{t}
in an algorithm, we assume the transition kernels in (
1
) are known and can be
simulated.

\displaystyle U_{t}=a(U_{t-1},V_{t})\,,\qquad Y_{t}=h(U_{t},W_{t})\,,

(43)

where
\{V_{t}\}
and
\{W_{t}\}
are i.i.d sequences with known and easy to sample from distributions, such as Gaussians. Then, starting from two sets of independent samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{\widetilde{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from the initial distribution
\pi_{0}
, the training samples are generated according to

\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{i}^{t}&=\left[a(\cdot,V_{t}^{i})\circ\widehat{T}_{t-1}(Y_{t-1},\cdot)\circ...\circ\widehat{T}_{1}(Y_{1},\cdot)\circ a(\cdot,V_{1}^{i})\right](x_{i})\,,\\
u_{i}^{t}&=\left[a(\cdot,\widetilde{V}_{t}^{i})\circ\widehat{T}_{t-1}(Y_{t-1},\cdot)\circ...\circ\widehat{T}_{1}(Y_{1},\cdot)\circ a(\cdot,\widetilde{V}_{1}^{i})\right](\widetilde{x}_{i})\,,\\
y_{i}^{t}&=h(u_{i}^{t},W_{t}^{i})\,.\end{split}

(44)

with
\widehat{T}_{\tau}=\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}
, and
\{V_{\tau}^{i}\}_{i,\tau=1}^{N,t}
,
\{\widetilde{V}_{\tau}^{i}\}_{i,\tau=1}^{N,t}
and
\{W_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
being independent copies of
V_{t}
and
W_{t}
. Note that, in order to ensure independence across all times, the samples that are used to train
\widehat{\phi}_{\tau}
, for
\tau<t
, should be independent of the samples used to train
\widehat{\phi}_{t}
. Therefore, at each time
t
, the samples

\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
\{\widetilde{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
\{V_{\tau}^{i}\}_{i,\tau=1}^{N,t}
,
\{\widetilde{V}_{\tau}^{i}\}_{i,\tau=1}^{N,t}
and
\{W_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
that are used in (
44
) should be regenerated. Moreover, this ideal procedure requires the storage of all of the previous maps
\{\widehat{T}_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{t}
.

The ideal procedure is useful in simplifying the error analysis. However, computationally, it becomes intensive as the time
t
grows. A more practical approach is to simulate an interacting particle system according to

\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{i}^{t}&=a(\cdot,V_{t}^{i})\circ\widehat{T}_{t-1}(Y_{t-1},v_{i}^{t-1})\,,\qquad v^{0}_{i}=x_{i}\\
u_{i}^{t}&=a(\cdot,\widetilde{V}_{t}^{i})\circ\widehat{T}_{t-1}(Y_{t-1},u_{i}^{t-1})\,,\qquad u^{0}_{i}=x_{i}\\
y_{i}^{t}&=h(u_{i}^{t},W_{t})\,.\end{split}

(45)

Unlike (
44
), the interacting particle system does not require storing the maps and regeneration of the samples. The improvement in computational efficiency comes at the expense that the sampled particles become correlated, making our theoretical analysis not applicable. The error analysis for this algorithm is more challenging and may be studied under a propagation of chaos analysis
[
103
]
.

Finally, an alternative between (
44
) and (
45
) is provided by the resampled OTF algorithm which involves an additional resampling step to ensure the independence of the particles

\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{i}^{t}&=a(\cdot,V_{t}^{i})\circ\widehat{T}_{t-1}(Y_{t-1},v_{\sigma_{i}}^{t-1})\,,\\
u_{i}^{t}&=a(\cdot,\widetilde{V}_{t}^{i})\circ\widehat{T}_{t-1}(Y_{t-1},u_{\sigma^{\prime}_{i}}^{t-1})\,,\\
y_{i}^{t}&=h(u_{i}^{t},W_{t})\,.\end{split}

(46)

where
\sigma_{i}\sim\textnormal{Unif}\{1,...,N\}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
is an independent copy of
\sigma_{i}
.
While less amenable to applications than (
45
), an error analysis for the resampled algorithm in (
46
) can be derived from ours by introducing an extra resampling error of order
O(1/\sqrt{N})
.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we perform several experiments and benchmarks to compare the performance of the OTF algorithm alongside other filtering algorithms like EnKF
[
54
]
and SIR
[
47
]
.
The Python code for reproducing the numerical results is available online
14
14

14

https://github.com/Mohd9485/OTF_SIAM

We begin with a brief chronological account of how the main OTF approach has developed over the past few years.

The optimization problem (
11
) involves the convex conjugate
\phi^{*}
, which is numerically challenging to approximate for a general class of functions. This issue is resolved using the identity
\phi(y,v)=\max_{\varphi\in\textit{CVX}_{\mathcal{U}}}v^{\top}\nabla_{u}\varphi(y,v)-\phi^{*}(y,\nabla_{u}\varphi(y,v))
, derived from Fenchel-Young inequality
[
55
]
, and representing the convex conjugate
\phi^{*}
with a function
\psi\in\textit{CVX}_{\mathcal{U}}
, leading to a min-max problem of the form

\min_{\psi\in\textit{CVX}_{\mathcal{U}}}\,\max_{\varphi\in\textit{CVX}_{\mathcal{U}}}\int\big[v^{\top}\nabla_{u}\varphi(y,v)-\psi(y,\nabla_{u}\varphi(y,v))\big]{\,\rm{d}}\eta(y,v)+\int\psi(y,u){\,\rm{d}}\nu(y,u).

(47)

The map
\nabla_{u}\varphi(y,v)
, resulting from the solution to this min-max problem, serves as the desired conditional OT map
T^{\dagger}(y,v)

[
77
,
104
]
.

As proposed in
[
2
, sec.(V)]
, it is numerically useful to relax the constraint that the conditional OT map is of gradient form, and replace
\nabla_{u}\varphi(y,u)
with the maximization over all maps
T(y,u)
. This relaxation allows more freedom in the parameterization of the OT map and facilitates the optimization task. The resulting optimization problem becomes

\min_{\psi\in\textit{CVX}_{\mathcal{U}}}\,\max_{T\in\mathcal{M}(\eta)}\int\big[v^{\top}T(y,v)-\psi(y,T(y,v))\big]{\,\rm{d}}\eta(y,v)+\int\psi(y,u){\,\rm{d}}\nu(y,u)\,,

(48)

where
\mathcal{M}(\eta)
are the set of
\eta
-measurable maps.

The formulation of (
48
) requires parameterizing
\psi
as a convex function. To enforce this, previous approaches
[
77
,
104
,
2
]
have resorted to the use of Input Convex Neural Networks (ICNNs)
[
6
]
that are challenging to train
[
23
,
72
]
. Alternatively,
[
5
, App. A.3]
derives a formulation which, replacing
\psi
with the representation
\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}-\psi(y,u)
, turns the min-max problem to the max-min problem:

\max_{\psi\in\textit{c-CCV}_{\mathcal{U}}}\,\min_{T\in\mathcal{M}(\eta)}\int\big[c(T(y,v),v)-\psi(y,T(y,v))\big]{\,\rm{d}}\eta(y,v)+\int\psi(y,u){\,\rm{d}}\nu(y,u),

(49)

with the cost

c(u,u^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}
, and the class of
c
-concave maps in the
u
-coordinate
\textit{c-CCV}_{\mathcal{U}}

We recall that the
c
-concavity of
\psi(y,\cdot)
is equivalent to the convexity of
\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}-\psi(y,\cdot)
, a condition that is
easier to enforce in practice.

In the filtering setup of (
46
), the objective of (
49
) can then be approximated empirically using samples as

J_{(N)}^{t}(\psi,T)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\tfrac{1}{2}\|T(y_{i}^{t},v_{i}^{t})-v_{i}^{t}\|^{2}-\psi(y_{i}^{t},T(y_{i}^{t},v_{i}^{t}))+\psi(y_{i}^{t},u_{i}^{t})\Big].

(50)

Throughout all numerical experiments, we draw
(y_{i}^{t},u_{i}^{t})
according to (
46
) and form
v_{i}^{t}
by shuffling the
\{u_{i}^{t}\}
, rather than generating
v_{i}^{t}
via (
46
).
We denote by
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{T}
the parametric classes representing the potential
\psi
and the map
T
, respectively. Then we solve

\max_{\psi\in\mathcal{F}}\,\min_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\,J_{(N)}^{t}(\psi,T)+\lambda_{T}R_{(N)}^{t}(T)+\lambda_{\psi}R_{(N)}^{t}(\psi).

(51)

We enforce the
c
-concavity of the potentials
\psi(y,\cdot)
and the monotonicity of the transport maps
T(y,\cdot)
(recall that by Brenier’s theorem
T^{\dagger}(y,\cdot)
is monotone) through the addition of two regularization terms:

\displaystyle R_{(N)}^{t}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}g_{T}\left(\langle T(y_{i}^{t},v_{i}^{t})-T(y_{i}^{t},v_{j}^{t}),v_{j}^{t}-v_{i}^{t}\rangle\right)

(52)

\displaystyle R_{(N)}^{t}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{\psi}\left(|\mathop{}\!\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}_{u}\psi(y_{i}^{t},u_{i}^{t})|^{2}\right)

(53)

where
g_{T},g_{\psi}
denote smooth monotone functions and
\mathop{}\!\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}_{u}
is the Laplace operator.

In our numerical experiments, both
\psi
and
T
are parameterized as ResNet-type neural networks and
the parameters are updated via a gradient ascent–descent procedure using Adam. To reduce the computational overhead, the number of iterations per time step is gradually decreased, since
consecutive OT maps often vary slightly between successive time steps.

4.1
Utilizing an EnKF-based reference measure

In this section, we propose to replace the independent coupling structure of the reference measure
\eta
with an EnKF-based reference measure to improve the approximation of conditional OT maps in the nearly Gaussian settings. To be precise, consider the
t
-th step of the filter, where we aim to find the conditional OT map
T_{t}
from the reference measure
\eta^{t}(y,v)=\nu^{t}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\eta^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(v)
to the target measure
\nu^{t}(y,u)=h(y\mid u)\eta^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(u)
. We change the source distribution
\eta^{t}
with the distribution resulting from an EnKF approximation defined according to

\eta^{t}_{\text{EnKF}}(y,w)=\nu^{t}_{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\eta^{t}_{\text{EnKF}}(w\mid y)
.
Here
\eta^{t}_{\text{EnKF}}(w\mid y)
is the conditional kernel associated with the EnKF stochastic map

w=\overline{v}+K^{t}(y-\overline{y}),\quad(\overline{y},\overline{v})\sim h(\overline{y}\mid\overline{v})\eta^{t}_{\mathcal{U}}(\overline{v})\,,\qquad K^{t}:=\text{Cov}(\overline{v},\overline{y})\text{Cov}(\overline{y})^{-1}\,.

Solving the optimization problem (
11
) with
\eta^{t}_{\text{EnKF}}
as the source and
\nu^{t}
as the target distribution, concludes the map
T_{t}
such that
T_{t}(y,\cdot)\#\eta^{t}_{\text{EnKF}}(\cdot\mid y)=\nu^{t}(\cdot\mid y)
a.e.
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. The usefulness of this procedure is highlighted in the setting where the target measure
\nu^{t}
is Gaussian. In this case,
\eta^{t}_{\text{EnKF}}
becomes equal to the target measure
\nu^{t}
, resulting in an identity conditional OT map
T_{t}(y,w)=w
. Therefore, in the nearly Gaussian setting, it is expected that the optimal map
T_{t}
is nearly identity, i.e.

T_{t}(y,w)=w+\widetilde{T}(y,w)

where
\widetilde{T}
is a perturbation.

We equip our OTF algorithm with the EnKF-based reference measure through minor adjustments to the optimization problem formulation (
51
).
In particular, we replace samples
(y^{t}_{i},v^{t}_{i})\sim\eta^{t}
in the objective function (
50
), with samples
(y^{t}_{i},w^{t}_{i})\sim\eta^{t}_{\text{EnKF}}
. These new samples are obtained according to
w^{t}_{i}=v^{t}_{i}+\widehat{K}^{t}(y^{t}_{i}-\overline{y}^{t}_{i})
, where
(y^{t}_{i},v^{t}_{i})\sim\eta^{t}
, and
\overline{y}^{t}_{i}\sim h(\cdot\mid v^{t}_{i})
. The matrix
\widehat{K}^{t}:=\widehat{\text{Cov}}(\overline{v},\overline{y})\widehat{\text{Cov}}(\overline{y})^{-1}
is the
empirical approximation to
K^{t}
.
With these adjustments, the objective function (
50
) becomes

J_{(N)}^{t}\bigl(\psi,\widetilde{T}\bigr)\;:=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl[\,\psi\bigl(y_{i}^{t},u_{i}^{t}\bigr)\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|\widetilde{T}\bigl(y_{i}^{t},w_{i}^{t}\bigr)\bigr\|^{2}\;-\;\psi\Bigl(y_{i}^{t},\;\widetilde{T}\bigl(y_{i}^{t},w_{i}^{t}\bigr)\;+\;w_{i}^{t}\Bigr)\Bigr].

(54)

Finally, denoting the optimal solution by
\widetilde{T}_{t}
, samples from the conditional distribution
\nu^{t}(\cdot\mid y)
are obtained according to
w^{t,y}_{i}+\widetilde{T}_{t}(y,w^{t,y}_{i})
where
w^{t,y}_{i}=v^{t}_{i}+\widehat{K}^{t}(y-\overline{y}^{t}_{i})
,
\overline{y}^{t}_{i}\sim h(\cdot\mid v^{t}_{i})
. The corresponding implementation details are summarized in
Algorithm

1
.

5
Conclusion

We outlined a quantitative analysis of conditional OT maps for conditional simulation, as well as their extension to filtering and data assimilation via the OTF algorithm. In our theoretical analysis of conditional OT we outlined a “slow rate” result with a simple proof technique and minimal assumptions on the reference and target measures, followed by a stronger “fast rate” result that uses stronger assumptions regarding smoothness and Poincaré inequalities of the reference and target conditionals. We then extended these results to OTF algorithms that compute conditional OT maps from particles to approximately perform the Bayesian updates. We showed that under appropriate assumptions, our bounds for conditional OT maps can be extended to a bound on the filtering errors of OTF. Finally, we presented some extensions to the OTF methodology, including an additional EnKF layer that helps with nearly Gaussian problems.

While our theoretical analysis yields a first quantitative bound for conditional OT problems and the OTF algorithm, it leads us to a number of interesting open questions and future directions of research. Most notably, verifying some of our assumptions is challenging in practice and in particular, the Lipshitzness of the Brenier maps
\nabla_{u}\phi(y,\cdot)
with respect to
y
is challenging to establish and is currently open when
\mathcal{Y}
is not compact. It is also interesting to try to relax some of our assumptions regarding Poincaré constants and smoothness of Brenier potentials in order to extend our fast rates to more practical problems. Another interesting direction of research is to incorporate uncertainties regarding the dynamic update
\mathcal{A}
. While we always assumed that this operator is exact, in most practical problems, we are unsure of the state dynamics and may even want to estimate it from empirical data.

We present a fundamental lemma that serves as the basis for our subsequent error analysis. This result builds upon key insights from the prior works

\mathcal{S}^{c}(\phi,y):=\mathcal{S}(\phi,y)-\mathcal{S}(\phi^{\dagger},y),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{S}^{c}(\phi)\coloneqq\mathcal{S}(\phi)-\mathcal{S}(\phi^{{\dagger}}),

(56)

for any function
\phi
, where
\mathcal{S}
is defined according to (
10
) and
\phi^{\dagger}
is the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{S}(\phi)
, whose existence is ensured in
Proposition

5

conditionally optimal Brenier potential function. Furthermore, recall that
for
\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}
a.e.

\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,\cdot)\#\eta_{\mathcal{U}}=\nu(\cdot\mid y)

Lemma 28
(Map stability).

Suppose
\mathcal{F}
satisfies
6
. Then for every
\phi\in\mathcal{F}

it holds that

\frac{1}{2\beta_{\text{max}}}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}-\nabla_{u}\phi\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{S}^{c}(\phi)\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha_{\text{min}}}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}-\nabla_{u}\phi\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}.

Note that the above result does not require any smoothness or
convexity assumptions on
\phi^{\dagger}
but only on the
approximation class
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

This result is an extension of
[
46
, Prop. 2.1]
. The idea is to leverage the duality between convexity and smoothness. We begin with the upper bound.
Recall that
\phi(y,\cdot)
is
\alpha(y)
-strongly convex in
u
iff
\phi^{*}(y,\cdot)
is
\frac{1}{\alpha(y)}
-smooth in
u
. Hence, for any
z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\phi^{*}(y,w)\leq\phi^{*}(y,z)+\langle\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}(y,z),w-z\rangle+\frac{1}{2\alpha(y)}\|z-w\|^{2}.

Set
z=\nabla_{u}\phi(y,u)
and
w=\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)
. Using the fact that
\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi(y,u))=u
, and
\langle u,\nabla_{u}\phi(y,u)\rangle=\phi(y,u)+\phi^{*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi(y,u))
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u))+\phi(y,u)\leq\langle u,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)\rangle+\frac{1}{2\alpha(y)}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)-\nabla_{u}\phi(y,u)\|^{2}.

Integrating over
u\sim\eta_{\mathcal{U}}
, noting that
\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\#\eta_{\mathcal{U}}=\nu(\cdot\mid u)
, and recalling (
9
) and (
10
), we obtain the bound

\mathcal{S}(\phi,y)\leq\int\langle u,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)\rangle+\frac{1}{2\alpha(y)}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)-\nabla_{u}\phi(y,u)\|^{2}\,{\,\rm{d}}\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u).

Now since
\mathcal{S}(\phi^{\dagger},y)=\int\langle u,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)\rangle{\,\rm{d}}\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u)
, the excess risk satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{c}(\phi,y)=\mathcal{S}(\phi,y)-\mathcal{S}(\phi^{\dagger},y)\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha(y)}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,\cdot)-\nabla_{u}\phi(y,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L_{\eta_{\mathcal{U}}}^{2}}.

Integrating over

, and applying the inequality
\frac{1}{2\alpha(y)}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha_{\text{min}}}
concludes the upper-bound.
The proof for the lower-bound follows identically, using the dual statement:
\phi(y,\cdot)
is
\beta(y)
-smooth iff
\phi^{*}(y,\cdot)
is
\frac{1}{\beta(y)}

Suppose

Define

Since
\phi

\phi^{*}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{*}(\phi^{*})=\int\phi(y,u)d\eta(y,u)+\int\phi^{*}(y,u)d\nu(y,u)=\mathcal{S}(\phi),

By

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta_{max}}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger*}\|_{L_{\nu}^{2}}^{2}

B.2
Proof of
Theorem

7
(Slow rate)

Before presenting the proof we collect two technical results:
an upper bound on the covering number of the function class
\mathcal{F}
and its conjugate class
\mathcal{F}^{*}
(defined below), and a classic generalization bound for bounded function classes from empirical process theory.

Lemma 30
.

Define
\mathcal{F}^{*}:=\{\phi^{*}\mid\phi\in\mathcal{F}\}
.
Then for any
\delta>0
,

\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U})})\leq\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U})}).

Proof.

For any
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{F}
we have that

\displaystyle|\phi^{*}(y,u)-\psi^{*}(y,u)|

\displaystyle=|\sup_{v}\{\langle u,v\rangle-\phi(y,v)\}-\sup_{v}\{\langle u,v\rangle-\psi(y,v)\}|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v}|\phi(y,v)-\psi(y,v)|\leq\sup_{y,v}|\phi(y,v)-\psi(y,v)|=\|\phi-\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U})}.

Therefore, if
\{\phi_{1},...,\phi_{N}\}
forms a
\delta
-cover of
\mathcal{F}
, then
\{\phi_{1}^{*},...,\phi_{N}^{*}\}
forms a
\delta
-cover of
\mathcal{F}^{*}

which yields the desired result.

Next, we recall a classical generalization bound for bounded function classes.

Proposition 31
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of real-valued functions on a vector space
\mathcal{X}

such that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}\leq R
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{F}
and
let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim\mu
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}{\sup}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})-\mu(\phi)\right]\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta,

where
C>0
is a universal constant and the expectation is with respect to the empirical samples.

Proof.

The proof is based on two fundamental results in statistical learning theory. First, using the symmetrization lemma
[
101
, Lem. 26.2]
, we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}{\sup}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})-\mu(\phi)\right]\leq\frac{2}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{i}\phi(x_{i})\right]\,,

where
\epsilon_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
are a Rademacher random variables and the quantity on the right-hand side is known as the Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}

[
70
]
. Next, observe that the process

Z_{\phi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{i}\phi(x_{i})
has sub-Gaussian increments
Z_{\phi}-Z_{\phi^{\prime}}

which allows us to use the chaining technique to bound the
Rademacher complexity in terms of
Dudley’s integral (see
[
117
, Thm 5.22, Ex. 5.24]
or
[
115
, Thm. 8.1.3, Rem.8.1.5, Thm. 8.2.3]
for more details):

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{i}\phi(x_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{48}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{2R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{2,\mu^{N}})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{48}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{2R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},2\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{48}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{2R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta/2,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{96}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta\,,

where the second step follows by triangle inequality, the third one by the rescaling property of the covering number, and the last one by change of variable.

We are now in position to present the main proof for this section.

Proof of
Theorem

7
.

We begin by deriving a bias-variance decomposition for the excess risk
\mathcal{S}^{c}(\widehat{\phi})
.
For simplicity define

\widetilde{\phi}:=\argmin_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\|\nabla_{u}\phi-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L^{2}_{\eta}},

(59)

and suppose this minimizer is defined (up to constant shifts). If the minimizer does not
exist we can repeat the rest of our proof with
\widetilde{\phi}
replaced
with a minimizing sequence.
Then we can decompose the excess risk of
\widehat{\phi}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{c}(\widehat{\phi})

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\phi})-\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{\phi})]+[\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{\phi})-\mathcal{S}(\phi^{\dagger})]

\displaystyle=\left[(\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\phi})-\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{\phi}))+(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{\phi})-\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\phi}))+(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\phi})-\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{\phi}))\right]+\left[\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{\phi})-\mathcal{S}(\phi^{\dagger})\right].

Since
\widehat{\phi}=\arg\min_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\phi)
is optimal, it follows that
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{\phi})-\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\widetilde{\phi})\leq 0
. Thus,

\mathcal{S}^{c}(\widehat{\phi})\leq\mathcal{S}^{c}(\widetilde{\phi})+2\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathcal{S}(\phi)-\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\phi)|.

We view this bound as a bias-variance decomposition. The first term is the
excess risk of
\widetilde{\phi}
encoding the bias in approximating

\phi^{\dagger}
due to the choice of the class
\mathcal{F}
. The second term
encodes the stochastic errors or the variance part of the error bound due to randomness
in the empirical approximation of
\mathcal{S}
with
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
.
Upon application of
Lemma

28
we can bound the bias term

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{c}(\widetilde{\phi})\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha_{\text{min}}}||\nabla_{u}\widetilde{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}.

The expectation of the variance term can be further decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathcal{S}(\phi)-\widehat{\mathcal{S}}(\phi)|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}|(\eta-\eta^{N})(\phi)|\Bigr]+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}|(\nu-\nu^{N})(\phi^{*})|\Bigr],

where we used the shorthand notation
\eta^{N}
and
\nu^{N}
for the empirical
measures associated to
\eta
and
\nu
respectively.
By
6
and
Lemma

30
, there exists
R>0
such that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq R
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{F}
and the same bound holds for all
\phi^{*}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
. Applying
Proposition

31
with
\tau=0
, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}|(\eta-\eta^{N})(\phi)|\Bigr]\leq C\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}})}\,{\,\rm{d}}\delta,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}|(\nu-\nu^{N})(\phi^{*})|\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}})}\,{\,\rm{d}}\delta

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}})}\,{\,\rm{d}}\delta.

By the covering number assumption in
6
,

\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}})\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}\delta^{-\gamma}\log(1+\delta^{-1}),

and hence,

\int_{0}^{R}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta\leq\sqrt{C_{\mathcal{F}}}\int_{0}^{R}\delta^{-\gamma/2}\sqrt{\log(1+\delta^{-1})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta.

Combining all terms, we conclude:

\mathcal{S}^{c}(\widehat{\phi})\leq C\left[\frac{1}{\alpha_{\text{min}}}\|\nabla_{u}\widetilde{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\eta}}+\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathcal{F}}}{N}}\int_{0}^{R}\delta^{-\gamma/2}\sqrt{\log(1+\delta^{-1})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta\right].

(60)

Using
Lemma

28
once again along with

Lemma

32
below (to bound the
integral on the right hand side) we arrive at the
desired bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\beta_{\text{max}}\left[\frac{1}{\alpha_{\text{min}}}\|\nabla_{u}\widetilde{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{R^{2}C_{\mathcal{F}}}{N}}\right]\,,

where
C>0
is a universal constant.

Let us now present a calculation that gives a bound on
the integral on the right hand side of (

)
in terms of the parameter
R
.

Lemma 32
.

For all
R\geq 1
and
\gamma\in[0,1)
it
holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\delta^{-\gamma/2}\sqrt{\log(1+\delta^{-1})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta\leq 4R.

Proof.

Decompose the integral into two intervals
[0,1]
and
(1,R]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\delta^{-\gamma/2}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\log(1+\delta^{-1})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta=\int_{0}^{1}\delta^{-\gamma/2}\sqrt{\log(1+\delta^{-1})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta+\int_{1}^{R}\delta^{-\gamma/2}\sqrt{\log(1+\delta^{-1})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\delta^{-\gamma/2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}{\,\rm{d}}\delta+\sqrt{\log(2)}\int_{1}^{R}\delta^{-\gamma/2}{\,\rm{d}}\delta

\displaystyle\overset{s=\log(2/\delta)}{=}2^{1-\gamma/2}\int_{\log(2/R)}^{\infty}e^{-(1-\gamma/2)s}s^{1/2}{\,\rm{d}}s+\frac{2\sqrt{\log(2)}}{2-\gamma}\left(R^{1-\gamma/2}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{1-\gamma/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(1-\gamma/2)s}s^{1/2}{\,\rm{d}}s+\frac{2\sqrt{\log(2)}}{2-\gamma}\left(R^{1-\gamma/2}-1\right)

\displaystyle=2^{1-\gamma/2}\frac{\Gamma(3/2)}{2(1-\gamma/2)^{3/2}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\log(2)}}{2-\gamma}\left(R^{1-\gamma/2}-1\right)

\displaystyle=2^{-(1+\gamma/2)}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{(1-\gamma/2)^{3/2}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\log(2)}}{2-\gamma}\left(R^{1-\gamma/2}-1\right)\,.

(61)

Simplifying the right hand side using the fact that

and

\displaystyle 2^{-(1+\gamma/2)}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{(1-\gamma/2)^{3/2}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\log(2)}}{2-\gamma}\left(R^{1-\gamma/2}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{(1-1/2)^{3/2}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\log(2)}}{2-1}\left(R-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi}R+2\sqrt{\log(2)}R\leq 4R.

Proof of

Since the proof is long and technical we begin with a discussion of the overall strategy

Strategy of the proof and some preliminaries

We begin with an error bound on the excess risk

\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{N}\phi(y_{i},u_{i})-\mathbb{E}\phi(y,u)
for an i.i.d. set
of samples using well-established bounds from the theory of
empirical processes. In particular, we give bounds on the
expectation of the excess risk as well as a high-probability/tail bound. Our interest in such a bound is clear in light
of
Lemma

28
and proof of
Theorem

7
.
However, we wish to obtain an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}
that
is
\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{2+\gamma}})
rather than
\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{4}})
.
To do this we need to control the excess risk
by localizing our analysis in a neighborhood of the true map
\phi^{\dagger}
.

The following proposition
is familiar in the empirical process theory literature:

Proposition 33
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on a vector space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d}}{\sim}\mu
. Suppose
\mathcal{F}
is a class of real-valued functions such that
\|\phi\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\leq r
and
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}\leq R
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{F}
. For any
r>0
, define

\displaystyle J_{N}(\mathcal{F}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{r}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})})}{\,\rm{d}}\delta+\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{R}\log\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}){\,\rm{d}}\delta\,,

(62)

Then, it holds that:

1.

There exists a universal constant
C_{\textnormal{exp}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}{\sup}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})-\mu(\phi)\right]\leq C_{\textnormal{exp}}J_{N}(\mathcal{F})\,.

(63)

2.

There exists a universal constant

C_{\textnormal{prob}}>0
such that for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\underset{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}{\sup}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})-\mu(\phi)\geq C_{\textnormal{prob}}\left(J_{N}(\mathcal{F})+r\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}+R\frac{t}{N}\right)\right]\leq\exp(-t).

(64)

Proof.

The bound in (
63

[
46

by taking, in the notation of that result,

\epsilon=\widetilde{\epsilon}=\eta=0

We note that our setup is much simpler than
[
46
]
which suggests a
simpler proof may be possible, see for example
[
56

The high probability bound in (

from the tail bound
[
111
, Thm. 2.14.25]
, which provides a way to estimate with high probability how the process
\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})-\mu(\phi)
deviates from its mean, which is controlled by
J_{N}(\mathcal{F})
according to (
63

[
111
, Thm. 2.14.25]
set

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{G}_{N}\coloneqq\underset{\phi\in\mathcal{F}}{\sup}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})-\mu(\phi)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{N}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\mathbb{G}_{N}\,,

to obtain the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{G}_{N}>C\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mu_{N}+\frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(-D\min\left\{\frac{s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}},\frac{s\sqrt{N}}{R}\right\}\right)\,,

for universal constants
C,D

To express the bound in terms of

while satisfying both tail-regime constraints, set

\frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}\coloneqq\max\left\{r\sqrt{\frac{t}{DN}},\frac{Rt}{DN}\right\}
so that

\displaystyle\frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}\leq r\sqrt{\frac{t}{DN}}+\frac{Rt}{DN},\quad\textnormal{and}\;\min\left\{\frac{s^{2}}{r^{2}},\frac{s\sqrt{N}}{R}\right\}=\frac{t}{D}.

This allows us to rewrite the bound as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{G}_{N}>C\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mu_{N}+r\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}+R\frac{t}{N}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(-t\right)\,,

(65)

absorbing the constant
\max\{1/D,1/\sqrt{D},1\}
in

.
Ultimately, (
64

65

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{G}_{N}>C\left(J_{N}(\mathcal{F})+r\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}+R\frac{t}{N}\right)\right)\\
&\hskip 34.44434pt\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{G}_{N}>C\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mu_{N}+r\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}+R\frac{t}{N}\right)\right)\,,\end{split}

which is given immediately by the bound
\frac{\mu_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\leq BJ_{N}(\mathcal{F})
of (

) and absorbing
B
into
C
.

We can further simplify

Lemma 34
.

Assume
\mathcal{N}(\delta,\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}})\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}\delta^{-\gamma}\log(1+\delta^{-1})

Proof.

Using the hypothesis of the theorem we can readily write

\displaystyle J_{N}(\mathcal{F})\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathcal{F}}}{N}}I_{1}+\frac{C_{\mathcal{F}}}{N}I_{2}\,,

where we introduced the quantities

Below we will show that

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 10Q\,\epsilon^{1-\gamma/2}\sqrt{\log(1+\epsilon^{-1})},

(68)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2R(1-\gamma)^{-2}\,,

(69)

Bound for

Introduce the rescaling

\displaystyle I_{1}

where we note that the last inequality holds since

Bound for

Decompose

\displaystyle\begin{split}\beta_{\text{max}}\theta(N,{\text{bias}}(\widetilde{\phi})&+\sigma,t)+\left(\frac{\beta_{\text{max}}}{\alpha_{\text{min}}}+1\right){\text{bias}}(\widetilde{\phi})^{2}\\

&+\beta_{\text{max}}R\frac{1}{N}t\,,\end{split}

where the notation

The expectation bound:

With the high-probability bound at hand we now move on to obtaining an expectation

This part of the proof is essentially a calculation that relates our tail bound to

So far we have shown that, with probability

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}\leq 2C_{\textnormal{bias}}^{2}\left(\frac{\log(N)}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+4C_{\sigma}^{2}\left(\left(\frac{\log(N)}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+\frac{t+1}{N}\right)\,,

Then, by splitting the integral we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}=\textnormal{I}+\textnormal{II}\,,

where we wrote

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{2C_{\textnormal{bias}}^{2}\left(\frac{\log(N)}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+4C_{\sigma}^{2}\left(\left(\frac{\log(N)}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+\frac{t_{0}+1}{N}\right)}\mathbb{P}\left(\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}>x\right){\,\rm{d}}x\,,

while the second term is bounded for

\displaystyle=\frac{4C_{\sigma}^{2}}{N}\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg[\|\nabla_{u}\widehat{\phi}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt>2C_{\textnormal{bias}}^{2}\left(\frac{\log(N)}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+4C_{\sigma}^{2}\left(\left(\frac{\log(N)}{N}\right)^{\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}+\frac{t+1}{N}\right)\bigg]{\,\rm{d}}t

\displaystyle\|\phi-\phi^{\dagger}-\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(\phi(y,\cdot)-\phi^{\dagger}(y,\cdot))\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}

Next, we prove

\displaystyle\|\phi^{*}-\phi^{{\dagger}*}-\nu(\cdot|y)

\displaystyle(\phi^{*}(\cdot,u)-\phi^{{\dagger}*}(\cdot,u))\|_{L_{\nu}^{2}}^{2}

82

The Poincaré inequality for
\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L_{\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{PI}^{\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}}\|\nabla_{y}F\|_{L_{\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}}^{2}\,.

By the Leibniz’s integral rule,

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}F=\text{I}+\text{II}\,,

where we introduced

\displaystyle\text{I}:=\int\nabla_{y}\Bigr(\phi(y,u)-\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)\Bigr)d\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u),

\displaystyle\text{II}:=\nabla_{y}\int\Bigr(\phi^{*}(y,u)-\phi^{{\dagger}*}(y,u)\Bigr)d\nu(u\mid y).

Recalling that

\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger}}(y,\cdot)\#\eta_{\mathcal{U}}=\nu(\cdot\mid y)

\displaystyle\text{II}=\nabla_{y}

\displaystyle\int\Bigr(\phi^{*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u))-\phi^{{\dagger}*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u))\Bigr){\,\rm{d}}\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u)=\text{III}-\text{IV},

\displaystyle\text{III}:=\int\nabla_{y}\Bigr[\phi^{*}\Bigr(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)\Bigr)\Bigr]d\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u)\,,

III

\displaystyle=\int-\nabla_{y}\phi\Bigr(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}\Bigr(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)\Bigr)\Bigr)+\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u))\nabla_{yu}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)d\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u).

Similarly,

\displaystyle=\int-\nabla_{y}\phi^{\dagger}\Bigr(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger}*}\Bigr(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)\Bigr)\Bigr)+\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger}*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u))\nabla_{yu}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)d\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u)

where the last step follows from the fact that
\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,\cdot)^{-1}=\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger}*}(y,\cdot)

\nabla_{y}F(y)=\text{I}+\text{II}=\text{I}+\text{III}-\text{IV}=\text{V}+\text{VI}

\displaystyle\text{V}:=\int\nabla_{y}\phi(y,u)-\nabla_{y}\phi\Bigr(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}\Bigr(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)\Bigr)\Bigr)d\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u),

\displaystyle\text{VI}:=\int\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u))\nabla_{yu}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)-\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger}*}(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u))\nabla_{yu}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)d\eta_{\mathcal{U}}(u).

By change of measure
\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger}*}(y,\cdot)\#\nu(\cdot|y)=\eta_{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle\text{V}=\int\nabla_{y}\phi(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger}*}(y,u))-\nabla_{y}\phi(y,\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}(y,u))d\nu(u\mid y),

\displaystyle\text{VI}=\int\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}(y,u)\nabla_{yu}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)-\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger},*}(y,u)\nabla_{yu}\phi^{\dagger}(y,u)d\nu(u\mid y).

Similarly, applying Hölder’s inequality (

Then, by the triangle inequality, we can bound
\|\nabla_{y}F\|_{L_{\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{y}F\|_{L_{\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\|\nabla_{y}F(y)\|^{2}d\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq 2\int\|\text{V}\|^{2}+\|\text{VI}\|^{2}d\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}(y)

\displaystyle=4L^{2}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{{\dagger}*}-\nabla_{u}\phi^{*}\|_{L_{\nu}^{2}}^{2}\leq 4L^{2}\frac{\beta_{\text{max}}}{\alpha_{\text{min}}}\|\nabla_{u}\phi^{\dagger}-\nabla_{u}\phi\|_{L_{\eta}^{2}}^{2}\,,

.
