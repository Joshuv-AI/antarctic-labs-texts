Observations Meet Actions: Learning Control-Sufficient Representations for Robust Policy Generalization
Yuliang Gu yuliang3@illinois.edu, Hongpeng Cao cao.hongpeng@tum.de, Marco Caccamo mcaccamo@tum.de, Naira Hovakimyan nhovakim@illinois.edu

Abstract
Capturing latent variations ( “contexts” ) is key to deploying reinforcement-learning (RL) agents beyond their training regime. We recast context-based RL as a dual inference–control problem and formally characterize two properties and their hierarchy: observation sufficiency (preserving all predictive information) and control sufficiency (retaining decision-making relevant information). Exploiting this dichotomy, we derive a contextual evidence lower bound(ELBO)-style objective that cleanly separates representation learning from policy learning and optimizes it with Bottlenecked Contextual Policy Optimization (BCPO) , an algorithm that places a variational information-bottleneck encoder in front of any off-policy policy learner. On standard continuous-control benchmarks with shifting physical parameters, BCPO matches or surpasses other baselines while using fewer samples and retaining performance far outside the training regime. The framework unifies theory, diagnostics, and practice for context-based RL.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has recently demonstrated (super-)human proficiency from mastering complex games
(
Silver et al. 2017
)
to executing dexterous robotic manipulation
(
Andrychowicz et al. 2020
)
. These achievements, however, were accomplished in controlled environments whose dynamics and objectives remain fixed during training and evaluation. Real-world applications rarely enjoy such static conditions. For example, robots face wear-and-tear, payload changes, or drifting sensor calibrations; autonomous vehicles encounter new road conditions and evolving traffic patterns; and assistive agents must adapt to users’ changing preferences
(
Mao et al. 2023
)
.

Practical deployments must therefore cope with
latent contexts
, unobserved variables that can change from episode to episode by altering either the dynamics (e.g., a mobile robot carrying an unknown load), the reward structure (e.g., a newly specified goal), or the observation process (e.g., temporary sensor noise). When the true context drifts beyond the training distribution, monolithic “single-context” policies can suffer abrupt performance degradation
(
Zhang et al. 2018
)
.

A widely adopted approach is domain randomization and its extensions (e.g., curriculum-based sampling
(
Eimer et al. 2021
)
). By exposing the agent to a broad spectrum of environments, domain randomization encourages robustness to nuisance factors
(
Tobin et al. 2017
)
. Despite its simplicity, this method demands detailed prior knowledge of all task-relevant variations and often requires virtually unlimited simulation budgets. Moreover, its black-box nature leaves the deployed agent with no explicit understanding of the specific context it encounters at test time.

A more transparent alternative casts the challenge as a
dual
task of (i)
latent context inference
and (ii)
conditional control

(
Oord et al. 2018
;
Rakelly et al. 2019
;
Lee et al. 2020b
;
Benjamins et al.
)
. A dedicated
context encoder
summarizes past interactions into a compact latent code, which the policy then conditions on to select actions. This “grey‑box” architecture retains interpretability while allowing each module to exploit specialized advances on each end: powerful representation learners such as variational and contrastive methods
(
Kingma and Welling 2013
)
for inference, and proven reinforcement learning algorithms such as Soft Actor‑Critic (SAC)
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
for control. The resulting
modular
pipeline combines expressive context representations with sample‑efficient policy optimization, yielding agents that generalize more reliably across different scenarios.

Despite its encouraging empirical gains, the
information structure
underlying such dual architecture in reinforcement learning is still poorly understood. In particular, several foundational questions remain obscure, or their answers are dispersed across disparate research communities:

•

Inference Side.

What is the minimum amount of information an encoder must keep to “name” the hidden context variable with high confidence?

•

Control Side.

Which parts of the information does a policy need to “act” optimally? Is full reconstruction of the state(-action) value landscape necessary?

•

Coupling.

How are the information demands of inference and control intrinsically linked, and can this link be exploited to build sample-efficient, robust algorithms?

Answering these questions above sharpens the theoretical picture of context-based RL and, crucially, shows
how the representation learning(inference) must interact with the policy optimization(control)
. This paper provides those answers through three contributions:

1.

Unified theoretical framework.
We give precise definitions of
observation sufficiency
(for inference) and
control sufficiency
(for decision-making), establish their hierarchy, and prove when and how one implies
the other.

2.

Principled Optimization Objective.
Embedding these definitions in a variational RL view, we
derive an
evidence lower bound
(ELBO) that cleanly separates representation refinement from policy improvement.
A novel
information residual
decomposes into intrinsic
(processing and encoding) and extrinsic (replay) information gaps, giving a transparent optimization pipeline.

3.

Algorithmic Realization and Validation.
We instantiate the theory in
Bottlenecked Contextual
Policy Optimization
(
BCPO
), which allows plug-and-play of any off-the-shelf max-entropy RL methods. Experiments on continuous-control benchmarks show superior sample efficiency and robustness to context variations.

Together, these contributions offer an end-to-end route from
information-theoretic definitions to a robust, sample-efficient algorithm for context-based RL. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section
2
reviews information-theoretic and RL preliminaries; Sections
3
&
4
develop the sufficiency hierarchy and the information-residual decomposition; Sections
5
&
6
present residual minimization and the
BCPO
algorithm; Section
7
reports empirical results; Section
8
situates our work within the literature; and Section
9
concludes with limitations and directions for future research.

Preliminaries

2.1
Reinforcement Learning

Consider a Markov decision process (MDP)
Sutton et al. 1998

(\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{A},\,\mathcal{T},\,r,\gamma),

where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
the action space,
\mathcal{T}
the stochastic transition kernel with probability distribution
p(s^{\prime}|s,a)
, and
r(s,a)
the reward function. For notational convenience, we set the discount factor
\gamma=1
. The reinforcement learning (RL) objective is to find a (stochastic) policy
\pi(a|s)
that maximizes the expected return

J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tau)}\Bigl[R(\tau)\Bigr],\quad R(\tau):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r(s_{t},a_{t})

(2.1)

under the trajectory distribution

p(\tau)=p(s_{1},a_{1},\dots,s_{T},a_{T})=p(s_{1})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})\;\pi(a_{t}|s_{t}).

RL as Probabilistic Inference.

Following the control-as-inference framework
(
Ziebart 2010
;
Levine 2018
)
, we attach a binary optimality variable
o_{t}
to each
(s_{t},a_{t})
with

p(o_{t}=1\mid s_{t},a_{t})\propto\exp\{r(s_{t},a_{t})\}
. The joint
density of a trajectory
\tau
and the
optimal state-action event

o_{1:T}=1
becomes

p(\tau,o_{1:T}=1)\propto p(\tau)\exp\{R(\tau)\},

where
p(\tau)
is the trajectory distribution induced by
\pi
and

R(\tau)=\sum_{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})
. Let
q_{\pi}(\tau)
be the
controlled
trajectory distribution induced by a policy

\pi
. Minimizing

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(q_{\pi}(\tau)\,\|\,p(\tau\mid o_{1:T}=1)\bigr)

is equivalent to
maximizing
the evidence lower bound (ELBO)
(
Levine 2018
)
:

\max_{\pi}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{q_{\pi}}\Bigl[R(\tau)-\log q_{\pi}(\tau)\Bigr].

Because
-\log q_{\pi}(\tau)=\sum_{t}\!-\!\log\pi(a_{t}\!\mid\!s_{t})
up
to constants, and writing
\mathcal{H}\bigl(\pi(\!\cdot\!\mid s_{t})\bigr)

for the action entropy, we obtain the
maximum-entropy RL (MaxEnt RL)
objective

J\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{q_{\pi}}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(r(s_{t},a_{t})+\mathcal{H}\!\bigl(\pi(\!\cdot\!\mid s_{t})\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr].

(2.2)

2.2
Information–theoretic preliminaries

Let
(X,Y)\sim p(x,y)
be a pair of random variables with marginals

p(x)
and
p(y)
.

Entropy and mutual information.

The (Shannon) entropy of
X
is

H(X)\;=\;-\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\!\bigl[\log p(x)\bigr].

The
mutual information
(MI) between
X
and
Y
measures how much
knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about the other
(
Cover 1999
)
:

I(X;Y)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\!\Bigl[\log\tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}\Bigr]\;=\;H(X)-H(X\!\mid Y)\;=\;H(Y)-H(Y\!\mid X),

i.e. the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the joint distribution and
the product of the marginals:

I(X;Y)=\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\bigr).

Data–processing inequality (DPI).

If
X\!\to\!Z\!\to\!Y
forms a Markov chain, then any
post-processing

cannot increase information:

I(X;Y)\;\leq\;I(X;Z).

Equality holds
iff

X
and
Y
are conditionally independent given

Z
, i.e.
X\!\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\mid Z
.

Information Bottleneck (IB).

Given an
input

X
and a
relevant target

Y
, the
Information Bottleneck principle

(
Tishby et al. 2000
;
Alemi et al. 2016
)
seeks a stochastic encoder

q_{\phi}(z|x)
that produces a compressed latent representation

Z\!=\!Z_{\phi}(X)
while retaining maximal predictive power about
Y
.
The canonical Lagrangian form is

\min_{q_{\phi}(z\mid x)}\;\beta\;I(X;Z)\;-\;I(Z;Y),

where
I_{\phi}(\cdot;\cdot)
denotes MI under the joint distribution
induced by
q_{\phi}
, and the non-negative hyper-parameter

\beta
trades
compression
(
I(X;Z)
) against
relevance

(
I(Z;Y)
).

3
Contextual Reinforcement Learning

We consider
contextual reinforcement learning (cRL)
, where the
environment’s dynamics are modulated at the episode level by an

unobserved
latent variable—called the
context
—that is
sampled once at the beginning of each episode and remains fixed
thereafter. This formalism captures many practical scenarios, including domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer, context-augmented policies, and goal-conditioned control.

Contextual MDP.

Formally, every context

c\in\mathcal{C}
instantiates a Markov decision process

\mathcal{M}(c)\;:=\;\bigl(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}_{c},r_{c}\bigr),

where
\mathcal{T}_{c}(s^{\prime}\!\mid s,a)
is the state–transition kernel and
r_{c}(s,a)
is the associated reward function. We assume that both the dynamics and the reward share the same context parameterization.

At the start of an episode, the environment draws

c\sim p(c)
1
1

1

Unless stated otherwise,
p(c)
is taken to be uniform over
\mathcal{C}
.

and the agent,
without observing
c
, interacts for
T
steps,
producing a trajectory

\tau=(s_{1:T},a_{1:T})

drawn from the joint distribution
p(c,\tau)
.

Objective.

A
context-conditioned policy

\pi(a\mid s,c)

achieves expected return

J(\pi)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{p(c,\tau)}\!\Bigl[R(\tau)\Bigr],\qquad R(\tau)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{c}\bigl(s_{t},a_{t}\bigr).

(3.1)

If
c
were
observed
, maximizing (
3.1
)
reduces to standard RL in the augmented state

\hat{s}:=(s,c)
. Because
c
is hidden and stochastic, the agent
must
infer
it from interaction before acting.

Motivation.

Hidden contexts create two information demands:

1.

Observation sufficiency.

How much information must the agent gather to identify the
context?

2.

Control sufficiency.

How much information is required to act
near-optimally without ever revealing the true context?

Generally speaking, the gap between the two is precisely the information about
c
that is
irrelevant
for decision-making. Empirically, many RL methods exploit this idea implicitly: contrastive
predictive coding
(
Oord et al. 2018
)
, goal-conditioned
latent models
(
Zeng et al. 2023
)
, meta-RL context
inference
(
Rakelly et al. 2019
)
, domain-randomization with invariant features
(
Higgins et al. 2017
)
,
and adversarial domain generalization
(
Hansen and Wang 2021
)
.
Yet these methods rely on heuristics; a
principled framework
that quantifies the trade-off and guides algorithm design remains absent. The remainder of this paper supplies such a framework, giving information-theoretic definitions of both sufficiency notions, revealing their fundamental relationship, and proposing a practical algorithm.

4
Observation & Control Sufficiency

4.1
Observation (representation) Sufficiency

During an episode, the agent observes a
contextual
interaction history

\tau=(s_{1:T},a_{1:T})\sim p(\tau|c),

but never the latent context
C
itself. We cast the “observation (representation)
problem” as an
inference
task: an
encoder

q_{\phi}(z\mid\tau)

, parameterized by
\phi
, compresses the trajectory into a latent code
Z
, inducing the
data-processing chain

C\;\to\;\tau\;\to\;Z.

Intuitively, the encoder compresses the information-rich trajectory
\tau
into a summary
Z
whose role is to extract exactly the bits of
\tau
that reveal which hidden
C
generated the data, so
that
Z
can serve as a surrogate for the unobserved context. The processing order
C\to\tau\to Z
forms a Markov chain, which imposes two crucial physical constraints:
(i)
All information about
C
available to the encoder must flow through the trajectory, and
(ii)

Z
can never be
more
informative about the context than the trajectory itself.

Processing an
entire
trajectory is often unnecessary and, for
high-dimensional control tasks, computationally prohibitive. In many systems, the context is revealed by only a few early transitions, e.g., a cart–pole whose pole mass can be inferred from the first swings. We
therefore introduce a length-
k
processing window

O_{k}:=\bigl(s_{1:k},a_{1:k}\bigr),\qquad k\in\mathcal{T}:=\{1,\dots,T\},

and feed this window to the encoder,
q_{\phi}(z\mid O_{k})
. For notational simplicity, we drop the subscript and write
O
once
k
is fixed. Before formalizing sufficiency, we impose an operational predictability requirement on the chosen window to ensure that the window
O
is information-rich enough for inference.

{assumption}

[Processing window]

There exists
0<\delta<1
such that the Bayes error of predicting

C
from
O
satisfies

P_{e}(O):=\!\min_{\hat{C}}\Pr\!\bigl[\hat{C}(O)\neq C\bigr]\;\leq\;\delta.

Assumption
4.1
does not dictate
how

the agent recovers
C
; it merely claims that
some
decoder/estimator can
achieve Bayes error at most
\delta
.
If the window is too short (e.g.
k=1
in systems where inertia
matters), the assumption fails, and by the

data-processing inequality
no representation learned from that
window can retrieve the missing contextual information.

Remark 1

(Assumption
4.1
is operational)
.

Assumption
4.1
converts a difficult

dynamical
property–how strongly the context shapes transitions–into a simple
data-driven
test: the chosen window

O
must carry enough statistical signal to identify
C
with small
error. It imposes
no
reliance on independence, stationarity, or special information structure (e.g. linear–Gaussian). In practice, one can adopt an iterative “window-growing” strategy: start with a small

k
and increase it until empirical validation (or the theoretical threshold in Lemma
4
) confirms that

P_{e}(O)\leq\delta
. This approach is operationally convenient and sidesteps model specification issues, and we revisit it in detail in later sections.

Definition 2

(Observation sufficiency)
.

Let the window
O
satisfy Assumption
4.1
.
An encoder
q_{\phi}(z\mid o)
is observation sufficient for
the latent context
C
if

I(C;O)\;=\;I(C;Z),

i.e., the code
Z
preserves all information about
C
that is
contained in the processing window
O
.

Proposition 3

(Characterization via DPI)
.

Given the Markov chain
C\!\to\!O\!\to\!Z
,

I(C;O)=I(C;Z)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;I(C;O\mid Z)=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;C\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;O\;\mid\;Z.

Proof.

Apply the chain rule for mutual information and DPI. See
Cover 1999
(Thm. 2.8.1) for the full argument.
∎

Definition
2
demands that
Z
preserve

exactly
the pieces of
O
that explain the latent context
C
. Hence, any nuisance variation in
O
(sensor noise, irrelevant scene
details, reward noise,
etc.
) is stripped away, yielding a
minimal signal for identifying the context. This sharpness is desirable both theoretically, because it
enables a clean connection to the control sufficiency criterion we
introduce next, and practically, because it concentrates the
encoder’s capacity on context relevant information, yielding a compact representation for any downstream task.

The next lemma makes this notion concrete by relating mutual information loss to Bayes error: an observation sufficient code
Z
inherits the same Bayes error from the data window
O
.

Lemma 4

(Sharpness via Fano’s inequality)
.

Let
N=|\mathcal{C}|
and
C
be uniform on
\mathcal{C}
. Suppose the window
O
satisfies
Assumption
4.1
with Bayes error

P_{e}(O)\leq\delta
.

1.

If
q_{\phi}(z|o)
is observation-sufficient, then

P_{e}(Z)=P_{e}(O)\leq\delta
.

2.

If the encoder discards
\varepsilon>0
bits,
i.e.
I(C;O)-I(C;Z)=\varepsilon
,
every decoder that sees only
Z
yields more error

P_{e}(Z)\;\geq\;\delta+\varepsilon/\log N.

Proof.

Fano’s inequality.

By Assumption
4.1
the window
O
satisfies

P_{e}(O)\leq\delta
. Fano’s inequality for finite alphabets gives

I(C;O)\;\geq\;(1-\delta)\log N-\log 2.

(4.1)

Statement 1 (observation sufficiency).
Under the Markov chain
C\!\to\!O\!\to\!Z
, observation sufficiency

\,I(C;O)=I(C;Z)\,
implies

I(C;O\mid Z)=0

(Prop.
3
), or equivalently

C\perp\!\!\!\perp O\mid Z.

Hence, for every
(o,z)
with
q_{\phi}(z\mid o)>0
, we have

p(c\mid o,z)=p(c\mid z).

(4.2)

Using the definition of Bayes error and (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle P_{e}(O)

\displaystyle=\min_{\hat{C}}\Pr\!\bigl[\hat{C}(O)\neq C\bigr]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{O}\bigl[\max_{c}p(c|O)\bigr]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{O,Z}\!\bigl[\max_{c}p(c\mid O,Z)\bigr]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\!\bigl[\max_{c}p(c\mid Z)\bigr]

\displaystyle=P_{e}(Z).

Thus, if the encoder is observation sufficient, we have

P_{e}(Z)=P_{e}(O)\leq\delta
.

Statement 2 (sharpness).
Suppose
I(C;O)-I(C;Z)=\varepsilon>0
.
Combining (
4.1
) with this relation yields

I(C;Z)\;\geq\;(1-\delta)\log N-\log 2-\varepsilon.

Applying Fano’s inequality to
Z
gives

\displaystyle P_{e}(Z)

\displaystyle\geq\;1-\frac{I(C;Z)+\log 2}{\log N}

\displaystyle\geq\;1-\frac{(1-\delta)\log N-\varepsilon}{\log N}=\delta+\frac{\varepsilon}{\log N},

which gives statement 2.
∎

Observation sufficiency converts a “good-enough” data window

O
into a latent code
Z
that is equally informative about
the context: every bit in
Z
serves to identify
C
and none is
wasted (Lemma
4
). For
any
downstream prediction or classification task, this is ideal, because
Z
can replace
C
as a drop-in surrogate.

Policy learning, however, has a different objective. To select
high-return actions, the agent must exploit precisely those features that drive future rewards and transitions, not merely
the bits that help “name” the context. The next section introduces
control sufficiency
, a criterion that aligns the latent code
Z
with the cMDP’s value structure.

Remark 5

(“Soft” sufficiency via the Information Bottleneck)
.

In practice, we often prefer a small information gap

I(C;O)-I(C;Z)\leq\epsilon
(statement 2 in Lemma
4
), exchanging losslessness for a simpler,
potentially more robust code. Formally, this is a rate distortion problem
(
Cover 1999
)
: we seek the

cheapest
representation
Z
(low rate
I(O;Z)
) that
retains almost all context-relevant information (high
I(Z;C)
. A convenient Lagrangian, known as the
information bottleneck
(IB)

(
Tishby et al. 2000
)
, is

\min_{q(z|o)}\;\beta\,I(O;Z)\;-\;I(C;Z),

where the multiplier
\beta>0
controls the trade-off. A smaller
\beta
preserves more context information, and a larger
\beta
encourages additional compression. We will leverage
this convenient IB formulation when designing the algorithmic components in later sections.

4.2
Control Sufficiency

Observation sufficiency (Section
4.1
) guarantees
that a latent code
Z
preserves every bit of information in the
window
O
that helps
identify
the hidden context
C
. Policy learning, however, asks a different question:
how well can an agent
exploit
that code to choose
(near-)optimal actions?
Formally, given an encoder
q_{\phi}
and a
Z
-conditioned policy

\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,z)
, we compare the achieved return to the context-aware optimum.

Definition 6

(Control sufficiency (weak))
.

Denote the optimal contextual return

J^{\star}:=\max_{\pi(a\mid s,c)}J(\pi),

where
J(\pi)
is defined in (
3.1
).
The encoder
q_{\phi}
is weakly control sufficient if there exists a

Z
-conditioned policy
\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,z)
such that

J\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}\bigr)=J^{\star}.

(4.3)

By definition, control sufficiency is inherently a
joint
property: the code

Z
produced by the encoder is only as good as the policy that can exploit it. Any residual uncertainty about
C
is harmless so long as the accompanying policy can map
that code to actions that achieve the maximal long-term return; what matters is that the encoder–policy pair preserves the
reward-relevant information. The next proposition formalizes this intuition by showing that such a pair aligns the optimal state-action functions in an
expectation-wise
sense.

Proposition 7

(Expectation-wise value alignment)
.

Let
(q_{\phi},\pi_{\theta})
be an encoder–policy pair and define

Q^{\star}(s,a,c)\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)

as the optimal
Q
-functions when the agent observes either the true
context
c
or only the code
z
. Suppose

1.

Weak control sufficiency
:
the
Z
-conditioned policy achieves the contextual optimum,

J(\pi_{\theta})=J^{\star}
.

2.

Markov in augmented state
:
the augmented state
(s,z)
is Markov, i.e.
P(s_{t+1}\mid s_{1:t},a_{1:t},c,z)=P(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t},c,z)
.

Then,

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,Q^{\star}(s,a,C)\mid Z=z\bigr].

(4.4)

Proof.

Step 1: joint optimality.

Control sufficiency implies

J(\pi_{\theta})=J^{\star}
.
Because the discounted return is the
unique
fixed point of the
Bellman optimality operator, we have

V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s,c)=V^{\star}(s,c)\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(s,a,c)=Q^{\star}(s,a,c)\quad\forall(s,a,c).

Step 2 optimality in the
(s,z)
-augmented MDP.

The policy
\pi_{\theta}
depends only on the pair
(s,z)
, so

Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(s,a,z)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(s,a,C)\mid Z=z\bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,Q^{\star}(s,a,C)\mid Z=z\bigr].

By the second assumption, the augmented state
\hat{s}:=(s,z)
remains Markov, so dynamic programming arguments apply. Since
J(\pi_{\theta})
is already the
maximal
return in this
augmented MDP,
\pi_{\theta}
must also be optimal for it, i.e.
Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(s,a,z)=Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)
.
Combining with the equality above yields exactly

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\,Q^{\star}(s,a,C)\mid Z=z\bigr],

which is (
4.4
).
∎

Proposition
7
shows that weak control sufficiency aligns state-action values only in expectation. For any code value
z
, the encoder groups a subset of contexts

\mathcal{\{}c\subset\mathcal{C}:q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0\},
2
2

2

q_{\phi}(z|c)
is obtained from the window-based encoder
q_{\phi}(z|o)
by marginalizing the contextual observation from

.

and the optimal

in the

-augmented MDP is simply the

of the context-optimal

’s:

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)\;=\;\sum_{c}Q^{\star}(s,a,c)\,\Pr\!\bigl[C=c\mid Z=z\bigr].

In other words, the

equals the
average
of the context-optimal
Q

C\!\mid\!Z=z
. Thus, the pair
(q_{\phi},\pi_{\theta})
achieves the optimal

Remark 8

(Weak control sufficiency in practice)
.

In practice, a widely used approach is to augment the policy network with an auxiliary context head. Training typically minimizes a joint loss of the form:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}\;+\;\alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}},\qquad\alpha>0,

(4.5)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}
is the reinforcement learning objective (e.g. a policy-gradient or actor–critic loss),
\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}
is a supervised loss that tries to predict the context from shared features (e.g., typically a cross‐entropy or contrastive loss
(
Oord et al. 2018
)
). The hyperparameter
\alpha
balances the two terms and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RL}}

dominates
. Because the loss is jointly optimized, the network is free to
merge
context variations that do not change the expected
return, precisely the expectation-wise characterization by weak control sufficiency. The resulting
Z
is therefore sufficient for high average
performance, yet it still hides residual uncertainty about which factors in
C
are actually in effect.

A stronger notion of control sufficiency.

The weak definition characterizes the sufficiency in the sense of expected optimal return, yet it has two practical drawbacks:
(i)
high TD variance during training: a critic trained on the augmented state
(s,z)
must learn the
average

Q
–value across all contexts aggregated by the same code value
z
. The larger the mixture, the noisier the TD targets,
(ii)
drift under distribution shift: if the mixture
\Pr[C\mid Z=z]
changes (e.g. because the encoder is typically updated jointly with the policy), the averaged target itself drifts. We therefore introduce a stronger notion of control sufficiency.

Definition 9

(Control sufficiency (strong))
.

The encoder
q_{\phi}
is strongly control sufficient if there exists a

Z
-conditioned policy
\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,z)
such that, for almost every
(s,a,c,z)
with

q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0
, one has

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)=Q^{\star}(s,a,c).

(4.6)

This stronger condition rules out the averaging effects in the weak Definition
6
, requiring a point-wise match of optimal
Q
-values.

Corollary 10

(Strong
\;\Rightarrow\;
weak control sufficiency)
.

If the encoder
q_{\phi}(z\mid o)
is strongly
control sufficient in the sense of
Definition
9
, then it is also weakly control sufficient.

Proof.

Strong sufficiency gives the point-wise identity

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)=Q^{\star}(s,a,c)
whenever

q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0
.
Hence, for every state–code pair
(s,z)
the policy

\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,z)=\mathbf{1}\!\bigl\{a=\arg\max_{a^{\prime}}Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a^{\prime},z)\bigr\}

coincides with an optimal context-aware action wherever the pair has non-zero probability. This policy, therefore, achieves the
optimal return
J^{\star}
.
∎

Compared with the weak characterization, the point-wise criterion (
4.6
) is both theoretically and practically preferable, because of

•

Lower variance:

TD targets now are
point-wise
, thus critics converge faster;

•

Robustness:

Point-wise equality is robust to shifts in the distribution
p(c)
;

•

Modularity:

The representation and control layers can be trained
separately
:
once
q_{\phi}
is fixed, any off-the-shelf RL algorithm can
operate on the augmented state
(s,z)
;

•

Clean theory:
Interpretable relationship with observation sufficiency (see subsequent sections).

4.3
Control–Observation Sufficiency Relationship

We now compare the two key notions introduced so far.

•

Observation sufficiency

(Def.
2
): the encoder
q_{\phi}
keeps all relevant information about the latent context
C
in the processing windon
O
,

I(C;O)=I(C;Z)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;I(C;O\mid Z)=0.

In essence,
Z
can “name” the context in any inference task.

•

Control sufficiency

(Def.
6
–
9
): there exists an
encoder–policy pair
(q_{\phi},\pi_{\theta})
such that

J(\pi_{\theta})=J^{\star}\quad(\text{weak}),\qquad Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)=Q^{\star}(s,a,c)\;(\text{strong})

whenever
q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0
. Weak sufficiency aligns state-action values in expectation; strong sufficiency aligns them point-wise.

Lemma 11
.

Every strongly control sufficient encoder is
observation sufficient. The converse is false: one can build contextual MDPs where an encoder is observation sufficient but not strongly control sufficient.

Proof.

(i) control
\;\Rightarrow\;
observation.

Assume
q_{\phi}
is control-sufficient but, for
contradiction,
not
observation sufficient.
Then,
I(C;O\mid Z)>0
, so there exist

c_{1}\neq c_{2}
and
z
with

p(c_{1}\mid z)>0,\qquad p(c_{2}\mid z)>0.

(4.7)

Because the two contexts differ, at least one of the following holds:

1.

Different rewards:

There is
(\bar{s},\bar{a})
s.t.
r_{c_{1}}(\bar{s},\bar{a})\neq r_{c_{2}}(\bar{s},\bar{a})
.
Consequently,

Q^{\star}(\bar{s},\bar{a},c_{1})\neq Q^{\star}(\bar{s},\bar{a},c_{2})
.

2.

Different transitions:

There exists
(\bar{s},\bar{a})
and
\bar{s}^{\prime}
with

P_{c_{1}}(\bar{s}^{\prime}\mid\bar{s},\bar{a})\neq P_{c_{2}}(\bar{s}^{\prime}\mid\bar{s},\bar{a})
.
Since the Bellman optimality operator is a contraction,
iterating it reveals a pair
(\bar{s},\bar{a})
such that

Q^{\star}(\bar{s},\bar{a},c_{1})\neq Q^{\star}(\bar{s},\bar{a},c_{2})
.

Hence, in either case we find
(\bar{s},\bar{a})
with

Q^{\star}(\bar{s},\bar{a},c_{1})\neq Q^{\star}(\bar{s},\bar{a},c_{2}).

(4.8)

However, strong control sufficiency implies the point-wise equality

Q^{\star}_{Z}(\bar{s},\bar{a},z)=Q^{\star}(\bar{s},\bar{a},c_{1})=Q^{\star}(\bar{s},\bar{a},c_{2}),\qquad\text{for all }c\text{ with }q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0,

contradicting (
4.7
). Therefore,
I(C;O\mid Z)=0
and the encoder is observation sufficient.

(ii) Observation
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
strong control.

We construct a one–step contextual bandit:

•

Contexts
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{0},c_{1}\}
with
\Pr[c_{0}]=\Pr[c_{1}]=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

•

Actions
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{+},a_{-}\}
.

•

Rewards

r_{c_{1}}(a_{+})=+1,\;r_{c_{1}}(a_{-})=-1,\;r_{c_{0}}(a_{+})=-1,\;r_{c_{0}}(a_{-})=+1
.

•

Observations
O\in\{o_{+},o_{-}\}
with

\Pr[o_{+}\mid c_{1}]=p,\;\Pr[o_{+}\mid c_{0}]=1-p
for any
p\in(0,1)\setminus\{\tfrac{1}{2}\}
.

Define the deterministic encoder

Z:=\Pr[C=c_{1}\mid O]\in\{p,1-p\}
.
Because
Z
is a measurable function of
O
,

I(C;Z)=I(C;O)
, the encoder is therefore observation-sufficient.

The expected reward of action
a_{+}
conditioned on
Z=z
is

\mathbb{E}[r(a_{+})\mid Z=z]\;=\;z\cdot(+1)+(1-z)\cdot(-1)\;=\;2z-1.

The expected reward of
a_{-}
is the negative of this. Hence, the (Bayes-)optimal policy chooses
a_{+}
iff
z>\tfrac{1}{2}
. Because
p\in(0,1)\setminus\{\tfrac{1}{2}\}
, one of the two code values
is below
1/2
and the other above, so the policy is well defined.

For either code value
z\in\{p,1-p\}
, the policy yields
the return
|2z-1|
. The expected return of the best
Z
-conditioned policy is therefore

J^{\star}_{Z}\;=\;|2p-1|\;<\;1\;=\;J^{\star},

where the inequality is because of
p\neq\tfrac{1}{2}
. Hence, no
Z
-conditioned policy can match the context-optimal return. Consequently, their point-wise optimal
Q
-values at
a_{+}
differ in sign, violating the control sufficiency. Therefore, observation sufficiency does not imply (strong) control sufficiency, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma
11
puts the two notions of sufficiency in a strict hierarchy: strong control sufficiency implies observation sufficiency, not the opposite. From a practitioner’s viewpoint, we typically begin at the
opposite
end of this chain. In most tasks, it is almost always
easier
to obtain one of the weaker properties:

1.

Observation sufficiency
: learn a code
Z
that
maximizes the mutual information
I(Z;C)
or, equivalently,
minimizes the Bayes error of predicting the context.

2.

Weak control sufficiency
: train an encoder–policy
pair
(q_{\phi},\pi_{\theta})
that achieves the highest
expected return (e.g. by the joint loss
(
4.5
) in Remark
8
).

The key question is therefore:

How can we upgrade either of these “easy-to-obtain” solutions to the preferred stronger notion of control sufficiency?

Achieving the strong property brings benefits that are highly desirable in practice, e.g., lower TD variance, robustness to
distributional shift, and modular plug-and-play training.

The next theorem shows that, provided the processing window is

lossless
, obtaining
both
observation sufficiency and weak control sufficiency is the solution to lift the pair to the stronger notion.

Theorem 12
.

Let the processing window
O
be lossless, i.e. Assumption
4.1
holds with
\delta=0
.
If an encoder–policy pair
(q_{\phi},\pi_{\theta})
satisfies both observation sufficiency and weak control sufficiency in the sense of Definition
2
and
6
:

I(C;O)=I(C;Z)\qquad\text{and}\qquad J\bigl(\pi_{\theta}\bigr)=J^{\star}

then the pair is
strongly
control-sufficient, i.e.
Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)=Q^{\star}(s,a,c)
whenever

q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0
(Definition
9
).

Proof.

Step 1: Deterministic context given
Z
.

Observation sufficiency gives

I(C;O)=I(C;Z)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;I(C;O\mid Z)=0.

Using
I(C;O)=H(C)-H(C\mid O)
and

I(C;Z)=H(C)-H(C\mid Z)
,

H(C\mid Z)=H(C\mid O).

The window is
lossless
by Assumption
4.1
with

\delta=0
(zero Bayes
error,
P_{e}(O)=0
). Fano’s inequality then forces

H(C\mid O)=0
(see Thm 2.10.1 in
Cover 1999
).
Substituting this into the above equality yields
H(C\mid Z)=0
. Zero conditional entropy means that
C
is a deterministic function of

Z
: there exists a measurable
f
such that

C=f(Z)\quad\text{a.s.}

In particular, for any code value
z
with
\Pr[Z=z]>0
, the conditional
distribution
C\mid Z=z
is the point mass at
c=f(z)
.

Step 2: Weak value equality.

Weak control sufficiency and Proposition
7
give,
for every
(s,a,z)
with
\Pr[Z=z]>0
,

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[Q^{\star}(s,a,C)\mid Z=z\bigr].

Since
C=f(Z)
a.s., the conditional expectation
simplifies to

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)\;=\;Q^{\star}\bigl(s,a,f(z)\bigr).

Step 3: Point-wise matching.

Fix
(z,c)
with
q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0
.
Because
\Pr[C=c]\,q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0
, the joint event

\{Z=z,\,C=c\}
has positive probability.
Conditioning on
Z=z
forces
C=f(z)
almost surely, so

c=f(z)
.
Hence, whenever
q_{\phi}(z\mid c)>0
,

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)=Q^{\star}(s,a,c),

which is exactly the point-wise criterion in
Definition
9
.
Therefore the encoder–policy pair
(q_{\phi},\pi_{\theta})
is strongly control sufficient, completing the proof.
∎

Theorem
12
makes the intuition formal: once the code
Z
is a perfect drop-in surrogate for the context
C
, solving the
Z
-conditioned RL problem on the
(s,z)
-augmented MDP is
equivalent
to solving the original contextual MDP. Put differently, observation sufficiency guarantees
that the agent “
knows the right question
” (which context it is
in), and weak control sufficiency guarantees that it “
has the
right answer on average
” (optimal expected return). Together, these
two properties collapse the averaging and promote the representation to the
strong
notion of control sufficiency.

This result provides the theoretical backbone for the optimization
objective we develop next. Our goal is to
simultaneously
(i)
drive the encoder toward observation sufficiency and (ii) maximize the
return under the current code.

4.4
ELBO and Information Residual

The results in Sections
4.1
–
4.3
tell us:

\text{Observation sufficiency}\;+\;\text{Weak control sufficiency}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{Strong control sufficiency}.

In the remainder of this section, we turn this pipeline into an objective that any standard RL algorithm can optimize. To that end, we
fix an encoder and ask the auxiliary question:

With a fixed encoder
q_{\phi}(z\mid o)
,
how close can a
Z
-conditioned policy

\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,z)
get to the context–aware optimum
J^{\star}
?

Let
p_{\mathcal{D}}(o)
be the empirical distribution of
observation windows. The fixed encoder then induces a prior

q_{\phi}(z)\;=\;\int q_{\phi}(z\mid o)\,p_{\mathcal{D}}(o)\,do,

which is the agent’s belief about
Z
at the start of an episode.

Given this belief, one might try to model trajectories under a
Z
-conditioned policy by

p_{\pi_{\theta}}(\tau\mid z)=p(s_{1})\,\prod_{t=1}^{T}p\bigl(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t},z\bigr)\,\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t},z),

letting the latent code directly influence future states.
This induces the “optimistic dynamics
p\bigl(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t},z\bigr)
” that breaks the true causal structure (see
(
Levine 2018
)
) because
Z
is determined
after
the trajectory is generated, not before.

The simple causal correction is to let
Z
affect only the actions,
keeping the environment dynamics fixed:

q_{\theta}(\tau\mid c,z):=p(s_{1})\,\prod_{t=1}^{T}p\bigl(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t},c\bigr)\,\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t},z).

(4.9)

Here, the hidden context
c
governs the dynamics, while the latent code
z
informs the agent’s actions. With this causally
correct model in place, we can work in the control-as-inference (CaI) framework.

Proposition 13

(Contextual ELBO)
.

Let
\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,z)
be a
Z
-conditioned policy and

\alpha>0
an entropy temperature.
With the trajectory model

q_{\theta}(\tau\mid c,z)
in (
4.9
),
define the joint variational distribution

q(c,z,\tau):=p(c)\,q_{\phi}(z\mid c)\,q_{\theta}(\tau\mid c,z).

Then

\underbrace{\log\int p(c)\,p(\tau\mid c)\,e^{R(\tau)}\,dc\,d\tau}_{\text{log evidence}}\;\geq\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\Bigl[R(\tau)+\alpha\!\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{H}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\,\cdot\!\mid s_{t},z)\bigr)\Bigr]}_{\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{Z}(\theta)}\;-\;\underbrace{\bigl[I(C;\tau)-I(C;Z)\bigr]}_{\displaystyle\Delta I},

(4.10)

where all expectations and mutual information terms are taken with respect to the joint distribution
q(c,z,\tau)
.

Proof.

Under the CaI framework, we take the unnormalized “true” posterior as

p^{\star}(\tau,c)\;\propto\;p(c)\,p(\tau\mid c)\,e^{R(\tau)}.

By Jensen’s inequality, we have

\log\!\!\int p^{\star}(\tau,c)\,d\tau\,dc\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\log p^{\star}(\tau,c)-\log q(c,z,\tau)\bigr].

Expand the RHS of the inequality and insert the definitions of
p^{\star}
and
q
:

RHS

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[R(\tau)]}_{(i)}\;-\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log q_{\phi}(z\mid c)]}_{(ii)}

\displaystyle\quad+\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\Bigl[\log p(\tau\mid c)-\log q_{\theta}(\tau\mid c,z)\Bigr]}_{(iii)}.

Using the identity

I(C;Z)=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log q_{\phi}(z\mid c)-\log q_{\phi}(z)]
, we have

-\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log q_{\phi}(z\mid c)]\;=\;I(C;Z)+H\!\bigl(q_{\phi}(Z)\bigr).

The marginal entropy
H(q_{\phi}(Z))
is a constant w.r.t.
\theta
, so we drop it. Write

\log\bigl[p(\tau\mid c)/q_{\theta}(\tau\mid c,z)\bigr]=\sum_{t}\log\!\bigl[\pi(a_{t}\mid s_{t},c)/\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t},z)\bigr].

Taking the expectation and recognizing

\mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t},z))=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[\log\pi_{\theta}]
, we have

(iii)\;=\;\alpha\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\mathcal{H}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t},z)\bigr)\bigr]-I(C;\tau).

Combining (i), (ii), (iii), we get:

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[R(\tau)+\alpha\sum_{t}\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\bigl[\mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t},z))\bigr]}_{\mathcal{J}_{Z}(\theta)}\;-\;\underbrace{\bigl[I(C;\tau)-I(C;Z)\bigr].}_{\Delta I}

∎

Proposition
13
splits the log evidence into two intuitive
terms:

•

Z
-surrogate control objective

\mathcal{J}_{Z}(\theta)
.

It is the usual entropy-regularized return, but computed under the

frozen
code
Z
. Maximizing

\mathcal{J}_{Z}(\theta)
with respect to the policy parameters
\theta

is the same as running a standard maximum-entropy RL
algorithm on the
(s,z)
-augmented MDP. This addresses the
weak
control
sufficiency part: “How good can a policy be if it treats
Z
as the context?”.

•

Information residual

\Delta I=I(C;\tau)-I(C;Z)
.

The first term,
I(C;\tau)
, is the total amount of context inevitably leaked by the environment through trajectories.
The second term,
I(C;Z)
, is the portion of that leakage the
encoder has already captured.
Their difference quantifies “the unknown” part that no

Z
-conditioned policy can ever exploit.
Once the
Z
surrogate control objective is maximized,
\Delta I
becomes an
upper bound
on any further return improvement: shrinking it effectively raises the performance ceiling imposed by the current representation.

Theorem 14

(Information Residual)
.

Fix an encoder
q_{\phi}
and the causal model
(
4.9
).
With
\alpha=0
in the ELBO,

\mathcal{J}_{Z}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{q}[R(\tau)],\qquad\Delta I:=I(C;\tau)-I(C;Z),

If the Markov chain
C\!\to\!\tau\!\to\!Z
holds, then

\Delta I=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\max_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{Z}(\theta)=J^{\star}.

In particular, if a policy
\pi_{\theta^{\star}}
maximises

\mathcal{J}_{Z}
and
\Delta I(\phi)=0
, then

J(\pi_{\theta^{\star}})=J^{\star}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
)

\Delta I=0
, i.e.
I(C;\tau)=I(C;Z)
.
Under the chain
C\!\rightarrow\!\tau\!\rightarrow\!Z
and zero residual
\Delta=0
give
I(C;\tau\mid Z)=0
, which implies
conditional independence:

q(c,\tau\mid z)=q(c\mid z)\,q(\tau\mid z).

(4.11)

Choose an optimal per-context policy

\pi_{c}^{\star}
with return
J_{c}^{\star}=J^{\star}
and let

\tilde{p}_{c}(\tau)
be its trajectory distribution.
Define the
Z
-mixture policy

\pi_{Z}^{\star}(a\mid s,z):=\sum_{c}q(c\mid z)\,\pi_{c}^{\star}(a\mid s).

Under (
4.11
), the induced trajectory distribution is also a mixture

q(\tau\mid z)=\sum_{c}q(c\mid z)\,\tilde{p}_{c}(\tau),

then

\mathbb{E}_{q(\tau\mid z)}[R(\tau)]=\sum_{c}q(c\mid z)\,J^{\star}=J^{\star}\quad\forall z.

Therefore,
J(\pi_{Z}^{\star})=J^{\star}
. Any maximizer

\theta^{\star}
of
\mathcal{J}_{Z}
achieves at least this value, but
by definition no policy can exceed
J^{\star}
, hence

J(\pi_{\theta^{\star}})=J^{\star}
.

(
\Leftarrow
)

Conversely, suppose that there exists
\theta
with
J(\pi_{\theta})=J^{\star}
.
By Proposition
7
, we have

Q^{\star}_{Z}(s,a,z)=\mathbb{E}[Q^{\star}(s,a,C)\mid Z=z].

If
\Delta I>0
, some
z
mixes two contexts whose

Q^{\star}
values differ, contradicting the equality above and the
optimal return. Therefore
\Delta I(\phi)=0
.
∎

Theorem
14
recasts the “
obs. + weak

\Rightarrow
strong
” argument in pure optimization form.
Once the surrogate objective
\mathcal{J}_{Z}(\theta)
has been pushed
to its maximum, the
entire
return gap is captured by the single
scalar

\Delta I.

Hence, information residual
\Delta I
acts as a
tight certificate
: driving the
residual small is
exactly
what closes the performance gap
between the
Z
-conditioned MDP and the true contextual optimum.

Let
\Delta I
be parametrized by the encoder parameters

\phi
, the objective splits cleanly into two
decoupled
blocks, suggesting the following alternating scheme:

\displaystyle\textbf{(A) Policy optimization: }

\displaystyle\theta\;\leftarrow\;\arg\max_{\theta}\;\mathcal{J}_{Z}(\theta)

\displaystyle\text{// standard MaxEnt RL on }(s,z),

\displaystyle\textbf{(B) Residual minimization: }

\displaystyle\phi\;\leftarrow\;\arg\min_{\phi}\;\Delta I(\phi)

\displaystyle\text{// shrink the information gap.}

Step (A)
improves control performance for the
current

representation;

Step (B)
tightens the representation itself,
either by increasing
I(C;Z)
(better inference) or by reducing

I(C;\tau)
(making behavior more context-agnostic).

While
\mathcal{J}_{Z}
can be maximized with any off-the-shelf RL algorithm (e.g., SAC), minimizing the information residual
\Delta I(\phi)
is more delicate. The next section makes explicit decomposition of
\Delta I
and introduces practical strategies for each component.

5
Minimizing Information Residual

Before developing concrete algorithms, it helps to decompose the information
residual

\Delta I=I(C;\tau)-I(C;Z)

into two conceptually different parts. Insert and subtract

I(C;O)
to obtain

\Delta I\;=\;\underbrace{I(C;\tau)-I(C;O)}_{\textsc{Processing\penalty\ Gap}}\;+\;\underbrace{I(C;O)-I(C;Z)}_{\textsc{Encoder\penalty\ Gap}}.

•

Processing Gap

captures the information lost when the full trajectory

\tau
is truncated to a length-
k
window
O
.
It depends on
how
the data are collected and can be
reduced empirically (Section
5.1
).

•

Encoder Gap

is the additional loss incurred when the window
O
is
compressed into the latent code
Z
via the learned encoder

q_{\phi}(z\mid o)
.
This is a pure representation learning problem (Section
5.2
).

5.1
Processing Gap

Truncating the full trajectory
\tau=(S_{1:T},A_{1:T})
to a length-
k

prefix
O=g_{k}(\tau)=(S_{1:k},A_{1:k})
inevitably discards context
information carried by the
tail
indices

\mathcal{T}_{\!{\setminus k}}\!:=\{k+1,\dots,T\}
.
By the chain rule of mutual information
Cover 1999
and the Markov property of the
MDP, one can show that this gap is the information “tail”:

\textsc{processing gap}\;:=\;I(C;\tau)-I(C;O)\;=\;\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{{\setminus k}}}I\bigl(C;\,(S_{t},A_{t})\,\big|\,S_{1:t-1},A_{1:t-1}\bigr).

(5.1)

Every omitted index contributes the conditional term

I\bigl(C;(S_{t},A_{t})\mid S_{1:t-1},A_{1:t-1}\bigr)

to the processing gap.
Whether the tail in (
5.1
) dies out or not is an intrinsic property of the controlled process:

•

Long-memory (slow causal propagation):

If the context must travel a long causal chain
before showing up in
(S_{t},A_{t})
, every extra step still
reveals new information. The gap shrinks only when the
entire
trajectory is kept in the window.

•

Fast-mixing:

In many controlled systems, the conditional term in
(
5.1
) decays exponentially after a mixing time

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}
; then a modest window

k\!\approx\!T-\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}
already makes the tail negligible
(
Bradley 2005
)
.

•

Stationary & ergodic:

When the triple
(C,S_{t},A_{t})
is jointly stationary and ergodic, the conditional MI settles to a
constant rate

I_{\infty}(C;S,A)
. Every step beyond the chosen window leaks the same small amount of context information, so the entire tail contributes a flat penalty
(T-k)I_{\infty}(C;S,A)
(by Shannon’s asymptotic equipartition property (AEP)
(
Cover 1999
)
).

Bounding the tail term in (
5.1
) is usually infeasible: the leakage rate depends on mixing or ergodic
constants of the
controlled
stochastic processes, a property that is both
task-specific and rarely available in closed form. Rather than pursue such model-centric analysis, we re-invoke our operational Assumption
4.1
. That single data–driven condition turns into an immediate information bound.

Corollary 15
.

If Assumption
4.1
holds and

|\mathcal{C}|=N
, then

\textsc{Processing Gap}\;\leq\;h_{2}(\delta)\;+\;\delta\log(N-1),

where
h_{2}(\delta)=-\delta\log\delta-(1-\delta)\log(1-\delta)
is the binary entropy.

Proof.

For a finite context alphabet, Fano’s inequality gives

H(C\!\mid O)\leq h_{2}(\delta)+\delta\log(N-1).

Given the definition
I(C;O)=H(C)-H(C|O)
, we have

I(C;O)\geq H(C)-h_{2}(\delta)-\delta\log(N-1).

Combining it with the fact
I(C;\tau)\leq H(C)
yields the stated bound.
∎

A small Bayes error on the window translates directly
into a small processing gap: when
P_{e}(O)=\delta
, at most

\mathcal{O}(\delta)
bits of context remain hidden in the tail. Thus, by task-specific window-engineering, one obtains an information-theoretic guarantee.

5.2
Encoder Gap

Once the
Processing Gap
has been engineered away, the remaining term in
\Delta I
is the

\textsc{Encoder Gap}\;:=\;I(C;O)-I(C;Z),

i.e.
, the context information present in the window
O
but
discarded by the encoder
q_{\phi}(z\mid o)
.
Because
C\!\rightarrow\!O\!\rightarrow\!Z
is a Markov chain, the
gap is
exactly
the violation of
observation sufficiency

(Definition
2
).

Information Bottleneck View.

The term
I(C;O)
is fixed once the window is chosen, so shrinking the
encoder gap means increasing
I(C;Z)
while paying a rate
penalty
I(Z;O)
(see Remark
5
). This penalty view leads to the IB objective
3
3

3

We use the
compression–weighted convention common in modern IB literature.

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}(\phi):=\beta\,I(Z;O)\;-\;I(C;Z),\qquad 0<\beta<1.

(5.2)

Here,
\beta
controls how costly it is to keep extra bits from the window once sufficiency is reached.

Proposition 16

(Observation sufficiency and the IB loss)
.

Let
C\!\to\!O\!\to\!Z
and define

\varepsilon(\phi):=I(Z;O)-I(C;Z)\geq 0.

Then for any encoder
q_{\phi}
and
0<\beta<1
, we have

0\;\leq\;\varepsilon(\phi)\;\leq\;\beta^{-1}\!\bigl[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}(\phi)+(\beta-1)\,I(C;O)\bigr].

(5.3)

Moreover,

\varepsilon(\phi)=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}(\phi)=-(1-\beta)\,I(C;O),

so the encoder is observation-sufficient
iff it attains the global minimum of the IB loss.

Proof.

Write
I(Z;O)=I(C;Z)+\varepsilon
. Then

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}=\beta\bigl[I(C;Z)+\varepsilon\bigr]-I(C;Z)=(\beta-1)I(C;Z)+\beta\varepsilon.

Rearranging

\varepsilon=\beta^{-1}\bigl[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}-(\beta-1)I(C;Z)\bigr].

Since
I(C;Z)\leq I(C;O)
(by DPI) and
\beta-1<0
, replacing
I(C;Z)
by

I(C;O)
yields the upper bound in (
5.3
).
If
\varepsilon=0
, then
I(C;Z)=I(C;O)
, so

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}=-(1-\beta)I(C;O)
.
Conversely, the IB loss achieves this value
only
when

\varepsilon=0
, completing the equivalence.
∎

Proposition
16
rewrites the encoder gap

I(C;O)-I(C;Z)
as a single IB loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}(\phi)
. Its global minimum is
equivalent
to observation sufficiency, and
the bound (

) tells us exactly how far any iterate remains from that target. Because
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}
depends only on the encoder and the

stable, low-variance

that keeps
Z
from collapsing (even while policy gradients or critic estimates fluctuate during training.)

Putting the pieces together.

With the processing gap bounded and the information bottleneck view on the encoder gap, the ELBO admits an envelope form

renders the contextual RL problem

separable
. Once the IB loss hits its minimum

Preview: replay gap.

The
Processing
and
Encoder
gaps are
intrinsic
components of the information residual: they would exist even if the policy and encoder were updated on fresh data from the current policy distribution. In practice, however, both RL and representation learning implementations rely on a replay buffer that stores trajectories generated by
past
policies.
The resulting mismatch introduces an additional
extrinsic
error that we term as the Replay Gap.

5.3
Replay Gap: information leakage due to experience replay

Experience replay is the “working heart” of large-scale RL, but its statistical consequences differ sharply for
value
estimates and
information-theoretic
objectives.

Value estimate.

The reuse is safe for the
value
terms because the
returns
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\theta}}[R_{c}]
, state values
V_{\theta}(s,c)
and
advantages
A_{\theta}(s,a,c)
are
linear
in the trajectory law
q_{\theta}(c,\tau)
. Re-using off-policy data

(c,\tau)\sim q_{\mathcal{B}}
is therefore unbiased under standard importance weights
w=q_{\theta}/q_{\mathcal{B}}
. Techniques such as clipping or V-trace style corrections keep the variance finite
(
Precup et al. 2000
;
Munos et al. 2016
;
Espeholt et al. 2018
)
.

MI estimate.

The mutual information is a

non–linear
functional of the underlying trajectory law

q
. Evaluating it with stale data incurs the
replay gap
:

\Gamma_{\textsc{replay}}\;:=\;\bigl|\,I_{q_{\theta}}(C;X)-I_{q_{\mathcal{B}}}(C;X)\bigr|,

(5.5)

where
X
can be the window
O
, the code
Z
, or any further feature derived from the trajectory. This implies that even a small drift in likelihood ratios (i.e.,
\log w
) perturbs every log term in a multiplicative, non-canceling way, so
stale
context clues can “leak” into the estimator and be spuriously exploited by
the policy.

A cheerful fact about the replay gap is that when the behavior policy
drifts only slightly from one update to the next,

q_{\!\mathcal{B}}\!\approx\!q_{\theta}
and, under
any
sensible
statistical metric, the bias

\Gamma_{\textsc{replay}}
must be small. We now make this intuition concrete:

Proposition 17

(Replay gap bound under importance weights)
.

Let
q_{\theta}(c,\tau)
be the current on-policy distribution
over contexts
c\in\mathcal{C}
(
|\mathcal{C}|=N>2
) and trajectories

\tau
, and let
q_{\mathcal{B}}(c,\tau)
denote the replay-buffer
distribution.
Assume the trajectory–level importance weights are clipped

w(c,\tau):=\frac{q_{\theta}(c,\tau)}{q_{\!\mathcal{B}}(c,\tau)}\;\in\;[1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon],\qquad 0<\epsilon<1.

An auxiliary (processed) variable
X
is generated from
\tau
by a Markov kernel. Then the replay gap satisfies

\Gamma_{\textsc{replay}}=|I_{q_{\theta}}(C;X)-I_{q_{\!\mathcal{B}}}(C;X)|\;\leq\;\frac{\epsilon}{2}\log(N-1)+h_{2}(\frac{\epsilon}{2}),

where
h_{2}
is the binary entropy.

Proof.

Step 1. From importance weights to a TV bound.

Recall the total variation (TV) distance,

\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c,\tau}|P(c,\tau)-Q(c,\tau)|.

With the clipped likelihood ratio

w(c,\tau)=q_{\theta}(c,\tau)/q_{\mathcal{B}}(c,\tau)\in[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon],

we have

|q_{\theta}(c,\tau)-q_{\!\mathcal{B}}(c,\tau)|=|w(c,\tau)-1|\,q_{\mathcal{B}}(c,\tau)\;\leq\;\epsilon\,q_{\mathcal{B}}(c,\tau).

Summing over
(c,\tau)
gives a TV bound:

\|q_{\theta}-q_{\!\mathcal{B}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c,\tau}\epsilon\,q_{\mathcal{B}}(c,\tau)=\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Step 2. Propagate the TV bound via data processing.

Introduce the
processing channel

K:\;(c,\tau)\;\longmapsto\;(c,x),\qquad K(c,x\mid c,\tau)=\kappa(x\mid\tau)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{c\}},

where the context label is copied, while
x
is obtained through
the Markov kernel
\kappa(\cdot\mid\tau)
. Because the
same
channel is applied to both measures, the strong
data–processing inequality for TV

(
Polyanskiy and Wu 2017
)
gives

\|q^{CX}_{\theta}-q^{CX}_{\mathcal{B}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\eta_{K}\|q_{\theta}-q_{\mathcal{B}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|q_{\theta}-q_{\mathcal{B}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}},

where the
\eta_{K}\leq 1
is the Dobrushin coefficient.

Step 3. Continuity of entropy.

Write

I(C;X)=H(C)-H(C\mid X)
. The marginal entropy
H(C)
is fixed by definition, so only the conditional term varies.
For two joint laws
P,Q
on a product alphabet

\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{X}
with

\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)\leq\Delta\leq 1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}
, the continuity of (conditional) entropy gives
Cover 1999
;
Berta et al. 2025

|H_{P}(C\mid X)-H_{Q}(C\mid X)|\;\leq\;\Delta\,\log(|\mathcal{C}|-1)+h_{2}(\Delta).

Taking
\Delta=\epsilon/2
yields the inequality:

\Gamma_{\textsc{replay}}=|I_{q_{\theta}}(C;X)-I_{q_{\!\mathcal{B}}}(C;X)|\;\leq\;\frac{\epsilon}{2}\log(N-1)+h_{2}(\frac{\epsilon}{2}).

∎

Proposition
17
says that the replay bias

\Gamma_{\textsc{replay}}
grows
super-linearly
with the policy
drift
\epsilon
and is amplified by a

\log\bigl(|\mathcal{C}|\bigr)
factor. When the context alphabet is
large (or effectively continuous), this amplification is not negligible. Two practical rules follow immediately:

1.

Keep the observation module
“on-policy”
:

Train the encoder
q_{\phi}(z|o)
and sample the latent code
Z
from the induced prior only on freshly collected trajectories (i.e., small distributional mismatch). This ensures the optimizable encoder gap, not replay bias, dominates the information residual.

2.

Control the
effective
context alphabet:

Limit
N
during optimization, which can be done, e.g., via coarse quantization, curriculum strategy that progressively refines context resolution, or task-specific grouping of similar contexts. A smaller effective alphabet provably reduces the
\log N
amplification in the replay bound.

Combined with the processing and encoder bounds, the replay result completes the error decomposition for the information residual
\Delta I
: every term now is either

intrinsically limited
(pre-processing),

directly optimized
(IB loss),
or
provably bounded
(controlled replay bias).

7.3
Discussion

Failure modes analysis.

Our error decomposition of information residual pinpoints why some explicit-context baselines succeed in certain regimes yet stall in others.

MSE
treats context inference as a
point–estimate

problem: given a history window
O
it emits a single, fully

deterministic
guess
\hat{c}
and feeds
(s,\hat{c})
to the
policy. This is equivalent to assuming the posterior

q(c|O)=\delta(c-\hat{c})
, i.e. the context is noise-free once

O
is observed. Under our information residual decomposition, such an
assumption declares the
Encoder Gap
closed as soon as the

training
MSE is small. At test time, however, the same predictor is systematically biased; the policy, co-adapted to
\hat{c}
,
receives a mismatched pair
(s,\hat{c})
. State trajectories drift
outside the predictors’ training support, errors compound, and
performance collapses. This reveals that the zero gap is a by-product of the misspecified point-estimate assumption rather than the observation sufficiency.

ObsAug
can be read as a “
BCPO
without the bottleneck”: it simply feeds the entire window
O
to the policy.
When the state is low-dimensional (e.g.,
CartPole/Hopper
), this shortcut already makes the
Processing Gap
negligible and nothing
catastrophic happens. But as the task DoF rises, the augmented state becomes high-dimensional: the critic must now use a high-variance value function from a signal filled with redundant context bits. In our decomposition, this redundancy
is
the
Encoder Gap
: because
ObsAug
never shrinks it, every replayed batch re-injects noise into the update and magnifies the
Replay Gap
. Consequently, both sample efficiency and asymptotic return fall behind

BCPO
precisely on the high-DoF tasks where explicit information–bottleneck compression matters most.

PEARL
appears consistently mid-pack because two opposing
errors nearly cancel. First,
Rakelly et al. 2019
mitigates replay bias with a “recent buffer” heuristic, so the
Replay Gap
term is small, a fact predicted by Lem. 3.3. Second,
PEARL
makes a strong
factorization
assumption inside its
data window:

q_{\phi}\!\bigl(z\mid o_{1:k}\bigr)\;\propto\;\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{\phi}\!\bigl(z\mid o_{i}\bigr),

where
\Phi
is a Gaussian factor that results in a Gaussian posterior. This collapses temporal correlations and leaves a residual

Processing Gap
that
BCPO
avoids.
Moreover, the meta-RL objective maximizes
average
return over all
contexts, whereas
BCPO
learns distinct latent-conditioned behaviors. Thus,
PEARL
rarely tops the chart but seldom crashes either.

In short, the information residual perspective tells that an explicit context robust policy must keep the
Processing
,
Encoder
,
Replay
gaps simultaneously small. Under point-estimate assumption, MSE reopens the
Encoder Gap
during testing;
ObsAug
ignores
Encoder Gap
and amplifies the
Replay Gap
;
PEARL
leaves a residual in the
Processing Gap
through its factorization assumption.
BCPO
is the only method that simultaneously and explicitly tackles all, which is precisely why it is fast, stable, and robust across the full spectrum of tasks.

Implicit vs. explicit context policy.

From an algorithm design perspective, our study reaffirms a “no–free–lunch” principle for contextual RL.
Implicit
approaches (
DR, RR, self-paced curriculum
) pay
zero
on representation/observation and work well when the context manifold is simple, but they hit a performance ceiling once task DoF or context entropy increases: the single policy must
simultaneously
infer
and
control
, which wastes its function approximation budget (e.g., neural networks).

Explicit
methods introduce a dedicated context channel, but that channel comes with extra parameters, inference overhead, and the risk of new failure modes.
BCPO
shows that this complexity pays off
only
when the channel is carefully designed: the data-processing window is informative yet compact, posterior uncertainty is explicitly modeled, and replay drift is controlled. Remove any one of these safeguards, and the added machinery yields diminishing returns, or in the worst case, increases computation without narrowing the performance gap.

8
Related works

Contextual & meta RL.

Contextual RL
learns a single policy that generalizes across tasks with contextual inputs
(
Hallak et al. 2015
;
Benjamins et al.
;
Kupcsik et al. 2013
;
Oord et al. 2018
;
Lee et al. 2020b
)
. Context-based meta RL methods (e.g.,
Gao et al. 2024
;
Pong et al. 2022
) and PEARL in particular
(
Rakelly et al. 2019
)
infer a latent task embedding from recent/behavioral experience and condition the
policy on that embedding. Pearl explicitly uses replay heuristics to
train off-policy critics. Conservative Q-learning pushes this replay idea further by penalizing actions outside the behavior support
(
Kumar et al. 2020
)
.

Randomization & curriculum.

Domain randomization broadens the training context exposure by sampling
diverse environment parameters
(
Andrychowicz et al. 2020
;
Margolis et al. 2022
)
, and curriculum schedulers progressively expand the task set to stabilize
learning
(
Mehta et al. 2020
;
Eimer et al. 2021
;
Klink et al. 2020
)
.

Representation learning, IB, & information structure.

A growing body of work studies
representation–oriented
RL through the lens of predictive or world models. Many employ
information bottleneck regularization to encourage latent codes that are compact yet decision–relevant, e.g. DeepMDP
(
Gelada et al. 2019
)
, Dreamer
(
Hafner et al. 2023
)
, stochastic latent actor-critic
(
Lee et al. 2020a
)
, and many more. Closer to our
control–sufficiency
viewpoint,
Huang et al. 2022
explicitly optimizes latent variables that preserve the action–value ordering.

Historical remark.

For completeness, we relate our framework and algorithm to classical results in stochastic control. The modular structure exploited by
BCPO
—and, more broadly, by many context–aware RL methods—echoes the classical separation principle: under suitable structural assumptions, the estimation and control sub-problems can be solved independently and then combined without loss of optimality
(
Wonham 1968
;
Georgiou and Lindquist 2013
;
Åström 2012
;
Fleming and Rishel 2012
)
.
The canonical illustration is the pairing of the Kalman filter and the LQR controller
(
Kalman 1960
)
.
Inspired by the same idea,
BCPO
explicitly optimizes for a
separable
representation–policy learning problem to promote algorithmic simplicity, sample efficiency, and stronger generalization in data-driven RL settings.

9
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presents a unified inference–control perspective on context-based RL with latent, episode-level variations. We formalized the gap between
observation sufficiency
(knowing the latent context) and
control sufficiency
(acting optimally) through an
information residual
, derived a tractable ELBO-style objective whose two terms cleanly separate representation learning (inference) from policy improvement (control), and instantiated the theory in
BCPO
.

The results presented here aim to serve both theory and practice. For theorists, the framework offers a unified information-theoretic lens that subsumes earlier, disparate notions—such as representation quality and value-function mismatch—to study the inference–control structure in RL. For practitioners, the residual’s explicit decomposition turns abstract information-theoretical terms into concrete diagnostics. Thus, the framework not only clarifies
what
matters in context-based RL, but also
how
to improve existing algorithms through the
BCPO
procedure.

The present study assumes fixed, episode-level contexts and relies on variational MI estimators. This leaves several natural extensions. Extending the sufficiency theory to non-stationary and within-episode context drift is non-trivial and may require new definitions. Combining
BCPO
with latent-variable dynamics/world models could yield planning-aware bottlenecks and a sharper inference–control separation. Additionally, embedding explicit risk or information-budget constraints into
BCPO
is a promising route toward safe, real-world deployment.

Appendix A
Variational Information Bottleneck

In this section, we provide a detailed exposition of the
Variational Information Bottleneck
(VIB) framework. Recall the information bottleneck objective (
5.2
) in its parametric form:

\min_{\phi}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}}(\phi)\;=\;-\mathrm{I}_{\phi}(Z;C)\;+\;\beta\ \mathrm{I}_{\phi}(Z;O),

(A.1)

where
\beta>0
regulates the trade-off between
relevance
and
compression
. Because direct computation of these mutual information (MI) terms is generally intractable, we adopt variational bounds to derive a practical training objective.

Upper-bounding
\mathrm{I}_{\phi}(Z;O)
.

When the conditional distribution
p(z|o)
is given (i.e., an encoder
p_{\phi}(z|o)
parametrized by
\phi
), a tractable variational upper bound can be built by introducing variational approximation
r(z)
to the unknown
p(z)

(
Barber and Agakov 2004
;
Poole et al. 2019
)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{\phi}(Z;O)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{p(z,o)}\Bigl[\log\frac{p_{\phi}(z|o)}{p(z)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{p(z,o)}\Bigl[\log\frac{p_{\phi}(z|o)r(z)}{p(z)r(z)}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{p(z,o)}\Bigl[\log\frac{p_{\phi}(z|o)}{r(z)}\Bigr]-\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p(z)\|r(z)\bigr)}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{p(o)}\Bigl[\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_{\phi}(z|o)\|r(z)\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\;\triangleq\;\mathrm{I}_{\text{MIN}}(\phi).

(A.2)

Hence, minimizing
\mathrm{I}_{\text{MIN}}(\phi)

upper bounds
the compression term
I_{\phi}(Z;O)
.

In practice, the conditional (encoding) distribution
p_{\phi}(z|o)
is often chosen to be a Gaussian
\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(z|\mu_{\phi}(o),\Sigma_{\phi}(o)\bigr)
, with neural network parametrization
\mu_{\phi}
and
\Sigma_{\phi}
. In this case, the KL term admits a closed-form expression and can be optimized via the standard re-parameterization trick
(
Kingma and Welling 2013
)
.

Lower-bounding
\mathrm{I}_{\phi}(Z;C)
.

A variety of variational lower bounds
(
Barber and Agakov 2004
;
Oord et al. 2018
;
Poole et al. 2019
)
and mutual-information estimators
(
Belghazi et al. 2018
)
can be categorized according to whether they explicitly model the conditional distributions
p(c|z)
(or
p(z|c)
). We broadly group these approaches into
explicit
(supervised) and
implicit
(unsupervised or semi-supervised) settings:

•

Explicit (Supervised).

If the context
C
is observed as labels (e.g., one-hot label, or trajectory index), one can introduce a variational
decoder

q_{\phi}(c|z)
to recover the labels from
Z
. In this scenario, we can invoke the classical
Barber–Agakov
bound
(
Barber and Agakov 2004
)
on mutual information, which follows from the non-negativity of the KL divergence:

I_{\phi}(Z;C)\;\geq\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(z,c)}\bigl[\log q_{\phi}(c|z)\bigr]}_{\displaystyle\text{label reconstruction}}\;+\;\underbrace{H(C)}_{\displaystyle\text{label entropy}}\;\triangleq\;\mathrm{I}^{e}_{\text{MAX}}(\phi)

(A.3)

The term
H(C)
is independent of the decoder and thus cannot be optimized with respect to

q_{\phi}
. Consequently, the optimization boils down to maximizing

\mathbb{E}_{p(z,c)}\bigl[\log q_{\phi}(c|z)\bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{p(z)}\Bigl[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(c|z)}\bigl[\log q_{\phi}(c|z)\bigr]}_{\displaystyle-H(p,q_{\phi})}\Bigr],

which corresponds to a standard cross-entropy (label reconstruction) objective. Combining this
variational bound
\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{e}(\phi)
with
\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{MIN}}(\phi)
yields a
tractable information bottleneck objective, essentially the VIB formulation from

(
Alemi et al. 2016
)
.

•

Implicit (Semi-supervised).
In many practical scenarios,
C
may be partially labeled or unobservable. Nonetheless, observations
\boldsymbol{o}_{1:K}\sim p(o_{1:K}|\boldsymbol{c}_{i})
from an underlying
c_{i}
share similar structures, and thus the corresponding representations
\boldsymbol{z}_{1:K}\sim p(z_{1:K}|o_{1:K},\boldsymbol{c}_{i})
reflect this shared structure. By grouping similar representations while separating dissimilar ones, we effectively maximize the mutual information between
Z
and
C
by clustering. This can be achieved by optimizing InfoNCE, a contrastive loss that encourages the model to distinguish between positive (similar) and negative (dissimilar) pairs
Oord et al. 2018
. Here,
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
can be used as
pseudo-labels
to facilitate contrastive sampling.

Concretely, suppose we draw a set of
Z^{K}=\{\mathbf{z}^{\text{pos}},\mathbf{z}^{\text{neg}}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}^{\text{neg}}_{K-1}\}
, where
\mathbf{z}^{\text{pos}}
is an embedding sample from
p(z|o,\boldsymbol{c}_{i})
(the positive), and
\{\mathbf{z}^{\text{neg}}_{j}\}
are embedding samples from
p(z|o,\boldsymbol{c}\neq\boldsymbol{c}_{i})
. The InfoNCE objective is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}}(\psi)=-\mathbb{E}_{Z^{K}}\Biggl[\log\frac{f_{\psi}\bigl(\mathbf{z}^{\text{pos}},\mathbf{z}_{i}\bigr)}{\sum_{z_{j}\in Z^{K}}f_{\psi}\bigl(\mathbf{z}^{\text{pos}},\mathbf{z}_{j}\bigr)}\Biggr],

where
f_{\psi}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a learnable critic function (e.g., a bilinear function

f_{\psi}(z,z^{\prime})=z^{T}W_{\psi}z^{\prime}
) that scores the similarity between samples.
As a categorical cross-entropy loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}}
effectively classifies
the positive sample
\mathbf{z}^{\text{pos}}
against
K-1
negatives. Combining the InfoNCE loss with the variational bound
\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{MAX}}^{e}(\phi)

results in a tractable
implicit
VIB objective.

Note that while the explicit VIB objective is strictly a variational lower bound of the original IB
objective
Alemi et al. 2016
, this implicit IB objective is rather an approximation. Although
the InfoNCE loss is known to be a variational lower bound on mutual information
Poole et al. 2019
,
we use it here primarily as a contrastive loss to softly partition the latent embeddings
Z

into a mixture of distributions (i.e., cluster embeddings from the same context). Once
p(z)

evolves into a mixture distribution, it aligns with the solution structure of IB (see the self-consistent equations of IB solution in
Tishby et al. 2000
;
Slonim and Tishby 1999
).

By combining these bounds, we obtain a tractable
Variational IB Loss
. While the supervised approach often offers computational efficiency, the implicit one yields more robust and generalizable representations, particularly in settings where labeled data is limited but there is a large pool of observations (as is often the case in RL).
