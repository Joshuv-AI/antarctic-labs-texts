Equilibrium Strategies for the N-agent Mean-Variance Investment Problem over a Random Horizon
Xiaoqing Liang, Jie Xiong, Ying Yang

Abstract
We study equilibrium feedback strategies for a family of dynamic mean-variance problems with competition among a large group of agents. We assume that the time horizon is random and each agent’s risk aversion depends dynamically on the current wealth. We consider both the finite population game and the corresponding mean-field one. Each agent can invest in a risk-free asset and a specific individual stock, which is correlated with other stocks by a common noise. By applying stochastic control theory, we derive the extended Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) system of equations for both n n -agent and mean-field games. Under an exponentially distributed random horizon, we explicitly obtain the equilibrium feedback strategies and the value functions in both cases. Our results show that the agent’s equilibrium feedback strategy depends not only on his/her current wealth but also on the wealth of other competitors. Moreover, when the risk aversion is state-independent and the risk-free interest rate is set to zero, the equilibrium strategies degenerate to constants, which is identical to the unique equilibrium obtained in Lacker and Zariphopoulou, 2019 with exponential risk preferences; when the competition parameter goes to zero and the risk aversion equals some specific value, the equilibrium strategies coincide with the ones derived in Landriault et al., 2018 . MSC2020 Codes : Primary 93E20, 91G10 Secondary 91G80, 60H30. Keywords : mean-variance criterion; equilibrium feedback strategy; random horizon; state-dependent risk aversion; investment



1
Introduction

Stochastic optimal control problems aim to analyze a set of control variables that enable the controller to achieve a desired target for a controlled state process. In traditional stochastic control problems, Pontryagin’s maximum principle and Bellman’s dynamic programming are two commonly used methods for finding optimal solutions (e.g., see
Yong and Zhou, 2012
). A crucial requirement for applying Bellman’s dynamic programming is that the problem must be time-consistent, meaning that an optimal strategy chosen at the current time will remain optimal in the future. However, this property is violated in time-inconsistent control problems, particularly in the mean-variance (MV) problems, where the presence of the variance term leads to the failure of the iterated expectation property.

In the existing literature, two main approaches are typically used to address the issue of time inconsistency. The first is the pre-commitment approach, which optimizes the objective function at the initial time and requires the controller to commit to this strategy throughout the control period. In MV criterion, the pre-commitment strategy involves setting the expectation of terminal wealth as a static constant, allowing the dynamic programming principle to be applied. A great deal of research focus on solving MV optimization problems from the pre-commitment perspective. See, for instance,
Li and Ng, 2000
and
Zhou and Li, 2000
embedded the original MV problem into a stochastic linear-quadratic control problem and derived the optimal investment strategies from tractable auxiliary problems in both discrete-time and continuous-time frameworks.
Bielecki et al., 2005
studied a continuous-time MV portfolio selection problem with random coefficients and bankruptcy constraints.
Xiong and Zhou, 2007
studied a continuous-time portfolio selection problem under the MV framework in an incomplete information market, where only the past prices of the stocks and the bond are available to the investors.

However, pre-commitment strategies are not optimal at future time points, making them time-inconsistent. An alternative approach to addressing the MV criterion is to frame the problem within a game-theoretic context, aiming to determine the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium. In this framework, the problem is modeled as a non-cooperative game, where each time point is treated as a separate player.

Strotz, 1973
was the first to formally apply a game-theoretic approach to deal with a time-inconsistent deterministic Ramsay problem.
Björk and Murgoci, 2010
considered a general class of time-inconsistent objective functions in a Markovian setting, deriving an extended Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation and providing a verification theorem.
Björk et al., 2014
developed a time-consistent strategy for a continuous-time MV portfolio problem with state-dependent risk aversion.
Landriault et al., 2018
applied stochastic control theory to derive the extended HJB system in both discrete and continuous-time frameworks, investigating equilibrium feedback strategies with a state-dependent risk aversion and random time horizon. More recently, this methodology has been extended to many different frameworks, for example,
Pun, 2018
and
Yan et al., 2020
on portfolio selection,
Zeng et al., 2013
and
Chen and Yang, 2020
on reinsurance and investment,
Liang et al., 2014
and
Zhang et al., 2025
on pension fund optimization.

Most of the research mentioned above has focused on a single-agent or two-agent scenarios. However, in practice, agents often compete with many others in large-scale markets, where relative performance becomes a crucial factor in their decision-making. In this context,
Lacker and Zariphopoulou, 2019
studied optimal portfolio management with relative concerns in both finite and infinite populations, deriving time-consistent equilibrium strategies for constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) and constant relative risk aversion (CRRA).
Lacker and Soret, 2020
further examined portfolio optimization for competitive agents with CRRA utilities over a finite time horizon, where each agent’s utility depends on both their absolute wealth and consumption and their relative wealth and consumption compared to the average among other agents.
Guan and Hu, 2022
derived time-consistent investment and reinsurance strategies for MV problems in multi-agent settings and mean-field games.
Bo et al., 2024
studied multiple agents in mean-field interactions, analyzing their terminal wealth under exponential utility with relative performance. They established both finite
n
-agent game equilibria and mean-field game equilibria, exploring their convergence.

In this paper, we explore a class of dynamic mean-variance problems involving competition among both finite and infinite numbers of agents. In order to have a more realistic model, we assume a random time horizon and each agent’s risk aversion dynamically depends on their current wealth. Each agent is allowed to invest in a risk-free asset and a specific individual stock, which is correlated with other stocks through a common noise factor. Using stochastic control theory, we prove a general verification theorem for this multi-agent, state-dependent optimization problem, which includes an extended HJB system of equations for both
n
-agent and mean-field games. When the time horizon follows an exponential distribution, the problem becomes time-independent. Thus, solving the extended HJB equation reduces to solving a series of time-independent ordinary differential equations (ODEs). In both
n
-agent and mean-field games, we explicitly derive the equilibrium feedback strategies and value functions by solving these ODEs. Our results show that the agent’s equilibrium feedback strategy depends on both the agent’s current wealth and the wealth of other competitors.

Our study makes the following contributions. First, we extend the work of
Lacker and Zariphopoulou, 2019
to a multi-agent MV frameworks. In
Lacker and Zariphopoulou, 2019
, the equilibrium strategy consists of two parts: one is the traditional Merton portfolio, which does not consider relative performance concerns, and the other depends on competition parameters. However, the equilibrium strategy in that model is independent of the agent’s wealth. In contrast, our model incorporates state-dependent risk aversion and a random horizon, resulting in a more realistic equilibrium strategy that depends on the agent’s current wealth, the competitors’ current wealth, and both competition-dependent and independent terms. Furthermore, we find that when risk aversion is state-independent and the risk-free interest rate is zero, our equilibrium strategies reduce to constant values, which match the unique equilibria derived in
Lacker and Zariphopoulou, 2019
with exponential risk preferences.

Second,
Guan and Hu, 2022
also studied a time-consistent MV optimization problem in multi-agent and mean-field game frameworks. However, they assumed state-independent risk aversion, meaning the equilibrium strategies in their model are independent of agents’ wealth. By incorporating state-dependent risk aversion, our model presents a significantly more complex problem. However, using the time-consistent theory developed in
Björk and Murgoci, 2010
, we derive an explicit form of the equilibrium strategy for the case where the random time horizon is exponentially distributed.

Finally, we extend the work of
Landriault et al., 2018
to multi-agent game and the mean-field game frameworks.
We show that when the competition parameter approaches zero and risk aversion reaches specific values, the equilibrium strategies coincide with those in
Landriault et al., 2018
. Additionally, we examine the limiting behavior of the equilibrium strategies as the hazard rate of the random horizon approaches infinity, and show that the time-consistent equilibrium of the
n
-agent game converges to the equilibrium of the mean-field game (MFG) as
n\to\infty
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we study an
n
-agent mean-variance investment problem with a random horizon and state-dependent risk aversion. Subsection
2.1
formulates the
n
-agent game. In Subsection
2.2
, we prove a verification theorem, which includes an extended HJB system of equations. Then, we consider the case where the random horizon follows an exponential distribution. Under this assumption, the extended HJB equation simplifies to a series of time-independent ordinary differential equations, and we derive the equilibrium feedback strategies by solving these ODEs. In Section
3
, we study the limit as
n\to\infty
and analyze the equilibrium strategies in the mean-field game. The problem is formulated in Subsection
3.1
, and, similar to Subsection
2.2
, we prove a verification theorem and derive the equilibrium strategy by solving the corresponding extended HJB system for this case in Subsection
3.2
. Subsection
3.3
establishes the connection between the
n
-agent game and the mean-field game. Finally, in Section
4
, we provide several numerical examples to further explore the effects and sensitivity of the parameters on the equilibrium strategies.

2
The
n
-agent game

In this section, we formulate the continuous-time
n
-agent investment problem using a mean-variance criterion over a random horizon. In Subsection
2.1
, we introduce the financial market, the objective, the definition of the equilibrium feedback strategy, and the corresponding equilibrium value function. In Subsection
2.2
, we first prove a general verification theorem, and then we use it to find the solutions of the
n
-agent game when the random horizon is exponentially distributed, that is, the hazard rate of the random horizon is a positive constant.

2.1
Model formulation

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered, complete probability space satisfying the usual conditions. Suppose that
\bm{W}=(W_{1}(t),\ldots,W_{n}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is an
n
-dimensional standard Brownian motion and
B=(B(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a one-dimensional standard Brownian motion, both of them are defined on this probability space and are mutually independent.

There are
n
agents in the financial market, indexed by
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, along with
n
risky assets and one risk-free asset. Each agent can invest in the risk-free asset and a stock. More precisely,
the
i
-th agent can invest in a risk-free asset, whose price is denoted by
S_{0}(t)
satisfying the following equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{0}(t)}{S_{0}(t)}=r\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\qquad S_{0}(0)=s_{0},

(2.1)

in which
r\geq 0
is the constant interest rate. She can also invest in a stock, we call it the
i
-th stock, whose price process
S_{i}=\{S_{i}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{i}(t)}{S_{i}(t)}

\displaystyle=b_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t+\xi_{i}\,\mathrm{d}W_{i}(t)+\sigma_{i}\,\mathrm{d}B(t),\quad\quad S_{i}(0)=s_{i},

(2.2)

in which
b_{i}(>r)
is the yield of stock,
\xi_{i}(\geq 0)
is its idiosyncratic volatility, and
\sigma_{i}(\geq 0)
is the common volatility with
\xi_{i}+\sigma_{i}>0
.
In our paper, we use the Brownian motion
\bm{W}
to represent the idiosyncratic risk factors for the stocks, and
B
to represent the common risk factor that affects the entire market.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
\pi_{i}(t)
represent the amount of money that the
i
-th agent invests in the
i
-th stock. Then, the
i
-th agent’s wealth process
X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}=\{X^{{\bm{\pi}}_{i}}_{i}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
under strategy
{\bm{\pi}}_{i}=\{\pi_{i}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
follows the dynamics

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=[rX^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(t)+(b_{i}-r)\pi_{i}(t)]\,\mathrm{d}t+\xi_{i}\pi_{i}(t)\,\mathrm{d}W_{i}(t)+\sigma_{i}\pi_{i}(t)\,\mathrm{d}B(t).

(2.3)

We use
\bm{X}^{\bm{\pi}}=\big(X^{\bm{\pi}_{1}}_{1}(t),\ldots,X^{\bm{\pi}_{n}}_{n}(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}
to denote the
n
agents’ wealth process under the strategy
\bm{\pi}=(\bm{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\pi}_{n})
. To address the investment problem of the
i
-th agent, we introduce the definition of an
admissible feedback strategy
for the
i
-th agent.

Definition 2.1

(Admissible Strategy)
.

For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, a feedback strategy
\bm{\pi}_{i}=\big(\pi_{i}\big(t,\bm{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)\big)\big)_{t\geq 0}
is called admissible if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\int_{0}^{t}\pi_{i}^{2}(s,\bm{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty
almost surely for all
t\geq 0
.

(2)

The SDE (
2.3
) has a unique strong solution for any
X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(0)=x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.

We use
\bm{\Pi}_{i}
to denote the set of the
i
-th agent’s all admissible feedback strategies.

Let
\tau
be a random variable defined on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, and it is independent of the Brownian motions
(\bm{W},B)
. We use
\lambda(t)
to denote the time-dependent hazard rate function of
\tau
such that

\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s\right).

We consider a mean-variance optimization portfolio problem for each agent over a random horizon
(0,\tau]
. For each agent, we assume they are concerned about the relative wealth comparing to others.
Define

\overline{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(t),

the
n
agents’ average wealth at time
t
under the strategy
\bm{\pi}
.
In order to have a more realistic model,
Björk et al., 2014
studied a mean-variance portfolio optimization problem when the risk aversion depends dynamically on current wealth, and they derived an analytic solution when the risk aversion
\gamma(x)=\gamma/x
. In the single-period mean-variance analysis, the objective function on
[0,1]
is to maximize

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{\bm{\pi}}_{1}]-\frac{\gamma}{2x}\text{Var}[X^{\bm{\pi}}_{1}].

(2.4)

If
X_{0}=x>0
, then maximizing (
2.4
) is equivalent to maximizing

2x\cdot\mathbb{E}[X^{\bm{\pi}}_{1}]-{\gamma}\text{Var}[X^{\bm{\pi}}_{1}].

Thus, the two objective functionals yield the same equilibrium control strategy.
In light of this, in this paper, the
i
-th agent seeks to maximize
J_{i}(t,\bm{x};\bm{\pi})
, in which
J_{i}
has the following form:

\displaystyle J_{i}(t,\bm{x};\bm{\pi})=({\mu_{i1}x_{i}+\mu_{i2}})\mathbb{E}_{t,\bm{x},t+}[X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(\tau)-\phi_{i}\overline{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(\tau)]-{\gamma_{i}}\text{Var}_{t,\bm{x},t+}[X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(\tau)-\phi_{i}\overline{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(\tau)].

(2.5)

Here,
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is the agents’ initial wealth at time
t
, and
\mathbb{E}_{t,\bm{x},t+}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\bm{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)=\bm{x},\tau>t]
. Moreover, we use
X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(t)-\phi_{i}\overline{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)
to denote the
i
-th agent’s relative wealth with
\phi_{i}\in[0,1]
representing the
i
-th agent’s risk preference regarding their own wealth relative to the overall average wealth. If
\phi_{i}
is large, the agent probably focuses more on the relative wealth than their own wealth.
\gamma_{i}>0
is the
i
-th agent’s risk aversion towards variance.
\mu_{i1}
and
\mu_{i2}
are non-negative constants. Due to the presence of
\overline{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(\tau)
in the objective, the optimal decision of the
i
-th agent combines with the other competitors’ optimal control strategies. We want to find the Nash equilibrium for this
n
-agent game. To this end, we introduce an auxiliary process

Y^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}:=\{Y^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
as

Y^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}(t):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}X^{\bm{\pi}_{j}}_{j}(t),

in which

{\bm{\pi}_{{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}}:=(\bm{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\pi}_{i-1},\bm{\pi}_{i+1},\ldots,\bm{\pi}_{n})
, and then
Y^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}
satisfies the following SDE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}(t)=\left[rY^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}(t)+\widehat{\underline{br}\pi}(t)\right]\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\xi_{j}\pi_{j}(t)\mathrm{d}W_{j}(t)+\widehat{\sigma\pi}(t)\mathrm{d}B(t),

(2.6)

with
\widehat{\underline{br}\pi}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}(b_{j}-r)\pi_{j}(t)
and
\widehat{\sigma\pi}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}\sigma_{j}\pi_{j}(t)
. By using the auxiliary process
Y^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}
, we can rewrite (
2.5
) as follows

\displaystyle J_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i};\bm{\pi})

\displaystyle=(\mu_{i1}x_{i}+\mu_{i2})\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i},t+}\left[\left(1-\frac{\phi_{i}}{n}\right)X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(\tau)-\phi_{i}Y^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}(\tau)\right]

(2.7)

\displaystyle\quad-\gamma_{i}\text{Var}_{t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i},t+}\left[\left(1-\frac{\phi_{i}}{n}\right)X^{\bm{\pi}_{i}}_{i}(\tau)-\phi_{i}Y^{\bm{\pi}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}(\tau)\right],

(2.8)

with
y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}x_{j}
.

We show the definition of the equilibrium feedback strategy concerning the
n
coupled stochastic control problems addressed by
n
competitive agents.

Definition 2.2

(Time-consistent equilibrium feedback strategy)
.

For a given admissible feedback trading strategy
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}=(\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{n})
with
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{i}=\{\widehat{\pi}_{i}(t,{X}^{\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{i}}_{i}(t),Y^{\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}(t))\}_{t\geq 0}\in\bm{\Pi}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, any fixed
(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, and a fixed number
\epsilon>0
; then define a new vector
(\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\pi}_{i}^{\epsilon},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{n})
by

\pi_{i}^{\epsilon}(s,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})=\begin{cases}\pi_{i},&\text{for }{t\leq s<(t+\epsilon)\wedge\tau,}\\
\widehat{\pi}_{i}(s,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}),&\text{for }(t+\epsilon)\wedge\tau\leq s\leq\tau.\end{cases}

If for all
(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{J_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i};\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{i},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{n})-J_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i};\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\pi}^{\epsilon}_{i},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{n})}{\epsilon}\geq 0,

(2.9)

then
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}
is a time-consistent equilibrium feedback strategy,
and the corresponding equilibrium value function for the
i
-th agent is given by

V_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})=J_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i};\widehat{\bm{\pi}}),~~~~\text{for}~~i=1,\ldots,n.

2.2
Solution to the
n
-agent game

In this subection, we first prove a verification theorem for our problem by employing a decomposition approach similar to that in
Kryger and Steffensen, 2010
. For the sake of brevity, the proof is deferred to Appendix
A
. After that, we use this theorem to derive the time-consistent equilibrium feedback strategies for the
n
-agent game when the hazard rate of the random horizon is a positive constant. Given a function
g(t,x,y)\in C^{1,2,2}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
, and for any real numbers
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{n})
, define the infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\pi}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}^{{\pi}}g(t,x,y)=

\displaystyle\,g_{t}+g_{x}[rx+(b_{i}-r)\pi_{i}]+g_{y}[ry+\widehat{\underline{br}\pi}]

(2.10)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}g_{xx}\left[\xi_{i}^{2}\pi_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\pi_{i}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{2}g_{yy}\left[\widehat{\xi\pi}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma\pi}^{2}\right]

(2.11)

\displaystyle+g_{xy}\sigma_{i}\pi_{i}\widehat{\sigma\pi},

(2.12)

in which
\widehat{\xi\pi}^{2}=\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\xi_{j}\pi_{j}\right)^{2}
,
\widehat{\underline{br}\pi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}(b_{j}-r)\pi_{j}
, and
\widehat{\sigma\pi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}\sigma_{j}\pi_{j}
.
Define a function
f\in C^{1,2,2,2,2}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{4})
by

\displaystyle f(t,x,y,u,v)=(\mu_{i1}x+\mu_{i2})u-\gamma_{i}(v-u^{2}),

(2.13)

where
u
and
v
are two functions depending on
(t,x,y)
.

Theorem 2.1

(Verification Theorem)
.

Suppose that the hazard rate
\lambda(t)
has support on all of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and suppose that there exist three real-valued functions
V_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})
,
G_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})
,
H_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})\in C^{1,2,2}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
for any
i=1,\ldots,n
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

For all
(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lambda(t)\bigg\{V_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})-(\mu_{i1}x_{i}+\mu_{i2})\Big(\big(1-\frac{\phi_{i}}{n}\big)x_{i}-\phi_{i}y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}\Big)+{\gamma_{i}}\Big[G_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})-\Big((1-\frac{\phi_{i}}{n})x_{i}-\phi_{i}y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}\Big)\Big]^{2}\bigg\}

(2.14)

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\pi}}\{\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\widehat{\pi}(i)}V_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})-\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\widehat{\pi}(i)}f(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i},G_{i},H_{i})+f_{G}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i},G_{i},H_{i})\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\widehat{\pi}(i)}G_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})

(2.15)

\displaystyle\quad+f_{H}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i},G_{i},H_{i})\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\widehat{\pi}(i)}H_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})\},

(2.16)

in which
\widehat{\pi}(i)=(\widehat{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\pi_{i},\ldots,\widehat{\pi}_{n})
. Let
\widehat{\pi}_{i}
denote the function of
(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})
that attains the supremum in (
2.16
) and
\widehat{\pi}=(\widehat{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\pi}_{i},\ldots,\widehat{\pi}_{n})
.

(2)

For all
(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lambda(t)\Big[G_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})-\Big(\big(1-\frac{\phi_{i}}{n}\big)x_{i}-\phi_{i}y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}\Big)\Big]=\mathcal{L}^{\widehat{{\pi}}}_{i}G_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}).

(2.17)

(3)

For all
(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lambda(t)\Big[H_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})-\Big(\big(1-\frac{\phi_{i}}{n}\big)x_{i}-\phi_{i}y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}\Big)^{2}\Big]=\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\widehat{{\pi}}}H_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}).

(2.18)

(4)

A transversality condition holds. Specifically, for all
(t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{i},y_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}\Big[e^{-\int_{t}^{s}\lambda(v)\mathrm{d}v}j\big(s,X^{\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{i}}_{i}(s),Y^{\widehat{\bm{\pi}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}}_{\scalebox{0.75}{-}i}(s)\big)\Big]=0,

(2.19)

for
j=G_{i}
,
H_{i}

3
The mean-field game (MFG)

In this section, we study the equilibrium feedback strategy for the game as the number of agents
n
tends to infinity. In this case, the
n
-agent game converges to a mean-field game with a continuum of agents. In Subsection
3.1
, we introduce the type space, the objective, and the definition of the mean-field equilibrium (MFE) feedback strategy. In Subsection
3.2
, we first present a general verification theorem. Then, we use it to find the solutions of the mean-field game when the hazard rate of the random horizon is constant. Finally, we derive the relationship between the equilibrium feedback strategy in the
n
-agent game and the MFE in the mean-field game in Subsection
3.3
.

3.1
Model formulation

We start by defining the type vector and the type space. For the
n
-agent game, we denote the parameters of the
i
-th agent by the following type vector

\displaystyle\eta_{i}:=(x_{i},b_{i},\xi_{i},\sigma_{i},\phi_{i},\gamma_{i},\mu_{1i},\mu_{2i}),\qquad\text{for}~~i=1,\ldots,n.

(3.1)

We allow these parameters to depend on
n
, but for simplicity, we will not add an index
n
to the type vector
\eta_{i}
. These type vectors induce an empirical measure
m_{n}
, called the type distribution, which is a probability measure on the type space

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{e}:=\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)^{3}\times[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times[0,\infty)^{2},

given by

\displaystyle m_{n}(A)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf{1}}_{A}(\eta_{i})\quad\text{for Borel sets }A\subset\mathcal{Z}^{e}.

(3.2)

We then find that the equilibrium strategy
\widehat{\pi}_{i}
computed in Theorem
2.2
depends only on the
i
-th agent’s own type vector
\eta_{i}
and the type distribution
m_{n}
of all type vectors. Now, we assume that the empirical measure
m_{n}
converges weakly to a limit measure
m
on the space
\mathcal{Z}^{e}
in the sense that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Z}^{e}}f\,\mathrm{d}m_{n}\to\int_{\mathcal{Z}^{e}}f\,\mathrm{d}m,

(3.3)

for any bounded continuous function
f
on
\mathcal{Z}^{e}
when the number of agents tend to infinity, that is
n\to\infty
. We define
\eta=(x,b,\xi,\sigma,\phi,\gamma,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
as a random variable with this limiting distribution
m
on the space
\mathcal{Z}^{e}
. The distribution of this random type vector reflects the law of type vectors of a continuum of agents, where a single realization of the random type vector represents the type assigned to a single representative agent.

We start to formulate the MFG, and then derive the expression of the equilibrium feedback strategy for the MFG. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered complete probability space satisfying the usual conditions, which supports a two-dimensional Brownian motion
(W,B)
and a random type vector
\eta=(x,b,\xi,\sigma,\phi,\gamma,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
. Here,
\eta
is measurable with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and is independent of both
W
and
B
. Let
\mathbb{F}^{B}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{B}\}_{t\geq 0}
denote the natural filtration generated by the Brownian motion
B
.

We assume that the financial market is the same as that considered in Subsection
2.1
except that the idiosyncratic risk factors for the stocks are replaced by
W=\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
. Furthermore, we assume that the risk-free interest rate
r
is identical for all agents. Let
\pi(t)
represent the amount of money that the representative agent invests in the risky stock. Then, the dynamic of the representative agent’s wealth process
X^{\bm{\pi}}=\{X^{{\bm{\pi}}}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
under strategy
{\bm{\pi}}=\{\pi(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the following SDE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{\bm{\pi}}(t)

\displaystyle=[rX^{\bm{\pi}}(t)+(b-r)\pi(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\xi\pi(t)\mathrm{d}W(t)+\sigma\pi(t)\mathrm{d}B(t).

(3.4)

In order to solve the mean-field game, we restrict our attention to the average wealth of the continuum of agents, which is represented by the stochastic process
\{\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
of the form
\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^{\bm{\pi}}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{B}]
for all
t\geq 0
. By taking the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\mathcal{F}^{B}_{t}]
on both sides of (
3.4
), we obtain the SDE of
\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)
as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)=[r\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)+\overline{\underline{br}\pi}(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\overline{\sigma\pi}(t)\mathrm{d}B(t),~~\text{for all}~~t\geq 0,

(3.5)

where
\overline{\underline{br}\pi}(t)=\mathbb{E}[(b-r)\pi(t)]
, and
\overline{\sigma\pi}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\sigma\pi(t)]
.

Definition 3.1

(Admissible Strategy)
.

A feedback strategy
\bm{\pi}=\{\pi(t,X^{\bm{\pi}}(t),\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t))\}_{t\geq 0}
is called admissible if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\int_{0}^{t}|\pi(s,X^{\bm{\pi}}(s),\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(s))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big]<\infty
almost surely for all
t\geq 0
.

(2)

The SDE (
3.4
) has a unique strong solution for any
X^{\bm{\pi}}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We use
\bm{\Pi}^{m}
to denote the set of the representative agent’s all admissible feedback strategies.

We also assume the time horizon is random. Let
\tau
be a random variable defined in the above probability space, which is independent of the Brownian motion
(W,B)
and the type vector
\eta
, and we use
\lambda(t)
to denote the time-dependent hazard rate function of
\tau
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)=\exp\Big(-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s\Big).

In the MFG framework, we assume that the representative agent has a mean-variance investment preference with a state-dependent risk aversion, concerning about the average wealth
\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)
. Thus, the objective for the representative agent is to maximize

\displaystyle J(t,x,\bar{x};{\bm{\pi}})=(\mu_{1}x+\mu_{2})\mathbb{E}_{t,x,\bar{x},t+}[X^{\bm{\pi}}(\tau)-\phi\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(\tau)]-\gamma Var_{t,x,\bar{x},t+}[X^{\bm{\pi}}(\tau)-\phi\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(\tau)],

(3.6)

in which
\mathbb{E}_{t,x,\bar{x},t+}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)={x},\bar{X}^{\bm{\pi}}(t)=\bar{x},\tau>t]
.

Now, we give the definition of a time-consistent mean-field equilibrium feedback strategy in the MFG framework.

Definition 3.2

(Time-consistent mean-field equilibrium feedback strategy)
.

For an admissible feedback strategy
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}=\{\widehat{\pi}(t,X^{\widehat{\bm{\pi}}}(t),\bar{X}^{\widehat{\bm{\pi}}}(t))\}_{t\geq 0}
, any fixed
(t,x,\bar{x})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, a fixed number
\epsilon>0
, and a real number
\pi
, define a feedback strategy
\bm{\pi}^{\epsilon}
by

\pi^{\epsilon}(s,x,\bar{x})=\begin{cases}\pi,&\text{for }~~{t\leq s<(t+\epsilon)\wedge\tau,}\\
\widehat{\pi}(s,x,\bar{x}),&\text{for }~~(t+\epsilon)\wedge\tau\leq s\leq\tau.\end{cases}

If for all
(t,x,\bar{x})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{J(t,x,\bar{x};\widehat{\bm{\pi}})-J(t,x,\bar{x};{\bm{\pi}^{\epsilon}})}{\epsilon}\geq 0,

(3.7)

then
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}
is a time-consistent mean-field equilibrium (MFE) feedback strategy,
and the corresponding equilibrium value function is given by

V(t,x,\bar{x})=J(t,x,\bar{x};\widehat{\bm{\pi}}),

with
\bar{X}^{\widehat{\bm{\pi}}}(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^{\widehat{\bm{\pi}}}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{B}]
for all
t\geq 0
.

3.2

2.1

First, for any real number

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle m}^{{\pi}}g(t,x,\bar{x})=

\displaystyle g_{t}+g_{x}[rx+(b-r)\pi]+g_{\bar{x}}[r\bar{x}+\overline{\underline{br}\pi}(t)]

(3.8)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}g_{xx}[\xi^{2}\pi^{2}+\sigma^{2}\pi^{2}]+\frac{1}{2}g_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}\overline{\sigma\pi}(t)^{2}+g_{x\bar{x}}\sigma\pi\overline{\sigma\pi}(t).

(3.9)

Define a function

Theorem 3.1
