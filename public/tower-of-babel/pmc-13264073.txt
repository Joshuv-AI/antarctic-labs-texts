Tracing aesthetic experience from perception and conception to appraisal using deep convolutional neural networks
Authors: Yi Lin, Lukas Verlinde, Johan Wagemans, Hans Op de Beeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAesthetic experience is a multi-level process, yet current theoretical models do not link the different components within a unified framework. Here, we use human-aligned deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) to capture these components and probe their underlying mechanisms. We show that a recently proposed DCNN-based integration metric—reflecting reduced representational complexity—strongly predicts perceptual fluency of paintings. This effect peaks in early layers and for fine spatial detail, with distinct layers aligning to different fluency-related features, indicating stage-specific mechanisms. However, this perceptually grounded metric predicts liking less accurately, indicating a partial dissociation between fluency and liking. Contrastingly, deeper layers capture higher-level conceptual information (e.g., style), and ridge regression on layer-wise activations predicts liking more accurately, with performance increasing across depth. Domain comparisons further reveal distinct layer and spatial-scale dependencies for paintings versus natural scenes. Together, layer-resolved DCNN modeling offers a principled framework for tracing the multiple stages of aesthetic appreciation.Subject areas: Cognitive neuroscience, Neural networks, Computer science, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Visual aesthetic experience is a complex and dynamic process shaped by interactions between the properties of an object (e.g., a painting) and those of the perceiver (i.e., the “eye of the beholder”). Such objects span diverse domains, including natural scenes,1 buildings,2 works of visual art (e.g., paintings3), as well as digital artifacts such as websites and user interfaces4,5 and industrial products such as cars.6 As a result, a broad empirical literature and multiple theoretical frameworks have been proposed to characterize the component processes of aesthetic experience, as summarized in several reviews and meta-analyses.3,7,8,9,10,11,12 A common theme across these accounts is that aesthetic experience is not merely an endpoint judgment but unfolds over time through interacting processing stages12. This motivates a central open question: how is sensory input transformed into distinct representations associated with these processes, ultimately shaping aesthetic experience? Here, we test highly human-aligned artificial neural networks as computational models to trace and explain the potential stages of information processing in the human brain that give rise to aesthetic appreciation. We focus on paintings because of their diversity—providing a challenging test case—and because aesthetic evaluation is particularly central in this domain.

Aesthetic experience unfolds across multiple levels of processing within a single viewing episode. When viewing an image, humans engage in a cascade of processes—beginning with perceptual analysis, progressing through higher-level conceptual processing, and culminating in affective appraisal. At the perceptual level, many studies have focused on subjective, visual fluency—the ease with which an image is processed—which has been closely linked with aesthetic judgments such as liking and beauty.1,8,13,14,15,16,17 Fluency of processing refers to its remarkable consistency across individuals,18 suggesting shared underlying mechanisms in visual processing. At the conceptual level, a range of factors have been linked to aesthetic appreciation of paintings, including style/abstractness,18,19 the image content and type (e.g., portrait vs. landscape18,19), and features related to visual recognition.19,20

Yet, most empirical studies have examined these components in isolation, failing to reflect the ecological reality of how these processes interact and unfold dynamically over time. Meanwhile, theoretical accounts have heavily relied on such fragmented evidence, lacking direct and systematic biological support for how a single stimulus is processed across perceptual and conceptual levels to produce nuanced aesthetic appraisal. This gap underscores the need for more integrative approaches capable of capturing the full trajectory of aesthetic processing—from low-level perceptual components to high-level appreciation—within a unified framework. In other words, we aim to understand how sensory input is transformed into multiple levels of representation that collectively shape aesthetic experience.

In this study, we address prior limitations by leveraging highly human-aligned deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs)—computational models inspired by the primate visual system and shown to share important similarities with human vision in terms of information processing strategies.21,22,23,24,25 More specifically, DCNNs comprise hierarchical layers that are often taken to approximate key stages of the visual processing hierarchy in the human brain: early layers predominantly capture low-level visual properties, whereas later layers represent increasingly complex and abstract features.21,24 For instance, Kubilius et al.24 orthogonalized physical and perceived shape dimensions and showed a clear layer-dependent dissociation: shallow layers tracked physical similarity, while deeper layers aligned more closely with human perceptual similarity judgments. Converging evidence further links layer-specific DCNN representations to neural data.22,25 For example, intracranial recordings from human face-selective neuronal populations show a close match in face-space geometry with a face-trained DCNN, and this correspondence is layer-dependent rather than uniformly strongest at the network output.22 Together, these findings motivate a layer-resolved approach: rather than treating a DCNN as a single monolithic model, we analyze representations across network depth to approximate stage-wise information processing and to localize the stages at which different information is expressed.

Building on this framework, we use DCNN layer activations to trace potential stages of processing that give rise to aesthetic appreciation. Specifically, we aim to (1) predict multiple levels of processing within a unified framework and (2) gain insight into the mechanisms that may underlie aesthetic experience. Notably, we focus on population-average responses; modeling viewer-specific factors (e.g., age, gender, and education) and individual differences is beyond the scope of the present study.

More specifically, we asked a series of key questions: first, can DCNNs capture the perceptual signature of fluency, and if so, which aspects of visual feature processing contribute to this perceptual ease? Second, can fluency-related DCNN representations predict aesthetic appreciation, or do higher-level conceptual processes play a more decisive role in evaluating paintings? Finally, at what stage—i.e., in which DCNN layers—do conceptual signals emerge, and what types of conceptual information can be distinguished? Our main analyses use the VGG16 architecture, both to ensure methodological continuity with prior study1 and to leverage its well-defined hierarchical structure as a framework for stage-resolved analyses of how visual representations evolve across processing depth.22,26,27 To assess generalizability beyond a single backbone, we additionally report results from alternative architectures (Figure S1).

Another crucial issue is that, to study the role of multiple components in aesthetic appreciation, we must dissociate the targeted components and avoid confounds. Typically, the relevant factors are highly correlated. To address this challenge, we placed particular emphasis on the Leuven orthogonalized art dataset (LOAD).18 LOAD is a high-dimensional, experimentally balanced set of 343 paintings (Figure 1) that systematically vary along perceptual (e.g., fluency), conceptual (e.g., image content and style), and affective (e.g., liking) dimensions. This dataset offers a rare opportunity to investigate aesthetic experience in a tightly controlled yet ecologically valid context. To verify the conclusions in a larger yet less controlled dataset, we also used the Leuven art personalized image set28(LAPIS), a collection of 11,345 paintings (Figure 1). Finally, LAPIS-landscapes, a curated subset of landscape paintings, enabled direct comparisons between responses to artworks and natural scene photographs.1

Overview of the image set

Using layer-wise DCNN activations, we first showed that a previously proposed DCNN-based integration metric1—quantifying the deviation between part-based and whole-image DCNN activations to reflect spatial integration, and originally developed merely to predict beauty in natural scene photographs—reliably captures subjective fluency for paintings. We demonstrate here that this integration metric relates to the processing of several specific visual features (e.g., self-similarity) in different DCNN layers, providing deeper insight into the mechanisms contributing to perceived fluency. However, in contrast to Nara and Kaiser’s findings for natural-scene photographs,1 the integration metric predicted aesthetic judgments for paintings less accurately, even when restricting analyses to landscape paintings or using DCNNs pretrained on more art-like images (Figure S2). Moreover, for artworks, the strongest prediction of aesthetic judgments with the current VGG16-based approach relied on fine-scale image visual information and peaked in early layers, whereas for the scene photographs in Nara and Kaiser,1 coarser, intermediate-level image visual information yielded stronger predictions that peaked in late-middle layers. These results suggest that the predictive power of visual integration—and the processing stages that support it—varies substantially across image domains. At the conceptual level, we examined where and how artistic style and higher-level/abstract representational features contribute to aesthetic judgment. A linear model trained directly on DCNN activations outperformed the integration metric in predicting liking judgments, with performance increasing across successive layers. These findings suggest that while subjective fluency and integration capture one dimension of aesthetic experience, additional outputs from conceptual-level processing—represented at higher layers within the same visual architecture—play a broader role in aesthetic appraisal. Overall, the results underscore the value of artificial neural networks for probing complex cognitive phenomena across multiple levels of processing.

Results

DCNNs capture subjective fluency

As a first step, we asked whether DCNNs can capture subjective fluency—the ease with which an image is processed—a perceptual-level component of aesthetic experience known for its consistency across individuals. To test this, we adopted the integration metric proposed by Nara and Kaiser.1 This metric quantifies the coherence and integration of visual representations. The underlying idea is that when image elements are perceived as an integrated, coherent whole—rather than as disjointed parts—the visual system represents the input more efficiently (i.e., with reduced representational complexity). To operationalize this, the integration metric computes the sign-inverted correlation between VGG-16 layer’s activation pattern for the whole image and the average activation pattern of its two complementary halves. We extracted activations from VGG16’s convolutional and fully connected layers (16 layers in total; Figure 2). Under this definition, a higher sign-inverted correlation indicates stronger integration, which we interpret as reflecting reduced representational complexity and, potentially, greater perceptual fluency. Importantly, this metric is computed separately at each layer, thereby providing a layer-resolved view of representational change across network depth and serving as an operational proxy for processing stages in human vision. For the main analyses, we used VGG16 pretrained on ImageNet for object categorization.

Another key strength of this integration metric is its sensitivity to spatial granularity—that is, the scale at which visual information is integrated. Specifically, image halves were generated at five levels of spatial resolution (2 × 2 to 32 × 32 grid divisions, Figure 3 top legend), allowing us to assess whether coarse or fine spatial information better predicts human ratings of fluency. Notably, Nara and Kaiser1 only tested that DCNN integration is correlated to the beauty of scene images. While they speculated that this correlation might be due to a relationship between DCNN integration and subjective fluency, they never measured this relationship. We used the LOAD18 to obtain ground-truth subjective ratings of fluency. LOAD includes ratings from over 50 participants per painting and features a balanced variation across perceptual, conceptual, and affective dimensions, making it particularly well-suited for evaluating multi-level aesthetic processes.

We correlated the subjective fluency ratings for the 343 paintings with the DCNN-derived integration scores for each layer and spatial granularity. The results revealed strong associations between the integration metric and human fluency judgments, with peak correlations reaching r = 0.60 (Figures 3A and 3B shows the scatterplot for this highest correlation). Notably, most of the observed correlations fall in the moderate-to-large effect-size range based on meta-analytic summaries of typical correlation magnitudes in psychology.29,30 In addition, integration scores computed at finer spatial granularities (e.g., 32 × 32 divisions) consistently showed the strongest correlations, suggesting that high-resolution visual structure plays a critical role in processing fluency during painting perception.

To benchmark the predictive utility of the integration metric, we compared it to other established fluency-related visual features (Figure 4A). Using the R package imagefluency,31 we computed five visual features: visual simplicity, vertical and horizontal symmetry, contrast, and self-similarity. While most of these features showed statistically significant correlations with fluency ratings (Figure 4B)—simplicity (r = 0.37, pFDR < 0.001), vertical symmetry (r = 0.34, pFDR < 0.001), horizontal symmetry (r = 0.26, pFDR < 0.001), self-similarity (r = 0.13, pFDR = 0.023)—their effect sizes were consistently lower than those of the integration metric. Visual contrast showed no association (r = 0.00, pFDR = 0.95).

Together, these results suggest that the DCNN-derived integration metric not only captures subjective fluency significantly but also does so more effectively than traditional image-based feature extractions—demonstrating that DCNNs can model the perceptual-level outputs of aesthetic experience with appreciable predictive power.

Mapping indices of objective fluency across layers

Having established that the DCNN-based integration metric effectively captures subjective fluency, we next examined how this signal evolves across layers—a step that can illuminate the potential mechanisms underlying fluency perception in humans.

Although the five visual features computed via the imagefluency R package31 generally showed weaker correlations with subjective fluency ratings than the integration metric (Figure 4B), four of them—simplicity, vertical symmetry, horizontal symmetry, and self-similarity—were nonetheless significantly associated with fluency. This pattern potentially suggests that fluency arises from a combination of multiple visual cues processed at different levels.

To investigate this further, we correlated the integration metric with each of these visual features across VGG-16 layers. Simplicity showed the strongest relationship, peaking in early to intermediate layers (up to r = 0.76; Figure 4C). Vertical and horizontal symmetry were also most correlated with integration in early to mid-level layers (r = 0.42 and r = 0.34, respectively; Figures 4D and 4E). In contrast, self-similarity—a more global feature—showed its strongest correspondence with integration in intermediate to late-intermediate layers (up to r = 0.29; Figure 4F). In addition, self-similarity showed a different pattern across granularities, as it is the only visual feature for which correlations are highest with coarser granularities. These results were further validated via permutation tests (Figure S3).

Together, these findings suggest that the DCNN-derived integration metric reflects a composite of multiple visual computations. The peak correlations observed at different layers support the idea that fluency is processed through multiple stages, each emphasizing distinct visual features. In this framework, DCNNs offer a computational tool for modeling the layered nature of fluency, capturing how various features converge to shape subjective experience.

Finally, combining all the measured fluency-related variables together, we performed a variance decomposition analysis (Figures 4G and S4) to quantify the unique and shared contributions of five predictors—DCNN integration, simplicity, vertical symmetry, horizontal symmetry, and self-similarity—to subjective fluency. To place predictors on a common scale, each variable was rank-transformed and z-scored before analysis. Integration metric explained a nontrivial amount of unique variance in fluency, while also sharing substantial variance with the objective visual features; this shared component was most pronounced in shallow network layers (Figure 4G; Shared_inte_vis). Together, these results validate that integration partially overlaps with the 4 fluency-related visual features but also captures additional information that improves prediction of subjective fluency.

DCNN-derived integration measure predicts aesthetic appraisals of paintings less accurately

Prior literature suggests that fluency facilitates aesthetic experience,1,14,15 but its role may vary across contexts. To test this, we first focused on LOAD and correlated DCNN-derived integration scores with aesthetic liking ratings.

Contrary to suggestions that fluency might strongly influence aesthetic ratings, the integration metric exhibited only modest predictive power with liking, with a peak correlation of r = 0.31 (Figure 5A). This modest effect closely mirrors the previously reported correlation of r = 0.30 between subjective fluency ratings and liking within the LOAD dataset.18 Figure 5B shows the scatterplot of integration value versus liking at spatial scale 32, layer 13—where integration exhibited its highest correlation with fluency. However, the positive trend observed in Figure 3B between integration and fluency is absent when integration is correlated with liking—Figure 5B demonstrates that paintings with low fluency/low integration can still receive high liking scores. These results reveal a substantial difference in terms of how DCNN-derived integration relates to subjective fluency and aesthetic appraisal: while DCNN-derived integration aligns well with fluency, it appears less suited for predicting aesthetic liking.

These findings stand in contrast with results by Nara and Kaiser,1 who reported a strong correlation between DCNN-derived integration and beauty, which we indeed replicated for their dataset (Figure S5). Since beauty (in Nara and Kaiser’s study) and liking (in the LOAD dataset) could, in principle, reflect distinct constructs, we had a separate group of participants to rate the LOAD paintings for beauty. Beauty and liking ratings were highly correlated (r = 0.80, p < 0.01), indicating that the weak effect observed in LOAD cannot be attributed solely to differences between these two aesthetic judgments.

We also considered whether the results from LOAD depended on the pretraining material of the DCNN. Repeating key analyses using a stylized VGG-16 model pretrained on Stylized ImageNet32 for object categorization revealed highly consistent results across models (Figure S2), suggesting that the failure of ImageNet-trained VGG-16 was not due to an inability to generalize from natural images (i.e., pretrain materials from ImageNet) to more stylized images like paintings.

Of course, one might argue that the weak correlation observed in LOAD reflects dataset-specific characteristics—namely, its design to dissociate different components of aesthetic experience and its relatively limited number of stimuli. To address these concerns, we turned to the larger and more diverse LAPIS dataset,28 which includes 11,345 paintings spanning 26 art styles. Notably, unlike LOAD, LAPIS includes no experimental manipulation of image selection and collects beauty ratings rather than liking judgments. Correlating DCNN-derived integration values with beauty ratings in LAPIS revealed an unexpected pattern (Figure 5C): correlations were notably low in mid-level layers (layers 4–8) and increased only in the deeper layers—deviating from the profile observed in LOAD, as well as from the pattern across layers in the earlier work by Nara and Kaiser,1 who found highest correlations in the late convolutional layers. We hypothesized that the higher proportion of highly abstract artworks in LAPIS (e.g., color field paintings), compared to LOAD, might obscure the relationship between visual integration and aesthetic beauty. To test this, we curated a subset of 2,742 representational landscape paintings—LAPIS-landscapes—which exceeds LOAD in size, while focusing on more representative art content, thereby allowing for more targeted validation. Strikingly, the correlation pattern for LAPIS-landscapes (Figure 5D) closely resembled that of LOAD, with only modest correlations between DCNN-derived integration and aesthetics appraisal at the shallow layers. Across all three painting (sub)datasets, the finest spatial scale (scale 32)—consistent with the fluency results—showed the strongest correlations for predicting liking relative to other spatial scales, suggesting that detailed visual information plays a critical role in aesthetic appraisal (liking/beauty) during painting perception. Notably, this pattern again differs from Nara and Kaiser’s findings1 using natural-scene photos, where the strongest correlations were observed at scales 8, intermediate layers. Taken together, these results suggest that, in the context of painting appreciation, aesthetic experience is not captured well alone by a DCNN-based integration measure—or by the fluency-related processing that the integration metric appears to reflect—in contrast to prior findings for natural-scene photographs. More broadly, the cross-domain comparison (paintings vs. natural-scene photographs) suggests that the computational strategies supporting aesthetic appreciation may differ across visual media. We further validated the key domain-specific finding using an additional DCNN architecture (Figure S1). To the extent that stronger correlations are sometimes found, as in the latest DCNN layers for LAPIS (Figure 5C) and for scene photographs by Nara and Kaiser,1 these correlations might be caused by a confounding between beauty and subjective fluency. When we mostly dissociate these two factors, as we did deliberately for the LOAD dataset, DCNN-derived integration goes with fluency (Figure 3A) rather than beauty or liking (Figure 5A).

Artistic style affects aesthetic appraisal and final-layer DCNN-derived integration

How do the conceptual properties of paintings relate to aesthetic appraisals and DCNN representations? We hypothesized that artistic style might play a role for three reasons. First, artistic style is a core feature of paintings, inherently integrating both perceptual and conceptual dimensions. In particular, perceptual elements give rise to variation in both abstractness and recognizability, which in turn engages higher-level conceptual processing. Second, we expect factors such as abstraction and recognizability to be reflected in the final DCNN layer—precisely where we observe at least a modest rise in the correlation between DCNN-derived integration and liking or beauty across all datasets. Third, the correlation rise is the most prominent for LAPIS, which is the dataset with the largest variation in artistic style and abstraction.

To test this hypothesis, we first performed an ANOVA to test whether style affects beauty ratings and DCNN-derived integration values at the final layer, using the LAPIS stimulus set with 26 styles. This analysis revealed a significant main effect of style on both beauty ratings (F(25, 11319) = 477.9) and DCNN-derived integration values at the final layer across different spatial scales (Fscale2(25, 11319) = 161.5; Fscale4(25, 11319) = 202.1; Fscale8(25, 11319) = 210.8; Fscale16(25, 11319) = 319.2; Fscale32(25, 11319) = 397.1).

To further probe this relationship, our in-group art historian, Dr. Stephanie De Winter, categorized the 26 styles into four broader categories based on level of abstraction, following the division described in the LAPIS dataset28 (The simplified style-mapping table is provided as Table S1) As shown in Figure 6 (32 × 32 scale; see Figure S6 for other scales), both beauty ratings and final-layer integration values decrease as abstraction increases. These results suggest that representational styles are both more aesthetically preferred and associated with higher DCNN-derived integration values at the final layer.

Interestingly, the final layer does not only show an increase in the correlation between DCNN-derived integration and aesthetic appraisal, but also a decline in correlation with subjective fluency (Figure 3A). This divergence suggests that final-layer integration may capture higher-level conceptual features—such as artistic style or abstractness—rather than purely reflecting fluency.

Taken together, these results show that artistic style modulates DCNN-derived integration values, DCNN representations, and aesthetic appraisals. Moreover, integration at the final DCNN layer may reflect higher-level conceptual information beyond fluency.

Full DCNN representations predict of aesthetic judgments

Although the DCNN-based integration metric was not a strong predictor of aesthetic judgments in paintings, DCNN representations may nevertheless contain predictive information for liking/beauty that is not captured by this integration measure. To test this possibility and quantify the predictive signal available in DCNN representations, we trained ridge regression models on layer-wise activation patterns as a diagnostic approach.

Using ridge regression (Figure 7A), we trained models on layer-wise VGG-16 activation patterns to predict aesthetic liking for unseen paintings. We implemented it within a nested cross-validation framework (10-fold, 15 bootstraps) on the LOAD dataset, which is the dataset that allows for the best dissociation of aesthetic judgments from fluency. Prediction performance was assessed by correlating predicted and actual ratings, and then averaging across folds and bootstraps (Figure 7B). We evaluated whether these correlations exceeded a threshold of r = 0.31, which was the highest correlation observed between integration and liking (Figure 5A). This value was therefore used as a benchmark to assess whether our full-representation-based DCNN predictions of aesthetic judgment (Figure 7B) were meaningfully greater than those based solely on the integration value.

Results indicated that DCNN activation patterns contain information predictive of aesthetic preference beyond what is captured by the integration metric, with prediction accuracy increasing across successive layers (Figure 7B). In this context, ridge regression provides an upper-bound estimate of the predictability achievable from the full DCNN feature space. To evaluate the reliability of predictions from the full DCNN feature space, we compared the DCNN’s final-layer liking predictions to the internal consistency of human participants. For each participant, we correlated their liking ratings with the leave-one-out group mean (i.e., the mean of all other participants), yielding a distribution of inter-subject reliability scores (Figure 7C). This distribution provides a benchmark for the predictability of individual ratings given the group. The DCNN’s prediction accuracy fell within this human reliability range. Together, these findings suggest that, in the context of viewing paintings, DCNN-derived representations provide a useful computational proxy for certain components of human aesthetic evaluation that are shared among individuals.

Discussion

Overview and contributions

Visual aesthetic experience is a complex and dynamic process shaped by interactions across multiple levels of perceptual and conceptual processing. A central contribution of this study is the use of a unified computational framework—DCNNs—to trace the full trajectory of aesthetic processing, from low-level perceptual fluency to high-level aesthetic appraisal. By systematically examining how DCNNs capture fluency, and how conceptual-level features—such as artistic style, abstraction, image type, and recognition-related DCNN feature embeddings—influence aesthetic judgments, we offer new insights into the architecture of aesthetic experience and advance our understanding of how distinct components interact to shape subjective evaluations.

Integration captures fluency, and fluency is multi-determined

A strong correlation between the DCNN-derived integration measure and subjective fluency ratings supports part of the framework proposed by Nara and Kaiser,1 reinforcing the link between fluency and integration. This positive result further suggests that fluency may operate as a domain-general process—occurring robustly across different visual domains (e.g., paintings and natural scenes). Additionally, the low correlation between subjective fluency ratings and five fluency-related visual features indicates that fluency cannot be accounted for by any single visual feature. Instead, our analysis of correlations between DCNN-derived integration values and visual features31 across layers and spatial scales highlights the complexity of fluency processing. Each feature peaked at different layers, suggesting that fluency arises from the integration of multiple visual features at distinct stages of the visual hierarchy. Moreover, spatial scale played a critical role: while finer resolutions generally yielded higher correlations for most fluency related visual features, self-similarity showed stronger associations at coarser scales. This may suggest that human fluency processing is adaptive, involving flexible shifts in focus to extract relevant information. Finally, our variance decomposition analysis showed that the integration metric shares variance with the fluency-related visual features, but also explains additional unique variance in subjective fluency.

The contribution of fluency to aesthetic judgment is domain-dependent

The influence of fluency and integration on aesthetic judgments appears to be domain-specific—that is, it varies across different visual contexts. Contrary to the findings of Nara and Kaiser,1 who used scene photographs as stimuli, in the context of painting appreciation we generally observed only modest correlations—typically under more stringent manipulations (e.g., LOAD, LAPIS-landscape)—between the DCNN-derived integration measure and aesthetic appraisals. This suggests a partial dissociation between fluency and aesthetic appreciation, particularly in the context of art. Notably, prior studies focusing on art perception have also reported only modest associations between fluency and liking.18

It is important to note that our results do not imply that fluency plays no role in aesthetic experience. On the contrary, we argue that fluency is a key component of aesthetic experience—but its influence on judgment varies depending on the context. Indeed, prior literature has suggested that fluency can elicit positive affect. Stimuli associated with higher fluency—such as those that are more familiar or symmetrical—are often preferred by naive perceivers.14 However, visual experience is inherently dynamic and complex.33 An increase in fluency—like disfluency reduction or the occurrence of an “aha” moment—can also enhance aesthetic experience. These effects highlight that, in more complex or ambiguous contexts, the pathway to aesthetic appreciation may involve a more intricate interplay of factors. Paintings, in particular, contain a rich combination of elements—including color, composition, style, and semantic content—that interact in complex ways. It is therefore not surprising that aesthetic liking in art cannot be strongly determined by fluency alone, further revealing that the integration-based framework of Nara and Kaiser1 is clearly limited as a framework to explain aesthetic appraisals. Taken together, these findings support the view that fluency may serve as a domain-general mechanism, but its influence on aesthetic judgments is modulated by content-specific factors—such as abstraction, style, and semantic richness in the domain of visual art. Our results provide direct empirical evidence to refine existing theories of aesthetic experience.

Spatial scale and layer depth as signatures of domain-specific processing strategies

One striking difference between our findings and those of Nara and Kaiser1 concerns spatial scale. Overall, we found that finer resolutions were generally more effective in capturing relationships with fluency, beauty, and liking. However, Nara and Kaiser,1 whose stimuli consisted of photographs, reported the opposite pattern: correlations peaked at intermediate scales and decreased as spatial resolution became finer. Notably, we validated this domain-specific spatial-scale pattern even using different DCNN model architectures other than VGG16 (Figure S1). This discrepancy may suggest that humans flexibly adjust their processing strategies for different image types during aesthetic experience. This discrepancy may suggest that humans are flexible in processing different types of images during aesthetic experiences. When viewing paintings, participants might focus more on fine-scale details compared to how they process natural images. Indeed, previous research has shown that participants can distinguish artistic styles of cut-out paintings based on subtle details, such as brushstroke coarseness and smoothness.34 Many machine learning models for aesthetics ignore this aspect, which is why CHARM35 has recently been developed to acknowledge the importance of composition, high resolution, aspect ratio, and multi-scale information.

A second domain-specific signature concerns the network depth at which correlations peak. In our painting data, with VGG16, the strongest and most reliable associations with aesthetic judgments emerged in earlier-to-intermediate layers rather than late-intermediate layers, whereas Nara and Kaiser1 reported peak effects at late-intermediate layers for photographs. We observed a similar disparity in peak layer indices when repeating the analyses with an alternative architecture (Figure S1), consistent with the conclusion that the stage of processing most relevant to aesthetic appraisal differs across image domains.

A layer-resolved view separates perceptual and conceptual contributions

Taking a closer look at the integration-based correlation results on LOAD and LAPIS-landscapes, we found that the correlations between integration values and fluency, beauty, or liking were generally lower in the late-intermediate layers (layers 11–13) and the fully connected layers (layers 14–15, but not layer 16) compared to the shallower layers. This pattern suggests that integration, and even fluency, rely primarily on visual representations in the shallower layers.

In contrast, when we move beyond integration values or fluency, we find that several higher-level concepts, including style and liking, are better explained by the later layers. For instance, the DCNN’s encoded features at the final layer predict human liking well—and do so even more reliably when predicting group average than when predicting by the individual ratings. These results imply that: (1) aesthetic experience involves multiple levels of processing; (2) different aesthetic dimensions may depend on different levels of visual representations; and (3) DCNNs provide a valuable tool for tracing complex, multifaceted cognitive processes.

Relation to prior work and what distinguishes our approach

Reviewing the broader literature, it is not uncommon to find artificial networks applied to predict aesthetic experience.4,5,6,19,20,28,36,37 Yet, several aspects distinguish our work. First, we provide a clear definition of the distinct processes within the aesthetic experience. In contrast, many prior studies report predicting “aesthetic score,” “aesthetic preference,” or “aesthetic quality,” and it is unclear whether these terms are interchangeable or whether participants interpret them consistently—an ambiguity that undermines comparability. Second, many results are constrained or biased by the employed stimuli. Some studies manipulated only a few dimensions,36,38 producing small and restricted datasets, whereas large-scale datasets19,28,37,39 capture broad patterns at the cost of dimensional control, leading to systematic biases. The divergent results of LOAD (with stronger control) and LAPIS (with weaker control) provide strong evidence for this claim. Similar issues can be seen in work such as Iigaya et al.,19 who reported that concreteness is a critical factor in aesthetic preference; yet concreteness is inherently confounded with style, which itself plays a substantial role in aesthetic experience. Critically, even with extensive manipulations, our results demonstrate that we successfully modeled distinct processes within aesthetic episodes. Moreover, compared with the findings of Conwell et al.,39 who predicted beauty from a large art image dataset, our prediction accuracy for liking is higher. This further demonstrates that extensive manipulation/selection of dimensions does not hinder the research goal but rather facilitates a deeper understanding of aesthetic experience. Third, in contrast to many computer-science approaches that aim to identify specific predictive attributes (e.g., contrast) or focus solely on predicting aesthetic judgments, our layer-wise DCNN methodology allows us to examine how multiple components of aesthetic experience unfold across processing stages by linking behavioral measures to layer-specific DCNN representations. This layer-resolved approach enables us to ask where in the hierarchy different aesthetic-related signals are most strongly expressed (e.g., fluency, style-related information, and aesthetic judgment), thereby providing more mechanistic insight into the computations that may support aesthetic appraisal. Relatedly, Tang et al.40 also used a VGG-style architecture to derive proxies of fluency, focusing on merely global measures of processing cost/efficiency based on overall network activation (e.g., counting active units or “metabolic cost”). In contrast, our approach explicitly targets the stage-wise transformation of information, offers additional insight into the processes underlying aesthetic experience. Finally, our project is guided by a philosophy that differs from that of Conwell et al.,39 who argue for using visual machine models primarily to capture perceptual encoding as a way to explain higher-level affective responses. We contend that higher-level influences, such as prior knowledge, play an important role. However, to control for such confounds in our experiment, we intentionally selected lesser-known painters and paintings, thereby focusing attention on the visual modality per se. We further believe that one of the key insights offered by DCNNs is how representations are transformed across layers and how these transformations link to human behavioral data—suggesting that humans may deploy a similar strategy.

Robustness and validation

Of course, one might argue that DCNNs diverge from human mechanisms and therefore be skeptical of the findings. However, our findings suggest that this may not be the case here. For instance, Singer et al.41 found that VGG-16 is biased toward texture, resulting in dissimilar representations of photographs and line drawings in the later stages of the network, whereas early and intermediate layers maintain greater similarity. In contrast, humans process both photographs and line drawings with comparable ease. In our study, we also considered whether texture bias might influence the observed correlation pattern and, to address this, used a second VGG-16 model trained on stylized ImageNet. However, we found a very similar pattern of correlations (Figure S2), suggesting that texture bias alone may not fully explain our main findings.

In addition, we also tested whether the reported patterns are specific to VGG-16. Specifically, we repeated the key analyses using an additional model selected based on the Brain-Score Vision leaderboard (https://www.brain-score.org/), which benchmarks model alignment to neural and behavioral measurements. We used ConvNeXt-Large (convnext_large_mlp:clip_laion2b_augreg_ft_in1k_384), a modern architecture with strong reported brain/behavioral alignment. This model uses CLIP-style pretraining on LAION-2B with augmentation/regularization and is subsequently fine-tuned on ImageNet-1K at 384 × 384 resolution. We extracted layer-wise representations from the depthwise-convolution components and computed the same metrics as in the main analyses (Figure S1). We replicated the key findings observed with VGG-16: (1) significant correlations among integration, fluency, and aesthetic judgments; (2) domain-dependent spatial-scale effects, with a similar ordering of the optimal spatial scale across architectures for different types of visual media; and (3) distinct layer-wise peak patterns, such that for all models the peak correlation between integration and fluency occurred earlier than the peak correlation between integration and liking. These results suggest that the main conclusions generalize across architectures.

Closing

Taken together, these findings highlight the value of integrating psychological theory with computational modeling to investigate the mechanisms underlying aesthetic experience. Importantly, our results also underscore the need for well-balanced, ecologically valid stimulus sets to ensure that artificial neural networks can meaningfully approximate aspects of human aesthetic processing. By tracing how perceptual and conceptual information unfolds across DCNNs in relation to aesthetic judgments, we propose a unified, biologically inspired framework for modeling aesthetic experience and for guiding future interdisciplinary research.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations and directions for future work, including potential threats to internal, external, and construct validity. With respect to internal validity, although the integration metric was robustly associated with fluency, a substantial portion of variance in fluency ratings remains unexplained. Future work could therefore develop more precise objective indices of fluency and further clarify which additional perceptual components contribute to this rapid processing. Regarding external validity, although we focus on visual art and compare our results with natural scenes, aesthetic experience extends to other domains such as digital media (e.g., websites and user interfaces) and product/industrial design. It remains an open question whether the same stage-wise processing patterns—and the integration metric—generalize or differentiate across those domains. In terms of construct validity, the DCNN layer-wise computation used in the study represent an operational computational definition. It should therefore be interpreted with caution and may not be equated directly with a psychological construct or a specific neural architecture. Similarly, although DCNN depth provides a convenient stage-resolved analytical framework, it should not be taken to imply a strict one-to-one correspondence with discrete neural processing stages. Further behavioral and neuroimaging work will be necessary to determine the extent to which it reflects shared underlying processes. Finally, the VGG models used here were pretrained for object recognition. This choice is appropriate for our stimuli, which typically contain multiple objects elements, and we treat the networks as feature extractors rather than task-optimized models. Nevertheless, for domains where aesthetic judgments may depend on more specialized representations (e.g., face attractiveness or product-design aesthetics), it may be valuable to evaluate whether task- or domain-adapted models (e.g., fine-tuned networks or models trained on domain-specific datasets) yield different patterns of integration and prediction.

Experimental model and study participant details

The LOAD18 dataset included 301 participants (33% female; mean age = 33.28 years, SD = 11.26, range = 18–71 years).

As for LAPIS,28 the dataset included 532 participants (41% female; mean age = 33.42 years, SD = 11.74, range = 18–71 years).

Method details

Leuven orthogonalized art dataset (load): Behavioral ratings and participants

LOAD18 is a Western painting image dataset in which multiple dimensions of processing are partially dissociated and systematically balanced across other dimensions (content × emotional valence × liking × style/level of abstractness). It also includes an extensive collection of aesthetic appraisals, such as liking and fluency. The dataset consists of 343 paintings and is available as an open resource (https://osf.io/cufnj/). Notably, while LOAD contains abstract art, the degree of abstraction is limited—even in the most abstract paintings, viewers can still identify human figures, non-human elements, or shapes.

Each painting in the LOAD dataset was rated by 50 or 51 participants. The full stimulus set was divided into six distinct subsets, with each subset rated by a different group of participants, resulting in a total of 301 participants. Participants were at least 17 years old, reported good mental health, had no color vision deficiencies, and came from a range of countries. The average age was 33.28 years (SD = 11.26), with an age range of 18 to 71 years.

For behavioral ratings, we used only the liking and fluency data from LOAD. Before rating, participants were provided with detailed explanations of all relevant terminology (see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/gf3k8_v1, Supplementary Materials, for more details). For fluency, participants rated each image on a 100-point scale, ranging from low fluency to high fluency. High fluency indicates that the image was easily processed and understood by the participant. For liking, participants answered the question, “How much do you like the artwork?” using a 7-point Likert scale (I dislike it a lot, I dislike it, I dislike it somewhat, I neither dislike nor like it, I like it somewhat, I like it, I like it a lot).

Leuven art personalized image set (LAPIS) dataset: Behavioral ratings and participants

LAPIS28 is an image dataset consisting of 11,683 paintings rated for beauty by a large number of participants (https://sites.google.com/view/lapisdataset). The paintings were sourced from WikiArt and span 26 artistic styles (based on WikiArt’s style labels), covering a diverse range of motifs.

Each painting was rated by an average of 27.2 participants. The participants came from various countries, were at least 17 years old, in good mental health, and had no color blindness. They rated beauty for each image on a 100-point scale, ranging from low to high beauty.

Since there is an overlap between LAPIS and LOAD, we removed duplicate paintings from LAPIS for analysis, resulting in 11,345 unique paintings. In addition, the authors of LAPIS labeled the genres. For the LAPIS-Landscapes subset analysis, we selected paintings categorized as landscape or cityscape based on these genre labels, resulting in 2,742 paintings.

To investigate whether abstractness influences beauty and integration measures, we further collaborated with art historian Dr. Stephanie De Winter, who developed four broad style categories based on degrees of abstraction. The first category is Representational (or figurative) art, which includes artworks that depict recognizable subjects. The second category, Representational abstract art – identifiable, consists of styles such as Impressionism, Expressionism, Fauvism, and Cubism, where subjects remain discernible despite abstraction. The third category, Non-representational abstraction – geometric, includes movements like Constructivism, Suprematism, Post-Abstract Expressionism, Optical (Op) Art, and Minimal Art, characterized by structured, geometric compositions. Finally, the fourth category, Non-representational abstraction – lyrical, encompasses styles such as Action Painting, Color Field Painting, and Post-Painterly Abstraction, which emphasize expressive, fluid forms without clear representational elements. Further details can be found in the LAPIS manuscript.

DCNN analysis: Integration measure

The integration measure followed the method outlined by Nara and Kaiser,1 with their scripts available at https://zenodo.org/records/10930709. This approach is based on the idea that when an image is well-integrated, it is perceived as less complex and more fluent, meaning that its overall structure is simpler than the sum of its individual visual features. To quantify this, the method evaluates how effectively a whole image can be predicted based on a combination of its individual parts. The lower level of correlation implies the higher integration and fluency. Finally, the sign of the resulting correlations was flipped to yield a measure of integration.

VGG16 was the main DCNN model used in this study. We used two pretrained VGG16 networks as feature extractors: an ImageNet-trained model and a Stylized-ImageNet-trained model.32 The integration measure protocol began with rescaling images to 640 × 640 pixels. Note that we have verified that rescaling does not influence the results, as shown in Figure S7. After rescaling, we applied spatial masks to systematically remove half of each image. Following the approach of Nara and Kaiser,1 we created five spatial scales, dividing images into grids of 2 × 2, 4 × 4, 8 × 8, 16 × 16, and 32 × 32 squares. Each mask retained either all odd or all even squares, ensuring that two complementary halves were generated for each image at every spatial scale. The full images and their corresponding halves were then passed through the neural network. Layer-wise activation patterns were extracted separately from the convolutional and fully connected layers (Figure 2A), yielding 16 layers in total. Finally, we computed the correlation between the activation patterns of the full image and the averaged activation patterns of its two corresponding halves at each spatial scale and each layer. The correlation was then sign-inverted to obtain the integration measure. We also applied our implementation of these methods to the stimulus set and ratings of Nara and Kaiser,1 and verified that we could fully replicate their findings on their dataset. Thus, any divergence in findings using our stimulus sets are not caused by a discrepancy in the implementation of the methods and scripts.

Visual features associated with fluency

We used the R package imagefluency (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/imagefluency/index.html) to compute five visual features related to fluency.31 These features include visual simplicity, visual symmetry (separately measured along the horizontal and vertical axes), visual contrast, and visual self-similarity.

Visual simplicity is defined as the inverse of image complexity and is estimated through image compression algorithms. Simple images contain less visual information and therefore achieve higher compression rates, whereas complex images are harder to compress and yield lower simplicity values. Visual symmetry is quantified by comparing pixel similarity across mirror axes. Both vertical and horizontal symmetry are computed, with the algorithm allowing slight shifts of the axis to account for perceptual variations in symmetry detection. Visual contrast is measured as the root mean square (RMS) contrast, corresponding to the standard deviation of normalized pixel intensities; higher values indicate stronger luminance variation. Finally, visual self-similarity was quantified from the slope of the log–log power spectrum, reflecting the degree of fractal-like statistical regularities in the image. Together, these measures provide objective indices of basic aesthetic principles that support fluent cognitive processing.

Correlation analysis

For LOAD, we computed Spearman correlations between subjective fluency ratings or liking and the integration measure, applying FDR correction to all results. Additionally, for correlations between the integration measure and visual features associated with fluency, we used Spearman correlations with FDR correction. Similarly, we performed Spearman correlations between fluency ratings and visual features previously associated with fluency, applying the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction.

For LAPIS and LAPIS-Landscapes, we conducted Spearman correlations between beauty ratings and the integration measure, with all results Bonferroni corrected. We also examined correlations between the integration measure and visual features associated with fluency using Spearman correlations, applying Bonferroni correction.

Note that we applied both FDR and Bonferroni correction in the data analysis. The choice of correction method was based on the data structure: When the dataset had a small sample size (e.g., LOAD) but required correction for multiple comparisons many times (e.g., DCNN integration with like, involving 80 comparisons), we opted for FDR to reduce the possibility of Type II errors. In contrast, when dealing with larger sample sizes (e.g., LAPIS), we applied Bonferroni correction.

Variance decomposition analysis

We quantified the unique and shared contributions of five predictors to subjective fluency ratings: a DCNN-derived integration metric and four fluency-related visual features (simplicity, vertical symmetry, horizontal symmetry, and self-similarity). To place predictors on a common scale and reduce sensitivity to distributional differences, each predictor was rank-transformed and z-scored prior to analysis. For each layer and spatial scale, we fit a full linear model including all predictors and estimated each predictor’s unique explained variance as the reduction in R2 when that predictor was removed from the full model (namely, ΔR2). We additionally computed the shared variance between the integration metric and the four fluency-related visual features. In short, we used three models: a full model including integration and all fluency-related visual features, an all fluency-related visual features-only model, and an integration-only model. The shared component is Rshared_inte_vis2=Rfull2−(Rfull2−Rvis2)−(Rfull2−Rinte2).

RIDGE regression analysis using DCNN activations to predict aesthetic liking

To test whether features represented in deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) could predict aesthetic appraisal, we used activation patterns extracted from a pretrained VGG-16 network to predict liking ratings for individual paintings. Importantly, the DCNN itself was not further trained or fine-tuned on the LOAD. Rather, pretrained VGG-16 were used as a feature extractor to provide input for a ridge regression model, following a standard decoding approach widely used in neuroscience and computational modeling.42 Notably, the sample size in the present analysis (N = 343) is appropriate for this type of analysis and is comparable to sample sizes used in related studies.42

We implemented a nested cross-validation framework (10 outer folds, 5 inner folds) with 15 bootstrap iterations per layer. For each VGG-16 layer, the activation maps were flattened into one-dimensional feature vectors for each painting. These activation patterns served as input to the regression model, with liking scores as the output. Prior to modeling, all data were z-scored using parameters derived from the training data to ensure normalization was applied independently across folds. To reduce dimensionality and enhance signal relevance, feature selection was performed using univariate Pearson correlations between features and the normalized liking scores. The top 300 features were retained for model training.

For each bootstrap iteration, nested cross-validation was used to optimize the ridge regression regularization parameter (λ), with λ values sampled logarithmically from 10 to 10,000. For each inner fold, models were trained on 80% of the training data and validated on the remaining 20%, using the correlation between predicted and actual liking scores as the evaluation metric. The λ yielding the highest average validation accuracy was selected for final model training. The outer loop then used this best λ to train a model on the full training set and predict liking scores for the held-out test set. Prediction accuracy was quantified by the Pearson correlation between predicted and actual liking scores. This process was repeated for each fold and bootstrap iteration.

The mean prediction accuracy across bootstraps was computed for each VGG-16 layer. This analysis provided a layer-wise profile of how well DCNN activations could predict human aesthetic liking judgments. Final results were saved and used in a one-sample t-test to evaluate how well the model based on raw DCNN activation patterns could predict liking, comparing to a benchmark value of r = 0.31. This threshold was chosen based on highest correlation observed between integration and liking. Bonferroni correction was applied across layers to control for multiple comparisons.

To benchmark the predictive performance of the DCNN model against the inter-subject consistency of human participants’ aesthetic ratings, we further bootstrapped the DCNN’s final-layer prediction 300 times to generate a comparison distribution.

Notably, we used L2 regularization because DCNN feature vectors are high-dimensional and can exhibit collinearity across units within layers. L2 regularization yields a stable linear readout in this regime by shrinking weights while retaining correlated predictors, and it has been widely used for predicting neural and behavioral measures from high-dimensional feature spaces.42

Inter-subject consistency of human ratings

To benchmark the predictive performance of the DCNN model, we evaluated the internal consistency of human participants’ aesthetic ratings. Specifically, we computed inter-subject liking similarity by correlating each participant’s liking ratings with the mean ratings of all other participants (using a leave-one-out approach). This procedure was applied to every participant in the LOAD dataset, yielding a distribution of correlation coefficients that quantifies how well each individual’s ratings align with the group consensus. The resulting histogram served as a baseline for human-level predictability, enabling a direct comparison between the DCNN’s prediction accuracy and the natural variability observed among individuals.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB R2022b and Python 3.8. Correlation analyses were used to examine the relationships between integration and fluency (Figure 3), visual features and fluency (Figure 4), visual features and integration (Figure 4), integration and liking (Figure 5), and integration and beauty (Figure 5). ANOVAs were used to test the main effect of artistic style on integration and beauty (Figure 6). One-sample t-tests were used to assess whether the prediction accuracy of ridge regression models exceeded baseline performance (Figure 7). Variance decomposition analyses were used to quantify the relative contributions of integration and visual features to subjective fluency (Figure 4). Details of each analysis, including the statistical tests, sample sizes, correction methods, and exact p values, are reported in the corresponding figure legends and STAR Methods sections.
